Cognitive-Enhancing Effects of Hesperetin-Rich CNE against Neuronal Damage and Memory Impairment by Regulating AKT/Nrf2 and BDNF/CREB Signaling
Authors: Hee Ju Kim, Yeo Eun Kim, Jae Sik Yu, Gakyung Lee, Sang Suk Kim, Hyun Ok Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitrus nippokoreana, a traditional Korean citrus species, is rich in flavonoids and polyphenols with antioxidant properties; however, its neuroprotective potential remains unclear. Neurodegeneration is closely associated with oxidative stress, neuronal apoptosis, and impaired synaptic signaling. In this study, we investigated the effects of C. nippokoreana peel extract (CNE, 30% ethanol) using in vitro and in vivo models. CNE significantly protected HT22 hippocampal neurons against glutamate-induced oxidative stress by reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and apoptosis, while regulating AKT/Nrf2-associated antioxidant pathways and enhancing BDNF/CREB signaling. In a scopolamine-induced mouse model, oral administration of CNE (50 or 100 mg/kg/day) improved cognitive performance in behavioral tests. CNE also restored the expression of BDNF, CREB, and HO-1, and reduced acetylcholinesterase activity in the hippocampus. Histological analysis confirmed reduced neuronal damage. Furthermore, serum metabolomics revealed modulation of tryptophan metabolism, phosphatidylcholine species, and redox-related pathways. To explore constituents potentially contributing to the neuroprotective effects of CNE, major compounds identified by phytochemical profiling were screened in glutamate-induced HT22 cells. Several phytochemicals exhibited protective effects, suggesting that the biological activity of CNE may result from the combined actions of multiple constituents rather than a single compound. These findings suggest that CNE attenuates oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage and improves cognitive function through coordinated regulation of antioxidant defense, neurotrophic signaling, and cholinergic function.Keywords: Citrus nippokoreana, Neuroprotection, Antioxidant, Glutamate, Scopolamine, Metabolomics

INTRODUCTION

Neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Huntington’s disease (HD), are characterized by progressive loss of neurons, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline. These disorders are commonly associated with oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, all of which contribute to neuronal damage and degeneration of brain function (Kim et al., 2020). Although continuous efforts have been made, effective therapies for neurodegeneration remain limited. Therefore, targeting multiple pathological mechanisms is considered a promising strategy for neuroprotection. Recently, dietary phytochemicals derived from fruits and vegetables have attracted considerable attention as potential neuroprotective agents because of their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.

To model these pathological features, glutamate-induced excitotoxicity is widely used as an in vitro system that mimics neuronal damage (Jeong et al., 2024). Glutamate, the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, plays essential roles in synaptic transmission and plasticity (Kim et al., 2020). However, excessive glutamate accumulation causes overstimulation of receptors, leading to calcium overload, increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neuronal death through oxidative stress-mediated apoptotic pathways (Lee et al., 2014). Scopolamine-induced cognitive impairment is commonly used as an in vivo model of cholinergic dysfunction. Scopolamine, a muscarinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist, disrupts cholinergic transmission, particularly in the hippocampus and cortex, leading to memory deficits (Ansari et al., 2025). This model shows key features of cholinergic dysfunction observed in AD and is widely used to evaluate potential therapeutic agents for cognitive disorders (Park et al., 2025).

Given the complexity of neurodegenerative pathology, attention has increasingly focused on natural extracts with multi-target neuroprotective potential (Chen et al., 2021). Among these, citrus family fruits are well known for containing health-promoting constituents, including vitamin C, carotenoids, polyphenols, limonoids, and dietary fiber. These compounds are especially abundant in the fruit peel, where flavonoids, a class of strong natural antioxidants, are highly concentrated. Flavonoids have been associated with a range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and respiratory protective effects (Jin et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022). Citrus nippokoreana peel ethanol extract (CNE), a Korean traditional citrus variety cultivated on Jeju Island, South Korea, is rich in flavonoids and other phytochemicals known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Preliminary reports, including conference abstracts and a dissertation, have indicated that C. nippokoreana exhibits anti-inflammatory potential in macrophage models (Go et al., 2020; Kurniawati, 2024). However, its impact on the central nervous system remains poorly understood.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective potential of CNE, we focused on key pathological processes associated with neuronal damage, including oxidative stress, apoptosis, and neurotrophic signaling. Excessive ROS generation contributes to neuronal cell death through oxidative stress-mediated apoptotic pathways, whereas endogenous defense pathways such as AKT/Nrf2-associated antioxidant pathways and BDNF/CREB signaling counteract oxidative damage and support neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity (Chen et al., 2025). Based on this framework, we hypothesized that CNE exerts neuroprotective effects by attenuating oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage, suppressing apoptosis, and enhancing neurotrophic signaling through regulation of antioxidant defense and CREB-dependent neuroplasticity.

To further investigate whether these molecular effects are associated with systemic metabolic changes, we performed serum metabolomics profiling in scopolamine-induced mice. Metabolomics analysis enables comprehensive evaluation of biochemical alterations related to neurotransmission, oxidative balance, and membrane integrity. We examined changes in tryptophan metabolism, phosphatidylcholine species, and redox-related metabolites to determine whether metabolic modulation is linked to the neuroprotective effects of CNE (Xu et al., 2024). Overall, this study aimed to elucidate the neuroprotective mechanisms of CNE using integrated molecular and metabolomics approaches.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of C. nippokoreana extract (CNE) and chemical reagents

The C. nippokoreana used in this study was provided by the Citrus Research Institute, National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science (RDA, Korea). The plant material was authenticated by a taxonomist at the institute, and a voucher specimen has been deposited under the National Agrobiodiversity Center accession number IT233685. After washing, the fruit was separated into peel and pulp, and the peel was dried and ground into powder. The dried C. nippokoreana peel powder was extracted with 30% (v/v) ethanol at a solvent-to-solid ratio of 30 mL/g of dried material (1:30, w/v) using ultrasonic extraction for 1 h under temperature-controlled conditions. During the extraction, the temperature was maintained below 40°C to minimize thermal degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive compounds. After extraction, the solution was centrifuged to collect the supernatant, which was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator and freeze-dried to obtain the final 30% ethanol extract of C. nippokoreana peel (CNE) for use in experiments. The extraction yield was 18% (w/w), calculated based on the dry weight of the peel. Chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade, and acetonitrile (HPLC grade) and water (HPLC grade) were purchased from Honeywell Burdick & Jackson (Muskegon, MI, USA). Formic acid (Optima LC/MS) was purchased from Fisher Chemical (Fair Lawn, USA). Hesperidin, hesperetin, neohesperidin, quercetin, tangeritin, naringenin, narirutin, and nobiletin were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Phytochemical profiling of CNE using UPLC-Orbitrap-MS

The metabolite profiling of CNE was performed using a Vanquish Flex ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic separation was conducted on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in distilled water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). UV/Vis detection was performed using a Vanquish Diode Array Detector FG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at wavelengths of 210, 254, and 315 nm. All analysis was conducted at the Biopolymer Research Center for Advanced Materials (Sejong University, Seoul, Korea).

Cell culture and viability

The HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line was kindly provided by Prof. Yong-Keun Jung (Seoul National University, Seoul, Korea). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3×103 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. To assess cytotoxicity, the cells were treated with various concentrations of CNE (25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) for 24 h. To evaluate the protective effect of CNE from glutamate treatment, cells were pretreated with CNE (50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) for 1 h, followed by co-treatment with 5 mM glutamate (L-glutamic acid monosodium salt hydrate, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 23 h. After incubation, 10 µL of EZ-Cytox reagent (DoGENBio Co., Ltd, Seoul, Korea) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Cell viability was measured at 450 nm using a microplate absorbance reader (Multiskan SkyHigh, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Measurement of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). HT22 cells were plated in 60 mm culture dishes at a density of 1×106 cells per dish and incubated for 24 h. Cells were pretreated with CNE (50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) for 1 h, followed by co-treatment with 5 mM glutamate for 18 h. After treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCF-DA in phenol red-free medium in the incubator for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and ROS levels were analyzed based on the percentage of fluorescent-positive cells.

Annexin V/Propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, HT22 cells were plated in 60 mm culture dishes at a density of 1×106 cells per dish and incubated for 24 h. The cells were then pretreated with CNE (50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) for 1 h, followed by co-treatment with 5 mM glutamate for 18 h to induce apoptosis. After treatment, the cells were collected and resuspended in binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) were added to the cell suspension and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence signals were analyzed using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), with 5,000 events recorded per sample. Additionally, stained cells were mounted on glass slides and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to visually confirm apoptosis.

Western blot

The total proteins from HT22 cells and mouse tissues were extracted using PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, Korea). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or 3% BSA in TBST for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies at the dilution ratios specified in Table 1. After washing, the membranes were incubated with a HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:2,000 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and visualized using chemiluminescence (Vilber Fusion system). Band intensities were quantified using EvolutionCapt Edge software. For western blot analysis, membranes were sequentially stripped and reprobed with different primary antibodies. Therefore, identical loading control blots are presented for proteins analyzed from the same membrane.

Isolation of Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Proteins

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared using the NE-PER™ Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Extracted proteins were used for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Animal experimental design and treatments

Male C57BL/6N mice (6 weeks old, weighing 19-21 g) were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, Korea) and housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 3°C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into five groups (n=10 per group): control, scopolamine, scopolamine+donepezil, scopolamine+CNE (50 mg/kg), scopolamine+CNE (100 mg/kg). CNE and donepezil hydrochloride (4 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and administered orally once daily. Scopolamine hydrobromide (2 mg/kg/day; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in PBS and administered intraperitoneally 30 min before behavioral tests to induce cognitive impairment. Vehicle control mice received the same volume of PBS. The doses of CNE (50 and 100 mg/kg/day) were selected based on commonly used ranges for crude plant extracts in neuroprotective studies and preliminary tolerability observations. No signs of gastrointestinal distress, abnormal behavior, or body weight loss were observed during the experimental period. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sejong University and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

The NORT was performed to assess recognition memory, with the procedure modified from a previous study (Kudara et al., 2023). Mice were habituated to an empty gray acrylic box (40×40×40 cm) for 5 min/day over two consecutive days. On the third day (training phase), two identical objects were placed in the box, and mice were allowed to explore for 5 min. Scopolamine was injected immediately after training. On the following day (test phase), one familiar and one novel object were introduced, and exploration was recorded for 5 min. Y-maze test

The Y-maze test was used to evaluate spatial working memory. The maze consisted of three identical gray arms (30 cm long, 8 cm wide, 15 cm high) arranged at 120º angles. Mice were placed at the end of one arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. The sequence and number of arm entries were observed and manually recorded by an experimenter. Passive avoidance test (PAT)

The PAT was conducted using a shuttle box consisting of two compartments, one illuminated and one dark, separated by a partition. Each mouse was initially placed in the illuminated compartment, and upon entry into the dark compartment, an electric shock (0.4 mA, 4 s) was delivered through the grid floor. The latency to re-enter the dark compartment was recorded as the step-through latency. To assess long-term memory, the latency was measured again 24 h later by reintroducing the animal into the illuminated compartment without shock delivery. After each trial, the apparatus was cleaned with 70% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues from the preceding animal.

Cresyl violet solution staining

Cresyl violet solution staining (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was performed to assess neuronal morphology and cell distribution in mouse brain sections. Briefly, paraffin-embedded brain tissues were sectioned at a 5 µm thickness and mounted onto glass slides. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%) to distilled water. The rehydrated sections were incubated in 0.1% cresyl violet acetate solution for 3-5 min at room temperature. After staining, the sections were rinsed briefly in distilled water to remove excess background stain. The sections were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 95%, 100%), cleared in xylene, and cover-lipped with mounting medium. The stained sections were observed and imaged under a light microscope. Neuronal cell bodies were identified by their characteristic Nissl substance staining in the cytoplasm.

PathoGreen histofluorescent staining

PathoGreen™ histofluorescent stain (Biotium, CA, USA) was used to detect neuronal degeneration. Experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the brain was perfused and fixed with 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Then, the brain was cut into 50 μm coronal slices, which were dried at 60°C for 30 min. Slices were incubated with 0.06% KMnO4 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 1,000×PathoGreen™ stock solution at RT for 10 min each. After air-drying, slices were incubated in xylene and then mounted onto glass slides. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Serum metabolomics analysis

Serum metabolomic profiling was performed using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (MS) coupled with a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to investigate systemic metabolic alterations following CNE administration. At the end of behavioral testing, whole blood was collected and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 15 min at 4°C to obtain serum. For metabolite extraction, 90 µL of cold 80% methanol containing internal standard (IS) mixture, comprising 15:0-18:1-d7-PC, succinic acid-d4, cholic acid-d4, alanine-d4, and L-tryptophan-(indole-d5) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to 30 µL of serum. The samples were vortexed for 30 sec and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. A 70 µL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal volumes from each sample. All samples were transferred to LC vials, and 5 µL was injected for analysis. The protocol and procedure were modified from those in a previous study (Lee et al., 2021, 2023). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) under a gradient at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. MS detection was performed in both positive and negative ion modes using full scan acquisition. The data was processed with Compound Discoverer software 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak extraction, alignment, and MS2 fragmentation matching using databases. Multivariate analyses, including PCA clustering, were conducted to evaluate group differences. The reproducibility and reliability of the metabolomic data were ensured through repeated analysis of QC samples across the batch (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparison was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Quantification of Nissl, PathoGreen staining, and Annexin V staining was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For relative quantification, values were normalized to the control group and expressed as percentages of the control.

RESULTS

Phytochemical profiling of CNE using UPLC-Orbitrap-MS

The chemical composition of CNE was profiled using UPLC-Orbitrap-MS analysis as shown in Fig. 1A and 1B. This high-resolution platform provides accurate molecular mass information and fragmentation spectra, enabling structural annotation of metabolites even in the absence of authentic reference compounds. Compared with the given standards, the metabolites identified from the CNE extracts were hesperidin, hesperetin, neohesperidin, quercetin, tangeritin, naringenin, narirutin, and nobiletin. All the identified metabolites were compared with experimental mass data to calculate the ppm value and characterized tentatively based on their mass spectral data. The exact masses were obtained by the possible deprotonated molecules and protonated adducts in the ESI negative and positive mode. The principal metabolites tentatively assigned by comparison with the standards are summarized in Table 2. Identification was based on exact mass measurements within a 5-ppm tolerance and interpretation of MS/MS fragmentation, which were further cross-referenced against entries in the Natural Products Atlas database (https://www.npatlas.org/) and standards (Choi et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2019). To investigate which constituents may contribute to the neuroprotective activity of CNE, major compounds identified through phytochemical profiling were individually evaluated in glutamate-treated HT22 cells using an Ez-Cytox assay (Fig. 1C). Quercetin and hesperetin showed relatively stronger protective effects compared with other tested compounds, whereas several constituents exhibited modest or limited activity. These findings suggest that the neuroprotective effect of CNE may arise from the combined contribution of multiple constituents rather than a single dominant compound.

Neuroprotective effect of CNE on glutamate-induced oxidative stress in HT22 cells

We first evaluated the cytotoxicity of CNE in HT22 cells. As shown in Fig. 2A, CNE treatment significantly increased cell viability at concentrations up to 200 µg/mL, whereas a slight decrease was observed at concentrations above 400 µg/mL. Based on these results, concentrations ranging from 50 to 200 µg/mL were selected for subsequent experiments. To assess the neuroprotective effect of CNE, HT22 cells were pretreated with CNE for 1 h and then exposed to glutamate (5 mM) for 23 h. As shown in Fig. 2B, glutamate treatment markedly reduced cell viability compared with the vehicle control group. In contrast, CNE treatment significantly restored cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with 200 µg/mL recovering viability to near control levels. We next examined whether the protective effect of CNE was associated with modulation of oxidative stress. As shown in Fig. 2C, glutamate treatment significantly increased intracellular ROS levels in HT22 cells, whereas CNE markedly attenuated ROS accumulation in a dose-dependent manner. Collectively, these results indicate that CNE protects HT22 cells against glutamate-induced oxidative stress by reducing ROS accumulation and preserving cell viability, suggesting its potential role in mitigating oxidative stress-mediated neuronal damage.

Activation of the antioxidant defense system in HT22 cells by CNE

To investigate whether the antioxidant effect of CNE is mediated through the AKT/Nrf2 pathway, we first examined the time-dependent activation of AKT, which is known as an upstream regulator of Nrf2 signaling. As shown in Fig. 3A, p-AKT was increased in a time-dependent manner following CNE treatment. We next evaluated whether CNE induces the nuclear translocation of Nrf2. HT22 cells were treated with CNE (200 μg/mL), and the subcellular localization of Nrf2 was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 3B, nuclear Nrf2 expression was significantly increased over time, while cytosolic Nrf2 expression decreased, indicating activation and translocation of Nrf2. To determine whether Nrf2 activation leads to enhanced antioxidant defense under oxidative stress conditions, we examined the expression of downstream target genes in glutamate-treated cells. As shown in Fig. 3C, CNE significantly upregulated the protein expression of GCLC, NQO-1, and HO-1 compared to the glutamate- treated group. Collectively, these findings indicate that CNE activates the AKT/Nrf2 signaling pathway, leading to nuclear translocation of Nrf2 and subsequent induction of cytoprotective antioxidant enzymes, thereby contributing to the attenuation of glutamate-induced oxidative stress.

Regulatory effect of CNE on apoptosis-related protein expression in HT22 cells

We next examined whether the protective effects of CNE against oxidative stress are associated with the regulation of apoptosis in glutamate-treated HT22 cells. Fluorescence imaging using Annexin V staining showed that glutamate markedly increased apoptotic signals, whereas co-treatment with CNE significantly reduced fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4A). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis using Annexin V/PI staining demonstrated that glutamate significantly increased the apoptotic rate (Q2+Q4), which was dose-dependently suppressed by CNE treatment (Fig. 4B). To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. Western blot analysis revealed that glutamate treatment increased the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, indicating activation of pro-apoptotic signaling. In contrast, CNE treatment restored this balance by upregulating Bcl-2 and downregulating Bax expression (Fig. 4C). Taken together, these results suggest that CNE suppresses oxidative stress-induced apoptotic signaling by restoring the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins, thereby contributing to neuronal cell survival.

Activation of BDNF/CREB pathway in HT22 cells by CNE

Given that neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity are regulated by neurotrophic signaling, and may be influenced by oxidative stress, we next investigated whether CNE modulates the BDNF/CREB pathway in glutamate-treated HT22 cells. As shown in Fig. 5A, glutamate treatment significantly decreased the expression of BDNF and p-CREB. In contrast, CNE co-treatment restored their expression in a dose-dependent manner. To investigate whether TrkB-associated signaling is involved in the neuroprotective effects of CNE, we used K252a (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), a selective TrkB inhibitor. HT22 cells were treated with glutamate with or without CNE, in the presence or absence of K252a. As shown in Fig. 5B and 5C, glutamate treatment significantly decreased cell viability and p-CREB expression, whereas CNE co-treatment markedly reversed these effects. However, co-treatment with K252a significantly attenuated the protective effects of CNE on both cell viability and p-CREB expression. These findings suggest that TrkB-associated CREB signaling may contribute to the neuroprotective effects of CNE under oxidative stress conditions.

CNE improves scopolamine-induced memory impairment in behavioral test

To determine whether the neuroprotective effects of CNE observed in vitro translate into improvements in cognitive function in vivo, we established a scopolamine-induced memory impairment model in mice. The overall experimental design, including animal grouping and timeline, is shown in Fig. 6A. Briefly, mice were orally administered CNE (50 or 100 mg/kg/day) or donepezil (4 mg/kg/day), an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, once daily during the experimental period, and scopolamine (2 mg/kg/day) was intraperitoneally injected prior to behavioral testing. Cognitive performance was assessed using the novel object recognition test (NORT), passive avoidance test (PAT), and Y-maze test. As shown in Fig. 6B-6E, scopolamine treatment significantly impaired performance across all tests, as indicated by decreased discrimination index in the NORT, reduced step through latency in the PAT, and decreased spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze. In contrast, CNE administration significantly improved cognitive performance in a dose-dependent manner, with effects comparable to those of donepezil. No significant differences were observed in total arm entries among the experimental groups (Fig. 6E), indicating that the observed changes in spontaneous alternation were not due to alterations in locomotor activity, but rather reflect improvements in spatial working memory. These results demonstrate that CNE effectively ameliorates cognitive deficits in vivo, supporting its neuroprotective potential and suggesting that its beneficial effects may be associated with the modulation of oxidative stress and synaptic signaling pathways observed in vitro.

Protective effect of CNE against neuronal damage in mice

To further validate whether the improvements in cognitive function were associated with structural neuronal protection, we performed histological analyses of the hippocampus using Nissl staining and PathoGreen fluorescence staining. Nissl staining revealed that scopolamine injection markedly reduced neuronal density, particularly in the CA3 region, while no significant changes were observed in the CA1 and dentate gyrus (DG, hilus) regions (Fig. 7A, 7B). CNE treatment significantly restored neuronal density in the CA3 region, showing a comparable effect to donepezil. In contrast, PathoGreen fluorescence staining showed a pronounced increase in neuronal degeneration across all hippocampal subregions (CA1, CA3, and DG, including DG+hilus) following scopolamine treatment (Fig. 7C, 7D). CNE treatment markedly reduced fluorescence intensity in all regions. Quantitative analysis confirmed that both 50 and 100 mg/kg CNE significantly decreased PathoGreen intensity compared to the scopolamine group, indicating a dose-dependent protective effect against neuronal degeneration. Nissl staining primarily detects overt neuronal loss, whereas PathoGreen fluorescence is more sensitive to early or subtle neuronal degeneration (Kadar et al., 2009; Rosario et al., 2022; Schmued et al., 1997). Taken together, these results demonstrate that CNE protects against both overt neuronal loss and early-stage neurodegeneration, supporting its neuroprotective effects observed in behavioral and molecular analyses.

Effect of CNE on cholinergic, neurotrophic, and antioxidant markers in the hippocampus and cortex

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of CNE observed in behavioral and histological analyses, we examined the expression of cholinergic, neurotrophic, and antioxidant markers in the hippocampus and cortex. Scopolamine treatment significantly increased acetylcholinesterase (AChE) expression in both brain regions (Fig. 8A), indicating cholinergic dysfunction. In contrast, CNE administration markedly reduced AChE expression to levels comparable to those observed in the donepezil-treated group. Consistent with the in vitro findings, scopolamine markedly decreased the expression of neurotrophic signaling markers, including BDNF and p-CREB, whereas CNE treatment significantly restored their expression (Fig. 8B). In addition, scopolamine reduced the expression of antioxidant markers GCLC and HO-1, indicating impaired endogenous antioxidant defense. CNE treatment significantly upregulated both GCLC and HO-1 expression (Fig. 8C). Taken together, these results indicate that CNE alleviates scopolamine-induced cognitive impairment by restoring cholinergic function, enhancing neurotrophic signaling, and activating antioxidant defense pathways, consistent with the mechanisms observed in vitro.

CNE-mediated systemic changes in neurotransmission and oxidative stress

To further support the molecular mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of CNE, we performed serum metabolomic profiling to assess systemic metabolic alterations. Multivariate analysis revealed a clear separation between the scopolamine and CNE-treated groups in both positive and negative ionization modes, indicating distinct metabolic profiles (Fig. 9A). Among these altered metabolites, several metabolites associated with neurotransmission and redox balance were significantly altered. Tryptophan-derived metabolites, including 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) and serotonin (5-HT), were decreased in the scopolamine group but were restored following CNE administration. A similar recovery pattern was observed for 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (Fig. 9B). In addition, several phosphatidylcholine (PC) species and acetylcholine levels were reduced in the scopolamine group and recovered toward control levels after CNE treatment (Fig. 9C).

Furthermore, antioxidant-related metabolites, including the GSH/GSSG ratio and adenosine, were significantly increased by CNE treatment (Fig. 9D), indicating improved redox balance. These findings suggest that CNE modulates key metabolic pathways related to neurotransmission, membrane integrity, and oxidative stress (Fig. 9E). Collectively, these results indicate that CNE restores scopolamine-induced metabolic alterations in the tryptophan, glycerophospholipid, and glutathione pathways, supporting the neuroprotective mechanisms associated with antioxidant defense and synaptic signaling observed in vitro and in vivo.

DISCUSSION

Citrus nippokoreana, a native citrus species cultivated on Jeju Island, remains largely unexplored in food- and nutrition-related research. In contrast, other citrus species such as C. sunki, C. reticulata, and C. unshiu have long been used in East Asia for gastrointestinal and inflammatory disorders, and contemporary studies confirm that citrus flavonoids such as hesperidin and nobiletin exert potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Kim et al., 2019, 2020). In this context, our findings provide the first evidence that CNE exerts neuroprotective effects, extending the functional relevance of citrus-derived compounds to the central nervous system (Fig. 1B). UPLC-Orbitrap-MS analysis revealed that CNE contains multiple citrus flavonoids, including hesperidin, nobiletin, narirutin, and naringenin, which have been individually reported to exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties (Zhang et al., 2016). Although individual flavonoids showed neuroprotective effects in glutamate-treated HT22 cells (Fig. 1C), the overall efficacy of CNE is likely attributable to the synergistic and multi-target actions of its composite phytochemicals. This is particularly relevant given that polymethoxyflavones such as nobiletin often exhibit strong neuroprotective potency despite lower abundance (Mitra et al., 2022). Although several identified flavonoids exhibited neuroprotective activity in the screening assay, the overall efficacy of CNE is unlikely to be attributed to a single constituent. Rather, multiple phytochemicals may act synergistically to contribute to the observed biological effects.

Mechanistically, CNE attenuated glutamate-induced oxidative stress in HT22 cells through regulation of AKT/Nrf2-associated antioxidant pathways and upregulation of HO-1, NQO-1, and GCLC, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis and suppressing apoptotic cell death (Fig. 3). These effects are consistent with previous reports describing antioxidant regulation by citrus flavonoids (Basha et al., 2025). Interestingly, glutamate treatment alone also increased certain antioxidant-related proteins compared with the control group, which may reflect an endogenous compensatory response against oxidative stress. CNE treatment further enhanced this antioxidant response, suggesting reinforcement of cellular defense mechanisms under oxidative stress conditions. In addition, CNE restored BDNF and p-CREB expression (Fig. 5A), and inhibition using K252a suggested that TrkB-associated neurotrophic signaling contributes to its protective effects (Fig. 5B, 5C). These findings support coordinated interactions between antioxidant defense and neurotrophic signaling pathways in the neuroprotective effects of CNE. Furthermore, given that neurodegeneration involves complex interactions between neurons and glial cells, the anti-inflammatory effects of CNE in BV2 microglia provide an additional complementary mechanism. CNE suppressed LPS-induced NO production and iNOS and COX-2 expression (Supplementary Fig. 2), consistent with inhibition of MAPK signaling pathways (Morimoto et al., 2023), thereby potentially contributing to neuronal protection under inflammatory conditions. These findings are also in agreement with previous reports showing that citrus flavonoids regulate microglial activation and inflammatory responses (Wang et al., 2018). In vivo, CNE ameliorated scopolamine-induced cognitive impairment, as demonstrated by improved performance in the NORT, Y-maze, and PAT tests (Fig. 6B-6D). Histological analyses further confirmed neuroprotective effects, with Nissl staining indicating preservation of neuronal density in the CA3 region (Fig. 7A, 7B) and PathoGreen staining detecting broader protection across hippocampal subregions (Fig. 7C, 7D) (Pak et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). These findings suggest that CNE protects against both overt neuronal loss and early neurodegenerative changes. Consistently, CNE restored the expression of BDNF, p-CREB, and CREB while reducing AChE levels in the hippocampus and cortex (Fig. 8), indicating recovery of cholinergic function and neurotrophic signaling. In addition, CNE treatment showed a tendency to restore antioxidant-related proteins such as HO-1 and GCLC, suggesting improvement of endogenous antioxidant defense pathways. These findings are generally consistent with the molecular mechanisms observed in vitro and with previous reports demonstrating the involvement of these pathways in cognitive function and neuroprotection (Kim et al., 2020; Mosalam et al., 2025).

Serum metabolomics analysis provided additional systemic insights. As shown in Fig. 9B, the restoration of tryptophan metabolites, including 5-HTP, serotonin, and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid, may contribute to improved neurotrophic signaling and cognitive outcomes (Farooqui et al., 2000; Roth et al., 2021), while recovery of phosphatidylcholine species suggests improved membrane integrity and cholinergic neurotransmission (Fig. 9C) (Lee et al., 2018; Simpson et al., 2015; Whiley et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2019; Yoon et al., 2024). Increased GSH/GSSG ratio and adenosine levels (Fig. 9D) further indicate enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity (Ansari and Scheff, 2011; Detcheverry et al., 2023) and neuromodulatory activity (Kwon et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2022). Although the blood-brain barrier permeability of individual CNE constituents was not assessed in this study, previous reports have shown that flavonoids including quercetin and hesperetin can reach the brain and exert neuroprotective effects (Hasan et al., 2023; Youdim et al., 2003). Thus, these compounds may have contributed to the biological activities observed in the present study (Rarinca et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the neuroprotective efficacy of CNE is unlikely to be explained by a single constituent alone and may instead result from the combined effects of multiple phytochemicals present in the extract (Youdim et al., 2003). Further studies are required to investigate the pharmacokinetic properties and brain accessibility of these compounds. These effects may arise from a combination of indirect peripheral modulation and potential brain accessibility of low molecular weight flavonoids, warranting further pharmacokinetic investigation. Taken together, the present results suggest that antioxidant regulation through the AKT/Nrf2 pathway may represent a key mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of CNE. However, the recovery of cholinergic function and neurotrophic signaling observed in scopolamine-treated mice indicates that additional pathways are also involved. Therefore, the beneficial effects of CNE are likely attributable to the coordinated regulation of multiple pathways rather than a single mechanism. Overall, our findings highlight that C. nippokoreana, though underexplored compared with other citrus species, possesses significant neuroprotective potential. By regulating oxidative stress, neurotrophic signaling, cholinergic dysfunction, and metabolic homeostasis, CNE represents a multi-target strategy suited to the multifactorial nature of neurodegenerative disorders (Fig. 10). Importantly, the pleiotropic yet moderate modulation of multiple signaling and metabolic pathways observed in this study aligns well with the conceptual framework of functional foods, which aim to restore physiological homeostasis rather than exert strong single-target pharmacological effects. These results extend the health-promoting scope of citrus species to the nervous system and support the development of C. nippokoreana as a functional food and nutraceutical candidate. Future studies employing lipidomics, gut-brain axis-related metabolic analyses, and genetic manipulation, such as Nrf2 or BDNF modulation, will further refine its mechanistic and translational relevance.

Proposed schematic illustration of the neuroprotective mechanism of CNE and associated metabolic pathway modulation.

In conclusion, Citrus nippokoreana peel extract (CNE) exerted neuroprotective effects against glutamate-induced oxidative stress in HT22 cells and improved cognitive impairment in scopolamine-treated mice. CNE attenuated oxidative stress and apoptotic cell death while enhancing antioxidant defense and BDNF/CREB-associated neurotrophic signaling. In addition, serum metabolomics analysis suggested that CNE modulated metabolic pathways associated with redox balance and neurotransmission. Collectively, these findings suggest that CNE may serve as a promising natural therapeutic candidate for neurodegenerative disorders through multi-target neuroprotective mechanisms.
