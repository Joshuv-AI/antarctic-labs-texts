Yield estimation and operational parameter evaluation of three crude oils using aspen HYSYS simulation-based
Authors: Wan Nazihah Liyana Wan Jusoh, Madiah Binti Omar, Kishore Bingi, Rosdiazli Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Yield estimation significantly contributes to oil refineries, especially for the newcomer crude oil, and concurrently diminishes the sole dependency on laboratory analysis. Implementing simulation modelling, Aspen HYSYS, for designing distillation unit operations has improved yield prediction. This paper aims to simulate a distillation column using Aspen HYSYS V14 software to estimate the yield of crude oil without specification of product flowrate. Three crude oils from the actual refinery data were applied and compared in understanding the factors affecting yield flowrate and column temperature profiles, such as the crude oil feed flowrate, bottom steam flowrate, and column pressure (top and bottom). The developed model has successfully predicted the yield of crude oil with a comparable error, demonstrating a minimum temperature profile error ranging from 0.07% to 1.04% (except for the bottom column), and deviations of 1.60% to 20.48% for flowrate across three samples. The parameter evaluation found that a higher bottom steam flowrate would reduce the light and medium products by 2% and increase the heavy products by approximately 5%. A higher pressure of the top column significantly affects flowrate of kerosene by the increment of ~ 2.45%, while the increase of bottom pressure slightly reduces the products flowrate, especially, for the heavier range.

Introduction

The oil and gas industry is a fast-paced sector that contributes substantially to global income generation, with the annual gross domestic product (GDP) worldwide supported by 32%, and Malaysia, in particular, contributing up to 20% in 20231,2. To align with demand and technological advancements, Aspen Technology offers a great opportunity to ease simulation and replicate actual processes. Aspen Technology is a global software provider, offering services that mimic the various real-world chemical process operations, such as chemical production and oil and gas refining3. It intentionally enables refineries to simulate, visualize and analyse the process while minimizing energy consumption, maximizing business efficiency, and optimizing sustainability and safety goals.

Most oil refineries rely on process simulation for the purposes of optimization and prediction of final products. The crude oil from the oil reservoir is converted into various products such as naphtha, kerosene, diesel, and gas oil (AGO) through the distillation column4. The distillation column is the common unit operation that focuses on separating the crude oil based on its boiling point and composition, concurrently providing an excellent overview of the yield expected. The refineries typically model the simulation by replicating the actual process unit and operating conditions, such as temperature, pressure, and flow. A study utilized a mixture of three types of Mexican crude oil to provide a dynamic simulation of a distillation column by Aspen HYSYS5. The simulation starts with steady-state modelling before being converted to a dynamic simulation; however, the simulation specified the final product flowrates and no yield estimation being mentioned and available in this study. Besides, a study using Bonny crude oil resulted in the product prediction error between 1.9% and 7% by using the oil properties input data, including the light end composition, density and boiling temperature6. The simulation provides a good result with low errors however no yield estimation was considered as product rates were specified.

Some literature focuses on predicting crude oil from the crude assay by simulating the actual process without the final product flowrate specification. A study using Basrah crude oil, blended both light and medium oil to predict the production rates7. The crude assay information was added, and a distillation simulation was conducted using the “Three Side Stream Crude” column as the main column; however, the study mentioned that the simulation was facing failure in converging, making the simulation non-replicable. The study concluded that the production target of diesel was aligned with simulation findings8. did a simulation on Sarir crude oil in separating the light and heavy naphtha, kerosene, diesel, and residual using Aspen HYSYS V11. The simulation column is equipped with 34 stages, and the crude oil properties information supplied by experimental assay, including API gravity, American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) distillation, and true boiling point (TBP) distillation. The product’s prediction error ranges from 9.22% to 12.5%, except for naphtha, which has the lowest error of 0.36%.

However, there is limited literature, especially on the methodology for yield estimation, which is not dependent on product flowrate or volume specification. The current literature primarily focuses on optimization by specifying the end product fractions and required yield, causing ignorance and interruption in actual yield prediction, which does not depend on the crude assay properties. Besides, the variability of crude oil composition also affects the development of model simulation and prediction capability, as it potentially requires additional processes and parameters. This study used three different crude assays to understand the variability of prediction and aims to develop a distillation column simulation without the specification of the product fractions and flowrates. The product separation was categorized solely based on the crude oil properties and relied on the draw stage temperature and stage number.

Besides, the study aimed to address the lack of systematic evaluation of operational parameters in crude oil distillation. Although previous studies have explored various operational parameter changes, the effect of individual parameters on the predicted assay remains unclear and limited, especially in the variation of crude oil assays. The effects of feed crude oil flowrate, bottom steam flowrate, and column pressure at both the top and bottom columns were examined to determine their individual influences on the final yield. Through the parameter evaluation, this study advances beyond the existing literature by providing a deeper understanding of how the individual factors affecting the yield optimization vary between the crude oil assays. Additionally, detailed steps in developing the simulation model were provided in this study to increase the replicability and accommodate the crude oil yield estimation.

Methodology

Crude assay characterization

This study was conducted using Aspen HYSYS V14 software, developed by Aspen Technology (Massachusetts, USA), and the crude assay was obtained from Petroliam Nasional Berhad (PETRONAS), a local Malaysian oil and gas company3. In simulating Aspen HYSYS, the crude assay is a necessary information representing the feed material condition and properties. The crude assay properties, which include density and boiling point, are compulsory information; meanwhile, the light hydrocarbon composition can be introduced as supporting material. The addition of light hydrocarbons simultaneously improves the simulation by initiating the prediction of hydrocarbons; otherwise, it would be entirely predicted by the software. The Peng-Robinson thermodynamic package was chosen as the property package based on its superior behaviour for the prediction of liquid densities.

Based on the specified assay properties, the oil manager package was used to input the assay data and convert it into the blend oil, which was then specified as feed crude oil. The blend oil also provides a preliminary analysis and results in oil properties such as calculated TBP, mass fraction and product distribution plot. The distribution plot then predicts the crude oil into a few products based on the cut point temperature. The results collected from the assay characterization were presented and discussed in the discussion section.

Model simulation

The simulation process begins after a successful characterization of the feed crude oil, which involves changing the software interface from the property tab to the simulation workbook. Properties such as the operating column, utilities, controller, and valve can be obtained from the model palette. As this study focuses on simulating the distillation column, the simulation interface is presented in Fig. 1, which is occupied by a series of heaters and a distillation column. The blended oil, which is defined as crude oil, was introduced to the heater. A series of heaters was introduced at the early stage of the process to gradually increase the temperature of the crude oil until it reached 340 °C. This feed temperature is in the flash zone allowed for vaporisation of light and medium fraction while maintaining the heavier components in liquid form. Based on the literature, the usage of multiple heaters in series or parallel would reduce the reboiler duty and minimize energy consumption by utilizing the heat recovered from the high-temperature process stream9,10. The outlet stream of Heater 3 was specified as ATM Feed, which was then introduced at the bottom of the distillation column.

Fig. 1Simulation overview of crude oil distillation column equipped with three heaters and a distillation unit.

Simulation overview of crude oil distillation column equipped with three heaters and a distillation unit.

The simulation process of the distillation column (T-100) was conducted by following the Aspen HYSYS manual with some adjustments depending on the process3. A refluxed absorber was added to the simulation workbook obtained from the separator section of the model palette. The refluxed absorber was named as a crude oil distillation column, where the separation of characterized crude oil into products, such as off gas, naphtha, light kerosene, heavy kerosene, and gas oil (AGO). In the refluxed absorber column input expert, the number of stages was set to 35, and the feed entered at stage 33. 35 stages of the distillation column are the optimized stage for good fractionation. Reducing the stage number causes less accurate product draw temperature, while increasing it does not provide any significant changes to the yield and leads to a higher cost. The feed stage slightly above the bottom column ensures an efficient separation, good contact between vapor-liquid and no interruption occurred in the reboiler. The bottom steam stream was added as a heat generator for the column, as specified in Table 1 and fed at the bottom stage (stage 35). The condenser at the top column was selected as partial, which generated two overhead outlets in gas and liquid form, off gas and naphtha. Other specifications were specified, including the wastewater stream, the bottom liquid outlet stream designated as ATM residue, and the condenser energy stream labelled as cond duty.

On the second page of the input expert (pressure profile), certain settings were applied, including a condenser pressure of 140 kPa, a condenser pressure drop of 60 kPa, and a bottom stage pressure of 230 kPa. Meanwhile, the next page is the optional temperature estimates page, which is not compulsory and may be excluded. According to the literature, the estimated temperature was defined as the condenser temperature estimated at 40 °C, the top stage temperature at 120 °C, and the bottom stage temperature at 340 °C. On page 4 of the input expert, only the vapor rate was specified as 0 kgmole/h and the others remained unspecified. After completing the column design, the interface will move to the Atmos column property window.

In the Atmos column, the Design and Monitor page is used to deactivate the non-specified parameter and only keep the Vap Prod Rate as active. Then, specify the distillate rate as 150 m3/h on a volume basis before activating the parameter, making only both the vap prod rate and distillate rate as the active checkboxes, and automatically set the degree of freedom to zero. Once all specified, click the “Run” button to start the simulation and ensure a “Converged” notification appears in the green colour button.

Settings for side operation and product cut point

Side Operation

The side operation was introduced to draw the product from the main distillation column using Side Strippers, which are available in the HYSYS software. There are two types of side strippers (SS) available: the reboiled stripper and the steam stripper. For this simulation, three side strippers were defined for the three side products draw, one reboiled side stripper for kerosene, and two steam stripped side strippers for kerosene/LGO (light gas oil) and gas oil. The pump around (PA) was added to enhance the efficiency of the column by reducing the column temperature, allowing for higher heat recovery and controlling the liquid flow in the other column section. This PA operated by drawing a hot liquid stream, then cooled before returning to a higher stage and the PA rate was fixed throughout all model simulation. The side operation for each product draw was designed sequentially in the Side Ops Input Expert, available in the Atmos column on the Side Ops page.

The first side operation specification was described for gas oil using steam stripped side strippers. The information was defined by clicking on the “Add side stripper” button and following the details in Table 2 before installation. Then, proceed with the pump around specification at the same Side Ops Input Expert to define the specification as mentioned in Table 2, and then install. In the Atmos column, three specifications are required to ensure a zero degree of freedom and allow the simulation to run. The gas oil SS product flow, PA rate, and PA duty were defined as stated in Table 2, and ensure that the active box is checked. The gas oil steam stream needs to be specified as mentioned in Table 1. Once all the required information has been defined, the simulation starts and allows the system to converge.

As mentioned in the Aspen HYSYS manual, some modifications to distillation column operating conditions are required to manage the energy and heat balance due to the absence of a reboiler. The addition of a side duty energy stream requires careful consideration for further specification, as some amendments are necessary (activation and deactivation of the specification). An energy stream named Trim Duty was added to the main flowsheet without specifying any duty. In the Atmos Column (design and connection page), the inlet stage of Trim Duty was specified to 28_Main Tower; however, it still has 1 degree of freedom and requires additional specification. The overflash is added using the Add Spec function and column liquid flow, specified at stage 27_Main Tower, with a volume flow of 23.00 m³/h. In the Design and Monitor tab, activate the overflash and deactivate the Kerosene SS Boil Up Ratio in the active column. To ensure a constant heat duty specification, the Kerosene SS_Reb Duty was set as active and specified with 7.9 × 107 kJ/h. The simulation was returned to the monitor page, and the degree of freedom was set to zero to run and converged simulation.

Petroleum assay

Petroleum assay analysis is one of the stream analysis features available in Aspen HYSYS, used to support the prediction of product flowrate using the cut point temperature. This petroleum assay is attached to each of the product streams, and the specification was conducted in the property section. By indicating the petroleum assay, a wider range of cut point temperatures can be chosen for the individual product. The attachment of the petroleum assay was conducted in the Atmos column by choosing the individual material stream, then selecting the attachment and analysis tab. Then, click the Create button to choose the Petroleum Assay option, and this process was repeated for all product streams.

Cut point temperature

To improve the efficiency of the Aspen HYSYS simulation in predicting crude oil yield, the product flow specification is replaced with a cut point temperature specification in the Design and Monitor tab. Four cut point temperatures are required to replace the distillate (naphtha), kerosene, kerosene/LGO, and gas oil flowrate. In this study, the cut point temperature was specified based on the actual crude assay to enhance the product comparison and error calculation. Initially, naphtha cut point temperature was specified at 135 °C, kerosene at 170 °C, kerosene/LGO at 230 °C, and gas oil at 370 °C. However, this study utilized three crude oil samples, with specifications varying based on the sample, as presented in Table 5. After adding cut point temperatures, the product flow specification needs to be deactivated and the cut point set as active.

From the composite plot, the mass percent increases with an increase in temperature, showing a good agreement between the input boiling point and the calculated TBP for all crude oil samples. Meanwhile, for the property plot, the calculated TBP was compared with the calculated ASTM D2887. It is observed that the calculated ASTM D2887 tends to have a lower temperature prediction than the calculated TBP across all samples until it reaches 350 °C, and then starts surpassing the TBP values. All samples expressed a slight gap at lower and higher temperatures. ASTM D2887 works efficiently for crude oil composition with a high aliphatic hydrocarbon content compared to high aromatic and heterocyclic hydrocarbon content11. The hydrocarbon composition significantly affects the TBP and ASTM D2887, as aliphatic hydrocarbons express a lower boiling point, meanwhile, the aromatic hydrocarbons have a higher boiling point due to their structural chains.

From the property plot (Fig. 3), S2 has a shifted calculated TBP to a higher temperature at the middle-heavy distillates, explaining a higher aromatic composition compared to a gradual and lower graph for S1 and S3. The composition of the crude oil highly influenced the prediction. As shown in Table 7, the actual light hydrocarbon composition from the crude assay of S2 is eventually higher than that of S1 and S3, explaining its higher aromatic properties, although at the light distillates. Another study reported a 96% deviation between TBP and simulated distillation (ASTM D2887), indicating that such a small deviation is still considered tolerable and may potentially happen12. In the distribution plot, the crude oil was separated into five cuts (naphtha, kerosene, kerosene/LGO, gas oil, and residue), resulting in different mass fractions. All samples expressed gas oil as the highest product distribution, followed by naphtha cuts.

Simulation comparison

The characterized crude oil was directed to the simulation tab as the main feed stream and subjected to several heating cycles prior to the distillation process, following the schematic flow diagram in Fig. 4. The study found that S1 and S3 have almost identical composition values, with a slight shift for temperatures above 100 °C for S3 (Fig. 5). The shifted graph indicates that S3 requires a higher temperature to separate the crude oil compared to S1 at higher temperatures. Additionally, S3 also expressed a slight increase in composition value for temperatures above 100 °C. Both S1 and S3 exhibit high composition peaks at temperatures of 113 °C/118°C, 155 °C/159°C, and 253 °C/261°C, for S1 and S3, respectively. These peaks strongly refer to the product distribution peaks of naphtha, kerosene, kerosene/LGO, and gas oil, which is further confirmed in the subsequent section. Meanwhile, for S2, it demonstrates different trends, with the highest composition value peaks at 294 °C, 281 °C, and 438 °C related to the difference in input data. It is acknowledged that the composition value of the feed across predicted temperature significantly impacted the final product of the distillation process.

Product Flowrate Error

Based on the study objective, crude oil was separated into various product fractions, and then the simulated flowrate was compared with the actual refinery data. Table 8 shows the product flowrate error percentage across S1, S2, and S3 calculated from the actual refinery assay and simulated distillation column. S1 expressed a higher error of −16.15% for naphtha flowrate and the lowest error of 2.75% for kerosene/LGO. Similarly, for the S2 product error, −16.33% and − 1.68% were analysed for the kerosene/LGO and gas oil flowrate. However, the percentage error of S3 is the highest among all three samples, ranging from − 21.31% to 12.77%. In general, the simulation may produce approximately 20% error in yield prediction, which aligns with other studies on predicting product distillates from crude assay. A study by13 yielded product prediction errors of 12.53%, 4.39%, 1.54%, and 7.94% for naphtha, kerosene, kerosene/LGO, and gas oil, which closely align with the sample S1 prediction. In simulating the distillation column, some limitations on the usage of the thermodynamics model may neglect the heavy hydrocarbon behaviour. Besides, the common assumptions, such as on the pressure variations, heat losses and tray efficiency, would misalign with the simulation limit and lead to some interruptions. Meanwhile, in industrial practice, the variability of crude oil and some uncertainties in laboratory testing also contribute to the deviations upon comparison with the model simulation.

The variations in error between the samples are strongly influenced by the specified parameters as mentioned previously: the density, light composition and boiling point as tabulated in Table 7. Draw stage temperature error

In addition to the product fraction flowrate, the draw stage temperature was studied and the error is presented in Table 9. Overall, the percentage error of the light and medium fractions is lower than 6.14%, except for gas oil, which exhibits a higher error, ranging from 21% to 24%. Common industrial practices are facing several problems such as flooding, pressure and temperature fluctuations, tray degradation, fouling and blockage of equipment. These issues can be controlled and managed through the model simulation, as it provides replication of actual refinery processes. For example, in controlling the flooding issue, the simulation can calculate and estimate the maximum feed flowrate and reflux ratio required for a certain developed model and set the limit to avoid unwanted incidents. Similarly to the pressure and temperature control, some fluctuation in the column can be directly detected by the simulation. This paper focused on the yield prediction with the intention of reducing industrial cost, time and human resources in conducting manual yield calculation on every newcomer crude oil. By using the simulation method, the amount of laboratory work can be reduced to only very basic testing as the input for the simulation. Based on the error percentage from the three crude oils, this model fits perfectly for the aliphatic hydrocarbon crude oil with slightly lower light hydrocarbon value, such as the S1 and S3.

Parameter evaluation on the yield and column temperature

The parameter evaluation was performed using the latest simulation with the adjust function, which is able to achieve less error compared to the actual data. In this section, four parameters affecting the product fraction were examined, including crude oil feed flowrate, bottom steam flowrates, and column pressures (top and bottom), by analysing the flowrates of each product fraction and temperature profiles. A detailed discussion of each influencing factor was reported in the next section.

Effect of Feed Flowrate

The crude oil feed flowrate was manipulated by using an interval of 25 kg/s within the range of 25 kg/s to 175 kg/s, as illustrated in Fig. 9 for the product flowrate. Based on the collected data, flowrate increment is consistent; for example, naphtha sample S1 shows product flowrates increase by ~ 5.7% for each interval, which leads to the final product of 39.79 kg/s at feed crude oil of 175 kg/s. A similar trend was also confirmed by other crude oil samples. Furthermore, the study recognised the equivalent product fraction distribution among the total product fraction within the same feed flowrate. For example, the distribution of naphtha and kerosene in sample S1 is 25.5% and 14.23%, respectively, at a feed flowrate of 75 kg/s. A similar percentage was observed at 100 kg/s with the distribution of 25.5% for naphtha and 14.17% for kerosene. This finding suggests a linear relationship between the feed flowrate and the product flowrate for each fraction.

Effect of Bottom Steam Flowrate

Bottom steam, also known as the stripping steam, refers to the steam stream supplied to the bottom of the distillation column with the intention of providing additional heat to support the fractionation process. The presence of the bottom steam would improve the separation efficiency of lighter boiling temperature components. The vapor generated will move upwards and condense at the top before being recycled back as reflux17. Steam would decrease the amount of liquid retained and accelerate the vapor passage across the column. From an economic view, the use of bottom steam would reduce energy consumption and simultaneously minimise energy cost18,19. The effect of steam flowrate was studied to understand the behaviour of the fractionation process and its impact on product flowrate and column temperature. The bottom steam is introduced at stage 35, the lowest stage of the distillation column, at a temperature of 350℃. The flowrate of bottom steam was varied in 0.5 kg/s intervals, starting from 0.444 kg/s to 2.944 kg/s.

Figure 11a demonstrates the comparison of 0.444 kg/s and 2.944 kg/s, as the flowrate variation between intervals follows the trends and has similar increments or decrements. As the steam flowrate increases, the product fraction flowrate slightly reduces for naphtha, kerosene and kerosene/LGO. For the three product fractions, sample S2 shows the lowest difference among all three samples, indicating the least affected sample on the steam flowrate. However, a reversal situation occurred for gas oil in which the product flowrate increases with the steam flowrate, and the highest enhancement occurs in sample S2. These findings show that bottom steam flowrate improves gas oil production while reducing the light and medium product fractions. A significant enhancement in S2 gas oil product flowrate is explained by the highest temperature profile with an increase of 3.7°C in stage 32, followed by samples S3 and S1.

The detailed finding was also supported by the study on the temperature profiles and the deviation was observed to occur after the feed stream stage, similar to the study on variation of feed flowrate (Fig. 11b). Although the same deviation occurred, the trends are opposite, as the lower bottom steam flowrate resulted in less offset. The deviation increases as the bottom steam flowrate increases, where the higher flowrate of bottom steam, the lower the bottom column temperature. For example, sample S1 has reduced the temperature from 310.2°C to 257.2°C, for flowrate of 0.444 kg/s and 2.944 kg/s, respectively. This situation relates to the enhanced steam stripping effect, in which increased bottom steam injection lowers the partial pressure of hydrocarbons. This shifts the vapor-liquid equilibrium, facilitating the vaporization of heavier components and enhancing the accumulation of lighter hydrocarbons in the vapor phase.

The increase in the bottom steam flow enhances the steam stripping by reducing the partial pressure of hydrocarbons in the vapor phase, which aligns with Dalton’s law of partial pressure20–22. With the reduction in partial pressure of hydrocarbons, it reduces the required equilibrium boiling temperature for the vaporization and simultaneously facilitates the transfer of heavier hydrocarbon components into the vapor phase without the help of excessive thermal input. The injection of bottom steam also contributes to the latent heat, which sustains the production of vapor and influences the local vapor-liquid equilibrium condition within the stripping section in the distillation column. In the refining industry, maximizing the recovery of light and medium fractions requires careful consideration of optimizing the bottom steam flowrate to ensure an efficient refining process. Meanwhile, operations targeting enhanced heavy fraction recovery would consider a higher steam flowrate to further lower hydrocarbon partial pressure, thereby facilitating the vaporization of heavier components into the distillates. However, for a more efficient heavy gas oil production rate23, stated that the pump-around flowrate is a crucial parameter compared to the bottom stripping steam flowrate.

Effect of Column Pressure (Top and Bottom)

The fractionation process highly depends on the column temperature and simultaneously indicates the dependency on pressure due to PVT correlation. Column pressure holds important roles, particularly in separation and economics, based on the volatility of each component and the presence of azeotropes24. Based on the PVT correlation, pressure and temperature have a direct relationship, whereas an increase in pressure leads to a higher temperature. A careful consideration is needed and it is recommended to maintain the top column pressure to be lower than the bottom pressure. This would reduce the potential of flooding, weeping or backflow and ensure the occurrence of light hydrocarbon separation at the top column25–27. In simulating the distillation column, top pressure and bottom pressure are the compulsory parameters that require specification. This section focuses on manipulating the top and bottom pressure to understand the impact on the product fraction and temperature profiles.

A) Effect Top Column Pressure.

A study on the top column pressure was conducted by varying the pressure from 1.1 bar to 1.7 bar in increments of 0.1 bar. The significant difference between low and high pressure is clearly seen in naphtha and kerosene product flowrate as shown in Fig. 12a. As pressure increases, the naphtha flowrate decreases, whereas the kerosene product tends to increase. The flowrate difference indicates a portion of naphtha was transferred and classified as kerosene at a higher top column pressure. This situation is closely related to the temperature factor, in which an increase in pressure led to an increase in temperature, making a certain portion of naphtha appear in the kerosene cut point temperature ranges. Although with minimal differences, this finding demonstrates the influence of top pressure on the light product fractions. Meanwhile, for kerosene/LGO and gas oil, the deviation is statistically insignificant and remains unchanged, indicating the invariance of top column pressure. However, the top pressure is also important for the operation of the overall distillation column as it significantly affects the column vapor-liquid equilibrium.

The changes in light fraction product flowrate are justified by the column temperature profiles presented in Fig. 12b. The graph shows deviations at the lower stage number referring to the top distillation column. The top column pressure of 1.1 bar demonstrates the lowest temperature, and it begins to increase with the top pressure. This stability corresponds to the product draw stages located at stages 25 and 32, respectively. In relation, operational pressure dictates the temperature profile and phase distribution within the column. A lower pressure decrease the boiling point of the mixture, resulting in a lower column profile temperature at lower stage numbers and increased yield of naphtha. As the pressure increases, the elevated boiling points and reduced relative volatility lead to an upward shift in temperature profiles and a decline in the distillate flowrate. To summarize, the top column pressure acts as a primary control for the thermodynamic driving force, directly affecting the recovery and distributions of light-medium distillates.

B) Effect Bottom Column Pressure.

The effect of bottom column pressure is being studied by varying in intervals of 0.1 bar within the range of 2.0 bar to 2.6 bar. The findings in Fig. 13a illustrate a negligible difference for the naphtha and kerosene products. However, there are noticeable changes for the kerosene/LGO and gas oil in all samples. It is observed that the product flowrate of kerosene/LGO and gas oil was reduced as the bottom pressure increased. For example, in sample S2, the flowrate of kerosene/LGO at 2.0 bar is 8.72 kg/s and is reduced to 8.43 kg/s at 2.6 bar, indicating a 3.3% reduction. Similarly to gas oil, the product flowrate reduced from 47.50 kg/s to 46.97 kg/s. This result suggests the effects of bottom column pressure on the medium and heavy distillation fraction.

The findings are supported by the temperature profiles at different stage numbers, as illustrated in Fig. 13b. The deviation occurred at a higher stage number, referring to the bottom column stages, which is in contrast to the findings of top pressure. The deviation becomes clearly observable after stage 10, corresponding with the draw stage of the column for kerosene/LGO and gas oil. Additionally, this observation explains the unchanged flowrates of naphtha and kerosene at the lower stage number. The plot demonstrates that lower bottom column pressure resulted in a lower temperature profile, and the temperature began to rise in correspondence to the increase in pressure. At lower bottom column pressure, a higher product flowrate of kerosene/LGO and gas oil, and a lower temperature profile. Meanwhile, as the bottom pressure increases, the product flowrate and temperature are inversely proportional, resulting in a decrease in product flowrate with an increase in temperature. The study concludes that the column pressure directly impacts the product yields due to the temperature variation through the column. High temperature at the bottom of the distillation column is likely to increase the production of vapor and instantly reduce the efficiency of the medium-heavy crude oil fractionation.

The parameter evaluation concluded that each factor parameter has a substantial impact on the simulated model, particularly on the distillation unit operation and the prediction of product fractions. An increased crude oil feed flowrate significantly enhances the product flowrate and raises the bottom column temperature after the feed stage. In contrast, an elevated bottom steam flowrate improves gas oil production, suppresses the flowrates of light and medium products, and results in a lower bottom column temperature after the feed stage. Furthermore, the column pressure closely correlates with the column temperature and the fractionation process. Variations in the top column pressure affect the production of naphtha and kerosene, where increased pressure results in a lower naphtha flowrate and a higher kerosene flowrate, along with an increase in the temperature profile at the top of the column.

Meanwhile, changes in the bottom column pressure influence the kerosene/LGO and gas oil product fractions. A rise in operating pressure results in a decrease in product flowrate and an upward shift in the temperature profile, particularly at the bottom distillation column (stripping section). This finding reflects the pressure dependency of vapor-liquid equilibrium thermodynamics, in which increased system pressure shifts the phase equilibrium and elevates the boiling temperature of hydrocarbon components. In this operating condition, the relative volatility between light and heavy components generally decreases and leads to a reduction in the compositional difference between the vapor and liquid phase in the equilibrium stages. Therefore, to achieve comparable vaporization, a higher thermal input is required, which also causes a simultaneous reduction of relative volatility at higher pressure and weakens the thermodynamic driving forces. This limits the separation performance and decreases the recovery of lighter fractions in the product streams.

For refineries focused on producing light and medium fractions, careful consideration should be given to the bottom steam flowrate, as a higher flowrate may reduce productivity. Additionally, the top column pressure plays a key role in differentiating between light and medium fraction products. As the recommendation for the crude oil yield prediction, the integration of software simulation with the digital twin provides a hybrid approach to improve the prediction, especially in the optimization process28,29.

Conclusion

In conclusion, simulating a distillation column to estimate crude oil yield provides a valuable overview of product flowrates. The proposed model demonstrates successful predictions across three crude oil samples, with deviations ranging from 1.68% to 21.31%. The study confirms that crude oil properties are critical, as the model’s accuracy heavily depends on those properties. The column temperature profiles also align well with actual data, except for the bottom column temperature, which shows some deviation. Manipulating operational parameters such as crude oil feed flowrate, bottom steam flowrate, and top and bottom column pressures reveals their effects on product flowrates and temperature profiles, offering insights for product optimization. Specifically, both product flowrate and bottom column temperature increase with higher feed flowrate. In contrast, increased bottom steam flowrate reduces the flowrates of light and medium products. Higher top column pressure enhances kerosene production but reduces naphtha flowrate, while increased bottom column pressure decreases the flowrates of kerosene/LGO and gas oil.

This study suggests that distillation column simulation is a reliable method for predicting crude oil yield and identifying key parameters for optimization. Developing a standardized distillation model that can provide accurate predictions across various crude oil types, based solely on crude assay data, is highly beneficial for the refining industry. Future work should focus on standardizing distillation column modelling through column sizing or control systems to improve prediction accuracy and minimize discrepancies with actual plant data. Additionally, refining model optimization based on influential parameters can enhance product quality and overall distillation performance.
