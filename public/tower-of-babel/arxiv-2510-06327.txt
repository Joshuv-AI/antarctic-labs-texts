Bayesian Calibration of Gravitational-Wave Detectors Using Null Streams Without Waveform Assumptions
Isaac C. F. Wong, Francesco Cireddu, Milan Wils, Tom Colemont, Harsh Narola, Chris Van Den Broeck, Tjonnie G. F. Li

Abstract
We introduce a Bayesian null-stream method to constrain calibration errors in closed-geometry gravitational-wave (GW) detector networks. Unlike prior methods requiring electromagnetic counterparts or waveform models, this method uses sky-independent null streams to calibrate the detectors with any GW signals, independent of general relativity or waveform assumptions. We show a proof-of-concept study to demonstrate the feasibility of the method. We discuss prospects for next-generation detectors like Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer, and LISA, where enhanced calibration accuracy will advance low-frequency GW science.

Sky-independent null stream—
Null stream is a linear combination of strain data from a network of
GW
detectors that cancel
GW
signals, regardless of their morphology, under ideal conditions
Gürsel and Tinto 1989
.
For general networks, null stream construction requires the source’s sky position, as detector responses depend on
GW
direction.
However, for closed-geometry networks like triangles, null stream is sky-independent in the long-wavelength approximation, where the sum of detector tensors
\mathbf{D}_{j}
vanishes:
\sum_{j=1}^{3}\mathbf{D}_{j}=\mathbf{0}

Wong and Li 2022
, which holds at low frequencies.
For the
ET
, with 10-km arms, it is valid below
\sim 1
kHz
Virtuoso and Milotti 2025
.
For
LISA
, with
2.5\times 10^{6}
km arms, it breaks above
\sim 1
mHz
Hartwig et al. 2023
.
These thresholds are order-of-magnitude estimates, as systematic errors exceeding statistical uncertainties in null-stream analyses require further investigation.
Within the frequency range where the sky-independent null stream is accurate, it enables
GW
detector calibration using astrophysical signals without waveform assumptions.
Moreover, the null stream cancels arbitrary
GW
polarizations, including scalar, vector, and tensor modes from modified gravity theories, as shown in Eq. (5) of
Wong and Li 2022
.
Unlike calibration methods relying on
EM
observations and assuming
GR

Pitkin et al. 2016
;
Essick and Holz 2019
, null-stream-based calibration is
GR
-independent, preserving the detector network’s ability to test fundamental physics without bias.

Self-calibration—
Ref.
Schutz and Sathyaprakash 2020
introduced self-calibration for
GW
detector networks, using a Sagnac-based null stream in a triangular configuration,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{Sagnac}}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\tilde{d}_{i}(f)

(1)

\displaystyle=\tilde{n}_{\mathrm{Sagnac}}(f)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}C_{i}(f)\tilde{s}_{i}(f)

where
\tilde{d}_{i}(f)
is the strain data from the
i
th detector,
\tilde{n}_{\mathrm{Sagnac}}(f)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\tilde{n}_{i}(f)
is the Sagnac combination of the detector noise
\tilde{n}_{i}(f)
from the individual detectors,
C_{i}(f)
is the complex calibration error, and
\tilde{s}_{i}(f)
is the
GW
signal in the
i
th detector.
A matched filtering analysis is then performed on this Sagnac combination to estimate the calibration errors.
This approach requires explicit waveform templates to estimate calibration errors, which inherently relies on accurate theoretical waveform predictions and assumes the validity of
GR
, limiting its model independence.

Null-stream calibration—
In this work, we propose a model-independent approach, modifying the null-stream construction to account for calibration errors without waveform assumptions.
Our approach leverages GW signals to infer calibration errors without assuming their morphology or
GR
, enabling unbiased tests of fundamental physics.

The observation model for a network of
GW
detectors is

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(f)=\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{F}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(f)+\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\,,

(2)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(f)
is the frequency-domain strain data,
\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})
a diagonal matrix of complex calibration errors for each detector parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\mathbf{F}
the antenna pattern matrix,
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(f)
the
GW
polarizations, and
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)
the detector noise with power spectral density matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}(f)

Sutton et al. 2010
;
Pang et al. 2020
;
Wong et al. 2021
;
Cireddu et al. 2024
.
The impact of calibration errors on the detector noise is absorbed into
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)
and therefore
\mathbf{S}_{n}(f)
.
In this work, we only consider a network of three detectors.

Following
Sutton et al. 2010
;
Pang et al. 2020
;
Wong et al. 2021
, we construct the null stream via a projection matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{null}}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})

(3)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{W}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})[\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{W}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})]^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})

where
\mathbf{I}_{3}
is a
3\times 3
identity matrix,
\mathbf{W}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sqrt{2\Delta f}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1/2}(f)\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{F}
, and
\Delta f=1/T
is the frequency resolution for data duration
T
.
The null stream is given by
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}(f)=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{null}}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{w}(f)
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{w}(f)=\sqrt{2\Delta f}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1/2}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(f)
is the whitened strain data.
With true calibration parameters (
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{true}}
), it contains no
GW
signals and follows a standard complex normal distribution in a one-dimensional subspace.

To enhance
PE
and mitigate noise transients, we transform the null stream to the time-frequency domain via an orthogonal wavelet transform, yielding
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathrm{TF}}(t,f;\boldsymbol{\theta})

Necula et al. 2012
.
The log-likelihood is

\log p(\mathbf{d}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j,k\in\mathcal{I}}-\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\tilde{\vb{z}}_{\mathrm{TF}}(t_{j}, f_{k}; \boldsymbol{\theta})}^{2}+\mathrm{constant}

(4)

where
\mathcal{I}
selects time-frequency regions containing
GW
signals.

This approach is equally applicable to triangular
ET
and
LISA
, leveraging low-frequency
GW
signals to enhance calibration precision, thereby broadening the framework’s impact across next-generation
GW
observatories.

Results—

To evaluate the efficacy of our Bayesian null-stream method for constraining
GW
detector calibration errors, we conduct simulations within a triangular
ET
network.
We analyze 15 data segments, each containing a
BBH
signal generated with the
IMPhenomXPHM
waveform model
Pratten et al. 2021
, with the source parameters detailed in the Supplemental Material.
The luminosity distance is adjusted to yield network
signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs)
uniformly spanning 20 to 300, typical for the triangular
ET

Branchesi et al. 2023
.
This setup aims to test the degree of calibration error constraints across diverse signal strengths, as stronger signals are expected to yield tighter constraints due to a larger calibration-induced signal leakage to the null stream.
We model calibration errors
\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})
using cubic splines for amplitude and phase per detector, defined as
C_{j}(f_{k})=(1+\delta A_{j}(f_{k}))e^{i\delta\phi_{j}(f_{k})}
for the
j
th detector at frequency
f_{k}
corresponding to the
k
th spline knot
Farr et al. 2014
.
The error at each spline knot is drawn from Gaussian distributions informed by the calibration uncertainties of LIGO Livingston (median and
1\sigma
values) over the one-hour interval beginning at an arbitrarily chosen GPS time 1256894100, during the third observing run
LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA Collaboration 2021
.
Given the relative stability of calibration uncertainties throughout the observing run, we adopt the uncertainties at this GPS time as representative.
To evaluate the method’s effectiveness in constraining calibration errors, we selected the realization of calibration errors that maximizes the null stream
SNR
, corresponding to the most significant calibration-induced signal leakage. This choice, essential for a proof-of-concept study, ensures that improvements in calibration precision are evident, as small calibration errors with low null stream
SNR
would produce limited constraints that are difficult to discern.
Detector noise, assumed uncorrelated between detectors, is simulated using the ET-D sensitivity curve
Hild et al. 2011
.
We use the same calibration errors and noise realizations across all 15 simulations to isolate how calibration constraints vary solely with the strength of signal leakage into the null stream.

nullcal
package

4
), and Bayesian inference is performed with
Bilby

;

using the

;

.
Calibration errors for the three

Farr et al. 2014

The calibration parameters

For the prior settings, we assign Gaussian priors to
\boldsymbol{\theta}

These priors reflect constraints from instrumental methods, such as

We quantify the calibration constraint by computing the geometric mean of the generalized standard deviation
\bar{\sigma}

1/(2N)
, where
N
is the number of calibration parameters.
This metric, equivalent to the square root of the geometric mean of the covariance matrix eigenvalues, represents the typical scale of uncertainty per parameter dimension in the multidimensional parameter space.

of posterior samples to that of prior samples as a function of network

The y-axis shows a linear decrease of this ratio with increasing

Degeneracy: The null stream method exhibits two key degeneracies affecting calibration error constraints.
First, a common mode degeneracy arises because the likelihood, incorporating the projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{null}}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})
in Eq. (
3
), is invariant under a common scaling of the calibration errors
\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})
across detectors, mimicking a modification of the source’s luminosity distance
Schutz and Sathyaprakash 2020
.
Consequently, relative calibration errors between detectors are constrained more tightly than absolute errors.
This method complements, rather than replaces, instrumental calibration techniques.
Instrumental measurements, encoded as priors, partially break this degeneracy, ensuring informative posteriors for absolute errors, though relative errors remain better constrained.
Second, when the
GW
signal is polarized i.e.
\tilde{h}_{\times}(f)=\varepsilon e^{i\psi}\tilde{h}_{+}(f)
, with ellipticity
\varepsilon
and orientation angle
\psi
, two equivalent solutions for
\mathbf{C}(f;\boldsymbol{\theta})
exist (up to a scaling factor), leading to broader posteriors.
Detailed analysis is provided in the Supplemental Material.

The degeneracy is mitigated by higher-order harmonics in the
GW
emission or, more effectively for next-generation detectors like
ET
and
LISA
, by multiple
GW
signals overlapping in the frequency domain.
Figure
2
shows the ratio of
\bar{\sigma}
of posterior to prior samples for setups with one to three
GW
signals, each scaled to a total null stream
SNR
of 26.3, as detailed in the Supplemental Material.
The ratio decreases noticeably with more signals: from 0.824 with a single signal, to 0.698 with two signals, and to 0.646 with three signals. The most substantial gain occurs when going from one to two signals due to greater degeneracy breaking for polarized signals. Beyond two signals, the improvement diminishes, leveling off as additional signals contribute less to resolving the degeneracy.

Conclusion and perspectives—
In conclusion, this work significantly advances the concept of self-calibration proposed by
Schutz and Sathyaprakash 2020
for closed-geometry
GW
detector networks.
Building on this idea, we present a different strategy and develop the method into a model-independent framework by leveraging null-stream analysis, in contrast to the matched-filtering-based approach of
Schutz and Sathyaprakash 2020
, which relies on specific waveform assumptions.
Unlike prior methods requiring
EM
counterparts and waveform assumptions, this approach leverages sky-independent null streams to utilize all
GW
signals, including astrophysical events and cosmological backgrounds, free from systematics due to waveform and
GR
assumptions.
We have conducted experiments to demonstrate that constraints on calibration errors tighten linearly with increasing network
SNR
(20–300) and multiple frequency-overlapping signals, breaking the degeneracy from polarizations, as detailed in the Supplemental Material.
We also provide a practical implementation of the framework in the
nullcal
package
Placeholder 2025a
.

This precision is critical for next-generation
GW
detectors like
ET
and
Cosmic Explorer (CE)
, guiding optimal network geometry design to leverage the sky-independent null stream for precise calibration of the detectors, and for
LISA
, where the T channel being insensitive to
GW
signals below
\sim 1\,\mathrm{mHz}
enables the application of this method at low frequencies.
Enhanced calibration precision at low frequencies is critical for next-generation
GW
detectors, enabling robust scientific studies of low-frequency sources, such as precise
PE
for massive black hole binaries, early-warning alerts for binary neutron star mergers, and detection of primordial black holes, thus advancing
GW
astronomy
Abac et al. 2025
.

The proof-of-concept study demonstrates the feasibility of the method to constrain calibration uncertainties.
However, the corresponding
PE
improvements for source properties depend on calibration error frequencies and signal content, requiring careful experimental design to give robust conclusions.
Specifically, using the same data segment to constrain calibration uncertainties and perform
PE
double-counts the
GW
signal information, leading to overestimated precision or systematic errors.
Instead, off-source data must be used to measure calibration uncertainties, ensuring the uncertainty of
PE
on on-source signals is not underestimated.
A large-scale
mock data challenge (MDC)
with diverse
GW
populations injected into extended data stretches is essential to quantify these impacts, simulating realistic scenarios and avoiding arbitrary signal choices.

In future work, we plan to apply this null-stream method to a large-scale
MDC
for the triangular
ET
, simulating realistic
GW
populations based on established models for
BBH
mergers,
BNS
mergers, and other astrophysical sources.
By incorporating
ET
’s projected sensitivity and generating signals from population distributions, the
MDC
will enable a comprehensive assessment of the expected calibration error constraints achievable over extended observation periods.
We anticipate quantifying the degree of improvement in relative and absolute calibration precision, particularly at low frequencies (1–1000 Hz).
In this regime, the long-wavelength approximation is sufficiently accurate, ensuring that the sky-independent null stream holds.
The presence of multiple overlapping signals further breaks parameter degeneracies and enhances constraints on calibration errors.
This study will provide key insights into the calibration accuracy attainable for
ET
, informing network optimization and advancing low-frequency
GW
science.

Appendix C
Degeneracy of the likelihood function

The likelihood function of the null stream method exhibits two primary degeneracies that impact calibration error constraints.
First, a common mode degeneracy arises because the likelihood is invariant under a common scaling of calibration errors across detectors
Schutz and Sathyaprakash 2020
.
For a closed-geometry network with data

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(f)=\mathbf{C}(f)\sum_{i}\mathbf{F}_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}(f)+\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)

(S1)

where
\mathbf{C}(f)=C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)\mathbf{I}_{3}
represents the common mode calibration error,
\mathbf{F}_{i}
is the antenna pattern matrix for the
i
th source,
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}(f)
are the
GW
polarizations, and
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)
is noise, the effect of
C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)
is equivalent to rescaling the
GW
signal:

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}(f)=\sum_{i}\mathbf{F}_{i}\left[C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}(f)\right]+\tilde{\mathbf{n}}(f)\,.

(S2)

This is evident in the projection matrix.
Define the whitened antenna pattern matrix without calibration errors as

\mathbf{W}(f)=\sqrt{2\Delta f}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1/2}(f)\mathbf{F}\,,

(S3)

and with common mode errors as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{\prime}(f)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\Delta f}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1/2}(f)C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)\mathbf{I}_{3}\mathbf{F}

(S4)

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)\mathbf{W}(f)\,.

The null projection matrix becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{null}}(f)

(S5)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}(f)\left[\mathbf{W}^{\prime\dagger}(f)\mathbf{W}^{\prime}(f)\right]^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\prime\dagger}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{3}-\absolutevalue{C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)}^{2}\mathbf{W}(f)\left[\absolutevalue{C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)}^{2}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f)\mathbf{W}(f)\right]^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{W}(f)\left[\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f)\mathbf{W}(f)\right]^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{\dagger}(f)

which is invariant under
C_{\mathrm{com}}(f)
, demonstrating that the likelihood function is unaffected by common mode calibration errors.
Thus, relative calibration errors are better constrained than absolute errors.

The second degeneracy arises for polarized
GW
signals, where
\tilde{h}_{\times}(f)=z\tilde{h}_{+}(f)
and
z
is a complex number.
Specifically, the
GW
signals are elliptically polarized where
z=i\epsilon
when the source is a quasi-circular binary with a low mass ratio, as is the case for most events detected during the first three observing runs of the LIGO and Virgo detectors.
For this scenario, the signal is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{s}}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}(f)\mathbf{F}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}(f)

(S6)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}(f)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{+}&\mathbf{f}_{\times}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{h}_{+}(f)\\
\tilde{h}_{\times}(f)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}(f)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{+}&\mathbf{f}_{\times}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{h}_{+}(f)\\
i\epsilon\tilde{h}_{+}(f)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}(f)(\mathbf{f}_{+}+i\epsilon\mathbf{f}_{\times})\tilde{h}_{+}(f)

where
\mathbf{f}_{+}
and
\mathbf{f}_{\times}
are the
3\times 1
antenna pattern function vectors for the
+
and
\times
polarizations, respectively.
This reduces the signal space dimensionality from two to one, yielding two null vectors orthogonal to
\mathbf{C}(f)(\mathbf{f}_{+}+i\epsilon\mathbf{f}_{\times})
, corresponding to two distinct null streams.
This degeneracy broadens the posterior distribution of calibration parameters.
Multiple
GW
signals overlapping in the frequency domain, with differing ellipticities and orientations, break this degeneracy, tightening constraints on calibration errors.

Appendix D
Calibration error constraints at other frequencies

In the main text, we presented the posterior distributions of relative calibration errors at 32 Hz.
Here, we present the posterior distributions of relative calibration errors across the entire frequency range (8–512 Hz) analyzed, in Fig.
S2
, for the three-signal case with a null stream
SNR
of 26.3.
The shaded regions represent 90% credible intervals, and the dashed lines indicate the true values.
Across the frequency range, the posteriors are significantly tighter than the priors, accurately recovering the true values, demonstrating the method’s effectiveness in constraining calibration errors throughout the analyzed frequency band.
Remarkably, at low frequencies around 10 Hz, the true relative calibration errors for the amplitude between ET2 and ET1 are outside the 90% credible interval of the prior, yet the posterior successfully recovers the true value, showcasing the method’s capability to enhance calibration precision in the situation where the state of the interferometers at the moment deviates from the instrumental measurements over a longer period.
The deviation of the injected calibration errors from the 90% credible interval of the prior at certain frequencies is due to the selection of the calibration error realization that maximizes the null stream
SNR
.
This demonstrates the method’s ability to improve calibration precision even when the actual calibration errors deviate significantly from prior expectations.
