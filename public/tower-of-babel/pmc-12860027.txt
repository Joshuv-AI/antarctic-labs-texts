Clinical presentation, surgical management, and long-term outcomes of pharyngeal sialoceles in dogs: 6 cases (2015–2025)
Authors: Vassiliki Tsioli, Eugenia Flouraki, Theodora Zacharopoulou, Ioannis Panopoulos, Dimitra Psalla, Mandalena Markou, Epameinondas Loukopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPharyngeal sialoceles are a rare form of salivary mucocele in dogs and may present with upper airway obstruction. Limited data are available regarding their clinical presentation and optimal management. The objectives of this study were to describe the clinical signs, diagnostic findings, surgical treatment, and outcomes in six dogs with pharyngeal sialoceles.Case presentationMedical records of six dogs diagnosed and/or surgically treated for pharyngeal sialoceles were retrospectively reviewed. Data collected included signalment, clinical signs, diagnostic procedures, surgical approach, and follow-up outcomes. Dogs ranged in age from 1.5 to 5 years (median 3.5 years), with a predominance of males (83%). Clinical signs included hypersalivation, cervical swelling, stridor, and dyspnea. Four cases were left-sided and two right-sided; three dogs had concurrent cervical sialoceles. Cytology confirmed the diagnosis in all cases. Radiography was performed in three dogs and computed tomography was performed in one. All underwent mandibular and sublingual sialoadenectomy via a ventral approach, with partial excision of the pharyngeal wall. No postoperative complications or recurrences were observed during follow-up (12–48 months).ConclusionsPharyngeal sialoceles, while rare, should be considered in dogs presenting with upper airway signs. Complete surgical excision of the mandibular and sublingual glands, along with partial removal of the sialocele, appears to be effective and associated with a favorable prognosis.

Introduction

A sialocele is a disorder of the salivary glands or their ducts, characterized by the localized accumulation of saliva in the surrounding soft tissues [1–8]. The underlying cause remains unknown in many cases; however, potential etiologies include trauma, foreign bodies, salivary duct obstruction (such as sialoliths), ductal stenosis, and neoplasia [1, 2, 4–6, 8–12]. In one reported case, infection with Dirofilaria immitis was implicated as a probable cause [13]. Sialoceles are not true cysts—they are lined by nonsecretory granulation tissue that forms as a secondary response to inflammation caused by the extravasation of saliva into adjacent tissues [6, 8, 14].

In dogs, sialoceles most commonly arise from defects in the polystomatic sublingual lobules or from the major or minor sublingual ducts. Four main types of sialoceles have been described: cervical, sublingual, pharyngeal, and zygomatic. Of these, cervical and sublingual types are the most commonly reported [3, 5–8, 11, 12, 15–17]. In addition to the major salivary glands, dogs also have numerous minor salivary glands distributed throughout the oral cavity and pharynx, which are categorized by location as labial, buccal, molar, lingual, or palatine [18]. Sialoceles originating from these minor glands are rarely reported.

Certain dog breeds appear to be predisposed to sialocele formation, including Poodles, German Shepherds, Dachshunds, Australian Silky Terriers, and Greyhounds [6, 19]. In contrast, sialoceles are infrequently reported in cats [4, 20–27].

Clinical signs of a sialocele vary depending on its anatomical location. Diagnostic imaging modalities used to identify sialoceles include radiography [19, 28], ultrasonography [29], magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [30], and computed tomography (CT) [26, 30–35]. While cervical and sublingual sialoceles are well-documented in the literature, pharyngeal sialoceles are rare and less well understood.

Pharyngeal sialoceles involve the accumulation of saliva within the pharyngeal wall, often leading to clinical signs that differ from other types. Affected dogs may present with dysphagia, ptyalism, coughing, and dyspnea [2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 36]. Both acute and chronic upper airway obstruction can occur in severe cases. Diagnosis can be particularly challenging, as pharyngeal sialoceles are not easily visible without a detailed oral and pharyngeal examination.

Only a few case reports of pharyngeal sialoceles have been published in the veterinary literature. The aim of this study is to describe the clinical presentation, diagnostic features, surgical management, and long-term outcomes of pharyngeal sialoceles in six dogs, in order to improve understanding and guide treatment of this uncommon condition.

Methods

All patients included were client-owned dogs and all investigations were conducted as part of clinically indicated procedures. Medical records of dogs diagnosed and/or treated surgically for pharyngeal sialoceles were retrospectively reviewed between 2015 and 2025. Data collected included signalment, clinical signs, diagnostic findings (pharyngeal and any concurrent sialoceles), surgical procedures performed, and postoperative outcomes (Table 1).

Computed tomography (CT) of the head was performed using a helical scanner (Revolution ACT GE; General Healthcare Medical Systems).

All dogs were premedicated with dexmedetomidine (Dexdomitor; 0.5 mg/mL; Pfizer) at 5 µg/kg and either pethidine (Pethidina chloridato; Molteni) at 2 mg/kg or morphine (Morphina chloridato; Molteni) at 0.2 mg/kg intramuscularly. Cefuroxime (Zinacef; GlaxoSmithKline) was administered at 20 mg/kg intravenously for perioperative prophylaxis. Anesthesia was induced with propofol (Propofol; Fresenius Kabi) at 1 mg/kg intravenously to effect and was maintained, after intubation, with isoflurane (IsoFlo; Abbott) in oxygen.

Surgical treatment consisted of mandibular and sublingual sialoadenectomy in all dogs, combined with partial excision of the pharyngeal wall of the sialocele (Fig. 1). A ventral surgical approach was used in all six dogs. In the three dogs with concurrent cervical sialoceles, a Penrose drain was placed postoperatively.

Results

Six dogs met the inclusion criteria. Three of the dogs were mixed-breed (50%), two were Labrador Retrievers (33%) and one was a Maltese (17%).

The median age of the dogs was 3.5 years (range: 1.5–5 years) (mean: 3 years and 2 months), and the median body weight was 25 kg (range: 7–40 kg) (mean: 22.7 kg). Presenting clinical signs included hypersalivation (5/6 dogs, 83%), cervical swelling (3/6, 50%), inspiratory stridor (3/6, 50%), cough (2/6, 33%), dysphagia (2/6, 33%), cyanosis (1/6, 17%), and dyspnea (1/6, 17%). Duration of clinical signs ranged from 1 day to 4 weeks.

In five dogs (5/6), aspiration of the pharyngeal swelling during oral examination yielded mucoid fluid consistent with a sialocele. In three of those dogs (3/6) presenting with a concurrent fluctuant, painless cervical swelling, aspiration revealed a blood-stained, viscous fluid. Cytological evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of sialocele in three cases. In one dog (1/6) aspiration was non-diagnostic.

In the three dogs with cervical swelling, radiographs demonstrated a soft tissue opacity in the region, though no sialoliths were identified. Contrast sialography was not performed in any case.

In one dog (Case 2), oral examination revealed a ventral swelling of the left side of the soft palate (Fig. 2), which was only apparent following palpation of the corresponding cervical region. In this dog CT of the head was performed using, pre- and post-intravenous contrast medium administration (2 min after intravenous injection of iohexol [Omnipaque; GE Healthcare], 2 ml/kg). CT revealed a multilobular, thin-walled, fluid-filled cystic lesion approximately 4 cm in diameter, located rostromedial to the left mandibular salivary gland and ventromedial to the ipsilateral digastric muscle. The previously described lesion protruded into the oral cavity, causing dorsal compression of the soft palate and nasopharynx (Fig. 3). The lesion was hypoattenuating (20 Hounsfield units [HU]) with thin (< 1 mm) rim enhancement. The left medial retropharyngeal lymph node was mildly enlarged (11 mm on the short axis), oval-shaped, with a normal attenuation in the pre- and post-contrast images.

A diagnosis of pharyngeal sialocele was established in all six dogs. The lesion was left-sided in four cases (67%) and right-sided in two cases (33%). Hematology and serum biochemistry results were within normal limits in all animals.

Postoperatively all dogs received oral meloxicam (Metacam; Boehringer Ingelheim) at 0.1 mg/kg per os once daily for four days and paracetamol (Apotel; UniPharma) at 10 mg/kg per os twice daily for three days. Recovery was uneventful in all cases, and animals were discharged within 24–48 h following surgery.

Histopathological examination was performed in four cases which revealed consistent features across all samples, including cystic structures lacking an epithelial lining, lined instead by a variably mature connective tissue pseudocapsule. The cystic spaces were filled with amorphous, basophilic, mucinous material often exhibiting a finely granular texture. Foci of immature or mature granulation tissue were observed in the cyst walls, often accompanied by mild to moderate lymphoplasmacytic infiltration. In three cases, mild calcification was detected focally within the cyst wall. The adjacent salivary ducts displayed dilation, and occasional acinar atrophy was evident. Notably, one case demonstrated extensive granulation tissue formation, multifocal chronic inflammation, surface calcium salt deposition, and multiple foci of osseous metaplasia within the connective tissue (Fig. 4). Rare epithelial-lined invaginations composed of cuboidal cells were present in areas communicating with surrounding stroma, likely representing distorted ductal remnants.

Discussion

Pharyngeal sialoceles are considered relatively uncommon in dogs [2, 4–6, 8, 10, 15–17, 36]. The aetiology remains unclear in approximately 88% of cases [15–17], and no definitive underlying cause was identified in any dog in this series.

Benjamino et al. (2012) reported a predisposition for pharyngeal sialoceles in small breed dogs; however, most dogs in the present study were medium to large breeds, suggesting that body size may not be a consistent risk factor. Nasopharyngeal sialoceles have also been reported in brachycephalic dogs [35], yet none of the dogs described here were brachycephalic.

The dogs in this study were younger than those in previous reports, with a median age of 3.5 years (mean: 3 years and 2 months) compared to a reported median of 6 years (mean: 7.3 years) [10, 15, 36]. The sex distribution (83% male) was consistent with previous findings [10, 15, 36].

Clinical signs of pharyngeal sialoceles typically reflect upper respiratory involvement and may differ from those associated with cervical or sublingual sialoceles. Inspiratory stridor, coughing, and dyspnoea were observed in this series and may necessitate urgent intervention. One dog presented with cyanosis requiring emergency management. In this case oxygen supplementation, vascular access and immediate intubation of the trachea was performed followed by sialocele drainage by aspiration. In another case (Dog 2), the accumulation of saliva rostromedial to the mandibular salivary gland resulted in dorsal displacement of the soft palate and nasopharynx—an uncommon anatomical location for a pharyngeal sialocele.

Concurrent sialoceles affecting multiple anatomical sites are generally considered uncommon. However, three dogs (50%) in this study had both pharyngeal and ipsilateral cervical sialoceles, similar to the 43% rate of multifocal involvement reported by Benjamino et al. (2012).

Fine-needle aspiration is a valuable diagnostic tool in suspected sialoceles, typically yielding viscous, pale yellow to pink fluid consistent with saliva. In this series, aspiration was diagnostic in five dogs, with cytological confirmation of sialocele in three. Differential diagnoses include neoplasia, abscesses, Rathke’s cleft cysts, and other causes of upper airway obstruction [17, 36].

Histopathology was performed in 4/6 cases due to financial constraints of the owners. The histopathological findings observed in these four cases are largely consistent with the histopathological characteristics described in the veterinary literature for canine sialoceles. As expected, all lesions lacked an epithelial lining and displayed pseudocapsules composed of fibrous or granulation tissue, aligning with reports by Lieske and Rissi (2020) and Olimpo et al. (2023) [37]. The presence of mucinous material centrally and the occurrence of ductal dilation at the periphery support the notion of ductal rupture and extravasation of saliva, consistent with chronic mucocele formation. The case showing osseous metaplasia represents a rare but previously documented phenomenon, as reported in isolated cases [38], suggesting a prolonged inflammatory response and stromal reorganization. The focal epithelial linings observed in one case may correspond to entrapped or reactive ductal epithelium, a finding sporadically noted in chronic lesions but not commonly emphasized in published case series. Overall, our cases reinforce the variability in chronic inflammatory response and stromal remodeling associated with canine sialoceles.

Computed tomography (CT) proved especially useful in one case in this study where the lesion was only identified on palpation of the cervical region. CT enabled detailed localisation of the lesion, demonstrated its effect on the nasopharynx, and facilitated surgical planning. Previous studies have highlighted CT’s diagnostic value in cases of pharyngeal sialocele and its superiority over other modalities for anatomical definition and detection of concurrent sialoliths [30, 32, 33, 35].

Surgical excision of the affected salivary glands remains the definitive treatment for sialoceles. Drainage alone carries a high risk of recurrence [10, 14]. In this study, all six dogs underwent mandibular and sublingual sialoadenectomy via a ventral approach, along with partial excision of the pharyngeal sialocele. In cases with airway obstruction, marsupialisation or partial resection of the sialocele may be necessary to alleviate clinical signs.

Drainage was also performed in the three dogs with concurrent cervical involvement. In these three dogs a Penrose drain was used for 24–48 h postoperatively, covered with a sterile absorbable dressing. The use of passive drains has a high risk of ascending bacterial infection compared to closed drainage systems. However, closed suction drains have a higher cost and due to their smaller lumens are more easily blocked by viscous exudates, such as in cases of saliva [39].

Sialoadenectomy may be approached laterally or ventrally [8, 14, 16]. The ventral approach, employed in all cases here, is more extensive and allows for complete removal of the sublingual gland’s rostral lobules by tunnelling beneath the digastricus muscle [8, 16].

Reported complications of sialoadenectomy include seroma formation, infection, haemorrhage, and recurrence [6]. Recurrence has been associated with incomplete sublingual gland removal, excision of the incorrect side, or misidentification of a regional lymph node [6, 8, 16]. Despite published recurrence rates of up to 5%, no recurrences were observed in this series over follow-up periods ranging from 12 to 48 months.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective nature precluded standardisation of diagnostic and surgical protocols, and findings were dependent on the accuracy of medical records. The small sample size limits the ability to draw broad conclusions regarding breed predisposition, treatment strategies, and outcomes. Further prospective studies with larger populations are needed to better characterise the disease and optimise management approaches.

Conclusion

Pharyngeal sialoceles are an uncommon cause of upper airway obstruction in dogs and can present with a variety of clinical signs, most notably respiratory distress. Diagnosis may require a combination of thorough oral and pharyngeal examination, imaging, and fine-needle aspiration. This case series demonstrates that mandibular and sublingual sialoadenectomy, combined with partial excision of the pharyngeal sialocele, is an effective surgical approach. A ventral approach provided adequate exposure and successful outcomes in all cases. The absence of recurrence in this series highlights the importance of complete gland removal. However, larger prospective studies are warranted to better define optimal diagnostic and surgical strategies for this rare condition.
