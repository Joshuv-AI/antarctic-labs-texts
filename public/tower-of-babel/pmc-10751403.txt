Comment on “Sarcopenia is associated with a greater risk of polypharmacy and number of medications: a systematic review and meta‐analysis” by Prokopidis et al.
Authors: Xiaoming Zhang, Yunzhi Yang
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

We recently read the meta‐analysis conducted by Prokopidis and his colleagues with great interest. 1 They showed that older people with sarcopenia had a great risk of polypharmacy or number of medications, compared to those without sarcopenia. The authors have conducted a commendable study, highlighting a strong correlation between sarcopenia and polypharmacy. Given the frequent coexistence of sarcopenia and polypharmacy, future research endeavours should aim to elucidate the mechanisms behind their mutual causation. However, we believe there are some additional questions that warrant discussion.

Firstly, the authors synthesized evidence on the association between sarcopenia and polypharmacy among older individuals, yielding an odds ratio (OR) of 1.65 (95% CI: 1.23–2.20%). It is important to note that the authors calculated the crude OR for this association, which may not fully reflect its independent association. Extracting data from fully adjusted logistic regression models in the original studies and pooling these results could provide a more accurate depiction of the independent association between sarcopenia and polypharmacy. Therefore, presenting the crude association in this article could enhance readers' understanding.

Secondly, we suggest that the authors conduct further analyses based on the original studies. We conducted a meta‐analysis ourselves and found that the pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among sarcopenic older individuals was 50.9% (95% CI: 41.0–60.8%), as shown in Figure 1A. A previous study 2 reported a lower prevalence of polypharmacy (45%, 95% CI: 37–54%) among older individuals aged 65 years and older, which suggests that polypharmacy is more prevalent among those with sarcopenia.

(A) The pooled prevalence of polypharmacy among sarcopenic older people. (B) Sensitivity analysis for the association between sarcopenia and polypharmacy among older people. (C) Sensitivity analysis for the number of medications in older subjects with sarcopenia versus those without sarcopenia.

Thirdly, the systematic review yielded an interesting result in the subgroup analysis based on the population. Sarcopenic older individuals did not exhibit a higher risk of polypharmacy compared to those without sarcopenia among inpatients (pooled OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 0.65–1.89%, P > 0.05). However, subgroup analyses based on population for the impact of sarcopenia on the number of medications showed that sarcopenic individuals had a higher number of medications compared to those without sarcopenia (pooled MD: 1.39, 95% CI: 0.59–2.19%, P < 0.05). This apparent inconsistency should be discussed.

We propose some possible explanations for these contrasting results. Firstly, inpatients typically have acute and chronic diseases, which means both sarcopenic and non‐sarcopenic individuals may have a high number of medications due to their multiple health conditions. As a result, there may be no significant difference in the prevalence of polypharmacy (≥5 medications) between these two groups. However, when examining medications as continuous variables, the sarcopenic group had approximately two more medications than their counterparts. Thus, the impact of sarcopenia on polypharmacy may be more pronounced among community‐dwelling older individuals than hospitalized older individuals.

Fourthly, while the authors performed multiple sensitivity analyses, such as excluding specific studies, the results remained largely consistent. We believe that a common sensitivity analysis, involving the removal of each original study and combining the remaining studies, can determine the stability and robustness of the results. These analyses indicated that the pooled OR for the association between sarcopenia and polypharmacy ranged from 1.59 (95% CI: 1.15–2.19%) to 1.79 (95% CI: 1.33–2.40%), with no significant changes (Figure 1B). However, in terms of the number of medications, the sensitivity analysis revealed a significant change after omitting Cebriai Iranzo's study, with the pooled mean difference being 0.87 (95% CI: 0.24–1.60%) (Figure 1C). Therefore, caution is advised when interpreting the impact of sarcopenia on the number of medications, and future high‐quality cohort studies with large sample sizes should be conducted to explore this relationship among older individuals.
