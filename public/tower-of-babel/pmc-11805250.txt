To Evaluate and Compare the Microleakage of Chitosan Nano Hydrogel-Incorporated MTA and Glass Ionomer Cements When used as a Retrograde Filling Material
Authors: Vinaykumar G. Suryavanshi, Ravi K. Tale, Vivekanand D. Kolvekar, Rashmi Chordiya, P S Amal, B Abijeet
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:An inadequate apical seal stands out as a primary cause of disappointment in surgical endodontic procedures. Therefore, the choice of root-end filling material should aim to prevent the leakage of potential contaminants into the periapical tissue.Aim:To evaluate and compare microleakage of chitosan nano hydrogel-incorporated mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and glass ionomer cement (GIC) when used as a retrograde filling material.Materials and Methods:Sixty single-rooted premolars were decoronated at cemento enamel junction (CEJ), and root canals were prepared and obturated, with the coronal part sealed with a composite. The dye absorption was measured by an automatic spectrophotometer.Results:The results showed that the MTA + chitosan group had less microleakage values compared to the MTA group but was not statistically significant and GIC + chitosan had less microleakage values compared to the GIC group and was statistically significant.Conclusion:Chitosan-incorporated MTA and chitosan-incorporated GIC can be considered as retrograde filling material; however, further in vitro and in vivo research and long-term clinical studies are required.

INTRODUCTION

Conventional endodontic treatment is effective in most cases; however, a case of failed root canal treatment points toward endodontic surgery.[1] Peri-radicular surgery consists of various procedures such as simple curettage/removal of infected root apex/retrograde filling, or a combination of the above.[1] Root-end filling material is used to seal the gap between root canal space and peri radicular tissues. Numerous materials have been suggested and used in the past as root-end fillings; among them, mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and glass ionomer cement (GIC) are commonly used.[2] GIC is an excellent restorative material with properties similar to that of dentine.[34] Because of its biocompatibility, chemical adhesion, and good marginal sealing abilities, it is commonly used as a retrograde filling material. However, their major drawbacks are early moisture sensitivity and solubility when used as a root end-filling material. Also, GIC has inferior fracture toughness when compared to resin-based restorative materials.[5] MTA has excellent properties such as biocompatibility, sealing ability, biomineralizer, nontoxic, does not resorb, nearly no solubility, radio-opaque, minimal leakage, and sets in a damp atmosphere.[67] Despite all the above-mentioned advantages, long setting time and poor handling properties still remain to be resolved. Chitosan is a partially or completely deacetylated derivative of chitin.[8] Due to its characteristics of biodegradability, biocompatibility, hydrophilic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial properties, bioactivity and to be processed in different forms.[9] However, adding material to already existing material should not hamper the existing properties the material possesses.[10] A vital property required for the success of any dental material used for restoration is its ability to bond to tooth structure in such a way that there is no leakage from the margins.

METHODOLOGY

Sixty human single-rooted teeth were decoronated at CEJ using a diamond disk. The access cavity was prepared and pulp tissue was extirpated with a barbed broach. A 10-size K file was inserted 1 mm beyond the apical foramen and the working length was established 1 mm short of the apex. Root canals were enlarged using a Protaper next rotary file (6%, 30 size) with a standard irrigation technique (3% sodium hypochlorite and root canal (RC) Prep). Then, it was stored in normal saline until obturation for 7 days. Master cone was confirmed using IOPA, and canals were dried using absorbent paper points and obturated with 6% 30-size gutta-percha using an AH plus sealer. The coronal parts of root canals were sealed with a composite. Then, specimens were resected apically at 90 degrees to the long axis of the root using a straight fissure bur removing 3 mm of the apex. Next, 1.5 mm retro cavities were prepared using ultrasonic retro tips (size S12/90) under an operating microscope.

Graph 1 illustrates the mean microleakage values for both MTA and MTA + chitosan groups as well as GIC and GIC + chitosan groups. For MTA and MTA + chitosan, the mean microleakage value of the MTA + chitosan group was slightly lower than that of the MTA group; however, the difference was not statistically significant. However, for GIC and GIC + chitosan, the mean microleakage value of the GIC + chitosan group was lower than that of the GIC group, and the difference was statistically significant. In summary, although the addition of chitosan did not significantly affect the microleakage in MTA-treated samples, it significantly reduced microleakage in GIC-treated samples.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, the MTA + chitosan group had slightly lower microleakage values compared to the MTA group but was not found to be statistically significant. The lower microleakage values of the chitosan + MTA group observed in this study can be attributed to the various structural and morphological changes it undergoes when mixed with chitosan. Thus, in the presence of chitosan, the acidity would be less, and the extent of corrosion, pore formation, and subsequent microleakage through the pores would be limited.[5678910] Protonation of chitosan is also known to absorb other molecules into the root canal dentin, resulting in increased penetration into the dentinal tubules. This improves handling properties, causes minor changes in MTA morphologic structure, and increases pH and protonation. Hydroxyapatite forming ability of chitosan can be considered as the postulated mechanism for the least microleakage observed in this group. Chitosan when incorporated into GIC had statistically better results in apical leakage prevention than conventional GIC. Berger et al.[7] in 2004 proposed that covalently crossed-linked chitosan behaves like a smart hydrogel and undergoes reversible discontinuous volume phase changes in response to external physiochemical changes such as temperature and pH and chitosan hydrogel. This in turn would counteract the microleakage tendency of GIC. Thus, chitosan nano hydrogel is known to increase the mechanical and adhesion properties of conventional GIC, which can be attributed to the decrease in microleakage of the GIC + chitosan group. Therefore, based on the results of our study, the sealing ability of MTA and GIC was not negatively affected by the addition of chitosan nano hydrogel.

CONCLUSION

This study concludes that chitosan nano-hydro when incorporated into MTA and GIC liquid to improve the handling and mechanical properties did not affect the sealing ability when used as a root end-filling material. This study also concluded that chitosan-incorporated GIC has better apical sealing properties than conventional GIC due to its improved moisture sensitivity and adhesion. Chitosan-incorporated MTA and GIC stand far better as a retrograde-filling material than MTA and GIC. However, further in vitro and in vivo research and long-term clinical studies are required.
