Association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: The mediating role of functional disability and depressive symptoms
Authors: Huihui He, Suhang Wang, Xiao Huang, Yueping Li, Liwei Jing, Tianwei Xu, Raoping Tu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have documented the impact of intergenerational contact on cognitive function in Chinese adults, however, few have focused on the possible mediating pathways. This study aimed to test a hypothetical model in which functional disability and depressive symptoms mediate the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function.MethodsThis longitudinal study included data of 3666 participants aged 45 years or older (mean age: 60.2 years) from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) from 2011 to 2015. Intergenerational contact was measured as the frequency of contact with children and categorized as frequent (≥ 1 time/week) or infrequent (< 1 time/week). Cognitive function was measured in two dimensions: episodic memory and executive function. Depressive symptoms and functional disability were assessed as continuous variables using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale, Activities of Daily Living, and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scales. The mediating pathways were quantified using the SPSS PROCESS macro.ResultsFrequent intergenerational contact correlated with a better cognitive function (coefficient: 0.73, 95%CI: 0.39 to 1.06), with plausible mediated pathways via functional disability without depressive symptoms (coefficient: 0.03, 95%CI: 0 to 0.06, proportion mediated: 4.11%), depressive symptoms without functional disability (coefficient: 0.04, 95%CI: 0.01 to 0.08, proportion mediated: 5.48%), and functional disability and depressive symptoms in a chain (coefficient: 0.01, 95%CI: 0 to 0.02, proportion mediated: 1.37%).ConclusionFunctional disability and depressive symptoms may partly explain the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms underlying the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20756-7.Keywords: Intergenerational contact, Cognitive function, Functional disability, Depressive symptoms

Background

The decline in cognitive function is been considered as one of the most deleterious but inevitable aspects of aging [1]. Recent data indicated that among Chinese adults aged 55 or older, the prevalence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia was 15.4% and 6.0%, respectively [2, 3]. As a result, the cost of dementia in China in 2020 reached 360.8 billion USD, imposing a tremendous burden on families and healthcare systems [4]. Since there is currently no effective pharmaceutical treatment for dementia, it is important to identify modifiable intermediate risk factors that could prevent cognitive decline [5].

Intergenerational support and cognitive function

When we examine the factors affecting cognitive function in older adults, it is important to consider the cultural context of intergenerational support, especially in Asian countries where blood relations have been emphasized, and older parent-adult child connections are a core family attachment [6]. According to the social integration theory proposed by Berkman et al., intergenerational support is a type of social support that provides instrumental, informational, appraisal, and emotional support. This theoretical framework has further made it possible to link intergenerational support to better cognitive function through health behavior (e.g., exercise promotion), psychological (e.g., reduction of depression), and physiological pathways (e.g., lowering levels of allostatic load) [7]. As a core element of intergenerational support for older adults, the frequency of contact between aging parents and adult children is considered an important indicator of intergenerational relationships, which may impact the cognitive function of aging parents [8, 9]. One systematic review concluded that maintaining strong intergenerational contact is essential for promoting cognitive function and mental health outcomes in older adults, for example, intergenerational contact can improve cognitive function by stimulating brain activity and releasing neurochemicals such as oxytocin and dopamine, reducing loneliness and social isolation by providing a sense of purpose and value and opportunities to share life experiences and skills, reducing stress and anxiety levels by providing a sense of belonging, purpose, and emotional fulfillment [10]. Although previous studies have revealed that intergenerational support plays a key role in the material and psychological well-being of middle-aged parents [11], most have focused on the effect of intergenerational support on cognitive function in aging parents. For example, a longitudinal study in South Korea showed that non-visual contact (i.e., contact by telephone, letter, or email) with the first-born child is beneficial to parents’ (aged ≥ 50 years) cognitive function [12]. Another longitudinal study on older Chinese adults also found that children’s frequent visits were associated with a lower incidence of cognitive impairment in their parents (aged ≥ 65 years) [13]. In addition, middle-aged individuals are likely to experience cognitive decline, possibly because of the onset of hypertension and diabetes [14]. This is especially the case among Chinese middle-aged adults, among whom the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes has reached as high as 38% and 16% respectively [15], and a few studies have simultaneously explored the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function among middle-aged and older parents and identified the factors that may mediate this association.

The potential mediating effect of functional disability/depressive symptoms

Disability plays an important role in mediating the effects of intergenerational contact on cognitive function. Having a strong social network is associated with fewer functional limitations in older adults [16, 17], and older adults with physical disabilities present faster cognitive decline than those with pre-physical disabilities [18]. These findings not only support the idea that individuals who maintain cognitive capabilities have a behavioral and psychosocial profiles that differ from those with cognitive decline [19], but also indicate that physical function may strengthen the association between social support and cognitive function [20]. The conceptual model of social relationships hypothesizes that health is impacted by social support not only through physiological pathways, but also through psychological pathways [7]. Several European studies indicated that infrequent parent-child contact was associated with increases in parents’ depressive symptoms [21, 22], which has been recognized as the most common mental illnesses in middle-aged and older adults, with a prevalence ranging from 31.3 to 39.9% in China, and is thought to be a risk factor for cognitive decline [23, 24]. Although many studies have confirmed this longitudinal relationship between depressive symptoms and cognitive function among older people [25], regular monitoring of mental health conditions and cognitive function is still scarce in China’s national survey and is absent in the national essential public health services that are free of charge to all citizens (e.g., medical check-ups and chronic disease follow-up), let alone discussion on the role of depressive symptoms in the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function at the national level.

The potential chain mediating role of functional disability and depressive symptoms

Health problems may limit one’s capacity to participate in daily activities and reduce the sense of control over environmental challenges, thereby contributing to feelings of helplessness and hopelessness [26]. Studies have shown that disability in old age may be associated with a more severe level of depressive symptoms [26, 27]. This evidence suggests that the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function may be first affected by the functional disability, and then by depressive symptoms.

This study aims to examine whether the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function is mediated by functional disability and depressive symptoms that occurred during the follow-up period using a longitudinal sample of 3666 middle-aged and older Chinese adults.

Methods

Data and sample

Data for this study were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort survey comprising people aged 45 years and older. Eligible people were selected by multi-stage probability sampling. The first wave of the survey started in 2011, with each wave being followed up every 2–3 years, including an assessment of the participants’ social, economic, and health status [27]. In the current study, we used the data from three waves (2011, 2013, and 2015) of CHARLS (Figs. 1 and 2).

Timeline for the measurement variables

As shown in Fig. 1, after excluding those who (1) were younger than 45 years old (n = 368); (2) had missing information on intergenerational contact (n = 9847); (3) were lost or refused to follow-up (n = 1726); and (4) had no information on cognitive function in 2015 (n = 2098), 3666 participants remained in the analytical sample.

All participants signed an informed consent form. All CHARLS waves were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Peking University (approval no. IRB00001052–11015).

Measures

Intergenerational contact

Intergenerational contact was measured by the frequency of contact between older parents and their adult children who were not cohabiting with their parents at baseline (2011), using two questions: (1) “How often do you have face-to-face contacts?”, and (2) “How often do you contact by phone/by message/on WeChat/by mail/by email?” Each question has nine options, with frequency ranging from “almost never” to “almost every day”. The most frequent contact between the two questions was used to define the degree of contact. Those with one or more contacts every week were treated as having frequent contact, otherwise, they were treated as having infrequent contact [28].

Cognitive function

Face-to-face interviews, including episodic memory, orientation, attention, and visual construction, were used to evaluate cognitive function at baseline and follow-up examinations. Episodic memory was measured by asking participants to immediately and with a delay (i.e., 5 min later) repeat as many words as they could after the interviewers read 10 random words; summary scores of the immediate (ranging from 0 to 10) and delayed recalls were calculated (ranging from 0 to 10). The measurements for orientation and attention were mainly based on a reduced form of Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status, including the recognition of date: month, day, year, season, and day of the week (ranging from 0 to 5), and serial subtraction of 7s from 100 (up to five times) (ranging from 0 to 5). Visual construction was evaluated based on the ability to redraw a picture of overlapping pentagons (ranging from 0 to 1). The participants received one point for each correct answer. Summary scores for episodic memory, orientation, attention, and visual construction were calculated (ranging from 0 to 31), with higher scores representing better cognitive function [29].

Functional disability

Self-reported limitations in activities of daily living (ADL) (six items: eating, bathing, dressing, getting in and out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling urination and defecation) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) (six items: doing household chores, preparing meals, shopping, making phone calls, managing money, and taking medications) were used to measure the degree of functional disability. Each item consists of four response options (1. No difficulty; 2. Difficulty but can be done; 3. Some difficulty and need assistance; 4. Unable to complete), with a value of 1 assigned to those who cannot complete independently or are restricted, and a value of 0 assigned to those who can complete independently. We then combined these items into a continuous scale (range: 0–12) to calculate the total number of ADL and IADL limitations, for enhancing the sensitivity of the measurement [30]. Higher scores indicated worse physical function. This was assessed during the first follow-up examination (2013).

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale, with four options for each item: 0 = rarely or none of the time, less than 1 day; 1 = some or a little of the time, 1–2 days; 2 = occasionally or a moderate amount of the time, 3–4 days and 3 = most or all of the time, 5–7 days. Amongst the 10 questions, eight questions were negative statements, and two questions were positive statements. Responses were recorded on a scale from 0 (rarely) to 3 (most of the time) for negative questions and from 3 (rarely) to 0 (most of the time) for positive questions. Summary scores of the 10 questions were calculated (ranging from 0 to 30), with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms [31]. Additionally, a cutoff score of ≥ 10 was employed to identify respondents with significant depressive symptoms [32]. The scores for depressive symptoms were calculated on the same occasion (2013) when the measurement of functional disability was conducted.

Covariates

According to previous studies on aspects of cognitive function, we chose the age of 60 as the cutoff for dichotomizing age into middle-aged adults (45–59) and older adults (≥ 60) [33]. Education was classified as ’≤ 6 years’ and ’> 6 years’ based on the maximum years of schooling. Marital status was categorized as married or nonmarried (separated/ divorced/widowed/single). Residential location was divided into ‘rural’ and ‘urban’ areas. Smoking status was dichotomized as current non-smokers and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was categorized as occasional drinkers (less than or equal to 3 times per week) and habitual drinkers (more than 3 times per week). Chronic diseases were defined as whether they had ever been diagnosed by a doctor with any chronic diseases or taken any treatments currently and categorized into “Yes” or “No”.

Statistical analysis

First, to compare the characteristics of the frequent and infrequent intergenerational contact categories, we conducted an independent t-test for continuous variables and a chi-square test for categorical variables. Second, given that it is important to hypothesize that the mediator is significantly correlated with both the independent and dependent variables in the mediation model, which could provide the basis for mediation analysis, and the variables “intergenerational contact, functional disability, and cognitive function” did not follow a normal distribution, therefore, the correlations between intergenerational contact, functional disability, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function were assessed by Spearman correlation analyses. Third, since PROCESS is an observed-variable modeling tool that relies on ordinary least squares (OLS) and appears more reasonable when testing serial or sequential and parallel mediation, bootstrap confidence intervals were a suitable approach for detecting specific indirect effects [34]. We applied the PROCESS macro of Model 6, which was developed by Andrew F. Hayes, to examine the mediating role of functional disability and depressive symptoms in the relationship between intergenerational contact and cognitive function, and used the bias-corrected bootstrap method with 5000 resamples to test the significance of the mediation effect [35]. Age, sex, education, residential location, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and chronic diseases were introduced into the models as covariates.

We performed five sensitivity analyses by repeating the mediation analyses: (1) excluding participants with emotional or psychiatric problems or memory-related diseases to reduce potential recall bias; (2) excluding participants with MCI at baseline or 2013 to limit the sample to those without MCI; (3) restricting participants to those who had no functional disability and depressive symptom at baseline; (4) including participants with missing data on intergenerational contact or those lost to follow-up, with missing values replaced using multiple imputations by chained equations; and (5) conducting two subgroup analyses based on the type of intergenerational contact (visual vs. non-visual) and age group (45–60 vs. ≥60).

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Among the 3666 participants, 42.0% were females. The mean age was 60.2 ± 8.3 years old, and 72.4% of the parents have contacted with their adult children frequently (≥ 1 time per week). Compared to participants with frequent contact, those with infrequent contact were more likely to be older, male, less educated, non-married, be from rural areas, have functional disabilities and depressive symptoms, and lower cognitive function scores (Table 1). Compared to participants with complete data, the participants we observed were more likely to live in rural areas, be older, female, unmarried, current smokers, habitual drinkers, have high levels of education and chronic diseases, and exhibit fewer depressive symptoms, less functional disability, and lower cognitive function. Mediation pathways via functional disability and depressive symptoms

Results for the mediating effects of functional disability and depressive symptoms on the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function are shown in Fig. 3 and Table 3. The total effect of intergenerational contact on cognitive function was 0.73 (95%CI: 0.39 to 1.06), indicating that if parents had a higher frequency of intergenerational contact with adult children, they were more likely to have an increased cognitive function. The regression coefficient for the mediating effect represented the change in cognitive function for every unit change in intergenerational contact, which was mediated by functional disability (coefficient = 0.03, 95%CI: 0 to 0.06) or depressive symptoms (coefficient = 0.04, 95%CI: 0.01 to 0.08). In addition, we found a chain mediating effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms on the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function (coefficient = 0.01, 95%CI: 0 to 0.02). Depressive symptoms accounted for the largest mediating effect compared to functional disability, functional disability and depressive symptoms in the chain (5.48% vs. 4.11% vs. 1.37%). These results revealed that intergenerational contact can indirectly predict cognitive function not only through the independent mediating effect of functional disability or depressive symptoms, but also through the chain mediating effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms.

Sensitivity analysis

Similar results were obtained regardless of the exclusion of participants with emotional psychiatric problems or memory-related diseases at baseline, or participants with MCI at baseline or in 2013, or those with functional disability and depressive symptoms at baseline (Table S2). In addition, after imputing missing data on intergenerational contacts or dropouts, all results remained largely unchanged (Table S2). Based on a subgroup analysis by intergenerational contact, the mediation effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms on the association between non-visual contact and cognitive function was observed (coefficient = 0.08, 95%CI: 0.03 to 0.13), but no such mediating effect on the association between visual contact and cognitive function (coefficient = 0.03, 95%CI: -0.01 to 0.07) (Table S3). The age-subgroup analysis showed that there was no age difference in the mediating effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms on the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function (Table S3).

Discussion

In this study, we found that functional disability and depressive symptoms partially mediated the longitudinal association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Among them, depressive symptoms accounted for the largest mediating effect, even after considering the chain mediating effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study to investigate the potential mediating role of functional disability and depressive symptoms in the relationship between intergenerational contact and cognitive function. A previous cross-sectional study conducting restrictive cubic spline analysis using the same database showed that infrequent contact with adult children was more likely to cause depressive symptoms, which was consistent with our findings [36]. However, a cross-sectional study based on CHARLS 2018 using logistic regression found that older adults (aged 60 years and above) with infrequent emotional communication were less likely to experience disabilities [37]. This may be because of the inclusion of middle-aged adults at baseline in our study. This speculation was confirmed by additional analysis (data not shown) in which similar trends were observed when we followed the criteria of aforementioned study, that is, only focused on the older adults. Moreover, a recent cross-sectional study based on the Chinese Family Panel Survey (CFPS) found that intergenerational relationships may be weakened by the dysfunction, and played a buffering role between the functional limitation and the happiness by performing structural equation modeling analysis [38]. Our study extends this work by providing longitudinal evidence and considering cognitive function as an outcome. It would also be possible to perform a causal mediation analysis by disentangling the mediator and exposure-mediator interactions, which might be more useful for understanding the mechanism. However, this three-way decomposition may require a larger statistical size and may be a direction for future research.

The present study demonstrated the mediating effect of functional disability on intergenerational contact and cognitive function, which is supported by previous studies. Adults aged 50 years and older with infrequent family contact are more likely to suffer from functional limitations in Europe [39], which has been demonstrated to be significantly associated with faster cognitive decline among older adults [18, 40]. One possible explanation may be that aging parents who contacted their adult children infrequently are less likely to have healthy lifestyles because adult children may provide instrumental support to meet their health needs (e.g., providing financial support for parents to help access health services) and modulate health behaviors (e.g., monitoring tobacco use among parents), thus negatively affecting physical functioning [41, 42]. Previous studies have also indicated that the frequency of intergenerational contact is related to whether the middle-aged parents were disabled or not [11]. Our finding that those who infrequently contacted adult children were more likely to have functional disabilities (Table 1) supports this speculation to some extent. On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that older adults with functional disabilities were less likely to engage in physical activity (26.2% vs.35.2%), which can lead to reduced cognitive reserves and further cause a decline in cognitive function [43]. Our study confirmed the mediating role of depressive symptoms in the association between intergenerational contact and cognitive function. Previous research provides evidence that the association between social relations and adverse health outcomes may be influenced by psychological factors [44]. This may be possible because social relationships can act as a buffer against emotional stress (e.g., by having someone to confide in or provide advice) [45], reducing adverse psychological reactions. Mental stimulation improves cognitive strategies and increases neuronal growth, contributing to cognitive reserves by activating and strengthening of neurobiological pathways [46]. Our findings show that those in contact with adult children frequently had lower depressive symptom scores (Table 1), which supports this explanation. Conversely, infrequent parent-child contact may increase the levels of stress hormones such as cortisol, which have been shown to induce negative emotions [47]. This can lead to further damage to the hippocampus by increasing glucocorticoid levels in the brain, inducing inflammation and vascular disease, and eventually contributing to an impaired level of cognitive function [22, 43]. In this study, we also found that depressive symptoms accounted for a larger mediating effect than functional disability (5.48% vs.4.11%). One possible explanation for this is that the impact of intergenerational contact on depressive symptom mainly manifests as emotional support, whereas functional disability is instrumental support. The experiences of emotional support (e.g., providing advice and comfort to the respondent) were more positive than instrumental support (e.g., receiving help with cleaning, household chores, or odd jobs) when it is from kin, as instrumental support threatens autonomy [48].

Another important finding is the sequential mediating effect of functional disability and depressive symptom. A previous study has shown that social support has a positive effect on individuals’ physical and mental health [45]. Our findings confirm this positive effect that family contact may correlate with better physical function (i.e., enhancing the ability to perform ADL and instrumental ADL) and mental health (i.e., reducing depressive symptoms) in middle-aged and older adults (Table 2). Moreover, the results of this study showed that functional disability positively correlated with depressive symptoms, consistent with the results of Ahmad et al. [49]. The experience of physical disability generally disrupts routine life patterns and requires subsequent behavioral adjustment (i.e., needing help), and reactions such as the sense of worthlessness or chronic stress can lead to an increase in depressive symptoms [50].

Our findings regarding visual and non-visual contact are further in line with a previous Korean study showing that the effect of non-visual contact on cognitive function is more beneficial than that of visual contact [12]. One possible explanation is that non-visual contact is a more convenient way to connect with family members, especially when parents are far from their children. Our study builds on this finding by investigating the mediating effects of functional disability and depressive symptoms on this association. However, no such associations were found between non-visual (weekly contact with children by phone or email) and visual (living with others or often being visited by children) contacts [51, 52] and cognitive function in two other Chinese cohort studies. These were possibly due to the age range of the study population in Korea being the same as our study (aged ≥ 45), but different from aforementioned two Chinese studies (aged ≥ 60 and ≥ 80 respectively). Our findings need to be confirmed in future studies using the same range of study population.

Public health implications

Our study revealed three mediating pathways between intergenerational contact and cognitive function: functional disability, depressive symptoms, and the chain effect of functional disability and depressive symptoms. These results may improve our understanding of the potential mechanisms linking intergenerational contact with cognitive function in middle-aged and older adults. Our results may further facilitate the current efforts to increase the effectiveness of intergenerational contact to prevent cognitive decline, such as the integration of family caregivers into the aged care team to provide individualized care, and the renovation of age-friendly homes [53]. Currently, the available policies in China to improve intergenerational support encompasses the inclusion of special additional deductions such as expenditure for older parents, children’s education for the calculation of individual taxable income, and the establishment of family mutual-aid accounts in health insurance. However, possible mediators to strengthen the effectiveness of these policies have not yet been emphasized. Our results could advance toward this end by targeting the identified mediators and simultaneously developing a risk assessment of cognitive decline concerning intergenerational contact, functional ability, and mental health. This is particularly important because 15.5% of adults (aged ≥ 50 years) had MCI in China [2] and no effective pharmaceutical treatment for cognitive decline currently. Additionally, it has been suggested that the frequency and duration of contact are key determinants of the strength or quality of intergenerational relationships. Spontaneous interactions alone may be insufficient to establish strong intergenerational relationships among community-dwelling adults [54, 55]. Increasing the frequency of intergenerational contact could help sustain and enhance intergenerational support among these adults as well [54]. This evidence suggests that focusing on increasing the frequency of intergenerational contact may be a feasible intervention for improving intergenerational relationships and support.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has some strengths. We used nationally representative large sample and prospective design, which allows the results generalized in the similar intergenerational culture, and the control for a variety of potentially confounding factors in the main analysis to make the results more robust. Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations. Firstly, the main variables in this study were self-reported, and there may have been a potential recall bias, likely contributing to an overestimation of the effect. Secondly, this study has selection bias, possibly due to non-responsiveness at the baseline and dropouts during follow-up. Nevertheless, we used multiple imputations to address this issue and showed consistent results before and after the imputation. Selection bias may not have been a major concern in this study. However, multiple imputation could not fully address the existence of immortal time bias. As the missing of intergenerational contact was likely due to only the primary respondent in each household being investigated about the frequency of intergenerational contact (total households: 10,199), and one-third of dropouts owning to death, respectively, our final analytical sample might be overrepresented by participants who were more likely to live in rural areas, be older, female, current smokers, habitual drinkers, have high levels of education and chronic diseases, and exhibit fewer depressive symptoms, less functional disability, and lower cognitive function, compared with the total population. We cannot rule out the possibility that factors connecting non-responsiveness and dropout might be modifiers of the associations we observed. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution. Thirdly, the quantity of intergenerational contact was assessed in this study, leaving its quality of intergenerational contact uninvestigated. The quality of contact may be exemplified by the content of contact, satisfaction, or time range. Lastly, some potential confounders such as income, and spouse support were not available in this study, which may have caused residual confounding; therefore, our results need to be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Frequent contact between middle-aged and older parents and their adult children may have an indirect effect on cognitive function through physical function and mental health, and also highlight the importance of psychosocial components in determining the level of cognitive function in middle-aged and older parents.
