Predictive modelling of Uranium (238U) and Thorium (232Th) in soils of Central India: integrating ICP-MS/MS, spectroscopic, and machine learning models
Authors: G S Tagore, Devid Kumar Sahu, Y M Sharma, Sanjay Srivastava, R K Nema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal Positioning System (GPS) based 2216 surface soil (0–15 cm) samples were collected across the Kymore Plateau and Satpura hill zone of Madhya Pradesh, India using multi-layer sampling strategy to ensure spatial representativeness. The concentrations of Uranium (238U) and Thorium (232Th) were analysed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS/MS) with spectral data acquired through a Spectro-radiometer. The 238U concentrations ranged from 0.15 to 852.15 ppb (mean: 94.92 ppb), while 232Th ranged from 0.06 to 1385.06 ppb (mean: 71.73 ppb). Soil pH exhibited a positive correlation with 238U and a negative correlation with ²³²Th. Several machine learning (ML) models were evaluated for prediction, including Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR), Support Vector Regression(SVR), Random Forest (RF), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) and Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), the ANN demonstrated the balanced and robust performance. For 238U, ANN achieved a testing coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.36 ( transformed data), root mean square error (RMSE) of 2.87, and ratio of performance to deviation (RPD) of 1.25, while for ²³²Th it achieved the highest testing R² of 0.56, RMSE of 0.46, and RPD of 1.51. Data transformation square root of 238U () and logarithmic of 232Th (ln 232Th) improved model performance by stabilizing variance. Geostatistical analysis using an exponential semivariogram model indicated moderate spatial dependence (SD) for 238U (nugget-to-sill (N/S) ratio ~ 43%, range 18.23 km) and moderate to strong SD for ²³²Th (30%, range 11.06 km). Validation results root mean square standardised error (RMSS ≈ 1) confirmed reliable uncertainty estimation. Spatial distribution maps revealed 232Th enrichment in the Panna-Katni region and 238U enrichment in the Northern districts (Katni-Panna-Satna) and Southern Seoni, with Eastern districts of study area showing relatively lower concentrations.Keywords: Uranium, Thorium, Spectro-radiometer, Machine learning models, ICP-MS/MS, Geostatistical, GPSSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Uranium (238U) and Thorium (232Th), naturally occurring radioactive elements with atomic numbers 92 and 90 and atomic weights of 238.03 and 232.04, respectively, were discovered by German Chemist Martin Heinrich Klaproth in 1789. In its crystalline state, U exhibits valence states ranged from + 3 to + 6; however, only hexavalent uranyl ions (UO22+) are both thermodynamically and kinetically stable in aqueous solutions relevant to biological systems. U forms several oxides such as UO2, U3O8, and UO3. Uranyl compounds, or uranates, are typically produced by fusing U with naturally occurring carbonates.

Phosphorus (P) fertilizers are known to increase 238U and 232Th concentrations in agricultural soils, raising environmental and human health concerns. Elevated levels of these elements are commonly found in P-rich soils. Due to their chemical toxicity and radio-toxicity, 238U and 232Th are considered significant environmental pollutants, capable of causing harm to humans and ecosystems1–3. Similar to other heavy metals and radio-nuclides, 238U can enter the food chain through soil-to-plant transfer4.

U is present in the Earth’s crust at concentrations of 2 to 3 mg kg− 1, but anthropogenic activities such as mining, ore milling, nuclear fuel processing, weapons development, and nuclear accidents have led to increased levels in soil systems. These elevated concentrations pose risks to both human and ecological health5,6. Furthermore, land use changes and climate variability can alter soil physicochemical properties, influencing U behaviour in terrestrial environments.

The environmental mobility and bioavailability of 238U are determined not just by total concentrations, but also by its chemical speciation and association with different soil fractions. U associated with exchangeable fractions is considered the most bio-available, whereas forms bound to residual, oxidizable, or reducible fractions tend to be more stable and less accessible-unless soil conditions such as pH or redox potential (Eh) change7. Over time, U may redistribute among these soil fractions, potentially altering its bioavailability due to soil ageing processes.

The continuous application of phosphate fertilizers contributes to the accumulation of 238U in soils8, with higher concentrations typically observed in top-soils compared to sub-soils9. Mobilized 238U can leach into surface and groundwater, raising further health concerns based on both epidemiological and animal studies10,11.

To monitor and assess heavy metal contamination, hyper-spectral visible and near-infrared (VNIR) spectroscopy has emerged as a promising tool. When combined with appropriate calibration techniques, it allows for the rapid, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly prediction of soil heavy metal content12,13. Unlike traditional in situ methods, hyper-spectral remote sensing offers significant advantages for large-scale monitoring.To the best of our knowledge, no studies exist that specifically examined the feasibility to predict higher dimensional geochemical data from lower dimensional and legacy geochemical data. However, there have been attempts to use machine learning and auxiliary/ancillary data to predict geochemical elemental concentrations for the purpose of mapping. In the past, various machine learning algorithms have been demonstrated to be able to predict elemental concentrations14. This study aims to develop a predictive model for mapping 238U and 232Thconcentrations in agricultural soils using hyper-spectral data, thereby supporting environmental and human health risk assessments.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Kymore Plateau and Satpura Hill Zone in central Madhya Pradesh, India were selected that includes the districts of Jabalpur, Katni, Seoni, Panna, Rewa, Sidhi, Singhroli, and Satna. This zone is situated between 21º 35’ 19” to 25º 08’ 15” North latitude and 79º 15’ 17” to 82° 48’ 30” East longitude. The total geographical area of this zone is 49.97 lakh ha. There is a wide variation in physiographic, soils, rainfall, irrigationand cropping pattern in various part of the zone. The region is characterized by a tropical climate with amean annual rainfall of 1428 mm.

Sample collection, processing and analysis

Sampling strategy

Multi-layer thematic overlay: GPS based 2216 surface soil samples (0–15 cm) were collected from farmer’s fields during the off season of 2022–2023. The selection of these fields was based on a multi-layer thematic overlay. Sampling strategy utilizes a geospatial multi-layer overlay technique to ensure high spatial representativeness across the study area. By intersecting the soil type the map downloaded from Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) https://www.fao.org/soils-portal/en/ and soil geomorphology of Geological Survey of India (GSI) with Remote Sensing data based agricultural land use, downloaded from https://bhuvan-app1.nrsc.gov.in/thematic/thematic/index.php and Environmental Systems Research Institute (ESRI) Sentinel 2 Land Cover Explorer https://livingatlas.arcgis.com/landcoverexplorer/#mapCenter=35.13695%2C11.13721%2C11&mode=step&timeExtent=2017%2C2023&year=2023. The landscape is partitioned into distinct biophysical units. These units are further refined by AESR (Agro-Ecological Sub-Region) zones to account for climatic variability and constrained within district boundaries using a 5 km x 5 km grid downloaded from https://soilgrids.org/. This intersection creates “Thematic Strata” where each sampling point represents a unique combination of soil properties, land management, and climate15. By selecting sites within these intersecting polygons, the strategy eliminates spatial bias and ensures that the samples are statistically representative of the entire district’s diverse agricultural landscape using ArcGIS Earth 1.x downloaded from https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-earth/overview. Multi-criteria weighted-overlay analysis performed using Quantum Geographic Information System (QGIS) version 3.34 downloaded from https://qgis.org. (Figs. 1 and 2).

Soil sample processing

The soil samples were air dried to remove moisture, crushed using a wooden hammer, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. After that 238U and 232Th content in soil were determined by following the procedure given by16. Soil extracted with AB-DTPA extracting solution (consisting of 1 M NH4HCO3 + 0.005 N DTPA, buffered at pH 7.6) in 1:2 ratio and shaken for 15 min in a temperature-controlled shaker (at 25° C). The supernatant was filtered with Whatman No 42 and the concentration of 238U and 232Th in filtrate was determined using ICP-MS/MS (Triple Quadrupole (QQQ) Model-Agilent 8900 Agilent Technologies, Tokyo, Japan).

ICP-MS/MS

Using a single quadrupole (MS) detector, ions generated in the plasma are filtered by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), allowing direct quantification of isotopes such as 238U and 232Th. While this mode offers high sensitivity and simplicity, it is susceptible to polyatomic and isobaric interferences, which can be partially mitigated by collision/reaction gases like helium (He) or hydrogen (H2). In contrast, tandem quadrupole (MS/MS) employs a Q1-q2-Q3 configuration, where Q1 selects the precursor ion, q2 serves as a collision/reaction cell, and Q3 analyzes the product ion. This setup significantly improves interference removal, by converting 238U to 238U16O+ to separate it from hydride or polyatomic interferences, enabling accurate quantification even in complex matrices such as seawater or sediments.

The instrument was set at an RF power of 1550 W, with argon (Ar) plasma gas flow at 15 L min− 1 and auxiliary gas flow at 0.9 L min⁻¹, while the nebulizer gas flow (1.0 L min− 1) was optimized daily. Samples were introduced via a MicroMist nebulizer coupled to a Peltier-cooled spray chamber, and nickel sampler and skimmer cones were used. High-purity He (≥ 99.999%) served as the collision gas in MS/MS mode at a flow rate of 5 mL min− 1 (optimized daily) to remove polyatomic interferences, with Q1 set to the target mass and Q2 monitoring the same mass unless mass-shift mode was required; high-purity Ar (≥ 99.999%) was used as the plasma gas.

Quantification was performed using multi-element external calibration standards prepared from certified stock solutions traceable to National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), typically within a calibration range of 0.1 to 100 µg L− 1 depending on the element, and internal standards were introduced. Calibration curves were accepted with linearity of R² ≥ 0.995. Instrument and method detection limits (IDLs and MDLs) were calculated as three times the standard deviation of procedural blanks (n ≥ 7), and limits of quantification (LOQs) were defined as ten times the standard deviation of blanks.

Prior to analysis, the instrument was optimized daily using the Agilent tuning solution according to the manufacturer’s recommended procedure to achieve maximum sensitivity, stable signal intensity, low oxide formation (CeO⁺/Ce⁺ < 2%), and minimal doubly charged ion formation (Ce²⁺/Ce⁺ < 3%).

Calibration was carried out using certified multi-element standard solutions supplied by Agilent Technologies (Environmental Calibration Standards), traceable to NIST. Calibration for U and Th was performed using external multi-point calibration standards at 0, 25, 50, and 100 ppb [Fig. 3 (a) and (b)].

(a) and (b) Calibration curve of Th and U using ICP-MS/MS.

Standards were prepared from certified stock solutions traceable to NIST and diluted in 2% (v/v) HNO₃ trace metal grade from Sigma Aldrich (J.T. Bekar) to match the sample matrix. A blank (0 ppb) was included to verify background correction. Calibration curves were generated using linear regression with an acceptance criterion of R² ≥ 0.995. Quality assurance and quality control measures included analysis of procedural blanks with each batch, continuing calibration verification every 10 samples (acceptable recovery 90–110%), spike recoveries of 85–115%, certified reference materials analyzed with each batch within certified recovery ranges, and duplicate analyses of at least 10% of samples with relative standard deviation (RSD) below 5%.

Continuing calibration verification standards were analyzed periodically during sample runs to confirm instrument stability and accuracy, with acceptable recoveries within 90–110% (Fig. 4).

A fitted semi-variogram consists of three fundamental components: the nugget, range, and sill (Fig. 4).

The semi-variogram equalization takes off a height known as the sill (C0 + C1), which is divided into two parts: the nugget (C0) and the partial sill (C1). The N/S ratio, (C0/(C0 + C1), was calculated in order to characterize the SD of the data. Strong spatial dependency is indicated by a N/S ratio of less than 25%, and moderate SD is suggested by a ratio of greater than 75%19.

Validation

To evaluate the predictive performance, key validation Mean Error (ME) should ideally be close to 0, which indicates that the model is unbiased and not over- or under-estimating values. Precision is accessed via the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), where the smallest possible value is preferred. To check the consistency of the error, the Mean Standardized Error (MSE) should also be close to 0. Finally, the Root Mean Square Standardized Error (RMSSE) serves as a diagnostic for the variance estimation; it should ideally be equal to 1, as values significantly > 1 suggest the model is underestimating the true variability, while values < 1 indicate an overestimation.

Spectral data acquisition

The spectra of soil samples recorded with the help of Spectro-radiometer (RS-3500). Air-dried samples were placed evenly on a rectangular black disk of 5 cm diameter and 2 cm depth by tapping the tray on a table to ensure a smooth surface. The soil-filled rectangular black disk was kept on the dark background of a table and light reflectance was measured. Reflectance measurements were taken and spectral signatures were made in the 350 to 3500 nm wavelength region of the spectrum (Fig. 6).

ML models for prediction

Several models, including PLSR, SVR, Random Forest, ANN, XGBoost, is a method developed by Chen based on gradient boosting20. To address the overfitting problem, XGBoost adds a regularization component to the objective function21. CatBoost, is a permutation-based approach that differs from conventional algorithms. It is a distinctive method for processing categorical data in data processing22. In order to manage category features, CatBoost uses target statistics as additional numerical features. This is a highly successful strategy that minimizes information loss. CatBoost uses Ordered Boosting, a type of gradient-based regularization that prevents overfitting by limiting model complexity. LightGBM and ELM were used using MATLAB (Matrix Laboratory) R2023b version 23.2.0 trial at the MathWorks website to predict 238U and 232Th content in soil.

Flow chart for prediction

Soil samples were analyzed for 238U and 232Th using ICP-MS/MS, while hyper spectral data were collected using a Spectro-radiometer, forming a complete dataset of 2,216 samples. The complete dataset was divided into training and testing sets in an 80:20 ratio. Mathematically, the training set size was calculated as (0.80 × 2216 = 1773), and the testing set size was calculated as (0.20 × 2216 = 443). The whole data set every fifth sample was assigned to the testing set. The dataset was divided into 80% for training and 20% for testing set. The training dataset was used to derive the spectral model and the testing dataset was applied to check the predictive performance of the newly developed model and tested the model’s performance using the testing dataset. The model’s performance evaluated using metrics such as R2, RMSE and residual prediction deviation (RPD) values (Fig. 7).

Effect of pH on 238U and 232Th content in soil

The results presented in Table 3 and depicted in Fig. 8 showed that the 238U concentration in soil across varying pH ranges showed a clear trend: mean 238U concentrations increased with pH, ranged from 74.12 ppb in strongly acidic soils (pH < 5.0) to 119.86 ppb in alkaline soils (pH > 8.0), with an overall mean of 94.87 ppb. Similarly, the mean 238U concentration generally increases from acidic to alkaline conditions, suggesting that it is more mobile or available in higher pH environments. In acidic soils, 238U tends to bind more strongly to mineral surfaces and organic matter, reducing its mobility and bioavailability. As pH increases, 238U becomes more soluble, in the presence of carbonate, forming stable uranyl-carbonate complexes, which explains the elevated concentrations in neutral to alkaline soils (pH 6.6 to 8.0 or > 8.0).

The ensemble tree-based models (RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost) exhibited high R2 values (0.83 to 0.84) and low RMSE (40.66) in the training set. Conclusions

The concentrations of 238U and 232Th were within the typical global soil ranges and did not exceed recommended safety thresholds. Nevertheless, located hotspots in Panna and Katni warrant further monitoring. Soil pH was found to influence the availability of 238U and 232Th, and showed a positive correlation with 238U content but a negative correlation with 232Th content. Among the ML models tested, the ANN showed the good performance for estimating 238U and 232Th concentrations in soils. Geostatistical mapping using Ordinary Kriging revealed the spatial heterogeneity of 238U and 232Th distribution, which may support future geological, environmental assessment.
