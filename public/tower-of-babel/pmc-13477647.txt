New-Onset Psychotic Episode as the Primary Manifestation One Week After Liver Transplantation: A Case Report and Comprehensive Review About the Risk Factors
Authors: Yanqun Zheng, Zhuyao Lin, Mo Zhu, Qianwei Wang, Shenxun Shi, Chunhong Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPostoperative neurological and neuropsychiatric complications (NNC) after liver transplantation (LT) have become an important research concern, but the difficulties arise with the diagnosis of the majority subclinical form of NNC.Case ReportWe report a middle-aged man who developed fluctuating psychotic symptoms, marked personality change, and behavioral disturbance approximately one week after LT. No overt focal neurological deficits, such as hemiparesis, aphasia, or seizures, were observed. Brain MRI showed small periventricular/subcortical white-matter lesions with hyperintensity on T2WI and FLAIR images and punctate hyperintensity on DWI. The findings were interpreted as suggestive of suspected silent ischemic brain injury. Tacrolimus-related neurotoxicity, PRES, metabolic/hepatic encephalopathy, infection, seizure-related states, and alcohol-related encephalopathy were considered in the differential diagnosis. Contemporaneous tacrolimus trough levels and EEG data were unavailable, so tacrolimus neurotoxicity and non-convulsive seizure-related psychiatric symptoms could not be completely excluded. The patient received symptomatic psychiatric treatment and rehabilitation, and his neuropsychiatric symptoms gradually resolved, with stable liver function at the last follow-up.ConclusionThis case illustrates that suspected silent ischemic brain injury after LT may present primarily as fluctuating psychotic symptoms and personality or behavioral changes, and may be easily mistaken for postoperative delirium, calcineurin-inhibitor neurotoxicity, infection, or metabolic/hepatic encephalopathy. In atypical or high-risk patients, early brain MRI may help identify potential brain involvement and guide timely neurological and neuropsychiatric evaluation.Key MessageNew-onset psychotic symptoms after LT should not be automatically attributed to postoperative delirium. Even when MRI findings do not definitively confirm acute infarction, timely neuropsychiatric assessment and early brain MRI may help identify potential structural brain involvement in high-risk or atypical patients.

Background

Postoperative NNC has become the research emphasis, with long-term survival after LT gradually improving. The incidence of NNC after LT has been reported to range from 10% to 47%, which is significantly higher than that of other solid organ transplants, and is closely associated with patient mortality.1,2 The spectrum of NNC includes encephalopathy, seizures, psychotic disorders, stroke, tremor and posterior leukoencephalopathy (PLE) syndrome, etc. The causes of NNC are highly variable, acute vascular lesions are less common than delirium or metabolic encephalopathy, and ischemic stroke has been reported in approximately 1%~4% of patients,3 with most vascular events occurring within 30 days after LT.4 Neurological assessment after LT is often limited by intubation, prolonged bed rest, postoperative weakness, residual anesthetic effects, and delirium; therefore, when patients lack focal signs such as hemiparesis, aphasia, or seizures and present mainly with new-onset psychotic symptoms, silent brain injury may be misattributed to delirium, medication effects, or metabolic encephalopathy. Chronic liver disease, liver failure, and immunosuppressants such as tacrolimus may increase vulnerability to brain injury through blood-brain barrier dysfunction, coagulopathy, metabolic disturbance, infection, arrhythmia, and drug-related neurotoxicity, and may mimic stroke or psychotic symptoms. Early brain MRI may help identify potential brain injury in atypical or high-risk patients. We report a patient who developed prominent psychotic symptoms one week after LT, with MRI findings suggestive of suspected silent ischemic brain injury, and review pretransplant risk factors that may help identify patients requiring closer neuropsychiatric monitoring.

Case Representation

The patient was a 62-year-old man admitted for LT with cirrhotic liver failure. The patient presented with malaise and jaundice eight weeks ago and developed hepatic encephalopathy, liver failure, renal failure, pleural effusion and ascites, coagulation abnormalities in week 7th. After symptomatic supportive treatment with albumin and anti-infective drugs, his general condition improved and he was admitted to our hospital in week 8th for successful allogeneic in situ LT from deceased donor organ recipients. Tacrolimus was started at a low dose of 0.01 mg/kg/day immediately after transplantation. Neuropsychiatric symptoms occurred one week after surgery and delirium status was considered possible. He had a partial response to low-dose antipsychotics, but continued to have intermittent and variable psychiatric symptoms, with significant personality changes in the intervals. The patient had no clear history of psychiatric illness, and his symptoms did not completely resolve with improvement in the level of consciousness. To exclude structural brain injury or other intracranial lesions, the clinical team promptly arranged brain MRI, which revealed multiple new infarcts adjacent to the bilateral ventricular areas, which revealed multiple new infarcts adjacent to bilateral ventricular areas. Physical examination revealed generalized yellow discoloration, and no neuropathic signs were noted except for near-normal muscle strength in the legs postoperatively (4+ grade) due to prolonged bed rest. Bedside neurological examination did not reveal definite focal neurological deficits; however, prolonged postoperative bed rest, weakness, intubation-related communication difficulty, and limitations of the consultation setting restricted a complete neurological examination. Systematic cognitive scales and neuropsychological assessment could not be completed. Pre- and post-operative laboratory values included blood ammonia, hemoglobin, total bilirubin, AST, ALT, INR, creatinine and blood potassium (see Table 1). A computed tomography (CT) scan showed fatty liver and ascites. Electrocardiogram detected a new-onset atrial fibrillation. No obvious thrombus but several small spots were observed in the endothelium of the arteries of both lower limbs by ultrasound. Liver pathology suggested cirrhosis uncompensated subacute severe hepatitis. The patient’s body mass index was 24.5 kg/cm2 (height 185 cm, weight 84 kg). He had suffered from hepatitis E when he was young and had a long history of heavy alcohol consumption, more than 1kg of white wine per day, and had been abstaining from alcohol for 3 months. As psychiatric symptoms were prominent in this case, we concentrated on reviewing the evolution of the patients’ general neuropsychiatric symptoms. He had no history of mania or depression, no significant withdrawal symptoms following abstinence, and no history of similar psychiatric episodes. One week before the LT, he developed a hepatic coma, manifesting primarily in slurred speech, lethargy and jaundice, and became lucid after conservative treatment. However, one week after LT, he became irritable and uncooperative combined with poor clarity of consciousness, and slurred speech due to intubation. Psychiatry re-evaluated the patient one week later and found a fluctuating decline in consciousness with intermittent rich and variable psychotic symptoms. Most of the time, the patient was in a relatively less agitated intermittent period, usually in a clear state of consciousness, and the psychotic symptoms were not prominent, while the personality change was obvious, specifically manifested as impulse control disorders, rich emotional experience, frustration and helplessness when words could not be understood, recklessness, increased provocativeness, and sleep disturbances.

Occasionally, during episodes of psychotic symptoms were obvious, a mild disorientation and poor emotional stability could be overserved. The patient sometimes shows only disorientation and poor emotional stability; sometimes it may lead to delusions of persecution, such as the claim that “there is a gang of several people who want to harm me”; there are also cases of active disobedience and impulsive and irritable temperament when the patient is under clearly conscious. The patient has a rich and varied psychiatric symptomatology that is intermittent and not clearly correlated with the level of consciousness. Although the patient had no obvious neuropathologic signs, we could not exclude the possibility of cerebral organic psychiatric disorders such as comorbid alcoholic encephalopathy and stroke.

We switched to quetiapine 25 mg/d orally, added vitamin B supplementation, and applied cranial MRI, which revealed scattered new infarct foci adjacent to bilateral cerebral ventricles (see Figure 1). Symptomatic and rehabilitative supportive care was provided. The patient was followed up for 3 months with tacrolimus and low-dose quetiapine, and remained with grade 4+ lower limb muscle strength, stable liver function, and resolution of clinical neuropsychiatric symptoms. Discussion

Studies have shown that psychiatric symptoms after liver transplantation may be associated with delirium, anxiety and depressive symptoms, calcineurin inhibitor-related psychotic symptoms, hallucinations, and mania.5 Patients with vascular-related neurologic complications often present with seizures and coma, and have very high mortality rates.4 In contrast, difficulties arise with the diagnosis of the majority subclinical form of NNC. The present case represents an atypical psychiatric presentation within the spectrum of post-LT NNC: the patient developed new-onset psychotic symptoms, personality change, and behavioral disturbance one week after surgery, accompanied by MRI abnormalities, but lacked typical focal neurological signs such as hemiparesis, aphasia, or seizures. This case suggests that psychotic symptoms after LT should not be simply attributed to postoperative delirium, medication reactions, or metabolic abnormalities; instead, potential structural or functional brain injury should also be considered within the framework of NNC.

Differential Diagnosis in NNC

Common complications after LT include acute renal failure, graft rejection, infection, development of diabetes, and biliary complications.6 Altered extra-cerebral function, metabolic disturbance, infection, vascular events, seizure-related states, and immunosuppressive medications are important contributors to postoperative NNC. These conditions can present with altered consciousness, behavioral disturbance, psychotic symptoms, seizures, focal neurological signs, or stroke-like manifestations. Up to 5% of patients have severe symptoms, including psychosis, hallucinations, dysarthria, visual loss, seizures, cerebellar ataxia, paresis, and leukoencephalopathy7,8 making a specific diagnosis difficult. Consideration of all diagnostic possibilities in any LT patient presenting with encephalopathy or new-onset psychiatric symptoms is essential, including infectious etiologies, stroke, malignancy, seizures, adverse drug reactions, metabolic or hepatic encephalopathy, and other organic causes. Tacrolimus neurotoxicity and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) are particularly important differential diagnoses in post-LT patients with psychotic symptoms.9,10 Tacrolimus-related neurotoxicity may present with tremor, altered mental status, seizures, hallucinations, delusions, behavioral disturbance, PRES, or stroke-like symptoms, and may mimic or coexist with vascular brain injury. In the present case, contemporaneous tacrolimus trough levels were unavailable; therefore, tacrolimus-related neurotoxicity could not be completely excluded. The improvement of symptoms after low-dose antipsychotic treatment also suggests the possibility of reversible brain dysfunction, including medication-related neurotoxicity, delirium, or metabolic encephalopathy, rather than proving a single vascular mechanism.

Pre-existing condition can exacerbate neurological issues and must be taken into account. For example, immunosuppression prior to transplantation predisposes to opportunistic infectious encephalopathies,11 Metabolic or hepatic encephalopathy may affect consciousness, emotion, and behavior through abnormalities in blood ammonia, liver and renal function, electrolytes, coagulation status, infection, and systemic inflammation. Alcohol-related encephalopathy, including Wernicke encephalopathy, was also considered because the patient had a long history of heavy alcohol use. However, he had abstained from alcohol for three months, had no clear withdrawal symptoms, received vitamin B supplementation, and showed periventricular lesions rather than the typical symmetric mammillary body, periaqueductal, or medial thalamic changes of Wernicke encephalopathy. Seizure-related psychiatric symptoms should also be considered, because old or silent ischemic lesions may lower the seizure threshold and occasionally present as non-convulsive status epilepticus or postictal/peri-ictal psychosis. As EEG was not performed, seizure-related psychosis could not be fully excluded. Also, undiagnosed neurodegenerative diseases in the perioperative setting may be associated with the development of postoperative NNC.12

It is also important to emphasize the diagnostic value of physical examination and ancillary tests. In several case reports of LT with NNC that we consult, the physical examination, especially the neurologic test, is passive or even absent. We believe that the main reason for this is that the patient’s muscle strength decreased due to prolonged bed rest before and after surgery and the after-effects of anesthesia, and the postoperative intubation itself led to ambiguous speech and the patient was unable to cooperate well during the examination, resulting in the omission of important physical signs.

Postoperative complications of cerebral infarction are extremely rare but has been reported in specific clinical contexts, including paradoxical embolic stroke after therapeutic plasma exchange in an LT recipient with atrial septal defect, metabolic stroke after LT for propionic acidemia, and PRES with intracranial hemorrhage and cortical infarction after LT.13–15 These reports suggest that vascular or stroke-like brain injury can occur after LT, but the mechanisms are heterogeneous and often differ from the present case. Therefore, in patients with post-LT psychotic symptoms, vascular brain injury should be considered together with tacrolimus neurotoxicity, PRES, metabolic encephalopathy, infection, and seizure-related states.

Another good point of identification is the time of onset. The timing of onset of individual CNS lesions is most common within 30 days (43%) after LT, followed by 1 year later (25%), and 30 to 90 days (20%). Postoperative short-term complications were predominantly vascular events (acute hemorrhages, infarcts, or subdural hematomas) or infectious lesions, and the immunosuppressive associated neurotoxicity. Long-term lesions were almost exclusively a result of complicated etiologies.1,16

In this patient, the onset of psychotic symptoms one week after LT, together with new-onset atrial fibrillation, perioperative coagulopathy, and MRI abnormalities, supported early evaluation for post-LT NNC and possible brain involvement. Overall, the diagnosis should integrate MRI findings, DWI/ADC features, drug levels, EEG when indicated, metabolic and infectious workup, and vascular risk assessment.

Mechanism that LT Has a Higher NNC Risk Than That of Other Solid Organs Transplant

The higher incidence of neurologic complications following LT can be attributed to a combination of factors, including the unique metabolic functions of the liver, the use of immunosuppressive medications, the surgical procedure itself, and the complex interplay of metabolic and physiological changes that occur during the transplantation process. The nervous system is altered by chronic liver disease functionally and structurally, like acidosis and cerebral edema, etc. The stress of surgery, the post-transplantation recovery period, and the use of immunosuppressive drugs can exacerbate these pre-existing neurological issues. Pre-transplant hepatic encephalopathy, through alterations in the blood-brain barrier, swelling of astrocytes, loss of neurons and dysregulation of GABAergic signaling, has been reported to strongly predispose to NCs or poor cognition after LT.17–19 Also, liver dysregulation leads to a decrease in coagulation factors, an effect that may further exacerbate thrombosis in patients with a predisposition to bleeding or thrombosis. Studies have shown delirium rates of 10% in cadaveric liver transplants and 47% in living livers,17,20–22 also suggesting that hepatic reactive factors are associated with the development of psychiatric symptoms. Further, the liver is a major site of copper metabolism, and the effect of copper excretion from the transplanted liver is not equivalent to the effect of copper excretion from healthy individuals, and copper through the diet may aggravated intracranial copper deposition.23

Clinical Manifestations in Organic Mental Disorders

The severity of this patient’s psychiatric symptoms was not exactly in accord with the deterioration of the level of consciousness. Typically, the fluctuation of consciousness and psychiatric symptoms in delirium is consistent, ie, improvement in consciousness is followed by a decrease in psychiatric symptoms. When psychiatric symptoms predominate in organic disease, we must broaden our thinking to consider the possibility of co-morbid cerebra disease origin psychotic disorder. There may be persistent pathological changes in the brain, and psychiatric symptoms may persist as a result of these changes, even if consciousness improves at some point. Usually, psychiatric symptoms are rather fixed and stable in their presentation within the same class of disease, suggesting that the variability of symptoms is a good point of differentiation.

The relationship between the psychotic episode and the MRI abnormalities should be interpreted as possibly related. The patient developed new-onset hallucinations, delusions, behavioral disturbance, impaired impulse control, and marked personality change one week after LT, but lacked typical focal neurological signs such as hemiparesis, aphasia, or seizures. Small periventricular or deep frontal white-matter lesions may interrupt fronto-subcortical circuits, especially prefrontal-striatal-thalamic pathways, which are involved in executive function, emotional regulation, motivation, impulse control, and reality testing.24,25

Disruption of these circuits may reduce prefrontal regulatory control and lead to disinhibition, impulsivity, emotional instability, impaired judgment, and delusion-like interpretations. Therefore, even small periventricular lesions without prominent focal neurological deficits may manifest as vivid, fluctuating psychotic symptoms or delirium-like behavioral abnormalities in a vulnerable postoperative brain state. This mechanism provides a plausible explanation for why psychiatric symptoms were the dominant clinical manifestation in this case.

Differences exist in the prevalence of brain function abnormalities in liver failure and cirrhosis due to different underlying etiologies. According to a single center, the likelihood of NNC due to LT for Wilson disease is reported to be 46.7%-60%, increasing the risk of NNC (HR: 6.64, 95% CI: 2.33–18.93, p=0.00).9,18,25,27 Announced, liver disease and its most common underlying factors, alcohol substance, have an increase prevalence in population with mental health. However, no sufficient data have been observed to support that alcohol use alone is strongly associated with NNC after LT.31–33 One study has found that heavy alcohol use among deceased donors does not affect recipient outcomes after LT, suggesting that alcohol use itself does not increase the risk of NNC.33 Established predictors of alcohol-related NNC are combination of alcohol, metabolic dysregulation, and may present as a manifestation of other conditions such as increased serum level of ammonia or shorter duration of sobriety. Although previous studies reported different rates of neurologic complications between deceased-donor and living-donor LT recipients (20.4% vs 26.7%, p<0.05),34,35 our recalculation using pooled data did not show a significant difference. Therefore, donor type was not included as a pretransplant predictor in Table 2.

Limitations

This case has several limitations. First, contemporaneous tacrolimus trough levels were not obtained; therefore, immunosuppressant-related neurotoxicity could not be completely excluded. Second, EEG was not performed; therefore, non-convulsive status epilepticus or postictal/peri-ictal psychosis could not be completely ruled out. Nevertheless, the patient had new-onset atrial fibrillation, perioperative coagulation abnormalities, a long history of alcohol use, and liver failure-related vulnerability of brain function, which made vascular or other organic brain dysfunction clinically plausible.

Conclusion

The incidence of post-NNC is quite high and is usually characterized by mild symptoms such as psychiatric symptoms, as in this case, and not many of these are attributed to organic brain disease. Careful consultation and follow-up will help identify this group of patients, and we have compiled a list of predictive factors that may aid in clinical diagnosis and prediction. This case also suggests that new-onset, persistent, or fluctuating psychotic symptoms after LT should prompt early consideration of brain involvement. Early brain MRI may help evaluate suspected silent acute ischemic brain injury in atypical or high-risk patients.
