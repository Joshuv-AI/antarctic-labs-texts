Exploring biomarkers related to coagulation and immunity in myocardial infarction based on bioinformatics
Authors: Jiwen Liu, Xuemei Chen, Xinmeichen Meng, Hui Huang, Jun Zhao, Zeyi Cheng, Lu Zhang, Junling Huang, Dongfeng Guo, Xiaoyan Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of global mortality, with the immune and coagulation systems playing pivotal roles in its pathophysiology. This study aimed to identify biomarkers associated with immune-related genes (IRGs) and coagulation-related genes (CRGs) in MI and to explore their potential regulatory mechanisms in treatment. Data from the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets were utilized. Four genes (IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14) were identified, exhibiting significantly elevated serum levels in patients with MI. Their distribution across tissues and single cells was further analyzed. Methylation site analysis of the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) biomarker revealed high-confidence methylation sites in IGF2R, PLAUR, and MAPK14. The RNA-binding protein (RBP) interactions were examined, revealing high-affinity binding sites for TARDBP in both IGF2R and MAPK14. GeneMANIA analysis highlighted biomarker-associated genes enriched in positive kinase regulation. Furthermore, a total of 20 potential drugs were predicted, with albuterol sulfate identified as a target for IGF2R, and molecular docking confirming the strongest binding affinity in the IGF2R-albuterol sulfate complex.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24313-z.Subject terms: Cardiovascular diseases, Biomarkers, Cardiology

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI), or heart attack, is a leading cause of mortality globally, representing a major public health challenge1. MI occurs when blood flow to a segment of the heart muscle is abruptly blocked, causing tissue damage or necrosis due to oxygen deprivation2. This condition is predominantly triggered by the rupture of atherosclerotic plaques in the coronary arteries, leading to thrombus formation and subsequent obstruction of blood flow2. With the availability of coronary interventions, bypass surgery, and pharmacological treatments, early diagnosis and risk assessment have become essential for significantly reducing MI-related mortality3. While traditional biomarkers for early MI diagnosis, such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI), and creatine kinase-MB, are highly sensitive, they often lack the specificity required to reliably distinguish MI from other conditions4–6. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets that offer greater sensitivity and specificity while minimizing adverse reactions. This would greatly enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable more targeted, effective MI treatments.

The coagulation system, also known as the hemostatic system, is a complex and finely regulated biological process crucial for maintaining vascular integrity and preventing excessive blood loss following injury7. This system involves a series of enzymatic reactions that lead to blood clot formation, primarily through the activation of coagulation factors and platelets7. It operates in four key stages: vascular constriction, platelet adhesion and activation, the coagulation cascade, and fibrinolysis7. The coagulation cascade is particularly vital, consisting of intrinsic, extrinsic, and common pathways that converge to generate thrombin, which then converts fibrinogen to fibrin, stabilizing the blood clot8. When a plaque ruptures, transcription factor (TF) exposed at the rupture site activates the extrinsic pathway of the coagulation cascade, triggering rapid thrombin generation and clot formation9. This process not only contributes to vessel occlusion but also amplifies the inflammatory response, destabilizing plaques further and promoting additional thrombotic events10. Recent studies underscore the significance of coagulation-related genes in the onset and progression of MI11. Genetic variations in coagulation factors, such as Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, and variations in fibrinogen genes, can influence an individual’s thrombosis risk12–14. These genetic factors may modify the efficiency of the coagulation cascade, predisposing individuals to thrombotic events that can lead to MI. Understanding these genetic influences is pivotal for developing personalized therapeutic strategies that reduce thrombotic risk and improve cardiovascular outcomes15.

The immune response is critical in the development of coronary atherosclerosis and the onset of MI. Increasing evidence highlights the importance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes and the inflammatory response in MI pathogenesis16. Immune-related genes play pivotal roles in the initiation, progression, and prognosis of MI, significantly influencing patient outcomes17. For example, studies have shown that in pig hearts undergoing reperfusion, there is a significant increase in lymphocyte apoptosis in peripheral blood, along with infiltration of proinflammatory Th1 lymphocytes within the first 48 h18. Similarly, in patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) following MI, a notable reduction in circulating T lymphocytes was observed within the first 24 h18.

Additionally, a rat model of MI revealed detectable immune gene expression as early as 30 min after MI onset, highlighting the potential of immune-related gene expression as an early diagnostic marker for MI19. Macrophages are among the most active cell types during various phases post-MI, participating in both pathological inflammation and the resolution processes after MI20. Caspase-recruitment domain family member 9 (CARD9) in macrophages contributes to inflammation, increased matrix metalloproteinase activity, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis, all of which exacerbate cardiac dysfunction and adverse remodeling post-MI through upregulation of lipocalin 2 (Lcn2) expression21.

Immunothrombosis, a defense mechanism that limits pathogen spread by forming local thrombi, involves the coordinated action of the coagulation system and immune cells such as leukocytes, platelets, and endothelial cells. While this process helps restrict pathogen invasion, it can become dysregulated under certain conditions, leading to pathological thrombosis and tissue damage22,23. Immunothrombosis is implicated in various diseases, including sepsis, venous thromboembolism (VTE), ischemia-reperfusion injury, COVID-19-associated coagulopathy, and MI. It plays a pivotal role not only in physiological coagulation but also in numerous pathological thrombotic conditions. In diseases like COVID-19 and sepsis, immunothrombosis mediates both thrombotic and inflammatory responses[22,​23]. In MI, immunothrombosis also plays a critical role. Following MI, localized inflammation activates endothelial cells and leukocytes, promoting the release of TF and chemokines, which activate platelets and form thrombi, further aggravating myocardial damage22. Unlike immunothrombosis associated with pathogen infections, immunothrombosis in MI is aseptic. Given its significant role in MI pathophysiology, further investigation into genes associated with immune-coagulation interactions is essential for enhancing MI diagnosis and treatment.

This study identified coagulation-related genes (CRGs) using the AmiGO and MSigDB databases and immune-related genes (IRGs) from the ImmPort database. Two MI-related datasets were sourced from the GEO database. Through differential analysis, LASSO, GSEA, and GSVA, the study linked CRGs, IRGs, and MI to identify potential biomarkers. These biomarkers were then analyzed for RNA methylation modifications and RNA-binding protein (RBP) regulatory networks, providing valuable insights into the roles of CRGs and IRGs in MI and laying the groundwork for future research.

Results

A total of 11 candidate genes were identified

The analysis workflow of this study was shown in Suppl. Figure 1. First, through screening, 1,211 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in GSE61144, with 667 upregulated and 544 downregulated (Fig. 1A-B). Intersections between 422 coagulation-related genes (CRGs), 1,793 immune-related genes (IRGs), and 1,211 DEGs resulted in 11 candidate genes (IGF2R, TGFA, TGFBR3, FURIN, MMP9, C5AR1, PLAUR, MAPK14, ACTG1, SYK, and F2RL1) (Fig. 1C).

Functional analysis and chromosomal localization of candidate genes

To further analyze the biological functions and potential roles of the candidate genes, this study performed enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The GO enrichment analysis of the candidate genes identified 503 enriched terms, primarily related to wound healing, blood coagulation, hemostasis, and the negative regulation of inflammatory responses to antigenic stimuli (Fig. 2A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified 12 enriched pathways, including neutrophil extracellular trap formation, proteoglycans in cancer, leukocyte transendothelial migration, platelet activation, and fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis (Fig. 2B). Following the removal of three discrete genes, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network displayed eight nodes and eight edges (Fig. 2C). Notably, MMP9 and FURIN were found to interact with each other. Spearman correlation analysis showed a significant positive correlation between MMP9 and C5AR1 (r = 0.90, p < 0.05) and a significant negative correlation between TGFA and TGFBR3 (r = −0.83, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). The chromosomal locations of the candidate genes revealed that MAPK14 and IGF2R were located on chromosome 6, while PLAUR and C5AR1 were located on chromosome 19 (Fig. 2E).

IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14 were identified as biomarkers

In the process of screening for MI-related biomarkers, this study identified biomarkers through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis and multi-dataset validation. From the candidate genes, four genes (IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14) were selected as key genes in the LASSO analysis at lambda.min = 0.03046089 (Fig. 3A). These four genes showed consistent and significant expression trends (p < 0.05) in both the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets, with all being highly expressed in the MI group (Fig. 3B). Consequently, these four genes (IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14) were validated as biomarkers. ELISA testing of these biomarkers in healthy individuals and patients with MI confirmed that they were upregulated in the MI group, further validating the bioinformatics results (Fig. 3C, Suppl. Tbl. 1).

Discussion of specific signaling mechanisms associated with IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14 biomarkers

To explore the potential mechanisms of action of the 4 biomarkers in the occurrence and development of MI, this study performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and gene set variation analysis (GSVA). GSEA was performed on the four biomarkers, and the top three pathways with both positive and negative enrichment scores were selected for visualization. Among these, the gene IGF2R was enriched in 22 pathways, with the top three pathways showing positive and negative enrichment scores being IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, TNFA signaling via NF-κB, inflammatory response, oxidative phosphorylation, MYC-regulated target V1, and MYC-regulated target V2 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, 98 pathways exhibited differences between MI and control samples, according to the screening criterion of p.adj < 0.05. Of these, 53 pathways were activated, and 45 were inhibited in MI samples. The top five activated and top five inhibited pathways were selected for further analysis. Bar graphs and heat maps of enrichment scores for these pathways were generated. The top five activated pathways included pantothenic acid and coenzyme A biosynthesis, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, adipocytokine signaling pathway, FcγR-mediated phagocytosis, and dorso-ventral axis formation. The top five inhibited pathways comprised citric acid cycle recirculation, primary immune deficiencies, Parkinson’s disease disorders, asthma, and ribosome biogenesis (Fig. 4B-C).

As shown in Fig. 5A, IGF2R was most highly expressed in the tongue, TGFA in the esophagus, and both PLAUR and MAPK14 in the bone marrow. RNA methylation modification, RBP regulatory networks, and genemania analysis of biomarkers were useful for exploring biological pathways of MI

To explore the epigenetic regulatory mechanisms and molecular interaction networks of the biomarkers, this study analyzed their m6A modification sites, RNA-binding protein (RBP) regulatory relationships, and interactions with co-expressed genes. The N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification sites of the biomarkers were investigated, revealing that IGF2R contained three high-confidence m6A modification sites, one of which begins at site 751 in the RNA secondary structure. MAPK14 and PLAUR each had m6A modification sites starting at sites 508 and 692, respectively, in the RNA secondary structure. Notably, TGFA was found to lack any m6A modification sites (Fig. 6A, Suppl. Figure 3 A). The RBPs associated with these biomarkers were also explored. TGFA and PLAUR did not have corresponding RBPs, while 9 and 7 RBPs were predicted for IGF2R and MAPK14, respectively. The gene-RBP regulatory network comprised 13 nodes and 16 edges, with RBFOX2, U2AF2, TARDBP, ELAVL1, and HNRNPC being the RBP nodes common to both MAPK14 and IGF2R (Fig. 6B). Further analysis of the binding probability between biomarkers and RBPs focused on TARDBP, which exhibited relatively high clusterNum and clipExpNum thresholds for IGF2R and MAPK14. As shown in Fig. 6C, sequences bound by TARDBP were analyzed, revealing that three segments of IGF2R exhibited a binding probability of 1 to TARDBP, suggesting a higher likelihood of binding. Additionally, the analysis showed that IGF2R had a significant number of binding sequences, indicated by the presence of more red dots, suggesting stronger interactions with TARDBP (Suppl. Figure 3B-C). Furthermore, GeneMANIA was employed to construct and analyze the interaction network of biomarkers and their co-expressed genes. Twenty interacting genes were identified by querying IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14. The interaction network was predominantly composed of physical interactions (77.64%), followed by co-expression interactions (8.01%). The top five related functions of the biomarkers and their interacting genes included positive regulation of protein serine/threonine kinase activity, regulation of MAP kinase activity, positive regulation of MAP kinase activity, protein serine/threonine kinase activity, and activation of protein kinase activity (Suppl. Figure 3D).

Regulatory network analysis of biomarkers could help explore potential mechanisms of MI

To analyze the regulatory networks of biomarkers at the post-transcriptional and transcriptional levels, this study further predicted their related miRNAs, lncRNAs, transcription factors (TFs), and miRNA-SNP-mRNA relationships. miRNAs were predicted using the miRTarBase, miRDB, and TargetScan databases, yielding 189, 558, and 4,345 miRNAs, respectively. The intersection of these results identified 35 common miRNAs. Additionally, the corresponding lncRNAs for these miRNAs were predicted using the Starbase database (clipExpNum ≥ 10), with 14 miRNAs showing 29 relationships with lncRNAs, leading to the identification of 13 non-redundant lncRNAs. The lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network comprised 30 nodes and 43 edges (Fig. 7A). TFs associated with the biomarkers were screened in the ChEA3 database, with a score threshold of < 100. This analysis predicted 14 TFs for IGF2R, 13 for MAPK14, 20 for PLAUR, and 2 for TGFA, including 11 shared TFs. A common set of 7 TFs was identified across all three biomarkers. The TF-mRNA network consisted of 29 nodes and 49 edges, with H1S1 co-targeting IGF2R, PLAUR, and TGFA (Fig. 7B). A miRNA-SNP-mRNA network diagram was generated based on the miRNA-target relationships. All three biomarkers (IGF2R, TGFA, and MAPK14) were associated with corresponding miRNA-SNP-mRNA relationship pairs. Notably, TGFA had one relationship pair, while IGF2R and MAPK14 had two each. For example, the TGFA-rs1350404796-hsa-miR-152-3P relationship pair was identified (Fig. 7C).

Predicted drugs bind well to biomarkers

To explore the potential clinical associations and therapeutic application value of biomarkers in cardiovascular diseases, this study analyzed their related diseases and targeted drugs through database retrieval and verified them via molecular docking. The CTD database was queried for biomarker-related cardiovascular diseases, revealing that IGF2R was linked to four diseases, while TGFA and MAPK14 were each associated with two diseases. The mRNA-disease network, comprising 11 nodes and 8 edges, visualized these relationships. MAPK14 was specifically linked to cardiomyopathy and myocardial ischemia (Fig. 8A). A search in the DGIdb database identified 20 potential drugs targeting the biomarkers. The biomarker-drug network consisted of 24 nodes and 20 edges, with albuterol sulfate identified as a target for IGF2R (Fig. 8B). Further screening of the top-scoring drug-biomarker relationships in the DGIdb database revealed three significant pairs: IGF2R-Albuterol Sulfate, PLAUR-Ruxolitinib, and MAPK14-Methoxsalen. Molecular docking simulations for these pairs showed docking scores of −7.3, −7.1, and − 6.3 kcal/mol for IGF2R-Albuterol Sulfate (Fig. 8C), PLAUR-Ruxolitinib (Fig. 8D), and MAPK14-Methoxsalen (Fig. 8E), respectively, indicating strong binding affinities between the small molecules and their target biomarkers.

Discussion

MI is a severe cardiovascular condition involving multiple mechanisms, including coagulation, immune response, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Following MI, local ischemia activates the coagulation pathway, leading to thrombus formation that exacerbates myocardial damage. Concurrently, ischemia and injury signals trigger immune cell infiltration, particularly macrophage migration and activation. This study aims to explore the interplay between coagulation and immunity in MI to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, thereby supporting early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies.

Initially, 11 candidate genes were identified, with IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14 emerging as key biomarkers significantly implicated in biological processes such as wound healing, coagulation, and inflammation. Notably, IGF2R’s involvement in the IL6-JAK-STAT3 and TNF-NFKB signaling pathways highlights its critical role in inflammatory regulation. Furthermore, RNA methylation and RBP network analyses revealed post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, particularly involving IGF2R and MAPK14, which play vital roles in MI. GeneMANIA analysis confirmed the involvement of these biomarkers in MAP kinase and protein kinase activities, while drug interaction studies identified potential therapeutic agents, positioning them as promising targets for MI treatment.

The insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF2R) plays a critical role in cell proliferation, apoptosis, protein synthesis, and metabolism24. Under pathological conditions such as hypertension and MI, cardiomyocytes contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling, with a significant upregulation of IGF-2 and its receptor IGF-2R25,26. Mice lacking IGF-2R display enlarged hearts and abnormal development of the interventricular septum27. Angiotensin II (Ang II) enhances IGF-2 expression via the ERK and JNK pathways, a process that can be inhibited by MEK and JNK inhibitors25,28. Elevated IGF-2/IGF-2R levels are associated with pathological changes in cardiomyocytes, including hypertrophy, fibrosis, and apoptosis29. IGF-2R also binds to other M6P-containing ligands and regulates cellular functions through various signaling pathways, such as CaMKIIδ, calcineurin, and TGF-β, contributing to myocardial fibrosis, apoptosis, and pathological hypertrophy, as well as extracellular matrix remodeling via plasminogen activation30–32. Thus, IGF2R plays a critical role not only in the physiological function of cardiomyocytes but also in the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases.

TGFA, a member of the epidermal growth factor (EGF) family, has a high affinity for the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)33. Binding of TGFA to EGFR causes receptor dimerization and activation of tyrosine kinase activity, promoting cell proliferation, invasion, apoptosis inhibition, and angiogenesis. TGFA is strongly linked to conditions such as non-syndromic facial clefts, knee osteoarthritis, lung fibrosis, and various cancers34. Additionally, TGFA is associated with poor prognosis in ischemic stroke35, although its relationship with acute MI (AMI) has yet to be reported.

PLAUR, also known as the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (SuPAR), is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored receptor protein primarily located in detergent-soluble membranes. It consists of three structural domains (DI, DII, and DIII) and is encoded by the PLAUR gene36. PLAUR interacts with Monocyte Chemotactic Protein 1, Tumor Necrosis Factor, and Interleukins 1 and 6, enhancing inflammatory activity in atherosclerotic plaques36,37. Elevated PLAUR expression in atherosclerotic macrophages is linked to hypoxia and the production of fibrinolytic enzymes, influencing foam cell formation38. PLAUR is implicated in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis, suggesting its pivotal role in the onset and development of MI. This highlights its potential as a promising diagnostic and therapeutic target.

MAPK14, also known as p38 alpha, is a key member of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, crucial for cellular responses to stress and inflammation. This kinase participates in various cellular processes, including differentiation, apoptosis, and autophagy, and is particularly important in regulating immune responses, stress adaptation, and cell proliferation39. MAPK14 is activated through phosphorylation by upstream kinases, which then phosphorylate downstream substrates, driving its involvement in inflammation and cell cycle regulation39. It plays a central role in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammatory conditions. Notably, Anthony J. Muslin and colleagues demonstrated that MAPK14 may promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis through Bcl-X(L) deamidation in MI40. However, the exact role of MAPK14 in MI remains to be fully elucidated.

RBPs are crucial in regulating RNA throughout its lifecycle, interacting with RNA through various structural motifs and mechanisms41. The present study identified 9 RBPs associated with IGF2R, 7 with MAPK14, and 5 common RBPs shared between both genes. Among these, TARDBP was highlighted as having a high binding probability with both IGF2R and MAPK14, playing a critical role in regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation42. These findings emphasize the importance of RBPs and their interactions with genes, providing valuable insights into the occurrence and progression of MI. This research offers potential for developing therapeutic strategies for MI.

The inflammatory response is closely linked to MI. A moderate inflammatory response is essential for cardiac repair, whereas excessive inflammation can cause direct damage to cardiac cells, exacerbating the extent of MI43. Numerous studies have explored the relationship between inflammation and MI. After MI, macrophages are recruited to the ischemic region of heart tissue, where they mediate both pro-inflammatory responses (Isg15hi and MHCII + Il1b+) and anti-inflammatory responses (Trem2hi)44. The balance between these macrophage phenotypes is critical in determining the outcome after MI. Even the same inflammatory factor, such as S100A9, exhibits different effects depending on the duration of exposure: short-term (7 days) exposure promotes inflammation, while long-term (21 days) exposure suppresses it, highlighting the complexity of the inflammatory response in MI45. In the present study, all four biomarkers were significantly enriched in the inflammation pathway, suggesting that they are potential targets for anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory therapies. However, the specific mechanisms involved in MI require further investigation.

The IGF2R-targeting drug albuterol sulfate demonstrated the strongest binding affinity in molecular docking. However, as a β-adrenergic receptor agonist, albuterol sulfate may exert adverse cardiac effects46, and thus its application in myocardial infarction (MI) should be approached with caution. In addition, Ruxolitinib exhibited high affinity for PLAUR, and findings from the RESPONSE trial suggested that patients treated with Ruxolitinib showed a reduced risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE)47, indicating a potential role in MI by mitigating thrombotic risk. Overall, these docking results provide only preliminary insights for drug repurposing in MI, and extensive in vitro, in vivo, and preclinical studies are still required to validate their therapeutic potential.Recent studies have demonstrated that lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, coffee consumption) and seasonal variations are closely associated with the risk of myocardial infarction (MI)48,49. Whether these factors interact with the biomarkers identified in this study warrants further exploration. Specifically, clinical evidence has shown that smoking increases the risk of atrial fibrillation and exacerbates MI by inducing chronic inflammation50. Coffee consumption, due to components such as caffeine, may influence platelet function or metabolic pathways, thereby indirectly promoting thrombosis and affecting cardiovascular health51,52. Moreover, the seasonal pattern of MI is strongly linked to seasonal fluctuations in mood and stress49. During cold seasons, shorter daylight duration and lower ambient temperatures enhance sympathetic activity and blood pressure reactivity to cold exposure, elevate coagulation factor levels, and stimulate thrombus formation, collectively increasing MI risk53. Taken together, smoking, caffeine intake, and cold-season conditions may amplify MI risk through pathways involving inflammation, platelet activation, and coagulation system upregulation. The biomarkers identified in this study are implicated in these key biological processes, suggesting potential regulatory connections. In future work, we plan to investigate whether lifestyle and environmental factors such as smoking, coffee consumption, and seasonal variation influence MI development by modulating these biomarkers. Elucidating these interactions may provide scientific evidence to inform public health strategies and optimize prevention and intervention measures for MI.

In addition, this study innovatively analyzed repetitive elements within the genomic regions of the key genes. From a compositional perspective, long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) accounted for the largest proportion (32.80%), primarily represented by LINE1 (L1), the most active transposable element in the human genome, followed by short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs). In contrast, long terminal repeat (LTR) elements and DNA transposons constituted relatively smaller fractions. This distribution suggests that the genomic regions of IGF2R, PLAUR, and TGFA are enriched in repetitive elements with transpositional activity or potential regulatory functions. Functionally, these findings provide novel insights into the pathophysiological role of the three biomarkers in MI. On one hand, the high abundance of LINE1 and ALUs indicates that these elements may regulate the expression of IGF2R, PLAUR, and TGFA, thereby participating in MI-related molecular mechanisms. On the other hand, the presence of ancient repeats (such as MIRs, LINE2, and ERV-associated LTR elements) may reflect the evolutionary conservation of these genes in the cardiovascular system. Overall, this analysis highlights future research directions, such as investigating how LINE1 and ALU elements regulate the differential expression of these biomarkers in MI patients, which would deepen our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying MI biomarkers.

In conclusion, our findings strongly indicate that the four genes IGF2R, TGFA, PLAUR, and MAPK14 are highly correlated with MI. However, this study primarily relies on bioinformatics analysis, which has certain limitations. First, bioinformatics analysis depends on existing databases and models, which may be influenced by data quality and selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability and precision of the findings. Additionally, while this study has identified a correlation between these genes and MI, experimental evidence verifying their specific roles in the underlying pathological processes is lacking. Therefore, future research should integrate in vivo experiments to further validate the functions of these biomarkers in MI and assess their practical efficacy in clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE61144 (GPL6106) included 10 normal and 7 MI peripheral blood samples, while GSE60993 (GPL6884) contained 7 normal and 7 MI peripheral blood samples. Additionally, 2,483 IRGs were retrieved from the Immunology Database and Analysis Portal (ImmPort, https://www.immport.org/home), with 1,793 IRGs remaining after duplicate removal. A total of 422 unique CRGs were sourced from the AmiGO database (https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo) and the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

Differential expression analysis in GSE61144

To identify DEGs between normal and MI samples in the GSE61144 dataset, differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” package (version 3.52.4)54, with |log2 fold change (FC)| >0.5 and p.adj < 0.05 as the screening criteria. For visualization of the DEGs, volcano plots and heat maps were generated using the “ggplot2” (version 3.4.1)55 and “pheatmap” (version 1.1.9)56 packages, respectively. The top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes were selected for display in both the volcano plot and heat map.

Identification and analysis of candidate genes

To identify genes associated with both coagulation and immunity, DEGs, CRGs, and IRGs were intersected to screen for candidate genes. GO and KEGG analyses were performed on the candidate genes using the “clusterProfiler” package (version 4.7.1.3)57, with a threshold of p.adj < 0.05. To explore the interactions between the candidate genes, they were input into the STRING database (https://string-db.org) to construct a PPI network with a confidence level of 0.4. Spearman correlation analysis was then conducted to calculate the correlations between the candidate genes, considering a correlation coefficient |r| >0.3 and p < 0.05 as significant. Heat maps were generated to present the results. Finally, to map the chromosomal locations of the candidate genes, the chromosomal start positions were obtained from the annotation files, and the genes’ locations on the chromosomes were visualized using the “RCircos” package (version 1.2.2)58.

Screening biomarkers

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was performed using the “glmnet” package (version 4.1.4)59, with results obtained at the lambda minimum. The genes identified through this analysis were designated as key genes. The expression of these key genes was then validated in the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets. Key genes that exhibited consistent expression trends and significant differences in both datasets (p < 0.05) were designated as biomarkers.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To understand the functions of the biomarkers and their associated pathways, the correlation coefficients of the biomarkers and all genes were calculated separately using all samples from the GSE61144 dataset, and the genes were ranked from highest to lowest. The background gene set used was the h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt gene set. GSEA was then performed on the ranked genes using “clusterProfiler” with thresholds of |NES| >1 and p < 0.05. The top three pathways with the highest enrichment scores, both positive and negative, were selected for visualization.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

To investigate the pathway changes between MI and normal samples in the training set GSE61144, the c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt background gene set was downloaded from the MSigDB database, encompassing 186 KEGG pathways. GSVA enrichment analysis was performed for all genes in MI and normal samples using the “GSVA” package (version 1.42.0)60. The GSVA scores for MI and normal samples were compared using the “limma” package, with a screening criterion of p.adj < 0.05. A positive t-value (t >0) indicated pathway activation in the disease group, whereas a negative t-value (t < 0) indicated pathway inhibition in the MI group.

Biomarker distribution analysis

To examine biomarker expression at the mRNA and protein levels, the biomarkers were queried in the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), and expression levels across different tissues were visualized as histograms. The top five tissues with the highest biomarker expression were selected and used to construct tissue-gene network maps using the “Cytoscape” package (version 3.10.0) (PMID: 36035208). Furthermore, the expression of biomarkers in various single cell types across human tissues was also explored.

RNA methylation modification

For the analysis of m6A modification sites of the biomarkers, the fasta sequences of the mRNAs were first obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The sequences were then imported into the sequence-based RNA adenosine methylation site predictor (SRAMP, http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp/) to predict the m6A modification sites and their positions in the RNA secondary structure.

RNA-binding protein (RBP) regulatory network and genemania analysis

To explore RBPs associated with the biomarkers, RBPs interacting with the biomarkers were predicted using the Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (ENCORI, https://rnasysu.com/encori/) database, with screening thresholds set to clusterNum ≥ 10 and clipExpNum ≥ 10. The gene-RBP regulatory network was visualized using “Cytoscape.” Additionally, to further predict the binding probability between biomarkers and RBPs, the mRNA sequences of the biomarkers were input into the RBPsuite database (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/RBPsuite/), selecting the linear RNA and general models for analysis. Finally, interaction networks between biomarkers and their co-expressed genes were constructed and analyzed using the GeneMANIA database (http://genemania.org/).

Regulatory network analysis and analysis of repetitive elements of key gene

To construct the ceRNA regulatory network of biomarkers, miRNAs associated with the biomarkers were predicted using the miRTarBase (http://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/php/index.php), miRDB (http://mirdb.org/), and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) databases. The intersecting miRNAs from each database were identified, and the corresponding lncRNAs for these miRNAs were predicted using the StarBase database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) (clipExpNum ≥ 10). To visualize the results, the “Cytoscape” package was employed to create and display the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network. Additionally, TFs interacting with biomarkers were identified using the ChEA3 database (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3/), with a screening threshold of TF scores < 100. The TF-mRNA network was visualized using “Cytoscape.”

miRNA-associated single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), including single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and disease-associated variants (DRVs) within miRNAs and their target-binding sites, can affect miRNA function and biogenesis, thus influencing phenotypic outcomes. The miRNASNP-v3 database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/miRNASNP/) provides a comprehensive resource for SNVs and novel functions, including DRVs. This study analyzed the effects of 7,161,741 SNPs and 505,417 DRVs on 1,897 pre-miRNAs (2,630 mature miRNAs) and the 3’ UTRs of 18,152 genes. Based on the miRNAs from the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network, miRNA-targeting SNPs were examined through the miRNASNP-v3 database to assess how SNPs in the miRNA seed region impact the binding of miRNAs to gene 3’ UTR regions. miRNA-SNP-mRNA pairs were selected and visualized using “Cytoscape.”

Finally, the genomic sequences of key genes were obtained from NCBI GenBank, and the genomic sequences of the key genes were annotated using the RepeatMasker tool.

Disease association analysis

To explore the relationship between biomarkers and cardiovascular disease, the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, https://ctdbase.org/) was queried for biomarker-associated cardiovascular diseases, focusing on diseases with direct evidence of markers and mechanisms. The mRNA-disease network was visualized using “Cytoscape.”

Drug prediction and molecular Docking

To explore potential drugs for MI treatment, biomarkers were queried for drug candidates in the DGIdb database (https://dgidb.org), and the “Cytoscape” package was used to visualize the biomarker-drug network. Additionally, the binding affinity of biomarkers to predicted drugs was examined. Drugs with the highest scores in the DGIdb database, along with the corresponding biomarkers, were selected. The 3D structures of the drugs were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the protein structures of the biomarkers were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/search/advanced/structure). Molecular docking was performed on the selected drug-biomarker pairs, with docking scores below − 1.2 kcal/mol indicating a high binding affinity between the drug and the biomarker.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

A total of 20 blood samples (10 normal and 10 MI) were collected from the Shanghai Gongli Hospital, China. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Biomarker levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd). Samples were added to a coated 96-well plate and incubated with detection and secondary antibodies. After adding the substrate and stopping the reaction, absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and concentrations were determined using a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Correlation tests were conducted using Spearman’s correlation test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Normality and equal variance were assessed before statistical analysis, and comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-tests. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.1), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.
