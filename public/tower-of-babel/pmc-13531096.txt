Point of Care Testing and Recombinase Polymerase Amplification
Authors: Manfred Weidmann, Ahmed Abd El Wahed
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Recombinase Polymerase Amplification (RPA) is coming of age and is poised to develop into a serious candidate for Point‐of‐care diagnostics. RPA has been around for almost 20 years. Originally developed by academics Nial Armes and Olaf Piepenburg, it was first published in 2006, and a first patent was issued in 2007. In the first decade of the 2000s, several isothermal molecular amplification methods emerged [1] but real‐time RPA stood out at the time as its design format was reminiscent of real‐time PCR (two primers and a probe). The probe used to confirm specific amplification was intuitively easier to accept than the backfolding FIP/BIB primers of Loop Mediated Isothermal Amplification (LAMP), which in essence do the same. To this day, this misunderstanding of the LAMP mechanism still leads to the development of additional real‐time probe system for LAMP in order to add a confirmatory probe [2, 3, 4, 5]. This may, however, also be the case because of the well‐documented unspecific amplification issues of LAMP [6].

In terms of sensitivity, both LAMP and RPA have been shown to be as sensitive as real‐time PCR in numerous studies; RT‐RPA, however, sometimes struggles in the last decade of sensitivity. An intriguing recent analysis speculates that, in contrast to fully biphasic UvsX‐GP32 droplets formed in the RPA reaction, the addition of RT leads to fully miscible droplets including BsuI, which inhibits UvsX and thus may cause the loss of sensitivity in RT‐RPA [7].

Both LAMP and RPA struggle with one tube multiplexing but the introduction of cascade amplification using CRISPR/Cas12 and 13 systems which digest ssDNA or ssRNA reports offers solutions but the most promising appear to be spatial multiplexing concepts on surfaces or digital droplets.

From the onset RPA was available in lyophilised pellets for DNA and RNA targets which made it a popular choice for research into cold chain independent point of care diagnostics [8]. Other features included the speed of amplification to the analytical limit of detection from 5 to 20 min at very low temperatures. The low reaction temperature generically implies a reduced engineering effort and associated costs for a POCT device and even inspired some assay developments with body heat driven RPA [9]. Over the years RPA was implemented in a plethora of microfluidic devices [10].

During the Covid pandemic, widespread testing outside of centralised laboratories provided advantages for general epidemic control and contact tracing in particular and has changed attitudes towards POCT [11]. Subsequently, the FDA invited the diagnostic industry to submit tests for home testing and most recently approved a PCR home test by US Company Visby Medical Inc. for sexually transmitted diseases. It uses a disposable heavily engineered “device‐cartridge” with embedded electronics to steer a continuous flow heated serpentine PCR channel which seems a waste to dispose of after one test (patent US20250128262A1). Another disposable electronics concept was the much simpler LUCIRA device for a SARS‐CoV‐2 LAMP assay [12]. Marketed as device‐free disposable tests, both POCTs in truth generate electronic waste and do not meet the last E in the RERASSURED criteria which stands for “Equipment free or simple environmentally friendly” [13].

Only recently cost efficient and scalable simple multi use POCT devices have emerged such as the CPod (CRISPR assay [14]), minoo (RPA assay [15]), and Pebble device (LAMP assay [16]). CPod and minoo use a Light Emitting Diode (LED) and the onboard multi‐spectral digital sensor AS7341, for fluorescence detection while the Pebble uses a mini digital camera to detect a colorimetric reaction. The minoo device received CE‐IVD legacy certification in May 2022 for a SARS‐CoV‐2 RT‐RPA assay using a simple extraction procedure for throat swabs. While many others have created similar direct extraction concepts e.g., using TCEP buffer for SHERLOCK and LAMP assays [17, 18, 19], the bottleneck extraction for more complicated sample types is still not solved.

In terms of multiplexing, the Biofire FilmArray system for multiplex PCR [20] has already demonstrated that spatial multiplexing is preferential to one‐pot multiplexing, which is true for the workload needed to balance one‐pot reactions and for the verification and validation effort for one‐pot reactions versus spatially separated individual reactions. It is therefore to be expected that the simple multi use POCT devices will also move in this direction.

Only a few device publications have listed cost of goods (CoGS) for isothermal and portable PCR devices (range 50–193$ per device [21, 22]), therefore, with a margin added it is safe to estimate the CoGs within 250€ for the above devices. They can be categorized as environmentally friendly as at least the minoo device was verified to be functional for at least 1000 runs [15]. If they solve the extraction issue, these simple readers could help to provide molecular diagnostics for infectious agents to the 47% of the global population that has little to no access to diagnostics at all, thus solving the last mile problem in providing access to diagnostics close to the patient [23]. In principle, these test platforms would be deliverable to users at reduced sales prices if upscaled production was outsourced to international electronics/mobile phone manufacturers. Local biochemistry production would allow dissemination of affordable respective assays close to the WHO requirement of 1$/test [13]. In comparison, current microfluidic and microsystem POCT systems in general fail to consider the economy of scale for mass production and market [24].

RPA has been rated as the most promising isothermal molecular amplification technique since it utilizes unmodified oligonucleotides, rapidly amplifies targets at only slightly elevated temperature without compromising the stringency of amplification, and uses probe‐based target verification in its real time format [1]. A meta‐analysis of molecular POC assays for SARS‐CoV‐2 concluded that seven RT‐RPA/RAA assays achieved detection sensitivity level similar to diagnosis on purified RNA samples by PCR from crude patient samples [25]. RPA was used in multiple field studies in mobile laboratories and produced sufficient evidence demonstrating that the performance of RPA is suitable for point of care use. In view of original RPA patents running out in 2026 the first open‐source protocols for RPA mix production have been published [26, 27]. Apart from the original Twist DX kit which currently is not available for RNA detection, three RPA copy chemistries are available in China: Recombinase Aided Amplification (RAA [28], Qitian, https://en.qt‐bio.com/Pro/12.html), Enzymatic Recombinase Amplification (ERA [29], GenDX, https://www.igeneye.com/), and Multienzyme Isothermal Rapid Amplification (MIRA [30], Amp‐Future Biotech Co. Ltd. https://www.amp‐futurebio.com/products). The impetus to copy the successful RPA concept goes even as far as claiming to have invented a probe system which is identical to the one originally published in 2006 [31]. In the area of lateral flow detection RPA is beginning to overtake LAMP with 490 publications versus 412 recorded on PubMed. Taken together RPA is catching up on LAMP (Figure 1) and has the potential to develop into a serious candidate for POCT implementation which is certainly the view in Asia as 61% of RPA publications originate from China alone.
