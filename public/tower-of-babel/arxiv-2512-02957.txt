Systematic construction of ROCN Bell-inequalities
Arturo Konderak, Patryk Michalski

Abstract
Self-testing constitutes one of the most powerful forms of device certification, enabling a complete and device-independent characterization of a quantum apparatus solely from the observed correlations. In recent work by the authors [ 23 ] , a general framework was introduced for constructing Bell inequalities that self-test entire families of Clifford generators. In this manuscript, we develop an alternative and complementary self-testing criterion based on symmetric spanning sets. This formulation provides an explicit and constructive route to designing self-testing Bell inequalities in arbitrary dimensions.



1.
Introduction

Bell non-locality is one of the most distinguishing features of quantum theory. It provides a rigorous and experimentally testable certification of the quantum nature of correlations between spatially separated systems. Consider, for example, two distant parties, Alice and Bob, who share a quantum resource—typically an entangled state. When each party performs local measurements on their respective subsystems, the resulting joint statistics can, in certain scenarios, exhibit correlations stronger than those permitted by any classical model based on local hidden variables
[
4
]
. In such cases, the correlations are said to be
non-local

[
7
]
.

The importance of non-locality is difficult to overstate, as it underpins a broad range of quantum certification protocols, including device-independent (DI) randomness certification
[
2
]
, quantum cryptography
[
32
]
, quantum communication complexity
[
8
]
, and the self-testing of quantum devices
[
33
]
. Experimental investigations of Bell non-locality date back to the 1970s
[
15
]
, but it was not until 2015 that the first loophole-free demonstrations were achieved
[
19
]
.

One of the most fundamental tools for certifying non-locality is the violation of a Bell inequality, which any local model must satisfy. Beyond that, Bell inequalities can also be used to characterize, for instance, full non-locality
[
6
,
31
]
, enable device-independent quantum cryptography
[
1
,
12
]
, certify the dimensionality of a quantum channel
[
13
]
, or demonstrate the infiniteness of the number of measurements
[
35
]
. Viewing the Hamiltonian of a multipartite system as a Bell operator, one can prove that the ground state exhibits non-local properties
[
38
]
Remarkably, there exist cases in which the maximal violation of a Bell inequality also certifies the underlying experimental apparatus—that is, both the shared quantum state and the measurements performed by the parties
[
33
]
. In such situations, the Bell inequality is said to
self-test
a reference quantum strategy.

The seminal CHSH inequality
[
9
]
provides the simplest, and still one of the most powerful, examples of a Bell inequality. In this scenario, each party performs only two dichotomic measurements, and the maximal violation of the CHSH inequality self-tests the maximally entangled qubit state. Several generalizations of CHSH have been proposed for higher-dimensional systems. Notably, the Elegant Bell inequality
[
16
]
achieves its maximal violation by employing measurements derived from SIC-POVMs, while other extensions include the Hadamard Bell inequalities
[
18
]
. Additionally, a general class of Bell inequalities based on Platonic solids was introduced in Ref.
[
25
]
. More recently, the ROCN class of Bell inequalities was proposed in Ref.
[
23
]
, for which the quantum bound can be derived analytically. Under suitable structural conditions, the maximal violation of ROCN inequalities allows for the self-testing of maximally entangled states in higher dimensions.

In this article, we further investigate the structure of ROCN Bell inequalities. In particular, Ref.
[
23
]
provides a necessary and sufficient condition for a Bell inequality to self-test the reference strategy. However, this condition can appear abstract from a physical perspective. We therefore begin by providing a more intuitive, physically motivated interpretation of this criterion. Moreover, by analyzing the structure of symmetric Hilbert spaces, we derive a sufficient condition for an ROCN Bell inequality to be self-testing: namely, the columns of the correlation matrix should form a
symmetric spanning set
. This result enables a systematic construction of families of ROCN Bell inequalities that self-test any even number of Majorana observables. Such constructions are particularly relevant given the central role of Majorana observables and Clifford groups across physics, from quantum thermodynamics
[
3
]
and quantum field theory
[
10
]
to quantum computing
[
24
,
28
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2.
, we provide an introduction to Bell non-locality, with a focus on ROCN Bell inequalities (Subsection
2.1.
) and self-testing (Subsection
2.2.
). Section
3.
recalls the definition of the symmetric two-fold tensor product and introduces the notion of a symmetric spanning set. In Theorem
2
, we present an explicit family of vectors whose two-fold tensor products span the full symmetric subspace. Section
4.
provides a physically transparent criterion for self-testing within the ROCN framework. Combining this result with Theorem
2
, Section
5.
describes a construction of ROCN Bell inequalities that self-test any even number of Clifford generators. Finally, in Section
6.
, we illustrate the procedure with explicit examples involving two and four Clifford generators.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we present the main concepts related to Bell non-locality. In Subsection
2.1.
, we introduce the notion of Bell inequalities, and in Subsection
2.2.
, we discuss the concept of self-testing. Throughout, the discussion is specialized to ROCN Bell inequalities.

2.1.
ROCN Bell inequalities

We consider a bipartite scenario in which two parties, Alice and Bob, have access to local Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}
, respectively, and share a quantum state

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{AB}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}.

(2.1)

Each party performs local measurements. We focus on the case where all measurements have two outcomes, labeled by
\pm 1
. In this setting, Alice’s and Bob’s measurements can be represented by self-adjoint observables
A_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
and
B_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n
, satisfying
A_{i}^{2}=B_{j}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
.

The quantity
p(a,b|i,j)
, denoting the probability of obtaining outcomes
a,b=\pm 1
upon measuring the observables
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
, is referred to as a quantum correlation. It can be expressed through an affine transformation in terms of expectation values
[
17
]
:

p(a,b|i,j)=\frac{1}{4}\left[1+(-1)^{\frac{1-a}{2}}\langle A_{i}\rangle+(-1)^{\frac{1-b}{2}}\langle B_{j}\rangle-(-1)^{\frac{a+b}{2}}\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle\right].

(2.2)

Thus, quantum correlations may equivalently be studied in the space of probability distributions or in terms of expectation values.

A Bell inequality provides a criterion to determine whether a given correlation exhibits nonlocality. Such inequalities are formulated in terms of a Bell operator, which, for correlation-type Bell inequalities, takes the form

\mathcal{B}_{h}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{ij}A_{i}\otimes B_{j}.

(2.3)

Note that for correlation-type Bell operators, no linear terms in the local observables
\langle A_{i}\rangle
or
\langle B_{j}\rangle
appear. The coefficients
h_{ij}
form a real
m\times n
matrix, and the associated Bell inequality is defined as

I_{h}:=\langle\mathcal{B}_{h}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{ij}\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle\leqslant\beta_{C}^{h},

(2.4)

where
\beta_{C}^{h}
denotes the
classical
or
local bound
. This bound corresponds to the maximal value of
I_{h}
over all correlations that factorize as
\langle A_{i}B_{j}\rangle=\langle A_{i}\rangle\langle B_{j}\rangle
with
\langle A_{i}\rangle,\langle B_{j}\rangle\in\{-1,+1\}
. We refer to
I_{h}
as the
Bell functional
. For a given quantum realization, if
I_{h}>\beta_{C}
, the corresponding correlations are said to be nonlocal. The
quantum bound
, denoted by
\beta_{Q}^{h}
, is the maximal attainable value of the Bell functional
I_{h}
over all possible quantum realizations.

The family of ROCN Bell inequalities is defined by requiring that the coefficient matrix
h
satisfy the ROCN properties introduced in Ref.
[
23
]
. For completeness, we recall the definition below.

DEFINITION 1
(ROCN matrix)

Let
h
be a real
m\times n
matrix with
m\leqslant n
. We say that
h
is a
row-orthogonal and column-normalized (ROCN) matrix
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\forall\;i,k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}:\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{ij}h_{kj}=\delta_{ik}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{ij}^{2}\quad\text{(orthogonality of rows)},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\forall\;j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{ij}^{2}=1\quad\text{(normalization of columns)}.

(2.6)

We additionally assume that each row contains at least one non-zero entry.

Examples of ROCN matrices include orthogonal matrices (for
m=n
) or semiorthogonal matrices. Notably, both the CHSH and Elegant Bell inequalities (EBI) can be expressed in ROCN form. The quantum bound
\beta_{Q}^{h}
associated with a ROCN Bell inequality can be computed explicitly:

\beta_{Q}^{h}=\sup_{\ket{\psi},\{A_{i}\},\{B_{j}\}}\matrixelement{\psi}{\mathcal B_h}{\psi}=n.

(2.7)

This bound follows from a sum-of-squares (SOS) decomposition; see Ref.
[
23
]
for details.

The maximal quantum value is attained by the following quantum strategy. Let
r=\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, and consider local Hilbert spaces

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}=\mathbb{C}^{d},

(2.8)

where
d=2^{r}
. Denote by

\ket{ \Phi_d}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ket{i}_{\mathrm{A}}\otimes\ket{i}_{\mathrm{B}}

(2.9)

the maximally entangled state between Alice and Bob. Let Alice’s observables
\{\widetilde{A}_{i}\}_{i}
be a family of pairwise anticommuting operators satisfying

\{\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{A}_{k}\}=2\delta_{ik}\mathbb{I},\qquad i,k=1,\dots,m,

(2.10)

and define Bob’s observables
\{\widetilde{B}_{j}\}_{j}
by

\widetilde{B}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{ij}\widetilde{A}_{i}^{T},\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(2.11)

where
\widetilde{A}_{i}^{T}
denotes the transpose of
\widetilde{A}_{i}
. We can now introduce the following definition.

DEFINITION 2

Let
h
be an
m\times n
ROCN matrix. The
canonical
or
reference strategy
for achieving the quantum bound
n
of the ROCN Bell functional
I_{h}
consists of the maximally entangled state (
) together with the observables defined in Eqs. (
) and (
).

It is worth noting that the anticommuting observables (
) can be explicitly represented using the Jordan–Wigner representation
[
20
]
on the space
\bigotimes_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
; see also Ref.
[
23
]
.

2.2.
Self-testing with ROCN Bell inequalities

In certain scenarios, a set of nonlocal quantum correlations
\{p(a,b|i,j)\}
uniquely determines the quantum state and measurement operators used by Alice and Bob. This property is known as self-testing. The notion of uniqueness is understood only up to local transformations that leave the observed correlations invariant. We formalize this in the following definition.

DEFINITION 3

Let a set quantum correlations
\{p(a,b|i,j)\}
be generated by a bipartite quantum state

\ket*{\widetilde\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}

(2.12)

and by local measurement operators

\{\widetilde{A}_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,m}\quad\{\widetilde{B}_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,n}.

(2.13)

We say that
\{p(a,b|i,j)\}
self-tests the quantum state
\ket*{\widetilde\psi}
and the measurement operators
\{\widetilde{A}_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{\widetilde{B}_{j}\}_{j}
if, for any state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}
and observables
\{A_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i}
and
\{B_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j}
that reproduce the same correlations
\{p(a,b|i,j)\}
, there exist local isometries

U_{\mathrm{A}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime},\quad U_{\mathrm{B}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}

(2.14)

such that

U_{\mathrm{A}}\otimes U_{\mathrm{B}}\ket{\psi'}=\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket*{\varphi_{\mathrm{junk}}}

(2.15)

and

U_{\mathrm{A}}A_{i}U_{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}=\widetilde{A}_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{A}^{\prime}},\quad U_{\mathrm{B}}B_{j}U_{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}=\widetilde{B}_{j}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathrm{B}^{\prime}}.

(2.16)

Here,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}
are auxiliary Hilbert spaces, and
\ket*{\varphi_{\mathrm{junk}}}
denotes a normalized state supported on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\prime}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}
.

Suppose that the maximal quantum value
\beta_{Q}^{h}
of a Bell functional
I_{h}
can be achieved only by a specific set of quantum correlations
\{p(a,b|i,j)\}
, and that this correlation self-tests a particular quantum strategy. In that case, we say that the Bell inequality itself self-tests the corresponding quantum strategy.

As an example, consider the CHSH Bell functional

I_{\mathrm{CHSH}}:=\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle+\langle A_{1}B_{2}\rangle+\langle A_{2}B_{1}\rangle-\langle A_{2}B_{2}\rangle,

(2.17)

whose maximal quantum value is
2\sqrt{2}
. This value uniquely identifies (up to local isometries) the maximally entangled two-qubit state

\ket{\Phi_2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}+\ket{2}\otimes\ket{2}\right)

(2.18)

together with the local measurements

\displaystyle A_{1}=\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle,\quad A_{2}=\sigma_{x},

(2.19)

\displaystyle B_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma_{z}+\sigma_{x})

\displaystyle,\quad B_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma_{z}-\sigma_{x}).

(2.20)

Hence, the CHSH inequality self-tests both the maximally entangled bipartite qubit state and the corresponding measurement operators realizing the maximal quantum violation.

For ROCN Bell inequalities, a necessary and sufficient condition for self-testing is provided by the following Theorem
[
23
]
.

THEOREM 1
(Self-testing from ROCN Bell inequalities)

Let
h
be a
m\times n
ROCN matrix, with
m
even. Define the matrix

M_{j,(i,k)}=h_{ji}h_{jk},\quad j=1,\dots,m,\quad 1\leqslant i<k\leqslant n,

(2.21)

where each pair
(i,k)
is treated as a single, unique column index. Then, the maximal violation of the Bell inequality
I_{h}
self-tests the canonical strategy introduced in Definition
2
if and only if
M
has full column rank:

\mathrm{rank}(M)=\frac{1}{2}{n(n-1)}.

(2.22)

Remark 1

Observe that for the matrix to have full column rank, the number of rows must be greater than or equal to the number of columns of
M
. Furthermore, Eq. (
2.5
) implies that the rows of
M
are linearly dependent. Therefore, in order to obtain the rank (
), the number of rows must strictly exceed the number of columns:

n>\frac{1}{2}m(m-1).

(2.23)

This inequality imposes a constraint on the number of observables required for each observer to certify the measurements.

The case where
m
is odd is more subtle. In particular, Alice’s operators can be transformed as

A_{j}\mapsto A_{j}\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\dots,m-1,\quad A_{m}\mapsto-A_{m},

(2.24)

without altering the anticommutation relations among the operators. Consequently, two distinct and nonequivalent strategies can give rise to the same quantum correlations. This subtlety and its implications for self-testing are discussed in detail in Ref.
[
23
]
.

3.
Symmetric subspaces

Symmetric subspaces arise naturally in the description of bosonic systems, reflecting the indistinguishability of identical particles
[
30
]
. They are fundamental in various branches of physics. For example, collective emission phenomena such as superradiance and subradiance can be formulated in terms of Dicke states, which form a distinguished basis of the symmetric subspace
[
11
,
39
]
. The entanglement properties of symmetric states have been extensively explored: several entanglement criteria become equivalent when restricted to symmetric states
[
36
]
, and for certain families of symmetric states, separability is fully characterized by the PPT criterion
[
27
]
. However, this equivalence does not hold for all symmetric states
[
5
,
37
,
29
]
. Furthermore, special classes of symmetric states, such as GHZ and Dicke states
[
34
,
14
]
, are known to be self-testable, as are antisymmetric Slater states
[
21
]
. See also Ref.
[
22
]
for a review on symmetric quantum systems. We now revisit the main definitions of symmetric subspaces for bipartite systems and construct explicit examples of subspaces spanning the symmetric sector.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and define the symmetric projector, which acts on a simple tensor
\ket{u}\otimes\ket{v}
as

\mathcal{S}(\ket{u}\otimes\ket{v})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{u}\otimes\ket{v}+\ket{v}\otimes\ket{u}\right).

(3.25)

The operator
\mathcal{S}
extends linearly to all elements of the tensor product space
\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Its range is the symmetric subspace
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})
. For two normalized vectors
\ket{v}
and
\ket{w}
, the symmetric tensor product
\ket{v}\vee\ket{w}
is defined as

\ket{v}\vee\ket{w}=\frac{1}{\norm{\mathcal S(\ket{u}\otimes\ket{v})}}\mathcal{S}(\ket{u}\otimes\ket{v}).

(3.26)

Let
\{\ket{e_i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then, the following family of vectors span all the symmetric subspace:

\{{\ket{e_i}\vee\ket{e_k}}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\leqslant m\}.

(3.27)

These vectors are linearly independent, and hence the dimension of the symmetric subspace
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})
is
m(m+1)/2
.
For any
i<k
, we observe that

2\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_i}\otimes\ket{e_k})=(\ket{e_i}+\ket{e_k})\otimes(\ket{e_i}+\ket{e_k})-\ket{e_i}{\otimes\ket{e_i}}-\ket{e_k}{\otimes\ket{e_k}}.

(3.28)

Introducing the shorthand notation
\ket{u}^{\otimes 2}=\ket{u}\otimes\ket{u}
, we find that the set of vectors

\{\left(\ket{e_i}+\ket{e_k}\right)^{\otimes 2}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\leqslant m\}

(3.29)

also forms a basis of the symmetric subspace
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})
. We define a set of vectors
\{\ket{v_i}\}_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
to be a symmetric spanning set if the two-fold tensor products
\{\ket{v_i}^{\otimes 2}\}_{i}
span the whole symmetric subspace
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})

[
26
]
. As we will see in the following section, symmetric spanning sets can be used to construct ROCN matrices that self-test the canonical strategy introduced in Definition
2
.

For this purpose, for each
i=1,\dots,m
, consider the family of vectors

\left\{\frac{\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus k}}}{\sqrt{2}}:k=1,\dots,m-1\right\}\cup\{\ket{e_i}:i=1,\dots,m\},

(3.30)

where
i\oplus k
denotes the sum modulo
m
. Each vector in Eq. (
) is normalized, though the vectors are not necessarily mutually orthogonal. We then define

\left\{\ket{a_{ik}}:k=1,\dots,m\right\}

(3.31)

as the orthonormal system obtained from (
) via the Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization procedure. This construction allows us to formulate the following result.

THEOREM 2

Let
\{\ket{e_i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be an orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and, for each
i=1,\dots,m
, let
\{\ket{a_{ik}}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be defined as in Eq. (
). Then, the set of vectors

\left\{\ket{e_i}:1\leqslant i\leqslant m\right\}\ \cup\left\{\ket{a_{ik}}:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\leqslant m\right\}

(3.32)

forms a symmetric spanning set

Proof:

We first express each vector
\ket{a_{ik}}
recursively in the basis
\{\ket{e_j}\}_{j}
:

\displaystyle\ket{a_{i1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus 1}}}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle\ket{a_{i2}}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}}\ket{e_i}-{\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}}\ket*{e_{i\oplus 1}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\ket{e_{i\oplus 2}},

\displaystyle\ket{a_{ik}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}\left(\ket{e_i}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}\right)+\beta_{k}\ket{e_{i\oplus k}}.

(3.33)

This structure arises because each
\ket{a_{ik}}
is orthogonal to
\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}
for all
1\leqslant j<k
. Explicit calculation via the Gram–Schmidt procedure shows that

\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k+k^{2}}},\quad\beta_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{1+k}}.

(3.34)

Indeed, for the vector
(\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus k}})/\sqrt{2}
, the orthogonalization step reads

\frac{\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus k}}}{\sqrt{2}}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\outerproduct*{a_{ij}}{a_{ij}}\frac{\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus k}}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\ket{e_i}+\ket*{e_{i\oplus k}}}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{j}\ket*{a_{ij}}.

(3.35)

Comparing this with the general form of
\ket{a_{ik}}
yields the relation

\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\beta_{k}}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{j}^{2}.

(3.36)

Together with the normalization condition
k\alpha_{k}^{2}+\beta_{k}^{2}=1
, this gives Eq. (
) by induction.

Next, we show that the two-fold tensor products of the vectors in Eq. (
) span the symmetric subspace. Write
\ket{a_{ik}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\gamma_{kj}\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}
, so that

\displaystyle\ket{a_{ik}}^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,\ell=0}^{k}\gamma_{kj}\gamma_{k\ell}\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}\otimes\ket*{e_{i\oplus\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\char 17\relax=1}^{k}\ \gamma_{kj}^{2}\ket{e_{i\oplus j}}^{\otimes 2}+2\sum_{j<\ell}^{k}\gamma_{kj}\gamma_{k\ell}\mathcal{S}(\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus\ell}}).

(3.37)

We now show by induction on
k
that each symmetric tensor
\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_i}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus k}})
lies in the span of the set in Eq. (
). For the base case of
k=1
and arbitrary
i
, one directly obtains

2\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_{i}}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus{1}}})=\ket{a_{i1}}^{\otimes 2}-\ket{e_i}^{\otimes 2}-\ket{e_{i\oplus 1}}^{\otimes 2},

(3.38)

which is clearly in the span of (
).
Assume now that
\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_{i}}\otimes\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}})
lies in the span of the set in Eq. (
) for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
. From Eq. (
3.37
), we can isolate the term
\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_i}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus k}})
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_{i}}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\ket{a_{ik}}^{\otimes 2}}-\sum_{\char 17\relax=1}^{k}\ \gamma_{kj}^{2}\ket{e_{i\oplus j}}^{\otimes 2}\right]

\displaystyle-\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}j<\ell\\
(j,\ell)\neq(0,k)\end{subarray}}}\gamma_{kj}\gamma_{k\ell}\mathcal{S}(\ket*{e_{i\oplus j}}\otimes\ket*{e_{i\oplus\ell}}).

(3.39)

By the induction hypothesis, all terms on the right-hand side are in the span of the set (
), and therefore so is
\mathcal{S}(\ket{e_i}\otimes\ket{e_{i\oplus k}})
. Hence, by induction, the set in Eq. (
) spans the entire symmetric subspace
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})
.

\square

We note that the total number of elements in (
) is
m(m+1)
, which exceeds the dimension
\tfrac{1}{2}m(m+1)
of the symmetric subspace; thus, the system is not minimal, and the elements are linearly dependent.

4.
Sufficient condition for self-testing of an ROCN Bell inequality

We are now ready to establish a connection between the symmetric subspace and the self-testing condition for ROCN matrices. As shown in Theorem
1
, self-testing of a given ROCN inequality is completely determined by the rank of the associated matrix
M
. Although the criterion (
) provides a necessary and sufficient condition, its formulation may appear somewhat abstract from a physical perspective. To obtain a more transparent and operational viewpoint, we now derive an equivalent characterization expressed directly in terms of the column vectors of the defining matrix
h
.

THEOREM 3

Let
h
be an
m\times n
ROCN matrix with
m
even, and let
\{\ket{h_j}\}_{j}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
denote its column vectors. Then the maximal quantum value of the ROCN Bell functional
I_{h}
self-tests the canonical strategy introduced in Definition
2
if and only if, for every symmetric vector
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})
with null diagonal elements,
(\bra{e_i}\otimes\bra{e_i})\ket{\psi}=0
for
i=1,\dots,m
, the following condition holds:

\forall\;j=1,\dots,n:\quad\left(\bra{h_j}\otimes\bra{h_j}\right)\ket{\psi}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\ket{\psi}=0.

(4.40)

In particular, if
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m})=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{h_j}\otimes\ket{h_j}:j=1,\dots,{n}\}
, then the maximal quantum value of the ROCN Bell functional
I_{h}
self-tests the canonical strategy of Definition
2
.

Proof:

First, observe that each column vector
\ket{h_j}
of
h

\ket{h_j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{ij}\ket{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m},

7.
Conclusions

Self-testing constitutes one of the strongest forms of certification of quantum resources. It is a fully device-independent technique: no assumptions are made about the internal functioning of the devices, and yet, in certain scenarios, the maximal violation of a Bell inequality uniquely determines the underlying quantum state and measurements. The class of ROCN Bell inequalities, introduced in Ref.
[
23
]
, provides a broad family of such examples. These inequalities generalize the CHSH and Elegant Bell inequalities, admit an analytic characterization of the quantum bound, and come equipped with a necessary and sufficient condition for self-testing.

In this paper, we explored explicit constructions of ROCN Bell inequalities and identified a sufficient condition for self-testing of the canonical strategy. In particular, we showed that it is sufficient for the column vectors to form a symmetric spanning set, meaning that their two-fold tensor products span the entire symmetric subspace. Building on this criterion, we presented an explicit family of ROCN Bell inequalities that self-test the canonical strategy associated with any even number of Clifford generators. While this construction is general and conceptually simple, it is not optimal: it uses significantly more columns than the minimal number required for self-testing. This observation points to an interesting direction for future research—namely, the design of more efficient constructions that achieve self-testing with a minimal number of columns while preserving the analytic structure of ROCN inequalities.
