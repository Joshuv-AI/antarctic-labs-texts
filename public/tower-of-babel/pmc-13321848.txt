Sleep quality and learning style preferences among medical students at Golestan university of medical sciences: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Niloofar Minaee, Fatemeh Movahedi, Kimia Mojtahedi, Shoreh Vosough, Fatemeh Mehravar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSleep is essential for cognitive performance and academic success. Medical students frequently experience sleep disturbances. This study examined the relationship between sleep quality, learning modality preferences, and demographic factors among medical students. MethodIn this cross-sectional study, 546 students from Golestan University of Medical Sciences were selected via stratified random sampling in 2023–2024. Data were collected using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the VARK questionnaire, and a demographic survey. Analyses included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, independent t-tests, Pearson correlation, and multiple logistic regression.ResultOver half (53.6%) reported poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5). The auditory style was dominant (mean = 5.42 ± 2.11). No direct correlation existed between global PSQI scores and VARK styles (p > 0.05). However, regression analysis indicated that maternal education (OR = 1.42, p = 0.028) and female gender (OR = 1.85 for kinesthetic style, p = 0.004) significantly predicted sleep quality and learning style preference, respectively.ConclusionPoor sleep quality is prevalent but not directly linked to learning modality preferences. Maternal education and gender significantly influence sleep and kinesthetic learning. Further research is needed to explore the indirect mechanisms linking sleep quality, family factors, and learning modality preferences, as well as to evaluate whether sleep hygiene interventions improve student outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Sleep occupies approximately one-third of human life and should not be perceived as a period of inactivity. On the contrary, it plays a critical role in alleviating psychological stress and pressures, significantly contributing to individual recovery and enhancing overall quality of life [1]. Blanc et al. demonstrated that nighttime insomnia can adversely affect quality of life, leading to increased irritability and agitation [2]. Sleep disorders also profoundly impact social life, causing numerous psychosocial maladjustments that result in negative and undesirable outcomes for an individual’s quality of life and functioning [3]. The American Psychiatric Association categorizes sleep disorders into four primary groups: (1) disorders of sleep initiation and maintenance with early awakening, (2) hypersomnolence, (3) disorders of the sleep-wake schedule, and (4) parasomnias [4].

Among university students, sleep quality is associated with socioeconomic status, academic grade point average (GPA), and marital status [5]. Financial security, lack of related worries, and strong family support systems are also influential factors in improving their sleep quality [5]. Lund and colleagues found that disruptions in the sleep-wake cycle of students often stem from lifestyle changes, exposure to new psychosocial stressors, demanding academic schedules, and the consumption of caffeine and alcohol. Furthermore, substance misuse, heavy course loads, and unforeseen personal issues are significant factors in the onset and persistence of sleep disturbances among this population [6].

Sleep is a fundamental factor influencing learning. Therefore, achieving effective learning necessitates sufficient sleep [7]. According to the National Sleep Foundation, 7–8 h of sleep is essential for optimal cognitive function in adults [8]. Research has consistently shown that poor sleep quality, delayed sleep onset, early awakening, and fragmented nighttime sleep severely impair learning capacity and academic performance [9]. Pagel, in an investigation of sleep-wake patterns among students, found that 69.7% of students with low GPAs had difficulty falling asleep. Moreover, 65.6% of this group reported frequently pacing at night due to an inability to sleep, and 72.7% of those with poor sleep quality faced challenges with concentration and attention during the day [10].

Sleep quality has been shown to influence several cognitive functions that are fundamental to learning, including attention, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [11–13]. These cognitive processes may, in turn, affect how students engage with different modalities of information presentation. For example, a student experiencing daytime sleepiness and reduced attentional capacity might struggle to follow a lengthy auditory lecture but could remain engaged with visual diagrams or hands-on kinesthetic activities. Conversely, another student with good sleep quality may have the cognitive reserve to flexibly adapt to any modality. However, it is important to note that while sleep quality may influence the effectiveness with which a student uses a particular learning modality, it may not alter the student’s underlying preference for that modality. The current study explores whether any association exists between global sleep quality and self-reported learning modality preferences, without assuming causation or directionality.

Learning is a highly complex variable influenced by multiple cognitive and environmental factors. Learning is a highly complex variable influenced by numerous factors. One significant factor affecting learner outcomes is their learning style [14]. A learning style is defined as a habitual behavior, unique to each individual, for acquiring knowledge, skills, or feedback through study or experience. It is the preferred method learners choose for assimilating educational material over alternative approaches [15]. In simpler terms, learning modality preferences are the distinct and personal methods learners employ to process new information and concepts [14].

One widely used framework for understanding how learners prefer to receive information is the VARK model, which categorizes learning modality preferences into four types: Visual (learning through diagrams, charts, and visual aids), Aural (learning through listening to lectures and discussions), Read/Write (learning through reading texts and taking notes), and Kinesthetic (learning through hands-on activities and practical experiences) [16]. The VARK questionnaire has been validated in Persian and used extensively in medical education research to assess students’ self-reported preferences for information presentation [17]. In Iran, studies focusing on the learning modality preferences of university students remain limited [14]. Research indicates that the learning modality preferences of nursing students vary across nations and cultural contexts [18].

A prevalent perspective holds that nighttime sleep promotes effective learning and performance. Contrary to the belief that brain activity diminishes during sleep, the brain undergoes a shift in the type of activity, actively contributing to skill consolidation and improvement [19]. Sleep disorders can significantly impact both the quality of life and the academic performance of students [5]. The transition to university education and student life often introduces circumstances that can disrupt an individual’s sleep habits and alter their sleep patterns [20].

It is important to acknowledge that while learning style frameworks such as VARK are widely used in educational research and practice, their pedagogical validity has been the subject of considerable debate. Seminal critical reviews have concluded that there is no robust empirical evidence to support the premise that tailoring instruction to students’ preferred learning styles improves learning outcomes [21]. More recently, researchers have characterized the belief in the effectiveness of learning styles as a persistent “neuromyth” in education [22]. Specifically regarding the VARK model, studies have failed to demonstrate that matching instructional methods to students’ VARK preferences enhances academic achievement. Therefore, in this study, we treat the VARK instrument not as a measure of fixed cognitive traits or as a prescriptive tool for instructional design, but solely as a means of assessing students’ self-reported modality preferences for receiving information. Our aim is to explore descriptive associations between these preferences and sleep quality, rather than to advocate for style-matching instructional design or to claim that such matching improves learning outcomes.

Methods

This descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional study was conducted among students actively enrolled at Golestan University of Medical Sciences during the 2023–2024 academic year. The study protocol received approval from the university’s Institutional Ethics Committee.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were required to meet the following criteria: (a) no documented evidence of significant physical or psychological disorders, as verified by medical records; (b) no current use of prescribed hypnotic or sedative medications; and (c) not having experienced a major recent life crisis, such as the death of a close relative, a fire, migration, or a serious accident. Furthermore, participants must have completed at least one academic semester at the university. The primary reason for exclusion from the final analysis was the incomplete submission of any of the study questionnaires.

The minimum required sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.0.1). The calculation was based on an effect size (|ρ|) of 0.16, drawn from a prior relevant study [15], with a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 95%. This yielded an initial sample size of 497. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, this figure was increased by 10%, resulting in a final target sample of 546 participants.

A stratified convenience sampling method proportional to faculty size was employed to maximize representation across the university. The total student population was first divided into strata based on their faculty affiliation. The sample quota for each faculty was then calculated according to its proportion within the overall university population (Table 1). Consequently, the Faculty of Medicine—being the largest faculty—contributed the highest number of participants (n = 145 out of 341 from medical disciplines when combined with the International Campus medical students). This proportional approach resulted in an intentionally unbalanced distribution across faculties, reflecting the actual student population. However, we explicitly acknowledge that valid statistical comparisons across faculties are not feasible due to the disproportionate sample sizes, and our findings primarily reflect the experiences of medical students, who constitute the majority of the sample. Subsequently, participants from each faculty stratum were recruited using a convenience sampling approach until the faculty-specific quota was met.

Data collection instruments

Data were gathered through a set of three questionnaires administered to the participants. First, a Demographic and Academic Information Form was used to collect data on age, gender, marital status, place of residence, parental education level, blood type, field of study, faculty affiliation, cumulative grade point average (GPA), current academic semester, alcohol use, and smoking status.

Learning modality preferences were assessed using the Persian version of the VARK (Visual, Aural, Read/Write, Kinesthetic) Learning modality preferences self-reported Questionnaire. The original English tool was developed by Fleming (version 7.8). Its validated Persian adaptation, used in this study, was established by Amini et al., who reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.868 for internal consistency [23]. This 16-item multiple-choice questionnaire presents respondents with learning scenarios, each followed by four options corresponding to the V, A, R, and K modalities. Respondents rank these options from most to least applicable (1 to 4). An individual’s dominant learning style is determined by summing scores for each modality. A mixed style is assigned if the total scores for two or more modalities are equal.

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a self-reported measure of sleep quality. Its psychometric properties were evaluated in a Persian version by Farhi Moghadam et al. in 2012, reporting a sensitivity of 0.94, specificity of 0.72, and Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 [24]. The PSQI contains 19 items grouped into seven subscales: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each subscale is scored from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates no problem, 1 indicates a moderate problem, 2 a serious problem, and 3 a very serious problem. The global PSQI score is the sum of the seven subscale scores and ranges from 0 to 21. A total score greater than 5 indicates poor sleep quality, distinguishing individuals with good sleep from those with inadequate sleep.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26). The normality of the distribution for continuous variables was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as means and standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables.

For inferential analysis, a combination of parametric and non-parametric tests was used based on the data distribution. Bivariate analyses included independent samples t-tests, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), Pearson’s correlation, and Chi-square tests. When the assumptions for parametric tests were not met, their non-parametric equivalents—such as the Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis H test, or Fisher’s exact test—were applied. To identify independent predictors of the key outcomes, multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. Two primary models were built: the first with “Poor Sleep Quality” (PSQI > 5) as the dependent variable, and the second with a “Dominant Kinesthetic Learning Style” as the dependent variable. Variables showing a significant association (p < 0.1) in bivariate analyses or those considered theoretically important were entered as independent variables into the respective models. The results of the regression analyses are reported as adjusted Odds Ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all final model interpretations.

Two primary logistic regression models were constructed. The first model used “Poor Sleep Quality” (PSQI > 5) as the dependent variable. For the second model, the dependent variable was “Dominant Kinesthetic Learning Style” (defined as a kinesthetic score above the median). This specific modality was selected because it was the only VARK style that showed a statistically significant gender difference in bivariate analyses (independent t-test, p = 0.003), and we aimed to explore whether this association remained after adjusting for potential confounders. We acknowledge that focusing exclusively on the kinesthetic modality is a partial analytical approach; a more comprehensive strategy would employ multinomial regression or multivariate methods to examine all four VARK modalities simultaneously.

Regarding missing data, the demographic variables of age, cumulative GPA, and academic semester were optional in the survey instrument; consequently, these items had higher rates of non-response (84, 90, and 60 valid responses, respectively). No imputation methods were applied for missing data. For descriptive statistics and bivariate analyses involving these variables, only available cases were used (listwise deletion). Sensitivity considerations indicated that the missingness was likely random, as the main dependent variables (sleep quality and kinesthetic learning preference) and their key predictors (gender, maternal education, residence) had less than 5% missing data across the full sample of 546 participants. Therefore, the primary logistic regression models were conducted on the full sample without imputation.

Results

Demographic and academic characteristics

This study included 546 students from Golestan University of Medical Sciences. The demographic profile revealed that the majority of participants were female (56.0%, n = 306) and single (93.6%, n = 511). The average age of the participants was 21.80 years (SD = 2.78), though age data was available for only 84 individuals. Academically, the mean cumulative Grade Point Average (GPA) was 16.88 out of 20 (SD = 1.37, n = 90), indicating a relatively high level of academic performance. The average student was enrolled in their fifth academic semester (Mean = 4.97, SD = 2.75, n = 60). The sample was proportionally drawn from all seven faculties of the university, with the largest contributions from the Medical School (33%, n = 145) and the International Campus (25%, n = 115), ensuring faculty-level representativeness. Regarding living arrangements, just over half of the students (51.2%, n = 279) resided in private homes, while 40.8% (n = 223) lived in university dormitories. The reported use of substances was low, with only 1.6% (n = 9) acknowledging alcohol use and 4.0% (n = 22) reporting smoking (Table 2).

Prevalence of sleep disorders and learning modality preferences

A central finding of this study was the high prevalence of poor sleep quality among the participants. More than half of the students (53.6%) scored above the clinical threshold on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI > 5), signifying poor sleep quality. The overall mean PSQI score was 6.12 (SD = 2.73), which falls within the “poor sleep” range.

Assessment of learning modality preferences using the VARK questionnaire showed a clear hierarchy of preferences. The auditory (aural) learning style was the most dominant, with a mean score of 5.42 (SD = 2.11). This was followed by the visual style (Mean = 4.29, SD = 2.18), the read/write style (Mean = 4.16, SD = 2.05), and lastly, the kinesthetic style (Mean = 2.14, SD = 1.68).

Multivariate regression analyses

To identify independent predictors of the primary outcomes while controlling for potential confounders, two separate multiple logistic regression models were constructed.

The first model aimed to predict poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5). Variables entered into the model included maternal education, gender, place of residence, smoking status, and alcohol use. After adjustment, maternal education remained a significant independent predictor (Table 6). The odds ratio for maternal education was 1.42 (95% CI: 1.04–1.94, p = 0.028), indicating that for each one-level increase in maternal education (e.g., from diploma to bachelor’s degree), the odds of poor sleep quality increased by 42%. This unexpected finding—that higher maternal education is associated with worse sleep quality in students—is addressed in the Discussion. This model accounted for approximately 11% of the variance in sleep quality (Nagelkerke R² = 0.11). Gender was not a significant predictor in the final model.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between sleep quality and learning modality preferences among medical students at a single university in Iran. The primary findings confirm several well-documented patterns in the literature: a high prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students (53.6%), a dominant auditory learning preference, and no direct correlation between global PSQI scores and VARK dimensions. Rather than claiming novel discovery, this study serves as a local confirmation of these established patterns within the specific context of Golestan University of Medical Sciences. However, two exploratory findings—the unexpected positive association between higher maternal education and poor sleep quality, and the significant gender difference in kinesthetic preference—offer modest new insights that merit further investigation. We do not claim to have identified deep mechanisms linking sleep to learning preferences; such mechanistic understanding would require longitudinal or experimental designs beyond the scope of this cross-sectional study.

The finding that over half (53.6%) of the participants suffered from poor sleep quality (PSQI > 5) aligns with a substantial body of literature documenting a high prevalence of sleep disturbances in university students globally. This is consistent with studies within the Iranian context [1, 2], confirming that sleep disorders have become a pervasive issue in academic environments. The demanding nature of medical education, characterized by rigorous academic schedules, night shifts during clinical rotations, financial and social stressors, and excessive use of digital technology, likely contributes significantly to this epidemic. As noted previously [17, 18], medical students are at a uniquely elevated risk for sleep problems due to the inherent stressors of their chosen field. This is a critical public health concern, as future healthcare providers are themselves experiencing compromised health in a domain fundamental to cognitive and physical restoration.

Regarding learning modality preferences, the auditory (aural) style emerged as the most prevalent among participants, while the kinesthetic style was the least common. This distribution likely mirrors the dominant pedagogical methods in traditional medical education, which heavily rely on lectures, conferences, and group discussions—methods that primarily cater to auditory learners. Conversely, the relative scarcity of a kinesthetic preference may reflect limited opportunities for hands-on, experiential learning in certain phases of the curriculum. This pattern corroborates findings among nursing students [25, 26], suggesting a common instructional paradigm across medical sciences that may not fully accommodate the diverse sensory preferences of all learners.

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the analysis revealed no significant direct correlation between global sleep quality scores and any of the four VARK learning modality preferences. This suggests that while sleep deprivation undoubtedly impairs cognitive function and academic performance, it may not selectively alter an individual’s inherent preference for how they best receive information. In other words, students tend to retain their dominant learning style irrespective of their current sleep quality. This finding is supported by prior research [27, 28], which also reported no significant link between these variables. This independence can be theoretically explained by the nature of the constructs: learning modality preferences are believed to be shaped by enduring personality traits, past educational experiences, and personal inclinations, whereas sleep quality is more dynamically influenced by immediate environmental, physiological, and psychological factors.

However, a nuanced exploration indicated that specific components of sleep, such as daytime dysfunction due to sleepiness, showed weak but significant correlations with certain styles [29]. This subtlety hints at a more complex, indirect relationship where poor sleep may impair the efficacy of applying one’s preferred learning style, rather than changing the preference itself. This warrants more detailed investigation into how sleep subdomains interact with cognitive processing.

Female students demonstrated significantly higher kinesthetic learning preference scores compared to male students (p = 0.003), and female gender independently predicted kinesthetic preference (OR = 1.85, p = 0.004). The observed gender difference may reflect differences in self-reported preferences or socialization patterns, but the current study design does not allow us to determine any underlying mechanism. Further research is needed to confirm this finding and explore its potential causes.

The finding that higher maternal education was associated with poorer sleep quality among students was unexpected and contradicts some previous studies [5]. This counterintuitive result may be explained by residual confounding, social desirability bias in self-reported sleep measures, or unmeasured factors such as higher academic expectations from highly educated parents, which could increase student stress and sleep disruption. Alternatively, it may reflect a type I error. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this relationship. Second, female gender was a strong independent predictor of a preference for the kinesthetic learning style. This intriguing difference may reflect differences in self-reported preferences or socialization patterns, meriting dedicated research [30]. Further research is needed to confirm whether this finding is replicable and to explore potential mediating factors.

Finally, the lack of association between academic variables (GPA, semester) and learning modality preferences implies that academic success in medical school is not inherently tied to a single “optimal” learning style. Instead, success may depend more on a student’s metacognitive ability to recognize and flexibly adapt their study strategies to different learning tasks and instructional formats. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. The use of self-reported questionnaires may introduce bias. The convenience sampling within strata, though strengthening faculty representation, may limit the generalizability of findings to all medical students. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single university.

A further limitation of this study concerns the conceptual status of learning modality preferences. Although we used the VARK questionnaire to assess students’ preferred modes of information reception, these self-reported preferences do not equate to fixed cognitive styles or innate learning capacities. As noted in the introduction, the pedagogical validity of matching instructional methods to learning styles has been seriously questioned, and the concept is widely regarded as an educational “neuromyth” [17, 18]. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as describing associations with students’ stated preferences rather than as evidence for stable, trait-like learning styles that should be preferentially accommodated in teaching.

Our sampling strategy—stratified convenience sampling—also limits generalizability. While stratification by faculty ensured proportional representation, convenience sampling within strata may have introduced selection bias. Consequently, findings should be extrapolated with caution to other student populations. Furthermore, because we used proportional sampling by faculty size, the Faculty of Medicine contributed the largest number of participants. This intentional imbalance limits the validity of cross-faculty comparisons, and our results primarily reflect the medical student population. Oversampling of smaller faculties in future studies would be necessary to enable meaningful comparisons across disciplines. Regarding analytical approach, our regression analysis focused exclusively on the kinesthetic learning modality as the dependent variable, selected because it was the only style showing a significant gender difference in bivariate analyses. This approach is inherently arbitrary and incomplete; multinomial regression examining all four VARK modalities simultaneously would have been preferable. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation. Additionally, our study did not measure several known confounders of sleep quality, including caffeine consumption, screen time before bedtime, physical activity, academic workload, and pre-existing psychological conditions (e.g., anxiety or depression) [15, 19, 22]. The absence of these variables limits causal inference and may have introduced residual confounding. Future studies should measure and adjust for these factors.

As noted in the introduction, the VARK framework itself is controversial. There is no robust empirical evidence that matching instruction to students’ preferred learning styles improves learning outcomes [21, 22]. The VARK questionnaire measures self-reported preferences, not fixed cognitive traits, and our findings should not be interpreted as endorsing style-matching instructional design. We acknowledge this as a fundamental conceptual limitation of any study employing learning styles frameworks. A final limitation concerns the low explanatory power of our regression models, with Nagelkerke R² values of 0.11 for poor sleep quality and 0.09 for kinesthetic learning preference. This indicates that the majority of variance in both outcomes remains unaccounted for by our selected predictors. Consequently, our models have limited utility for prediction or explanation, and the findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive. The low R² values also suggest that other important variables—such as psychological distress, academic stress, screen time, physical activity, caffeine consumption, and social support—are likely stronger determinants of sleep quality and learning preferences than the demographic factors we examined. Researchers should interpret the identified associations with caution and prioritize the measurement of these additional variables in future investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores a high prevalence of suboptimal sleep quality among medical students at Golestan University of Medical Sciences, confirming it as a major health concern for this population. The auditory learning modality preference was dominant, reflecting the current teaching landscape. While no direct link was established between overall sleep quality and learning modality preferences, two exploratory associations were identified: higher maternal education was unexpectedly associated with increased odds of poor sleep quality, and female gender significantly predicted a kinesthetic learning preference. Academic achievement indicators were independent of learning modality preferences. These findings collectively suggest that sleep quality and learning modality preferences operate through largely independent pathways in influencing the student experience. Further research—particularly longitudinal and mechanistic studies—is needed to explore the unexpected relationship between maternal education and student sleep quality, as well as the gender difference in kinesthetic preference, including potential mediating factors such as academic pressure, family expectations, and socialization patterns.
