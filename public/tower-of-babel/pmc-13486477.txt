Development of a pH-responsive starch-based nanocarrier polyacrylic acid containing SiO2 for controlled quercetin release in cancer therapy
Authors: Mehrab Pourmadadi, Pegah Poorkhalili, Maryam Hosseini, Samira Piran, Amir Reza Hafezi, Abdullah Kamaluldeen, Mohammad Najafi, Tahmineh Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer is still a major worldwide health concern, and traditional treatment methods can have serious side effects. In order to improve the therapeutic use of quercetin (QC) in the treatment of cancer, this work presents a newly developed pH-responsive nanocomposite. The water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) double emulsion approach was used to create the nanocarrier, which is an effective QC delivery system made up of silica nanoparticles (SiO2), polyacrylic acid (PAA), and starch (S). With entrapment and loading efficiencies of 79.50% and 46%, respectively, the addition of SiO2 nanoparticles greatly improves the S/PAA hydrogel matrix’s entrapment and drug-loading capabilities. the dynamic light scattering (DLS) studies, zeta potential, and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) showed the average particle size to be 171.25 nm and zeta potential to be -50.12 mV, demonstrating a stable well-defined nanoscale structure. The pH-sensitive character of the prepared nanocarrier is further highlighted by in vitro drug release studies that show QC is released in a sustained and controlled way in acidic media compared to physiological pH. The Higuchi model has the best fit at pH 7.4, indicating that drug diffusion is mainly controlled by solubility constraints in the polymer matrix. The Korsmeyer-Peppas model, on the other hand, has the most accurate representation at pH 5.4, indicating an anomalous transport mechanism. These mathematical models further clarify the release kinetics. Furthermore, cytotoxicity tests employing the MTT assay verify the QC-loaded nanocomposite’s strong inhibitory effects on HCT-116 colon cancer cells while proving its biocompatibility with L929 fibroblast cells. Together, our results demonstrate the S/PAA/SiO2@QC nanocomposite’s potential as an effective and focused therapeutic platform for the treatment of cancer, providing a viable substitute for traditional drug delivery methods.

Introduction

A group of illnesses collectively referred to as cancer are defined by the unchecked growth of aberrant cells, or neoplasms, which results in the loss of normal cell structure and function1–3. These aberrant cells can quickly proliferate, trigger angiogenesis, avoid immune detection, and migrate to other areas of the body, eventually leading to serious cancers4–6. Tens of millions of people are still affected by cancer every year, despite tremendous advancements in both medicine and technology7–9. One of the most urgent global health issues is cancer, which is the second greatest cause of mortality worldwide after cardiovascular illnesses10. Thus, prompt and efficient therapy is necessary to stop unchecked tumor development and lessen the burden on cancer patients11. Modern cancer treatment uses a multidisciplinary approach that includes radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and surgical operations including mastectomy and lumpectomy12,13. Despite the fact that these therapeutic approaches have greatly improved patient outcomes, they nevertheless have major drawbacks14. While surgical methods may carry the danger of organ damage and subsequent problems, chemotherapy and radiation therapy frequently result in significant side effects, such as nausea and exhaustion15. Conventional methods of treatment can inadvertently increase cancer cell resistance and are largely ineffective, especially at advanced or metastatic stages. Furthermore, their intrusiveness and cost can be extremely devastating to patients’ quality of life and economic security16. Researchers are actively investigating novel treatments based on cutting-edge nanotechnology to solve these issues17.

Nanotechnology has been widely used in recent decades, and medication delivery using nanoparticles has emerged as a viable therapy strategy for cancer. Drug-loaded nanoparticles provide several benefits over conventional chemotherapy, including increased stability, less toxicity, higher effectiveness, increased specificity, improved tolerance, and a high drug-loading capacity18. Additionally, using nanocarriers offers a number of benefits over traditional drug delivery methods, such as improved biodistribution, a longer plasma half-life, and accurate drug delivery to the tumor microenvironment through endothelium layer penetration10. The advantages of nanoscale carriers come from both their compositional variety and their adjustable tininess. Lipids (e.g., liposomes and solid lipid nanoparticles), inorganic particles (e.g., SiO2 nanoparticles), and polymers (e.g., polymeric nanoparticles) are examples of ingredients that may be used to prepare them18. Polymers used in the preparation of nanocarriers are divided into two categories: natural substances like starch (S), alginate, and gelatin, and synthetic ones like polyacrylic acid (PAA), poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL), and polylactide (PLA)18. Since organic-based carriers, especially polymers, have great biocompatibility, low toxicity, and adjustable biodegradability, they are the most often used nanocarrier types in nanomedicine formulations. This renders them optimal for the administration of oncological therapeutics. These carriers offer advantages such as high drug-loading capacity, targeted administration, and controlled release; nevertheless, they also have disadvantages, including restricted tumor penetration and poor stability in the circulation. Incorporating inorganic nanoparticles into nanoparticle systems offers benefits such as reduced immunogenicity and enhanced solubility and stability of medicinal medicines. They have poor biocompatibility, a low drug-loading capacity, and the potential to be hazardous, notwithstanding these benefits. In order to get around these restrictions, hybrid nano-architectures that combine inorganic nanoparticles and organic molecules have been created. These nano-architectures minimize the drawbacks of each carrier type while maximizing their respective advantages19–21.

A biopolymer, starch (S) has advantageous qualities including cost effectiveness, biodegradability, and biocompatibility. Additionally, it has good characteristics like low viscosity, mechanical stability, transparency, and swelling. Furthermore, starch has other features that make it uniquely suitable to produce hydrogel beads for nanocomposite material. These include prolonged thermal stability, environmental sustainability, salt resistance, high thermal stability, and improved cross-linking ability within hydrogel frameworks22. Although curcumin (CUR) has excellent anticancer activity against a variety of tumor cell lines, Pereira et al.23 showed that its low solubility and quick breakdown at physiological pH limit its therapeutic use. They created starch-based microspheres crosslinked with N, N-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) for CUR delivery in order to overcome these difficulties. These microspheres allowed for a regulated, pH-responsive release profile and demonstrated great loading efficiency throughout a range of CUR concentrations. The prolonged release of CUR from the microspheres considerably increased its anticancer activity, boosting cytotoxicity against Caco-2 and HCT-116 tumor cells by up to 40-fold, according to cytotoxicity studies. Moreover, problems such as inadequate water solubility, weak hydrophilicity, limited bioavailability, and chemical instability similar to CUR exist for quercetin (QC), A flavonoid having several biological advantages, such as anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and especially anticancer properties, QC is found in plants and vegetables. By stopping cancer cells from proliferating, it successfully combats colon cancer cells as well as lymphoid and myeloid leukemia cells. Although the problems mentioned above cause it to be extracted from the body before it reaches its destinations. Therefore, there is a need for appropriate drug delivery systems to take advantage of the benefits of this natural anti-cancer drug. A water-soluble, reasonably priced polymer with biocompatibility and biodegradability, PAA finds extensive usage in a range of biomedical applications24. It is known for its anionic nature, which enables it to form hydrogels that can undergo significant changes in response to environmental factors like pH25. The inclusion of carboxyl functional groups causes the network of the polymer to significantly expand26. Among its many uses in the pharmaceutical and medical industries are dispersion agents in food processing, oral suspensions, drug delivery systems, bioadhesives, and cosmetic formulations27,28. MoS2 nanosheets loaded with QC, PAA, and chitosan were utilized by Pourmadadi et al.29 to produce a pH-sensitive, biocompatible nanocomposite for the targeted treatment of breast cancer. They found high stability, strong polymer linkages, and enhanced drug loading and encapsulation. Biological investigations confirmed that the nanocomposite effectively destroyed cancerous cells by releasing 68% of QC under neutral conditions and 97% in acidic ones. Since SiO2 nanoparticles are easy to synthesize, have a unique mesoporous structure, uniform morphology, adjustable surface properties, precisely controlled size, excellent biocompatibility and versatile surface functionalization, they have been thoroughly studied as a possible nanocarrier for anticancer drug delivery. Anticancer medications can be loaded efficiently thanks to their adjustable pore size and volume, which also makes it easier for a variety of therapeutic compounds to adsorb30–32. As a result, SiO2 nanoparticles are a viable platform for creating an ideal drug delivery system as they are easily functionalized with targeted agents and incorporated into controlled release systems33. Mishra et al.34 developed a pH-sensitive drug delivery system that targets human colorectal cancer cells (HCT-116) using mesoporous SiO2 nanoparticles. Acid-labile Fe3O4 nanoparticles and SiO2 nanoparticles functionalized with folic acid and loaded with QC were combined to create the composite. This composite lowered tumor development and induced apoptosis significantly. Scientists are focusing more towards the development of environmentally responsive nanoparticles in a bid to enhance their medicinal applications. These nanoparticles experience physicochemical alterations in response to external stimuli, which facilitates site-specific drug release35,36. By responding to internal stimuli such as pH changes and enzyme levels or external stimuli such as magnetic fields and ultrasound, stimuli-responsive nanoparticles provide precise control over the quantity, location, and timing of anticancer drug delivery. This targeted approach ensures selective release of medication within targeted sites, enhances tumor selectivity, reduces side effects, and enhances treatment efficacy37. The pathogenic phenotype of tumors is a lower extracellular pH compared to that of normal tissues. Due to the heightened metabolic activity of the tumor, anaerobic glycolysis generates more lactic acid, which results in this acidity. The acidic tumor microenvironment is also contributed to by the inefficient removal of carbon dioxide, lactic acid, and other metabolic waste products38,39. Due to this, the tumor microenvironment pH is slightly acidic (below 6.5), which is lower than the physiological pH of blood and normal tissues (approximately 7.4)40. To achieve selective transportation to tumor sites, this research employs pH-sensitive nanoparticles as a drug delivery system. In vitro, these nanocarriers facilitate pH-dependent release of medication, with higher release under acidic conditions that mimic the tumor microenvironment.

This study presents the development of a pH-responsive nanocarrier composed of S, PAA, and SiO2, designed to effectively deliver QC to cancer cells. The QC was incorporated into the carrier, which was subsequently emulsified through a water-in-oil method to achieve a spherical form and a consistent nano-sized distribution. FTIR, XRD, FE-SEM, DLS, and zeta potential approaches were among the many methods employed to examine the resulting nanocarrier. In vitro recordings were made of both physiological and acidic pH QC releasing properties. Using MTT, the new platform’s improved anti-cancer activity against human colon cancer cell lines was confirmed.

Materials and methods

Materials

Several sources provided the materials utilized in this investigation. The materials supplied by Merck Co. (Germany) included PAA, starch (C6H10O5), SiO2 nanoparticles, olive oil, ethanol, ethyl acetate, high-quality acetic acid (purity > 99%), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Sigma-Aldrich (USA) supplied the fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin/EDTA (0.25%), DMEM, penicillin/streptomycin, dialysis bags (12 kDa cutoff), MTT powder, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and QC. Pasteur Institute in Iran, provided the HCT-116 and L929 cell lines utilized in this study.

Synthesis of S/PAA/SiO2 nanocarrier and drug (QC) loading procedure

First 0.7 g of S was dissolved in 30 mL of 2% (v/v) acetic acid at room temperature using a magnetic stirrer to create a uniform 2% (w/v) S solution. The produced solution was then mixed with 0.35 g of PAA powder and agitated until completely dissolved. For ten minutes, the resultant mixture was treated with ultrasonic waves. Then, using a magnetic stirrer, 30 mg of SiO2 nanoparticles were added and mixed well. A QC-loaded S/PAA/SiO2 hydrogel was formed after the QC drug was added to the formulation until the final hydrogel drug concentration was 5 µg/mL. This was done while stirring continuously for 30 min to guarantee uniform dispersion.

To prepare the W/O/W double emulsion, the QC-loaded S/PAA/SiO₂ hydrogel (10 mL) served as the inner aqueous phase (W₁). This was added dropwise into 10 mL of olive oil containing 2% (v/v) SPAN 80 (as a lipophilic surfactant) under vigorous magnetic stirring (800 rpm) at room temperature, forming a primary water-in-oil (W₁/O) emulsion after 30 min of stirring. Subsequently, 10 mL of distilled water (the outer aqueous phase, W₂) was added slowly to the W₁/O emulsion under continued stirring (600 rpm) for another 30 min, resulting in a water-in-oil-in-water (W₁/O/W₂) double emulsion. After removing the emulsion from the stirrer, phase separation was permitted for half an hour. The drug-loaded nanocarriers were separated from the aqueous phase by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 12 min after the oil phase was removed with a pipette. Samples were freeze-dried at various stages to create a powdered form. They were first kept at -20 °C until they were frozen solid, and then they were lyophilized.

Characteristic assessments of nanosystem

FTIR spectroscopy (Thermo, AVATAR, USA) was used in the investigation to identify the functional groups that were present in each component of the nanocomposites and to analyze the interactions between the drug, SiO2, and polymers (S/PAA). S, PAA, SiO2, S/PAA, S/PAA/SiO2, and S/PAA/SiO2@QC samples were prepared using the KBr pellet procedure. These samples’ spectra were captured between 400 and 4000 cm− 1 in order to evaluate molecular interactions and verify that components were successfully included.

The crystalline and amorphous characteristics of the powdered samples were assessed using XRD analysis. Using a PHILIPS PW1730 diffractometer (Amsterdam, Netherlands), the analysis was conducted at 25 °C. The crystalline phases and unit cell diameters of the materials were clarified by this study, which also confirmed structural features and possible modifications in crystallinity brought on by composite production.

The S/PAA/SiO2@QC nanocomposites’ surface morphology was examined with field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). A TESCAN MIRA III microscope was employed for the FE-SEM analysis, providing accurate data regarding the morphological and structural characteristics of the nanocomposites. Additionally, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was employed to measure the hydrodynamic radius, and Zeta potential analysis was performed to determine the average surface potential, using a SZ100z analyzer (Horiba Jobin Yvon).

After that, 1 mL of ethyl acetate was added, and the solution was shaken in the ethyl acetate to dissolve the unencapsulated medication individually. Following phase separation, drug’s absorbance at 370 nm was measured by spectrophotometric method. In vitro study of drug release

The membrane diffusion method was used to assess the drug release from the nanocarriers. Each dialysis bag was filled with 1 mL of the drug-loaded nanocarrier solution, which was then tightly closed on both ends. After preparing four dialysis bags, they were submerged in 15 mL of PBS solutions with varying pH values (7.4 and 5.4) and 20% (v/v) ethanol. At replicate physiological circumstances, the samples were put on a heating stirrer that was set at 37 °C. An equivalent volume of new buffer solution was added to each dialysis bag after 300 µL of solution was removed at prearranged intervals (0, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h). A UV-Vis spectrophotometer set at 370 nm was used to detect the drug’s distinctive absorption. A standard calibration curve, often referred to as a release profile, was used to calculate the concentration of the released medication at each time point. MTT assay

The MTT assay was employed to evaluate the viability of cells in the fibroblast cell line (L929) and the colon cancer cell line (HCT-116). To produce purple formazan crystals for this colorimetric assay, a yellow tetrazolium salt must be reduced. The cells were originally cultivated in a mixture of 1% penicillin-streptomycin DMEM and 10% FBS, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. The cells were seeded onto 96-well plates after 24 h of incubation. Post-treatment, the cells were evaluated against untreated control, free QC, S, S/PAA, S/PAA/SiO2, and S/PAA/SiO2@QC formulations. Following a 48-hour incubation period, each well received 100 µL of MTT solution, which was then shaken for three minutes50. After washing the cells with PBS, 200 µL of DMSO was used to dissolve the formazan crystals. A microplate reader was used to detect the absorbance of the resultant solution at 570 nm. The cellular viability of the treated cells was then contrasted with that of the control sample, which had 100% viability.

Statistical analysis

A one-way ANOVA with a Student’s t-test was used for statistical analysis, and the experimental data were reported as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as P-values below 0.05.

Results and discussion

FTIR

Functional groups in the raw materials and produced nanocomposites were examined using FTIR spectroscopy. The individual components’ and the final composites’ FTIR spectra were obtained, as shown in Fig. 1. Absorption bands at 765, 858, and 925 cm− 1 in the starch spectrum are linked to C–O–C stretching vibrations seen in carbohydrate ring structures. The symmetric bending and deformation of the C–H and CH2 groups is associated with the peaks at 1384 and 1467 cm− 1. The extensive absorption in the 3300–3600 cm− 1 region suggests O–H stretching vibrations, but the band at 1648 cm− 1 is ascribed to the bending vibrations of the C–O bond in hydroxyl groups51. Lastly, the stretching vibrations of aliphatic C-H bonds are linked to the absorption band at 2925 cm− 152.

The wide band seen in the PAA sodium salt FTIR spectrum between 3000 and 3720 cm− 1 is associated with the O–H stretching vibrations, which are ascribed to hydrogen bonding and water content. The signal at 1125 cm− 1 is connected with C–O stretching vibrations, whereas the peak at 1637 cm− 1 is indicative of the asymmetric stretching of carboxylate groups53.

Three distinct absorption bands that correspond to the bending, symmetric stretching, and antisymmetric stretching vibrations that are typical of SiO2 are seen within the 410–860 cm− 1 area54. The asymmetric stretching vibration of the Si–O–Si bonds is responsible for the absorption peak at 1106 cm− 1 seen in the SiO2 spectrum55.

Both the S/PAA and S/PAA/SiO2 nanocomposites preserve the distinctive peaks of their constituent parts, as seen in Fig. 1. A weak band appeared at 1126 cm− 1 when QC was added to the S/PAA/SiO2 nanocomposite. This band was caused by the aromatic ring’s C = C stretching, which showed that the medication had been successfully loaded onto the nanocomposite56.

XRD

An essential method for analyzing the crystal structure and confirming the composition of nanocomposites is XRD examination. The stacked XRD patterns of the generated nanocomposites and pure components are displayed in Fig. 2. The semi-crystalline structure of the natural starch is characterized by large diffraction peaks at around 15.4°, 17.5°, and 22.8°57. Around 22°, SiO2 nanoparticles showed a broad, faint peak, suggesting that they were amorphous58. Since PAA is an amorphous organic polymer, it did not exhibit any distinct diffraction peaks, which is consistent with results from previous investigations59. Our results verified that PAA had a wide, faint peak at around 18°, indicating that it is amorphous. In contrast to the mostly amorphous components of starch and PAA, the S/PAA hydrogel’s XRD pattern showed distinct peaks at 31.7°, 45.5°, 56.5°, and 75.2°, indicating a more crystalline structure. The homogeneous distribution of amorphous SiO2 inside the hydrogel matrix caused the peaks to become less sharp and wider upon the addition of SiO2 nanoparticles to the S/PAA hydrogel, indicating decreased crystallinity. Typically, pure QC has pronounced diffraction peaks between 10° and 30°60. No extra peaks showed up for the QC-loaded nanocarrier, and the diffraction pattern was quite similar to the basic nanocarrier’s. This shows that QC was successfully incorporated into the nanocomposite. The low weight% of QC in the final formulation is the reason for the absence of identifiable peaks; its amount is inadequate to generate discernible diffraction signals. However, the steady pattern indicates that the medicine has been loaded successfully without substantially altering the nanocarrier’s crystalline structure.

FE-SEM

FE-SEM was used to examine the morphology of S/PAA/SiO2@QC (Fig. 3). It is evident from the pictures that the majority of the nanosystems are spherical in form and have a dense, inflexible structure. The most efficient shape of nanoparticles for drug delivery applications is thought to be spherical ones61. This spherical shape exhibits improved medication delivery efficiency and inhibits unintentional drug release. The uniformity of the nanocomposite’s surface indicates that its constituent parts work well together62. Using the W/O/W approach, the nanoparticles are expected to form a spherical morphology with the SiO₂ nanoparticles, which give a substantial amount of free space, most likely produced the core’s porous three-dimensional (3D) network and the S/PAA matrix surrounding it, suggesting a possible core-shell-type organization. However, we acknowledge that FE-SEM only provides surface imaging; thus, this proposed structure remains a hypothesis based on the synthesis method. Our group previously used an identical W/O/W procedure for a PEG-AGA-Fe₂O₃ nanocomposite and confirmed spherical morphology by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)63,64. However, nanoparticles frequently agglomerate when added to a polymer matrix. This is usually the result of the nanoparticles’ mutual attraction, which is aided by chemical interactions or van der Waals forces65.

DLS and zeta potential analyses of the nanocomposite

The particle size distribution of solutions and suspensions is ascertained using the physical procedure of DLS analysis. Based on the Brownian motion of the particles, this technique assesses the scattering and variations in light intensity following interaction with the laser light. The DLS experiment results for the QC-loaded S/PAA/SiO2 nanocomposite are shown in Fig. 4a. With an average size of 171.25 nm, the nanocarrier’s particle size distribution spans from 118 to 279 nm. According to studies, nanosystems with particles smaller than 100 nm may penetrate most cell barriers without acting selectively, which could result in cytotoxicity. Cancer cells, on the other hand, let bigger particles, up to 200 nm, to pass their membranes because of their aberrant tissue structure and fast development66. Because of this, the nanocarrier’s size is ideal for selectively entering cancer cells while preventing it from penetrating healthy cells52.

Zeta potential tests were used to evaluate the nanocomposite’s colloidal stability, and the results indicated a mean zeta potential of -50.12 mV (Fig. 4b). Since absolute values higher than − 30 mV are often linked to superior nanoparticle stability, this high value shows the stability of the created nanosystem67. Aggregation is prevented by the high electrical repulsion between nanoparticles. Because of its partly deprotonated hydroxyl groups at neutral pH, QC has a negative charge, which raises the loaded nanocomposite’s net negative charge overall68. According to recent studies, negatively charged nanocomposites and nanocarriers are more biocompatible than positively charged ones69,70. Additionally, compared to their positively charged counterparts, the negative charge reduces cytotoxicity and facilitates the improved clearance of nanocarriers by macrophages71.

Drug loading and entrapment efficiency

One important factor in medication delivery systems is the effectiveness of LE. Reducing medication waste and guaranteeing the carrier’s best performance depend on achieving a high LE. Furthermore, precise EE monitoring is essential to preventing overdose and guaranteeing the drug’s safe and efficient release29,72. In this investigation, The unbound drugs were isolated from the nanocarriers via dialysis, and the amount of the drug was then determined using UV-Vis spectroscopy at 370 nm. For the formulation without SiO2 nanoparticles, the amount of EE and LE were determined to be 60.25% and 39.50%, respectively. These results, however, increased with the addition of SiO2 nanoparticles, rising to 46% for LE and 79.50% for EE.

Because of its hydrophobic properties, the QC medication has trouble loading73. The S, PAA, and SiO2 nanocarriers’ increased specific surface area is responsible for the enhanced EE and LE seen in this investigation. Because SiO2 is permeable, a sizable quantity of QC may be enclosed and attached to the nanocarrier. According to earlier research73, QC is hydrophilic due to the hydroxyl groups in its structure, even though its skeletal structure gives it hydrophobic properties. The creation of hydrogen bonds between the hydroxyl groups of QC and the silanol groups of SiO2 and the carboxylic acid groups of PAA is the main mechanism for QC loading. The FTIR spectra validate the effective integration of QC and confirm this interaction. Furthermore, π–π stacking interactions between the nanocarrier’s constituents and the aromatic rings of QC help to further stabilize the medication29.

in vitro drug release

The nanocarriers were subjected to two distinct pH values throughout the dialysis procedure at 5.4, which represents malignant tissue, and 7.4, which represents healthy tissue. The QC release from the nanocarrier at 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h was 28%, 41%, 57%, 79%, and 94.25%, respectively, under acidic circumstances (pH = 5.4), as seen in Fig. 5. Conversely, the release rates were 19%, 25%, 36%, 47%, and 54% at physiological circumstances (pH = 7.4). These findings suggest a prolonged and consistent release of the medication from the nanocomposite, which is probably caused by a number of characteristics of the materials used to create the nanocomposite. Furthermore, comparing the data at both pH levels reveals a greater drug release at acidic pH, confirming the synthetic drug delivery system’s pH-responsiveness. Pourmadadi et al. [40], used S/zein/g-C3N4 as nanocarrier and observed that the drug 5-fluorouracil was released more efficiently and steadily under acidic conditions compared to physiological conditions.

The polar functional groups on the medication and the components of the nanocomposite get protonated as the ambient H+ concentration rises. The nanocomposite swells as a result of the repulsive forces created by this protonation, which disrupt their physical relationships [52]. In order to facilitate the quicker release of QC, the swelling increases the free volume inside the polymer network. By contrast, the hydrogel structure does not break down in a neutral environment, which means that less medication is released [73]. Since the surrounding S/PAA structure regulates the pace of QC release from the porous SiO2 nanoparticles, this release pattern is perfect for a medication delivery system. While the acidic milieu of tumor tissues stimulates the S/PAA layer, causing it to grow rapidly and release QC, the S/PAA layer at pH 7.4 limits the release of QC and lessening its detrimental effects on healthy cells.

MTT assay

Figure 7 displays the cell viability of HCT-116 and L929 cells treated with free QC, drug-loaded nanocarrier, and S/PAA/SiO2 nanocomposite. L929 cells treated with S and S/PAA nanoparticles maintained a viability comparable to the control group. Furthermore, compared to malignant cell lines, the S/PAA/SiO2 and S/PAA/SiO2@QC nanocomposites greatly reduced cell death in L929 cells, indicating the drug delivery system’s biocompatibility. After 48 h of polymer treatment, no discernible harm was seen when cytotoxicity was compared to an untreated control group. HCT-116 cells showed just 1% cell death in the S and S/PAA groups. The cell survival of HCT-116 cells rose to 94% when SiO2 was added to the polymeric network, demonstrating the biocompatibility of the S/PAA/SiO₂ nanocomposite. Furthermore, compared to free QC, The cytotoxicity of the S/PAA/SiO2@QC towards the cancer cell line was 47%, or around 15% greater. In comparison to the free anti-cancer medicine, this could be attributed to the generation of reactive oxygen species, which reduces drug wastage and side effects while exerting a more potent anti-proliferative effect on cancer cells.

Conclusion

Issues including limited bioavailability, instability, and non-specific action limit QC’s medicinal potential. In order to get over these restrictions, a twofold W/O/W emulsion method was used to develop a pH-responsive QC-loaded nanocomposite (S/PAA/SiO2), with SiO2 nanoparticles improving drug loading and encapsulation efficiency. Strong chemical interactions were found by FTIR analysis, which helped to create a profile of continuous release. Protonation of functional groups at lower pH disrupted the nanocarrier, enabling improved QC release and taking advantage of the pH difference between normal and malignant tissues for regulated drug delivery, according to drug release tests conducted at pH 7.4 and 5.4. A progressive release profile was demonstrated in vitro, enhancing QC’s biological half-life and biodistribution. The extended release resulted in increased cytotoxicity, leading the MTT assay on HCT-116 cells to demonstrate that QC-loaded nanoparticles exhibited superior anticancer activity compared to free QC. Moreover, the MTT assay on L929 fibroblasts demonstrated reduced cytotoxicity compared to free QC, signifying the biocompatibility of the nanocarrier. This formulation offers an efficient drug delivery system while effectively mitigating many principal limitations of QC as an antineoplastic agent. The biological evaluation in this work relied on MTT assays, which measure cell viability but do not confirm specific mechanisms such as apoptosis, reactive oxygen species generation, or cellular uptake. Future work to evaluate the in vivo capability of the nanocarrier presented in this study could address the limitations of the in vitro section mentioned in the current work and reveal the practical potential of this nanocarrier. The quantitative performance metrics establish a useful benchmark for comparing future quercetin delivery systems.
