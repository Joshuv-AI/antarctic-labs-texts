Clinical management of Xpert® MTB/RIF ultra trace results among presumptive tuberculosis patients in Lebanon: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Marianne Antar Soutou, Sirine Abou Ismail, Anne-Marie Farhat, Soukaina Basma, Laurent Raskine, François-Xavier Babin, Farah Jradi, Hiam Yaacoub, Marianne Abifadel, Josette Najjar-Pellet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterpreting trace results from the Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra assay, a frontline diagnostic tool for tuberculosis, remains challenging, particularly in low-tuberculosis burden settings. This study aimed to provide practical recommendations for managing trace-positive patients in Lebanon based on clinical, radiological, and bacteriological data.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted at the National Tuberculosis Reference Laboratory in Lebanon, Laboratoire Rodolphe Mérieux, from January 2020 to December 2024. Samples referred by the Lebanese National Tuberculosis Program for both Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra and mycobacterial culture were included. Non-conforming samples or those with invalid/error Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra results were excluded. For patients with trace-positive results, correlations were assessed with microbiological parameters (microscopy and culture), clinical characteristics (symptoms, prior TB history and treatment initiation) and radiological findings. Diagnostic accuracy and case classification were assessed using a Composite Clinical Reference Standard (CRS) that excluded the index test to avoid incorporation bias.ResultsAmong 1,061 Ultra tests, 42 (4.0%) yielded trace-positive results. Patients with trace results had a mean age of 42 years (range: 0–75), and 59.5% were female. Two patients (4.8%) had a history of prior tuberculosis treatment, and one (2.4%) was HIV-positive. Most trace-positive results (33 cases; 78.6%) were derived from respiratory specimens. Culture confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex in 9 cases (21.4%). Using the CRS, 20 patients (47.6%) were classified as Definite TB (9 patients) or Probable TB (11 patients) and initiated first‑line therapy; 17 of them (85.0%) achieved favorable outcomes.ConclusionIn low‑burden settings like Lebanon, the interpretation of Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra trace results is strengthened by applying a Composite Clinical Reference Standard that integrates clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria. This approach supports more informed clinical classification, guides appropriate treatment decisions, and was associated with favorable outcomes in our cohort. These exploratory findings support the potential value of CRS‑based frameworks and warrant confirmation in prospective studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13333-3.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a global public health threat. In 2023, TB once again became the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, after being surpassed by COVID-19 for three years [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO), in the latest report, estimated 10.8 million new TB cases and 1.25 million deaths. Despite considerable advances in diagnostics and treatment, challenges persist, especially in the detection and management of TB cases [1].

The introduction of the Xpert® MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, USA) in 2010 marked a significant advancement in the diagnosis of TB and the detection of rifampicin (RIF) resistance [2]. However, this point-of-care test had limited sensitivity in smear-negative pulmonary TB and in paucibacillary TB cases such as extra-pulmonary TB, people living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (PLHIV) and pediatric TB [3–5]. To address these limitations, the next-generation Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra (hereafter “Ultra”) was developed in 2017, offering improved sensitivity for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) and RIF resistance [6–8]. This increased sensitivity is mainly due to the inclusion of two multicopy amplification targets, IS6110 and IS1081 [8]. Nonetheless, a major challenge with the Ultra assay is interpreting trace results, which indicate minimal levels of bacterial DNA and do not provide information on RIF resistance [8]. This limitation complicates the clinical management of presumptive TB patients.

Initial WHO guidance recommended interpreting a “trace call” for PLHIV, children, and extrapulmonary specimens as a true-positive TB result and thus initiating treatment. For HIV-negative presumptive TB patients, especially those with a history of prior TB treatment, a second sputum Ultra test was suggested, but it was often impractical in many settings, leaving clinicians uncertain about whether to initiate treatment. In response to these challenges, WHO revised its recommendations in 2020, advising that a trace result should be considered a true-positive TB diagnosis in HIV-negative individuals without a prior TB episode or recent TB treatment [9].

Lebanon is classified as a low TB burden country with an estimated incidence of 10 cases per 100,000 in 2024 and a low prevalence of HIV infection [10]. However, this country hosts a heterogeneous population, including refugees and migrant workers, which poses an ongoing risk for TB transmission and complicates the epidemiological landscape. The National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) of the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health (MOPH) has established a comprehensive algorithm for the management of presumptive TB cases, which recommends performing Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra assay and direct smear microscopy when the chest X-ray shows TB-related abnormalities.

This retrospective observational study, covering the period from 2020 to 2024, aims to assess the frequency of trace readouts in routine clinical practice in Lebanon and to provide guidance on managing patients with trace results by integrating available clinical, radiological, and bacteriological data.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the National Tuberculosis Reference Laboratory (NTRL), Laboratoire Rodolphe Mérieux (LRM), Lebanon, covering the period from January 2020 to December 2024. Clinical samples were referred through the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) of the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health. The program operates through nine TB control centers across the country, with the central unit located in Karantina (Beirut governorate) and eight peripheral centers distributed as follows: two in the North (Halba and Tripoli), three in the South (Saida, Tyre and Nabatiye), two in the Beqaa Valley (El Hermel and Zahlé), and one in Mount Lebanon (Al-Barouk).

Eligibility criteria

Samples were eligible for inclusion when both mycobacterial culture and Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra testing were requested. Non-compliant specimens, as well as those yielding invalid or error results on Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra, were excluded.

Ethical considerations

This study was a retrospective analysis of routinely collected data generated through standard diagnostic testing, with no study-related interventions or additional data collection performed. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Saint Joseph University of Beirut (Reference: CER-2025-236). The requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Ethics Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized routinely collected data.

Laboratory methods

All microbiology testing was conducted at the NTRL, the Rodolphe Mérieux Laboratory in Beirut, Lebanon. Specimens were processed and analyzed according to the laboratory’s standard operating procedures, ensuring full compliance with biosafety and biosecurity standards, and following manufacturer instructions.

Processing of clinical specimens

Specimen handling and processing were performed in the Biosafety Level 3 laboratory of the LRM. Briefly, non-sterile clinical specimens (excluding sterile body fluids) were processed using the N-acetyl-L-cysteine-sodium hydroxide (NALC-NaOH) digestion and decontamination method. Samples were transferred to 50 mL conical tubes containing NALC tablets and decontaminated by adding an equal volume of NaOH. To neutralize NaOH activity and lower the pH of the specimen, phosphate buffer was added to a final volume of 50 mL. The samples were then concentrated by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C after which the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of sterile phosphate buffer.

Mycobacterial culture

For mycobacterial culture, 500 µL of decontaminated sample or the sterile clinical specimen was inoculated into Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tubes (MGIT) liquid medium and incubated at 37 °C in the automated BACTEC MGIT 960 system (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA) for up to 42 days. In parallel, 250 µL was cultured in Löwenstein-Jensen solid medium and incubated at 37 °C for up to 56 days.

Identification of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex

The presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) was confirmed using the BD MGIT™ TBc Identification Test (MPT64 antigen immunochromatographic assay; Becton Dickinson) [11].

Drug susceptibility testing

First-line drug susceptibility testing was performed on all MTBC-positive cultures using the BACTEC MGIT 960 system with SIRE and PZA kits (Becton Dickinson), following manufacturer protocols [12].

Xpert MTB/RIF ultra assay

The Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra assay (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA) was performed following manufacturer’s instructions [13]. Briefly, 500 µL of decontaminated sample was mixed with 1.5 mL of sample reagent (1:3 ratio) for 15 min at room temperature. The suspension was then transferred to a single-use Xpert Ultra cartridges and loaded onto the GeneXpert IV module. Results were generated as negative or positive with semi-quantitative categories for MTB DNA detection, reported as “High, Medium, Low, Very Low, or Trace”, depending on the bacterial load detected. As for RIF resistance, the assay provides a categorical result reported as “Detected”, “Not Detected” or “Indeterminate” (assigned only to samples with a “Trace result”). In this cohort, no repeat Xpert Ultra testing was performed for samples yielding a Trace result.

Data collection

The study population included patients with “Trace detected results” from the NTRL TB database, selected according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Demographic data, clinical characteristics, and laboratory results - including acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear results, culture result and time to positivity, MTBC identification - were extracted for analysis. Complementary, clinical and radiological information were retrieved from the NTP TB database. Treatment outcomes were categorized as favorable when clinical and imaging findings indicated resolution, and unfavorable when findings suggest active TB. All data were anonymized and processed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365 apps for enterprise).

Case classification using a composite Clinical Reference Standard (CRS)

To better assess the clinical significance of Ultra trace results, a Composite Clinical Reference Standard (CRS) was applied to compensate for the limited sensitivity of mycobacterial culture in paucibacillary and extrapulmonary disease. The CSR integrated (i) microbiological confirmation (culture in liquid and/or solid media; smear when available); (ii) clinical features consistent with WHO TB case definitions (e.g., fever, cough > 2 weeks, weight loss, hemoptysis, night sweats, fatigue, dyspnea, chest pain); and (iii) radiological signs suggestive of TB on chest radiography or computed tomography (e.g., cavitation, miliary pattern, infiltrates, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, caseous nodules). Decision to initiate anti‑TB therapy, as recorded in the NTP registry, was considered supportive evidence within the CRS framework.

Patients were classified as follows:

Importantly, the index test (Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra trace result) was excluded from the CRS to avoid incorporation bias. This allowed us to evaluate the clinical impact of Ultra trace results against a reference standard independent of the test itself, better reflecting real‑world decision-making in a low‑burden setting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM® SPSS® Statistics version 26. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate associations between disease site and demographic variables (gender, age group, country of origin, and governorate), as well as the relationship between type of primary sample and culture outcome. Statistical significance was defined at p-value < 0.05.

Results

Prevalence of Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra trace results

Between January 2020 and December 2024, a total of 1,061 Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra tests were performed and had valid results. Of these, 767 (72.3%) were negative and 294 (27.7%) positive. As shown in Fig. 1, among the positive results, 80 (27.2%) were categorized as “Detected High,” 29 (9.9%) as “Detected Medium,” 99 (33.7%) as “Detected Low,” 44 (15.0%) as “Detected Very Low,” and 42 (14.3%) as “Trace Detected.” Overall, the trace results accounted for a minority of 4.0% of the Ultra test results.

Microbiological results of trace positive patients

Among the 42 samples, respiratory specimens accounted for 78.6%, with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) being the most frequently collected type. All samples were negative for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear.

As detailed in Table 2, 29 samples (69.0%) showed no mycobacterial growth, while 13 (31.0%) were culture-positive for mycobacteria. Of these, 9 yielded MTBC, and 4 yielded non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM).

Culture confirmation of MTBC was highest for gastric aspirates within respiratory samples and for biopsies within non-respiratory samples without a significant association between the type of primary sample and culture results.

The overall mean time to positivity (TTP) was 21.9 days. For respiratory samples, TTP was 23.5 days, whereas non-respiratory samples had a shorter mean TTP of 18.2 days; this difference was not statistically significant.

Description of the clinical and radiological characteristics of the study population

Among the study population, the majority (40/42, 95.2%) were newly diagnosed with TB, whereas only two (4.8%) had a history of previous TB treatment. None of the previously treated patients were initiated on TB therapy during the study period. Among new presumptive TB cases, clinical assessment focused on identifying symptoms suggestive of TB, including a cough lasting more than two weeks, fever, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, and loss of appetite. These symptoms were reported in 18 patients (42.9%). Radiological evaluation was subsequently performed in symptomatic individuals, and all 18 showed abnormal imaging findings compatible with TB. In addition, one asymptomatic patient with a high clinical suspicion of TB presented radiological abnormalities and had a culture-confirmed MTBC isolate, bringing the total number of patients with radiological abnormalities to 19/42 (45.2%). Biopsy specimens, when available, were further assessed for compatible histopathological findings; four patients underwent biopsy, of whom two showed features consistent with TB, while no histopathological information was available for the remaining two. Cases showing both clinical and radiological evidence of TB were referred to the LRM for culture confirmation. Contacts of known TB-positive cases were screened using the Tuberculin Skin Test (TST) test to identify latent TB infection.

Final diagnostic classification, treatment initiation, and patient outcomes

Among the 42 patients with an Ultra trace result, final diagnostic classification was established based on the CRS integrating clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings detailed in Supplementary Document 1 (Descriptive clinical, radiological, and microbiological elements guiding treatment decisions in the study population). As summarized in Fig. 2, nine patients (21.4%) were classified as confirmed TB, defined by culture-positive identification of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Eleven patients (26.2%) were classified as probable TB, as they presented compatible clinical and/or radiological findings, were initiated on anti-tuberculosis treatment, and demonstrated documented clinical or radiological improvement despite negative cultures. The remaining 22 patients (52.4%) were categorized as not TB, based on the absence of supportive clinical, radiological, or microbiological evidence and the absence of anti-tuberculosis treatment initiation.

Overall, 20 patients (47.6%) were started on anti-tuberculosis therapy, all receiving first-line regimens in accordance with national guidelines. Among treated patients, 17 (85%) successfully completed treatment, while 3 (15%) had unfavorable outcomes: one patient left the country during treatment, one had treatment discontinued, and one died.

Stratified analyses by subgroups

To address potential differences in the clinical relevance of trace results, analyses were stratified into three subgroups: Pediatric subgroup (n = 5), Adult Pulmonary subgroup (n = 29) and Adult Extrapulmonary subgroup (n = 8), as summarized in Table 3.

Pediatric subgroup

The pediatric subgroup accounted for 5 of 42 patients (12%), all of whom were newly diagnosed and had documented TB exposure. All children demonstrated clinical symptoms and/or radiological abnormalities compatible with TB. Positive cultures for MTBC were obtained in three pediatric cases (60%), reflecting a higher proportion of culture confirmation than among adults. All five children were initiated on anti‑TB therapy based on the combination of compatible clinical–radiological findings and supportive Ultra trace positivity. All children demonstrated favorable clinical improvement during follow‑up.

Adult Extrapulmonary subgroup

The adult extrapulmonary subgroup comprised eight cases (19%). Most patients (6/8, 75%) presented with clinical symptoms and/or radiological abnormalities suggestive of extrapulmonary TB, and histopathology—when available (2 out of 4 cases of biopsy samples)—was compatible with TB. MTBC was cultured in three patients (37.5%), while the remaining samples were culture‑negative; no NTM isolates were detected. A total of 6 patients (75%) were initiated on anti‑TB therapy, guided by clinical findings, radiological abnormalities, supportive histopathology, and/or culture confirmation. Among treated patients, five completed therapy successfully, while one patient left the country during treatment. Two patients were not treated. One had a pleural fluid sample with no TB‑suggestive symptoms, no radiological abnormalities, and a negative MTBC culture. The other had a biopsy sample without TB-compatible symptoms; histopathology was unavailable and MTBC culture was negative.

Adult Pulmonary subgroup

The adult pulmonary subgroup comprised 29 cases (69%). Ten patients (34.5%) presented with TB‑suggestive symptoms, and all symptomatic patients had abnormal chest imaging. The remaining cases were clinically asymptomatic at presentation and did not undergo chest imaging. Culture results showed that MTBC was isolated in 3 patients (10.3%), NTM organisms were detected in 4 patients (13.8%), and the remaining 22 patients (75.9%) had negative cultures. A total of 9 adults (31%) were initiated on anti‑TB therapy, typically when symptoms and radiological abnormalities were present and/or when MTBC culture was positive. Among treated adults, seven completed therapy successfully, while two experienced unfavorable outcomes: one treatment discontinuation and one death during treatment. The remaining 20 patients (69%) were not treated. These cases lacked TB‑suggestive symptoms, radiological abnormalities, and MTBC culture positivity. Among the untreated group, two had a history of previous TB treatment but did not present any clinical, radiological, or microbiological evidence suggestive of active TB requiring retreatment.

Discussion

This retrospective observational study evaluated the prevalence and clinical relevance of Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra trace results among presumptive TB patients in Lebanon, a low TB and HIV burden setting. We found that trace results accounted for 14.3% (42/294) of Ultra-positive cases. This proportion aligns with the first multi-country evaluation involving 21 laboratories, which reported a prevalence of 11.9% (317/2,659) among Ultra-positive samples [14]. Similar rates have also been described in both low and high TB burden settings. In low-burden countries, Amedeo et al. reported a prevalence of 17.5% in Bologna, Italy [15]; Piersimoni et al. 8.3% in another Italian cohort [16]; Martin-Higuera et al. found 12.5% in Madrid, Spain [17] and Opota et al. observed 10% in Lausanne, Switzerland [18]. Similar or slightly higher proportions were observed in high-burden contexts, including 16.7% in South Africa [19] and 12–17% in Uganda [20].

It is noteworthy that the number of samples tested during 2020 and 2021 was markedly lower than in other years. This decrease is largely attributable to the disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which restricted access to healthcare services, reduced routine diagnostic activities, and diverted laboratory capacity toward SARS-CoV-2 testing [21]. At the same time, Lebanon experienced large-scale emigration of both migrant workers and Lebanese citizens driven by the country’s severe economic collapse [22] and the 2020 Beirut Blast [23], resulting in fewer individuals accessing health services. These combined factors explain the reduced testing volume observed during this period.

The demographic profile of our cohort is consistent with national TB trends in Lebanon [10], where most cases occur among adults residing in urban areas, and reflect the country’s diverse population, including Lebanese nationals (52.4%), Syrian refugees (21.4%), and Ethiopian migrants (16.7%). This diversity highlights the role of migration and displacement in shaping TB epidemiology, as refugee and migrant populations are known to face increased vulnerability due to factors such as crowded living environments, disrupted healthcare access, and socioeconomic instability [24, 25]. Only one patient (2.4%) was HIV-positive, in line with Lebanon’s generally low HIV prevalence [26].

From a microbiological perspective, 78.6% of trace-positive samples were of respiratory origin, consistent with findings from the first multi-country evaluation involving 21 laboratories, where pulmonary specimens represented 68% of cases [14]. All trace-positive samples were negative by AFB smear, while culture was positive for MTBC in 21.4% of cases. This limited culture yield, together with consistent smear negativity, is compatible with the low bacillary loads typically associated with trace calls. The prolonged time‑to‑positivity observed among the few culture‑positive trace samples further reinforces this interpretation, as extended TTP is a well‑recognized marker of paucibacillary disease and aligns with the microbiological profile expected for trace results. Nevertheless, given the small number of culture‑positive cases, the relevance of TTP patterns in this context should be interpreted cautiously. These findings are also consistent with a recent review [27], which reported that most trace-positive samples were culture-negative, that culture positivity was lower among patients with trace results (23.5%) compared to those with non-trace Ultra results (60.5%). Together, these observations support the interpretation of trace results as indicative of a low bacterial burden, a scenario in which conventional diagnostic methods such as smear microscopy and culture often lack sufficient sensitivity. In addition, four trace-positive respiratory samples yielded NTM in BAL culture—Mycobacterium szulgai (n = 2), M. chelonae (n = 1), and M. gordonae (n = 1). Only one patient exhibited clinical and radiological features compatible with TB and was therefore treated. In the remaining three, the absence of TB-suggestive findings suggests that the Ultra trace likely reflected residual or non-viable MTBC DNA, a recognized cause of false-positive trace results in low-burden setting [28]. This reinforces the need to integrate symptoms, imaging, and culture rather than act on trace calls alone. The coexistence of NTM culture with a trace-positive Ultra result may have several explanations and depends partly on the species identified. Notably, M. gordonae is a well-known environmental contaminant of water systems and bronchoscopic procedures, making true infection unlikely in its case [29]. In contrast, M. szulgai is considered a potentially pathogenic NTM species; while our asymptomatic patients did not meet diagnostic criteria for TB disease, such isolates generally warrant further clinical correlation. For M. chelonae, infections are rare and occur predominantly in immunocompromised individuals [30], making colonization more likely in our clinical context. Overall, these findings reinforce that Ultra trace results should always be interpreted alongside clinical, radiological, and microbiological data rather than used in isolation for treatment decisions.

In this cohort, treatment initiation for trace‑positive patients followed the Composite Clinical Reference Standard, which integrates clinical symptoms, radiological findings, and microbiological evidence. Using this approach, 20 patients (47.6%) were started on anti‑TB therapy, including 9 classified as Confirmed TB and 11 as Probable TB. All received first‑line regimens according to national guidelines, consistent with Lebanon’s low prevalence of multidrug‑resistant TB [10]. To further support the relevance of using a composite CRS, we compared the performance of trace results when culture alone was used as the reference versus when evaluated against the CRS. Using culture as the sole reference, the positive predictive value (PPV) of an Ultra trace result was 21.4% (9/42; 95% CI 10.3–36.8). When re-evaluated against the CRS, the PPV increased markedly to 47.6% (20/42; 95% CI 32.0–63.6), underscoring the limitations of relying exclusively on culture in paucibacillary disease and highlighting the added clinical value of a CRS‑based framework in real‑world settings. The high proportion of favorable outcomes among treated patients (85%) further supports the appropriateness of CRS‑guided decisions. Notably, this success rate mirrors the national TB treatment success rate of 85% [10]. Although individual clinical follow‑up duration was not systematically available due to the retrospective design, all untreated patients were passively followed through the NTP, which mandatorily registers all TB diagnoses and is the sole provider of anti‑tuberculosis medications in Lebanon. From the time of the initial Ultra trace result until the end of the 2024 study period, none of the untreated patients were registered at the NTP for TB diagnosis or treatment. Conversely, the decision not to initiate treatment in two patients with a previous history of tuberculosis, negative cultures, and trace Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra results highlights an important point in result interpretation. Studies have shown that nucleic acid amplification tests, such as the Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra, can detect residual mycobacterial DNA in patients who have completed treatment, leading to false-positive results [31, 32]. The test’s specificity is particularly reduced among patients treated within the past two years [6, 33, 34]. In this context, the trace results were therefore interpreted as likely reflecting residual DNA rather than active disease. Overall, these findings collectively emphasize the importance of interpreting Ultra trace results within an CRS approach—combining clinical, radiological, and microbiological data—rather than relying on microbiological results alone.

Subgroup analysis showed that the influence of trace results on clinical management varied across the three major patient categories. Among pediatric patients, trace calls had a greater impact on treatment decisions. Most children presented with compatible clinical or radiological findings, and the higher rate of culture confirmation (60%) in this group made clinicians more inclined to initiate treatment despite low bacillary load. However, this observation should be interpreted with caution due to the very small sample size. Nevertheless, it suggests that Ultra trace detections may provide added diagnostic value in children, a population in whom TB often presents with low bacillary burden and nonspecific clinical features that complicate diagnosis, making even low-level molecular signals useful for supporting timely treatment initiation. This observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the clinical relevance of Ultra trace calls in pediatric TB, including data from a multicenter accuracy study [35] showing that the increased sensitivity of Xpert Ultra—compared with Xpert MTB/RIF—improves case detection in paucibacillary pediatric disease. Larger pediatric cohorts, however, are needed to validate this trend and determine its generalizability. In adult pulmonary cases, trace results were interpreted within the broader CRS framework, and treatment was initiated only when trace positivity aligned with symptoms or imaging suggestive of active pulmonary TB. In contrast, among adult extrapulmonary cases, Ultra trace results played a more supportive rather than decisive role. Most patients (6/8) presented with clinical symptoms and/or radiological abnormalities compatible with extrapulmonary TB, and in these instances the trace signal contributed as an additional element that strengthened the diagnostic impression within the CRS framework. Treatment initiation in this group relied primarily on the integration of clinical features, imaging findings, and—when available—histopathology or culture confirmation, with the trace result functioning as a complementary indicator that pushed the diagnostic assessment toward TB rather than driving the decision alone. Conversely, the two untreated patients in this group lacked any clinical or radiological evidence of TB and had negative cultures, illustrating that a trace result, in isolation, was insufficient to justify treatment when other CRS components did not support active disease. Across all three groups, these patterns demonstrate that trace results influenced treatment initiation only when supported by CRS components, emphasizing the need for integrated clinical, radiological, and microbiological assessment.

The main limitation of this study is the relatively small number of trace-positive cases (n = 42), with particularly limited representation of key subgroups such as pediatric patients, individuals with a history of prior TB treatment and extrapulmonary TB. This restricts statistical power and limits the generalizability of subgroup observations. As a result, no statistical analysis was conducted to assess potential associations between treatment outcomes (favorable or unfavorable) and variables such as demographic and clinical characteristics or type of primary sample. Therefore, observations regarding favorable outcomes following early treatment initiation should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory. Second, repeat Ultra testing was not performed for trace-positive samples. This was primarily due to the invasive nature of most specimens, as 37 of the 42 Ultra-positive index samples (88.1%) were obtained through procedures that are not easily repeatable such as bronchoalveolar lavage, gastric aspirates, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, or tissue biopsies. While this approach is consistent with WHO recommendations for HIV-negative individuals without prior TB in the Lebanese context, the absence of repeat testing may limit comparability with settings where repeat sampling is feasible or routinely recommended. Third, although the CRS does not include the Ultra trace result, clinicians’ treatment decisions were likely influenced by trace positivity, which may have introduced residual incorporation bias. Finally, given the retrospective design of the study, follow‑up was limited to information available in clinical records, preventing assessment of long‑term outcomes, relapse rates, or microbiological cure. Nevertheless, among untreated patients with documented follow‑up, no clinical progression suggestive of active TB was observed during the recorded follow‑up period.

Conclusion

This study provides the first national-level data on the management of Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra trace results in Lebanon. In this low-burden setting, applying a Composite Clinical Reference Standard (CRS) that integrates clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria allowed trace results to be more systematically classified within routine practice. Compared with culture alone, the CRS framework captured additional clinically relevant cases and better reflected real-world decision-making. Treatment was initiated when trace positivity was supported by complementary CRS components, and the favorable outcomes observed in our cohort further support the clinical relevance of this integrated approach. While these findings remain exploratory, they suggest that a CRS-based framework can guide the interpretation and management of trace-positive results and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies.
