sim2art: Accurate Articulated Object Modeling from a Single Video using Synthetic Training Data Only
Arslan Artykov, Corentin Sautier, Vincent Lepetit

Abstract
Understanding articulated objects is a fundamental challenge in robotics and digital twin creation. To effectively model such objects, it is essential to recover both part segmentation and the underlying joint parameters. Despite the importance of this task, previous work has largely focused on setups like multi-view systems, object scanning, or static cameras. In this paper, we present the first data-driven approach that jointly predicts part segmentation and joint parameters from monocular video captured with a freely moving camera. Trained solely on synthetic data, our method demonstrates strong generalization to real-world objects, offering a scalable and practical solution for articulated object understanding. Our approach operates directly on casually recorded video, making it suitable for real-time applications in dynamic environments. Project webpage: https://aartykov.github.io/sim2art/

Introduction

Articulated object understanding is a fundamental problem in robotics and digital twin creation. While the topic has sparkled strong interest from the computer vision community recently, most of the current approaches require complex setups, such as multi-view captures or object scanning under different configurations
20
;
38
;
16
. Other methods take as input a single RGB-D image
12
;
14
, however this often results in imprecise 3D determination of articulations.

By contrast, we propose a method that can recover an articulated 3D model for an object given a single monocular video captured with a freely moving camera. Some recent works have considered recently such input
21
;
1
.
For instance, Reart
21
samples points on the object at each time step and optimizes on the object structure. Another line of work
1
considers long 2D point tracks to enforce geometric constraints. However, despite advances in point tracking, extracting reliable long tracks remains challenging.

Like Reart, we therefore also sample points on the target object’s surface in each frame, but we turn to a data-driven approach. We also attach to the points their scene flow, which we obtain by tracking the points over a single time step. This adds motion information without introducing large errors like long tracks would do. Optionally, we also consider the point semantics as captured by DINOv3
32
.

Not only can this representation be computed more reliably from the input images than long tracks, but it also generalizes well from synthetic data to real data: 3D points and their scene flows can be obtained in a straightforward way from simple synthetic rendering. We simply add Gaussian noise to simulate extraction errors we will get on real data. DINOv3 features also generalize very well from synthetic rendering to real data. As a result, we can train our architecture on synthetic video sequences. For this purpose, we created a dataset made of synthetic videos exhibiting moving articulated objects seen from moving and static cameras.

We use a Transformer architecture which takes these points, their scene flows, and their appearances as input and predict part segmentation and joint parameters. We consider an arbitrary number of parts and use a linear assignment formulation inspired by DETR
2
to obtain the predictions.

We evaluate our method, which we call sim2art, on standard datasets for articulated object reconstruction, and show that our approach significantly outperforms the state of the art in terms of robustness and accuracy.

We believe that sim2art is the first data-driven approach to jointly infer part segmentation and joint parameters of articulated objects from single monocular videos captured by a freely moving camera. Our method is trained exclusively on synthetic data, yet generalizes strongly to real-world sequences. This reliance on synthetic data is a major advantage, as collecting and annotating real data for this task is particularly challenging. Synthetic datasets, in contrast, can be generated effortlessly, and scaling them further is likely to boost our method’s performance even more.

Like us, several approaches already rely on machine learning to infer the structure of articulated objects from known categories. Early works, like A-SDF

The key difference between these earlier learning-based approaches and ours is that we take as input a video sequence, which is a convenient way to capture new objects. Moreover, our representation of the video, made of temporal point clouds associated with scene flows and DINOv3 features, makes our method generalize from synthetic videos to real ones, while predicting accurate joint parameters.

The method in

Instead of relying on learning-based priors,

REACTO reconstructs surface geometry but does not estimate joint parameters, whereas Articulate-Anything predicts joint parameters for single-part objects but does not reconstruct geometry—instead retrieving a mesh from an existing database. Our method takes as input a sequences of images, from which we obtain the masks of the objects, the depth maps, and the camera parameters. We sample 2D points over the masks, lift them to 3D, and augment them with their scene flows and DINOv3 features. From this input, we predict the part segmentations, joint parameters for each part, and amounts of motions for each part and each time step.

Our goal is to segment the target object into articulated parts and predict its joint parameters from a casually captured video sequence. To that end, we use as input 3D points sampled on the target object’s surface in each frame, as illustrated in Figure
3
. We train our model purely with synthetic data and generalize to real-world objects during inference time without any additional finetuning. For inference on real-world data, we rely on ViPE
10
to obtain depth maps and camera parameters, however, during training we use only simulated data. We describe our approach in more details below.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given a monocular video sequence made of
T
images, we build a sequence of point clouds
P_{t}=\{p_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{p}}
, where each point
p_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is expressed in the coordinate system of the first camera. Each point
p_{t}^{i}
is obtained by sampling a random pixel
m_{t}^{i}
on the object mask for frame
t
and lifting it to 3D using the depth map for frame
t
. In practice, for real sequences, we use the open-source tool ViPE to acquire camera parameters with depth maps and SAM2
29
to calculate the background masks from the real-world data. We use
N_{p}=2048
in all our experiments. We normalize the point coordinates into range [-1, 1].

We jointly predict a part label for each point
p_{t}^{i}
and the joint parameters for each part.

3.2
Point Cloud Sequence Feature Extraction

3D scene flow.

For each point
p_{t}^{i}
, we
estimate its scene flow
f_{t}^{i}\in{\mathds{R}}^{3}
, its 3D translation between time steps
t
and
t+1
. For real-world data, we use GMSF
42
to calculate per-point scene flow.

Semantic features.

We employ the DINOv3 foundation model
32
to extract per-frame feature maps
\Psi_{t}\in{\mathds{R}}^{H\times W\times 1024}
from each frame of the video sequence, where
H\times W
is the resolution of the feature map. Per-point features are then sampled from
\Psi_{t}
using bilinear grid sampling and projected by a small MLP to a 3-dimensional space, yielding the final image features
\phi_{t}^{i}
for each point
p_{t}^{i}
.

3.3
Encoder

The encoder part of our architecture is inspired by the one introduced by
6
for point cloud video processing tasks such as 3D action recognition. However, we made critical changes to properly introduce the scene flow information and the DINO features as additional input, which provide a performance boost. Similarly, we show how to output the joint parameters for each object parts.

Like
6
, we first subsample
T\times N_{k}
keypoints obtained by applying farthest point sampling (FPS)
28
to each input point cloud
P_{t}
. We can then compute a feature vector
{\bf f}(k_{t}^{i})
for each keypoint
k_{t}^{i}
:

{\bf f}(k_{t}^{i})=\sum_{(p,\bar{t^{\prime}})\in G(k_{t}^{i})}[(p-k_{t}^{i})^{\top},t^{\prime}-t,\bar{v},\bar{\phi},\bar{t}]^{\top}\>,

(1)

where
G(k_{t}^{i})
is the set of points
p
in the neighborhood of
k_{t}^{i}
and their corresponding time step
t^{\prime}
in the spatio-temporal neighborhood of keypoint
k_{t}^{i}
. This spatio-temporal neighborhood is defined by an interval of
r_{t}
time steps and spatial radius
r_{s}
. Compared to
6
, we add at this stage the scene flow information and the DINOv3 features. More exactly,
{\bf f}(k_{t}^{i})
depends on
\bar{v}
and
\bar{\phi}
, the average scene flow and the average DINOv3 feature in neighborhood
G(k_{t}^{i})
. We also inject the normalizes time information
\bar{t}=t/T
, as we observed that it improves the joint prediction.

We then apply 2 convolutional layers and an MLP to the
{\bf f}(k_{t}^{i})
and obtain
{\bf f}_{e}(k_{t}^{i})
vectors of dimension 512 as output of our encoder.

3.4
Decoder

Fan et al.
5
uses directly the spatio-temporal features
{\bf f}_{e}(k_{t}^{i})
as input to a self-attention mechanism. We also add time information again by giving as input to our decoder feature vectors of the form

{\bf f}^{\prime}(k_{t}^{i})=[{\bf f}_{e}(k_{t}^{i});\gamma(t)]\>,

(2)

for each vector
{\bf f}_{e}(k_{t}^{i})
output by the encoder.
\gamma(t)=[\sin(2^{k}\pi t),\cos(2^{k}\pi t)]_{k=0}^{L}
is a positional encoding for frame index
t
. We use
L=6
.
We then perform video-level self-attention on the
{\bf f}^{\prime}
vectors.

Finally, we propagate features
{\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(k_{t}^{i})
from the keypoints
k_{t}^{i}
to the original 3D points
p_{t}^{i}
:

{\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(p_{t}^{i})=\frac{\sum_{k\in G_{p}(p_{t}^{i})}w(k,p_{t}^{i}){\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(k)}{\sum_{k\in G(p_{t}^{i})}w(k,p_{t}^{i})}

(3)

where
w(k,p_{t}^{i})=\frac{1}{||k-p_{t}^{i}||^{2}}
and
G_{p}(p)
is the set of nearest neighbors of point
p
.

3.5
Part Labels and Joint Parameters Prediction

Part label prediction.

Computing
{\bf d}(p_{t}^{i})=\text{softmax}({\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(p_{t}^{i}).Q^{\top})
, where matrix
Q
is made of learnable queries, gives us a distribution over the parts for each point
p_{t}^{i}
, predicting to which part
p_{t}^{i}
belongs to.

Joint parameters prediction.

We also extend
5
to predict the joint parameters for each part. To this end, we aggregate the per-point features
{\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(p_{t}^{i})
into per-part features
{\bf f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(m)
, where
m
is the part index:

{\bf f}_{\text{part}}(m)=\frac{\sum_{t}\sum_{i}{\bf d}(p_{t}^{i})_{m}{\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(p_{t}^{i})}{\sum_{t}\sum_{i}{\bf d}(p_{t}^{i})_{m}}\>.

(4)

We feed each per-part feature
{\bf f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(m)
to an MLP with multiple heads, which outputs joint type, translation axis, rotation axis, and pivot point.

We parametrize revolute joints by a rotation axis in the form of a unit vector
{\bf a}_{r}\in{\mathds{R}}^{3}
, a pivot point
{\bf p}\in{\mathds{R}}^{3}
, and a rotation amount
{\bf r}_{t}\in{\mathds{R}}
for each time step
t
. Prismatic joints are parametrized by a joint axis in the form of a unit vector
{\bf a}_{p}\in{\mathds{R}}^{3}
and a translation amount
{\bf d}_{t}\in{\mathds{R}}
for each time step
t
.

In addition to the revolute and prismatic joint parts, we also consider a ’static’ part for the object as some parts do not move.
We predict the joint type and three sets of parameters, corresponding to an axis and a pivot point for the revolute joint, or another axis for the prismatic joint. At inference, only the relevant parameters are used.

Amount of motion prediction.

We also need to predict the amount of motion (rotation or translation) for each part and each time step. To do so, we aggregate features by calculating one feature vector for each part at each timestep:

{\bf f}_{\text{motion}}(m,t)=\frac{\sum_{i}{\bf d}(p_{t}^{i})_{m}{\bf f}^{\prime\prime}(p_{t}^{i})}{\sum_{i}{\bf d}(p_{t}^{i})_{m}}\>.

(5)

Then, we feed the aggregated features to a specific decoding MLP head, to predict the per-part rotation/translation amount at each timestep.

3.6
Loss Functions

We predict a fixed
M=20
number of parts, which is generally much greater than the true number of parts in the ground-truth labels. The ordering of the predicted parts has no reason to match the ordering of the ground-truth ones. To solve this, we use the Hungarian Matching as in
2
to match the predictions to the ground-truth ones during training, with the cost of assignment computed as a weighted sum given by the binary crossentropy loss and the dice loss between the predicted and ground truth parts. This loss is the same as the one used to train the part segmentation:

{\cal L}_{\text{partlabels}}=w_{\text{BCE}}{\cal L}\>_{\text{BCE}}+w_{\text{Dice}}{\cal L}\>_{\text{Dice}}\>,

(6)

where
w_{\text{BCE}}=2
and
w_{\text{Dice}}=1
.

To predict the joint types, we use the cross-entropy loss:

{\cal L}_{\text{jointtypes}}=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log\frac{\exp(z_{i,y_{i}})}{\sum_{c=1}^{3}\exp(z_{i,c})}\>,

(7)

where
y_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
represents the ground-truth joint type for part
i
, in which 0, 1, 2 corresponds to revolute, prismatic, and static joint, respectively.

For predicting the axis of rotation, we need to predict a 3D line, which we parameterize by a 3D point (usually called ’pivot point’ in this context) and a direction vector. The translation axis is parametrized by a direction vector only. To learn predicting the direction vector, we use the geodesic loss on the rotation between the predicted and ground-truth direction:

{\cal L}_{\text{axes}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i}\text{arccos}(\;\text{clamp}(\hat{a}_{i}.a_{i},-1,1)\;)\>,

(8)

where
a_{i}
is the ground truth direction vector for part
i
, and
\hat{a}_{i}
the predicted direction vector.

Pivot points can be predicted anywhere along the line. We thus use point-to-line distance between the predicted pivot point and the line defined by the “ground truth” pivot point and the ground truth direction vector of the axis:

{\cal L}_{\text{pivots}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}||a\times(\ \hat{p}_{i}-p_{i})||\>,

(9)

where
\hat{p}_{i}
and
p_{i}
represent predicted and ground-truth pivot points, respectively.

For the motion amount prediction, we use the L1 loss:

{\cal L}_{\text{motionamounts}}=\frac{1}{MT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{M}|\hat{m}_{i,t}-m_{i,t}|\>,

(10)

where
m_{i,t}
is the ground truth amount of motion for part
i
at time
t
, which can be a translation in centimeters or a rotation in degrees, and
\hat{m}_{i,t}
the corresponding predicted value.

The final loss is simply the sum of these losses, without weights:

{\cal L}_{\text{jointtypes}}+{\cal L}_{\text{axes}}+{\cal L}_{\text{pivots}}+{\cal L}_{\text{partlabels}}+{\cal L}_{\text{motionamounts}}\>.

(11)

As mentioned earlier, many existing approaches require multi-view captures or object scanning under different configurations. Instead, our method takes as input a single monocular video captured with a freely moving camera.
1
proposed a method that takes the same input, but did not release the code. Comparisons with previous methods is therefore not easy. We compare here our method to GAMMA
41
, Reart
21
, and Articulate-Anything
17
, which input and output are the closest to ours.

GAMMA is a feed-forward method taking as input a point cloud of an articulated object at a single articulation state and predicts per-point part labels along with joint parameters. We retrained GAMMA on our point cloud sequences, and during inference, the final joint parameters are obtained by averaging the per-timestep joint predictions across the sequence.

Reart and Articulate-Anything are optimization-based methods. Reart is the most comparable recent method to ours: They predict part labels for the point set at the chosen canonical timestep along with joint parameters from the input point cloud sequence. Finally, Articulate-Anything is a state-of-the-art method predicting joint parameters for the object part meshes retrieved from a mesh database given an input video. As Articulate-Anything is designed to predict joint parameters for single-part object videos, we restrict its comparison to single-part objects.

We implemented our method using PyTorch
27
. We trained and tested our model on a single A100 GPU.

4.1
Datasets

For evaluation and comparison, we created two datasets.

Synthetic dataset.

We created a first dataset made of videos of objects from the PartNet-Mobility dataset
22
. We chose 12 representative categories, which include various configurations of articulations. Each object has one or multiple revolute and/or prismatic joints. In total, the dataset contains 416 object instances, which are split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 70%, 15%, and 15%, respectively. RGB-D sequences were rendered from a monocular camera freely moving around each object in a PyBullet

environment. The point cloud sequences were then obtained by back-projecting
N=2048

For large objects, such as storage furniture, the camera trajectory was kept short to capture only the front side of the object. For smaller objects, however, the camera trajectory spans a larger range to cover more views. To better simulate human motion, we slightly tilt the camera around its viewing axis. The camera begins from random poses sampled on the upper hemisphere of the object and follows a circular, arc-like trajectory of varying length and direction.

Real-world dataset.

We created a dataset by capturing videos of a box, laptop, stapler, eyeglasses, and a cabinet with an iPhone camera. A human operator is interacting with the articulated object while another person is capturing the video by moving around the object. We then process the videos with ViPE

Additionally, we obtain the background masks with SAM2.

4.2
Metrics

We evaluate part segmentation prediction using the widely adopted mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) metric. The axis-angle prediction is assessed by the sign-agnostic axis-angle error (in degrees) between the predicted and ground-truth joint axes, following the evaluation protocol of GAMMA and Articulate-Anything. The axis position (pivot point) error (in centimeters) is computed as the point-to-line distance between predicted and ground-truth axes.

Additionally, we report ’part rotation’ and ’part translation’ errors, the motion’s magnitude prediction errors per frame averaged over the sequence. Those errors correspond to the geodesic distance for revolute joints and the Euclidean distance for prismatic joints. Finally, we report joint type classification accuracy (in percents), corresponding to the proportion of correctly predicted joint types.

5
Conclusion

We presented a method for estimating the joint parameters, part decomposition, and motion magnitudes of an articulated object at every time step from a single input video. Our approach is both robust and accurate across a variety of challenging scenarios. Notably, our method relies exclusively on synthetic data for training. This is particularly valuable, as collecting and annotating real-world data for this problem is extremely time-consuming and often impractical. Synthetic data, by contrast, can be produced in large quantities. As a result, our framework can be scaled easily, and further expanding the synthetic training set should lead to even stronger performance and broader generalization.
