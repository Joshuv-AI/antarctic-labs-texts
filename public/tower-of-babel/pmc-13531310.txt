Multi-omics causal inference of childhood asthma triggered by ambient particulate matter
Authors: Hua Li, Xiaotao Ren, Xiaoping Lei, Yi Li, Wenbin Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe causal impact of fine particulate matter (PM2.5), an established environmental risk factor, on childhood asthma and its biological mechanisms remain to be elucidated. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the causal association between PM2.5 and childhood asthma and to dissect the mediating role of plasma proteins through a multi-omics integrated Mendelian randomisation (MR) framework.MethodsTwo-sample MR was performed on large-scale genome-wide association data to estimate the causal effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma. Genes commonly associated with PM2.5 and childhood asthma were screened by transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) and subjected to enrichment analyses and MR. Mediator proteins were identified by two-step MR. Potential adverse effects were scanned by phenome-wide MR (Phe-MR).ResultsMR revealed a significant positive causal effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma (OR=1.897, 95% CI: 1.063–3.388, p=0.030). TWAS highlighted 70 genes co-expressed in PM2.5 and childhood asthma that were enriched in inflammatory pathways such as lysosome- and leukocyte-mediated immunity. MEAF6 was validated as a protective gene and RNF40 as a risk gene for childhood asthma. Two-step MR identified FUT10 as a positive mediator mediating 19.3% of the causal effect, and CD200 and MANBA as negative mediator proteins. Phe-MR indicated the association of these genes and proteins with multiple other diseases, implying possible adverse effects from therapeutic intervention.ConclusionLong-term PM2.5 exposure is causally linked to childhood asthma with MEAF6, RNF40, CD200, MANBA and FUT10 identified as key molecules. The study provides new evidence for the biological mechanisms linking PM2.5 to childhood asthma.

Introduction

Asthma is the most common chronic respiratory disease in childhood, characterised by airway inflammation, bronchial hyperresponsiveness and limited reversible airflow [1]. In 2021, 95.7 million children worldwide were affected, corresponding to an age-standardised prevalence of 4758 per 100 000 [2]. Although slowly declining annually, the incidence of childhood asthma rises in regions with high socio-demographic indices [3]. Notably, inadequate management of childhood asthma can trigger airway remodelling that changes the airway structure, leading to arrested lung-function growth and elevated risk of COPD [4, 5]. These long-term consequences underscore the need for early diagnosis and intervention.

Childhood is a critical window for lung and immune system development, during which the respiratory tract is exquisitely sensitive to environmental exposures [6]. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a major driver of childhood asthma. Childhood asthma risk rises for 3% for every 1 µg·m−3 increment in exposure to PM2.5 [7]. PM2.5 exposure raises blood concentrations of interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor-α and other inflammatory markers [8]. Plasma proteins initiate and sustain airway inflammation, indicating their role in forming a molecular bridge between PM2.5 and childhood asthma. Current multi-omics and genetic epidemiological studies have focused on adult or mixed populations, neglecting the special developmental stage of childhood and has not systematically identified the causal molecular mediators.

This study innovatively integrates multi-omics and Mendelian randomisation (MR) to evaluate the causal effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma and to quantify the mediation of plasma proteins. Using proteome-wide two-step MR and mediation analysis, we confirmed that PM2.5 causally increases childhood asthma risk and identified prioritised, druggable plasma protein targets. The approach built a strong bridge linking environmental epidemiology, causal inference methodology and targeted prevention strategies. The findings inform early prevention, targeted intervention and therapeutic strategies for childhood asthma.

Methods

Study design

In accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology-MR guidelines [9], our MR estimates relied on three assumptions: 1) Relevance: the instrumental variables (IVs) are strongly associated with the exposure; 2) Independence: the IVs are independent of confounders; and 3) Exclusion: the IVs influence the outcome only through the exposure, without direct impact on the outcome.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) data sources

The GWAS PM2.5 exposure data were sourced from OpenGWAS (ID: ukb-b-10817) (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The UK Biobank (UKB) provided the data, which were collected under the framework of the European Study of Cohorts for Air Pollution Effects (ESCAPE). ESCAPE estimated PM2.5 concentrations of the residential areas of 423 796 participants of European ancestry using land use regression models [10].

The PM2.5 exposure data were sourced from OpenGWAS (ID: ukb-b-24006), originating from UKB (field ID 24006). PM2.5 effect size reflects a per-standard-deviation increase in exposure, rather than a per 1 µg·m−3 increase, due to the standardisation applied by the PHESANT pipeline. According to the original UKB data, 1 sd of PM2.5 equates to ∼1.06 µg·m−3.

The GWAS outcome data of childhood asthma were sourced from the FinnGen database, R12 release (https://www.finngen.fi/en), comprising 268 241 samples (8402 childhood asthma). Asthma was defined using codes J45 and J46 in the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision. The diagnosis of childhood asthma was restricted to participants under 16 years of age [11].

The expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data were downloaded from the eQTLGen consortium that includes a meta-analysis of whole-blood gene expression in 31 684 adults, covering 19 942 genes [12].

Plasma proteome pQTL data were sourced from the SomaScan platform, comprising 4907 plasma proteins identified in 35 559 Icelanders [13]. GWAS data are provided in table 1. The projects were conducted by independent research teams under distinct recruitment frameworks. No systematic sample sharing or recruitment overlap was reported.

IV selection and MR analysis

IV selection criteria were designed to minimise potential biases from reverse causality and pleiotropy: 1) single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) must meet the genome-wide significance threshold, p<5×10−8; 2) SNPs must be in linkage equilibrium (r2=0.001, 10 000 kb); 3) exposure- and outcome-SNP alleles were harmonised to align effect directions, as effect alleles may be opposite between exposure and outcome; 4) IVs with potential reverse causality were removed by Steiger filtering; 5) SNPs must have an F-statistic >10 to avoid weak instrument bias; 6) SNPs with horizontal pleiotropy were removed by MR-PRESSO (pleiotropy residual sum and outlier) and MR-Egger regression; 7) exposures with fewer than three valid IVs were removed to ensure reliable results.

Five MR methods were employed: 1) random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW), assuming that all IVs are valid and directional pleiotropy is absent, exhibits the highest statistical efficiency and power; 2) weighted median provides valid estimates even when up to 50% of the information originates from invalid IVs; 3) MR-Egger regression yields a slope representing the causal effect estimate and is robust to directional pleiotropy from invalid IVs; 4) weighted mode clusters SNPs into subsets based on the similarity of their causal effect estimates and estimates the causal effect from the subset containing the largest number of SNPs; 5) simple mode, based on a more fundamental hypothesis, avoids over reliance on weighting or modelling assumptions to enhance the integrity of sensitivity analyses. An association was considered statistically significant when the IVW-derived p-value was <0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of MR findings. When >two SNPs were available in the IV, Cochran's Q was computed to evaluate heterogeneity. If heterogeneity was significant, estimates were adjusted with the random-effects IVW model. Estimates with horizontal pleiotropy were removed with MR-Egger regression and the MR-PRESSO global test when the MR-Egger intercept is significant (p<0.05). Reverse causality was avoided with MR Steiger and reverse MR. Leave-one-out analyses were further performed to evaluate the influence of individual SNPs on the estimates. Under the transcriptome- or proteome-wide MR screening framework, multiple hypothesis testing elevated risks of false positives. To control the false discovery rate (FDR) from multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR <0.05), maintaining statistical power while ensuring robust causal inference. The exposure and outcome GWAS data were standardised using the “harmonise_data” function (action=2) to ensure consistent effect orientation and resolve allele mismatches. Briefly: 1) the exposure and outcome SNPs were aligned to maintain consistent effect alleles; 2) palindromic SNPs (A/T and C/G) were removed when the minor allele frequency was close to 0.5, i.e. when strand direction could not be reliably determined to avoid orientation errors; 3) the effect direction of the outcome was reversed by multiplying β by −1, when alleles between exposure and outcome were complementary (e.g. A/G versus C/T) and the direction could be inferred from allele frequencies, thereby preserving comparability of effect estimates; and 4) SNPs that could not be harmonised were excluded, including those present in only one dataset or whose allele information was insufficient to determine orientation. All harmonised SNPs used the same effect allele across exposure and outcome GWAS, ensuring consistent causal direction and enhancing MR reliability.

Colocalisation analysis

Genetic colocalisation analysis was employed using the “coloc” R package (v5.2.3) to test whether the identified genes/proteins and childhood asthma share causal variants. Based on a Bayesian model, the colocalisation analysis provides the posterior probability of the hypothesis (PPH) regarding the association of two traits in one area. Five hypotheses were considered: H0: no association with either trait; H1: association only with trait 1; H2: association only with trait 2; H3: two distinct causal variants; H4: a single shared causal variant [14]. To capture as many local regulatory variants related to the target protein or gene as possible, SNPs within 50 kb upstream and 100 kb downstream of the corresponding gene locus were extracted for colocalisation calculation. Evidence was evaluated based on the PPH4 for H4: PPH4 >0.8 was considered strong evidence of colocalisation, while 0.5<PPH4≤0.8 was interpreted as weak evidence.

Transcriptome-wide association study

Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) analysis was performed using the FUSION tool for the PM2.5–childhood asthma association at the transcriptomic level. Whole-blood eQTL data of European individuals were obtained from the GTEx Consortium v8 (https://gtexportal.org/) [15]. SNP-gene expression effects were estimated using a reference transcriptome. The optimal expression weight was selected for each gene based on top1, BLUP, lasso, enet and BSLMM. GWAS Z-scores for PM2.5 and childhood asthma were integrated with the expression weights to compute the gene-level TWAS statistics to screen for genes associated with susceptibility to PM2.5 exposure or childhood asthma. A PTWAS <0.05 indicates a suggestively associated gene. An FDRTWAS <0.05 indicates a significant gene.

Functional enrichment and protein–protein interaction

To investigate the biological mechanisms underlying PM2.5 and childhood asthma co-expressed genes, gene ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package (p<0.05). Using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), high-confidence interactions (interaction score ≥0.4) were selected to construct the protein–protein interaction (PPI network), revealing potential cooperative patterns among genes in the pathogenesis of childhood asthma [16].

Mediation analysis

A two-step MR mediation analysis was conducted at the proteomic level to identify key plasma proteins mediating the PM2.5. First, the causal effect of PM2.5 on plasma proteins was evaluated, excluding those with significant horizontal pleiotropy. Second, the causal relationship between these proteins and childhood asthma was examined. The total effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma was denoted as β. The effect of PM2.5 on a protein was denoted as β1. The effect of that protein on childhood asthma was denoted as β2. Mediation effect was quantified as β1×β2, with 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated via the delta method. Each protein's contribution is reported as (β1×β2)/β [17].

Phenome-wide MR (Phe-MR)

To comprehensively evaluate potential adverse effects of genes and proteins related to childhood asthma, a Phe-MR analysis was performed to quantify their causal associations with a spectrum of diseases. GWAS data were derived from the UKB, encompassing 1403 disease phenotypes in 408 961 white British participants. Diseases were categorised with the PheCodes system [18]. 784 phenotypes with >500 cases were retained for analysis (supplementary table S3). Phe-MR revealed significant causal associations of several genes and plasma proteins with diseases in 17 disease categories (p<0.05). Each 1 sd increase in their expression alters the risk of specific diseases. Data are available at: https://www.leelabsg.org/resources

Results

Genetic association between PM2.5 and childhood asthma

We conducted strict quality control and applied the F-statistic range (30 to 70) to filter IVs (supplementary table S1). IVW MR indicated a significant positive causal effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma (OR=1.897, 95% CI: 1.063–3.388, p=0.030). All complementary MR methods showed directionally consistent estimates (figure 2a,b). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness and reliability of the MR findings (table 2, figure 2c).

TWAS, functional enrichment and MR validation of co-expressed genes

Using whole-blood transcriptomic data, TWAS by the FUSION method identified 731 genes related to PM2.5 (pTWAS<0.05) (figure 3a, supplementary table S2), and 790 genes related to childhood asthma (pTWAS<0.05), among which 73 had FDR <0.05 (figure 3b, supplementary table S3). Overlapping the two gene sets generated 70 genes co-expressed by PM2.5 and childhood asthma (figure 3c). GO analysis demonstrated their predominant involvement in blood coagulation regulation, positive regulation of adipocyte differentiation and leukocyte-mediated immunity (figure 3d). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted cofactor biosynthesis, ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis, and lysosomal pathways (figure 3e). The interaction network of the co-expressed genes identified MEAF6 and KAT8 as hub nodes (figure 3f), suggesting their central roles in the PM2.5–childhood asthma association.

To assess causality between the 70 co-expressed genes and childhood asthma, two-sample MR was applied. The detailed MR procedure is listed in supplementary figure S1. Genetic association between plasma proteins and childhood asthma and mediation analysis

MR was performed between the 410 proteins and childhood asthma, with the workflow given in supplementary figure S3. Valid plasma protein-related IVs (SNPs >2) are shown in supplementary table S7, with IV intensity ranging between 29 and 4273. IVW estimates revealed 18 plasma proteins causally associated with childhood asthma risk (p<0.05; figure 4b, supplementary table S8). 12 of them showed inverse correlation, while the other six showed a positive correlation (supplementary figure S4). After Benjamini–Hochberg correction, CD200 (OR=0.546, 95% CI: 0.417–0.715, p=1.14E-05, FDR=0.001) and MANBA (OR=0.832, 95% CI: 0.763–0.907, p=3.05E-05, FDR=0.002) remained negatively correlated with childhood asthma: FUT10 remained positively correlated (OR=1.236, 95% CI: 1.126–1.357, p=8.92E-06, FDR=0.001). Other analyses showed a consistent direction (figure 4c). A stricter Bonferroni correction was applied: significance was declared when p<0.05 was divided by the number of protein–childhood asthma MR tests (i.e., p<0.05/201=2.5E-04). The associations of CD200, MANBA and FUT10 with childhood asthma remained significant. Sensitive analyses detected no pleiotropy or heterogeneity, confirming robust and reliable MR results (supplementary table S9).

Colocalisation analysis provided strong evidence (PPH4>0.8) for CD200 and MANBA (supplementary table S10). Leave-one-out analysis confirmed a robust association of the three PM2.5-related genes with childhood asthma (supplementary figure S5). Reverse MR found no evidence of reverse causality (supplementary figure S6).

Mediation analysis showed that FUT10 alone mediated 19.3% (95% CI: −0.9–39.5%) of the total causal effect of PM2.5 on childhood asthma. Based on 5000 iterations of parametric bootstrap, the resulting 95% CI (1.4–41.5%) aligned with the delta method estimate (18.2%), confirming the robustness of the mediation estimate (table 4). Multivariable MR (MVMR) analysis, adjusted for PM2.5 exposure, indicated an independent effect of FUT10 protein on childhood asthma (supplementary table S11). The estimated mediation proportion was 10.1% (−2.06–22.2%) using the product-of-coefficients method and 9.2% (0–24.7%) via bootstrap, both supporting a consistent mediation pattern (supplementary table S12). The two-step MR estimate (19%) differed from the MVMR result (10%). This divergence was expected because the two-step approach does not adjust for correlation between the exposure and the mediator, potentially overestimating the mediated effect. MVMR simultaneously incorporates the exposure and the mediator, estimating a more robust and conservative conditional effect. Therefore, despite the numerical discrepancy, both methods suggest a mediating role of FUT10 in the PM2.5–childhood asthma association, thereby enhancing the credibility of this mediation effect.

Phe-MR findings

Supplementary table S13 summarises the Phe-MR results for plasma proteins, CD200, MANBA and FUT10, and the key genes, MEAF6 and RNF40, against 784 disease phenotypes. After correction, the proteins and genes were significantly associated with 28 different diseases or traits (p<0.003) (supplementary figure S7). CD200 positively correlated with digestive system diseases, MANBA with anxiety disorders and MEAF6 with selected ocular conditions, including aphakia and other lens disorders. The effect direction of proteins and genes on asthma in the Phe-MR analysis mirrored the primary MR estimates (supplementary figure S7), strengthening the reliability of our causal inferences.

Discussion

Our multi-omics MR analyses reveal that PM2.5 exposure causally increases childhood asthma risk and plasma proteins potentially mediate this effect. TWAS analysis identified MEAF6 as a protective gene and RNF40 as a risk gene for childhood asthma. Beyond the epidemiological association between PM2.5 and asthma, genetic and multi-omics analyses were employed to identify the mediating roles of MEAF6, RNF40 and the plasma protein FUT10 in PM2.5 influencing childhood asthma. Notably, FUT10 mediated ∼20% of the PM2.5 effect. Glycosylation modifications may represent a crucial mechanistic node and a potential therapeutic target.

The effect size of PM2.5 on childhood asthma risk in our study (OR=1.897) is slightly larger than that reported in traditional observational studies [19]. The developing respiratory system in children, undergoing alveolarisation and immune maturation, is highly sensitive to toxic components of PM2.5 such as ultrafine particles, metals and persistent organic pollutants [20]. Long-term exposure may act through multiple synergistic pathways, including oxidative stress, epigenetic modifications and immune dysregulation. This may lead to sustained airway inflammation and structural remodelling, amplifying disease risk. Another MR study reported a relatively strong association between PM2.5 and asthma (OR=1.5280) [21], lending external support to our results. Nevertheless, the effect estimate warrants cautious interpretation. Future replication in larger and more diverse populations with optimised IVs is needed.

Our multi-omics analysis revealed the enrichment of PM2.5 and childhood asthma co-expressed genes in inflammatory pathways such as lysosome- and leukocyte-mediated immunity, which may help us understand the pathophysiology of childhood asthma. PM2.5 particles can reduce lysosomal acid stability, injuring airway epithelial cells [22]. Moreover, PM2.5 regulates reactive oxygen species production through neutrophil endocytosis, triggering or exacerbating inflammatory responses leading to bronchial epithelial cell apoptosis [23].

We identified MEAF6 as a protective gene and RNF40 as a risk gene for childhood asthma. MEAF6, a core component of the MYST–family histone acetyl–transferase complex, governs chromatin remodelling and gene transcription [24]. Histone acetylation regulates endothelial function, inflammation, oxidative stress and barrier integrity [25]. Loss of MEAF6 could impair these adaptive responses through histone acetylation. Essential for cell proliferation, activation of MEAF6 may benefit the regeneration of airway epithelial cells, thereby preventing the invasion of pathogens [26]. Histone acetylation also participates in airway inflammation and asthma pathogenesis, including regulating the transcription of Th2/Th17 inflammatory genes and airway remodelling [27, 28]. The involvement of MEAF6 in asthma-related epigenetic regulation thus has clear biological plausibility.

RNF40 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that catalyses monoubiquitination of H2Bub1 [29]. The RNF20/RNF40-mediated H2Bub1 is required for efficient transcriptional elongation and full activation of nuclear factor-κB-dependent inflammatory genes [30]. Loss of RNF40 can significantly impair cytokine production during immune responses [31]. We hypothesise that PM2.5 exposure may alter chromatin states and amplify inflammatory transcriptional programmes triggered by environmental factors via genetic or epigenetic regulation of RNF40, thereby driving childhood asthma. However, direct experimental evidence confirming this role of RNF40 is lacking, necessitating further validation in asthmatic airway epithelial cells or animal models.

FUT10, a key enzyme in the O-fucosylation of glycoproteins expressed in immune and epithelial cells, critically regulates inflammatory and immune responses [32]. Abnormal expression of FUT family members in airway epithelial cells is closely related to inflammatory regulation. For example, high expression of FUT2 in 16HBE bronchial epithelial cells promotes inflammatory factor release and apoptosis [33]. Thus, FUT2 may amplify airway inflammation, disrupt mucosal barrier function, and promote tissue remodelling to mediate airway inflammatory responses in PM2.5-induced childhood asthma. However, our findings on FUT10 reflect associations within genetic–environmental pathways and are not yet translatable to therapeutic applications.

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on European-based data limits generalisability to other populations. Genetic, environmental and behavioural differences across populations may alter these associations, warranting validation in diverse ethnic groups. Second, the modest PPH value between FUT10 and childhood asthma colocalisation may weaken the causal inference. Third, although horizontal pleiotropy was mitigated through sensitivity analyses, residual pleiotropy may introduce bias. Finally, our conclusions, grounded in MR assumptions that remain unverifiable in practice, should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

Long-term PM2.5 exposure is a causal risk factor for childhood asthma. MEAF6, RNF40, CD200, MANBA and FUT10 are key mediators across the transcriptomics and plasma proteomics. These findings illuminate the mechanisms underlying PM2.5-driven childhood asthma. Phe-MR signals potential adverse effects of key molecules. Overall, we lay a scientific foundation for early risk prediction, targeted intervention and safety evaluation of future therapies in childhood asthma.
