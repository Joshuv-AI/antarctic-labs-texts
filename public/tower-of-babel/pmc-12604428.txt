Advances in biomedical applications of Se-based nanozymes
Authors: Yanping Feng, JinYi Chen, Congqin Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelenium (Se) is an essential trace element for the human body, serving as a component of several natural enzymes and playing a crucial role in a variety of biological functions. However, the extraction of natural selenoenzymes presents significant challenges, including high cost and instability. To address this challenge, researchers have initiated investigations into the potential of combining Se with nanotechnology. This approach involves the development of Se-based nanomaterials, including monomeric Se, Se dioxide, and molybdenum selenide, with the objective of replacing natural selenoenzymes. The objective of this review is to present a comprehensive overview of the recent advancements in Se-based nanozymes and to elucidate their potential for biomedical applications. This review commences with an examination of the typical enzyme-mimicking activities (e.g., peroxidase-POD, glutathione peroxidase-GPx, superoxide dismutase-SOD, catalase-CAT) that have been achieved by Se-based nanomaterials. Secondly, we examined the regulatory mechanisms that govern the activities of Se-based nanozymes. Subsequently, a synthesis of their applications in various diseases facilitated a more comprehensive understanding of the function of Se-based nanozymes and their potential therapeutic effects. Finally, an outlook on the future development of Se-based nanozymes was presented, including their potential for clinical applications and technical challenges that need to be addressed. The synthesis of these research advances is intended to provide a clearer perspective and direction for the application of Se-based nanozymes in biomedicine.Graphical abstract Keywords: Nanozyme, Se, Biomedicine, Catalysis, Enzyme activity

Introduction

Natural enzymes are biocatalysts that participate in chemical reactions in living organisms with specificity and high efficiency. The catalytic activities of enzymes are challenging to replicate with synthetic catalysts. These synthetic catalysts often exhibit structural instability, sensitivity to the environment, and high costs and difficulties in recycling, which significantly impede their development and application [1]. In light of the rapid advancement of nanotechnology, researchers are exploring the potential of synthesizing artificial enzymes through nanotechnology to substitute for natural enzymes. Nanomaterials possess distinctive physical and chemical attributes, including optics, magnetism, and electricity. The integration of enzyme catalytic efficacy and nanotechnology enables the development of artificial enzymes with enhanced functionalities [2]. Moreover, these synthetic catalysts exhibit superior catalytic activity compared to their natural counterparts [3].

In 2007, the group of Xiyun Yan made the initial discovery that ferrosoferric oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4 NPs) exhibited POD-like activity [4]. Since then, the term “nanozymes” has been used to describe artificial enzyme mimics. The verification of enzyme-like activities was extended to a number of other nanomaterials, including metals [5], metal oxides [6], monatomic substances [7], sulfides [8] and carbon-based materials [9]. Nanozymes have been demonstrated to be capable of replacing natural enzymes, offering several advantages including high stability, facile preparation and modification, low cost, and tunable catalytic activity. Consequently, they have been widely employed in a range of applications, including those pertaining to detection and diagnostics [10], antitumor agents [11] and antimicrobial compounds [12].

Selenium (Se) is a vital trace element, and Se deficiency can result in a range of health issues, which include anemia in patients with heart failure [13], degenerative diseases of the heart and skeletal muscles [14] and other conditions. Se is a widely utilized potential nutrient in nutritional supplements that can prevent a multitude of ailments, including cardiovascular diseases [15], viral infections [16], immune system dysfunction [17], neurological loss [18] and cancer [19], among others. The majority of these physiological functions are achieved through the regulation of selenoproteins, which serve as the fundamental components of numerous selenoenzymes [20]. Selenoenzymes perform a variety of essential biological functions within the body, including the regulation of growth, development, immune function, and antioxidant defense [21]. For example, phospholipid hydroperoxide glutathione peroxidase (PHG-PX) exists as a monomer with selenocysteine (Se-Cys) as the active center [22].

Based on the structural features of selenoenzymes, researchers have synthesized Se-based nanozymes to modulate and enhance their Se-based nanozymes activity by complexing or modifying them with different forms of nanomaterials. The Se-based nanozymes have small particle size and large specific surface area, showing high biological activity. Compared with the traditional inorganic Se compounds, Se-based nanomaterials have lower toxicity and good biocompatibility, which exist stably in the organism and play a certain biological function, and is eventually metabolized and excreted by the human body. It has been demonstrated that Se-based nanozymes have considerable potential for application in biomedical fields, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer therapies [23–25]. Compared to natural selenoenzymes, synthesized Se-based nanozymes differ significantly in several key aspects. In terms of catalytic efficiency, while natural selenoenzymes exhibit high specificity and efficiency under physiological conditions due to their precise active sites (e.g., selenocysteine in glutathione peroxidase). Most artificially synthesized Se-based nanozymes exhibit lower catalytic activity than natural selenoenzymes, while semi-synthetic Se-based nanozymes where the Se content and precise positioning at the active site play a crucial role in improving catalytic efficiency display activity levels close to those of natural selenoenzymes [26]. Regarding stability, natural selenoenzymes are often sensitive to environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and proteases, leading to easy denaturation and loss of activity [27, 28], whereas Se-based nanozymes, composed of nanomaterials, demonstrate excellent stability under harsh conditions, including extreme temperatures and pH values, as well as resistance to enzymatic degradation [29, 30]. In terms of production cost, natural selenoenzymes typically require complex extraction and purification processes from biological sources, resulting in high costs and limited scalability, while Se-based nanozymes can be synthesized through chemical or physical methods with controlled preparation, lower production costs, and easier large-scale manufacturing [31, 32].

This review critically summarized the latest advances in Se-based nanozymes and their applications in biomedical fields, while also exploring the key limitations and unresolved challenges of their applications. Firstly, the types of enzymes mimicked by Se-based nanomaterials (Fig. 2) were introduced, including POD, CAT, GPx, and SOD. The enzyme-mimicking ability, activity regulation, and selectivity were reviewed, and their inherent drawbacks were analyzed, such as potential toxicity and insufficient in vivo stability under physiological conditions. Subsequently, their therapeutic applications, including antibacterial, antitumor, antidiabetic, and anti-neuroinflammatory effects, as well as diagnostic/biosensing functionalities were emphasized, and these prospects were intentionally contrasted with existing translational barriers, including off-target effects in complex biological systems and the lack of standardized activity evaluation protocols. Finally, this review highlighted key application gaps, particularly long-term biosafety concerns, which must be addressed prior to clinical translation. By presenting the potential applications and fundamental limitations of Se-based nanozymes, this review aimed to provide a balanced perspective to stimulate targeted research to address these dual aspects.

Synthesis of Se-based nanozymes

Synthesis of se modified with polymer compounds

Natural selenoenzymes are highly efficient biocatalysts in the human body, exhibiting extremely high enzymatic activity and participating in various biological processes. However, the extraction of selenoenzymes is complex, costly, and unstable. Although nano-Se can mimic the effects of selenoenzymes, it still falls short under conditions requiring high enzymatic activity. Therefore, researchers have combined Se elements with polymeric compounds to enhance enzymatic activity. During the synthesis of Se-modified polymers, researchers meticulously select polymers with specific functional groups as carriers, which can undergo chemical reactions with Se elements to firmly bind them to the polymer chains [33]. This binding method not only retains the biological activity of Se elements but also endows the polymers with new properties, such as improved biocompatibility, higher stability, and stronger enzyme-binding capacity [34].

In the synthesis process of Se nanoparticles (Se NPs), polymeric compounds such as sodium alginate (SA), chitosan (CS), bovine serum albumin (BSA), tannin (TA) or polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) are introduced. The incorporation of these polymers can alter the morphology, size, stability, and specific surface area of Se NPs, thereby further influencing their enzymatic activity. The majority of synthesized Se NPs exhibited a spherical morphology, and their size significantly influenced their biological activity, with smaller particles demonstrating higher bioactivity. This relationship suggests that reducing the size of Se NPs can enhance their effectiveness in biological applications. Polymeric compounds such as CS, SA, BSA, TA and PVP can interact with Se NPs to enhance their stability. These interactions primarily involve hydrogen bonds formed between the hydroxyl groups of the polymers and Se, which contribute to the stabilization of Se NPs. Additionally, these polymeric compounds can uniformly disperse Se species through hydrogen bonds, facilitating the formation of stable Se NPs [50]. Furthermore, the protective effect of the polymer chains significantly reduces enzyme activity loss under extreme conditions (such as high temperatures, strong acids, and strong bases), thereby broadening the application scope of the enzyme. These polymeric compounds themselves possess good biocompatibility, thus reducing the toxicity of Se NPs [51]. Moreover, they impart unique charges and specific surface areas to Se NPs, providing more active sites and further enhancing their enzymatic activity [52]. Notably, the advantage of their combination lies in their unique structure and properties, which can significantly affect enzymatic activity. On one hand, the Se elements in Se-modified polymers can interact with the active sites in the enzyme, altering its conformation and microenvironment, thereby promoting enzyme-substrate binding and catalytic reactions. On the other hand, the introduction of polymer chains can increase the enzyme’s surface area and charge distribution, improving the chances of enzyme-substrate contact and reaction rates [53]. In summary, polymer-modified Se demonstrates significant advantages and potential in enhancing enzymatic activity. Through rational synthetic design and precise condition control, Se NPs with high catalytic performance and good stability can be prepared.

However, there are also some problems in the modification of Se-based nanozymes by polymer compounds. For example, Wu et al. [54] pointed out that defect engineering mainly affected the activity of nanozyme by changing the number and structure of active sites, while polymer modification might change the surface structure and properties of nanozyme to a certain extent, thus affecting the exposure of its active sites and the combination with substrates, resulting in decreased catalytic activity. Wang et al. [55] found that the selectivity and specificity of nanozymes were sometimes worse than those of natural enzymes, and even polymer-modified nanozymes might not achieve the high selectivity and specificity of natural enzymes. In complex biological systems, polymer modification may have a certain catalytic effect on non-target substrates, thus affecting its application in specific biomedicine. In addition, in terms of biological safety, although many polymer compounds have good biocompatibility, the long-term metabolic process and potential effects of polymer-modified nanozyme in organisms still need further study. Hühn et al. [56] indicated that polymer coating wound affect the interaction between nanoparticles and biomolecules, and the protein crown formed might change the biocompatibility and toxicity of nanoparticles, thus having adverse effects on cells.

Collectively, these results indicate that the stability, dispersibility, and enzymatic activity of Se NPs can be enhanced through polymer modification, while toxicity is reduced and the range of applications is expanded. On the other hand, their surface structure and exposure of active sites may be altered, leading to a decrease in catalytic activity, and their selectivity and specificity still fall short of those of natural enzymes, posing a risk of off-target effects, with the long-term biosafety and metabolic impacts requiring further in-depth study.

This table demonstrated that the complexation of Se with different metals (Cu, Fe, Mo, Ti, Mn, Zn, etc.) could significantly alter the chemical composition, morphology, and size of Se NPs, endowing them with unique physicochemical properties. These Se-based nanozymes not only retained the inherent bioactivity of Se but also acquired enhanced functionalities by addition of metals, including improved photothermal conversion capabilities, nuclear magnetic resonance effects, electron transfer capacities, and multi-enzyme mimicking activities. Notably, the enhancement of enzymatic activity through optimized electron transfer mechanisms had been comprehensively validated. The formation of stable chemical bonds between metals and Se, ensuring the long-term structural integrity and catalytic performance of these nanozymes in biological systems or complex environments.

Although metal modification can endow Se-based nanozymes with new functions and significantly improve its catalytic activity, it is accompanied by some shortcomings that cannot be ignored. For example, Gao et al. [71] found that SnSe nanosheets could simulate the key dehydrogenase in cell metabolism interfering with the cycle of cell tricarboxylic acid, leading to cell death, which indicated that metal-modified Se-based nanozymes produced toxicity by affecting cell metabolism. Similarly, Wu et al. [72] pointed out that metal-doped graphene had OXD-like activity and induced cellular immune response. Wu et al. [73] observed that compared with the short-term exposure and repetitive exposure, rat lung macrophages exhibited the augmented oxidative stress, dysfunction of mitochondria, damage of DNA and disorder of calcium homeostasis under the long-term NiSe2/rGO exposure. Derfus et al. [74] reported that cytotoxicity correlateed with the liberation of free Cd2+ ions due to deterioration of the CdSe lattice. Although the proper coupling of Se and metal ions can reduce cytotoxicity [75, 76], there may still be potential risks when it is used in organisms, and the accumulation of metal ions in the body may cause irreversible damage to organs such as liver and kidney.

In summary, Se can enhance the activity of nanozymes by achieving greater electron capacity, larger specific surface area, and more active sites, which are achieved by optimizing the composition, morphology, and size of the metallic material. However, the defects associated with metal-modified nanozyme cannot be ignored. When using this method to improve the performance of Se-based nanozymes, it is necessary to give full play to its advantages and solve these problems at the same time.

The preparation of nanozymes obtained through Se and carbon-based material composites typically involves a series of complex chemical reactions. For instance, Se elements can be uniformly deposited on the surface or within the carbon-based materials to form Se-carbon composites through methods such as chemical vapor deposition, electrochemical deposition, or sol-gel processes [78]. Furthermore, the combination of Se and carbon-based materials can further enhance their bioactivity, stability, and targeting ability, which can be achieved by introducing different functional groups or biomolecules, thereby meeting the demand for multifunctional nanomaterials in the biomedical field [79]. Classification and catalytic mechanism of Se-based nanozymes

Classification of Se-based nanozymes

POD-like

The discovery of nanozymes can be traced back to 2007, when the research group of Xiyun Yan demonstrated that magnetic Fe₃O₄ NPs exhibited POD-like activity. In other words, the earliest studies of nanozymes commenced with an investigation of POD-like activity. The Fe₃O₄ NPs exhibit POD-like activity, whereby they catalyze the oxidation of a corresponding substrate in the presence of H₂O₂. This process entails the oxidation of the colorless reagent 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) to the blue oxidation state (oxTMB), as well as the oxidation of other substrates, including diaminobenzidine (DAB) and o-phenylenediamine (OPD) [87], which results in a color shift from colorless to brown and yellow.

Subsequently, in addition to iron-containing substrates [88], noble metals [89], MOF materials [90], carbon-based materials [91] and Se-based nanomaterials [92], further substrates have been demonstrated to possess POD-like activities. For instance, Se-based nanomaterials, including Cu2−xSe NPs, have garnered interest due to their noteworthy POD-like activity. The Cu2−xSe NPs have been demonstrated to oxidize H₂O₂ to ·OH and ·O2−. In the presence of TMB, effectively catalyzed the chromogenic substrate TMB, which was oxidized to blue oxTMB, exhibiting good POD-like activity (Fig. 3a-b). It was observed that the POD-like activity of Cu2−xSe NPs exhibited a tendency to increase with rising concentration (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, the POD-like activity was found to be closely related to pH (Fig. 3d) and temperature (Fig. 3e), indicating that the POD-like activity could be modified. It was observed that Cu2−xSe NPs demonstrated robust POD-like activity across a broad pH range (5–8) and temperature range (25–60 °C), surpassing the limitations imposed by traditional enzymes, which tend to gradually lose their activity at temperatures exceeding 37 °C [93]. To ascertain the nature of the radicals produced, this study employed fluorescence spectroscopy and ESR tests, which demonstrated the formation of ·OH and superoxide ·O2− (Fig. 3f-i) [94].

Furthermore, as evidenced by the studies on Se NPs [92], CdSe NPs [95] and mixed metal oxides (MMOs) containing Se vacancies [96], these materials were capable of oxidizing colorless TMB to blue oxTMB in the presence of H₂O₂ and exhibited notable POD-like activity in detecting free radicals, such as ·OH, through ESR tests.

GPx-like

GPx is a crucial antioxidant enzyme for cells. It relies on GSH to break down hydroperoxides (ROOH), thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage [97]. The active site of natural GPx contains selenocysteine (Sec), which is the key to own catalytic activity. During the GPx-catalyzed reaction, Sec undergoes a redox cycle in which it is first oxidized to selenohydrin (SeOH), and then reduced back to Sec by GSH. This process allows the Se atoms to exhibit a greater affinity for peroxides, enabling the reduction of peroxides (such as H₂O₂) faster and more efficient to water. These ultimately protect cells from oxidative stress. In order to emulate the functions of GPx, Zhang et al. [98] constructed Se-based nanomaterials with a structural configuration that enabled the replacement of natural GPx. The Se was attached to gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) to augment the catalytic velocity of Se, which exhibited GPx-like activity approximately 14 times greater than that of the free peptide. Moreover, Wang et al. [99] prepared nanocomposites with GPx-like activity (PDASeCys) by compositing selenocystine (SeCys, an oxidized dimer of Sec that required reduction to Sec prior to participating in biological functions) and PDA (Fig. 4a). The GPx-like activities were evaluated using the scavenging rates of stable DPPH· and ABTS+·. The percentage of Se content in the complexes also affected the magnitude of GPx-like activity, and the PDASeCys NPs exhibited the greatest free radical scavenging efficiency when DA/Se (PDA/Se) = 4:1 (Fig. 4b, c). In the presence of H2O2, PDASeCys NPs were observed to remove H2O2 and catalyze the oxidation of reduced GSH to GSSG (Fig. 4d). Meanwhile, it was demonstrated that both H₂O₂ and GSH were essential for this oxidation process (Fig. 4e-f).

Although natural GPx relies primarily on organic selenocysteine (Sec) for its antioxidant effects, it is not necessary to mimic the organic structure of GPx in order to achieve GPx-like activity. For instance, Ringuet et al. [100] demonstrated that inorganic Se-based nanozymes were also capable of exhibiting similar GPx functions. Some findings have indicated that dietary Se, whether ingested in organic or inorganic nanoforms, was effectively incorporated into tissues and significantly enhanced GPx protein levels and its activity. In the case of inorganic nanosized Se, it did not rely on the endogenous mechanisms of intestinal bacteria. Rather, it was reduced to selenite metabolites and subsequently converted to selenophosphate, which was further synthesized to selenocysteine and incorporated into selenoproteins [101]. These results indicate that, regardless of the metabolic pathway, inorganic Se also ultimately promotes the expression and activity of GPx proteins, thereby exhibiting functional similarity to organic Se.

While dietary Se intake in either organic or inorganic forms has been demonstrated to significantly elevate the level and activity of GPx proteins, subsequent studies have begun to explore the potential of inorganic Se-based nanozymes in GPx-like activities. For example, Zhang et al. [102] designed an artificial selenoenzyme containing a Se core-shell multielement in bionic cascade nanozymes (GOx@IM-Se-Ph@TA-Fe NPs) (Fig. 5a and b) and evaluated its GPx-like activity by the Michaelis-Menten equation, obtaining a value of 45.9 µM/min (Figs. 5c-e). Specifically, the GOx@IM-Se-Ph@TA-Fe NPs were observed to cause the H₂O₂ concentration to peak within 60 min after ATP treatment (Fig. 5f), which suggested an ATP-dependent reactivity. Furthermore, 2-mercapto-5-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB) was employed as a substrate model to assess the GPx-like activity of GOx@IM-Se-Ph@TA-Fe NPs. The UV absorbance of TNB exhibited a continuous decline over a 4-hour period, indicating of a reduction in the free sulfhydryl (GSH) content. This outcome substantiated the assertion that GOx@IM-Se-Ph@TA-Fe NPs exhibited GPx-like catalytic degradation of GSH activity (Fig. 5g).

Furthermore, other forms of inorganic Se-based nanomaterials, including Se NPs prepared using BSA [103], mannose-functionalized Se nanoparticles (M-Se NPs) [104] and diselenide polymers [105], have also exhibited notable GPx-like activity and have been shown to effectively promote the consumption of GSH.

SOD-like

In addition to GPx, other enzymes demonstrate the same capacity to scavenge free radicals. Natural SOD plays a biocatalytic role in the conversion of ·O2− into O2 and H2O2. In recent years, Se-based nanozymes have garnered significant attention due to their potential to emulate SOD-like activity. The SOD-like activity has been extensively utilized in various fields, including protection against oxidative stress through a comparable free radical scavenging mechanism. In particular, Se has enabled Se-based nanozymes to exhibit s SOD-like activity to those of single Se nanomaterials by combining with multicomponent. For example, Lai et al. [106] synthesized tea polyphenol-functionalized Se nanoparticles (Se@TE NPs) via a microwave-assisted method. The scavenging of ABTS+·, DPPH·, and ·O2−, as well as the SOD-like activities of the Se@TE NPs, exhibited dose-dependent effects and were significantly higher than those of Se NPs alone. Furthermore, Zhao et al. [25] employed diselenide-bonded biomimetic redox-responsive precursor micelles, DPC@Pt@M, which demonstrated SOD-like activity of 8740.65 U/mg as determined by the SOD kit. Additionally, the micelles exhibited a stable activity over a wide pH range (pH = 5.7–8.7) under the NBT assay. Moreover, the ESR assay corroborated the scavenging effect of this Se-based nanozyme on ·O2−. These findings suggest that multifunctional Se-based nanozymes exhibit enhanced enzyme activity compared to their single Se-based counterparts.

Consequently, numerous researchers have endeavored to enhance the SOD-like activity of other nanomaterials by introducing Se, with the objective of further enhancing their SOD-like activity. For example, Xiao et al. [68] designed an iron oxide nanoparticle (MCDION-Se) coated with manganese carbonate and deposited by nano-Se. The nano-Se effectively activated the SOD-like activity and promoted the generation of the superoxide anion radical (SOAR) in tumor tissues. This was based on the reaction between SOD and SOAR, which increased the H₂O₂ in cancer cells. Subsequently, the generated H₂O₂ would be further catalyzed to ·OH by Mn²⁺ ions and iron oxide nanoparticles (IONs) (Fig. 6), which resulted in the apoptosis of cancer cells.

Additionally, cerium-based nanozymes, a prevalent nanomaterial with SOD-like activity, have garnered significant interest in recent years [107, 108]. To enhance the activity of cerium-based nanozymes, Gong et al. [109] investigated cerium (Ce) and Se co-doped carbon dots (CS-CDs) complexes and successfully prepared SOD-like catalytically active materials with a double stacking effect. The complexes demonstrated enhanced efficacy in the scavenging of ·O2−, exhibiting inhibitory potential that surpassed that of Ce alone. Furthermore, the scavenging activity demonstrated a concentration-dependent behavior for CS-CDs, indicating that the scavenging activity increased gradually with the elevation of nanozymes concentration.

CAT-like

Natural CAT catalyzes the conversion of H₂O₂ into O₂ and H₂O [110], a reaction that is integral to the functioning of antioxidant defense systems. In recent years, there has been a great deal of interest in Se-based nanomaterials due to their CAT-like activity. It has been demonstrated that Se NPs display notable advantages in CAT-like activity. For example, in a study conducted by Liu et al. [111], the antioxidant properties of chitosan-coated Se NPs and Te NPs were investigated using the ABTS+· assay (Fig. 7a). The control group consisted of Te NPs encapsulated in BSA. The experimental results demonstrated that Se NPs exhibited enhanced ABTS+· scavenging activity (Fig. 7b) and antioxidant capacity (Fig. 7c) in comparison to water-soluble VE (Trolox) and Te NPs. Moreover, the reaction rate of Se NPs was 1.123 µM/s higher than that of the natural antioxidant Trolox (Fig. 7d). Furthermore, the CAT-like activity of Se NPs was found to increase with concentration, accompanied by oxygen production (Fig. 7f). The Se NPs demonstrated a greater antioxidant capacity in reducing ROS content through their notable CAT-like activity, whereas Te NPs, despite their environmental stability and hydration catalytic properties, exhibited considerably lower antioxidant activity than Se NPs, rendering Se NPs more advantageous for antioxidant applications.

In addition to Se NPs, Se composite nanomaterials have been investigated with the objective of enhancing their CAT-like activities. This has involved grafting novel chiral ligands with suitable stereochemical features to nanozymes, with the expectation that this will enhance the substrate selectivity and enantioselectivity, and thus enhance the activity of nanozymes. This approach has discussed in detail in references [112, 113]. In light of these findings, Liu et al. [114] proceeded to synthesize chiral D-type and L-type Se@CeO2 super particles (Se@CeO2 SPs) and evaluate their CAT-like activity through fluorescence spectroscopy. The findings demonstrated that the CAT-like activity of Se@CeO2 SPs was markedly superior to that of Se NPs and CeO2 NPs. Furthermore, Wang et al. [115] synthesized a Se-containing bifunctional cross-linker and obtained nano-integrated cascade enzymes (h-ANEs) in a one-step process. These h-ANEs deplete oxidative stress-induced ROS through in vivo cascade reactions, generating water and oxygen and thereby expressing CAT-like activity with enhanced effects.

Multi-enzymatic activity

Most nanozymes possess multi-enzyme activities, such as CeO2 with SOD-like and CAT-like activities [116], Prussian blue nanoparticles with POD-like, CAT-like and SOD-like activities [117], and Mn3O4 nanozymes that functionally SOD, CAT and GPx-like [118]. As described in the previous sections, Se NPs also possessed multi-enzyme activities. When Se is compounded with other materials, not only its enzyme activity can be significantly enhanced, but also multi-enzyme activity can be realized at the same time, giving Se-based nanozymes a broader application potential for a variety of therapeutic scenarios. For example, Tian et al. [62] synthesized a Fe2NC@Se nanozymes with Se-containing MOF (Se-MOF) shell-layer structure, which was able to convert colorless TMB into blue oxTMB with the participation of H2O2 and generate bubbles, as well as oxidize GSH to GSSG, resulting in the reduction of characteristic NADPH absorption peaks. These demonstrated that nanozymes could simultaneously perform POD, CAT, and GPx-like catalytic activities. Zhu et al. [63] reported a novel multifunctional nanozyme based on Se-enriched Prussian blue (Se-HMPB) nanoparticles. As previously described, PB itself possessed multiple enzyme-like activities, including CAT, POD, and SOD activities, and the Se coating imparts GPx-like activity to the nano-enzyme. Compared with the same concentration of PB, Se-HMPB nanozymes exhibited better scavenging abilities to ·OH and ·O2−, and generated O2 at higher concentrations than PB, i.e. The enzyme-like activity of Se-HMPB nanozymes was significantly enhanced by Se doping. Yan et al. [119] reported that poly (ethylene glycol)-modified Mo3Se4 nanofilms (PMNFs) possessed multiple enzymatic activity properties to achieve scavenging efficacy to ROS. Three representative ROS were selected for this experiment to measure the ability of PMNFs to remove ROS, including H2O2, ·O2−, and ·OH. More than 85% of the free radicals were eliminated when treated with 500 µg/mL PMNFs.

In addition to the complex multielement, the size of the nanozymes has a significant effect on their enzymatic activity. It has been found that the nano-effect becomes more significant as the size decreases, leading to an increase in enzyme activity [120]. For example, Li et al. [121] designed a novel hydrophilic nanobiocoupler lactoferrin (Lf)-modified Au-Bi2Se3 nanodots (NDs) (Fig. 8a) with an average size of 4–6 nm, which possessed a variety of enzyme activities such as SOD, GPx, and CAT-like. These NDs could be enriched in the vicinity of mitochondria to maintain the mitochondrial membrane potential and control the cellular ROS level. In this experiment, the multi-enzymatic activities of Au-Bi2Se3 NDs were investigated using spectrophotometric methods and commercial kits. The ability of Au-Bi2Se3 NDs to scavenge ·O2− was first evaluated using the WST-1 method, and the solution resulted in the formation of blue dirt, i.e., Au-Bi2Se3 exhibited SOD-like activity (Fig. 8b). The CAT and GPx-like activities could work together to scavenge H2O2 from the cells, and as shown in Fig. 8c, the presence of Au-Bi2Se3 resulted in an increase in H2O2 absorbance. The GPx-like activity of Au-Bi2Se3 NDs was determined by monitoring the change in absorbance of NADPH at 340 nm (Fig. 8d). Au-Bi2Se3 NDs in the presence of H2O2 caused TMB to appear blue at 600 nm, highlighting the catalytic ability of TMB and mimicking the ability of POD (Fig. 8e).

Consequently, since Se-based nanozymes have been confirmed to possess POD, SOD, GPx, and CAT-like activities, they play a role in regulating intracellular ROS balance in biomedical applications, overcoming the stability limitations of natural enzymes. Although Se-based nanozymes have demonstrated significant improvements in catalytic efficiency and environmental tolerance, most studies have focused on activity verification while lacking in-depth analysis of catalytic mechanisms, which may restrict their clinical translation.

Catalytic mechanisms of Se-based nanozymes

Se-based nanozymes exhibit diverse enzymatic activities, investigating their mechanisms of action is crucial for understanding these activities and exploring ways to enhance them. The catalytic activity of Se-based nanozymes stems from their unique electronic configuration and multivalent transition characteristics. At the molecular level, the large atomic radius (1.15 Å) and high polarizability of Se atoms facilitate the delocalization of outer-shell electrons, forming redox-active sites [122]. On the nanoscale, Se NPs expose abundant active sites on their surfaces, where Se²⁻ (selenide state) forms coordination bonds with transition metals (Ti, Mo) via lone pair electrons. For instance, in TiSe₂ crystals, the distorted octahedral coordination geometry of Se²⁻ with Ti induces local lattice strain, which modifies the electronic structure of Se sites. This structural distortion enhances their electron-donating capability [123]. Elemental Se (Se⁰) in Se NPs adopts a helical chain-like structure with a Se-Se bond energy of approximately 172 kJ/mol. This configuration promotes reversible valence transitions between Se⁰ and Se²⁺ through electron delocalization effects. The presence of coordinatively unsaturated sites on the surface facilitates electron transfer processes, enabling ROS scavenging in physiological environment [124]. Higher oxidation states of Se (Se⁴⁺/Se⁶⁺) serve as strong oxidative centers. For example, SeO₃²⁻, mediated by GSH, undergoes a three-step radical reaction pathway involving GS-Se⁻/GS-Se· intermediates to convert ·O₂⁻ into ·OH. The reaction efficiency is regulated by the dynamic formation and cleavage of Se-S bonds [125].

Additionally, Se vacancies have a significant influence on the enzyme mimetic activities of Se-based nanozymes. Taking TiSe2−x nanozymes as an example, the POD activity assay was performed using p-phenylenediamine (PPD), which could oxidize PPD to oxPPD in the presence of H2O2, producing a mixture of reddish-purple and yellow color. DFT calculations revealed the adsorption of PPD on TiSe2−x and the effect of Se vacancies on it. The adsorption of PPD on TiSe2 tended to adsorb vertically on TiSe2−x, while on TiSe2−x, one end of the PPD molecule was almost inserted into the vacancy due to the Se vacancy, leaving only one end in contact with the free PPD molecule. This unique spatial configuration increased the spatial site resistance between the free PPD molecules and inhibited the free reaction between them. The charge density difference further confirmed the enhancement of PPD adsorption on TiSe2−x with easier charge transfer from Se vacancies to PPD [126].

Taking the nanopore array MoSe2 as another example, the property of accelerated electron transport driven by the metal MoSe2 induced by the nanopore array could promote the intrinsic activity and higher active site utilization of the whole POD-like catalytic process. First, the introduction of Se vacancies affected the phase transition of MoSe2 (Fig. 9a, b), acquired a uniform free electron gas distribution (Fig. 9c), exhibited stronger H2O2 adsorption (Fig. 9d), and narrowed the band gap, which increased the electron density near the Fermi energy level and facilitated the electron transfer (Fig. 9e-h). MoSe2 with Se vacancies obtained a lower transition energy state (Fig. 9i), and the Se vacancy corresponded to a relay station for electron transfer, which induced and accelerated the POD-like catalysis (Fig. 9j), manifested by the oxidation of TMB to the blue oxTMB product with the participation of H2O2, which showed the most pronounced characteristic absorption intensity at 652 nm (Fig. 9k-m) [65].

Owing to the large atomic radius of Se, outer electrons are easily lost, making − 2 valent Se-based nanozymes have outstanding antioxidant effects, which can convert H₂O₂ into O2 to mimic native CAT activity [127]. For example, Zhang et al. [23] prepared organic Se (−2 valence)-doped carbon nitride quantum dots (SegCN-QDs) nanozyme, which exhibited significant reduction ability with a peak reduction current of 195.6 mA near − 1.7 V (Fig. 10a). The results of CV analysis proved that SegCN-QDs had the possibility of reducing ROS. Based on the macroscopic observation images of O₂ generation (Fig. 10b) and quantitative content measurement data (Fig. 10c), it was conclusively determined that the material exhibited CAT-like enzymatic activity characteristics. The catalytic mechanism involves the rate-determining step (RDS) of surface-adsorbed O atom (O*) formation from H2O2 decomposition (H2O2 → H2O* + O*), where SegCN-QDs exhibit a lower co-adsorption energy for H2O* and O* intermediates (−1.74 eV) compared to CNs (−0.84 eV), indicating reduced energy barriers for the RDS and consequently enhanced CAT-like activity through Se atom-mediated facilitation of H2O2 decomposition into O2 and H2O (Fig. 10d).The results reveals the − 2-valence selenium-based nanozymes leverage the large atomic radius of Se to facilitate electron delocalization, endowing them with strong reducing capacity. The key mechanism of their CAT-like activity lies in the Se-mediated reduction of the energy barrier for the rate-determining step of O* formation, thereby efficiently catalyzing the conversion of H₂O₂ into O₂.

The core mechanism of selenium-based nanozymes to simulate SOD activity lies in the multivalent redox properties of Se elements (Se⁰/Se²⁻/Se⁴⁺), and the dissociation reaction of ·O₂⁻ is realized through the formation and breaking of Se-O bonds. Among them, the adsorption strength of Se-O bonds is a key factor in regulating catalytic efficiency, which directly affects the transformation process of ·O₂⁻ to H₂O₂ and O₂ [128]. Additionally, Se can be used as a direct bandgap semiconductor, and its flexible valence state not only promotes carrier migration, but also the Se vacancy (Vsₑ) generated during crystal transformation becomes the key to regulating nanozyme activity. For example, Fig. 11a-b showed the detailed structure and density of state (DOS) of the four Fe-doped or Se vacancy models, among which Fe-CoSe-V(Se) had the highest state density (15.12 eV) at the Fermi level, indicating its important contribution to electron transport. O₂ was first adsorbed to form *OOH intermediates, and charge density difference analysis (Fig. 11c-d) showed that Fe-CoSe-V(Se) had a more significant electron transfer. The free energies of *OOH Gibbs of each model were CoSe (+ 0.60 eV), CoSe-V(Se) (−0.17 eV), Fe-CoSe (+ 0.33 eV) and Fe-CoSe-V(Se) (+ 0.04 eV), which proved that Fe-CoSe-V(Se) achieved competitive adsorption through lateral bonding (Fig. 11e). Figure 11f showed that the adsorption energies of CoSe and Fe-CoSe-V(Se) for *OOH are + 0.6 eV and − 1.37 eV, respectively. Figure 11g showed that Fe-CoSe-V(Se) had an optimal UL value (0.61 V) at 4.18 eV. Figure 11h clarified that Fe-CoSe-V(Se) directly converted *OOH to H₂O₂ [129]. This result reveals that selenium-based nanozymes can enhance the ·O₂⁻ disproportionation efficiency through Se vacancy-induced electron redistribution, which has the potential to be applied in simulating the catalytic function of native SOD.

In studies investigating Se-based nanozymes with GPx-like activity, Se has been highlighted as a component of natural GPx. Natural GPx is just a selenoenzyme whose main function is the oxidation of organic peroxides by catalyzing H2O2 using GSH as a cofactor [130]. The catalytic mechanism of GPx involves first the oxidation of selenohydrin (GPx-SeH) by peroxides to produce selenic acid (GPx-SeOH). Under physiological conditions, selenic acid can react with glutathione to form enzyme-conjugated selenosulfide (GPx-Se-SG), a process that simultaneously regenerates selenol and releases GSSG. However, at higher peroxide and lower glutathione levels, selenol is further oxidized to the corresponding selenic acid (GPx-SeO2H) (Fig. 12a). Given the critical role of Se redox chemistry in GPx in cytoprotection, synthetic Se compounds that mimic the function of the natural enzyme, such as ebselen and its analogs (Fig. 12b) or diselenides, have been reported [131]. In the presence of GSH, these compounds catalyzed the reduction of H2O2 and organic peroxides. The formation of selenic acid was also observed [132].

Enzyme catalysis is also affected by the strength between Se bonds. Some researchers have synthesized CS-modified VSe2/Mn (VSe2/Mn-CS NSs). Between VSe2, the Se-Se layer exhibited weak van der Waals interactions within the Se-Se layer, capable of responding to GSH. The VSe2 moiety showed superior GSH depletion, a phenomenon confirmed by XPS analysis, generating Se-Se bonds. Thus, glutathione could interact with Se-Se bonds in VSe2/Mn-CS NSs, thereby reducing glutathione levels [133].

Given these findings, as shown in Fig. 13, the catalytic mechanism of Se-based nanozymes originates from their multivalent state transitions and unique electronic structures (e.g., large atomic radius, high polarizability). By regulating electron redistribution, lattice strain, and adsorption energy through selenium vacancy engineering, these Se-based nanozymes enhance active site exposure and electron transfer efficiency, thereby mimicking the activities of natural enzymes such as CAT, POD, SOD, and GPx. However, practical applications require balancing their redox dynamics with structural stability.

Biomedical applications of Se-based nanozymes

Antimicrobial

The clinical treatment of infectious diseases usually uses antibiotics, but with the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, bacteria can produce antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), converting “superbugs” [134]. With the continuous development of nanozyme technology, its application in the field of antibacterial is receiving more and more attention. Such nanomaterials with unique catalytic properties provide a new solution to the global problem of antimicrobial resistance through their highly efficient antimicrobial activity. Nanozymes with POD-like activity can induce oxidative stress by catalyzing the production of ROS, such as ∙OH, 1O2, and ∙O2−, which cause irreversible damage to bacterial cells [135, 136]. In recent years, much attention has been paid to the antimicrobial applications of Se-based nanozymes, which utilize their POD-like activity to achieve effective antimicrobial effects even at low doses [137, 138].

For example, Lian et al. [139] prepared MnCo2S3.1Se0.9/NF composites by incorporating S2– and Se2– into MnCo2O4/Ni foam (NF) by a simple hydrothermal method. The material possessed excellent POD-like activity, and its catalytic Vmax value (the reaction speed when the rate of enzyme-catalyzed reaction reached the maximum) even exceeded that of natural horseradish POD-like, and exhibited a good bactericidal effect against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), which was mainly due to the production of ·O2– by H2O2. The enzyme-like activity was maintained at a stable level after 25 cycles of use, indicating that the Se-based nanozymes were more catalytically stable than natural enzymes. Chen et al. [96] designed novel ultrathin Se-doped Ni-V mixed metal oxides (Ni-V MMOs). The Se-doped materials exhibited richer oxygen vacancies and more variable elemental states than the undoped Se-doped Ni-V MMOs, which significantly enhanced their POD-like activity. The bactericidal effect was mainly attributed to the generation of ROS, such as ·OH and ·O2–. In the absence of H2O2, 3NiV-Se-350 exhibited 71.04% bactericidal activity, while in the presence of H2O2, its bactericidal activity against E. coli was as high as 98.89%. Therefore, it can be concluded that the antimicrobial mechanism of Se-based nanozymes is mainly due to their POD-like activity, which generates ROS for sterilization.

In addition to the general elimination of planktonic bacteria, Se-based nanozymes have shown reliable bactericidal properties against recalcitrant bacterial films. For example, Mu et al. [140] developed composites of Se NPs co-doped with Mo elements into FePc nanorods. This Se-based nanozyme could catalyze the production of ·OH from H2O2 in vivo, and possessed significant antimicrobial effects against E. coli and S. aureus, and their mechanism of action included the disruption of cell membranes and the inhibition of biofilm formation, and for the impaired wound repair caused by biofilm formation in the S. aureus caused wounds.

Building on these observations, Se-based nanozymes exhibit unique catalytic advantages in the antibacterial field. Through their POD-like activities, they efficiently catalyze H₂O₂ to generate ROS (·OH and ·O₂⁻), effectively eradicating drug-resistant bacteria and biofilms while demonstrating superior catalytic stability compared to natural enzymes. However, their antibacterial efficacy is highly dependent on H₂O₂ concentration. In localized scenarios such as wound infections, H₂O₂ levels can be artificially regulated, but in systemic or deep-tissue infections, endogenous H₂O₂ levels may be insufficient, compromising their antibacterial performance. Furthermore, although short-term studies have confirmed the favorable biocompatibility of selenium-based nanozymes, their long-term safety requires thorough evaluation, including the degradation kinetics of nanomaterials, metabolic clearance pathways of metal ions (e.g., Mn/Co/Ni), and potential oxidative stress risks to normal tissues.

Anti-inflammatory

Oxidative stress is an important issue, mainly due to the excessive generation of ROS. Oxidative stress leads to an excessive inflammatory response and is a major contributor to rapid cell death and organ failure [141]. To counteract these pathological processes, traditional antioxidant drugs such as VC and VE are used clinically, but their limited stability and bioavailability make them ineffective in certain pathological situations [142]. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, such as aspirin and ibuprofen, are also used to reduce prostaglandin production by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) activity, thereby reducing inflammation and associated oxidative stress. Despite their efficacy in controlling inflammation, prolonged use is associated with risks such as gastrointestinal side effects and nephrotoxicity [143]. Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone and dexamethasone) can also reduce the inflammatory response by inhibiting immune cell activity, thereby indirectly decreasing oxidative stress. In addition, Steroids are effective in the short-term treatment of acute inflammation, but long-term use can lead to side effects such as immunosuppression and metabolic disorders [144].

Unlike traditional antioxidants, Se-based nanozymes can inhibit inflammatory damage with their unique bioactivity and multiple enzyme activities such as SOD, CAT and GPx-like activities with eliminating ROS. And they can also maintain better stability and enzyme-like activities in the inflammatory microenvironment, which is an effective means to possess antioxidative stress therapy. For example, self-assembled nanocomposites of Se and PDA, which have GPx-like activity, mimic intracellular enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants, catalyze the generation of non-toxic products from H2O2 in the presence of GSH, and protect cells from oxidative stress damage. Finally, the efficacy of this Se-based nanoparticle enzyme in the treatment of pneumonia was further demonstrated in a murine pneumonia model [145]. Chen et al. [146] constructed a novel mesoporous Se-hyaluronic acid nanoparticle enzyme therapeutic system (MSe-HA NPs) with high specific surface area and ultra-high GPx activity. Therapeutic treatment was achieved by eliminating ROS in inflammatory macrophages to treat local inflammatory and septic injuries (Fig. 14a). Later, Chen et al. [147] synthesized multifunctional Se nanozymes (CWP-Se@Man) by cationic water-soluble columnar aromatics (CWP) and mannose (Man) modification. After loading with CCR2-siRNA, CWP-Se@Man achieved electroneutrality and targeting co-driven with 15 times increase in mucus permeability, effectively penetrated the lung mucus layer and delivered CCR2-siRNA into macrophages. In the inflammatory mode, the CCR2-siRNA-loaded CWP-Se@Man nanotherapeutic system inhibited chemotaxis and ROS scavenging in a GPx-like manner, effectively counteracting acute lung injury (ALI) (Fig. 14b).

Additionally, some literatures have mentioned the combination of three enzyme activities, SOD, CAT and GPx-like, to achieve anti-inflammatory effects due to the fact that many materials have multiple enzyme activities and the synergistic effect of multiple enzyme combinations showed higher results in anti-inflammation compared to single enzyme activities. For example, Ai et al. [148] reported a manganese dioxide encapsulated Se-melanin (Se@Me@MnO2) nanozyme prepared by radical polymerization and in situ oxidative reduction, and in vitro experiments showed that the nanozyme possessed a variety of enzyme activities for scavenging ROS. The essence of the mechanism lied in the fact that Se, Me and MnO2 possessed stable and unpaired electrons, which worked together in a synergistic and rapid electron transfer to achieve rapid scavenging of H2O2, hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions. It has been shown to be highly effective as an anti-inflammatory agent within the cell, and these biocompatible nanozymes exhibit cytoprotective effects by resisting ROS-mediated damage, thereby reducing inflammation. Huang et al. [149] designed a Se-doped copper formate (Cuf-Se) nanozyme for the treatment of atherosclerosis, which possesses SOD and GPx-like activities. Cuf-Se can efficiently scavenge ROS, inhibit cellular senescence, and prevent the formation of foam cells. It acts on macrophages to reduce cellular ROS levels and lipid oxidation, thereby significantly inhibiting inflammation-related processes (Fig. 14c). Deng et al. [150] developed an amorphous Ru@CoSe nanolayer with multiple enzymatic activities (CAT, GPx, and SOD-like) that catalyzed the elimination of ROS in an ultra-fast and broad-spectrum manner. In vivo experiments showed that this nanozyme exhibited superior ROS scavenging ability in inflammatory diabetic wounds, which were characterized by high ROS levels that hindered stem cell proliferation, differentiation, and healing. (Fig. 14d).

The inflammatory process is complex, and the alleviation of oxidative stress by selenium-based nanozymes through scavenging ROS is one of their anti-inflammatory links. The introduction of selenium also endows nanozymes with the ability to further enhance anti-inflammatory effects through other immunomodulatory methods such as regulating immune-related cells and molecules, which specifically include: the promotion of tryptophan metabolite production to inhibit pro-inflammatory TLR4-NF-κB signaling activity in mucosal macrophages [151]; the activation of the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway [152], which can regulate selenoprotein expression, reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6), decrease ROS levels, improve anti-inflammatory and antioxidant stress responses, and alleviate toxicity [153]; and the alleviation of oxidative stress and inhibition of ferroptosis through the Sirt1/Nrf2/GPX4 pathway to reduce the inflammatory environment [154].

Taken together, Se-based nanozymes exhibit high catalytic efficiency, targeted delivery capacity, and immunomodulatory functions in anti-inflammatory therapy by synergistically eliminating ROS through the mimicry of multiple enzyme activities, including SOD, CAT, and GPx. However, their clinical applications still face challenges such as dose-dependent selenium toxicity, long-term metabolic risks associated with metallic components (e.g., Mn, Ru), and limitations in delivery efficiency under complex physiological conditions, necessitating further optimization of safety-focused preparation processes.

Antioxidant

Aging is a process of progressive decline in the physiological functions of an organism over time, involving a variety of physiological dysfunctions such as reduced fertility and vulnerability to endogenous and external threats [155]. Oxidative stress not only triggers inflammation under the aforementioned conditions, but also plays an important role in the aging process of organisms. By increasing the production of ROS, the oxidative process is intensified, thereby promoting aging. Se-based nanozymes play an enzyme-like catalytic role in scavenging excessive ROS for anti-inflammatory purposes, treating inflammation-induced damage in organisms. They also delay oxidative damage in animals, humans, and plants, while counteracting aging-related oxidative stress with excellent stability and biosafety.

For example, Bai et al. [37] designed chitosan/citrate complexes (SeNPs-C/C) loaded with Se NPs. The clinical potential of SeNPs-C/C was evaluated using a D-galactose (D-gal)-induced aging mouse model. Oral administration of SeNPs-C/C significantly inhibited oxidative stress in aging mice by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes. SeNPs-C/C could be further developed to defend against D-gal-induced aging process. Huan et al. [156] synthesized Se and graphene (GO-Se) composite nanomaterials with excellent GPx-like properties, which could effectively scavenge H2O2 and protect cells from oxidative stress. It utilized H2O2 to react with these Se-based nanozymes to generate Se oxide intermediates, which then reacted with thiols (GSH) to generate GSSG. Se fractions were returned to the nano-Se state. In the presence of GR and NADPH, GSSG was reduced to GSH, and the Se nano-Se was then reacted with another H2O2 molecule. This study found high enzymatic activity of this Se-based nanozyme at pH close to physiological conditions.

In the field of ophthalmology, Dry Eye Disease (DED) is a common disease closely related to oxidative stress. It is closely related to oxidative damage caused by oxidative stress. In a study, mice suffering from dry eye syndrome were successfully treated by developing an aldehyde-functionalized F127 hydrogel eye drop with multifunctional antioxidant Cu2−xSe nanoparticles. The experimental results showed that such nanoparticles possessed SOD and GPx-like activities, which could effectively scavenge ROS and attenuate oxidative damage, thereby treating dry eye [157].

Se plays a key role in antioxidant, and Se deficiency can lead to Se deficiency when clinically deficient. For example, Bai et al. [158] synthesized Se NPs and embedded them in chitosan microspheres to form Se NPs-M. Se NPs-M had strong antioxidant activity, with significantly increased Se retention and enzyme levels. And the significant increase in Se retention, GPx, SOD and CAT levels were experimentally demonstrated. Se NPs-M acted as a Se supplement with antioxidant properties against Se deficiency in animals and humans.

In studying the antioxidant properties of Se-based nanozymes, Li et al. [82] used different approaches and designs to jointly explore their potential in scavenging ROS and protecting biological systems from oxidative damage. First, Se-CQDs, green fluorescent carbon quantum dots synthesized by hydrothermal treatment of selenocystine, exhibited remarkable redox-reversible fluorescence properties with a quantum yield of about 7.6%, which endowed them with excellent performance in scavenging ROS. Intracellularly, Se-CQDs successfully reduced the ROS concentration and provided effective protection for biological systems (Fig. 15a). On the other hand, Tian et al. [62] designed Se-containing MOF shell layer of dual Fe-atom nanozymes (Fe2NC@Se), which Se-based nanozymes exhibited excellent multi-enzyme catalytic activities, including CAT, SOD and GPx-like activities. In vivo experiments showed that Fe2NC@Se nanoparticles successfully scavenged intracellular ROS and effectively inhibited neuroapoptosis induced by cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (Fig. 15b). Liu et al. [111] revealed the mechanism of Se-based nanozymes with Se/Se2– active centers in density functional theory calculations, which contributed to ROS scavenging, through the study of chitosan-coated Se nanozymes. Biological experiments confirmed that mice treated with Se-based nanozymes showed significant effects in inhibiting oxidation, maintained a high survival rate for 30 days, and maintained a 100% survival rate (Fig. 15c). In addition, tea polyphenol-functionalized Se nanoparticles (Se@TE NPs) synthesized by Lai et al. [159] via a microwave-assisted method exhibited potent ROS scavenging ability in vitro and effectively inhibited cisplatin-induced apoptosis in HK-2 cells. In vivo, Se@TE NPs were converted into Se-containing amino acids, such as Se-Met and Se-Cys2, which can inhibit ROS production by activating selenoenzymes. Increased SOD and GPx-like activities and decreased MDA levels in kidney tissues of treated mice contributed to the reversal of cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury (Fig. 15d).

In addition to their antioxidant properties in animal models, Se-based nanozymes also play a vital role in plant growth. A study was conducted to investigate the effects of different concentrations of Se NPs solutions (0, 5, 10, and 15 mg/L) on the physiological and biochemical properties, nutrition, antioxidant defense system, and growth of coriander under cadmium stress. It was found that the addition of Se NPs increased plant growth characteristics, chlorophyll content, total soluble sugars, relative leaf water content and gas exchange parameters, which reduced cadmium toxicity. It suggests that Se NPs can protect cellular structures and promote antioxidant responses by increasing antioxidant enzyme activities and scavenging free radicals and ROS [160]. Studies have also evaluated the stability of GSH, MPS, and Se-MPS (a selenopeptide derived from selenated brown rice protein hydrolysis products) under various processing conditions and measured their ABTS+· scavenging capacity. The ABTS+· scavenging capacity of GSH was significantly reduced under heat treatment, high salt density, and long-term storage conditions, and the antioxidant activity of Se-MPS did not change much under all conditions. In terms of stability and antioxidant activity, Se-MPS was superior to GSH and MPS [161].

To synthesize these findings, Se-based nanozymes exhibit excellent antioxidant properties across animal, human, and plant systems by mimicking the activities of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., GPx, SOD, CAT). These nanozymes not only scavenge excessive ROS to alleviate oxidative damage associated with inflammation and aging but also counteract selenium deficiency by modulating the activity of selenium-dependent enzymes (GPx). While short-term experiments indicate favorable biocompatibility, the long-term toxicological risks of selenium accumulation remain incompletely elucidated. Furthermore, the dynamic metabolic pathways of Se-based nanozymes in complex biological systems and their interactions with endogenous antioxidant networks still lack comprehensive mechanistic understanding.

Antitumor

Cancer is one of the greatest global health threats, causing millions of deaths each year [162]. Although the optimization of conventional therapies and the improvement of diagnostic techniques have led to a reduction in mortality and morbidity, the challenge of developing novel strategies to combat different types of cancer remains daunting. Nanotechnology offers new hope for cancer treatment by overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies (e.g., lack of water solubility, poor pharmacokinetics, and insufficient specificity) [163]. In the field of nanotechnology, nanozymes with their unique and efficient enzyme-like catalytic activity have demonstrated excellent performance in anti-tumor therapy in the biomedical field. Se-based nanozymes with POD-like activity can catalyze the conversion of excess H₂O₂ in tumors into highly reactive ·OH, effectively killing cancer cells. In addition, Se-based nanozymes with CAT-like activity are able to enhance the photodynamic effect of photosensitizers in hypoxic environments. Meanwhile, through the antioxidant enzyme-like catalytic activity, excess H2O2 or superoxide anion in inflamed tissues can be converted into non-toxic products by CAT, SOD or GPx-like activities, thus effectively mitigating oxidative damage in the anti-cancer process.

Yao et al. [164] initiated a series of studies on Se-based nanozyme materials in the field of nanomedicine to explore their potential applications in cancer treatment and diagnosis. First, Bi2Se3-MnO2@BSA nanomaterials were prepared by preparing Bi2Se3-MnO2@BSA nanomaterials, which possessed strong X-ray attenuation and CAT-like activity. During radiotherapy, the material was able to generate more oxygen in the tumor region, thus improving the radiation resistance of the tumor. Compared with MnO2@BSA, Bi2Se3-MnO2@BSA also exhibited higher catalytic activity due to the addition of Se element, which helped to kill tumor cells more effectively. In addition, the material performed well in computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, providing a versatile therapeutic diagnostic nano-agent for improved imaging-guided tumor radiotherapy sensitization performance. Mahaveer P. et al. [165] designed Se polymeric nanoparticles (Se@CMHA NPs) functionalized with cetyl hyaluronic acid (CMHA) loaded with specific adriamycin (DOX). This design not only exhibited enhanced cytotoxic potential against human cancer cells, but also amplified cancer ROS stress, intervened and induced cancer cell death. Mahaveer P. et al. [166] again successfully synthesized biomolecule-capped SeNPs using Lactobacillus casei ATCC 393, a probiotic microorganism, in the presence of sodium selenite and under anaerobic conditions, the Se-based nanozyme exhibited good GPx-like activity, induced apoptosis in HepG2 cells, and converted excess ROS into non-toxic substances to mitigate oxidative damage.

In further research, Wang et al. developed Se-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (Se-HANs). Compared to HANs with lower or no Se content, 10% Se-HANs showed a stronger inhibitory effect. These particles showed significant antitumor activity in ex vivo experiments by modulating ROS production and cysteine-asparaginase-dependent apoptotic pathway, which effectively induced apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 16a) [167]. In addition, degradable Bi2Se3 nanoparticles (PVP- Bi2Se3@Sec NPs) based on poly(vinylpyrrolidone) and selenocysteine substituted cysteine modifications with X-ray attenuation and NIR absorption were also synthesized. In vivo, some of the Se could be released from the NPs into the blood circulation, which might enhance immune function and reduce the systemic side effects of radiation. Increased SOD and GPx-like activities of Se-based nanozymes were found after in vivo radiotherapy, which improved immune function and reduced side effects (Fig. 16b) [168]. Finally, Wang et al. [169] prepared Cu2Se hollow nanocubes (HNCs) by anion-exchange method using Cu2O nano-cubes (NCs) as templates and synthesized Cu2S or Cu2Se or CuSSe HNCs. By adjusting the reaction time, a high NIR II photothermal conversion efficiency (50.89%) and a good analog of the Fenton-like properties of Cu2Se HNCs were obtained. After surface coating, the polyethyleneglycolized Cu2Se HNCs had good water solubility and biocompatibility. In both in vitro and in vivo experiments, it was demonstrated that photothermal therapy (PTT) and CDT based on PEG-Cu₂Se HNCs exhibited significant synergistic effects. Under H₂O₂ and laser conditions, PEG-Cu₂Se HNCs generated high levels of ·OH, effectively inducing apoptosis in cancer cells (Fig. 16c).

By mimicking multiple enzyme activities (POD-like, CAT-like, and GPx-like), Se-based nanozymes exhibit multimodal synergistic effects in cancer therapy. Not only can they catalyze the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂, thereby effectively alleviating hypoxia in the cancer microenvironment, but they can also specifically damage cancer cells through dual mechanisms. Compared to normal cells, cancer cells typically exhibit higher baseline levels of ROS due to their heightened metabolic activity. On one hand, Se-based nanozymes enhance the generation of ·OH through their POD-like activity, further exacerbating oxidative stress and leading to cancer cell damage. On the other hand, they interfere with the self-defense mechanisms of cancer cells by inhibiting the function of key antioxidant molecules (GSH), disrupting their redox balance. Additionally, Se-based nanozymes possess photothermal/radiotherapy sensitization capabilities, enabling a synergistic strategy that integrates therapy and diagnosis. Through material engineering strategies (e.g., Bi₂Se₃-MnO₂@BSA, Cu₂Se hollow nanocubes), their biocompatibility, targeting efficiency, and catalytic performance have been significantly enhanced. However, clinical translation remains constrained by long-term metabolic uncertainties of Se-based nanozymes, and tumor microenvironment complexity, necessitating further solutions for dose control and catalytic efficiency optimization.

Antidiabetic

Diabetes has emerged as one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders globally, primarily classified into three forms: type 1, type 2 (T2DM, accounting for >90% of cases), and gestational diabetes mellitus [170]. Current epidemiological data from the International Diabetes Federation reveal that approximately 537 million individuals worldwide are affected, with projections indicating a rise to 783 million by 2045 [171]. The core pathological features of diabetes mellitus encompass persistent hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction. Recent studies have established that excessive accumulation of ROS is critically involved in the pathogenesis and progression of diabetes and its complications [172]. Recent therapeutic advances demonstrate that Se-based nanomaterials can functionally mimic endogenous selenoproteins (e.g., GPx and thioredoxin reductase), effectively scavenging excessive ROS to ameliorate oxidative stress and its downstream pathological consequences.

At the molecular level, Se exerts its therapeutic effects through multiple pathways. Polianskyte-Prause et al. identified ebselen as a potent inhibitor of SHIP2 (Src homology 2 domain-containing inositol-5-phosphatase 2), demonstrating its dual capacity to mitigate insulin resistance while enhancing antioxidant defenses through SHIP2 phosphatase inhibition [173]. Lv et al. fabricated a peptide-Se/Zn bimetallic complex that orchestrates systemic glucose metabolism modulation. Their work delineated a mechanistic cascade wherein Se⁴⁺ release activates the cAMP/AMPK signaling axis, subsequently upregulating glutathione peroxidase expression to potentiate glucose uptake and metabolic efficiency [170]. These findings collectively verified that Se was a multifunctional therapeutic agent capable of concurrently targeting insulin sensitivity, oxidative stress, and metabolic regulation in diabetes.

Islet β cell protection and functional restoration are essential for diabetes treatment, but current drugs cannot reverse its damage mechanisms. Studies have found that β cell death in T2DM is associated with excess ROS, and β cells are susceptible to oxidative damage due to weak antioxidant capacity (e.g., GPX1 insufficiency) [174]. Huang et al. [175] developed a novel Se nanodots as a GPX1 prodrug that released Se in a high-ROS environment to promote GPX1 expression, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and improved mitochondrial function, thereby protecting β cells. In addition, abnormal aggregation of human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP) fibrils is a key causative factor in islet amyloid deposition in T2DM. Li et al. [176] found that selenium-containing phycocyanin (Se-PC) inhibited the formation of nanoscale fibrils by interfering with hIAPP binding and prevents oligomers from growing into larger particles. Se-PC also mitigates the apoptosis induced by β cells by changing the structure of hIAPP, while inhibiting the production of ROS during fibrosis.

Hyperglycemia significantly enhances oxidative stress responses by activating polyol pathways, abnormally stimulating protein kinase C (PKC) signaling, and promoting the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), resulting in excessive ROS production, ultimately impairing pancreatic islet β cell function. It is worth noting that this persistent state of oxidative stress not only accelerates the decline of β cell function, but is also directly related to the occurrence and development of various diabetic complications, including abnormal bone metabolism, vascular dysfunction, delayed wound repair, and multisystem dysfunction, further confirming the key regulatory role of ROS in diabetes and its systemic pathological processes [177]. As one of the important complications of diabetes, diabetic bone disease is closely related to the imbalance of the “angiogenesis-osteogenesis” axis. Studies have confirmed that excessive accumulation of ROS and decreased GPx activity caused by oxidative stress in diabetic state directly inhibited osteogenesis. To solve this key problem, Wu et al. [178] developed Se-doped carbon quantum dots (SeYCQDs) with a GPx mimicking activity based on Se-rich yeast (SeY), which could efficiently scavenge ROS, repair mitochondrial function in endothelial cells, and regulate the expression of related genes, successfully alleviating the above imbalance. In the vascular complications of T2DM, Ishikura et al. [179] found found that the insulin resistance-related hepatic secretion protein selenoprotein P (SeP, encoded by SEPP1) was elevated in diabetic patients. Experiments confirmed that glucose could upregulate the expression of SEPP1 in hepatocytes, while physiological concentration of SeP could inhibit VEGF-induced proliferation, migration and ROS production of HUVEC, and block VEGFR2/ERK1/2 phosphorylation.

Additionally, diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of the most severe diabetic complications, leading to limb dysfunction, reduced quality of life, and substantial healthcare costs. The core clinical challenge lies in patients’ susceptibility to diabetic foot infections (DFIs) and compromised immunity, which are primary causes of amputations. Elevated bacterial loads and excessive ROS drive chronic inflammation, disrupting immune homeostasis and impairing healing [180]. To address these challenges, Wei et al. [181] developed a Janus nano system combining antibacterial photodynamic therapy with Se-phospholipid (Se-DOPE) antioxidant mechanisms, further enhanced by liposomal nanozymes (SeL) exhibiting GPx activity, achieving synergistic antimicrobial and antioxidant effects. Jin et al. [177] engineered two redox-responsive Zn(II)-porphyrin covalent organic framework (COF) hydrogels that release antibacterial and reparative Zn(II) ions upon ROS-triggered cleavage of disulfide/diselenide bonds, while simultaneously exhibiting GOx-like activity to degrade local glucose. Su et al. [182] integrated octadecane thiol functionalized polymethacrylic acid (ODT-PMMA) ligands and copper selenide nanoparticles (CuSe NPs) into a decellularized dermal matrix/alginate scaffold. Animal studies have confirmed a dual function: superior antimicrobial efficacy (reducing bacterial viability to 1.97% in abscess models) and accelerated healing. Shen et al. [183] developed an ultrasound-responsive nanocomposite “lever” hydrogel in which ROS-sensitive diselenide liposomes are sterilized by ROS-sensitive diselenide liposomes during infection and trigger diselelenium bond breaks to release drugs, dynamically balancing ROS levels (Fig. 18a). Li et al. [184] designed a Se/chlorine hybrid nanoparticle (SeC@PA)-functionalized microneedle bandage with bidirectional reactive species (RS) modulation: enhancing RS bactericidal activity in glutathione (GSH)-rich infected areas while exerting anti-inflammatory RS scavenging in GSH-deficient wound regions, coupled with M2 macrophage polarization. Its dopamine coating enhances biofilm penetration, enabling cascade-mediated biofilm disruption and healing promotion (Fig. 18b). He et al. [138] constructed an electromagnetic-responsive microneedle (Fe-Se-HA MNs) system comprising functionalized hyaluronic acid (HA), Fe3O4, and micelle-protected Se nanoparticles (SeNPs@LAS). These microneedles penetrated eschars and biofilms for deep magnetothermal sterilization. Subsequent release of HAase-triggered Se NPs regulated redox balance and stimulates angiogenesis in diabetic wounds (Fig. 18c). Zhao et al. [185] developed a system encapsulating chondroitin sulfate-modified Se NPs (CS@SeNPs) with broad-spectrum antimicrobial, free radical scavenging, and tissue regeneration capabilities (Fig. 18d).

For the problem of ROS accumulation and inflammatory cascade in diabetic wounds, Zhang et al. [186] developed a soluble bilayer GelMA microneedle (MN) with an outer layer loaded with Se-doped carbon quantum dots (Se-CQDs) to rapidly clear ROS, and an inner layer of slow-release alfalfa (AST) to anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenesis. Experiments have shown that it promotes endothelial migration by regulating cytoskeletal dynamics and peroxisome function, establishes a low-oxidation-anti-inflammatory microenvironment, and accelerates wound repair. Li et al. [187] developed a selenide-based polydopamine-enhanced composite hydrogel (DSeP@PB) that integrates polydopamine nanoparticles (PDANPs) with Prussian blue nanozymes into the selenol-terminal PEG matrix by a one-pot method, achieving large-scale preparation without organic solvents. The hydrogel has excellent mechanical properties and injectability, and its dynamic diselelenium bonds confer oxidation/reduction responsive degradation and light-controlled nanozyme release. Experiments have confirmed that DSeP@PB has ROS scavenging and immunomodulatory effects, which can effectively reduce oxidative damage and inflammation. Liu et al. [188] found that biosynthesized nanoSe can scavenge ROS and synergistically regulate macrophage polarization and promote diabetic skin barrier repair.

To summarize, Se-based materials show synergistic therapeutic potentials in diabetes mellitus and its complications (etc. islet protection, bone disease repair, vascular lesions, and wound healing) by mimicking the function of natural selenoprotein and regulating oxidative stress and metabolic pathways with multiple targets, including the antibacterial-antioxidant synergies, microenvironment-responsive releasing, and multi-mechanism joint repairs. However, it must be noted that most studies have primarily focused on the ability of Se-based nanozymes to alleviate diabetes-related complications by reducing ROS levels induced by diabetes, while insufficiently demonstrating their direct regulatory effects on core diabetic pathophysiological mechanisms (e.g., insulin resistance, β-cell dysfunction). This limitation in therapeutic targeting may compromise their efficacy as a fundamental treatment strategy for diabetes.

Anti-neuroinflammatory

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and spinal cord injury (SCI) represent the most pressing challenges in aging societies. Neurons, which execute fundamental functions like signal transmission and network integration in the central nervous system, are the primary targets of these disorders [189]. Neuroinflammation and oxidative stress form a tightly coupled bidirectional regulatory network during disease progression, mutually reinforcing each other in a vicious cycle. Key pathological mechanisms of neuroinflammation include a sustained microglial activation induced by aberrant α-synuclein aggregation, along with a cascading release of inflammatory mediators and an excessive ROS accumulation, collectively driving neuronal damages [190]. Se-based compounds have been demonstrated unique advantages in neuroinflammatory contexts due to their low toxicity, high bioactivity, and dual capacity to reduce ROS generation and mitigate oxidative injury, thereby exerting notable anti-inflammatory effects [191]. For instance, Zhou et al. [192] developed a polydopamine (PDA)-coated diselenide-bridged mesoporous silica nanoparticle platform which responsively released encapsulated caffeic acid phenethyl ester to scavenge ROS, promoting microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype and suppressing neuroinflammation. Similarly, Geng et al. [193] synthesized multifunctional metallopolyphenol-coated Se nanoparticles (BSe@MT) by integrating albumin-dispersed Se nanoparticles (BSe) with manganese ion–tannic acid complexes. BSe@MT exhibited SOD-like and GPx-like activities, effectively clearing intracellular ROS to inhibit neuronal apoptosis and attenuate microglial inflammation in vitro. These nanozymes could regulate the neuroprotective function of microglia and astrocytes, and ameliorate neuroinflammation in a short period of time.

Se-based nanomaterials have been extensively investigated in diversing neuroinflammatory disorders. In AD models, Gong et al. [194] reported a multifunctional Se-polydopamine nanozyme with broad-spectrum antioxidant activity. This system effectively scavenges ROS, protects normal cells from oxidative damage, restores mitochondrial homeostasis, shifts microglial polarization from proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, and promotes neural repair. Wang et al. [195] engineered dual-Se nanospheres (CLNDSe NPs) functionalized with a 2AAR agonist (CGS), enabling targeted blood-brain barrier delivery in vitro and in vivo. LPFFD peptide-modified CLNDSe NPs suppressed amyloid-β (Aβ42) aggregation and mitigated Aβ42-induced neurotoxicity by inhibiting oxidative damages and mitochondrial dysfunctions. In vivo administration of CLNDSe NPs restored GPX1/4 antioxidant capacity, reduced neuronal loss and neurofibrillary tangles, prevented ferroptosis, and ultimately improved cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 19a). For ALS, Zhang et al. [196] demonstrated that diphenyl diselenide (DPDS) reduced protein nitration by lowering the nitric oxide (NO) and ROS levels, thereby suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and conferring neuroprotection in ALS models. In SCI, Liu et al. [197] developed a self-reinforcing Se nanotherapeutic co-loaded with 2,3,5,6-tetramethylpyrazine and ginsenoside Rg1. The nanoSe component attenuated ROS accumulation, restored mitochondrial function, and reduced oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage (Fig. 19b). Rao et al. [198] synthesized APS-coated and tanshinone IIA (TSIIA)-loaded SeNPs, which protected SCI rat spinal neurons by enhancing the GPx-like activity and the reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) level, likely via the metabolic conversion to selenocysteine (SeCys2) and the modulation of antioxidant selenoproteins. Ji et al. [199] designed a Se-based antioxidant system with dihydroxy-functionalized boron-dipyrromethene dyes, which synergistically augmented ROS scavenging via Caveolin 1 (Cav1)-dependent pathways to restore spinal cord tissue and motor function in SCI mice. In thromboembolic, Li et al. [200] developed CREKA peptide-decorated, idebenone-loaded micelles with diselenide bonds for ROS-responsive drug release. This system targeted microthrombi in ischemic penumbrae, concurrently clearing ROS and releasing drugs to inhibit neuronal ferroptosis, suppress glial hyperactivation, and ameliorate inflammatory microenvironments. Kong et al. [201] engineered a diselenide-based ROS-responsive nanoparticle platform with cell membrane-camouflaged brain targeting, offering a novel stimulus-responsive nanomedicine for ischemic stroke therapy (Fig. 19c). For PD, Zhang et al. [23] synthesized organic Se (− 2 valence)-doped graphitic carbon nitride quantum dots (SeCN-QDs) exhibiting SOD and CAT-like activities. These nanozymes converted ROS to O2, dampening the ROS–NF-κB/NLRP3 axis at PD sites to alleviate neuroinflammation (Fig. 19d). Wang et al. [202] designed BV2 microglial membrane-camouflaged biomimetic multienzyme gallic acid-Se nanoparticles, which simultaneously inhibited neuronal ferroptosis (via GPX4-mediated lipid peroxide reduction) and microglial pyroptosis (by blocking HSP90/NLRP3/Caspase-1 signaling), thereby enhancing neuronal survival.

In summary, Se-based nanomaterials exhibited therapeutic potentials for various neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and spinal cord injury. Their core mechanisms involve scavenging ROS, modulating antioxidant enzyme activities (e.g., GPX1/4, SOD-like/CAT-like), suppressing microglial hyperactivation (shifting from proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes), and blocking inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome, NF-κB). These actions mitigated the vicious cycle of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Concurrently, targeted delivery strategies (e.g., blood-brain barrier penetration, cell membrane camouflage) and functional modifications (e.g., peptide conjugation, drug loading) enhance therapeutic efficacy, ultimately improving neuronal survival and cognitive/motor functions. However, current studies have insufficient validation of long-term intervention effects on chronic neurodegenerative progression. Additionally, the metabolic pathways and potential neurotoxicity of certain materials (e.g., diphenyl diselenide) require more systematic safety evaluations.

Disease diagnosis/biosensing

As a family of redox-active small molecules, ROS play dual roles in various physiological and pathological processes: they serve as important cellular signaling molecules, yet excessive accumulation triggers oxidative stress damage, contributing to the development of diseases such as bacterial infections, cancer, and ischemic tissue injury. Therefore, accurate monitoring and regulation of ROS levels are crucial for the early diagnosis and treatment of diseases. In recent years, Se-based nanomaterials have been widely applied in biosensing and disease therapy due to their unique mimetic enzyme activities and ROS-responsive properties. Through diverse detection techniques including fluorescence, chemiluminescence, photoacoustic (PA) imaging, and electrochemistry, researchers have developed a series of ROS quantitative analysis methods based on Se chemistry. For instance, Cao et al. [203] designed chiral molybdenum diselenide (MoSe₂) nanoparticle probes modified with the fluorescent molecule cyanine 3 (Cy3), which exhibit significant circular dichroism (CD) signals at 390 nm and 550 nm, while Cy3 fluorescence at 560 nm is effectively quenched by MoSe₂ nanoparticles. Upon reaction with ROS, MoSe₂ nanoparticles undergo oxidation, leading to decreased CD signals (detection limit of 0.0093 nmol/10⁶ cells) and recovery of Cy3 fluorescence (detection limit of 0.024 nmol/10⁶ cells). This dual-signal response mechanism arises from redox reactions of Mo⁴⁺ and Se²⁻ on the nanoparticle surface, ensuring high selectivity and sensitivity in complex biological environments and enabling successful application in in vivo ROS level monitoring (Fig. 20a).

In the field of cancer diagnosis and treatment, Jo et al. [204] developed a diselenide-crosslinked carbon dot hydrogel (dsCD-Hydrogel) to detect ROS levels in the cancer cell microenvironment by real-time monitoring of conductivity changes. Its wireless sensing system can connect to smartphones for data visualization, and the ROS-scavenging ability of hydrogel significantly reduced tumor volume. Kim et al. [205] constructed an electrochemical sensor based on diselenide-functionalized hyaluronic acid polymer dots (PD(HA/DP)-DiSe), which achieved highly selective discrimination between MDA-MB-231 and HeLa cancer cells through Se-Se bond cleavage-induced surface morphological changes (from spherical to sheet-like) and impedance alterations (increasing from ~ 850 Ω to 1200 Ω), with detection sensitivity enabling differentiation of 5% cell ratio differences (Fig. 20b). In biological infection applications, Benson et al. [206] developed a nitrobenzoselenadiazole-vancomycin conjugate with environment-sensitive photodynamic activity, which not only detects MRSA biofilms with a sensitivity of 2.3 nM but also efficiently generates singlet oxygen under 530 nm light irradiation (quantum yield ΦΔ = 0.38), killing 99.9% of drug-resistant strains within 30 min. Its small molecular weight (< 600 Da) ensures good tissue permeability. Gao et al. [207] developed a multifunctional hydrogel by integrating GSH, selenoglutathione (GSeH), biosynthetic Se nanoparticles (BioSeNPs), and cellulose nanocrystals (CNC), achieving synergistic performance enhancement. The incorporation of BioSeNPs significantly improved the antioxidant capacity and antibacterial properties of hydrogel, and it exhibited unique electrochemical characteristics with excellent sensing potential (Fig. 20c). For ischemic tissue recovery, Ma et al. [208] designed a PLGA-Se-Se-PEG-IMTP (HI@PSeP-IMTP) nano-delivery system capable of co-loading hesperetin and indocyanine green (ICG). Through near-infrared fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging, it enabled precise localization of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) regions (with signal intensities increased by 32% and 40%, respectively), while synergistically scavenging ROS and inhibiting cardiac inflammation, reducing IL-6 levels by 67% (Fig. 20d). Subba et al. [209] reported a hypoxia-responsive diselenide hydrogel (HS-PD), which enabled multimodal monitoring via ROS-triggered chemical bond cleavage: conductivity increased by 78.1% (ΔR from 15.4 to 3.4 kΩ), compressive modulus increased from 12 kPa to 28 kPa, and SOD2 expression in ischemic muscle was upregulated by 3.2-fold, significantly promoting tissue repair. Waypa et al. [210] revealed the central role of mitochondria in hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV). Selective inhibition experiments confirmed that proximal electron transport chain (ETC) inhibitors (rotenone, hydroxy thiazole, diphenylene iodonium) completely abolish the HPV response, while distal inhibitors (cyanide, antimycin A) only partially attenuate it; in ρ⁰ cells lacking ETC, the hypoxic response persists but is specifically blocked by the Se-containing antioxidant ebselen and copper-zinc superoxide dismutase inhibitors. Results showed that during hypoxia, pulmonary artery myocardial cells generate H₂O₂ via proximal ETC (complexes I/III) (DCFH fluorescence increased by 2.7-fold), a process inhibitable by methimazole; the Se compound ebselen specifically interrupts HPV signaling by regulating ROS scavenging capacity without affecting basal contractile function. This confirms HPV is independent of mitochondrial ATP, instead utilizing ROS generated via proximal ETC Se-dependent redox reactions as second messengers to achieve oxygen-sensing function.

Overall, given the dual role of ROS in disease development, rational utilization of the unique redox properties of Se-based nanozymes enables the development of various highly sensitive detection technologies and targeted therapeutic strategies. Through multimodal integrated diagnosis and treatment approaches (including electrochemical sensing, photodynamic therapy, ROS-responsive drug delivery, etc.), a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions between Se-based nanozymes and ROS in cancer, infectious diseases, and ischemic injuries can be gained. However, current technologies still face the challenge that long-term use of Se-based nanozymes in complex biological environments may disrupt endogenous redox homeostasis. Additionally, the tissue permeability and metabolic mechanisms of certain small-molecule Se probes (e.g., nitrobenzoselenadiazole) require further validation.

Biosafety and toxicity of Se-based nanozymes

The biomedical applications of Se-based nanozymes still face multiple biosafety challenges, primarily concerning ROS-related toxicity risks and long-term accumulation effects. These materials play crucial roles in various therapeutic scenarios by bidirectionally modulating ROS (both generating and scavenging) through mimicking natural enzyme activities. However, in antibacterial or anticancer applications, excessive ROS generation may induce oxidative stress in healthy cells, leading to a loss of membrane integrity, protein conformational changes, and DNA damages. Conversely, during antioxidant or anti-inflammatory therapies, increasing material concentrations for sufficient ROS clearance may cause dose-dependent toxicity. Thus, a precise dosage control and ROS-balancing capacity is essential to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissues.

Oxidative stress in cells is commonly assessed via mitochondrial reductase activity. Zhang et al. [211] demonstrated that 10 µM SE@CS/CSP NPs scavenged >30% of ABTS+· within 30 min. DCFH-DA assays confirmed that cisplatin (DDP) persistently elevated ROS, whereas SE@CS/CSP NPs significantly reduced ROS levels over 1–4 h. Chen et al. [212] incubated Se NPs (≤ 100 µM) with primary cardiomyocytes (NRVM) and H9C2 cells for 24 h, revealing no significant toxicity. For antioxidant evaluation, 300 µM H2O2 was established as the optimal oxidative damage concentration in H9C2 cells, with 25 µM Se NPs identified as the most protective dose. Quispe et al. [213] reported that (PhSe)2 (2 µM, 48 h pre-treatment) fully or partially suppressed DCFH2 oxidation and mitochondrial oxidant production (via MitoSOX) in HT22 cells exposed to 40–100 µM tert-BuOOH, while mitigating tert-BuOOH-induced mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction (OCR reduction). Mechanistically, (PhSe)2 upregulated GPx activity (6–8×), GSH levels (86.2%), and gene expression of Gpx1 (4×) and Gclc (2.3×), enhancing cellular antioxidant defenses to sustain viability even under 600 µM tert-BuOOH.

Se shares analogous chemical properties with its periodic table congeners tellurium (Te) and sulfur (S). Li Xiao et al. [214] demonstrated that bone-mimetic Se-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (B-SeHANs) exhibit selective toxicity toward osteosarcoma cells (MNNG/HOS), suppressing viability by 50% at >20 µg/mL (48 h) while remaining non-toxic to normal rBMSCs (safe at 50 µg/mL). Comparatively, Zhang et al. [215] reported that Te-MBG (40 µg/mL) significantly inhibited MG63 osteosarcoma cells (P < 0.01), whereas 0Te-MBG showed no toxicity. Zhai et al. [216] synthesized dexamethasone-loaded polythioketal nanoparticles (PTKNPs@Dex), revealing dose-dependent cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 cells at >20 µg/mL (CCK-8 assay). These findings underscore the superior biosafety profile of Se-based nanozymes and highlight the critical role of material design in defining their therapeutic windows.

Another important safety consideration is the absorption and distribution of Se-based nanozymes in the body. Excessive absorption and distribution of Se may affect health. Zhu et al. [217] investigated the anti-tumor effect of nanodrugs using a BALB/c nude mouse model transplanted with HT29 cells, treated tumor-bearing mice with TCPP@SeSe-CPT (10 mg/kg) for 16 days, and found that TCPP@SeSe-CPT showed tumor suppression effect without significant toxicity. Xu et al. [218] investigated the potential side effects of diselenide-bridged hyaluronic acid nanogel (SeNG) by orally administering PBS and SeNG (50 mg/kg) to BALB/c mice for 7 days. Histological analysis after 11 days revealed no significant toxicity in major organs (liver, spleen, kidney, heart, colon, and intestine). Xie et al. [219] evaluated the toxicity and therapeutic efficacy of Se nanoparticles (Mal-LET-SeNPs) in a DNCB-induced dermatitis model using 40 female BALB/c mice. Histopathological examination (H&E staining), cutaneous Se content quantification, and selenoprotein mRNA expression analysis after 39 days demonstrated no significant toxicity at the high dose of 125 mg/mL, with concurrent amelioration of dermatitis symptoms. These preliminary assessments indicate favorable tolerability of Mal-LET-SeNPs in murine models, supporting the biosafety of Se-based nanomaterials via both topical and oral administration routes, while acknowledging the necessity for further comprehensive studies.

Additionally, prolonged exposure to Se-based nanozymes may lead to the accumulation of Se elemental molecules in the body. While short-term application typically does not cause significant toxicity, long-term use may lead to a gradual buildup of Se, particularly in metabolic organs such as the liver and kidneys, potentially leading to chronic toxicity and an increased risk of organ damage. Detailed long-term toxicological studies are necessary to assess this risk. In the previously mentioned studies, there was no long-term toxicity analysis. Further research is needed to explore the potential cumulative toxicity of Se-based nanozymes in different biomedical applications.

Exploration of Se-based nanozymes in clinical translation

As illustrated in the above sections, many researches have proved that Se-based nanozymes exhibited excellent therapeutic efficacy and biocompatibility in biomedical applications.

While, the clinical translation of these Se-based nanozymes is still challenging. For example, in oral delivery systems, targeted modification strategies need to be designed to reduce potential side effects; in wound treatment, composite strategies with advanced dressings should be explored to enhance therapeutic outcomes. These application-oriented optimizations should be systematically considered in preclinical studies and proactively reflected in patent strategies. Through searches in global patent databases (worldwide.espacenet.com), using the keywords “selenium”, “nano”, “application”, and “ROS”, 940 relevant patents have been identified. In these patents, the preparation of nano-Se mostly adopts chemical reduction methods (such as using sodium selenite as the selenium source and glucose, ascorbic acid, etc. as reducing agents) or biosynthesis methods. In biomedicine, its applications focus on anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidation, radiotherapy protection, neurological disease treatment, etc. It is mainly distributed in China, with a small number involved in the United States, Europe, etc. The years range from 2015 to 2025, mainly from 2021 to 2023. For instance, the patent titled “Self-assembled Se peptide nano material for targeting mitochondria as well as preparation method and application of self-assembled Se peptide nano material” (ID: CN116350615A) provided a self-assembled Se-peptide nanomaterial composed of selenoamino acids, assembly peptide (FFK) and mitochondrial-targeting groups, which achieved targeted mitochondrial delivery, enhanced ROS scavenging capability, and anti-inflammatory, cytoprotective and precision therapeutic effects [220]. Additionally, the patent “Se-coated zinc/cerium-based nanozyme and application thereof in regulating intestinal microenvironment to treat inflammatory bowel disease” (ID: CN119454614A) described a Se-coated Zn/Ce-based nanozyme that achieved synergistic treatment of inflammatory bowel disease through multi-pathway ROS clearance, Nrf2 antioxidant pathway activation and gut microbiota regulation, while inhibiting intestinal barrier ferroptosis [221]. Furthermore, the patent “Use of nano-selenium cordyceps militaris aqueous extract in reducing radiotherapy injury and protective agent” (ID: WO2023082217A1) provided a protective agent containing nano-selenium Cordyceps militaris aqueous extract, which could reduce radiotherapy-induced injury to human organs or muscles by lowering radiotherapy-caused ROS elevation, exerting strong cell repair effect and protecting against myocardial injury, with low toxicity [222]. These patents provide important insights into the potential biomedical applications of Se-based nanozymes, and their anticipated results will further validate clinical efficacy and safety.

A search of the “ClinicalTrials.gov” website revealed 319 clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of Se-based interventions using the keywords “selenium”, of which 202 have been completed, with the years ranging from 2010 to 2025. For example, the trial titled “Selenium and Prostate Cancer: Clinical Trial on Availability to Prostate Tissue and Effects on Gene Expression” (ID: NCT00446901) aimed to determine whether Se supplementation altered Se levels and gene expression profiles in prostate tissue [223]. The trial “Effects of Se and Melatonin on Ocular Ischemic Syndrome” (ID: NCT04005222) seeked to evaluate the impact of one-month administration of Se, melatonin, or their combination on anterior chamber (AC) malondialdehyde (MDA) and GSH levels in patients with ocular ischemic syndrome [224]. The study “Se in the Prevention of Cancer” (ID: NCT00022165) was designed to assess the effectiveness of Se in cancer prevention among healthy individuals [225]. Additionally, “Se Supplementation in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) Patients” (ID: NCT00186706) investigated whether oral Se supplementation increased antioxidant blood levels in patients with smoking-related lung disease [226]. These results indicated that Se had been clinically applied as a dietary supplement, but there was insufficient relevant data to support the feasibility of Se-based nanoparticles in biomedical applications.

In summary, Se-based nanozymes have demonstrated remarkable therapeutic potential and excellent biocompatibility in biomedical applications. However, clinical translation data remain limited, necessitating further optimization of carrier design, targeted modification strategies, and composite application approaches. Through continuous technological innovation, these nanozymes are expected to overcome the limitations of conventional therapies, accelerate their clinical translation, and ultimately provide more efficient and precise solutions for disease treatment.

Conclusion and outlook

Se was officially recognized as an essential and important trace element in the human body in 1957. Since then, scientists have conducted in-depth studies on the mechanism of action of Se, covering both at the basic science and clinical levels, and it has even been extensively explored and investigated in other fields [227, 228]. Se serves as a component of a variety of natural enzymes, providing them with crucial catalytic functions. However, due to the high price and insufficient catalytic stability of natural enzymes, scientists have followed the development of nanotechnology and investigated a Se-based nanozyme with low cost, structurally stable, and possessing a variety of physicochemical properties as an alternative to Se-containing natural enzymes. Se-based nanozymes have attracted widespread attention due to their unique structure and properties, including their ease of preparation, cost-effectiveness, excellent enzymatic activity and stability.

This review provides an exhaustive categorization and in-depth discussion of Se-based nanozymes, and further explores their potential applications in various fields. First, the Se-based nanozymes were classified into different types of enzymatic activities, including POD-like activity, GPx-like activity, CAT-like activity, SOD-like activity, and multi-enzyme activity including the above enzymatic activities, and the practical applications of Se-based nanozymes were further explored and investigated from the perspectives of different enzymatic activities. Second, the application of Se-based nanozymes was thoroughly investigated in the fields of biomedicine. In the field of biomedicine, the nanozymes have antibacterial, antitumor, antidiabetic, anti-neuroinflammatory and disease diagnosis/biosensing properties.

Despite significant progress in the design, synthesis, and application of Se-based nanozymes, key limitations must be addressed to advance their development (Fig. 21). (1) Mechanistic Clarity: While Se-based nanozymes exhibit diverse enzymatic activities, their catalytic mechanisms (e.g., substrate interactions, reaction pathways in biological systems) remain poorly understood, necessitating in-depth studies to elucidate the origins of their high activity; (2) Biomedical Safety: Current toxicity assessments are limited to low-dose effects, ignoring degradability, long-term exposure risks, dosage-dependent responses, and impacts on distinct biological systems, requiring comprehensive evaluations of distribution, metabolism, and excretion; (3) Therapeutic Efficacy and Targeting: Off-target effects pose significant risks to therapeutic outcomes, highlighting the critical need for precision targeting strategies and improved tissue-specific delivery to enhance drug efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects; (4) Clinical Translation Barriers: As emphasized in Sect. "Classification and catalytic mechanism of Se-based nanozymes", challenges in translating Se-based nanozymes from preclinical research to clinical applications persist, which demand systematic solutions to bridge the gap between laboratory innovation and clinical practice.
