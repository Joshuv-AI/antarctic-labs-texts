Predictive value of clinico-radiological variables in the management of suspected foreign body aspiration in children: A ten-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Asha Jyothi Penugonda, Ilakya Devadas, Koshy Alan Valiaveetil, Timri Palani Murugan, Jovin Chris Antony, Balamurugan Durai, J John Samuel, Rajeev Zachariah Kompithra, John Mathai, Debasis Das Adhikari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Definite history is not always present in children with foreign body aspiration (FBA), hence necessitating a high index of suspicion.Objective:To assess the predictive value of clinico-radiological variables among children presenting with features of suspected FBA and to document their course in a tertiary care teaching hospital.Materials and Methods:In this retrospective observational study, we included children aged below 15 years presenting with clinical features of suspected FBA. Data was obtained from case records. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis (MVA) was performed to determine statistically significant predictors of FBA, and Weighted Risk Scores (WRS) were calculated to determine the significance of the model.Results:Of the 296 children included in the study and having undergone bronchoscopy, 84.5% were toddlers; 269 (90.87%) had a foreign body (FB) removed. Organic material was commonly found with 64.3% being peanut and right main bronchus, the commonest lodging site (50.6%). Of the 54 (18%) children with symptomatic but unwitnessed FBA, 45 (83.3%) had a FB retrieved. Choking with sudden onset cough, stridor, unilateral decreased breath sounds, abnormal chest radiograph, unilateral hyperinflation and mediastinal shift correlated with FBA (P < 0.05). MVA confirmed statistical significance with choking with sudden onset cough to predict FBA (P = 0.024) with overall sensitivity and specificity of 60.9% and 77.8%, respectively. Total WRS with cut-off >2 showed good power of discrimination with sensitivity, specificity and AUC of 60.2%, 77.8% and 0.704%, respectively.Conclusions:The proposed predictive value-based scoring system enhances accuracy in decision-making regarding bronchoscopy in children with suspected FBA.

Introduction

Foreign body aspiration (FBA) is a paediatric emergency that can assume life-threatening proportions.[1] It affects toddlers with a male predominance.[234] The most life-threatening consequence in a foreign body aspiration is acute onset respiratory distress[5] which can result in sudden death, especially among toddlers.[6]

The sudden onset of choking and intractable cough with or without vomiting, defined as ‘penetration syndrome’, is the most common symptom in children with FBA.[7] Presentation can depend on the degree of airway obstruction, and hence, the classic triad of cough, wheeze and decreased breath sounds may not always be present.[8] The sensitivity and specificity of a chest radiograph in diagnosing a FBA can vary from 66 to 88% and from 30 to 71.4%, respectively.[9] FBA can thus be misdiagnosed as asthma, upper respiratory tract infection, pneumonia or croup which can delay the definitive treatment, and lead to persistent/recurrent pneumonia and later on, even to chronic suppurative lung diseases like bronchiectasis.[10] Bronchoscopy is the gold standard for timely diagnosis and therapy of FBA.[1112]

Using the predictive value based on clinico-radiological features to diagnose FBA could improve accuracy in decision-making.[1314] This study aimed to determine the clinico-radiological features and assess their predictive value among children presenting with suspected FBA and evolve a scoring system that can help facilitate the decision to timely bronchoscopy.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study was done in the Paediatric Emergency Services (PES), based on data collected over ten years—from January 2010 to December 2019. All children presenting with clinical features of suspected FBA were included in the study. Children with foreign body in the ear or nose, a history of aspiration of milk or corrosives or hydrocarbons were excluded. Details of demography, clinico-radiological features, complications and management were collected from hospital case records. Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Ethics Committee approval (IRB min no. 13887 dated 24.03.2021) were obtained before starting the study.

Statistical analysis

All the categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Cross-tabulations were used to find the association between the categorical predictor variables and FBA. After checking the normality of continuous data, a nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used, and the median IQR was reported if the distribution was found to be nonnormal. A diagnostic test accuracy measure was used to determine the sensitivity, specificity and AUC using the ROC curve to evaluate the predictive value of predictor variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to find the predictive value of clinico-radiological features of FBA from statistically significant variables in bivariate analysis with P value <0.1. The Weighted Risk Score (WRS) was calculated for each predictor variable by dividing the beta coefficient by the smallest coefficient, and the nearest number was rounded off as an integer. The ROC curve was done by using the WRS and the variables associated with positive FBA. A histogram was used to show the frequency of WRS. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0.0.

The median age of the study population was 18 months (range: 6 months–14 years) with 84.5% toddlers, 12.2% infants and a male/female ratio of 2:1. Median time from symptom onset to presentation to our emergency department was three days (range: 10 min–5 years). At presentation, the mean oxygen saturation was 95% or more.

All the 296 underwent rigid bronchoscopy and 269 (90.8%) had a foreign body removed, while 27 had a negative bronchoscopy. The foreign body retrieval rate among toddlers and older children was 90.4% (226/250) and 93.8% (43/46), respectively.

In 221 children, a foreign body was lodged in the right main bronchus 50.6% and left main bronchus 31.6%, 8.6% were in the trachea, and the rest were more distally. The commonest foreign body retrieved was organic in nature, usually a seed; with peanut being the most common (64.3%, n = 173). The FB commonly encountered was organic in children < three years (95.6%, 215/225), while it was usually inorganic in nature among children > three years (73.2%, 30/41) which was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Inorganic foreign bodies retrieved were reeds of the whistle, parts of pen and plastic beads. In three children, the retrieved foreign body could not be identified since they were crushed and piecemeal.

242/296 (81.8%) had a history suggestive of foreign body ingestion witnessed by the parents including those with sudden onset choking with cough suggestive of laryngeal penetration syndrome, yet only 65 children (26.9%) presented within 24 hours and 58 (23.9%), 21 (8.7%) and 98 (40.5%) presented at 25–48, 49–72 and >72 hours, respectively; 224/242 (92.6%) had FBA confirmed on bronchoscopy. Thirty-six per cent (87/242) of witnessed FBA episodes were initially treated as a respiratory tract infection with nebulizations and antibiotics but eventually required bronchoscopy, which was positive in 90.8% (79/87).

Of the 54/296 (18.2%) without a history of witnessed FBA, the most common clinical features were cough, breathlessness and unilateral decreased breath sounds. None presented within 24 hours while three (5.6%), four (7.4%) and 47 (87%) presented at 25–48, 49–72 and >72 hours, respectively; 83.3% (45/54) had FBA.

Choking, stridor, unilateral decreased breath sounds, abnormal chest radiograph, ipsilateral hyperinflation and mediastinal shift on chest radiographs were significantly associated with FBA with a P value of < 0.05 as shown in Table 2. The incidence of FBA among children with stridor was 100%, but the classic triad of symptoms suggestive of FBA which includes cough, wheezing and decreased breath sounds was present in only 83 (28%) children. Choking with sudden onset cough, noisy breathing and stridor were associated with the foreign body in the trachea, whereas unilateral decreased breath sounds were associated with foreign body in the right or left bronchus, respectively (P = <0.05).

Although stridor and ipsilateral hyperinflation were significantly associated with FBA, stridor (seen in 100% cases of FBA) and unilateral hyperinflation (a subset of abnormal chest radiograph) were not taken forward for further analysis so as to retain the stability of the model.

MVA showed that choking with sudden onset cough significantly predicted FBA with a P value of 0.024 while the model predicted FBA with an overall sensitivity, specificity and AUC of 60.9, 77.8 and 0.755, respectively. Hosmer and Lemeshow test for goodness of fit and calibration for the logistic regression model was 0.908, which also supports the fitness of the model.

The WRS was calculated for each predictor variable by dividing the beta coefficient by the smallest coefficient, and the nearest number was rounded off as an integer. The total WRS ranged from 0 to 8. A score of 0, 1, 2–3, 4–5 and >5 showed FBA in 81.2%, 83.3%, 87.1%, 96.2% and 100%, respectively, as shown in Figure 1.

Weighted Risk Scores and ROC Curve

We then did a ROC curve analysis by using the WRS and the statistically significant variables associated with FBA to assess their diagnostic performance and we found a significant association between WRS and FBA with a P value of < 0.05 [Figure 1].

At a cut-off of >2, the score showed significant discrimination power to detect FBA with a sensitivity and specificity of 60.2 and 77.8, respectively. The AUC was 0.704, which suggests that the model has moderate discriminatory power.

In our study, only 16/296 subjects had a score of 0, of which 4.3% (13/296) had a positive bronchoscopy for FBA. In our study, 26 children with recurrent respiratory symptoms underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) chest and a foreign body was found in CECT among 22 (84.6%) children.

Of the 296 children who underwent bronchoscopy, intraoperative complications like airway oedema, and difficult extraction due to adhesion or impaction were noticed in 10.1% of children, while tear or laceration of the bronchus, bleeding, desaturation, and bradycardia with cardiac arrest intraoperatively were noticed in 2.3%. Of these children, two had complications noted in the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) like secondary pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome and septic shock; and one child succumbed to these complications. The median duration of hospital stays for children who did not require PICU admission was 2.2 days (range 2–5 days, n = 217), while it was 4.0 days (range 2–12 days, n = 79) for those who required PICU admission. Discussion

FBA, which accounts for 0.13% of all admissions to our PES, is associated with significant morbidity and rarely mortality, especially among toddlers. In our study, choking with sudden onset cough, stridor, unilateral decreased breath sounds, abnormal chest radiographs with unilateral hyperinflation and mediastinal shift showed a statistically significant association with FBA; with stridor being an ominous sign present in 100% of cases of bronchoscopy proven FBA. On MVA, WRS for choking with sudden onset cough, unilateral decreased breath sounds, abnormal chest radiographs and mediastinal shift were 3, 1, 1 and 3, respectively. The scoring system for FBA derived from the study yielded a predictive value of 81.2%, 83.3%, 87.1%, 96.2% and 100% for cumulative WRS scores of 0, 1, 2–3, 4–5 and >5, respectively.

The median age of our study population was 18 months (range—6 months to 14 years) comparable to other studies.[515] Toddlers are particularly vulnerable to FBA which has been attributed to the development of pincer grasp, the tendency of children to put objects in their mouths, inadequate dentition, lack of well-coordinated swallowing reflex and oral explorative behaviour.[1516] Our study showed that inherently adventurous boys were the most affected.[12]

Rigid bronchoscopy is the gold standard treatment for FBA.[12] In our study, a foreign body was retrieved in 269 (90.8%) which was a higher incidence of foreign body removal as compared to other studies.[517] Peanut was the most common organic foreign body, while whistle was the common inorganic object removed, similar to other studies.[518] The most common location of the foreign body was the right main bronchus followed by the left main bronchus, trachea and others which included secondary and tertiary bronchi. However, the angle made by the main bronchi with trachea is similar on both sides until the age of 15 years, resulting in an expected equal incidence of foreign body in either bronchus.[19] Our study showed a 19% higher incidence of foreign body lodgement in the right main bronchus as opposed to the left main bronchus. Indeed, a careful examination must be done during bronchoscopy on either side.

Intraoperative complications were encountered in 12.5% (37/296) such as desaturation, bleeding, bradycardia and cardiopulmonary arrest. These complications have been documented in previously published similar studies.[92021] The difficulties faced during bronchoscopy were due to the spherical shape of the foreign body, bronchospasm, prolonged duration of foreign body impaction, granulation tissue formation and increased secretions.

26.7% (79/296) of children including those with intraoperative complications, preoperative dyspnoea and respiratory failure were observed in the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU). Two children developed complications during PICU stay, of whom one (0.3%) died. Our death rate is lower than death rates (0.42–2%) in previously published studies.[2223]

In our study, 81.8% (n = 242) children had a history of witnessed FBA, yet only 65 children (26.9%) presented within 24 hours. Also, 87% (47/54) of children without a history of witnessed FBA presented after 72 hours. Moreover, 32% (87/269) of children who were initially treated as a respiratory tract infection were later diagnosed as FBA. Thus, FBA is unrecognized by parents or a wrong diagnosis due to other differentials can lead to late diagnosis.[15] All of the above highlight the need for a scoring system to enhance diagnostic accuracy and prevent delay in diagnosis.

In our study, the significant clinico-radiological variables associated with FBA as compared to previously published studies were—choking with sudden onset cough,[1314] stridor,[14] unilateral reduced air entry,[131424] abnormal chest radiograph,[131424] unilateral hyperinflation[1324] and mediastinal shift.[24] Thirty-six per cent of children with normal chest radiograph in our study had FBA confirmed on bronchoscopy, while other studies have showed normal chest radiographs in 3.5–20%.[25] Divarci et al.[25] showed that a combination of positive history with clinical and radiologic findings had 91% sensitivity in predicting FBA.

MVA of statistically significant variables with adequate numbers showed that choking with sudden onset cough was significantly associated with FBA and the cumulative sensitivity and specificity were 60.9% and 77.8%, respectively. The devised scoring system in our study showed that a score of >5 had a predictive value of 100%, while a score of 0 did not rule out the diagnosis. Cumulative WRS with cut-off >2 showed good power of discrimination with sensitivity of 60.2%, specificity of 77.8%, AUC of 0.704 and 100% predictive value for score >5, comparable to studies done by Fasseeh et al. and Özyüksel et al.[1324]

Two recent Middle Eastern study publications from Israel, the first formulating a Foreign Body Aspiration Score (FOBAS) based on a retrospective study[26] and the second, prospectively validating the derived FOBAS algorithm[27] showed high sensitivity and specificity for the presence of FBA in children, leading to better clinical outcomes. The FOBAS parameters of significance—clinical features of a choking episode, sudden cough, exposure to nuts, absence of fever and rhinitis, stridor, and unilateral auscultatory and radiological findings are comparable to the significant clinico-radiological parameters derived from our study. Our scoring system to predict the FBA diagnosis can help in early decision-making, thereby enabling timely management of children with FBA.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study from Asia that has devised a scoring system to predict FBA in children based on clinico-radiological parameters. Although there are a few similar studies from the Middle East and Europe, most are single-centre studies, retrospective in nature, with small sample sizes thereby inherently entailing a high risk of bias.[28]

Nevertheless, per PROBAST criteria (2019)[29] for participants, predictors, outcome and analysis, the risk of bias in our study is lower for the following reasons. Unlike previously published studies, our study had also included subjects not initially admitted for suspected FBA but later found bronchoscopy positive in the course of their illness. Moreover, the number of Events Per Variable (EPV) in our study was >10, thereby avoiding overfitting.[2829]

Overall, our study, while reiterating the necessity of bronchoscopy for every suspected case of FBA, has devised a scoring system that enhances the diagnostic accuracy of FBA.

Conclusions and Key Messages -Implications for the Primary Care Physician

The role of the primary care physician is pivotal in not only creating parental awareness towards prevention of FBA, but also in early recognition, initial management and timely referral for bronchoscopy in suspected FBA. The primary care physician should instruct parents and caregivers to closely monitor infants and toddlers, especially during feeding and play. Parents should also be educated about measures to make surroundings litter-proof and safe for ambulant preschoolers and to recognize instantly the seriousness of the symptomatology of laryngeal penetration.

The primary care physician is usually the first point of contact in a case of suspected FBA. In acute airway obstruction, the importance of performing emergency steps to clear the airway if the child cannot cough, vocalize or breathe (Heimlich manoeuvre in a child above one year/alternating sequences of five back blows and five chest thrusts for infants); and chest compressions (cardiopulmonary resuscitation—30 compressions and two breaths) if the infant or child becomes unresponsive, while avoiding blind finger sweeps, cannot be overemphasized.[30]

However, as our study clearly highlights, clinico-radiological features may not be classical for all children with suspected foreign body aspiration, which could lead to a delay in diagnosis. A high index of suspicion is needed to diagnose foreign body aspiration among children, especially toddlers.

Hence, assessing the predictive value of significantly associated clinical and radiological features of foreign body aspiration is critical, so that a predictive value-based scoring system can be derived to help in early decision making. However, only a few such studies have been published globally and to our knowledge, none from Asia.

In our ten-year retrospective cohort study, we have derived a new scoring system based on the predictive value of significant clinico-radiological parameters, which would enable the clinician to enhance diagnostic accuracy and decide on early bronchoscopic intervention in this potentially life-threatening emergency in children.
