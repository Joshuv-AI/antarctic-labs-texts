Multimodal deep learning and genetic algorithms for adaptive optimization of injection stretch blow molding with nonconformal temperature control
Authors: Nahyun Kim, Yongjae Jeon, Pil-Ho Lee, Sang Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In blow molding with non-conformal temperature control, changes in environmental thermal conditions can affect product quality even when machine settings remain unchanged. Conventional studies often rely on offline quality evaluation or physics-based simulations, which are computationally expensive and difficult to deploy for in-situ process adjustment. This study proposes an in-situ adaptive optimization framework that integrates multimodal deep learning with a genetic algorithm. Thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables are used to predict the post-heating preform temperature distribution. A weighted loss function is paired with dropout regularization to improve performance in quality-sensitive regions. The estimated temperature distribution then guides the search for lamp settings that satisfy target quality constraints. Condition-level 5-fold cross-validation showed that the optimized multimodal prediction model achieved MAEs of 2.299 ± 0.245 °C for full thermal images and 1.174 ± 0.223 °C for region of interest (ROI) centerline surface-temperature profiles. The proposed optimization framework suppressed representative defect modes in the evaluated validation cases, including whitening, incomplete molding, and combined whitening–bursting. During prolonged operation, thermal drift was addressed through re-optimization with updated environmental inputs, restoring stable product quality. The computational optimization was completed within 10 s in the main validation cases, including 7.94 s for adaptive re-optimization under thermal drift, which is substantially shorter than the available process window of 115.4 s before blowing. This reported time represents the calculation time required to identify updated lamp settings and should be distinguished from the physical response time required for the heating system and preform to realize the optimized temperature field. Overall, the study indicates that multimodal temperature prediction and genetic algorithm-based optimization can support supervisory adaptive process adjustment while remaining compatible with the practical process cycle of thermally sensitive manufacturing processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-69683-0.

Introduction

Injection stretch blow molding (ISBM) is widely used to manufacture polyethylene terephthalate (PET) containers for beverage, packaging, and industrial applications. In ISBM, an injection-molded preform is reheated above its glass transition temperature and then biaxially stretched in a mold cavity by a stretch rod and internal air pressure1. Because it can produce lightweight and mechanically robust containers at high throughput, ISBM plays an important role in PET container manufacturing, which has grown into a global market worth hundreds of billions of dollars2,3. As demand grows for containers with more complex geometries and improved functional performance, precise and stable control of the ISBM process has become increasingly important.

In conventional blow molding, preforms are typically heated uniformly before deformation. For products with complex geometries, however, uniform heating is often insufficient because it does not provide the local temperature control required for stable deformation. In non-conformal blow molding, a non-uniform axial temperature profile is intentionally introduced to control local deformation according to the target product geometry. Therefore, representative defects such as whitening, incomplete molding, and bursting are better interpreted as being associated with local deviations from a quality-qualified temperature profile required for stable deformation, rather than with temperature non-uniformity itself. Non-conformal temperature control addresses this issue by independently adjusting multiple heating lamps along the preform length, thereby forming an axial thermal profile suited to the required deformation behavior. With this localized heating strategy, material deformation can be controlled more precisely, leading to improved product quality. Previous studies have shown that molding defects and quality variations in ISBM are closely related to the coupled effects of preform temperature, strain rate, stretching history, blowing pressure, and PET material behavior near the glass transition temperature4–6. Excessive local deformation or thermomechanical instability can lead to bursting or severe wall-thickness nonuniformity, whereas insufficient local heating or inadequate material transport can result in incomplete molding. Whitening has also been associated with local structural changes during stretching, such as strain-induced crystallization or deformation-induced optical scattering, and is therefore influenced by both the local thermal state and deformation history. Accordingly, previous studies have used constitutive modeling, self-heating analysis, finite element simulation, and preform temperature optimization to predict deformation behavior, temperature evolution, and final thickness distribution in stretch blow molding4–7. More recent work has also emphasized the importance of identifying material-model parameters from observed ISBM data to improve predictive reliability8.

This additional control capability also increases the difficulty of parameter selection. Because each lamp zone has its own setting, the process involves multiple interacting variables, and the resulting search space becomes difficult to explore through manual trial-and-error adjustment. The problem is further complicated by thermal drift during operation. Changes in heater and fan ambient temperatures and heat accumulation inside the equipment can shift the thermal state of the process during continuous operation, so identical parameter settings do not always produce the same product quality.

From the viewpoint of process physics, the preform temperature distribution before blowing directly affects material stretching behavior and, consequently, the final thickness distribution and mechanical properties of the molded product9–11. Although finite element-based simulations are useful for analyzing these thermal–mechanical interactions, their computational cost limits their practical use for rapid in-situ process adjustment during industrial operation.

Data-driven modeling has recently become a practical alternative for predicting process quality in manufacturing systems. In particular, multimodal deep learning can combine heterogeneous data sources, including images, sensor signals, and process parameters, to learn nonlinear relationships that are difficult to represent using a single data type12,13. However, most blow molding studies have focused on offline analysis, material-model calibration, or simulation-based optimization under fixed thermal conditions, and relatively little attention has been given to adaptive optimization using measured preform temperature distributions and equipment-level thermal drift information during industrial operation. Although optimization methods, including genetic algorithm (GA)-based approaches, have been applied to stretch blow molding and other manufacturing problems7,14–16, their practical effectiveness depends strongly on prediction accuracy and may be limited when the optimization relies mainly on simulation-derived data or fixed environmental conditions.

This study therefore proposes an in-situ adaptive optimization framework that couples multimodal temperature prediction with genetic algorithm-based process optimization. The predictive model estimates the post-heating temperature distribution of the preform from thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables measured during industrial operation. The predicted temperature field is then used as a process-state indicator to evaluate candidate settings and guide the search for optimized lamp temperature settings.

The proposed framework is validated on industrial-scale blow molding equipment under defect-inducing and changing thermal conditions, showing its ability to suppress representative defects and support stable product quality under the evaluated conditions. The multimodal design reflects the complementary information contained in each input: thermal images represent the spatial thermal state of the preform, lamp settings describe the applied heating conditions, and environmental variables capture thermal drift inside the equipment. By integrating these inputs, the model can represent process-dependent thermal variations more accurately than single-modality approaches. Non-conformal blow molding process and data acquisition

Process description

In the ISBM process considered in this study, an injection-molded preform is reheated above its glass transition temperature and then biaxially stretched using a stretch rod and internal air pressure to form the final product. The preform temperature distribution before blowing is one of the primary factors governing deformation behavior and final product quality.

The blow molding sequence used in this study is shown in Fig. 2. First, the preform is non-uniformly heated by independently controlled heaters to form an axial temperature profile. Unlike conventional uniform heating, this approach selectively heats specific regions of the preform according to the required deformation behavior. The temperature-conditioned preform is then transferred to the mold, where it undergoes axial stretching and radial expansion driven by the stretch rod and internal air pressure. This thermo-mechanical sequence produces the final molded product.

Overview of the blow molding process.

Controlling the temperature distribution makes it possible to regulate localized deformation during blowing. However, it also increases control complexity because multiple lamp temperature settings must be determined simultaneously. In the present system, the heating profile is generated by independently controlled lamp zones, making the process sensitive to both lamp settings and surrounding thermal conditions. Therefore, representative defects such as whitening, incomplete molding, and bursting are better interpreted as being associated with deviations from the intended quality-qualified thermal profile, rather than with temperature non-uniformity itself, highlighting the need for accurate thermal control. In this study, these representative defect types were selected based on a failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)-based process defect analysis, and the detailed evaluation criteria and scoring results are provided in supplementary material.

Data acquisition and dataset construction

Multimodal data were collected from the blow molding process to model the relationship between process conditions and the resulting temperature distribution. The dataset consists of thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables.

Fig. 3Multimodal data including thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables.

Multimodal data including thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables.

Figure 3 shows the overall structure of the collected data. Thermal images were acquired before and after heating using an optris PI XI 400 infrared camera to obtain spatial temperature information of the preform. The emissivity setting of the camera was fixed at 0.95 throughout all measurements based on the manufacturer’s guideline for plastics measured in the long-wave IR range. Emissivity variation during heating was not independently measured or dynamically corrected in this study. Lamp temperature settings for each heating zone were obtained from the programmable logic controller (PLC) as set values (SVs). Experimental data were acquired only when the corresponding process values (PVs) reached the allowable threshold range, ensuring stable heating conditions before image acquisition. Environmental variables, including temperatures near the heater and fan, were measured using non-contact sensors to represent the thermal conditions inside the equipment. These heterogeneous inputs respectively describe the spatial thermal state of the preform, the control inputs applied to the process, and the thermal conditions inside the equipment. Heating time was not included as an input variable because it was fixed throughout the experiments and therefore did not provide independent variation for model training.

Before constructing the dataset, correlation-based influence analysis was performed to examine the relative effect of each lamp on the preform temperature distribution. As shown in Supplementary C and Fig. S3, the analysis includes both the pointwise relative influence of each lamp along the region of interest (ROI) centerline surface-temperature profile and the actual geometric relationship between the heaters and the preform. The results showed that Lamp 10 did not have the lowest influence overall, but belonged to the lower-influence group among the ten lamps. This trend is consistent with the heater–preform geometry, where Lamp 10 is mainly associated with the upper preform region and partially overlaps a region outside the main effective preform body. Therefore, nine lamp variables were selected for the subsequent experimental design and model development to reduce input redundancy while retaining the dominant thermal control effects of the heating system.

To ensure consistency between model inputs and outputs, all thermal images were acquired under fixed experimental conditions. As shown in Fig. 4, the preform region was extracted from each raw thermal image, and a region of interest (ROI) was defined to standardize the spatial domain. The final cropped image size was 180 × 40 pixels. In Fig. 4, the x- and y-axes represent pixel coordinates of the cropped ROI, and the color bar indicates temperature in °C with a range of 0 to 130 °C. The physical length scale is indicated by the measured spatial conversion factor of approximately 0.82 mm/pixel in the preform image.

The collected data were then organized into a dataset for training the multimodal prediction model. A fractional factorial design based on an orthogonal array was used to efficiently cover the high-dimensional process space defined by the lamp variables. A full factorial design with nine lamp variables and five levels per variable would require 1,953,125 experimental conditions, which is impractical in terms of time and cost. In contrast, fractional factorial designs can evaluate the effects of multiple controllable factors with a substantially smaller number of experiments17. Orthogonality and balanced level allocation further improve the efficiency of experimental design by allowing multiple factor effects to be analyzed under limited experimental resources18.

In this study, five temperature levels with 5 °C intervals were assigned to each of the nine lamp variables. A total of 51 experimental conditions were generated, consisting of 50 orthogonal-array-based conditions and one additional center condition. Each condition was repeated twice, resulting in a total of 102 cases. The experiments were repeated to improve data reliability and reduce measurement variability. Detailed physical and thermal properties of the resin, including density, crystallinity, crystalline peak melting point, and thermal conductivity, are provided in Supplementary Table S11.

Multimodal temperature prediction model

Problem formulation

The objective of the prediction model is to predict the post-heating temperature distribution of the preform from measured process conditions. The temperature distribution before blowing is a critical state variable because it directly influences deformation behavior and final product quality. Therefore, accurate estimation of the post-heating temperature field is essential for optimizing non-conformal blow molding.

Accordingly, a data-driven model is used to describe the relationship between process inputs and the resulting temperature distribution. The inputs consist of pre-heating thermal images, lamp temperature settings, and environmental variables, while the output is the post-heating thermal image of the preform. The model must account for both control inputs and environmental variables, because identical lamp settings can produce different preform temperatures when heater and fan ambient temperatures change during operation.

By learning this relationship, the model estimates the thermal state of the preform under given process conditions. In Sect. 4, this predicted temperature distribution is used as a surrogate process state for optimization. The ROI centerline surface-temperature profile extracted from the predicted image is also used as the key quantity for fitness evaluation in the genetic algorithm. In this study, this profile refers to the pixel-wise surface-temperature values extracted along the central axial line of the cropped ROI in the infrared thermal image, not to the internal centerline temperature of the preform.

Model architecture

Figure 5 illustrates the structure of the multimodal deep learning model used in this study. Multimodal deep learning learns representations from multiple information sources by processing and combining heterogeneous data through dedicated network layers19,20. Based on this concept, the proposed model uses separate neural network components to process heterogeneous input data.

Thermal images are processed by a two-dimensional convolutional neural network (2D CNN) to extract spatial temperature features. Lamp temperature settings are treated as sequential inputs and processed by a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN). This design reflects the axial arrangement of the heating lamps and their overlapping thermal influence along the preform length. Environmental variables, including heater and fan temperatures, are processed by a fully connected neural network because they provide scalar information on the thermal conditions inside the equipment.

Features extracted from each modality are then combined into a unified representation and passed to a decoder network to reconstruct the post-heating temperature distribution. This structure allows the model to learn nonlinear relationships among the input variables and their combined effects on the temperature distribution. This multimodal architecture is suitable for the present process because it integrates spatial thermal information, control inputs, and environmental variables within a single predictive framework.

Training and validation

The model was trained using the dataset collected from the blow molding process, and its performance was evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and measured temperature distributions. MAE was selected because it directly represents the temperature prediction error in degrees Celsius. Although weighted mean absolute error (wMAE) was used as the training loss to empirically emphasize the heated preform region, MAE was used as the evaluation metric to provide a direct and physically interpretable error value in degrees Celsius.

To improve prediction stability in the heated preform region, weighted mean absolute error (wMAE) was used as the training loss function. In the wMAE calculation, the weight assigned to each pixel was defined as the measured temperature value of that pixel in the ground-truth thermal image expressed in °C. Therefore, the weights were assigned pixel-wise rather than as manually selected discrete constants. This weighting was used as an empirical modeling strategy to emphasize the heated preform region and reduce the relative influence of the low-temperature background during model training. However, directly using temperature values in °C as weights is temperature-scale dependent and should not be interpreted as a scale-invariant physical weighting scheme. In addition, higher-temperature regions are not universally more important for product quality, because insufficient local heating can also cause incomplete molding. Therefore, the wMAE formulation is interpreted here as an empirical training strategy for improving prediction stability in the present dataset, rather than as a general physical weighting criterion for defect formation. Dropout layers were also incorporated into the network to reduce overfitting and improve generalization. Dropout is a regularization technique that randomly deactivates a fraction of neurons during training, thereby reducing co-adaptation and overfitting21. Several dropout configurations were evaluated to identify an appropriate setting. A dropout rate of 0.2 degraded performance, producing an MAE of 5.5566 °C, whereas two dropout layers with a rate of 0.5 improved prediction accuracy and reduced the error to 3.2597 °C. This result indicates that appropriate regularization is important for stabilizing spatial temperature prediction.

To further improve model performance, grid-search-based hyperparameter tuning was performed for key network parameters, including the learning rate, convolutional kernel size, number of filters, and number of hidden units. The optimized model achieved the lowest prediction error of 2.9487 °C, showing the combined effect of wMAE, dropout, and hyperparameter optimization.

Table 2 summarizes the performance of the different model configurations and shows the improvement obtained by adding each component. The results show that the proposed multimodal model with wMAE, dropout, and optimized hyperparameters provides the most accurate and stable prediction of the preform temperature distribution. These improvements are important for the subsequent optimization stage because the accuracy and stability of the temperature prediction model directly affect the reliability of the optimization results. Rather than representing a universal sufficiency threshold, the final MAE of 2.9487 °C was interpreted as practically useful surrogate accuracy for the present optimization task2. This interpretation is supported by the improved ROI centerline surface-temperature profile, which is directly used in the GA fitness evaluation.

Figure 7 compares the predicted temperature distributions and corresponding ROI centerline surface-temperature profiles for different model configurations under identical input conditions. As wMAE, dropout, and hyperparameter tuning are added, the overfitting patterns observed in the preform region are gradually reduced, and the predicted thermal images show smoother boundaries and improved spatial consistency.

The ROI centerline surface-temperature profiles also show improved prediction stability. The baseline model produces large fluctuations and discontinuities in the temperature profile, indicating unstable prediction behavior. In contrast, the final model produces a smoother and more continuous temperature profile that better follows the physical trend of the measured preform temperature distribution. Because the ROI centerline surface-temperature profile is used for genetic algorithm fitness evaluation in Sect. 4, its accuracy and continuity are particularly important. These results indicate that the proposed model not only reduces prediction error but also provides stable and process-relevant temperature estimates, which are important for reliable process optimization.

Condition-level cross-validation for generalization assessment

To evaluate the generalization ability of the prediction model under previously unseen process conditions, condition-level 5-fold cross-validation was performed using the 102 experimental cases. The data were split at the experimental-condition level rather than at the individual-sample level; therefore, repeated cases obtained under the same condition were assigned to the same fold to prevent information leakage between the training and validation sets. For each fold, the model was trained on four folds and evaluated on the remaining fold. Both the full thermal image MAE and the ROI centerline surface-temperature profile MAE were calculated. The latter was additionally evaluated because it is directly used as the fitness quantity in the genetic algorithm-based optimization. The condition-level 5-fold cross-validation results are summarized in Table 3.

The final optimized model achieved a full thermal image MAE of 2.299 ± 0.245 °C and a ROI centerline surface-temperature profile MAE of 1.174 ± 0.223 °C across the five folds. These results support the generalization capability of the model within the investigated process space. Compared with the previously reported MAE of 2.9487 °C in Table 2, the condition-level cross-validation results indicate that the reported prediction accuracy was not limited to a single data split. It should be noted that hyperparameter tuning was not performed independently within each training fold. The final model configuration was selected using the available dataset, and condition-level 5-fold cross-validation was then performed to evaluate this selected configuration. Therefore, the validation should be interpreted as a condition-level assessment of the selected model rather than as a fully nested cross-validation procedure. This non-nested strategy may slightly overestimate generalization performance, and independent nested validation with larger datasets remains a topic for future work.

Process optimization and validation

Optimization problem formulation

The objective of process optimization is to determine lamp temperature settings that produce a post-heating preform temperature distribution corresponding to defect-free forming conditions. In non-conformal blow molding, this optimization problem is challenging because the heating profile is controlled by multiple independent lamp zones, while the resulting temperature field is affected by nonlinear thermal interactions and changing environmental conditions. Therefore, simple trial-and-error tuning is insufficient for identifying reliable process parameters under practical operating conditions.

Let x denote the vector of lamp temperature settings, and let the current process state be defined by the pre-heating thermal image and environmental measurements. For each candidate solution, the post-heating temperature distribution is estimated using the multimodal temperature prediction model developed in Sect. 3. The optimization objective is to minimize the deviation between the predicted ROI centerline surface-temperature profile and a predefined target profile associated with defect-free molding under the investigated conditions. Thus, the optimization is performed not directly on the final product geometry, but on a physically meaningful thermal state variable that governs subsequent deformation.

Accordingly, the fitness function was defined as the mean absolute error between the predicted ROI centerline surface-temperature profile and the target profile. The target profile was defined as a quality-qualified reference surface-temperature profile obtained from experimentally confirmed defect-free molding conditions for the investigated PET preform and container geometry. Expert process knowledge was used to confirm that this reference profile was consistent with stable axial and radial deformation behavior during blowing. This target profile was not intended to represent a universal optimum for all products, materials, or cycle conditions, but served as a practical reference state for guiding lamp-setting optimization within the investigated process window. The GA was terminated when the MAE between the predicted ROI centerline surface-temperature profile and the target profile became lower than 3 °C. This formulation is consistent with the practical control objective of driving the preform temperature field toward a quality-qualified thermal condition before blowing.

A genetic algorithm was adopted because the optimization problem is high-dimensional, nonlinear, and not directly differentiable22. The control space includes nine lamp variables, and even five-level discretization produces a combinatorial search space that is impractical to explore exhaustively. In addition, the optimization objective is evaluated through a learned multimodal predictor and an image-derived centerline profile rather than an explicit analytical function. Under these conditions, a population-based global search strategy is more appropriate than gradient-based optimization or manual tuning. Therefore, the GA is used not as a generic optimizer, but as a search method for process parameters within a surrogate-defined thermal state space.

Multimodal prediction–GA integrated optimization framework

Figure 8 shows the proposed optimization framework. The multimodal prediction model developed in Sect. 3 serves as a surrogate evaluator within the GA loop. For the current process state, the pre-heating thermal image and environmental variables are first obtained from the equipment, and candidate lamp temperature settings are generated as chromosomes in the GA population. Each candidate solution is then evaluated by the trained multimodal model, which predicts the corresponding post-heating temperature distribution. The ROI centerline surface-temperature profile is extracted from the predicted image and compared with the target profile to calculate the fitness value.

Based on the fitness values, the GA iteratively updates the population through selection, uniform crossover, and mutation until the termination condition is met. In this study, the GA was configured with a population size of 20, five parents, a mutation rate of 0.1, and a maximum of 200 iterations. The population size was selected to balance search diversity and computational efficiency: a larger population increases the number of candidate evaluations per generation, whereas an excessively small population may reduce search diversity and increase the risk of premature convergence23. Uniform crossover was used to preserve search diversity across lamp variables by selecting genes from two parent chromosomes according to a crossover mask24. Mutation was then applied to randomly perturb genes in the offspring population, reducing the risk of premature convergence to local optima and supporting global exploration25. If the predicted centerline profile satisfies the target tolerance, the corresponding lamp settings are selected as the optimized process parameters. Otherwise, a new generation is generated and evaluated through the same predictor–fitness loop.

The key feature of the proposed framework is the direct integration of a multimodal temperature predictor trained on real process data with GA-based optimization under thermal drift during operation. While previous GA-based blow molding optimization studies have largely relied on finite element method (FEM) or simulation-derived data, the present framework evaluates candidate solutions using real thermal images, actual lamp settings, and environmental measurements. As a result, the optimization accounts for both the intended control inputs and the current thermal condition of the equipment, enabling adaptive process adjustment under practical manufacturing conditions.

Optimization results and validation

The proposed optimization framework was validated on industrial blow molding equipment under defect-inducing conditions and changing thermal conditions. The validation considered three representative defect scenarios selected from the FMEA analysis provided in the supplementary material—whitening, incomplete molding, and combined whitening–bursting—as well as environmental drift during prolonged operation. Defect suppression, convergence behavior, optimization time, and practical applicability within the process cycle were used as the main evaluation criteria. The defect-inducing validation procedure is schematically illustrated in Fig. 9.

The framework was first evaluated under defect-inducing conditions. Under the conventional process settings, defects were observed in all representative cases. After applying the optimized lamp settings obtained from the proposed multimodal prediction–GA framework, the representative defects were suppressed and defect-free products were obtained in the evaluated cases. Convergence behavior and computational efficiency were evaluated using the iteration count and computational optimization time, which were 3–4 iterations and 4.3–9.7 s, respectively. Here, the optimization time refers only to the computational time required for the GA to identify updated lamp temperature settings. It does not include the physical response time required for the heating system and preform to reach the corresponding temperature field after the updated settings are applied. Detailed termination records for each defect scenario are provided in supplementary material. These results indicate that the proposed framework can identify practically valid process parameters under different initial defect conditions.

Figure 10 summarizes representative defect suppression results, including predicted preform temperature distributions and corresponding molded product images. In each case, the optimized lamp settings changed the predicted preform temperature distribution according to the defect type and improved the molded product quality. In the whitening case, the localized high-temperature concentration near the upper region was reduced. In the incomplete molding case, the temperature in the insufficiently heated region was increased. In the combined whitening–bursting case, the concentrated high-temperature region was redistributed into a more stable profile. These changes indicate that the optimization framework does not simply search for arbitrary settings, but drives the thermal state toward a deformation-favorable condition.

The robustness of the framework was further evaluated under thermal drift during prolonged operation. Figure 11 schematically illustrates the environmental robustness validation procedure. In the initial state, defect-free production was achieved after optimization at heater and fan ambient temperatures of 29 °C and 22 °C, respectively. However, after approximately 260 cycles of continuous operation, thermal accumulation increased these temperatures to 37 °C and 24 °C, and whitening reappeared under the previously optimized fixed settings. Reapplying the proposed optimization framework with the updated environmental inputs produced a new set of lamp temperatures and restored defect-free product quality. The re-optimization converged in four iterations and required 7.94 s of computational time, indicating that the framework can calculate updated process parameters rapidly after environmental drift. However, the physical realization of the updated thermal state depends on the response of the lamps, the surrounding thermal environment, and the preform heating dynamics after the new settings are applied.

Figure 12 presents the adaptive optimization results under changing thermal conditions, including the initial optimization and re-optimization processes. The corresponding environmental conditions and optimization states are summarized in Table 4, showing that the framework can respond to environmental drift instead of assuming fixed process conditions.

Optimization performance also depended strongly on the prediction quality of the multimodal model. In an additional model-quality comparison, a lower-accuracy prediction model failed to satisfy the termination condition, whereas the final optimized model satisfied the termination condition in five iterations and 12.8 s. This result supports the conclusion in Sect. 3 that stable and process-relevant temperature prediction is important for reliable optimization. Thus, the contribution of this study lies not in applying GA alone, but in effectively coupling GA with a process-relevant surrogate model trained on real data.

Finally, the practical applicability of the proposed framework for adaptive optimization was evaluated. In the actual equipment configuration, the available time window between preform loading and blowing was approximately 115.4 s. The computational optimization was completed within 4.3–9.7 s in the main validation cases, including 7.94 s for adaptive re-optimization under thermal drift. Because these computation times are substantially shorter than the available process window, the calculation step itself is compatible with the practical process cycle of the blow molding process. However, the reported optimization time should be distinguished from the physical response time of the heating system and preform. After updated lamp settings are transferred to the equipment, the preform temperature field changes according to the thermal response of the lamps, the surrounding thermal environment, and the preform heating dynamics. Therefore, the proposed framework should be interpreted as a supervisory adaptive optimization strategy that rapidly updates lamp settings, while practical implementation must account for possible transition cycles before the optimized temperature profile is fully established. Overall, the results show that the proposed multimodal prediction–GA framework supports supervisory adaptive process optimization by linking multimodal thermal-state prediction with real-process parameter optimization.

Discussion

This study suggests that the preform temperature field can serve as a useful process-state variable for non-conformal blow molding under the investigated conditions. Rather than relying only on post-process quality inspection or indirect quality indicators, the proposed framework predicts the preform thermal state before blowing and uses it as the basis for process optimization. Because the preform temperature distribution strongly influences subsequent deformation behavior, this approach provides a process-relevant basis for guiding product-quality control.

The results emphasize the importance of multimodal data fusion for robust temperature prediction. Thermal images describe the spatial thermal state of the preform, lamp temperature settings represent the applied heating inputs, and environmental variables capture thermal drift inside the equipment. Integrating these complementary data sources allows the model to better represent the actual thermal response of the process under varying operating conditions. The use of wMAE and dropout further improves prediction stability by increasing accuracy in quality-critical regions and reducing overfitting.

Integrating the prediction model with a genetic algorithm provides a practical approach to high-dimensional and nonlinear process optimization. Non-conformal blow molding involves multiple lamp variables and nonlinear thermal interactions, and the quality-oriented objective does not provide explicit gradient information. Under these conditions, the GA is not used as a standalone optimizer; instead, it is coupled with a multimodal predictor trained on real process data to evaluate candidate lamp settings based on the predicted thermal state. For practical implementation, the proposed framework should be regarded as a supervisory adaptive optimization method rather than an instantaneous closed-loop controller. The GA-based optimization rapidly calculates updated lamp settings, but the actual formation of the optimized preform temperature field is governed by the thermal response of the lamps, the equipment environment, and the preform. Accordingly, transition cycles may be required after applying new lamp settings, and these cycles should be considered when implementing the framework in continuous production. In industrial operation, the proposed framework could also be extended to preemptive re-optimization before visible defects occur. Instead of triggering re-optimization only after product defects are observed, the environmental variables, predicted ROI centerline surface-temperature profile, and deviation from the quality-qualified reference profile could be continuously monitored. When the predicted profile deviation or thermal-drift trend exceeds a predefined threshold, GA-based re-optimization could be triggered to update the lamp settings before the process reaches a defect-inducing state. The threshold for such preemptive re-optimization should be determined from long-term production data linking thermal drift, profile deviation, and product quality, and this remains an important topic for future work.

The quality-qualified reference profile used as the target represents a defect-free thermal state confirmed for the investigated preform and container geometry. In practical terms, this profile is consistent with sufficient local stretchability and reduced excessive thermal concentration along the ROI centerline surface-temperature profile. Therefore, the optimization objective was to reduce local deviations from this experimentally confirmed reference state rather than to impose a uniform temperature distribution. The relationship between the optimized temperature profile and defect suppression should be interpreted as a process-relevant correlation supported by the validation experiments, rather than as a complete mechanistic proof of defect formation. In the whitening case, the defect-inducing condition showed a local deviation from the quality-qualified reference profile, and defect-free molding was obtained after the optimized settings reduced this deviation. In the incomplete molding case, the optimized profile increased the temperature in the insufficiently heated region, which is consistent with improved local stretchability of the PET preform. In the combined whitening–bursting case, the optimized settings redistributed the concentrated thermal region toward the reference profile, which is consistent with more stable deformation during blowing. However, the present study did not directly measure local strain evolution, wall-thickness distribution, or mold-side thermal history. Therefore, the proposed framework indicates that reducing deviations from a quality-qualified surface-temperature profile can support suppression of representative defect cases under the tested conditions, while the detailed physical mechanisms of individual defect modes require further investigation.

Several limitations should be addressed in future work. The framework was validated using a specific preform geometry and equipment configuration; therefore, its generalizability to other product shapes or machine setups requires further investigation. In addition, although the computational optimization time was shorter than the available process window, the physical response time required for the heating system and preform to fully realize the optimized temperature field was not independently quantified in this study. The target temperature profile was defined as a quality-qualified reference profile for the specific PET preform, container geometry, and equipment configuration investigated in this study; therefore, a different target profile may be required for different products, shorter cycle times, or alternative quality objectives. In addition, the optimization variables were limited to lamp temperature settings, and other process variables, such as blowing pressure, stretching speed, and cooling conditions, were not considered. Future work should extend the framework to additional process variables, automate target-profile definition, and link the predicted temperature field more directly to final product properties such as thickness distribution and mechanical performance.

Conclusion

This study proposed a data-driven optimization framework that integrates multimodal temperature prediction with genetic algorithm-based process optimization for non-conformal blow molding. The framework was designed to predict the post-heating preform temperature distribution and use it as a surrogate process state for deriving optimized lamp temperature settings.

The optimized multimodal model achieved condition-level 5-fold cross-validation MAEs of 2.299 ± 0.245 °C for full thermal images and 1.174 ± 0.223 °C for ROI centerline surface-temperature profiles. Based on the predicted temperature field, the multimodal prediction–GA framework derived process conditions that suppressed representative defects and produced defect-free products in the evaluated validation cases, including whitening, incomplete molding, and combined whitening–bursting.

Experimental validation on industrial equipment further demonstrated that the proposed framework can adapt to changing thermal conditions during continuous operation. After thermal drift occurred during prolonged operation, re-optimization using updated environmental inputs restored defect-free product quality. In the main validation cases, the computational optimization was completed within 10 s, which is substantially shorter than the available process window of 115.4 s before blowing. This result indicates that the calculation of updated lamp settings is compatible with the practical process window. However, practical implementation must also consider the physical response time of the heating system and preform after the optimized settings are applied.

These results suggest that, under the investigated process conditions, integrating multimodal temperature prediction with genetic algorithm-based optimization can support adaptive adjustment of lamp settings by reducing deviations from a quality-qualified surface-temperature profile. The observed defect suppression should be interpreted as an experimentally supported correlation between surface-temperature profile deviation and product quality, rather than as a complete mechanistic demonstration of defect formation. Future work will focus on extending the framework to additional process variables, quantifying thermal response and transition cycles, and linking the predicted temperature field more directly to final product properties such as thickness distribution and mechanical performance.
