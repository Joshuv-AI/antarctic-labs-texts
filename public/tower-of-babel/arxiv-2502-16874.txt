A dynamic copula model for probabilistic forecasting of non-Gaussian multivariate time series
John Zito, Daniel R. Kowal

Abstract
Multivariate time series (MTS) data often include a heterogeneous mix of non-Gaussian distributional features (asymmetry, multimodality, heavy tails) and data types (continuous and discrete variables). Traditional MTS methods based on convenient parametric distributions are typically ill-equipped to model this heterogeneity. Copula models provide an appealing alternative, but present significant obstacles for fully Bayesian inference and probabilistic forecasting. To overcome these challenges, we propose a novel and general strategy for posterior approximation in MTS copula models and apply it to a Gaussian copula built from a dynamic factor model. This framework provides scalable, fully Bayesian inference for cross-sectional and serial dependencies and nonparametrically learns heterogeneous marginal distributions. We validate this approach by establishing posterior consistency and confirm excellent finite-sample performance even under model misspecification using simulated data. We apply our method to crime count and macroeconomic MTS data and find superior probabilistic forecasting performance compared to popular MTS models. These results demonstrate that the proposed method is a versatile, general-purpose utility for probabilistic forecasting of MTS that works well across of range of applications with minimal user input.



Keywords:
Bayesian methods; factor model; prediction; state space model

1
Introduction

Multivariate time series (MTS) data arise in economics
(
Karlsson, 2013
)
, finance
(
Chib et al., 2009
)
, neuroscience
(
Chiang et al., 2024
)
, and many other fields. In MTS analysis, we seek to model the joint dynamics of many time series and generate probabilistic forecasts of future data. Figures
1
and
2
display two examples of MTS datasets: monthly crime case counts in New South Wales, Australia and quarterly US macroeconomic aggregates (plots for all series are in the supplementary material). A signal feature of these data is that each variable possesses its own unique distributional features, distinct from the other variables, and often non-Gaussian. Amongst the count-valued crime variables, we observe heavy tails (blackmail) and multimodality (escape from custody). Amongst the continuous macro variables, we observe multimodality (funds rate, yield spread) and asymmetry (unemployment). Occasionally, MTS include both discrete and continuous variables (e.g.,
Debaly and Truquet, 2023
). Faced with such MTS data, the challenge becomes modeling the cross-sectional and serial dependencies among the variables while simultaneously capturing the marginal distributional features and data types of each series.

Figure 1:
Monthly crime counts in New South Wales, Australia (January 1995 - December 2023). The full collection of variables is given in the supplementary material.

Figure 2:
Quarterly US macroeconomic aggregates (1965Q1 - 2019Q4). The full collection of variables is given in the supplementary material.

The rich literature on MTS analysis is strong on the subject of modeling cross-sectional and serial dependence, but perhaps weaker on the subject of flexibly capturing series-specific features within a multivariate framework. Most workhorse methods, such as vector autoregressive moving average (VARMA) models
(
Lütkepohl, 2006
)
or dynamic linear models (DLMs;
West and Harrison, 1997
), are based on convenient parametric families that make strict distributional assumptions: each variable is Gaussian, or each variable is Student’s
t
, etc.
There is a modest literature on multivariate count-valued time series
(
Fokianos, 2024
)
, including the class of dynamic generalized linear models
(
West et al., 1985
)
and the recent work of
Aktekin et al. (2018)
;
Aktekin et al. (2020)
, but these methods make similarly sweeping parametric distributional assumptions. None of these methods apply for variables of mixed data types. As such, the usual methods are limited in the face of MTS that include a heterogeneous mix of distributional features and data types.

This weakness has motivated recent interest in copula models for MTS. Copula models provide a convenient framework for heterogeneous data because they effectively decouple the specification of the dependence structure between variables (and possibly across time) from the marginal behavior of each variable. Through this decoupling, each variable is assigned its own unrestricted marginal distribution and then these marginal distributions are “stitched together” in a principled way that captures the various dependencies but preserves the variable-specific behavior.

Copula methods for MTS fall into two main categories.
In the first, a copula model is used to define the transition distribution in an autoregressive MTS model

(
Chen and Fan, 2006
;
Patton, 2013
;
Creal and Tsay, 2015
;
Debaly and Truquet, 2023
)
.
In the second, which we pursue here, a large copula defines the joint distribution across all variables and all time points
(
Rémillard et al., 2012
;
Smith, 2015
;
Smith and Vahey, 2016
)
. To be operational for MTS, these models must carefully consider cross-sectional and serial dependencies, forecasting recursions, and stationarity, among other things. These models are parametrized by two sets of unobservables: the copula parameters that model all (cross-sectional and serial) dependencies and the marginal distributions (or margins) that model the individual features of each variable. These constitute distinct “levers” that we can manipulate in order to model a broad array of heterogeneous MTS data.

A primary goal of this paper is to generate
probabilistic
forecasts for MTS, and in particular interval and density forecasts that convey reliable uncertainty quantification. We adopt a Bayesian approach, which is especially well-suited for this task via the posterior predictive distribution. However, posterior predictive inference requires careful accounting for the uncertainties in all model unobservables—which requires learning the joint posterior for all copula parameters and all marginal distributions.
Unfortunately, existing Bayesian methods fall short in this difficult task. The vast majority of Bayesian approaches simply fix the margins at point estimates and then proceed with inference for the copula parameters as if the margins were known (e.g.,
Creal and Tsay, 2015
;
Smith and Vahey, 2016
). Clearly, this strategy does not properly account for the uncertainty in the unknown margins.
Smith (2015)
is an exception that jointly infers the copula parameters and the margins, but requires parametric marginal distributions that are often inadequate for heterogeneous data. Furthermore, because of 1) the need to specify and tune each parametric margin and 2) the computational complexity of the posterior sampling algorithm, this approach can struggle to scale to larger cross-sections of variables
(
Loaiza-Maya and Smith, 2019
)
.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a new approach for Bayesian inference and probabilistic forecasting with MTS copula models. Our approach combines a rank-based posterior approximation for the copula parameters with a nonparametric procedure to infer the marginal distributions (Section
3
). Crucially, this approach minimizes user-intensive tuning: it does not require explicit specification of any marginal model, applies for mixed data types, and delivers posterior inference for the margins that adapts to the particular features of each variable.
Taken together, this scheme provides an automated and computationally tractable method for joint inference on all model unknowns along with probabilistic forecasts.
We validate our approach by providing model-trusting posterior consistency theory (Section
4
), and then show that excellent performance is still achieved under model misspecification using simulated data (Section
6
). We apply our novel estimation scheme to a copula time series process built from a dynamic factor model, which is particularly well-suited for heterogeneous MTS with moderate to large cross-sections. We compare this model to a basket of popular alternatives in a pair of probabilistic forecasting comparisons based on the crime count and macroeconomic datasets (Section
7
). Supplementary materials include proofs, additional simulation results, and details about the datasets. Replication files are available in the
GitHub
repository

johnczito/DynamicCopula
.

2
Background

2.1
Copula models for multivariate time series

We study MTS
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{y}_{1},\,\mathbf{y}_{2},\,...,\,\mathbf{y}_{T}\}
, where
\mathbf{y}_{t}=[y_{t,1}\,y_{t,2}\,\cdots y_{t,n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
is a cross-section of
n
constituent univariate time series
\{y_{t,i}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Each series could have its own unique distributional features or data type, possibly quite different from all the others. Our goal is to construct a probabilistic forecasting model for
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
that can capture this heterogeneity while simultaneously modeling both cross-sectional and serial dependencies. In order to do this, we use copula models.

A copula model is a joint probability distribution that is constructed to have an arbitrary set of marginal distributions. The “inversion” or “implicit” construction of a copula model
(
Smith, 2023
)
has the following generative form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{1:T}

\displaystyle\sim p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})

(1)

\displaystyle y_{t,i}

\displaystyle=F_{t,i}^{-1}\circ G_{t,i}(z_{t,i})

(2)

where
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\,\mathbf{z}_{2},\,...,\,\mathbf{z}_{T}\}
,
\mathbf{z}_{t}=[z_{t,1}\,z_{t,2}\,\cdots z_{t,n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are latent variables with a joint distribution
p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})
parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and marginal distributions
G_{t,i}
. The construction in (
1
,
2
) is not specific to MTS, but rather defines a joint probability model
p(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
that decouples the dependencies of
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
(modeled by (
1
) and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
) and the margins
y_{t,i}\sim F_{t,i}
.

As a concrete example, the
Gaussian copula
features a Gaussian latent distribution
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{C}_{\theta})
with correlation
\mathbf{C}_{\theta}
indexed by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Each margin for
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
is standard Gaussian,
G_{t,i}=\Phi
. Despite its apparent simplicity, this model accommodates arbitrary marginal distributions,
y_{t,i}\sim F_{t,i}
, with dependencies among
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
determined by
\mathbf{C}_{\theta}
. Because of the probability integral transform
F_{t,i}^{-1}\circ\Phi
, the dependencies among
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
can be quite complex, including nonlinearities.

To adapt (
1
,
2
) for MTS, we consider each level in turn. First, the latent model (
1
) determines both serial and cross-sectional dependencies. We adopt a forward-looking, recursive characterization and instead specify a transition distribution
p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
for the latent MTS. This framing grants access to a broad collection of MTS models, such as VARMAs, DLMs, dynamic factor models, etc. and operationalizes (
1
,
2
) as a forecasting model for MTS. Specific choices are given in Section
2.2
, but our methods are general.

Second, we impose some structure on the margins
\{F_{t,i}\}
, which in (
2
) are variable- and time-specific. We require (
1
,
2
) to be strictly stationary: set
F_{t,i}=F_{i}
for all
t
and specify
p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
so that
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
is strictly stationary. Now,
F_{i}
is the (unknown) marginal stationary distribution of
\{y_{t,i}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and
G_{t,i}=G_{i}
for all
t
.
Then our generic template for specifying a MTS copula model is

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})

(3)

\displaystyle y_{t,i}

\displaystyle=F_{i}^{-1}\circ G_{i}(z_{t,i}).

(4)

Different choices of
p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
produce different model configurations and propagate cross-sectional and serial dependencies to
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
. Variable-specific features are governed by the margins
\{F_{i}\}
, which can accommodate a heterogeneous mix of distributional features and data types. The model unknowns are the copula parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and the margins
\{F_{i}\}
; the latent stationary distributions
\{G_{i}\}
are often known in closed-form and otherwise may be simulated from (
3
). We evaluate our methods against real data that may violate strict stationarity in Section
7
.

2.2
Dynamic Gaussian factor copula models

In order to specify a latent transition distribution
p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:t-1},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
with stationary dynamics, we turn to the rich class of linear, Gaussian time series models. These are well-studied, and bring with them a set of well-developed inferential tools that will ease the burden of computation later. To handle moderate to large cross-sections
n
, we specify a dynamic factor model (DFM) for (
3
) and refer to this model with (
4
) as a
dynamic Gaussian factor copula
(DGFC):

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2}\mathbf{x}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\overset{\textrm{indep}}{\sim}\text{N}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t},\,\mathbf{V})

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}_{k}(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).

(6)

The DFM is applied to the latent variable
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, which is rescaled by
\mathbf{D}_{0}=\text{diag}\{\text{var}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\}
so that
\mathbf{z}_{t}
in (
3
,
4
) is marginally (over
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
) a standard Gaussian process with
G_{i}=\Phi
. The factor model in (
5
) expresses
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in terms of a static loadings matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
(
n\times k
) and dynamic factors
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k\ll n
and
\mathbf{V}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{v})
. These lower-dimensional factors
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
follow a vector autoregression (VAR) in (
6
), which is the primary source of serial dependence in the model. The dynamics are determined by the transition matrix
\mathbf{G}
, which we restrict to have eigenvalues in the unit circle for stationarity. In aggregate, the static parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\mathbf{G},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\,\mathbf{v}\}
govern the cross-sectional and serial dependencies of
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
(and thus
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
); a detailed accounting of these relationships is provided in the supplementary material. Default prior specifications are discussed in Section
3.4
.

DFMs were introduced by
Geweke (1977)
and are popular in macroeconometrics, where they are used to model large cross-sections of macroeconomic time series with a small number of latent factors
(
Stock and Watson, 2016
)
.
Kim and Nelson (1998)
introduced Bayesian analysis of these models, subsequently expanded upon by
Aguilar and West (2000)
and
Bai and Wang (2015)
, among many others. In our setting, DFMs are especially useful because they enable application of (
3
,
4
) for moderate to large cross-sections. In particular, DFMs provide both 1) model parsimony for MTS and 2) tractable properties that facilitate efficient posterior computations (see Section
3.4
). However, our methods apply more broadly, including stationary Gaussian processes for
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
and VAR copula models
(
Smith and Vahey, 2016
;
Fan et al., 2023
)
.

3
Bayesian inference and probabilistic forecasting

Proceeding with the general MTS copula model (
3
,
4
), there are two sets of unobservables: the static parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that govern the cross-sectional and serial dependencies in
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
and the marginal distributions
F_{1:n}=\{F_{1},\,F_{2},\,...,\,F_{n}\}
that govern the individual distributional features of each series. From a Bayesian perspective, the goal is to access the full, joint posterior distribution
p(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
. This term is also the main ingredient for probabilistic forecasting via the multi-step posterior predictive distribution
p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
, which we illustrate below.

The primary challenge here is the margins
F_{1:n}
. Joint posterior inference for
F_{1:n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is sufficiently difficult that most Bayesian copula methods do not even attempt it, and instead fix the margins at point estimates

(
Creal and Tsay, 2015
;
Smith and Vahey, 2016
)
. This approach does not properly account for the uncertainty in the margins
F_{1:n}
, which can be substantial for short- to medium-length time series, and implicates probabilistic forecasting from the posterior predictive distribution. Parametric margins offer a partial resolution
(
Smith, 2015
)
, but limit the distributional flexibility and still require sophisticated and computationally intensive algorithms.

In this environment, we seek a posterior approximation for
p(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
that 1) properly accounts for the joint uncertainties in
F_{1:n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, 2) allows for nonparametric modeling of each
F_{i}
with minimal tuning, and 3) facilitates efficient computing. To provide an overview of the proposed approach, we begin with the following representation of the joint posterior under (
3
,
4
):

\displaystyle p(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})

\displaystyle=\int p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{1:T}

(7)

\displaystyle=\int p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\ p(F_{1:n}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{1:T}

(8)

which applies a data augmentation in (
7
) and a marginal-conditional decomposition in (
8
).

Our approximation strategy sequentially considers the terms in the integrand of (
8
). First,
p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
is unconditional on the margins
F_{1:n}
, which apparently requires an intractable marginalization. Instead, we approximate this distribution with a
rank posterior

(
Hoff, 2007
)
that conditions only on the ranks of
\{y_{t,i}\}_{t=1}^{T}
for each series (Section
3.1
). Crucially, rank posteriors are invariant to the margins
F_{1:n}
and thus approximate
p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
without the need to specify, estimate, or marginalize over
F_{1:n}
.
However, full posterior inference for the margins remains a priority and is essential for probabilistic forecasting. Thus, the rank posterior alone is insufficient for our goals. This is resolved by our treatment of the second term,
p(F_{1:n}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
: we show that the margins
F_{1:n}
are essentially determined when given
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
and
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
(Section
3.2
). Remarkably, this approach does not require any explicit model for
F_{1:n}
and thus delivers automatic and nonparametric inference for these marginal stationary distributions.

In conjunction, these approximations produce the pseudo-posterior

\tilde{p}(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=\int\tilde{p}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\ \tilde{p}(F_{1:n}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{1:T}.

(9)

Subsequently, we will show that (
9
) is especially convenient and efficient to compute for many choices of (
3
,
4
) (Algorithm
1
) and demonstrates favorable theoretical (Section
4
) and empirical (Section
6
) properties for joint inference on
F_{1:n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Lastly, we build upon the approximation (
9
) to enable probabilistic forecasting. Similar to (
7
,
8
), the multi-step posterior predictive distribution can be expressed via data augmentation, now using both historical and
H
-step ahead latent data
\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\mathbf{z}_{T+1:T+H}\}
:

p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=\int p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H}\,\textrm{d}F_{1:n}\,\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Again using marginal-conditional decompositions, the multi-step posterior predictive distribution can be represented conveniently as

p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=\int p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\ \times\\
p(\mathbf{z}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,\textrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H}\textrm{d}F_{1:n}\,\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}.

(10)

First,
p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T+H},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=p(\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{T+1:T+H},\,F_{1:n})

is degenerate: given latent data and the margins, the (future) observations are completely determined by the probability integral transform (
4
). Second,
p(\mathbf{z}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=p(\mathbf{z}_{T+1:T+H}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})
is simply the
H
-step forecasting distribution of the latent time series (
3
) with given parameters. Finally, the third term is precisely what we approximate in the integrand of (
9
), i.e.,
\tilde{p}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
. Crucially, access to (
10
) requires minimal computing effort beyond the posterior approximation in (
9
) and fully leverages the forward-looking specification (
3
) of the latent model (
1
); details are in Algorithm
2
.
With this, we can produce and evaluate the full range of point, interval, and density forecasts—which is not typical for MTS copula models.

3.1
The rank posterior

The first task is to approximate
p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
, which notably does not depend on the margins
F_{1:n}
. Rather than specifying a prior on
F_{1:n}
and attempting to integrate analytically (which would be intractable), we propose a rank-based posterior approximation. This strategy offers several key features: 1) it is invariant to the margins
F_{1:n}
, yet our approach still provides joint inference for
F_{1:n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(Section
3.2
); 2) it is computationally efficient for many latent MTS models (
3
) using Gibbs sampling (see the supplementary material, Algorithm
3
); and 3) it offers favorable theoretical properties (Section
4
).

To see why the rank posterior is especially useful for MTS copula models (
3
,
4
), notice that
F_{i}^{-1}\circ G_{i}
is nondecreasing, so
z_{t,i}<z_{t^{\prime},i}
whenever
y_{t,i}<y_{t^{\prime},i}
, for each series
i=1,\,...,\,n
. Crucially, this is true regardless of the margins
F_{1:n}
. The basic idea is to replace the observed data values with their relative ordering in our information set, which results in some information loss but removes the need to specify, estimate, or marginalize over
F_{1:n}
at this stage. More specifically,
(
4
) implies that
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
must obey the same partial ordering as
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
, meaning that
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
must reside in the set

\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}:\max_{k}\{z_{k,i}:y_{k,i}<y_{t,i}\}<z_{t,i}<\min_{k}\{z_{k,i}:y_{t,i}<y_{k,i}\}\}.

For continuous variables, this defines a total ordering, and the set
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
encodes the same information as the ranks of the data
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
. When
y_{t,i}=y_{t^{\prime},i}
(e.g., for discrete variables), then
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
defines only a partial ordering.

Our approximation of
p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
relies on the following decomposition, which we derive in the supplementary material (Appendix
A.1
):

p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})=\underbrace{p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}_{\text{rank posterior}}\,\times\,\frac{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T}),\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}}{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}.

(11)

We use the
rank posterior

p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}
as our approximation
\tilde{p}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
in (
9
). The intuition, which we formalize later in Theorem
2
, is that the ranks contain most of the relevant information for inference and thus the righthand term in (
11
) is negligible. Crucially, the rank posterior is invariant to the margins: this occurs because the rank
likelihood
for the model unobservables satisfies
p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,F_{1:n}\}=p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}
.
This obviates the need to marginalize over
F_{1:n}
analytically, or to even specify a model for
F_{1:n}
. Similar strategies have been used successfully with independent and identically distributed data
(
Hoff, 2007
;
Murray et al., 2013
)
, but these previous approaches did not consider MTS data, inference for the margins
F_{1:n}
, or probabilistic forecasting.

To estimate the rank posterior for MTS copula models, we design a general Gibbs sampling algorithm. There are two main blocks:

1.

p\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}=p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T})
is exactly the posterior distribution under the MTS model (
3
) given data
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
; and

2.

p\{z_{t,i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\setminus\{z_{t,i}\},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}=p(z_{t,i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\setminus\{z_{t,i}\})\ \mathbf{I}\{z_{t,i}\in(L_{t,i},U_{t,i})\}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
and
i=1,\ldots,n
is the full conditional distribution of (
3
) truncated to an interval with endpoints
L_{t,i}=\max_{t^{\prime}}\{z_{t^{\prime},i}:y_{t^{\prime},i}<y_{t,i}\}
and
U_{t,i}=\min_{t^{\prime}}\{z_{t^{\prime},i}:y_{t^{\prime},i}>y_{t,i}\}
.

Block 1 allows us to apply existing algorithms for MTS models, including VARMAs, DLMs, DFMs, etc. While these models may be unsatisfactory when applied directly to MTS with heterogeneous features (Figures
1
and
2
), the computational tools for these models are immediately applicable for MTS copula models (
3
,
4
). For the DGFC with DFM (
5
,
6
), we provide the sampling steps in Algorithm
3
.
In Block 2, we further decompose
p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}
, which truncates the
Tn
-dimensional joint distribution in (
3
), into univariate distributions truncated to an interval. This massively improves computational scalability in both
T
and
n
, albeit at some expense to Monte Carlo efficiency. When (
3
) is a Gaussian time series model, the joint and full conditional distributions may be written
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
z_{t,i}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\setminus\{z_{t,i}\}\sim\text{N}(\mu_{t,i},\,\sigma^{2}_{t,i})
, respectively, where the moments derive from standard conditioning results for Gaussian variables. Conveniently, this shows that Block 2 is rather generic and broadly applicable across latent Gaussian time series models. Note that in general, computing
(\mu_{t,i},\,\sigma^{2}_{t,i})
requires a large matrix inversion. However, our implementation of the DGFC circumvents this issue, namely because
z_{t,i}
is conditionally independent of
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\setminus\{z_{t,i}\}
given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and the factors
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1:T}
(see Algorithm
3
).

3.2
Inference for the marginal stationary distributions

The second task is to approximate
p(F_{1:n}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
, which would complete the joint posterior distribution for all copula parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and margins
F_{1:n}
in (
9
). At first glance, this appears infeasible: the approximation
\tilde{p}(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
from Section
3.1
used the rank posterior (
11
) and eschewed any specification, estimation, or marginalization over
F_{1:n}
. Yet perhaps surprisingly, we show that is both possible and rather straightforward to identify
p(F_{1:n}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
—and to do so without any new modeling assumptions or specifications of
F_{1:n}
.

For ease of exposition, we discuss a single
F_{i}
, and we momentarily drop the variable index
i
for simplicity. Since the conditioning set includes the latent data
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
, the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and the data
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
, these are all treated as given in this section.

The singular appearance of
F
in the MTS copula model (
3
,
4
) occurs in the probability integral transform
y_{t}=F^{-1}\circ G(z_{t})
, which implies that
F(y_{t})=G(z_{t})
. Crucially, with our conditioning set, both
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
and
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
are given. Then
F
is known
exactly
at each
\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
(see Figure
3
, top). Put another way,
p\{F(x)\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\}
is degenerate at
G(z_{t})
whenever
x=y_{t}
. For any unobserved point
x\not\in\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, the value of
F
remains uncertain. Typically, this is where a prior specification for
F
would contribute some information or smoothness. However, specifying a prior for the marginal distribution of each series is user-intensive even for moderate cross-sections and requires careful consideration of each variable’s distributional features, support, and data type (e.g., real-valued, positive continuous, count, etc.). Thus, we pursue a simpler approach free of user input.

Notice that
F
must be bounded, non-decreasing, and pass through the pairs
\{(y_{t},\,G(z_{t}))\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Thus, we simply approximate
p\{F(x)\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta},\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\}
to be degenerate at a distribution function
\tilde{F}
that interpolates
\{(y_{t},\,G(z_{t}))\}_{t=1}^{T}
. As
T
grows, the points along this curve will become dense and the approximation errors will dissipate.
By default, we adopt the simplest interpolation strategy (Figure
3
, middle): a step function that is zero for
x<\min\{y_{1:T}\}
, one for
x\geq\max\{y_{1:T}\}
, and otherwise (
\min\{y_{1:T}\}\leq x<\max\{y_{1:T}\}
) equal to

\tilde{F}(x)=\underset{t}{\max}\{G(z_{t}):y_{t}\leq x\}.

(12)

We refer to (
12
) as the
margin adjustment
, following the terminology of
Feldman and Kowal (2024)
, who used a similar idea for imputation of independent and identically distributed data. When
F
is continuous, each
y_{t}
has a unique value, and so
\tilde{F}
is a step function passing through the pairs
(y_{t},\,G(z_{t}))
(Figure
3
, middle). When
F
is discrete and each unique value of
y
could have multiple values of
G(z)
associated with it, (
12
) takes the largest.

This approximation is simple, computationally efficient, and well-behaved (Theorem
1
). Other interpolations may be preferred in some settings, such as a monotone spline that interpolates
\{(y_{t},\,G(z_{t}))\}_{t=1}^{T}
to provide smoothness in place of a step function. However, we emphasize that the central feature is the interpolation; the behavior of
F(x)
at unobserved points
x\not\in\{y_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
becomes less and less important as
T
grows.

Figure 3:
Given
z_{t}
, the unknown CDF
F
(blue) must pass through the points
\{(y_{t},\,G(z_{t}))\}_{t=1}^{T}
(top). The margin adjustment interpolates these points with a step function
\tilde{F}
(middle). But in practice the
G(z_{t})
are uncertain quantities with a posterior distribution inherited from
z_{t}
(left margin, bottom), which then induces a posterior distribution for
\tilde{F}
(bottom).

3.4
Priors and posterior computation for the DGFC

We now discuss our default prior specifications for the DGFC. First, the parameters in (
5
) are the factor loadings
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=[\lambda_{i,l}]
and the (diagonal) variances
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1}\,v_{2}\,\cdots\,v_{n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
. For the factor loadings, we use the multiplicative gamma process prior
(
Bhattacharya and Dunson, 2011
)
, which provides ordered shrinkage across the columns of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
to reduce sensitivity to the specified number of factors
k
. The variances are assigned
v_{i}^{-1}\sim\text{iid}\,\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{0},\,\beta_{0})
. Next, we use a conditionally conjugate prior for the VAR parameters in (
6
),
\mathbf{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\sim\text{MNIW}_{k,k}(d_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0},\,\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\,\mathbf{O}^{-1}_{0})
, truncated so that
\mathbf{G}
always has eigenvalues within the unit circle for stationarity. In each of the simulations and applications that follow, we use the same default prior hyperparameters:
k=\lceil 0.7n\rceil
,
d_{0}=k+1
,
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
,
\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{O}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
,
\alpha_{0}=1
,
\beta_{0}=0.3
. Further details are in the supplement.

These priors are all conditionally conjugate within (
5
,
6
), which leads to straightforward and efficient sampling steps for the main blocks outlined in Section
3.1
. For instance,
(\mathbf{G},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
can be drawn using standard conjugacy results for VARs
(
Karlsson, 2013
)
, while
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1:T}
appears within a linear, (conditionally) Gaussian state space system (
5
,
6
) and can be drawn using the Kalman simulation smoother. Finally, the latent data
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}
are conditionally independent given
\{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\mathbf{v}\}
, which removes all matrix inversions from their truncated sampling steps (Section
3.1
) and dramatically improves computational scalability. Details on the Gibbs sampling algorithm are in the supplementary material (Algorithm
3
).

4
Theoretical results

We study the asymptotic behavior of the pseudo-posterior
\tilde{p}(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
in (
9
).
To begin, we state a technical result demonstrating that the margin adjustment (
12
) approximates the true distribution in the context of a model like (
3
,
4
). All proofs are in the supplementary material.

Theorem 1
.

Let
Z_{t}
be a strictly stationary ergodic process with continuous marginal distribution
G(x)=P(Z_{1}\leq x)
, and let
F
be any continuous distribution function. If we have
Y_{t}=F^{-1}\circ G(Z_{t})
and define
\tilde{F}
as in (
12
), then
\tilde{F}(x)\overset{a.s.}{\to}F(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
as
T\to\infty
.

Next, we show that the rank posterior
p\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}
provides consistent inferences for the MTS copula parameters. We do so under a Gaussian VAR for the latent MTS model (
3
):

\mathbf{z}_{t}=\tilde{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{z}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})

(13)

Posterior consistency requires parameter identification, and the copula parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\tilde{\mathbf{G}},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\}
are only identified if
\tilde{\mathbf{G}}
has eigenvalues within the unit circle, and if the stationary covariance matrix given by
\text{vec}(\mathbf{C}_{0})=(\mathbf{I}_{n^{2}}-\tilde{\mathbf{G}}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{G}})^{-1}\text{vec}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
is a correlation matrix. This also implies that the margin (or stationary distribution) of (
13
) is
G_{i}=\Phi
.
We discuss this further in Appendix
A.3
, but for the purposes of the theorems, we assume that our prior for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is supported only on the subset
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\times\text{SPD}_{n}
where these conditions hold. In addition, we require the following:

(A.1)

\mathbf{y}_{1:\infty}
is distributed according to
H^{\infty}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},F_{1:n}}
, the joint distribution implied by (
4
,
13
);

(A.2)

F_{i}
are all continuous;

(A.3)

\Pi
is a prior distribution on
\Theta
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is its density with respect to some measure
\nu
;

(A.4)

\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})>0
a.e. with respect to
\nu
.

Theorem 2
.

Under (A.1)–(A.4),
for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
a.e.
\nu
and for any neighborhood
A\ni\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, we have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\Pi\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in A\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}=1\text{ a.s. }H^{\infty}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},F_{1:n}}.

This result implies that the ignored ratio term in (
11
) becomes negligible as
T
grows. Lastly, we show that our pseudo-posterior for the margins (
12
) provides consistent inferences. We use
\tilde{\Pi}
to denote the marginal margin adjustment posterior distribution implied by (
9
):

Theorem 3
.

Under (A.1)–(A.4), define
\tilde{F}_{i}
as in (
12
). For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and any neighborhood
A\ni F_{i}(x)
, we have

\lim_{T\to\infty}\tilde{\Pi}\{\tilde{F}_{i}(x)\in A\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\}=1\text{ a.s. }H^{\infty}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},F_{1:n}}.

Taken together, these results provide asymptotic validation for our posterior approximation strategy via the pseudo-posterior
\tilde{p}(F_{1:n},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})
in (
9
).
We confirm these theoretical results using simulations in the supplementary material (Appendix
A.6
).

The main limitations here are 1) these results assume model correctness, both of the MTS copula model (
3
,
4
) and the specific latent dynamics of (
13
), and 2) they use continuous margins. Our empirical evaluations with simulated (Section
6
) and real (Section
7
) data remove these limitations and consider misspecified models with continuous and discrete MTS data.

5
Competing methods

We advocate the proposed approach as a versatile, general-purpose utility for probabilistic forecasting of MTS data. To establish this, we compare the DGFC against benchmark competitors that are used for probabilistic forecasting of MTS across a broad range of scenarios with minimal tuning. Specifically, we focus on Bayesian VARs (
BVAR
) and dynamic linear models (
DLM
). Bayesian VARs apply (
13
) to
\mathbf{y}
instead of
\mathbf{z}
and thus have a (conditionally) Gaussian marginal distribution for each variable. We incorporate the estimation procedure and shrinkage priors from
Giannone et al. (2015)
, which jointly models the VAR coefficients and error covariance matrix using a a conjugate matrix-normal-inverse-Wishart prior with “Minnesota”-type shrinkage toward a random walk for each variable. We adopt the default prior settings from the
BVAR
package
(
Kuschnig and Vashold, 2021
)
in
R
. Next, we consider DLMs (or state space models) of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{W})

(14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{V})

(15)

and implemented in the
dlm

(
Petris, 2010
)
and
KFAS

(
Helske, 2017
)
packages in
R
. The latter also supports dynamic
generalized
linear models (DGLMs) for modeling non-Gaussian data: DGLMs model count data by replacing (
14
) with
y_{t,i}\,|\,\mu_{t,i}\sim\text{Poisson}(\exp(\mu_{t,i}))
or a negative-binomial distribution, with serial and cross-sectional dependencies modeled via (
15
).
By default, the static parameters
\mathbf{W}
(when included) and
\mathbf{V}
are estimated via maximum likelihood.

6
Simulation results

We evaluate estimation, inference, and probabilistic forecasting using simulated data. To reflect practical usage, and to complement our theoretical analysis (Section
4
), we focus on misspecified models that wholly or partly deviate from the DGFC. We consider three data-generating processes (DGPs), each of which yields different cross-sectional and serial dependencies along with different marginal stationary distributions. For each of these DGPs, the margins are known exactly, which allows us to evaluate estimation and inference for the proposed margin adjustment. However, our approach does
not
assume knowledge of any marginal stationary distributions.

The DGPs are the following:

•

VARMA
:
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{b}_{0}+\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{p}\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}_{t-\ell}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}\mathbf{C}_{j}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-j}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
with
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).
Subject to conditions on the parameter matrices, this is a stationary Gaussian process and the marginal moments can be computed exactly

(
Lütkepohl, 2006
)
. We set
(n,\,p,\,q)=(2,\,3,\,6)
and draw random parameters
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\sim\text{IW}_{n}(n+1,\,\mathbf{I}_{n})
,
\mathbf{C}_{l}\sim\text{iid}\,\text{N}(0,\,1)
,
\mathbf{b}_{0}\sim\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{I}_{n})
, and
\mathbf{B}_{l}\sim\text{iid}\,\text{N}(0,\,0.1)
, redrawing the
\{\mathbf{B}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{p}
until they imply stationarity.

•

VARCH
:
\mathbf{y}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{t-1}\sim t_{n}\{\nu,\,\mathbf{0},\,(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{y}_{t-1}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})/\nu\}
with
\nu>n+2
is a multivariate generalization of the autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity model based on the multivariate-
t
distribution
(
Pitt and Walker, 2005
)
,
with stationary distribution
\mathbf{y}_{t}\sim t_{n}\{\nu-1,\,\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{A}/(\nu-1)\}
. This DGP is challenging: the dynamics are nonlinear and the stationary distributions have heavy tails. We set
n=2
and draw
(\nu-n-2)\sim\text{Gamma}(1,1)
and
\mathbf{A}\sim\text{IW}_{n}(n+1,\,\mathbf{I}_{n})
.

•

VARMA Copula
: we replace the DFM in (
5
,
6
) with
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\text{VARMA}_{n}(p,\,q)
for
(n,\,p,\,q)=(2,\,1,\,1)
and specify a heterogeneous mix of marginal distributions,
F_{1}=\text{Poisson}(\lambda=5)
and
F_{2}=\text{Skew-}t(\text{df}=3,\,\mu=0,\,\sigma=1,\,\alpha=2)
from
Azzalini and Capitanio (2003)
. The VARMA parameters are simulated as in the
VARMA
case above.

These DGPs present significant challenges: in each case, the DGFC has misspecified dynamics and no knowledge about the marginal stationary distributions. In that context, we seek to answer two main questions:

1.

Does the margin adjustment posterior provide accurate estimates with precise and well-calibrated uncertainty quantification for unknown marginal stationary distributions?

2.

Does our (pseudo-)posterior predictive distribution provide accurate probabilistic forecasts compared to popular MTS alternatives?

6.2
Probabilistic forecast evaluation

We next compare the probabilistic forecasts from the proposed DGFC against the competitors from Section
5
using simulated data from our three DGPs. We do this with a recursive, out-of-sample forecasting exercise. We first simulate a time series of length
T
from a DGP. Then, we recursively refit each of our models on a expanding window of this data. So for each time point
t=t_{0},\ldots,T

t_{0}>h

\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t+h}

\mathbf{y}_{t+h}

We use three metrics to summarize forecasting accuracy. Point forecasting accuracy is measured using mean squared error for continuous variables and mean absolute error for count variables. Interval forecasts, computed as 95% highest posterior predictive density regions approximated from the posterior draws, are evaluated by average size and empirical coverage. Finally, we evaluate the entire density forecast using the mean continuous ranked probability score
(
Gneiting and Raftery, 2007
)
. The metrics are averaged over a period from
t_{0}=10
to
T=300
.

8
Conclusion

In this article, we proposed a posterior approximation strategy MTS copula models and applied it to a Gaussian copula based on a dynamic factor model in order to forecast MTS with a heterogeneous mix of distributional features and data types across variables. Our novel estimation scheme allows us to circumvent the usual limitations of MTS copula models by producing full Bayesian uncertainty quantification for all model unobservables without requiring variable-specific tuning or unwieldy posterior samplers.

We demonstrated the efficacy of our approach in theoretical analyses and simulation studies, and in real data applications to crime counts and macroeconomic aggregates. Across these explorations, we confronted our method with a wide range of data, both real and simulated: cross-sections of all counts, cross-sections of all continuous variables, mixed cross-sections, variables with skew, heavy tails, multiple modes, zero-inflation. We also toured various forms of time series dynamics: autoregressive, moving average, linear, nonlinear, conditionally heteroskedastic, as well as real data that may not even be stationary. In all cases, our proposed approach worked exceptionally well without any user input or tuning: the results were generated using the same default priors and posterior sampling algorithm. Any variable- or application-specific data types and distributional features were automatically learned without any ad hoc interventions by the analyst. As such, we offer our approach as a versatile, general-purpose utility for MTS forecasting that works well across of range of applications with minimal user-intensive tuning.

There are several promising avenues for future work. First, our approach may be extended for binary and categorical variables, for instance by replacing the rank posterior approximation (Section
3.1
) with a rank-probit posterior
(
Feldman and Kowal, 2022
)
. Second, we aim to incorporate nonstationary dynamics in (
3
) with a more general link (
2
), which would require careful modifications of the margin adjustment (Section
3.2
). Third, our rank posterior approximation uses
ranks instead of observed data values, which may boost robustness—and thus combat a main limitation of Gaussian copulas.
Finally, our broader framework applies for general MTS copula models; it would be interesting to explore the rich collection of non-Gaussian copulas.

A.1
The rank posterior

The true posterior and the rank posterior are related as follows:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T})

\displaystyle=p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{1:T},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}

\displaystyle=\frac{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}\ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T},\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T}),\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}}{p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}\ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}{p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}\ p(\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta})}}{{p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}}\,\times\,\frac{{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T}),\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}}}{{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}}

\displaystyle=p\{\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}\,\times\,\frac{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T}),\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\}}{p\{\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\,|\,\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:T})\}}.

The first line follows from the fact that, given
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle\text{vec}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})

are
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-measurable. Armed with these, a consistent, rank-based estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be constructed along the lines of
Fan et al. (2023)
, which requires that the marginal distributions be continuous. The matrix
\mathbf{C}
in (
16
) can be estimated with
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{T}=\sin(\pi\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{T}/2)
, where

\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{T}=\frac{2}{(T-1)(T-2)}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{T-1}\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{T-1}\text{sign}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j}\\
\mathbf{u}_{i+1}-\mathbf{u}_{j+1}\end{bmatrix}\text{sign}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{u}_{j}\\
\mathbf{u}_{i+1}-\mathbf{u}_{j+1}\end{bmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}.

Given those estimates, we can calculate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}=\{\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{T},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{T}\}
by applying (
17
,
18
) using the contents of
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{T}
.
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}
is a function of the
\mathbf{u}_{t}
, which are
\mathcal{R}_{\infty}
-measurable, and so we have the result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3

This closely follows
Feldman and Kowal (2024)
:

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Fix arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
i=1,\,2,\,...,\,n
. We are done if we can produce a consistent estimator of
F_{i}(x)
that is measureable with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t})\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
. Then the result follows again from Doob’s theorem (Lemma
5
). To begin, consider the marginal rank event

\mathcal{D}(y_{1:t,i})=\{z_{1:t,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}:y_{k,i}<y_{k^{\prime},i}\implies z_{k,i}<z_{k^{\prime},i}\}.

The
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\mathcal{D}(y_{1:t,i})\}_{t=1}^{T}
is a sub
\sigma
-field of the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Functions measureable with respect to the first will therefore be measureable with respect to the second, and it suffices to work with the first.

Since we always have
z_{1:t,i}\in\mathcal{D}(y_{1:t,i})
, then
\tilde{F}_{i}(x)=\max_{t}\{\Phi(z_{t,i}):y_{t,i}\leq x\}
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\mathcal{D}(y_{1:t,i})\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Theorem
1
says that
\tilde{F}_{i}(x)
consistently estimates
F_{i}(x)
in the context of our Gaussian VAR copula, so
F_{i}(x)
is measurable with respect to
\{\mathcal{D}(y_{1:t,i})\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
, and hence
\{\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t})\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
.

∎

Appendix B
The dependence structure of the DGFC

Our dynamic factor model (
5
-
6
) can be rewritten as
\mathbf{z}_{1:T}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, and the structure of the correlation matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
encodes the cross-sectional and serial dependencies that are passed to
\mathbf{y}_{t}
through the copula link functions. We now make this structure explicit. To begin, assume that the matrix
\mathbf{G}
has eigenvalues inside the unit circle, which we enforce during inference. This implies that the VAR(1) in (
6
) defines a stationary, mean-zero Gaussian process. As such, we have that
\boldsymbol{\eta}=[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\,\cdots\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{T}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\sim\text{N}_{Tk}(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
with

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-3}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-2}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-2}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-3}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-1}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{T-2}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}\end{bmatrix},

and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}=\text{cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t},\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t-h})
for any
t
. From (
5
) we see that
\mathbf{x}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}\left((\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\boldsymbol{\eta},\,\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{V}\right)
, and so marginally,
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}\left(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)
with
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})+\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{V}
. This implies a familiar-looking fact about Gaussian factor models, that
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0})
with
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{0}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{V}
for all
t=1,\,...,\,T
, but in addition the
\mathbf{x}_{t}
are serially correlated according to the contents of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. We now also see that
\mathbf{D}_{0}
in (
6
) refers to the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}
, which are the same for all
t
. Lastly, (
6
) implies that
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2})\mathbf{x}\sim\text{N}_{Tn}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
with correlation matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2})\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2})
. In particular,
\mathbf{z}_{t}\sim\text{N}_{n}(\mathbf{0},\,\mathbf{C}_{0})
, where
\text{corr}(\mathbf{z}_{t})=\text{cov}(\mathbf{z}_{t})=\mathbf{C}_{0}=\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{0}\mathbf{D}_{0}^{-1/2}
for all
t
. Posterior means of this functional are displayed in Figures
6
and
7
. These figures also visualize
\text{corr}(\mathbf{z}_{t+1},\,\mathbf{z}_{t})
, which is an off-diagonal block of
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

Appendix C
Prior details and posterior computation

We choose a conditionally conjugate prior
\mathbf{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\sim\text{MNIW}_{k,k}(d_{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0},\,\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},\,\mathbf{O}^{-1}_{0})
for the VAR parameters in (
6
), and to ensure that this process is stationary, we truncate the prior (and consequently the posterior) so that
\mathbf{G}
always has eigenvalues within the unit circle. For the variances
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1}\,v_{2}\,\cdots\,v_{n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
, we take
v_{i}^{-1}\sim\text{iid}\,\text{Gamma}(\alpha_{0},\,\beta_{0})
. For the factor loadings
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=[\lambda_{i,l}]
, we use the multiplicative gamma process prior of
Bhattacharya and Dunson (2011)
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i,l}

\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{indep}}{\sim}\text{N}(0,\,\phi^{-1}_{i,l}\tau_{l}^{-1})

(19)

\displaystyle\phi_{i,l}

\displaystyle\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\,\text{Gamma}(\nu_{0}/2,\,\nu_{0}/2)

(20)

\displaystyle\tau_{l}

\displaystyle=\prod_{h=1}^{l}\delta_{h}\quad\begin{array}[]{l}\delta_{1}\sim\text{Gamma}(a_{0},\,1)\\
\delta_{h}\overset{\textrm{iid}}{\sim}\text{Gamma}(b_{0},\,1).\end{array}

The
\phi_{i,l}
are local scale parameters, the
\tau_{l}
are global scale parameters, and the main advantage of this ordered shrinkage prior is to reduce sensitivity to the choice of factor dimension
k
. We collect these hyperparameters in
\boldsymbol{\Phi}=[\phi_{i,l}]
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=[\tau_{1}\,\tau_{2}\,\cdots\,\tau_{k}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
, and we include them in the set of copula parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\mathbf{G},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\,\mathbf{v},\,\boldsymbol{\Phi},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\}
that we estimate. In each of the simulations and applications, we use the same default prior hyperparameters:
k=\lceil 0.7n\rceil
,
d_{0}=k+1
,
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
,
\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{O}_{0}=\mathbf{I}_{k}
,
\alpha_{0}=1
,
\beta_{0}=0.3
,
\nu_{0}=3
