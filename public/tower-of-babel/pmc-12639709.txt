Program evaluation of an urban community diagnosis program incorporating fieldwork using a realist approach
Authors: Junji Haruta, Takayuki Ando, Hirohisa Fujikawa, Junichi Hirahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding social determinants of health is essential for medical education, particularly in complex urban settings. This study evaluated a fieldwork-based community diagnosis program designed to help medical students identify community health issues, appreciate diverse social contexts, and integrate theoretical frameworks into practical learning.MethodsThe program was integrated into a two-week general medicine clerkship at X Medical School in Tokyo in 2022–2023. It included an orientation on ecological systems theory and phenomenological reduction, followed by urban fieldwork, student presentations, peer and faculty feedback, and a final report. A realist approach using Context-Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) framework guided the evaluation, focusing on how contextual factors influenced learning mechanisms to produce specific outcomes. Data sources included students’ reports, reflective writings, and feedback session transcripts. Qualitative analysis was conducted to identify patterns of learning and interaction. Students’ understanding of community health and ability to apply theoretical models were assessed using structured rubrics.ResultsThe program significantly improved students’ ability to observe, analyze, and interpret complex urban community dynamics. Orientation sessions helped students overcome theory-laden biases and expand their observational skills. Fieldwork, coupled with iterative feedback, facilitated double-loop learning, enabling students to reassess assumptions and refine their perspectives. Positionality theory encouraged reflection on power dynamics, leading students to critically examine implicit biases and understand the perspectives of marginalized populations. Through phenomenological reduction, students recognized biases in their biomedical training and shifted toward a holistic understanding of community health, encompassing ecological and social determinants. Peer and faculty feedback promoted intersubjectivity, allowing students to develop a layered appreciation of urban diversity and social complexity. Students reported an increased ability to connect theoretical knowledge with real-world health disparities, proposing actionable, sustainable interventions for urban communities.ConclusionsThis realist approach demonstrated that integrating fieldwork with reflective learning and theory-based frameworks can foster transformative educational experiences. The findings highlight the importance of incorporating community-based programs into medical curricula to prepare future physicians to address health disparities and complex social determinants effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08239-8.

Background

The global trend in medical education increasingly emphasizes the need to understand not only biomedical factors, such as genetics and environmental exposures, but also the social determinants of health (SDH), which encompass socioeconomic, environmental, and healthcare factors [8, 11, 30]. A significant reason for this is the limited socialization regarding structural inequalities of medical students, many of whom come from relatively affluent backgrounds with limited exposure to the lives of socially vulnerable populations [12, 14, 26]. Additionally, the upbringing of medical students in rapidly changing societies may make them less attuned to health disparities rooted in socioeconomic factors [23, 43, 54]. For those accustomed to living in densely populated urban areas with complex structures, understanding communities where they did not grow up can be particularly challenging.

Some studies have shown that by learning to identify health determinants within a community and developing skills to assess community health, physicians can effectively serve diverse and underserved populations (Andermann & CLEAR Collaboration, [4]; Daniel et al., [16, 44]. Many U.S. medical schools have established curricula that focus on community and population health, cultural sensitivity, health disparities, and socioeconomic determinants of health, and created frameworks that enhance training in preventive and population health [27].

In Japan, several initiatives aimed at the understanding of SDH have been reported, including online programs that utilize community diagnosis education [19, 37]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines community diagnosis as “a process of describing the health status of citizens and the factors affecting their health, both quantitatively and qualitatively [27].” This involves identifying problems, proposing improvements, and encouraging action. Community diagnosis programs have been shown to be particularly effective in rural areas or learning’ own communities. In this context, ‘communities’ refer to geographically defined areas where people live and interact, such as neighborhoods, towns, or villages. These communities may also encompass social groups or networks formed through shared experiences, values, or cultural practices, which contribute to the local identity and collective health concerns of the population. These environments benefit from the presence of tutors familiar with the communities, the students’ prior knowledge of the area, and the fact that such communities are often easier to understand to compared to large urban areas [2, 13, 47]. We previously reported that online community diagnosis programs help medical students understand certain communities, and thereby enable them to appreciate the value of inquiry-based learning, increase intrinsic interest in communities, improve learning accountability, and engage in reflective knowledge adaptation [19].

While online education offers advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness, remote implementation, and schedule flexibility [24, 52]—especially within Japan’s densely packed medical education curriculum—it has limitations [33]. Online learning alone does not provide the tangible, authentic experience of community life that fieldwork can offer [1, 42]. Urban fieldwork, with its inherent sociological and experiential data collection, differs fundamentally from online or suburban community diagnosis [3, 13, 19, 47]. In urban settings, where communities are influenced by such inter-related factors as ethnicity, socioeconomic status, industry, geography, and history, fieldwork-based community diagnosis programs provide a transformative learning experience which not only enables medical students to re-envision familiar urban landscapes but also helps them engage with unfamiliar social medicine content [17, 49, 50]; Zuk M [56]),.

To explore these complex learning processes, we have employed a realist approach as a program evaluation method [21, 55]. Reviews of SDH-focused education have highlighted the scarcity of detailed program descriptions and lack of established tools and methods for assessing learners and programs [8, 20, 36, 37]. Realist evaluation seeks to investigate how programs function by examining their dynamics within specific contexts. Here, using the context-mechanism-outcome (CMO) framework (Pawson, Ray, Nick Tilley, 1997), we analyzed our urban community diagnosis program to clarify the learning patterns of medical students, specifically by documenting how various contextual factors (C) interacted with mechanisms (M) to produce specific learning outcomes (O).

Method

Study design

This study employed a realist approach, a paradigm situated between positivism and social constructivism, to answer the question: “What works, for whom, in what circumstances, and why?” [38, 39] The realist approach begins with a working hypothesis about the educational program in its specific context. Intentional data collection, both quantitative and qualitative, is used to explore, verify, and refine this hypothesis. The explanatory process follows the formula Context + Mechanism = Outcome, abbreviated as CMO(Pawson, Ray, Nick Tilley, 1997). The key to validating CMO lies in aligning the hypothesis with the data collected, ensuring consistency and integration [15, 35, 45, 55].

Medical curriculum in Japan

Understanding the background of this study requires a brief overview of Japan’s medical education curriculum. After completing high school, students enter a six-year program in medical school, which begins in April [51]. The first two years cover foundational medical subjects, such as anatomy and physiology, while the third and fourth years focus on clinical subjects like internal medicine and surgery. To qualify for clinical practice, students must pass the Computer-Based Testing (CBT) and Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE). X university in Tokyo has a clinical training period that extends over 72 weeks, beginning in January of the fourth year and concluding in November of the sixth year. In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, we developed the online delivery of a community diagnosis program that focused on local community health. This program served as a basis for the new fieldwork-based community diagnosis program.

General medicine clinical practice

At X University in Tokyo, the general medicine clinical rotations take place from January of the fourth year to December of the fifth year, with students rotating in groups of 5–6 every two weeks. During the pandemic, we implemented an online community diagnosis program, designed to help students recognize local community characteristics and analyze health issues in the specific local community. When clinical practice resumed in January 2022, we transitioned to an in-person fieldwork model. The program was led by JHaru, a board-certified family physician with a Ph.D. in realist approaches and qualitative research, and was supported by TA and HF, both experienced family physicians with expertise in qualitative studies involving free-response data and reporting.

Learning theory

Our previous online program was based on experiential learning, communities of practice, situated learning, and variation theory, and emphasized core learning through distinctions in temporal, spatial, and social dimensions [19]. Learning was defined as “the important identification of things that occur under interaction between the learner and the learning object; and the variations that arise in this interaction are what take the essential characteristics of things into thought based on variation theory [29]. The fieldwork component utilized the phenomenological reduction of Husserl [22], which encourages students to suspend preconceptions (epoché) and observe phenomena as they appeared. In the orientation, JHaru explained the importance of acknowledging biases and valuing perspectives rooted in the specific context to understand local community health issues.

Program schedule

The community diagnosis program commenced with a one-hour orientation via Zoom on the first day of clinical practice. During this session, students were introduced to the purpose of community diagnosis and provided an overview of relevant ecological systems theory [18], complete with specific examples. Key observation points included awareness of personal biases and preconceptions [32], integration of online data with field observations, identification of potential community issues, and formulation of action plans based on community resources and data-driven insights. Following the orientation, students selected urban areas within one hour’s commute. Ten days later, they presented their initial community diagnosis via Zoom, sharing findings and receiving feedback from both peers and faculty (JHaru). Feedback during these presentations incorporated phenomenological reduction, using questions to encourage students to expand their perspectives based on variation theory. This included prompting students to suspend judgment to avoid ethnocentric views and guiding them to reconsider assumptions and overlooked details in their observations.

To help students systematically follow the community diagnosis process, a structured report template with five steps was provided [19]:

At the end of the two-week program, students submitted a structured report which incorporated feedback, hypotheses, and recommendations for community interventions as action plans. To minimize bias, TA or HF conducted exit interviews with the students to gather reflective interviews on their learning experiences.

Student assessment

Summative evaluation was conducted by JHaru using a rubric (supplemental table) shared with students on the program’s first day. This assessment included evidence from intermediate and final presentations, structured reports, and observation of students’ engagement and responsiveness to peer and faculty feedback. The rubric considered students’ awareness of biases, value perceptions, logical explanation of community issues, and the feasibility of proposed action plans.

Setting and study participants

This study took place from January 2022 to December 2023 at the Department of General Medicine at X University, a private medical school in Tokyo. The annual tuition at X University approaches 4 million yen (approximately USD 27,000). Although participants’ socioeconomic status was not formally assessed, the high tuition cost suggests that many students may come from relatively affluent backgrounds. Participants included fourth- and fifth-year medical students enrolled in the basic clinical clerkship program in general medicine.

Realist approach

Our analysis followed Pawson’s four-step framework, which is fundamental to the realist approach [41]. This framework aims to understand the interactions between CMO to reveal how, why, and under what conditions a program works. Initially, we formulated working hypotheses regarding the CMO configurations of the program using both deductive and inductive methods. These hypotheses served as a guiding structure for intentional qualitative data collection, primarily based on students’ reports, reflective interviews and writings, and field notes.

Hypothesis development for the components of CMOs: In the realist approach, “Context” represents the conditions under which the program operates, such as the students’ backgrounds, learning materials, and subject matter. “Mechanism” refers to the process by which educational interventions and resources are interpreted and acted upon by individuals, encompassing both the internal responses and the educational activities themselves. “Outcome” reflects the results of these interactions, showing how specific combinations of context and mechanism produce distinct learning effects. We began by hypothesizing potential mechanisms, drawing on Astbury and Leeuw’s emphasis on distinguishing between mechanisms and program interventions [6]. For instance, an outcome may be an increase in students’ knowledge and readiness for practice, stemming from a mechanism activated by specific contextual and intervention variables. CMOs hypotheses: Dalkin et al. propose an alternative formula that emphasizes the dual role of mechanisms in intervention: Context + Mechanism (Intervention) → Mechanism (Reasoning) = Outcome [15]. Here, mechanisms are explained as both tangible facts and interpretative reasoning. This distinction allowed us to capture nuanced relationships, where contextual variations (such as different neighborhoods) activated distinct student responses, highlighting their reasoning and learning processes. Iterative data collection and hypothesis testing: Throughout the two-year study, we used iterative cycles of data collection and analysis to test and refine CMO hypotheses. For example, students’ final reports and oral reflective writing revealed varied learning patterns across different urban contexts. These iterative refinements aimed to uncover consistent CMO patterns that not only explained learning outcomes but also revealed transferable insights applicable to broader medical education contexts. Triangulation and validation: To enhance the rigor and validity of our findings, TA, HF, and JHira in the Department of General Medicine in X university independently reviewed the data and interpretations [40]. We employed triangulation to ensure consistency across different data sources, including field notes, student reports and reflective writings. The primary data for this study consist of student reports. No interviews were conducted specifically for the purpose of this study, and therefore, no verbatim transcripts were generated. However, reflective interviews conducted by TA and HF as part of the program were used to confirm the interpretation of the results and ensure alignment with the students’ perspectives.

This process verified that our CMO hypotheses accurately represented the mechanisms at play and were supported by the collected data, making the learning patterns in community-based medical education explicit and accessible for further adaptation.

The resultant CMO patterns provide an in-depth understanding of how medical students develop multi-dimensional perspectives on community health through structured fieldwork, particularly in complex urban settings. This evaluation not only contributes to the growing body of realist research in medical education but also provides practical insights for curriculum developers seeking to enhance students’ competencies in social medicine.

Reflexivity

JHaru, the primary analyst of the students’ reports and reflective writings, is an educator well-versed in the realist approach and a physician specializing in general medicine. Additionally, JHaru has expertise in ethnography, enabling the observation of interactions between educators and students and the incorporation of fieldwork methods. Both JHaru and HF hold doctoral degrees in medical education. TA has a Master’s degree in Public Health, while JHira has a background in basic research. All researchers are affiliated with the Department of General Medicine, bringing diverse experiences to the study. The authors offered a balanced perspective of insider and outsider views on the context of the students’ reports and reflection.

Ethics considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Keio University Ethics Committee (Approval No.: 20241001) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were provided with the opportunity to opt-out via the Keio University Center for Medical Education website, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

The study participants were fourth- and fifth-year medical students who completed the two-week general medicine program between January 2022 and December 2023. A total of 225 students participated, of whom 55 were female. All participants were Japanese citizens, and most grew up in urban environments, such as large cities or suburban areas.

Working hypothesis testing and CMO patterns

Through iterative testing of our initial hypotheses, we refined five major CMO patterns, reflecting how specific contexts (C) interacted with mechanisms (M) to yield distinct learning outcomes (O). To elucidate the causal relationships within each CMO pattern, the interactions between faculty and students during the community diagnosis process were systematically analyzed and articulated based on field notes. The orientation sessions and feedback on students’ presentations were designed to provide both a comprehensive overview while incorporating specific, illustrative examples. The feedback emphasized variation theory and phenomenological reduction (Table 1), and encouraged students to broaden their perspectives, challenge preconceptions, and reflect on biases. Summary and interpretation of learning patterns

The following section synthesizes the key learning patterns derived from these CMO configurations, demonstrating how contextual features activated learning mechanisms that contributed to students’ development of ecological, sociological, and reflective perspectives.

Theory-ladenness of observation and its overcoming: development of a sociological perspective

Medical students with limited sociological knowledge and difficulty identifying observation points in community diagnosis (C) were guided through orientation sessions that employed theory-laden observation techniques. These sessions encouraged students to become aware of their preconceived notions and provided instruction on observation points, core data items for community assessment, and the importance of preliminary data investigations prior to fieldwork (M). By applying these techniques, students recognized discrepancies between online research and direct field observations, fostering a deeper understanding of community characteristics. For instance, some students noted gaps between statistical data and the realities they observed, prompting them to contextualize this information sociologically. Through iterative feedback and reflective practice (M), students overcame biases, broadened their perspectives, and expanded their observational focus. As a result, they developed the ability to interpret community characteristics beyond assigned data, identify gaps between initial hypotheses and fieldwork findings, and refine their interpretations based on feedback (O). This approach facilitated the adoption of a more holistic perspective, allowing students to acknowledge the interconnectedness of community factors and gain a deeper understanding of community complexities.

Transition from biomedical to ecological systems perspective through contrasts

Medical students, typically trained to prioritize biomedical perspectives (C), were guided to integrate action plans for community diagnosis that applied ecological systems theory. This approach accounted for cultural norms and socioeconomic factors influencing health (M). Through report writing and reflective exercises, students engaged in contrastive learning, temporarily “bracketing” their existing biomedical assumptions to adopt a multi-level perspective encompassing the ecological systems framework (microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem, chronosystem) (M). This process enabled students to better understand the dynamic and layered interactions between individual health and broader community factors, fostering a shift from a solely biomedical focus to a holistic ecological systems perspective. As a result, students developed a comprehensive understanding of the socio-environmental impacts on health, recognizing complex interactions among social, economic, and cultural influences on disease and well-being (O). This transformative learning experience highlighted the value of an ecological approach in understanding health complexities, bridging individual and community perspectives.

Reflecting on authority and imagining minority perspectives: insights from positionality theory

Medical students from relatively affluent backgrounds, often holding implicit biases toward vulnerable populations (C), participated in community observations and interviews. These activities were guided by educators to help students avoid biases, supplemented by questions and feedback on their diagnostic results (M). Positionality theory was employed as a learning framework, encouraging students to reflect on their own social positions, power dynamics between healthcare providers and patients, and the implications of labeling and discrimination in healthcare. This reflective practice fostered an awareness of the structural and systemic factors that affect care quality and accessibility, particularly for minority and vulnerable populations (M). As a result, students recognized the power imbalances inherent in healthcare relationships, reflected on their own discriminatory attitudes, and developed greater empathy and sensitivity toward social minorities. This process enabled students to cultivate a deeper understanding of the social determinants of health and the importance of equitable, patient-centered care (O). While all individuals may hold implicit biases irrespective of their backgrounds, some students – based on their own reflections – identified privileged upbringings as limiting their prior exposure to structural disadvantage. In this study, reflections based on positionality theory allowed all students—not just those from privileged backgrounds—to critically examine their assumptions and better understand power dynamics and social marginalization.

Awareness of perspective and information granularity through double-loop learning

Groups of 5–6 students conducted independent community diagnoses in urban areas, followed by presentations and discussions (C). Through dialogue with peers and faculty during these sessions, students shared differing perspectives and interpretations, prompting them to recognize limitations and biases in their observations (M). This iterative process involved double-loop learning, where students revisited their foundational assumptions, reshaped their interpretative frameworks, and incorporated continuous feedback. This approach heightened their awareness of perspective diversity and the granularity of information in social contexts (M). As a result, students developed a deeper understanding of complex social contexts and refined their personal perspectives through iterative self-reflection and collaborative engagement (O). By engaging in this process, students expanded their ability to critically analyze social contexts, appreciating the nuanced interplay of diverse perspectives and detailed information, ultimately fostering a more comprehensive understanding of community health dynamics.

Intersubjectivity in understanding diverse urban settings

Medical students conducted independent community diagnoses in various urban districts, initially perceived as homogeneous, despite presenting diverse socio-economic and demographic characteristics (C). Through peer feedback sessions, students shared findings from their respective areas, highlighting varied perspectives and local details. This exchange of viewpoints facilitated intersubjectivity, where shared observations and discussions enriched the students’ understanding of urban diversity (M). By engaging with different perspectives, students moved beyond homogeneous or stereotypical views, instead developing a nuanced appreciation of the multi-dimensional complexities of metropolitan communities (M). As a result, students cultivated a layered understanding of urban settings, recognizing the intricate socio-economic and cultural dynamics that shape diverse urban areas (O). The intersubjective feedback process enabled students to embrace a more comprehensive and reflective approach to urban community diagnosis, fostering a deeper appreciation for the diversity inherent in metropolitan contexts.

Discussion

This study revealed the learning patterns of medical students in an urban community diagnosis program that integrated fieldwork. The findings suggest that incorporating field-based experiences in densely populated urban areas enabled students to cultivate perspectives distinct from those developed through online programs. Unlike online learning [19], which primarily engage students in theoretical inquiry and comparative studies to connect community concepts with specific issues, the incorporation of fieldwork provided transformative real-life experiences. These experiences not only facilitated profound shifts in perspective but also allowed students to actively immerse themselves in the complex dynamics of urban community health. Unlike previous online settings, where students often described their engagement as superficial and comparable to high school research projects [19], the hands-on nature of fieldwork allowed them to develop a deeper connection with the community and its unique challenges. This shift was evident in the absence of prior critiques and emergence of reflective insights, demonstrating the program’s significant impact on fostering authentic community engagement and advancing ecological and intersubjective understanding among students.

Our program’s design, emphasizing ecological systems theory [18] and phenomenological reduction [32], played a significant role in guiding students’ reflections and observations. Medical students, whose training often leans heavily on memorization of biomedical knowledge and efficiency-driven analysis [28, 34], were encouraged to adopt a more holistic view of community health. Through the theory-ladenness of observations and feedback mechanisms [9, 53], students not only expanded their observational skills but also experienced the multifaceted nature of communities, blending sensory experiences with reflective insights. Theory-ladenness permeates various stages of the scientific process, including perception, attention, thinking, experimentation, memory, and communication. These top-down processes, often unconscious, can both aid and hinder the accurate representation of reality [9]. Positionality provides a framework for understanding how sociological processes influence students’ identities, both in perpetuating deficit thinking and in challenging it, contributing to the development of culturally responsive pedagogical approaches [10, 31]. This approach used theory-laden observation and positionality theory to deepen students’ critical thinking and cultural awareness. By challenging preconceptions, it highlighted the impact of cultural, social, and economic factors on health, preparing future physicians to address health disparities in diverse societies.

The urban fieldwork also provided students with the time and experiential breadth necessary for double-loop learning, which allowed them to revisit and reconstruct their own cognitive frameworks [5]. Double-loop learning is defined as occurring “when errors are corrected by changing the governing values and then the actions” [5]. In group presentations and feedback sessions, students engaged in an iterative process of learning that fostered awareness of the granularity of social contexts and the limitations of their assumptions. This reflective practice facilitated students’ ability to view urban communities through an intersubjective lens, ultimately enriching their understanding of the complexities within urban settings [25, 48].

Additionally, while community-based learning is often associated with rural or underserved areas, this program highlighted the unique complexities and public health needs within urban settings. Each urban district has distinct demographic, cultural, and socio-economic characteristics that influence community health, and understanding these nuances is crucial for future physicians working in urban healthcare settings [7, 46]. The variation theory emphasized in this program allowed students to discern differences among urban areas, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach to community health and fostering a nuanced understanding of urban public health.

Limitations and future directions

This study had several limitations. First, data collection and analysis were based on hypotheses and theories constructed by the researchers, which may introduce researcher bias. To mitigate this, three authors critically reviewed the data and refined it over two program cycles. Second, the study was conducted within a single institution among specific faculty members, which may limit its generalizability. However, the CMO configurations described in this study offer a transferable framework that can guide the development and evaluation of similar community diagnosis programs in other educational contexts, particularly those involving social and behavioral sciences in medical curricula. By documenting learning patterns through CMO, this study provides a structured framework for developing and evaluating programs in other settings. While this program focused on urban community diagnosis, these learning patterns offer insights into curriculum development for understanding complex social structures beyond community diagnosis, and may inform the design of educational programs that encourage reflective practice in medical students. For institutions considering similar programs, we recommend allocating sufficient time for orientation to theory-laden observation, securing diverse field sites to maximize variation in student experience, and establishing structured feedback systems incorporating double-loop learning. Additionally, templates guiding hypothesis-driven observation can support consistent engagement across student groups.

Conclusion

In this community diagnosis program incorporating fieldwork in urban settings, we identified distinct learning patterns among medical students through the application of a realist approach. Students gained sociological insights rooted in theory-laden observation, developed an understanding of ecological systems through phenomenological reduction, and engaged in double-loop learning to re-evaluate their perspectives and assumptions. By navigating the multifaceted structure of urban communities, students were able to deepen their understanding of complex social determinants of health, which thereby fostered an enriched comprehension of public health issues within densely populated areas. These identified learning patterns provide valuable insights for the development of curricula aimed at training future physicians to critically engage with the complexities inherent to community healthcare.
