Amplifying Randomized Encodings & Applications
Pouria Fallahpour, Alex B. Grilo, Garazi Muguruza, Mahshid Riahinia

Abstract
A randomized encoding for a promise problem is a randomized reduction whose outcome distribution on input x x can be simulated within some distance d d , called privacy, using only one bit of information about x x : whether it is a YES or NO instance. The encoding is one-sided if this property holds only for YES instances. Our main contribution is showing that (classical and quantum) one-sided randomized encodings have privacy and correctness amplification: any problem with a randomized encoding with privacy 1 − 1 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) 1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n) and error 1 / 2 − 1 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) 1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n) , also has an encoding with negligible privacy and error. We then use this result to obtain the following statements: - We show that NISZK, the class of problems that have a non-interactive (statistical) zero-knowledge proof, has strong zero-knowledge amplification, i.e., every problem with a non-interactive zero-knowledge proof with zero-knowledge error 1 − 1 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) 1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n) , also has a non-interactive zero-knowledge proof with negligible zero-knowledge error. This solves a problem open since the work of Goldreich, Sahai, and Vadhan (CRYPTO ’99). - The worst-case hardness of a problem that has a perfect (i.e., zero error) one-sided encoding implies one-way functions (OWFs). And if the encoding is quantum, it implies one-way state generators (OWSGs). We then conclude that by removing the error from one-sided randomized encodings, the worst-case hardness of the complexity class SZK becomes sufficient for the existence of OWFs. - Weak and imperfect indistinguishability obfuscation (iO) implies OWFs assuming that the Polynomial Hierarchy does not collapse to its third level. Here, weakness means that the computational distance between the obfuscated circuit and the original circuit is 1 − 1 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) 1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n) , and imperfectness means that the error is 1 / 2 − 1 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) 1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n) . To achieve this privacy and correctness amplification, we study randomized encodings through the lens of lossy reductions (Ball et al. ITCS 2020). In particular, we introduce an extended and flexible notion of lossy reductions, and show that it is equivalent to randomized encodings. This equivalence serves as the base of our main results and might be of independent interest.

Introduction

For a function
f
and an input
x
, can one encode
x
into a string
z_{x}
that reveals nothing about
x
beyond
f(x)
? Of course, the value
f(x)
is itself a valid candidate for the encoding, so the question becomes non-trivial if we require that computing the encoding be simpler than computing
f
. It can be shown that deterministic encodings cannot generally satisfy this requirement
[
5
]
. However, randomness changes the picture: for a wide range of complex functions
f
, there exist randomized encodings that are far simpler to evaluate than
f
itself. The gap between the complexity of
f(x)
and that of
x\mapsto z_{x}
, together with the privacy of
z_{x}
, makes randomized encodings a rich tool for complexity theory as well as cryptography.

Ishai and Kushilevitz
[
39
]
showed that every function computable by a polynomial-size non-deterministic branching program (
\mathsf{NL}/\mathsf{poly}
) has a randomized encoding that can be evaluated as a polynomial of degree
3
. Consequently, securely evaluating any such function reduces to securely evaluating degree-
3
polynomials, which yields round-efficient secure-computation protocols for all
\mathsf{NL}/\mathsf{poly}
. Applebaum, Ishai, and Kushilevitz
[
6
]
pushed this further, and replaced the degree-
3
polynomials by constant-depth circuits of locality
4
(denoted by
\mathsf{NC}^{0}_{4}
). The evaluation of any function in
\mathsf{NL}/\mathsf{poly}
, including candidate pseudorandom generators (PRGs) and pseudorandom functions (PRFs), can thus be brought all the way down to
\mathsf{NC}^{0}_{4}
, which provides an evidence to the utility of randomized encodings.
Brakerski and Yuen
[
18
]
introduced a quantum analogue, i.e., quantum encodings, and showed applications in quantum zero-knowledge proofs.

More generally, for any promise problem
\Pi
one can study randomized encodings of its characteristic function
\chi_{\Pi}
, which on input
x
outputs
1
if
x\in\Pi_{Y}
and
0
if
x\in\Pi_{N}
. In this terms, the discussion above implies that the class of problems with
\mathsf{NC}^{0}_{4}
encodings contains
\mathsf{NL}/\mathsf{poly}
. What if we ask only that the encoding be computable in polynomial time? The resulting class has already been characterized
[
7
]
:

\mathsf{RE}[d(n),\mu(n)]
denotes the class of all problems
\Pi
for which there exists a polynomial-time algorithm
\mathsf{Enc}
(the randomized encoding) that satisfies the following two properties:

-

(
d
-Privacy) There exists a polynomial-time algorithm
\mathsf{Sim}
such that for all
n
-bit valid instances
x
:

\|\mathsf{Sim}(1^{n},\chi_{\Pi}(x))-\mathsf{Enc}(x)\|\leq d(n)~,

where
\|X-Y\|
denotes the statistical distance between two distributions
X
and
Y
.

-

(
\mu
-Correctness) There exists a (possibly unbounded) algorithm
\mathsf{Dec}
such that for all
n
-bit valid instances
x
:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{Enc}(x))\neq\chi_{\Pi}(x)\right]\leq\mu(n)~.

If the existence of the simulator is only guaranteed for the YES instances, the encoding is called
one-sided
and the corresponding class of promise problems is denoted by
1\mathsf{RE}[d(n),\mu(n)]
. When
d(n),\mu(n)=\mathsf{negl}(n)
, we drop these arguments for simplicity.

The class
\mathsf{RE}
trivially contains
\mathsf{BPP}
, that is the class of problems admitting probabilistic polynomial-time solvers with bounded error. However, no explicit non-trivial encoding is known for any problem beyond
\mathsf{BPP}
. There is evidence that the containment is nonetheless strict: the work of
[
1
]
showed that under suitable computational hardness assumptions,
\mathsf{RE}
must be strictly larger than
\mathsf{BPP}
. On the flip side, Applebaum and Raykov
[
7
]
showed that
1\mathsf{RE}
is equivalent to
\mathsf{NISZK}
, the class of all problems admitting a non-interactive (statistical) zero-knowledge proof system. Since
\mathsf{RE}\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}
, this bounds both classes. Despite these results,
\mathsf{RE}
and
1\mathsf{RE}
remain little understood, and yet important objects of study. Randomized encodings in constant depth gave us cryptography of remarkably low complexity; in the polynomial-time regime, separating
\mathsf{RE}
from
\mathsf{BPP}
would give us cryptography of another kind, namely OWFs
[
7
]
. Moreover, the close connection between polynomial-time randomized encodings and non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs makes them a natural tool for studying proof systems.
In this work, we study the properties of randomized encodings and show various applications to complexity theory and cryptography.

1.1
Our Contribution

In this work, we introduce a new technique for
privacy and correctness amplification
of
1\mathsf{RE}
.
More precisely, we prove the following lemma:

Key Lemma

(Privacy & Correctness Amplification of 1
\mathsf{RE}
)
.

For any sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

1\mathsf{RE}\left[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)\right]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}\left[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)\right]\,,

up to some polynomial-size non-uniform advice. Moreover, if the error of the left-hand side is zero, the error of the right-hand side will also be zero.

The above result is obtained by studying the
lossy
properties of randomized encodings. More precisely, we show that the class
1\mathsf{RE}
can be equivalently characterized by a quantitative measure that we call
sparse lossiness
.
Let us first define this measure formally.
Our starting point is the notion of
lossy reductions
, introduced by Ball et al.
[
9
]
.
In their definition, a reduction
R
for a promise problem
\Pi
is
\lambda(n)
-lossy if
I(X;R(X))\leq\lambda(n)
for
all
distributions
X
supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We relax this requirement as follows. Call
X
a
sparse uniform
distribution if it is uniform over a set of
\mathsf{poly}(n)
strings that lies entirely in
\Pi_{Y}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
or entirely in
\Pi_{N}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
. We then say that a reduction
R
is
\lambda(n)
-
sparsely lossy
if
I(X;R(X))\leq\lambda(n)
for all such
X
. In other words, we only require
R
to lose information on sparse uniform distributions of YES or NO instances.
We let
\mathsf{SL}[\lambda(n),\mu(n)]
denote the class of all problems that have
\lambda(n)
-
sparsely lossy
reductions with correctness error
\mu(n)
. Moreover, the reduction is said to be
one-sided
sparsely lossy
if it is
sparsely lossy
only on sparse uniform distributions over YES instances; we denote the corresponding class of problems by
1\mathsf{SL}[\lambda(n),\mu(n)]
. This refined definition allows us to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

For any error function
\mu(n)\leq 1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
the following statements hold:

1.

One-sided randomized encodings with inverse-polynomial privacy are equivalent to one-sided sparsely lossy reductions:

-

\forall d(n)\leq 1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
, we have
1\mathsf{RE}\left[d(n),\mu(n)\right]\subseteq 1\mathsf{SL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]
. Moreover, if
\mu(n)=0
, the error of the right-hand side is
0
.

-

We have
1\mathsf{SL}[O(\log n),\mu(n)]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}\left[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)\right]
.

2.

Randomized encodings are partially equivalent to sparsely lossy reductions:

-

We have
\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]\subseteq\mathsf{SL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]
.

-

We have
\mathsf{SL}[O(\log n),\mu(n)]\subseteq\mathsf{RE}\left[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),\mu(n)\right]
.
1
1

1

The inclusion can be extended to
sparsely lossy

f
-reductions, the reductions that take multiple instance as input such as an
Or
reduction. More details can be found in Section
3
.

The statements hold up to some polynomial-size non-uniform advice.

One can verify that the first item of this theorem implies our Key Lemma. The conceptual connection between randomized encodings and lossy reductions was already observed by
[
9
]
. In Theorem
1
, we make this connection precise and give an exact quantitative relation between the two.

Quantum Setting.
The techniques we use to prove Theorem
1
are generic, and largely independent of whether the underlying objects are quantum or classical. This allows us to obtain a quantum version of the statement. More precisely, let
\mathsf{QRE}
and
1\mathsf{QRE}
be the quantum analogues of
\mathsf{RE}
and
1\mathsf{RE}
, where the encoding and the simulator are allowed to be quantum algorithms. The inputs, which are instances of computational problems, remain classical, and statistical distance is replaced by trace distance. With these definitions, Theorem
1
and Key Lemma hold verbatim for
\mathsf{QRE}
and
1\mathsf{QRE}
.

Despite its simple statement, the Key Lemma is a powerful tool for obtaining further results. In particular, we use it to obtain the following applications.

Application 1: Zero-knowledge Amplification of NISZK.

Recall that
\mathsf{SZK}
is the class of problems who have a zero-knowledge interactive proof system. More precisely, in a zero-knowledge proof system for
\Pi
, an unbounded prover interacts with a polynomially-bounded verifier in order to prove that a given input
x\in\Pi_{Y}
is a YES instance, without revealing any extra information about
x
beyond this membership. Three quantities
(c(n),s(n),\varepsilon(n))
measure the quality of the proof system:

-

Completeness
: For any
x\in\Pi_{Y}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
, the verifier accepts with probability at least
1-c(n)
.

-

Soundness
: For any
x\in\Pi_{N}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
and every possibly malicious prover, the verifier accepts with probability at most
s(n)
.

-

Zero-Knowledge
: There exists a polynomial-time algorithm
\mathsf{Sim}
such that for every
x\in\Pi_{Y}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
, the distribution of
\mathsf{Sim}(x)
is within
\varepsilon(n)
statistical distance from the honest transcript of the protocol.

In its non-interactive variant, denoted
\mathsf{NISZK}
, the interaction between the prover and the verifier is limited to a single message, and both parties have access to a shared randomness that is sampled from an
a priori
fixed distribution, known as the common reference string.

In the usual definition of
\mathsf{SZK}
(respectively
\mathsf{NISZK}
), all three parameters are set to be negligible. Moreover, as long as
\varepsilon(n)
is negligible, any protocol with
c(n)+s(n)\leq 1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
can be amplified into one where all three parameters are negligible, and in fact exponentially small
[
34
,
56
]
. Amplifying the zero-knowledge property itself, meaning reducing
\varepsilon(n)
when it is not already negligible, appears to be more challenging. The one known result in this direction is due to Goldreich, Sahai, and Vadhan
[
34
]
, who considered a
weak
zero-knowledge property for
\mathsf{NISZK}
. This property requires that for every inverse polynomial
1/p(n)
, there exists a polynomial-time simulator whose output is within statistical distance
1/p(n)
of the honest transcript. They showed that any such protocol can be amplified to one with a single simulator of negligible error. A similar result holds for
\mathsf{SZK}

[
56
]
.
Yet, the following question has remained unanswered ever since:

Question 1. Can the zero-knowledge guarantee of
\mathsf{SZK}
or
\mathsf{NISZK}
protocols be amplified from inverse-polynomial to negligible error?

We prove the following theorem which answers this question in the affirmative for
\mathsf{NISZK}
:

Theorem 2
.

For every
c(n),s(n),\varepsilon(n)
satisfying
2s(n)+c(n)+\varepsilon(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
, it holds that
\mathsf{NISZK}[c(n),s(n),\varepsilon(n)]\subseteq\mathsf{NISZK}
(up to some non-uniform polynomial-size advice).

In particular, when
c(n)=s(n)=\mathsf{negl}(n)
and
\varepsilon(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
, the theorem above yields a zero-knowledge amplification for
\mathsf{NISZK}
from inverse-polynomial to negligible.

Application 2: OWFs and the Worst-Case Hardness of SZK.

One-way functions (OWFs) are fundamental cryptographic tools whose existence can be viewed as
the minimal assumption required for cryptography.
It is not known whether OWFs unconditionally exist or not.
Instead, their existence is often based on the worst-case hardness of concrete computational problems such as DLOG
[
27
]
, RSA
[
55
]
, lattice and code-based
problems
[
2
,
4

48
,
54
]
and more.
While a polynomial-time algorithm for any of these problems would have considerable implications for cryptography, the widespread belief of their worst-case hardness is based less on
structural

evidence and more on the failed
algorithmic
attempts in directly solving them.
In the broader sense, relying on just a few specific problems remains limiting.
A more robust approach is to look for OWFs based on the worst-case hardness of a large class of problems.
These types of connections provide stronger evidence for the existence of OWFs.

For OWFs to exist, it is necessary that
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}
. Whether this is sufficient is arguably one of the most important questions in the theory of cryptography.
In a series of works
[

\mathsf{SZK}
is the class of problems that have statistical zero-knowledge interactive proofs and is inside
\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{co}\mathsf{NP}
(in the non-uniform setting). On the other hand, many of the widely used assumptions such as lattice-based or group-based ones

Yet, none of them are complete for

This suggests that

showed that the the

yields OWFs.
Later, Ostrovsky and Wigderson

A positive result relating OWFs to the worst-case hardness of

Question 2. Can we show that the worst-case hardness of
\mathsf{SZK}
suffices for the existence of OWFs?

We show that an error removal for
1\mathsf{RE}
, even with loose privacy, is sufficient to prove that the worst-case hardness of
\mathsf{SZK}
implies the existence of OWFs. More formally,

Theorem 3
.

If
1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}\left[1-1/{\mathsf{poly}(n)},0\right]
(up to some non-uniform polynomial-size advice), and
\mathsf{SZK}

4
4

4

We note that by the Key Lemma, the privacy can always shrink to negligible, but we keep

We expect that an error removal algorithm for
1\mathsf{RE}
exists.
Without considering the privacy, a polynomial-time randomized reduction can be derandomized via the textbook repeat-then-majority procedures. Here, the challenge is to manipulate the randomness in order to not completely lose the structure (privacy).

Application 4: One-Way State Generators.

We also obtain a weaker variant of Theorem
3
for
inefficient-verifier one-way state generators
(IV-OWSG). Roughly speaking, an IV-OWSG consists of a polynomial-time quantum algorithm
\mathsf{StateGen}
that maps

to a quantum state
\rho_{k}
and a possibly unbounded algorithm
\mathsf{Ver}
that, given
k
and a state
\rho
, either accepts or rejects. For instance, one way to instantiate
\mathsf{Ver}
is to check whether
\rho
is close to
\rho_{k}
in trace distance. Moreover, it must be hard for any polynomial-time adversary to find a
k^{\prime}
, given multiple copies of
\rho_{k}
, such that
\mathsf{Ver}(k^{\prime},\rho_{k})
accepts. When
\rho_{k}
is a pure quantum state for every fixed

refers to those quantum encodings whose

Technical Overview

In this section, we briefly present the core technical tools that we use. We first discuss the proof of Theorem

We then sketch the proofs of Theorems
2
,
3
and

Proof sketch of Theorem
1
(Randomized encodings and
sparsely lossy
reductions).

In what follows, we sketch the proof of Theorem
1
separately for the two directions of the inclusions.

From sparse lossiness to encodings.

The difficulty in going from
\mathsf{SL}
to
\mathsf{RE}
lies in extracting a privacy simulator out of the
sparse lossiness
property.
To make this happen, we resort to the
disguising lemma
by Drucker
[
28
]
.
The proof works for general
f
-reductions
, but for simplicity, we restrict the discussion to regular reductions. Let
\Pi\in\mathsf{SL}
and
R
be a reduction from
\Pi
to some other problem that is
sparsely lossy
. The original variant of the disguising lemma states that if a mapping
R
is compressing, then for any subset
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, there exists a sparse distribution
D_{S}
supported on
S
, such that for all
y\in S
, the distribution of
R(y)
is statistically close to that of
R(D_{S})
, where the closeness depends on the amount of compression achieved by
R
—therefore, if
R
is sufficiently compressing, then
R

disguises

y
. More precisely,
\mathbb{E}_{D_{S}}[\|R(y)-R(D_{S})\|]\leq\delta^{*}
, where
\delta^{*}\approx 1+2^{-\lambda}
, if
R
compresses an
n
-bit instance to
\lambda
bits. Looking at it differently, since
R
is a reduction from a problem
\Pi
, we can set
S:=\Pi_{Y}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
and conclude that
R(D_{S})
is close to
R(y)
in average, for every YES instance
y
. In other words,
R(D_{S})
is a simulator for YES instances. Therefore, we obtain randomized encodings from compressing reductions. In other words, the class of problems with compressing reductions are a subset of those with randomized encodings.

While the original disguising lemma is stated for compressing reductions, the work of Ball et al.
[
9
]
extends it for lossy reductions
. Our challenge is to extend it even further for
sparsely lossy
reductions.

In order to sketch our improvements of this lemma,
we first briefly go over Drucker’s proof. Consider the following simultaneous-move two-player game: Given a set
S
and
d\leq|S|
, Player
1
chooses a
d
-sparse distribution
D
supported on
S
, and Player
2
chooses an element

(1)

Before using the minimax theorem, we restrict Player

For the one-sided variant, we show how to reduce the privacy to negligible in the next parts.

From encodings to sparse lossiness.

Assume that
\Pi\in\mathsf{RE}
. Recall that the privacy property of the randomized encoding puts an upper-bound on the statistical distance between the output distributions of the encoding
\mathsf{Enc}(x)
and the simulator
\mathsf{Sim}(\chi_{\Pi}(x))
. Since
\mathsf{Sim}
depends only on
\chi_{\Pi}(x)
(i.e., whether
x
is a YES or NO instance), rather than the whole input
x
, intuitively,
\mathsf{Enc}
should discard all information about its input beyond its membership in the language. Yet, the proof is not immediate. We sketch this proof below.

We first explain why
\mathsf{Enc}
is sparsely lossy for the case where privacy
d
is negligible.
We have to show that
I(X;\mathsf{Enc}(X))
is bounded for every distribution
X
that is sparse, splitting and uniform, as required by the definition of the sparse lossiness, provided earlier in this section. For any two random variables
X
and
Y
, it holds that
I(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}_{Y}[D_{\textrm{KL}}(p_{X|Y}\|p_{X})]
where
D_{\textrm{KL}}
is the KL-divergence.
It is therefore sufficient to bound
D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(p_{X|\mathsf{Enc}(X)}\ \|\ p_{X}\right)
.
We do this by leveraging the
reverse Pinsker’s inequality
; for any two distributions
p\ll q
(i.e.,
p
is absolutely continuous with respect to
q
), the reverse Pinsker’s inequality bounds
D_{\textrm{KL}}(p\|q)\leq(\log e/\alpha_{q})\cdot\Delta(p,q)^{2}
, where
\alpha_{q}=\min_{x}\{q(x)>0\}
. Applying this bound and taking the expectation over
\mathsf{Enc}(X)
yields an upper bound on the lossiness of
\mathsf{Enc}
as follows:
I(X;\mathsf{Enc}(X))\leq O(d^{2}/\alpha_{X})
, where
d
denotes the privacy of the encoding. Since
X
is uniform over
\mathsf{poly}(n)
-many elements, it holds that
\alpha_{X}=1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
. Therefore, if
d=\mathsf{negl}(n)
, we have that that
I(X;\mathsf{Enc}(X))\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)
. In other words,
\mathsf{Enc}
is a sparsely lossy reduction for
\Pi
as long as
d=\mathsf{negl}(n)
.

For extending this result to larger values of
d(n)
, we use the
XOR lemma

[
56
]
. In a nutshell, XOR lemma takes as input a polynomial
q(n)
and transforms a pair of circuits into a new pair whose statistical distance is that of the initial pair to the power of
q(n)
. Suppose that
d(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)

. Applying it to
(\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Sim})
yields a new pair
(P,T)
.
A similar argument as in the previous paragraph gives us an upper bound on
I(X;P(X))\leq O(d^{2q}/\alpha_{X})=\mathsf{negl}(n)
, when
q(n)
is a sufficiently large polynomial. Therefore, it follows that
P
is a sparsely lossy mapping for
\Pi
. However the correctness error of
P
is not necessarily negligible. In fact,
one can show that
\Delta(P(x_{Y}),P(x_{N}))\geq 1-2\mu
for YES and NO instances
x_{Y}
and
x_{N}
, when
\Delta(\mathsf{Enc}(x_{Y}),\mathsf{Enc}(x_{N}))\geq 1-2\mu
. In other words, the error remains the same (see Lemma
2.6
for details). In order to reduce the error, we consider the new amplified reduction
P^{\otimes\ell}
, where we repeat
P
for
\ell
times. By the direct product lemma
[
56
]
, for a sufficiently large polynomial
\ell(n)
, we get
\Delta(P^{\otimes\ell}(x_{Y}),P^{\otimes\ell}(x_{N}))\geq 1-2\exp(-\ell(1-2\mu)^2/2)=1-\mathsf{negl}(n)
while the effect on the mutual information is
I(X;P^{\otimes\ell}(X))\leq O(\ell\cdot d^{2q}/\alpha_{X})=\mathsf{negl}(n)
. This gives us

1\mathsf{RE}[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),\mu(n)]\subseteq 1\mathsf{SL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]\,.

(3)

For two-sided randomized encodings, i.e., when the simulator works on both YES and NO instances, the amplification no longer works.
In the output of the XOR lemma,
P
(and similarly
T
) is not solely obtained from
\mathsf{Enc}
and internally runs
\mathsf{Sim}
as well. Let
\mathsf{Sim}(1)
and
\mathsf{Sim}(0)
be the simulators for YES and NO instances, respectively. The issue comes from the fact that we obtain different
P
circuits when we apply the XOR lemma to
(\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Sim}(1))
and
(\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Sim}(0))
. This prevents from coming to agreement for the choice of the reduction. Therefore, this method only works for proving the sparse lossiness of one-sided encodings.

From negligible
sparse lossiness
to negligible privacy.

When
\lambda
, the
sparse lossiness
quantity, is sufficiently small, we construct another encoding different from the one obtained by disguising lemma.
Let
\Pi\in\mathsf{SL}[\lambda,\mu]
for some
\lambda\ll 1
and
R
be its corresponding
sparsely lossy
reduction. Moreover, take as advice
x_{1}^{*}\in\Pi_{Y}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
and
x_{0}^{*}\in\Pi_{N}\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
(in the one-sided variant, only
x_{1}^{*}
suffices). The randomized encoding for
\Pi
is as follows:

\mathsf{Enc}(y):=R\left(y\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\forall b\in\{0,1\}:\mathsf{Sim}(b):=R\left(x_{b}^{*}\right)\,.

The correctness transfers immediately by definition.
For computing the privacy, we proceed as follows. Fix an input
y
and let
b:=\chi_{\Pi}(y)
. We must bound
\|\mathsf{Enc}(y)-\mathsf{Sim}(b)\|
from above. Let
X
be a random variable that is
y
with probability
1/2
and
x^{*}_{b}
otherwise. Note that
X
has a uniform distribution over
2
elements. Therefore, the
sparse lossiness
guarantee that
I(X;R(X))\leq\lambda
. Let
M
be a random variable that is
\mathsf{Enc}(y)
with probability
1/2
and
\mathsf{Sim}(b)
otherwise. Then, we have that

\displaystyle I(X;R(X))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{R(X)|X}\|p_{R(X)})]=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathsf{Enc}(y)}\|p_{M})+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathsf{Sim}(b)}\|p_{M})\,.

The right-hand side can be bounded from below by
\|\mathsf{Enc}(y)-\mathsf{Sim}(b)\|^{2}/(2\ln 2)

according to Pinsker’s inequality and the fact that
\|\mathsf{Enc}(y)-M\|=\|\mathsf{Sim}(b)-M\|=\|\mathsf{Enc}(y)-\mathsf{Sim}(b)\|/2
. Therefore, if
\lambda(n)=\mathsf{negl}(n)
, the privacy of the encoding would be negligible. In other words, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{SL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]/\mathsf{poly}(n)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle 1\mathsf{SL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]

\displaystyle\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mu(n)]/\mathsf{poly}(n)\,.

Finally, combining Equations (
2
), (
3
), and (
4
) yield Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
2
(amplifying
\mathsf{NISZK}
).

The proof is a direct application of Key Lemma. Applebaum and Raykov implicitly (see
[
7
, Theorme 7 & 8]
) show that for every integer
k\geq 1
, it holds that
\mathsf{NISZK}[c,s,\varepsilon]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[k(c+\varepsilon),(2s)^{k}+k(c+\varepsilon)]
. By taking
k=1
and imposing the condition
2s(n)+c(n)+\varepsilon(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
the former collapses to
1\mathsf{RE}[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)]
which itself is inside
1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]
by Key Lemma. Finally, note that
1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]
is equivalent to
\mathsf{NISZK}
up to some polynomial-size advice
[
7
, Therorem 1]
. This completes the proof.

Proof sketch of Theorem
3
(from
\mathsf{SZK}
to OWFs via error removal of
1\mathsf{RE}
).

Let
\Pi\in 1\mathsf{RE}[d(n),0]
be a worst-case-hard problem with encoding
\mathsf{Enc}
and one-sided simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
.
7
7

7

Note that since
\Pi\in 1\mathsf{RE}
, the simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
works only on YES instances. We therefore omit
\chi_{\Pi}(x)
from the input of
\mathsf{Sim}
, as it is always 1 in this case.

The circuits
\mathsf{Enc}
and
\mathsf{Sim}
are randomized and take random coins as input. Without loss of generality, we can assume that they both use
\kappa
bits of randomness. Note that
\kappa=\omega(\log n)
since otherwise
\Pi\in\mathsf{BPP}
.

Let
C_{0}(\cdot)

and
C_{1}[y](\cdot)
respectively denote
\mathsf{Sim}(\cdot)
and
\mathsf{Enc}(y;\cdot)
whose randomness is considered as the input. Let
\mathsf{F}:\{0,1\}^{\kappa}\times\{0,1\}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{*}
be the following function:

\mathsf{F}(b,r):=\begin{cases}C_{0}(r)&b=0\\
C_{1}[x](r)&b=1\end{cases}\,

where

is a fixed YES instance of
\Pi
.
We show that
\mathsf{F}
is a weak OWF, meaning that the probability of any polynomial-time adversary successfully inverting it on a random
\kappa
-bit input is at most
3/4
. This implies the existence of OWFs via Yao’s hardness amplification
[
65
]
.
Assume that there exists an inverter
\mathcal{A}

for
\mathsf{F}
that succeeds with probability larger than
3/4
. We use
\mathcal{A}
to decide any given instance
y
of
\Pi
as follows: Compute
C_{1}(r)
,
and
C_{1}[y](r)
for a random value
r
, and then return
C_{b}(r)
to
\mathcal{A}
. If
b=0
,
then
\mathcal{A}
receives an instance of the function
\mathsf{F}
and can therefore invert
it. This does not help us with solving
\Pi
. Conversely, when
b=1
, if
y

is a YES instance, then by the privacy of the encoding, the statistical distance between
C_{1}[y]
and
C_{1}[x]
would be at most
2d
. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}
would succeed to invert it with probability
>3/4-2d
. On the other hand,
when
b=1
, if
y
is a NO instance, then
C_{1}[y]
and
C_{1}[x]
have distinct support. This is because the encoding has perfect correctness. Therefore, if
b=1

and
y
is a NO instance, then there would be no preimage for the value that
\mathcal{A}
aims to invert, which results
in
\mathcal{A}
failing. Now, assume that
d\leq 1/4
. Then we have
3/4-2d\geq 1/4
and, therefore, there is a constant gap between the success rate of
\mathcal{A}
for YES and NO instances of
y
. By repeating this test for several times, we can decide

by
observing the success rate of
\mathcal{A}
. Therefore,
\mathsf{F}
is a weak OWF in this case.

So far, we have required that

. Fortunately, this limitation can be lifted according to Key Lemma since
1\mathsf{RE}[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),0]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[1/4,0]
. Therefore the worst-case hardness of
1\mathsf{RE}[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),0]
implies OWFs.
On the other hand, note that
1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]=\mathsf{NISZK}

[
7
]
(up to non-uniform advice) and that
\mathsf{NISZK}
is worst-case hard if and only if

is worst-case hard
[

]
. Hence, the worst-case hardness of
\mathsf{SZK}
implies the worst-case hardness of

and if
1\mathsf{RE}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),0]

Proof sketch of Theorem
4
(from weak & imperfect iO to OWFs).

To obtain the theorem, we analyze the statistical behavior of obfuscation. An algorithm is said to be
\alpha(n)
-statistical obfuscation (sO)
if it satisfies the following indistinguishability property: the obfuscations of any two functionally-equivalent circuits on
n
-bit inputs are at most
\alpha(n)
-statistically far. We show that if sO with distance
\alpha(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
and error
\varepsilon(n)=1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
exists, then
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP}/\mathsf{Poly}
, i.e., the Polynomial Hierarchy collapses to its third level.
8
8

8

Recall that an obfuscation
\mathsf{O}
has error
\varepsilon(n)
if for every circuit over
n
-bit inputs, it holds that
\Pr[\exists x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\mathsf{O}(C)(x)\neq C(x)]\leq\varepsilon(n)
.

The key idea is to leverage sO
to build a randomized encoding for the unique satisfiability (
Unique
Sat
) problem. This problem asks to decide whether a
3\textsc{CNF}
formula over
n
variables has a satisfiable assignment under the promise that it has at most one of such. Works of
[
44
,
38
]
observe that sO gives a worst-case to average-case reduction for
Unique
Sat
. We follow the same blueprint. More precisely, if a circuit
C
is a YES instance of
Unique
Sat
, i.e., it evaluates to 1 on exactly one input, then a random shift of
C
, namely the circuit
C_{z}(x)=C(x\oplus z)
, where
z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{}_{\$}}}{{\leftarrow}}\{0,1\}^{n}
, has a truth-table whose distribution is identical to that of a random point function
. Therefore,
\text{sO}(C_{z})
is statistically close to
\text{sO}(\mathds{1}_{x=x^{*}})
, where
x^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{}_{\$}}}{{\leftarrow}}\{0,1\}^{n}
.
This gives us a one-sided randomized encoding for
Unique
Sat
, with encoding
\mathsf{Enc}(C;r,z)=\text{sO}(C_{z};r)
and simulator
\mathsf{Sim}(1;r,z)=\text{sO}(\mathds{1}_{x=z};r)
(which works for YES instances).
As a result
\mathsf{UP}\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[\alpha,\varepsilon]
, where
\alpha
is the statistical indistinguishability bound of sO and
\varepsilon
is the error.
9
9

9

This part must be done carefully and requires a change of variable from
n
, the size of the input, to
|C|
the instance size since the class
1\mathsf{RE}
is parameterized by the instance size. Also note that
Unique
Sat
is complete for
\mathsf{UP}
.
By using Key Lemma, we can amplify the privacy and the correctness which leads to the following:

\mathsf{UP}\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}[\alpha,\varepsilon]\subseteq 1\mathsf{RE}\left[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)\right]/\mathsf{poly}=\mathsf{NISZK}/\mathsf{poly}\,.

Hence,
\mathsf{UP}
collapses to
\mathsf{SZK}/\mathsf{poly}
. Finally, via the Valiant-Vazirani polynomial-time reduction of
3\textsc{Sat}
to
Unique
Sat
, this sequence implies that
3\textsc{Sat}\in\mathsf{SZK}/\penalty\mathsf{poly}
, meaning that the Polynomial Hierarchy collapses to its third level.

In fact, a two-sided randomized encoding for
Unique
Sat
can be obtained by the exact same idea of leveraging the obfuscation. This is because for a non-satisfiable circuit
C
, we have that
\text{sO}(C;r)\approx\text{sO}(Z;r)
where
Z
is the zero function. However, the rest of the argument does not hold due to lack of a privacy amplification and error reduction for two-sided encodings.

We now draw the final conclusion of this part. The preceding analysis implies that, unless the PH collapses, any (
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
)-sO requires either
\alpha
to be negligibly close to
1
, or
\varepsilon
to be negligibly close to
1/2
. Now consider an iO with computational distance
\tau(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
and error
\varepsilon(n)=1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
. It follows that for any such iO, there exist two families of circuits
\{C_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{C^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n}
such that their obfuscated variants are statistically far, as the statistical distance has to be
\alpha(n)=1-\mathsf{negl}(n)
(or else, the PH collapses), and computationally close, as we assumed the computational distance to be
\tau(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
. This statistically-far-but-computationally-close pair of distributions is an
EFI pair
, which is known to imply OWFs
[
33
,
51
,
12
]
. Putting everything together, assuming the existence of iO with computational distance
\tau(n)=1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
and error
\varepsilon(n)=1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)
implies the existence of OWFs unless the PH collapses to its third level.

1.4
Open Questions

The most important question that remains open is an error removal for
1\mathsf{RE}
. As we showed, such an amplification would immediately implies OWFs from the worst-case hardness of
\mathsf{SZK}
. Even a weaker amplification such as the following one yields the same result: if we can efficiently (up to some advice) recognize the instances or/and the random coins on which the encoding errs. Also working with the sparsely lossy characterization of
1\mathsf{RE}
might be helpful. We leave this question to the future work.

Preliminaries

In this work, we always consider non-uniform algorithms. All classical algorithms are quantum algorithms, therefore, we mostly use the quantum formalism for generalization and simplification. When the distinction is necessary, we explicitly mention it in the beginning of a section or inside a statement.

. The set of all permutations over
[n]
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{+}
the set of positive real numbers.

A function
f(n)
is
\mathsf{poly}_{\ell}(n)
if
f(n)=O(n^{\ell})
(we drop
\ell
when the degree is not specified), is
\mathsf{negl}(n)

\mathsf{subexp}(n)

f(n)=2^{O(n^{c})}

.

Uniform and
s
-Uniform Distributions.
For any set

\mathcal{U}_{S}

Promise Problems.
A Promise Problem
\Pi
consists of two disjoint sets
\Pi_{Y}
and
\Pi_{N}
in
\{0,1\}^{*}
, respectively referred to as the set of YES and NO instances. Problem
\Pi
asks to decide whether a given instance, which is promised to lie in
\Pi_{Y}\cup\Pi_{N}
, belongs
\Pi_{Y}
or
\Pi_{N}
.

Definition 1

(Characteristic Function of a Promise Problem)
.

For a promise problem
\Pi
, its characteristic function is the map
\chi_{\Pi}(x):\{0,1\}^{*}\rightarrow\{0,1,\star\}
given by

{\chi}_{\Pi}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in\Pi_{Y}\\
0&\text{if }x\in\Pi_{N}\\
\star&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}~.

In this work, we consider worst-case hardness as introduced below.

Definition 2
.

A promise problem
\Pi
is worst-case hard, if for any (randomized or quantum depending on the context) polynomial-time algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and any sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an input
x\in(\Pi_{Y}\cup\Pi_{N})\cap\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(x)=\chi_{\Pi}(x)]<2/3
.

Classical information.
Given two probability distributions
X
and
Y
over
\Sigma
, their statistical distance, also called total variation distance, is defined as

\Delta(X,Y):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Sigma}|\Pr(X=x)-\Pr(Y=x)|~.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence or classical relative entropy of
X
with respect to
Y
is defined as

D_{\textrm{KL}}(X\|Y):=\sum_{x\in\Sigma}\Pr(X=x)\log(\frac{\Pr(X=x)}{\Pr(Y=x)})~.

Lemma 2.1

(Chernoff-Hoeffding Bound)

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{k}
be mutually independent random variables in
[0,1]
and
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}]
. For every

, it holds that
\mathrm{Pr}[|\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}-\mu|>t]\leq 2\exp(-2t^2/k)
.

Quantum information.

For any two mixed states
\rho
and
\sigma

D_{\textrm{tr}}:=\Tr(|\rho-\sigma|)/2
denote their trace distance.
Let
R:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{MS}_{m}
be any quantum mapping and

a random variable supported over

. We let

\displaystyle\rho_{X,R(X)}:=\sum\limits_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Pr_{X}(x)\outerproduct{x}{x}\otimes R(x)\ .

(5)

For a mixed state
\rho
, we let
S(\rho):=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_{2}\rho)
denote the Von Neumann entropy of
\rho
.
The quantum mutual information of two subsystems

and

is defined as follows. Let
\rho_{AB}
be their joint state, then

I_{q}(A;B)_{\rho}:=S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{AB})\ ,

where
\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\rho_{A}B)
and
\rho_{B}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\rho_{A}B)
. For the sake of simplicity, we sometimes drop the subscripts
q
and

. When working with quantum systems

implicitly refers to

.

The following lemma states that if the outcome of a measurement is close to deterministic, then it must not alter much the state.

Lemma 2.2

(Gentle Measurement Lemma
[
64
]
)

Let
\rho
be a mixed state and
\{\Lambda,I-\Lambda\}
a two-outcome POVM with
\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda\rho)\geq 1-\varepsilon
, then
D_{\textrm{tr}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon}/2
, where
\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}\rho\sqrt{\Lambda}}{\mathrm{Tr}(\Lambda\rho)}
.

For two quantum states
\sigma,\rho
stored in two different registers
A,B
, the swap test is executed on the registers
A,B
and a control register
C
initialized to
\outerproduct{1}{1}
.
It applies Hadamard on
C
, swaps
A
and
B
conditioned on
C
, and measures
B
on the Hadamard basis.

Lemma 2.3

(SWAP Test
[
20
]
)

The SWAP test on input
(\sigma,\rho)
outputs 1 with probability
(1+\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\sigma))/2
, in which case we say that it passes the test. For pure states
\ket{\sigma},\ket{\rho}
, it equals to
(1+|\innerproduct{\rho}{\sigma}|^{2})/2
.

Given that the trace distance of two pure states
\ket{\sigma},\ket{\rho}
can be expressed in terms of the inner product uniquely as
\sqrt{1-|\innerproduct{\rho}{\sigma}|^{2}}
, the SWAP test can also be used to calculate their trace distance.

Distance notation.

To avoid repeated proofs, we use the following definition to unify the distance analysis in both classical and quantum settings.

Definition 3
.

We use the notation
\|X-Y\|
to refer to the statistical distance
\Delta(X,Y)
when variables
X,Y
are classical distributions, or the trace distance when they are quantum states.

We will use the following lemmas.

Lemma 2.4

(Direct Product Lemma
[
61
,
56
]
)

Let
X,Y
be two quantum states or classical distributions such that
\|X-Y\|=\varepsilon
. For any
k
, it holds that

1-2\exp\left(\frac{-k\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\right)\leq\left\|X^{\otimes k}-Y^{\otimes k}\right\|\leq k\varepsilon\,.

Lemma 2.5

(XOR Lemma
[
61
,
56
]
)

Let
\mathsf{XOR}
be a function that maps the tuple
(X_{0},X_{1},1^{k})
to
(Z_{0},Z_{1})
as follows:

Z_{0}:
Uniformly sample
(b_{1},\cdots,b_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{k}
such that
b_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus b_{k}=0
and output a sample from
X_{b_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{b_{k}}
.

Z_{1}:
Uniformly sample
(b_{1},\cdots,b_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{k}
such that
b_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus b_{k}=1
and output a sample from
X_{b_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{b_{k}}
.

Then
\|Z_{0}-Z_{1}\|=\|X_{0}-X_{1}\|^{k}
.

Lemma 2.6

Let
(X_{0},X_{1})
and
(Y_{0},Y_{1})
be such that
\|X_{0}-Y_{0}\|\geq\varepsilon
and
\|X_{1}-Y_{1}\|\geq\delta
. Let
(P,\cdot):=\mathsf{XOR}(X_{0},X_{1},1^{k})
and
(Q,\cdot):=\mathsf{XOR}(Y_{0},Y_{1},1^{k})
for some
k\geq 2
. Then
\|P-Q\|\geq(\varepsilon+\delta)/2
.

Proof.

Consider the first component of
P
. The marginal distribution of
b_{1}
(see Lemma
2.5
) is uniform. Thus with half probability
b_{1}=0
and the first component is selected from
X_{0}
. Same holds for
Q
: when
b_{1}=0
, its first component is
Y_{0}
. Note that the distance is bounded by
\varepsilon
in this case. Same holds for
b_{1}=1
with the lower bound
\delta
. This completes the proof.
∎

Permutation-invariant function.

A function is permutation-invariant if applying any permutation to its inputs does not alter its output value. This includes a wide range of functions, such as
\and,\textsc{Or}
,
Maj
, and
Parity
.
We use the following technical lemma about non-constant permutation-invariant functions.

Lemma 2.7

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a non-constant permutation-invariant function. Then there exists an integer
1\leq p\leq m
such that

f(\ \underbrace{1,1,\ldots,1}_{p-1},0,0,\ldots,0)=0\,,\quad\text{and}\quad f(\ \underbrace{1,1,\ldots,1}_{p},0,0,\ldots,0)=1\ .

We let
p(f)
denote the minimum choice of such an integer.

Proof.

The set
\{0,1\}^{m}
can be partitioned into
m+1
equivalence classes where each class consists of strings with the same number of
1
’s. We note that the result of a permutation on an input falls in the same equivalence class. Therefore, since the function is permutation-invariant, then the evaluation of
f
over each input is determined by its class. Because the function is non-constant, there must exist two consecutive classes (the classes can be ordered by the number of
1
’s that they represent) with different evaluation under
f
. This completes the proof.
∎

Complexity Classes

In this section, we define useful complexity classes. We begin by the class of problems that have “lossy” reductions. In our analysis, we consider both randomized functions and quantum mappings. All the statements hold with respect to both cases.
We explicitly highlight the distinction when the analysis requires to distinguish between the two cases.

Classically, a randomized function

.
Below, we also consider general mappings with classical input and quantum output.

Definition 9

Let

(classical mapping) or

-lossy for an
m
-tuple distribution

over

For the sake of simplicity, we say that
R
is
\lambda
-lossy, if it is
\lambda
-lossy for all
m
-tuple distributions.

As we will see later, it is useful to limit the input distributions to have a particular “splitting” structure.

Definition 10

Let

or
\mathrm{Supp}(X_{i})\subseteq S_{1}
.

An example of such distributions is

where

and

have disjoint supports.

In this work, we consider distributions that are not only sparse but also are uniform.

Definition 11

For example, distribution
(\mathcal{U}_{n},\cdots,\mathcal{U}_{n})

In this section, we show that

Definition 15

Main theorem of this section is the following:

Theorem 4.1

For any permutation-invariant function

and any polynomial

, it hold that
\mathsf{SL}_{f}[\lambda,\mu]\subseteq\mathsf{RE}[\delta+1/q,\mu]/\mathsf{poly}
and
\mathsf{QSL}_{f}[\lambda,\mu]\subseteq\mathsf{QRE}[\delta+1/q,\mu]/\mathsf{poly}
where

The advice size itself is a polynomial in

The technical core for this theorem is an extended variant of

.
The disguising lemma first appeared in
[

Theorem 5.2

Theorem 5.3

The following statement holds:

1\mathsf{QRE}[1-1/\mathsf{poly}(n),1/2-1/\mathsf{poly}(n)]\subseteq 1\mathsf{QSL}[\mathsf{negl}(n),\mathsf{negl}(n)]\,.

We first recall the required preliminaries.
We define the quantum conditional entropy of a two-system state
\rho_{AB}
as follows

\displaystyle S(A|B):=S(\rho_{AB})-S(\rho_{B})\,,

The quantum mutual information in terms of conditional quantum entropy is

I(A;B)_{\rho}=S(\rho_{A})-S(A|B)_{\rho}=S(\rho_{B})-S(B|A)_{\rho}\,.

Lemma 5.2

Let
\rho_{AB}
be a quantum state in two subsystems
A
and
B
, with marginal states
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
. The following properties hold.

1.

The Von Neumann entropy is additive for tensor product states:
S(\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})\,.

2.

Conditioning does not increase entropy:
S(\rho_{A})\geq|S(A|B)_{\rho}|\,.

: The quantity of

Two-Player games.
A two-player, simultaneous-move, zero-sum game is specified by a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}
. Player 1 chooses a row index
i\in[a]
and Player 2 chooses a column index
j\in[b]
, and Player 2 receives the payoff
\mathbf{M}_{ij}
from Player 1. The goal of Player 1 is minimizing the expected payoff, while Player 2 opts to maximize it. The row and column indices are called the pure strategies of Player 1 and Player 2, respectively. The mixed strategies are distributions or possible choices of indices. A mixed strategy is
s
-uniform if it is sampled uniformly from a multiset of at most
s
pure strategies.

Lemma 0.A.1

(
[
59
]
)

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be two mixed strategies for Player 1 and 2, respectively. It holds that

\min_{\mathcal{P}}\max_{j}\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathcal{P}}[\mathbf{M}_{ij}]=\max_{\mathcal{Q}}\min_{i}\mathbb{E}_{j\sim\mathcal{Q}}[\mathbf{M}_{ij}].

The value of the game, which we denote by
\omega(\mathbf{M})
, is the optimal expected value guaranteed by the above lemma.
The following lemma shows that each player has nearly-optimal
s
-uniform strategy when

is chosen to be logarithm of the number of pure strategies of the opponent.

(

where
