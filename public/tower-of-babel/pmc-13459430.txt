Association of 14 triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices with new-onset cardiovascular disease in middle-aged and older adults: evidence from the CHARLS and ELSA aging cohorts
Authors: Zhang-le Zhu, Hui-fen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be a predominant contributor to global illness and death rates.Insulin resistance and adiposity are closely linked to cardiometabolic risk, but the comparative value of single-time and cumulative average triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-based indices for predicting new-onset CVD remains uncertain. This study aimed to compare multiple TyG-based indicators, evaluate the contribution of cumulative exposure, and develop a clinically accessible risk-stratification tool.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study incorporated a total of 5,028 middle-aged and older adult participants, drawn from two major national aging surveys: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). Cumulative average TyG-based indicators were constructed using two pre-follow-up measurement waves, with follow-up beginning in 2015 for CHARLS and 2008 for ELSA. Seven single-time TyG-based indicators and seven cumulative average indices were evaluated. Cox regression analysis, restricted cubic splines(RCS), random-effects meta-analysis, subgroup analyses, mediation analyses, and predictive performance assessments were performed. A nomogram and web-based prediction tool were developed for 5-year CVD risk estimation.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 5.0 years in CHARLS and 13.5 years in ELSA, 1,067 incident CVD events occurred. In the pooled cohort, all 14 TyG-based indicators were significantly associated with incident CVD. Composite indices integrating TyG with obesity-related measures generally showed stronger associations and better predictive performance than the TyG index alone. Cumulative average indices provided only modest improvement over their single-time counterparts, with heterogeneity across cohorts. Restricted cubic spline analyses showed nonlinear associations for TyG-BMI and cumTyG-BMI, whereas other indices showed approximately linear associations. CumTyG-BRI showed relatively robust cross-cohort performance, with an HR of 1.54 (95%CI, 1.27–1.86) for Q4 versus Q1 and low between-cohort heterogeneity (I² = 6.6%). Mediation analysis suggested that hypertension partly mediated the association between CumTyG-BRI and CVD risk, accounting for 20.6% of the total effect. A TyG-BRI-based nomogram showed moderate discrimination, with a C-index of 0.679, and was deployed as an online tool for preliminary 5-year CVD risk estimation.ConclusionsCumTyG-BRI may be a relatively robust TyG-related indicator for predicting new-onset CVD across cohorts, with low heterogeneity and an approximately linear association. Hypertension may partly mediate this association. Introduction

CVD continues to be a predominant contributor to global illness and death, especially within populations of middle-aged and elderly individuals. The pathogenesis of CVD is intrinsically linked to the chronic progression and aggregation of various metabolic dysregulations. These encompass conditions such as insulin resistance, excessive body fat accumulation, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and impaired renal function [1, 2].The recently proposed cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework further highlights the biological interconnection among metabolic dysfunction, renal impairment, and cardiovascular injury [2]. Therefore, simple and reproducible indicators that capture early cardiometabolic burden may help improve CVD risk stratification before overt clinical events occur.

Insulin resistance is a key factor in the development of CVD, as it contributes to endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and vascular remodeling [3, 4]. While the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is recognized as the gold standard for measuring insulin resistance, its complexity and resource-intensive nature make it unsuitable for routine clinical practice or large-scale epidemiological research. As a result, TyG index—derived from fasting triglyceride and fasting glucose levels—has gained widespread acceptance as a practical and reliable surrogate marker for assessing insulin resistance [5].Numerous cohort studies and meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that higher TyG index values are linked to an increased risk of CVD, coronary artery disease, stroke, and overall mortality [6, 7]. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the TyG index primarily captures disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism. As such, it may not fully account for the additional cardiovascular risks associated with obesity and variations in body fat distribution.

To more comprehensively characterize the combined burden of insulin resistance and adiposity, several composite indices derived from the TyG index have been developed. These indices integrate the TyG index with key anthropometric measures, such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and the body roundness index (BRI). Emerging research indicates that these enhanced indices may offer superior predictive value and provide additional insights for assessing cardiometabolic risk when compared to the traditional TyG index alone [8–10].Meanwhile, emerging evidence within the CKM framework indicates that TyG-based indicators are associated with CVD incidence and mortality across different CKM stages, supporting their relevance as integrated metabolic-risk markers [11, 12].

Nevertheless, important knowledge gaps remain. First, most previous studies relied on single-time measurements, which may be affected by short-term biological variability and may not adequately reflect sustained exposure to insulin resistance and adiposity. Recent longitudinal studies using TyG trajectories or repeated TyG assessments have shown that dynamic changes in TyG are associated with incident CVD and cardiovascular mortality [13, 14].However, evidence regarding cumulative average TyG-based composite indices remains limited. Second, most available studies were conducted in single-country or single-population settings, and cross-population validation remains insufficient. This is important because the distribution and predictive performance of TyG-based indicators may vary by ethnicity, socioeconomic background, lifestyle, and healthcare context. Third, although multiple TyG-based indicators have been proposed, direct comparisons between single-time and cumulative average indices within the same analytical framework remain scarce.

To address these gaps, we used two independent longitudinal aging cohorts, the CHARLS and ELSA, to evaluate the associations of single-time and cumulative average TyG-based indicators with new-onset CVD. We further compared their predictive performance, examined dose-response patterns, assessed cross-cohort consistency, and explored potential mediating pathways. By integrating repeated metabolic measurements and validation across two distinct populations, this study aimed to identify a robust and clinically interpretable TyG-based indicator for CVD risk stratification in aging populations.

Methods

Study population and design

This study is based on data from two large-scale, prospective, national longitudinal surveys. Harmonized data, developed by the Gateway to Global Aging Data, were used for inclusion and exclusion procedures to harmonize variable construction across the different databases.

CHARLS targets adults aged 45 and older in China. The baseline survey for the study was initiated in 2011, encompassing 150 county-level units and 450 communities distributed across 28 provinces. A total of 17,707 participants were initially enrolled, achieving a response rate of 80.5%. Follow-up assessments were conducted every two years thereafter. ELSA targets adults aged 50 and older in England, its baseline survey was initiated in 2002–2003, with biennial follow-ups.

For the CHARLS cohort, 11,847 participants with baseline data in 2011 and blood test data in both 2011 and 2015 were initially included. The year 2015 was used as the baseline, and data from 2011 to 2015 were used to construct the cumulative average TyG (triglyceride-glucose) index. First, individuals with missing data on triglycerides, blood glucose, body weight, height, or waist circumference in 2011 or 2015, as well as those aged < 50 years, were excluded. After further excluding individuals with pre-existing cardiovascular disease at the 2015 baseline, 3,800 participants were finally included. These participants were followed from 2015 to 2020, with a median follow-up of 5 years, to monitor dynamic changes in incident cardiovascular disease within the cohort.

For the ELSA cohort, 7,666 participants with baseline data in 2004 and blood test data in both 2004 and 2008 were initially included. Applying the same exclusion criteria as for CHARLS, individuals with missing data on the aforementioned variables, those aged < 50 years, and those with pre-existing cardiovascular disease in 2008 were excluded, the final sample size for the study was 1,228 participants. These participants were followed biennially from 2008 to 2021, with a median follow-up of 13.5 years, to track incident cardiovascular disease. Both studies adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards (CHARLS: Institutional Review Board of Peking University, IRB 00001052–11015; ELSA: jointly approved by the University College London Research Ethics Committee and the research ethics committees of participating regions). All participants provided written informed consent.

Definition of TyG-based indicators

Based on previously published formulas [15, 16], the TyG index and its derived composite indicators were constructed in both cohorts, including TyG-body roundness index (TyG-BRI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-weight-adjusted waist index (TyG-WWI), TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) and TyG-a body shape index (TyG-ABSI).

Variables prefixed with “Cum” denote the cumulative average of the corresponding indices, including cumulative average TyG (cumTyG) and its derived composite indices: CumTyG-BMI, CumTyG-WC, CumTyG-WHtR, CumTyG-BRI, CumTyG-WWI, and CumTyG-ABSI.

Cumulative average exposure is commonly used in longitudinal epidemiology to approximate the sustained burden of a risk factor over a defined exposure-assessment window. For metabolic indicators, this approach may better reflect medium- to long-term metabolic homeostasis, such as persistent insulin resistance and adiposity-related metabolic stress, than a single-time measurement that may be affected by short-term biological variability. In this study, cumulative average TyG-based indicators were calculated using two pre-follow-up blood-measurement waves. Although this two-point cumulative average is mathematically equivalent to the arithmetic mean of the two measurements, it represents the average metabolic burden during the exposure-assessment period rather than a single isolated measurement. For the CHARLS cohort, cumulative average TyG-based indicators were calculated as the mean of the 2011 and 2015 measurements: CumIndex = (Index_2011 + Index_2015) / 2 [17]. For the ELSA cohort, cumulative average TyG-based indicators were calculated as the mean of the 2004 and 2008 measurements: CumIndex = (Index_2004 + Index_2008 / 2.

The specific formulas are as follows:

TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI.

TyG-WHtR = TyG × WHtR.

TyG-WC = TyG × WC.

TyG-BRI = TyG × BRI.

TyG-ABSI = TyG × ABSI.

TyG-WWI = TyG × WWI.

BRI = 364.2 − 365.5 ×√1 − (WC (cm) /2π)²/ (height (cm) /2)².

BMI=weight (kg) / height²(m²).

WWI = WC (cm) /√weight (kg).

WHtR = WC (cm) / height (cm).

ABSI = WC(m) / (BMI (kg/m²)2/3× height (m)¹/²).

Definition of outcomes

Study outcomes were standardized across both databases using the Harmonized data portal developed by the Gateway to Global Aging. CVD was defined based on participant-reported, physician-diagnosed heart disease (indicates whether the respondent reported having coronary heart disease, heart attack, congestive heart failure, angina, or other heart problems) or stroke (indicates whether the respondent reported having a stroke).

Covariates

The following five categories of variables were included as study covariates: (1) demographic data—age, gender, marital status (married/unmarried), educational level (college or above/secondary school/primary school or below), and current residence (urban/rural); (2) lifestyle data—status of smoking(current/former/never) and alcohol consumption status (current/former/never); (3) medical history—physician-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia (yes/no).

For the CHARLS cohort, blood samples were obtained by on-site medical personnel and promptly transported to hospital laboratories within the local area for analysis, where standardized methods and equipment were used for analysis. Heart rate was assessed by employing an OMRON HEM7200 digital blood pressure monitor to measure the pulse. After the participant had rested quietly, three measurements were taken at 45‑second intervals, and the average value was used as the final result. Height, body weight, and waist circumference were measured by trained survey staff following standardized protocols. Height was measured with Seca™ 213 stadiometer, which has a precision of 0.1 cm. Body weight was assessed using Omron™ HN‑286 electronic scale, accurate to 0.1 kg. Waist circumference was taken at the level of the lower border of the umbilicus, with an error margin of less than 0.5 cm. All measurements were conducted while participants wore light clothing and were barefoot.

For the ELSA cohort, blood samples were collected by trained nurses during home visits, transported under cold conditions, stored at − 80 °C, and then sent to a central laboratory (Royal Victoria Infirmary, Newcastle upon Tyne) for standardized analysis and quality control prior to analysis. Physical measurements were completed by trained nurses during home visits. Blood pressure was assessed utilizing an Omron HEM‑907 automated sphygmomanometer. Following a five-minute period of seated rest, three consecutive readings were obtained at one-minute intervals, with the mean of these measurements employed for subsequent analysis. Height was determined using a Leicester portable stadiometer (accurate to 0.1 cm), body weight was measured with a Tanita THD‑305 electronic scale (accurate to 0.1 kg), and waist circumference was recorded at the midpoint between the anterior superior iliac spine and the lower costal margin (accurate to 0.1 cm).

Statistical analysis

Missing data are summarized in Table S1. Missing covariate data were addressed through multiple imputation via chained equations, operating under the assumption that data were missing at random. The resulting estimates were subsequently pooled and integrated using Rubin’s rules. Exposure-related variables, including triglycerides, fasting blood glucose, waist circumference, weight, and height, were winsorized at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme values. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using appropriate statistical methods, including analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Kruskal–Wallis test, the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test. Participants were categorized based on quartile divisions for each TyG-based indicators.

Associations between TyG-based indicators and incident CVD were examined using Cox regression. Each index was analysed as quartiles, with the lowest quartile as the reference, and as a standardized continuous variable per 1-standard-deviation increase. Four sequential models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for gender and age; Model 3 further adjusted for residence, education, marital status, smoke and drink; and Model 4 additionally adjusted for diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia. In the combined cohort, cohort source was further included as a covariate. Statistical significance of linear trends across quartiles was evaluated by modeling quartile categories as continuous ordinal variables in a regression analysis.

To evaluate cross-cohort consistency, cohort-specific estimates derived from CHARLS and ELSA were combined via a random-effects meta-analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using fixed-effect models. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified utilizing the I² statistic and assessed for significance with Cochran’s Q test.

RCS models were used to examine dose-response associations between TyG-based indicators and incident CVD in the pooled cohort, with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles and using the median as the referent. Models were adjusted for the same covariates as Model 4. The overall significance and potential nonlinear patterns in the associations were assessed through likelihood ratio tests. For indices showing evidence of nonlinearity, the shape of the association was interpreted descriptively. Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate cumulative CVD incidence across quartiles of each TyG-based index, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. To improve comparability across cohorts with different follow-up durations, pooled curves were truncated at 5 years.

Predictive performance was evaluated by adding each TyG-based index to the fully adjusted baseline model. The CHARLS cohort was used for model development, and the ELSA cohort was used for external validation. Discrimination was evaluated using the C-index and the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for predicting incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) at both 5-year and 13.5-year follow-up intervals.Integrated discrimination improvement(IDI) and net reclassification improvement(NRI) were calculated to assess the incremental predictive value of each index.

Subgroup analyses were performed for selected TyG-based indicators, including TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-BRI, CumTyG-BMI, CumTyG-WC, CumTyG-WHtR, and CumTyG-BRI. Subgroups were defined by age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia status. Interaction was assessed by including cross-product terms between the index and subgroup variable.

Based on the overall results from association analyses, predictive performance, meta-analysis, subgroup analyses, and cross-cohort consistency, CumTyG-BRI was selected for mediation analysis. Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, diastolic blood pressure and systolic blood pressure were examined as candidate mediators using bootstrap procedures in the comb cohort.

A 5-year CVD risk prediction model incorporating TyG-BRI and the fully adjusted covariates was developed.A nomogram along with an interactive web-based prediction tool were developed, with internal validation conducted through 500 bootstrap resamples. The model’s performance was evaluated using the optimism-corrected C-index and calibration plots.

As a sensitivity analysis, Fine–Gray competing-risk models were fitted to account for death as a competing event, using the same covariate adjustment strategy as the primary Cox models.

All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.2. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 5,028 participants were included in this study, comprising 3,800 from the CHARLS cohort and 1,228 from the ELSA cohort. The baseline characteristics of the CHARLS cohort stratified by quartiles of the TyG index are presented in Table 1. For the ELSA cohort, see Table S2. In the CHARLS cohort, the median age was 64 years (IQR: 59–70), 50.4% were female, and the majority were married (80.6%), lived in rural areas (69.1%), and had an educational level of elementary school or below (76.6%). The prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia were 32.8%, 9.7%, and 16.1%, respectively. In the ELSA cohort, the median age was 65 years (IQR: 60–71), 56.9% were female, most participants were married (95.0%) and urban residents (59.3%), with a predominant educational level of middle school (54.4%). Current smoking and drinking rates were 11.1% and 90.5%, respectively, and the prevalences of hypertension and dyslipidemia were 36.2% and 37.1%, respectively. Across TyG quartiles, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, triglycerides, CRP, and all anthropometric indices showed significant graded trends in both cohorts (all P < 0.001). Participants in Q4 were more likely to be male and current smokers and drinkers, and had higher prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia than those in Q1. Between the two cohorts, CHARLS participants were more likely to be rural residents with lower education levels, whereas ELSA participants were predominantly urban residents with higher education levels. ELSA participants had higher systolic blood pressure (132.5 vs. 127.0 mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (75.5 vs. 73.5 mmHg), body weight (75.5 vs. 56.8 kg), waist circumference (94.9 vs. 84.9 cm), and BMI (26.9 vs. 23.1 kg/m²) than CHARLS participants. The median triglyceride levels were comparable between the two cohorts (111.9 vs. 115.1 mg/dL), while fasting glucose was higher in CHARLS (95.5 vs. 86.5 mg/dL). ELSA had a higher prevalence of dyslipidemia (37.1% vs. 16.1%), whereas CHARLS had a higher prevalence of diabetes (9.7% vs. 2.4%).

Discriminatory performance of TyG-based indicators for CVD prediction

To evaluate the discriminatory ability of TyG-based indicators for CVD risk, we trained Cox Model 4 in the CHARLS cohort and performed external validation in the ELSA cohort, with ROC curves constructed at two time points: 5-year cutoff (60 months) and longest follow-up (approximately 13.5 years). In the training set, AUCs were comparable across all 14 indices, ranging from 0.638 (CumTyG-ABSI, CumTyG-WWI) to 0.642 (CumTyG-BMI). In the 5-year cutoff validation set, AUCs ranged from 0.605 (TyG) to 0.622 (CumTyG-BRI), with CumTyG-BRI (AUC 0.622, 95% CI 0.561–0.682), TyG-BRI (AUC 0.620, 95% CI 0.561–0.680), CumTyG-BMI (AUC 0.618, 95% CI 0.558–0.678), and TyG-WC (AUC 0.617, 95% CI 0.557–0.678) performing relatively better, while TyG (AUC 0.605, 95% CI 0.544–0.666) and TyG-ABSI (AUC 0.607, 95% CI 0.546–0.668) performed relatively worse. In the longest follow-up validation set, AUCs generally decreased, ranging from 0.564 (TyG, TyG-ABSI) to 0.579 (CumTyG-BRI), suggesting reduced predictive performance with extended follow-up. DeLong tests showed that most pairwise AUC differences did not reach statistical significance in the training set (P > 0.05); in the 5-year cutoff validation set, differences were also mostly nonsignificant, although TyG versus TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR and TyG-BRI reached statistical significance; in the longest follow-up validation set, TyG showed significant differences from most adiposity-integrated indices (e.g., TyG-WC, TyG-BMI, TyG-BRI, all P < 0.05). Overall, all indices demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability (AUC 0.60–0.64), with adiposity-integrated indices marginally outperforming TyG alone in the validation sets, though the magnitude of advantage was limited.See in Fig. 5.

To assess the added predictive utility of TyG-based indicators markers over traditional cardiovascular risk factors, we conducted NRI and IDI analyses. In the CHARLS cohort (Table S7), six indices showed significant IDI (IDI 0.0011–0.0021, P < 0.05), with the largest improvements for cumTyG-WC (IDI 0.0021, P = 0.003) and cumTyG-BRI (IDI 0.0019, P = 0.010); for NRI, six indices were significant (NRI 0.081–0.117, P < 0.05), with the greatest improvements for cumTyG-WC (NRI 0.117, P = 0.002) and TyG-WC (NRI 0.113, P = 0.003). In the ELSA cohort (Table S8), six indices showed significant IDI (IDI 0.0039–0.0052, P < 0.05), with the largest improvements for TyG-WHtR (IDI 0.0052, P = 0.015) and TyG-BRI (IDI 0.0049, P = 0.022), and the absolute IDI values were higher than those in the CHARLS and combined cohorts; however, NRI improvements were relatively limited, with only TyG-BMI (NRI 0.145, P = 0.033) and TyG-WC (NRI 0.146, P = 0.033) reaching statistical significance, while the remaining indices showed nonsignificant NRI.

Subgroup analyses: Associations of TyG-based indicators with CVD risk

To assess the consistency of associations across different populations, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia for all eight indices (Table S9). Overall, the effect directions of most indices were consistent with the main analysis across subgroups, and most interaction tests were nonsignificant (P for interaction > 0.05), suggesting limited modification effects of these covariates. Notably, CumTyG-BRI showed consistent effect directions across all subgroups (Fig. 6), aged ≥ 65 years (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.09–1.85, P = 0.008), female (HR 1.37, 95% CI 1.02–1.84, P = 0.035), without hypertension (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.04–1.66, P = 0.020), without diabetes (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.09–1.62, P = 0.004), without dyslipidemia (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.01–1.57, P = 0.040), and with dyslipidemia (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.07–2.36, P = 0.022), with all interactions being non‑significant(all P for interaction 0.32–0.93), indicating a robust association with CVD risk across different populations. Among the remaining indices, only TyG-BRI showed significant heterogeneity between participants with and without diabetes (P for interaction = 0.021).

Mediation analysis

Given the robust effect of CumTyG-BRI across cohorts in Cox analyses, the absence of significant interactions in subgroup analyses (all P for interaction > 0.05), its incremental predictive value demonstrated by ROC and NRI/IDI analyses, and low heterogeneity in the meta-analysis (I² = 0.0664), we selected CumTyG-BRI as the exposure variable for mediation analysis to explore the mediating roles of hypertension, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, and dyslipidemia in the association between CumTyG-BRI and CVD risk (Fig. 7). Mediation analysis showed that hypertension had the most significant mediating effect (indirect effect = 0.00279, Sobel P < 0.001), accounting for 20.63% of the total effect. Systolic blood pressure also showed a significant mediating role (proportion mediated 13.84%). Dyslipidemia (proportion mediated 8.02%, Sobel P < 0.001) and diabetes (proportion mediated 7.33%, Sobel P = 0.016) showed relatively smaller but statistically significant mediating effects. Diastolic blood pressure had a modest mediating effect (proportion mediated 5.90%). Path coefficients showed that CumTyG-BRI was significantly positively associated with hypertension (a = 0.038, 95% CI 0.033–0.043), systolic blood pressure (a = 0.268, 95% CI 0.226–0.310), diastolic blood pressure (a = 0.138, 95% CI 0.114–0.163), diabetes (a = 0.038, 95% CI 0.029–0.047), and dyslipidemia (a = 0.016, 95% CI 0.010–0.022); these mediators were also significantly positively associated with CVD risk (b = 0.007–0.469, all P < 0.05). These findings suggest that the effect of CumTyG-BRI on CVD risk is partially mediated through blood pressure, glycemic, and lipid pathways, with hypertension representing the most important mediating pathway.

Development of an online assessment tool based on a nomogram

Although CumTyG-BRI showed robust performance in Cox regression, subgroup analysis, meta-analysis, and mediation analysis, its calculation requires longitudinal follow-up data, limiting its clinical utility. In contrast, TyG-BRI can be calculated using a single measurement and thus has better clinical accessibility. Therefore, this study selected TyG-BRI as the core exposure variable and constructed an individualized 5-year CVD risk nomogram based on the combined cohort of 5,028 participants (Fig. 8). The nomogram incorporated TyG-BRI, age, gender, smok, drink, marital status, education, residence, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cohort. The model achieved a C-index of 0.679 (MAE = 0.028), indicating moderate discriminative ability. The calibration curve showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with a calibration slope of 0.985, a mean predicted probability of 0.839, and a mean observed probability of 0.837. Based on this nomogram, we developed an online 5-year CVD risk assessment tool (https://baozhadekele.shinyapps.io/TyG_BRI_Shiny_Improved/). By entering clinical parameters including fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, height, waist circumference, demographic information, and medical history, users can obtain real-time individualized 5-year CVD risk probabilities and stratified health recommendations.

Sensitivity analysis

To address the possibility that death might act as a competing event affecting CVD risk estimation, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the Fine-Gray competing risks regression model. In the CHARLS cohort (N = 3800, median follow-up 5 years, 761 CVD events, 183 deaths), Fine-Gray competing risks results treating death as a competing event were highly consistent with the primary Cox proportional hazards analysis (Table S10). Among 28 statistical tests (quartile and Z-score for 14 indicators), 24 were consistent with the primary analysis, with only 4 discrepancies: TyG-WHtR Q4 (Cox HR = 1.25, 95%CI 0.99–1.58, P = 0.061 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.28, 95%CI 1.02–1.61, P = 0.035) and Z-score (Cox HR per 1-SD = 1.08, 95%CI 0.99–1.17, P = 0.075 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.08, 95%CI 1.00–1.17, P = 0.048), and CumTyG-WHtR Q4 (Cox HR = 1.23, 95%CI 0.98–1.55, P = 0.073 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.26, 95%CI 1.00–1.57, P = 0.048) and Z-score (Cox HR = 1.08, 95%CI 0.99–1.17, P = 0.069 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.09, 95%CI 1.00–1.18, P = 0.038), which were marginally non-significant in the primary Cox analysis but reached statistical significance in the Fine-Gray model. In the ELSA cohort (N = 1228, median follow-up 13.5 years, 306 CVD events, 26 deaths), Fine-Gray competing risks analyses also confirmed the robustness of the primary findings (Table S11). Among 28 tests, 26 were were concordant with primary analysis, with only 2 discrepancies: TyG-WWI Z-score (Cox HR per 1-SD = 1.14, 95%CI 1.01–1.29, P = 0.031 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.12, 95%CI 0.99–1.27, P = 0.063) and CumTyG-WWI Q4 (Cox HR = 1.45, 95%CI 1.02–2.07, P = 0.039 vs. Fine-Gray SHR = 1.37, 95%CI 0.97–1.96, P = 0.077), which were significant in the Cox model but became marginally non-significant in the Fine-Gray model. Overall, SHR estimates from Fine-Gray models were slightly attenuated compared to corresponding Cox HR estimates, as expected in the presence of competing events, yet the direction, statistical significance, and relative ranking of all indicators remained largely consistent with the primary analysis, indicating that competing events did not materially alter the core conclusions of this study.

Discussion

This study systematically compared 14 TyG-based indicators for predicting incident CVD in order and middle-aged adults from two independent longitudinal cohorts. Three major findings emerged. First, composite indices integrating TyG with obesity-related measures, particularly TyG-BMI, TyG-BRI, CumTyG-BMI, and CumTyG-BRI, showed stronger associations with incident CVD than the TyG index alone. Second, cumulative average TyG-based indicators provided only modest and inconsistent improvement over single-timepoint indices, suggesting that the added value of two-point cumulative exposure may be limited. Third, CumTyG-BRI showed relatively robust cross-cohort performance, low heterogeneity, and an approximately linear association with CVD risk, with hypertension identified as an important mediating pathway.The comparative analysis of the 14 indices confirmed that TyG-based composite indicators incorporating anthropometric measures were more informative than the TyG index alone for new-onset CVD risk stratification. This finding is consistent with growing evidence that the combined burden of insulin resistance and adiposity may be more closely related to cardiovascular risk than either metabolic abnormality alone [18, 19]. The TyG index reflects glucose-lipid metabolic disturbance, whereas anthropometric measures such as BMI, WHtR, and BRI capture different dimensions of general or central adiposity. Among these measures, WHtR and BRI may be particularly relevant because central obesity is closely associated with visceral fat accumulation, chronic low-grade inflammation, free fatty acid release, endothelial dysfunction, and worsening insulin resistance [20]. Therefore, indices such as TyG-BRI and CumTyG-BRI may better capture the combined metabolic and adiposity-related burden underlying CVD development. In contrast, TyG-ABSI showed relatively weaker predictive performance in this study, with lower AUC values and less consistent improvement in risk reclassification. This may reflect the limited incremental value of more complex body-shape indicators over simpler measures of general or central adiposity in order and middle-aged adults [21–22]. The relatively better performance of TyG-BRI is biologically plausible because BRI integrates waist circumference and height to approximate body roundness and abdominal adiposity, which are closely linked to cardiometabolic risk [14, 23]. These findings suggest that, for population-level CVD risk stratification, TyG-based indicators incorporating central or general obesity may be more practical and informative than indices based on body-shape complexity.

TyG-BMI and CumTyG-BMI, which exhibited a nonlinear association with new-onset CVD, whereas linear associations were observed for all other indices. The restricted cubic spline for CumTyG-BMI identified an inflection point at 218.61, beyond which the risk of CVD increased more steeply. This nonlinearity, and the potential for a threshold effect, is not entirely without precedent and may be attributed to the composite nature of the index itself. BMI is a well-recognized, though imperfect, marker of adiposity, and its relationship with cardiovascular outcomes in older populations is frequently J- or U-shaped, a phenomenon often referred to as the “obesity paradox” [20]. The TyG-BMI index, by multiplying the TyG index by BMI, could be amplifying this complex, non-monotonic relationship. In younger cohorts or those without significant comorbidities, a linear increase in risk with rising TyG-BMI may be evident [24]. However, in our cohort of middle-aged and older adults, a moderate degree of adiposity may confer some protective metabolic reserve, a benefit that is lost and rapidly reversed as adiposity and insulin resistance become severe. This threshold effect highlights a critical consideration for clinical application: a single, uniform risk gradient should not be assumed across the entire spectrum of values for TyG-BMI, and the identification of a specific cutoff may be useful for targeted interventions.

The greater cross-cohort heterogeneity observed for TyG-BMI and CumTyG-BMI further supports the need for cautious interpretation. Although all indices were associated with CVD risk, the magnitude of association for BMI-based indices varied more substantially between CHARLS and ELSA. This heterogeneity may be partly explained by differences in baseline body composition, obesity distribution, ethnicity, lifestyle, and healthcare context between the two cohorts. ELSA participants had a higher mean BMI than CHARLS participants, but the metabolic meaning of a given BMI value may differ across populations. BMI reflects total body mass rather than visceral adiposity, making BMI-based indices more vulnerable to population-specific differences in fat distribution and lean mass. In contrast, indices related to central adiposity, such as BRI-based indicators, may provide more consistent risk information across populations [20, 25].An important finding of this study is that cumulative average TyG-based indicators did not consistently outperform their single-timepoint counterparts. In the CHARLS and pooled cohorts, several cumulative indices, including CumTyG-BRI, CumTyG-BMI, and CumTyG-WC, showed modest improvements in AUC, NRI, or IDI. However, these improvements were small in absolute magnitude and were not consistently observed in the ELSA cohort. This suggests that although cumulative average exposure may better approximate sustained metabolic burden than a single measurement, its incremental predictive value may depend on cohort characteristics, follow-up duration, sample size, event number, and measurement consistency. Moreover, because the cumulative average indices in this study were based on only two pre-follow-up measurements, they may not fully capture complex long-term metabolic trajectories or time-weighted exposure patterns. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of long-term metabolic trajectories and dynamic changes in TyG-based markers [26, 27], and future studies with more repeated biomarker measurements are needed to determine whether trajectory-based or refined cumulative exposure metrics provide additional predictive value.

The mediation analysis provided a mechanistic framework for the observed associations, revealing that hypertension served as the most significant mediator, accounting for over 20% of the effect of CumTyG-BRI on incident CVD. Systolic blood pressure, dyslipidemia, and diabetes also played substantial, albeit smaller, roles. These findings are highly concordant with a large body of pathophysiological research. Insulin resistance is a fundamental driver of hypertension through multiple mechanisms, including activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, increased renal sodium reabsorption, enhanced sympathetic nervous system activity, and direct impairment of vascular endothelial function [28–30]. Using the TyG index as a surrogate for IR, and central obesity, as captured by BRI, synergistically promote these pathways. The elevated risk associated with a high TyG-BRI is thus, in large part, channeled through the development of elevated blood pressure. This aligns with Bayesian network analyses demonstrating that hypertension and chronic kidney disease are key intermediate factors on the pathway from insulin resistance to CVD [29]. The relatively modest yet significant mediating effect of dyslipidemia is consistent with studies showing that inflammation and blood pressure are more potent mediators than lipid levels alone in some populations [31]. These results strongly support a causal pathway where insulin resistance and central obesity promote a cascade of cardiometabolic disorders, culminating in CVD. This further underscores the principle that strategies designed to enhance insulin responsiveness and decrease abdominal adiposity are essential (e.g., lifestyle modification, GLP-1 receptor agonists) would yield benefits by simultaneously targeting multiple mediating risk factors [32, 33].

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, although the use of two large, independent cohorts from different continents strengthens the generalizability of our findings, the inherent differences in population characteristics and potential variations in measurement protocols contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Second, CVD events were ascertained through self-report in both cohorts, which, while standard for large-scale epidemiological studies, is subject to recall bias and underestimation compared to adjudicated events. Third, although multicollinearity diagnostics showed no serious collinearity among the covariates included in the fully adjusted models, and although we rigorously adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic factors, the observational design precludes causal inference. Residual confounding from factors unavailable or not consistently measured across both cohorts, such as dietary patterns, physical activity, medication use and adherence, inflammatory markers, familial CVD history, and other unmeasured cardiometabolic risk factors, cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, because the databases contain a limited amount of blood test data in the databases and the impact of missing values, we were unable to utilize additional waves of blood test data to construct more comprehensive cumulative exposure indices. Therefore, the cumulative average TyG-based indicators in this study were based on only two pre-follow-up measurements. Although this approach reflects the average metabolic burden during the exposure-assessment window, it could not capture more complex long-term trajectories, time-weighted exposure patterns across multiple waves, or short-term changes occurring between survey waves. Finally, we could not evaluate integrating the TyG index with other emerging inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index, a combination that might further optimize risk prediction [34, 35]. Future research should focus on externally validating these indices in other ethnic groups, establishing clinically meaningful cut-off values, and exploring their utility in guiding preventive interventions. The integration of cumulative risk metrics into dynamic risk models could usher in a new era of more precise, personalized cardiometabolic prevention.

Conclusion

Composite TyG-based indicators incorporating obesity measures showed stronger associations with incident CVD than the TyG index alone, whereas cumulative average indices provided only modest and inconsistent improvement over single-timepoint indices. CumTyG-BRI emerged as a relatively robust indicator across cohorts, with low heterogeneity and an approximately linear association. Hypertension may partly mediate this association. The TyG-BRI-based online prediction tool further supports the potential translation of these findings into preliminary CVD risk stratification, although prospective multicenter validation is needed before routine clinical use.
