Wasserstein Concentration of Empirical Measures for Dependent Data via the Method of Moments
Arash A. Amini, Luciano Vinas

Abstract
We establish a general concentration result for the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical measure of a sequence of random variables and its expectation. Unlike standard results that rely on independence (e.g., Sanov’s theorem) or specific mixing conditions, our result requires only two conditions: (1) control over the variance of the empirical moments, and (2) a flexible tail condition we term Ψ r n \Psi_{r_{n}} -sub-Gaussianity. This approach allows for significant dependencies between variables, provided their algebraic moments behave predictably. The proof uses the method of moments combined with a polynomial approximation of Lipschitz functions via Jackson kernels, allowing us to translate moment concentration into topological concentration.



1
Introduction

The convergence of the empirical measure
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i}}
to a deterministic limit
\bar{\mu}
is a fundamental problem in probability and statistics. When the variables
Y_{i}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), classical results such as Sanov’s theorem or the Varadarajan theorem provide strong guarantees. However, in many modern high-dimensional settings—such as the spectral distribution of random matrices, interacting particle systems, or feature propagation in graph neural networks—the variables
Y_{i}
exhibit complex dependency structures.

In such dependent settings, establishing independence or mixing properties can be intractable. However, it is often possible to compute the
moments
of the system,
\mathbb{E}[n^{-1}\sum Y_{i}^{k}]
, via combinatorial techniques (e.g., trace methods or walk counting). The classical
Method of Moments
[
4
, Section 2.5]
uses these algebraic quantities to prove weak convergence.

In this note, we provide a “glue” theorem that upgrades algebraic moment control to concentration in the 1-Wasserstein metric (
W_{1}
). Specifically, we show that if the variance of the empirical moments vanishes asymptotically, and the tails of the distribution decay sufficiently fast, then the empirical measure concentrates in
W_{1}
.

A key technical contribution is the handling of the test functions. While moment convergence naturally controls polynomials, the
W_{1}
metric is defined via Lipschitz functions. To bridge this gap, we employ a polynomial approximation of Lipschitz functions using Chebyshev-Jackson kernels. This allows us to control the size of the coefficients of the polynomial, while achieving the optimal rate of approximation for Lipschitz functions, both of which are necessary for the proof.

2
Preliminaries

Let
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i,n}}
be the empirical measure of a triangular array of real-valued random variables
\{Y_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. We denote the expected measure as
\bar{\mu}_{n}=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{n}]
.
The 1-Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by the dual form:

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}(1)}\left|\int fd\mu-\int fd\nu\right|,

(1)

where
\operatorname{Lip}(1)
denotes the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

2.1
The
\Psi_{r}
Norm and Asymptotic Sub-Gaussianity

A central feature of our analysis is a tail condition that is milder than uniform sub-Gaussianity. We consider a sequence of parameters
r_{n}
that may grow with
n
.

Definition 1

(Truncated
\Psi_{r}
Norm)
.

For a real number
r\geq 2
, let
\Psi_{r}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\frac{x^{2j}}{j!}
. The
\Psi_{r}
norm of a random variable
X
is defined as:

\|X\|_{\Psi_{r}}=\inf\left\{K>0:\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{r}(|X|/K)\right]\leq 1\right\}.

(2)

If
r\to\infty
,
\Psi_{r}(x)
approaches
\exp(x^{2})-1
, recovering the standard
\psi_{2}
(sub-Gaussian) norm. However, for finite
r
,
\|X\|_{\Psi_{r}}<\infty
only implies that moments up to order
r
behave like those of a sub-Gaussian variable. This allows us to work with variables that may not be strictly sub-Gaussian for fixed
n
, provided their moments are well-behaved up to a growing order
r_{n}
.

Lemma 2

(Moments and Tails)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
\|X\|_{\Psi_{r}}\leq K
. Then:

1.

(Moment Bound) For all
p\in[2,2\lfloor r/2\rfloor]
,
(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p})^{1/p}\leq C_{1}K\sqrt{p}
.

2.

(Tail Bound) There exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that for
t\geq c_{0}K
:

\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq t)\leq\exp\left(-c_{1}\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}},\lfloor r/2\rfloor\right\}\right).

The proof of part (a) follows standard Orlicz norm arguments. For part (b), see
[
3
, Lemma 25]
.

3
Main Results

Our main result establishes
W_{1}
concentration based on moment variance and tail decay.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\{Y_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a triangular array of real-valued random variables. Let
\mu_{n}
be their empirical measure and
\bar{\mu}_{n}=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{n}]
. Assume there exists a sequence
r_{n}=\omega(1)
such that:

(a)

Uniform Tail Control:
The variables are uniformly
\Psi_{r_{n}}
sub-Gaussian. That is, there exists
\zeta>0
such that

\sup_{i\in[n]}\|Y_{i,n}\|_{\Psi_{r_{n}}}\leq\zeta.

(b)

Moment Concentration:
For every fixed integer
k\geq 1
, the variance of the empirical moments vanishes:

\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,n}^{k}\right)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Then, the expected Wasserstein distance vanishes:

\mathbb{E}\bigl[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})\bigr]\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Condition (b) is satisfied trivially if the variables are i.i.d., but it also holds for many dependent systems where correlations decay sufficiently fast (e.g., spectral statistics of random matrices, node features in sparse graphs). Condition (a) ensures that while the variables need not be bounded, their mass does not escape to infinity too quickly.

3.1
Extension to Random Vectors

The method of moments is particularly powerful in high dimensions where computing projected moments
\mathbb{E}\langle\theta,Y\rangle^{k}
is often feasible. We extend Theorem
3
to random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We denote the absolute first moment of a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
M_{1}(\mu)=\int\|x\|d\mu(x)
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
Y_{i,n}
be random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{i,n}}
and
\bar{\mu}_{n}=\mathbb{E}\mu_{n}
. Assume there exists a sequence
r_{n}=\omega(1)
such that for every unit vector
\theta\in S^{d-1}
:

(a)

Uniform Projected Tails:
The projections are uniformly
\Psi_{r_{n}}
sub-Gaussian:

\sup_{i\in[n]}\|\langle\theta,Y_{i,n}\rangle\|_{\Psi_{r_{n}}}\leq\zeta(\theta)<\infty.

(b)

Projected Moment Concentration:
For every fixed
k\geq 1
:

\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\theta,Y_{i,n}\rangle^{k}\right)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(c)

Bounded First Moment:

\sup_{n\geq 1}M_{1}(\bar{\mu}_{n})<\infty
.

Then,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})\bigr]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

4
Polynomial approximations

To prove the main result, we require results on polynomial approximation of Lipschitz functions. Standard Weierstrass approximation yields a rate of
m^{-1/2}
for approximation by polynomials of degree
m
, which is too slow. The optimal rate of approximation for Lipschitz functions is
m^{-1}
, which will be sufficient. However, we also need to control the size of the coefficients of the polynomial.
We use the Chebyshev–Jackson approximation, which achieves this balance.

We begin with a bound on the coefficients of Chebyshev polynomials.

Lemma 5
.

Let
T_{k}
be the
k
th Chebyshev polynomial, and let
[T_{k}]_{j}
be the coefficient of
x^{j}
in
T_{k}(x)
. Then,
|[T_{k}]_{0}|\leq 1
and

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq k}|[T_{k}]_{j}|\leq(1+\sqrt{2})^{k}\leq 3^{k}.

Proof.

The first part is clear, since
[T_{k}]_{0}\in\{0,1\}
. For the second part, from the recurrence relation
T_{k+1}(x)=2xT_{k}(x)-T_{k-1}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle|[T_{k+1}]_{j}|\leq 2|[T_{k}]_{j-1}|+|[T_{k-1}]_{j}|.

Assuming the result holds as
\max_{1\leq j\leq k}|[T_{k}]_{j}|\leq c^{k}
for some constant
c
and
for all
T_{r},r\leq k
, we have
|[T_{k+1}]_{j}|\leq 2\cdot c^{k}+c^{k-1}
. Then, if
2c^{k}+c^{k-1}\leq c^{k+1}
, the result follows by induction. This inequality holds for
c\geq 1+\sqrt{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 6

(Chebyshev–Jackson approximation)
.

Let
B\geq 3
. Then, for any
f:[-B,B]\to\mathbb{R}

1
-Lipschitz with
f(0)=0
, there exists a polynomial
P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}x^{j}
, with
m\in 4\mathbb{N}
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\,\in\,[-B,B]}|f(x)-P(x)|\leq\frac{18B}{m},\quad\text{and}\quad|c_{j}|\leq 6B\cdot 3^{m-j},\quad\text{for all }j\geq 0.

Proof.

Consider an
L
-Lipschitz function
g
on
[-1,1]
with
g(0)=0
. Then, for each
m\in 4\mathbb{N}
, there is a polynomial of the form

Q_{m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\lambda_{k,m}a_{k}(g)T_{k}(x)

where
\lambda_{k,m}
are derived from a Jackson kernel, satisfying
0\leq\lambda_{k,m}\leq 1
and
a_{k}(g)
are the Chebyshev coefficients of
g
, such that

\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|g(x)-Q_{m}(x)|\leq\frac{18L}{m},\quad|a_{k}(g)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{8/\pi}L}{k},\quad k\geq 1.

See Facts 3.2 and 3.3 in
[
2
]
.
The Chebyshev coefficients are given by

a_{k}(g)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{g(x)T_{k}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx,\quad k\geq 1,

and for
k=0
, the same formula holds with
2/\pi
replaced with
1/\pi
. For
k=0
, using
g(0)=0
so that
|g(x)|\leq L|x|
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, and
T_{0}(x)=1
, we have

\displaystyle|a_{0}(g)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{L|x|}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\frac{2L}{\pi}.

Thus a crude upper bound that works for all
k\geq 0
is
|a_{k}(g)|\leq 2L
.

Let
a_{k,m}=\lambda_{k,m}a_{k}(g)
and note that
|a_{k,m}|\leq 2L
for all
k\geq 0
, by the above discussion.
Rewriting
Q_{m}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j}
, one has

b_{j}=\sum_{k=j}^{m}a_{k,m}[T_{k}]_{j}

where
[T_{k}]_{j}
is the coefficient of
x^{j}
in
T_{k}(x)
.
It follows from Lemma
5
that

\displaystyle|b_{j}|\leq\sum_{k=j}^{m}2L\cdot 3^{k}\leq 2L\cdot 3^{m}\sum_{k=j}^{m}3^{k-m}\leq 2L\cdot 3^{m}\frac{1}{1-3^{-1}}\leq 6L\cdot 3^{m}

for all
j\geq 0
.

If
f
is 1-Lipschitz on
[-B,B]
with
f(0)=0
, then
g(x)=f(Bx)
is
B
-Lipschitz on
[-1,1]
with
g(0)=0
. Let
Q_{m}
be the above polynomial for
g
, and let
P(x)=Q_{m}(x/B)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(b_{j}/B^{j})x^{j}=:\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}x^{j}
. Then,

|c_{j}|\leq 6B\frac{3^{m}}{B^{j}}\leq 6B\cdot 3^{m-j}

assuming
B\geq 3
. We also have
\sup_{x\in[-B,B]}|f(x)-P(x)|=\sup_{x\in[-1,1]}|g(x)-Q_{m}(x)|\leq\frac{18B}{m}
. The proof is complete.
∎

5
Proof of Main Result (Scalar Case)

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let us write
\operatorname{Lip}(f)=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|}
for the Lipschitz constant of
f
. Consider the set of functions

\mathcal{L}=\{f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\operatorname{Lip}(f)\leq 1,f(0)=0\},\quad\mathcal{L}_{B}=\{f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B}\mid f\in\mathcal{L},\;B>0\}.

Let
\varpi_{n}:=\mu_{n}-\bar{\mu}_{n}
. By the dual characterization of
W_{1}
, we have

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}}|\varpi_{n}f|.

By breaking
f=f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B}+f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B}
, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{B}}|\varpi_{n}f|+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}}|\varpi_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|.

(3)

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and consider the second term first.
For any integrable
f
, we have

\displaystyle|\varpi_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|+|\bar{\mu}_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{n}(|f|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})+\bar{\mu}_{n}(|f|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B}).

(4)

For
f\in\mathcal{L}
, we have
|f(x)|=|f(x)-f(0)|\leq|x-0|
.

Then, we have

\displaystyle|\varpi_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{n}(|x|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})+\bar{\mu}_{n}(|x|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})

Taking the supremum over
f\in\mathcal{L}
and then expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}}|\varpi_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2\bar{\mu}_{n}(|x|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl(|Y_{i,n}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|Y_{i,n}|>B\}}\bigr).

Take
n
large enough so that

r_{n}\geq 2\Bigl(\frac{B^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}+1\Bigr)

(5)

which we will verify at the end. Also, take
B\geq B_{0}(\zeta):=c_{0}\zeta
where
c_{0}
is the constant in Lemma
2
(b). Then, by this lemma, we have
\mathbb{P}(|Y_{i,n}|>B)\leq\exp(-c_{1}B^{2}/\zeta^{2})
, and by Lemma
2
(a), we have

\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,n}^{2}]\leq 2C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}
. Then, by Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(|Y_{i,n}|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|Y_{i,n}|>B\}}\bigr)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i,n}|^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(|Y_{i,n}|>B)}\leq\sqrt{2}C_{1}\zeta\cdot\exp(-cB^{2}/2\zeta^{2}).

Taking
B\geq B_{1}(\zeta)
for
B_{1}(\zeta)
large enough, the RHS can be made
\leq\epsilon
, which gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}}|\varpi_{n}(f\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|\leq 2\epsilon.

Consider now the first term in (
3
).
Viewing
\mathcal{L}_{B}
as a subspace of
(C_{b}([-B,B]),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
, by restricting to
[-B,B]
,
\mathcal{L}_{B}
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous, hence by Arzelà–Ascoli, it is relatively compact in the sup-norm topology. This, in turn, implies
\mathcal{L}_{B}
is totally bounded. Then, there exists
f_{1},\ldots,f_{M}\in\mathcal{L}_{B}
that form an
\epsilon
-net for
\mathcal{L}_{B}
in sup-norm, for some
M=M(\epsilon,B)<\infty
. That is, for any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{B}
, there is
f_{\ell}
such that
\|f-f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, hence

\displaystyle|\varpi_{n}f|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;|\varpi_{n}(f-f_{\ell})|+|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\|\varpi_{n}\|_{\text{TV}}\cdot\|f-f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}+|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|\;\leq\;2\epsilon+|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|.

Taking supremum over
f\in\mathcal{L}_{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{B}}|\varpi_{n}f|

\displaystyle\;\leq\;2\epsilon+\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|.

Take
B\geq 3
. By Lemma
6
, each
f_{\ell}
admits a (truncated) polynomial
Q_{\ell}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq B\}}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j\ell}x^{j}
, with
m=4\lceil C_{2}B/\epsilon\rceil\in 4\mathbb{N}
(can take
C_{2}=18
) such that

\|f_{\ell}-Q_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,

and
|c_{j\ell}|\leq 6B\cdot 3^{m-j}=:a_{j}
for all
j\geq 0
and
\ell\in[M]
. We have

|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|\leq\|\varpi_{n}\|_{\text{TV}}\cdot\|f_{\ell}-Q_{\ell}\|_{\infty}+|\varpi_{n}Q_{\ell}|.

It follows that

\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}f_{\ell}|\leq 2\epsilon+\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}Q_{\ell}|

and we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}Q_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\ell\in[M]}\Bigl|\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j\ell}\,\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B})\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\bigl(\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|c_{j\ell}|\bigr)\cdot|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B})|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\,|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B})|

We have

\displaystyle|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq B})|\leq|\varpi_{n}(x^{j})|+|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|.

Then, for the second term, using (
4
), we have, for all
j\in[m]
,

\displaystyle|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{n}(|x^{j}|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})+\bar{\mu}_{n}(|x^{j}|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{n}(|x^{m}|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})+\bar{\mu}_{n}(|x^{m}|\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B}).

Taking maximum over
j\in[m]
, followed by expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{j\in[m]}|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{\mu}_{n}(|x|^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl(|Y_{i,n}|^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|Y_{i,n}|>B\}}\bigr).

Take
n
large enough so that

r_{n}\geq 2m=8\lceil C_{2}B/\epsilon\rceil,

(6)

which we will verify at the end.
Then, by Lemma
2
(a) we have

\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i,n}|^{2m}]\leq(C_{1}\zeta)^{2m}(2m)^{m}=(2C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}m)^{m}
. Then, by Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(|Y_{i,n}|^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|Y_{i,n}|>B\}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i,n}|^{2m}]\cdot\mathbb{P}(|Y_{i,n}|>B)}

\displaystyle\leq(2C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}m)^{m}\cdot\exp(-cB^{2}/2\zeta^{2})

Using
a_{j}=6B\cdot 3^{m-j}
, we have
\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\leq 9B\cdot 3^{m}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\Bigr)\cdot\mathbb{E}\sup_{j\in[m]}|\varpi_{n}(x^{j}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|>B})|

\displaystyle\leq 9B\cdot 3^{m}\cdot 2(2C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}m)^{m}\cdot\exp(-cB^{2}/2\zeta^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 18\exp\Bigl(\log B+m\log(6C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}m)-cB^{2}/2\zeta^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 18\exp\biggl(\log B+4\lceil C_{2}B/\epsilon\rceil\log\Bigl(24C_{1}^{2}\zeta^{2}\lceil C_{2}B/\epsilon\rceil\Bigr)-cB^{2}/2\zeta^{2}\biggr).

Since
B^{2}
grows faster than
B\log B
, the RHS can be made
\leq\epsilon
for
B\geq B_{2}(\zeta,\epsilon)
for some
B_{2}(\zeta,\epsilon)
large enough. For this choice of
B
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}Q_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\mathbb{E}|\varpi_{n}(x^{j})|+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,n}^{j}\Bigr)}+\epsilon.

By assumption (b) of the theorem,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\operatorname{Var}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,n}^{j}\Bigr)\leq\epsilon^{2}/(\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_{j})^{2}

(7)

for sufficiently large
n
. This gives
\mathbb{E}\sup_{\ell\in[M]}|\varpi_{n}Q_{\ell}|\leq 2\epsilon
. Putting the pieces together, we have

\mathbb{E}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{B}}|\varpi_{n}f|\leq 2\epsilon+2\epsilon+2\epsilon=6\epsilon.

All in all, taking
B=\max\{3,B_{0}(\zeta),B_{1}(\zeta),B_{2}(\zeta,\epsilon)\}
, and
n
large enough so that (
5
) and (
6
) are satisfied for the chosen
B
, and (
7
) holds, we obtain
\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu}_{n})\leq 8\epsilon
. The proof is complete.
∎

6
Proof of Vector Extension (Theorem
4
)

To prove the vector case, we rely on the fact that convergence of one-dimensional projections (which we established in Theorem
3
) implies convergence of the full measure, provided the first moments are controlled.

We use the notation
\eta_{\theta}
to denote the pushforward of a measure
\eta
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the projection
x\mapsto\langle\theta,x\rangle
.

Lemma 7

(Lipschitz Continuity of Projections)
.

Let
\eta
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

W_{1}(\eta_{\theta_{1}},\eta_{\theta_{2}})\leq\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|M_{1}(\eta).

Proof.

Let
X\sim\eta
. Using the dual formulation of
W_{1}
for measures on
\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle W_{1}(\eta_{\theta_{1}},\eta_{\theta_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}(1)}\bigl|\mathbb{E}f(\langle\theta_{1},X\rangle)-\mathbb{E}f(\langle\theta_{2},X\rangle)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}(1)}\mathbb{E}\bigl|f(\langle\theta_{1},X\rangle)-f(\langle\theta_{2},X\rangle)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|\langle\theta_{1}-\theta_{2},X\rangle|\leq\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\|X\|.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following proposition allows us to upgrade scalar convergence to vector convergence.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\{\mu_{n}\}
and
\{\eta_{n}\}
be random probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with expectations
\bar{\mu}_{n}
and
\bar{\eta}_{n}
. Assume:

1.

\sup_{n\geq 1}(M_{1}(\bar{\mu}_{n})+M_{1}(\bar{\eta}_{n}))<\infty
.

2.

For every
\theta\in S^{d-1}
,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta},\eta_{n,\theta})\bigr]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Then,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\eta_{n})\bigr]\to 0
.

Proof.

Let
L_{n}=M_{1}(\mu_{n})+M_{1}(\eta_{n})
. By Lemma
7
and the triangle inequality, the map
\theta\mapsto W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta},\eta_{n,\theta})
is
L_{n}
-Lipschitz on the sphere. Specifically,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta_{1}},\eta_{n,\theta_{1}})-W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta_{2}},\eta_{n,\theta_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta_{1}},\mu_{n,\theta_{2}})+W_{1}(\eta_{n,\theta_{1}},\eta_{n,\theta_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq L_{n}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|.

Bayraktar and Guo
[
1
]
proved that the
W_{1}
distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is controlled by the supremum over projections: there exists a constant
C(d)
such that

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\eta_{n})\leq C(d)\sup_{\theta\in S^{d-1}}W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta},\eta_{n,\theta}).

Let
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}\}
be an
\epsilon
-net of
S^{d-1}
. For any
\theta
, let
\theta_{j}
be its nearest neighbor. Then:

W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta},\eta_{n,\theta})\leq W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta_{j}},\eta_{n,\theta_{j}})+L_{n}\epsilon.

Taking the expectation of the supremum,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{n},\eta_{n})]

\displaystyle\leq C(d)\left(\epsilon\,\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}W_{1}(\mu_{n,\theta_{j}},\eta_{n,\theta_{j}})\right).

Note that
\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]=M_{1}(\bar{\mu}_{n})+M_{1}(\bar{\eta}_{n})
, which is uniformly bounded by assumption. The sum term vanishes as
n\to\infty
by hypothesis (Condition 2). Taking
n\to\infty
then
\epsilon\to 0
yields the result.
∎
