Surface-based deep inspiration breath-hold radiotherapy in left-sided breast cancer: final results from the SAVE-HEART study
Authors: S. Schönecker, L. Angelini, A. Gaasch, A. Zinn, D. Konnerth, C. Heinz, Y. Xiong, K. Unger, G. Landry, I. Meattini, M. Braun, M. Pölcher, N. Harbeck, R. Würstlein, M. Niyazi, C. Belka, M. Pazos, S. Corradini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdjuvant radiotherapy (RT) plays an essential role in the management of early breast cancer (BC), but can lead to cardiovascular and lung toxicities. RT in deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH) often allows better protection of organs at risk. This prospective study compares surface-guided DIBH with free breathing (FB) in patients with left-sided BC, by evaluating individual cardiovascular risks and treatment plan dosimetry.Patients and methodsThe study enrolled 585 patients from October 2016 to January 2021 with left-sided invasive breast carcinoma with indicated adjuvant RT of the breast/thoracic wall with or without regional lymph nodes. The ability to hold breath for 20 s was a prerequisite. The treatments were either hypofractionated (HF; 40.05 Gy/15Fx) or normofractionated (NF; 50.00 Gy/25Fx). DIBH was applied using the automatically triggered surface guidance system Catalyst with audio–video feedback. Computed tomography and surface data were acquired during both DIBH and FB. The primary endpoint of the study was the comparative evaluation of heart dose reduction using DIBH.ResultsPlan dosimetry was significantly improved by DIBH. The mean and maximum doses to the heart and the left coronary artery were significantly reduced by 36%-42% in HF and NF plans (P < 0.001), while the mean ipsilateral lung dose was reduced by 12%-14% (P < 0.001). Furthermore, DIBH resulted in a 5% reduction in the cumulative 10-year cardiovascular disease risk (10-year cardiovascular disease risk) compared with FB (3.59% to 3.41%; P < 0.001).ConclusionTo the best of our knowledge, this is the largest prospective study showing better sparing for cardiac and ipsilateral lung doses with surface-guided DIBH compared with FB in patients with left-sided BC.

Introduction

Adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) is a crucial component of treatment for early breast cancer (BC) following breast-conserving surgery, as it has been shown to improve local control, disease-free survival, and overall survival.1,2 However, especially in patients with left-sided BC, RT is associated with an increased risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and lung morbidities on long-term follow-up.3,4 The physiopathology of radiation-induced CVD involves micro- and macroangiopathy of small vessels and coronary arteries, which can lead to ischemic stroke and/or myocardial infarction.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Despite efforts, it remains difficult to identify a clear dose–response relationship. In 2013, Darby et al.10 reported a linear increase of 7.4% in the occurrence of major coronary events per Gray (Gy) of the mean heart dose. However, even recent research has been unable to establish a safe threshold for radiation-induced CVD.11, 12, 13 Certain systemic therapies, such as anthracyclines or trastuzumab, may have a synergistic effect and increase the risk of CVD toxicity.14

Regarding pulmonary side-effects, the excess relative risk (ERR) for secondary lung cancer has been estimated to be 0.11 per Gy whole lung dose.15 Thanks to technological advancements in radiation oncology and the increasing understanding of long-term toxicities, new RT techniques have been developed to optimize dose distributions and improve sparing of the heart, left anterior descending (LAD) artery, and lung. Intensity-modulated radiation therapy or helical tomotherapy can achieve lower mean doses to the heart and the LAD artery, but this often comes at the expense of increased low-dose exposure in the surrounding organs, with unknown long-term consequences.16, 17, 18, 19 By contrast, the deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) technique can increase the anatomical distance of the heart from the chest wall. This can minimize the dose to intrathoracic organs at risk without compromising target dose coverage or increasing low-dose scatter.20

Available DIBH techniques differ in terms of equipment, treatment monitoring, and patient feedback systems. The simplest method is voluntary DIBH, which requires no additional equipment and relies on the light field or lasers of the treatment device. By contrast, equipment-based techniques can be categorized into surface- or spirometry-based devices, often equipped with visual feedback systems for the patient. The range of options spans from simple in-house developments to commercial systems that can interlock with treatment delivery and offer automatic gating.21

Surface-based systems use optical scanning to continuously monitor the patient’s external surface in real-time during treatment.22 Light patterns are projected onto the patient and reprojections are recorded using charge-coupled device cameras. Ceiling-mounted camera units provide a three-dimensional (3D) surface reconstruction of the external surface of the patient. The optical surface scanning systems track respiratory motion and capture a reference surface scan in DIBH during computed tomography (CT) simulation and treatment. A gating window is individually set, based on the patient’s stable and reproducible DIBH during training. Audio/visual feedback systems, such as video goggles or tablet visualization tools, provide patients with real-time respiratory signal feedback.23 The advantage of these systems is that they enable patient positioning management, intrafractional motion control, and respiratory gating evaluation without additional radiation exposure. Furthermore, they can be interlocked with treatment delivery to allow automatic gating. In this prospective study, we aimed to compare surface-guided DIBH with free breathing (FB) in patients with left-sided BC, by evaluating individual cardiovascular risks and treatment plan dosimetry.

Patients and methods

This analysis includes a prospective series of patients with BC treated at our institution between October 2016 and January 2021.

The main inclusion criteria were the presence of histologically confirmed left-sided invasive breast carcinoma or carcinoma in situ treated with breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy with a subsequent indication for post-operative RT of the breast/thoracic wall with and without locoregional lymph nodes. All patients underwent a training session for DIBH performance before study inclusion. All patients were required to maintain the DIBH for at least 20 s. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

This prospective single-center study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethical Committee of the LMU Medical Faculty (approved on 22 October 2013, approval number 496-12) and registered at the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS ID: DRKS00011213).

All patients underwent two simulation CTs with identical setup (supine position with raised arms) using 3-mm slice thickness: one in FB and one in DIBH. During CT simulation, the laser-based Sentinel system (C-RAD, Uppsala, Sweden) was utilized to create a reference surface scan in FB and to define the individual gating window for DIBH. Initially, the gating window was set to 4 mm around the repeated voluntary DIBH amplitude level, but could be reduced to a minimum of 2 mm, based on the patient’s breath-hold performance.23,24 During treatment delivery at the linear accelerator (linac), the optical surface scanning system Catalyst (C-RAD) was used for patient setup and treatment delivery in DIBH. Furthermore, Epson Moverio BT-300 Smart Glasses (Epson Inc., Suwa, Japan) were used as an optical feedback tool through a Bluetooth connection.

For treatment planning, the heart and the LAD artery were contoured according to the atlas by Feng et al.25 The clinical target volume consisted of the residual left breast/chest wall, excluding the ribs, muscles, or skin. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the clinical target volume isotropically with a margin of 5 mm.26, 27, 28, 29 The treatment plans used either a normofractionated (NF; 50.00 Gy in 2.00 Gy/dose), or hypofractionated (HF; 40.05 Gy in 2.67 Gy/dose) schedule, and were delivered using a 3D conformational field-in-field technique. The boost was not considered in the dosimetric evaluation for better comparability. Treatment planning was carried out using the treatment planning software Oncentra 4.3 (Nucletron, Veenendaal, the Netherlands). For all plans, the used algorithm for dose calculation was collapsed cone convolution with a 3 mm × 3 mm calculation grid. Dosimetric and volumetric data for various parameters of interest were recorded, such as PTV (Vtot), heart (Dmean, Dmax, V5Gy, V10Gy V20Gy), LAD artery (Dmean, Dmax, D2), and ipsilateral lung (Dmean, Vtot, V5Gy, V10Gy, V20Gy). In the final step, we evaluated the differences between variables (Δ′x′) in the FB and DIBH plans. The primary endpoint of the present study was the comparative evaluation of heart dose reduction using DIBH.

Regarding radiation-induced risk estimates, an individual cardiovascular risk profile was created for all patients. For this purpose, a specific questionnaire was elaborated to document all baseline cardiovascular risk factors. To determine the baseline CVD risk for major coronary events (sudden cardiac death or myocardial infarction) over the next 10 years (i.e. 10-year CVD risk), the Prospective Cardiovascular Münster (PROCAM) score was calculated using available blood values, including lipid levels (high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, and triglycerides), gender, age, systolic blood pressure, smoking habits, family history, and diabetes status.30 Patients with a calculated baseline 10-year CVD risk >10% were informed and counseled regarding primary prevention measures on an individual basis.31

The ERR of radiation-induced CVD over a 10-year period was calculated using the individual mean heart dose derived from the dose-volume histograms of the treatment plans in DIBH and FB of each patient. It was assumed that the 10-year CVD risk increases linearly by 7.4% per Gy mean heart dose based on the study of Darby et al.10 The formula used for this calculation was ERR = mean heart dose (Gy) × δDarby (Gy−1), where δDarby = 0.074 Gy−1. The ERR was then used to determine the 10-year excess absolute risk (EAR) of RT, by considering the relative increase of baseline risks after RT as follows: EAR = ERR × baseline risk, where baseline risk = individual baseline risk calculated by the PROCAM score. Finally, the EAR was used to calculate the cumulative radiation-induced 10-year CVD risk by adding it to the individual baseline risk: Cumulative 10-year CVD risk = baseline risk + EAR.

Descriptive statistics such as mean, value ranges, standard deviation, and/or percentage were calculated for all quantitative variables. To compare the dosimetric data of the two techniques, paired samples t-tests were carried out. In addition, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to investigate the interaction between variables. A significance level of P = 0.05 was applied to all results. IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, United States), was used for the statistical analysis.

Among all patients, 526/585 (90%) received RT after breast-conserving surgery (16% with elective lymph node irradiation), and 59/585 (10%) received postmastectomy radiation (76% with elective lymph node irradiation). Regarding the fractionation schedules, 367/585 (63%) received HF irradiation and 218/585 (37%) an NF treatment. Of note, the mean PTV volume increased by 1% in DIBH in HF plans, while in NF plans it did not significantly change (P < 0.001).

The mean dose to the heart was significantly reduced in both HF and NF plans during DIBH, with a reduction of 37% in HF plans (from 1.91 to 1.21 Gy) and 36% in NF plans (from 2.80 to 1.78 Gy; P < 0.001). In addition, the maximum dose to the heart was also reduced by 42% in HF plans (from 34.22 to 19.85 Gy) and by 41% in NF plans (from 42.45 to 25.07 Gy; P < 0.001). The Dmean, Dmax, and D2% to the LAD artery were reduced in both HF and NF plans during DIBH compared with FB, with reductions of 58%, 47%, and 55%, respectively (P < 0.001). For example, the near-maximum dose to the LAD artery was reduced from 21.86 to 9.80 Gy in HF plans (P < 0.001).

The mean volume of the ipsilateral lung (Vtot) increased by 68% and 69% in HF and NF plans, respectively, during DIBH (P < 0.001). Further, the mean ipsilateral lung Dmean was reduced by 12% and 14% in HF and NF plans, during DIBH (P < 0.001). The V20Gy, V10Gy, and V5Gy means in the lung were significantly higher in DIBH plans compared with FB plans (P < 0.001). Table 2 and Figure 2 provide a summary of the dosimetric analysis. The statistically most significant correlation was found between the mean LAD artery and the mean heart dose reduction in DIBH versus FB plans (r = 0.764; R2 = 0.584). A significant correlation was also found between the mean LAD artery and the mean heart dose for FB (r = 0.742; R2 = 0.551) as well as for DIBH (r = 0.681; R2 = 0.463; Figure 3). The mean estimated radiation-induced risk (ERR) of 10-year CVD following DIBH RT was 0.11 (range 0.04-0.37), compared with 0.17 (range 0.06-0.67) after FB irradiation [Δ: 0.06 ≙ 35%; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.057-0.068; P < 0.001]. This translated into a mean EAR of 0.33% (range 0%-5.31%) for DIBH and 0.51% (range 0%-10.18%) for FB, representing a 35% reduction in 10-year CVD EAR with DIBH (Δ: 0.18 ≙ 35%; 95% CI 0.14-0.23; P < 0.001). The mean cumulative 10-year CVD risk was 3.41% (range 0%-42.65%) for DIBH and 3.59% (range 0%-47.18%) for FB, after accounting for all baseline characteristics (Δ: 0.18 ≙ 5%; 95% CI 0.14-0.23; P < 0.001). Discussion

Several recent studies have revealed that DIBH can significantly reduce the radiation dose of thoracic organs at risk of patients with left-sided BC.32, 33, 34 Likewise, the present large-scale prospective study, involving nearly 600 patients, has demonstrated that DIBH leads to a considerable reduction in the dose administered to the heart, the LAD artery, and the ipsilateral lung compared with FB (Table 2 and Figure 2). This study is the largest to date to provide prospective dosimetric results using a surface guided radiotherapy (SGRT) system for the DIBH technique in BC. DIBH was carried out using the Sentinel/Catalyst system. The level of DIBH is determined during CT simulation by the laser-based Sentinel system and is set at a level that ensures stable and reproducible breath hold on a patient-specific basis. During treatment at the linac, the Catalyst system projects optical light patterns onto the patient’s skin surface and captures the reprojections using charge-coupled device cameras. This creates a 3D reconstruction of the patient’s external surface, allowing for the tracking of respiratory motion and DIBH. To assist patients in guiding the DIBH, video goggles provide real-time feedback on their respiratory signal.23

Previous studies of DIBH using 3D-conformal radiation therapy were limited to smaller series. Two retrospective studies using an SGRT DIBH technique with the Catalyst HD system by Wolf et al.35 and Knöchelmann et al.36 reported significant reductions in the mean dose for the heart, the LAD artery, and the left lung in 130 and 357 patients, respectively. Wolf et al.35 demonstrated reductions of 41%, 71%, and 7%, respectively, while Knöchelmann et al.36 showed comparable mean dose reductions of 47% for the heart, 32% for the LAD artery, and 9% for the left lung. Both studies revealed similar dosimetric benefits from DIBH in NF and HF plans, as seen in the current study.

In two other prospective studies,37,38 88 and 25 patients were treated using a similar SGRT technique (AlignRT, VisionRT, London, UK), with significant mean dose reductions observed for the heart and the LAD artery. The reductions were comparable to the present study, with 56% and 65% (P < 0.001) mean dose reductions for the heart, and 42% and 45% (P < 0.001) mean dose reductions for the LAD artery, respectively. By contrast, Ferdinand et al.39 reported dosimetric results for 31 patients with BC treated with a spirometric technique (Active Breathing Control; Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden), showing slightly smaller reductions in the mean dose for the heart by 39% (P < 0.001), the LAD artery by 30% (P < 0.001), and the ipsilateral lung by 10% (P = 0.03). While it may seem that the effect of the spirometric technique is smaller than that of SGRT DIBH, it is important to emphasize that comparing values from different studies is not an appropriate method for evaluating technology efficacy.

Overall, the variation in mean dose reduction for the LAD artery in DIBH compared with FB was found to be highly variable across cited studies, ranging from 11% to 71%, with the present study reporting 58%. This variability may also be attributed to difficulties in contouring the thin LAD artery structure, which is often not clearly visible on simulation CTs without contrast. In addition, there is a lack of standardization in LAD artery dose constraints, with some studies40 reporting a maximum LAD artery dose of <45.4 Gy correlated with lower cardiac mortality, while others do not consider LAD artery contouring necessary due to a linear correlation between the LAD artery and the heart mean dose. Evans et al.41 have found a linear correlation between the LAD artery mean dose and the heart mean dose, with an increase in the LAD artery mean dose by 4.8 Gy per 1 Gy in the heart mean dose. In our study, we could show similar results with an increase of 5.82 Gy per 1 Gy in FB and 4.82 Gy per 1 Gy in DIBH. Our data confirm this correlation between the heart and the LAD artery mean dose, as demonstrated in Figure 3, with a statistically significant correlation between the two mean heart doses (FB R2 = 0.551; DIBH R2 = 0.463). This anatomical relationship can be explained by the LAD artery’s usual course around the heart’s apex and proximity to the radiation fields. Nonetheless, because the LAD artery dose is directly linked to the risk of coronary stenosis,42 it is reasonable to provide maximum dose values. As for the volumetric lung parameters, the volumes exposed to 20, 10, and 5 Gy showed significantly higher dose exposure in DIBH. However, these findings are not unexpected because the lung volume expands considerably during DIBH, and the dosimetric analysis does not account for the decrease in lung density during DIBH, which could overestimate the dose exposure.43 DIBH plans resulted in a larger total lung volume compared with FB plans, with a 69% increase in total lung volume. Because of the uneven expansion of lung tissue throughout the entire lung, the change in lung density cannot simply be measured. To calculate the actual difference in irradiated lung tissue between FB and DIBH, highly complex analyses of tissue densities across the entire lung would be required, which is not feasible in practice. The study cited above demonstrated this effect using a dose–mass histogram instead of a dose–volume histogram, as is common in radiation therapy.

The findings of small retrospective studies by Oechsner et al.43 and Wolf et al.35 suggested that ipsilateral lung volume change could be used to predict the benefit of DIBH, as it correlated with a reduction in heart dose. However, our prospective data did not confirm this correlation.

Numerous studies have endeavored to identify specific anatomical, radiological, or cardiovascular criteria that would indicate whether DIBH is preferable to FB, but so far, none have been able to determine definitive thresholds or predictors that would be suitable for selecting patients.36,39,44, 45, 46, 47

Our perspective is that it may not be wise to limit the use of DIBH only to patients who have high-risk features, as even low-risk patients should have the opportunity to reduce their long-term risk of CVD as much as possible. In accordance with the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle, it is crucial to minimize radiation exposure to healthy structures during radiation therapy. Therefore we recommend that all patients with left-sided BC who are eligible for this treatment should be offered the DIBH technique. It is essential to explain the DIBH procedure accurately to patients and recommend breath-hold training a few days before the simulation CT. Kim et al.48 found that patients who received a 5-day breath-hold training before treatment had a lower dose to the heart than those without training.

Our analysis of cardiac risk modeling revealed that the average baseline 10-year CVD risk was 3.08%. The EAR in FB plans was 0.51%, while in DIBH plans, it was 0.33%, indicating a 35% reduction in EAR compared with FB plans. However, the impact of radiation therapy on the baseline CVD risk was relatively minor, resulting in a cumulative 10-year CVD risk of 3.59% in FB plans and 3.41% in DIBH plans. Therefore the difference in the cumulative 10-year CVD risk between patients treated with DIBH plans and FB plans was only 5%. Although a 5% difference in the 10-year CVD risk between patients treated with DIBH plans and FB plans may appear small, it is crucial to recognize that this percentage represents individuals who are at risk of experiencing major coronary events, such as sudden cardiac death or myocardial infarction, within the next decade. It is also worth noting that this risk may further increase with more extended follow-up. Given the severity of these cardiac events and their potential impact on patients’ health and well-being, preventing them should remain a top priority for radiation oncologists.

Some patients with BC have a higher risk of dying from a CVD or suffering a heart attack within the next 10 years than experiencing a tumor recurrence or dying from their tumor disease. Let’s take, for example, a patient with a PROCAM score of 30 (a pronounced cardiovascular risk profile with, for instance, high BMI, smoking, and diabetes mellitus). The risk for recurrence in T2 luminal A after surgery is assumed to be 10% (EAR) over 10 years. In a worst-case scenario (highest value from our data), a recommended adjuvant radiation of the left breast without DIBH would increase the risk for a serious cardiac event over 10 years by 10%, bringing it to 40%. The risk of local recurrence is reduced from 10% to 5% after 10 years. However, if radiation is carried out in deep inspiration and the anatomy improves correspondingly, the additional risk can be reduced to <5%. This would mean that the patient would have not a 40% but a 35% risk of experiencing a serious cardiac event within 10 years. Certainly, this example does not represent the standard for all patients with BC, but it illustrates the significance of some decisions and techniques used.

Moreover, out of the total number of patients in our cohort, 34 individuals (7%) had a PROCAM score indicating a baseline 10-year CVD risk exceeding 10%. Thus it is crucial to assess the baseline CVD risk before administering radiation therapy, as it allows for careful monitoring of high-risk patients during follow-up. In addition, promoting primary prevention measures as healthy lifestyle changes, such as quitting smoking, increasing physical activity, and maintaining a balanced diet, can help reduce modifiable risk factors and mitigate the potential adverse effects of radiation therapy in these patients by reducing the baseline CVD risks.31

One intriguing finding was that HF plans showed a significant increase in the PTV of +1% during DIBH compared with FB plans (P < 0.001). This phenomenon may be attributed to changes in the chest wall profile that occur during breath hold, resulting in variations in the target volume’s size.

The single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the data available for follow-up are very limited, precluding a comprehensive assessment of long-term clinical outcomes. The reliance on the PROCAM score for baseline CVD risk assessment may also introduce some variability, given its limitations in certain populations. Another limitation of this study could be the use of the mean heart dose for risk calculation. The original study of Darby et al.10 did not use dose parameters from real treatment plans, but estimates of cardiac dose based on reconstructions of patients with standard anatomy and common RT regimens.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest prospective study demonstrating a significant reduction in cardiac and lung radiation exposure using a surface-guided DIBH technique. Based on our findings, we believe that whenever feasible, patients with left-sided BC who require radiation therapy should be treated using DIBH techniques.
