Catecholaminergic neurons boost fibroblast osteogenic activity in keloid
Authors: Fangzhou LOU, Jihuan LIANG, Yang SUN, Fengjiao ZHANG, Xiaojie CAI, Siyu Deng, Zhenyao Xu, Qili PENG, Aie Xu, Rui CHEN, Honglin WANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Keloids represent a paradigm of fibroproliferative disorders marked by abnormal extracellular matrix deposition, yet the neuronal-stromal interactions driving fibroblast dysregulation remain poorly understood. Through single-cell RNA sequencing of human keloid specimens, we identify RUNX family transcription factor 2 (RUNX2)+ osteogenic fibroblasts exhibiting ectopic integrin-binding sialoprotein (IBSP) deposition, consistent with a hybrid fibro-osseous reprogramming. Keloid fibroblasts display catecholamine response signatures, correlating with excessive tyrosine hydroxylase-positive adrenergic innervation in keloid stroma. Crucially, β1-adrenergic receptor (ADRB1) activation by epinephrine/norepinephrine induces IBSP production exclusively in keloid-derived fibroblasts via canonical cAMP–PKA–CREB signaling. Through detailed mechanistic analysis, we demonstrate that CREB-activated Translin (TSN) facilitates nuclear-to-cytoplasmic IBSP mRNA transport, enabling efficient translation in RUNX2+ fibroblasts. Therapeutic interventions targeting catecholamine biosynthesis or adrenergic innervation, and pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of ADRB1 effectively prevent keloid-like pathology in a rodent model. Our study provides the demonstration of neuronal-adrenergic signaling driving fibrotic/osteogenic matrix overproduction through TSN-mediated RNA trafficking, revealing therapeutic targets for keloid management.

Introduction

Keloid disorder is defined by pathological scar overgrowth extending beyond original wound margins, driven by hyperproliferative fibroblasts that deposit excessive and disorganized extracellular matrix (ECM) components1. Characteristic tissue rigidity, hyperpigmentation, and progressive expansion over years create significant clinical burdens including disfigurement, restricted mobility, and psychological morbidity2. Despite employing adjuvant therapies like intralesional corticosteroids following surgical excision, recurrence rates remain alarmingly high due to the absence of consensus therapeutic guidelines3. While TGF-β signaling dysregulation and biomechanical stress are recognized contributors4,5, the precise molecular drivers of keloid persistence remain elusive, hampering development of targeted interventions.

Recent studies used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to dissect the pathogenesis and heterogeneity of keloid. Deng et al. found that Asporin (ASPN)+ mesenchymal fibroblasts were pro-fibrotic and responsible for excessive collagen generation in keloid6. Liu et al. found that TGF-β and Eph-ephrin signals were critical for keloid fibrogenesis and angiogenesis7, and Shim et al. later combined spatial transcriptome with scRNA-seq to highlight the abnormal fibrovascular interaction in keloid pathogenesis8. These multilayered omics approaches collectively establish fibroblast heterogeneity as the linchpin of keloid pathogenesis.

Very recently, other single-cell studies revealed Schwann cells as unexpected orchestrators of keloid microenvironments. Persistent populations of dedifferentiated Schwann cells display a pro-fibrotic phenotype marked by collagen-aligned spatial organization and enhanced ECM production capabilities9. These neural-derived cells promote M2 macrophage polarization through secreted factors9, while engaging fibroblasts via SEMA3C-mediated signaling pathways10, establishing a neuro-stromal crosstalk. Notably, keloid Schwann cells exhibit ectopic expression of developmental markers typically restricted to peripheral nerve repair11, suggesting pathological recapitulation of neural regeneration programs drives fibrosis.

We here performed scRNA-seq on keloid skin samples in comparison to healthy skin samples. Our data uncovered two keloid-confined populations of cells that expressed integrin binding sialoprotein (IBSP), which were IBSPhi Schwann cells that constituted <3% of total keloid dermal cells, and RUNX family transcription factor 2 (RUNX2)+ fibroblasts expressing IBSP that constituted ~25% of total keloid dermal cells, as quantified using RNAscope-based in situ hybridization. Given that IBSP constitutes ~12% of the non-collagenous proteins in human bone, its ectopic accumulation, together with ALP upregulation and calcium deposition, indicates a hybrid fibro-osseous reprogramming. This aberrant matrix composition likely drives the pathological rigidity of keloids beyond classical fibrosis. We showed that keloid fibroblasts highly expressed RUNX2 as the master transcription factor of osteoblast12, and were responding to catecholamine neurotransmitters via adrenoceptor beta 1 (ADRB1). Ex vivo experiments revealed that the catecholamine neurotransmitter epinephrine drove the production of IBSP from keloid fibroblasts though a post-transcriptional mechanism mediated by the mRNA transporter Translin (TSN). A rat model of keloid recapitulated the characteristics of neuro-stromal interaction and was repressed by catecholaminergic neuron deletion, catecholamine neurotransmitter synthesization blockage, and ADRB1 inhibition. Taken together, our study sheds light on the osteogenic property of keloid fibroblasts governed by catecholaminergic neurons and provides a route for the design of therapeutic strategies targeting the neuro-stromal interaction in keloid.

Results

Stromal cell abnormities identified by scRNA-Seq of human keloid skin

Patients with clinically diagnosed keloids existing for ≥ 1 year, not treated previously, and age ≥ 18 years were included in this study. Patient information is provided in Supplementary Data 1. We first performed transcriptomic profiling on 5 keloid skin samples using the 10X Genomics platform. After quality control and doublet exclusion, 43,992 keloid cells were integrated and unbiasedly clustered. We performed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction and partitioned the cells into 16 clusters (Fig. 1a, b; Supplementary Fig. 1). Accordingly, keratinocytes (clusters 0, 2, 4, 6, 11, 13, 15 expressing KRT genes), stromal cells (clusters 3, 5 expressing COL genes), vascular endothelial cells (cluster 1 expressing PECAM1), T cells (cluster 7 expressing CD3E), melanocytes (cluster 8 expressing PMEL), myeloid cells (cluster 9 expressing LYZ), mast cells (cluster 10 expressing TPSAB1), lymphatic endothelial cells (cluster 12 expressing LYVE1), and cells highly expressing the osteoblast marker IBSP (cluster 14) were identified (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1). Different types of keloid cells were subsetted from the data matrix and integrated respectively with normal cell types expressing the same marker genes from the datasets we previously generated (GSA accessions: HRA003418 and HRA006130) using Seurat’s reciprocal PCA-based batch correction to align shared biological states while removing technical variation. All datasets underwent uniform normalization (10,000 transcripts/cell) and variable gene selection (top 2,000 genes) prior to integration. Batch effects were validated as negligible post-correction through UMAP visualization. Keratinocytes expressing KRT17, KRT23, or KRT79 decreased in keloid skin, suggestive of the absence of hair follicles (HFs) in keloid (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Keloid keratinocytes were characterized by high expression of genes associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT, Supplementary Fig. 2c), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of K14+K5+ basal keratinocytes reinforced the EMT signatures and responses to TGF-β signaling (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Melanocyte functions were altered in keloid, and their responding to catecholamine and growth hormone may explain the increased pigmentation of keloid skin (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b)13. We did not find obvious differences in myeloid cells and T cells of keloid skin from those of normal skin (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 5), but proliferative dendritic cells were increased among myeloid cells in keloid.

A central role of fibroblasts to promote keloid formation is well established14. Stromal cells from 5 healthy skin samples of a large skin single-cell transcriptomic dataset (EMBL-EBI accession: E-MTAB-8142)15 were extracted from the data matrix (selection criteria: COL1A2 > 0) and integrated with the keloid stromal cells (clusters 3, 5 in Fig. 1b) in the same way described above. The integrated stromal cells were then subjected to UMAP dimensional reduction and partitioned unbiasedly (Fig. 1c), and the cells mainly separated as RGS5+ perivascular cells and FBLN1+ fibroblasts (Fig. 1d). Keloid-confined stromal cell clusters were clusters 2 and 10 (Fig. 1c), which were identified as POSTN+ASPN+ mesenchymal fibroblasts and MYH11+RERGL+ vascular smooth muscle cells respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6a; Fig. 1e). These data were consistent to previous findings showing that abnormal angiogenesis is characteristic of keloid7,8,16, and the proportion of mesenchymal fibroblasts is increased in keloid as compared to normal scar6. GSEA of POSTN+ASPN+ fibroblasts revealed that keloid-specific fibroblasts were responding to TGF-β signaling, involved in ECM remodeling (gene sets “collagen fibril organization” and “bone development”), and unexpectedly regulating neurite guidance (Fig. 1f). Accordingly, these POSTN+ASPN+ fibroblasts constituted 40 ~ 50% of total fibroblasts across the keloid samples (Supplementary Data 2), suggestive of their dominance in constructing the matrix environment in keloid dermis.

OGN, a molecule shown to induce ectopic bone formation in conjunction with TGF-β17, was specifically highly expressed by keloid mesenchymal fibroblasts (Fig. 2a). Notably, RUNX2, as the key transcription factor driving osteoblast differentiation12, was a hallmark of keloid mesenchymal fibroblasts, which highlights a tendency of fibroblasts to differentiate into osteoblasts in keloid (Fig. 2a). We noticed a previously unreported small cell population (cluster 14) highly expressing IBSP (osteoblast marker) and S100B (glia cell marker) in keloid (Fig. 2b; and Supplementary Fig. 1), but the identity of these cells remained unclear based on our single-cell transcriptomics data. To more accurately define these cells, we took advantage of the Single Cell Gene Expression Flex on the 10X Genomics platform, which uses nucleotide probes to detect protein-coding genes and may better visualize genes not so abundantly expressed. We analyzed dermis samples from 3 healthy donors and 3 keloid patients and concluded that IBSPhi keloid cells were Schwann cells as they expressed countermark genes including SOX10, S100B, NRXN1, CDH19, ERBB3, NGFR and MPZ (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). This new scRNA-seq dataset also validated the osteoblast differentiative tendency of keloid fibroblasts as it reproduced the increase of RUNX2+ fibroblasts and the reduction of normal fibroblast subclusters marked by WIF1 and (or) LEPR (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c).

Identification of RUNX2+IBSP⁺ keloid fibroblasts via RNAscope

Given that IBSPhi Schwann cells were present in keloid dermis, and RUNX2 is the transcription factor of IBSP in stromal cells18, we asked whether RUNX2+ keloid fibroblasts expressed IBSP. Although 10X Genomics-based scRNA-seq tends to drop genes with lower expression levels in a single cell19, the transcriptomic profiling dataset unveiled several RUNX2+IBSP+ fibroblasts in keloid (Supplementary Fig. 9a), and the probe-based detection gave a slightly better resolution of these RUNX2+IBSP+ fibroblasts which expressed lower IBSP than IBSPhi keloid Schwann cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b; and Supplementary Fig. 9b). We performed RNAscope-based fluorescence in situ hybridization on 3 keloid samples from those used for single-cell transcriptomic profiling to precisely quantify keloid dermal cells that expressed RUNX2 and (or) IBSP and found that (1) IBSPhi Schwann cells constituted <3% (2.65 ± 0.13%, mean ± SD) of total dermal cells; (2) most RUNX2+ fibroblasts expressed IBSP, though lower than IBSPhi Schwann cells; and (3) RUNX2+IBSP+ fibroblasts constituted ~25% (24.43 ± 11.31%, mean ± SD) of total dermal cells (Fig. 2c). IBSP immunoreactivity localized to the cytoplasm and pericellular regions of RUNX2+ fibroblasts (Fig. 2d; and Supplementary Fig. 9c), consistent with its role as a secreted osteogenic factor.

ECM mineralization and ectopic calcification in keloid

Because IBSP directly promotes ossification20, we asked if keloid skin underwent genuine mineralization. Histochemical analyses revealed distinct matrix remodeling in keloid pathology. Alcian Blue staining demonstrated elevated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) accumulation in the reticular dermis of keloid (Fig. 2e), indicative of cartilage-like matrix remodeling. Notably, we observed significantly higher expression of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), a canonical enzyme and early marker for mineralization21, in keloid tissues compared to normal skin controls (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, Alizarin Red staining identified obvious calcium deposition in adjacent regions of the keloid reticular dermis (Fig. 2g). The spatial segregation of GAG-rich and mineralized zones, coupled with universal ALP upregulation, suggests a coordinated and sequential ECM dysregulation during keloid progression. Collectively, these findings establish extracellular matrix mineralization, driven by an active osteogenic program, as a hallmark of advanced keloid pathology, implicating RUNX2⁺IBSP⁺ fibroblasts in both fibrotic stiffening and aberrant calcification.

IBSP production by keloid fibroblasts in response to catecholaminergic neurons

To characterize the intrinsic alterations of keloid fibroblasts in contrast to normal fibroblasts, we compared the transcriptional differences between the shared fibroblast populations (clusters 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12 in Fig. 1c) of keloid and normal skin and found that keloid fibroblasts were responding to catecholamine (Fig. 3a). Specifically, GNAS and GNB1 respectively encoding the stimulatory G protein alpha subunit (Gsα) and G protein subunit beta 1 (Gβ) coupled to GPCR were increased in keloid stromal cells, so as ATP2B4 associated to cellular response to epinephrine stimulus and APP involved in cellular response to norepinephrine stimulus (Fig. 3b). Because catecholamine is produced as neurotransmitters by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive nerves, we visualized TH+ neurites in normal and keloid skin (Fig. 3c). TH+ neurites were mainly found in the HFs of normal human skin, reflecting the innervation of postganglionic sympathetic neurons (Fig. 3c). Because keloid fibroblasts were regulated by catecholamine and were osteogenic, we assumed that catecholamine orchestrated fibroblast osteogenesis in keloid. To test this, we treated fibroblasts isolated from normal skin and keloid skin with catecholamine neurotransmitters epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine, and detected RUNX2, phospho-RUNX2 (pRUNX2, as the indicator of RUNX2 activity), and IBSP expression. Keloid fibroblasts highly expressed RUNX2, but RUNX2 expression or activity was not significantly affected by catecholamine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In contrast, epinephrine and norepinephrine increased IBSP production in keloid fibroblasts by 2.58-fold and 1.74-fold, respectively (Fig. 3d). Thus, we provide evidence that inordinate catecholaminergic innervation in keloid facilitated catecholamine-mediated IBSP production from keloid fibroblasts.

ADRB1-dependent IBSP expression in keloid fibroblasts

To understand the mechanism through which catecholamine governs the osteogenesis of keloid fibroblasts, we extracted and cultured fibroblasts from normal and keloid skin samples and measured the mRNA expression of adrenergic receptors22. The results showed that ADRB1 was consistently highly expressed by keloid fibroblasts in contrast to normal fibroblasts (Fig. 4a), which was further verified by immunofluorescence staining of keloid skin sections showing increased expression of ADRB1 in RUNX2+ keloid fibroblasts compared to fibroblasts in healthy skin (Fig. 4b). To assess whether catecholamine induces IBSP through ADRB1, we treated keloid fibroblasts with epinephrine in the setting of siRNA-mediated ADRB1 silencing and found that IBSP production was blocked in the absence of ADRB1 (Supplementary Fig. 10b and Fig. 4c). We then used bulk RNA-seq to further understand the ADRB1-dependent effects of epinephrine on keloid fibroblasts. Epinephrine treatment for 6 h or 24 h marginally altered the transcriptome of keloid fibroblasts, with myofibroblast differentiation-associated genes slightly increased after 6 h-treatment of epinephrine and partially suppressed by metoprolol (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 3). IBSP expression did not change in the RNA-seq data, and we monitored IBSP expression at different time points after epinephrine treatment using qPCR, with 3 pairs of primers to ensure the accuracy. Our data showed that epinephrine did not regulate IBSP at the mRNA level (Fig. 4f), and further investigation was needed to explain why epinephrine promoted the generation of IBSP protein.

Translational availability of IBSP dominated by the mRNA transporter TSN

Thus far, our data indicated that epinephrine might promote IBSP expression via post-transcriptional mechanisms. Through surveying the bulk RNA-seq data, we found that TSN, which encodes an RNA-binding protein, was increased in keloid fibroblasts treated with epinephrine for 24 h, and its expression was suppressed by metoprolol-mediated antagonism of ADRB1 (Fig. 5a). Human keloid skin showed increased TSN expression in the dermis as compared to normal control skin (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, TSN was mainly located in the nuclei of the cultured keloid fibroblasts, and epinephrine treatment resulted in the formation of TSN puncta in the cytoplasm, which was inhibited by metoprolol (Fig. 5c). Given that TSN regulates the transportation and translation of certain mRNAs24–26, we tested if TSN was involved in IBSP protein generation in keloid fibroblasts. To further reveal the role of TSN in regulating the translation of IBSP, we first visualized the mRNA of IBSP using RNAscope in keloid fibroblasts upon epinephrine stimulation in the presence or absence of TSN. Ex vivo cultured keloid fibroblasts expressed the mRNAs of RUNX2 and IBSP, with IBSP mRNA mainly located in the nuclei (Fig. 5e). Notably, epinephrine treatment translocated IBSP mRNA from the nuclei to the cytoplasm, and such an effect was not observed in keloid fibroblasts with TSN deficiency (Fig. 5e). We then measured the translation efficiency of IBSP mRNA through RNA immunoprecipitation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (EIF4E). Quantitation of IBSP mRNA pulled by an EIF4E antibody revealed that keloid fibroblasts pulsed with epinephrine harbored increased IBSP mRNA undergoing translation, while TSN deficiency blocked IBSP translation facilitated by epinephrine (Fig. 5f). Collectively, epinephrine-induced TSN drove IBSP expression through regulating mRNA trafficking and translational availability in keloid fibroblasts.

Given that epinephrine promoted TSN transcription through ADRB1 (Fig. 5a), we investigated whether the ADRB1–cAMP–PKA–CREB axis was required for TSN expression. Our data showed that inhibition of PKA or CREB significantly decreased TSN expression at both the RNA and protein levels in keloid fibroblasts (Fig. 6b, c). Thus, we demonstrated that ADRB1–cAMP–PKA–CREB signaling drives TSN expression in keloid fibroblasts.

Alleviated keloid-like disease after catecholaminergic neuron ablation in rats

We next thought to investigate the in vivo interaction between TH+ neurons and keloid fibroblasts and the functional significance of such an interaction. Murine tail skin does not contain panniculus carnosus, and its characteristics including thick and cornified epidermis, sparse hair follicles and more pronounced adaptive immunity, make it akin to human skin structurally and cellularly much more than dorsal skin28,29. We thus generated an excisional wound on rat tail and stretched the scar after re-epithelialization to mimic the development of human hypertrophic scar and keloid30 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). This model overrepresented pathological features of human hypertrophic scar and keloid, including increased α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) as the marker of myofibroblasts31, and excessive synthesis of Collagen I as an indicator of ECM over-deposition32 (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). Importantly, the rat model recapitulated the keloid neuro-stromal characteristics, which were inordinate catecholaminergic innervation (Supplementary Fig. 11d), emergence of Runx2+ fibroblasts highly expressing Adrb1 (Supplementary Fig. 11e), and significant deposition of Ibsp in the dermis (Supplementary Fig. 11f). Although we found increased GAG accumulation as indicated by positive signals of Alcian Blue staining (Supplementary Fig. 11g), calcification was not observed in this rat model of keloid, suggesting that our experimental timeline likely captures earlier pathology stages.

To more precisely define the role of locally generated norepinephrine and epinephrine in rendering the keloid-associated pathological features of dermal fibroblasts, we topically injected Nepicastat, a dopamine-β-hydroxylase inhibitor that restraints norepinephrine and epinephrine generation34, to the stretched wounds and assessed the keloid-like histological phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Our data showed that Nepicastat treatment decreased scar thickness and α-SMA expression but did not affect the deposition of Collagen I (Supplementary Fig. 12b–d). Importantly, Nepicastat potently diminished Runx2 and Ibsp expression, as well as GAG accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 12e, f). This establishes that locally synthesized norepinephrine and epinephrine drive osteogenic fibroblast activation during keloid pathogenesis.

Therapeutic effects of ADRB1 antagonist on rat keloid-like phenotype

We assessed the in vivo function of Adrb1 in the rat stretched wound model through intra-wound injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV) for delivering short hairpin RNA (shRNA) that targets Adrb1 (Supplementary Fig. 13a). AAV harboring shAdrb1 efficiently abolished Adrb1 expression by dermal fibroblasts in the stretched wound (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Histological analyses revealed that topical deletion of Adrb1 reduced scar thickness and Collagen I deposition but did not influence α-SMA expression (Supplementary Fig. 13c-e). Notably, topical silencing of Adrb1 decreased the number of Runx2+Ibsp+ fibroblasts and prevented GAG accumulation, suggesting a necessary role of Adrb1 in regulating the osteogenic activity of fibroblasts during the development of keloid-like pathology (Supplementary Fig. 13f, g).

To further explore the translational potential of our findings, we used the ADRB1 antagonist metoprolol to treat the rats with stretched wounds (Fig. 8a). Systematic administration of metoprolol through drinking water suppressed the development of the keloid-like pathological phenotype, with the scar thickness significantly decreased after metoprolol treatment (Fig. 8b). We assessed the classical pathological indicators, Collagen I and α-SMA, and found that metoprolol not only reduced keloid-associated ECM deposition but also repressed the activation of myofibroblasts (Fig. 8c, d). Importantly, metoprolol reduced the number of Runx2+ fibroblasts, inhibited Ibsp production, and decreased GAG accumulation (Fig. 8e, f). Discussion

Our study identifies a distinct population of RUNX2⁺IBSP⁺ fibroblasts in keloid lesions that undergo a hybrid fibro-osseous reprogramming, as evidenced by increased ALP and calcium deposition in human keloid tissues. These fibroblasts exhibit elevated expression of RUNX2, a key transcription factor regulating osteogenesis, and demonstrate active osteogenic activity through the secretion of cartilage- and bone-associated ECM proteins. Critically, transcriptional profiling revealed catecholamine response pathways in keloid fibroblasts, which prompted further investigation into the role of sympathetic innervation. We discovered aberrantly dense catecholaminergic nerve fibers within keloid lesions, where epinephrine signaling via ADRB1 promotes TSN-dependent IBSP mRNA transport and translation. Preclinical validation underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting this neuro-stromal axis: in a rat model replicating hyperinnervation and ADRB1 overexpression, both sympathetic denervation and β1-adrenergic antagonism suppressed IBSP deposition and prevented scar hypertrophy.

RUNX2+ keloid fibroblasts readily express IBSP mRNA, but do not produce massive IBSP protein without receiving catecholaminergic neuronal signals. The neurotransmitter epinephrine activates ADRB1–cAMP–PKA–CREB axis in keloid fibroblasts and subsequently induces the transcription of TSN. Reported to transport the mRNAs of brain derived neurotrophic factor (BNDF) and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CAMKII), TSN was recognized important for the post-transcriptional control of gene expression and local protein synthesis predominantly in neurons26,35. We here uncovered the indispensable role of TSN in transporting the mRNA of IBSP from the nuclei to the cytosol and thus regulating the production of IBSP protein and the pathological behaviors of keloid fibroblasts. Our findings highlight that mRNA localization as a common mechanism for targeting proteins to regions of neurons is also critical for the function of fibroblasts in the context of keloid as a paradigm of fibrotic diseases, which calls for further investigation into mechanisms involving RNA translocation and protein synthesis and discovery of related drug targets.

The role of neurons in keloid is not well-characterized as previous studies disputed over the density alteration of neurites in keloid36–38. These studies focused on the nociceptive sensations of keloid skin and used pan-neuron markers like S100 and PGP9.5 to stain the neurites. IBSP expression in keloid fibroblasts is governed by epinephrine and norepinephrine derived from TH+ catecholaminergic neurites in keloid skin, and these nerve fibers are probably of sympathetic origin, as the TH+ dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons were discovered in rodents but not in humans39,40. Such crosstalk between sympathetic nerves and stromal cells was also noticed in mouse gonadal adipose tissue to control adipose mesenchymal cell function, and in femur to regulate mesenchymal stromal cell commitment41,42. In normal human skin, sympathetic nerves wrap around HFs to regulate the behaviors of HF stem cells and melanocyte stem cells; in keloid skin, the structure of HF is absent, which disrupts the crosstalk between sympathetic neurons and stem cells within the HF43. Therefore, our findings reveal a disease-specific neuro-stromal interaction that contributes to keloid pathology.

We explored the translational potential of our finding that catecholaminergic nerves promote IBSP deposition through activating ADRB1 on keloid fibroblasts. We acknowledge the inherent limitations of our animal model. First, rodent DRG contain TH+ C-low threshold mechanoreceptors not found in human44, presenting a species-specific neuronal difference. Second, while human keloid tissues exhibited obvious calcification foci, frank mineralization was not detected in the rat stretched wound model within the experimental timeframe. This discrepancy suggests that the rat model captures the early-stage molecular reprogramming rather than the advanced osseous phenotype. Nevertheless, the model faithfully recapitulated the critical upstream drivers of the human pathology: abnormal innervation of TH+ neurons and the emergence of RUNX2+ fibroblasts expressing Adrb1 and Ibsp. Thus, despite the absence of mineral deposits, the model remains a valid platform for evaluating therapeutic interventions targeting the neuro-stromal signaling axis. Our data demonstrated that metoprolol, a selective β1-adrenergic antagonist, effectively reduced pathological ECM deposition and prevented keloid-like progression. This is supported by clinical evidence showing reduced keloid incidence in cardiac device recipients treated with β-blockers45. Our findings suggest that targeting the hybrid fibro-osseous reprogramming—specifically the adrenergic-driven IBSP accumulation—offers a potent strategy to disrupt the cycle of stiffness and recurrence, even before macroscopic calcification occurs.

While our study establishes ADRB1 overexpression as a critical mediator of catecholamine hypersensitivity in keloid fibroblasts, the upstream regulatory mechanisms driving ADRB1 dysregulation remain unresolved. The transcriptional or epigenetic basis for elevated ADRB1 expression in RUNX2+ fibroblasts warrants further investigation, particularly whether catecholamine exposure induces receptor upregulation via feedback mechanisms, or if keloid-specific chromatin remodeling enables constitutive ADRB1 activation. Future studies interrogating ADRB1 promoter activity, histone modifications, and single-cell chromatin accessibility in RUNX2⁺ fibroblasts could delineate the origin of this pathogenic receptor overexpression, potentially revealing therapeutic targets to disrupt neuro-stromal crosstalk.

Methods

Human subjects

Keloid samples and normal skin specimens were taken from patients and healthy donors who were undergoing plastic surgery. Patient information is provided in Supplementary Data 1. Patients with clinically diagnosed keloids existing for ≥ 1 year, not treated previously, and age ≥ 18 years were included. Exclusions included active infection, keloid radiotherapy history, recent immunosuppressants, pregnancy, or coagulation disorders. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Boards of Renji Hospital and Shanghai General Hospital (No. 2017RA135 and No. 2023SQ161).

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6 weeks-of-age) were purchased from Charles River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals with the approval (SYXK-2019-0028) of the Scientific Investigation Board of Shanghai General Hospital. Throughout these experimental studies, all efforts were made to alleviate any suffering, and the rats were euthanized by CO2 inhalation.

Single-cell transcriptomics and analysis

Fresh skin biopsies were placed in saline at 4 °C prior to processing. The epidermis was separated from the dermis by Dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich cat. 4693) digestion overnight at 4 °C. Single-cell suspensions were generated by enzyme digestion as previously reported46. The cells were then subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to exclude doublets, debris, and DAPI-positive dead cells. Sorted cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 0.04% BSA in PBS. Chromium Single Cell 5’ v3 (10X Genomics) libraries were prepared using the Chromium Controller according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting libraries were sequenced with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Trimmed data were processed using the CellRanger (version 3.0, 10X Genomics) and further filtered, processed, and analyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.9.9)47. Cells with fewer than 200 genes, more than 6,000 genes, or more than 5% mitochondria content were removed. Doublets were predicted using DoubletFinder and removed48. The filtered data were normalized using a scaling factor of 10,000 to generate transcripts per kilobase million (TPM)-like values. We integrated 5 filtered keloid samples using the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions with default parameters (dimensionality = 30). The top 2,000 most variable genes were selected using the FindVariableFeatures function and the genes were then used for principal component analysis (PCA). Clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with a resolution of 0.2. Normal keratinocytes, melanocytes and immune cells were extracted from our datasets HRA003418 and HRA006130 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human/); and integrated with the keloid cell compartments respectively. Normal stromal cells, which were also processed through enzymatic digestion and FACS sorting, were extracted from samples SKN8104899, SKN8105197, 4820STDY7388991, 4280STDY7388999 and 4820STDY7389007 within the dataset E-MTAB-8142 and integrated with the keloid stromal cells. Clustering was performed with a resolution selected for different datasets to discern major lineages without excessive fragmentation. GSEA was performed to identify enriched pathways49,50.

Probe-based single cell gene expression flex and analysis

Fresh skin biopsies were placed in saline at 4 °C prior to processing. The epidermis was separated from the dermis by Dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich cat. 4693) digestion overnight at 4 °C. Dermal single-cell suspensions were generated by enzyme digestion as previously reported46. The dermal cells were fixed in a Fixation Buffer containing 4% Formaldehyde and 1× Conc. Fix & Perm Buffer (10X Genomics cat. PN-2000517) at 4 °C for 20 h. Afterwards, the fixation was quenched, and the cells were stored at −80 °C until probe hybridization. Chromium Fixed RNA Kit, Human Transcriptome (10X Genomics cat. PN-1000474) was used for hybridization and library preparation. The resulting libraries were sequenced with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Trimmed data were processed using the CellRanger (version 3.0, 10X Genomics) and further filtered, processed, and analyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.9.9)47.

RNAscope

Human keloid paraffin sections were dewaxed, and single-molecule FISH experiments were carried out using an RNAscope™ Multiplex Fluorescent Assay v2 along with an RNAscope™ Probe-Hs-IBSP (ACD cat. 587221) and an RNAscopeTM Probe-Hs-RUNX2-C3 (ACD cat. 440071-C3). Human keloid stromal cells were cultured in glass bottom dishes (NEST cat. 801002) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were then dehydrated, rehydrated and permeabilized, and FISH was carried out using the RNAscope™ Multiplex Fluorescent Assay v2, RNAscope™ Probe-Hs-IBSP, and RNAscope™ Probe-Hs-RUNX2-C3. The samples were mounted with a fluorescent mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich cat. DUO82040) and visualized under a confocal microscope (Leica, STELLARIS 8 DIVE).

Immunofluorescence

Skin specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were dewaxed, and antigen unmasked. The slices were blocked with 5% goat sera/1% BSA/PBS for 1 h and stained with anti-RUNX2 (Abcam cat. ab192256, 1:1,000 dilution; Novus cat. AF2006, 1:100 dilution), anti-IBSP (Santa Cruz cat. sc-73630, 1:500 dilution; Invitrogen cat. PA5-114915, 1:200 dilution), anti-ALP (Absin cat. abs158623, 1:200 dilution), anti-ADRB1 (Novus cat. NB600-978, 1:100 dilution), anti-Collagen I (Absin cat. abs131984, 1:200 dilution), and anti-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, Proteintech cat. 67735-1-Ig, 1:400 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. Keloid fibroblasts were seeded on glass-bottom culture dishes and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were washed, blocked with 5% goat serum/1% BSA/PBS for 1 h, and stained with anti-TSN (Absin cat. abs133594, 1:250 dilution) and Phalloidin (Absin cat. abs47048271, 1:200 dilution). Thereafter, the slices or cells were rinsed three times in 0.1% Tween 20/PBS and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies (all from Invitrogen) at a 1:1000 dilution for 1 h at RT in the dark. DAPI (BD Biosciences cat. 564907) incubation at a 1:2000 dilution at RT for 10 min was used for nuclei staining. After being washed in 0.1% Tween 20/PBS, the slices or cells were mounted with a fluorescent mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich cat. DUO82040) and visualized under a confocal microscope (Leica, STELLARIS 8 DIVE). TSN puncta quantification was performed through systematic random sampling of five non-overlapping microscopic fields, with stage movement intervals of 500 μm to ensure spatial independence. Cells were included for analysis only when exhibiting fully visible nuclei within the field. Puncta were defined as cytoplasmic foci meeting three criteria: (1) ≥ 1 distinct TSN+ structures per cell, (2) localization outside DAPI-defined nuclear masks, and (3) fluorescence intensity ≥ 3-fold above background levels measured in adjacent cytoplasmic regions.

Alcian Blue staining and quantification

Paraffin-embedded skin sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and rinsed in 3% acetic acid. Sections were stained with 1% Alcian Blue 8GX (pH = 2.5, Sigma-Aldrich cat. A3157) in 3% acetic acid for 20 min at RT. After rinsing in running tap water for 5 min, sections were counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red (Beyotime cat. C0151) for 2 min. Slides were dehydrated through ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. GAG-rich regions stained turquoise. To ensure strict comparability and avoid bias arising from variations in total biopsy size, we defined a standardized Region of Interest (ROI) for quantification. For human skin samples, we analyzed a fixed area of 2.7 mm2 within the reticular dermis; for rat skin samples, we analyzed a fixed area of 1.8 mm2. We used ImageJ for quantification within these standardized ROIs. Images were converted to 8-bit format, and the blue channel was isolated to specifically target GAG staining. Positive signal areas were identified through manual threshold adjustment (intensity range 60–255) to exclude background, and the Integrated Density (IntDen) was measured to quantify the GAGs.

Alizarin Red staining and quantification

Deparaffinized sections were incubated in 2% Alizarin Red S (pH = 4.2, Sigma-Aldrich cat. A5533) for 10 min at 37 °C. After rinsing in distilled water, slides were differentiated in 0.5% HCl/70% ethanol for 15 sec to remove nonspecific staining. Sections were dehydrated in acetone, cleared in xylene, and mounted. Calcium deposits appeared bright dark red under brightfield microscopy. We analyzed an ROI of 2.7 mm2 within the human reticular dermis and used ImageJ for quantification within the standardized ROIs. After 8-bit conversion, color inversion was applied to highlight mineralized nodules. Positive signals were segmented using manual threshold adjustment, and the total Integrated Density (IntDen) was calculated to quantify the calcium load.

Whole mount immunofluorescence

Whole mount samples were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions of Rapiclear® 1.52 Solution (SunJin Lab cat. RC152001). Skin samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After washed with PBS for 3 times, the samples were permeabilized in 2% Triton X-100/0.05% sodium azide/PBS for 2 days at RT. The samples were then washed with PBS and blocked with 10% goat sera/1% Triton-X 100/2.5% DMSO/0.2% sodium azide/PBS on an orbital shaker for 2 days at 4 °C. The samples were stained with anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, GeneTex cat. GTX113016, 1:100 dilution) for 2 days at 4 °C. Thereafter, the samples were washed with 3% NaCl/0.2% Triton-X 100/PBS for 3 times at RT and then overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, the samples were incubated with a secondary antibody (from Invitrogen) for 2 days at 4 °C and washed with 3% NaCl/0.2% Triton-X 100/PBS for 3 times at RT and then overnight at 4 °C. After washed with PBS for 3 times, the samples were stained with DAPI (BD Biosciences cat. 564907, 1:2000 dilution) at RT for 1.5 h. After washed with PBS for 3 times, the samples were cleared and mounted using a Rapiclear® 1.52 Solution and placed in iSpacer (SunJin Lab cat. IS007) microchambers. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica, TSC SP8) with an HC PL APO CS2 20×/0.75 IMM objective, a scan speed of 400 Hz and a step size of 0.5 μm. The X/Y acquiring format was set as 1024 pixel × 1024 pixel, and Z-stack size was determined by specimen thickness respectively. Axoden (https://github.com/raqueladaia/AxoDen/blob/main/README.md) was used to quantify the percentages of areas receiving innervation.

Fibroblast isolation and culture

Keloid and normal skin samples were washed thoroughly with cold PBS to remove blood. After the removal of fat, the samples were cut into 1 mm × 2 mm pieces and placed in 6-well plates (pre-coated with gelatin) with 2–3 pieces per well. The samples were maintained in DMEM/high glucose (VivaCell cat. 06-1055-57-1ACS) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Gibco cat. C0232) and antibiotics (Solarbio cat. P7630) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2 weeks until the cell confluence reached 80%. The cells were then harvested and sub-cultured for further assays.

Fibroblast stimulation

Keloid or normal fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. The next day, the cells were treated or not with 10 nM epinephrine (MCE cat. HY-B0447B), 10 nM norepinephrine (MCE cat. HY-13715), or 10 nM dopamine (MCE cat. HY-B0451A) for 24 h before the collection for immunoblotting; or treated or not with 10 nM epinephrine for 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h before the collection for qPCR. For the assays involving siRNAs, keloid fibroblasts were transfected with the indicated siRNAs for 24 h and then incubated in refreshed medium containing 10 nM epinephrine for 24 h before the collection for immunoblotting or RNAscope. For the assays involving ADRB1 antagonism, keloid fibroblasts were treated with 1 μM metoprolol (MCE cat. HY-17503) for 30 min before the addition of epinephrine. For the assays involving PKA or CREB antagonism, keloid fibroblasts were treated with 1 μM PKA Inhibitor Fragment (6-22) amide TFA (MCE cat. HY-P1290A) or the CREB inhibitor 666-15 (MCE cat. HY-101120) for 1 h before the addition of epinephrine. The cells were then incubated for another 30 min, 6 h or 24 h before the collection for immunoblotting, qPCR, bulk RNA-seq, immunofluorescence or ELISA.

qPCR

Total RNA from cultured fibroblasts was isolated using RNAiso Reagent (TaKaRa cat. 9108) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix (Vazyme cat. Melting curve analysis was performed for each reaction to confirm single-peak amplification. The relative expression of target genes was confirmed using the quantity of target gene/quantity of GAPDH. RNA interference

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was synthesized by GenePharma: ADRB1 sense: CUCGUCCGUAGUCUCCUUC, ADRB1 anti-sense: GAAGGAGACUACGGACGAG; TSN sense: CUACGACGGAUUGAAAUAU, TSN anti-sense: AUAUUUCAAUCCGUCGUAG. Fibroblasts were seeded at 60% confluence in antibiotic-free medium 24 h pre-transfection. siRNA-Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen cat. 11668019) complexes were prepared using 50 nM siRNA and 2 μL reagent per well (6-well plate), incubated for 20 min at RT, then added to cells for 6 h before medium replacement. Knockdown efficiency was validated at 48 h post-transfection via immunoblotting.

Bulk RNA-seq

Total RNA from cultured fibroblasts was isolated using RNAiso Reagent (TaKaRa cat. 9108). cDNA libraries were prepared using a VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme cat. NR605-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). The adaptor sequences were trimmed from the raw paired-end reads using Skewer51. The sequences were then aligned to GRCh38 using STAR52 and assembled using StringTie53. The read counts were further normalized into FPKM values, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq254 with the criteria set as |log2FC | ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05. GSEA was performed to identify enriched pathways49,50.

RNA immunoprecipitation

RNA Immunoprecipitation to measure translation efficiency was performed as previously reported55. Briefly, keloid fibroblasts were transfected with control or TSN siRNA for 24 h and then incubated with 10 nM epinephrine or not for another 24 h. The cells were lysed on ice in a polysome lysis buffer containing 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 0.5% IGEPAL® CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich cat. I8896), 1 mM DTT, 100 U/ml RNase inhibitor (Beyotime cat. R0102), 2 mM vanadyl ribonucleoside complexes (Beyotime cat. R0108) and protease inhibitor cocktail (APExBIO cat. K1007). After preclearing with protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck cat. B23202) for twice at 4°C for 1 h, input controls were reserved, and the lysates were incubated with eIF4E antibodies (Santa Cruz cat. sc-9976) for overnight at 4°C. The next day, protein A/G magnetic beads were added to precipitate the antibodies and the immunoprecipitates were washed with the polysome lysis buffer containing 1 M urea for 4 times. All samples (with input controls) were then incubated in the polysome lysis buffer containing 0.1% SDS and proteinase K. The samples were then subjected to RNA isolation using RNAiso Reagent (TaKaRa cat. 9108) and GlycoBlue™ (Invitrogen cat. AM9515)-mediated RNA precipitation. The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix (Vazyme cat. R333-01). qPCR was conducted with the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme cat. Q331-03) in a QuantStudio 7 Flex (Applied Biosystems). IBSP primers forward: TACACGGGCGCCAATGAATA and reverse: GCCATCGTAGCCTTGTCCTT were used.

Elisa

Keloid fibroblasts were treated with metoprolol for 1 h before the addition of epinephrine. After incubation for another 30 minutes, the cells were washed three times with PBS, resuspended in lysis buffer and frozen at −80 °C. After three freeze-thaw cycles, the samples were centrifuged to remove cell debris, and the supernatants were collected to 96-well plates. The cAMP levels were measured using cAMP Parameter Assay Kit (R&D Systems cat. KGE002B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the data were collected using a Multiskan GO (Thermo Scientific).

ChIP-qPCR

ChIP was performed using the SimpleCHIP enzymatic chromatin immunoprecipitation kit (Cell Signaling Technology cat. 9002) according to the manufacturer’s instruction with minor modifications. In brief, keloid fibroblasts were harvested and cross-linked with 1% (v/v) formaldehyde for 10 min at RT. Subsequently, the nuclei were isolated by lysing the cytoplasmic fraction, and chromatin was digested into fragments of 150–900 bp with micrococcal nuclease for 20 min at 37°C, followed by ultrasonic disruption of the nuclear membrane using a standard microtip and a Scientz JY92-IIDN sonicator (three pulses, 50% amplitude and duty cycle 30%). The sonicated nuclear fractions were divided for input control and for overnight incubation at 4 °C with 2 μg of either anti-CREB1 (Proteintech cat. 12208-1-AP) or the negative control IgG. After incubation with 30 μl ChIP grade protein G-agarose beads for 2 h at 4 °C, the Ab-protein-DNA complexes were eluted from the beads and digested by proteinase K for 2 h at 65 °C, followed by spin column-based purification of DNA. Finally, genomic DNA recovered from the ChIP were qPCR amplified with primers specific to the CREB binding elements of the TSN promoter region. The primers used to detect the TSN promoter sequence were as follows: forward: GACGGTCGTGGCGTAAGAC, reverse: GGCAACCAGCGCAATCAATC. The signals were expressed as a percentage of the total input chromatin.

The rat stretched wound model and treatment

The rat stretched wound model was constructed as previously described30. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized by pentobarbital sodium and 6-mm2 square excisional wounds were created 3 centimeters away from the body on the dorsum of the tails using scalpels with the panniculus carnosus carefully removed (day 0). The wounds were then covered with sterile gauze and the mechanical stretching started on day 14 after re-epithelialization. Customized stainless rings with a diameter of 2 cm were fixed to the abdominal side of the tails to bend it in accordance with the curvature of the ring for 14 days or 28 days to achieve the pathological appearance of hypertrophic scarring. For TH+ neuron ablation, the rats with stretched wounds were intraperitoneally injected with 50 mg/kg 6-OHDA on days 15, 17, 24, 26, 33 and 35. For dopamine-β-hydroxylase inhibition, 5 mg/kg Nepicastat was intradermally injected to the wound region on day 16 and every other day afterwards. For AAV treatment, 4 × 1010 AAV6 particles harboring the Adrb1 targeting shRNA: 5’-GGACTTCGGTAGACGTGCTAT-3’ or a scrambled control shRNA were intradermally injected to the wound region on days 12 and 21. For ADRB1 blockage, the rats with stretched wounds took metoprolol (2 mg/mL) dissolved in drinking water from day 16 to day 42.

Statistical analysis

For immunofluorescence sections, we randomly selected 5 non-overlapping fields of view of identical dimensions within the reticular dermis and calculated the fluorescence mean intensity for each field using the Intensity Correlation Analysis plugin in ImageJ56. Mean intensities were averaged to represent the sample. Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ with GAPDH as loading control. Phosphorylated protein levels were expressed as the ratios of phosphorylated to total protein. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 and are presented as the mean ± SD. A Student’s t-test was used to compare two conditions, and an analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni or Newman-Keuls correction was used for multiple comparisons.
