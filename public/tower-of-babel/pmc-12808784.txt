Features of formation and neutralization of environmentally hazardous CrO₃ in chromium pyrometallurgy
Authors: Alexander Akberdin, Alexander Kim, Alexey Orlov, Ruslan Sultangaziyev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper examines the behavior of environmentally hazardous hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) during the production of chromium-containing ferroalloys. Based on the evaluation of changes in Gibbs free energy, the oxidation of trivalent chromium in ore at the top of the smelting furnace to hexavalent chromium is shown to be possible due to interaction with atmospheric oxygen in the workshop and lime added during the process. It is hypothesized that the likelihood of Cr(VI) formation inside the furnace itself is low, due to the strictly reducing nature of the ferrochrome production process. The presence of Cr(VI) in slags is believed to result from the interaction of relic ore minerals containing trivalent chromium, such as Cr₂FeO₄ and Cr₂MgO₄, with atmospheric oxygen during slag tapping and transportation in ladles to waste disposal areas. Comprehensive thermodynamic modeling involving industrial slags and carbonaceous reducers has demonstrated the feasibility of converting all chromium and iron oxides into a metal phase consisting of chromium and iron carbides, similar to carbon-rich ferrochrome. It is recommended to agglomerate slag wastes containing Cr(VI) with bitumen prior to smelting. Bitumen serves not only as a binder but also as a reducing agent. Experiments conducted in a high-temperature furnace have confirmed the feasibility of implementing this technology in industrial conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31840-2.

Introduction

Chromium is an essential element for human life. Despite its biological importance, some of its compounds are toxic. They exert general toxic, irritant, cumulative, allergenic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic effects on the human and animal body. The toxicity of these compounds is so significant that, according to the European Union Directive 2003/53/EC, the content of water-soluble chromium in products such as cement and cement mixtures must not exceed 0.0002%, while in aqueous solutions, the maximum allowable concentration is 0.05 mg/dm³ as per Kazakhstan’s sanitary regulations.

The main anthropogenic sources of chromium emissions are ferroalloy and steel production plants, sodium and potassium chromate manufacturing facilities (accounting for 80% of total emissions), as well as enterprises that burn oil and coal (15%). Kazakhstan possesses substantial reserves of chromite ores and processes them into various chromium derivatives. The main ore mineral of chromium-containing ores is chromite FeO∙Cr₂O₃. In natural minerals, chromium is partially substituted by aluminum, while iron is substituted by magnesium. The ores also contain the following principal mineral varieties of chromespinelides: manganochromite (Mg, Fe)O∙Cr₂O₃; chrompicotite (Mg, Fe)O∙(Cr, Al)₂O₃; and alumochromite FeO∙(Cr, Al)₂O₃. Scientists from various countries have established that environmentally hazardous hexavalent chromium (designated as CrO₃, Cr⁶⁺, or Cr(VI)) is frequently formed at almost all stages of chromite ore processing. Its presence has been detected even during dry grinding of chromite ores.

In chromium pyrometallurgy, Cr(VI) is generated during the smelting of all alloy types: in small amounts in closed furnaces and when using acidic slags, and in larger amounts in open furnaces and with basic slags1. In these processes, Cr(VI) is found both in the dust of off-gases and in the slags discharged from the furnaces. Its concentration in these materials significantly exceeds permissible levels, making neutralization efforts necessary.

Proposals for neutralizing Cr(VI) through high-temperature treatment of Cr(VI)-containing waste slags and sludges are noteworthy2–5. However, there are no documented cases of such processes being implemented. Far more common are proposals for treating wastewater containing Cr(VI). The most widespread method involves aqueous chemical reduction of Cr(VI) in solid waste using ferrous compounds such as iron(II) chloride or iron(II) sulfate6–8. This method, however, is not suitable for regions with water scarcity, as is the case in Kazakhstan. More importantly, while removing Cr(VI) from groundwater, this approach introduces environmentally hazardous iron chloride and sulfate into the environment.

This study attempts to provide a theoretical assessment of the conditions under which Cr(VI) forms in chromium pyrometallurgy and proposes methods for its neutralization, which have been tested under real industrial conditions.

Methods

This study was conducted using the example of refined ferrochrome grades smelted from Kazakhstani chromite ores. Theoretical assessment of Cr(VI) behavior in this process and during the reductive smelting of resulting slags was carried out using thermodynamic software packages HSC and TERRA9,10.

The slags generated in this process are characterized by high basicity (CaO/SiO₂ = 1.8–1.9) and tend to disintegrate into fine powder upon cooling due to the phase inversion of calcium orthosilicate (Ca₂SiO₄) contained within them. These slags are stored in long-term storage facilities. For reductive smelting aimed at Cr(VI) neutralization, the slags were agglomerated using BN-IV grade bitumen as a binder (and a carbon source) in an industrial-scale unit provided by Rentan company. This facility enables low-temperature preheating of the charge (130–150 °C) and briquetting into pieces of 20 × 30 mm.

The addition of 0.3–0.5% B₂O₃ to the disintegrating slags prevents the inversion of calcium orthosilicate (Ca₂SiO₄), allowing the slags to retain a solid, lumpy form after cooling11.

The smelting of briquetted and lumpy slags was carried out in a high-temperature resistance furnace using graphite crucibles. Analysis of the smelting products (metal and slag) was conducted in the certified laboratory of the Zh. Abishev Chemical-Metallurgical Institute.

Practical evidence for this is the detection of CrO₃ in aspiration dust from the smelting of ferrochrome and ferrosilicochrome in amounts ranging from 0.04 to 0.6%13. However, hexavalent chromium is also found in slags discharged from the furnace. According to12, natural slags have been shown to contain calcium chromitochromate (9CaO·4CrO₃·Cr₂O₃) and calcium chromate (CaCrO₄). Due to the low oxidation potential in the ore-reduction furnace—lower than that required for the stability of CrO₃14–16 — it is assumed that the presence of CrO₃ in slags is the result of interaction between relic chromite ores in the form of MgO·Cr₂O₃ and FeO·Cr₂O₃ with oxygen from the ambient air during tapping and transportation to the waste dump.

To neutralize the hexavalent chromium contained in aspiration dust and slags, it is necessary to reduce its oxidation state from + 6 to + 3 or 0—that is, to convert it into safer forms such as Cr₂O₃ or metallic chromium. For this, reducing agents are required. The most accessible and widespread among them is carbon, found in coke, coal, oil, and carbon-based materials. Such materials can be introduced into the ladle during slag tapping or added to the charge for re-smelting dumped slags. At the temperature range typical for product discharge from the furnace (1900–2100 K), chromium and iron oxide compounds—including CrO₃—are not present in the slag (Fig. 1a). Their cations are reduced during smelting and transferred into the metal phase, where they exist in the form of chromium (Cr₇C₃) and iron (Fe₃C) carbides (Fig. 1b).

For a series of experiments, briquettes were made from slag produced during the smelting of refined ferrochrome grades. BN-IV grade bitumen was used as both a binder and a carbon source. To stabilize the slag and prevent disintegration, the charge was supplemented with calcined borate ore from the Inder deposit.

The briquetting process included the following steps: drying of the charge, feeding it at 100–120 °C into a mechanical mixer, where liquid bitumen at 150 °C was simultaneously introduced. Pressing pressure ranged from 15 to 50 MPa. The binder (bitumen) content was varied between 5% and 15%.

The resulting briquettes were tested using an INSTRON 5900 series machine designed for measuring loads during tensile, compression, and bending tests (Fig. 2).

For testing, the briquettes were placed one at a time in the center of the hardened area on the lower platen. The load was applied at a constant crosshead speed of 15 mm/min throughout the entire testing period. The load was recorded until the complete destruction of the briquette. Before each new test, the platens were thoroughly cleaned.

As evident from the obtained data, to achieve the optimal briquette strength of 3.5 kN per briquette, an addition of 10% BN-IV grade bitumen is required.

The experimental verification of the technology was carried out in a Tammann high-temperature resistance furnace. The charge was loaded into a graphite crucible, placed in the furnace, and heated to a temperature of 1650 °C. The temperature was measured using a tungsten-rhenium thermocouple (WR 5/20). The heating rate was 15 °C per minute. Once the target temperature was reached, the crucibles were held at that temperature for 20 min.

The implementation of reductive smelting of slags using carbon-containing materials (coke and bitumen) allows for the complete elimination of hexavalent chromium from processing products, thus confirming the effectiveness of the proposed technology. As a result of thermal treatment at 1650 °C, the oxide forms of chromium—including CrO₃—were fully reduced to metallic chromium and its carbides. The use of both coke and bituminous briquettes showed comparable results in terms of metal quality; however, briquettes with bitumen exhibited higher carbon content (up to 8.75%) and elevated silicon levels, which should be considered when selecting processing modes and charge composition depending on the desired alloy characteristics.

Conclusions

During the processing of chromite ores—especially in the smelting of chromium ferroalloys in open furnaces with the use of lime—the formation of hexavalent chromium (Cr VI) is confirmed as a possibility already at the furnace top. This is due to the interaction of chromite spinels (Cr₂FeO₄ and Cr₂MgO₄) with atmospheric oxygen and CaO, and is accompanied by thermodynamically favorable reactions with negative Gibbs free energy change values.The formation of Cr VI during the smelting process itself is unlikely due to the reducing environment; however, its presence in aspiration dust and waste slags indicates post-smelting oxidation of residual chromite phases under exposure to air.Thermodynamic analysis and experimental investigations confirmed the feasibility of reducing both hexavalent and trivalent chromium to safe metallic forms using carbon, sourced from coke or bitumen. The reduction products include chromium (Cr₇C₃) and iron (Fe₃C) carbides, which constitute the structure of carbonaceous ferrochrome. A slag briquetting technology has been developed and successfully tested, using BN-IV grade bitumen that performs a dual function—as a binder and as a reductant. An optimal bitumen content of 10% ensures the necessary briquette strength (≈ 3.5 kN), suitable for further industrial processing. The reductive smelting of these briquettes in a high-temperature furnace effectively reduced Cr VI content in the slag and ensured high chromium recovery into the metal. The obtained alloy contained a high chromium concentration (≈ 74–76%), meeting the standards for carbon ferrochrome.The proposed technology can be recommended for industrial implementation to reduce environmental hazards in chromium ferroalloy production and enable effective utilization of slags containing hexavalent chromium. The implementation of the proposed technology at the Aktobe Ferroalloy Plant will make it possible to utilize the disintegrated low-carbon ferrochrome slag accumulated in the slag dumps, which has a negative impact on the environmental situation in the region. The economic efficiency of the technology will increase due to the use of molten slag as construction aggregate, the additional recovery of chromium and iron, and the reduction of environmental penalties.
