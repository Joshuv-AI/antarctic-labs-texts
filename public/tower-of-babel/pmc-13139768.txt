Auditory Skills and Cognitive Functions in Adult Hearing Aid Users: A Cross‐Sectional Observational Comparison Study
Authors: Mert Kılıç, Eyyup Kara, Alperen Kılıç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate learning and memory functions in adults using hearing aids (HA) and examine their relationship with auditory skills.MethodsThe study group consisted of 32 adults aged 30–59 years with bilateral symmetrical sensorineural hearing loss who had used bilateral HA for at least 1 year. The control group included 32 age‐matched normal‐hearing (NH) individuals. Participants completed the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT), Visual Aural Digit Span Test‐Form B (VADS‐B), Spectral‐Temporally Modulated Ripple Test (SMRT), Random Gap Detection Test (RGDT), Matrix Sentence Test (MST), and Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale (SSQ).ResultsHA users showed significantly poorer suprathreshold auditory performance than controls across all auditory measures (all p < 0.001; ηp 2 ranging from 0.417 to 0.763). However, no overall group differences were observed in memory performance (p = 0.237, ηp 2 = 0.110). Group scores were similar for verbal learning, delayed recall, recognition, and visual short‐term memory (p > 0.05), whereas HA users performed lower only in auditory short‐term memory (p = 0.019, ηp 2 = 0.087), although this finding was not robust after correction for multiple comparisons. Within the HA group, age explained a substantial proportion of variance in auditory–verbal memory performance (R 2 = 0.587, p < 0.001), and auditory abilities provided an additional contribution to the explained variance after controlling for age (ΔR 2 = 0.149). No significant auditory–memory relationships were found in NH adults.ConclusionMemory performance was generally preserved in HA users despite marked auditory processing deficits, with a selective, but exploratory, pattern suggesting a potential vulnerability in auditory short‐term memory. The association between auditory processing and auditory–verbal memory observed only in HA users suggests that degraded auditory input may be associated with differences in memory performance even when audibility is supported by amplification, highlighting the potentially modality‐specific nature of auditory–cognitive interactions.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Hearing loss (HL) has psychophysical, psychosocial, and cognitive consequences, leading to communication difficulties, social isolation, depression, and reduced quality of life [1]. Untreated HL has also been associated with accelerated cognitive decline, impairments in executive function and memory, and an increased risk of dementia [2, 3]. Furthermore, HL has been identified as one of the largest potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia [4].

Evidence regarding the cognitive effects of hearing aid (HA) use remains mixed. Some studies report positive effects across cognitive domains [5, 6, 7, 8], whereas others suggest limited or no impact on cognitive decline [9, 10]. HAs may improve auditory perception by enhancing the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) and reducing listening effort, yet these perceptual benefits do not necessarily translate into improvements in central cognitive processes [11]. Consequently, the dynamic relationships between auditory processing and cognitive functions such as verbal learning and memory in HA users remain incompletely understood [12, 13].

The present study had two primary aims. First, verbal learning, short‐term memory (STM), and long‐term memory (LTM) were evaluated in HA users across auditory and visual modalities and compared with normal‐hearing (NH) adults. Second, the relationships between cognitive functions and auditory abilities—including spectral–temporal resolution, speech perception in noise, spatial perception, and hearing quality—were examined within the HA group. While previous research has largely focused on older adults, cognitive changes related to HL may emerge earlier in midlife [14, 15]. Accordingly, this study investigated auditory–cognitive interactions in younger and middle‐aged adults with HL.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Thirty‐two adults aged 30–59 years with bilateral, symmetrical moderate to moderately severe sensorineural HL who had used bilateral HAs regularly (≥ 8 h/day) for at least 1 year were included as the HA group. Thirty‐two age‐matched NH adults served as controls. Groups did not differ in education level (p = 0.800). All HA users wore receiver‐in‐the‐ear (RITE) devices.

Inclusion criteria for the HA group included normal otoscopic and immittance findings, speech discrimination score (SDS) ≥ 52%, HL duration < 10 years, aided thresholds ≤ 30 dB HL (500–4000 Hz), Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores between 24 and 30, and no retrocochlear pathology, cognitive impairment, neurological, psychiatric, or visual conditions affecting testing (Table 1). Procedures

The study was approved by Istanbul University‐Cerrahpaşa Clinical Research Ethics Committee (23.02.2024/921886), and all participants provided informed consent. Cognitive status was screened using the MMSE, and only individuals scoring within normal limits (24–30) were included. All participants underwent otoscopic examination, immittance testing, pure‐tone audiometry, and speech audiometry prior to enrollment. HA users were evaluated in free‐field under aided conditions, and daily HA use was verified via data logging.

All participants in the HA group were users of bilateral RITE devices fitted in accordance with routine clinical practice. Although HA devices were not standardized across participants in terms of fitting prescription (e.g., NAL‐NL2), real‐ear verification procedures, or signal‐processing features (e.g., noise reduction, directionality), all participants were experienced users with stable device use. All participants were regular HA users with verified daily use and similar aided audibility levels, and testing conditions were strictly standardized to reduce methodological variability. To minimize variability related to testing conditions, all auditory assessments were conducted under strictly standardized free‐field conditions using a single loudspeaker positioned at 0° azimuth and fixed presentation levels.

Auditory skills were assessed using the Spectral‐Temporally Modulated Ripple Test (SMRT), Random Gap Detection Test (RGDT), Matrix Sentence Test (MST), and Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale (SSQ). Cognitive measures included the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) and the Visual Aural Digit Span Test—Form B (VADS‐B).

SMRT

Spectral resolution was assessed using the SMRT [16] in a sound‐treated booth under free‐field conditions. Stimuli were presented via loudspeaker at 65 dB SPL, with participants seated 1 m from the speaker at 0° azimuth. In each trial, three stimuli (two reference and one target) were presented, and participants identified the stimulus that differed from the others. Performance was recorded as ripple per octave (RPO).

RGDT

Temporal resolution was assessed using the RGDT [17]. Stimuli at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz were presented in a sound‐treated booth under free‐field conditions via a loudspeaker positioned 1 m from the participant at 0° azimuth. Stimuli were delivered at 50 dB SL. Participants indicated whether they perceived one or two sounds, and the shortest detectable gap (ms) was recorded for each frequency. A composite RGDT threshold was calculated as the mean across the four frequencies.

MST

Speech perception in noise was assessed using the adaptive MST [18]. Testing was conducted in a sound‐treated booth under free‐field conditions with a loudspeaker positioned 1 m from the participant at 0° azimuth. The noise level was fixed at 65 dB SPL, and the speech level was adaptively varied to determine the speech reception threshold (SRT) in noise, expressed as the SNR for 50% correct recognition.

SSQ

Subjective hearing abilities were assessed using the SSQ [19]. The SSQ evaluates speech perception, spatial perception, and quality of hearing in everyday listening situations. Subscale mean scores were calculated, and an overall SSQ score was obtained by averaging the three subscales.

AVLT

The AVLT [20] was used to evaluate verbal learning and memory. The test assesses STM, verbal learning across repeated trials, and LTM based on both delayed free recall and recognition‐based memory. Participants completed five learning trials (A1–A5), followed by an interference list, immediate recall, delayed recall after a 20‐min interval, and a recognition task. For analysis, the A1–A5 mean score (verbal learning), delayed recall (A7), and recognition scores were used as indices of verbal learning and LTM.

VADS‐B

The VADS‐B [21] was used to assess STM across different input (aural vs. visual) and response (oral vs. written) modalities. The test includes four subtests: Aural‐Oral, Visual‐Oral, Aural‐Written, and Visual‐Written, in which participants recall digit sequences in the same order either verbally or in writing. Two composite scores were analyzed: the Aural Input score (Aural‐Oral + Aural‐Written) as an index of auditory STM and the Visual Input score (Visual‐Oral + Visual‐Written) as an index of visual STM in line with the study objectives.

Conceptual Model

A conceptual model was developed to illustrate the presumed relationships among age, HL severity, aided audibility, suprathreshold auditory processing, and performance on the VADS‐B Aural Input subtest (Figure 2). In this model, HL severity is assumed to influence both aided audibility and suprathreshold processing. Suprathreshold processing was conceptualized as the primary pathway underlying task performance, whereas aided audibility was included as a secondary or potential confounding factor. Age was modeled as influencing both HL and task performance.

This model was not intended to be formally tested using path analysis but rather to provide a theoretical framework for interpreting the findings and to highlight potential sources of confounding, particularly the contribution of audibility to performance on the VADS‐B Aural Input subtest.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1; d = 0.80, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) indicated a required sample of 52 participants. The final sample (N = 64) exceeded this requirement. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v27 (IBM Corp., USA). Data were screened for normality and homogeneity prior to analysis. Group differences in auditory outcomes were examined using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) with age as a covariate. Group differences in auditory outcomes were examined using MANCOVA with age as a covariate. As Box's M indicated violations of covariance homogeneity, Pillai's Trace was used to interpret multivariate effects, followed by univariate analyses to further explore individual outcomes.

Within‐group correlations (Pearson or Spearman) examined associations between auditory and memory measures. Because no significant correlations were observed in the control group, hierarchical regressions were conducted only within the HA group.

To reflect the common underlying processes shared across auditory–verbal memory measures, an auditory–verbal composite score was constructed. This composite included standardized (z‐score) values of VADS‐B Aural Input, AVLT learning (A1–A5), AVLT delayed recall (A7), and AVLT recognition scores. The included measures were selected because they all rely, to varying degrees, on auditory‐verbal encoding and retrieval processes. Standardization was applied to ensure comparability across measures with different score ranges. This approach was adopted to provide an integrated index of auditory–verbal memory performance, reduce dimensionality, and enhance interpretability of the regression models. VADS‐B Visual Input was analyzed separately as an index of visual STM. Hierarchical regression models examined whether auditory measures predicted memory performance beyond age, with age entered in the first step and auditory predictors entered in the second step.

Results

Group Comparisons (Confirmatory Analyses)

A total of 64 participants (32 HA users, 32 controls) were included in the analyses. MANCOVA was conducted to examine group differences across four auditory measures (SMRT, RGDT, MST/SRT‐in‐noise, SSQ), controlling for age. Box's M indicated a violation of covariance homogeneity; therefore, Pillai's Trace was interpreted. The multivariate effect of group was significant, Pillai's Trace = 0.805, F(4,58) = 59.86, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.805, indicating a large overall effect. Follow‐up univariate analyses revealed significant group differences across all auditory outcomes, with the HA group demonstrating poorer spectral resolution (SMRT), F(1,61) = 68.12, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.528; temporal resolution (RGDT), F(1,61) = 43.58, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.417; speech‐in‐noise performance (SRT‐in‐noise), F(1,61) = 113.83, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.651; and self‐reported hearing ability (SSQ), F(1,61) = 196.45, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.763. Bonferroni‐adjusted alpha (0.05/4 = 0.0125) was applied; all univariate effects remained significant (Figure 3).

A second MANCOVA was conducted to examine group differences across five memory outcomes (VADS‐B Aural Input, VADS‐B Visual Input, AVLT A1–A5 mean learning score, AVLT A7 delayed recall, and AVLT recognition), controlling for age. Box's M indicated unequal covariance matrices; therefore, Pillai's Trace was interpreted. The multivariate effect of group was not significant, Pillai's Trace = 0.110, F(5,57) = 1.40, p = 0.237, ηp 2 = 0.110, indicating no overall group differences in memory performance. Given the non‐significant multivariate effect, follow‐up univariate results were considered exploratory and interpreted descriptively. Auditory STM was examined separately due to its direct dependence on auditory input and its expected sensitivity to graded auditory processing in HL. In line with this rationale, this outcome was retained for exploratory, theory‐driven interpretation. Follow‐up univariate analyses revealed a significant group effect only for auditory STM (VADS‐B Aural‐Input), with the HA group scoring lower than controls, F(1,61) = 5.82, p = 0.019, ηp 2 = 0.087. However, this effect (p = 0.019) did not survive Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.05/5 = 0.01). No significant group differences were observed for visual STM (VADS‐B Visual‐Input), F = 0.21, p = 0.649; verbal learning (A1–A5 mean), F = 1.25, p = 0.269; delayed recall (A7), F = 0.40, p = 0.528; or recognition memory, F = 0.26, p = 0.615 (Figure 4).

Within‐Group Associations (Exploratory Analyses)

Within‐group association analyses were conducted separately for the HA group (n = 32) and the control group (n = 32).

Within the control group, correlation analyses showed no significant associations between auditory measures and memory outcomes (all p > 0.05) (Figure 5A). Therefore, regression analyses were not conducted for this group. Within the HA group, correlation analyses indicated multiple statistically significant associations between auditory measures and memory outcomes (p < 0.05) (Figure 5B). This pattern provided the basis for conducting regression analyses exclusively in the HA group.

Group‐based correlation patterns between auditory measures and memory outcomes. Panel A represents the control group, and Panel B represents the hearing aid group. Color scale represents correlation strength (blue = positive, red = negative). Asterisks indicate significant correlations.

Hierarchical regression analyses were conducted within the HA group to examine whether auditory abilities predicted auditory–verbal memory performance after accounting for age‐related variance. In the first step, age emerged as a strong negative predictor, explaining a substantial proportion of variance in memory performance (R 2 = 0.587, p < 0.001). When auditory predictors were added, the overall model remained significant and explained additional variance beyond age alone (ΔR 2 = 0.149). Age continued to be a significant negative predictor. Auditory measures showed positive but non‐significant trends with memory performance (p > 0.05), whereas SRT‐in‐noise did not contribute significantly to the model (Table 2). As a sensitivity analysis, the auditory–verbal composite was recalculated excluding the VADS‐B Aural Input score. When the hierarchical regression model was repeated, auditory predictors showed more pronounced associations with memory performance. Specifically, temporal resolution (RGDT; p = 0.005) and self‐reported hearing ability (SSQ; p = 0.009) emerged as significant predictors, while age remained a significant negative predictor (p = 0.001). In addition, the inclusion of auditory predictors explained a greater proportion of additional variance in the revised model (ΔR 2 = 0.177) than in the original model including VADS‐B Aural Input (ΔR 2 = 0.149). These findings suggest that the relationship between auditory abilities and memory performance became more apparent when the VADS‐B Aural Input component was excluded.

Discussion

The present study compared auditory and memory performance between adults with NH and HA users, focusing on modality‐specific and domain‐specific patterns rather than broad cognitive differences. As expected, the HA group showed substantial deficits in suprathreshold auditory processing—including spectral resolution, temporal resolution, and speech‐in‐noise perception—despite aided audibility being within the normal range. In contrast, multivariate analyses revealed no differences in overall memory performance. Scores were similar between groups for visual STM, verbal learning, delayed recall, and recognition. A significant difference emerged only in auditory STM (VADS‐B Aural Input), suggesting a selective, task‐specific vulnerability under conditions requiring rapid, context‐free auditory encoding and sequential processing.

Consistent with the non‐significant multivariate effect for memory outcomes, AVLT performance did not differ between HA users and NH adults, indicating that verbal learning and LTM abilities (delayed recall and recognition) were largely similar across groups. This finding aligns with research showing that amplification can enhance cognitive performance on tasks involving verbal memory, STM, working memory, and auditory attention [6, 22], suggesting that improved audibility and reduced listening effort may support higher‐level cognitive functioning. In addition, experimental evidence suggests that reducing listening effort may free cognitive resources for higher‐level processing; for example, Sarampalis et al. [23] demonstrated that HA noise reduction can lower cognitive load and improve performance on a secondary task. Longitudinal and cross‐sectional studies have further reported that HA use may be associated with better cognitive outcomes or slower cognitive decline [8, 24, 25]. By contrast, other studies have reported no significant cognitive differences between HA users and non‐users [3, 9, 10, 26]. As the present study is cross‐sectional, causal interpretations cannot be made; however, the lack of group differences across AVLT learning, recall, and recognition scores suggests that higher‐level verbal learning and LTM functions can remain relatively preserved in HA users when audibility is supported by amplification.

The selective deficit observed in auditory STM contrasts with the preserved performance across visual and verbal learning measures, highlighting a modality‐specific vulnerability in HA users. Although amplification improves access to speech cues, the suprathreshold auditory deficits identified in this study—reduced spectral resolution, elevated temporal gap thresholds, and poorer speech‐in‐noise perception—suggest that aided listening still imposes additional perceptual load. Tasks such as VADS‐B Aural Input require rapid encoding of context‐free auditory sequences, which depends on fine‐grained spectral and temporal cues. When these cues remain degraded despite amplification, the auditory system must allocate additional cognitive resources for perceptual analysis, reducing the capacity available for memory encoding. This interpretation aligns with the view that degraded auditory input increases reliance on top‐down mechanisms and elevates cognitive load [27], which may disproportionately impair performance on tasks requiring fast auditory sequencing. Importantly, when applying a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, this effect does not remain statistically significant. Therefore, it should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory in nature. Nevertheless, the observed pattern remains theoretically meaningful, as auditory STM tasks place unique demands on perceptual and cognitive systems under degraded auditory input. As illustrated in the conceptual model (Figure 2), performance on the VADS‐B Aural Input subtest likely reflects both auditory‐perceptual processes and suprathreshold auditory processing. Although aided audibility may contribute to performance, suprathreshold auditory processing emerged as a more prominent factor in the present findings. Importantly, no significant group differences were observed in the other memory measures, suggesting that the observed differences in the VADS‐B Aural Input condition cannot be attributed solely to a general memory deficit. This pattern supports the interpretation that task performance may reflect a combination of perceptual access and suprathreshold auditory processing demands rather than purely auditory or purely memory‐related processes. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the VADS‐B Aural Input subtest as a pure measure of auditory STM. The proposed model should be interpreted as a conceptual framework rather than a formally tested causal model.

The absence of any relationship between auditory predictors and visual STM further reinforces the modality‐specific nature of the cognitive patterns observed in HA users. Visual information is processed through an independent subsystem of working memory—the visuospatial sketchpad—which operates separately from the phonological loop responsible for auditory information [28]. Because visual encoding does not rely on acoustic clarity, it remains unaffected by the spectral or temporal degradation that characterizes aided auditory input. Prior research in multisensory processing also shows that visual cues can support or enhance auditory perception [29, 30, 31, 32], whereas the reverse is not observed, indicating that visual processing remains comparatively stable under auditory degradation. In this context, the preserved visual STM performance in the HA group reflects the robustness of visual processing. In contrast, auditory STM tasks place demands on both perceptual and cognitive systems, making them more vulnerable to the effects of degraded auditory input. Consistent with this, the visual memory model was not significant after auditory predictors were added, indicating no independent contribution of auditory processing to visual STM.

A central finding of the present study concerns the dissociation between groups in the relationship between auditory and memory performance. In NH adults, auditory abilities showed no meaningful association with any memory outcomes, consistent with a system in which perceptual processing imposes minimal cognitive demand. This absence of association may also partially reflect restricted score variability due to ceiling‐level performance on both auditory and memory tasks, limiting the sensitivity to detect correlations in this group. In contrast, multiple significant associations emerged in the HA group, prompting hierarchical regression analyses. These analyses indicated that age accounted for a substantial proportion of variance in the auditory–verbal memory composite, and the independent contributions of individual auditory predictors were modest after age was considered. This pattern suggests that the auditory–memory relationship in HA users may partly reflect shared age‐related variance, while residual auditory processing differences may still contribute in a domain‐specific manner. These findings align with research indicating that processing noise or degraded speech increases memory load [33, 34] and that auditory processing can constrain cognitive performance in adults with HL [35, 36, 37]. The sensitivity analysis further clarified this relationship. When the auditory–verbal composite was recalculated excluding the VADS‐B Aural Input measure, auditory predictors showed stronger and statistically significant associations with memory performance. In particular, temporal resolution (RGDT) and self‐reported hearing ability (SSQ) emerged as significant predictors, and the additional variance explained by auditory measures increased from ΔR 2 = 0.149 to ΔR 2 = 0.177. This pattern suggests that the original composite may have combined partially distinct components, with VADS‐B Aural Input reflecting a more specific auditory STM process that may obscure broader auditory–verbal relationships captured by AVLT‐based measures. When auditory information is degraded, cognitive encoding becomes more dependent on perceptual quality, explaining why memory performance in HA users is more strongly tied to auditory ability than in NH listeners.

Age emerged as a robust predictor, consistent with literature on age‐related declines in processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory [38, 39, 40]. Importantly, although age accounted for a substantial proportion of variance, auditory predictors showed consistent associations in the expected directions, suggesting a modest but domain‐specific contribution of auditory processing beyond age‐related effects. These findings underscore the need to consider age as a covariate in auditory–cognitive research and support the use of age‐controlled multivariate designs in future studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding whether enhanced auditory processing supports better memory performance or whether individuals with stronger cognitive profiles benefit more from HA use. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships. Second, although age was statistically controlled, the modest sample size may limit the detection of small effects and reduce the precision, stability, and statistical power of regression estimates. In addition, alternative covariate specifications (e.g., inclusion of additional demographic variables or models without covariates) were not systematically examined. Given the modest sample size, including multiple covariates could increase the risk of model overfitting and reduce statistical power. Future studies with larger samples should explore the robustness of these findings across different model specifications. Third, detailed information regarding HA fitting parameters and device‐specific signal‐processing features was not consistently available, and HA devices were not standardized across participants. This variability may have contributed to individual differences in auditory performance. Finally, auditory and memory measures in the NH group showed restricted variability, likely contributing to the absence of significant correlations in this group; using more challenging auditory and cognitive tasks in future research may help to minimize ceiling effects. In addition, the VADS‐B Aural Input measure may partly reflect auditory‐perceptual processing demands rather than a pure index of auditory STM, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In summary, HA users demonstrated preserved performance across visual STM, verbal learning, delayed recall, and recognition, with a selective, but exploratory, pattern suggesting vulnerability in auditory STM. Despite marked group differences in suprathreshold auditory processing, no overall group differences in memory performance were observed. In HA users, auditory abilities showed consistent, albeit modest, associations with auditory–verbal memory performance, whereas no such relationships were observed in NH adults. These findings highlight the modality‐specific and perceptually dependent nature of auditory memory processes under degraded input and suggest that auditory STM appears to be particularly sensitive to residual perceptual challenges despite amplification. Incorporating sequential auditory tasks and multimodal strategies into rehabilitation may support memory performance in adults with HL. Future longitudinal and controlled studies are needed to clarify the directionality of auditory–cognitive relationships and the potential long‐term cognitive benefits of HA use.
