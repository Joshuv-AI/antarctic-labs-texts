The digital bioeconomy: microbiomes as a stress test for global digital sequence information governance
Authors: Pierre-Mathieu Pélissier, Indré Karciauskaité, Tanja Kostic, Emmanuelle Maguin, Angela Sessitsch, Kosuke Shiraishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital sequence information governance has entered a high-stakes implementation era. The Convention on Biological Diversity’s ‘Cali Fund’ is operationalised as the financial engine of its multilateral mechanism (MLM), with similar frameworks under negotiation across other international ocean and agricultural treaties. However, because microorganisms and their functional clusters transcend national borders, traditional bilateral mechanisms are difficult to implement. This policy briefing uses microbiomes as a stress test for these evolving MLMs, evaluating three primary challenges: the holobiont ‘data sandwich’, the ‘oblivious user’ awareness gap, and overlapping jurisdictions. By analysing these friction points, we discuss pathways to ensure global MLMs support both innovation and equitable benefit-sharing.

Sustainability statement

The primary focus of this article is UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 17 (Partnerships for the Goals), as it addresses the international governance and cooperation necessary for equitable benefit-sharing in the digital bioeconomy. By focusing on the governance of microbiome and holobiont data, it promotes conservation, sustainable use of biodiversity, and equitable benefit-sharing. It aligns with growing public and private sector efforts to improve soil health, ensure sustainable land use, and enable a fair, innovation-driven bioeconomy. Additionally, this work contributes to the SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), SDG 13 (Climate Action) and SDG 15 (Life on Land).

Introduction

The digital bioeconomy has shifted genetic resource access from physical sampling to global digital sequence information (DSI) exchange. This shift prompted an access-and-benefits sharing mechanisms framework redesign, operationalising the Convention on Biological Diversity’s (CBD) ‘Cali Fund’ as a multilateral mechanism (MLM) to fairly capture data value. Similar frameworks are currently being negotiated under the BBNJ (Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction) agreement and the ITPGRFA (International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture), marking a decisive move away from traditional bilateral negotiations toward a global approach (Scholz et al. 2022).

This transition is critical for the microbial world. High rates of global dispersal and metabolic functions across continents make pinpointing a single sovereign origin biologically impossible (Louca 2022, Flinkstrom et al. 2024), disrupting traditional bilateral mechanisms. MLMs provide a suitable alternative by creating a global pool of benefits irrespective of the geographical source of data. However, as the field shifts toward studying entire communities, we argue that microbiomes raise specific challenges for global MLMs. In this briefing, we subject the current MLM logic to a ‘stress test’ by examining how the unique complexity of microbiomes, functioning as transboundary, multi-jurisdictional ecological units, exposes gaps in existing governance frameworks for complex biological systems.

The holobiont and resulting ‘data sandwich’ challenge

The microbiome field introduces the ‘holobiont data sandwich’ challenge, where value derives from the host organism and its associated microbiome functioning as an integrated ecological unit (Bordenstein 2015). This creates a scenario where host DSI may be subject to national regulations (the Nagoya Protocol), microbial DSI falls under an MLM, while environmental metadata remains in a grey zone. Navigating this overlapping legal landscape is increasingly complex, as the digital bioeconomy requires untangling multiple international frameworks to move from mere access to the functional use of microbial resources (Faggionato et al. 2026a). If a company derives value from the interaction of a microbiome with its hosts, for instance, by developing a microbial coating that only works on the roots of a bilaterally governed wheat variety, would complying solely with the CBD’s MLM for the microbiome DSI be sufficient? Recent guidelines suggest that such multi-layered scenarios often lead to common misconceptions and implementation challenges regarding access and benefit-sharing compliance (Faggionato et al. 2026b).

This raises a question of governance: accounting for microbiomes’ interactions within a holobiome is important to develop fair and equitable MLMs as to not make them structurally blind to synergistic value. A solution could be to consider the host, microbiome, and core environmental parameters to be governed as a single ‘functional unit’, alongside strengthened interoperability between repositories. It is, however, highly complex politically to implement as it would imply modifying existing bilateral protocols, and countries would need to waive their sovereignty on specific hosts and bilateral benefits because it is associated with a microbiome governed by an MLM. To effectively resolve this ‘data sandwich’ while respecting existing sovereignty, MLMs cannot supersede bilateral mechanisms and should hence adapt and adjust to payments already made under other frameworks to avoid triggering a ‘double taxation’ and drive MLM participants away.

Addressing the ‘oblivious user’ awareness gap

Another microbiome-related implementation hurdle for MLMs is the ‘oblivious user’. Many companies using microbiome DSI perceive themselves as data or technology firms rather than ‘biodiversity users’, especially the ones whose business models are not traditionally associated with biodiversity such as in agri-tech, food processing, or waste management. While firms self-associated with biodiversity and/or bioprospecting activities such as TierraViva AI [the first private contributor to the CBD Cali Fund (The Cali Fund 2026)] or BaseCamp Research are engaged in the access-and-benefit sharing debate, the broader industry remains disconnected. This disconnect is exacerbated by the fact that microbiome DSI is often public, and ‘open access’ may be interpreted as ‘freedom from benefit-sharing obligations’. Furthermore, evidence suggests that researchers are also often not aware of these mechanisms during their academic career (Faggionato et al. 2026b), and do not take up new reflexes when switching to the private industry sector. We must debate whether voluntary compliance is sufficient or if the ‘oblivious user’ requires a more rigorous, even automated, enforcement logic. For instance, registration in a database of DSI for the purpose of a patent application could require a certificate of compliance to an MLM. Softer approaches such as education and information, for instance through prompts at the point of data download or analysis might also help tackle this ‘oblivious user’ issue.

Avoiding MLMs stacking penalty

Finally, the borderless nature of microbiomes challenges the spatially defined international treaties. A functional microbiome unit may for instance be found in international waters, triggering the BBNJ MLM, or in coastal waters under the CBD jurisdiction, exposing users to the risk of dual payments. The CBD’s Cali Fund sets contribution thresholds targeting large entities, but overlapping jurisdictional claims across the CBD, BBNJ, and ITPGRFA could complicate compliance obligations and reduce legal certainty for companies developing products based on microbiome-related DSI. Cross-treaty collaboration would simplify users’ participation and reinforce their legal certainty, a key requisite for large corporations to participate. The community should explore streamlined compliance pathways, for instance a DSI clearing house serving as a single point of financial compliance, in charge of redistributing funds to the different MLMs and removing these tasks from the user. Such approaches merit further examination as potential mechanisms for reducing transaction costs, increasing transparency, and improving participation in future DSI governance systems.

Conclusion

MLMs represent a shift from bilateral friction toward a global pool of benefits, a necessity since microorganisms do not respect national borders and functional clusters are often transboundary. By using the microbiome as a ‘stress test’, the global community can refine MLMs into robust engines for both innovation and fairness. Highly functioning MLMs provide financial means, but also nonmonetary benefits that can support innovation in biodiversity custodian countries, not only by supporting data uploading but also by valorising data directly on site. As we approach COP17, the focus must shift to the practical implementation of a system that provides legal certainty for innovators while ensuring the digital bioeconomy supports the stewards of our biological heritage.
