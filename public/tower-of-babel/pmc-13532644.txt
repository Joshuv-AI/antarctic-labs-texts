Unilateral Scleral Icterus as a Rare Presentation of Choroidal Metastasis in Transdifferentiated EGFR-Mutant Lung Cancer: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Oz Levinkron, Thubeena Manickavasagar, Sanjay Popat, Richard Scawn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnilateral scleral icterus is an extremely rare clinical finding and is not typically associated with systemic hyperbilirubinemia. We report the case of a 28-year-old man with biopsy-proven transdifferentiated neuroendocrine lung carcinoma arising from EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), who presented with painless, localized yellow discoloration of the temporal sclera of the right eye without systemic features of jaundice. Ophthalmic examination revealed reduced visual acuity in the affected eye and an elevated temporal chorio-retinal mass. B-scan ultrasonography demonstrated a dome-shaped lesion measuring 6 mm in thickness with moderate internal reflectivity. Laboratory investigations, including serum bilirubin and liver enzymes, were normal, and systemic imaging showed no hepatic or biliary pathology. The patient was diagnosed with choroidal metastasis and treated with urgent external beam radiotherapy followed by systemic chemotherapy. The unilateral scleral icterus was attributed to localized xanthochromic changes related to the choroidal metastasis. This case highlights unilateral scleral icterus as a rare ocular sign of choroidal metastasis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which choroidal metastasis—specifically from transdifferentiated neuroendocrine lung carcinoma—presented with unilateral scleral yellowing. Previously published cases of unilateral scleral icterus have been associated with subconjunctival or suprachoroidal hemorrhage, prior ocular surgery, localized facial malignancy, or treatment-related vascular changes rather than metastatic infiltration. This report expands the differential diagnosis of unilateral scleral discoloration and underscores the importance of prompt ophthalmologic evaluation in oncology patients with new ocular findings, even in the absence of visual symptoms or biochemical evidence of jaundice. It also emphasizes the value of multidisciplinary collaboration between ophthalmology and oncology in recognizing atypical manifestations of advanced malignancy.

He commenced Osimertinib, remaining progression-free for 16 months. At progression, treatment was switched to Carboplatin-Pemetrexed with Amivantamab (a bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET). After six cycles, he had further multifocal lung progression and received palliative radiotherapy to the left hilar lymph node. He commenced third line treatment with Atezolizumab, Bevacizumab, Carboplatin and Paclitaxel. Eight months later, new liver metastases were detected. Liver biopsy demonstrated small cell differentiation, with persistent EGFR 19del and TP53 mutation on tissue NGS, consistent with histological transdifferentiation.

He received two cycles of gemcitabine and carboplatin before further progression in the liver and bones, prompting a switch to cyclophosphomide, doxorubicin and vincristine (CAV).

After one cycle of CAV, he developed unilateral icterus and was referred for ophthalmologic assessment.

Management

The patient received urgent external beam radiotherapy to the right eye (20 Gy in 5 fractions) and continued CAV chemotherapy with no clinical evidence of progression at this site.

Discussion

Unilateral Scleral Icterus: Summary of Previously Reported Cases

Scleral icterus typically reflects systemic hyperbilirubinemia, as bilirubin deposits in elastin-rich scleral tissue when serum levels exceed approximately 34–51 µmol/L (2–3 mg/dL). Because hyperbilirubinemia is systemic, scleral discoloration is almost invariably bilateral and due to prehepatic, hepatic and post hepatic causes.

Reports of unilateral scleral icterus are rare, with 14 reported in a recent literature review.1 Tissues with a high elastin content such as the sclera or conjunctiva appear to have a particular predilection for bilirubin deposition.2,3 These reports underscore that scleral discoloration need not always reflect systemic hepatic dysfunction; rather, local vascular alterations or tissue changes may mimic icterus. Importantly, most previously described cases still occurred in the setting of systemic hyperbilirubinemia or local vascular phenomena. Purely local tumour-related causes are extremely uncommon.4

Ocular Metastases in Lung Cancer

The choroid is the most common ocular site for metastatic disease due to its high vascularity and blood flow, facilitating hematogenous tumour cell seeding.5 Choroidal metastases account for the majority of intraocular metastatic lesions—estimated at 80–90% in large reviews—and lung cancer is among the leading primary tumours responsible, particularly in male patients.5

Lung cancer accounts for roughly 21–29% of choroidal metastases in aggregated oncology series, second only to breast carcinoma in incidence.5 Unlike breast metastases, choroidal metastasis from lung cancer more often presents unilaterally and uni-focally, and frequently exhibits a yellow-cream coloured lesion on fundus examination with associated subretinal fluid or detachment.5 In reported cases, ocular metastases may present with visual symptoms including decreased acuity, floaters, photopsia, or fields defects, or may even be an initial clinical indicator of occult systemic malignancy.6 Indeed, case reports highlight choroidal metastases as the first sign of small cell lung cancer, emphasizing the importance of ocular evaluation in patients with new visual symptoms.6

The incidence of clinically evident choroidal metastases among patients with metastatic lung cancer is variable; autopsy and screening studies suggest prevalence may range from under 1% to over 7%, though clinically apparent cases are rarer.7 Despite being an uncommon manifestation, choroidal involvement correlates with advanced systemic disease and often a poor prognosis. In the present case, the development of ocular metastasis may reflect prolonged survival achieved through multiple lines of systemic anticancer therapy, thereby permitting the emergence of metastatic spread to less common anatomical sites.

Aetiology of Unilateral Scleral Icterus

This case differs from classical scleral icterus with normal serum bilirubin and liver function tests, the absence of systemic jaundice yet with evidence of localised and sectoral discoloration.

Unilateral icterus aetiology can be divided into two pathophysiology categories. The first group is patients with elevated serum bilirubin coupled with local anatomical factors such as radiation lymphatic compromise or stroke induced vascular impairment that limit bilirubin deposition on one side or a unilateral conjunctivitis in with hepatitis patient causing increased bilirubin deposition on the conjunctivitis side.

The second group has normal serum bilirubin with unusual unilateral ophthalmic choroid pathology causing xanthochromic fluid deposition following choroidal detachment, suprachoroidal hemorrhage or the one other reported previous case of choroidal metastasis causing unilateral icterus.8–13

The likely mechanism of icterus in our case was either haemorrhage within the metastasis and xanthochromic breakdown or, perhaps less likely given the normal bilirubin levels, a metastasis induced choroidal effusion with extravasation of bilirubin into the conjunctiva. Rather than bilirubin deposition, the “icterus-like” appearance likely reflects tumour-related structural or vascular changes. Despite the patient receiving immunotherapy, this presentation is not immune-mediated and did not complicate the radiotherapy treatment received for management of the choroidal metastasis.

This case is of interest given the rarity of this presentation and underlying cause of intraocular metastasis, expanding the differential diagnosis of unilateral scleral icterus in published case reports. In oncology patients, jaundice reflexively prompts investigation for hepatobiliary dysfunction, particularly in the context of liver metastases. This case demonstrates that normal bilirubin does not exclude malignant causes for scleral discoloration. The patient’s young age and history of EGFR-mutant NSCLC with small-cell trans-differentiation represent a biologically aggressive disease phenotype. Ocular metastasis in this molecular context is not well characterized in current literature, expanding the clinical phenotype of metastatic neuroendocrine transformation.

This unusual case underscores the importance of urgent ophthalmologic evaluation of new unilateral ocular discoloration in cancer patients, even in the absence of visual complaints.

Conclusion

Unilateral scleral icterus is an uncommon presentation. Early recognition allows timely imaging and radiotherapy, potentially preserving vision and improving quality of life. This case highlights the need for joint expertise of oncology and ophthalmology specialists in the diagnosis and management asymmetric icterus.
