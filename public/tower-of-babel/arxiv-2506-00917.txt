Q-learning with Posterior Sampling
Priyank Agrawal, Shipra Agrawal, Azmat Azati

Abstract
Bayesian posterior sampling techniques have demonstrated superior empirical performance in many exploration-exploitation settings. However, their theoretical analysis remains a challenge, especially in complex settings like reinforcement learning. In this paper, we introduce Q-Learning with Posterior Sampling (PSQL), a simple Q-learning-based algorithm that uses Gaussian posteriors on Q-values for exploration, akin to the popular Thompson Sampling algorithm in the multi-armed bandit setting. We show that in the tabular episodic MDP setting, PSQL achieves a regret bound of O ~ ​ ( H 2 ​ S ​ A ​ T ) \tilde{O}(H^{2}\sqrt{SAT}) , closely matching the known lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( H ​ S ​ A ​ T ) \Omega(H\sqrt{SAT}) . Here, S, A denote the number of states and actions in the underlying Markov Decision Process (MDP), and T = K ​ H T=KH with K K being the number of episodes and H H being the planning horizon. Our work provides several new technical insights into the core challenges in combining posterior sampling with dynamic programming and TD-learning-based RL algorithms, along with novel ideas for resolving those difficulties. We hope this will form a starting point for analyzing this efficient and important algorithmic technique in even more complex RL settings.

Introduction

In a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem, an agent interacts sequentially with an unknown environment and uses the observed outcomes to learn an interaction strategy. The underlying mathematical model for RL is a Markov Decision Process (MDP). In the tabular episodic RL setting, there is an underlying MDP with a finite state space
{\cal S}
, a finite action space
{\cal A}
and a planning horizon
H
. On taking an action
a
in state
s
at step
h
, the environment produces a reward and next state from the (unknown) reward model
R_{h}(s,a)
and transition probability model
P_{h}(s,a)
of the underlying MDP.

Q-learning
Watkins and Dayan 1992
is a classic dynamic programming (DP)-based algorithm for RL.
The DP equations (aka Bellman equations) provide a recursive expression for the optimal expected reward achievable from any state and action of the MDP, aka the
Q
-values, in terms of the optimal value achievable in the next state.
Specifically, for any given
s\in{\cal S},a\in{\cal A},h\in[H]
, the Q-value
Q_{h}(s,a)
is given by:

Q_{h}(s,a)=\max_{a\in{\cal A}}R_{h}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in{\cal S}}P_{h}(s,a,s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime}),\text{ with}

(1)

\textstyle V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{a^{\prime}\in{\cal A}}Q_{h+1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Here, the terminal value
V_{H+1}(s)
is
0
for all
s
. The optimal action in state
s
is then given by the argmax action in the above.

In the reinforcement learning setting, where the reward and transition models of the MDP are unknown, the
Q
-learning algorithm uses the celebrated Temporal Difference (TD) learning idea
Sutton 1988
to construct increasingly accurate estimates of
Q
-values using past observations.
The key idea here is to construct an estimate of the right hand side of the Bellman equation, aka
target
, by
bootstrapping
the current estimate
\widehat{V}_{h+1}
for the next step value function. That is, on playing an action
a
in state
s
at step
h
, and observing reward
r_{h}
and next state
s^{\prime}
, the target
z
is typically constructed as:

z\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=r_{h}+\widehat{V}_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

And then the estimate
{\widehat{Q}}_{h}(s,a)
for
Q_{h}(s,a)
is updated to fit the Bellman equations using the
Q-learning update rule
1

1

Or,
\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)\leftarrow\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)+\alpha_{n}(z-\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a))

z-\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)

\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{n})\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)+\alpha_{n}z.

(2)

Here

is an important parameter of the
Q
-learning algorithm, referred to as the learning rate. It is typically a function of the number of previous visits
n

There are several ways to interpret the Q-learning update rule. The traditional frequentist interpretation popularized by
Mnih et al. 2015
interprets this update as a gradient descent step for a least squares regression error minimization problem. Another, arguably more insightful, interpretation of
Q
-learning can be obtained using Bayesian inference theory. Specifically, if we assume a Gaussian prior

\textstyle{\cal N}(\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a),\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n-1})

for the target

\textstyle{\cal N}(\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a),\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}),\qquad\qquad\text{ where }\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{n})\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)+\alpha_{n}z

(3)

(See Section
3.1
for further details.)

Importantly, the Bayesian posterior tracks not just the mean but also the variance or uncertainty in the
Q
-value estimate.
This uncertainty quantification is particularly useful for managing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff for regret minimization in reinforcement learning.

Intuitively, the state and actions with a small number of past visits (i.e., small
n
) have large uncertainty in their current
Q
-value estimate, and should be explored more. The posterior sampling approaches implement this idea by simply taking a sample from the posterior, which is likely to be closer to the mean (less exploration) for actions with small posterior variance, and away from the mean (more exploration) for those with large variance.

Following this intuition, we introduce Q-learning with Posterior Sampling (PSQL) algorithm that maintains a posterior on Q-values for every state and action. Then, to decide an action in any given state, it simply generates a sample from the posterior for each action, and plays the arg max action of the sampled
Q
-values.

Our setting and main result

We consider an online reinforcement learning setting. In this setting, the algorithm interacts with environment in
K
sequential episodes, each containing
H
steps. At step
h=1,\ldots,H
of each episode
k
, the algorithm observes the current state
s_{k,h}
, takes an action
a_{k,h}
and observes a reward
r_{k,h}
and the next state
s_{k,h+1}
.

The reward and next state are generated by the environment according to a fixed underlying MDP
({\cal S},{\cal A},R,P)
, so that
\Pr(s_{k,h+1}=s^{\prime}|s_{k,h}=s,a_{k,h}=a)=P_{h}(s,a,s^{\prime}),\mathbb{E}[r_{k,h}|s_{k,h}=s,a_{k,h}=a]=R_{h}(s,a)
. However, the reward functions and the transition probability distributions
R_{h},P_{h},h=1,\ldots,H
are apriori unknown to the algorithm. The goal is to minimize total regret compared to the optimal value given by the dynamic programming equation (
1
). Specifically, let
\pi_{k}
denote the policy used by the algorithm in episode
k
, so that
a_{k,h}=\pi^{k}(s_{k,h})
. We aim to bound regret, defined as

\textstyle\text{Reg}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(V_{1}(s_{k,1})-V^{\pi_{k}}(s_{k,1}))

(4)

for any set of starting states
s_{k,1},k=1,\ldots,K
.

A regret minimizing algorithm uses past observations to learn about the MDP and improve its actions.
Since the algorithm can only observe the environment’s response at a visited state and action, a main challenge in this problem is managing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. This refers to the dilemma between picking actions that are most likely to be optimal according to the observations made so far, versus picking actions for that allow visiting less-explored states and actions. Following work on managing exploration-exploitation tradeoffs in multi-armed bandit (MAB) settings, two main approaches for exploration exploitation are optimistic approaches based on UCB and posterior sampling approaches (aka Thompson Sampling in MAB).

In this paper, we present a Q-learning algorithm with posterior sampling (PSQL) that achieves the following regret bound. Here
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides absolute constants and logarithmic factors.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

The cumulative regret of our PSQL (Algorithm
1
) in
K
episodes with horizon
H
is bounded as

\text{Reg}(K)\leq\tilde{O}\mathinner{\left(H^{2}\sqrt{SAT}\right)},
where
T=KH
.

Next, we derive the posterior sampling approach considered in this paper, and relate it to the other popular versions of Q-learning for regret minimization in RL.

Algorithm design

We present our Q-learning with Posterior Sampling (PSQL) algorithm. We first present a Bayesian posterior-based derivation of the Q-learning update rule, which forms the basis for our algorithm design.

3.1
Posterior derivation

An insightful interpretation of the Q-learning update rule can be obtained using Bayesian inference. Let
\theta
denote the Bayesian parameter that we are inferring, which in our case is the quantity
Q_{h}(s,a)
. Given a prior
p
on
\theta
, log likelihood function
\ell(\theta,\cdot)
, and a sample
z
, the Bayesian posterior
q
is given by the Bayes rule:

q(\theta)\propto p(\theta)\cdot\exp(\ell(\theta,z))

(5)

which can also be derived as an optimal solution of the following optimization problem (see Chapter 10 in
Bishop and Nasrabadi 2006
), whose objective is commonly referred to as Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

\max_{q}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim q}[\ell(\theta,z)]-KL(q||p)

(6)

where
KL(\cdot||\cdot)=\int_{\theta}q(\theta)\log(\frac{q(\theta)}{p(\theta)})
denotes KL-divergence function.
It is well known that when
p(\cdot)
is Gaussian, say
{\cal N}(\widehat{\mu},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n-1})
, and the likelihood given
\theta
is Gaussian
{\cal N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
, then the posterior
q(\cdot)
is given by the Gaussian distribution

\textstyle{\cal N}(\widehat{\mu},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}),\text{ with }\widehat{\mu}\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{n})\widehat{\mu}+\alpha_{n}z

(7)

with
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
. Therefore, substituting
\theta
as
Q_{h}(s,a)
and
\widehat{\mu}
as
\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)
, the above yields the Q-learning learning update rule (
2
) with learning rate
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
.

A caveat is that the above assumes
z
to be an unbiased sample from the target distribution, whereas in Q-learning,
z
is biased due to bootstrapping. In a recent work,
Jin et al. 2018
observed that in order to account for this bias and obtain theoretical guarantees for Q-learning, the learning rate needs to be adjusted to
\alpha_{n}=\frac{H+1}{H+n}
. In fact, Bayesian inference can also provide a meaningful interpretation of this modified learning rate proposed in
Jin et al. 2018
.
Consider the following “regularized" Bayesian inference problem
Khan and Rue 2023
which adds an entropy term to the ELBO objective in (
6
):

\max_{q}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim q}[\ell(\theta,z)]-KL(q||p)+\lambda_{n}{\cal H}(q)

(8)

where
{\cal H}(q)
denotes the entropy of the posterior. Then, we show (Lemma
7
) that with weight
\lambda_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{H}{n}
, when the prior
p(\theta)
is Gaussian
{\cal N}(\widehat{\mu},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n-1})
, and the likelihood of
z
given
\theta
is Gaussian
{\cal N}(\theta,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{H+1})
, then the posterior
q(\cdot)
is given by the Gaussian distribution
in (
7
), but
with the learning rate
\alpha_{n}=\frac{H+1}{H+n}
suggested in
Jin et al. 2018
.
A derivation is provided in Appendix
B
.

Intuitively, since we suspect bias in the target
z
, (
8
) introduces additional uncertainty in the posterior via entropy. The weight
\lambda_{n}
of this entropy term decreases as the number of samples increases and the bootstrapped target is expected to have lower bias. This derivation may be of independent interest as it provides a more intuitive explanation of the modified learning rate schedule proposed in
Jin et al. 2018
, where it was motivated mainly by the mechanics of regret analysis.

Substituting
\theta
as
Q_{h}(s,a)
and
\widehat{\mu}
has
\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)
in (
8
), we derive that given a Gaussian prior
{\cal N}(\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a),\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n-1})
over
Q_{h}(s,a)
, Gaussian likelihood
{\cal N}(Q_{h}(s,a),\frac{\sigma^{2}}{H+1})
on target
z
, the following posterior maximizes the regularized ELBO objective:

\textstyle{\cal N}(\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a),\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}),\,\text{where}\qquad\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{n})\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)+\alpha_{n}z,

(9)

where
\alpha_{n}=\frac{H+1}{H+n}
, and
n
is the number of samples for
s,a
observed so far.

The above posterior derivation forms the basis of our algorithm design presented next.

\displaystyle\mbox{ with }\textstyle J=J(\delta)\coloneqq\frac{\log(SAT/\delta)}{\log(1/(1-p_{1}))},\quad p_{1}=\Phi(-1)-\frac{\delta}{H}-\delta^{\prime}.

(11)

Then,

\overline{V}_{h+1}(s^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\max_{j\in[J]}\tilde{V}^{j},\mbox{ and }z=r+\overline{V}(s^{\prime}).

Observe that the above procedure computes a
\overline{V}_{h+1}(s^{\prime})
that is more optimistic than single sample max (
\max_{a^{\prime}}\tilde{Q}_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
) but not as optimistic as multiple sample max (
\max_{a^{\prime}}\max_{j\in[J]}\tilde{Q}_{h}^{j}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
). Here,
\tilde{Q}_{h}^{j}
denotes
j
th sample from the distribution of
\tilde{Q}_{h}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
. The importance and rationale of this target computation procedure will be further explained in regret analysis (Section
4
).

Finally, the algorithm uses the computed target
z
to update the posterior mean via the Q-learning update rule, with
\alpha_{n}=\frac{H+1}{H+n}
:

\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)\leftarrow(1-\alpha_{n})\widehat{Q}_{h}(s,a)+\alpha z.

4.1
Challenges and techniques.

Q-learning with posterior sampling poses several unique challenges for theoretical analysis.

Challenges under vanilla posterior sampling.

A key difficulty in analyzing Bayesian posterior sampling algorithms is the absence of high probability optimism (i.e., the property that the estimates upper bound the true parameters). To appreciate the importance of optimism, observe that the regret of an algorithm over
K
episodes can be decomposed into two buckets:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathinner{\left(V^{*}_{1}(s_{k,1})-V^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(s_{k,1})\right)}\leq\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(V^{*}_{1}(s_{k,1})-\tilde{Q}_{k,1}(s_{k,1},a_{k,1}))}_{\text{Optimism error}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\tilde{Q}_{k,1}(s_{k,1},a_{k,1})-V^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(s_{k,1}))}_{\text{Estimation error}}.

(12)

In optimistic algorithms like UCBQL
Jin et al. 2018
, there is no optimism error since the UCB estimate is a high-confidence upper bound on the optimal value function. For posterior sampling, in the
N
-armed bandit setting,
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
made a breakthrough by showing that optimism holds with constant probability in exploration phase, and is sufficient to bound the regret. However, the recursive nature of Q-learning update makes achieving even a constant probability of optimism non-trivial in Q-learning.
To see this intuitively, suppose that if we have a constant probability
p
of optimism of posteriors on value functions in state
H
. Then, due to the recursive nature of
Q
-value update, the optimism of stage
H-1
value requires optimism of stage
H
value; leading to
p^{2}
probability of optimism in stage
H-1
. Continuing this way, we get an exponentially small probability of optimism for stage
1
.

Challenges under optimistic posterior sampling.

To get around the issues with constant probability optimism, many posterior sampling algorithms (e.g., model-based PSRL
Agrawal and Jia 2022
, MNL-bandit
Agrawal et al. 2017
,
Tiapkin et al. 2022
etc.) taking max over multiple (say
J
) samples from the posterior in order to get high probability optimism. This modification is sometimes referred to as optimistic posterior sampling.
Unfortunately, in the Q-learning setting, we believe that such optimistic sampling would lead to exponential accumulation of errors, even for
J
as small as
2
. Below, we provide a rough intuitive argument.

To see this, suppose that the posterior on
Q
-value
\tilde{Q}_{H}
for stage
H
has a variance
\sigma^{2}
and we take max over
J
samples from this posterior to construct value function estimates
\tilde{V}_{H}
. Then, standard techniques can be used to show that
\tilde{V}_{H}
will be optimistic with high probability, and will have an error bound of
\sigma\sqrt{J\log(1/\delta)}
with probability
1-\delta
.

Now, since this value function estimate
\tilde{V}_{H}
will be used to construct the target for stage
H-1
and update
\widehat{Q}_{H-1}
, this will lead to a posterior
mean estimation error
of
\sigma\sqrt{J\log(1/\delta)}
in
\widehat{Q}_{H-1}
with probability
1-\delta
.
Then, in order to get even a
constant probability optimism
, the posterior of stage
H-1
needs to have enough variance to overcome the mean estimation error, so the variance of
\tilde{Q}_{H-1}
will need to be inflated to
\sigma^{2}J
. And then, for high probability optimism, we need to take
J
samples from this
inflated variance
posterior, so that the error bound on
\tilde{V}_{H-1}
will be
\sigma\sqrt{J^{2}\log(1/\delta)}
with probability
1-\delta
. Continuing this argument, we get that at stage
1
, the error bound for
\tilde{V}_{1}
will be of the order
\sigma\sqrt{J^{H}\log(1/\delta)}
, i.e., exponential in
H
for any
J\geq 2
. To summarize, the usual trick of obtaining high probability optimism by taking multiple samples from the posterior doesn’t work for Q-learning, at least not without further novel ideas.

Our techniques.

To handle the above-mentioned challenges, our algorithm uses a combination of vanilla (single-sample) and optimistic (multiple-sample) posterior sampling for action selection and target computation, respectively. See Algorithm
1
,
2
for details.

A key observation utilized here is that to break the recursive multiplicative decay in constant probability of optimism, we just need to ensure high probability optimism of the next-stage value function estimate used in the target computation. So, perhaps a solution would be to use optimistic posterior sampling in target computation
only
. That is, in the target computation, compute the next stage value function estimate as argmax over
multiple samples
from the posterior on Q-values.
However, now we run into the problem that the played action (single sample argmax of Q-values) may not match the action used in the target computation (multiple sample argmax). This poses difficulties in controlling the target estimation error, since the action used in the target computation may not have enough plays, and therefore, its variance may not decrease fast enough.

The final piece of the puzzle in our algorithm design and analysis is to use the argmax action
\widehat{a}
of the posterior mean in our target computation. (For details, see Algorithm
2
.) In our regret analysis, we show that this action
\widehat{a}
either matches the played action with constant probability, or has a smaller variance (Lemma
5
). In the next subsection, we provide a proof sketch that formally puts together all these ideas to obtain our main regret bound.

Proof sketch

We provide a proof sketch for Theorem

(b) Bounding estimation error.

In Q-learning, an estimate of the next stage value function (here,

This is essentially captured by the following lemma.

Lemma 4

(Posterior mean estimation error)

With probability at least

\displaystyle\textstyle\widehat{Q}_{k,h}(s,a)-Q^{*}_{h}(s,a)\leq\sqrt{\sigma(N_{k,h}(s,a))^{2}\eta}+\alpha_{n}^{0}H+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{n}^{i}\mathinner{\left(\overline{V}_{k_{i},h+1}(s_{k_{i},h+1})-V^{*}_{h+1}(s_{k_{i},h+1})\right)},

For a given

where
p_{2}=\Phi(-1)-(A+2)\delta
, and
\delta
as defined in Theorem

.

Lemma 6

(Cumulative estimation error.)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds for all

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathinner{\left(\tilde{Q}_{k,h}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h})-V^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(s_{k,h})\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\mathinner{\left(H^{2}\sqrt{SAT}\log(JSAT/\delta)\right)}.

(c) Putting it all together.

To obtain the regret bound in Theorem

5
Conclusion

We presented a posterior sampling-based approach for incorporating exploration in Q-learning. Our PSQL algorithm is derived from an insightful Bayesian inference framework and shows promising empirical performance in preliminary experiments. (Detailed experimental setup and empirical results on additional environments are provided in Appendix
A
.) We proved a
\tilde{O}(H^{2}\sqrt{SAT})
regret bound in a tabular episodic RL setting that closely matches the known lower bound.
Future directions include a theoretical analysis of the vanilla version of PSQL that uses a single sample from next stage posterior in the target computation. The vanilla version outperforms Algorithm
1
empirically but is significantly harder to analyze. Another avenue is tightening the
H
dependence in the regret bound; Appendix
F
outlines a sketch for improving it by
\sqrt{H}
although at the expense of making the algorithm more complex. Further refinements are potentially achievable using techniques from
Li et al. 2021a
;
Zhang et al. 2020
.

Broader impact statement.

Our research goal is to make reinforcement learning algorithms more efficient. Although the work is primarily theoretical, the insights provided here could help guide the design of efficient RL systems. Since the research is focused on foundational methods and does not involve any real-world deployment, we do not expect any negative societal impacts.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{h=1}^{H}c(\sqrt{\frac{H}{n+1}\cdot(\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{n}\overline{V}_{h+1}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h})+H)\eta}+\frac{\sqrt{H^{7}SA\eta}\cdot\tau}{n+1}),

(46)

Further,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sqrt{\frac{H}{n+1}\cdot(\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{n}\overline{V}_{k,h+1}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h})+H)}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sqrt{\frac{H}{n+1}\cdot\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{n}\overline{V}_{k,h+1}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h})}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sqrt{\frac{H^{2}}{n+1}}

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
