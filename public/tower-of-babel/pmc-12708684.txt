Prothrombotic fibrin clot properties are associated with lower live births rate in women with obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome: a cohort study
Authors: Magdalena Piróg, Michał Ząbczyk, Joanna Natorska, Robert Jach, Anetta Undas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFormation of compact plasma fibrin clots with impaired lysability was observed in obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome (OAPS). Whether unfavorably modified fibrin clot properties contribute to future obstetric complications in OAPS remains unknown.ObjectivesTo investigate whether plasma fibrin clot phenotype is associated with pregnancy outcomes in OAPS.MethodsIn patients with OAPS and controls matched for age, body mass index (BMI), and type of obstetric complications (both n = 62) fibrin clot permeability (Ks) and clot lysis time (CLT), thrombin activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), plasminogen, α2-antiplasmin and endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) were determined. Pregnancy and live births were recorded during a median follow-up period of 54 months.ResultsAt baseline, obstetric complication rates did not differ between the groups. During follow-up, pregnancy was confirmed in 72.6% of women with OAPS and 71% of controls, with identical proportions of live births (51.6%; n = 32). Among OAPS, women with live births compared to women without live births had higher baseline Ks (+ 18.2%, p < 0.0001) without any difference in CLT. Baseline Ks ≤6.1 × 10− 9cm2 discriminated lower live birth rates in OAPS. Pregnant women with OAPS had higher ETP (+ 5.1%) and TAFI (+ 14.4%), along with lower plasminogen (-6%) and α2-antiplasmin (-6.7%) compared with pregnant controls. Women with live births showed slightly higher ETP (+ 1.5%) and increased TAFI level (+ 12.0%) with decreased plasminogen (-10%), and α2-antiplasmin (-10%) compared to the controls with live births (all p < 0.05).ConclusionProthrombotic fibrin clot properties are observed in women with OAPS with lower live birth rate, suggesting a potential role of fibrin-related mechanisms in this manifestation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00296-025-06061-5.Keywords: Fibrin, Fibrinolysis, Antiphospholipid syndrome, Pregnancy

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by the persistent presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [1], associated with increased risk of thromboembolic events (TE) [2, 3], along with obstetric complications (OAPS) such as recurrent miscarriages, fetal death, preeclampsia (PEC), and placental insufficiency (PI) below 34 weeks of pregnancy [4, 5].

The underlying pathophysiology of APS is explained by the “two-hit” model, where first specific aPL-dependent antigenic reaction induces endothelial dysfunction, and the second, environmental trigger leads to its clinical manifestation. In OAPS defective spiral arterial remodeling, mediated mostly by increased anti-ß2-glycoprotein I (aß2GPI) production, lead to impaired trophoblast proliferation, migration and invasion, and, consequently, abnormal early placentation [6, 7]. The standard therapeutic approach to OAPS consists of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) combined with low-dose aspirin (ASA), although it fails in approximately 20–30% of patients [8]. Emerging data further indicate that hydroxychloroquine may improve obstetric outcomes in women with OAPS [9].

In thrombotic APS, a prothrombotic fibrin clot phenotype, characterized by the formation of compact fibrin networks with reduced plasma clot permeability (Ks) and impaired susceptibility to fibrinolysis as reflected by prolonged clot lysis time (CLT), has been consistently reported [10, 11]. Longer lysis time is partially determined by increased levels of endogenous fibrinolytic inhibitors, namely plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1 (PAI-1) and thrombin activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI). This unfavorable fibrin architecture is thought to contribute to thromboembolic events [11]. Recent evidence indicates that a similar fibrin clot phenotype is present in women with OAPS [12], suggesting that altered clot structure and function may play a role not only in thrombosis but also in pregnancy-related complications. Since adverse pregnancy outcomes can occur in OAPS despite guideline-recommended antithrombotic therapy, a better understanding of underlying clot abnormalities may help identify women at persistent risk.

Therefore, we hypothesized that OAPS patients with a more compact and less lysable fibrin clot phenotype may remain at increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes despite standard treatment. Our aim was to evaluate the associations between fibrin clot porosity and susceptibility to lysis and subsequent pregnancy outcomes in women with OAPS during long-term follow-up.

Methods

Patients

Between January 2014 and March 2017 at the Department of Thromboembolic Disorders, Institute of Cardiology in Krakow, Poland, we enrolled women with pregnancy loss, with suspicion of APS. The study population and design have been presented in detail recently [12]. Briefly, all participants were enrolled in the study at least 3 months after the last obstetric complication including recurrent miscarriages, fetal death, preeclampsia (PE), and placental insufficiency (PI) defined according to standard definitions [13]. The control group was selected from women free of OAPS, matched for age, body mass index (BMI), and type of obstetric complications, which were diagnosed in our center during the same time.

Exclusion criteria included condition with increased risk of TE, including prior TE (documented pulmonary embolism, deep-vein thrombosis, ischemic stroke, peripheral artery embolism or myocardial infarction), exacerbation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), C-reactive protein (CRP) > 10 mg/L, acute infection, known cancer, hormonal contraceptives, pregnancy, kidney failure (serum creatinine ≥ 120 µM), severe liver injury, anemia (hemoglobin < 10 g/dL) inherited thrombophilia, and current anticoagulation.

We collected demographics, data on risk factors, along with comorbidities. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) was diagnosed based on the American College of Rheumatology classification criteria [14]. Comorbidities were defined as described [12].

The Ethics Committee at Jagiellonian University Medical College approved the study, and participants provided informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (December 2024 version).

APS diagnosis

OAPS was diagnosed based on one laboratory and one clinical criterium for the disease [5]. All aPL were determined twice: at least 3 months after the end of pregnancy and at least 12 weeks apart to exclude clinically unimportant or transient antibodies [15]. LA was assessed as recommended [15].

Levels of IgG and IgM isotypes of aCL, aß2GPI along with antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin (aPS/PT) antibodies were measured with the ELISA kits (INOVA San Diego, CA, US). Women with OAPS were divided into groups with moderate (40–79 units) and high (≥ 80 units) levels of aCL/aβ2GPI IgM/IgG, measured based on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) [5]. The cutoff values for both IgG and IgM isotypes of aPS/PT antibodies, i.e. >30 international enzyme units (UI), were suggested as positive by the manufacturer.

Women with OAPS were divided into two groups with the high-risk and low-risk aPL profile according to the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recommendations [5]. A high-risk aPL profile presented the presence of LA (in ≥2 occasions at least 12 weeks apart), or of double (any combination of LA, aCL and/or aβ2GPI antibodies in moderate/high levels) or triple (all three subtypes) aPL positivity, or the presence of persistently high aPL titers. Women with the low-risk aPL profile were characterized with isolated aCL or aβ2GPI antibodies at low-to-medium titers.

Follow-up

Follow-up was carried out on a 6-month basis by a visit at the center or phone calls. Clinical events recorded during follow-up were pregnancies and live births. Pregnancy was confirmed after transvaginal ultrasound. The live birth was defined as complete expulsion or extraction from its mother of a product of human conception, irrespective of the duration of pregnancy, which, after such expulsion or extraction, breathes, or shows any other evidence of life such as beating of the heart, pulsation of the umbilical cord, or definite movement of voluntary muscles, whether or not the umbilical cord has been cut or the placenta is attached. Antithrombotic therapy consisted of ASA 75–100 mg/day and enoxaparin 40 mg/day, initiated upon confirmation of pregnancy and continued up to 6 weeks postpartum. In the control group the use of prophylactic low-molecular-dose heparins and their daily doses were documented. Intravenous immunoglobulin or hydroxychloroquine treatment were not administered.

The last visit at the center or telephone contact before June 2023 was considered as the end of follow-up.

Procedures

Laboratory investigations

Venous blood samples were drawn with minimal stasis using atraumatic venipuncture between 08.00 and 10.00 AM, after an overnight fast and a 10-minute rest. To acquire platelet-poor plasma blood samples were collected into 3.2% trisodium citrate tubes (Monovette, Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) at a ratio of 9:1 and centrifuged for 20 min at 2500 g within 30 min. Blood drawn into serum tubes (Monovette, Sarstedt) was centrifuged at 1600 g for 10 min at 4 °C Samples were immediately frozen, and kept in small aliquots at − 80 °C until analysis.

None of the participants was receiving heparin therapy at the time of blood sampling; therefore, heparin-neutralizing procedures or anti-Xa screening were not required.

High-sensitivity (hs) CRP, fibrinogen, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time/INR, and D-dimer were determined using routine laboratory techniques.

Von Willebrand factor (vWF) activity (ristocetin cofactor activity; VWF: RCo) was determined turbidimetrically (BCS XP System, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) and the reference range was 58–172%. PAI-1 antigen was determined by ELISA (American Diagnostica, Stamford, CT, US). TAFI antigen was determined with an ELISA (Chromogenix, Lexington, MA, US). Plasma antiplasmin and plasminogen were measured by chromogenic assays (STA Stachrom 2-antiplasmin and STA Stachrom plasminogen, Diagnostica Stago, Asnieres, France) and the reference ranges were 75–135% [10, 16, 17].

Fibrin clot parameters

Ks, reflecting an average pore size within fibrin mesh, was assessed using calcium chloride (20 mmol/L) and exogenous human thrombin (1 U/mL; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, US) added to citrated plasma (120 µL), as described [18]. Tubes containing the clots were connected via plastic tubing to a reservoir of a Tris buffer and its volume flowing through the gels was measured. A permeation coefficient (Ks), which indicates an average pore size in fibrin networks was assessed using a pressure-driven system and measured from the equation: Ks = QxLxƞ/txAxΔp, where Q is the flow rate in time t; L, the length of a fibrin gel; ƞ, the viscosity of liquid (in poise); A, the cross-sectional area (in cm2) and Δp, a differential pressure (in dyne/cm2). The lower Ks values, the more compact fibrin clot structure. The coefficients of intra- and inter-assay variations for CLT were 6.7 and 7.1%.

CLT, reflecting plasma fibrin clot susceptibility to enzymatic degradation, was evaluated in citrated plasma mixed with calcium chloride (15 nM), diluted human tissue factor (TF; 0.6 pM; Innovin, Siemens, Marburg, Germany), phospholipid vesicles (12 µM), and recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (60 ng/mL; Boehringer Ingelheim, Ingelheim, Germany). Turbidity of the mixture was assessed in 96-well microplate at 405 nm at 37 °C using a Perkin-Elmer Lambda 4B spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), as described previously [19]. CLT was defined as the time from midpoint of the clear-to-maximum-turbid transition to the midpoint of the maximum-turbid-to-clear transition. The coefficients of intra- and inter-assay variations for CLT were 4.8% and 5.4%.

An internal control (normal pooled plasma) was used in each run of Ks and CLT assessment to control variability of fibrin measures and all samples were run in duplicates.

Thrombin generation

Thrombin generation, reflecting the overall plasma capacity to generate thrombin, was measured by using calibrated automated thrombography (CAT), performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions in the 96-well plate fluorometer (Ascent Reader, Thermolabsystems OY, Helsinki, Finland) equipped with the 390/460 filter set at a temperature of 37 °C [3]. Briefly, 20 µl of the PPP-Reagent or Thrombin Calibrator (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières sur Seine Cedex, France) were mixed with 80 µl of plasma and 20 µl of FluCa solution (Diagnostica Stago) was added automatically to start the reaction. The maximum concentration of thrombin formed during the specified time period (60 min) was presented as the thrombin peak, and the area under the curve represented endogenous thrombin potential (ETP). Variability of the assay was 7.2%.

Statistical analyses

The study was powered to have a 90% chance of detecting a 10% difference in Ks and CLT using a p value of 0.01 based on the values of Ks and CLT as described previously (17). To demonstrate such a difference or greater in Ks, at least 38 individuals were required per group, while for CLT, at least 18 participants were needed, based on the values from the previous article (18).

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median and quartiles 1–3 (Q1-Q3), as appropriate. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test together with visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis followed by Mann–Whitney’s tests for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. The Holm adjustment was used for multiple comparisons. Categorical variables were analyzed using either the chi2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient were calculated to assess the linear correlations between variables with a normal or non-normal distribution, respectively. Intergroup comparisons were adjusted for potential confounding variables using linear regression modeling.

Associations between the variables were expressed as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with the STATISTICA 13.0 software (StatSoft, Poland).

Results

At baseline

The final analysis included women with OAPS (n = 62) aged 33 (29.0–38.0) years and BMI 25.6 ± 3.4 kg/m2 with well-matched controls (n = 62). We observed comparable demographic variables, along with comorbidities in both women with OAPS and the controls, regardless the pregnancy or live birth status (Supplementary Table 1).

PEC or PI with/without fetal death were observed as the most common obstetric complications among both women with OAPS (80.6%) and controls (79.0%). There were no differences in obstetric outcomes including recurrent pregnancy loss or fetal death, PEC, and PI between the 2 groups.

Follow-up

During a median follow-up period of 54 (49.0–58.0) months, no one was lost to follow-up. Forty-five (72.6%) women with OAPS had confirmed pregnancy and thirty-two (51.6%) of women with OAPS had live births. Regarding the controls, forty-four women (71.0%) were pregnant and thirty-two (51.6%) had live births.

APS parameters

There were no differences in aPL levels between women with OAPS with and without pregnancy during follow up (Table 1). OAPS women with live births during follow up showed less frequently positive LA (– 50.0%) along with lower aCL IgG (– 35.4%) and aPS/PT IgG (– 31.1%) levels. No differences in aPL levels regardless the pregnancy status were observed in women with OAPS (Supplementary Table 1). Of note, no differences in hsCRP were observed with regard to pregnancy or live birth in OAPS patients (1.89 [1.28–2.35] vs. 1.88 [1.71–2.20] mg/L and 1.86 [1.62–1.94] vs. 1.79 [1.53–1.88], respectively, both p > 0.05).

Intergroup analysis showed that pregnant women with OAPS had higher ETP (+ 5.1%) and TAFI Ag levels (+ 14.4%), along with lower plasminogen (– 6%), and antiplasmin (– 6.7%) in comparison to pregnant controls (all p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 1).

Live births

No differences in coagulation and fibrinolysis parameters were observed in women with OAPS with or without live births (Table 2). Regarding women with OAPS and live births, slightly higher ETP (+ 1.5%) and increased TAFI Ag levels (+ 12.0%), along with lower plasminogen (– 10%), and antiplasmin (– 10%) were observed compared to the controls with live births (all p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 1).

Women with OAPS with live births had higher baseline Ks (+ 18.2%, p < 0.0001) without any difference in CLT compared to women with OAPS and without live births. However, compared to controls with live births, women with OAPS and live births had reduced Ks (– 10.0%, Fig. 1C) and longer CLT (+ 19.0%, Fig. 1D) (all p < 0.001).

Increased Ks was associated with increased live birth rates observed both in OAPS (+ 17.3%; Fig. 2A) and in the control group (+ 5%, 7.4 [7.0–7.9] vs. 7.1 [6.0–7.7] x10− 9cm2, p = 0.038) and remained significant also after adjustment for fibrinogen (p = 0.0004 for OAPS and p = 0.018 for controls). CLT did not differ in women with OAPS with or without live birth (Fig. 2B) but was shorter in controls who gave live birth compared to the remainder (– 13.3%, 85 [79–94] vs. 98 [79–108] min, p = 0.028). In the OAPS group the area under curve (AUC) for Ks value that discriminates live births was 0.80 (95% CI:0.69–0.91; cut-off, 6.1 × 10− 9cm2; sensitivity, 0.813; specificity, 0.733).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to show that during long-term follow-up OAPS with live births had looser fibrin clot networks assessed before pregnancy. Moreover, compared to the controls with comparable pregnancy outcomes, women with OAPS with confirmed pregnancy and live births displayed slightly higher thrombin generation and longer CLT likely associated with increased TAFI and lower plasminogen. These findings suggest a mild shift toward a procoagulant and antifibrinolytic profile, which might be correlated with successful placental development in OAPS pregnancies, anyhow, it requires further investigation. Our study showed similarities between OAPS without live births and thrombotic APS [4], regarding prothrombotic fibrin clot phenotype, particularly related to reduced fibrin clot porosity.

Current evidence showed thar LMWH and ASA reduce miscarriage rates, while the treatment with hydroxychloroquine may improve live birth rates and reduce preterm delivery [6]. Moreover, it has been observed that hydroxychloroquine may improve obstetric outcomes in women with OAPS [9]. Consistent with previous studies [8, 20], pregnancy was confirmed among 72.6% of women with OAPS, whereas live births were observed in 51.6% of women with OAPS. Interestingly, we observed a higher live birth rate in OAPS who presented looser clots, which suggests that fibrin-related mechanisms, likely leading to an increased local fibrin generation, might partially contribute to the failure of the recommended strategy in pregnant individuals with aPL [4]. Moreover, higher Ks was consistently associated with higher live birth rates in controls, indicating that clot porosity may be one of determinants of pregnancy outcomes in OAPS. The diagnostic utility of this key fibrin measure was further supported by ROC analysis, showing good discrimination for live birth in OAPS, highlighting Ks as a potential biomarker associated with better obstetric outcomes.

Multiple studies have reported varying outcomes regarding the impact of systemic autoimmune diseases on pregnancy i.e., SLE, rheumatoid arthritis (RA) or Takayasu’s arthritis (TA) [21]. Regarding SLE, reduced pregnancy rates and live births along with hypertension and PE were observed [22]. On the other hand, women with TA had significantly higher rates of pregnancy-related complications including PE, preterm birth, miscarriage along with stillbirth, especially when arterial hypertension was diagnosed, showing that TA negatively affects pregnancy outcomes [23]. All above studies have shown that not only uncontrolled disease activity, but the presence of autoimmune disease per se is a strong predictor of adverse neonatal outcomes including, TE, PE, fetal loss, and preterm birth.

It might be speculated that other fibrin-improving therapy might be needed in such patients, which is in line with our previous observation [24]. Notably, we have shown about 14% higher Ks in individuals with positive pregnancy outcomes after in vitro fertilization with gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist [17, 18].

Studies investigating thrombin generation in women with OAPS are limited. Nevertheless, it has been observed that adequate thrombin generation is required for proper blastocyst implantation, remodeling of the spiral arteries, together with subsequent trophoblast invasion [25, 26]. On the other hand, hypercoagulability has recently been reported as a predictor of pregnancy failure [27]. Of note, several studies showed that coagulation at the local - uteroplacental interface differs substantially from the systemic coagulation [28]. The intervillous space along with spiral arteries are covered by trophoblast and decidual cells that constitutively express high levels of tissue factor and PAI, which lead to a procoagulant and antifibrinolytic state [29]. Therefore, it has been hypothesized that placental hemostasis is regulated locally and may not be fully reflected by markers of systemic coagulation.

In the current study, participants with live births, in both women with OAPS and controls, had increased ETP when compared to women without live births.

Furthermore, we have observed higher TAFI levels in OAPS with live births in contrary to the controls with live births, which reflects stronger antifibrinolytic activity. Increased ETP along with elevated TAFI may promote a prothrombotic state even when if fibrin clot networks appear more porous [6]. Moreover, TAFI-mediated inhibition of fibrinolysis may compensate weaker fibrin structure by prolonging clot persistence [30]. Taken together, higher ETP and TAFI likely reflect OAPS-related prothrombotic state. However, our observation that less porous fibrin structure, more prone to fibrinolysis was associated with higher chance for giving a live birth warrants further investigation, since the mechanism beyond this phenomenon remains unknown.

We showed that women with OAPS and live births had negative LA, lower aPL and aPS/PT titers compared to women with OAPS and without live births. Though aPS/PT antibodies have not been included in the latest APS classification criteria [5], higher titers of aPS/PT antibodies were found as significant independent predictors of adverse pregnancy outcomes [31].

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is limited, although the studied groups (OAPS versus controls) were well-matched for potential confounders, including the type of obstetric complications, and the study was adequately powered to show differences in the two main fibrin clot properties. Second, fibrin clot properties were evaluated once; therefore, changes in clot parameters with time cannot be excluded [32]. However, all samples were collected outside pregnancy to ensure standardized conditions across participants and to avoid pregnancy-related confounding fluctuations in analyzed parameters. Moreover, we have not determined other potential modulators of pregnancy outcomes i.e., genetic or inflammatory factors. Finally, this study has not shown cause-effective relationship, and results should be interpreted with caution until further studies validate these findings in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

The study showed that women with OAPS and positive pregnancy outcomes during follow-up had looser fibrin network, which might have practical implications and highlights the need for novel therapeutic approaches. Our results indicate that subtle alterations in fibrin clot structure and function may play a role in determining pregnancy success in OAPS.
