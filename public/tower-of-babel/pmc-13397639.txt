Gender inequality in the impact of climate shock on physical health and its spillover effect
Authors: Hongkun Zhao, Zhuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical health disparities between male and female constitute a significant yet underexplored manifestation of gender inequality, a challenge increasingly exacerbated by climate shock.MethodsThis paper explores the gender inequality in the impact of climate shock on physical health and its spillover effect. We develop a theoretical framework of intra-household resource allocation, and employ micro-level panel datasets matched with a climate physical risk dataset to estimate the impacts of climate shock using panel fixed-effect models.ResultsWe find that climate shock significantly reduces the activity of daily living and health expenditure, with female disproportionately affected. In male-preferred households, the deterioration of male’s activity of daily living generates negative spillovers that further undermine female’s activity of daily living, whereas in households without a strong male preference, this spillover is attenuated. Channel analysis shows that climate shock impairs the activity of daily living through reduced exercise and social participation. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that the physical health-reducing effects of climate shock are more pronounced among rural, low-educated, unmarried and unretired subgroups. Improving access to local employment opportunities and healthcare resources is effective in enhancing health adaptation to climate shock.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the need for gender-responsive adaptation strategies that prioritize equitable access to healthcare resources to strengthen physical health resilience under escalating climate risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14791-w.

Introduction

Promoting universal health and functional well-being remains a cornerstone of global development, as anchored in the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals 3. However, the escalating climate crisis has emerged as a systemic threat to these objectives, disproportionately eroding the physical health and functional capacity of vulnerable populations. Activities of Daily Living (ADL), a critical barometer of physical autonomy and quality of life, are increasingly compromised by climate-induced physiological stress and restricted mobility [1]. While climate physical risks are exogenous and ubiquitous, their physical health manifestations are far from uniform. Growing evidence suggests that gendered vulnerabilities, rooted in physiological differences, socio-economic disparities, and intra-household power dynamics, dictate the “unequal burden” individuals bear under identical climate exposure [2]. In low- and middle-income countries, where institutional support is often fragmented, these physical health disparities not only diminish individual productivity but also deepen systemic gender inequality, necessitating a profound understanding of how climate shock reshapes the landscape of physical health across genders [3]. Crucially, this paper argues that the household is not merely a passive recipient of climate shocks but an active site where pre-existing gender preferences determine who bears the health cost and who receives financial protection. Therefore, this study aims to uncover the intra-household spillover effect, specifically gendered resource allocation, that transform a universal climate shock into a gendered physical health penalty.

Extensive research has documented the physiological toll of climate shock, traditionally focusing on mortality rates or acute morbidities such as cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. However, a nascent but vital body of literature is shifting attention toward functional health indicators, particularly ADL, which serve as a more comprehensive measure of long-term climate resilience and human capital maintenance [4, 5]. While the direct physical impacts of extreme temperatures and environmental shocks are increasingly recognized, existing studies often treat individuals as isolated units, neglecting the complex intra-household dynamics that mediate these impacts [6]. This black box of the household is where gender inequalities are most acutely manifested. Yet the literature has largely focused on aggregate socio-economic outcomes rather than the granular reallocation of health resources within the domestic sphere [7, 8]. Specifically, the potential for physical health spillovers, where the functional deterioration of one household member, particularly a male, triggers a chain reaction of resource competition or increased caregiving burdens that further undermine the physical health of female members, remains a critical yet underexplored dimension of climate-induced inequality.

Complementing the erosion of functional capacity, the financial strain of climate shock serves as a secondary yet equally potent driver of physical health inequality. Extreme weather events inevitably escalate healthcare costs as households attempt to mitigate physiological damage and functional impairment [9]. However, these financial burdens are rarely distributed equitably within the domestic sphere. In resource-constrained environments, healthcare expenditures often exhibit a pro-male bias, where health-related investments are prioritized for male members as they are frequently perceived as the primary economic pillars of the family [10, 11]. This creates a precarious double burden for women: they are simultaneously more vulnerable to the functional decline triggered by climate shocks and more likely to be marginalized during the subsequent allocation of remedial medical resources [12]. Existing scholarship, while acknowledging the aggregate surge in health costs, has yet to sufficiently scrutinize the disconnect between physical impairment and financial mitigation at the micro-level. Specifically, how gendered preferences transform a universal environmental risk into an asymmetrical health penalty remains a critical blind spot that this study aims to illuminate.

This research seeks to provide a granular analysis of how climate shock influences physical health and health expenditure, with a specific focus on gender inequality and intra-household spillover. Our study is structured four primary objectives. First, we study the gender inequality in the impact of climate shock on physical health and health expenditure. Second, we rigorously examine the intra-household spillover effects, exploring how the functional deterioration of male members, conditioned by gendered allocation preferences, asymmetrically exacerbates the physical health burdens of females. Third, we delineate the channels through which climate shock impair physical health, specifically investigating the roles of curtailed physical exercise and social activity. Finally, we assess the role of improved access to local employment opportunities and healthcare resources in mitigating the gender-differentiated physical health impacts of climate shock, as well as its effectiveness in supporting health adaptation to climate shock.

This paper has three primary contributions. First, we redefine the climate-health nexus by focusing on ADL, a structural measure of human capital, rather than subjective well-being. By utilizing the intra-household bargaining perspective, we move beyond individual-centric analyses to uncover the health-financial disconnect, the phenomenon where climate-induced physical health impairment in females fails to trigger the same compensatory financial protection as observed in males. This provides a more rigorous empirical basis for understanding how intra-household gender preference transform universal climate shock into physical health inequality. Second, this study demonstrates that climate shock doesn’t merely direct affect physical health, but specifically through the restriction of exercise and social activity. Third, we evaluate the framework of health adaptation to climate shock. We provide evidence that the restoration of physical health is more contingent on the accessibility of healthcare resources than on employment. This distinction is vital for developing gender-responsive health adaptation to climate shock that targets the specific physical health vulnerabilities exacerbated by climate shock.

Conceptual framework

To better guide the empirical analysis, we establish a conceptual framework to model gender inequality in the effects of climate shock on individuals’ physical health and health expenditure [13, 14]. Specifically, we explore how gender preferences shape both the direction and magnitude of these effects.

We begin with a simplified model of intra-household resource allocation, where haggle over price to decide the savings and health expenditure of male and female. The model considers a household with two individuals, including a male and a female. Following [15], we assume that households possess a utility function (UT), which is separable and depends on sub-utility derived from earnings (E) and other outcomes of interest (C) [16–18]. Data and variables

Data source

Data

This study primarily relies on longitudinal microdata from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey administered by Peking University. CHARLS is designed to collect information on the demographic characteristics, health status, healthcare utilization, and socioeconomic conditions of respondents in China, as well as their households. The survey adopts a multi-stage, stratified probability sampling strategy, covering 450 village-level units across 150 county-level administrative divisions in mainland China, thereby ensuring broad geographic and socioeconomic representativeness. Data are collected at the individual, household, and community levels, allowing for detailed analysis of intra-household resource allocation. To construct a balanced and consistent panel suitable for longitudinal analysis, we combine five consecutive survey waves (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). All waves are harmonized following the official CHARLS panel guidelines to ensure consistency in variable definitions and measurement over time. This multi-wave structure enables us to exploit within-individual variation and to identify the dynamic effects of climate shock on physical health and health expenditure.

Ethical considerations

The Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University have verified that the study complies with research ethics (ethical code: IRB00001052-11015). All procedures involving human participants adhered to ethical standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Before holding the interviews, the participants were asked to sign an informed consent form if they were willing to participate in the study. The participants were also informed that they were free to withdraw at any point of the research and the time of the interviews would be set by their agreement. For performing the observations in accordance with the ethical considerations, the participants were observed with prior notice and in an overt manner.

Dependent variable is the physical health of respondents, which is assessed through the activities of daily living (ADL) [20, 21]. ADL consists of six key activities: can or can’t dress (Dress), bath (Bath), eat (Dine), getting in and out of bed (Get-up), go to the toilet (WC), and pee and poo (Relieve), independently. To assess ADL, each activity is assigned a value of 1 if the individual is able to perform it independently without assistance. Scores for all six activities are then added together. The total score ranges from a maximum of 6, indicating complete independence in all activities, to a minimum of 0, indicating that assistance is required for each activity.

We operationalize health expenditure using respondents’ total medical costs over the past month, serving as a proxy indicator for respondents’ resource investments in physical health improvement [22]. This measure inherently assumes that healthcare utilization reflects intentional efforts to address physical health well-being. We interpret higher health expenditure as a household’s revealed preference to invest in an individual’s physical health recovery. However, we acknowledge it also correlates with illness severity, making the contrast between rising expenditure for male and stagnant expenditure for female particularly telling of allocation preferences. Empirical strategy

To rigorously identify the causal nexus between climate shock and physical health, we employ a high-dimensional fixed-effects panel framework [14]. The CPR metric is constructed to capture the annual frequency of extreme weather days, defined as periods where localized meteorological parameters breach region-specific historical thresholds. A fundamental premise of our identification strategy lies in the decoupling of long-run climatic shifts from short-term idiosyncratic shocks. Unlike secular climate trends which may exhibit predictable spatial patterns, these threshold-exceeding events are characterized by idiosyncratic temporal volatility and are serially uncorrelated. This inherent stochasticity ensures that such weather-driven shocks are orthogonal to unobserved individual or regional characteristics, satisfying the requirements for treating them as exogenous variations in our baseline specification. 8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y_{i,h,c,t}} = {\beta _0} + {\beta _1}CP{R_{c,t}} + {\beta _2}Contro{l_{i,h,c,t}} + {\delta _t} + {\lambda _i} + {\eta _c} + {\varepsilon _{i,h,c,t}}$$\end{document}

In our primary specification, Yi,h,c,t denotes the outcomes of interest—physical functional capacity (measured by ADL scores) and healthcare expenditure—for individual i in household h within city c at time t. The model incorporates a comprehensive vector of Controli,h,c,t to account for time-varying individual and household-level characteristics. To purge the estimates of latent confounding factors, we integrate a multi-level fixed-effect structure: δt captures macro-level temporal shocks common to all units, while λi and ηc absorb time-invariant unobservables at the individual and city levels, respectively. We estimate the baseline model using a panel fixed-effect model, which allows us to control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity across individuals and common shocks over time. This approach is well suited to our setting, as it mitigates bias arising from omitted factors that are constant within individuals but correlated with both climate exposure and physical health outcomes. However, the two-way fixed effect estimates may be sensitive to heterogeneous treatment effects across groups and time. To address these limitations, we complement the baseline specification with a series of robustness checks.

Gendered impacts of climate shock on physical health and health expenditure

Examining the gendered impacts of climate shock on intra-household physical health and health expenditure is critical, particularly given persistent gender and age disparities in vulnerability and resource allocation. Significant differences in physical health responses across household members necessitate a disaggregated analysis to better understand distributional consequences and inform targeted policy interventions. To quantify heterogeneous effects across age-gender cohorts, we estimate Eq. (9), with Table 3 reporting interaction-adjusted marginal effects of CPR.

Panel A reports the effects of CPR on ADL. The results reveal pronounced heterogeneity across demographic groups. CPR is associated with statistically significant deteriorations in ADL among adult men, elderly men, and elderly women. By contrast, the estimated effect for boys is negative but relatively small in magnitude, indicating that although boys’ physical health status worsens under climate shock, the extent of deterioration is substantially more limited than that observed among older cohorts. For girls, the estimated coefficient is positive and statistically insignificant, suggesting no robust adverse effect of CPR on ADL. This pattern can be rationalized by age- and gender-specific vulnerability profiles. Children, especially boys, typically exhibit stronger baseline physical resilience and faster physiological recovery, which may buffer the functional health consequences of climate shock. In addition, boys often face fewer caregiving responsibilities and lower cumulative exposure to chronic health risks within households, limiting the extent to which climate stressors translate into observable declines in daily functioning. In contrast, adults and the elderly are more likely to suffer from pre-existing physical health conditions and cumulative exposure to environmental stress, making their ADL more sensitive to climate risks.

Intra-household spillover effects of climate shock impacts on physical health and health expenditure

To explore intra-household spillover effects of climate shock on physical health and health expenditure, we classify individuals according to whether they co-reside with members of the opposite sex. Specifically, male individuals are divided into those living with at least one female household member and those living without females, while female individuals are divided into those living with at least one male household member and those living without males. This classification is motivated by the theoretical framework of intra-household resource allocation under gender preference. In households where both genders are present, climate-induced physical health shocks may trigger reallocations of limited health resources across members, with prioritization shaped by gender-based preferences. In contrast, in same-gender households, such gendered allocation constraints are absent, allowing us to isolate the role of gender composition in mediating intra-household spillovers. While spillovers may also exist among individuals of the same gender, our focus on intra-household spillovers related to gender preferences, which emphasizes that unequal treatment arises primarily along gender preference rather than within-gender age hierarchies. Examining opposite-gender co-residence therefore provides a more direct test of whether gender preference amplifies the unequal physical health consequences of climate shock.

The results for male individuals using Eq. (11) are presented in Table 4. For males, CPR leads to a significant decrease in ADL scores across most cohorts (Panel A), particularly for boys in households with females (Panel A, column 4), suggesting a substantial decline in their physical health. However, their health expenditure tends to decrease in households where no females are present (Panel B), potentially reflecting a lack of caregiving support or budget constraints in single-male households. This suggests that when male members’ physical health is compromised by climate shocks, households, driven by male-centered preferences, allocate significant financial resources to mitigate these impacts. While girls and women also suffer physical health declines under climate shock, their health expenditure does not show a commensurate increase in households where males are present. Specifically, for females in households with no males, health expenditure increases significantly to buffer the shock (Table 5, Column 2). Endogeneity. Endogeneity issues, stemming principally from omitted variable bias and simultaneous causality, pose a potential threat to the identification of the causal link between climate shock, physical health, and financial burdens. First, unobserved individual heterogeneity, such as genetic resilience, long-standing lifestyle habits, or inherent environmental adaptability, might correlate with both an individual’s functional health status (ADL) and their exposure to climatic risks. Failure to account for these latent factors could result in biased estimates. Second, a reverse causality channel may exist. Individuals with deteriorating ADL or higher health spending may alter their behavioral patterns (e.g., reducing outdoor mobility or labor supply) in ways that modify their actual exposure to extreme climate events.

To address these identification challenges and isolate the exogenous variation in climate shock, we implement a Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) strategy, utilizing city-level altitude as an instrumental variable (IV) for the CPR. Altitude is a standard geographic instrument in environmental economics literature [24, 25]. It satisfies the relevance condition because altitude is a fundamental determinant of local meteorological patterns, strongly predicting temperature variability and precipitation levels. Furthermore, as a time-invariant geological feature determined by natural terrain formation, altitude satisfies the exclusion restriction. It plausibly influences individual physical health outcomes and health expenditures solely through its impact on long-term climatic conditions rather than through direct biological pathways.

Table 11 presents the 2SLS estimation results. Column (1) reports the first-stage regression, revealing that altitude is a robust positive predictor of CPR, statistically significant at the 1% level. The validity of the instrument is supported by rigorous diagnostic tests. The Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistic (1615.132) strongly rejects the null hypothesis of under-identification (p < 0.01), and the Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic (1930.797) far exceeds the Stock-Yogo critical value of 16.38 (at the 10% level), alleviating concerns regarding weak instrumentation. To verify the exogeneity of the instrumental variable, the Hansen J test for over-identification is conducted. The p-value of the Hansen J statistic is 0.000, indicating that the instrumental variables satisfy the exogeneity assumption.

Columns (2) and (3) display the second-stage results. Even after correcting for potential endogeneity, the impact of climate shock remains robust. The coefficient for ADL is significantly negative, indicating that increased climate shock erodes physical health. Conversely, the coefficient for health expenditure is significantly positive, confirming that climate shocks escalate medical financial burdens. These findings align with the baseline results in both direction and statistical significance, reinforcing the causal interpretation that climate shock deteriorates physical health and drives up healthcare expenditure.

Channels and unequal selection effects

Channels

Climate shock may influence physical health and health expenditure through following channels. First, climate shock may discourage outdoor activities, reduce exercise time and thereby deteriorate cardiovascular physical health and immunity. This, in turn, may heighten physical health risks, decrease expenditures on preventive healthcare, and raise costs associated with treatment [26]. Second, climate shock may undermine social integration by restricting outdoor mobility, community engagement, and interpersonal interactions, thereby intensifying social isolation and functional health deterioration. This decline in social participation may further exacerbate physical functional limitations, as the lack of social stimulation and peer support is known to accelerate functional decay and aging [27]. Against this backdrop, this paper examines the mechanisms through which climate shock affects ADL and health expenditure along three key channels—exercise and social participation—and empirically tests these pathways.

The analysis in this section is designed to explore plausible mechanisms linking climate risks to physical health outcomes rather than to estimate formal causal mediation effects. Specifically, the proposed channels are treated as intermediate outcomes that are influenced by climate risks and are closely related to physical health, allowing us to assess whether climate exposure operates through these dimensions. While formal mediation approaches, such as structural equation modeling with Sobel or Monte Carlo tests, are well suited for identifying causal transmission channels, their implementation requires strong assumptions regarding functional form, error independence, and the absence of unobserved confounding between mediators and physical health outcomes. Given data limitations and identification concerns, we adopt a reduced-form framework and interpret the results as suggestive evidence on potential pathways rather than definitive mediation effects.

Exercise

Maintaining consistent physical activity is a fundamental prerequisite for sustaining functional independence and preventing physiological decay. However, the escalating volatility of climate physical risks tends to obstruct established fitness routines by degrading the quality of the outdoor environment, thereby forcing individuals to recalibrate their health-seeking behaviors [28]. To examine this behavioral channel, we evaluate how climate shocks reshape the intensity and duration of exercise. The empirical evidence presented in Table 122 (Columns 1–3) reveals a significant structural shift: while climate physical risk (CPR) correlates with a marginal increase in low-intensity exercise hours, it exerts a substantial negative pressure on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and aggregate exercise time. The coefficients for these core variables are both economically and statistically significant. This phenomenon suggests a substitution effect driven by environmental constraints; individuals may pivot toward milder, indoor-compatible movements to circumvent the physiological hazards of extreme outdoor temperatures [29, 30]. Nevertheless, the observed decline in strenuous physical engagement is critical, as it directly undermines musculoskeletal strength and aerobic capacity, providing a plausible pathway for the observed increase in ADL scores and the subsequent deterioration of functional health. Critically, this reduction in moderate-to-high intensity exercise may disproportionately affect male, who traditionally rely more on vigorous physical upkeep to maintain labor productivity. This specific form of physiological capital depreciation likely triggers the intra-household bargaining dynamics observed earlier, compelling households to allocate preferential medical expenditure to males to restore their physical capacity.

Social participation

Social participation is critical to maintaining physical functional capacity and informed physical health-related decisions. Climate shock can have far-reaching effects on individuals’ social networks through extreme weather events that act as physical barriers to communal gatherings and informal support systems. We use whether visiting neighbors or interacting with friends as proxy variables for an individual’s level of social participation. At the micro level, climate shock is likely to reduce the frequency of social participation, leading to social withdrawal and diminished functional resilience [31]. The estimation results of Columns (5) in Table 12 show that climate shock significantly reduces social participation. This implies that climate shock weakens the social participation of individuals, further influence individual physical health. This finding offers a vital explanation for the gendered physical health penalty. Given that female’s functional health maintenance often relies more critically on informal social networks and communal support, the climate-induced contraction of social participation disproportionately undermines their ADL status. Unlike the financial compensation observed for males, this loss of social capital is less visible and harder to substitute with monetary expenditure, thereby exacerbating the physical health vulnerability of females.

Unequal selection effects

In this section, we scrutinize the heterogeneous sensitivities of functional health to climate stressors, focusing on how socio-institutional barriers and domestic configurations modulate the baseline impacts. We hypothesize that structural disparities, specifically those related to rural residency and limited educational attainment, exacerbate the functional health penalty for females by restricting their access to adaptive resources. Furthermore, we explore how intra-household dynamics, such as marital status and life-cycle transitions (retirement), influence bargaining power and social support networks, potentially intensifying the physical health-deteriorating effects of CPR. For the purpose of this analysis, ‘low-educated’ is defined as individuals who have not completed middle school. To empirically identify these differential vulnerabilities, we integrate interaction terms between CPR and indicators of structural and domestic characteristics into our primary estimation framework. These associations should be interpreted as reduced-form correlations that are consistent with behavioral adjustment under climate stress, rather than as evidence of a causal or mediating mechanism.

Urban-rural divides and educational attainment

The empirical results regarding structural gradients are presented in Table 13. Individuals residing in rural areas or possessing lower educational levels exhibit a more pronounced surge in ADL, indicating greater functional fragility, and a relative stagnation in health expenditure following climate shocks. These vulnerabilities are consistent across various age-gender cohorts (Appendix Tables C8–C9). This intergroup divergence can be attributed to the protection gap caused by limited physical health literacy and the scarcity of high-quality medical infrastructure in marginalized regions. Our findings align with the theory of asymmetric environmental risk absorption, where lower socioeconomic status diminishes an individual’s capacity to buffer the physiological and behavioral disruptions caused by escalating climate risks [32].

Physical health adaptation to climate shock

Institutional support, particularly through the provision of localized medical infrastructure and stable labor markets, serves as a critical buffer against the functional erosion triggered by climate stressors. While enhanced healthcare accessibility provides a direct remedial pathway for mitigating physical impairment, employment stability bolsters functional resilience by securing economic resources and maintaining protective social networks [34, 35]. To quantify the efficacy of these mitigation strategies, we estimate the marginal effects of healthcare and employment availability on the ADL trajectories across different demographic cohorts. Throughout this section, “access to healthcare resources” is interpreted as effective access, the realized ability to obtain timely medical care, rather than as a purely supply-side measure of healthcare availability. Conclusion

This paper estimates the impact of climate shock on physical health. We construct a theoretical model of intra-household resource allocation and derive gender differences in climate shock impacts on physical health and health expenditure, and intra-household spillovers across gender preference contexts. Based on CHARLS dataset, this paper provides novel empirical evidence on the direct impact of climate shock on physical health and health expenditure, gender inequality and its intra-household spillovers, analyzed using reduced-form model. We also evaluate the effectiveness of access to local employment opportunities and healthcare resources in supporting residents’ physical health adaptation to climate shock.

The estimation results suggest that climate shock exacerbates ADL and increase the burden of health expenditure. In particular, males are in a better position than females to pay for health expenditure, and shocks to males’ physical health caused by climate shock have negative spillover effects on females’ physical health and on health expenditure in male-headed households. The channels of impact are reduced exercise and social participation. Moreover, when structural disparities and intra-household dynamics are taken into account, such impacts show unequal selection effects, which are mainly concentrated in rural, low-educated, unmarried and unretired groups. Access to local employment opportunities and healthcare resources plays a key role in mitigating the adverse effects of climate shock on physical health.

This paper highlights the importance of considering intra-household spillovers for a comprehensive understanding of climate shock impacts. In particular, exposure to the same climate physical risks may affect male and female asymmetrically due to household budget constraints and patriarchy. Such unintended negative spillovers exacerbate gender inequalities in physical health and health expenditure.

Over the past decades, China has implemented a series of physical health adaptation policies aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of climate shock on public health, particularly among vulnerable populations. Notably, the healthy cities pilot policy has facilitated the development of a comprehensive health protection system, and has expanded access to affordable primary healthcare through community health centers and primary care networks, ensuring timely medical support for the dispossessed. While these measures vary in coverage and implementation efficacy, empirical evidence underscores their significant role in improving population health and reducing physical health disparities. As climate shock intensifies physical health risks, policymakers must adopt targeted adaptation strategies that effectively address the physical health needs of diverse demographic groups. Empirical research in this paper, particularly on the negative intra-household spillover effects of climate shock on females’ physical health and health expenditure, offers valuable insights for policy formulation in other developing regions.

Our findings offer several implications for improving health adaptation policies in China. First, the pronounced gender- and age-specific health responses to climate shock suggest that uniform adaptation strategies may be insufficient. Health adaptation policies should incorporate targeted interventions that prioritize vulnerable groups, particularly females and older individuals, whose health appears more adversely affected by climate exposure. Second, the results indicate that increased access to local employment opportunities and healthcare resources is beneficial to physical health and health equity, highlighting the need for adaptation policies to integrate employment services and preventive health care into climate adaptation frameworks. Specifically, this underscores the need for adaptation policies that not only build hospitals but also ensure financial access to primary care for women in rural areas, potentially through targeted subsidies integrated with climate resilience programs. Third, the differential effectiveness of health investments across demographic groups underscores the importance of aligning adaptation policies with intra-household resource allocation dynamics, for example by strengthening public provision of child and adolescent health services to mitigate potential gender biases in household decision-making. Climate adaptation policies in China should move beyond expanding healthcare access and instead emphasize targeted, health-oriented, and equity-sensitive interventions.
