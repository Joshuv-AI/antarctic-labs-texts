Intraparenchymal cyst formation after invasive intracranial pressure monitoring: a rare complication. Illustrative case
Authors: Heng-Chun Liao, Yung-Shen Juan, Furen Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDIntraparenchymal cysts are rare complications that are most commonly associated with ventriculoperitoneal shunt or external ventricular drain malfunction.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe a rare case of intraparenchymal cyst formation after intraparenchymal intracranial pressure (ICP) monitor placement for traumatic brain injury. Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring is frequently used in neurocritical care. ICP monitors are placed to record the pressure inside the skull and allow clinicians to take actions in time. Common complications of invasive ICP monitoring could be categorized into infection, bleeding, misplacement, and other technical problems.1,2 Intraparenchymal cyst formation is one of the rare complications in patients with ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunts or external ventricular drains (EVDs). To our knowledge, no prior case of intraparenchymal cyst associated with invasive ICP monitoring has been reported in the literature.

We present the case of a man who received intraparenchymal ICP monitoring due to a traffic accident and experienced intraparenchymal cyst formation after removal of the ICP monitor. We discuss the clinical course, intraoperative findings, pathogenesis of the cyst, treatment, and potential means of prevention in this case.

Illustrative Case

A 49-year-old man with a history of asthma and experiencing multiple traumas presented to the emergency department of a local hospital after a traffic accident. He was soon transferred to a medical center with an initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E2V5M1 on arrival. Scattered foci of a subarachnoid and intracerebral hemorrhage were detected on CT imaging (Fig. 1A). After neurosurgical consultation, an ICP monitor was placed according to guidelines. A Codman MicroSensor ICP device (Integra LifeSciences) was placed intraparenchymally at the right frontal lobe (hospital day 1). The patient was then admitted to the intensive care unit. Two days after placement of the monitor (hospital day 3), the first follow-up CT scan showed stable condition of the brain, with no evidence of any cyst-like lesion (Fig. 1B). Because the patient had also been neurologically stable with a GCS score of E3VTM6, the monitor was removed 4 days after its placement (hospital day 5). The removed device tip was sent for bacterial culture with negative results.

The arrow indicates the residual intraparenchymal cyst with a small hemorrhage.

However, altered mental status was noticed on the next day (hospital day 6), with the GCS score changing from a baseline of E3VTM6 to E2VTM4. An emergency CT scan revealed an intracerebral cyst with minimal hemorrhage and perifocal edema at the right frontal lobe, where the ICP monitor was previously placed (Fig. 1C). To determine the etiology, an emergency MR study was done on the same day. A cystic lesion with T2-hyperintense content, measuring up to 3.7 × 2.8 × 1.9 cm, was revealed at the right frontal lobe. Marked perifocal edema was also noted on MRI (Fig. 2A and B). Given the history of ICP monitor placement, his condition was considered to be clinically compatible with an intrapernchymal pericatheter cyst. The patient’s neurological condition continued to deteriorate and he eventually underwent surgical drainage of the intraparenchymal cyst on hospital day 6. The bacterial culture of cyst fluid sampled during the operation was negative. Postoperative CT imaging on hospital day 9 showed a small hemorrhage in the residual cyst cavity (Fig. 1D). The patient’s mental status gradually recovered postoperatively with concurrent medical treatment. On hospital day 26, he was discharged from the surgical department for rehabilitation.

At the 5-month follow-up at the outpatient clinic, an MRI study was repeated and showed near-total resolution of the cyst and edema (Fig. 2C and D).

Discussion

Observations

Intraparenchymal cyst is a rare complication that mostly occurs in children with VP shunts, especially in those with obstructed or infected shunts. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of intraparenchymal ICP monitor–related intraparenchymal cyst formation. The cyst can be asymptomatic, but it can also lead to the compression of adjacent structures or even brain herniation due to increased ICP.

ICP monitors could be placed into the epidural space, subdural space, brain parenchyma, or ventricles. Epidural catheter placement sometimes fails to monitor the central ICP due to biomechanically strong dura mater. Unlike subdural placement, intraparenchymal and intraventricular placement of the catheter required opening the arachnoid membrane and pia mater. In our hospital, intraparenchymal ICP monitors are usually placed 2–3 cm into the cerebral parenchyma, without the need to puncture the ependymal lining.3 In comparison, placement of EVDs or ventricular CSF shunts always requires the additional opening of the ependyma in order to drain CSF.

Intraparenchymal cyst is considered a rare complication of malfunctioning ventricular drainage devices. In most cases, the obstruction of such drainage devices leads to hydrocephalus and increased ICP, with expanded ventricles but without the formation of new cysts.4,5 The intraparenchymal cysts are not accompanied by expansion of the ventricular compartments;5 in contrast, the mass effect of the cyst and its perifocal edema are what lead to increased ICP and deteriorating neurological conditions. Thus, cyst drainage along with shunt revision is often the treatment of choice. However, a minority of cases have been reported to be involved in cyst formation around functioning shunts or drains, leading to the hypothesis that obstruction of the catheter may not be the only cause.5,6

To better describe the etiology of intraparenchymal cyst formation, several mechanisms have been postulated. The ‘‘direct infusion edema model’’ describes direct CSF leakage into surrounding interstitial brain tissue as the underlying mechanism, and blockage of the distal drainage catheter was merely the trigger.5–9 Another hypothesis involves backflow of CSF from taut ventricles into the pericatheter space via the relative weak point, where the catheter penetrates the ependyma.7,8,10 Further reactive gliosis around the obstructed catheter could also contribute to cyst formation rather than pure parenchymal edema.10

For invasive ICP monitoring, common complications include infection, bleeding, misplacement, and other technical problems.1 In our case, the major issue was none of these, but rather new cyst formation. Cyst formation is an unusual consequence of intraparenchymal ICP monitoring, but it has been reported in patients with EVDs or VP shunts. It is postulated that, with opening of the arachnoid membrane, placement of the ICP monitoring device may cause a tissue reaction similar to that in the placement of a ventricular catheter. Moreover, because of the deep placement of the ICP monitor, we cannot exclude puncture of the ependyma lining, leading to further bleeding and CSF communication between the cyst and the lateral ventricle. Nevertheless, the possibility of subclinical infection can not be totally excluded, even if both the CSF culture and device tip culture show negative results for bacterial infection.

The most common treatment of symptomatic pericatheter cyst in EVD- or shunt-related cases is cyst drainage along with device revision.11 The procedure not only decreases the size of the cyst but also replaces the malfunctioning device with a new and patent one. Isolated drainage of the cyst has also been reported to be effective in relieving symptoms. Follow-up CT and MR imaging studies in such cases revealed no recurrence.12,13 In asymptomatic cases, aggressive intervention may not be necessary as several reports have shown spontaneous resolution of the cyst with conservative management.6,7

In our case, since the ICP monitoring device itself had no drainage function, isolated drainage of the cyst was first performed to reduce the mass effect. The tissue reaction and the CSF backflow of the potential ventricular puncture were considered the major mechanisms of cyst formation in our case. Brain MRI at the 5-month postoperative follow-up showed good treatment effect with near-total resolution of the cyst and small areas of residual brain edema and encephalomalacia.

Lessons

Invasive ICP monitoring is a cornerstone of neurocritical care. New cyst formation after placement of the ICP monitoring device is a rare complication, in contrast to common ones such as infection, bleeding, and misplacement. We report the first case of intraparenchymal cyst formation after the removal of such a device, requiring surgical cyst drainage due to deteriorating neurological condition from cyst formation. The pathogenesis is considered to be related to the opening of the leptomeninges and the potential puncture of the lateral ventricle, and thus subdural placement of the ICP monitor could have prevented cyst formation in this case.
