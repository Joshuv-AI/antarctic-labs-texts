Sugarcane waste-derived carbon quantum dots–chlorophyll/bacterial cellulose composites for solar-driven antibiotic degradation
Authors: Linda Kumalayanti, Thanisorn Mahatnirunkul, Yothin Chimupala, Pairot Moontragoon, Supree Pinitsoontorn, Navadecho Chankhunthod
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA bio-based CQDs–Chl/BC nanocomposite (NCs) was synthesized from sugarcane bagasse, sugarcane leaves, and bacterial cellulose (BC) via a green bio-construction strategy to enhance antibiotic removal through a synergistic adsorption–photocatalysis mechanism. CQDs were prepared by a microwave-assisted method, Chl was extracted from sugarcane leaves, and BC provided a renewable support matrix. The incorporation of CQDs and Chl broadened visible-light absorption and improved charge transfer, as confirmed by UV–Vis spectroscopy, FTIR, PL, TEM–EDS, and XPS analyses. Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) and photoinduced electron transfer (PET) minimized charge recombination, while LC–MS spectra of degradation products identified transient intermediates of ciprofloxacin (CIP) and oxytetracycline (OTC), validating the proposed photocatalytic pathway. Under solar irradiation, the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs achieved near-complete degradation of CIP (98.83 ± 0.46%) and OTC (98.59 ± 0.50%) at 2 mg/L within 180 min, outperforming CQDs/BC (k = 0.01044 min−1) and Chl/BC (k = 0.01109 min−1), with a pseudo-first-order rate constant of 0.02642 min−1. The composite also exhibited strong adsorption capacity, excellent recyclability, and structural stability, retaining 67.7% efficiency after five cycles, with FTIR and XPS confirming integrity post-reuse. Biotoxicity assays revealed no intrinsic antibacterial activity, underscoring its environmental safety. The novelty of this study lies in the dual biomass valorization approach, where CQDs from sugarcane bagasse and Chl from sugarcane leaves were synergistically integrated with BC to couple adsorption, photosensitization, and advanced charge-transfer processes in one photocatalyst. Introduction

The widespread occurrence of pharmaceuticals and personal care products (PPCPs) in aquatic environments has emerged as a critical environmental and public health concern1. Among these contaminants, antibiotics are of particular concern due to their widespread use, persistence, and contribution to antimicrobial resistance. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin (CIP) and tetracycline antibiotics such as oxytetracycline (OTC) are frequently detected in natural waters and wastewater effluents because of their chemical stability and poor biodegradability. Conventional biological and physicochemical treatment processes have proven inadequate for their complete removal, allowing these pollutants to persist in aquatic systems2. In Thailand, for example, CIP concentrations of up to 6.0 µg/L and OTC concentrations of 0.4 µg/L have been reported in wastewater treatment plants and receiving waters3, levels that exceed environmental safety thresholds and far surpass the recommended limit of 1.0 µg/L for antibiotics in surface water4. Such findings underscore the urgent need for effective, reliable, and sustainable water treatment technologies.

Advanced treatment technologies such as advanced oxidation processes5, adsorption6, and photocatalytic7 have been investigated to address this challenge. Among these, photocatalysis has garnered particular attention owing to its ability to harness light to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of degrading a wide range of organic pollutants. Traditional photocatalysts like titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and zinc oxide (ZnO) have demonstrated strong oxidation capability under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, yet their practical deployment is limited by intrinsic drawbacks. Their wide bandgaps restrict light absorption to the UV region, while rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers severely diminishes photocatalytic efficiency under solar irradiation. Various modification strategies, such as metal doping8, heterojunction formation, and sensitization with photosensitizers, have been employed to overcome these challenges, however issues related to photocatalyst stability, sustainability, and scalability persist, limiting their widespread use in real-world wastewater treatment applications.

Recent advances have focused on the development of alternative visible-light-driven photocatalysts, including graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄)9, copper oxide (CuO)10, and bismuth vanadate (BIVO4)11. These systems have reported degradation efficiencies of up to 93.5%, 86.8%, and 75%, respectively, demonstrating the potential of visible-light photocatalysis for antibiotic remediation. While these materials exhibited enhanced photocatalytic activity under visible light, their performance is still compromised by rapid charge carrier recombination12–14, photocorrosion, and limited long-term stability. Additionally, many synthetic strategies for these materials involve complex, energy-intensive, and environmentally hazardous processes, raising concerns about their large-scale applicability and environmental impact. Therefore, the search for sustainable, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly photocatalytic materials remains a crucial challenge in the field of wastewater treatment.

To overcome these limitations, carbon-based materials have emerged as promising candidates due to their unique optical, electronic, and structural properties. Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) are particularly promising due to their strong photoluminescence, tunable bandgap, high surface area, and excellent electron-transfer capability. Their ability to function as electron reservoirs improves charge separation and suppresses recombination losses in photocatalytic systems. Importantly, their facile and green synthesis from biomass-derived precursors makes them an eco-friendly alternative to conventional semiconductor photocatalysts. In parallel, bacterial cellulose (BC), a biodegradable and renewable biopolymer with an interconnected porous structure and high mechanical stability, has been investigated as a support matrix for photocatalysts. Its abundant hydroxyl groups facilitate strong interfacial interactions while its porous architecture enhances pollutant adsorption, enabling synergy between adsorption and photocatalysis15,16. By integrating CQDs into the BC matrix through their surface functional groups, seamless incorporation into polymeric systems is achieved9,17. Additionally, chlorophyll (Chl), a naturally abundant pigment and photosensitizer, can extend light absorption into the visible region and promote charge separation through photoinduced electron transfer (PET)18–21.

Despite these advantages, the synergistic integration of CQDs, BC, and Chl into a single nanocomposite system for antibiotic degradation has not been extensively studied. Existing reports have focused primarily on CQD-modified semiconductors or cellulose-based supports, but the combination of CQDs and natural photosensitizers within a biodegradable support matrix remains underdeveloped.

Here, we present a dual biomass valorization strategy by synthesizing CQDs from sugarcane bagasse and extracting Chl from sugarcane leaves, thereby integrating two sugarcane residues into a single nanocomposite. Previous reports have demonstrated CQDs derived from bagasse22 and chlorophyll extracted from sugarcane leaves23 separately, but their combined utilization in one photocatalytic material has been rarely addressed. In particular, the role of FRET interactions between CQDs and Chl in improving charge carrier dynamics has not been systematically addressed, nor has the multifunctional role of BC in stabilizing the composite, enriching pollutant pre-concentration, and enhancing reusability been fully elucidated. his knowledge gap provides an opportunity to design multifunctional photocatalysts that unite sustainable synthesis, synergistic charge transfer, and adsorption–photocatalysis coupling.

In response to these challenges, this work reports the synthesis and comprehensive evaluation of a novel bio-derived nanocomposite (CQDs–Chl/BC NCs) designed for efficient antibiotic degradation under solar irradiation. The strategy integrates dual biomass valorization by synthesizing CQDs from sugarcane bagasse and extracting Chl from sugarcane leaves, with BC serving as a biodegradable support matrix. This design not only provides a sustainable synthesis pathway but also leverages FRET to extend light absorption and accelerate charge separation, thereby enhancing photocatalytic efficiency. The CQDs–Chl/BC NCs were systematically characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), photoluminescence (PL), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Photocatalytic performance was assessed through degradation kinetics, stability, and reusability studies under solar irradiation, and a mechanistic model was proposed to elucidate the roles of energy transfer, electron–hole interactions, and reactive radical formation in driving enhanced photocatalysis.

The originality of this work lies not in the preparation of CQDs or Chl individually, but in their synergistic integration with BC to form a multifunctional nanocomposite that unites waste valorization with advanced energy-transfer pathways. Dual photophysical enhancement via FRET and PET collectively broaden the light absorption range, accelerate charge separation, and suppress recombination, while the BC matrix contributes abundant hydroxyl groups and a porous network that stabilize the hybrid and enrich pollutant pre-concentration through adsorption. Unlike previously reported cellulose- or CQD-based photocatalysts, which typically rely on a single biomass precursor or lack defined charge-transfer mechanisms, the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs integrate sustainable feedstocks with adsorption–photocatalysis coupling to deliver a high-performance system. As a result, the composites achieved reproducible near-complete degradation of CIP and OTC within 180 min (CIP: 98.83 ± 0.46%; OTC: 98.59 ± 0.50% at 2 mg/L), while maintaining a high rate constant, strong stability over five cycles, and low material cost. These attributes collectively establish CQDs–Chl/BC NCs as a sustainable and efficient photocatalyst that surpasses previously reported cellulose-based systems in both efficiency and environmental relevance.

Method

Chlorophyll extraction

Chlorophyll was extracted from sugarcane leaves using an ethanol-based method24. The sugarcane leaves were first washed and air-dried at room temperature. After drying, they were chopped using a blender. A total of 20 g of the sliced leaves was weighed and mixed with 80 mL of ethanol. The mixture was then subjected to sonication for 30 min, the final concentrations of 55.6 mg/L. After extraction, the solution was filtered using filter paper, and the obtained chlorophyll extract was stored in a refrigerator, protected from light exposure.

Synthesis of bacterial cellulose (BC)

BC was synthesized by cultivating Komagataeibacter xylinusbacteria, following the methodology described in our previous study15. Briefly, the culture medium is prepared using two types: Glucose Yeast Extract Broth (GYEB) and Glucose Yeast Extract Agar (GYEA). In GYEB, 100 g of glucose and 10 g of yeast are combined, dissolved in 1000 mL of water, and a 50 mL portion is sealed in a glass bottle for autoclaving at 115 ◦C for 25 min. GYEA involves weighing yeast, agar, and glucose into a purple-shaped bottle, autoclaving and cooling before adding calcium carbonate.

Following medium preparation, bacterial cultivation is carried out through two methods: liquid culture and gel-based culture. In liquid culture, 1 mL of bacterial culture is pipetted into a 50 mL glass bottle with GYEB, loosely sealed, and incubated at 30 ◦C for 14 days. Gel-based culture in purple-shaped bottles involves transferring bacterial colonies to GYEA, autoclaving, cooling, and incubating with shaking at 30 ◦C for two days. The subsequent step, harvesting and cleaning, specifically for glass bottles, includes aspirating bacterial cellulose, boiling it in filtered water at 100 ◦C for an hour (repeated twice), soaking it in sodium hydroxide solutions of 0.5 M for 15 min and 1.25 M for 24 h, and finally rinsing until the bacterial cellulose turns white.

Synthesis of CQDs from sugarcane bagasse by microwave-assisted method

CQDs were synthesized from sugarcane bagasse using a microwave-assisted method, following the procedure outlined in our previous study22. Briefly, 500 mg sugarcane bagasse was mixed with 500 mg of citric acid (CA) in 25 mL of deionized water. The pH was then brought to 7 by adding ammonia (NH3) under magnetic stirring to obtain a uniform solution. Importantly, ammonia served not only as a pH adjuster but also as a nitrogen source, enabling the incorporation of N functionalities into the CQDs during microwave irradiation. This mixture was irradiated in a household microwave at 950 W for 10 min, followed by 30 min of sonication to enhance dispersion, and subsequently filtered through filter paper to isolate CQDs.

Fabrication of BC-based nanocomposites (CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs)

The process for fabricating CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs involved integrating CQDs and Chl into the BC matrix. First, BC was cut into dimensions of 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 mm an average weight of about 0.5 g. To prepare CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs, the following solutions were prepared: 3 mL of 10% w/v PVA was added to 1 mL of 10 mg/L CQDs, and 1 mL of 10 mg/L Chl, respectively. BC was then added to each solution. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 1 h, followed by gentle stirring at 50 °C for another 12 h under dark conditions to ensure proper integration of the components.

Photocatalytic activity

The BC, CQDs (10 mg/L), Chl (10 mg/L), CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs were used as catalyst, to study the effect of adsorption-catalytic process of each component. Each component size is 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 mm an average weight of about 0.5 g. A 20 mL solution of ciprofloxacin (CIP) and oxytetracycline (OTC) at concentrations of 2, 5, and 10 mg/L was prepared and kept in the dark for 1 h to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The photocatalytic experiments were then performed at neutral pH and temperature 27 °C under a Peccell solar simulator (PEC-L011) for 3 h. The neutral pH condition was selected based on earlier studies9, which reported that a near-neutral environment (around pH 6) promotes CIP and OTC degradation more effectively than acidic or alkaline conditions. A control test in the absence of catalyst was also performed for comparison. The degradation of CIP was monitored by recording UV–Vis spectra using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer, with concentration changes evaluated at the maximum absorption wavelength of 278 nm. After each run, the photocatalyst was retrieved, rinsed with deionized water, and reused for up to five successive cycles to evaluate its stability and reusability. Biotoxicity assessment on Escherichia coli

The biotoxicity of the samples was evaluated by monitoring the growth of Escherichia coli K-12 (CGMCC 1.365) exposed to (i) CQDs–Chl/BC NCs (M1), (ii) the soaking solution of CQDs–Chl/BC NCs in deionized water (M2), (iii) the photocatalytic degradation products of CIP (M3), and (iv) a control. The evaluation followed established toxicity assessment protocols reported in the literature25. Biotoxicity was inferred from the number of E. coli colonies formed on LB agar plates, where the detoxification efficiency of each sample was inversely proportional to the extent of E. coli growth inhibition relative to the control.

Result and discussion

The UV-Vis spectra, shown in Fig. 1, revealed distinct absorption peaks that correlated with the optical properties of the CQDs, Chl, and BC-based nanocomposites. Figure 1a illustrated the absorbance spectrum of CQDs derived from sugarcane bagasse. The characteristic peak observed in the UV region at 235 nm was attributed to π-π* transitions, which indicated the presence of conjugated C = C bonds within the CQDs. Additionally, a shoulder near 335 nm was observed, which suggested the presence of n-π* transitions, indicating the conjugation of C = O bond17,26,27. These results were consistent with the structural features of CQDs, confirming their conjugated nature and potential for electronic transitions in the UV range.

UV-Vis absorption spectra of (a) CQDs derived from sugarcane bagasse, (b) chlorophyll extracted from sugarcane leaves, and (c) BC-based NCs.

Chl was selected as a natural porphyrinic photosensitizer because its strong Soret and Qy absorptions overlap the emission of our solar simulator, and its excited-state energetics enable electron injection into CQDs while promoting ROS formation. Beyond light harvesting, the conjugated macrocycle facilitates π–π interactions with CIP, increasing local pollutant concentration at the catalyst surface. As shown from UV-Vis spectra (Fig. 1b) that the prominent peaks observed at approximately 419 nm and 436 nm were assigned to the Soret band, a distinctive feature of chlorophyll molecules that was linked to π-π* transitions in the conjugated porphyrin ring. Furthermore, the Q-band absorption peaks at around 613 nm, 615 nm, 669 nm, and 671 nm were attributed from π–π* transitions that excite electrons from the ground state to the lowest singlet excited state, a highly delocalized structure that enabled efficient absorption of high-energy visible photons from sunlight28–30. This characteristic emphasized the crucial role of Chl-a in light absorption for photosynthesis and its effectiveness as a photoreceptor.

Figure 1c presented the UV-Vis absorption spectra of synthesized BC-based NCs, including BC, CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs. BC showed strong UV absorption (~ 200–350 nm) but limited visible light absorption. Upon incorporation of CQDs, a slight enhancement in absorption was observed in the UV and lower visible region (~ 300–500 nm), which was attributed to the light absorption properties of CQDs. These properties were attributed to π-π* transitions and defect-related absorption26. The introduction of Chl further extended the absorption range into the visible region, particularly in the Soret band and Q-band, confirming the successful integration of Chl into the composite and enabling improved light-harvesting capabilities. Notably, the CQDs-Chl/BC NCs demonstrated the most extensive absorption range, covering both the UV and visible spectra. The enhanced absorption in the blue (419 and 436 nm) and red (615 and 671 nm) regions suggested that light utilization was efficiently increased, which was expected to improve photocatalytic performance. The synergistic effect between CQDs and Chl in the BC matrix was found to enhance charge separation and extend the photoresponse, making the CQDs-Chl/BC NCs a promising material for visible-light-driven photocatalytic applications.

Figure S1 displayed the fluorescence of the catalyst under 395 nm UV light. BC (a) shows no detectable emission, while Chl/BC (b) also exhibits negligible fluorescence due to the limited light-harvesting ability of Chl alone in this matrix. In contrast, CQDs/BC (c) displays a clear bluish-green fluorescence, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs (d) show the most intense and uniform emission, confirming that only the CQDs-containing composites exhibit significant fluorescence under UV excitation. The strong luminescence of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs indicates the successful integration of CQDs and Chl into the BC framework.

Following the UV-Vis analysis, the FTIR spectra shown in Fig. 2 were analyzed to provide further insights into the chemical interactions within the nanocomposites. The broad O-H stretching band around 3350 cm−127,31, observed in all samples, indicated strong hydrogen bonding between functional groups. A decrease in the intensity of the O-H stretching band from BC to CQDs-Chl/BC NCs suggested a reduction in free hydroxyl groups. This decrease was likely due to interactions between the hydroxyl groups of BC and the functional groups of CQDs and Chl, which could have hindered the availability of free hydroxyl groups32. It was postulated that the hydrophobic nature of the Chl porphyrin ring and the hydrophobic domains of the CQDs contributed to the observed reduction in the composite’s overall hydrophilicity, thereby altering the hydrogen-bonding network in BC.

FTIR spectra of BC, Chl/BC, CQDs/BC, and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

The presence of Chl was confirmed by a characteristic peak around 2925 cm−1, attributed to C-H stretching from methyl, methylene, or methine groups in Chl, which was observed in both the Chl/BC and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs spectra33. The C-H stretching vibration at 2348 cm−1, along with the C-H bending vibration at 1490 cm−1, provided additional evidence of Chl incorporation. The C = O stretching peak at ~ 1729 cm−1 was also characteristic of the carbonyl group in Chl, further supporting its integration into the composite. These findings were consistent with the characteristic UV-Vis absorption peaks at ~ 419, 436, 615, and 671 nm, which were attributed to π-π* electronic transitions in the porphyrin ring.

The C = C stretching band was observed at 1635 cm−1, indicative of conjugated π-systems, aligned with the enhanced visible-light absorption evidenced in the Chl/BC and CQDs-Chl/BC composites. This feature suggested that the incorporation of Chl contributed significantly to the composites’ visible-light absorption capacity. Additionally, the C-N stretching vibration at 1289 cm−1 and the C-O-C stretching vibration at 1045 cm−1 indicated interactions between nitrogen-containing functional groups of CQDs and the BC matrix. Overall, the FTIR and UV-Vis analysis complemented each other, confirming that incorporating CQDs and Chl on BC matrix effectively extends the optical absorption from the UV to the visible region.

PL spectra presented in Fig. 3 provided strong evidence for the successful incorporation of CQDs and Chl into the BC matrix. In Fig. 3a, CQDs exhibited a broad PL emission, which was typically attributed to quantum confinement effects and edge defects in the CQDs34. Additionally, the Chl spectrum showed a strong emission in the red region (600–700 nm), characteristic of its intrinsic fluorescence resulting from π-π* transitions within the Chl structure35. This emission is well known for Chl and reflects its unique light-harvesting properties. The PL spectra of BC-based NCs presented in Fig. 3b revealed a more detailed comparison of the interactions between CQDs and BC, as well as Chl and BC. The PL spectrum of CQDs/BC closely resembled that of pristine CQDs, although a new shoulder peak was observed. This indicated that the interaction between CQDs and BC likely led to the formation of new surface defects or the introduction of functional groups, which affected the emission profile. Similarly, the incorporation of Chl into BC resulted in a PL profile that was comparable to that of pristine Chl, suggesting minimal interaction or modification of the Chl structure within the composites.

PL spectra with an excitation wavelength of 365 nm: (a) in aqueous solutions of CQDs (black) and Chl (red) and (b) for BC-based NCs, including CQDs/BC (grey), Chl/BC (red), and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs (blue).

However, the PL spectrum of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs showed a noticeable shift in the emission spectrum, which suggested the occurrence of Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) between the CQDs and Chl. FRET was identified as a distinct mechanism of energy transfer between two molecules, known as fluorophores, which could be readily excited by photons35,36. The observed increase in PL intensity in CQDs-Chl/BC NCs compared to both Chl and CQDs was consistent with the expected conditions for FRET, where the energy from the CQDs donor was efficiently transferred to the Chl acceptor35,37,38. The enhancement in emission intensity of the acceptor (Chl) indicated that CQDs acted as an effective energy donor, facilitating the FRET process and enhancing the overall emission of the composite.

BET and BJH analyses were conducted to evaluate the textural properties of BC, CQDs/BC, Chl/BC, and CQDs–Chl/BC NCs. As shown in Figure S2a, all samples exhibited type IV N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms with distinct hysteresis loops at high relative pressure (p/p⁰ > 0.8), characteristic of mesoporous structures. Pristine BC displayed the highest specific surface area, reflecting its highly porous fibrous network. After incorporation of CQDs, Chl, or both, the surface area decreased slightly, which can be attributed to partial pore filling or surface coverage by these functional components.

The BJH pore size distribution curves (Fig. S2b) further supported this observation. All samples exhibited predominant pore sizes in the mesoporous region (< 30 nm). BC showed a broader pore distribution, consistent with its higher porosity, while CQDs–Chl/BC NCs exhibited a narrower and more uniform pore distribution with higher intensity in the small-pore region. This structural modification indicated that the integration of CQDs and Chl into the BC network not only reduced the total surface area but also improved mesopore accessibility, thereby enhancing contact between pollutants and active sites. Such features are highly advantageous for photocatalysis, as they promote rapid pollutant diffusion and strengthen adsorption–photocatalysis synergy, which is essential for the observed high degradation efficiency.

TEM analysis was performed to investigate the morphological and structural features of BC before and after the integration of CQDs and Chl, confirming the successful formation of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of CQDs, shown in Fig. 4a, displayed well-dispersed carbon quantum dots with an average particle size of 1.1 ± 0.4 nm. The inset revealed distinct lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.207 nm, corresponding to the (100) plane of graphitic carbon, confirming their graphitic nature and quantum confinement properties. These characteristics aligned with previous studies39,40. The CQDs appeared to be well-distributed without significant aggregation, suggesting effective synthesis and stabilization. TEM image of pristine BC, presented in Fig. 4b, revealed a highly porous, fibrous network, a characteristic that ensures a high surface area, mechanical flexibility, and enhanced adsorption capability. This interconnected fiber structure within the BC matrix facilitated mass transport and material dispersion, making it an ideal support material for nanocomposite fabrication. Additionally, Fig. 4b confirmed the entangled and layered fiber arrangement, with the fiber diameter measured at 37.6 ± 2.8 nm, as supported by particle size distribution analysis. The high aspect ratio of BC fibers suggested that they provided a robust structural matrix capable of supporting nanomaterial dispersion while maintaining high porosity. These morphological features are particularly advantageous for enhanced pollutant adsorption and catalyst stability, which are key factors in photocatalytic applications. Furthermore, elemental mapping confirmed the presence of carbon (C) and oxygen (O), reinforcing the organic composition of BC. These structural characteristics demonstrated the suitability of BC fibers as a support material in nanocomposite applications.

The electronic properties of BC-based NCs were investigated by XPS. In Fig. 7a, the C 1 s spectrum of BC exhibited characteristic peaks at 285.1 eV (C–C/C–H), 286.6 eV (C–OH/C–O–C), and 288.1 eV (COO), which were indicative of the hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups present in the cellulose structure41. Upon the incorporation of Chl into the BC matrix (Fig. 7b), the C 1 s spectrum revealed a shift in binding energies, with a new peak appearing at 289.3 eV, corresponding to the C = O bond. This shift indicated the introduction of additional oxygen-containing functional groups due to the carbonyl groups present in the Chl structure. The C = O bond in Chl is a key feature of its porphyrin ring and plays an essential role in the Chl’s light absorption and energy transfer capabilities. The presence of this functional group further enhances the interaction of Chl with the BC matrix, providing a more functionalized composite.

C 1 s spectra of (a) BC (b) Chl/BC (c) CQDs/BC (d) CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

In Fig. 7c, the C 1 s spectrum of the CQDs/BC composite showed a more pronounced contribution from oxygen-containing groups, with peaks at 284.9 eV (C–C/C–H), 286.5 eV (C–OH/C–O–C), 288.3 eV (O–C = O), and 289.2 eV (C = O)42,43. The addition of CQDs introduced a greater amount of oxygenated carbon species compared to BC alone, suggesting that CQDs were functionalized with oxygen-containing groups. These functional groups, particularly the C = O and O–C = O, likely arose from surface oxidation of the CQDs during synthesis or post-treatment processes. The increased presence of these oxygen functionalities can enhance the interaction between the CQDs and the BC matrix, which may improve the composite’s reactivity, stability, and potential for photocatalytic applications. In Fig. 7d, the C 1 s spectrum of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs showed a similar pattern to that of CQDs/BC, but with slight increase in peak intensities, particularly in the C–OH/C–O–C (286.3 eV) and C = O (289.2 eV) regions. The increase in the intensity of the C = O peak was attributed to the combined effects of the CQDs and Chl, both of which contain carbonyl functional groups. The CQDs contributed carbonyl groups from their surface functionalization, while the Chl contained carbonyl groups in the central ketone ring of its porphyrin structure, as well as an ester group in its phytol tail35. The enhanced C = O signal in the CQDs-Chl/BC NCs spectrum indicated a synergistic effect between the components, where the carbonyl groups of both CQDs and Chl played a crucial role in enhancing the composite’s overall electronic properties and surface chemistry.

The O 1 s spectra (Fig. 8) were carefully deconvoluted, and characteristic peaks corresponding to C(O*)O, C = O, –OH, and C–O species were identified across all samples. A distinct COO peak was observed at 534.8, 534.9, 534.7, and 534.9 eV for BC, Chl/BC, CQDs/BC, and CQDs–Chl/BC NCs, respectively, consistent with oxygen-containing functional groups also confirmed in the C 1 s spectra. The C = O peak appeared at 531.7 eV in Chl/BC, 531.8 eV in CQDs/BC, and 531.4 eV in CQDs–Chl/BC NCs, confirming the incorporation of Chl and CQDs, both of which introduce conjugated carbonyl and hydroxyl groups. The relative enhancement of these oxygenated species indicates increased capability for hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions, which can facilitate strong adsorption of antibiotic molecules. Moreover, the slight shifts in binding energies and the emergence of new oxygen functionalities were directly attributed to the incorporation of Chl and CQDs into the BC matrix, which supplied additional hydroxyl and carbonyl groups. These modifications are crucial for improving surface reactivity, enhancing charge transfer, and stabilizing ROS intermediates. The O 1 s results were consistent with the C 1 s spectra, confirming successful functionalization of BC with CQDs and Chl and supporting the proposed mechanism of adsorption–photocatalysis in the composites.

O 1 s spectra of (a) BC (b) Chl/BC (c) CQDs/BC (d) CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

The deconvoluted N 1 s spectra of Chl/BC (Fig. 9a) revealed the presence of multiple nitrogen species including peaks at 399.2 eV (Pyridinic N), 400.2 eV (Pyrrolic N), 401.4 eV (Graphitic N), 402.5 eV (Quaternary N), 404.3 eV (Oxidized N), and 407.0 eV (Chemisorbed N)44,45. The predominance of pyridinic and pyrrolic nitrogen suggested that nitrogen had been incorporated into aromatic structures and pyrrole-like configurations, contributing to charge transfer and electron-donating characteristics. The pyridinic nitrogen peak of Chl/BC exhibited a slight shift compared to CQDs/BC and CQDs-Chl/BC NCs, indicating possible interactions with vacancies or edge defects in the carbon lattice, which altered its binding energy46. The presence of oxidized and chemisorbed nitrogen species suggested surface functionalization due to Chl modification, which likely enhanced the nitrogen bonding diversity within the BC matrix. The chemisorbed nitrogen species at ~ 407 eV has been attributed to highly oxidized nitrogen species, such as nitrate (NO₃⁻) or nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) adsorbates, indicating strong interactions with oxygen46,47. Compared to chlorophyll-derived carbonized polymer dots synthesized by Liu et al.32, the pyridinic and pyrrolic peaks exhibited slight shifts appearing at 398.0 eV and 399.5 eV, respectively, while the graphitic nitrogen peak remained stable. The presence of quaternary and oxidized nitrogen species in this study, absent in Liu et al.‘s report, was attributed to unique modifications in nitrogen bonding.

N 1 s spectra of (a) Chl/BC (b) CQDs/BC (c) CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

The N 1 s spectrum of CQDs/BC (Fig. 9b) exhibited peaks at 398.7 eV (Pyridinic N), 400.2 eV (Pyrrolic N), 401.7 eV (Graphitic N), 402.8 eV (Quaternary N), 404.4 eV (Oxidized N), and 406.8 eV (Chemisorbed N)44,48. A shift in the pyridinic N peak to lower binding energy was observed, along with an increased intensity of pyrrolic and graphitic nitrogen, indicating enhanced nitrogen incorporation due to CQDs. The lower oxidized nitrogen content suggested that CQDs suppressed nitrogen oxidation, unlike Chl/BC, where higher oxidation levels were detected. The increase in graphitic and quaternary nitrogen implied that CQDs facilitated the formation of electron-donating species, thereby improving charge transfer properties. The presence of carboxyl groups in CQDs likely contributed to enhanced electron mobility, further reinforcing the role of graphitic nitrogen in conductivity improvements. These observations indicated that the nitrogen functionalities in CQDs played a key role in improving the electron transfer properties within the composite material49–51.

The N 1 s spectrum of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs (Fig. 9c) displayed peaks at 398.7 eV (Pyridinic N), 400.2 eV (Pyrrolic N), 401.8 eV (Graphitic N), 402.8 eV (Quaternary N), 404.6 eV (Oxidized N), and 406.0 eV (Chemisorbed N). The balanced nitrogen distribution in these composites suggested synergistic interactions between CQDs, Chl, and BC, leading to enhanced electronic stability. The lower oxidized nitrogen content compared to Chl/BC indicated that CQDs mitigated oxidation, likely by stabilizing electronic structures and limiting oxygen incorporation. This effect could be attributed to CQDs acting as electron donors, modifying the electronic environment, and preventing excessive oxidation. Additionally, CQDs might have influenced nitrogen bonding, altering the distribution of nitrogen species in a way that favored charge transport efficiency.

A comparative XPS analysis revealed distinct nitrogen functionalities across the composites. Chl/BC exhibited the highest oxidized and chemisorbed nitrogen content, indicating enhanced surface functionalization, while CQDs/BC had the highest graphitic and pyrrolic nitrogen content, improving electron mobility and charge transfer efficiency. CQDs-Chl/BC NCs showed a more uniform nitrogen distribution with reduced oxidation, suggesting a stabilized electronic structure. These findings confirmed that CQDs enhanced nitrogen doping, stabilized electronic properties, and improved charge mobility, making CQDs-Chl/BC NCs a promising candidate for photocatalytic applications.

The adsorption–photocatalytic performance of BC-based NCs toward CIP and OTC was evaluated at neutral pH and 25 °C. A photolysis blank (no catalyst) was first performed to isolate light-only effects. As shown in Fig. 10a, c, CQDs and Chl individually outperformed BC and photolysis yet remained modest in activity. In contrast, CQDs–Chl/BC achieved the highest rates, with apparent pseudo-first-order constants of 0.02642 min−1 (CIP) and 0.02849 min−1 (OTC) (Table S2), consistent with improved light harvesting and FRET/PET-assisted charge separation, while BC provided adsorption pre-concentration and structural support.

Photocatalytic degradation kinetics were obtained under identical conditions (neutral pH, 25 °C). For CIP: (a) − ln(C/C₀), (b) C/C₀. For OTC: (c) − ln(C/C₀), (d) C/C₀.

The corresponding C/C0​ profiles (Fig. 10b, d) showed that all samples were equilibrated in the dark for 60 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium, evidenced by the flat baselines from − 60 to 0 min. Upon illumination, C/C0​ decreased sharply, confirming photocatalysis as the dominant removal pathway. BC alone exhibited measurable dark adsorption but negligible photoactivity, in line with its role as a porous, hydrophilic support15. CQDs displayed intrinsic photocatalytic activity via light absorption and charge generation associated with π–π* transition of the sp2C = C double bonds within their inner core52, though performance was limited by recombination. Chl showed moderate activity by absorbing visible light and mediating electron transfer28, but was likewise constrained by charge-separation inefficiency. Their co-integration within BC mitigated these limitations and yielded the highest pseudo-first-order rate constant.

PVA was employed primarily as a binder/matrix stabilizer rather than as a major adsorption site. Through hydrophilic − OH groups, a hydrated, porous network was formed that improved mass transport and exposed Chl and CQDs to solution. By contrast, Chl (extended π-conjugation, polar substituents) and CQDs interacted more strongly with CIP/OTC than PVA via π–π stacking with aromatic rings and hydrogen-bonding/electrostatic contacts with carboxylate/amine groups. Previous studies have shown that the aromatic system of Chl enhances the affinity of materials toward organic molecules via such noncovalent interactions24. Beyond adsorption, Chl contributed visible-light harvesting and CQDs broadened absorption and assisted interfacial charge transfer, jointly supporting photocatalysis.

Spectral evolution further corroborated the concentration dependence of CQDs–Chl/BC performance. At 2 mg/L, the diagnostic bands of CIP (Fig. S3a) and OTC (Fig. S3d) had nearly vanished by 120–180 min, indicating high efficiency, whereas at 5 and 10 mg/L (Figs. S3b, c, e, f) band decay was slower and residual absorbance persisted after irradiation. The decline with increasing C0 was attributed to (i) inner-filter/light screening, whereby higher solution absorbance reduces the photon flux reaching the catalyst, and (ii) active-site/adsorption saturation, whereby a fixed population of reactive sites is increasingly competed for at larger C0​. Triplicate runs under identical conditions with the CQDs–Chl/BC catalyst confirmed excellent reproducibility: CIP showed 99.21% removal (SD = 0.13; %RSD = 0.13%) and OTC showed 98.10% removal (SD = 0.50; %RSD = 0.51%), both well within the customary ≤ 2% criterion (Table S2). Under identical conditions, OTC was degraded faster than CIP. The higher lability of OTC was considered to arise from its multiple hydroxyl/amide groups and more extended conjugation, which favored interaction with reactive oxygen species and facilitated bond cleavage. By contrast, the rigid fluoroquinolone core and fluorine substituent in CIP were understood to impart greater oxidative resistance. Collectively, the 2 mg/L case highlights the composite’s intrinsic photocatalytic potential by enabling superior light penetration and site utilization. Thus, photocatalytic efficiency was governed jointly by composite design (CQDs–Chl synergy within BC) and pollutant structure, with CQDs–Chl/BC NCs delivering the most effective removal. The superior performance of CQDs–Chl/BC NCs is attributed to synergistic light harvesting and interfacial charge separation within the BC scaffold: Chl functions as a visible-light photosensitizer, while CQDs provide broadband absorption and electron mediation. FRET from CQDs to Chl further broadens the excitation window and accelerates energy/charge transfer, suppressing recombination and enhancing reaction rates, consistent with FRET-assisted photocatalysis reports35. The BC network increases the effective adsorption area and maintains a hydrated, porous architecture that promotes mass transport and structural stability, collectively yielding superior photocatalytic activity.

The reusability of the CQDs–Chl/BC NC photocatalyst was evaluated over five successive cycles for CIP and OTC degradation (Fig. 11a). After each cycle, the material was collected, washed with deionized water, and reused. A progressive decline in activity was observed, with the fifth-cycle degradation efficiencies decreasing to 67.7% (CIP) and 65.2% (OTC). This loss was attributed to partial loss of active material during recovery/washing and gradual deactivation of surface-active sites. In particular, chlorophyll is known to undergo photo-oxidative degradation under repeated irradiation, diminishing light-harvesting capacity; in parallel, prolonged exposure likely induced partial photobleaching of both CQDs and Chl. Minor leaching and/or surface modification of Chl and CQDs during the washing steps may also have reduced the population of active charge-transfer sites over time18,33. Despite this performance decay, the composite retained its macroscopic integrity throughout the tests, indicating good chemical stability and suggesting viability for repeated use in photocatalytic applications.

CQDs-Chl/BC NCs for 5 cycles (a) Reusability (b) FTIR spectra.

To probe structural stability and functional-group integrity during cycling, FTIR spectra were recorded after five consecutive runs of CIP and OTC degradation (Fig. 11b). Prior to photocatalysis, relatively low band intensities were observed, including a feature at ~ 2348 cm−1, assignable to adsorbed CO₂ or other volatile surface species; this band disappeared after reaction, consistent with release or transformation during photocatalysis. Post-reaction spectra exhibited clear intensity increases in the O–H/N–H stretching region (3200–3400 cm−1) and in bands associated with C = O (1729 cm−1), C = C (1635 cm−1), C–N (1289 cm−1), and C–O–C (1045 cm−1). These enhancements indicated adsorption of CIP/OTC-derived functionalities onto the CQDs–Chl/BC matrix via hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and other noncovalent forces. The spectral evolution supported an adsorption-led process that promoted intimate contact between pollutant molecules and reactive sites, thereby facilitating subsequent oxidative degradation.

Post-cycle XPS further revealed pollutant-dependent surface modification of the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs (Fig. S5). In C 1 s, both systems showed increased contributions from oxygenated carbon species after photocatalysis, consistent with surface oxidation and functional-group transformation. The N 1 s region (Fig. S5c, d) displayed components assignable to pyridinic N (~ 398 eV), pyrrolic N (~ 399 eV), graphitic N (~ 401 eV), quaternary N (~ 402 eV), oxidized N (~ 404–405 eV), and chemisorbed N (~ 406 eV); subtle intensity variations between CIP and OTC suggested involvement of nitrogen functionalities in electron transfer and pollutant binding, with CIP exhibiting slightly higher quaternary/oxidized N. The O 1 s spectra (Fig. S5e, f) comprised O = C (~ 531 eV), –OH (~ 532–533 eV), C–O (~ 534 eV), and carboxyl-related oxygen (~ 535 eV); post-reaction increases, particularly in hydroxyl and carbonyl components, were consistent with reactive oxygen species (ROS) participation in the degradation pathway. Collectively, these results confirmed active adsorption–oxidation on the composite surface and indicated modest pollutant-specific differences arising from distinct molecular structures and degradation intermediates.

The active species involved in the photocatalytic degradation by CQDs-Chl/BC NCs under solar simulator irradiation for 3 h were investigated using scavenger tests (Fig. 12). Without any scavenger, the degradation efficiency reached 99.2%. The addition of IPA (•OH scavenger) significantly reduced efficiency to 39.2%, indicating that hydroxyl radicals are the main reactive species. EDTA (h+ scavenger) and BQ (O₂•⁻ scavenger) decreased the efficiency to 60.6% and 81.3%, respectively, suggesting that holes and superoxide radicals also contribute but to a lesser extent. Overall, •OH radicals play the dominant role in the photocatalytic activity of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

Scavenger test of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs.

The biotoxicity of the prepared samples was evaluated using E. coli colony count analysis (Table S5 and Fig. S6). E. coli was selected as the test strain due to its well-established sensitivity to a wide range of toxic compounds and its common use as a model microorganism in biotoxicity studies. The control sample exhibited a colony density of 2.0 × 104 CFU m/L, representing normal bacterial growth in the absence of treatment. For the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs (M1) and the DI water–soaked CQDs–Chl/BC NCs (M2), colony counts of 1.2 × 104 CFU m/L and 4.6 × 104 CFU m/L, respectively, were observed. These values were comparable to or even higher than the control, indicating that neither the composite itself nor any leached components from the material exhibited significant antimicrobial activity.

In contrast, the photocatalytic degradation product of CIP (M3) showed a substantial reduction in microbial load, with a colony count of 1.1 × 103 CFU m/L—corresponding to approximately a 10-fold decrease relative to the untreated control. Since no inhibition was observed for M1 or M2, the reduced colony density in M3 was attributed to the presence of partially degraded CIP products rather than intrinsic toxicity of the catalyst. Importantly, the absence of antibacterial activity in the pristine CQDs–Chl/BC NCs confirmed that the composite itself was environmentally benign. Therefore, once photocatalysis achieves complete or near-complete degradation, the catalyst can enable a safe release pathway for treated water into aquatic environments, thereby safeguarding microbial balance and minimizing ecological risks.

The LC–MS spectra of CIP after photocatalytic with CQDs–Chl/BC NCs (Fig S4a and S4c) reveals a distinct fragment that provide insight into the degradation pathway. The absence of the molecular ion of CIP at m/z 332 confirms that the parent antibiotic was almost completely degraded under the reaction conditions. Several prominent low-mass peaks, including m/z 58, 98, and 120, can be assigned to the cleavage products of the piperazine ring, which is known to be highly susceptible to oxidative attack by reactive oxygen species. The observed peaks closely match those reported in prior studies on CQDs/TiO2 catalysts22. Overall, the dominance of low-m/z ions together with the diminished intensity of mid-range fragments demonstrates that the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs facilitates rapid piperazine cleavage followed by progressive oxidation of the aromatic system, driving CIP toward smaller organic species.

The mass fragmentation pathway of OTC, (m/z 461 [M + H]+) proceeds through a series of sequential cleavages that reflect its polycyclic and highly functionalized structure (Fig S4b and S4d). The first stage involves the formation of a near-parent fragment at m/z 453, which likely arises from a minor neutral loss or dehydrogenation while maintaining the tetracyclic backbone. Further dealkylation or dehydration generates the m/z 437 fragment, indicating additional weakening of the dimethylamino substituent and side-chain functionalities. A more substantial structural change occurs with the appearance of the m/z 279 ion, which results from retro-cleavage of the tetracyclic scaffold, producing a stable two-ring fragment. Continued breakdown yields the m/z 201 ion, corresponding to a smaller single-ring fragment stabilized by oxygenated groups. At later stages, extensive degradation of these intermediate fragments leads to the production of low-mass ions (m/z 114, 85, and 58), which are typical of deep fragmentation involving loss of CO, CO₂, H₂O, or small hydrocarbon units. Overall, the fragmentation pathway reflects progressive dealkylation, decarbonylation, and ring scission processes, with the early stages (453 and 437) retaining much of the parent structure and the later stages (279, 201, and low-mass ions) reflecting substantial breakdown of the tetracycline core. These results confirm that the catalyst not only decomposes the parent molecule but also promotes advanced oxidation processes that favor extensive degradation and potential mineralization.

The comparison of cellulose-based catalysts for antibiotic degradation (Table 1) clearly highlighted the superior performance of the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs, which achieved 98.83 ± 0.46% CIP and 98.59 ± 0.50% OTC removal at 2 mg/L under solar simulation within 180 min. It should be noted that degradation efficiencies are influenced by experimental conditions, including pollutant concentration, pH, irradiation source, and reaction time, and therefore direct comparisons must be interpreted cautiously. The remarkable efficiency of the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs was attributed to the synergistic contribution of adsorption and photocatalysis. The BC matrix provided abundant hydroxyl groups and a porous network that facilitated strong adsorption of antibiotic molecules, while the incorporation of CQDs and Chl significantly enhanced photocatalytic activity by improving visible-light absorption and accelerating charge transfer. In contrast, the Cellulose/MMO/PVA biosorbent achieved only 83.0% removal, primarily through adsorption, with minimal photocatalytic contribution. The CuFe₂O₄@methyl cellulose (MC) composite reached 80.7% degradation under UV-C light within 90 min, but its reliance on high-energy UV irradiation limited its solar applicability. Similarly, the Ni/NiO-doped chitosan–cellulose catalyst achieved 87.0% removal under sunlight in 20 min, demonstrating rapid degradation but lacking sustainability in terms of precursor cost and stability. By comparison, the enhanced performance of the CQDs–Chl/BC NCs was primarily associated with efficient charge separation and FRET–sensitized fluorescence enhancement between CQDs and Ch35, which broadened light absorption and promoted effective photocatalysis. The BC network further supported this process by adsorbing antibiotic molecules and ensuring intimate pollutant–catalyst contact, thereby reinforcing the overall degradation efficiency.

The proposed mechanism for the enhanced photocatalytic activity of CQDs-Chl/BC NCs was outlined as follows in Fig. 13. Under solar irradiation, the photocatalytic process was initiated as Chl was excited from its ground state (S0) to its first excited singlet state (S1) and subsequently underwent intersystem crossing (ISC) to form the triplet state (3Chl)53,54. This well-established photophysical behaviour, characteristic of natural photosynthesis, was identified as the critical first step in the cascade of energy and electron transfer events within the composites.

Proposed charge transfer mechanism in the CQDs-Chl/BC NCs under light irradiation.

In the triplet state, excited Chl acted as an effective electron donor, transferring stored energy non-radiatively to the CQDs. This photoinduced electron transfer (PET) played a crucial role in stabilizing the photogenerated charge carriers and minimizing recombination losses, thereby ensuring a higher availability of electrons for subsequent redox reactions involved in the CIP and OTC degradation. In parallel, the CQDs were directly excited by solar photons, particularly in the ultraviolet range, which generated additional electron–hole pairs via π–π* transitions in their sp2 carbon cores35. The resulting emission spectrum significantly overlapped with the absorption bands of Chl, thereby facilitating an efficient FRET from the CQDs (donors) to the Chl molecules (acceptors). This energy transfer was optimized by the close spatial proximity and proper alignment of the CQDs and Chl within the BC matrix. Superoxide radicals (·O₂⁻) were produced by the generated electrons and subsequently used in the reduction of CIP and OTC, while oxidation reactions were carried out by the photogenerated holes to oxidize water or other species, resulting in the formation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) that further contributed to CIP and OTC degradation.

The BC matrix, derived from sustainable sources, provided a highly porous and hydrophilic support structure that ensured intimate contact between the CQDs and Chl. This integration not only enhanced the PET and FRET processes but also increased the adsorption of CIP and OTC molecules by concentrating them at the active catalytic sites. Consequently, the BC matrix was found to contribute significantly to both the structural stability and the overall photocatalytic efficiency of the composite, while also facilitating recovery and reuse over multiple treatment cycles.

The synergistic interactions among Chl, CQDs, and BC culminated in a highly efficient photocatalytic system for CIP and OTC degradation. Stabilized electrons, generated through both direct CQD excitation and FRET-mediated energy transfer, were utilized in reduction reactions that cleaved the molecular bonds of CIP, while the photogenerated holes oxidized water to produce hydroxyl radicals that further enhanced pollutant degradation. This integrated mechanism, which effectively minimized charge recombination and photocorrosion, resulted in an impressive 99.2% degradation of CIP under solar-driven conditions within 180 min, with only a slight reduction in efficiency after multiple cycles. These findings underscored the potential of the composite system as a sustainable, green solution for advanced wastewater remediation.

It was also recognized that the presence of foreign species in real wastewater could influence photocatalytic degradation. Inorganic anions such as Cl−, SO42−, CO32−, and HCO3− may compete for adsorption sites or act as radical scavengers, thereby quenching ROS and decreasing degradation efficiency, while cations such as Na+ and Ca2+ may alter surface charge interactions at the catalyst–pollutant interface. In addition, natural organic matter (NOM) may compete for active sites and absorb incident photons, further suppressing photocatalytic activity. As the present work was conducted in model solutions to establish the intrinsic adsorption–photocatalysis mechanism of CQDs–Chl/BC NCs, future studies will systematically evaluate their performance in complex matrices containing electrolytes and NOM, as well as in authentic wastewater samples. Such investigations will be essential to assess the practical viability of the CQDs–Chl/BC nanocomposites for large-scale water treatment applications.

Conclusion

CQDs–Chl/BC NCs were successfully synthesized through a green bio-construction strategy, producing a structurally stable and environmentally safe photocatalyst. The synergistic integration of CQDs and Chl within the BC matrix enhanced visible-light harvesting, facilitated charge separation through FRET/PET pathways, and coupled adsorption with photocatalysis, leading to highly efficient antibiotic degradation. The optimized composite achieved near-complete removal of ciprofloxacin and oxytetracycline within 180 min under solar irradiation. The nanocomposites also demonstrated excellent recyclability, maintaining high activity over five cycles with structural integrity confirmed by FTIR and XPS. Biotoxicity assays showed no intrinsic antibacterial effects, underscoring their environmental safety and suitability for aquatic applications. This confirms their potential for practical applications in wastewater treatment, offering a sustainable solution for environmental remediation.
