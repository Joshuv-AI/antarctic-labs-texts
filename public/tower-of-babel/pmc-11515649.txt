ER and doctors’ preparedness to manage status epilepticus: a multi-institutional survey from the Gaza Strip
Authors: Belal Aldabbour, Ayoub AbuNemer, Muhammed Ghazi Alfarra, Osama Aldabbour, Yousef Abu Zaydah, Haytham Abuzaid, Abd Al-Karim Sammour, Samah Elamassie, Ahmed Yassin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStatus epilepticus (SE) is a top neurological and medical emergency. Adequate staff knowledge and sufficient hospital resources are mandatory for timely management and better outcomes. This study aims to evaluate Palestinian ER doctors’ knowledge and beliefs towards SE and assess the state and availability of hospital resources needed to manage SE appropriately.MethodsThis cross-sectional study surveyed all ER doctors working in the six governmental hospitals in the Gaza Strip during June 2022. A questionnaire assessed the knowledge and beliefs toward SE and the practice of SE knowledge acquisition. Simultaneous field surveys determined the availability of essential medications and resources at each hospital and doctors’ actual awareness about the status of these resources. Linear regression determined predictors of higher knowledge scores.Results137 doctors participated (response rate 81%). Most were males, had graduated within six years of data collection, and 45.3% worked at Shifa Medical Complex. 63.5% of participants recognized the correct operational definition of status epilepticus. 85.4 and 83.9% recognized that it might cause permanent brain damage or death, respectively. Only 10.9% recognized the average mortality rate of a single convulsive SE episode. 44.5% believed that awaiting spontaneous SE resolution is a valid approach to management. Significant defects were noted in the awareness of subclinical SE, including its recognition and prevalence in ciritcally ill patients. Having a higher qualification and graduating from local medical schools predicted higher scores. In field surveys, EEG was unavailable at all hospitals, while CT scans and a neurologist physician were unavailable at two hospitals. IV phenytoin was the only second-line treatment available at all hospitals. IV valproate and levetiracetam were universally unavailable.ConclusionsThe survey revealed significant defects in Palestinian ER doctors’ knowledge and beliefs towards SE. Several hospitals also demonstrated a critical lack of resources to manage this condition appropriately. This should be addressed by the local health authorities to avoid potentially preventable SE-related mortality and morbidity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11792-5.Keywords: Status epilepticus; Emergency medical services; Health resources; Delivery of health care health knowledge, attitudes, practice; Developing nations

Background

Status epilepticus (SE) is a medical and neurological emergency that necessitates rapid intervention and prompt treatment [1]. It has long been recognized as a serious cause of morbidity and mortality. Babylonian tablets unearthed in Turkey in the 1950s described epileptic fits where the “possessing demon” possessed the patient many times. “It may go on for some time, but he will die,” the script concluded [2]. The International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) adopted the first definition of SE in 1964, which defined it as a “seizure [that] persists for a sufficient length of time or is repeated frequently enough to produce a fixed and enduring epileptic condition” [2]. Operationally, SE was first defined in 1993 as “a single epileptic seizure of more than 30 minutes duration or a series of epileptic seizures during which function is not regained between ictal events in a more than 30-minute period” [3].

The understanding that, on the molecular level, SE begins to cause neuronal damage very early in its course and the mounting evidence that associated worse prognosis to delayed initiation of SE treatment necessitated an updated operational definition with a shorter timeframe. In 2015, the ILAE designated two operational dimensions by which Generalized Convulsive Status Epilepticus “GCSE” is defined. The first is (t1), which is five minutes for GCSE and ten minutes for nonconvulsive/focal SE, represents the cutoff seizure duration beyond which spontaneous termination of epileptic activity is unlikely and which sets the threshold to begin the management. On the other hand, (t2), which is 30 minutes of continued seizure activity, is the duration that indicates a risk of long-term consequences such as permanent alteration of neuronal networks and function, neuronal damage, and neuronal death [1, 4, 5].

The annual incidence of SE episodes is between 1.29 and 81.3 per 100,000 population and is more frequent in older age groups [1, 6, 7]. The incidence also is generally believed to be higher in less developed countries [6]. The complications of status epilepticus are serious and diverse, and many are long-lasting. These include but are not limited to, brain edema, aspiration, brain anoxia, cardiac arrhythmias, hypoventilation, and sudden death [8]. Additionally, glutamate excitotoxicity during prolonged seizure activity can precipitate permanent neuronal damage that manifests as lasting cognitive deterioration, psychomotor impairment, or chronic refractory epilepsy [5]. The mortality rate for adults who present with the first episode of Generalized Convulsive Status Epilepticus (GCSE) varies depending on etiology and treatment setting. Still, the overall rate generally lies in the 10 to 20% range and is higher with older age and certain etiologies [8–10]. Undertreatment of SE is notoriously common, especially in the early stages, and has been reported in up to 80% of presentations in one study [11]. Considering these facts, the Gaza Strip’s 2.2 million population suffer hundreds to thousands of SE cases annually, with significant morbidity and mortality.

Medical Education in the Gaza Strip extends over six years and is divided into basic science (years 1–3) and clinical (years 4–6) levels, after which the graduate receives an M.D. degree from one of the two local medical schools. The Palestinian Board Program offers post-graduate training in several specialities, including the emergency medicine and internal medicine disciplines whose residents frequently cover medical emergency room (ER) services along with other general practitioners (M.D.s). ER doctors are the first to encounter patients presenting with SE and therefore must display a sense of urgency, sharp clinical skills, and sufficient knowledge. ERs must also be equipped with the medications and resources needed to guarantee timely recognition and management of SE and lessen mortality rates and the likelihood of complications.

The purpose of this study is twofold. First, it seeks to explore gaps in the knowledge and attitude of doctors working in the medical ERs of major hospitals in the Gaza Strip toward SE. Second, it seeks to evaluate the readiness of ERs in governmental hospitals in terms of the availability of medications and equipment necessary for the timely and effective management of patients presenting with SE. A literature review of related work revealed that no previous similar studies have been attempted before, neither in Palestine nor in any other country.

Methods

Design, population, and settings

This cross-sectional census survey recruited doctors (M.D. degree or higher) who worked at the emergency rooms (ER) of the six governmental hospitals in the Gaza Strip during the month of June 2022. These hospitals are the main providers of secondary and tertiary healthcare in the Gaza Strip [12]. The survey was conducted in the following centers: The Indonesian Hospital (Northern Governorate), Shifa Medical Complex (Gaza Governorate), Aqsa Hospital (Middle Governorate), Nasser and the European Hospitals (Khan Younis Governorate), and Najjar Hospital (Rafah Governorate). The study population was comprised of 143 doctors who worked at the ER across the six centers during the month of data collection.

Instrument development and validation

A structured questionnaire was developed for the purpose of conducting the present study (supplementary file). It consisted of four sections. The first section collected demographic information, including sex, age, university attended, year of graduation, years of experience, current work hospital, highest qualification, and a subjective estimation of the number of SE cases managed by the participant during the past year. The second section consisted of 20 questions created by two board-certified neurologists, which measured doctors’ knowledge about SE etiology, presentation, management, and complications. Eight of those questions simultaneously overlapped the knowledge (what participants know) and attitude (what they believe) constructs. The questions were all multiple-choice questions. Thirteen questions provided general statements with three possible answers (agree, disagree, I don’t know), while the remaining seven questions provided four choices with one correct answer. Correct answers were pre-determined by the neurologists. Each correctly answered question earned one mark, with possible total scores ranging (0–20). The third section comprised three questions that assessed the participants’ practice of acquiring and updating their knowledge about SE. The fourth section tested participants’ awareness about the availability of a list of resources and medications necessary for the effective management of SE. The answers were checked against the actual availability or lack of each item in each participant’s respective hospital, which was verified by the study team via field visits. Two expert neurologists confirmed the content validity of the questionnaire, and their comments were taken into consideration. Face validity was also ensured, including the aspects of questionnaire layout, logical construction, and the order of questions. The questions had to be obvious, simple, clear, and understandable, with minimal chances of misinterpretation. A pilot study was conducted on 10 doctors from another department, who completed the survey and returned feedback. The results of the pilot study were excluded from the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

After the assessment of the data, it was found to be nonparametric, so we applied nonparametric tests to the analysis. Categorical variables were described by frequencies and percentages. Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney tests were used to look for significant associations between categorically represented demographic variables and knowledge/attitude scores. Meanwhile, univariable and multivariable linear regression was used to detect the predictors of higher scores. Independent variables were sex, age, university attended, year of graduation, years of experience, current work hospital, highest qualification, and the number of SE cases treated annually. Statistical significance was set at a p-value less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2017. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the IRB at the Islamic University of Gaza (Approval number 2022/017). Administrative approvals were obtained from the Ministry of Health’s Human Resources Department. Written informed consent was obtained from participants. Data confidentiality was guaranteed and maintained throughout the collection and interpretation phases.

Results

Demographic characteristics of study participants

In total, 137 participants completed the survey (response rate 81%); most were males, young, and had graduated within six years of data collection. Nearly half had graduated from local medical schools. Also, nearly half had a clinical experience of less than two years. 45.3% worked in the emergency room at Shifa Medical Complex, the largest secondary and tertiary center in the Gaza Strip. The vast majority were general practitioners (M.D.) or residents, with only eight senior physicians who held board or master’s degrees. SE knowledge/attitude

Under two-thirds (63.5%) of participants recognized the current, time-based definition of an episode of status epilepticus, and only just over half (52.6%) knew that SE might present in a non-convulsive subclinical form (Nonconvulsive status epilepticus “NCSE”). Despite that most responders (83.9%) identified SE as a potential cause of mortality, only 10.9% correctly identified the average mortality rate of convulsive SE. The time-based treatment responsiveness of SE was acknowledged only by around two-thirds of participants, as 68.6% agreed that a better response could be expected with a sooner administration of medications, and 74.5% disagreed with the statement that medical history, physical examination, and investigations should be performed before starting management. On the other hand, 44.5% thought that patients with status epilepticus might be observed until convulsions stop spontaneously. Also, nearly a third believed that medical staff should try to open the patient’s mouth with a hard object during active seizure. In terms of drug knowledge, only half (50.4%) correctly identified the optimal first-line medication, and only 21.9% correctly recognized intravenous (IV) midazolam as the benzodiazepine with a third-line role in treatment. The practice of SE knowledge acquisition and update

Two-thirds of participants (66.4%) stated that they do not have access to printed or electronic SE treatment guidelines in their hospital. Also, only 31.4% stated that they were given teaching activities about SE management during the past year, while nearly a fifth (21.2%) were last educated about SE in medical school. On the other hand, just over half (55.5%) stated that they had revised SE and its management during the last year, and only 6.6% had not studied this subject for more than five years (Table 3).

Accessibility to resources and medications needed to manage SE and the staff’s awareness of their availability

Our survey of the hospitals revealed that none provided EEG services, while only 37.2% of participants recognized the lack of this facility in their hospitals. Two hospitals lacked both a neurologist physician and a functioning CT scan. Regarding medication, IV phenytoin and diazepam were available in the pharmacies of all six hospitals at the time of data collection. In contrast, IV sodium valproate and IV levetiracetam were universally unavailable, and IV midazolam was out of stock at one hospital (Al-Najjar Hospital) at the time of this study (Table 6).

Discussion

The present survey is the first attempt in the literature to evaluate doctors’ and hospitals’ readiness to manage SE using the KAP survey model. The study revealed major gaps in the knowledge and attitude of the doctors tasked with the first recognition and early management of SE in the ER. It also demonstrated a critical insufficiency in the resources and medications required to achieve the best outcomes.

The participants had serious lapses in their knowledge about the time-sensitive definition of SE and the need to actively manage SE in a timely manner. Studies have shown that seizures crossing the five-minute landmark are unlikely to remit spontaneously, and the likelihood of spontaneous remission was inversely related to seizure duration [13]. Studies on animal models and humans have also demonstrated the profound neuronal damage that SE may cause [14–17]. This damage could, in part, be due to the physiological and metabolic disturbances induced by SE, including hypoxia, hyperthermia, and cerebral blood flow alterations. Nonetheless, mounting evidence suggests that secondary brain damage is also an independent byproduct of SE itself [15, 18], mediated by the excessive action of glutamate on neurons, which promotes excitotoxicity and apoptosis [19]. These revelations presented the founding rationale behind the operational dimensions of SE known as t1 and t2.

Another major defect was noted in the awareness about NCSE, including its recognition as a clinical entity, its prevalence in critically ill patients with abnormal levels of consciousness, and the need to diagnose and treat it in refractory SE. It is well known that patients with refractory GCSE may present with minimal or no apparent motor activity, although the electroencephalograph (EEG) still reveals electrographic seizures [18]. In this clinical subset, the unabating electrical activity continues to cause neurotoxicity and incite permanent neuronal damage, necessitating aggressive and effective treatment similar to patients with visible convulsions [18].

Other identified defects were related to investigations and monitoring required during SE management. Hypoglycemia is an important cause of prolonged seizures. The first hypoglycemic seizure could progress to an overt SE in 32% of cases [20]. Thus, a finger-stick glucose check is an easily accessible investigation for rapidly determining hypoglycemia in patients with SE. Cardiac mentoring is also critical. Cardiac arrhythmias are frequent during and after seizures and in SE. They are also a potential cause of mortality in SE [21, 22] and are believed to be an important cause of Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) [23]. In one review, sinus tachycardia was present in 65.7% of SE presentations and bradycardia in 48.6%, while ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation and atrioventricular block were present in 11.4% and 2.9%, respectively [24]. Survivors of SE also remain at a significantly higher risk of arrhythmias up to 180 days after an SE episode is terminated [22].

The present study found significant qualitative and quantitative limitations in the drugs and facilities needed to manage SE appropriately. None of the surveyed facilities offered EEG services, which is critical for managing SE, especially in a critical care setting where third-line management takes place and where NCSE is common. Also, two hospitals lacked a neurologist. Similar critical limitations could be present in neighbouring countries or low-income countries from other regions as well, which invites further research. Equally concerning is the fact that a significant percentage of surveyed doctors were unaware of the availability of these items or lack thereof, which stipulates that appropriate use of these items will be less than possible and under than desired. In 2019, the ESETT trial was published, which was the first randomized controlled trial to compare second-line treatments of SE [25]. The study found no significant differences in the efficacy or adverse events between Fosphenytoin, Levetiracetam, and Valproic Acid [25]. This finding may be a relief to doctors practicing in poor or underserved areas with limited treatment options. Nonetheless, it is still important that the treating doctor has at his or her disposal the different treatment drugs as patient-related factors or side effect profiles might preclude some of those treatments in a portion of patients. It is also important to have adequate knowledge about the available drug(s), including their administration techniques and precautions. In this context, IV phenytoin was the only second-line drug available in all six of the surveyed hospitals. However, only half of the participants identified phenytoin as the second-line drug that requires cardiac monitoring. The FDA recommends continuous cardiac monitoring during and after phenytoin and fosphenytoin infusion [24], illustrating the importance of pinpointing this yet another gap in knowledge.

Finally, this study has implications for medical education curricula and teaching activities in the Gaza Strip. The majority of participants were recent graduates and board trainees who are expected to be up to date with current medical literature. Still, a significant number demonstrated knowledge gaps that need to be bridged. Also, an insufficient number of participants reported personally revising the topic of SE or receiving recent educational activities about it, which pinpoints gaps in their continuous medical education that should be addressed by training supervisors and the relevant Board committees. On the other hand, graduates of local medical schools and participants with higher degrees were found to possess a significantly higher level of SE knowledge compared to graduates from foreign schools and those with bachelor’s degrees. Despite the different gaps mentioned earlier, these correlations demonstrate a relative advantage of the education provided by the local schools and programs.

This study has several strengths. It is unique in that it is the first study to tackle its topic. It also recruited all doctors working at the ERs of the six major hospitals in the Gaza Strip, which empowers the results and permits the generalizability of its findings. The study also used combined methods that employed both questionnaire-based and field surveys, enriching the findings and reflecting the status of the hospitals and the workforce. On the other hand, the study lacked an observational arm to document participants’ practice in managing SE, which could provide deeper, much-needed insights that future studies might attempt to explore.

Conclusion

The study revealed deficiencies in the knowledge, awareness, and attitude of ER doctors managing patients with SE. It also touched base on the lack of ER resources needed for diagnosing and managing SE. Compounding the underdeveloped socioeconomic status and the underserved healthcare sector in Gaza, the findings of this study raise concerns that high estimates of SE incidence and mortality rates could be reasonable due to prevailing under-recognition and under-treatment. Measures like enhancing medical education about SE in medical schools, encouraging continuous learning and updates by ER doctors, increasing awareness about available tools and medications, and working to provide ERs with adequate resources will positively impact the management outcome of patients with SE in the Gaza Strip.
