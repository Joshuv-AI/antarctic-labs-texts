From submission to publication: An editor's perspective on why Eco-Environment & Health stands out
Authors: Li Li
Published: 2024

Each year, new academic journals emerge at an ever-expanding rate, making it increasingly difficult for authors to decide where to submit and publish their work. As a researcher myself, I too often face the challenge of selecting the “right” journal for my manuscripts, one that offers thoughtful, insightful, and respectful feedback to improve my manuscripts without the frustration of unqualified reviewers or hasty editorial decisions.

However, this ideal experience is not always realized. While peer review is meant to be a valuable process, it can sometimes be a source of frustration. For example, after a long waiting period, I may receive unfavorable feedback, including comments from reviewers who appear not to have thoroughly read or understood the manuscript, who lack the background or knowledgebase in my research area, or who even confuse it with other manuscripts on their desks.

The journal Eco-Environment & Health (EEH) is committed to changing this narrative. As an editor, I aim to handle each manuscript in the same considerate and rigorous manner that I would hope to experience for my own work, from submission to publication.

Upon receiving a manuscript, my fellow editors and I strive to provide a prompt desk review, seeking submissions with the potential for broad impact, general interest, and high quality and rigor. During the past years, more than 80​% of submissions to EEH were rejected without being sent out for peer review. Some papers are rejected due to significant errors, but more commonly, the reason is a lack of substantial novelty. Many manuscripts simply reiterate established findings that have been thoroughly documented in previous studies or even textbooks. Often, these studies apply new statistical techniques, rely on datasets from different populations, or employ visually appealing graphics. Notably, the increasing accessibility of computation tools (thanks to R, Python, and artificial intelligence, for instance) has indeed made it easier to conduct more sophisticated statistical analyses, handle larger datasets, and create more attractive visualizations. However, without new mechanistic insights, these efforts add minimal new value to the existing body of knowledge.

In many cases, manuscripts are rejected for their “patchwork” nature, as they compile elements that merely replicate or imitate existing studies or piece together findings that are already presented in previous publications. While these manuscripts may pretend to be exploring a new chemical pollutant in a less studied location or condition, they simply reiterate well-known information and fail to provide any fresh insights that would be relevant to a global audience. A typical example is the “tedious” combination of routine environmental monitoring, basic exposure dose calculations, and standard risk assessment approaches to show the occurrence of a new chemical pollutant in a specific location and its impact on a specific population. The environmental engineering field is also abundant in submissions that reassemble multiple previously published reaction mechanisms or degradation pathways to demonstrate the breakdown of a specific chemical under specific conditions, where no novel degradation pathways were identified or involved. Similarly, many environmental toxicology manuscripts reassemble multiple established toxicity mechanisms or signaling pathways to illustrate the toxicity of a particular chemical on a specific animal species or a specific cell line. These manuscripts are case studies to merely reaffirm established knowledge; however, they themselves do not establish any new knowledge.

Naturally, it is exciting when we find novel and impactful submissions. When this happens, my fellow editors and I solicit comments from at least three reviewers, but often more, to ensure we receive at least three thoughtful, insightful, and respectful evaluations. We greatly value detailed, constructive feedback from professional reviewers, even when a reviewer disagrees with others.

However, an editor's role is not merely to tally reviewer votes, where for example two recommendations for “minor revisions” outweigh a “rejection” or a single “rejection” automatically leads to a manuscript's rejection. Instead, an editor must apply independent judgment and scientific discernment, particularly when faced with conflicting or contrasting reviews.

More importantly, an editor should avoid passing on superficial or unhelpful reviews, such as comments lacking academic rigor, focusing on trivial or irrelevant aspects of the manuscript, or even generated by Artificial Intelligence, to authors because these only serve to frustrate the authors. In addition, an editor should be vigilant against “coerced citation”, where a reviewer requests citations to their own work without clear relevance. In such cases, the editor must assess whether the citations are justified and necessary, and if not, inform the authors that they are not required to include them.

In many instances, my fellow editors and I provide thorough feedback beyond simply copying and pasting reviewer comments or offering general critiques like “out of the journal's scope” or “lacking novelty”. We understand that lead authors often need detailed input to assess their contributions to the academic community, while graduate students and early-career researchers benefit from such feedback to improve their work.

After the reviewers are satisfied with the revisions, my fellow editors and I often have another thorough read to catch any remaining errors before sending the final acceptance email. Personally, I enjoy including a congratulatory message in the “editor's comments to authors” section of the acceptance mail, as this marks the most rewarding moment in the publication process. However, this is not the end. The baton is then passed to our colleagues who promote the manuscript on social media to ensure it reaches a wider audience.

As editors, we are like midwives, welcoming the birth of a new paper. We strive to provide efficient, professional, fair, and respectful support while also showing empathy and understanding to the authors. Through the collaborative efforts of our editorial team and reviewers, we hope you find our EEH journal to be the “right” platform for publishing your research.
