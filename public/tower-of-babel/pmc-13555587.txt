A primary care‐led school‐centered model for epilepsy detection and care among children in India
Authors: Sulena Sulena, Gagandeep Singh, Akanksha Nagar, Preeti Padda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe evaluated whether a primary care‐led, school‐centered epilepsy detection model, delivered through India's national school health program, Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK), could improve childhood epilepsy detection and the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of teachers and primary healthcare providers.MethodsA hybrid effectiveness‐implementation study using the Medical Research Council framework was conducted in Faridkot district, Punjab, India. A multidisciplinary team co‐designed a structured epilepsy education program (SEEP) delivered via a three‐tier cascade training model, culminating in RBSK Mobile Health Teams training primary healthcare providers (PHCPs), Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs), and Anganwadi workers, and government school teachers. Epilepsy detection was compared between pre‐intervention (2019–2020) and post‐intervention (2022–2023) using RBSK Child Health Registers, with screen‐positive children confirmed via EEG/MRI, acknowledging pandemic‐related confounding. KAP was assessed using validated questionnaires pre‐ and post‐training; focus group discussions and interviews explored feasibility and acceptability.ResultsThe intervention reached 1994 teachers (82%) across 398 schools (96% coverage) and PHCPs (ASHAs 88% [399/454]; Anganwadi workers 86% [466/542]). Epilepsy detection rates more than doubled, from 56 to 129 per 100 000 screened children (RR 2.3; 95% CI 1.4–3.8). Against a district prevalence of 35.4 per 10 000, detection efficiency improved from 15.7% to 36.5%. Of 51 confirmed post‐intervention cases, 42 (68% more than pre‐intervention) were previously undiagnosed. On multivariable logistic regression, post‐intervention children were more likely treatment‐naive (adjusted OR 2.47; 95% CI 1.32–4.61), consistent with, not proof of, reaching previously missed cases. KAP improved significantly across all groups (p < 0.001). Across 21 qualitative sessions (63 participants), stakeholders endorsed acceptability, effectiveness, and scalability.SignificanceEmbedding a structured epilepsy program within a national school health initiative was associated with improved epilepsy detection and care capacity in a low‐ and middle‐income country, without added specialist resources. The approach is scalable, adaptable to resource‐limited settings, and aligned with WHO's Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders (2022–2031), offering a promising model for narrowing the epilepsy treatment gap.Plain Language SummaryMany children with epilepsy in India go undiagnosed because they lack access to specialist care. We trained school teachers and community health workers through India's existing national school health program to recognize epilepsy, provide first aid, and refer children for treatment. After the program, twice as many children with epilepsy were identified compared to before, and teachers felt more confident managing epilepsy in the classroom. Because this model uses health workers and teachers who are already in place, it could be scaled up across India and adapted for other countries facing similar challenges.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy affects an estimated 50 million people worldwide; 27 million are children and adolescents, over half of all affected individuals. 1 , 2 , 3 The burden falls disproportionately on low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where the treatment gap remains large and structural barriers to diagnosis and care are formidable. 2 , 3 Childhood epilepsy remains under‐recognized and undertreated, with consequences extending beyond seizure control to educational participation, social inclusion, and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Although school‐based detection models have been applied to several childhood conditions, including infectious disease surveillance and mental health screening, 4 , 5 , 6 evidence specific to epilepsy is more limited. 7 Schools nonetheless remain a critical but underutilized platform for epilepsy identification and care. 8 In principle, all children with epilepsy should be attending school, uniquely positioning educational settings for systematic screening, first‐aid training, stigma reduction, and referral. Yet evidence on the effectiveness of school‐based screening, referral, and care pathways remains sparse, contributing to the omission of epilepsy from WHO/UNESCO school health guidelines. 9 , 10 National school health programs offer scalable frameworks to bridge this deficit without specialist infrastructure. 8

India's Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK), launched in 2013, provides such a framework: a national platform through which Mobile Health Teams (MHTs) screen children for 32 conditions, including epilepsy, and facilitate referral to care. 11 , 12 Our earlier work found that RBSK screening contributes to epilepsy detection but substantially under‐ascertains cases compared with school‐ and community‐based screening. 13 However, no study has evaluated whether a structured educational intervention can improve detection within RBSK or been embedded at scale.

Earlier studies have reported gaps in teachers' knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding epilepsy globally. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Using a complex intervention design aligned with the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework, 18 we addressed these gaps via a primary care‐led, school‐centered training model for teachers and community healthcare providers. To our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating a structured, MRC‐aligned epilepsy education intervention embedded within a national school health initiative in an LMIC. Given the absence of a comparison group, findings are presented as program‐associated changes rather than causal estimates. Here, we report its impact on epilepsy detection rates and KAP across the school and primary care ecosystem in a North Indian district.

METHODS

Study setting

This study was conducted in Faridkot district, Punjab, Northwest India (population 618 000; area 1472 km2). 19 The district comprises 414 public schools, 63 192 enrolled children, and 4169 teachers. RBSK, India's national child health screening program, operates through Mobile Health Teams (MHTs); in Faridkot, five MHTs, each comprising two AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homeopathy)‐trained physicians, a nurse, and a pharmacist, conducted annual school visits, screening children under 18 years for 32 conditions, including convulsive disorders. Each MHT covered 3–4 schools per day, spending 5–10 min per child. In the absence of a structured screening instrument for most conditions, including epilepsy, MHTs relied on clinical judgment and teacher‐reported information to identify children requiring referral. Screen‐positive findings and demographic details were recorded in school‐wise RBSK Child Health Registers, and affected children referred to a higher center for management. The district's primary healthcare infrastructure also comprised 461 Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and 542 Anganwadi workers, who functioned as community‐level primary healthcare providers (PHCPs).

Study design

We employed a hybrid effectiveness‐implementation design, combining a primary care‐led, school‐centered educational intervention for teachers and community healthcare providers with its implementation within the existing RBSK framework, and evaluated using a mixed‐methods approach. Quantitative outcomes comprised epilepsy detection rates from RBSK Child Health Register screening and knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) scores among teachers and PHCPs, assessed pre‐ and post‐intervention. Qualitative methods captured stakeholder perceptions of feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness. The COVID‐19 pandemic disrupted school operations and RBSK screening activities between 2020 and 2022, introducing a temporal gap between the pre‐intervention (2019–2020) and post‐intervention (2022–2023) periods, an important limitation, as pandemic‐related pent‐up healthcare demand could independently inflate post‐intervention detection irrespective of the training program. We addressed this threat to internal validity by: (i) using treatment‐naïve status among post‐intervention cases as the primary indicator of genuinely missed children being reached; (ii) applying Multiple Correspondence Analysis to distinguish diagnostic/treatment patterns from underlying clinical epidemiology; and (iii) framing all effect estimates as program‐associated observations rather than causal attributions.

Intervention development

The Structured Epilepsy Education Program (SEEP) was developed and evaluated across five phases mapped to the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for complex interventions 18 (Figure 1), with stakeholder engagement embedded throughout to ensure contextual appropriateness and feasibility within RBSK.

Study phases mapped to the Medical Research Council framework 18 for the development and evaluation of the intervention: Structured Epilepsy Education Program (SEEP).

Phase 1: Situational analysis

This phase characterized local barriers and facilitators to epilepsy awareness, detection, and referral through stakeholder consultations, semi‐structured interviews, and group discussions with representatives from the District Education Department, government schools, school administrators, RBSK MHTs, PHCPs, and parents of children with epilepsy. Baseline assessments captured knowledge, attitudes, current practices, training needs, and willingness to participate, informing the intervention's content, delivery strategy, and implementation plan.

Phase 2: Intervention development and formative refinement

A multidisciplinary task force (two neurologists, a pediatrician, a public health expert, a biostatistician, RBSK representatives, teachers, and school administrators) co‐designed SEEP informed by evidence synthesis, WHO mhGAP and International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) recommendations, baseline KAP findings, and behavioral theory, specifically the Theory of Planned Behavior and Social Cognitive Theory. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

Intervention

SEEP comprised a structured three‐hour interactive session delivered in Punjabi, covering epilepsy epidemiology and myths, seizure recognition, first aid and emergency management, RBSK referral pathways, stigma reduction, psychosocial support, and inclusive education practices. Delivery combined lectures, slide presentations, audiovisual demonstrations, case discussions, role‐play, question‐and‐answer sessions, and facilitated group discussion, supported by printed take‐home material. Core content was consistent across groups but role‐tailored: teachers received additional training on classroom seizure recognition, first aid, safe‐environment maintenance, stigma reduction, and referral; PHCPs on recognition, counseling, referral, and continuity of care; and MHTs on community screening, documentation, referral procedures, and school/health‐facility coordination (Figure 2).

Overview and delivery structure of the Intervention: Structured Epilepsy Education Program (SEEP).

The package underwent three validation rounds: an external expert panel (neurologists and public health specialists, n = 5) rated clinical accuracy and educational appropriateness via structured Likert‐scale tools; a teacher/administrator panel (n = 12) assessed clarity and classroom applicability; and an MHT panel (n = 6) assessed operational feasibility. Feedback from each round informed successive revisions.

Outcome instruments were developed concurrently. Separate teacher and PHCP KAP questionnaires were created de novo, translated into Punjabi, independently back‐translated, and reconciled for semantic equivalence; content validity was established through expert review, and both were piloted before use (Cronbach's α: teachers 0.84, PHCPs 0.83). The teacher questionnaire comprised 52 items (knowledge n = 26, attitudes n = 12, practices n = 14); the PHCP questionnaire comprised 30 items (knowledge n = 11, attitudes n = 10, practices n = 9). Items were dichotomously scored (correct = 1; incorrect/unsure/missing = 0) with negative items reverse‐coded, giving total score ranges of 0–52 (teachers) and 0–30 (PHCPs).

Phase 3: Baseline contextual assessment

Pre‐implementation service delivery and epilepsy burden were established from RBSK program registers (April 2019–March 2020) alongside a previously reported district‐wide school‐ and community‐based prevalence survey, 13 characterizing existing detection rates, referral pathways, and program performance as a baseline for post‐intervention comparison.

Phase 4: Intervention implementation

SEEP was delivered over 12 months (April 2021–March 2022) using a three‐tier cascade training‐of‐trainers model: master trainers (two neurologists and a pediatrician) trained the project team (supervisors) using a standardized facilitator manual; the project team trained all participating MHTs, who then delivered SEEP to teachers and PHCPs through structured workshops. Teacher training was held on‐site at each school (3–4 schools/day; approximately 3 hours/session; 10–12 participants); PHCP training was held at primary health centers and Anganwadi centers co‐located with school sessions (1–2 sessions/day; approximately 3 hours/session; 10–15 participants). Attendance was documented via training attendance registers, and refresher/virtual reinforcement sessions were held at 6 months. All facilitators used standardized slides, lesson plans, manuals, handbooks, and audiovisual materials.

The study team visited all participating schools and PHCP sessions to supervise delivery by RBSK MHTs and monitor fidelity through direct observation, structured checklists, and periodic review meetings. Participants completed KAP questionnaires immediately before and after training; no additional post‐training assessments were conducted. Teachers were trained to recognize suspected epilepsy, provide first aid, reduce classroom stigma, and refer suspected cases to the visiting MHT for diagnostic screening; screen‐positive children were registered in the Child Health Register and referred for clinical confirmation, as detailed below.

Phase 5: Outcome evaluation and iterative refinement

Effectiveness was evaluated using a mixed‐methods approach combining quantitative outcomes (RBSK Child Health Register screening rate, coverage, detection, referral, and teacher and PHCP KAP scores) with qualitative interviews and focus group discussions exploring acceptability, feasibility, and perceived usefulness among teachers and healthcare providers. Findings from both strands informed iterative refinement of SEEP and recommendations for its long‐term integration, scalability, and sustainability within RBSK.

Data collection

Quantitative

RBSK Child Health Screening Registers, obtained from the District Health Authority and recording health conditions identified by MHTs during routine school visits, were screened to identify children and adolescents who screened positive for a convulsive disorder during two 1‐year periods: 1 April 2019–31 March 2020 (pre‐intervention) and 1 April 2022–31 March 2023 (post‐intervention). The same screening approach and referral criteria were applied by RBSK MHTs in both periods, with no change other than the training intervention itself.

Case identification and confirmatory evaluation

Screening was based on MHT clinical judgment and teacher‐reported information applied to all children present at each school visit; it did not involve an individual diagnostic interview for every child; only children who screened positive proceeded to full diagnostic evaluation. School authorities facilitated outreach to parents of screen‐positive children, who were contacted by telephone and invited to the hospital for neurological evaluation. Screen‐positive children underwent a standardized minimum work‐up comprising at least 1 hour of awake‐and‐sleep electroencephalography (EEG) and neuroimaging at the local academic hospital; cases were classified as confirmed epilepsy or excluded where alternative diagnoses were established. 24 , 25 , 26 Active epilepsy was defined as two or more unprovoked seizures or ongoing antiseizure treatment within the preceding 5 years. 24 , 25

Evaluation in each period was performed by a study neurologist; the two neurologists discussed diagnostically uncertain cases together, but no formal inter‐rater reliability statistic was generated. Previously undiagnosed prevalent cases were estimated by excluding newly enrolled students with pre‐existing diagnoses and incident cases with seizure onset within the academic year. Detection efficiency was expressed as identified cases relative to expected cases, using a district prevalence estimate of 35.4 per 10 000 (95% CI 22.1–48.7) from the prior survey, with sensitivity analyses across the full CI bounds. 13

Qualitative

Stakeholder perspectives were explored through focus group discussions (8–10 participants each) and semi‐structured interviews with purposively selected PHCPs and teachers, conducted in Punjabi, audio‐recorded, transcribed, and translated into English. A semi‐structured guide addressed implementation constructs including attitude, burden, perceived effectiveness, ethicality, coherence, opportunity cost, and self‐efficacy. 27 Thematic analysis used a hybrid inductive‐deductive approach with constant comparison. As clinicians embedded within the study communities, the team acknowledged this positionality and remained alert to potential overestimation of acceptability and social desirability effects in responses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics. Pre‐ and post‐intervention KAP differences were assessed using paired t‐tests; chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables (p < 0.05). Prevalence and detection rates were calculated using the Poisson distribution with 95% CIs, with screened children as the denominator for detection rate and all registered students in government schools (obtained from the District Education Office) as the denominator for prevalence. Temporal trends were assessed using relative risks, and logistic regression identified factors associated with treatment gaps (dependent variable: antiseizure drug use; independent variables: intervention exposure, age, sex, epilepsy type; model fit via Nagelkerke R2). Multiple Correspondence Analysis explored relationships among diagnostic status, treatment history, epilepsy type, and neuroimaging findings. All analyses used IBM SPSS Statistics Version 20. For qualitative analysis, we additionally recorded the number and proportion of sessions (of 21 focus group discussions and interviews) in which each theme and sub‐theme was raised, reported descriptively alongside the thematic narrative.

Ethics

The study received approval from the Guru Gobind Singh Medical College Institutional Ethics Committee (GGS/IEC/19/14). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; adolescents aged 12–18 years also provided written assent.

RESULTS

Intervention validation and reach

SEEP demonstrated strong content, face, and construct validity. External expert review yielded a Content Validity Index of 0.96 (95% CI 0.73–0.99). The school‐based panel rated the module highly for comprehensibility, engagement, and practical utility, and teachers reported increased confidence in seizure recognition, first aid, and fostering inclusive classrooms; the MHT panel confirmed operational feasibility and field relevance. Two rounds of iterative revision addressed technical language, context‐specific examples, seizure‐first‐aid content, stigma reduction, and academic accommodations.

Construct validity was further supported by significant improvements in teacher KAP scores in the pilot validation cohort (n = 146), rising from 30.98 ± 4.62 to 42.54 ± 3.18 post‐training (p < 0.001). Delivered by RBSK MHTs under study‐team supervision, SEEP achieved 96% school coverage (398/414), reaching 83% of students (52 449/63192), 82% of teachers (1994/2439), all MHTs (12/12), 88% of ASHAs (399/454), and 86% of Anganwadi workers (466/542).

Epilepsy detection outcomes

Pre‐intervention period (April 2019–March 2020)

Of 63 192 enrolled students, 45 075 (71%) were screened by RBSK MHTs; 3470 (8%) were referred for any condition, and 42 (0.93 per 1000 screened) for convulsive disorders. Of these 42 screen‐positive children, 34 (81%) underwent neurological evaluation, and eight (19%) were untraceable. Epilepsy was confirmed in 25 children: a crude prevalence of 40 per 100 000 enrolled children (95% CI 24–55) and a detection rate of 56 per 100 000 screened children (95% CI 34–77). Details have been published elsewhere. 13

Post‐intervention period (April 2022–March 2023)

Of 68 625 enrolled children (42% girls; mean age 11 ± 4 years), 39 445 (57%) were screened; the remaining 29 180 (43%) were not screened, primarily due to COVID‐19‐related disruptions, delayed school reopenings, reduced attendance, and limited MHT availability during the post‐pandemic recovery period. Of those screened, 5191 (13%) were referred for any condition, and 64 (1.62 per 1000 screened) for convulsive disorders; of these, 57 (89%) underwent neurological evaluation, and seven (11%) were untraceable, declined, or had migrated (Figure 3). Epilepsy was confirmed in 51 children: a crude prevalence of 74 per 100 000 enrolled children (95% CI 54–95) and a detection rate of 129 per 100 000 screened children (95% CI 94–165) (Table 1). Of the six children evaluated but not confirmed to have epilepsy, five received alternative diagnoses (Bell's palsy, n = 1; syncope, n = 1; migraine, n = 2; psychogenic non‐epileptic seizures, n = 1) and one died before diagnostic confirmation could be completed (Figure 3).

Comparison of detection outcomes

The epilepsy detection rate more than doubled, from 56 to 129 per 100 000 screened children (RR 2.3; 95% CI 1.4–3.8). Detection efficiency—the proportion of expected cases identified, based on a district school‐based prevalence estimate of 35.4 per 10 000 (95% CI 22.1–48.7)—rose from 15.7% (95% CI 9.5–21.8%) pre‐intervention to 36.5% (95% CI 26.5–46.5%) post‐intervention, a gain of 20.9 percentage points; across the full prevalence CI bounds, post‐intervention efficiency ranged from 26.5% to 58.5%, indicating the improvement was held across assumptions.

Of 51 confirmed post‐intervention cases, five were newly enrolled students with pre‐existing diagnoses, and four were incident cases with seizure onset within the academic year; the remaining 42 were previously undiagnosed prevalent cases not previously known to the health system. This is equivalent to 168% of the 25 pre‐intervention cases, a 68% absolute increase in previously missed children identified. These 42 children came from within the 39 445 screened post‐intervention, not from the 29 180 not screened that year. Because RBSK Child Health Registers retain entries only for children who screen positive, register data could not establish whether these children had also been screened and screened negative during the earlier pre‐intervention round, or had not been screened at all.

Logistic regression showed post‐intervention children were significantly more likely to be treatment‐naïve (adjusted OR 2.47; 95% CI 1.32–4.61), while older age was associated with lower odds of untreated status (OR 0.83 per year; 95% CI 0.76–0.92). Multiple Correspondence Analysis identified Dimension 1 (28.7% of variance) as associated with diagnostic status and treatment history, and Dimension 2 (17.5%) as linked to epilepsy type and neuroimaging findings; screening year contributed significantly to Dimension 1, indicating that temporal changes reflected improved access to diagnostics and treatment rather than shifts in underlying clinical epidemiology. A recognized epilepsy syndrome was identified in 4% (1 of 25) of children pre‐intervention (Self‐Limited Epilepsy with Centrotemporal Spikes, n = 1) and 6% (3 of 51) post‐intervention (Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy, n = 2; Juvenile Absence Epilepsy, n = 1) (Figure 3). Qualitative findings

Eight focus group discussions were conducted with 50 participants: 12 female school teachers (mean age 42.9 ± 7.3 years), 12 RBSK MHT members (mean age 35.4 ± 3.1 years; six female), 12 ASHAs (all female; mean age 34.5 ± 6.0 years), and 14 Anganwadi workers and supervisors (all female; mean age 51.0 ± 6.9 years) complemented by 13 in‐depth interviews. Three themes emerged.

Theme 1: Appropriateness and acceptability

Raised in all sessions across all stakeholder groups, this theme captured a consistent shift from discriminatory attitudes toward empathy and active support for children with epilepsy. School‐based delivery was valued for reaching teachers, students, and parents simultaneously; one PHCP noted its suitability for conveying a message of non‐discrimination, and one teacher felt the school setting made the content equally relevant to all three groups. Social desirability effects cannot be excluded in qualitative responses to a program delivered by the research team, and this is acknowledged.

Theme 2: Perceived effectiveness

Raised in 18 of 21 sessions, this theme reflected greater confidence in managing epilepsy at home and in the classroom: PHCPs described how earlier access to the intervention would have aided understanding of the child's condition, and teachers expressed confidence in responding to seizures at school.

Theme 3: Feasibility and scalability

Raised in 19 of 21 sessions, this theme reflected the program's perceived accessibility, requiring no additional specialist training, though minor language simplification was suggested; participants strongly endorsed wider implementation, with community members requesting extension to surrounding villages.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the integration of a structured epilepsy education program within an existing primary care‐led national school health framework and found it associated with improved epilepsy detection and stakeholder capacity in a resource‐limited setting. Given the before‐and‐after design without a contemporaneous control group, the findings should be interpreted as program‐associated change rather than as a proven causal effect. Subject to this caveat, this is among the first studies to embed such a program within a national school health initiative in an LMIC.

Epilepsy detection rates more than doubled following the intervention (RR 2.3; 95% CI 1.4–3.8), with detection efficiency rising from 15.7% to 36.5%. Cases may have been missed at pre‐intervention screening because RBSK screening relied on MHT clinical judgment and teacher‐provided information rather than a standardized instrument for convulsive disorders. Post‐intervention, more complete information from trained teachers enabled more time‐efficient history‐taking and improved MHT referral accuracy, reflected in a lower non‐confirmation (“false‐positive”) rate (26%, n = 9, pre‐intervention vs. 11%, n = 6, post‐intervention), potentially improving ascertainment independent of any true change in disease burden.

The identification of previously undiagnosed children (n = 42) post‐intervention indicates a substantial unmet need within an existing screening program. These children were significantly more likely to be treatment‐naïve (OR 2.47; 95% CI 1.32–4.61), a finding we interpret cautiously, since treatment‐naïve status reflects absence of antiseizure medication at evaluation, not proof of prior non‐diagnosis: it may also indicate known cases whose treatment was never initiated or sustained because of non‐adherence, caregiver refusal, reliance on alternative care, or access barriers. We therefore regard this finding as consistent with, rather than proof of, the program reaching genuinely missed cases. Multiple Correspondence Analysis supported this interpretation, indicating post‐intervention gains reflected improved diagnostic and treatment access rather than a shift in underlying clinical epidemiology. Disentangling the relative contributions of improved screening, referral quality, evaluation consistency, a pandemic‐related care‐seeking rebound, and secular trends including social‐media/virtual epilepsy‐awareness content and a general rise in health‐seeking behavior was beyond the scope of this design and is a target for future controlled evaluation.

By leveraging RBSK MHTs to train teachers and PHCPs within existing school systems, the intervention expanded the reach of epilepsy care without parallel delivery structures, consistent with health‐system‐strengthening principles favoring integration over vertical program development. 28 Comparable task‐shifting approaches in Brazil and Nigeria have reported similar improvements, suggesting generalisability across LMIC contexts. 29 , 30

Qualitative findings complemented these results: increased teacher confidence in seizure first aid, reduced stigmatizing attitudes, and community demand for expansion indicate meaningful early behavioral and attitudinal change, supporting school‐centered models of community‐based neurological care consistent with WHO recommendations. 31 Despite these gains, approximately two‐thirds of expected cases remained undetected post‐intervention, a persistent treatment gap likely reflecting ongoing stigma, symptom‐disclosure reluctance, and access barriers, requiring complementary community‐based strategies alongside school health program expansion. 32 , 33

Limitations

The before‐and‐after design without a contemporaneous control limits causal inference; the two‐year interval spanning the COVID‐19 pandemic introduces temporal confounding that cannot be fully resolved, and the secular trends cannot be fully excluded or separated from the intervention's effect. Each period's evaluation was performed by a single, unblinded neurologist without a formal inter‐rater reliability check. Detection efficiency was estimated against prevalence data from a prior survey in the same population, introducing potential circularity; the wide prevalence confidence interval is addressed by reporting estimates across the full plausible range. KAP was assessed immediately post‐training with no longitudinal follow‐up; qualitative data were collected by the intervention‐delivering research team, so social desirability effects cannot be excluded; and single‐district recruitment limits generalisability.

Despite these limitations, the findings have practical implications. The higher proportion of treatment‐naïve cases identified post‐intervention suggests school‐embedded, primary care‐led approaches can help narrow epilepsy detection gaps within existing health systems, adding to the evidence base on school health platforms in neurological care and supporting re‐examination of their scope within WHO/UNESCO school health service frameworks. 9 Sustainable scale‐up may benefit from embedding epilepsy training in pre‐ and in‐service curricula, ongoing mentorship for frontline providers, periodic refresher training, and decision‐support tools. 33 Future research should prioritize controlled designs, longer‐term follow‐up, including continued surveillance in subsequent years, to distinguish a sustained program effect from transient post‐pandemic catch‐up, and evaluation of clinical outcomes and cost‐effectiveness across diverse LMIC settings.

CONCLUSION

This study found that embedding a structured epilepsy program within an existing national school health initiative was associated with improved epilepsy detection and care capacity in a low‐ and middle‐income country, without additional specialist resources. The approach appears scalable, potentially adaptable to other resource‐limited settings, and is directly aligned with the WHO Intersectoral Global Action Plan on Epilepsy and Other Neurological Disorders 2022–2031, offering a promising model for narrowing the epilepsy treatment gap in similar contexts.
