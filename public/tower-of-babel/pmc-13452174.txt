Predictors of Failure After Two-Stage Revision for Periprosthetic Joint Infections of the Knee
Authors: Praharsha Mulpur, Tarun Jayakumar, Kikkuri Rajeev Reddy, Karthik SJ, Kushal Hippalgaonkar, AV Gurava Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProsthetic joint infection (PJI) following total knee arthroplasty remains a serious complication. Although two-stage revision is considered the gold standard, predictors of failure remain unclear. This study aimed to identify factors associated with failure after two-stage revision for knee PJI using conventional statistics with exploratory machine-learning validation.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 154 patients who underwent two-stage revision for knee PJI between 2018 and 2022 at a high-volume arthroplasty center. All patients completed a minimum 24-month follow-up (mean 36.3 ± 20.3 months). Treatment success and failure were defined using MSIS Tier-1 criteria. Independent predictors of failure were identified using multivariate logistic regression and Random Forest classification model.ResultsMost infections were late in onset (91.6%), and 68.2% were culture-positive. Difficult-to-treat (DTT) organisms were identified in 22.1% of cases and were associated with significantly worse postoperative WOMAC, KSS, and OKS outcomes (all P < .001). Multivariate analysis identified DTT organisms (odds ratio 0.035, P < .001) and shorter time from index surgery to infection (odds ratio 1.033, P = .016) as independent predictors of failure. The random Forest model demonstrated an overall accuracy of 84.2% and identified DTT organisms, pre-revision C-reactive protein levels, and time from index surgery as the most influential variables contributing to classification performance. Most failures occurred within 30 months of reimplantation.ConclusionsDTT organisms and shorter time from index surgery to infection were independent predictors of failure following two-stage revision for knee PJI. Exploratory machine-learning analysis identified similar predictor patterns and supports the importance of microbiological factors in determining outcomes.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Two-stage revision, Difficult-to-treat organisms, Treatment failure, Machine learning

Introduction

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is the most dreaded complication after total joint arthroplasty, associated with significant patient morbidity and mortality. With rising case volumes of primary total knee arthroplasty, the financial burden of PJI revision is forecast to increase substantially in the coming decade [1]. Campbell et al [2] reported high mortality rates at medium to long term, even after a single revision procedure for PJI. With rising trends of antimicrobial resistance in the world, management of PJI is a difficult challenge for arthroplasty surgeons [[3], [4], [5]].

Surgical options for PJI include debridement, antibiotics, with implant retention (DAIR), two-stage revision, single-stage revision, isolated debridement or incision and drainage without modular exchange, and more recently 1.5-stage revision arthroplasty [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Of these, two-stage revision is widely considered the gold standard in the treatment of PJI [11]. The success of two-stage revision is attributed to the first-stage procedure which involves removal of the infected hardware, thorough debridement and application of an antibiotic-laden cement spacer. This reduces local microbiological burden, ensures biofilm eradication, and allows local antibiotic delivery [12].

However, the outcomes of two-stage revision are variable with reports of successful outcomes varying between 54 and 100% in previous reports. Lunz et al [13] reported variation of success rates ranging between 65 and 93%, just based on defining criteria of outcomes. Few studies have explored the factors influencing outcomes after two-stage revision for PJI.

The aim of this study was to report failure rates after two-stage revision TKA for PJI based on Delphi-consensus defined outcome criteria, with an emphasis on reporting factors predictive of failure of two-stage revision evaluated primarily using conventional statistical methods, with supplementary assessment using a random Forest classification model.

Material and methods

This study was a retrospective review of patients who underwent two-stage revision for PJI of the knee, at a single high-volume arthroplasty center, performed between January 1, 2018, and December 1, 2022. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical committee (SIEC/2023/562). All patients provided informed consent at the time of surgery for anonymized use of clinical and follow-up data for research purposes. The prospectively maintained institutional joint registry was electronically queried for “revision total knee arthroplasty” cases during the defined period and subsequently the search was narrowed based on etiology of revision for “infection” or “septic revision.”

A total of 183 patients underwent two-stage revision for PJI during the defined time frame. Nineteen patients were removed from analysis because of prior stage-1 procedure performed at an outside hospital, some of which underwent a redo stage 1 surgery at our institute. Of the remaining 164 patients, there were 10 patients lost to follow-up. A total of 154 patients were eligible for inclusion in the final analysis of outcomes after revision with a minimum follow-up of 24 months after the second-stage procedure (Fig. 1).

Patient selection flowchart.

Inclusion criteria consisted of patients with established PJI of the knee, with or without prior history of additional procedures such as incision and drainage or DAIR surgery prior to two-stage revision, with minimum clinical follow-up of 2 years after the second-stage procedure, with availability of culture reports and surgical details from the electronic health records. Patients without requisite follow-up, incomplete data, loss to follow-up at any point during two-stage revision were excluded from the final analysis.

Definitions

PJI was diagnosed at our institute initially based on the 2015 Musculo-Skeletal Infections Society (MSIS) and later based on the 2018 PJI definition by Parvizi et al. [14,15] During the first-stage surgery, the infected TKA prosthesis is completely removed, and in all cases a hand-made antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer was placed in the joint cavity. After a variable interval, based on trends of inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP) the second-stage prosthetic reimplantation was done. Reaspiration of the joint was done in all cases prior to the second-stage revision.

Detailed surgical protocol

Stage 1 revision

Skin preparation at the time of surgery was done with chlorhexidine skin scrub (Avagard, 3M Health Care, St. Paul, MN). Exposure performed via medial parapatellar approach. Aggressive debridement was done before and after removal of the TKA components. Minimum of 3-5 tissue samples were sent in all cases for microbiological tests. Any fluid samples over 5 ml were collected with sterile precautions and inoculated directly into BacT/ALERT culture bottles (BioMerieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France). Joint lavage was done with pulsed lavage, dilute Betadine, and hydrogen peroxide. Intramedullary reaming done, and reamed contents were part of the 5 samples sent for culture. Two batches of PALACOS R + G (Heraeus, GmBH, Wehrheim, Germany) with 2 g vancomycin powder added per 40 g cement was used to prepare antibiotic spacer, separately for the femur and tibial side. Meropenem was added to the cement in select Gram-negative (GN) infections. After curing of cement, the joint hemostasis was achieved after tourniquet deflation and wound closed in layers. Surgical drain was used at the time of closure. Antibiotics were administered intravenously after deflation of the tourniquet. In culture positive cases, antibiotics were decided with the involvement of a consultant grade infectious diseases specialist based on minimum inhibitory concentration cutoffs and administered accordingly. In culture negative cases, patients received 2 weeks of intravenous (IV) Cefoperazone-Sulbactam 1.5 g twice daily and oral doxycycline (100 mg orally twice daily) based on hospital antibiogram and infection prevention policy. Inflammatory markers were obtained every 14 days. Patients received antibiotics for a minimum of 6 weeks, 2 weeks IV, followed by 4 weeks of oral antibiotics before decision for reimplantation. Antibiotics were stopped 2 weeks prior to stage 2 procedure in most cases. However, this was arbitrary and not enforced. Patients with reduced or normal inflammatory markers at 6 weeks of IV + oral antibiotics proceeded directly for stage 2 revision without drug holiday. Significant down-trending inflammatory markers in the setting of satisfactory clinical improvement were considered acceptable for proceeding with reimplantation after discussion with infectious diseases’ specialists.

Stage 2 revision

Reimplantation was planned after normalization of biomarkers. Prophylactic IV antibiotics were administered 1-hour prior to incision. Spacer removal was done, and a thorough debridement was again performed to remove fibrous/scar tissue. Revision TKA was performed with either a varus-valgus constrained design or a hinged prosthesis based on collateral ligament status. All components were implanted using fully cemented fixation with antibiotic-loaded bone cement according to the principles of zonal fixation. Intramedullary cement restrictors were routinely utilized during preparation of stemmed components [16]. All patients received oral antibiotics for 2 weeks following the second-stage procedure based on the pathogen identified during the stage 1 procedure.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to report success/failure rate in the eradication of infection after two-stage revision for PJI of the knee. Successful outcomes were defined based on tier 1 MSIS criteria of outcomes after PJI published by Fillingham et al [17].

Postoperative functional scores were assessed based on culture report, infection type, and difficult-to-treat (DTT) organisms. Failure of treatment was defined as:

i.Persistent infection confirmed with sustained elevation of inflammatory markers with persistent clinical symptoms of pain.

Persistent infection confirmed with sustained elevation of inflammatory markers with persistent clinical symptoms of pain.

ii.Development of acute signs of infection after two-stage revision such as sudden onset swelling, local inflammatory signs of effusion, warmth, fever, and new sinus formation.

Development of acute signs of infection after two-stage revision such as sudden onset swelling, local inflammatory signs of effusion, warmth, fever, and new sinus formation.

iii.Requirement of a secondary procedure such as incision and drainage, DAIR, or redo stage 1 revision.

Requirement of a secondary procedure such as incision and drainage, DAIR, or redo stage 1 revision.

iv.Requirement of chronic suppressive oral antibiotics.

Requirement of chronic suppressive oral antibiotics.

Infections were classified as early (1 month or less after index surgery) or late (onset of symptoms beyond 4 weeks of index procedure) based on the classification by Tsukayama et al. [18] Late infections were either late chronic infections of acute hematogenous infections. Acute hematogenous infections are defined as late infections, with acute signs of infection with symptom duration ˂ 2 weeks. Microbiological characteristics consisted of defining culture-positive vs negative, and organisms in culture-positive cases classified as Gram positive (GP) or GN. DTT organisms are defined as those microbes resistant to biofilm active antimicrobials including tetracycline or rifampicin-resistant GP organisms, fluoroquinolone resistance gram negative bacteria, and fungal infections [19]. Functional outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at 2 years follow-up after stage 2 revision using Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), Knee Society Score (KSS), and Oxford Knee Score (OKS). Based on previously published validation studies, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was defined as 10-12 points for the WOMAC, 9-10 points for the KSS, and 5 points for the OKS. MCID thresholds were used to aid clinical interpretation of between-group differences [20]. Postoperative functional scores were assessed based on surgical scar status, culture report, infection type, and presence of DTT organisms.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were evaluated using the independent samples t-test and expressed as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables were reported as proportions and comparison done using the Chi-square test or Fisher-Exact test based on sample frequency of the outcome of concern. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent risk factors (including age, sex, ASA, Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI], early versus late infections, culture positive versus negative, organism identity, etc) with a confidence of 95%. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software for Windows (version 20.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

In addition to conventional statistical analyses, an exploratory machine learning analysis was performed using a supervised random Forest classification algorithm. The model was trained using treatment success vs failure, defined according to MSIS tier 1 criteria, as the dependent outcome variable. Predictor variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA classification, CCI, CRP, ESR, culture status, organism characteristics, DTT status, infection timing, and interval from index arthroplasty. Variable importance was assessed using F1-score contribution. Higher F1-scores indicate greater contribution to classification performance and should not be interpreted as direct measures of treatment failure risk.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 154 patients who underwent two-stage revision for PJI at our institute were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age was 63.46 ± 7.8 years, with a mean BMI of 30.54 ± 3.6 kg/m2. There was an almost equal proportion of women (N = 76, 49.3%) and men (N = 78, 51.7%) in the study. A majority of patients were classified as ASA class II (61%), with a mean CCI of 2.75 ± 1.2. The mean interval between the index surgery and diagnosis of PJI was 40.63 ± 27.7 months. All patients were followed up for a minimum duration of 2 years following surgery for PJI with a mean follow-up of 36.3 ± 20.3 months (range: 24 months-60 months) (Table 1).

Infection characteristics

Overall, 68.2% (n = 105) were culture positive, while 31.8% (n = 49) had no organism isolated during the course of treatment (culture-negative PJI). Among culture-positive cases, GP bacteria were the most frequently identified pathogens (n = 68, 44.2%), followed by GN organisms (n = 29, 18.8%), polymicrobial infections (n = 6, 3.9%), and fungal infections (n = 2, 1.3%). DTT organisms were identified in 22.1% (n = 34) of cases.

Early infections, defined as occurring within 4 weeks postoperatively, were diagnosed in 13 patients (8.4%), while the majority (91.6%) had late infections occurring beyond 4 weeks. An unhealthy surgical scar (those with dehiscence, wound drainage, and/or sinus tracts) was identified in 97 (63.0%) patients (Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified DTT organisms and time from index surgery to infection as independent predictors of treatment failure. The presence of DTT organisms was associated with significantly reduced odds of successful treatment (odds ratio [OR] = 0.035, 95% confidence interval 0.008-0.146, P < .001). Time from index surgery to infection was also independently associated with outcome, with longer intervals associated with improved odds of treatment success (OR = 1.033, 95% confidence interval 1.006-1.059, P = .016). In contrast, neither CRP (OR = 0.99, P = .585) nor ESR (OR = 0.99, P = .125) demonstrated independent associations with treatment outcome following covariate adjustment (Table 7).

Random Forest classification model

The random Forest model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 84.2%. DTT organisms demonstrated the highest predictive contribution (F1-score = 0.256), followed by pre-revision CRP levels (F1-score = 0.144) and time from index surgery to infection (F1-score = 0.135). Higher F1-scores indicate greater contribution to classification performance and should not be interpreted as direct measures of treatment failure risk. While DTT organisms and infection timing were also identified as significant predictors in the multivariable regression model, CRP demonstrated predictive importance only within the random Forest model and was not independently associated with treatment failure on regression analysis (OR = 0.99, P = .585) (Fig. 2).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed for both time to failure of surgery and time to mortality independently. The mean survival time for failure cases was 30.4 ± 22.7 months, with the steepest decline occurring within the first 20 to 30 months as shown in Figure 3. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that most failures occurred within the first 30 months, with culture-negative infections exhibiting the longest median survival time (48 months), while polymicrobial infections had the shortest (17 months). Expired cases had a mean survival time of 25.0 ± 13.9 months, with a more rapid decline in survival probability within the first 20 months (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Using MSIS tier 1 criteria, this study found that microbiological factors, such as infections with DTT organisms, were the strongest predictors of two-stage revision failure after TKA, outweighing host factors. Multivariable regression demonstrated that DTT organisms (OR = 0.035, P < .001) and shorter time from index surgery to infection (OR = 1.033, P = .016) were independently associated with treatment failure. Exploratory random Forest analysis similarly identified DTT organisms and infection timing as influential variables, while elevated CRP demonstrated predictive importance only within the machine learning model. Failure rates were unaffected by culture status, gram staining characteristics of the organism, infection timing, and most failures occurred within 30 months of the second-stage surgery.

One major finding of this study is the agreement between the conventional regression analysis and the exploratory machine learning assessment. Both methodologies consistently demonstrated that DTT organisms and time of infection were important predictors of outcome. CRP had predictive importance solely in the random Forest model but was not statistically significant in multivariable regression. The discrepancy is likely because machine learning techniques can capture complex nonlinear interactions between variables. Machine learning analysis is thus to be appreciated as complimentary, not as a replacement, to conventional statistical modeling.

Our observation that pathogen features dominate prognosis aligns with the series by Russo et al. [22], who identified DTT organisms as an independent risk factor for two-stage failure (OR ≈ 4.2), alongside prior surgeries and tibial bone defects. Their conclusions reinforce that microbiology often outweighs patient factors in predicting outcome.

We found no outcome difference by culture status, which is consistent with the knee-specific cohort studied by Kim et al. [23], who reported similar failure, reinfection, and mortality rates between culture-negative and culture-positive two-stage revisions, with only a higher reoperation rate between stages in culture-positive cases. Earlier reviews, such as Tan et al. [24], raised concern that culture-negative PJI carried “unacceptable” failure rates and advocated maximizing organism recovery before surgery, but these analyses pooled outcomes across procedures and joints. Our knee-only, two-stage data support the more recent, procedure-specific evidence that culture negativity per se should not be viewed as a contraindication to successful two-stage management.

Similarly, Gram stain class did not discriminate outcomes in our cohort. The literature is mixed: Azzam et al. [25] suggested GN PJI carries worse outcomes, whereas Rodríguez-Pardo et al. [26] reported parity with GP infection when managed with staged exchange. Our findings align with the latter, supporting the view that organism-specific resistance and DTT status may be more prognostically meaningful than Gram class alone.

Kapadia et al. [27] demonstrated that the majority of reinfections occur within the first 24–36 months, underscoring the need for intensified surveillance during this period.

Inflammatory markers featured prominently in our models. While single CRP thresholds have shown inconsistent predictive value, Shahi et al. [28] found that CRP trends during the interstage interval were more predictive of outcome, with rising or fluctuating values correlating with higher failure risk-directionally consistent with our finding that elevated CRP at diagnosis predicts failure.

We also found that a shorter interval from index TKA to PJI predicted failure, despite no crude difference when grouped into “early” vs “late” categories. This finding is similar to the observations of Zmistowski et al. [29], who noted that time-to-infection is informative as a continuous risk variable and may reflect higher organism virulence or biofilm maturity at presentation.

In our study host or patient factors such as age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, risk stratification based on the CCI were not statistically significant predictors of failure after the second-stage procedure. Bhanushali et al [30] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on 7 studies on patient-related predictors of failure after two-stage revision. In this report obesity, liver cirrhosis, and previous failed revisions for PJI are nonmodifiable risk factors, while IV drug use and smoking are modifiable risk factors predictive of failure. Similar to our study, sex, ASA class, diabetes mellitus, and inflammatory arthropathy did not predict treatment failure. Evidence on CCI was limited as per the report. Raney et al [31] reported significantly worse outcomes and failures in obese patients, with reinfections increased from 11.5% in nonobese to 19.0% in obese and 25.9% in morbidly obese patients.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the exploratory random Forest analysis was performed on a relatively modest sample size without external validation, which may limit model stability. The relatively modest F1-score values observed in the exploratory random Forest analysis likely reflect the limited sample size, low event frequency, and substantial overlap in clinical characteristics between treatment success and failure cohorts. Third, although MCID thresholds were used to aid interpretation of postoperative functional outcomes, these values were derived from previously published arthroplasty populations and may not be directly applicable to patients undergoing two-stage revision for PJI. Given that MCID is population-specific, the applicability of these thresholds to the present study population should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the follow-up duration, while sufficient to capture the majority of failures, may not account for very late recurrences.

In this study, DTT organisms (OR = 0.035, P < .001) and shorter time from index surgery to infection (OR = 1.033, P = .016) were independent predictors of failure following two-stage revision for PJI. Host variables including age, BMI, ASA class, and CCI demonstrated limited predictive value. exploratory random Forest analysis additionally identified inflammatory burden as contributing to outcome classification, although CRP was not independently associated with treatment outcome in multivariable regression.

Conclusions

In this study, DTT organisms and shorter time from index surgery to infection were independent predictors of failure following two-stage revision for PJI. Elevated pre-revision CRP levels demonstrated predictive importance but were not independently associated with treatment failure after multivariable adjustment. Host variables including age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, and CCI demonstrated limited predictive value.

Future large-volume multicenter studies are needed to better understand microbial trends and refine antimicrobial policies in the perioperative period.
