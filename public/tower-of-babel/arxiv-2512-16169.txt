Bayesian Smooth-Fit Extrapolation of the C 12 + C 12 {}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C} Astrophysical S S Factor
A. M. Mukhamedzhanov

Abstract
A Bayesian analysis of the astrophysical S S factor for the 12 C + 12 C {}^{12}\mathrm{C}+^{12}\mathrm{C} fusion reaction is presented, based on available experimental information at carbon–carbon relative energies E ≳ 2 ​ MeV E\gtrsim 2~\mathrm{MeV} , including direct measurements, indirect Coulomb-renormalized Trojan Horse Method (THM) results, and recent inverse-kinematics data. The Bayesian inference is performed on the quantity log 10 ⁡ S ∗ ​ ( E ) \log_{10}S^{*}(E) rather than on S ∗ ​ ( E ) S^{*}(E) itself, which naturally accommodates the wide dynamic range of the data and leads to approximately Gaussian uncertainties. Expressing the data in terms of log 10 ⁡ S ∗ ​ ( E ) \log_{10}S^{*}(E) reduces the relative scatter of the experimental points and enables a smooth global description. The logarithm of the astrophysical factor is parametrized by a quadratic polynomial in energy, and the posterior distribution of the fit coefficients is determined using a weighted Bayesian regression. From this posterior, a global median S ∗ ​ ( E ) S^{*}(E) curve is constructed, and the associated covariance matrix is used to define a low/medium/high (LO/MED/HI) band corresponding to a 68 % 68\% credible interval. This procedure yields a smooth and internally consistent description of the astrophysical factor over the energy range 1.0 ≤ E ≤ 3.5 ​ MeV 1.0\leq E\leq 3.5~\mathrm{MeV} . Particular emphasis is placed on the extrapolation below E cm = 2 ​ MeV E_{\mathrm{cm}}=2~\mathrm{MeV} , where the fusion reaction rate is most relevant for stellar carbon burning. At E cm = 1.5 ​ MeV E_{\mathrm{cm}}=1.5~\mathrm{MeV} , the posterior distribution yields S global ∗ ​ ( 1.5 ​ MeV ) = ( 2.13 − 0.01 + 0.01 ) × 10 16 ​ keV ​ b , S_{\mathrm{global}}^{*}(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})=\left(2.13^{+0.01}_{-0.01}\right)\times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{keV\,b}, corresponding to a 68 % 68\% credible interval. The extracted result is consistent with recent inverse-kinematics measurements and with Coulomb-corrected Trojan Horse Method constraints, providing a tightly constrained estimate of the 12 C + 12 C {}^{12}\mathrm{C}+^{12}\mathrm{C} fusion S S factor in the energy region relevant for stellar carbon burning.



I
Introduction

The
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion reaction plays a central
role in the late stages of stellar evolution and in scenarios of
explosive astrophysics. One of the most exciting topics of contemporary
nuclear astrophysics is the behavior and value of low-energy astrophysical factor
of the
{}^{12}{\rm C}+{}^{12}{\rm C}
fusion process which plays a very important role
in a wide variety of stellar burning scenarios such as massive
stars, type Ia supernovae and superbursts. One of the possible
scenarios of formation of Supernovae Ia is merging of
the binary system of two white dwarfs
Mori
. The outcome of
this merging scenario is controlled by the
{}^{12}{\rm C}+{}^{12}{\rm C}
fusion.

Reliable extrapolations of the astrophysical

S
factor to relative carbon–carbon energies

E\lesssim 2~\mathrm{MeV}
are required to constrain
reaction rates under stellar conditions. For more details see recently published
comprehensive review
Chieffi
. Direct measurements in this
energy regime are extremely challenging because of the rapidly
decreasing cross section and the increasing experimental background.
As a result, available experimental information is concentrated at
higher energies, and indirect constraints are required to anchor the
sub-barrier behavior.

Over the past decades, a diverse experimental landscape has emerged
for the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion reaction. Early
direct measurements were reported in
Refs.
Mazarakis
;
Becker
;
Spillane
;
Aguillera
, followed by more
recent direct studies performed by the Notre Dame group and
collaborators
Tan20
;
Tan24
and by the STELLA collaboration

Fruet
;
Nippert
. All available direct measurements are restricted
to energies
E_{\mathrm{cm}}\gtrsim 2.1~\mathrm{MeV}
, i.e. above the
Gamow window relevant for stellar carbon burning.

In addition to traditional direct measurements, two major experimental
developments have significantly advanced the understanding of the
low-energy behavior of the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion
reaction. These include the Trojan Horse Method (THM)

Tumino
, which provided the first indirect access to the
sub-barrier energy region, and the more recent inverse-kinematics
measurements by Nan
et al.

NanWang
. Through detailed
balance, the latter yield a direct determination of the

{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion
S
factor at low energies and
establish an experimentally constrained band in the region relevant
for astrophysical extrapolation. The initial plane-wave
analysis of the THM data suggested a pronounced enhancement of the
astrophysical
S
factor at low energies. Subsequent inclusion of
Coulomb–nuclear interactions in the THM analysis

MukTHM
significantly modified this behavior, suppressing the
enhancement and bringing the extracted
S
factor into qualitative
agreement with the inverse-kinematics results.
The most advanced microscopic calculations of the low-energy

S
factor for the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion reaction
have been reported in Refs.
Kimura21
;
Kimura23
. These calculations
do not support either the hindrance pattern proposed in
Ref.
Jiang
or the sharp low-energy enhancement inferred from the
original Trojan Horse Method analysis
Tumino
. A recent re-examination of the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion
reaction in
Hagino
has also found no
compelling evidence for a fusion hindrance pattern at low energies,
further underscoring the uncertainties associated with extrapolations
based on specific hindrance assumptions.

The central challenge is therefore to combine these heterogeneous
datasets in a statistically consistent manner, accounting for their
different energy coverages, normalization uncertainties, and underlying
systematic assumptions, while quantifying the resulting uncertainties
in a form suitable for reliable low-energy extrapolation and
astrophysical reaction-rate calculations.

The goal of the present work is not to model the detailed resonance
spectrum of the
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
system, but to
determine the smooth global trend of the astrophysical
S
factor over
a restricted energy window (1.5–3.5 MeV) in which multiple datasets
coexist. This enables a statistically robust determination of

S(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})
, which is a key input for astrophysical rate
evaluations.

To this end, a Bayesian-inspired smooth-fit framework is adopted in
which the logarithm of the astrophysical factor,

\log_{10}S(E)
, is represented by a low-order polynomial in the
carbon–carbon relative energy
E
. Working in logarithmic space reduces
the dynamic range spanned by the experimental data and allows the
heterogeneous datasets to be combined in a statistically stable
manner.

Within this framework, all selected datasets contribute coherently to
the inference of the model parameters, and their uncertainties are
propagated self-consistently through the full covariance matrix. In
contrast to a traditional
\chi^{2}
-based fit, the Bayesian approach
yields a full posterior probability distribution for the fit
parameters and for derived quantities. This enables the construction
of global low/medium/high (LO/MED/HI) curves of the astrophysical
factor, where the MED curve corresponds to the posterior median and the
LO and HI curves define the bounds of the credible interval, which
remain meaningful under extrapolation beyond the energy range directly
constrained by the data.

The resulting LO/MED/HI curves and the posterior distribution of

S(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})
provide a compact, transparent, and reproducible
summary of the global information currently available from both direct
and indirect measurements, and establish a statistically well-defined
basis for low-energy extrapolation and astrophysical reaction-rate
calculations.
The upper bound of
3.5~\mathrm{MeV}
is chosen to include the highest
energies at which multiple datasets overlap, while remaining within
the regime where a smooth parametrization of
\log_{10}S(E)
is
appropriate.

•

Fruet
;

, covering

•

Earlier direct measurements reported in

Mazarakis
;
Becker
;
Spillane
;
Aguillera
;

•

Inverse-kinematics measurements by Nan

NanWang
, which, through detailed balance, provide a

{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}

S

II.2
Indirect and inverse kinematics measurements

•

Coulomb–nuclear renormalized Trojan Horse Method (THM) data

MukTHM
.

To maintain a simple and transparent statistical scheme suitable for
reproducibility, the following uncertainty model is adopted.

1.

All datasets except for Ref.
NanWang
are assigned a
uniform fractional uncertainty

\delta_{i}=0.25,

(1)

corresponding to a
25\%
relative uncertainty on
S(E_{i})
.
This choice reflects the typical normalization uncertainties
quoted for the majority of direct and indirect measurements and
avoids the introduction of dataset-specific tuning parameters.

2.

The inverse-kinematics data of Ref.
NanWang
are provided
in the form of adopted central values
S_{i}^{\rm ad}(E_{i})

together with an energy-dependent
S
-factor band defined by
lower and upper bounds
S_{i}^{\rm lo}(E_{i})
and

S_{i}^{\rm hi}(E_{i})
.
A relative uncertainty is assigned as

\delta_{i}=\max\!\left[\frac{S_{i}^{\rm hi}(E_{i})-S_{i}^{\rm lo}(E_{i})}{2\,S_{i}^{\rm ad}(E_{i})},\;0.05\right],

(2)

imposing a minimum
5\%
uncertainty in order to avoid an
artificially narrow constraint.

The combined dataset constructed in this manner is used to constrain a
smooth representation of
\log_{10}S(E)
within the Bayesian framework
described in Sec.
I
. The adopted uncertainty assignments
play a direct role in determining the width of the global posterior
band and the posterior distribution of
S(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})
.
Accordingly, the quoted credible intervals should be interpreted as a
combined reflection of the available experimental information and the
conservative assumptions made in modeling the underlying uncertainties.

II.3
Quadratic Representation

As discussed in Sec.
I
, working with
\log_{10}S(E)

substantially reduces the dynamic range spanned by the experimental
data and leads to uncertainties that are approximately Gaussian. This
representation facilitates a statistically stable combination of
datasets obtained using different experimental techniques.

The logarithm of the astrophysical factor is assumed to be adequately
represented over the energy range

1.5\leq E\leq 3.5~\mathrm{MeV}
by a quadratic polynomial,

\displaystyle\log_{10}S(E)=a_{0}+a_{1}(E-E_{0})+a_{2}(E-E_{0})^{2},

(3)

where
E_{0}
is a fixed pivot energy chosen within the fitted range.
In the present analysis,
E_{0}=1.5~\mathrm{MeV}
is adopted.
Centering the polynomial at
E_{0}
improves numerical stability and
reduces correlations among the fitted coefficients. The resulting
posterior distributions are found to be insensitive to the specific
choice of the pivot energy within the interval

1.5
–
2.5~\mathrm{MeV}
.

For each data point
i
with measured (or derived) central value
S_{i}

and fractional uncertainty
\delta_{i}
, the corresponding uncertainty
in
\log_{10}S
is taken as

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}=\frac{\delta_{i}}{\ln 10},

(4)

which follows from standard error propagation.

A weighted least-squares fit in
\log_{10}S(E)
with weights

w_{i}=1/\sigma_{i}^{2}
is performed to determine the coefficient vector

\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})
together with its covariance matrix

\bm{C}
.

Because the model is linear in the coefficients
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
, and

a_{2}
, and the experimental uncertainties are treated as Gaussian in

\log_{10}S(E)
, the statistical uncertainties of the fitted
coefficients are themselves Gaussian. Under these conditions, the
covariance matrix
\bm{C}
fully specifies the width and shape of the
resulting parameter distribution and provides a Gaussian
approximation to the posterior distribution of the model parameters

\bm{a}
.

No prior theoretical constraints are imposed on the coefficients

a_{0}
,
a_{1}
, and
a_{2}
beyond the requirement of a smooth functional
form. In particular, neither the overall normalization nor the slope
or curvature of
\log_{10}S(E)
is restricted by hand. The fitted
parameter values and their uncertainties are therefore determined
entirely by the experimental data and their assigned errors.

II.4
Justification for the Quadratic Regression Model

A common concern is the use of a quadratic polynomial to represent

\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
in the presence of resonant structures in the data.
The following points address this concern:

1.

The quadratic model is applied over a
restricted
energy
interval (1.5–3.5 MeV), within which the smooth envelope of

\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
varies slowly.

2.

The polynomial parametrization is not intended as a physical
model of the reaction mechanism; it serves as a local regression
that approximates the smooth background trend.

3.

Narrow resonances appear as localized deviations from this
background and therefore do not bias the extracted global trend.

4.

The inclusion of higher-order polynomial terms does not
improve the Bayesian evidence and leads to additional coefficients
that are statistically insignificant.

5.

Robustness checks confirm that the extracted value of

S(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})
is stable under cubic or quartic extensions of
the polynomial, with deviations well below the quoted credible
intervals.

III
Bayesian formalism

III.1
Bayes’ theorem

The statistical inference problem addressed in this work is formulated
within a Bayesian framework. Given a set of experimental data
D
,
Bayes’ theorem relates the posterior probability density to the
likelihood and prior according to

\displaystyle P(S\,|\,D)=\frac{P(D\,|\,S)\,P(S)}{P(D)},

(5)

where
P(S\,|\,D)
is the posterior probability density for the
astrophysical factor,
P(D\,|\,S)
is the likelihood function, and

P(S)
represents prior information. The normalization factor
P(D)
,
often referred to as the Bayesian evidence, ensures that the posterior
is properly normalized. For more details of the Bayesian formalism see
Muk16OBa
.

In the present context, the data
D
consist of a heterogeneous
collection of direct and indirect measurements of the

{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion reaction, each characterized
by a measured (or derived) value of the astrophysical factor and an
associated uncertainty. The goal is not to infer
S(E)
independently
at each energy, but rather to determine a smooth global representation
that is statistically consistent with all available data and suitable
for low-energy extrapolation.

In the general formulation, the quantity of physical interest is the
probability density
P(S|D)
for the
S
factor. In practice, however,

S(E)
is not treated as an independent variable but is specified by a
finite set of polynomial coefficients
\bm{a}
through the adopted
parametrization, Eq. (
3
). The experimental data therefore
constrain the coefficients
\bm{a}
, leading naturally to the posterior
distribution
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
, which gives the probability density of

\bm{a}
for a given dataset
D
. The posterior probability density for

S(E)
at a given energy is then obtained by mapping the coefficient
posterior onto
S(E)
through the model relation
S(E)=S(E;\bm{a})
.

Thus,
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
plays the role of an intermediate quantity: it
encodes which coefficient values are consistent with the data and their
uncertainties, while the physically relevant distribution
P(S|D)
is
obtained by evaluating
S(E)
for the coefficient values allowed by

P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
. In this way, the uncertainty in
S(E)
follows
directly from the uncertainty in the fitted coefficients.

IV
Global posterior construction

The Bayesian inference is performed in logarithmic space by defining

\displaystyle y(E)\equiv\log_{10}S^{*}(E).

(6)

Here, the modified
S
factor

\displaystyle S^{*}(E)=S(E)\,e^{0.46\,E},

(7)

is introduced, which is commonly used in analyses of

{}^{12}{\rm C}+{}^{12}{\rm C}
fusion reactions
Patterson
.

The astrophysical factor
S^{*}(E)
is described by the polynomial
parametrization of Eq. (
3
), applied to the logarithmic
variable
y(E)=\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
. Each experimental dataset is mapped
to log space, with the corresponding uncertainties propagated
accordingly.

IV.1
Likelihood

The likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{k}
quantifies how well a given set of
polynomial coefficients
\bm{a}
reproduces the
k
th experimental
dataset. It is constructed under the assumption that the experimental
uncertainties in the logarithmic variable
y(E)=\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
are
Gaussian and statistically independent. The likelihood for the
k
th
dataset, containing
n_{k}
data points, is written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\bm{a})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{\bigl(y_{k,i}-y(E_{k,i};\bm{a})\bigr)^{2}}{2\,\sigma_{k,i}^{2}}\right],

(8)

where
y_{k,i}
and
\sigma_{k,i}
denote the measured value and
uncertainty of
\log_{10}S^{*}
at energy
E_{k,i}
, and
y(E;\bm{a})
is
the model prediction.

The global likelihood is defined as the product of the likelihoods of
all datasets,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(\bm{a})=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{k}(\bm{a}),

(9)

and is treated as a function of the model parameters
\bm{a}
for the
fixed experimental dataset
D
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(\bm{a})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(D\,|\,\bm{a}).

(10)

Normally,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(D\,|\,\bm{a})
is proportional
to the probability of obtaining the experimental dataset
D
for a
given choice of the model parameters
\bm{a}
. Since the data are fixed,
the likelihood is treated as a function of
\bm{a}
and is used to
assess how well different parameter values describe the data.

It is important to distinguish between the likelihood and the posterior
distribution. The likelihood quantifies how well a given choice of the
coefficients
\bm{a}
describes the data, whereas the posterior
distribution
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
shows which values of the coefficients
are consistent with the data and their uncertainties. In addition, the
posterior is a proper probability density: its integral over all
coefficient values
\bm{a}
is equal to unity. By contrast, the
likelihood is not normalized and serves only to compare the relative
quality of different parameter choices.

IV.2
Posterior for polynomial coefficients

The posterior distribution of the polynomial coefficients is

\displaystyle P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(D\,|\,\bm{a})\,\pi(\bm{a})}{\int d\bm{a}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{global}}(D\,|\,\bm{a})\,\pi(\bm{a})},

(11)

where
\pi(\bm{a})
denotes the adopted prior. The maximum-a-posteriori
(MAP) coefficients are obtained by maximizing this posterior,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}=\arg\max_{\bm{a}}\,P(\bm{a}\,|\,D),

(12)

which, for Gaussian experimental uncertainties and a flat prior, is
equivalent to minimizing the global
\chi^{2}(\bm{a})
.

For the adopted quadratic polynomial mapping of
\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
,
the coefficient vector is
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}
, and the
posterior distribution is approximated locally by a Gaussian form,

\displaystyle P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)\approx\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{a}_{\rm MAP},\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\,|\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}|^{1/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{a}-\bm{a}_{\rm MAP})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}^{-1}(\bm{a}-\bm{a}_{\rm MAP})\right].

(13)

Here and in the following,
P(\cdot)
denotes a probability density for
continuous variables. The matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}
is the
covariance matrix of the coefficient vector
\bm{a}
, and

|\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}|
denotes its determinant. In general,

\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}
is not diagonal in the coefficient basis
\{a_{i}\}
,
reflecting correlations between the coefficients induced by the global
fit. As a real symmetric positive-definite matrix,

\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}
can be diagonalized (see Appendix

A
); in this form, Monte Carlo sampling is
simplified because correlated variations of the coefficients can be
generated efficiently. The vector
\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}
denotes the MAP
estimator, i.e. the set of coefficient values
\{a_{i}\}
at which the
posterior density
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
attains its maximum.

The Gaussian approximation, Eq. (
13
), is justified by
two related features of the analysis. First, the polynomial model of
Eq. (
3
) is a smooth function of the coefficient vector, so
that the likelihood varies smoothly in the vicinity of its maximum.
Second, the global fit is constrained by a large number of experimental
data points spanning a broad energy range. In the present analysis, a
total of
N=614
experimental points enter the likelihood. Because many
data points jointly constrain only a three coefficients
\bm{a}
,
deviations from the optimal values degrade the agreement with many
measurements simultaneously and are therefore strongly suppressed.

To propagate the uncertainties of the fitted coefficients to derived
quantities, the Gaussian posterior of Eq. (
13
) is
sampled using
N_{\rm samp}=559
Monte Carlo realizations. The
distinction between the total number of experimental data points,

N=614
, and the number of Monte Carlo realizations,

N_{\rm samp}=559
, is important. All
N=614
experimental points enter
the likelihood and fully determine the posterior distribution in the
coefficient
\bm{a}
space, whereas the smaller number
N_{\rm samp}
reflects a
purely numerical choice made to obtain stable projections of the
coefficient uncertainties onto derived quantities such as
S^{*}(E)
.

In practical terms, this means that many alternative sets of the
polynomial coefficients are generated by randomly varying the best-fit
coefficients
\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}
within their estimated uncertainties and
correlations. Each such set represents one plausible
S^{*}(E)
curve
consistent with the experimental data. The collection of these curves
is used to determine the uncertainty band of
S^{*}(E)
.

In practice, each Monte Carlo realization of the coefficient vector is
generated as

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{(k)}=\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,\xi_{i}^{(k)}\,\bm{e}_{i},

(14)

where
\lambda_{i}
and
\bm{e}_{i}
are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}
, and
\xi_{i}^{(k)}
are
independent standard normal random variables with zero mean and unit
variance. Here
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
denotes the standard normal
distribution with probability density

\displaystyle f(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\right).

(15)

All experimental data enter exclusively through the likelihood and are
used only to construct the posterior distribution
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
of
the polynomial coefficients. The Monte Carlo procedure samples this
coefficient posterior and is used solely to propagate the resulting
parameter uncertainties to derived quantities such as

\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
and
S^{*}(E)
. No experimental data are resampled
or modified at this stage.

Each Monte Carlo realization therefore defines an
S^{*}(E)
curve that
provides an acceptable global description of the experimental data. No
single curve can be expected to pass through all experimental points
within their quoted uncertainties. This is unavoidable because
different experiments are not perfectly consistent, the reported
uncertainties are finite, and the combined dataset exhibits statistical
scatter as well as normalization differences between measurements.
Consequently, requiring agreement with every individual data point
within its error bar is neither mathematically possible nor implied by
the likelihood.

The overall workflow (pipeline) of the analysis is

\displaystyle\text{data}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}\rightarrow P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)\rightarrow S^{*}(E).

(16)

IV.3
From posterior for polynomial coefficients to the global posterior in
y(E)

After the global likelihood has been constructed from the experimental
data, the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimate of the polynomial
coefficients,
\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}
, is obtained by maximizing the posterior
distribution
P(\bm{a}\,|\,D)
, which for Gaussian experimental
uncertainties is equivalent to minimizing the global

\chi^{2}(\bm{a})
.

In the vicinity of this maximum, the posterior distribution of the
coefficients is well approximated by a Gaussian form,
Eq. (
13
). This Gaussian approximation defines the
range of coefficient values
\bm{a}
that remain compatible with the
experimental data and their quoted uncertainties.

To propagate the resulting uncertainty in the coefficients to physical
quantities, sets of coefficients
\bm{a}
are drawn from this Gaussian
distribution. For each such set, the fitted function is

\displaystyle y(E)=\log_{10}S^{*}(E)=a_{0}+a_{1}(E-E_{0})+a_{2}(E-E_{0})^{2}.

(17)

This polynomial expression can be written compactly as

\displaystyle y(E)=\bm{v}(E)\cdot\bm{a},

(18)

where
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2})
and

\displaystyle\bm{v}(E)=\bigl(1,\;E-E_{0},\;(E-E_{0})^{2}\bigr).

(19)

In this way, the uncertainty in the coefficients is mapped onto an
uncertainty in the function
y(E)
.

At the fixed energy
E=E_{0}
, the global posterior distribution of

\displaystyle y_{0}\equiv y(E_{0})

(20)

can be written explicitly as

\displaystyle P(y_{0}|D)

\displaystyle=\int P(\bm{a}|D)\,\delta\!\left(y_{0}-\bm{v}(E_{0})\cdot\bm{a}\right)\,d\bm{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\,\sigma_{y}^{2}}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y_{0}-\mu_{y})^{2}}{2\,\sigma_{y}^{2}}\right],

(21)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{y}=y(E_{0};\bm{a}_{\rm MAP}).

(22)

The variance of
y(E)
at the fixed energy
E_{0}
is obtained by standard
error propagation in terms of the gradient of
y(E)
with respect to the
coefficient vector
\bm{a}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}^{2}(E_{0})=\bigl(\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E)\bigr)_{E_{0}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}\bigl(\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E)\bigr)_{E_{0}}.

(23)

For the quadratic parametrization (
17
),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

Writing the covariance matrix in its eigenvector expansion,

\mathbf{C}_{\bm{a}}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}\,\bm{e}_{i}\bm{e}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
the variance at
E_{0}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}^{2}(E_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}\bigl[\bm{e}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E_{0})\bigr]^{2}.

(25)

In the special case that
\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E_{0})
is aligned with one
eigenvector, e.g.
\nabla_{\bm{a}}y(E_{0})=\bm{e}_{1}
, this reduces to

\sigma_{y}^{2}(E_{0})=\lambda_{1}
.

Before specializing to a fixed energy, it is instructive to examine the

V.3
Combination of Heterogeneous Datasets

The Bayesian framework provides a natural means of combining datasets
with different normalizations, energy coverages, and quoted
uncertainties within a single global analysis. All datasets enter the
likelihood on equal footing, with their relative influence determined
by the assigned uncertainties rather than through arbitrary weighting choices.

The adoption of a minimum uncertainty
\sigma_{y}
ensures that no single
dataset or localized structure can dominate the global fit. This
prescription stabilizes the extraction of the smooth background trend
of
\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
while preserving the overall experimental
information carried by the combined dataset.

V.4
Width of the Posterior

When combining many datasets with different normalizations and quoted
uncertainties, the requirement of a single smooth global trend places
strong constraints on the fit. Only a narrow band of background curves
can simultaneously provide an acceptable description of all datasets
without introducing systematic deviations. The resulting narrow
posterior therefore reflects the strength of the global constraint on
the smooth component of
\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
, rather than an attempt to
reproduce localized structures or individual resonances.

The width of the posterior is governed by the following considerations:

•

The narrowing arises naturally from the large number of
experimental points (
N=614
) and the presence of multiple datasets,
rather than from unusually small uncertainties in any individual
measurement.

•

The fit is designed to capture the smooth global trend of

\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
over the selected energy interval. Narrow
resonances appear as localized deviations from this trend and
therefore do not bias the extracted global behavior.

•

To prevent localized structures or clusters of closely spaced
points from dominating the fit, an effective uncertainty

\sigma_{i}^{\rm eff}=\sigma_{i}/\sqrt{N}
is adopted when combining
N

measurements within a given energy region. This prescription
stabilizes the determination of the global trend while preserving
the overall experimental information.

VI
Conclusions

A Bayesian analysis of the astrophysical
S
factor for the

{}^{12}\mathrm{C}+{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
fusion reaction has been performed
over the energy range
1.5
–
3.5~\mathrm{MeV}
using a smooth quadratic
parametrization of
\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
. The smooth background trend is
extracted through a weighted quadratic regression in logarithmic space,
ensuring that the inferred posterior reflects the collective constraint
imposed by all datasets rather than being dominated by individual
measurements.

The analysis is designed to determine the global background behavior of
the astrophysical factor, while treating narrow resonant structures as
localized deviations that do not bias the smooth envelope. Although
individual experimental datasets exhibit relatively wide uncertainty
bands, their combined requirement of consistency with a single smooth
trend places strong constraints on the fit.

As a result, the posterior distribution of the polynomial coefficients
is tightly constrained by the full experimental dataset, which includes
direct measurements, Coulomb-renormalized Trojan Horse Method results,
and recent inverse-kinematics data. The resulting narrow posterior band for the smooth component of

\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
therefore reflects the collective constraint
provided by the combined data, rather than being driven by any single
experiment.

The present analysis does not rely on specific assumptions about fusion
hindrance and instead constrains the smooth background trend directly
from the combined experimental data.

At the astrophysically relevant energy
E=1.5~\mathrm{MeV}
, the
posterior uncertainty is sufficiently small that the result can be
quoted directly. The inferred value is

\displaystyle S^{*}(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})=\left(2.13^{+0.01}_{-0.01}\right)\times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{keV\,b},

(35)

corresponding to the
68\%
credible interval. This value lies within
the range of earlier extrapolations, including

S^{*}(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})=3.16\times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{keV\,b}
from the
compilation of Caughlan and Fowler
Caughlan
and

S^{*}(1.5~\mathrm{MeV})=2.03\times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{keV\,b}
from the
analysis of Gasques
et al.

Gasques
.

The present work does not attempt a detailed modeling of individual
resonances or a full R-matrix evaluation. Instead, it provides a
statistically robust empirical determination of the smooth background
behavior of
\log_{10}S^{*}(E)
in the energy region most relevant for
astrophysical extrapolation. The Bayesian framework developed here is
transparent, reproducible, and readily extendable as new experimental
data become available or in combination with more detailed
resonance-based descriptions when warranted.
