Association of maternal VDR and VDBP gene polymorphisms with spontaneous preterm birth risks: a nested case–control study in China
Authors: Nana Li, Mengling Zhou, Lu Li, Yuting Li, Meixian Wang, Ying Deng, Yanping Wang, Jun Zhu, Shu Zhou, Ping Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of vitamin D on spontaneous preterm birth (SPTB) is still not well understood. This study aimed to explore the relationship between maternal vitamin D receptor (VDR) and vitamin D-binding protein (VDBP) gene polymorphisms, as well as gene-gene interactions, and their potential association with the risks of SPTB.MethodsA nested case-control study involving 200 pregnant women with SPTB and 199 controls with full-term delivery was performed. A total of eight maternal single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were genotyped, including those in the VDR gene at the rs731236, rs7975232, rs1544410, rs2228570, rs10783219, and rs11568820 loci, as well as those in the VDBP gene at the rs4588 and rs7041 loci. The multivariate logistic regressions were used to estimate the association between VDR and VDBP gene polymorphisms, including alleles, genotypes and haplotypes, and the risks of SPTB. Generalized multifactor dimensionality reduction (GMDR) was used to identify gene–gene interactions.ResultsAmong eight SNP loci, none of the minor alleles showed a significant association with the risks of SPTB. Additionally, the variant genotypes of these eight SNPs were not associated with the risks of SPTB under additive, dominant, and recessive genetic patterns. Moreover, three haplotypes in VDR gene and three haplotypes in VDBP gene were identified, but none of them demonstrated a significant association with the risks of SPTB. Lastly, no significant gene-gene interactions among the different SNPs were found to influence the risks of SPTB.ConclusionsCommon maternal polymorphisms in the VDR and VDBP genes were not significantly associated with the risks of SPTB, and no significant gene-gene interactions related to SPTB were observed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08519-7.

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, is a prevalent adverse outcome in pregnancy. Globally, the rate of PTB was around 9.9% in 2020 [1]. In China, the PTB rate has been rising, from 5.9% in 2012 to 6.4% in 2018 [2]. Most PTBs are spontaneous (SPTB), resulting from either preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) [3]. PTB-related complications are the primary cause of death in newborns and children under the age of five years [4, 5]. PTB places a significant burden on healthcare systems and causes substantial emotional and financial stress for families. As a result, PTB is a critical public health concern, and there is an urgent need for its effective preventive interventions.

The occurrence of PTB is influenced by a combination of environmental and genetic factors. Maternal and fetal characteristics, as well as exposure to environmental chemicals, have been linked to an increased risk of PTB [4, 6–9]. Recent research has highlighted specific genetic variants that may predispose individuals to PTB. Studies focusing on candidate genes have identified maternal genetic polymorphisms in genes related to inflammatory, immunological, tissue remodeling, endocrine, metabolic, and vascular pathways as potential contributors to PTB risks [10]. Additionally, genome-wide association studies have uncovered new genetic variants and biological pathways associated with a higher likelihood of PTB [11, 12]. Despite these advancements, the complete understanding of PTB’s underlying causes remains elusive.

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin used by the body for normal bone development and maintenance, plays a crucial role in various physiological processes. Vitamin D is metabolized first by 25-hydroxylase in the liver to its major circulating form 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], which is further converted by the 25-hydroxyvitamin D-1α-hydroxylase in the kidneys to the active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)2D] [13]. During pregnancy, adequate vitamin D concentrations are not only necessary to ensure appropriate maternal responses to the calcium demands of the fetus and neonatal handling of calcium, but also of great importance to guarantee the healthy development of a broad variety of tissues, including the immune system [14]. The relationship between vitamin D and PTB remains inconsistent across studies. For example, some meta-analyses and observational studies have reported an association between vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy and an increased risk of PTB [15–18]. However, one meta-analysis has observed that vitamin D supplementation makes little or no difference in the risks of PTB compared to no intervention or placebo [19].

The vitamin D receptor (VDR), encoded by the VDR gene, is essential for mediating the effects of vitamin D [20]. 1,25(OH)2D acts as a ligand-dependent transcriptional regulator in the genomic pathway which involves binding of ligand to VDR, and leads to transcriptional regulation of targeted genes, thereby mediating pleiotropic physiological effects, including immune modulation, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activities across human tissues [21]. As a multifactorial and heterogeneous condition, PTB can be triggered by infection and inflammation [22]. Given that the maternal-fetal interface is a prime area of immune regulation, the VDR system appears to play a critical role at this interface, which is hypothesized to be the physical site of PTB [23]. Polymorphisms in the VDR gene, such as ApaI (rs7975232), BsmI (rs1544410), Cdx2 (rs11568820), FokI (rs2228570), and TaqI. (rs731236), may influence its expression, mRNA stability, and overall functionality, thereby affecting vitamin D activity [24]. To date, research on the relationship between VDR polymorphisms and PTB has yielded inconsistent findings. Some studies suggest that certain VDR polymorphisms are associated with an increased risk of PTB [25–30], while others indicate a reduced risk [31]. Additionally, some studies have found no significant association between VDR polymorphisms and PTB [32, 33].

Vitamin D-binding protein (VDBP), encoded by the group-specific component (GC) gene, also referred to as the VDBP gene, modulates vitamin D action by controlling the levels of free 25(OH)D or 1,25(OH)2D available to activate the VDR [34]. VDBP itself has been implicated as a potential biomarker of pregnancy outcomes as it has been linked to several adverse outcomes including PTB [35]. VDBP is also known to be encoded by one of the most polymorphic genes in humans [36]. Polymorphisms in the VDBP gene can result in an altered affinity of their variant proteins for vitamin D metabolites [37, 38]. The most common polymorphisms in the VDBP gene, rs4588 and rs7041, are both located in exon 11 and have been reported to be associated with the risks of various diseases, including acute myeloid leukemia and adverse pregnancy outcomes [39–41]. Currently, only two studies have investigated the association between rs4588 and rs7041 polymorphisms and PTB risks, and neither found a significant correlation [33, 42].

In this study, we initially examined the relationship between common maternal genetic polymorphisms in VDR and VDBP, including alleles, genotypes and haplotypes, and the risks of spontaneous preterm birth (SPTB). Subsequently, we investigated how interactions among different single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) might influence the risks of SPTB.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants and SNP genotype distributions

Presented in Table 1 are the baseline characteristics of all 399 study subjects, including 199 controls and 200 SPTB cases who had preterm deliveries predominantly resulted from PPROM. There were significant differences in the distributions of maternal age (≥ 35 years in 2.51% controls vs. 10.50% in cases, P = 0.005), parental smoking or ETS exposure (69.85% in controls vs. 59.00% in cases, P = 0.024). The other variables, including maternal ethnicity, education level, gravidity, pre-pregnancy BMI (ppBMI), medication use, alcohol consumption, and folic acid supplements did not show significant differences.

Association between maternal haplotypes and SPTB risks

Two haplotype blocks of VDR gene, including block 1 (rs7975232-rs1544410) and block 2 (rs10783219-rs11568820), and one haplotype block of VDBP gene (rs4588- and rs7041) are identified by Haploview software, and shown in Supplementary Appendix, Figure S1 and Figure S2.

The association between maternal haplotypes and SPTB risks was shown in Table 4. In the VDR gene, the haplotypes A-T, A-C, C-C at rs7975232 and rs1544410 loci, and the haplotypes A-T, T-C, A-C at rs10783219 and rs11568820 loci were not statistically associated with SPTB risks. In addition, the haplotypes G-C, T-A, G-A at rs4588 and rs7041 loci of VDBP gene were also not associated with SPTB risks.

Discussion

PTB is traditionally defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation. However, a recent concept paper with a consensus of experts recommended that a novel taxonomy for preterm birth should be promoted as PTB is a very heterogeneous syndromes with diverse etiologies and phenotypes [43]. Among these, SPTB comprises multiple phenotypes. In the current study, we specifically focused on SPTB cases characterized by PPROM, and did not find significant associations between maternal VDR and VDBP polymorphisms, including alleles, genotypes and haplotypes, and the risks of SPTB. Additionally, no significant interactions among different SNPs were observed in relation to SPTB risks.

In this study, variations at rs731236, rs7975232, rs1544410, rs2228570, rs10783219, and rs11568820 loci of VDR gene in mothers, including alleles, genotypes, and haplotypes, showed no association with SPTB risks. Consistent with our findings, a study in the Slovenian population found no significant link between Cdx2, FokI, TaqI, BsmI, or ApaI loci and SPTB risks [32], while a study in the Chinese population reported no significant association between SNP rs10783219 and PTB risks [33]. Our results partially align with another study, which indicated that individual maternal TaqI, BsmI, and ApaI SNPs did not increase PTB risks, though certain genotype combinations (BsmI-ApaI-TaqI) were associated with a reduced risk of PTB [31]. It is worth noting that our results differed from some previous studies. For instance, among two studies in an Israeli population, one study identified that maternal FokI variant was associated with an increased risk for SPTB with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.317 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.143,9.627) [25], another study found that women homozygous to ApaI (AA) genotype had a significant two-fold increased risk for PTB (OR = 1.973, 95% CI: 1.183–3.289) compared to heterozygous women [27]. Besides, a study from China also observed maternal FokI AA genotype as a risk factor for PTB (OR = 3.320, 95% CI: 1.560–7.066) [26]. In addition, an Italian birth cohort study showed that compared to the GG and GA genotypes of FokI polymorphism, mothers who carried AA genotype exhibited a higher PTB risk (OR = 12.049; 95% CI = 2.606–55.709) [28]. The study also showed that BsmI (rs1544410) decreased the risk for PTB in a recessive model (AA versus GG + AG, OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.89) through meta-analysis [28]. Moreover, a study in Northeastern Brazilian population reported that rs11568820 AA genotype was associated with an increased PTB risk (OR = 3.62; 95% CI: 1.41–0.9.27) [29]. Furthermore, a study in Brazil population demonstrated that the BsmI/TT and ApaI/AA genotypes were associated with an increased PTB risk, and the OR were 2.46 (95% CI: 1.07–5.65) and 7.23 (95% CI: 2.14–24.48), respectively, whereas TaqI/GG genotype was associated with a decreased PTB risk (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.05–0.72) [30]. The study design, heterogeneity of study population, and the differences in sample size may partly explain the inconsistency of existing research results.

In the VDBP gene, polymorphisms influence various pathophysiological pathways [44]. Among polymorphic loci, rs7041 and rs4588 located in exon 11 with complete LD are most common SNPs and extensively studied [39–41]. These two polymorphic loci have been reported to be associated with ethnicity [45]. In the present study, maternal VDBP gene rs7041 and rs4588 variants, including alleles, genotypes and haplotypes, showed no significant association with SPTB risks. In agreement with our results, a study from the Chinese population found that maternal VDBP gene rs4588 and rs7041 were not significantly associated with PTB risks, whereas rs7041 genotypes were statistically associated with gestational week [33]. Similarly, a study from the Republic of Korea reported that, compared to the GG allele, the odds ratios for preterm labor in rs7041 GT and TT carriers were increased by 1.463 times (P = 0.728) and 1.675 times (P = 0.640), respectively, but these results were not statistically significant [42]. In addition to PTB, in a study on association analysis of the polymorphisms at rs7041 and rs4588 loci of mothers and newborns and neonatal birth anthropometry, including birth weight, head circumference, growth-heel length, except for the finding of a significant association between neonatal rs4588 and head circumference, no other associations were found [46]. Overall, research on VDBP gene polymorphisms and birth outcomes remains limited.

It is widely recognized that genes do not operate in isolation but instead interact with one another continuously [47]. Epistatic gene-gene interactions are likely more prevalent than commonly assumed. For example, in studies on genetic susceptibility to non-syndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate (NSCL/P), significant gene-gene interactions were shown to influence NSCL/P risks. These included interactions between rs935865 and rs2337980 in nicotinic cholinergic receptor genes [48], between rs6446693 in MSX1 and rs2268625 in TGFB3 [49], between rs13158309 in BHMT and rs10514154 in DMGDH [50], between rs2237583 in PON1 and rs17166879 in PON2 [51], between rs17563 in BMP4 and rs560426 in ARHGAP29 [52]. Additionally, a study revealed significant interactions between maternal SNPs rs1316753, rs1915706, and rs642431 in BHMT, which influenced the risks of congenital heart disease [53]. However, there have been no reports on the association between gene-gene interactions and PTB risks. In our study, no significant interactions were found among the six maternal SNPs in the VDR gene and the two SNPs in the VDBP gene in relation to the risks of SPTB.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it was designed as a nested case-control study within a prospective cohort, enabling the prospective collection of information and outcome data while helping to control for confounding factors. Second, in addition to investigating the association between single SNP and SPTB risks, we also explored whether the interactions between eight SNPs were associated with the risks of SPTB, which could improve the prediction of the risks of SPTB in pregnant women, and help us understand the complex genetic architecture of SPTB. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which might have reduced statistical power and obscured potential associations. Second, other risk factors for SPTB, such as pregnancy-induced hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus, and maternal vitamin D levels, were not considered. Third, this study focused on analyzing the association between SPTB and maternal genotypes at the eight SNPs that have been extensively studied which might overlook other potentially relevant SNPs. Future studies with larger sample sizes, adjustments for additional confounding factors and broader SNP coverage are needed to validate or challenge our results.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that polymorphisms in the VDR gene at rs731236, rs7975232, rs1544410, rs2228570, rs10783219, and rs11568820 loci, as well as polymorphisms in the VDBP gene at rs4588 and rs7041 loci, were not linked to the risks of SPTB in pregnant women from mainland China. Additionally, no significant gene-gene interactions related to SPTB were identified.

Materials and methods

Study population and epidemiological data collection

This nested case-control study was part of the maternal drug exposure birth cohort that was conducted in 49 collaborating hospitals from August 2018 to December 2021 [54]. The recruitment criteria for pregnant women included: (1) participating in first prenatal examination during 6 to 14 gestational weeks, (2) intending to establish health record and give birth in same hospital, and (3) understanding the study objectives and willingly to cooperate with the research procedures. The exclusion criteria included: (1) having mental health disorders and being unable to complete questionnaire survey and the research, (2) have dead fetus in utero during the first prenatal visit, and (3) HIV-positive.

During the pregnancy period, each pregnant woman completed the self-administered questionnaires under the guidance of the investigators for first, second, and third trimesters. The questionnaire used in this study has been published in our previous study [46]. Four mL of EDTA anticoagulated maternal blood were collected in first and second trimester, respectively. After standing for 30 min, blood cells were obtained by centrifugation and stored at – 80 °C until genotyping.

Gestational week assignment was based on the last menstrual period (LMP) or calculated by ultrasound if menstruation was irregular. SPTB is defined as delivery before 37 gestational weeks following the preterm labor, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), or cervical insufficiency [55]. However, according to clinical guidelines for the prevention and treatment of preterm birth (version 2024) in China, SPTB is defined as preterm delivery before 37 gestational weeks following either preterm labor or PPROM [56]. In this study, SPTB cases included pregnant women who: (1) donated blood specimens, (2) had singleton pregnancy, and (3) eventually had preterm deliveries predominantly resulted from preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). The control group included pregnant women who: (1) donated blood specimens, (2) had singleton pregnancy, and (3) eventually had full-term delivery, (4) had structurally and functionally normal newborns, (5) were randomly selected as control group.

All the participants signed an informed consent form. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University (No. K2017045) and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

DNA extraction and genotyping

Maternal genomic DNA was extracted with magnetic bead method (BioTeke, Wuxi, China) according to the recommended protocol.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in the VDR and VDBP gene were selected based on the following criteria: (1) minor allele frequency (MAF) was over 5% in Han Chinese, (2) the SNPs have been reported to have associations with diseases or metabolic level of Vitamin D, and (3) the SNPs have been extensively studied. Concerning the VDR gene, five common polymorphisms including Cdx2 (rs11568820), FokI (rs2228570), TaqI (rs731236), BsmI (rs1544410), and ApaI (rs7975232) have been extensively studied due to their impact on various physiological and pathological traits [24, 28, 57, 58]. Additionally, rs10783219 and rs11568820 on the VDR gene exhibit high linkage disequilibrium (LD), leading to the selection of rs10783219 as a surrogate for rs11568820 [59]. Concerning the VDR gene, rs7041 and rs4588 located in exon 11 with complete LD are most extensively studied polymorphism sites [39–41]. In total, eight SNPs were selected, including rs731236, rs7975232, rs1544410, rs2228570, rs10783219, rs11568820 of VDR gene, rs4588 and rs7041 of VDBP gene. These SNPs were genotyped via multiple-polymerase chain reaction amplification and next generation sequencing (iGeneTech Bioscience Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Detailed information about the variants and genotyping is presented in Supplementary Appendix, Table S1.

For quality-control assessment, 10% of the samples were randomly selected and genotyped repeatedly, requiring a consistency rate of 100%.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using Quanto 1.2.4 [60]. Based on the smallest minor allele frequency (MAF = 0.0645) among the 8 SNPs, a 6% PTB incidence, a two-tailed α = 0.05, 75% power, and an assumed OR = 2.1 under a dominant model, the estimated required sample size was 187 case-control pairs. To enhance robustness, our study included a total of 399 participants: 200 SPTB cases and 199 controls.

Statistical analyses

The comparison of baseline characteristics between two groups was conducted by χ2 test. These characteristics included maternal age at the time of the last menstrual period, ethnicity, education level, gravidity, pre-pregnancy body mass index (ppBMI, kg/m2), medication use, parental smoking or environmental tobacco smoke (ETS) exposure, maternal alcohol consumption, and folic acid supplement.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) test was assessed in control group by using Plink software (http://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/ld.shtml). The linkage disequilibrium test and haplotype structures were performed by using Haploview 4.2 software. Unconditional logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the association between each allele, genotype, haplotype and SPTB risks by using Plink software. Generalized multifactor dimensionality reduction (GMDR, version 0.7, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA) was used to calculate the association of interaction of eight SNPs and SPTB risks.

All analyses were adjusted for covariates. For multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to correct for bias. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
