Attachment representations in Afghan refugees undergoing psychological treatment: a pilot study
Authors: Thomas Egger, Manuela Gander, Britta Dumser, Theresa Koch, Anna Buchheim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Refugees are at elevated risk of trauma-related disorders. Unresolved attachment is a vulnerability factor for post-traumatic stress, yet its role in Afghan refugees remains underexplored. This is the first study to assess attachment representations in this group using a validated narrative-based instrument.Objective: To examine attachment representations and their associations with psychological distress in a clinical sample of Afghan refugees.Method: Forty Afghan refugees (25% female; mean age = 25.4, SD = 7.2) receiving outpatient treatment at a specialised centre for displaced persons in southern Germany completed the Adult Attachment Projective Picture System (AAP). Psychological distress was assessed with the HSCL-25 and PCL-5, and traumatic events with the Essen Trauma Inventory. Interpreter support and cultural adaptations were applied. Attachment classifications were compared with published reference data. Exploratory analyses examined pre–post symptom change.Results: Unresolved attachment was found in 45% of participants – more frequent than in a non-clinical Middle Eastern group (p < .001), but not significantly different from a Western clinical group (p = .295) or an Arabic-speaking PTSD sample (p = .028, ns). Rates were significantly lower compared to a Western PTSD sample (p < .001). Unresolved attachment was not linked to higher baseline symptom severity (Mann–Whitney tests ns). Exploratory Wilcoxon tests showed significant symptom reductions across all attachment groups (all p < .05, large effects).Conclusions: Unresolved attachment is common in Afghan refugees but may not predict symptom burden in chronic trauma contexts. Defensive processes related to CPTSD and cultural norms may shape how unresolved states present in narrative assessments. Findings highlight the need for culturally responsive, attachment-informed care that may inform clinical adaptation in refugee mental health.KEYWORDS: Adult attachment, refugees, PTSD, Afghanistan, cross-cultural assessment, unresolved CPTSD, adult attachment projective

Introduction

Refugees are disproportionately affected by mental health disorders (Blackmore, Gray, et al., 2020; Henkelmann et al., 2020), particularly post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression, with prevalence rates far exceeding those in the general population (Blackmore, Boyle, et al., 2020). The prevalence of war-related PTSD was found to rise over time following the end of hostilities (Zasiekina et al., 2025). This is particularly relevant for Afghan refugees. Afghans constitute one of the world’s largest forcibly displaced populations, with an estimated 5.8 million individuals affected (UNHCR, 2025) and over 100,000 asylum applications submitted in the European Union in 2023 (European Council on Refugees and Exiles, 2024).

Insecure attachment is robustly associated with PTSD (Cushing et al., 2024) and complex PTSD (Maercker et al., 2022). While both insecure attachment classifications – dismissing and preoccupied – are associated with increased psychological vulnerability, meta-analytic evidence indicates that disorganised/unresolved attachment patterns show the strongest associations with post-traumatic stress symptoms (Woodhouse et al., 2015; Barazzone et al., 2019). This makes the unresolved classification particularly relevant for understanding psychopathology in forcibly displaced populations.

Despite these associations, attachment in trauma-affected Afghan populations – particularly refugees – remains largely unstudied. To date, no empirical study has investigated attachment representations in Afghan refugees, despite their distinct cultural background, prolonged exposure to collective violence, and complex migration trajectories. Due to cumulative trauma – often beginning in childhood (Tian et al., 2025) and compounded by war-related stressors (Karatzias et al., 2023; Levin et al., 2024) – the relationship between attachment and psychological distress may be especially relevant in this group.

Attachment across cultures

Attachment is a biologically rooted system activated by fear or separation to ensure proximity to the caregiver (Cassidy, 2008). Through early caregiver interactions, children develop internal working models that shape later attachment representations (Bretherton & Munholland, 2008). Although originally established in Western populations, categorical distinctions between secure- and forms of insecure attachment have been identified globally (Chui & Leung, 2016; De Haene et al., 2013; Voges et al., 2019), including culturally distinct contexts such as Israeli kibbutzim (Van Ijzendoorn & Sagi-Schwartz, 2008). Still, the prevalence of specific categories varies across cultures (Bakermans-Kranenburg et al., 2024; Madigan et al., 2023).

Caregiving forms – such as how distress is soothed – differ markedly (Mesman et al., 2018). Western cultures often emphasise autonomy and verbal responsiveness, while many non-Western societies value obedience and emotional restraint (Keller et al., 2018). In collectivist societies, emotional restraint serves to maintain social harmony, particularly in close relationships (Ramzan & Amjad, 2017). As a collectivist culture, Afghanistan may foster similar dynamics, potentially shaping the expression of attachment-related defences. According to George and West (2001), Bowlby’s concept of defensive exclusion describes how threatening attachment experiences may be kept out of awareness by transforming meaningful emotional signals into fragmented or unintelligible information. They identify three principal forms of such defensive processing – deactivation, cognitive disconnection, and segregated systems – which correspond to distinct patterns of attachment organisation. While these processes may co-occur, each represents a characteristic way of regulating attachment distress and provides a conceptual basis for classifying adult attachment representations.

Critics argue that attachment theory is shaped by Western moral assumptions (LeVine, 2014) and overlooks caregiving forms such as alloparenting (Röttger-Rössler, 2014). The field is also criticised for its overreliance on WEIRD samples: Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic (Henrich et al., 2010). Although attachment theory claims cross-cultural validity, critics stress the need for culturally sensitive instruments (Tkaczyk et al., 2025) and interpretations (Venta et al., 2017). Several studies have examined Western instruments in non-Western settings (Gander et al., 2018; Sümer et al., 2025), suggesting that they yield meaningful results when universal mechanisms are prioritised and cultural distortions are minimised.

Unresolved attachment and refugee mental health

The association between attachment and mental health disorders in Western populations is well established (Dagan et al., 2018; van Leeuwen et al., 2020; Zortea et al., 2021). Insecure attachment styles – avoidant/dismissing and anxious/preoccupied – have been linked to increased psychological distress (Zhang et al., 2022), while secure attachment appears protective (Cooke et al., 2019; Groh et al., 2016). Early trauma in turn may impair the development of secure attachment (Ogle et al., 2015). The fourth attachment category, referred to as either ‘disorganised’1 or ‘unresolved,’ has been most strongly associated with PTSD symptoms (Woodhouse et al., 2015), and is frequently observed in clinical populations (Mullen, 2019), as well as among abusive parents (Lo et al., 2019). These patterns make it particularly relevant for the present study.

In infants, disorganised attachment manifests in contradictory or fearful responses to caregivers (Solomon et al., 2017), including freezing or trance-like expressions (Lyons-Ruth & Jacobvitz, 2008). The adult analogue – unresolved attachment – is characterised by narrative incoherence in the face of attachment fear. This is observed when defensive strategies fail to integrate traumatic or threatening experiences (George & West, 2001, 2011), with sudden disruptions in discourse indicating emotional disorganisation (Madigan et al., 2006). These narratives may resemble those of other attachment classifications in content but differ in coherence and defensive structure. This indicates that individuals with an unresolved classification may become temporarily overwhelmed by attachment-related fears and unable to regulate their emotional responses. Notably, the narrative content and underlying defensive processes can resemble those of other attachment groups (George & West, 2011). In contrast, secure, dismissing, and preoccupied classifications, despite their different strategies, share the capacity to maintain narrative coherence and rely on organised defensive functioning. This conceptual similarity justifies grouping the remaining three attachment classifications (secure, dismissing, preoccupied) as ‘resolved’ attachment, as all three reflect the use of structured strategies that preserve coherence in the face of attachment-related stress.

Adult unresolved attachment is less frequently studied, compared to child and adolescent literature (Pollard et al., 2023). It is primarily measured categorically with tools such as the Adult Attachment Interview, AAI (George et al., 1985) or the Adult Attachment Projective, AAP (George & West, 2001), although dimensional approaches also exist (Briere et al., 2019). Widely used dimensional instruments are based on self-report and more vulnerable to bias (Pollard et al., 2023). Moreover, they do not capture unconscious processes or unresolved states as defined in narrative-based systems. It is therefore recommended to use categorical methods like the AAI or AAP in clinical contexts (Ravitz et al., 2010). These tools align with Bowlby’s model of attachment as an internal working model shaped by early caregiver relationships (Bowlby, 1969, 1973, 1980).

Unresolved attachment is strongly associated with PTSD in Western samples (Barazzone et al., 2019; Woodhouse et al., 2015). In the only known study examining caregiving-related attachment beliefs in Afghan families, Mousavi et al. (2020) found that Afghan mothers tended to interpret child behaviour as a need for security and responded positively, highlighting the role of cultural models in attachment. Refugee studies on attachment and mental health often include heterogeneous samples from various countries, limiting cultural interpretation (Egger et al., 2025). In these samples, insecure attachment has been linked to post-traumatic stress (Kurath et al., 2024), interpersonal trauma (Morina et al., 2016), and parental PTSD (van Ee et al., 2016). However, few studies examine unresolved attachment directly. A recent review identified strong parallels in attachment–distress associations between refugees and Western populations. Nonetheless, culturally homogeneous refugee samples are underrepresented (Egger et al., 2025).

This pilot study addresses this gap by examining adult attachment in a clinical sample of Afghan refugees. To our knowledge, it is the first study to assess attachment in this group using a validated narrative-based instrument, while controlling for cultural heterogeneity. The aim of the study was to investigate the distribution of attachment representations and their associations with psychological distress, including changes over the course of psychological treatment. For analysis, participants classified as secure, dismissing, or preoccupied were grouped as ‘resolved’ and compared to those classified as ‘unresolved’ (George & West, 2001).

We hypothesised (H1) that the rate of unresolved attachment would be significantly higher than in Western clinical samples and a previously studied non-clinical Middle Eastern sample (as the closest available reference to a healthy Afghan population), but not significantly different from a Western PTSD group or an Arabic-speaking refugee sample with PTSD (as a culturally closer clinical comparison group).

This hypothesis was based on the assumption that prolonged exposure to collective violence, chronic instability, and repeated losses may overwhelm defensive capacities and increase the likelihood of unresolved attachment. Refugees from Afghanistan are often confronted with these multiple stress factors. De Haene et al. (2010) emphasised that refugee parents’ narratives illustrate how extreme disruptive life experiences can precipitate unresolved and insecure discourse in adulthood. Furthermore, epidemiological research in eastern Afghanistan reported very high rates of depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms (Scholte et al., 2004). Although conducted in one province, the authors note that decades of armed conflict and repression have led to a high prevalence of mental health symptoms throughout Afghanistan, supporting the assumption that unresolved attachment may be particularly prevalent in this population compared to Western clinical samples.

We further hypothesised (H2) that participants with unresolved attachment would show greater symptom severity at therapy intake compared to participants with resolved attachment types (secure, avoidant, preoccupied). Additionally, we explored (H3) whether symptom levels declined during psychological treatment and whether change differed between resolved and unresolved groups. Although not originally designed as a treatment study, available pre–post data permitted exploratory analysis. Based on previous findings, we expected symptom reductions in both groups but hypothesised that unresolved participants would show significantly smaller improvements. This rationale was based on the assumption that unresolved attachment is marked by disorganised defensive processes and affective dysregulation (George & West, 2011), which may impair the establishment of a stable therapeutic alliance and interfere with treatment processes. In the context of refugee populations, these vulnerabilities may be compounded by additional challenges such as language barriers, cultural differences in expressing distress, and migration-related stressors. Taken together, these factors may limit treatment responsiveness in unresolved participants.

Methods

This exploratory cross-sectional study investigated adult attachment representations and psychological symptoms in a clinical sample of Afghan refugees.

Recruitment and participants

Participants were recruited at Refugio Munich, a specialised treatment centre for individuals affected by forced displacement and/or torture in southern Germany. Psychotherapists at the centre informed eligible Afghan clients about the study, and interpreter-assisted preliminary meetings were held as needed. Inclusion criteria comprised age 18 or older, Afghan origin, current outpatient psychotherapy, and the absence of acute suicidality. Individuals with intellectual disabilities or those whom the therapist deemed unsuitable for participation (e.g. due to acute distress or limited capacity to consent) were excluded from the study. Eligibility was determined by the treating psychotherapists in routine clinical practice, based on intake and ongoing clinical interviews. Acute suicidality and suitability for participation were evaluated within these clinical assessments. No additional structured diagnostic instruments were used specifically for study screening. No predefined psychometric cut-offs were applied, as standardised thresholds were considered inappropriate for this linguistically and culturally diverse population. To reduce potential bias, eligibility decisions were made collaboratively within the multidisciplinary team and guided by ethical principles prioritising participant stability and voluntariness. Data were collected on-site between September 2022 and July 2023. Interpreter support and culturally sensitive options (e.g. same-sex interpreters) were offered. All participants were receiving ongoing outpatient psychological treatment at the specialised centre during data collection. Treatments were evidence-based and trauma-focused, delivered within the standard clinical services of the centre. Depending on individual needs, therapists employed elements of trauma-focused cognitive-behavioural therapy (TF-CBT), narrative exposure therapy (NET), Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing (EMDR), and psychodynamic trauma therapy. In line with national guidelines, all treatments included phases of stabilisation, trauma processing, and integration, adapted to each client’s symptom profile and cultural background. Sessions were conducted individually, usually on a weekly basis, and treatment duration varied according to clinical needs. Therapists worked within a multidisciplinary team specialised in refugee mental health and received regular supervision.

Of 41 initial participants, one was retrospectively excluded due to age. The final sample comprised 40 adults. Symptom data at therapy intake were available for 27 participants. For the remaining 13, symptom screening could not be completed due to acute distress or clinical vulnerability (e.g. dissociation, decompensation or intrusive trauma responses). Trauma data (ETI) were available for 20 participants, as some clients could not complete this questionnaire due to distress, literacy limitations, or time constraints. This high rate of missing data likely reflects not only psychological burden but also common barriers to assessment among refugees, such as stigma, limited language skills, unfamiliarity with Western health models, unstable living conditions, and mistrust of authorities (Byrow et al., 2020).

Procedure

The study received ethical approval from the responsible institutional ethics board. All participants provided written informed consent in simplified German or in translated form. Attachment interviews were conducted by a trained clinical researcher. Interpreters and therapists were present as needed. Sessions lasted approximately one hour, were audio-recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were reviewed in ambiguous cases. Therapists at the treatment centre screened participants for emotional stability beforehand. Grounding options were available. No interviews had to be discontinued. Participants received €15 in compensation. Questionnaires were either completed in validated written Persian versions or translated orally using forward–backward translation procedures in accordance with established best practices (Bontempo, 1993). Attachment-related procedures were also carried out with the help of oral translation.

All study materials were prepared in collaboration with an Afghan interpreter and a social worker of Afghan origin. All participants could request a same-gender interpreter or psychotherapist, in recognition of gender norms in some Afghan communities. Three members of the research team worked at the treatment centre. All professionals involved in data collection received regular training in culturally sensitive psychological treatment and trauma-informed research with refugees. The sample included individuals from diverse urban and rural backgrounds and educational levels, reflecting the heterogeneity of the Afghan refugee population.

Measures

Psychological distress was assessed with the German version of the Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-25; Glaesmer et al., 2014). The HSCL-25 consists of 25 items (10 anxiety, 15 depression) rated on a 4-point scale. It has shown excellent psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s α of .84 for anxiety, .92 for depression, and .94 for the total score in a representative German sample. The HSCL has been validated for use in cross-cultural contexts (Mollica et al., 1992; Tinghög & Carstensen, 2010)

PTSD symptoms were measured using the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015), a 20-item self-report scale corresponding to DSM-5 criteria. The PCL-5 has demonstrated excellent reliability (α = .94–.95) and good test–retest reliability (r = .82) as well as convergent validity with other PTSD measures. Its validity and cultural applicability for refugee populations have been demonstrated (Ibrahim et al., 2018; Magwood et al., 2023).

To enhance accessibility, a written Farsi version of both instruments was made available. This version was prepared by professional interpreters at the treatment centre, following a forward–backward translation procedure in line with best practice recommendations (Bontempo, 1993).

Traumatic life events were assessed with the Essen Trauma Inventory (ETI; Tagay et al., 2007), a validated German self-report instrument, also used in cross-cultural contexts (Tagay et al., 2008). The ETI captures exposure to 14 categories of traumatic events and offers the opportunity to name further traumatic experiences that were not specified. The ETI has demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = .95 for the total scale; subscales α = .77–.87) and good construct validity, with significant correlations to established trauma measures and general psychopathology scales.

Attachment in adulthood, hereafter referred to as attachment representation, were assessed using the Adult Attachment Projective Picture System (AAP; George & West, 2001), a validated narrative-based interview. It consists of eight black-and-white drawings designed to elicit responses to attachment-related themes such as separation and loss. Narratives are coded for defensive processes and content and classified into four categories: secure, dismissing, preoccupied, and unresolved (George et al., 1985; George & West, 2001). The AAP has demonstrated high interrater and test–retest reliability, as well as strong convergent validity with the Adult Attachment Interview, with no reported effects of verbal intelligence or social desirability (George & West, 2011). These findings have been replicated in adolescent samples, further supporting the AAP’s discriminant validity (Gander et al., 2017), and suitability for use in clinical (Buchheim et al., 2008; Buchheim & Gander, 2023) and refugee populations (Gander et al., 2018). Prior to use, the images were reviewed by staff members of Afghan background at the treatment centre to ensure cultural appropriateness. Only negligible concerns were raised.

Data analysis

All AAP narratives were independently coded by two certified AAP raters, based on the translated transcripts. These transcripts included both the participants’ original narratives (in translation) and the interpreter’s translated speech from the audio recordings. Both raters were blind to participants’ symptom levels. The interrater reliability for the four-way classification was κ = 0.80, indicating substantial agreement. In cases of disagreement, a third certified rater provided the final classification. When uncertainties arose in the interpretation of the audio material, an additional interpreter was consulted to clarify the intended meaning.

Psychometric data were analysed to test the study hypotheses and examine changes in symptom severity using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 30). HSCL-25 scores were computed if no more than 5 items (20%) were missing; person-level means were used in such cases (n = 7). PCL-5 scores were calculated if no more than 2 items were missing; two cases were imputed, and one was excluded due to missing data. For the ETI, only the total number of experienced and witnessed events was analysed. AAP classifications required complete narratives for all seven codable images, which were available for all 40 participants.

Hypothesis 1 was tested via Fisher’s exact tests (2 × 2; unresolved vs. resolved). To contextualise the distribution of attachment classifications, data from the Afghan sample were compared with published reference groups derived from two sources that both used the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI): Bakermans-Kranenburg et al. (2024) and Riber (2016). Although the present study used the Adult Attachment Projective Picture System (AAP; George & West, 2001), this measure is conceptually and methodologically highly similar to the AAI. Both instruments are narrative-based representational measures grounded in attachment theory and share the same classification system (secure, dismissing, preoccupied, and unresolved). George and West (2001) reported strong interjudge reliability (κ = .79) and high convergence between AAP and AAI classifications (κ = .84, p < .001), supporting their methodological comparability and allowing meaningful cross-method comparisons.

From Bakermans-Kranenburg et al. (2024), three AAI-based reference groups were included: (1) Western clinical samples (N = 4,222), (2) Western clinical PTSD samples (N = 161), and (3) a non-clinical Middle Eastern sample (N = 399). The Western groups primarily consisted of adults from Europe and North America. The non-clinical Middle Eastern sample consisted exclusively of women and included data primarily from Israel. While this dataset may not fully represent Arabic-speaking Middle Eastern populations, it constitutes the only available non-clinical AAI dataset from the region and therefore provides the closest possible cultural comparison.

The fourth reference group was taken from Riber (2016), who examined Arabic-speaking refugees with PTSD (N = 43) at an outpatient trauma clinic for multicultural refugees in Denmark. The sample consisted of 21 women and 22 men, with 46% aged 40–49, 21% aged 30–39, 28% aged 50–59, and 5% aged 18–29. Most participants were married (56%), 37% were divorced or separated, and a minority were single (5%) or widowed (2%). Regarding origin, language, and religion, 60% originated from Iraq, 30% from Lebanon, and 10% from other countries in the Middle Eastern region. This group was included as a culturally and clinically relevant reference sample of adult refugees with PTSD assessed with the AAI. Where needed, published percentages were normalised to 100%, and case numbers were estimated based on published data and reconstructed where necessary. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s h.

Hypothesis 2 was tested via Mann–Whitney U tests comparing baseline symptom severity (HSCL, PCL-5) between unresolved and resolved groups. Hypothesis 3 examined changes in symptom severity over the course of psychological treatment. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests assessed within-group changes, while Mann–Whitney U tests compared symptom reduction between groups. Effect sizes were reported as r or h. Bonferroni–Holm correction (Holm, 1979) addressed multiple testing; one- or two-tailed tests were used depending on hypothesis direction.

Results

Descriptive results

Forty Afghan refugees undergoing psychological treatment at the treatment centre were included in the study. All were assessed for attachment. Ages ranged from 18 to 52 years (M = 25.4, SD = 7.2); 25% were female. All assessments were conducted by clinicians trained in culturally sensitive methods. For Hypothesis 2, data were available for 27 participants who completed both the AAP and baseline symptom measures (HSCL, PCL-5). This subsample (M = 27.1, SD = 7.4) was 22.2% female and 66.7% single. Regarding integration, 33.3% were unemployed, 44.5% were attending school or language courses, and 11.1% were employed. One quarter (25%) had arrived as unaccompanied minors; 92.6% lacked permanent residency. Of the 26 participants for whom medication data were available, 69.2% were taking psychotropic medication.

For Hypothesis 3, pre–post symptom data were available for 17 participants. This group was comparable in age (M = 28.4, SD = 8.1) and gender (29.4% female); employment and residency mirrored the larger sample.

According to the Essen Trauma Inventory (ETI; N = 20; 25% female; M = 27.7 years, SD = 8.1), participants reported high cumulative exposure to multiple traumatic events. The most frequently reported trauma types were physical assault by another person (90%), serious accident, fire, or explosion (85%), death of a significant other (75%), captivity (65%), and natural disaster (60%). These findings indicate extensive exposure to both interpersonal and non-interpersonal trauma, reflecting the chronic and cumulative impact of war, displacement, and loss.

A detailed overview of all trauma categories is presented in the Supplementary Materials (Figure S1), together with full subgroup data for all analyses, including the exploratory Hypothesis 4 (Supplementary Materials, Table S1).

Participants with and without complete data did not differ meaningfully in age or gender distribution. Given the small sample size and limited statistical power, no formal drop-out analysis was performed; however, descriptive inspection indicated no systematic bias. Descriptive comparison of the subgroup characteristics (see Supplementary Table S1) showed no meaningful demographic differences across the analytical subsamples (H2–H4). Age, gender, marital status, and occupational situation were comparable, suggesting that selection bias due to incomplete data was unlikely.

Hypothesis 2 was not supported, as baseline symptom severity did not differ significantly between attachment groups. Contrary to expectations, the unresolved group reported even lower mean PTSD scores than the resolved group (M = 48.69 vs. 56.29), with a medium effect size despite the non-significant result.

Exploratory hypothesis 3: symptom change by attachment group

We examined whether symptom changes from pre- to post-treatment differed by attachment classification (see Table 3). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed significant symptom reductions from T1 to T2 in both groups. In the unresolved group (n = 10), reductions were observed for anxiety (p < .01, r = .77), depression (p < .01, r = .89), general distress (p < .01, r = .84), and PTSD symptoms (p < .01, r = .81). In the resolved group (n = 7), similar reductions were found: anxiety (p = .03, r = .70), depression (p = .01, r = .83), general distress (p = .01, r = .83), and PTSD symptoms (p < .01, r = .90).

Mann-Whitney U tests revealed no significant differences between unresolved and resolved attachment groups regarding absolute or relative change in HSCL scores (p = .16, r = .26). Effect sizes were in the small to medium range. For the PCL-5, no significant group differences were found for either absolute (p = .44, r = .04) or relative change (p = .24, r = .19). These findings cautiously indicate significant within-group improvements but no between-group differences.

Discussion

This pilot study is the first to assess attachment representations in severely traumatised adult Afghan refugees and, to our knowledge, the first to use a validated instrument for this purpose in any Afghan population – enabling meaningful comparisons with Western data. As hypothesised, unresolved attachment was common. Its prevalence exceeded that of a non-clinical Middle Eastern group and was similar to an Arabic-speaking refugee sample with PTSD.

However, contrary to expectations, it was significantly lower than in a Western PTSD sample and did not differ from a general Western clinical group. Contrary to expectations, unresolved attachment was not associated with greater symptom severity at intake. Moreover, no significant between-group differences emerged in symptom change or post-treatment severity.

In line with our exploratory hypothesis, both attachment groups showed significant symptom reductions over the course of therapy. Descriptively, unresolved participants even reported slightly lower symptom scores at treatment end.

Attachment and symptom severity in Afghan refugees

The lower unresolved rate in our sample compared to Western PTSD populations was unexpected. Although a percentage of 45 is relatively high, one might have expected even higher rates given the extremely adverse developmental conditions faced when growing up in Afghanistan. This includes limited access to primary care (Salem et al., 2024), malnutrition (Roozbeh et al., 2018), low health literacy (Fayaz et al., 2014) and high rates of mental disorders (Scholte et al., 2004). Given this evidence, it is striking that such a multifaceted burden among Afghan refugees does not appear to translate into a proportionally high rate of unresolved attachment representations. In refugees, unresolved discourse often reflects trauma, forced migration, and loss (De Haene et al., 2010). However, few studies quantify unresolved attachment in refugees. Existing data show wide variation: 2.7% in adolescents (Bettmann & Olson-Morrison, 2020), 26.5% in children (van Ee et al., 2016), and 69.8% in adults (Riber, 2016). In this study, most participants reported multiple traumatic experiences (ETI) and produced AAP narratives marked by distrust, helplessness, and globally negative representations of self and others. These patterns resemble the ICD-11 definition of Complex PTSD (CPTSD), which involves disturbances in affect regulation, self-concept, and relational functioning (WHO, 2025). Chronic, inescapable stress is also a known risk factor for CPTSD (Brewin et al., 2017), which has been observed in refugees (Liddell et al., 2019) and is frequently observed at our centre. A potential explanation for the lower-than-expected unresolved rate in our study may lie in CPTSD’s defensive nature. Chronic trauma may result in emotional numbing or detachment (Ford & Courtois, 2021), limiting narrative engagement with emotionally salient stimuli. In the AAP, this can suppress verbal signs of disorganisation, leading to a different classification. Such individuals may be categorised as dismissing despite extensive trauma exposure. Dismissing attachment is characterised by deactivating defensive strategies that distance the individual from emotional distress. Dismissing AAP narratives typically deflect from fear or vulner-ability, instead focusing on achievement, independence, or trivial detail (George & West, 2011). These patterns can mask underlying trauma-related disorganisation, particularly in individuals with complex trauma.

Moreover, participants in our sample originate from a collectivist culture in which emotions are generally more likely to be suppressed in everyday interactions compared to individualist societies (Huwaë & Schaafsma, 2018; Tahmouresi et al., 2014). A very recent study further found that although men typically report negative emotions such as fear or depression with lower intensity than women, this gender difference tends to be smaller in collectivist cultures and in societies characterised by greater gender inequality, such as Afghanistan (Wollast et al., 2025). However, given the limited and inconsistent data for attachment in refugees, further studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms and cultural expressions of unresolved attachment in forcibly displaced populations.

No significant baseline symptom differences were found between unresolved and resolved groups in this study. All exceeded clinical thresholds for anxiety, depression, and PTSD, and reported an average of over seven traumatic events (see Supplementary Material). A likely explanation for the absence of group differences is a ceiling effect (Taku et al., 2018), which may have limited variability and masked group differences – possibly also explaining null findings in exploratory analyses.

Nonetheless, both groups improved significantly during therapy, in line with previous findings on treatment efficacy in severely burdened refugee populations (Hettich et al., 2020; Kip et al., 2020). This suggests – albeit cautiously – that unresolved attachment may not hinder treatment-related improvement in this population. These exploratory results, however, are based on small subsamples and should be interpreted with caution. The absence of between-group differences may also reflect measurement overlap. A recent large-scale analysis (N = 3,218) found that preoccupied and unresolved AAI indicators loaded onto a shared factor. Although a three-factor model fit the data, the preoccupied and unresolved dimensions were highly correlated (Raby et al., 2022). Grouping secure, dismissing, and preoccupied participants into a single ‘resolved’ category may therefore obscure more nuanced associations. It is also possible that some participants initially classified as unresolved no longer met criteria for this category by the end of therapy, as shown in previous research (Buchheim et al., 2017, 2018). While psychological treatment has been shown to influence unresolved attachment, a follow-up assessment was not feasible in this study.

Cultural and religious influences on attachment and distress

In addition to clinical and structural factors, cultural models of attachment and mental health must be considered when interpreting data from Afghan refugees.

Cultural understandings of distress in Afghanistan shape how symptoms are expressed. These include gender norms (e.g. strict separation of men and women), the absence of direct terminology for PTSD in Afghan dialects, and context-specific daily stressors (Ventevogel & Faiz, 2018). Such factors highlight the importance of interpreters who are fluent not only in language but also in cultural frameworks. Interpreter-assisted care has been shown to improve both outcomes and the therapeutic alliance in refugee treatment (Fennig & Denov, 2021). However, shared national backgrounds may also evoke mistrust, and translation alone is insufficient without deeper cultural-linguistic sensitivity (Naseh et al., 2019). Given the high percentage of male participants in this sample, gender differences may have gone undetected. Further research may be required to disentangle gender-based variation in the expression of symptoms and attachment.

Religious values may also influence help-seeking behaviour and emotional expression. In Islamic contexts, suicide and self-harm are strongly prohibited, which may affect how suffering is disclosed (Paiman & Khan, 2017). However, cultural and religious influences on attachment in Afghan populations remain underexplored – reflecting a broader gap in refugee mental health research.

Integrating attachment into refugee mental health care

Consistent with previous findings (Egger et al., 2025), the current results demonstrate a close resemblance between attachment and mental health patterns in refugees and Western populations. These findings underscore the potential for integrating attachment-based therapeutic approaches more systematically into refugee treatment.

One example would be the implementation of strategies, based on mentalization/reflective functioning which refers to the capacity to interpret one’s own and others’ behaviour as expressions of underlying mental states (Fonagy & Target, 1997). Attachment style influences Reflective Functioning by shaping self- and other-understanding and openness to shared meaning-making (Fonagy et al., 2023). It is associated with psychopathology, therapy outcomes (Katznelson, 2014), and may mediate and moderate therapeutic change (Luyten et al., 2024). Clinically, fostering reflective functioning and – where appropriate – assuming a secondary attachment role may promote earned secure attachment in clients with early adversity (Filosa et al., 2024). Attachment-based therapies like ABFT (Diamond et al., 2016) may be useful as well, but require deep cultural adaptation, including attention to social roles, emotion norms, help-seeking behaviours, and collective healing models.

To be effective in refugee patients, therapeutic approaches require careful adjustment. Cultural responsiveness must extend to healthcare systems by providing conceptual clarity, reducing structural barriers, and involving refugees in the cultural adaptation of treatment approaches (Lau & Rodgers, 2021). A recent study (STARS) illustrates the feasibility and acceptability of a culturally adapted group intervention developed specifically for Afghan refugees (Dumser et al., 2025). Evidence suggests that deep structural adaptations – beyond surface-level modifications – are most effective (Hosseini et al., 2023). Therapeutic work with refugees is often emotionally intense. Nevertheless, posttraumatic growth has been observed even alongside high symptom levels, especially intrusive symptoms, suggesting that engagement with trauma may support psychological growth (Kangaslampi et al., 2022).

Strengths and limitations

This study offers rare and detailed insights into adult attachment representations in Afghan refugees – a severely under-researched group – using a standardised projective measure (AAP) and clinically rich, interpreter-supported data collection. The combination of narrative assessment, symptom measures, and the context real-world psychological treatment makes this study one of the first of its kind and a valuable contribution to cross-cultural attachment research. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered.

First, the sample size – particularly in the subgroup with complete psychometric data – was small, limiting statistical power and generalisability. The partially retrospective design also introduced variability in treatment duration at the time of AAP administration, which may have influenced attachment narratives or classifications (Konvalin et al., 2023).

Second, although interpreter-mediated procedures were implemented with cultural sensitivity and included professionals of Afghan heritage, some loss or transformation of meaning during translation or transcription cannot be ruled out. The first author’s positionality as a white European male may have influenced narrative disclosure – particularly among female participants – in light of gender norms and cultural expectations. Despite efforts to create a respectful and safe setting, cross-cultural and gendered dynamics may have shaped participants’ engagement with emotionally salient material.

Third, the emotionally charged nature of the AAP and the symptom questionnaires, combined with the intercultural setting, may have introduced social desirability or emotional inhibition. Feelings of shame or discomfort could have led participants to withhold or modify their narratives.

Forth, the non-clinical Middle Eastern reference group included data primarily from Israel, which may limit its representativeness for Arabic-speaking populations. Nevertheless, as no other AAI data from the Middle East are currently available, this sample provides the best-available regional reference for cross-cultural comparison.

Finally, all researchers were trained in Western psychological traditions. As researchers and clinicians involved in this study, we acknowledge that our own cultural backgrounds, clinical training, and professional roles may have influenced the design, implementation, and interpretation of the study. While efforts were made to enhance cultural sensitivity – such as collaboration with Afghan cultural mediators and the use of adapted materials – we recognise that unconscious biases and outsider perspectives may have shaped certain assumptions. Future studies may benefit from more explicit participatory designs and critical reflection on researcher positionality.

Conclusion

This exploratory study provides the first empirical data on adult attachment representations in Afghan refugees receiving psychological treatment. A high rate of unresolved attachment was found, alongside severe psychological distress and cumulative trauma exposure.

However, unresolved attachment was not associated with greater symptom severity or poorer treatment outcomes. This may reflect the defensive features of Complex PTSD (CPTSD), which is common in refugee groups and characterised by emotional numbing, detachment, and relational difficulties. Such patterns may reduce narrative disorganisation in projective measures like the AAP, potentially leading to underclassification of unresolved states. These findings underscore the need to interpret attachment carefully taking trauma complexity and cultural meaning into account.

Future studies should also investigate gender-specific pathways linking trauma exposure, attachment, and psychological adaptation among Afghan refugees. Afghan women, in particular, face disproportionate exposure to gender-based violence and systemic oppression. An estimated 50.8% of Afghan women aged 15–49 have experienced physical and/or sexual violence, with rates reaching up to 92% in some provinces; the situation has further deteriorated since August 2021 due to policies restricting women’s autonomy and dismantling GBV protection systems (UN Women, 2024). Understanding how these gendered experiences interact with attachment representations, trauma-related symptoms, and recovery processes could inform more gender-sensitive, resilience-oriented approaches to treatment.

Despite the small sample size, results suggest that attachment-based approaches – particularly those fostering reflective functioning and relational safety – may be beneficial in refugee treatment. However, their implementation requires deep cultural and structural adaptation, including sensitivity to gender norms, help-seeking behaviours, and collective models of healing. Further research is needed to clarify how CPTSD, culture, and attachment interact in shaping both symptom expression and treatment response in forcibly displaced populations.
