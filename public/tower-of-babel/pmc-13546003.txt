Beyond Traditional Psychiatric Care: A Systematic Review of Multidisciplinary Approaches to Mitigating Adolescent Suicide Risk
Authors: Nakaraj Pluetrattanabha, Thanyaporn Direksunthorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe World Health Organization estimates that more than 720,000 people die by suicide each year and that suicide is the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15 to 29 years; in the United States it ranks second among those aged 10 to 24 years. Rising adolescent ideation, planning and attempts have exposed the limits of siloed care.MethodsThis systematic review was reported according to PRISMA 2020, incorporating scoping elements for guidelines and policy frameworks. Nine databases were searched for English-language literature published between January 2018 and February 2026. Of 7496 records identified and 1680 screened after duplicate removal, 26 sources met the inclusion criteria. Design-appropriate instruments (AMSTAR 2, RoB 2, Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, CASP) were used to appraise quality. Given heterogeneous designs, no meta-analysis was performed; findings were synthesized narratively across five pre-specified domains, with risk determinants organized using the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP).ResultsHiTOP dimensions of fear, distress, externalization, thought disorder and traumatic stress organized otherwise disparate risk data. Medically and legally complex subgroups, including adolescents with thalassemia major and justice-involved youth, carried compounded risk requiring cross-sectoral care. In primary care, universal screening and the Safety Planning Intervention with structured follow-up were the best-supported components. Pharmacogenomic testing showed consistent genotype-outcome associations but modest and inconsistent clinical benefit in randomized data, with no adolescent trial reporting suicide outcomes. School programs improved help-seeking and mental health literacy more reliably than they reduced attempts. Digital monitoring achieved high temporal resolution but low positive predictive value. Role ambiguity, non-interoperable records and unmanaged care transitions were the dominant implementation barriers.ConclusionCoordinated multidisciplinary care is supported, but the strength of evidence differs sharply by component. Care navigation, shared risk registries and integrated behavioral health warrant priority; pharmacogenomic and algorithmic tools require adolescent-specific trials before routine adoption.Keywords: adolescent suicide, multidisciplinary team, interprofessional collaboration, pharmacogenomics, school-based prevention, digital health, primary care

Introduction

Suicide is among the leading causes of death in young people worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that more than 720,000 people die by suicide each year and that suicide is the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15 to 29 years.1 The problem is not confined to high-income settings. A survey of 146,345 adolescents aged 12 to 15 years across 54 low- and middle-income countries documented substantial ideation and attempt prevalence on every continent studied,2 and a nationwide survey of individuals reporting ideation found that 80.4% of suicide plans and 83.9% of attempts occurred within one year of the first onset of ideation.3 This narrow interval between the emergence of ideation and action is what makes early, widely distributed detection a global rather than a national requirement.

Surveillance data from the United States illustrate the trajectory within a well-characterized monitoring system. The suicide rate among children aged 10 to 14 years tripled between 2007 and 2018 and has since remained at historically high levels, while the rate among adolescents aged 15 to 19 years rose by approximately 57% between 2009 and 2017.4,5 Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System data for 2021 recorded higher rates of considering (30.0% versus 14.3%), planning (23.6% versus 11.6%) and attempting suicide (13.3% versus 6.6%) among female compared with male adolescents, alongside a 144% increase among Black youth aged 10 to 17 years between 2007 and 2020.4,6 These disparities, compounded by post-pandemic deterioration in adolescent mental health, prompted the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and partner organizations to declare a national emergency in child and adolescent mental health.7

Suicide is nevertheless preventable, and the international evidence base is conventionally organized across three levels of coverage.8,9 Universal interventions reach an entire population: school mental-health literacy curricula such as Youth Aware of Mental Health (YAM), which reduced incident suicide attempts and severe ideation in the SEYLE cluster-randomized trial conducted across ten European countries;10 restriction of access to lethal means; and responsible-reporting guidance for media and online platforms.8 Selective interventions target identifiable higher-risk groups, for example routine depression and suicide-risk screening at pediatric primary care visits,7,11 or focused assessment of adolescents living with chronic illness or in contact with the justice system. Indicated interventions serve young people already displaying suicidal behaviour and include the Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) delivered with structured follow-up contact, which in acute-care settings has been associated with fewer subsequent suicidal behaviours and improved engagement with outpatient treatment.12,13

These examples share a structural feature that is easily overlooked: no single profession or sector delivers more than one of them. Universal programs are delivered by teachers, selective screening by primary care clinicians, pharmacological safety by prescribers and pharmacogenomic services, continuous monitoring by digital platforms, and continuity of care by social workers and case managers. For the purposes of this review, a multidisciplinary approach denotes the coordinated involvement of two or more professional groups spanning, at minimum, health care, education, and social or legal services, which share information, hold agreed roles and work to a common care plan for the same adolescent. This is distinct from parallel service provision without formal linkage. Where coordination extends across organizational or ministerial boundaries, we use the term cross-system integration.

Recent reviews have summarized youth suicide prevention interventions,8,9 school-based programs,14 and risk management within primary care,15 but each has generally examined one sector at a time. None has combined those strands with pharmacological safety at the genomic level and the governance of algorithmic monitoring, and none has paired that synthesis with an explicit analysis of the structural conditions, namely role definition, information flow and accountability at care transitions, that determine whether any of these interventions can be delivered together. This review addresses that gap by answering four questions. (1) Which biopsychosocial determinants structure adolescent suicide risk, and can they be organized within a single dimensional framework? (2) Which multidisciplinary interventions are currently supported across clinical, genomic, educational and digital settings, and how strong is the evidence for each? (3) Which structural barriers impede interprofessional and cross-system collaboration in practice? (4) Which concrete organizational mechanisms are proposed to overcome those barriers?

Methods

Review Design and Reporting

This study was designed as a systematic review and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.16 Because the eligible literature spans randomized trials, observational epidemiology, qualitative implementation research and formal clinical guidance, two methodological decisions follow. First, elements of the Arksey and O’Malley framework as refined by Levac et al were used to chart guidelines and policy frameworks, which do not fit a conventional effect-estimate synthesis.17 We therefore describe the work as a systematic review incorporating scoping elements, and we make no claim to have produced a pooled estimate of intervention effect. Second, because the included designs differ in kind rather than only in quality, results are synthesized narratively in line with Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidance.18 No meta-analysis was performed.

The review was structured around five pre-specified domains: (i) biopsychosocial determinants of risk; (ii) primary care integration and clinical frameworks; (iii) pharmacogenomics and prescribing safety; (iv) school-based and community prevention; and (v) digital health and algorithmic monitoring, together with a cross-cutting analysis of structural barriers to collaboration. The protocol was not prospectively registered, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Rationale for Synthesizing Heterogeneous Designs

The included studies range from a genome-informed clinical analysis to an interrupted time-series evaluation of a school gatekeeper program. Combining them requires an explicit justification. Each included source was assigned a priori to the single domain of the prevention pathway it informs, and evidence was compared only within domains, never across them. Design-appropriate appraisal instruments were applied so that a cross-sectional prevalence study was never rated against criteria intended for a randomized trial, and the resulting rating is reported alongside every study in Table 1 so that readers can weight each contribution accordingly. The synthesis therefore answers the question of which components of a multidisciplinary pathway are supported and how firmly, rather than the question of how large a single pooled effect is. Strength of conclusion is graded verbally throughout: “supported by randomized evidence”, “supported by observational evidence”, and “emerging, not yet established”.

Search Strategy

Searches were executed across PubMed, Web of Science (Core Collection), Scopus, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, EBSCO and ProQuest. The search window ran from 1 January 2018 to 28 February 2026. The syntax combined Medical Subject Headings with free-text terms; the PubMed string was: (“adolescent”[MeSH] OR youth OR teenager) AND (“suicide”[MeSH] OR “suicidal ideation” OR self-harm) AND (multidisciplinary OR interprofessional OR “integrated care” OR pharmacogenomic* OR “digital health” OR “school-based” OR “primary care”). Reference lists of included articles were hand-searched. Seminal pre-2018 trials identified by that hand-search were retained as contextual references and are explicitly identified as such below.

Eligibility Criteria

Sources were eligible if (1) the target population comprised adolescents aged 10 to 19 years, with permissible extension to 24 years where transitional data were relevant to pediatric continuity of care; (2) the study addressed suicide prevention, suicidal ideation, or self-harming behaviour as a primary outcome or primary analytic focus; (3) it evaluated a multidisciplinary strategy, structural collaboration, screening protocol, or a technological or pharmacological innovation relevant to that pathway; and (4) it was a peer-reviewed empirical study (quantitative, qualitative or mixed-methods), a systematic or scoping review, or a formal clinical guideline. Exclusion criteria were non-peer-reviewed grey literature, studies restricted to adult populations, and studies lacking transparent methodological reporting. Language was restricted to English.

Selection, Data Extraction and Quality Appraisal

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by both authors, followed by independent full-text assessment; disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached. Data were extracted into a standardized template capturing setting, design, sample, intervention mechanics, primary outcomes and reported barriers to collaboration.

Methodological quality was appraised with instruments matched to design: AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews and meta-analyses,35 the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized trials,36 the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomized comparative and cross-sectional studies,37 and the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist for qualitative studies.38 Scoping reviews and clinical guidelines were not appraised with these instruments, and are identified accordingly in Table 1.

Included Evidence versus Contextual Sources

Twenty-six sources met the inclusion criteria and constitute the evidence base of this review. All twenty-six appear in Table 1; none is presented as an illustrative example only. A further group of references is cited for context and is not counted among the included studies: epidemiological surveillance reports and fact sheets, methodological instruments and reporting guidelines, landmark trials published before the search window, and one study from pediatric oncology used solely as an analogy for pharmacogenomic implementation. Results

Study Selection and Characteristics of Included Studies

The search returned 7496 records. After removal of 5816 duplicates, 1680 titles and abstracts were screened and 749 were excluded. Of 931 reports sought for retrieval, 623 could not be obtained in full text, leaving 308 reports assessed for eligibility. Two hundred and eighty-two were excluded because they were not peer-reviewed (n = 54), addressed exclusively adult populations (n = 196), or lacked transparent methodological reporting (n = 32). Twenty-six sources were included (Figure 1).

The included evidence comprises eight systematic reviews or meta-analyses, two scoping reviews, one narrative review of national surveillance data, one randomized controlled trial, one comparative cohort study with follow-up, one register-based cohort study, one longitudinal cohort study, five cross-sectional studies, four qualitative studies and two formal clinical guidelines. Study settings span North America, Europe, the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia and 54 low- and middle-income countries. Sample sizes range from 15 professionals in a qualitative interview study to 146,345 adolescents in a multi-country survey. Table 1 presents the full set of included sources with design, sample, principal finding and appraised quality.

Methodological Quality of the Evidence Base

Of the eight systematic reviews and meta-analyses appraised with AMSTAR 2, three were rated high confidence and five moderate confidence; none was rated critically low. The single included randomized trial was rated low risk of bias on RoB 2. Non-randomized comparative and cross-sectional studies scored between 7 and 8 of 9 on the NOS, with the most frequent deductions relating to non-response reporting and to reliance on self-reported outcomes. The four qualitative studies met between 8 and 10 of the 10 CASP criteria, with the recurring shortfall being limited reflexivity regarding the relationship between researcher and participants. Two scoping reviews, one narrative review and two clinical guidelines were not appraised with these instruments and their conclusions are therefore reported descriptively rather than as graded evidence.

The overall picture is of an evidence base that is methodologically sound in its epidemiological and review components, but thin in randomized evidence for the newer interventions. Only one included study was a randomized trial, and it concerned adults rather than adolescents. This asymmetry is reflected in the strength of the conclusions drawn below.

Biopsychosocial and Contextual Determinants of Risk

The HiTOP Model as an Organizing Framework

Systematic review evidence applying the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) identifies five interconnected dimensions associated with adolescent suicidal behaviour: fear, distress, externalization, thought disorder and traumatic stress. Adolescents with high negative emotionality and callousness show elevated risk of ideation, whereas higher effortful control operates as a mitigating factor.30 Dimensional models of this kind are useful here for a practical reason beyond nosology: they describe risk in gradations that correspond to the intensity of response a system can afford, which we develop into a resource-allocation proposal in the Discussion. The complementary defeat–entrapment model, synthesized across studies of adolescents and young adults, converges on the same conclusion that transdiagnostic dimensions predict ideation more consistently than categorical diagnoses.28

Suicidal ideation also manifests physiologically, which places primary care in a position to detect it. Coexisting anxiety and depression increase propensity through somatic symptoms including sleep disturbance, fatigue, palpitations and pervasive worthlessness. Both short, interrupted sleep and prolonged hypersomnic sleep are associated with higher likelihood of ideation and attempts, and increased sedentary time is associated with greater psychological risk.2,30

Relational, Peer and Systemic Stressors

Relational and systemic stressors act as environmental catalysts. Disruption of family dynamics, ranging from authoritarian parenting and emotional neglect to parental psychiatric disorder, substance use and intergenerational modelling of suicidal behaviour, elevates risk, whereas active listening, shared decision-making and emotional warmth foster emotional regulation and are among the more robust protective factors.30 Interpersonal risk is also patterned by identity: among transgender youth, interpersonal factors including family rejection and peer victimization accounted for a substantial share of suicidality risk.22 Elevated risk has likewise been documented at the intersection of autism and exceptional cognitive ability, a combination not captured by conventional risk screens.27 Longitudinal cohort data testing the interpersonal theory of suicide support thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness as proximal mechanisms rather than distal correlates.23

In the peer and school environment, microaggressions, bullying and physical aggression generate chronic humiliation and entrapment, with depression mediating the pathway from victimization to self-harm.28,30 Cyberbullying is a particularly persistent stressor because, unlike playground bullying, it is inescapable and occupies the same digital spaces in which adolescents seek connection. Qualitative analysis of 1895 social media posts following a widely publicized image-based abuse case in Spain documented how rapidly public online commentary can amplify humiliation and entrapment for a victim.24

Vulnerability in Medically and Socially Complex Subpopulations

Adolescents with Thalassemia Major

A scoping review of adolescents with thalassemia major (TM), a genetic haemoglobinopathy requiring lifelong transfusion, reported prevalence of suicidal ideation ranging from 28.8% to 78.7% across the included studies, a range that reflects genuine heterogeneity in setting and instrument as well as the small size of several samples.34

The pathway to this risk is biological as well as psychosocial, and specifying it clarifies why hematology and psychiatry must share the case. Repeated transfusion produces systemic iron overload. Because transferrin becomes saturated, non-transferrin-bound iron enters cells and catalyses Fenton chemistry, generating reactive oxygen species faster than antioxidant defences can neutralize them. In the central nervous system, this oxidative burden is compounded by chronic immune-inflammatory activation and by intermittent tissue hypoxia arising from anaemia. Neuroimaging and neuropsychological studies in transfusion-dependent populations describe iron deposition and white-matter change with a frontal and basal-ganglia predominance, together with measurable deficits in attention, working memory and executive function. The functional consequence is impairment of the prefrontal systems that support response inhibition, delay of gratification and the top-down regulation of limbic arousal. Impaired impulse control does not create suicidal ideation, but it lowers the threshold at which ideation converts into action, which is precisely the transition that determines mortality.34 Endocrine iron deposition adds a second route: pituitary siderosis produces hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, growth retardation and delayed puberty, and the resulting short stature and skeletal changes drive visible difference, social alienation from healthy peers and hopelessness. Mitigating this compounded risk requires hematology, endocrinology and mental health services working to a shared plan, with structured diagnostic interview such as the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS) used in preference to self-report scales alone.34 We note that the neurobiological chain described here is inferred from mechanistic and neurocognitive literature in thalassemia rather than demonstrated in a study that measured iron burden and suicidal behaviour in the same cohort; it is offered as a testable hypothesis for multidisciplinary research, not as an established causal pathway.

Justice-Involved Adolescents and the Problem of Data Silos

A qualitative thematic analysis of the pre-detention phase for adolescents involved in violent offences in West Sulawesi, Indonesia, identified five themes in the family crisis: emotional shock and self-blame on discovering the offence and subsequent ideation; family tension and exhaustion aggravated by the economic strain of legal fees and travel; community stigma directed particularly at mothers judged to have failed; reflective turning points at which parents moved from paralysis to safety planning; and a broad domain of unmet need for family mental health support. The absence of crisis counselling, psychoeducation on warning signs and legal navigation during this volatile window frequently left adolescent ideation unaddressed.32

Cross-system integration fails here for a specific and remediable reason: legal and health information systems are legally and technically separate. Custody records, court schedules and probation notes sit in justice databases; risk assessments, prescriptions and discharge summaries sit in clinical records; and neither is visible to the other at the moments that matter, namely first police contact, remand, and release to the community. Four mechanisms would narrow that gap without dissolving the confidentiality that properly separates the two systems. First, a minimum necessary dataset should be defined jointly and in advance, limited to information with direct safety relevance: current suicide risk status, current psychotropic prescriptions with prescriber contact, the existence and location of a safety plan, and the name of the responsible clinician. Diagnosis, session content and family history remain outside it. Second, disclosure should run on a consent-plus-safety-override basis, with adolescent assent and caregiver consent sought at intake and a narrowly drawn override, documented and auditable, for imminent risk to life. Third, exchange should be brokered by a named liaison, a health professional embedded in or formally attached to the youth justice service, rather than by direct database interoperability, which is where most privacy failures originate. Fourth, transitions should be governed by a standardized handover at each custody boundary, with a mandated mental health screen at first police contact and a scheduled clinical contact within 72 hours of release, since the post-release window carries elevated risk. Formalizing these arrangements in inter-ministerial memoranda of understanding between justice, health and education authorities converts them from local goodwill into an accountable standard.32,33

Primary Care Integration and Clinical Frameworks

General practitioners and pediatric primary care providers are the first point of contact for many adolescents in psychological crisis. Because a substantial proportion of people who die by suicide attend a health care setting in the preceding year, primary care is a logical site for detection and interception.15

The Blueprint for Youth Suicide Prevention

The AAP, with the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention and the National Institute of Mental Health, developed the Blueprint for Youth Suicide Prevention, which sets out a three-tiered pathway for outpatient and primary care settings.7 Brief screening applies validated instruments during routine visits to identify unvoiced risk; recommended tools include the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 Modified for Adolescents (PHQ-9M) and the Pediatric Symptom Checklist (PSC-17P).7,30 Brief suicide safety assessment follows a positive screen, using instruments such as the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) to assess imminence, planning, activating events and the balance of risk against protective factors.7,15 Disposition and triage then determine the level of care, from outpatient behavioural health referral to emergency assessment.7 Screening in this setting is not evenly applied: an analysis of 82,531 adolescents attending primary care found that those screened differed systematically from those at highest risk, indicating that universal rather than clinician-initiated screening is required if the approach is to reach its intended population.11

Integrated Behavioral Health Models

Execution of the Blueprint depends on structural integration of behavioural health within the primary care team. Successful models embed clinical social workers trained as behavioural health clinicians and provide psychiatric consultative leadership within the daily workflow, so that a positive screen triggers an immediate in-practice assessment rather than an external referral that may be delayed or lost.15,39

The Safety Planning Intervention

Historically, clinicians relied on “no-harm” or “no-suicide” contracts; contemporary consensus advises against them because they lack efficacy data and are frequently experienced as coercive, damaging the therapeutic alliance.7 The SPI is a brief, collaborative, person-centred cognitive-behavioural intervention that functions as a living document enabling the adolescent to de-escalate a crisis before emergency intervention becomes necessary.40 In adolescent practice it is inherently family-centric, and the caregiver has a distinct and specifiable task at every step; Table 2 sets out the six steps, the clinical rationale for each, and the corresponding caregiver role.

The SPI is among the better-supported components reviewed here. In a comparative cohort study of 1640 patients presenting to emergency departments, safety planning combined with structured telephone follow-up was associated with approximately half the odds of suicidal behaviour over six months and with roughly double the likelihood of attending outpatient treatment relative to usual care.12 A meta-analysis of brief acute-care suicide prevention interventions reached a consistent conclusion regarding subsequent attempts and linkage to follow-up care.13 Both bodies of evidence derive predominantly from mixed-age or adult samples; adolescent-specific randomized evidence for SPI remains limited, and this should temper the strength of any recommendation.

Pharmacogenomics and Prescribing Safety

Moderate to severe adolescent depression is commonly managed with psychotherapy combined with a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI). Pediatric SSRI prescribing carries a regulatory warning regarding treatment-emergent suicidal ideation and behaviour, which has historically constrained necessary treatment and obliged clinicians to weigh iatrogenic risk against the risk of untreated depression.26

SSRI metabolism depends substantially on the hepatic cytochrome P450 system, particularly the polymorphic CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 isoenzymes, whose variants determine whether an individual is a poor, intermediate, normal or ultrarapid metabolizer.26,31 A Danish register-based cohort study of young people treated with antidepressants reported associations between metabolic phenotype and adverse outcome in two directions. Poor CYP2C19 metabolizers prescribed standard doses of escitalopram, citalopram or sertraline show delayed clearance and higher drug exposure, with a higher frequency of adverse effects including akathisia, itself associated with self-harm, and a greater likelihood of treatment switching. Ultrarapid CYP2C19 metabolizers clear the drug before therapeutic concentrations are reached, leaving depression undertreated; young people with absent or reduced CYP2D6 function treated with fluoxetine showed higher rates of acute care contact for adverse reactions.26 The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) translates these findings into actionable dosing and drug-selection guidance for CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 phenotypes.31

The clinical benefit of acting on this information is more modest than the strength of the underlying genotype–phenotype associations implies, and this distinction matters. A meta-analysis of randomized trials of pharmacogenetic-guided prescribing in depression reported a higher likelihood of symptom remission relative to treatment as usual, but the pooled estimate rested on trials that were heterogeneous in test panel, comparator and outcome definition, and several were industry-sponsored.20 The largest pragmatic randomized trial to date, conducted in 1944 adults, found that providing pharmacogenomic results to prescribers produced a small, statistically significant reduction in prescribing of drugs with predicted gene–drug interactions and a modest remission advantage that was not sustained at 24 weeks.25 No adequately powered randomized trial has yet evaluated pharmacogenomic-guided prescribing with suicidal behaviour as a primary outcome in adolescents. Accordingly, the position supported by the current evidence is that pharmacogenomic information is a useful adjunct for drug selection and dosing where a genotype-guided recommendation exists, particularly after intolerance or non-response, rather than an established means of preventing suicide.

Implementation Considerations

Reviewers of this literature have rightly observed that scientific validity and implementability are separate questions, and the implementation gap for pharmacogenomics in adolescent psychiatry is substantial. Five barriers recur. Turnaround time determines clinical relevance: a result returned after two to three weeks arrives after the first prescription and after the window of highest treatment-emergent risk, so pre-emptive testing at the point of diagnosis, or panel results already held in the record, is more useful than reactive testing. Reimbursement is inconsistent; coverage for psychiatric pharmacogenomic panels varies by payer and jurisdiction and is frequently absent for pediatric indications, so out-of-pocket cost falls on families and introduces an equity gradient that runs opposite to the distribution of need. Cost-effectiveness evidence is correspondingly immature: existing economic models are sensitive to assumptions about remission benefit that the pragmatic trial data do not strongly support. Workforce and interpretation capacity is limited, since most pediatricians and child psychiatrists receive little pharmacogenomics training, which argues for embedding CPIC-based clinical decision support in the electronic health record and for pharmacist-led interpretation rather than clinician self-interpretation. Finally, allele frequency data underpinning phenotype assignment remain derived disproportionately from populations of European ancestry, so predictive accuracy is weaker in the populations that already experience the poorest access, and this should be stated when results are communicated to families. Progress therefore depends less on further demonstration of genotype–phenotype association than on decision support, reimbursement and workforce design.25,31

An analogy from an adjacent pediatric specialty illustrates the same implementation logic. Work mapping CYP3A5 polymorphism to cyclophosphamide efficacy and toxicity in pediatric rhabdomyosarcoma shows how genotype-informed dosing becomes routine only once laboratory turnaround, interpretive support and funding are solved together.41 This study is cited as a contextual analogy from pediatric oncology and is not part of the included evidence on adolescent suicide prevention.

School-Based and Community Preventive Interventions

Adolescents spend most of their waking hours in educational settings, which makes schools the most stable environment for population-level delivery. Effective school-based interventions do not convert educators into clinicians; they equip teachers, administrators and peers to act as the first link in a protection chain, recognizing warning signs and activating pre-established referral protocols.14,30 Delivery operates across the universal, selective and indicated tiers described earlier.9,30

Evidence-Based Programs

Several models have been evaluated. YAM uses a structured curriculum of interactive sessions and has been shown to improve help-seeking, mental health awareness and stigma at three-month follow-up in the included school-based literature;14 its strongest evidence remains the SEYLE cluster-randomized trial, in which YAM significantly reduced incident suicide attempts and severe ideation at 12 months relative to control.10 Signs of Suicide combines universal screening with peer leadership and education, shifting school culture toward recognition and referral.14 safeTALK and the locally developed Raahi program have been evaluated with interrupted time-series designs in under-resourced settings such as Gilgit-Baltistan, Pakistan; Raahi equips adolescents to engage peers in crisis, while the four-hour safeTALK workshop teaches the Tell, Ask, Listen and Keep Safe sequence to bridge recognition and professional referral.14

The evidence for these programs is stronger for proximal outcomes than for distal ones. Meta-analytic synthesis of youth suicide prevention finds reasonably consistent improvement in help-seeking intention, knowledge and stigma, with smaller and less consistent effects on suicide attempts, and few trials powered for suicide death.8,9 Gatekeeper training in particular improves identification and referral more reliably than it changes downstream clinical outcome, which is an argument for embedding it within a referral pathway that has capacity to receive referrals, not an argument against it.

Programme efficacy also depends on cross-curricular integration of socio-emotional skills and on enforcement of institutional policy addressing school climate, specifically anti-bullying and anti-cyberbullying mandates.14,30 Schools additionally require postvention protocols: return to school after psychiatric hospitalization is a high-risk process demanding structured coordination between clinical staff, school counsellors and families to manage reintegration and academic catch-up.30

Digital Health Innovations and Algorithmic Monitoring

Technology functions in both directions. Pro-suicide forums and algorithmically amplified content can exacerbate self-harm ideation, and analyses of aggregated web searches show correlation between searches for pro-suicide sites and rates of death by poisoning and suffocation among young people.42 Conversely, digital tools offer scalable monitoring and intervention that no clinic schedule can match.

Ecological Momentary Assessment and Predictive Modelling

Conventional assessment relies on episodic, retrospective self-report at scheduled appointments and misses the rapid fluctuation of adolescent suicidal ideation between visits. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) addresses this temporal gap by prompting adolescents several times daily to rate current suicidal thoughts, producing high-resolution data; a systematic review of EMA in suicide research confirms feasibility, acceptable compliance and, notably, no evidence that repeated questioning increases ideation.21 Combined with passive sensing of sleep, mobility and communication frequency, these data feed machine learning models intended to predict short-term risk.43

The limiting factor is predictive performance, not data volume. A systematic review and simulation of suicide prediction models found that although discrimination statistics appear favourable, positive predictive values are very low at realistic base rates, so the great majority of individuals flagged will not go on to attempt suicide.19 Meta-analytic evidence over five decades similarly shows that individual risk factors predict suicidal behaviour only slightly better than chance.44 Predictive monitoring should therefore be presented as an emerging aid to clinical judgement rather than as a triage instrument.

The Burden of Monitoring: Responsibility, False Alarms and Privacy

Continuous monitoring creates obligations that the technical literature rarely assigns, and these must be settled before deployment rather than after an adverse event.

Responsibility for response must be named in advance. A system that generates a risk alert creates a duty to act on it, and that duty falls on an identifiable person, not on a service in the abstract. Our view is that clinical accountability should rest with the adolescent’s named responsible clinician or care navigator during agreed hours, with a documented out-of-hours pathway to a crisis service, and that this allocation should be stated in writing to the adolescent and caregiver at enrolment. Where no such pathway exists, alerts should not be generated at all, since an unmonitored alert is worse than no alert: it transfers risk to the family while creating an appearance of surveillance.

False positives must be managed as a design parameter. Given the low positive predictive value of current models, most alerts will be false, and an unfiltered alert stream produces alert fatigue, desensitization and clinician burnout, the same failure mode documented for physiological monitoring in inpatient settings.19 Practical mitigations include tiered thresholds in which lower-tier signals trigger an automated supportive check-in or a self-guided coping module rather than a clinician alert; aggregation of signals over a defined window instead of alerting on single readings; requiring change from an individual’s own baseline rather than a population threshold; and routine audit of alert volume, response time and outcome, with thresholds recalibrated when the volume exceeds the responding team’s capacity.

Privacy involves a genuine trade-off rather than a solvable problem. Passive sensing data, including location, communication patterns and sleep, are among the most revealing categories of personal information, and their utility for prediction rises with their intrusiveness. Adolescents occupy a specific position: they hold developing rights to confidentiality while caregivers hold legal responsibility, so consent architecture must be explicit about what caregivers will and will not see. Defensible defaults include on-device processing with only derived risk scores transmitted, the shortest retention period consistent with clinical use, a clear statement of which data caregivers can access, an adolescent-controlled pause function, and prohibition of secondary commercial use. Adolescents who perceive monitoring as surveillance disengage, which removes the clinical benefit entirely; sustained consent is therefore a condition of effectiveness, not merely an ethical requirement. Digital Therapeutics and Telehealth

For adolescents facing geographic, financial or stigma-related barriers to in-person psychotherapy, digital modalities including gamified interventions, structured online modules and cognitive behavioural therapy for suicide prevention delivered by telehealth offer a scalable alternative.9,43 Moderated positive-psychology and peer-support platforms can enhance social connectedness and counter thwarted belongingness, a core component of the interpersonal theory of suicide.23,43 To be clinically useful these tools must be embedded in existing workflows as hybrid care, with algorithmic monitoring escalating to a human clinician under the response protocol described above rather than operating as standalone products.

Discussion

What This Review Adds

Three features distinguish this synthesis from recent reviews of adolescent suicide prevention. First, it places pharmacological safety at the genomic level within the multidisciplinary frame: reviews of youth suicide prevention have addressed psychosocial and school-based interventions,8,9,14 and reviews of psychiatric pharmacogenomics have addressed prescribing outcomes,20,25 but the two literatures are rarely read together, even though iatrogenic risk during antidepressant initiation is one of the few modifiable risks in this population. Second, it incorporates a medically complex comorbidity, thalassemia major, as a test case for cross-specialty integration, and specifies the biological pathway that makes hematological and psychiatric care inseparable in that group. Third, and most importantly, it treats structural conditions as findings rather than as concluding remarks: role definition, information flow and accountability at transitions are analyzed as determinants of whether any intervention above can be delivered, and two concrete mechanisms are proposed below. What this review does not add is pooled effect estimates; readers seeking those should consult the meta-analyses cited here.9,13,20

Role Ambiguity and the Burden on the Clinical Core

A qualitative study of interprofessional collaboration in Swiss suicide prevention programs, based on interviews with 15 professionals including psychologists, psychiatric nurses, consultant psychiatrists and resident physicians across cantonal and university services, found that the “clinical core” carries near-total responsibility for acute assessment and intervention. Bridging actors such as general practitioners and social workers operate only at transition points, and peripheral actors such as teachers and police are engaged episodically and without defined authority.33 A parallel qualitative study across Germany, Austria and Switzerland identified the same concentration of responsibility alongside fragmented national coordination.29

This architecture creates dependency on a small number of specialist professions, with predictable consequences: resource depletion, burnout and system fragility. When primary care providers work under severe time constraint or feel unequipped to manage suicidality after disclosure because no integrated behavioural health support exists, the default response is referral to an overloaded emergency service. That reflex fragments care and delays community-based treatment.15,33

Fragmentation of Information and High-Risk Care Transitions

The absence of interoperable information systems is arguably the most consequential structural barrier. When medical, psychiatric and educational records sit in separate silos, safety-critical history such as a recent psychiatric discharge, an intolerance to a prescribed antidepressant or an escalating pattern of digital risk scores does not reach the school counsellor or the primary care physician.33 Transitions of care, particularly discharge from inpatient psychiatric care to community and school settings, remain among the highest-risk windows. Discontinuity, delayed referral and failure to involve caregivers in safety planning produce preventable gaps.13,33

A HiTOP-Informed Model for Allocating Multidisciplinary Resources

The HiTOP framework is used in this literature descriptively, to classify psychopathology. Its hierarchical structure also suggests a way of matching intervention intensity to level of assessment, which is a practical answer to the resource concentration described above. The proposal is straightforward: the level at which a young person’s difficulties are characterized should determine who acts and how intensively, with lower tiers handled by trained non-specialists and higher-order spectra reserved for specialist and genomic resources.

At the level of individual signs and symptoms, such as insomnia, withdrawal or expressed worthlessness, detection is appropriately performed by trained gatekeepers, and the required action is recognition and a warm handoff. At the syndrome level, primary care applies structured screening and assessment, initiates safety planning and integrated behavioural health, and considers genotype-guided prescribing where an antidepressant is indicated. At the spectrum level, where distress, fear, externalizing, thought disorder or traumatic stress dimensions are elevated, specialist mental health assessment and a multi-agency plan are warranted. At the superspectrum level, where general psychopathology is markedly elevated, the case merits multidisciplinary case conference, assignment of a care navigator and the most intensive monitoring available. Pharmacogenomic testing and continuous digital monitoring, the two scarcest and most ethically demanding resources reviewed here, are thereby concentrated where the burden is greatest rather than distributed uniformly. Care Navigation and a Shared Risk Registry

Two mechanisms would address role ambiguity and information fragmentation directly.

A formal care navigator role establishes a single identifiable point of accountability for an adolescent across health, education and social care boundaries. The post should be substantive rather than an addition to an existing caseload, filled by a mental health nurse, clinical social worker or equivalent, with an explicit remit: maintaining the shared care plan, confirming attendance at the first appointment after any transition, holding the escalation pathway for digital alerts, and convening the multi-agency review. The evidence base for key-worker models in this specific population is limited, but the structural problem they address is documented consistently across the qualitative studies included here.29,33

A shared risk registry addresses the information problem with the minimum data necessary. Rather than full record interoperability, which is technically difficult and privacy-hostile, a registry would hold a defined minimum dataset: current risk tier, date and outcome of the most recent assessment, location of the current safety plan, responsible clinician and care navigator, current psychotropic prescriptions with any known pharmacogenomic contraindication, and dates of pending transitions. Access should be role-based and audited, with school staff seeing only the existence of a plan and the contact route rather than clinical detail. Programs including the Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program and its adolescent adaptation, and the Swiss SERO regional coordination project, demonstrate that shared documentation, unified regional screening protocols and joint cross-disciplinary training improve role clarity and build trust across professional cultures.29,33 National standards for post-attempt follow-up would convert these arrangements from local initiative into an expected standard of care.13,29

Limitations

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The protocol was not prospectively registered, and although screening and appraisal were performed independently by both authors, formal inter-rater agreement statistics were not calculated. Restriction to English-language, peer-reviewed literature will have excluded relevant work, particularly from settings where the burden is highest; the search window of 2018 to 2026 excludes older evidence, which we have partly offset by citing landmark pre-window trials as contextual references while excluding them from the graded synthesis. Only one included study was a randomized trial, and it enrolled adults, so conclusions about pharmacogenomic and digital interventions rest largely on observational and simulation evidence. The two studies of thalassemia major and of justice-involved adolescents are single-setting and not necessarily generalizable. No meta-analysis was performed and no formal certainty-of-evidence assessment such as GRADE was applied across outcomes, so strength of evidence is conveyed verbally rather than by a formal rating. Finally, the tiered allocation model, the care navigator specification and the shared registry dataset are proposals synthesized from the reviewed evidence; they have not been prospectively evaluated, and they should be read as hypotheses for implementation research rather than as recommendations supported by trial data.

Conclusion

Adolescent suicide is a multifactorial problem that does not yield to single-discipline solutions, and the evidence reviewed here supports coordinated multidisciplinary care. It does not support all components equally, and the distinction matters for practice. The best-supported elements are structural and unglamorous: universal rather than selective screening in pediatric primary care, behavioural health integrated within the primary care team, safety planning with structured follow-up contact, school programs embedded in referral pathways that have the capacity to receive referrals, and explicit management of the transition from inpatient care to community and school. Pharmacogenomic testing and algorithmic monitoring are scientifically well-founded and clinically promising, but adolescent-specific randomized evidence with suicide-related outcomes does not yet exist for either, and both raise implementation and governance questions, from reimbursement and turnaround time to the assignment of responsibility for responding to a false alarm, that remain unsettled. They should be introduced as adjuncts within defined pathways rather than as standard care.

Progress depends less on new technology than on dismantling the structural silos that prevent existing interventions from working together. Interoperability limited to a defined safety dataset, a formalized care navigation role, and sustained funding for integrated hybrid care are the practical prerequisites. The research priorities that follow are equally concrete: adolescent-specific trials of pharmacogenomic-guided prescribing with suicide-related outcomes, prospective evaluation of tiered digital monitoring with pre-specified response protocols, and implementation trials of care navigation and shared risk registries.
