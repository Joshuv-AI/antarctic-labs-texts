Construction of polyethylene glycol-cloaked mesoporous polydopamine nanomaterials co-loaded with anticancer drugs for photo/chemotherapy of lung cancer cells and elucidation of the cell death mechanism
Authors: Yueming Hu, Xiaolei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide. The mortality rate can be reduced through the development of novel therapeutic drugs for lung cancer. In this study, we developed nanoparticles (NPs) consisting of polyethylene glycol (PEG)-cloaked mesoporous polydopamine (MP) loaded with indocyanine green (IG) and talazoparib (TZ), designed to combine therapies for lung cancer. MP NPs were synthesized and surface-capped with methoxypolyethylene glycol amine. PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs were analyzed using dynamic light scattering, scanning electron microscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs were developed using the solvent diffusion technique to co-load IG and TZ. The reduction in drug-feeding ratios led to a decrease in the amount of drug loaded and an increase in the encapsulation efficiency. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, which showed excellent drug loading efficiency (IG: 9.9 ± 0.4% and 9.1 ± 0.2%; TZ: 6.8 ± 0.3% and 6.5 ± 0.2%), were chosen for further investigation. The hydrodynamic sizes ranged from 200 to 300 nm. These nanoparticles exhibited improved stability of IG in water, near-infrared laser-stimulated release of TZ, effective photothermal activity, excellent biocompatibility, and increased cellular uptake. These two nanoparticles demonstrated a stronger anticancer effect on lung cancer A549 cells than the combination of IG and TZ when irradiated with 808 nm light in vitro. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs efficiently triggered apoptosis in A549 cells. This study presents an innovative and efficient method for combination therapy against lung cancer cells.

Introduction

Cancer is the primary cause of mortality and is a substantial barrier to increasing life expectancy worldwide [1]. By 2027, the cancer mortality rate is expected to increase by 70%, predominantly affecting colorectal, breast, lung, and prostate cancers. In 2020, lung cancer was anticipated to be the second most prevalent cancer diagnosis and primary cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for approximately one in 10 (11.4%) cancer diagnoses and one in five (18.0%) deaths [2]. It is anticipated that there will be 2.2 million new cancer diagnoses and 1.8 million cancer-related fatalities [3]. Lung cancer is the primary cause of cancer-related mortality among men in 94 countries, which is partly attributable to its elevated fatality rate. The predominant causes of lung cancer are cigarette smoking and tobacco smoke exposure (85%) [4]. Currently, the treatment of lung cancer has advanced through nanomaterials for drug delivery, the development of nanotherapeutic systems, precise gene diagnosis, gene therapy, photothermal therapy (PTT), chemotherapeutic agents, and immunotherapy [5–7]. The rapid progress in nanomedicine has driven these advancements. Nanoparticle delivery systems possess distinct physical and chemical properties that enable them to simultaneously deliver multiple chemotherapeutic drugs, suppressive gene fragments, and immune enhancers [8]. This system can target specific tumor cells and achieve photothermal ablation, which is a promising strategy for tumor treatment. Moreover, it can reduce adverse reactions and improve treatment effectiveness [9].

Talazoparib (TZ; BMN-673) is recognized for its potent inhibition of PARP1 and PARP2, with greater potency at lower concentrations than those of previous generations of PARP1/2 inhibitors, such as olaparib or veliparib [10]. The cytotoxicity of TZ at nanomolar doses is thought to be associated with the entrapment of PARP-DNA complexes, as indicated by studies in PARP1-/- mouse models [11]. Nevertheless, the hydrophobic nature of TZ, along with its oral administration, results in a restricted amount of medication being absorbed by the body (approximately 50% after initial metabolism), which requires the administration of high doses to patients and increases the risk of toxicity in areas other than the intended site [12]. Therefore, TZ is a promising treatment option for patients with lung cancer harboring BRCA1 mutations. However, its long-term effectiveness may be limited, thereby restricting prolonged use [13, 14].

Photothermal therapy (PTT) is a prominent and successful approach to cancer treatment. The significance of this technology lies in its ability to achieve shorter treatment times, alleviate patient pain, and provide noticeable therapeutic effects while minimizing side effects (attributed to the low quantities of relatively innocuous photosensitizers) [15]. PTT utilizes photothermal agents to generate heat using visible or invisible light wavelengths, such as near-infrared (NIR), ultrasound, and microwaves. This heat increases the tissue temperature; thus, PTT is considered a minimally invasive procedure [16]. This technique eradicates cancer cells while sparing healthy cells because cancer cells are more sensitive to elevated temperatures. PTT uses laser devices to excite natural tissue chromophores and efficiently induce thermal tissue degradation [17]. Nanoparticles with optical properties have been employed to enhance PTT efficiency. Heat is generated only in the presence of nanoparticles and active light, which minimizes harm to cells other than tumor cells. The advantages of using nanoparticles in biological systems for thermal therapy include their small size, cytocompatibility, dispersion in a biocompatible solvent, heat generation upon external excitation, and bioavailability [18–21]. Modifying the fabrication process of specific nanostructures can precisely shift the absorption peak, resulting in a narrow spectral range. This adjustment enhances the PTT effect and improves its quality [22]. In addition, altering the surface of nanostructures by conjugating them with other molecules, such as polymers or antibodies, can improve the biological stability of nanomaterials and enhance their safety by addressing toxicological concerns. Nanoparticles can deliver pharmaceuticals to specific locations in the body and reduce the required dosage [23]. This enables safe administration of drugs while protecting non-target tissues and cells from harmful side effects [24].

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have emerged as alternative supports. They have attracted significant research attention owing to their distinct features, including large pore volume and specific surface area, adjustable pore structure, excellent thermal stability, and biocompatibility [25]. MSNs possess a consistent, spacious pore structure, enabling them to accommodate multiple substances and serve as effective, versatile carriers for various anticancer drugs [26–28]. Furthermore, the controllability of MSNs during delivery can be enhanced by adding molecular or polymeric components to their external surfaces. Based on these principles, the external surface of MSNs can be modified with controllable gatekeepers that respond to specific environmental triggers, such as antibodies, enzymes, temperature, magnetic fields, light, and pH [29]. This allows for precise delivery of therapeutic substances when required. To improve the ability of nanoparticles to target tumors and undergo tumor uptake, their outer surfaces are frequently modified with cell-interacting ligands, such as nucleic acids, small molecules, antibodies, and peptides [30]. This modification remarkably enhances the interaction between cancer cells and nanoparticles. However, surface decoration can be inefficient and burdensome if the nanoparticle surfaces lack chemically reactive functional groups [31]. This is because the nanoparticle surface must be activated using coupling agents or reactive linkers, followed by complex purification processes. We incorporated polydopamine (PDA) into mesoporous nanoparticles to address this obstacle. PDA can create a sticky layer on the surface of almost any material (surface) [32]. Furthermore, a PDA coating has been proven to be an exceptionally adaptable substrate for subsequent chemical reactions. Under conditions of weak alkalinity (pH: 8.5), the catechol group in dopamine undergoes initial oxidation to form quinone. This quinone then reacts with other catechols and quinones to produce a PDA film that is not soluble in water [33]. Ligand molecules that include nucleophilic functional groups, such as thiols or amines, can attach to the PDA layer via Schiff base and Michael addition reactions. This enables the introduction of various functional ligands onto different surfaces [25]. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is considered the most effective method for modifying the surface of nanoparticles to enhance their pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and immunological characteristics. PEGs with molecular weights of 2000 and 5000 Da are frequently utilized in PEGylation [34].

Development of a unique multifunctional nanoplatform specifically designed for combination lung cancer therapy is needed. Although PDA and indocyanine green (IG) have been previously studied, this specific combination and its application in lung cancer treatment have not been reported. This study involved the development of mesoporous PDA (MP) nanoparticles (PEG-MP) cloaked with PEG molecules with molecular weights of 2000 and 5000 Da. The objective of this study was to load both IG and TZ onto these nanoparticles for combined therapy in A549 cells. PEG-MP NPs were synthesized by surface modification of MP NPs using methoxy PEG amine. The resulting nanoparticles were analyzed using various techniques. Co-loading of TZ and IG into PEG-MP NPs was achieved using a solvent diffusion technique. Subsequently, the drug-loading characteristics, particle size, stability, zeta potential, PTT properties, and TZ release induced by NIR laser irradiation of the two nanoparticles were examined. Next, we assessed the cellular uptake, biocompatibility, and in vitro anticancer activities of the two nanoparticles in A549 lung cells. Morphological changes in A549 cells were analyzed by biochemical staining. In addition, cell death was examined using fluorescence microscopy.

Experimental section

Reagents and materials

All reagents and chemicals were used as received without further purification. Methoxy polyethylene glycol amine (MW 2000 and 5000, mPEG-NH2) and 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Ammonia solution, ethanol (EtOH), chloroform (CHCl3), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), acetonitrile (CH3CN), and other solvents were bought from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dopamine hydrochloride and talazoparib (TZ) were obtained from Ponsure Biological Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DMEM medium, penicillin/streptomycin, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were from Gibco (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA). Hoechst, Indocyanine green (IG), DAPI, 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2-H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), AO-EB Kit, Annexin-FITC, and Propidium Iodide (PI) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Human lung cancer cell line A549 lung and Mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line NIH3T3 cells were acquired from the Cell Bank of Shanghai, Chinese Academy of Sciences. All cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 supply.

Fabrication of MP NPs

The mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles were fabricated using a one-pot technique [35]. In summary, a solution was prepared by dissolving 0.2 g of PEG (F127) and 0.30 g of dopamine hydrochloride in 10 mL of dd-H2O and 5 mL of EtOH. Subsequently, 160 μL of 1, 3, and 5-trimethylbenzene was added, and the mixtures were ultrasonicated for 3 min, forming an emulsion. Subsequently, 750 µL of ammonia solution was gradually added to the mixtures, and the resulting mixtures were incubated at 30 °C for 3 h. As a result, the MP NPs were isolated by ultracentrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 45 min. The MP NPs were washed with absolute ethanol under ultrasonic conditions, repeating the process 3 times for 30 min each. The end products were diluted in dd-H2O for future utilization.

Fabrication of PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs

PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs were synthesized by cloaking the surface of MP with mPEG-NH2, where mPEG-NH2 (MW = 2000 and 5000). While stirring, a solution containing 50 mg of mPEG-NH2 was introduced into a 25 mL dispersion of MP. Subsequently, the subsequent mixtures were agitated at 30 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the nanoparticles were isolated using ultracentrifugation at 14,000 revolutions per minute for 45 min. The collected nanoparticles were rinsed with dd-H2O three times and then dispersed in dd-H2O for future usage.

Fabrication of TZ and IG co-loaded NPs

To develop PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs with co-loaded TZ and IG, 1000 μL of IG in DMSO and 500 μL of TZ in DMSO were gradually mixed drop by drop to a 10 mL dispersion of PEG2K-MP or PEG5K-MP NPs, respectively. Subsequently, the concoction was agitated at 25 °C for 24 h. The combination underwent dialysis using deionized water to eliminate unencapsulated TZ and IG. The freeze-drying process determined the drug loading (DL) of IG and TZ in 5 mL of the fabricated PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ dispersions. The determination of the IG loading content was performed [36]. The absorbance at 795 nm was used to determine the IG content by generating a standard calibration curve via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

Characterizations of NPs

The hydrodynamic diameter was measured using an advanced light-scattering system (Malvern Zetasizer Ultra, Malvern). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) pictures were viewed on a scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT700HR, JEOL) with an acceleration voltage of 30 kV. A transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400FLASH, JEOL) operated at 80 kV was used to obtain TEM images of biospecimens. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was characterized by a Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (VERTEX70, Bruker). Absorption was examined by UV-vis spectrophotometry (Lambda 45, PerkinElmer). DLS was used to study the colloidal stability of PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs. The stability of nanoparticles was investigated at 4 °C in dark conditions.

Assessment of in vitro drug release

A dialysis technique was employed to investigate the drug release of NPs. The IG and TZ were utilized as a drug in this investigation. 1 mL PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NP suspensions were transferred into dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-off of 14,000 Da and immersed in tubes containing various release media successively: PBS at pH 5.0 and pH 7.4. The tubes were immersed in a temperature-controlled water bath set to 120 rpm and maintained at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, samples of the release media were collected for quantitative analysis and replaced with fresh media. The TZ and IG concentrations in the release media were measured using the HPLC instrument [37]. A reversed-phase HPLC method was established and validated to quantify IG and TZ content in PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs. The HPLC system consisted of a Waters 600 HPLC Controller, a 717 Plus autosampler, and a 2996 photodiode-array detector with a NovaPak® C18 reverse-phase column (3.9 × 150 mm) from Waters (Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase was composed of 60:40 acidified water and acetonitrile, pH 2.5, with trifluoroacetic acid.

Assessment of in vitro photothermal effect

To verify the photothermal property, different concentrations of 1 mL aqueous dispersions of free IG, PEG2K-MP, PEG5K-MP, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs were prepared. Then, their temperature changes were recorded using an infrared thermal imaging camera during irradiation with an 808 nm laser. Analogously, the impact of laser power on photothermal performance was assessed by varying the laser power (1.5 W/cm2). Finally, photothermal stability was assessed using the method described in the previous report [37].

Cellular uptake investigation

The cellular uptake experiment was carried out using fluorescence microscopy. A549 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 1×104 cells per well (under a humidified 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C) overnight to allow the attachment of cells. A549 cells were incubated with PBS, TZ&IG combination, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (equal to 6.8 μg/mL of TZ (instead of FITC) and 9.9 μg/mL of IG) for 2 and 6 h. Then, the cells were rinsed with PBS thrice and fixed for 10 min. Later, the Hoechst solution was added to each well plate and incubated for another 10 min. At last, the cells were harvested and dispersed in PBS for fluorescence microscope analysis (Olympus IX73, Tokyo, Japan) [38].

The intracellular TZ and IG amounts were also quantified. A549 cells were incubated with PBS, TZ&IG combination, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (equal to 6.8 μg/mL and 9.9 μg/mL for TZ of IG) for different hours. Then, the A549 cells were rinsed 3 times with PBS for 10 min each. The cell lysates were added to a CH3CN solution. The HPLC examined the TZ amount, and a microplate reader investigated the IG amount [39].

Cytotoxicity on A549 and NIH3T3 cells

The fabricated PEG-MP NPs were examined on A549 and NIH3T3 cells to assess biocompatibility and cytotoxicity [40]. A549 and NIH3T3 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1×104 cells per well (under a humidified 5% carbon dioxide atmosphere at 37 °C) overnight to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to various MP-PEG NPs for 24 h. The standard MTT assay was used to assess the cell viability. Next, we added 20 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL) to each well and incubated them at 37 °C for 4 h. This was followed by adding 200 μL of DMSO to dissolve the crystals, and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (Mindray, China).

Anticancer activity

The anticancer effects of IG, TZ, IG&TZ, PEG2K-MP, PEG5K-MP, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, in the presence and absence of laser irradiation (808 nm), were assessed by MTT assay. NIH3T3 or A549 cells were planted in a 96-well plate at a density of 1×104 cells per well (under a humidified 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C) overnight to allow cell attachment for 24 h, and were separated into 2 groups. The irradiation group was cells irradiated at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 with an 808 nm laser [41].

Cell inhibition assay

The inhibitory effects of the fabricated IG&TZ, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs on A549 cells were examined. A549 cells and NIH3T3 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1×104 cells per well (under a humidified 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C) overnight to allow the attachment of cells. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to IG&TZ, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (at 4.2 μg/mL and 2.8 μg/mL for TZ and IG, respectively) with and without 808 nm irradiation. Next, we added 20 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL) to each well and incubated them at 37 °C for 4 h. This was followed by adding 200 μL of DMSO to dissolve the crystals, and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (Mindray, China) [41].

Live cell-dead and nuclear cell staining assay

A549 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 2×102 cells per dish with 1 mL of DMEM and incubated overnight. Then, A549 cells were cultured with TZ&IG combination, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (TZ: 5.6 μg/mL, IG: 8.4 μg/ml). The laser groups (PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs) were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 for 6 min. The cells were stained with the AO-EB or DAPI staining for 30 min before being analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus IX73, Tokyo, Japan) [42].

Propidium iodide staining assay

A549 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 2×102 cells per dish with 1 mL of DMEM and incubated overnight. Then, A549 cells were cultured with TZ&IG combination, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (TZ: 5.6 μg/mL, IG: 8.4 μg/mL). The laser groups (PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ or PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs) were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 for 6 min. The cells were stained with PI for 30 min before being analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus IX73, Tokyo, Japan) [43].

Statistical study

The experimental data were expressed as mean ± SD. A one-way ANOVA with a Tukey post hoc test or a two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to analyze experimental data across multiple groups. A two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test was used to analyze statistical differences between two groups. Significant differences are indicated by asterisks (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). All statistical results of the experiments were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Fabrication and characterizations of NPs

Initially, MP NPs were synthesized using a 50% ethanolic solution containing 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene and F127. MP NPs were fabricated through the self-polymerization method of dopamine using O2 as the oxidizing agent. Subsequently, mPEG-NH2 (MW 2000 or 5000) was conjugated to MP NPs at pH 11. The MP NPs exhibit a mean size of 224 nm, as shown in Figs. 1A and 1B. The FT-IR spectral analysis indicates that PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs exhibit a notable increase in the C-O-C stretching and C-H bending vibration for 1103 and 1410 cm-1, respectively, compared to MP NPs (Fig. 1C), implying the successful incorporation of PEG into MP NPs. In addition, compared to MP NPs, PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs exhibit distinct polymer layers on their surfaces, resulting in smoother nanoparticle surfaces (Fig. 1C). The structures, rough surfaces, and spherical morphologies of MP NPs are evident in SEM and TEM images (Fig. 1D-1G). After applying mPEG-NH2 for surface modification, we successfully fabricated PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs with mean sizes of 229 ± 3.5 nm and 248 ± 4.7 nm (Figs. 1A and 1B). The co-loading of IG and TZ into PEG-MP NPs was achieved via a solvent diffusion technique. Figure 2 demonstrates that reducing the drug-loading ratios leads to a drop in drug loading and an enhancement in the drug encapsulation ratio. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, with great drug loading and a restrained drug encapsulation ratio, were chosen for investigation. The loading contents of IG and TZ in the PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ NPs are 9.9 ± 0.4% and 6.8 ± 0.3%. The entrapment ratios for IG and TZ are 61.9 ± 4.1% and 72.8 ± 3.2% (Fig. 2). The IG and TZ contents in the chosen PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs are 9.2 ± 0.3% and 6.4 ± 0.2%, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2. The encapsulation ratios of IG and TZ are 56.9 ± 2.1% and 71.1 ± 3.2%, respectively, as indicated in Fig. 2. The variations in drug loading and encapsulation ratio between TZ and IG can be attributed to their distinct amounts and aqueous solubility. The mean size of PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs is 257.2 ± 6.2 nm and 272.1 ± 3.9 nm (Fig. 3A). Fabricated nanoparticles exhibit a tightly controlled size distribution, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of less than 0.3 and a negatively charged zeta potential. The presence of carbonyl and phenolic groups in polydopamine leads to negative zeta potentials. These negative zeta potentials can enhance nanoparticle stability by inducing electrical repulsion [44]. Furthermore, the PEG with altered surface properties can create a steric barrier, effectively preventing aggregate formation. The NPs’ negative zeta potentials can reduce their interactions with vascular endothelial cells and plasma proteins via electronic repulsion, thereby prolonging blood residence time, which is advantageous [45]. The UV-Vis analysis demonstrates that MP and PEG-MP NPs display bands characteristic of PDA. Additionally, the distinct bands of IG and TZ are observed in the UV-Vis of PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs (Fig. 3B), confirming the successful co-loading of IG and TZ. The absorbance band of IG undergoes substantial alterations upon encapsulation into NPs. When comparing free IG to PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, the absorbance bands of IG in the NPs are shifted to longer wavelengths by 11 nm and 17 nm, respectively (Fig. 3C–3E). This indicates that IG is enclosed within the NPs by hydrophobic and π-π interactions. The stability of Free IG in aqueous solutions is significantly poor, with a reduction in absorbance of about 60% at 785 nm within 5 days (Fig. 3F). By contrast, the absorbance of PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs at 785 nm decreases by 40% in the same period, as shown in Fig. 3F. IG is known to have a tendency to agglomerate and degrade rapidly in water due to its low stability in aqueous media. Concerning PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, the NPs contain a hydrophobic inner cavity where IG is enclosed. Examination of drug release

Fig. 4A–4F displays the combined release of IG and TZ from PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibit comparable drug-release profiles for both TZ and IG. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibit greater release of IG and TZ in a pH 7.4 PBS compared to a pH 5.0 PBS. IG and TZ are dialyzed more rapidly in the pH 7.4 PBS. The PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibit continuous release upon laser irradiation. When exposed to an 808 nm laser, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibit an increased release of TZ. This indicates that using an NIR laser can stimulate TZ release. For example, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs release 42.8 ± 4.11% and 40.2 ± 5.23% of TZ after 96 h in a pH=7.4 absence of laser exposure. However, when exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation, they released 62.36 ± 6.25% and 58.9 ± 7.21% of TZ after 96 h in the same buffer. The release of TZ induced by the NIR laser can be related to the photothermal effect of PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs. This effect increases the thermal motion of the loaded TZ molecules, thereby improving the diffusion of TZ within the NPs. The PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibit a reasonably rapid release of IG. Results of in vitro photothermal properties

Figure 5 presents the photothermal impacts of various samples. When exposed to an 808 nm laser with a power density of 1.5 W/cm2 for 6 min. The IG demonstrates a temperature increase that is both considerable and dose-dependent, as shown in Fig. 5A–5D. The temperature of unconstrained IG increase with exposure duration, reaching a at approximately 120 s. Subsequently, as the irradiation time increases, the temperature gradually decreases due to IG photobleaching. The PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs (at doses of a: 2.5 μg/mL, b: 5 μg/mL, c: 8 μg/mL, d: 10 μg/mL) similarly exhibit a substantial and proportional increase in temperature when exposed to laser radiation (Fig. 5A–5D). This temperature rise is attributed to their wide absorbance range. The temperature increase shown upon laser irradiation is much higher for PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs than free IG and MP NPs, as shown in Fig. 5E. Optimal biocompatibility of biological materials is crucial for the successful implementation of biomedical applications [47]. The compatibility of PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs with A549 and NIH3T3 cells was assessed using the MTT assay, as shown in Figs. 7C and 7D. Cell viability in all groups remains above 80% across the dose range, indicating that PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs exhibit minimal cytotoxicity.

The efficacy of various compounds, including free IG, free TZ, IG&TZ, PEG2000-MP, PEG5K-MP, PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ, and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, in inhibiting the growth of A549 cells was assessed using the MTT assay technique. The cells were exposed to the presence or absence of these compounds under 808 nm laser irradiation. According to the data in Figs. 8A and 8B, the cell viability of A549 cells treated with PEG2K-MP or PEG5K-MP NPs for 24 h is above 91%. Discussion

PDA-based MSNs, specifically MP, possess advantageous properties, including a porous architecture, good biocompatibility, and photothermal stability, making them appealing candidates for many applications [54]. These MP nanoparticles have been widely employed in combination therapies and function as versatile platforms for drug delivery, leveraging their broad surface areas and substantial pore capacities. The small changes in the hydrodynamic diameters of the PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs could be attributed to PEG surface modification. Furthermore, the application of PEG to the surface modification of MP NPs can improve nanoparticle stability, leading to greater stability of IG within PEG-MP NPs than within free IG. These findings suggest that PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, containing reasonable amounts of IG and TZ, were effectively synthesized and demonstrated a substantial improvement in IG stability. These results indicate the effective synthesis of PEG2K-MP and PEG5K-MP NPs. MP NPs have been shown to possess a high loading capacity and exhibit pH-responsive drug release. Given that the tumor microenvironment exhibits a lower pH than that of normal tissues, a pH-sensitive drug delivery system is beneficial [55]. Investigation of drug release demonstrated that both PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs exhibited prolonged TZ release, which was enhanced by NIR laser irradiation. This sustained release was advantageous for the combined therapeutic effects of IG and TZ when exposed to an NIR laser. This approach efficiently reduces drug release in healthy tissues, thus alleviating potential toxic side effects while enabling drug release in tumor tissues to enhance antitumor efficacy [56]. Ferguson et al. demonstrated that acidic pH-responsive poly(2-vinylpyridine) microcapsules released their cargo into the fly midgut. To further test the feasibility of the fly gut as a model system for testing smart cargo release from biocompatible nanoparticles, they tested the pH-triggered release of PDA- and PEG-coated MSNs and performed an in vivo comparative study using the better-characterized and genetically tractable species Drosophila melanogaster [57].

The enhanced permeability and retention effect refers to the preferential accumulation of nanomedicines in neoplastic tissues via the hyperpermeable tumor vasculature, thereby augmenting anticancer efficacy. MP NPs have been designed using various ligands to specifically target cancer cells. These nanoparticles demonstrated a multifaceted surface owing to the abundance of modifiable sites, facilitating the binding of ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, nucleic acids, and small molecules, thereby improving the accuracy of drug delivery and maximizing therapeutic effectiveness [58]. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ NPs demonstrated a 4.6-fold increase in intracellular TZ concentration and 2.1-fold increase in intracellular IG concentration compared with those in IG&TZ NPs. Similarly, PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs displayed a 4.1-fold increase in intracellular TZ concentration and 2.1-fold increase in intracellular IG concentration compared with those in IG&TZ NPs. These findings supported the notion that PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs enhanced the internalization of IG and TZ by cells, thus validating the inclusion of IG and TZ within PEG-MP NPs. These findings prove that PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs have potent anticancer activity in vitro when exposed to 808 nm laser light. MP NPs are linked to lung cancer and play roles in multiple aspects of cancer progression, including carcinogenesis, proliferation, survival, and metastasis. Prior clinical research has identified a unique class of lung cancer agents that target various tyrosine kinases [59]. In this study, we designed a delivery mechanism using MP NPs to examine their synergistic anticancer effects [60].

Recent advancements in lung cancer treatment have highlighted the revolutionary capabilities of MP NPs, which combine precise targeting, aptamer-mediated transport, and PTT to surpass traditional metal-organic frameworks and lipid-based systems [61]. Future studies should confirm the efficacy of MP NPs in xenografts reflecting the genetic heterogeneity of lung cancer and investigate synergistic combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors to enhance immunogenic cell death. MP NPs offer a versatile, multimodal approach for next-generation lung cancer treatment by integrating targeted molecular disruption with physical tumor ablation, contingent on addressing subtype-specific adaptability and thorough safety assessments to comply with increasingly stringent regulatory standards.

Conclusion

PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs were developed and evaluated for their effectiveness as a combination therapy for lung cancer. PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ+Laser and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ + Laser NPs demonstrated significant promise for combined therapy in treating lung cancer cells. Further studies with PEG2K-MP@IG&TZ and PEG5K-MP@IG&TZ NPs, extended evaluation periods, and comprehensive pharmacokinetic and safety assessments of lung cancer in vivo models, are required to confirm the therapeutic potential and translational applicability of this approach.
