CIGaRS I:Combined simulation-based inferencefrom SNæ Ia and host photometry
Konstantin Karchev, Roberto Trotta, Raul Jimenez

Abstract
Using type Ia supernovæ (SNæ Ia) as cosmological probes requires empirical corrections, which correlate with their host environment. We present a unified Bayesian hierarchical model designed to infer, from purely photometric observations, the intrinsic dependence of SN Ia brightness on progenitor properties (metallicity & age), the delay-time distribution (DTD) that governs their rate as a function of age, and cosmology, as well as the redshifts of all hosts. The model incorporates physics-based prescriptions for star formation and chemical evolution from Prospector - β, dust extinction of both galaxy and SN light, and observational selection effects. We show with simulations that intrinsic dependences on metallicity and age have distinct observational signatures, with metallicity mimicking the well-known step of SN Ia magnitudes across a host stellar mass of ∼ 1 × 10 10 M ⊙ \sim 1\text{\times}{10}^{10}\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\odot}} . We then demonstrate neural simulation-based inference of all model parameters from mock observations of ≈ 16 000 \approx 16\,000 SNæ Ia and their hosts up to redshift 0.9 0.9 . Our joint physics-based approach delivers robust and precise photometric redshifts ( < 0.01 <0.01 median scatter) and improved cosmological constraints, unlocking the full power of photometric data and paving the way for an end-to-end simulation-based analysis pipeline in the LSST era.

Introduction

It has long been known that the brightnesses, stretches, and colours of type Ia supernovæ (SNæ Ia) correlate with global properties of their host galaxies, e.g. morphology, stellar mass,
star-formation rate (SFR), metallicity, and/or stellar age

Sullivan et al. 2003
;
Sullivan et al. 2006
;
Aubourg et al. 2008
;
Neill et al. 2009
;
Brandt et al. 2010
;
Lampeitl et al. 2010
;
Sullivan et al. 2010
;
Childress et al. 2013
;
Johansson et al. 2013
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2023
;
Chung et al. 2024
)
. Similar dependences on the SNæ’s
local
environment have also been found
(
Rigault et al. 2013
;
Rigault et al. 2015
;
Rigault et al. 2020
;
Jones et al. 2015
;
Jones et al. 2018
;
Moreno-Raya et al. 2016a
;
Moreno-Raya et al. 2016b
;
Kim et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Roman et al. 2018
;
Rose et al. 2019
;
Kelsey et al. 2021
;
Kelsey et al. 2023
;
Ginolin et al. 2024a
;
Ginolin et al. 2024b
)
.
In current cosmological analyses
(
Brout et al. 2022
;
Rubin et al. 2025
;
Vincenzi et al. 2024
, e.g.)
, which use SNæ Ia as standardisable candles, it is common practice to (attempt to) correct for all these effects
en masse
via an
ad-hoc

“mass step”
: a magnitude offset between SNæ Ia hosted in galaxies with stellar masses above and below a given threshold, usually set to the sample median or fixed around
{10}^{10}\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
. The rationale is that stellar mass is a simple-to-estimate proxy for nearly all host properties. Unveiling the physical processes underlying host
–
SN Ia connections would thus not only enhance our understanding of SNæ Ia and their formation, but also deliver more accurate and precise constraints on dark energy by improving the standardisation procedure.

A supernova’s environment also has
extrinsic
effects on its light, which is dimmed and reddened by dust along the line of sight, both in the immediate surroundings of the progenitor system and in interstellar space within the host. It can also be scattered
into
the line of sight, significantly affecting finite-resolution photometry
(
Duarte et al. 2025
)
. Accounting for dust requires careful hierarchical modelling
(
Mandel et al. 2017
)
since a) extinction has a similar effect (redder
–
dimmer) to intrinsic colour-related standardisation (bluer
–
brighter) and b) there is a well-known relationship between the amount and properties of the dust in a galaxy and its SFR and stellar mass: the so-called
“star-forming main sequence”

(
Speagle et al. 2014
, see e.g.)
. Different studies have come to conflicting conclusions regarding this interplay: for example,
Brout and Scolnic 2021
attribute the mass step to differences in dust in low- and high-mass hosts rather than any intrinsic/physical/causal effect, while
Thorp and Mandel 2022
find a preference for a single dust law across stellar masses and a mass step of
\approx 0.06\pm\pm 0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{mag}
, with more massive galaxies hosting intrinsically brighter SNæ Ia. However, Bayesian model comparison using the same data and models
(
Karchev et al. 2023
)
mildly disfavours a non-zero mass step with posterior odds of
2
:
1
.

Resolving these degeneracies and arriving at a definite conclusion regarding host
–
SN Ia connections requires tackling
all
interrelated effects simultaneously, including the apparent dimming due to cosmological distance and selection biases. While Bayesian hierarchical modelling (BHM) provides the principled framework for doing this, traditional Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods require sampling jointly the vast number of latent parameters (tens
per SN
) and explicit computation
—
at every step
—
of the selection probability, which is intractable in realistic scenarios. To date, analyses either resort to
ad-hoc
approximations
(
Rubin et al. 2015
;
Rubin et al. 2025
;
Hinton et al. 2019
)
or are split in two separate steps
(
Thorp et al. 2021
;
Thorp and Mandel 2022
, see e.g.)
: first, the SNæ’s distance moduli (i.e. intrinsic brightnesses) are estimated and corrected for selection effects using a fiducial, possibly mis-specified, model
(
Kessler and Scolnic 2017
)
; then, they are correlated with galaxy properties obtained in a separate analysis (rarely considering the associated uncertainties
(
Shariff et al. 2016
)
) and/or used in a cosmological fit.

A two-step approach cannot account for many important statistical effects. For instance, the ages of SN Ia progenitors are not representative of their hosts’ stellar populations, and so regressing using the latter may be misleading. Moreover, standardisation implicitly gives higher weight to more massive hosts, where a larger number of SNæ occur
—
an instance of Eddington bias
(
Eddington 1913
, see also
Karchev and Trotta 2024
)
. Likewise, a preference for detecting (
apparently
) brighter objects biases the selected sample towards
intrinsically
brighter SNæ to a greater extent in dustier hosts, which are also more massive. Lastly, an
apparent
correlation
—

non-existent intrinsically

—
between SN brightnesses and properties of the hosts may be introduced by the use of photometric redshifts in the standardisation process due to the mass
–
age
–
redshift degeneracy.
Similarly, an
apparent
redshift dependence of SN properties
(
Lee et al. 2020
;
Nicolas et al. 2021
)
might arise due to the evolution of the host galaxies, e.g. their dust content
(
Popovic et al. 2024
;
Thorp et al. 2024
)
or metallicity or age.

In this paper, we present the first framework for combined inference and galaxy-related standardisation (CIGaRS) of SNæ Ia and their hosts, which addresses all of the above conceptual and methodological issues and can
unequivocally
disentangle intrinsic and extrinsic effects through physics-based forward modelling. We formulate our model in the context of simulation-based inference (SBI)
(
Cranmer et al. 2020
;
Lueckmann et al. 2021
, for overviews, see)
, a modern suite of Bayesian inference techniques that leverage the flexibility of neural networks (NNs) to obtain posterior distributions, given only training examples from a forward simulator. SBI is seeing rapid adoption across disciplines and has previously been applied in SN Ia studies to analyse dust distributions from collections of light curves
(
Karchev et al. 2024
;
Karchev et al. 2023
)
and perform cosmological inference from future-sized data sets (up to
{10}^{5}
SNæ Ia) in the presence of photometric-like redshift uncertainties
(
Karchev et al. 2023
)
and arbitrary selection effects
(
Karchev and Trotta 2024
)
.

While insights into the DTD and the host

SN Ia connections are an important output of our analysis, we envisage its primary future application to be cosmological inference from the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), soon to commence at the Vera Rubin Observatory. Due to the large spatial coverage and depth (
18\,000\text{\,}{\mathrm{deg}}^{2}
reaching redshift

\approx 1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the

(
Frohmaier et al. 2025
)
, only up to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the host galaxies will be followed up.

In view of this predominantly photometric scenario, we do not consider any spectroscopic observables or explicit redshift estimates, providing the inference network only with broadband photometry of the hosts and light-curve summaries for the SNæ Ia (we do assume perfect transient classification, noting that non-Ia contamination can be straightforwardly included in our framework).
Owing to the physical modelling of galaxy evolution and the pooling of strength introduced by the joint analysis, our framework delivers state-of-the-art photometric redshift estimates
as a by-product
, as well as considerably more stringent cosmological constraints than using SN data alone (even with spectroscopic redshifts). We explore more thoroughly the implications of these findings for SN cosmology with future data sets in a dedicated companion paper, focusing here on host
–
SN Ia connections and the DTD.

3
Conclusion and outlook

We have presented CIGaRS: the first unified and fully self-consistent framework for rigorous Bayesian analysis of SN Ia summary statistics and photometry of their hosts. It is based on a forward simulator that combines two hierarchical models:
Prospector
-
β and Simple
-
BayeSN, via a prescription for the occurrence of SNæ Ia (according to a delay-time distribution (DTD) convolved with non-trivial star-formation histories) and physical connections between their brightnesses and progenitor metallicity and/or age. CIGaRS qualitatively reproduces the observed SN Ia rate with redshift and the distribution of host stellar masses and makes detailed predictions about the apparent stellar-mass dependence induced by the underlying physical relations: a metallicity correlation resembles a smoothed step function across
{10}^{10}\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\odot}}
with symmetric Gaussian scatter, whereas an age correlation leads to a linear trend with stellar mass and highly skewed residuals.

We have demonstrated, for the first time, simultaneous neural simulation-based inference of the DTD parameters, brightness
–
metallicity/age correlations, a residual mass step, and cosmology purely from LSST-like host photometry and SN Ia summaries (brightness, stretch, and colour) of up to
\approx 16\,000
objects.
Our approach implicitly handles redshift estimation, host-related standardisation, and selection effects, while propagating all uncertainties, and thus remains accurate, precise, and scalable. Moreover, due to the unified hierarchical modelling and combination of SN and host data, we achieve robust photometric redshift estimates with state-of-the-art scatter and cosmological constraints that improve upon spectroscopic SN-only analyses.

We envisage extensions of the model to include spatial localisation of the stellar populations (and hence, of the SNæ) and of interstellar dust, peculiar velocities of the hosts and of the SNæ within them (which will allow inference of cosmological parameters controlling large-scale structure), the presence of non-Ia transients, their detection and characterisation directly from a time-series of telescope images, and the incorporation of spectroscopic data, where available. Remarkably, our inference framework can be applied to an arbitrarily complicated simulator and collections of observables without significant modifications, while retaining its scalability, and we expect it to be instrumental in maximising the scientific insight extracted from upcoming vast and detailed LSST observations.

Methods: Unified forward modelling of galaxies and SNae Ia

CiGARS is the first unified forward model for SN Ia cosmology. It is a diptych (see
figure

1
) of two state-of-the-art Bayesian hierarchical simulators for photometric galaxy observations (
\mathbf{d}^{h}
) and SN Ia summary statistics (
\mathbf{d}^{s}
), joined on three levels:

1.

by the probability of occurrence (equivalently, number
N_{\textrm{SN }}^{h}
) of SNæ Ia in a given host
h
, based on the latter’s intrinsic properties
\mathbf{g}^{h}
;

2.

by the distribution of intrinsic properties
\bm{\uplambda}^{s}
of the SNæ, which also depend on the
\mathbf{g}^{\opbraces{h}(s)}
of their respective hosts;

3.

by extrinsic effects of the host environment (e.g. dust) and other properties (e.g. cosmological redshift and peculiar velocity) on the SN light as it travels towards the observer.

The former two, which affect
\bm{\uplambda}
, can be considered
causal
connections, whereas the latter, which only affects the observables and data
\mathbf{d}
, are
coincidental
. One last connection can arise during data reduction (e.g. subtraction of the host light); we plan to implement it in a future extension that implements the image analysis procedure of transient surveys. In what follows, we describe in detail all components of CIGaRS (see also the full hierarchy in
figure

1
and the list of parameters in
table

1
), discussing also the planned improvements, mainly related to intra-host localisation of the SNæ.

4.1
Host model

Comprehensive treatment of the photo
–
spectral evolution of galaxies
—
see the pioneering studies using the MOPED method
(
Panter et al. 2003
;
Heavens et al. 2004
;
Panter et al. 2007
)
and e.g.
Mo et al. 2010
for a review
—
is beyond the scope of this work. At a high level, the intrinsic properties of a galaxy
h
, such as its star-formation history (SFH), its metallicity (
Z_{*}^{h}
), and interstellar dust content, are largely determined by the galaxy’s total stellar mass (
M_{*}^{h}
) and its cosmological redshift (
z_{\textrm{c}}^{h}
)
—
a proxy (given a cosmological model) for the age of the Universe (
T^{h}
) at the time the galaxy’s light is emitted. To represent the intrinsic correlations inherent in galaxy formation, we adopt an off-the-shelf Bayesian framework for galaxy photometry:
Prospector

(
Johnson et al. 2021
)
, as updated in
Prospector
-
β
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.

4.1.1
Star-formation history and chemical enrichment:
Prospector
-
β

Prospector
encodes a non-trivial SFH by discretising it in seven time bins (found to be the optimal number for describing moderate signal-to-noise observations
(
Tojeiro et al. 2007
)
) spanning
\mathopen{[}0;T^{h}\mathclose{]}
: the first two fixed to
\mathopen{[}0;$30\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$\mathclose{]}
and
\mathopen{[}$30\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$;$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$\mathclose{]}
, the next four logarithmically spaced within
\mathopen{[}$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$;$0.85\text{\,}{T^{h}{}}$\mathclose{]}
, and the last covering
\mathopen{[}$0.85\text{\,}{T^{h}{}}$;T^{h}\mathclose{]}
. The galaxy’s total mass is then distributed into seven stellar populations that represent the stars born during each history bin:

\mathbf{SFH}^{h}\equiv\quantity[\SFHgalpop]_{j=1}^{7},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{7}\mathrm{SFH}^{h,j}=M_{*}^{h}.

(1)

Assuming that all stars in a given population were born at approximately the same time (
t_{*}^{h,j}
, taken at the (linear) centre of the respective bin), the process of stellar population synthesis (SPS) produces the integrated light (before extinction) from all stars in a galaxy by convolving the emission spectra of single stellar populations with the SFH.

SPS also requires a prescription for the galaxy’s chemical enrichment history: the metallicities of all its stellar populations.
Prospector
assumes a single metallicity within a galaxy (
Z_{*}^{h}
), determining it (with appropriate scatter) from the total stellar mass as in
Gallazzi et al. 2005
. This has also been shown to be appropriate for modelling the progenitors of SNæ Ia
(
Bravo and Badenes 2011
)
, which is our ultimate goal.

4.1.2
Host dust extinction

The light emitted by stars is then extinguished (and reddened) by dust within the host.
Prospector
considers separate birth-cloud and diffuse dust components. The former only affects the youngest stellar population (i.e. acting only on the emission from the first time bin) and has a fixed wavelength dependence
\propto\lambda^{-1}
and optical depth
\tau_{1}^{h}

80
law
(
Kriek and Conroy 2013

Noll et al. 2009

with shape parameter
\delta^{h}

V

In
Prospector
-
β, the dust parameters are

independent from other galaxy properties; however, many studies have found

relationships from large galaxy surveys. We enforce the empirical relations of

\displaystyle\tau^{h}_{2}\equiv A_{\textit{V}}^{h}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\argopen\lparen 0.2+0.5\operatorname{ReLU}\argopen\lparen\opbraces{\log_{10}}\ifrac{\mathrm{SFR}^{h}}{$\mathrm{M_{\odot}}$}\argclose\rparen,$0.2$^{2}\argclose\rparen,

(2)

\displaystyle\delta^{h}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\argopen\lparen$-0.095$+$0.111$A_{\textit{V}}^{h}-$0.0066$\quantity(\smash{\Avgal})^{2}\vphantom{A_{\textit{V}}^{h}},$0.4$^{2}\argclose\rparen,

(3)

where the star-formation rate (SFR) is derived from the most recent (current) component of

\mathrm{SFR}^{h}\equiv\ifrac{\mathrm{SFH}^{h,1}}{$30\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$}

\ifrac{\tau_{1}^{h}}{\tau_{2}^{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}\argopen\lparen$1$,$0.3$^{2}\argclose\rparen

It is important to note that the above description of dust extinction is global, i.e. averaged over the spatial light distribution of the galaxy, since host photometry is usually integrated. It may thus not represent faithfully the
local

Photometry and detection of hosts

Given the above quantities (and parameters that describe the presence and emission of interstellar gas and an active galactic nucleus (AGN), which we randomly sample from the

4.2
SN Ia occurrence

The first connection between galaxies and SNæ Ia (or any other transient) is the latter’s emergence from the former’s stellar populations, which we describe through the delay-time distribution (DTD): the rate (per unit rest-frame time) of occurrence of SNæ Ia from a given stellar population (per unit stellar mass within it), as a function of the population’s age
t_{*}
. Therefore, within a survey of (observer-frame) duration
\mathcal{T}
, a number

\expectationvalue{\hostpopindex{\nsn}}=\frac{\mathcal{T}}{1+z^{h}}\times\mathrm{SFH}^{h,j}\times\operatorname{DTD}\argopen\lparen t_{*}^{h,j}\argclose\rparen

(5)

of SNæ Ia arise (
on expectation
) from galaxy
h

(recall that we assumed all stars within it have the same age
t_{*}^{h,j}

100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
, i.e. for the two youngest stellar populations, to allow for the creation of a white dwarf):

\operatorname{DTD}\argopen\lparen t\argclose\rparen=A\times\quantity(\flatfrac{t}{\si{\giga\year}})^{b}\times${\mathrm{M_{\odot}}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{yr}}^{-1}$\quad\text{for $t>$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{Gyr}$$},

(6)

and independent priors representing observational constraints
(
Heringer et al. 2017
;
Heringer et al. 2019
)
:

\displaystyle\opbraces{\log_{10}}A

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\argopen\lparen$-12.15$,$0.1$^{2}\argclose\rparen,

(7)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\argopen\lparen$-1.34$,$0.2$^{2}\argclose\rparen.

(8)

To realise this prescription, in each forward pass of the model, we iterate (in parallel on a GPU) over all stellar populations of all galaxies, i.e. over all
h,j

N_{\textrm{SN }}^{h,j}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\argopen\lparen\expectationvalue{\hostpopindex{\nsn}}\argclose\rparen

(9)

supernovæ, sampling their ages,
\quantity{\tstar^{\hostpopindexlabel, k}}_{k=1}^{N_{\textrm{SN }}^{h,j}}

and keep track of the host

Finally, it is also possible to keep track

in the simulator

of the stellar population

from which each SN has arisen and associate corresponding properties if these differ from one stellar population to another within the same host (e.g. to represent a fully realistic enrichment history through different

Baes et al. 2024

4.3
SN Ia model

Once we have determined how many SNæ Ia have occurred during a survey, we proceed to simulate their observables. This follows a similar path from intrinsic properties derived from a population model
informed by the host
of each object, through extinction, redshift, and distance, to noisy measurements and detection / sample selection.

4.3.1
Causal host
–
SN Ia connection(s)

The cornerstone of CIGaRS is an explicit parametrised dependence of the intrinsic SN Ia properties on the characteristics of their host and/or of their progenitor stellar population: in general,
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uplambda}^{s}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{g}^{h(s),j(s)},\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen
. Our choice of which links to include is motivated by observational evidence
(
Neill et al. 2009
;
Lampeitl et al. 2010
;
Sullivan et al. 2010
;
Childress et al. 2013
;
Johansson et al. 2013
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2023
;
Chung et al. 2024

and takes the form of a host-dependent magnitude offset
\variation{M}^{s}

(
Ginolin et al. 2024a
)

Since the physical source of
\variation{M}^{s}
is not yet established, we allow for multiple possibilities (in isolation or combination)
—
namely, metallicity

and progenitor age
t_{*}^{s}

—
with correlation coefficients, respectively
\gamma_{Z_{*}}
and
\gamma_{t_{*}}
, whose priors include
0
and wide ranges of positive and negative values. In keeping with current practice and as a
“catch-all”
parameter, we also allow for an

“mass step”

with a similar wide prior and a free (inferred) stellar-mass location

\variation{M}^{s}=\underbrace{\gamma_{Z_{*}}\times\opbraces{\log_{10}}\ifrac{Z_{*}^{s}}{$\mathrm{Z_{\odot}}$}}_{\text{metallicity}}+\underbrace{\gamma_{t_{*}}\times t_{*}^{s}}_{\text{age}}+\underbrace{\Delta M\times\mathbb{I}(M_{*}^{s}>M_{*}^{\textrm{step}})}_{\text{\emph{ad-hoc} mass step}},

(10)

where

returns
1
or
0
if its argument is true or false, respectively, and
\gamma_{Z_{*}},\gamma_{t_{*}},\Delta M,M_{*}^{\textrm{step}}\in\bm{\upgamma}
are global parameters. Inference of the presence and strength of the respective effects is performed by examining their posterior(s) and/or performing Bayesian model selection, which is most conveniently and rigorously achieved through simulation-based methods
(
Karchev et al. 2023
)
. Moreover, with SBI, all underlying correlations are accounted for and marginalised over when inferring cosmology, ensuring robustness of dark-energy inference against systematics arising from the host
–
SN Ia dependences.

The extrinsic absolute (rest-frame

We note the recent application of SN Ia observations to inference of large scale structure (LSS) parameters

4.3.5
Detection/selection of SNæ Ia

The detection and inclusion of SNæ in modern cosmological analyses is a complex process that considers the raw data quality, the goodness of light-curve fits, and the estimated summaries. Using host observations (e.g. for deriving redshifts and/or studying host

Since the present study does not specifically focus on selection effects, we adopt the same simple SN detection/selection procedure as in STAR NRE

4.4
Implementation details and simulated counts

Here, we discuss two technical details of our forward simulator.

Firstly, we note that
Prospector
relies on the cosmological model to map redshift to age:
T(z_{\textrm{c}},\mathcal{C})
. In principle, this calculation can be repeated for each sampled cosmology in our simulator; however, we do not expect this to have a noticeable effect on our results since SNæ Ia are mainly informative of cosmological distances rather than times; i.e., for values of
\mathcal{C}
consistent with a given SN Ia sample,
T(z_{\textrm{c}},\mathcal{C})
does not vary significantly with
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, we treat
T(z_{\textrm{c}})
as fixed to that consistent with the WMAP cosmology
(
Hinshaw et al. 2013
)
, as when training/creating
Prospector
-
β.

The second point concerns the number of objects that we simulate, which, in principle, depends on (and hence is informative of) the astrophysical and cosmological models (e.g. through a parameter
S_{8}
), as well as the surveyed sky area (
\Omega
). Currently, our high-level SN-focused simulator does not include these details since SN observations do not carry information about this connection, beyond the distribution of their redshifts, which we assume is extracted mainly from the hosts.

Hence, the purely host-related part of CIGaRS has no free global parameters up to the point where the
redshifted
(i.e. observer-frame) spectral flux

5
Methods: Set-based TMNRE for hierarchical models

Bayesian hierarchical models feature a large number of free parameters, which scales with the number of observed objects. Accurate, precise, and fast inference from future data is thus a major computational challenge that likelihood-based methods are ill-suited to address. Moreover, the likelihood requires explicit calculations of intractable probabilities, often leading to
ad-hoc
approximations. Simulation-based inference (SBI) addresses all these issues
—
scalability, realism, and rigour
—
by delivering marginal Bayesian posteriors, given data
\mathbf{D}_{0}
, for any parameters of interest
\bm{\uptheta}
, implicitly integrated over
all
nuisance parameters (
\bm{\upnu}
) and relevant stochastic processes (e.g. selection and other systematic effects):

\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uptheta}}\,\middle|\,{\mathbf{D}_{0}}\argclose\rparen\propto\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\mathbf{D}_{0}}\,\middle|\,{\bm{\uptheta}}\argclose\rparen=\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\times\int\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\mathbf{D}_{0}}\,\middle|\,{\bm{\upnu},\bm{\uptheta}}\argclose\rparen\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\upnu}}\,\middle|\,{\bm{\uptheta}}\argclose\rparen\differential{\nparams}.

(28)

SBI requires only
samples
from the prior (
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen
) and the marginal likelihood (
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\mathbf{D}}\,\middle|\,{\bm{\uptheta}}\argclose\rparen
), which are provided by a stochastic forward simulator that represents the Bayesian joint model
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uptheta}},{\mathbf{D}}\argclose\rparen
. There are different techniques (flavours of neural SBI) for using simulated pairs
\quantity(\iparams, \dataset)
to train a neural network (NN) and later perform inference from observed data
\mathbf{D}_{0}
. We adopt the approach called neural ratio estimation (NRE)
(
Hermans et al. 2020-07-13/2020-07-18
)
since it allows the greatest freedom in the NN architecture and in choosing priors for training and evaluation. It trains a network
\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta},\mathbf{D}\argclose\rparen
to approximate the single real number

\operatorname{r}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta},\mathbf{D}\argclose\rparen\equiv\frac{\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uptheta}},{\mathbf{D}}\argclose\rparen}{\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\mathbf{D}\argclose\rparen}=\frac{\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uptheta}}\,\middle|\,{\mathbf{D}}\argclose\rparen}{\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen}

(29)

by minimisation of the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss commonly used for classification tasks. Once trained,
\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta},\mathbf{D}_{0}\argclose\rparen
evaluated at the observed data can be simply multiplied by the prior or used to re-weight prior samples to represent the target posterior, according to the second equality in
equation

29
.

In principle,
\bm{\uptheta}
can represent any group of parameters we wish to derive a posterior for. However, scientific interpretations are usually based on one- or two-dimensional marginal distributions, and consequently, we will define the following groups of (global) parameters of interest
\bm{\upgamma}_{g}
:

•

one-dimensional:
M_{0},\sigma_{0},\alpha,\beta,\alpha_{c},M_{*}^{\textrm{step}}
;

•

two-dimensional:
\quantity[\dtdA, \dtdb]
for the DTD and
\quantity[\Omo, \Olo]
for cosmology;

•

two-dimensional:
\quantity[\Mstep, \gammaMZ]
,
\quantity[\Mstep, \gammaMt]
, and
\quantity[\gammaMZ, \gammaMt]
, forming all combinations of the
host
–
SN Ia-connection parameters: a so-called
“corner plot”
.

For each of them, we train
—
simultaneously
—
separate ratio estimators
\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}_{g}
, following shared data pre-processing.

5.1
Object-specific parameters

In addition to the global parameters, we will
—
simultaneously
—
train ratio estimators for all
\order{\nsel}
object-specific (local) parameters in the hierarchical model (here we will use the unified label
\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}}
, which represents
\mathbf{g}^{h}
and
\bm{\uplambda}^{s}
in CIGaRS collectively). While we can, as before, form local-parameter groups
\bm{\uplambda}_{g}
from parameters of
the same object
, we will infer each object-specific parameter
\lambda_{g}\in\quantity{\zc, \Mstar, \Zstar, \dustd, \tau_2, \saltxint, \saltcint}
marginally. While in
figure

6
we only show a subset of the results that represent scientific interest in the present study, estimating
all
local parameters during training helps extract informative features and ultimately improves inference of the global parameters.

In
51
and
50
, we demonstrated simultaneous marginal inference of all
\quantity{\objlparams}
for models in which the data-set size
N_{\textrm{sel}}
was known
a priori
, i.e. it could be fixed after a survey is performed because selection effects were not considered. Here, we extend the approach to simulators that produce data sets with varying
N_{\textrm{sel}}
, which introduces two complications.

The first concerns identifying the objects. Within a BHM, the
\quantity{\objlparams}
are
a-priori
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), conditional on the set of global parameters
\bm{\upgamma}
, and each influences the sampling distribution of only one observed
“object”
; i.e. we have the general model structure

\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\upgamma},\quantity{\objlparams}},{\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}}\argclose\rparen=\quantity[\prod_{\objindexlabel=1}^{\nsel} \prob(\objdata\given\objlparams, \gparams) \prob(\objlparams\given\gparams)]\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen.

(30)

Previously, we used a fixed ordered collection of
auxiliary
variables
\quantity[\objmetadata]_{i=1}^{N_{\textrm{sel}}}
to disambiguate the assignment of labels
i
, which effectively individualised the sampling distributions:

\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\mathbf{d}^{i}\,\middle|\,\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}},\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen\to\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\mathbf{d}^{i}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{a}^{i},\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}},\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen\to\operatorname{p}_{i}\argopen\lparen\mathbf{d}^{i}\,\middle|\,\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}},\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen.

(31)

However, the present simulator produces unordered (
exchangeable
) sets of
a-priori
undetermined sizes, and so the auxiliary variables need to become an
output
of the model, i.e. be incorporated into the observable
\mathbf{d}^{i}
. Indeed, CIGaRS explicitly models the host photometry (from which redshift is ultimately derived) and the observational (fit) uncertainties, which comprised
\mathbf{a}^{i}
in
51
and
50
. Thus, we can treat
\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}}
and
\mathbf{d}^{i}
as
realisations
of singular random variables
\bm{\uplambda}
and
\mathbf{d}
:

\quantity[\prod_{\objindexlabel} \prob(\data=\objdata\given\lparams=\objlparams, \gparams) \prob(\lparams=\objlparams\given\gparams)]\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen.

(32)

rather than collections of separate (i.i.d.) random variables, allowing us to train a
single
network to represent
all
posteriors

\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\smash{\bm{\uplambda}^{i}}\vphantom{\mathbf{d}^{i}}\,\middle|\,\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}\argclose\rparen\to\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uplambda}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}^{i},\cancel{\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}}\argclose\rparen,

(33)

where we have ignored the dependence on the full
\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}
since it is approximately redundant with the truncation of global parameters, as argued in
51
.

Secondly, a technical complication arises since we need training pairs

\bm{\uplambda},\mathbf{d}\sim\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uplambda}},{\mathbf{d}}\argclose\rparen=\int\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uplambda},\mathbf{d}}\,\middle|\,{\bm{\upgamma}}\argclose\rparen\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen\differential{\gparams},

(34)

but the simulator samples in proportion to
\opbraces{\expectationvalue{\nsel}}(\bm{\upgamma})\times\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen
rather than simply
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\upgamma}\argclose\rparen
. This is not a problem when
N_{\textrm{sel}}
is a fixed input and can otherwise be rectified by randomly selecting a fixed number
N_{\textrm{sub}}
of objects from the simulator output. In fact,
N_{\textrm{sub}}\coloneq 1
would be the natural choice in line with the above treatment of
\quantity{\objlparams}
and
\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}
as realisations of
\bm{\uplambda}
and
\mathbf{d}
, and we choose this for generating a validation set, which also represents the prior
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uplambda}\argclose\rparen
. Still, by setting a larger
N_{\textrm{sub}}
, we can cheaply (i.e. without extra simulation) enlarge the effective batch size for training local-parameter inference networks, and so we choose
N_{\textrm{sub}}\coloneq 100
in this case. Finally, for evaluating the results for the
N_{\textrm{sel},0}
objects in
\mathbf{D}_{0}
, we simply skip the sub-selection and use the full set (
N_{\textrm{sub}}\coloneq N_{\textrm{sel},0}
).

5.2
Network architecture: conditioned deep set++

The neural network we use (depicted, together with details of the sizes of its various layers, in
figure

4
) is based on the conditioned deep-set architecture from
43
(following
Zaheer et al. 2017
) but augmented in depth as in
Zhang et al. 2022-07-17/2022-07-23
due to the sophistication of the simulator and the inference tasks and with the addition of local-parameter estimators as in
51
and
50
.

Given a data set
\mathbf{D}\equiv\quantity{\objindex[\objindexlabel]{\data}}
with cardinality
N_{\textrm{sel}}
, we first pre-process each element
\mathbf{d}^{i}
with a small feed-forward network to (automatically) derive (non-linear)
“features”
:

\bm{\mathtt{d}}^{i}=\mathtt{DataPre}(\mathbf{d}^{i}),

(35)

which are generally useful across all inference tasks. These may include galaxy colours, (fiducially) standardised SN magnitudes, observational uncertainty (from the provided variances), as well as estimates of object-specific parameters directly usable in the respective downstream ratio estimators. While they are not forced to be directly
“meaningful”
to a human scientist, we plan to explore and interpret these features in future work.
The
\quantity{\nndatasn}
are then input for all ratio estimators.

Following
Zaheer et al. 2017
’s representation theorem
(
Zaheer et al. 2017
)
and the considerations laid out in
43
, the estimator for a group of global parameters
\bm{\uptheta}_{g}
makes use of two feed-forward networks
\hat{\phi}_{g}
,
\hat{\rho}_{g}
and takes the form

\ln\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}_{g}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g},\mathbf{D}\argclose\rparen\equiv\hat{\rho}_{g}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g},\bm{\mathtt{S}}_{g},N_{\textrm{sel}}\argclose\rparen,

(36)

with

\bm{\mathtt{S}}_{g}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\textrm{sel}}}\sum_{i}\hat{\phi}_{g}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g},\frac{\bm{\mathtt{d}}^{i}-\bm{\mathtt{m}}}{\bm{\mathtt{s}}},\bm{\mathtt{m}},\bm{\mathtt{s}}\argclose\rparen,

(37)

where
\bm{\mathtt{m}}
and
\bm{\mathtt{s}}
are, respectively, the mean and standard deviation of the
\quantity{\nndatasn}
. This implements the
\mathtt{SetNorm}
operation advocated by
Zhang et al. 2022-07-17/2022-07-23
but explicitly preserves the information contained in the set moments
\bm{\mathtt{m}}
and
\bm{\mathtt{s}}
. For expressivity (which allows the set aggregation to be a simple averaging operation),
\hat{\phi}_{g}
is a deep residual network that implements
“skip connections”
as proposed by
Zhang et al. 2022-07-17/2022-07-23
after combining its inputs
\quantity(\igiparams, \frac{\nndatasn-\nnmean}{\nnstd}, \nnmean, \nnstd)
into one vector through a single hidden network layer: see
figure

4
. Finally,
\hat{\rho}_{g}
similarly combines its inputs
\quantity(\igiparams, \ignnsummary, \nsel)
and feeds them through a few fully connected layers to output the single number
\ln\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}_{g}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g},\mathbf{D}\argclose\rparen
.

The ratio estimator for a local/object-specific parameter
\lambda_{g}
is simpler since it does not need to aggregate information across the set, and so it consists of a single feed-forward network:

\ln\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}_{g}\argopen\lparen\lambda_{g},\mathbf{d}^{i}\argclose\rparen=\opbraces{\mathtt{LocalNRE}_{g}}(\bm{\uplambda}_{g},\bm{\mathtt{d}}^{i}),

(38)

which can be applied in parallel over
N_{\textrm{sub}}
input data at the same time.

5.3
Truncation and fine-tuning

Neural SBI is
amortised
: once the network is trained, results can be quickly derived from numerous simulated (or real, if available) data realisations. While this can be exploited to verify and calibrate inference
(
Karchev et al. 2023
;
Karchev et al. 2024
, see e.g.)
, it requires training over a wide range of data, which can be inefficient if one is only interested in the results for a single
—

the real/target

—
data set
\mathbf{D}_{0}
.

In such cases, the simulation budget, training time, and network capacity can be optimised through various sequential SBI strategies, which modify (either continuously, or in a succession of
stages
) the prior
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\to\operatorname{\tilde{p}}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen
from which parameters are drawn, based on intermediate results during training. A simple yet effective prescription is
prior truncation

(
Miller et al. 2021
)
, in which the shape/form of
\operatorname{\tilde{p}}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen
is unchanged, but its support is restricted to a region
T(\mathbf{D}_{0})
in which the posterior density is significantly different from zero:

\bm{\uptheta}\notin T(\mathbf{D}_{0})\implies\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen{\bm{\uptheta}}\,\middle|\,{\mathbf{D}_{0}}\argclose\rparen\approx 0,

(39)

as approximated by a previously trained network evaluated at
\mathbf{D}_{0}
. Thus, the only needed modification is a trivial re-normalisation to account for the excluded prior probability mass:

\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\to\operatorname{\tilde{p}}_{T(\mathbf{D}_{0})}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen}{\int_{T(\mathbf{D}_{0})}\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}\argclose\rparen\differential{\iparams}}&\text{if $\bm{\uptheta}\in T(\mathbf{D}_{0})$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(40)

which leaves the posterior (given
\mathbf{D}_{0}
) unchanged while restricting simulated examples to be more similar to
\mathbf{D}_{0}
.

We apply truncation separately for each
global
inference group: in one dimension we use a simple contiguous interval that contains
99.99\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
approximate credibility and then sample training examples by analytically modifying the (simple: uniform or normal) prior; for two-dimensional groups, we either define an iso-likelihood contour (that again contains
99.99\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
approximate posterior mass) and use rejection sampling within it, or apply one-dimensional truncation separately for the two parameters (i.e. we truncate to an axis-aligned rectangular region) when they are not strongly correlated. The truncation regions are illustrated with gray shading in
figure

5
.

Once new training data is generated, we resume
—
instead of re-initialising
—
training, which means that the network is simply fine-tuned to give more accurate results in the
“zoomed-in”
parameter space, instead of forced to re-learn the analysis from scratch. This is especially relevant for the pre-processor and deep-set featurisers (described below), whose computations should not significantly depend on the parameter ranges.

5.3.1
Training details

Analysing the mock data with
\approx 1600
objects requires three rounds of truncation (starting from the priors in
table

1
and with the network in
figure

4
) for results to converge (judged by the difference between posteriors from successive rounds). In each, we generate
64\,000
full example data sets (with
6400
more for validation and to represent the priors when evaluating results) and train using the Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2017
)
and learning rate
1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
(reduced to
1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
when fine-tuning) for at most
200
epochs, although the loss typically plateaus earlier. On two NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a combined mini-batch size of
64
, one stage takes
\approx 24\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
, with the majority of the computation taken up by
back
-propagating through all
11
deep sets (for the separate global-parameter groups).

For the larger (
N_{\textrm{sel}}\approx$16\,000$
) data set, we take the final network from above and fine-tune it on simulations from the same final truncated prior region but with a tenfold increase in output size (on expectation). Since the deep-set architecture processes the full data at once, training it now requires roughly ten times more compute and memory, so we use
8
GPUs and a batch size of
32
, which again requires
24\text{\,}\mathrm{h}
per truncation stage (we only need one in this case).

Finally, for each global-parameter group
\bm{\upgamma}_{g}
, we pick the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss for the specific group, whereas for object-specific parameters
\lambda_{g}
, we pick the best checkpoint overall (lowest loss averaged over all groups). To represent the posteriors, we evaluate
\hat{\operatorname{\operatorname{r}}}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g},\mathbf{D}_{0}\argclose\rparen
over the parameter samples in the validation set, which represent
\operatorname{p}\argopen\lparen\bm{\uptheta}_{g}\argclose\rparen
, and use the results as importance weights for plotting contours and calculating posterior moments.
