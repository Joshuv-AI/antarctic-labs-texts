A Benchmark for Zero-Shot Belief Inference in Large Language Models
Joseph Malone, Rachith Aiyappa, Byunghwee Lee, Haewoon Kwak, Jisun An, Yong-Yeol Ahn

Abstract
Beliefs are central to how humans reason, communicate, and form social connections, yet most computational approaches to studying them remain confined to narrow sociopolitical contexts and rely on fine-tuning for optimal performance. Despite the growing use of large language models (LLMs) across disciplines, how well these systems generalize across diverse belief domains remains unclear. We introduce a systematic, reproducible benchmark that evaluates the ability of LLMs to predict individuals’ stances on a wide range of topics in a zero-shot setting using data from an online debate platform. The benchmark includes multiple informational conditions that isolate the contribution of demographic context and known prior beliefs to predictive success. Across several small- to medium-sized models, we find that providing more background information about an individual improves predictive accuracy, but performance varies substantially across belief domains. These findings reveal both the capacity and limitations of current LLMs to emulate human reasoning, advancing the study of machine behavior and offering a scalable framework for modeling belief systems beyond the sociopolitical sphere.



Keywords:
large language models; belief prediction; computational social science; machine behavior; zero-shot learning

Introduction

Beliefs structure the reasoning processes through which humans interpret and navigate the world.
Because reasoning is shaped through communication and shared experience, individual beliefs continually influence one another, forming complex networks that shape how people make sense of reality
[
36
]
.
Within these networks, shifts in one area of reasoning often correspond with changes in others, reflecting the structured nature of human thought.

Social scientists have observed that beliefs cluster across seemingly unrelated domains, linking political ideology with lifestyle preferences and cultural taste, such as the tendency for liberals to drink lattes
[
11
]
.
While traditionally explained through social contagion, where beliefs spread through imitation and reinforcement along network ties
[
8
,
28

This model posits that people adopt beliefs not merely through social contagion, but through perceived compatibility among ideas
[
16

.
Once such associations emerge, they can intensify through social reinforcement, as small initial alignments introduced by early movers cascade into large-scale partisan divisions
[
26
]
.
These findings highlight the structured and dynamic nature of human belief systems.
Understanding how such systems form and evolve is therefore essential for explaining patterns of reasoning and the social outcomes they produce.

Yet, despite decades of sociological research on the formation and spread of beliefs, our understanding of belief systems at scale remains limited.
Recent advances in artificial intelligence and, in particular, large language models (LLMs) offer a new avenue to study beliefs computationally, enabling large-scale analyses of how reasoning and social behavior emerge from patterns of belief
[

]
.
Other studies have used LLMs for related social inference tasks, such as simulating human behavior

Addressing this limitation is critical, as LLMs are prone to hallucinations

In this work, we evaluate how LLMs predict individuals’ beliefs across a wide range of topics under varying informational settings, contributing to machine behavior research
[

.
Concurrently, we provide a reproducible benchmark to systematically evaluate how LLMs emulate human belief reasoning across domains, enabling broader computational study of belief systems.

Using several contemporary small- to medium-sized LLMs, we evaluate belief prediction under varying informational conditions, from blind inference to context with demographic information and known beliefs of an individual.
We find that providing more contextual information consistently improves predictive accuracy, but performance varies considerably across belief categories.
These patterns reveal both the capacity and limitations of current LLMs to mirror human belief reasoning, advancing understanding of how models reason about human beliefs and offering insight into the social biases and reasoning patterns embedded within them.

Methodology

3.1
Dataset

We utilized the DDO dataset
[
14
]
to extract
belief statements
from users to evaluate the prediction capabilities of various LLMs.
The DDO dataset contains data spanning from October 15th, 2007, to September 19th, 2018 and provides three key types of information: demographic attributes of users (e.g. age, gender, ethnicity, religious ideology, etc.), various debate topics with arguments from two opposing sides, and stance votes (either “agree” or “disagree”) from observers who vote for the sides of the debates they agree with.
This information provides the foundation for constructing the belief statements we use in our experiments.

3.3
Data Pre-Processing and Chronological Splitting

We chronologically ordered the beliefs of each user from the processed DDO dataset to reflect more realistic prediction scenarios—using past beliefs to predict new beliefs.
The data was split into train and test subsets where every user appears in each set:

•

Training Set:
Also referred to as “context beliefs”, these are the oldest 80% of each user’s generated belief statements.
This set was used to provide context beliefs to the LLM at the time of prediction in the relevant experiment settings.

•

Test Set:
Also referred to as “test beliefs”, these are the remaining 20% of belief statements.
This set is used only for evaluation.
For each test belief, the model predicts the user’s stance based on varying input information dependent on the experiment setting (blind, demographics, context beliefs, context beliefs and demographics).

It is also important to mention that users who did not have enough total beliefs for the split ratio—users with less than five total belief statements—were omitted.

To prevent data leakage between the context and test sets, we also aim to eliminate any context beliefs whose semantic similarity is too similar to a test belief.
We do this by removing any any context belief whose cosine similarity with a belief in the user’s test set exceeded 0.8, computed using Sentence-BERT embeddings.

3.4
Task Definition

We evaluate how well LLMs make predictions about human belief systems in a binary classification setting.
To determine the level of context required for accurate prediction, we employ an informational ablation framework, systematically varying the background information provided to the LLM.
Specifically, for each user with a sufficient number of belief statements, we provide the model with incremental levels of background context—blind, demographics, context beliefs, and both (context beliefs + demographics)—and instruct it to predict the stance toward a held-out belief statement.
We record the results under each experiment setting for each tested LLM and compare performance across both models and belief categories.

3.6
Prompt Setup

It has been shown that encouraging LLMs to generate a series of intermediate reasoning steps—or a “chain-of-thought”—improves performance in complex reasoning tasks
[
37
]
.
LLMs are also highly sensitive to the wording of their instructions
[
39
]
.
To address both these issues, we use DSPy
[
34
]
for its structured input and output design and chain-of-thought module.
DSPy works by introducing
signatures
, which clearly structure the inputs and outputs of LLM interactions to better separate what the LLM should do and how it should be prompted to do it.
This way, we are better able to organize the varying amounts of background information of a user and extract the binary prediction outputs from LLMs (e.g. “agree” or “disagree”) while also benefiting from the superior reasoning ability of chain-of-thought answers.

We define our model inputs and expected outputs using DSPy’s signature interface, and use DSPy to internally generate the corresponding system message and user messages for each LLM call.
This is done to ensure consistent prompt formatting across models while abstracting the prompt engineering process.
Our system message can be found in §
7.2
, and an example user message and LLM response is provided below.

Respond with the corresponding output fields, starting with the field

Example Output Message

The following contains the layout of the previous example user message.

[[ ## reasoning ## ]]

This prompting strategy allows us to effectively evaluate how well each LLM predicts beliefs based on known information about a user and minimizes prompt-formatting differences across models.

5
Discussion

Across twenty-three belief categories and four information conditions, our results show a consistent pattern: predictive accuracy increases as models receive richer background information about the target individual.
Performance rises from the blind condition—where predictions reflect broad population priors—through the demographics-only and known beliefs-only (context) conditions, and peaks when both information types are combined.
This gradient indicates that base LLMs can use added cues to personalize their inferences beyond population averages.
Yet even in the most informative setting, the absolute accuracy remains moderate.
From a machine behavior perspective, this pattern suggests that LLMs exhibit structured but bounded forms of social reasoning—responding to contextual cues in ways that resemble human inference, yet rooted in surface-level correlations rather than deeper conceptual understanding.

The improvement from blind to information-rich settings reflects the different kinds of signals each source provides.
Demographic attributes supply high-level social priors—statistical cues about how people with similar backgrounds tend to respond to different issues—while prior belief statements offer direct, task-specific evidence of a user’s expressed opinions and worldview.
Prior beliefs therefore anchor predictions in a user’s expressed attitudes rather than demographic averages, whereas demographics alone capture only broad group tendencies.
When both information sources are available, demographics set a population-level baseline that context beliefs refine, yielding a modest gain that varies in size across belief categories.
A complementary pattern also emerges when we compare aggregation methods.

Aggregating model predictions via majority vote improves accuracy relative to averaging scores across models.
The gains are modest but consistent, suggesting that different models make partially complementary errors and encode demographic and contextual cues with varying emphases.
These variations plausibly arise from variation in model architecture, training corpora, and alignment procedures, leading each model to emphasize distinct patterns in prompt material.
This indicates that ensembles improve performance by exploiting model diversity, rather than by distilling some deeper, emergent understanding.

Despite these aggregate trends, performance varies markedly across belief domains.
Categories like religion, science, and politics achieve the highest accuracy—likely because they combine standardized, widely discussed language with strong links between belief and social identity.
This aligns with sociological accounts of belief clustering and associative diffusion
[
11
,
16
]
, as certain domains hold tighter networks of mutually reinforcing ideas while others remain more loosely organized.
Such variation suggests that LLMs are sensitive to the associative structure of belief systems: they perform best where social meaning is more internally coherent and consistent, and struggle where it is diffuse or weakly coupled.
Some performance variation may also reflect training data composition, as text in highly polarized domains tends to produce clearer linguistic distinctions between positions.
Still, even in the best-performing domains, overall accuracy remains moderate across models.

The modest overall accuracy across models highlights both the difficulty of belief prediction and the limits of zero-shot inference.
Some of this ceiling likely reflects noise in the task itself—users’ recorded stances do not always represent their true beliefs, nor are all prior statements guaranteed to be related or even consistent with the test proposition.
LLMs thus operate under both information sparsity and human inconsistency that no prompt context can completely resolve.
Beyond this data limitation, model biases also constrain performance: pretrained models absorb the demographic and ideological skews of the corpora they are trained on, which can privilege majority viewpoints or overfit to familiar demographic patterns.

These findings raise broader ethical and practical considerations.
The ability to infer individual attitudes from limited cues highlights both the potential and risk of belief modeling.
While such inference can be valuable for understanding model behavior or aspects of human belief systems themselves, it also exposes privacy and consent concerns and, given the moderate, variable accuracy of models, risks around fairness and misrepresentation.
Responsible use of belief-prediction frameworks requires transparency about uncertainty and inherent model limitations, clear constraints on applications, and careful evaluation about how demographic or contextual information is used.
Together, these findings position belief prediction as both a diagnostic tool to study LLM behavior and a testbed for understanding how models internalize social reasoning under minimal supervision.

6
Conclusion

We have introduced a systematic, reproducible benchmark for evaluating how large language models infer human beliefs in a zero-shot setting.
Using data from
Debate.org
, we tested multiple open-source models across four information conditions—from blind predictions to prompts including both demographic information and prior beliefs.

Providing richer background information consistently improved predictive accuracy, and ensemble aggregation enhanced stability by leveraging complementary model strengths.
Accuracy was highest in belief domains with well-defined opposing viewpoints and high public salience, reflecting the models’ sensitivity to structured and widely articulated patterns of reasoning.

Overall, our results suggest that LLMs can approximate individual belief patterns using minimal context but still fall short of capturing the full complexity and inconsistency of human reasoning.
The belief prediction task thus serves not only as a benchmark for model generalization but also as a testbed for studying how LLMs internalize and reproduce social reasoning.

Future work should extend this framework to larger and more diverse models, exploring how prompting strategies and training-data composition shape belief inference.
Examining cross-domain reasoning—how models use beliefs in one area to predict attitudes in another—could clarify whether such patterns reflect genuine social reasoning or surface correlations.
These directions are crucial for defining both the capabilities and the limits of LLMs as tools for computational social science.
