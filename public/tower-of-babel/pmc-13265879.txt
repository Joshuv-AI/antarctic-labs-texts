Effects of dietary metabolizable energy, crude protein and lysine levels on production performance and egg quality of Jingfen laying hens based on response surface methodology
Authors: Yan Cui, Shouqun Jiang, Qiuli Fan, Sai Zhang, Yibing Wang, Xiajing Lin, Fayuan Ding, Limin Chen, Dong Ruan, Jinling Ye, Kaiguo Gao, Zhongyong Gou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This experiment aimed to investigate the effects of dietary nitrogen-corrected metabolizable energy (MEn), crude protein (CP) and lysine (Lys) levels on the production performance and egg quality of Jingfen laying hens during their peak laying period. Dietary MEn levels were 2,500, 2,600 and 2,700 kcal/kg, CP levels were 15%, 16% and 17% and Lys levels were 0.7%, 0.8% and 0.9% respectively. A total of 450 twenty-week-old Jingfen layers with similar initial laying rates were selected and divided into 15 groups (12 factorial points + 3 center points) according to a response surface methodology (RSM) experimental design. Each group consisted of 3 replicates, with 10 hens per replicate housed in cages (2 hens per cage). The trial lasted 12 weeks. Dietary MEn level significantly affected average egg weight (EW) and daily egg mass (EM) (P < 0.05). Hens fed the high-MEn diet had significantly greater EW and EM compared to those fed the low-MEn diet (P < 0.05). Dietary Lys level significantly affected EW and EM (P < 0.05), with hens fed the high-Lys diet showing significantly higher EW and EM than those fed the low-Lys diet (P < 0.05). Dietary MEn level significantly influenced eggshell proportion (P < 0.05). Dietary CP level significantly affected Haugh unit (HU) (P < 0.05), with the eggs of hens fed a high-CP diet having significantly greater HU than those of hens fed the medium-CP diet (P < 0.05). Dietary MEn level significantly affected ovarian index and the number of dominant follicles (P < 0.05). The ovarian index of hens fed the medium-MEn and high-MEn diet was significantly greater than those of hens fed the low-MEn diet respectively (P < 0.05). The number of dominant follicles of hens fed the high-MEn diet was significantly greater than that of hens fed the low-MEn diet (P < 0.05). Dietary CP level significantly influenced plasma triglyceride (TG), uric acid (UA) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) concentrations (P < 0.05). Plasma TG, UA and LDL contents of hens fed the low-CP diet were significantly lower than those of hens fed the high-CP diet (P < 0.05). Dietary Lys level significantly affected plasma total protein concentration (P < 0.05), which was significantly higher of hens fed the high-Lys diet than that of hens fed the low-Lys diet (P < 0.05). Comprehensive analysis of all indicators using RSM revealed that, under the conditions of this trial, the optimal related dietary nutritional levels for maximizing production performance of Jingfen layers were: MEn 2,688 kcal/kg, CP 17.0% and Lys 0.9%. The corresponding daily precision requirements were MEn 245 kcal, CP 15.5 g and Lys 0.8 g.

Introduction

Global population growth continues to drive increasing demand for protein. Eggs, as a vital source of animal-derived protein, are now a widely consumed nutritious food worldwide. The production performance and egg quality of laying hens are influenced by numerous factors, with feed being a primary determinant. When formulating diets, metabolizable energy (ME) and crude protein (CP) levels are key nutritional parameters requiring primary consideration (Ding et al., 2016). Dietary ME and CP levels directly impact egg production performance and egg quality (Gunawardana et al., 2008; Li et al., 2013; Nahashon et al., 2007). Appropriate concentration of these nutrients meet the growth requirements of poultry, ensure health, and enhance feed utilization efficiency (Li et al., 2016). Insufficient ME supply can lead to reduced body weight, decreased egg production, and impaired eggshell quality. Conversely, excessive ME intake may cause fat deposition, reduced production performance and increased susceptibility to conditions such as fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (Rozenboim et al., 2016).

For laying hens, dietary protein level is crucial, particularly for the formation of yolk and albumen (Geng et al., 2022). Given their limited capacity to store protein, dietary CP concentration must align with the target level of egg production. Lysine (Lys) is typically the first or second limiting amino acid in most poultry diets (Coertze et al., 2025), and its deficiency directly leads to a reduction in feed intake (Alam et al., 2014). However, the interactions among ME, CP and Lys levels and their combined effects on performance and egg quality remain inadequately characterized, especially in indigenous breeds.

The Jingfen laying hen, an indigenous breed in China, is valued for its strong adaptability and excellent production performance. However, research on precision nutrition requirements and optimal feeding management of this breed remains lacking. In particular, no systematic study has yet established the optimal dietary ME, CP and Lys levels for Jingfen hens, nor has the potential synergistic or antagonistic effects among these nutrients been explored.

Therefore, this study used response surface methodology (RSM) to design experiment. Based on previous studies, we hypothesized that: (1) Increasing dietary MEn, CP and Lys levels within appropriate ranges would improve the production performance and egg quality of Jingfen No.6 laying hens during the peak laying period; (2) Significant interactions exist among dietary nitrogen-corrected ME (MEn), CP and Lys levels; (3) The RSM could accurately predict the optimal dietary nutrient combinations for maximizing production performance of hens. The aim is to investigate the effects of dietary ME, CP and Lys levels on the production performance and egg quality of Jingfen laying hens. The findings are expected to provide a scientific basis for the feeding management of this breed and establish a foundation for future optimization of feeding technologies. By explicitly testing these hypotheses, the findings are expected to provide a scientific basis for the feeding management of this breed and establish a foundation for future optimization of feeding technologies.

Materials and methods

Animal husbandry and management

Experimental design

The trial was conducted at the Animal Nutrition Experimental Farm of the Institute of Animal Science, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences in China. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval number: IAS 2022-037) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching.

A three-factor, three-level Box-Behnken design (BBD) of RSM was used. The BBD was selected because it requires fewer experimental runs than central composite designs while providing sufficient information for testing model lack of fit. The dietary levels were set as: MEn (2500, 2600, 2700 kcal/kg), CP (15%, 16%, 17%), Lys (0.7%, 0.8%, 0.9%). These levels were selected based on: Jingfen No.6 Laying Hen Breeding Management Manual (2022 Edition). A total of 15 treatments (12 factorial points + 3 center points) were generated using Design-Expert 13.0. The center points were replicated three times to estimate pure error and test model adequacy.

Model fitting procedure: data were fitted to a second-order polynomial regression model: Y = β₀ + ΣβᵢXᵢ + ΣβjXj +ΣβkXk + ΣβᵢᵢXᵢ² + ΣβjjXj²+ ΣβkkXk² + ΣβᵢjXᵢXj + ΣβᵢkXᵢXk + ΣβⱼkXjXk + ΣβᵢⱼkXᵢXⱼXk + ε, where Y = response variable, Xᵢ/Xⱼ/Xk = independent variables, β₀ = intercept, βᵢ/βj/βk = linear coefficient, βᵢᵢ/βjj/βkk = quadratic coefficient, βᵢj/βᵢk/βⱼk/βᵢⱼk = interaction coefficient, ε = random error.

A total of 450 Jingfen No.6 laying hens were selected and randomly allocated into 15 groups based on similar body weights and initial laying rates (90 ± 2%). Each of the 15 dietary treatments was randomly assigned to 3 replicate pens (biological replicates), with 10 hens per pen. Hens were housed in cages (2 hens per cage). Each pen served as the experimental unit for all statistical analyses. The goodness of fit and statistical significance of the model were evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA), including the P-values of the regression model and the lack-of-fit test. The coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R² and predicted R² were used to assess the explanatory power and predictive ability of the model. A significant regression model (P < 0.05) and a non-significant lack-of-fit (P > 0.05) were regarded as indicators of adequate model fitness. Measurements

Production performance

The initial body weight (BW) of the laying hens at the beginning of the experiment and their final BW at the end of the experiment were measured. Feeding amount and the remaining amount of feed were accurately recorded. Average daily feed intake (ADFI) was calculated throughout the experimental period. Hens’ health status was monitored daily and mortality was recorded. Eggs were collected once daily at 15:00. The following data were recorded per replicate: number of eggs laid, egg weight (EW), amount of defective eggs defined by Gou et al (2020, 2021).

Egg quality

At the end of the feeding trial, two eggs per replicate were randomly sampled, labeled and analyzed for egg quality within 24 hours. Parameters measured included eggshell breaking strength, yolk color score and Haugh unit (HU).

Slaughter traits

Two birds, representative of average egg production in each replicate, and having laid eggs the previous day (to standardize status in the laying cycle) were selected to slaughter and sample. Liver and abdominal fat (AF) tissue were immediately dissected post-slaughter. Liver index (%) = (liver weight / live BW) × 100. Abdominal fat percentage (%) = (AF weight / live BW) × 100.

Follicular development observation

Ovarian weight, oviduct length, oviduct weight, amount of dominant follicles (diameter ≥ 8 mm) and weight of dominant follicles were recorded post-slaughter.

Blood biochemical parameters

Blood (5 mL) was collected into evacuated tubes containing EDTA-K2 via wing vein of birds. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at -80°C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of triglycerides (TG), total protein, albumin, total cholesterol (TCHO), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and free fatty acids (FFA) were determined with commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Hepatic adipogenesis-related gene expression

Total RNA from liver and ovarian stromal tissue was isolated using Trizol reagent respectively. Reverse transcription used the PrimerScript RT Reagent Kit. Gene-specific primers were designed with Primer Premier 5.0 (Premier Biosoft International, Palo Alto, CA) based on NCBI GenBank sequences of chickens. Beta-actin served as the reference gene. All primers (sequences in Table 3) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Guangzhou Branch (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The specificity of all primers was verified by melting curve analysis, which showed a single peak for each gene. Amplification efficiencies were between 90% and 110%, determined by serial dilution of cDNA templates. Data analysis

Raw data were preprocessed using Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). The experimental unit for all analyses was the replicate (each replicate = 10 hens housed in 5 adjacent cages). ANOVA and Response surface analysis was conducted using Design-Expert 13.0 (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN). Interaction effects were evaluated by significance of interaction terms in the regression model, contour plot shape (elliptical = significant interaction, circular = non-significant) and response surface plot visualization. Results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Model adequacy was further evaluated by the lack-of-fit test, and a non-significant lack-of-fit (P > 0.05) confirmed that the model adequately described the experimental data. Finally, numerical optimization was applied to the statistically significant models to identify the optimal combination of dietary MEn, CP and Lys levels for the key performance indicators of laying hens. Non-significant terms (P > 0.05) were removed via stepwise regression. Model adequacy was assessed by R², adjusted R², predicted R² and lack-of-fit test.

Effects of dietary MEn, CP and Lys levels on ovarian and follicular development traits of laying hens

Discussion

Dietary ME is a fundamental factor influencing the performance of laying hens. While some studies suggest hens adjust feed intake in response to dietary energy density (Qian et al., 2024), the present study observed no significant effect of energy level on feed intake, consistent with findings by Mikulski et al. (2020) and Han et al. (2023).

Numerous studies have confirmed that increasing dietary energy levels significantly enhances egg production, EW and egg quality of laying hens (Sell et al., 1987). Results in the current study support the conclusion that the high-MEn diet significantly increased EW and daily EM. However, an optimal range exists for energy levels, beyond which benefits diminish. Nahashon et al. (2007) found that Pearl Grey guinea fowl hens fed a 2800 kcal/kg (ME) diet exhibited superior average egg production, EW, egg quality and shell thickness compared to those fed a 2900 kcal/kg (ME) diet. This suggests that energy excess may lead to excessive body fat deposition, negatively impacting production performance and increasing production costs (Frikha et al., 2009). The CP levels (15% to 17%) in the present study align with the lower range of current commercial recommendations for peak-phase laying hens (15% to 18.5%), reflecting the trend toward reduced-protein diets supplemented with crystalline amino acids (Keshavarz and Austic, 2004). The comparable production performance observed across the tested CP levels suggests that the lower protein bound of 15% was sufficient when Lys and other essential amino acids were adequately balanced, which is in agreement with previous studies demonstrating that low-CP diets supplemented with amino acids can maintain laying performance comparable to higher-protein diets (Azzam et al., 2017). The physiological basis for the observed energy effects lies in the hen’s regulatory mechanisms for energy homeostasis. The liver serves as the primary site of lipogenesis in laying hens, and energy supply modulates hepatic lipid metabolism through the mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) signaling pathway, which functions as a critical nutrient sensor integrating energy status with protein synthesis and cellular metabolism (Sengupta et al., 2010). When dietary energy exceeds the requirements for maintenance and egg production, excess energy is diverted toward hepatic lipogenesis and abdominal fat deposition through upregulated expression of fatty acid synthase (FASN) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA) (Hu et al., 2024). Hu et al. (2024) reported that an imbalanced energy-to-protein ratio (0.74 to 0.80) in low-protein diets upregulated genes related to fatty acid synthesis, including stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), FASN, and acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA), while simultaneously downregulating genes involved in fatty acid beta-oxidation such as acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase trifunctional multienzyme complex subunit alpha (HADHA), enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase (EHHADH), and acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 1 (ACAA1). This metabolic shift not only increases feed costs but also predisposes hens to fatty liver syndrome, which impairs hepatic synthesis of yolk precursors such as vitellogenin (VTG) and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), ultimately compromising egg production efficiency.

Lysine, as a limiting amino acid, is crucial for egg formation. The positive effects of high Lys levels on EW and EM observed in this study align with multiple research findings. For specific layer breeds (e.g., Hy-Line Brown, Hy-Line W-36, Dahlem Red), particularly during peak production phases, increasing the digestible Lys level in the diet (typically within the range of 0.73% to 0.95%) has been consistently shown in various trials to significantly increase EW, egg number, and daily EM (Scappaticcio et al., 2022; Coertze et al., 2025; Scappaticcio et al., 2021). The underlying molecular mechanism involves the role of lysine as a key regulator of protein synthesis through the mTOR signaling pathway. Lysine activates mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), which phosphorylates eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) and ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K1), thereby promoting translation initiation and protein synthesis in the liver and oviduct (Ruan et al., 2019). Additionally, lysine serves as the primary reference amino acid in the “ideal protein” concept, establishing the requirements for other essential amino acids including methionine, threonine, and tryptophan (Macelline et al., 2021). This regulatory function ensures optimal amino acid balance for the biosynthesis of egg proteins, particularly ovalbumin, which constitutes approximately 54% of total egg white protein and requires adequate lysine supply for its synthesis in the tubular gland cells of the magnum section of the oviduct (Macelline et al., 2021). Furthermore, lysine availability directly influences egg component deposition. Novak et al. (2004) demonstrated that increasing dietary lysine and total sulfur amino acid levels significantly affected egg production parameters and egg components in Dekalb Delta laying hens, with higher lysine levels increasing egg weight and altering albumen proportion. This response is primarily attributed to lysine optimizing dietary amino acid balance and promoting the biosynthesis of proteins required for the synthesis of egg components. At the hepatic level, adequate lysine supply upregulates the expression of genes involved in yolk precursor formation, including very-low-density apolipoprotein-II (ApoVLDL-II), which is essential for the assembly and secretion of VLDL particles targeted to the developing ovarian follicles (Ruan et al., 2019). Ruan et al. (2019) reported that dietary lysine linearly increased hepatic expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), and ApoVLDL-II, while reducing hepatic triglyceride and cholesterol contents in laying duck breeders, suggesting that lysine promotes protein turnover and lipid metabolism concurrently.

This study observed an increased eggshell proportion (an indicator of shell thickness) of hens fed the low-MEn diet, consistent with the findings of Nahashon et al. (2007), where hens fed ME with 2800 kcal/kg diet exhibited higher shell thickness than those fed with 2900 kcal/kg of ME. Furthermore, Li et al. (2013) also reported that excessively high energy levels may lead to reduced eggshell quality. Lower MEn levels may lead to increased feed intake of laying hens, consequently elevating the intake of minerals such as calcium and phosphorus (Coertze et al., 2025). Increased calcium intake has been demonstrated to significantly enhance eggshell weight, shell thickness and shell density (Safaa et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2021). Therefore, the improved eggshell proportion of hens fed the low-ME diet may be attributed to higher mineral intake, particularly calcium, resulting from increased feed consumption. In the current study, we also found increased feed intake of hens fed the low-MEn diet, although not significantly different. The interaction between dietary energy and protein supply plays a critical role in determining eggshell quality through mechanisms beyond simple mineral intake. The energy-to-protein ratio influences renal clearance of calcium and phosphorus as well as the activity of carbonic anhydrase in the shell gland, which is essential for eggshell calcification (Jonchère et al., 2010). Jonchère et al. (2010) conducted gene expression profiling of the chicken uterus (shell gland) and identified multiple eggshell proteins involved in the physical defense of the egg, revealing that the organic matrix proteins—including ovocalyxin-32, ovocleidin-116, and osteopontin—are critical for calcium carbonate crystal nucleation and shell strength. When dietary energy exceeds protein supply, the resulting metabolic imbalance increases hepatic lipogenesis and reduces the synthesis of these calcification-related proteins. Moreover, high-energy diets with inadequate protein may impair the production of mucin proteins in the shell gland epithelium. This explains why the optimal energy-to-protein ratio is essential for maintaining shell quality, as excessive energy relative to protein may compromise the protein-mediated processes of shell formation even when mineral intake is adequate.

Egg HU is a core indicator for assessing egg freshness and internal quality. Research has established that high CP diets significantly increase HU values (Keshavarz and Austic, 2004). Our results are consistent with those demonstrating that a high CP diet significantly improved HU. This improvement is mechanistically linked to the role of dietary protein in supporting albumin protein synthesis. Albumen height and HU reflect the viscosity of the thick albumen, which depends on the structural integrity of ovomucin-lysozyme complexes stabilized by disulfide bonds (Kakhki et al., 2016). Adequate crude protein intake ensures sufficient supply of sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine and cysteine) necessary for these disulfide linkages, while lysine contributes to the overall protein content and gel structure of the albumen (Kakhki et al., 2016).

The range of Lys levels evaluated in this study (0.7% to 0.9%) corresponds to digestible lysine intakes of approximately 640 to 820 mg/hen/day, assuming average daily feed intake of 91.4 g. These values are comparable to the digestible lysine requirements reported for brown-egg laying hens during peak production. Spangler et al. (2019) determined that 706 to 778 mg digestible lysine/hen/day was required for maximal egg mass and feed efficiency in Lohmann LSL-Lite hens from 22 to 47 weeks of age. Similarly, Kakhki et al. (2016) reported that increasing dietary digestible lysine from 0.62% to 0.86% (approximately 650 to 900 mg/hen/day) linearly improved egg production and egg mass in Hy-Line W-36 hens. The significant improvements in EW and EM observed in the present study with increasing Lys from 0.7% to 0.9% are consistent with these reported requirements. Regarding egg quality, Azzam et al. (2017) demonstrated that Thr supplementation in low-CP diets (14.16% CP) improved laying performance and intestinal immunity in Lohmann Brown hens during peak production, supporting the concept that adequate amino acid balance is critical when dietary protein is reduced. The present findings extend these observations by demonstrating that high-CP diets (17%) improved HU, whereas Lys level primarily affected production performance parameters rather than egg quality traits, highlighting the differential effects of dietary protein and individual amino acids on egg quality versus production traits.

Energy supply is critical for both egg production performance and reproductive function of laying hens (Mishra et al., 2020;Rama et al., 2023). It is well-established that egg production performance is highly dependent on the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, which regulates the synthesis and secretion of sex hormones (Peng et al., 2024). Follicular development is the fundamental physiological process underlying egg production, initiated and progressed by hormones secreted by the hypothalamus and pituitary (Hanlon et al., 2021). Lu et al. (2021) found that reducing dietary energy levels delayed ovarian development, decreased the number of hierarchical follicles, and reduced egg production. Our results corroborate those. This suggests that providing a higher ME level during the early sexual maturity phase is more conducive to ovarian development, increasing the number of ovarian follicles and thereby enhancing egg production performance. The interaction between energy and protein supply extends to follicular development through their combined effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis and hepatic yolk precursor synthesis. Concurrently, adequate protein supply, particularly lysine, is required for hepatic synthesis of vitellogenin and VLDLy, the primary yolk precursors that accumulate in developing follicles during the rapid yolk deposition phase (Walzem et al., 1999). Walzem et al. (1999) demonstrated that estrogen induces the assembly of VLDLy in the liver of egg-laying hens, a process that requires coordinated inputs of both energy (for VLDL assembly and secretion) and amino acids (for VTG and apolipoprotein synthesis). Ruan et al. (2019) further showed that dietary lysine promotes this process by upregulating ApoVLDL-II expression and enhancing PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation, thereby providing energy substrates for yolk precursor synthesis. When dietary energy is inadequate, the liver prioritizes energy-dependent maintenance functions over yolk precursor synthesis, reducing VTG and VLDL production and consequently limiting follicular growth regardless of amino acid availability. Conversely, when protein or lysine is limiting, VLDL particles may lack sufficient apolipoprotein components for proper assembly, impairing yolk lipid deposition even when energy supply is adequate. This interdependence underscores the importance of a balanced energy-protein supply for optimal follicular development and egg production.

Walzem et al. (2014) proposed that obesity resulting from energy excess can cause reproductive dysfunction of female birds. However, the results of this study showed that the AF% of hens fed the low-MEn diet was significantly lower compared to those fed the medium-MEn group, because dietary MEn deficiency failed to meet production requirements. This observation reflects the metabolic priority of energy allocation in laying hens. A possible explanation is that energy allocation in laying hens prioritizes egg production; high ME intake may be utilized to increase egg production or yolk synthesis efficiency, along with promoting fat deposition. Concurrently, the hens' regulatory mechanisms for energy balance (e.g., spontaneous reduction of feed intake or adaptive increase of basal metabolic rate) might further limit AF accumulation.

At the molecular level, energy-protein interactions regulate adiposity through hepatic lipid metabolism genes. Hu et al. (2024) reported that an imbalanced energy-to-protein ratio (0.74 to 0.80) in low-protein diets upregulated genes related to fatty acid synthesis, including stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA), while simultaneously downregulating genes involved in fatty acid beta-oxidation such as acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase trifunctional multienzyme complex subunit alpha (HADHA), enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase (EHHADH), and acetyl-CoA acyltransferase 1 (ACAA1). This coordinated gene expression pattern promotes triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes and increases abdominal fat deposition. The concurrent activation of lipogenesis and suppression of beta-oxidation represents a maladaptive metabolic response to energy excess relative to protein, which may be mediated by the sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) pathway, a master regulator of hepatic lipogenesis that responds to both insulin signaling and cellular energy status (Horton et al., 2002). These findings provide mechanistic insights into how dietary energy-protein imbalance influences body composition in laying hens.

Jiang et al. (2013) demonstrated that high-ME diets reduce BMD and tibial quality of laying hens, potentially linked to fat deposition and calcium metabolism disorders induced by ME excess. In this study, the TBS of hens fed the medium-MEn diet was significantly higher than those fed the high-MEn diet, further supporting the view that high-ME diets negatively impact skeletal quality of laying hens.

Based on previous research, the FTO, a key regulator of energy balance, has polymorphisms confirmed to influence fat deposition through processes like adipocyte differentiation and RNA methylation (Fontanesi et al., 2009; Loos et al., 2014). This study found that a high-Lys diet significantly suppressed hepatic FTO gene expression of laying hens. Concurrently, hens fed the high-Lys diet showed significantly higher EW and daily EM than those fed the low-Lys diet. These results suggest that a high-Lys diet may enhance egg production performance by down- regulating hepatic FTO expression, thereby optimizing energy allocation towards production.

The lysine-mediated suppression of FTO expression and its association with enhanced egg production performance may be linked to the mTOR signaling pathway and hepatic energy sensing mechanisms. FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated protein) functions as an N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA demethylase that regulates gene expression post-transcriptionally, influencing adipogenesis, energy expenditure, and lipid metabolism (Jia et al., 2011). Recent evidence indicates that amino acid availability, particularly lysine, modulates mTORC1 activity which in turn regulates metabolic processes (Sengupta et al., 2010). By suppressing FTO expression, high lysine levels may reduce m6A demethylation of mRNAs encoding lipogenic enzymes, thereby attenuating hepatic lipogenesis and promoting the partitioning of dietary energy toward egg production rather than body fat deposition. Furthermore, lysine regulates hepatic lipid metabolism through its effects on apolipoprotein synthesis. Ruan et al. (2019) demonstrated that increasing dietary lysine levels in laying duck breeders linearly upregulated the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), and very-low-density apolipoprotein-II (ApoVLDL-II), while reducing hepatic triglyceride and cholesterol contents. PPARα is a master regulator of fatty acid oxidation, and its upregulation by lysine enhances mitochondrial and peroxisomal beta-oxidation of fatty acids in the liver, thereby reducing hepatic lipid accumulation and potentially increasing the availability of energy substrates for egg production. The upregulation of ApoVLDL-II is particularly relevant to yolk formation, as this apolipoprotein is a specific component of VLDL particles that are targeted to ovarian follicles for yolk lipid deposition. Therefore, lysine appears to exert dual metabolic effects: suppressing lipogenesis through FTO downregulation while enhancing fatty acid oxidation and yolk precursor synthesis through PPARα pathway activation, collectively optimizing the hepatic metabolic profile for sustained egg production.

Response surface analysis in this study revealed significant interactions among key dietary nutrients, primarily driven by synergistic and balancing effects. The MEn × CP interaction: this study found a highly significant interactive effect of dietary MEn and CP levels on hen-day egg production and plasma albumin. This concurs with Sell et al. (1985), who reported significant interactions between ME and protein concentration on performance and carcass characteristics of turkeys. However, Kleyn and Ciacciariello (2023) reported no significant interaction between dietary protein and energy levels, differing from our findings and those mentioned above.

Lysine is the first limiting amino acid in corn-soybean meal diets for laying hens, with approximately 60% of consumed lysine deposited in egg proteins, primarily ovalbumin which constitutes 54% of egg white. High dietary lysine optimizes amino acid balance, increasing protein utilization efficiency for egg synthesis. Additionally, lysine stimulates IGF-1 secretion, which promotes ovarian follicle development and egg production (Peng et al., 2024). The significant MEn × CP interaction observed in this study can be explained by the energy-protein balance concept. Energy and protein requirements are interdependent: insufficient energy limits protein utilization for egg production, while excessive energy relative to protein leads to fat deposition rather than egg output. Our results suggest an optimal energy-protein ratio of 158 kcal MEn per 1% CP for Jingfen No.6 hens, slightly higher than recommended for some commercial strains (Kleyn and Ciacciariello, 2023), likely due to genetic differences in nutrient utilization efficiency.

The statistically predicted optimal dietary combination from the present study (MEn = 2,688 kcal/kg, CP = 17.0%, Lys = 0.90%) provides a useful reference for precision feeding of Jingfen laying hens during peak production. The lower MEn requirement likely reflects the specific metabolic characteristics of the Jingfen breed, which may have lower energy requirements than commercial Leghorn-type hens. Conversely, the higher CP and Lys requirements suggest that indigenous breeds such as Jingfen may have greater amino acid needs per unit of egg output, possibly due to differences in feed efficiency and protein utilization. This observation aligns with findings by Geng et al. (2022), who reported that dietary ME and CP levels affect performance and biochemical parameters differently in dual-purpose chickens compared with commercial layer strains. The discrepancy between the present results and the NRC (1994) standards underscores the necessity of breed-specific nutrient requirement databases, as the NRC values were primarily derived from studies using commercial White Leghorn-type hens nearly three decades ago and may not accurately reflect the nutritional needs of modern indigenous breeds with different genetic backgrounds and production potentials.

Conclusions

High-MEn diets enhance egg production performance. High-Lys diets significantly increase EM and improve egg quality by regulating protein metabolism (elevating plasma total protein), and suppressing hepatic FTO gene expression, along with promoting skeletal development (increasing tibia density/breaking strength). Pairwise combinations of MEn and CP, MEn and Lys, and CP and Lys significantly impacted multiple indicators, including hen-day egg production and hierarchical follicle weight. Contour plots predominantly exhibited elliptical shapes, indicating strong interactions. Based on response surface methodology integrating all indicators, the statistically predicted optimal dietary levels for Jingfen laying hens during the laying period were approximately MEn = 2688 kcal/kg, CP = 17.0%, and Lys = 0.90%, with corresponding predicted daily intake requirements of MEn = 245 kcal, CP = 15.5 g, and Lys = 0.82 g. Predicted outcomes included: daily EM = 46.1 g; F/EM = 1.99; EW = 50.26 g; HU = 80.1. These model-derived estimates should be regarded as provisional guidance supported by the current experimental conditions and response surface framework, acknowledging that RSM provides statistical approximations subject to model assumptions and experimental design constraints. Biological validation across diverse production environments is warranted before wider application.
