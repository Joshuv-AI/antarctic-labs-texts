Pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection burden in G20 countries in 2023
Authors: Yan Guo, Yonghan Luo, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of childhood lower respiratory infections, but updated G20 burden estimates are lacking. Using the Global Burden of Disease 2023 database, we analyzed deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for RSV-associated lower respiratory infections in children aged 0–14 years across G20 members from 1990 to 2023, with stratification by sex, age, and country. In 2023, an estimated 5,373 deaths and 483,162 DALYs occurred, with mortality and DALY rates having declined by ∼5.5% annually since 1990. The COVID-19 pandemic drove burden to a historic low in 2021, but a marked rebound followed in 2022–2023, though rates remained below pre-pandemic levels. Infants aged <1 year bore the heaviest burden (mortality 8.688 per 100,000), and India ranked highest, whereas Australia ranked lowest. Burden strongly correlated inversely with Sociodemographic Index. These findings highlight the need for precision prevention, targeting the youngest children and lower-resource G20 members.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infections among children worldwide. Belonging to the Pneumoviridae family, RSV is highly contagious and primarily transmitted via respiratory droplets and direct contact. In temperate regions, seasonal epidemics typically peak during winter and early spring. Although RSV can infect individuals across all age groups, infants, older adults, and immunocompromised populations are most susceptible to severe disease. Most children experience at least one RSV infection by the age of 2 years, with the primary infection often being the most severe, manifesting as clinical presentations ranging from mild upper respiratory symptoms to severe bronchiolitis, pneumonia, and even respiratory failure.1,2,3

Globally, the epidemiological characteristics, seasonal patterns, and disease burden of RSV have remained intensive research foci. Previous investigations have primarily concentrated on age distribution and disease severity.4,5,6,7 Nevertheless, existing studies on RSV disease burden exhibit substantial limitations. Most analyses include data only up to 2021 or earlier, thus failing to capture the dramatic epidemiological shifts during the post-pandemic era.8,9 Non-pharmaceutical interventions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic drove RSV transmission to historic lows; however, following the relaxation of containment measures, many countries and regions witnessed a vigorous resurgence in 2022–2023, accompanied by altered seasonal patterns and increased clinical severity.4,7,10

Furthermore, the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD), led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, systematically quantifies mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and other burden metrics across diverse countries, regions, ages, sexes, and years, thereby providing a scientific foundation for global health policy-making.11 Although the GBD has regularly published estimates of RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections, its analytical population encompasses all age groups and does not specifically focus on the pediatric population, which bears the most severe RSV consequences.12 Previous studies, such as Zhao et al.13 utilized GBD 1990–2021 data to describe the all-age RSV burden, reporting 31,525 deaths and 2.59 million DALYs globally in 2021, with the burden concentrated predominantly in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa8; Lian et al.14 further revealed an age-polarized shift of RSV burden toward older adults.9 However, none of these studies provided updated global pediatric RSV burden estimates covering the peak pandemic period and subsequent years (2022–2023), a critical time frame during which RSV epidemiological patterns likely underwent profound changes.

The G20 (Group of Twenty) comprises 19 countries and the European Union, representing roughly two-thirds of the world’s population and more than 80% of global GDP. These nations encompass a wide spectrum of economic development, health system capacities, and epidemiological profiles, making them a crucial focus for understanding RSV burden and guiding policy. Therefore, this study leveraged the most recent GBD 2023 database to systematically evaluate the burden of RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in children within G20 countries from 1990 to 2023. We describe the global and regional mortality landscape in 2023; analyze temporal trends from 1990 to 2023; delineate disparities across regions, countries, age groups, and sexes; explore the correlation between the Sociodemographic Index (SDI) and disease burden. The findings aim to provide evidence-based support for optimizing pediatric RSV prevention and control strategies in the post-pandemic era, as well as for the precise deployment of vaccines and monoclonal antibodies.

Results

Disease burden of pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in G20 in 2023

In 2023, the estimated number of deaths due to pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in G20 countries was 5,373.225 (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 3,715.553–7,862.622), corresponding to a mortality rate of 0.556 per 100,000 children (95% UI: 0.385–0.814). The DALYs were 483,161.884 (95% UI: 334,432.415–707,829.900), with a DALY rate of 50.020 per 100,000 (95% UI: 34.622–73.279). By sex, the mortality rate was 0.546 per 100,000 in males and 0.568 per 100,000 in females (Table 1) To further characterize the trajectory, we calculated segmented estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) for three distinct periods. The mortality rate declined with an EAPC of −4.06% (95% confidence interval [CI]: −4.17 to −3.94) during 1990–2019, followed by a sharp decline of −44.38% (95% CI: −98.37 to 1800.37) during 2019–2021, and then an increase with an EAPC of +13.54% (95% CI: −18.31 to 57.79) during 2021–2023. The DALY rate showed similar patterns, with EAPCs of −4.05% (95% CI: −4.17 to −3.94), −44.33% (95% CI: −98.37 to 1804.56), and +13.54% (95% CI: −18.65 to 58.47) across the three periods, respectively. These short-segment EAPC estimates are based on only three annual observations each and are presented as conditional point-estimate summaries; the wide CIs reflect substantial uncertainty and should be interpreted with caution. Discussion

This study, based on the GBD 2023 database, analyzed the burden of pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in G20 countries from 1990 to 2023. The findings revealed that the number of deaths and DALYs decreased substantially from 45,619.852 to 4,091,464.436 in 1990 to 5,373.225 and 483,161.884 in 2023, respectively. During the COVID-19 pandemic period (2020–2021), RSV-related disease burden reached a historic nadir but rebounded sharply from the pandemic-era nadir in 2022–2023, though the 2023 rates remained well below pre-pandemic levels. Age-stratified analyses demonstrated that infants aged <1 year bore the greatest burden and exhibited a rapid declining rate. Substantial country-level disparities were observed, with India carrying the heaviest burden. Moreover, SDI showed a strong inverse correlation with disease burden across G20 nations. These findings may provide updated evidence to inform the optimization of pediatric RSV prevention and control strategies and the targeted use of vaccines and monoclonal antibodies in the post-pandemic era.

The present study observed an atypical fluctuation pattern of pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection burden, characterized by a precipitous decline followed by a notable increase after 2019. Between 2020 and 2021, global RSV transmission fell to historic lows, a trend closely associated with the widespread implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions.4 Measures such as mask mandates, closure of public venues, and travel restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic profoundly disrupted RSV transmission chains, driving RSV detection rates to near-zero levels. This phenomenon was not unique to RSV. Multiple studies have shown that other respiratory pathogens, including influenza virus and parainfluenza virus, also experienced substantial declines in transmission during non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPI) implementation.15,16,17 However, unlike influenza and certain other viruses, RSV exhibited a more pronounced resurgence upon relaxation of NPIs. During 2022–2023, RSV activity in many countries and regions markedly exceeded pre-pandemic levels, a phenomenon termed “immune debt.”8,9 To quantify this descriptive increase within G20 countries, we compared mortality and DALY rates at three time points (Tables S2 and S3): the mortality rate declined by 69.0% from 2019 (1.395/100,000) to 2021 (0.432/100,000), then rebounded by 28.7% to 0.556/100,000 in 2023, with the most pronounced increases observed in Indonesia, Turkey, and Brazil, suggesting a substantial resurgence across G20 members following the relaxation of pandemic restrictions.

The notable increase in RSV activity during 2022–2023 carries important implications. First, epidemiological surveillance systems must maintain high sensitivity to enable early warnings of such changes. Second, clinicians should remain vigilant regarding potential changes in the clinical presentation of RSV infection, particularly rising trends in intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and the need for mechanical ventilation.

Our study found a marked age-specific distribution of pediatric RSV disease burden. Infants aged <1 year bore the highest burden, and younger age was associated with heavier burden. The propensity for severe RSV infection during early childhood may involve two key mechanisms. Anatomically, infants have narrow bronchial lumina and weak cartilaginous support, predisposing them to airway obstruction following mucous secretion. Immunologically, the infant immune system remains immature, with incompletely developed innate and adaptive immune responses.18,19

Based on established external evidence, certain infant groups should be prioritized for severe RSV screening and early intervention, particularly those aged <3 months, preterm and low-birth-weight infants, and those with underlying conditions such as congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, or neuromuscular disorders, as these are well-documented high-risk factors for severe RSV infection.9 During peak RSV seasons, pediatric emergency departments and outpatient clinics should enhance active screening of these high-risk infants.

Our study also revealed marked heterogeneity in the burden of pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections across G20 countries, with India, Indonesia, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa carrying the highest burdens. Zhao et al.,13 using GBD 2021 data, reported that the global burden was concentrated in low-SDI regions, a pattern that is also reflected within the G20, where lower-SDI members bear disproportionately higher disease burdens. The mechanisms underlying these disparities are multidimensional. First, in lower-resource settings within the G20, such as parts of India, Indonesia, and South Africa, inadequate primary healthcare facilities, shortages of trained personnel, and limited respiratory support equipment likely prevent many children with RSV from receiving timely and effective treatment. Second, malnutrition may compromise children’s mucosal barrier function and immune responses. Third, high population density, along with air and environmental pollution, increases RSV transmission risk. From a policy perspective, priority interventions should focus on the highest-burden G20 countries, particularly India, Indonesia, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa, while also addressing the needs of underserved populations within higher-income member states.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis demonstrated a significant inverse relationship between SDI and pediatric RSV disease burden, consistent with previous findings using GBD 2019 data, which also reported that lower-SDI regions carry the highest RSV burden. The advantages of high-SDI regions include well-established immunization programs, good nutritional status, advanced medical equipment, and highly trained personnel. These factors collectively create a protective network that shields children from severe RSV infection.

Our findings have clear implications for the precise implementation of RSV prevention strategies. First, in high-SDI regions, where the RSV burden is already low, prevention efforts should focus on further reducing hospitalization rates, including promoting routine neonatal administration of nirsevimab and enhancing targeted prevention for high-risk infants. Studies have shown that long-acting monoclonal antibodies can achieve an overall effectiveness of 79% against RSV hospitalization in children aged <24 months.20 Second, in middle-SDI regions, prevention priorities should consolidate existing immunization programs while establishing robust RSV surveillance networks. Third, in low-SDI regions, there is an urgent need to strengthen primary health care systems, improve nutritional status, and seek affordable and convenient RSV prevention options.

In conclusion, from 1990 to 2023, the disease burden of pediatric RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections declined substantially, with a descriptive increase observed from the 2021 nadir to 2023, although 2023 rates remained below pre-pandemic levels. The disease burden is highly concentrated in low-SDI regions and among children <1 year. There is a significant inverse correlation between pediatric RSV burden and SDI. Given the characteristics of age polarization and SDI correlation, RSV prevention and control efforts should adhere to the principle of precision stratification, prioritizing protection of the most vulnerable infant populations and the most resource-constrained regions.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the GBD database estimates rely on modeling approaches, and the quality and completeness of source data vary considerably across regions. Especially in low- and middle-income countries, the health information registration systems in many of these nations are not well developed, which may lead to deviations in the estimation of disease burdens in these regions. The national-level Spearman correlation analysis among G20 countries did not adjust for potential non-independence of data points, which may slightly affect the precision of the estimates. However, the limited number and geographic diversity of G20 members reduce the clustering effect. Second, because the latest data in the GBD 2023 database cover up to 2023, this study could not further analyze potential additional rebounds or seasonal pattern changes in RSV during 2024–2025. Future research should incorporate updated data to validate whether the trends observed in this study persist. Third, this study focused primarily on mortality and DALY burden of RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections and did not include the burden of outpatient or milder RSV infections, which are essential for comprehensively assessing the impact of RSV and formulating health policies. BAPC projections are statistical extrapolations based on pre-pandemic trends, not forecasts of interventions. Fourth, the overall EAPC masks the pandemic structural break; we therefore calculated segmented EAPCs, and the SDI analysis is ecological and descriptive, not causal. All secondary analyses used GBD point estimates, so uncertainty intervals are conditional on these estimates.

This study involved no experimental models or human participants. All analyses were conducted on de-identified, aggregated secondary data from the GBD 2023 database.

Method details

Data extraction

We extracted deaths and DALYs for RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infections in children aged 0–14 years in G20 countries from GBD 2023 (years 1990–2023). Extraction parameters: Etiology = “Respiratory syncytial virus”; Cause = “Lower respiratory infections”; Measure = “Deaths” and “DALYs”; Metric = “Number”, “Percent”, and “Rate”; Age = five subgroups (<1 year, 12–23 months, 2–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years); Sex = Male, Female, Both. G20 aggregate estimates were extracted directly from the GBD Results Tool as the pre-defined “G20” location aggregate.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3. Mortality and DALY rates per 100,000 children were calculated with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Temporal trends were assessed using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), calculated via log-linear regression: log(rate) = α + β × year + ε, where EAPC = 100 × (eˆβ − 1), with rates weighted by inverse variance. The 95% CIs for EAPC were derived from the standard error of β. For the post-2019 period, segmented EAPCs were calculated for 1990–2019, 2019–2021, and 2021–2023 as conditional point estimates; wide CIs reflect limited observations and should be interpreted cautiously. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the association between SDI and disease burden at the country level (n = 19, 2023 cross-section), with ρ and P-values reported. Rate change from 1990 to 2023 was calculated as (Rate2023 − Rate1990)/Rate1990, with 95% UIs from GBD draws. All tests were two-sided; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The EAPC and Spearman analyses used GBD point estimates rather than matched draws.
