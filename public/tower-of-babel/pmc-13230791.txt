Bulk and position-specific isotopic compositions of natural gas in the Permian Jiamuhe Formation, Zhongguai Uplift, Junggar Basin, China
Authors: Tao Wu, Wenqi Yan, Heyuan Wu, Weitao Wu, Taotao Jing, Hailei Liu, Hechong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the genetic types and accumulation characteristics of natural gas from the Permian Jiamuhe Formation in the Zhongguai Uplift, Junggar Basin, through an integrated geochemical analysis of 23 natural gas samples. The analytical techniques included chemical composition analysis, stable carbon (δ13C) and hydrogen (δ2H) isotope analysis of individual alkanes (C1–C4), and position-specific carbon isotope analysis of propane. The results indicate that the natural gases are predominantly dry gas, with an average dryness coefficient (C1/(C1–C5)) of 0.97 and methane content ranging from 75.23% to 95.98%. The stable carbon isotopic compositions generally show an increasing trend from methane to propane, although many samples exhibit partial C2–C3 isotopic rollover, reflecting complex isotopic behavior typical of thermogenic gases. Critically, the ethane carbon isotopic values (δ13C2: − 27.5‰ to − 24.6‰) are predominantly heavier than − 28‰, suggesting a coal-derived origin. Plotting on a Bernard diagram confirms their classification within the thermogenic gas field. The position-specific isotope data of propane reveals a consistent isotopic difference between the methylene and methyl carbon, indicating formation primarily via the n-propyl pathway during kerogen cracking, with no clear isotopic evidence for significant alteration. The study concludes that the natural gas in the Zhongguai Uplift is mainly coal-derived thermogenic gas generated from Type III kerogen, with its accumulation controlled by the interplay of hydrocarbon kitchens, fault-fracture migration pathways, and favorable reservoir conditions in the Jiamuhe Formation.Keywords: Zhongguai uplift, Junggar Basin, Natural gas geochemistry, Coal-derived gas, Position-specific isotope, Propane formation pathwaySubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The Zhongguai Uplift, located on the northwestern margin of the Junggar Basin, is a long-term inherited paleo-uplift. Natural gas accumulations within this uplift exhibit multi-source composite origins and significant genetic complexity1. The natural gases primarily consist of coal-type gas, oil-type gas, and mixed gases derived from both, with key geochemical indicators serving as the principal means of differentiation2. The distribution of these different genetic types of natural gas displays an orderly pattern both laterally and vertically, primarily controlled by the location of hydrocarbon kitchens and the migration pathways provided by the transport system3. In summary, the natural gas in the Zhongguai Uplift is typically of multi-source composite origin.

The bulk carbon isotope composition of individual alkanes is commonly employed to identify the origin and genetic types of natural gas. However, the interpretation based on a single geochemical parameter often carries inherent ambiguities. In recent years, with advancements in analytical techniques, position-specific carbon isotope analysis of propane has emerged as a powerful tool for fine-scale tracing of natural gas formation processes, secondary alterations, and precursor material structures4–10. In parallel, recent developments in optical sensing technologies combined with machine learning have significantly improved the capability for wide-range detection and quantification of C1–C5 hydrocarbons in complex gas systems, providing new opportunities for high-resolution gas compositional analysis in petroleum applications11. This approach examines the carbon isotope differences between the methyl (terminal) and methylene (central) positions in propane. Various analytical methods have been developed and applied by researchers to determine the position-specific carbon isotope distribution of propane, primarily including conversion into acetone and acetic acid, followed by GC–C–IRMS (gas chromatography-combustion-isotope ratio mass spectrometry)4; GC-pyrolysis-GC-C-IRMS5; quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)6; and fragmentation of 1-, 2-carbon ions in high-resolution IRMS7.

Therefore, in this study, systematic analyses were conducted on the collected natural gas samples from the Zhongguai Uplift, including chemical composition, bulk carbon isotope composition of individual alkanes, and position-specific carbon isotope composition of propane. On this basis, this study aims not simply to constrain the genetic type of natural gas, which has been broadly established for the Zhongguai Uplift, but to further evaluate the added value of position-specific carbon isotope analysis of propane. Specifically, this approach is used to (i) constrain propane formation pathways (n- versus iso-propyl), (ii) assess potential secondary alteration processes that are difficult to resolve using bulk isotopic compositions alone, and (iii) refine genetic interpretations by providing intramolecular-scale constraints beyond conventional geochemical indicators.

Geological settings

The Junggar Basin is a large superimposed intracontinental basin located in northwestern China, bounded by the Altai orogenic belt to the north, the Tianshan orogenic belt to the south, and the West Junggar orogenic belt to the west12. It experienced multiple tectonic events from the Late Paleozoic to the Cenozoic, resulting in a complex structural framework composed of uplifts and depressions13. The Zhongguai Uplift is situated in the northwestern part of the basin (Fig. 1a) and represents a long-term inherited paleouplift initiated during the Late Carboniferous–Early Permian14. It is structurally bounded by the Hongche fault zone to the west and is adjacent to the Mahu Depression to the north and the Shawan Depression to the south. Owing to its relatively stable tectonic evolution and persistent structural high, the Zhongguai Uplift has played an important role in controlling hydrocarbon migration and accumulation.

(a) Tectonic framework of the Zhongguai Uplift, Junggar Basin, China. (b) Stratigraphic architecture of the Permian Jiamuhe Formation.

The Permian Jiamuhe Formation was deposited during the Early Permian, corresponding to a tectono-sedimentary transition from post-orogenic extension to a relatively stable intracontinental depression stage in the Junggar Basin15,16. In the Zhongguai Uplift, the Jiamuhe Formation is widely developed and is mainly composed of conglomerates, sandstones, siltstones, and mudstones, indicating predominantly fluvial to fan-delta depositional environments (Fig. 1b)16. The formation is characterized by rapid lateral facies changes and frequent vertical lithologic alternations, reflecting strong sediment supply and variable hydrodynamic conditions. Multiple stages of tectonic reactivation led to the development of faults and fractures, which enhanced reservoir heterogeneity and provided effective migration pathways for natural gas. Combined with favorable reservoir properties and proximity to effective source rocks, the Jiamuhe Formation in the Zhongguai Uplift constitutes an important target for natural gas accumulation.

Samples and methods

Samples

In this study, seven natural gas samples were newly collected from wells in the Zhongguai Uplift using double-valve sampling cylinders and were analyzed for chemical composition, bulk carbon and hydrogen isotopes, and position-specific carbon isotope compositions of propane. In addition, compositional and bulk alkane carbon isotope data for 16 previously collected natural gas samples from the same study area were obtained from the PetroChina Xinjiang Oilfield Company database and published reports (see Table 1 for sample identification). These additional samples were not analyzed for position-specific isotopes in the present study but are included to provide regional compositional and isotopic context. The position-specific isotope analyses were conducted on a limited number of representative samples, and thus the results primarily reflect sample-scale characteristics rather than full regional variability.

Methods

Chemical composition

The chemical compositions of inorganic gases and C1–C3 hydrocarbons were determined using a Finnigan MAT 271 mass spectrometer, which provides high-precision quantification for light gaseous components. For C3 + hydrocarbons, analyses were conducted using a gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) to ensure improved chromatographic separation and quantification of heavier hydrocarbons. Separate analytical systems were used to optimize measurements across different molecular weight ranges. Calibration was performed using certified gas standards, and analytical precision for hydrocarbon components was better than 0.01%17.

Compound-specific isotope composition

Carbon and hydrogen isotopic compositions of natural gases were determined using a Thermo Finnigan Delta Plus XP isotope ratio mass spectrometer coupled to a Hewlett–Packard (HP) 5890II Gas Chromatograph. For carbon isotope analysis, the GC oven temperature was initially increased to 80 °C at a heating rate of 8 °C/min, then increased to 260 °C at 10 °C/min and maintained at this temperature for 10 min. Individual gaseous hydrocarbons separated by a capillary column (Plot Q, 30 m×0.32 mm i.d.) were combusted to CO2 and analyzed by IRMS. The δ13C values are reported relative to V-PDB in permil (‰). For hydrogen isotope analysis, the GC oven temperature was initially set to 40 °C for 4 min, then progressively increased to 80 °C at 10 °C/min, 140 °C at 5 °C/min and 260 °C at 30 °C/min. Individual gaseous hydrocarbons were converted to H2 before entering the IRMS for δ2H measurement. The δ2H values are reported relative to V-SMOW in permil (‰).

Position-specific isotope composition

Position-specific carbon isotope composition of the propane from natural gases was measured by GC-Pyrolysis-GC-C-IRMS method following Gilbert et al.. The natural gases were separated by the first GC column (HP-PLOT-Q, 30 m × 0.32 mm i.d., 10 μm film thickness). The temperature of the GC oven was initially set to 50 °C for 3 min, increased to 200 °C at 10 °C/min, and maintained at that temperature for 8 min. The separated compounds were transferred to a furnace at 790 °C for thermal pyrolysis and the reactor was made of a ceramic tube (500 mm long, 1.5 mm outer diameter and 0.7 mm ID). The pyrolysis fragments were separated by a second GC column (ZKAT-PLOT Al2O3/S, 30 m×0.32 mm i.d., 15 μm film thickness) with a constant oven temperature of 30 °C. The separated pyrolysis fragments were introduced into a GC-Isolink II for combustion at 1000 °C and the product CO2 was carried to a Thermo Scientific MAT 253 Plus IRMS for δ13C measurement. In the GC-Pyrolysis-GC-C-IRMS system, high purity helium gas was used as a carrier gas, and individual devices were linked via deactivated fused silica capillary columns (0.25 mm i.d.). Notably, Gilbert et al. observed that the pyrolysis products methane originates from the methyl group of propane, the ethane originates from the assemble of two methyl groups of propane during the pyrolysis, whereas ethylene originates from both the methyl and methylene groups during propane cracking. Based on this, the position-specific isotopic compositions of propane in natural gas samples can be calculated from different pyrolysis products. In this study, the measurement precisions of the position-specific carbon isotope compositions of propane are within ± 1.0‰18.

Results

Chemical compositions

Characteristics of hydrocarbon gases.

Characteristics of hydrocarbon gases.

The chemical composition characteristics of natural gas from the Zhongguai Uplift in the Junggar Basin are presented in Table 1. Overall, methane is the dominant component, with an average concentration of 91.26%, ranging from 75.23% to 95.98%. Ethane, as the primary heavy hydrocarbon component, has an average content of 1.82%, varying between 0.88% and 2.80%. The average propane content is 0.83%, within a range of 0.17% to 1.42%. The content of each component decreases significantly with increasing carbon number: iso-butane averages 0.20% (0.04–0.70%), n-butane 0.24% (0.04–0.57%), iso-pentane 0.06% (0.01–0.17%), and n-pentane 0.11% (0.00–0.36%). In summary, the natural gas samples are typical dry gas, characterized by high methane content, low concentrations of heavy hydrocarbons, and certain variability in component concentrations, particularly evident in methane and nitrogen.

The dryness coefficient (C1/(C1–C5) of natural gas is a key indicator for identifying its genetic type and thermal maturity19. Generally, the dryness coefficient (C1/(C1–C5) exceeds 0.99 for biogenic gas. For thermogenic natural gas, the dryness coefficient (C1/(C1–C5) increases with advancing maturity20. The dryness coefficient (C1/(C1–C5) of natural gas is commonly used as a descriptive parameter reflecting gas composition and, to some extent, thermal maturity19. However, dryness can also be influenced by migration fractionation, phase separation, leakage, and secondary processes20. Therefore, it does not independently constrain gas origin or cracking mechanisms. In this study, the average dryness coefficient of 0.97 indicates a methane-dominated dry gas composition, which is consistent with thermogenic gas characteristics when considered together with isotopic evidence, and should therefore not be regarded as an independent genetic indicator, but only interpreted in combination with isotopic constraints.

Characteristics of non-hydrocarbon gases.

Non-hydrocarbon gases in natural gas primarily include N2, CO2, H2, He, etc., and constitute an important component of natural gas. The sources of N2 are mainly threefold21,22: (1) Atmospheric nitrogen: Released from atmospheric nitrogen trapped by saturated water bodies during sedimentation or entering reservoir rocks from atmospheric nitrogen in groundwater within sediment pore waters. Generally, nitrogen associated with natural gas in young source rocks with shallow burial depths, within the range of groundwater activity, is primarily atmospheric. (2) Organically derived nitrogen: Formed during the biochemical alteration or thermal decomposition of nitrogen-containing organic compounds in sediments/rocks or nitrogen-containing compounds in petroleum. This is the main source of nitrogen in hydrocarbon-associated natural gas. (3) Magmatic nitrogen: Nitrogen released during magmatic activity. This type of nitrogen is commonly found in sealed fractures and pores of igneous rocks and can also migrate from volcanic areas to accumulate in sedimentary rocks. The N2 content in the natural gas samples collected from the samples in this study is generally low overall. However, three samples (ZJ904-1, ZJ904-2, and GH202) exhibit relatively high N2 content (average 21.22%). The overall distribution range of N2 is from 0.54% to 23.30%, with an average value of 5.27%.

Besides N2, CO2 is another important inorganic component in natural gas. The origins of CO2 are divided into two major categories: organic and inorganic. Inorganic carbon dioxide further has two sources: rock chemical reactions and magmatic origins, as detailed below: (1) Organic origin: Carbon dioxide is generated throughout all processes of sedimentary organic matter evolution during diagenesis. Significant CO2 can form from the biochemical degradation of organic matter; certain amounts can also be generated during the evolution of kerogen; additionally, the oxidation of crude oil and hydrocarbon gases can produce CO2. (2) Rock chemical reactions: Inorganically derived CO2 from rock reactions comes mainly from the thermal decomposition of carbonate rocks and reactions between inorganic minerals. Furthermore, reactions between carbonate rocks and various silicates can form epidote or chlorite while releasing CO2. (3) Magmatic origin: Magmatic CO2 is degassed from magmatic fluids during their ascent as temperature and pressure decrease. Geochemical studies indicate that CO2 is a primary form of mantle degassing into the upper crust and a product of magmatic degassing under high-temperature, high-oxygen fugacity conditions. The CO2 content in the collected natural gas samples is generally low, with an average content of 0.56%.

Discussion

Origins and genetic types of natural gas

Identification of genetic types of natural gas is fundamental for gas–source correlation and resource assessment2,23. Carbon and hydrogen isotopic compositions of individual alkane components have been widely applied as effective indicators for genetic discrimination of natural gas24. Based on extensive datasets from geological samples, previous studies established empirical relationships between vitrinite reflectance (Ro) of different source rocks and methane carbon isotopic compositions (δ13C1), which have been extensively used for gas-source correlation25. Moreover, distinct genetic types of natural gas are characterized by specific alkane carbon and hydrogen isotopic signatures, allowing integrated use of gas composition and isotopic parameters to constrain gas origin and formation mechanisms23. The systematic isotopic dataset obtained in this study provides a robust basis for genetic identification of natural gas in the Zhongguai Uplift.

Natural gas can be broadly classified into inorganic gas, organic gas, and mixed gas. Organic gas with economic significance can be further divided into oil-type gas generated from type I–IIA kerogen and coal-derived gas generated from type IIB–III kerogen, and can be subdivided into biogenic gas, transitional gas, thermogenic gas, and high-temperature cracking gas according to thermal maturity23. The Bernard diagram is commonly used to discriminate genetic types of natural gas, and the samples from the Zhongguai Uplift plot predominantly within the thermogenic gas field, indicating a dominant contribution from kerogen cracking (Fig. 4)26,27. The cross-plot of methane and ethane carbon isotopic compositions shows that the studied samples follow the isotopic evolution trend of type III kerogen cracking rather than that of type II kerogen (Fig. 523,28. In addition, most samples exhibit δ13C2 values higher than − 28‰, further suggesting a coal-derived gas origin. The methane carbon–hydrogen isotopic relationship indicates that the studied gases display isotopic characteristics comparable to typical coal-derived gases generated from terrestrial humic source rocks deposited in freshwater environments (Fig. 6)26. Furthermore, the relationship between methane hydrogen isotopic compositions and ethane carbon isotopic compositions clearly distinguishes the studied gases from oil-type gases and places them within the coal-type gas field (Fig. 7). Collectively, these results are consistent with a dominant contribution from coal-derived thermogenic gas likely generated from Type III kerogen. However, given potential isotopic overlap with high-maturity oil-associated gases and the absence of direct source rock petrographic constraints in this study, this interpretation should be regarded as geochemically consistent with a Type III kerogen source rather than as a uniquely diagnostic or definitive identification.

In addition to hydrocarbon components, several samples exhibit variable N2 contents (Table 1). Most samples show relatively low N2 concentrations, consistent with minor atmospheric or organic nitrogen contributions. However, a few samples display elevated N2 contents, which may reflect localized contributions from nitrogen released during thermal evolution of nitrogen-bearing organic matter or possible input associated with migration pathways. Given the absence of noble gas isotopic constraints in this study, a definitive distinction among atmospheric, organic, and deep-sourced nitrogen cannot be made. CO2 concentrations are generally low in the analyzed samples, suggesting limited contribution from carbonate decomposition or magmatic sources. The low CO2 contents are consistent with a dominantly thermogenic gas system without significant inorganic CO2 input.

Formation pathways of natural gas

Compared with bulk isotopic compositions, which mainly reflect integrated source and maturity signals, position-specific carbon isotope distributions provide additional constraints on reaction pathways at the molecular level, thereby offering a more process-oriented perspective on gas generation mechanisms. For the natural gas samples in this study, the position-specific carbon isotope distribution of propane falls within the range predicted by thermodynamic equilibrium models29, showing consistency with thermodynamic model predictions; however, this agreement does not imply that the system has reached equilibrium conditions. However, because temperature history, heating rate, and kerogen structural effects are not independently constrained in this study, this agreement does not uniquely confirm equilibrium control. During kerogen cracking, propane formation proceeds via two distinct pathways: the n-propyl pathway and the isopropyl pathway. These different formation pathways, due to variations in bond cleavage positions, yield propane molecules with distinct intramolecular carbon isotope distribution characteristics7,30. Generally, during carbon-carbon bond cleavage, the primary kinetic isotope effect (KIE) is exerted on the carbon atom directly involved in the bond breakage, while the secondary kinetic isotope effect on the adjacent carbon atom is comparatively smaller4,30. Consequently, in the n-propyl pathway for propane formation, the primary KIE occurs on the terminal methyl carbon, resulting in the central methylene carbon of the formed propane being isotopically heavier than the terminal methyl carbons. In contrast, in the isopropyl pathway, the primary KIE occurs on the central methylene carbon, leading to the terminal methyl carbons of the resulting propane being isotopically heavier than the central methylene carbon7. Figure 8 compares the position-specific carbon isotope composition of propane with the theoretical position-specific carbon isotope signatures of propane formed from different proportions of n-/iso-propyl sources. The relatively heavy carbon isotope composition of the central carbon may reflect secondary cracking effects; however, similar intramolecular isotope patterns can also arise during primary generation at high maturity. In the absence of quantitative comparison with experimentally derived data, this interpretation should be regarded as tentative and non-unique. Given the limited number of samples analyzed for position-specific isotopes, these results should be interpreted as indicative rather than regionally representative of the entire Zhongguai Uplift. Therefore, the observed intramolecular isotope signatures are consistent with a dominant contribution from the n-propyl pathway; however, alternative or mixed formation pathways, as well as possible evolutionary overprinting, cannot be excluded. In addition, recent studies have shown that kerogen structural heterogeneity and its interaction with the mineral matrix can exert significant control on hydrocarbon generation kinetics and isotopic characteristics, even within the same kerogen type31. This suggests that variations in maceral composition and organic–mineral associations may also influence the position-specific isotope signatures of propane. In the absence of detailed constraints on kerogen structure in the present study, such effects cannot be explicitly evaluated and may contribute to the observed isotopic variability.

Geochemical tracing of secondary alteration processes

Propane contains two distinct types of carbon atoms: methyl carbon atoms and methylene carbon atoms. Based on their differential reactivity in chemical reactions, the methyl carbon is classified as a primary carbon, whereas the methylene carbon is classified as a secondary carbon32. Studies on organic reaction mechanisms indicate that secondary carbon atoms (methylene carbon) exhibit higher reactivity than primary carbon atoms (methyl carbon) and are preferentially involved in chemical and biochemical processes33. This theoretical understanding has been corroborated by experimental research. For instance, Jaekel et al.32 conducted experiments on the anaerobic oxidation of propane by sulfate-reducing bacteria, demonstrating a significant preference and selectivity of these bacteria towards different carbon positions within propane. Their results confirmed that approximately 70% of the anaerobic oxidation occurred at the central carbon (methylene carbon), while only 30% took place at the terminal carbons (methyl carbons). Consequently, during microbial anaerobic oxidation of propane, particularly at low to moderate levels of oxidation, the bulk carbon isotope composition of propane remains relatively unchanged33. This is because the bulk isotope value represents the average composition of the two terminal carbons and the one central carbon, thereby masking the substantial isotope fractionation occurring specifically at the central carbon. Previous experimental studies have shown that as microbial anaerobic oxidation progresses, the carbon isotope composition of the central carbon (methylene carbon) becomes significantly enriched in 13C34. Furthermore, research by Liu et al.35 also indicated that Thermochemical Sulfate Reduction (TSR) preferentially oxidizes the central carbon of propane, leaving the terminal carbons largely unaffected35. Therefore, the intramolecular carbon isotope distribution within propane molecules holds significant potential for the precise identification of secondary alterations affecting natural gas accumulations.

The relationship between the position-specific carbon isotope composition of the methylene carbon and the bulk carbon isotope composition of propane in the natural gas reservoir investigated in this study is illustrated in Fig. 9. This relationship does not show isotopic patterns typically associated with pronounced microbial degradation and therefore provides no clear isotopic evidence for significant microbial oxidation in the analyzed samples; however, low-level alteration or earlier overprinting cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Based on the analyzed natural gas samples from selected wells in the Zhongguai Uplift, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The compositional and bulk isotopic data provide geochemical evidence consistent with a predominantly thermogenic origin associated with Type III kerogen.

The position-specific carbon isotope data provide additional constraints on formation mechanisms and are compatible with a dominant n-propyl pathway; however, this interpretation is model-dependent and not uniquely constrained.

The position-specific carbon isotope data provide additional constraints on formation mechanisms and are compatible with a dominant n-propyl pathway; however, this interpretation is model-dependent and not uniquely constrained.

The intramolecular isotope signatures show no clear isotopic evidence for significant secondary alteration, although subtle or earlier alteration processes cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, interpretations of alteration history remain dependent on model assumptions and indirect geochemical indicators. It should be noted that these interpretations are based on a limited number of samples and require further validation with broader spatial coverage.
