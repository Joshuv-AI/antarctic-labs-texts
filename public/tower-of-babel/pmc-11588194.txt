The Paper Sign Test – Assessment, Evaluation, and Diagnosis Method for Reactivated Lichen Planopilaris by Physician and Patient
Authors: Ali Abbasi, Ramin Rabani, Kambiz Kamyab, Hamidreza Mahmoudi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Lichen planopilaris (LPP) is an inflammatory disease that results in destruction of the hair follicle and replaces it with patchy permanent scarring alopecia; after stabilizing by treatment, reactivation is common and may be insidious. During remission, various methods are available for realizing that the disease is activated or not, but neither of them is practical for patients.Objectives:This study aims to evaluate the paper sign test for reactivated LPP by physician and patient.Patients and Methods:In a prospective study, during 2 years, 83 treated LPP patients have been divided into two groups of 41 and 42. Group 1 instructed for paper sign test which is to comb their hair over a white paper sheet to catch any fallen hairs, then turn the paper upside down, if all collected hair on paper completely separated, test is considered negative, if even one hair hangs onto the paper, the test is considered positive. Group 2 trained for clinical symptoms (hair shedding, scaling, itching, pain, tenderness, and burning).Results:From the first group, 13 patients had positive paper sign, and from the second group, 35 patients had clinical symptoms; after clinical examination and biopsy, 13 from Group 1 and 17 from Group 2 were confirmed to have reactivated disease. Patients in the first group who had been trained for paper sign test became aware of relapse sooner and came earlier for the evaluation of disease.Conclusion:Paper sign test is a reliable, noninvasive, and practical test for early detection of reactivated LPP by patients during remission period.

INTRODUCTION

LPP is a chronic, progressive inflammatory hair disease with an unknown etiology and a poorly understood pathogenesis. Due to peribulbar follicular inflammation, the hair follicle is destroyed and replaced with fibrotic tissue, resulting in patchy permanent scarring alopecia, primarily on the scalp. This condition causes significant cosmetic concern for the patients and brings about significant psychosocial morbidity. It is one of the common causes of scarring hair loss of the scalp,[12] and it can become an emergency as quickly enlarge.[2] LPP is more frequent in women than in men. The course of LPP in most cases is usually very slow and progressive. The disease may be asymptomatic or may be associated with increased hair shedding, scaling, itching, pain, tenderness, discomfort, and burning; diagnosis is based on the clinical and histopathological and direct immunofluorescence findings.[13]

Scarring in LPP usually appears as smooth white patches on the scalp. No hair follicle openings can be seen in the areas of scaring hair loss. Additionally, there may be redness and scale around hair follicles at the edges of active patches. Disease activity can be evaluated by examining hairs at the rim of patches. At the edges of these patches, hairs can be easily pulled out.[45]

After treatment, stability of the condition usually reveals asymptomatic, noninflammatory lesions and a negative hair pull test. At this stage, it clinically may not be distinguished from other lymphocytic inflammatory disorders that completely destroy the hair follicles. The only visible remnants are whitish scars and rarely some tufted hairs.[6]

The timing of LPP recurrences or reactivations is unpredictable. Hairs can easily be pulled out during the active stage and a significant increase in anagen hairs can be detected in both the hair pull and hair pluck tests.[78]

As LPP is a cicatricial disease, hair regrowth is by definition impossible, except in the early stages[4], so early diagnosis is vital to prevent further scarring of alopecia.

Currently, there is no effective long-term treatment for the arresting the progression of LPP and the available modalities are often disappointing, and less effective than that of lichen planus. The treatment goals are to block the progression or slowing down progression of hair loss and prevent further scaring alopecia.[6]

The therapeutic management often is quite challenging as relapses are common after local or systemic treatments.[19]

It is unpredictable whether the disease will recur following suspension of treatment in remission; if it does, it is usually an insidious process without the patient being aware of it for a considerable period of time, and alopecia can progress in a invidious manner.

As a result, patients must be closely monitored, educated, and informed that the disease might reactivate during periods of remission. More scarring lesions are likely to result from the time the disease first becomes active until the patient becomes aware of it. It is, therefore, crucial to diagnose reactivated alopecia at an early stage so that early therapy can be initiated to prevent further cicatricial alopecia.

It is a common question both for the patient and the physician: “When will the disease end?” There is no long-term study that can provide reliable answers about the duration of the disease.

It is an important question, when and with which symptoms the patient should know that the disease is reactivated? In other words, a patient in the remission period time of LPP how can know the disease is re-activated or not, especially when it is reactivated without symptom?

PATIENTS AND METHODS

The paper sign test

Various methods are available for diagnosis and evaluation of an reactivated LPP, such as dermoscopy, phototrichogram, trichoscan, trichogram, and scalp biopsy, but none are practical for a patient who is unaware his/her disease has been reactivated. There is a practical test, i.e., paper sign test; this is a good method for monitoring by the patient and getting an approximate assessment of the activated disease. It may be possible for patients to monitor their disease status by gently combing their scalp hair onto a white sheet of paper every day. The patient in remission is requested to comb gently and evenly over their head on a white paper and place all of the hair on a white paper sheet (A4), which is then turned upside down (inverted 180 degrees). If all the hairs on the paper are telogen hairs [Figure 1] (normal individuals usually lose 50 to 100 telogen hairs per day as a result of gentle traction and combing). In that case, they are removed from the paper sheet after the paper is turned over (negative paper sign). A negative paper sign indicates that the disorder is not active.

All hairs on the paper are completely separated from paper sheet (negative paper sign test)

If, however, at least one hair sticks firmly to the paper and hangs up, the test is positive since these gelatinous sticky hairs are anagen hairs [Figure 2]. (the presence of anagen hairs on a gentle combing test is pathologic). The presence of anagen hairs indicates active LPP.[5]

Hair sticks firmly to the paper, and hanged up to paper (positive paper sign test)

During 2013–2015, 83 LPP (76 women and 7 men, the female-to-male ratio was 11.8) established by clinical and biopsy [Figure 3], well controlled and stopped the treatment without any clinical activity, divided into two groups: Group 1-41 patients (38 women and 3 men aged 32–65 years) [Table 1] and Group 2-42 patients (38 women and 4 men aged 31–64 years) [Table 2]. Group I, the paper sign were trained, and mentioned them to do this paper sign daily, and asked them coming back immediately for full examination if they find out the paper sign is positive [Table 1], and Group 2 instructed for clinical symptoms such as scaling, itching, pain, tenderness and burning [Table 2]; in Group 1, 13 patients (12 women and 1 man) returned with positive paper sign, the mean time of stopping treatment till they discover the positive paper sign was about 3.2 months, and the mean time of finding positive paper sign till coming to the outpatient department was 5 days, the patients did not complained from itching, pain, tenderness, and burning; after clinical examination, we did not find any new lesions, but mild redness around each hair follicle was seen, hairs could easily pulled out and all biopsies were proven reactivated LPP. In Group 2, 35 patients (33 women and 2 men) aged 31–64 years returned with symptoms (clinical symptoms such as scaling, itching, pain, tenderness and burning); after full clinical examination, new lesion with perifollicular hyperkeratosis and scaling were seen; and doing biopsy, 17 of them were LPP, 3 were seborrheic dramatis, 3 were chronic telogen effluvium, and 12 were nonspecific; the mean time from stopping treatment till notifying symptoms was 6.5 months, and the mean time from finding the symptoms till coming outpatient department was 6.1 weeks (42.7 days).

RESULTS

Of 13 patients with positive paper signs in Group 1, 12 had active LPP confirmed by biopsy; however, from 35 patients in Group 2, 17 had active LPP confirmed by histopathological examination; the mean time to return in Group 2 was approximately 8.54 times greater than in Group 1. To put it another way, the time from becoming aware of the relapsing disease to returning to the doctor for Group 2 was longer than for Group 1.

DISCUSSION

During the active phase of the disease, inflammatory cells are infiltrated, and the pilosebaceous unit is destroyed.[7] A long mesenchymal dermal sheath covers the pilosebaceous unit. Under microscopic and even dermoscopic examination, these sticky hairs are anagen hairs. In contrast to telogen hairs, anagen hairs [Figure 4] are darkly pigmented, having triangular or delta-shaped bulbs with an angle to the hair shaft, and having an inner root sheath; whereas telogen hairs are seen as less pigmented with club-shaped hair bulbs and without inner root sheaths.

involved hair follicles in activated lichen planopilaris easily can be pulled out by gentle traction, these involved sticky hairs under microscope are anagen type

In activate phase of LPP, affected hairs are in the anagen phase and can be pulled out easily by gentle traction, such as combing, with a “gelatinous” root sheath[78910] [Figure 4]. Therefore, because of their viscosity properties, these involved anagen hairs can be firmly attached to the paper after drop off by gently combing. Anagen hair is often found at the margin of the alopecia in active patches of LPP, which indicates the disease’s activity.[1] Therefore, asymptomatic, non-inflammatory lesions, negative hair pull test, and negative paper sign test can be considered indicators of disease stability.[2] It is worth noting that this test can be positive in Alopecia areata, loose anagen syndrome, and toxic ingestion.

Re-examination for positive paper sign should be considered in the differential diagnosis to distinguish to rule in or out LPP.

CONCLUSION

Paper sign test is a novel, simple, patient-centered, reliable, noninvasive, practical, and cost-effective method of early detecting reactivated LPP to prevent the development of further cicatricial alopecia.
