A unified framework for characterizing response quality in surveys with multi-item scales
Authors: Nivedita Bhaktha, Henning Silber, Clemens M Lechner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-administered surveys are widely used but the quality of the survey responses have been a major concern. Low-quality responses threaten the validity and replicability of studies. To detect potentially problematic responses, many data quality indicators have been proposed. We propose a unifying conceptual framework for response quality issues in multi-item surveys as literature is scattered and suffers from a lack of conceptual and terminological clarity. The tripartite framework makes distinction between (1) response styles, (2) insufficient effort responding, and (3) response manipulation and delineates the relationship between different response quality indicators and the response quality issue(s) they measure. The framework provides a common theoretical point of reference and unified terminology for future work on response quality.Subject terms: Psychology, Sociology

Introduction

Self-administered surveys are widely used in social and behavioral science research. Often, such surveys are long and contain multi-item scales with categorical response options. Due to technological advancements, administering online surveys with multi-item scales have become ubiquitous, cheap, easy, and fast1,2. However, one of the abiding concerns for researchers is the quality of the responses from such self-administered surveys3–6. Response quality issues can arise due to various factors relating to the survey instrument, the survey situation, and the respondents. In case of the survey instrument, response quality is affected, for example, by the length and complexity of the survey instrument7,8 and the sensitivity of the questions9,10. The mode of survey data collection11 and the characteristics of the survey population, such as cognitive processing resources, conformity and attitude accessibility, and experience12,13 also affect the quality of responses. Carelessness, lack of motivation, or respondent fatigue5,14 also distort responses. The issue of respondents not paying enough attention to the questions, not putting enough effort into responding, or providing careless answers is commonly observed and has been long discussed5,15–18.

To improve response quality, researchers use a range of strategies, such as administering well-developed and tested survey instruments19,20, using shortened scales21, providing clear instructions22, or including attention checks23–25. Despite these efforts, the share of responses with quality issues is estimated to be around 3.5–50% of the sample5,26–28. It is important to note that the extent of low-quality responses is highly dependent on the characteristics of the respondents, the survey design, the questionnaire, and the criteria for identifying low quality responses5,26. Thus, the prevalence of low-quality responses can vary strongly across studies.

Response quality is a critical issue in survey research, as low response quality can introduce random or systematic errors into the data that compromise the validity and consistency of the measures. I also distort the relationship between the construct of interest and other measures29,30. Besides bivariate relationships, low response quality can have an impact on the results of factor analyses and statistical inferences27,31,32. Low-quality responses can also lead to spurious within-group variability and potential type II errors during hypothesis testing5. If a high proportion of respondents from a specific population provide low-quality data, this can make it more difficult or impossible to draw meaningful conclusions about that population4,6. Low- quality responses can also reduce the replicability of research findings by making it harder to obtain consistent estimates across different samples or settings33. In sum, low-quality responses can make it more difficult to generalize research findings and to build a cumulative body of knowledge that can inform theory and practice.

To avoid subsequent problems, it is essential to identify low-quality responses before using data for statistical analyses. Hence, recently, there has been a veritable surge in the literature concerning methods of detecting low-quality responses. This literature has produced many terms and indicators that researchers can use to characterize response quality. However, although a few attempts have been made to clarify different terminologies and concepts28,34, we observe that the literature is highly scattered across disciplines and suffers from a lack of conceptual and terminological clarity. Especially the underlying response quality concepts or issues are not well defined (e.g., in terms of the motivational or cognitive mechanisms they entail), and the link between the concept and their alleged indicators is weak at best. Additionally, response quality concepts and indicators are often created and used “ad hoc” without reference to underlying concepts. In fact, there is often little theoretical justification or empirical evidence that the indicators capture the concept—that is, the specific response quality issue they are designed to detect. This casts doubt on the validity of some of these indicators. Moreover, sometimes different terms are used for the same concepts, and at other times the same concept is described with different terms, leading to jingle jangle fallacies. This lack of conceptual and terminological rigor limits the utility of the indicators for researchers who intend to assess response quality and identify potentially problematic respondents in their data.

Against this background, we provide a comprehensive and structured overview of the response quality issues that have been discussed in the recent literature and the different indicators that have been proposed to detect low-quality responses in surveys with multi-item scales. We propose a unifying conceptual framework for response quality issues and delineate the relationship between the different response quality indicators and the response quality issue they measure. Our aim is threefold: first, we propose a common theoretical point of reference and unified terminology for future work on response quality to prevent the literature from becoming ever more scattered. Second, we want to inform applied researchers who are concerned about response quality about recent developments in the field and enable them to make informed choices about how to detect potential low-quality responses in their data. Third, to the extent possible, we establish a connection between the different indicators and the underlying response quality issue that they measure.

Common labels and definitions of low-quality responses

Response quality issues refer to latent problems in the response process that compromise the validity of observed data, such as inattention, insufficient effort, responding, or fatigue. These issues are not directly observable. In contrast, low-quality responses refer to the observable response patterns that arise when such response quality issues are present (e.g., random responding, straightlining, or inconsistent answers). These low-quality responses are then detected using indicators to infer the presence of underlying response quality issues35. Response quality issues are latent constructs that are not directly observable. Therefore, it becomes essential to address the validity of the response quality indicators because only then can researchers ensure that the response quality indicators accurately capture the latent response quality issues. Such validity assessments involve two key aspects—clearly defining the respective underlying response quality issue, and explicitly linking indicators to the response quality issue that they detect.

To provide a unifying framework for the different response quality issues that have been discussed in the literature, we need a full understanding of the terminologies and definitions used to describe low-quality responses. Different authors from different fields have used a diverse range of terminologies to denote what we call here “low-quality responses.” For example, researchers have discussed low-quality responses under the terms of survey satisficing21,36, careless responding5,34,37, insufficient effort responding18,38, misrepresentation26,39,40, and content nonresponsivity41. More specific terms, such as response styles42–44, acquiescent responding12,45,46, socially desirable responding47,48, and faking26,49, are also discussed as examples of low-quality responses. Some of these terms have been used interchangeably and synonymously in literature, and at times, the same term has been used to refer to different concepts, adding to the confusion in describing low-quality responses.

Some of these concept labels and definitions already imply rather strong assumptions about the response processes responsible for low-quality response, such as “carelessness” (e.g., respondents are unmotivated to respond accurately, so that they answer survey items without reading them thoroughly). For the most part, such an implicit assumption goes untested, and many of these assumptions may indeed be untestable. Some of the terminologies assume a certain process or mechanism for the issue (e.g., “content nonresponsivity”, “careless response”, and “insufficient effort responding”), while others do not (e.g., “meaningless data”, “low quality data”). Classification of response quality issues

To bring clarity to this terminological confusion, we need to understand and classify the different underlying response quality issues related to the labels displayed in Table 1. As a first step toward integration, we consider the cognitive process of responding to survey questions. Tourangeau and Rasinski51 proposed a cognitive response model with four major steps: item comprehension, information retrieval, integration, and response mapping. In the comprehension step, respondents understand and interpret the question at hand. In the retrieval step, respondents need to retrieve information relevant to answering the survey question from memory and bring it into consciousness. In the third step, respondents integrate the information they retrieved and make a judgement. They do so by combining, supplementing, and extrapolating the retrieved information. In the last step, respondents report their answers by selecting a response from a fixed set of response categories.

Response quality issues can arise in each of the four steps of the survey response process. Misinterpretation or inattentiveness while reading the item content (comprehension step) can cause response quality issues. This is because, even if the respondent adheres to the standards for all the subsequent steps, if respondents did not properly understand the item content, their responses no longer reflect the best answer to the item. Because of lack of effort or fatigue, the respondents may retrieve what is most accessible instead of the most important or relevant information (retrieval). This can further lead to irrelevant integration and mapping. Even when the respondents perform interpretation and retrieval well, problems can still occur if they are unable to combine the information well enough to make an informed judgement (integration). In the final stage of the response process, where an answer is selected from among the options presented (response mapping), there can be at least two issues. First, the response category that best represents the integrated response might not be selected. Second, the answers may undergo editing process with respect to social norms, such as socially desirable responding or acquiescence to avoid an unfavorable self-presentation.

There are four dimensions under which the observed response quality issues can be classified: intentionality, content responsivity, severity, and stability. First, intentionality refers to a respondent’s motivation in providing low-quality of responses. Using this property, we attempt to determine whether low-quality responses are given unintentionally and largely unconsciously, reflecting inadvertent issues during the cognitive response process (e.g., inattentiveness); or intentionally, reflecting conscious behavior to minimize effort; or even deliberately to distort the responses. Second, content responsivity reflects the response process in which a respondent may have considered the content of the question(s) while responding. Using this property, we attempt to determine if the low quality of responses is triggered by the question content or occurred independently. Third, severity refers to the magnitude of impact of the response quality issue on subsequent data analysis. Using this property, we can gauge the extent to which a response quality issue is likely to distort analysis results. Fourth, stability refers to the consistency of the variance components. A variance component is trait-like when it shows consistency across occasions and is attributable to enduring person-specific response tendencies. A variance component is state-like when it is occasion-specific, transient, or situational and thus depends on measurement design (related to response set, i.e., administration-specific behaviors). Stability indicates whether the observed variance is reproducible and consistent across occasions

When classifying response quality indicators, we believe that intentionality is the leading dimension as it sets the tone on whether a response quality issue is due to deliberate misrepresentation on the respondent’s part. As shown in Fig. 1 below, the three broad categories we propose are response styles, insufficient effort responding, and response manipulation. Response styles refers to unintentional patterns of answering that can cause respondents to deviate from their optimal answer. These deviations encompass patterns, such as acquiescent responding, mid-point responding, extreme responding, and social desirability bias. Insufficient effort responding refers to mainly intentional response distortions that may arise due to inattention, distractions, haste, fatigue, or misunderstandings, leading to non-systematic deviations from the respondents’ actual position. Response manipulation involves intentional distortion or manipulation of responses, which renders them meaningless and unrelated to the item content. This type of response is particularly problematic and increases the error variance in analyses. As shown in Fig. 1, there is some overlap in the three categories of response quality issues since the boundaries between the three dimensions (intentionality, content responsivity, and severity) are gradual and slightly fuzzy.

Response quality framework for multi-item scales.

This conceptual framework maps the four stages of the cognitive response process: comprehension, retrieval, judgment, and mapping, to the response quality of survey data. It classifies deviations from optimal behavior into a tripartite distinction of response styles (RS), insufficient effort responding (IER), and response manipulation (RM). These categories are differentiated along the dimensions of intentionality, content responsivity, severity of their impact on statistical analysis and stability over time. Note. This framework is based on the definitions and characteristics of different terminologies presented in Table 1 and the cognitive response model proposed by Tourangeau and Rasinski51.

We deliberately refrained from classifying the concept of survey satisficing in one of the three categories, as it mainly relates to the cognitive response process, but does not take the data analysis perspective into account. Survey satisficing describes response behaviors that occur when respondents expend only the amount of effort necessary to make an acceptable or satisfactory but not optimal decision21. Thus, survey satisficing, with the subcategories strong and weak satisficing, can be seen as a theory underlying and driving the first two response quality categories outlined here.

Response styles

“Response styles” refers to systematic response tendencies that shift respondents' answers away from their optimal answer. Response styles are typically seen as relatively enduring individual patterns in how people respond to surveys. Prime examples of such response patterns are acquiescent responding (“yeah-saying”), or the tendency to agree with items irrespective of content12; mid-point responding, or the tendency to use the middle category; and extreme responding, that is, the tendency to use the highest and/or lowest response category. Such response styles are largely unrelated to the respective item content. However, certain questions related to sensitive issues can evoke strong emotions, which in turn can elicit extreme responding. Some authors also view social desirability as a response style52–54. Desirable responding can be distinguished into two psychologically distinct components: (1) self-deceptive enhancement, an unintentional, overly positive self-view rooted in genuine but biased self-perception, and (2) impression management, the deliberate and strategic presentation of oneself in a favorable light55,56. This framework was expanded by mapping socially desirable responding onto an agency-communion structure, yielding four forms of self-presentation: (1) agentic enhancement (inflating competence), (2) communal enhancement (inflating warmth or morality), (3) agentic management (strategic self-promotion), and (4) communal management (strategic presentation of virtue or likability)57. Across these perspectives, unintentional socially desirable responding is typically considered a response style, whereas highly intentional forms are more appropriately classified as response manipulation. To deter desirable responding, scales often tend to use both positively and negatively worded items. This design choice introduces its own systematic bias in responses and cannot be treated as a methods artifact or transient but are trait-like (stable) response style58. Response styles lead to a shift in response that is systematic and can often be corrected for in the model. For example, acquiescent responding can be corrected for by modeling a random intercept in a confirmatory factor analysis model59 or in an exploratory factor analysis model60. Another example is the R package IRTrees, which is based on generalized linear mixed models and can be used to correct for extreme style responding61. Especially with such corrections, exclusion of low-quality responses is usually not required, although excluding respondents who show strong response styles can sometimes be warranted42,62. With respect to the cognitive response process, response styles are usually related to the fourth and last step, selecting a response option. The stability of response styles is trait-like (i.e., typically showing moderate to high stability), while their expression depends on the measurement design, the underlying tendency is stable63–65.

Insufficient effort responding

The term “insufficient effort responding” covers all types of mostly unintentional response distortions that arise from inattention, distractions, speeding, fatigue, or misinterpretations that lead to non-systematic shifts of items away from a respondent’s optimal answer5,26,27,34,37,66–68. It is also known as content nonresponsivity41, careless responding5,27, inattentive responding27,69,70, or meaningless data71. Respondents lack motivation and effort to answer carefully, leading to responses that do not accurately reflect respondents’ true levels of the target construct5,71. Different from response styles that represent systematic response shifts (e.g., toward higher agreement in the case of acquiescence), insufficient effort responding leads to unsystematic errors. In extreme cases of insufficient effort responding, the responses contribute little more than error variance and hence should be considered for exclusion from subsequent analysis. However, excluding observations can lead to a decrease in statistical power and threats to external validity. With respect to the cognitive response process, insufficient effort responding can occur at any of the four steps as respondents can short-cut comprehension, retrieval, integration, and/or mapping. For example, inconsistent responses can arise from various factors, including insufficient effort in comprehending the item content, inaccuracies during information retrieval, errors in integrating information, or simply carelessness in mapping responses. The stability of insufficient effort responding is mostly state-like (i.e., transient) as it exhibits low temporal stability across measurement occasions, and only a small proportion of the variance component is attributable to stable between-person differences63.

Response manipulation

We define “response manipulation” as all responses that respondents intentionally distort or blatantly manipulate in such a way that responses no longer have any meaningful relation to item content. Invalid responding can comprise both systematic patterns, such as respondents choosing response options that form a visual “zig zag” pattern or straight lining; and unsystematic patterns in which respondents choose response categories largely at random without paying attention to the item content or response category labels. This response behavior could be due to disinterest in the research or because of psychological reactance, or an extreme form of faking. In cases where respondents do pay attention to the item content, they might make a deliberate attempt to portray themselves uncharacteristically to others48,72. Response manipulation is highly problematic and similar to extreme cases of insufficient effort responding in that it leads to more error variance in the analysis, that is, it adds random noise. Therefore, it could be beneficial to exclude these from the analysis. With respect to the cognitive response process, response manipulation starts usually in the first step of the cognitive response process, as respondents do not engage in the item content at all or in an unintended way. The stability of response manipulation is state-like and driven by situational motives rather than lasting tendencies.

The three broad categories of response quality issues have several similarities and distinctions (this has been portrayed in Fig. 1 using an overlapping structure and dotted borders). When we work with survey data, it is often impossible to ascertain which of the three categories of the response quality issue is responsible for the low quality of response that we are observing in the data. This is because the three response quality issues can be at work at the same time across different respondents, and even for the same respondent, they need not be mutually exclusive. For example, we might come across a case of mid-point responding in which the respondent has selected the middle response option for all the items on a multi-item scale. This could be classified as a legitimate response with the respondent having complied by all four steps of the cognitive model. It could also be classified as mid-point responding as a case of response style, as the respondent might have complied with the first three steps of the cognitive response model (comprehension, retrieval, and integration) but might have unintentionally shifted their response in the mapping stage. Mid-point responding could also be classified as a case of insufficient effort responding, where the respondent might not have complied with one of the first three steps of the cognitive response process (e.g., comprehension), mostly unintentionally. Finally, mid-point responding could be classified as a case of response manipulation where the respondent largely disregards the item content and skips multiple steps of the cognitive response process by intentionally selecting the middle response option for all items on the scale. Given that there are valid reasons for classifying such responses as legitimate or low-quality responses of different types, it would be hard to identify a single underlying mechanism.

When considering the relationship between the three response quality issues and questionnaire length, we can speculate where in a survey interview each of the three issues might be most likely to occur. We assume that the longer a survey, the more likely issues of low effort and response manipulation are to occur because of respondents’ decreasing energy, attentiveness, and motivation. In contrast, response styles are likely to affect different parts of a survey interview more evenly. They might occur due to cultural aspects of respondents12,73 or due to the specific response scales used (e.g., agree-disagree or yes-no).

In any sample survey, responses to a multi-item scale likely contain low-quality responses that are due to all three response quality issues. Some respondents might have provided invalid responses, some might have provided insufficient effort responses, while others might have provided answers with systematic distortions due to response styles. Similarly, the same respondent might have produced different quality issues at the same time and/or in different parts of the questionnaire. Thus, we typically deal with a mix of low-quality responses arising due to multiple response quality issues. While it is difficult to ascertain the exact cause of low response quality, it is still worthwhile and important to investigate the underlying response quality issue in order to gain more insight regarding the reasons behind the low-quality responses. Such insights can be particularly useful in improving scale development and survey administration approaches. However, the first step toward discerning the response quality issue is the detection and identification of low-quality responses. In the next section, we will discuss frequently used response quality indicators and try to link them with their underlying response quality issue(s). While we aim to link response quality indicators with their underlying issues, it is important to acknowledge that this mapping may not be definitive. Many indicators often detect or identify a combination of concurrent response quality issues, making it a general and potentially unresolvable challenge in assessing response quality.

Detecting low-quality responses

Low-quality responses can be detected by using response quality indicators. Various methods and approaches have been proposed to detect low-quality responses in multi-item scales5,37,71. As seen from the example of mid-point responding, it is difficult to ascertain whether a response is accurate or it is instead affected by one or multiple response quality issues. To our knowledge, there is no single method that can accurately identify low-quality responses in the data with absolute certainty. They are mere indicators of potentially low-quality of responses. One usually needs multiple indicators tapping different underlying response quality issue to flag and classify low- quality responses with sufficient confidence.

Detecting low-quality responses through response quality indicators is complicated by the fact that various response quality issues can occur simultaneously, making it hard to disentangle them. Having achieved greater conceptual clarity on what response quality issues exist and how they differ, we can now attempt to establish a clearer relation between response quality issues and the most important response quality indicators that have been proposed in the literature. Still, we want to emphasize that one needs to exert caution in interpreting any of these indicators, as most of them are unlikely to be pure and unmistakable indicators of a single response quality issue. This important point is all too often given short shrift in the literature on response quality indicators, helpful as such indicators undoubtedly are.

In Fig. 2, we classify frequently used response quality indicators to detect low-quality responses. Specifically, these indicators use (a) item responses (responses to items of interest in the questionnaire), (b) check items (items in the questionnaire that were specifically included to assess response quality), and (c) metadata (additional data that are typically available in computer-assisted surveys). Whereas item responses are readily and easily available upon data collection, check items have to be included in the questionnaire before the survey data collection. Metadata are generally available for computer-assisted surveys, and the type of metadata available (e.g., response times, device used, screen size, IP address, country, mouse movements) depends on the technology used and the para data script.

Overview of frequently used response quality indicators.

This figure provides a taxonomy of frequently used response quality indicators, classified according to their data source: check items, item responses, and metadata. It provides a diagnostic roadmap for researchers to select the most appropriate tools for screening data.

Check items are considered to be an intrusive method of ascertaining the quality of the responses as they are placed as additional items in the questionnaire, and the respondents answer them along with other items of substantive research interest. Response quality indicators that make use of item responses and metadata are considered to be nonintrusive methods as no questionnaire modifications are required and the implementation is undetected by the respondents26. These nonintrusive methods are also known as statistical methods of response quality detection, as they require advanced computations and multiple steps26, while check items provide a simple classification that typically does not need further computation.

Check items

This method of low response quality detection has been termed "direct method” by DeSimone and Harms 26. Respondents are flagged to have provided low-quality response if they indicate their responses are untrustworthy26, provide illogical responses to bogus items74, or fail to follow instructions in instructed items75. Check items are typically easy to use and compute. They must be incorporated at the time of survey development.

Bogus items

Bogus items are fake, implausible, or unusual items that are included in surveys to check for respondent inattentiveness5. Incorrect responses to bogus items indicate that a respondent did not read the content of the respective item. This method has the disadvantage that some respondents may select the “wrong” answer on purpose. Bogus items should be written such that there is an unambiguous answer.

Directed items

Directed items instruct respondents to select a particular response option as their answer for the item5,24,70. For example, in a Likert scale, respondents might be instructed to select the response category “slight disagree”. Similar to bogus items, there is a chance that respondents select the “wrong” answer on purpose76.

Self-report items

Self-report data quality items ask the respondents to indicate how much effort and attention was devoted to responding5,77. However, answers to self-reports could be affected by social desirability bias. Also, the self-report items are usually placed toward the end of the questionnaire, making recency effects more likely.

Item responses

Item responses include indices calculated based on the responses. Item responses are nonintrusive methods of identifying low-quality responses. Some of the indicators based on item responses involve multiple steps and use advanced statistical methods such as person fit indices78, machine learning28. They can be calculated on any data set using multi-item scales.

Percent missing

This index measures the percentage or proportion of missing responses across all items for each respondent. Unmotivated respondents are likely to skip items; however, even highly motivated respondents may skip if they feel that they cannot provide an accurate response. Unanswered items pose limitations to most data analysis71, as they reduce the sample size.

Response pattern indices

Sometimes, respondents tend to provide responses in a visually discernible pattern if they are unmotivated or are disengaged with the content. Visual pattern indices are calculated across all multi-item scale items for each respondent. Some of the popular indicators used to identify the so-called patterned responses are introduced in the following.

Maximum longstring

This index refers to the length of the longest sequence of responses of an individual respondent5. A sequence is a set of consecutive responses with the same response option. Annoyed or unmotivated respondents often respond in straight vertical lines. The maximum longstring is used to detect straightlining. Scores on this index can range from 1 to p (p: total number of items). This indicator requires access to the sequence in which the items are presented to the respondents. Higher scores indicate invariable responding.

Intra-individual response variability

As a measure of intra-respondent variability, this response pattern index is the variance of responses across all items calculated for each respondent. A value close to zero indicates invariability (in the extreme, a variance of zero indicates straight lining), and high values could potentially indicate inattentiveness or extreme response style.

Mid-point response score

This index refers to the number of times a respondent has selected the central or neutral option as their response35. Each item is given a score of 1, for which the response is a mid-point response option. If a response is other than the mid-point, then the item is given a score of 0. These scores are summed over all items. The score can range from 0 to p (p: total number of items). Higher scores indicate mid-point style of responding.

Extreme response style score

This index refers to the number of times a respondent has selected extreme options as their response35. Each item is given a score of 1, for which the response is either of the two extreme response options. If the responses are in between response categories, then the item is given a score of 0. The score can range from 0 to p (p: total number of items). Higher scores indicate extreme style of responding.

Acquiescent response score

This index refers to the number of times a respondent's answers indicate agreement or yes-saying regardless of the item content35. Each item is given a score of 1, for which the response is one of the options on the positive side of the scale. If the responses are neutral or negative, then the item is given a score of 0. The score can range from 0 to p (p: total number of items). It should be noted that acquiescence is a continuous response style ranging from opposition (nay-saying) to acquiescence (yeah-saying). Alternatively, the mean across a set of balanced-keyed items (i.e., an equal number of positively and negatively keyed items with the same response scale) is often taken as an indicator of (dis-)acquiescence (see12, for a discussion).

Substantive pattern indices

These indices measure the consistency in responses. One can expect the responses of positively formulated items to be positively correlated as well, and vice versa. As these indices rely on the associations among items of related constructs, they can be called substantive pattern indices.

Psychometric synonyms

Correlations among all survey items are computed, and 30 item pairs with the largest positive correlation are identified. Within-person correlations across these 30 pairs are then used to identify inconsistent responses. Item pairs with correlations stronger than 0.6 are retained, and it is ensured that they are truly similar in meaning5.

Psychometric antonyms

Correlations among all survey items are computed, and 30 item pairs with the largest negative correlation are identified. Within-person correlations across these 30 pairs are then used to identify inconsistent responses. Item pairs with correlations stronger than −0.6 are retained, and it is ensured that they are truly opposite in meaning32.

Even-odd consistency

It is a measure of the consistency of responses of a unidimensional scale. Unidimensional scales are divided using odd-even split based on the order of item appearance. A within person correlation is computed based on the two sets of odd-even scores for each dimension. The correlations are then corrected using the Spearman–Brown split-half formula32.

Distribution indices

Indices that detect aberrant responses based on their positioning in the sample of responses are referred to as distribution indices. These indices are used to discern outlying responses in the sample. Some of the commonly used distribution indices are introduced in the following.

Mahalanobis distance

It is the distance between the set of responses to all items of a respondent and the distribution of all responses to all items79. Large distances indicate atypical answers. However, atypical responses can also be valid.

Guttman error score

It is a measure of consistency of responses compared to sample item means80. The responses of an individual are compared against the sample item means by coding response option higher than the item sample mean as 1, else 0. The total of all such scores forms the Guttman error score. Higher scores indicate inconsistent responses.

Item-based outlier score

The item-based outlier score counts the frequency of responses in unpopular answer categories81. The modal answer category has an outlier score of zero, the next less popular answer category has outlier score 1, and so on. Suspect respondents are found in the right tail of the score distribution.

Metadata

Response times and additional information obtained from computer-assisted surveys form metadata indices. Indicators based on metadata are fairly easy to compute. IP address can be used to ensure that only one set of responses have been recorded from a single IP address. This ensures that there is no data faking in web-based surveys. Keystroke measures are obtained from log data that collect the timing and type of every key press and key release made by the respondent. It has been seen that disengagement with the survey is correlated with high keystroke measures82. However, these data are not typically available and are generally not provided as part of secondary data.

Response time indices

Response time indicators provide a highly useful approach of obtaining additional insight into the respondent’s behavior83–85.

Total response time

A measure of the total time is taken by a respondent to complete answering the entire questionnaire71. Both fast and slow respondents are problematic.

Average response time per item

The total response time is divided by the total number of items in the questionnaire to derive the average response time per item86. Typically, respondents with average response time per item greater than six times the average response time per item across all respondents is considered to be problematic.

Relative completion speed

The median response time of the sample is divided by the response time of each individual respondent to derive the relative completion time. A factor of 3 indicates that a respondent was thrice as fast as the typical respondent. Relative completion time greater than 3 are indicative of low-quality of responses71.

Linking indicators with the response quality issues and practical properties

The response quality indicators introduced above can help to detect potentially problematic responses. However, it is important to understand the theoretical and practical properties of these indicators to use them effectively. The properties of response quality indicators relate to (a) the mapping of the indicators to the underlying response quality issues, (b) the suitability of the respective indicator in case of the use of categorical or Likert scale response scales, (c) the suitability in case of missingness, and (d) the requirement of comparison of a response with other responses in the sample.

Table 2 indicates for every response quality indicator listed in Fig. 2 to which response quality issue(s) it relates. Some of the indicators were developed for continuous data but are being used for Likert scale responses (as indicated by * in the table). While the use of such indicators might not pose a problem in the detection of low-quality responses, it is worthwhile to understand the suitability of the indicators in being applied for categorical responses. Another relevant issue is how to use the indicators in the presence of missing responses, as some indicators might not function properly if responses are missing and might require imputation of missing values, which can further complicate the matter. Some indicators consider only each respondent’s responses to describe response quality, whereas other indicators require a comparison of responses with all other respondents in a sample.

As Table 2 highlights, none of the response quality indicators does lend itself as a tool to detect one and only one of the three response quality issues. In fact, all of them are catch-all indicators that detect unusual responses (i.e., in terms of multivariate response distributions or response times) but provide no specific information as to the underlying mechanisms or processes that led to these unusual responses. For example, an indicator for “extreme responding” may pick up a respondent’s extreme response style—but one can never be sure whether it is not picking up other response styles (e.g., acquiescence, social desirability) and, even more importantly, invalid responding (e.g., visual patterns) at the same time. Similarly, an indicator that detects midpoint responding would capture responses of a balanced and careful or hesitant personality (i.e., a response style) but also invalid responding (i.e., straight lining). The same even applies to check items, which may capture insufficient effort responding or intentional failure. The fact that respondents may be inattentive on one construct but attentive on the next (i.e., that response behavior may change across the survey) further complicates issues. Hence, there is no clear relation between response quality indicators and the theoretical concepts which they are employed to measure. Researchers should refrain from using these response quality indicators as indicators for a single circumscribed quality issue. Instead, they need to recognize that response quality indicators reflect multiple underlying response behaviors that can have a multitude of causes (e.g., lack of motivation, fatigue, and distractions) that are difficult to disentangle in practice. As a consequence, several response quality indicators need to be combined, in practice, to obtain a complete picture of respondents’ potentially problematic response behavior.

Discussion

Understanding and addressing response quality in self-administered surveys is a critical concern for survey researchers and practitioners. Response quality issues can seriously impact data validity and, subsequently, the reliability of research findings. We propose a parsimonious new framework that categorizes and connects response quality issues with data quality indicators, providing a unified theoretical foundation and common terminology for response quality assessment in surveys with multi-item scales. The response quality framework outlined in this study classifies response quality issues into three primary categories: response styles, insufficient effort responding, and response manipulation. Each of these categories reflect a different combination of stability, intentionality, and cognitive demands. It represents distinct challenges that researchers may encounter when analyzing self-administered survey data. Our framework aligns with recent advances in latent state-trait theory and generalizability theory, both of which show that the structure and magnitude of measurement error arise from different cognitive and situational processes63–65.

A key aim of the framework is to establish an explicit connection between the many data quality indicators that have been proposed in the literature and the underlying response quality issues. Clarifying what response quality issue each indicator may reflect is a basic prerequisite to establishing the validity of these indicators. In practice, a range of deeper “hidden” issues, such as fluctuations in respondents’ mood or motivation, measurement representativeness, sampling adequacy, and other broader challenges of construct validity, pose significant threats to the quality of responses beyond the observable manifestations of low-quality responding. However, these issues cannot be solely diagnosed with empirical response behavior. Instead, the present research focused on indicators of low response quality from observed response patterns. To distinguish response patterns, researchers should use multi-indicator response quality assessments because no single metric reliably captures the breadth of issues. Our framework provides researchers with a foundation for understanding why certain indicators are appropriate for identifying specific issues and guidance on how to employ these indicators strategically. By making these connections, the framework enables researchers to make informed decisions about how to manage potentially problematic observations in their data. Whether through clearly justified and well-documented exclusion, down-weighting, robustness checks, or sophisticated modeling adjustments, researchers can improve the accuracy of their findings.

To facilitate appropriate and consistent usage of the response quality indicators discussed in this article, we recommend that case studies and simulations should be employed to derive context-specific thresholds (cutoffs), accounting for contextual factors, such as questionnaire length, cognitive demand, and sample characteristics. This approach avoids overreliance on arbitrary cutoffs and aligns with increasing calls for contextualized response-quality assessment37,74. As not all studies require the same level of scrutiny, we suggest three tiers of assessment: minimal, moderate, and extensive response quality assessment. (1) Minimal assessment is appropriate for short, low-stakes surveys or pilot studies. It includes the implementation of a small set of nonintrusive indicators (e.g., longstring indicator and Mahalanobis distance). (2) Moderate assessment is suitable for typical surveys, related to main or medium-sized data collections. Here, check items should be included during survey development, and post hoc response quality assessment should be conducted using multiple indicators and finally, sensitivity analysis should be performed for the fitted model. (3) Extensive assessment is recommended for high-stakes or large-scale surveys. Apart from including check items in the questionnaire, the assessment should incorporate flexible psychometric modeling techniques that can model response quality issues, such as IRTrees, IRT person-fit modeling, response-time analyses, latent state-trait modeling, or G-theory designs. This research might be accompanied by own simulations to identify study-specific thresholds, as well as sensitivity analysis. To promote transparency, it will be fundamental to report clearly which indicators were selected and used, how thresholds were determined (e.g., based on literature or own simulation), how many respondents were flagged, how exclusion affects substantive results (i.e., reporting sensitivity analysis using all available data), and which modeling strategies were used to adjust for method variance. For this purpose, it would be useful to develop appropriate response quality assessment documentation templates.

While the proposed framework is a crucial first step toward greater integration of response quality issues and rigor in response quality assessment, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The three categories of response quality issues might not fully encompass every nuance of response quality and provide more of a pragmatic heuristic than a rigorous classification at this stage. Although the proposed model is based on reasoning and thorough review of a large body of literature, it needs future research to test and validate the main assumptions. For example, future research could examine the motivational cognitive processes behind different response styles to demarcate them more clearly from each other. Additionally, there are numerous data quality indicators available, and this study selected those that are commonly used in the literature. Researchers should consider additional indicators when assessing response quality based on their specific research context. Finally, while this framework primarily focuses on online surveys, it has applicability to other survey modes, including mixed-mode surveys, and could benefit from further validation in diverse survey contexts.

We would also like to note that understanding the response quality of single items rather than multi-item scales is a similarly critical issue of social science research, since single items are often used in practice, especially outside psychological research. Several of the response quality indicators discussed in this manuscript (e.g., check items or response times) can be applied to single items either with or without minor adjustments, while others were especially designed to assess response quality of multi-item scales (e.g., response pattern indices or substantive pattern indices). Developing a unified response quality framework for single-item measures represents a promising direction for future research.

Looking into the future, the understanding of response quality is an evolving field that demands ongoing empirical research and further theoretical discussions. From a practical side, researchers could explore using combinations of data quality indicators and refined filtering approaches. Especially promising avenues for future methodological research are longitudinal and repeated-measurement designs to map the stability of responses through advanced quality assessment. Improving response quality in self-report data requires an integrated strategy that combines suitable research designs, multi-indicator diagnostics, simulation-based thresholds, flexible psychometric models, and transparent reporting. From a theoretical perspective, these approaches are consistent with the idea that response quality emerges from the dynamic interaction of study design, respondent behavior, and contextual factors.

In conclusion, we hope that this comprehensive response-quality framework serves as a useful resource for survey-based research. By linking common response-quality issues with relevant data quality indicators, it is designed to support informed decisions about how to handle flagged observations and, ultimately, to improve the accuracy of survey results.
