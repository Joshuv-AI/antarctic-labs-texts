Transfer learning and AI technology for family school community collaborative model research in university network security management
Authors: Qiongfang Feng, Yang’an Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To enhance universities’ risk identification and response capabilities in complex network environments, this study proposes a university network security management model integrating transfer learning and multi-agent collaborative governance mechanisms. It aims to construct an implementable intelligent governance system. Methodologically, this study develops an attention-enhanced transfer residual network that incorporates a channel attention mechanism to strengthen feature selection. The network achieves deep cross-domain alignment through integration with the maximum mean discrepancy method, significantly enhancing recognition accuracy under conditions of limited target domain data availability. Subsequently, a school-family-community collaborative response mechanism based on multi-agent game theory is constructed. The mechanism drives the generation of personalized intervention strategies through a risk scoring function to address response lags and responsibility ambiguity in traditional governance. Results show that the proposed model achieves an accuracy of 95.7%, a recall of 94.8%, a F1 score of 0.951, an Area Under the Curve of 0.973, and detection delay controlled within 29 ms. The integrated model proposed in this study demonstrates strong identification capability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31614-w.

Introduction

As China’s universities fully enter the stage of informatization and digitization development, campus networks have become critical support platforms for teaching, scientific research, management, and student life1,2. However, open network structures and diversified user behaviors have increasingly exposed universities to cyber attack risks, with attack types evolving toward diversification, concealment, and targeting. Typical behaviors such as illegal access, Virtual Private Network (VPN) circumvention, phishing attacks, and cyberbullying have frequently emerged, severely threatening the stability of university network environments and the security of data assets3.

In governance practice, university network security work faces three prominent challenges. First, the identification challenge: existing management systems mainly rely on rule configuration and manual inspections. They have limitations such as low identification accuracy and poor real-time performance when dealing with high-dimensional, unstructured behavioral data and complex attack paths4,5. Second, the collaboration challenge: students’ online behaviors are jointly influenced by family education, social exposure, and school guidance. The traditional intervention model implemented unilaterally by schools struggles to effectively address behavioral deviations in complex scenarios, leading to fragmented response paths and ambiguous responsibility definition6. Third, the technical challenge: high-quality security sample acquisition is difficult, especially in non-commercial fields like universities, where the lack of sufficient labeled data restricts the effective training and transfer capabilities of artificial intelligence (AI) models.

AI technology, particularly transfer learning, provides new ideas for breaking through data bottlenecks. This approach can leverage labeled data from enterprise-level or public domains to achieve knowledge transfer and model adaptation in target university scenarios, significantly enhancing the generalization and robustness of identification7,8. Meanwhile, the development of attention mechanisms in deep learning enables models to dynamically focus on key attack feature areas, improving identification sensitivity and efficiency against large-scale data backgrounds.

Against the backdrop of the national advocacy for “school-family-community collaborative education,” the deep integration of AI identification capabilities with multi-agent governance structures breaks through the closed pattern of traditional campus security management. It also holds promise for realizing intelligent, refined, and dynamic governance mechanisms. Starting from this, this study proposes a comprehensive university network security model integrating transfer learning and school-family-community collaborative mechanisms. This model focuses on the precise identification of network risks and multi-party linked interventions to provide a replicable and deployable technical support path for university network governance models.

The core goal of this study is to construct a transferable, interpretable, and collaborative university network security management system. It specifically includes (1) developing a network risk identification model based on transfer learning and attention mechanism to improve the attack detection accuracy and generalization ability in the complex network environment of universities; (2) a school-family-society collaborative governance mechanism is designed to realize the automation of risk intervention decisions and optimization of responses with the participation of multiple subjects.

The main contributions of this study include: (1) proposing a university network security identification model that integrates attention mechanism and transfer learning, achieving feature adaptive alignment between source domain enterprise data and target domain university logs, and significantly improving risk identification accuracy under small sample conditions; (2) building a collaborative governance mechanism among home, school, and community based on multi-agent game theory, introducing risk scoring and dynamic incentive functions, and solving the problems of response delay and role fragmentation in traditional campus safety governance. The structure of the study includes: Section “Related work” reviews relevant research; Section “Research model” introduces the model design and algorithm; Section “Experimental design and performance evaluation” presents experimental setup and performance evaluation; Section “Discussion” discusses the results and their implications; Section “Conclusion” summarizes the entire text and proposes future directions. The innovation of this study lies in the deep integration of transfer learning cross-domain knowledge and home school community collaborative governance for the first time, proposing an integrated framework of “intelligent recognition—collaborative intervention—dynamic feedback” to unify technological intelligence and social collaboration.

Related work

Network security and behavior analysis in higher education institutions

With the expansion of university network scale and diversification of user behavior, the academic community has conducted multidimensional research on university network security. Ijiga et al. (2024) used a fuzzy risk assessment model to classify potential threats to university network infrastructure and proposed differentiated security protection strategies9. Vaddadi et al. (2023) used access behavior clustering method to identify abnormal paths and violation patterns among students10. Kuglitsch et al. (2022) introduced time series and user profiling modeling techniques to achieve personalized risk prediction11. These studies have laid the foundation for understanding the scenarios of network threats in universities, but they generally rely on single domain data or rule driven methods, which still make it difficult to cope with cross-domain distribution differences in heterogeneous university environments.

Application of cross-domain transfer learning in network security identification

Transfer learning has shown significant advantages in dealing with inconsistent data distribution across environments, and is therefore widely used in tasks like intrusion detection, protocol identification, and traffic classification. Bhardwaj et al. (2024) introduced transfer neural networks into industrial control system IDS to improve detection accuracy in small sample scenarios12. Xu et al. (2024) used transfer residual structures and deep autoencoders to enhance cross domain feature abstraction13.

Frontier research on domain adaptation has proposed enhancement strategies such as multi-level alignment and dynamic model selection. For example, the domain adaptive multi-stage integration framework DAMEL significantly alleviates performance degradation caused by cross-domain differences14. Hossain et al. (2024) improved cross-domain classification performance in campus traffic recognition by aligning features using maximum mean difference (MMD)15. Neuro-Fuzzy Meta-Learning (NF-ML) integrates multiple UDA methods to address differences between nonlinear domains. In the field of risk assessment, algorithms based on adversarial feature alignment further reduce domain shift in disaster scenarios16. These studies indicate that transfer learning has potential in dealing with heterogeneous network data, but there are still issues such as negative transfer and insufficient cross protocol generalization in university environments.

Application of attention mechanism in network traffic and behavior recognition

The attention mechanism can highlight key information and is particularly important in high-dimensional network traffic characteristics. Combining residual networks and attention mechanisms in communication behavior recognition, modulation classification, and medical image anomaly detection can significantly enhance the model’s attention to key channels and improve generalization performance17. Liu et al. (2025) further demonstrated the synergistic effect of attention mechanism and deep network by enhancing multi-scale feature representation capability through structural optimization18.

These studies suggest that attention mechanisms can enhance feature selectivity, but their role in cross-domain transfer scenarios has not been fully studied. This study embeds lightweight channel attention into a transfer residual network to enhance sensitivity to sparse attack features and improve cross-domain recognition stability.

Collaborative governance and multi-subject intervention mechanism of family, school, and society

The combination of AI and educational governance has become a new research trend in the field of network behavior governance. Kam and Kim (2024) constructed a deep learning student psychological crisis prediction model19. Lérias et al. (2024) used machine learning to achieve automatic recognition of dormitory behavior in universities20. The empirical study by Lakshmi et al. (2023) emphasized the advantages of home, school, and community collaborative governance in controlling adolescent online behavior21.

However, existing governance methods mainly focus on qualitative research or rule-based systems, lacking data-driven dynamic game modeling capabilities. This study proposes a response collaboration mechanism based on multi-agent games, which can dynamically generate intervention strategies based on risk scores and achieve quantitative collaboration among families, schools, and communities.

Summary

In summary, existing research has achieved important results in the identification of network security in universities, transfer learning, attention mechanisms, and multi-agent governance. However, there are still problems: the domain distribution differences between different university networks have not been fully addressed; The negative transfer problem still lacks effective mitigation strategies in real-world scenarios. Therefore, this study proposes attention-enhanced transfer residual network + multi-agent collaborative governance framework, and realizes the complete chain optimization of cross-domain identification risk scoring multi-agent response.

Construction of the network security recognition model based on transfer learning

To enhance the robustness of cross domain recognition, this study extends the original three-layer transfer structure to a five layer transfer module, covering input embedding, shallow features, deep semantics, intermediate residuals, and classification decisions, achieving hierarchical feature alignment between the source and target domains. Meanwhile, a bidirectional MMD adaptive strategy is adopted, introducing a reverse alignment term for the target domain during training to construct symmetric distribution constraints, to alleviate the possible class boundary shift caused by one-way migration and enhance the adaptability to different subdomains. To improve the interpretability of migration, this study conducts feature contribution analysis after MMD alignment, identifies key protocol features through weight changes and mutual information evaluation, and uses t-SNE visualization to display the cross-domain clustering effect before and after alignment.

The domain adaptive transfer method used in this study is based on the theoretical basis that feature space alignment can reduce the classification risk caused by inconsistent source target distributions. University network users have a large scale, open access modes, diverse protocols, and data presents high-dimensional, unstable, and strong noise characteristics. The model needs to have strong distribution adaptability to maintain the generalization ability of attack recognition. Common attacks in datasets include DoS/DDoS, port scanning, identity spoofing, Trojan theft, and P2P abuse.

Considering the potential negative transfer risk in transfer learning, this study further introduces a multi-level feature filtering and bidirectional alignment mechanism to avoid excessive reliance on source domain noise features and improve the robustness of cross-domain recognition.

The introduction of the attention mechanism aims to simulate the information focusing process in human cognition. In high-dimensional network behavior features, attack behaviors often activate only a few key dimensions. The channel attention module dynamically adjusts the feature importance weights, effectively improving the model’s ability to respond to a small number of key signals, thus enhancing recognition stability and noise robustness in complex samples.

To ensure the model’s deployability in resource-constrained environments, the channel attention mechanism introduced in this study is designed based on a lightweight architecture without imposing significant computational burdens. Meanwhile, to verify its performance advantages under equivalent computational resources, various attention structures with equal parameter counts are set for comparative ablation experiments, including Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE), Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), and the developed lightweight module.

In cross-domain scenarios, negative transfer may occur when irrelevant or misleading knowledge from the source domain degrades the performance of the target domain. A collaborative response model based on multi-agent game theory is constructed to achieve collaborative governance of network risks among college students. The agents include the family, the school, and the community, with each party having decision-making and strategy rights in risk response. In the design of collaborative game strategies, the setting of subject response weights and incentive parameters refers to the parental participation, school decision-making processes, and community governance models under different cultural and management system backgrounds in different regions, thus enhancing the adaptability of the mechanism.

Model integration and flowchart design

The system operates as follows. Student network behavior data are first collected from campus terminals and processed through cleaning, encoding, and feature extraction. The transfer learning–based identification model then detects potential threats, labels events, and generates risk scores, which are passed to the family–school–community decision module. Based on the current game state, the system automatically recommends intervention strategies, while feedback from each party is used to update model parameters and response priorities.

The system workflow contains three flows: (1) data flow, from raw logs to feature vectors and risk scores; (2) control flow, covering model scheduling and parameter updates; and (3) response flow, describing the interaction among stakeholders. The integrated design supports distributed deployment and can be embedded into campus security systems for real-time warning and multi-agent collaboration (Fig. 2).Fig. 2System flowchart.

System flowchart.

To protect student privacy, the system incorporates federated transfer learning, ensuring that raw data never leave local nodes. Differential privacy is applied during feature extraction and alignment, adding controlled noise to sensitive fields, while the collaborative response module anonymizes all identification outputs. These mechanisms jointly enhance compliance and reduce ethical risks in AI-driven university network governance.

Experimental design and performance evaluation

Datasets collection and experimental environment

The target-domain data were collected from campus network gateway logs of three universities located in the eastern, central, and western regions, respectively. These institutions differ in policy environments, management systems, and network infrastructures, allowing the evaluation of cross-domain generalization and collaborative governance effects under diverse cultural and institutional backgrounds. All partner universities followed unified protocol-layer extraction and event-labeling standards, and data collection was approved through ethical review and user authorization.

Given the sensitivity of student behavior logs, multiple privacy-protection mechanisms were implemented. Differential privacy was applied during preprocessing to mask sensitive fields (e.g., access paths and MAC addresses), while federated learning enabled multi-university joint training without sharing raw data, ensuring compliance with educational data governance requirements.

The target-domain dataset includes real network traffic from 2021 to 2023 covering HTTP/HTTPS, DNS, FTP, and P2P protocols, with security platforms providing labels such as Trojan implantation, scanning, and brute-force attacks. The source-domain dataset consists of CIC-IDS2017 and NSL-KDD, offering complementary features for transfer learning. To assess adaptability to emerging protocols, additional traffic samples such as QUIC, gRPC, WebRTC, and Tor were included as an extended test subset. All data underwent unified cleaning, feature extraction (128-dimensional behavioral and protocol features), and label generation before modeling. The specific process is presented in Fig. 3:Fig. 3Experimental system architecture and data processing topology.

Experimental system architecture and data processing topology.

This study conducts experiments in a high-performance local server environment, running Ubuntu 20.04 LTS and building a deep learning model using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 2.0.1. The hardware platform consists of dual Intel Xeon Silver 4310 processors, two Nvidia RTX A6000 (48 GB) GPUs, 256 GB DDR4 ECC memory, and 4 TB NVMe SSD, and is deployed and run through Docker containers.

Parameters setting

The migration recognition model is trained using a learning rate of 0.0005 (Adam optimizer), a batch size of 64, and 50 training epochs with early stopping to avoid overfitting. A dropout rate of 0.3 is applied to mitigate feature co-adaptation. The model incorporates three migration layers with shared domain encoders and embeds a lightweight channel-attention module within the residual blocks. The MMD loss weight λ is set to 0.7 to balance classification accuracy and cross-domain alignment. Using CIC-IDS2017 as the source domain and campus network logs as the target domain, the model performs supervised training on the source domain while enforcing MMD-based feature alignment on the unlabeled target domain.

To ensure fair comparison, three baseline models—ResNet (no transfer), LSTM, and SVM—are configured using identical optimizers, batch sizes, and training epochs, with SVM adopting an RBF kernel (C = 1, γ auto-tuned). In addition, the collaborative governance mechanism is simulated based on a three-agent weighting framework23,24, where families, schools, and communities are assigned response weights of 0.25, 0.5, and 0.25, respectively, alongside constraints such as a 6-h response limit, a 0.65 intervention trigger threshold, dynamic risk-sensitivity coefficients (βH/βS/βC = 0.2/0.3/0.1), a minimum weight floor of 0.1, and normalization to maintain the total weight at 1.

Performance evaluation

Model performance comparison and ablation analysis

Several comparative experiments are conducted to validate the proposed method’s effectiveness in the context of campus network security identification and governance. Five evaluation metrics include Area Under the Curve (AUC), accuracy, recall, F1 score, and detection delay (milliseconds (ms)). The experiments involve multiple model baselines, diverse module combinations, and governance mechanism simulations. The compared models include: SVM, LSTM, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), standard ResNet (without migration), and the proposed Attention-Enhanced Transfer ResNet (AETR). An ablation study is conducted to comprehensively evaluate the contribution of each structural component to the overall performance. The following variants are designed. BaselineResNet does not include transfer or attention mechanisms. Transfer Only adds cross-domain representation sharing, but does not include alignment or attention mechanisms. Transfer + MMD includes domain distribution alignment, but does not include attention mechanisms. Transfer + Attention encompasses channel attention, but does not include MMD alignment. Complete AETR combines transfer learning, MMD alignment, and attention mechanisms. The performance of each variant is evaluated using the same dataset division and hyperparameter settings. Discussion

The proposed university network security identification and governance system demonstrates significant advantages through the combined strengths of transfer learning and the collaborative intervention mechanism. The transfer learning framework effectively mitigates label scarcity and feature distribution shifts in university networks, where heterogeneous behaviors and protocol variations frequently occur. By aligning source–target feature spaces and retaining supervised learning in the source domain, the model maintains stable detection performance across different subnets, user groups, and behavioral patterns. The lightweight channel attention module further enhances recognition sensitivity by selectively amplifying high-weight attack-related features, enabling the model to detect low-frequency harmful behaviors without increasing computational overhead. Beyond technical design, the collaborative governance mechanism improves intervention efficiency by incorporating quantifiable game feedback and dynamic strategy recommendation. Traditional university governance often suffers from fragmented responsibilities and slow responses, particularly during emergent security events. The proposed model utilizes risk scoring and dynamic weight adjustment to generate optimized intervention paths for families, schools, and communities, forming a more coherent governance loop. Despite its strong performance, several limitations remain. First, protocol generalization capability decreases slightly under highly encrypted or obfuscated traffic, such as QUIC and Tor, indicating the need for richer feature representations. Second, although pruning and FP16 acceleration reduce inference latency, high-concurrency scenarios may still trigger GPU bottlenecks, suggesting the value of heterogeneous or adaptive scheduling frameworks. Third, the risk-scoring mechanism does not yet fully incorporate social and cultural factors, which may affect accuracy in diverse governance environments. Finally, cross-regional deployment must address practical constraints related to data privacy and legal compliance. Future research will focus on localized adaptation of governance rules, reinforcement learning–based dynamic decision-making, and enhanced privacy-preserving deployment frameworks to improve system flexibility and real-world applicability.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel framework that combines attention-enhanced transfer learning with a game theory-based multi-agent response mechanism to address network security risks in heterogeneous university network environments. The model addresses domain distribution shift and label scarcity challenges through multi-agent feature alignment between source and target domains. It selectively emphasizes high-risk behavioral features via a lightweight attention mechanism to enhance adaptation. Experimental results on multi-campus datasets demonstrate high accuracy, generalization ability, and real-time response capability. The proposed collaborative governance mechanism dynamically generates intervention paths based on stakeholders’ intentions and risk scores, significantly improving the timeliness and success rate of responses. The framework has been proven effective in different institutional environments, showing strong deployment potential. Overall, this study confirms the feasibility and effectiveness of combining advanced machine learning technologies with responsive governance models for network security in educational environments. The study provides a scalable, adaptable, and privacy-focused method that can serve as a reference for intelligent risk management systems in education and other fields in the future.
