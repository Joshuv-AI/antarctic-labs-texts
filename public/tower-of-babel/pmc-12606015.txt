The Mediating Role of Dietary Behavior Among Rural Middle‐Aged and Young People in the Relationship Between Self‐Efficacy and Health‐Related Quality of Life: The Moderating Effect of Social Support—A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Xiaoran Wang, Shouzeng Niu, Dongyu Song, Jia Zhang, Xinyu Liu, Jing Zheng, Xiaoming Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) of rural middle‐aged and young people faces many challenges. This study aims to explore the relationship between self‐efficacy and HRQoL in this population, and the mediating role of dietary behavior and the moderating role of social support.MethodsThis study collected information from 718 rural middle‐aged and young people using convenience sampling and online survey questionnaires, used SPSS 26.0 and PROCESS 4.1 to test the hypothesis model.ResultsSelf‐efficacy positively predicted dietary behavior (β = 0.141, t = 3.771, p < 0.001). However, there was no significant relationship with HRQoL (β = 0.102, t = 1.846, p > 0.05). Dietary behavior significantly negatively predicted HRQoL (β = −0.154, t = −4.053, p < 0.001). In addition, the interaction term between dietary behavior and social support significantly positively predicted HRQoL (β = 0.094, t = 2.321, p < 0.05). It indicates that dietary behavior acts as a complete mediator, and social support can regulate the impact of self‐efficacy on HRQoL through dietary behavior.ConclusionMore attention needs to be paid to the HRQoL for rural middle‐aged and young people. Promoting social support and self‐efficacy may play a role in promoting health.

Introduction

In today's fast‐paced society, the health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) of middle‐aged and young adults, who are the backbone of social development, has emerged as a crucial topic in public health [1, 2]. Chronic work stress and occupational pressures in middle‐aged populations not only reduce HRQoL directly but also induce unhealthy dietary behaviors [3, 4]. Specifically, prolonged exposure to work‐related stress activates the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis, leading to sustained elevation of cortisol levels, which in turn exacerbates emotional exhaustion, anxiety, and depression—key components of the mental health dimension of HRQoL [2, 5]. Research has shown that the HRQoL levels of middle‐aged and young people with chronic diseases have significantly decreased [6], and due to fast‐paced lifestyles and workplace stress, most people suffer from severe sleep disorders or emotional exhaustion [5], highlighting the complexity of health risks and the urgency of intervention for this group. As a multidimensional health indicator covering physiological function, psychological health, social adaptation, and environmental interaction [7], HRQoL not only directly reflects an individual's functional status in the spectrum of disease and health, but also has a deeper impact on human capital accumulation and sustainable socioeconomic development [8]. The World Health Organization emphasizes (WHO) that improving HRQoL among young and middle‐aged people is a crucial step toward achieving “healthy aging” [9].

Self‐efficacy, as a core concept of social cognitive theory, is defined as an individual's confidence in performing behaviors that promote health [10], and plays a “psychological switch” role in health behavior decision‐making [11]. Research indicates that individuals with high self‐efficacy are more effective in activating cognitive resources, planning behavioral strategies, and maintaining goal‐directed efforts when facing health challenges [12]. Empirical research has found that there is a clear behavioral transmission pathway for the predictive effect of self‐efficacy on HRQoL, among cardiovascular disease patients, self‐efficacy indirectly improves physical function by promoting exercise adherence [13], in patients with chronic fatigue syndrome, improved stress management efficacy is related to reduced cortisol secretion and may improve symptoms through endocrine mechanisms [14]. However, existing research often focuses on the direct effects of self‐efficacy or single mediation pathways, lacking a systematic deconstruction of the health behavior mediation mechanisms specific to middle‐aged and young populations, particularly the pivotal role of dietary behavior.

Dietary behavior, as a key modifiable lifestyle factor, may serve as a theoretically significant mediating bridge between self‐efficacy and HRQoL. The young and middle‐aged population is at the peak of dual roles in “family and workplace,” and a healthy diet can provide the necessary energy and nutrients to support their high‐intensity work and life [3]. However, accelerated urbanization has promoted a nutritional transition [15], with increased consumption of processed foods and irregular eating schedules leading to vulnerable dietary patterns that directly affect their physical and mental state and quality of life [16], and may even have long‐term impacts on future health [17]. Longitudinally, poor dietary habits in middle age predict accelerated decline in HRQoL during aging, such as increased risk of metabolic diseases (e.g., diabetes, hypertension) and functional impairment (e.g., reduced mobility, self‐care ability) [18]. For rural young and middle‐aged populations, this long‐term impact may be exacerbated by limited access to healthcare resources, making early dietary intervention more critical [19, 20]. Healthy diets can directly benefit the physiological dimensions of HRQoL through biological mechanisms such as regulating gut microbiota and reducing systemic inflammation levels [21]. Additionally, the ritualistic characteristics of dietary behavior may promote HRQoL through psychological pathways, such as enhancing a sense of life control and improving emotional stability, suggesting the multidimensionality of mediating pathways [22]. It is noteworthy that the moderating effect of social support may reshape the aforementioned psychological‐behavioral‐health chain reaction [23]. Social support, as an important resource for individuals to cope with stress and maintain health, can come from various sources, including the warmth and care of family, sincere companionship from friends, collaboration and assistance from colleagues, and thoughtful care from social organizations [24], provides individuals with comprehensive emotional comfort, practical information, and tangible assistance [25]. Research by Aya et al. confirmed that participation in the Texercise Select lifestyle intervention improved middle‐aged and older adults' intake of fruits, vegetables, and water, as well as the level of specific dietary social support, with specific dietary social support playing a mediating role in the intervention's impact on fruit and vegetable intake [26].

Based on this, we propose a moderated mediation model testing three hypotheses: H1: Dietary behavior mediates the relationship between self‐efficacy and HRQoL in young and middle‐aged populations. H2: Self‐efficacy positively predicts healthy dietary behaviors. H3: Social support moderates the mediation effect of dietary behavior on HRQoL. In summary, this study constructs a moderated mediation model (Figure 1) to systematically and deeply investigate the relationships among self‐efficacy, dietary behavior, and HRQoL in young and middle‐aged populations, as well as the moderating role of social support.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This study targeted middle‐aged and young adults residing in rural Dongying City, and conducted an anonymous online questionnaire survey from December 2023 to December 2024 using convenient sampling. Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18 to 59 years; (2) residing in rural areas for at least 6 months; (3) no use of psychotropic medications before enrollment; (4) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) individuals with altered consciousness or psychiatric disorders; (2) those currently participating in other similar research projects; (3) unwilling participants. A total of 718 valid questionnaires were collected.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research Involving Humans of Dongying People's Hospital (Approval Number: DYYX‐2023‐218). All participants provided written informed consent before data collection.

Assessment Instruments

Demographic Information

The questionnaire was designed by the researcher based on literature review. The questionnaire included basic personal characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, educational level, monthly income, smoking, drinking and chronic diseases.

Self‐Efficacy

Chen et al. developed the New General Self‐Efficacy Scale (NGSES) in 2001 [27]. This study utilized a simplified version of the NGSES comprising three items, which assess confidence in achieving goals, adapting to change, and coping with challenges, with representative items such as: “When faced with difficult tasks, I am confident that I can accomplish them [28].” Responses are scored on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score reflects the participant's specific self‐efficacy across various situations, with higher scores indicating stronger self‐efficacy. The Cronbach's α for this scale was 0.950.

Dietary Behavior

The Chinese version of the Eating Behavior Scale short form (EBS‐BF) was utilized to assess participants' eating behaviors in this study [29]. This scale comprises seven dimensions: abnormal eating pace, satiety, eating style, body awareness, food content, alternative eating, and hydration, with one item dedicated to each dimension. Responses were scored on a 4‐point scale: 1 (strongly disagree), 2 (disagree), 3 (agree), and 4 (strongly agree). This scale uses reverse scoring, the total score reflects the participants' eating behavior, with higher scores indicate poorer dietary behaviors. The Cronbach's α was 0.917 in this scale.

HRQOL

The Chinese version of the EuroQol five‐dimensional five‐level (EQ‐5D‐5L) scale was utilized to assess the HRQoL of participants. The scale comprises five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. A 5‐point scoring system was employed, where scores of 1 to 5 indicate no problems, slight problems, moderate problems, severe problems, and extreme problems, respectively. The scores were converted into health utility values based on a utility value system derived from the preferences of the Chinese population [30]. The EQ‐5D‐5L is designed to measure an individual's health status, with higher health utility values reflecting better HRQoL. The Cronbach's α was 0.889 in this scale.

Social Support

The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) was utilized to assess participants' levels of social support across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support [31, 32], item example includes: “I can receive emotional help and support from my family when I need it.” A 7‐point Likert scale was employed, with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher total scores indicated greater perceived social support. The scale demonstrated a Cronbach's α of 0.950.

Method of Analysis

Data were inputted and a data set was constructed using Excel 2019. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Firstly, Harman's single‐factor test was used to evaluate common method biases. Secondly, independent sample t‐tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to evaluate the impact of demographic characteristics on the primary variables, and partial correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between variables. Finally, hypothesis testing and statistical evaluation were conducted using Model 14 in the PROCESS 4.1 plugin invented by Hayes, combined with the Bootstrap method and simple slope analysis [33]. All hypothesis tests in this study were conducted using two‐tailed tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Common‐Method Deviation Test

Harman's single‐factor test was conducted to test for common method bias, results indicated that three factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor explaining 30.289% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40% [34]. This suggests that significant common method bias is not present in this study.

Sample Characteristics

A total of 718 rural young and middle‐aged participants were included, with 51.9% female and 48.1% male. The 50–59 age group accounted for the largest proportion (38.8%), followed by 40–49 (35.0%), and 18–29 was the smallest (9.5%). Most were married (90.7%). Regarding education, junior high school graduates constituted the majority (47.6%), and bachelor's degree or above accounted for 18.2%. Monthly income of 1001‐3000 RMB group was the largest (37.6%). Most never drank (65.0%) or smoked (74.1%). Additionally, 13.4% had chronic diseases.

The results indicated significant differences in HRQoL among young and middle‐aged individuals based on chronic disease status (t = −2.973, p < 0.001), age (F = 6.231, p < 0.001), and education level (F = 4.160, p = 0.009), while no significant differences were found for the other independent variables. To mitigate the impact of these differences on the primary variables, chronic disease, age, and education level were included as control variables in subsequent analyses (Table 1).

Correlations

The results of the partial correlation analysis demonstrated that, significant correlations remained between self‐efficacy, dietary behavior, social support, and HRQoL after controlling for the covariants. Specifically, a positive correlation was observed between HRQoL and self‐efficacy (r = 0.124, p < 0.01), a negative correlation with dietary behavior (r = −0.108, p < 0.01), and a positive correlation with social support (r = 0.127, p < 0.01). Additionally, a positive correlation was found between dietary behavior and self‐efficacy (r = 0.140, p < 0.001), as well as between dietary behavior and social support (r = 0.131, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Moderated Mediation Analysis

To ensure the accuracy of the analysis, all data were standardized. As the results shown in Table 3, Equation 1 indicated a significant positive predictive effect of self‐efficacy on dietary behavior (β = 0.141, t = 3.771, p < 0.001). Equation 2 demonstrated that self‐efficacy did not predict HRQoL (β = 0.102, t = 1.846, p > 0.05), whereas dietary behavior had a significant negative predictive effect on HRQoL (β = −0.154, t = −4.053, p < 0.001). Additionally, the interaction term between dietary behavior and social support significantly predicted HRQoL positively (β = 0.094, t = 2.321, p < 0.05), indicating that social support moderated the effect of self‐efficacy on HRQoL through dietary behavior. Dietary behavior served as a complete mediator, while social support moderated the latter half of the mediating pathway.

To elucidate the moderating role of social support, this study investigated the impact of general self‐efficacy on HRQoL through dietary behavior as a mediating variable, under varying levels of social support. Specifically, for the low social support group (M‐1SD), dietary behavior was found to significantly negatively predict HRQoL (β = −0.248, t = −3.922, p < 0.001). In contrast, for the high social support group (M + 1 SD), the negative predictive effect of dietary behavior on HRQoL was not significant (β = −0.060, t = −1.300, p = 0.194). These findings indicate that increased levels of social support diminish the negative predictive effect of dietary behavior on HRQoL. Additionally, simple slope analysis revealed that as dietary behavior worsened, the HRQoL in the high social support group remained superior to that of the low social support group, with the decline in HRQoL being slower in the high social support group (Figure 2).

Discussion

The present study investigated the complex relationships among self‐efficacy, dietary behavior, HRQoL, and social support in a young and middle‐aged rural population. The findings provide robust support for the three proposed hypotheses.

The results confirmed a full mediation effect, where self‐efficacy did not directly predict HRQoL, but exerted its influence entirely through dietary behavior, which supports hypothesis 1. Specifically, higher self‐efficacy was associated with healthier dietary practices, which in turn predicted improved HRQoL. This aligns with social cognitive theory, which posits that self‐efficacy drives behavior change, ultimately impacting health outcomes, underscoring the central role of self‐efficacy in health behaviors [11]. Consistent with the findings of Bouwman et al. [35], it was demonstrated that brief self‐efficacy interventions can increase healthy dietary behaviors. Research focusing on specific groups, such as adolescents and middle‐aged individuals with type 2 diabetes, has indicated that self‐efficacy is one of the primary factors influencing healthy dietary behaviors, with dietary self‐efficacy showing significant importance in promoting healthful practices [36]. Therefore, the absence of a direct self‐efficacy‐HRQoL link underscores the necessity of targeting dietary behavior as a critical pathway for interventions aimed at enhancing quality of life in this population [37, 38].

Meanwhile, the study revealed that self‐efficacy positively predicted dietary behavior, while dietary behavior negatively predicted HRQoL, thereby supporting hypothesis 2. Previous studies have indicated that poor dietary habits, such as emotional eating and high‐risk dietary patterns, are closely associated with various health issues [39, 40, 41]. These health issues include nutritional imbalances, obesity, chronic metabolic diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [42, 43, 44, 45]. Furthermore, poor dietary habits have been linked to elevated stress levels, which may subsequently trigger anxiety and depression [2, 17]. Health literacy also plays a role in this pathway. Studies have shown that higher health literacy enhances the ability to translate self‐efficacy into healthy dietary behaviors [46], while educational level mediates socioeconomic influences on fruit and vegetable intake [47]. Other potential confounders may also influence the results, such as psychological well‐being, sleep quality, and work stress. Previous studies have shown that chronic stress can disrupt dietary rhythms and directly reduce HRQoL [48]. Additionally, poor sleep, prevalent in middle‐aged populations, may mediate the relationship between dietary behavior and HRQoL [49, 50]. Future studies should include these variables to control for their effects.

The demographic composition of the sample may also shape the observed relationships. The significant differences in HRQoL across age groups suggest that age‐related physiological changes could amplify the impact of dietary behavior on HRQoL, as older rural adults may face greater vulnerability to poor diet due to reduced physiological resilience. Similarly, the higher proportion of participants with junior high school education might moderate the translation of self‐efficacy into healthy dietary behaviors, as lower educational attainment could limit access to health information, potentially weakening the link between self‐efficacy and dietary practices. Additionally, the participants with chronic diseases—who exhibited lower HRQoL—may strengthen the negative association between poor dietary behavior and HRQoL, as chronic conditions can exacerbate the health consequences of suboptimal diets.

Additionally, the moderated mediation analysis revealed that social support significantly attenuated the negative impact of poor dietary behavior on HRQoL. At low social support levels, dietary behavior strongly predicted diminished HRQoL, whereas this effect became nonsignificant at high social support levels. This suggests that robust social networks buffer the adverse health consequences of suboptimal diets, likely through emotional reinforcement and practical assistance. This finding aligns with previous research, where Anderson et al. reported that support from family and friends positively influences dietary behaviors and quality among low‐income, urban minority adolescents [25]. Evidence has confirmed that improvements in diet‐specific social support contribute to increased fruit and vegetable intake [26]. Social support in rural China is characterized by close family and community networks, which is different from that in urban areas [19]. For instance, family‐based meal rituals in rural communities can enhance the positive impact of social support on dietary behavior. However, urbanization has disrupted this network, leading to greater consumption of processed foods, while rural populations may retain healthier traditional diets [20]. Therefore, in rural Settings, self‐efficacy may particularly influence dietary behavior by adhering to traditional health norms. Social networks, such as regular family meals and community food‐sharing practices, reinforce these behaviors, making individuals with high self‐efficacy more likely to utilize this social support to maintain a healthy diet, while low social support exacerbates the negative impact of low self‐efficacy on eating habits. This is in line with the view in social cognitive theory that social support and self‐efficacy may interact with each other [10]. Given these insights, promoting health among young and middle‐aged individuals could be achieved by enhancing self‐efficacy through health education and training. Simultaneously, greater support and assistance from family, friends, and society should be encouraged to create a supportive environment, ultimately aiming to improve dietary behaviors and HRQoL.

Collectively, these results validate a moderated mediation framework where self‐efficacy enhances HRQoL indirectly via dietary behavior, with social support acting as a protective moderator. Specific public health strategies could include: (1) Rural‐targeted health education programs to enhance self‐efficacy in meal planning and stress management; (2) Promoting family‐centered interventions via village committees to strengthen social support; (3) Integrating dietary guidance into primary healthcare services, leveraging local doctors to connect self‐efficacy building with actionable dietary goals. Such approaches could disrupt the cycle of poor dietary habits and low HRQoL, particularly in resource‐limited rural settings.

Limitations and Future Prospects

This study has certain limitations. The use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability to the broader rural population, as participants were not randomly selected. Additionally, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships between self‐efficacy, dietary behavior and HRQoL, and the self‐reporting method may be affected by recall and social expectation biases. Future research should employ probability sampling, increase sample size, and conduct comparative analyses across diverse settings. Longitudinal designs are recommended to establish temporal relationships. Incorporating objective measures of dietary behavior alongside self‐reports would improve data robustness.

Conclusions

This study focused on a rural young and middle‐aged population and examined the mediating role of dietary behavior between self‐efficacy and HRQoL using a moderated mediation model. The findings demonstrate that self‐efficacy does not directly influence HRQoL but operates entirely through its positive impact on healthier dietary practices, which subsequently enhance HRQoL. Furthermore, social support emerges as a protective buffer, mitigating the adverse effects of poor dietary behavior on HRQoL, particularly in individuals with stronger social networks. These results underscore the importance of integrating psychosocial and behavioral interventions to promote health in this demographic.
