Efficacy and Safety of Ropeginterferon Alfa-2b in Pregnant Women With Myeloproliferative Neoplasms: Case Series of Three Subtypes and Review
Authors: Hangyeol Kim, Sung-Eun Lee, Young Rok Do, Sung-Yong Kim, Junshik Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Managing myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) during pregnancy is challenging due to increased thrombotic risk and limited cytoreductive options. Ropeginterferon alfa-2b (ropeginterferon), a long-acting mono-pegylated interferon, has limited data in this setting. We report a Korean multicenter case series of four pregnancies across MPN subtypes, including prefibrotic/early primary myelofibrosis, essential thrombocythemia, and polycythemia vera. Ropeginterferon was used during pregnancy or prior to conception (250–500 μg every 2–12 weeks) with aspirin and/or low-molecular-weight heparin. Hematologic responses were sustained throughout pregnancy without treatment-limiting adverse events. All pregnancies resulted in live births without major maternal or neonatal complications. Within the limits of a small retrospective series, ropeginterferon appeared to be a feasible cytoreductive option in these MPN pregnancies, including pre-conception and intra-pregnancy use; these preliminary observations warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs)—polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF)—pose substantial challenges in women of reproductive age,123 conferring increased thrombotic and obstetric risk through combined physiologic hypercoagulability and a pathologic thrombophilic state.134 Compared with the general population, MPN pregnancies show higher miscarriage (20–26% vs. 12%) and lower live-birth rates (45–78% vs. 80–89%, worst in PV and PMF),5678 with excess maternal thrombosis, hemorrhage, pre-eclampsia, fetal growth restriction, and preterm birth.1248910 Because pregnancy is itself a prothrombotic state—conferring an approximately 4- to 5-fold increase in venous thromboembolism risk that rises further in the puerperium—superimposed on the myeloproliferative state, contemporary guidance effectively regards all MPN pregnancies as high-risk, irrespective of conventional disease-based risk stratification.1411

Management is complicated by the teratogenic potential of hydroxyurea and anagrelide,13111213 leaving interferon-alpha as the only established safe cytoreductive option.14151617 Ropeginterferon alfa-2b (ropeginterferon), a mono-pegylated interferon with an extended half-life, has shown superior tolerability over earlier formulations in non-pregnant patients,1819 but pregnancy data remain extremely limited.2021

To address this evidence gap, we report a Korean multicenter case series of ropeginterferon across MPN-subtype pregnancies to expand this limited evidence base and inform clinical decision-making.

This multicenter retrospective case series, following the CARE guidelines,22 included four pregnant women with MPN (prefibrotic/early PMF, ET, and PV) treated with ropeginterferon at four tertiary centers in Korea (2021–2025; Table 1). All consecutive ropeginterferon-treated MPN pregnancies were included, with none excluded. Follow-up (data cutoff February 2026) is ongoing, with postpartum durations of approximately 14, 18, 10, and 43 months for Cases 1–4, and no maternal or infant abnormalities have been observed yet.

The hematologic target in pregnancy is deliberately more permissive than the non-pregnant goal of normalization (e.g., < 400 × 109/L): we adopted a 400–600 × 109/L platelet target to balance thrombosis prevention against over-treatment and maternal cytopenia, while avoiding extreme thrombocytosis (> 1,000–1,500 × 109/L) (Supplementary Table 1).14111323

Clinical data—treatment exposure, concomitant antithrombotic therapy, hematologic parameters, and obstetric and neonatal outcomes—were collected from electronic medical records.

A 37-year-old woman with incidental leukocytosis and thrombocytosis was diagnosed as JAK2 V617F-positive prefibrotic/early PMF according to the 2022 World Health Organization classification criteria,24 with a hypercellular marrow (60–80%) and grade 1 reticulin fibrosis. Risk stratification using Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Scoring System for Transplantation-Age Patients with Primary Myelofibrosis (MIPSS70+ v2.0) and revised International Prognostic Score for Thrombosis in Essential Thrombocythemia (IPSET)-thrombosis indicated low and intermediate risk, respectively, and aspirin 100 mg daily was initiated.

Eleven months later, she presented at 5 weeks of gestation with marked thrombocytosis and leukocytosis (Fig. 1A). Although conventionally low-risk by MIPSS70+ v2.0, her pregnancy was regarded as high-risk owing to intermediate thrombosis risk (revised IPSET-thrombosis), marked thrombocytosis, and the proliferative prefibrotic/early PMF phenotype. Therefore, ropeginterferon was initiated at 250 μg subcutaneously, then escalated to 500 μg every 4 weeks by 13 weeks of gestation, with aspirin and prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH).

Platelet counts fell to 585 × 109/L by 22 weeks, and were maintained within the 400–600 × 109/L target, achieving a complete hematologic response. Transient grade 1 transaminase elevation and fever resolved without dose modification (Supplementary Table 1).

Fetal growth and placental function remained normal throughout pregnancy. Elective cesarean section at 39 weeks resulted in a healthy male infant. At 14 months postpartum, both mother and infant remained well.

This case suggests ropeginterferon may support adequate hematologic control and favorable obstetric outcomes even in prefibrotic/early PMF.

A 41-year-old woman with a history of three miscarriages was diagnosed with JAK2 V617F–positive ET. Although her ET was intermediate-risk by conventional disease-based thrombosis criteria (IPSET-T), her pregnancy was regarded as high-risk owing to a history of recurrent pregnancy loss. One year prior to planned assisted reproduction, treatment with aspirin and ropeginterferon (500 μg every 2 weeks) was initiated.

Hematologic parameters stabilized within 4–6 weeks, and a total of 18 doses were administered over 10 months. Following successful conception of dichorionic diamniotic twins, ropeginterferon was discontinued, and the patient was closely monitored throughout pregnancy.

Hematologic parameters remained stable after discontinuation throughout pregnancy (hemoglobin 12.2–14.1 g/dL; platelet count 296–472 × 109/L; white blood cell count 4.65–14.8 × 109/L) (Fig. 1B). Transient grade 1 headache, myalgia, and transaminase elevation at initiation resolved spontaneously (Supplementary Table 1).

The pregnancy course was uneventful, without thrombosis, hemorrhage, placental complications, or fetal loss. At 37 + 4 weeks of gestation, elective cesarean section resulted in the delivery of two healthy female infants with normal birth weights (2,890 g and 2,820 g, respectively; Supplementary Table 1).

This case suggests preconception control followed by discontinuation during pregnancy can sustain disease stability and favorable outcomes in this high-risk ET pregnancy with recurrent gestational loss.

A 36-year-old woman with JAK2 V617F–positive PV (diagnosed at 32 years of age) had a history of unexplained stillbirth at 28 weeks of gestation during a pregnancy at 34 years of age. Although low-risk by conventional disease-based PV criteria, subsequent pregnancy was regarded as high-risk owing to the prior stillbirth. She was therefore started on low-dose aspirin and ropeginterferon (500 μg every 12 weeks) before attempting a subsequent pregnancy.

A complete hematologic response was achieved after two doses, and she conceived approximately one year later. Hematologic parameters remained stable throughout pregnancy (hemoglobin 11.2–14.5 g/dL; hematocrit 31.8–43.9%; platelet count 229–514 × 109/L; white blood cell count 6.14–12.49 × 109/L) (Fig. 1C) without obstetric complications or recurrent stillbirth; a healthy male infant was delivered by cesarean section at 39 weeks (Supplementary Table 1).

This case suggests ropeginterferon may support disease control and favorable outcomes in PV with prior adverse obstetric history.

A 36-year-old nulligravid woman with JAK2 V617F–positive PV had been treated with hydroxyurea, anagrelide, and aspirin. Following the confirmation of her first pregnancy (5 weeks of gestation) at age 40, cytoreductive agents were discontinued, while aspirin was continued.

Two weeks later, marked rebound pan-myelosis developed, with the platelet count peaking at 2,074 × 109/L (white blood cell count 35.55 × 109/L; hematocrit 47.4%). Given the high thrombotic and bleeding risk—and although low-risk by conventional disease-based criteria, her pregnancy was managed as high-risk owing to the severe rebound pan-myelosis—ropeginterferon was reactively initiated at a platelet count of 1,762 × 109/L (at gestational age 13–14 weeks), with rapid dose escalation (maximum 500 μg by the third visit) (Fig. 1D); only transient grade 1 transaminase elevation occurred.

A partial response was achieved within 8–10 weeks (platelet 687 × 109/L), with subsequent disease control; a healthy male infant was delivered by cesarean section at 38 weeks (Supplementary Table 1).

This case illustrates a reactive rapid-escalation strategy when urgent control is required. The patient was previously published by Bang and Lee20; with the journal’s permission and the patient’s consent, the case is included here—without new clinical data—to contrast reactive rescue with the planned preconception approach of Cases 2 and 3.

In all four pregnancies, ropeginterferon was associated with durable hematologic responses and favorable outcomes. These outcomes appear promising relative to historical cohorts managed with aspirin and/or LMWH,25 particularly given the high-risk pregnancy features such as prior obstetric complications or marked thrombocytosis; however, this uncontrolled comparison should be interpreted with caution.134112627 Treatment was well tolerated, with no congenital abnormalities, intrauterine death, thrombosis, or major bleeding (Supplementary Table 1). These outcomes are notable against a still-limited evidence base (Table 2).

Contemporary recommendations endorse a risk-adapted approach: low-dose aspirin for nearly all MPN pregnancies, LMWH in high-risk or postpartum settings, and cytoreduction reserved for high-risk pregnancies (prior thrombosis, severe MPN-related obstetric complications, or extreme thrombocytosis), for which interferon-alpha is the agent of choice.1411 Within this framework, ropeginterferon offers favorable tolerability, although pregnancy data remain limited to isolated reports and pharmacokinetic findings.2021

Ropeginterferon was also selected due to practical considerations regarding drug availability in Korea: conventional interferons have largely been discontinued, and peginterferon alfa-2a faces severe global supply shortages. Consequently, ropeginterferon-being approved and reliably accessible domestically-served as the only available interferon at the time.20 In this context, our case series provides several insights.

First, in our limited experience, ropeginterferon was associated with appropriate hematologic responses and favorable pregnancy outcomes in each represented subtype, including prefibrotic/early PMF, a more proliferative phenotype.2829 However, with only one to two pregnancies per subtype, these observations remain preliminary. Second, preconception control followed by discontinuation during pregnancy (Cases 2 and 3) may maintain disease stability without the need for continuous cytoreduction. Third, dosing strategies can be adapted to specific clinical situations, including rapid dose escalation for rebound pan-myelosis, achieving stabilization within 8–10 weeks.

The safety profile appeared consistent with prior interferon use in pregnancy, with no clinically significant maternal or fetal adverse events observed, and recent pharmacokinetic data showing minimal transplacental passage and negligible breast-milk secretion provide further reassurance.21 None of the mothers breastfed, as all peripartum decisions predated the Tashi et al.21 report; these pharmacokinetic data may now inform individualized counseling.

This study is limited by its small sample size and uncontrolled retrospective design. Given the rarity of MPN pregnancies requiring cytoreduction, continued accumulation and reporting of real-world data will be essential to define the role of ropeginterferon.

In conclusion, real-world data on ropeginterferon in MPN pregnancy remain extremely scarce. Our experience suggests it may be a feasible cytoreductive option for selected high-risk MPN pregnancies; these preliminary observations should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.
