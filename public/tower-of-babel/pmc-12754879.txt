Prevalence and associated factors of hepatitis A virus infection in Gaza strip during the 2023–2025 conflict
Authors: Khalid Jamal Khadoura, Mona Abu Naseer, Aya Al-taramsi, Faten Al-faleet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis A virus (HAV) remains a major public health concern in humanitarian settings, especially where water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure is disrupted. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of HAV and identify sanitation-related risk factors during the 2023–2025 conflict in the Gaza Strip.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted in Gaza between March and June 2025 including 440 participants. Participants aged ≥ 6 years were recruited through convenience sampling. The survey assessed sociodemographic factors, HAV diagnosis and symptoms, and WASH-related conditions. Data were analyzed using logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for HAV infection. STATA ver. 14.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA) was used.ResultsAmong 440 participants (mean age 27.2 years, 57% female), 30.2% reported hepatitis A diagnosis during the 2023–2025 Gaza conflict. Infection was significantly higher among younger individuals and displaced populations, particularly residents of North Gaza. Living in shelters markedly increased infection risk (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 12.24; 95% CI: 4.66–32.15; p < 0.001). Poor sanitation conditions, including reliance on trucked water (AOR = 3.72; 95% CI: 1.19–11.60), inadequate handwashing before eating (AOR = 15.78; 95% CI: 6.24–39.89), and low satisfaction with toilet hygiene, were strongly associated with infection. Secondary education was protective (AOR = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.12–0.76). Interestingly, participants reporting rare access to clean drinking water had significantly lower odds of infection compared to those with consistent access (AOR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.03–0.45).ConclusionsThe outbreak in Gaza highlights the urgent need for prioritized interventions. Immediate WASH measures, targeted vaccination programs, and improved hygiene infrastructure in conflict zones to mitigate disease spread.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25488-w.

Introduction

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection is a major public health concern worldwide, particularly in low-resource and conflict-affected settings. The virus is primarily transmitted via the fecal–oral route, either through ingestion of contaminated water or food or via close contact with infected individuals [21]. While typically self-limiting, HAV can cause significant morbidity in older children and adults, and outbreaks can strain fragile healthcare systems.

Historically, HAV has been highly endemic in the Middle East, including the Gaza Strip. Seroprevalence studies in Gaza have reported rates exceeding 70% among children under 10 years in the early 2000 s [9, 10]. Over the past decade, improvements in sanitation in some areas and the absence of systematic vaccination programs have led to delayed exposure, increasing susceptibility among older children and adults [14, 15]. Despite these risks, there is currently no systematic peer-reviewed research on HAV prevalence and risk factors in Gaza during the ongoing conflict, representing a major knowledge gap that this study seeks to address.

The Gaza Strip faces unique challenges due to prolonged political instability, recurrent military escalations, and economic blockade. These factors have severely compromised sanitation, healthcare infrastructure, and access to clean water. In the 2023–2025 conflict, over one million individuals were displaced, many living in overcrowded shelters with limited access to hygiene services [7, 16, 17]. Vulnerable groups, including children, the elderly, and pregnant women, are particularly at risk of infection under these conditions.

Recent global studies highlight shifting HAV epidemiology in different regions. In Indonesia, the age at midpoint of population immunity has risen to young adulthood, indicating a transition from low to intermediate endemicity [13]. In Brazil, a comprehensive study found a 6.3% incidence of acute HAV infection among suspected hepatitis cases, with high anti-HAV IgG seroprevalence, emphasizing the need for targeted vaccination and prevention strategies [5]. These findings demonstrate global trends in HAV transmission and highlight the heightened risk for populations lacking vaccination coverage, such as in Gaza.

Zinszer and Abuzerr [23] found that WASH insecurity among displaced populations in Gaza was strongly associated with infectious disease outbreaks, including acute viral hepatitis. Recent evidence suggests several public health interventions have proven effective in reducing HAV transmission in comparable contexts. In Jordan, a routine two‐dose vaccination program targeting one-year-old children was modeled to reduce HAV incidence by 99% over 50 years and become cost-saving within six years of its introduction [6]. Seroprevalence studies in Jordan also show a shift in age of immunity, with children increasingly susceptible, reinforcing the need for such vaccination policies [3]. In Gaza, UNRWA has already responded to the hepatitis A outbreak among displaced populations with awareness campaigns, diagnosis services, and hygiene kit distributions in shelters, following reports of tens of thousands of cases in overcrowded settings with deficient water and sanitation [18, 19]. Globally, WHO emphasizes the importance of combining HAV vaccination, improved WASH infrastructure, and health education in humanitarian crises to prevent outbreaks and mitigate their impact [20].

This study therefore aims to fill critical knowledge gaps by estimating HAV prevalence among residents of the Gaza Strip during the 2025 conflict and identifying associated risk factors, including displacement, access to clean water and sanitation, and hygiene practices. Understanding these dynamics is essential to inform public health planning, targeted interventions, and outbreak prevention in conflict-affected regions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between March and May 2025 in the Gaza Strip, Palestine, during a period of active armed conflict. The Gaza Strip is a densely populated region facing ongoing humanitarian challenges, including displacement, overcrowding, and limited access to clean water and sanitation services. These conditions were further exacerbated during the 2025 conflict, resulting in heightened vulnerability to infectious diseases such as hepatitis A.

Study population and sampling

The study targeted residents of the Gaza Strip aged 6 years and older. Participants were selected using a non-probability convenience sampling technique due to the constraints of movement, safety, and communication during the conflict. Individuals were invited to participate voluntarily through community networks, local health organizations, and social media platforms. Eligibility criteria included: (1) current residence in Gaza during the outbreak period, (2) aged 6 years or older and able to communicate or express symptoms (3) ability to provide informed consent or assent (or assent for minors), (4) parental or guardian agreement to provide information on behalf of their child (where applicable), and (5) access to a digital device with internet connectivity.

The sample size was estimated using the formula for single population proportion, assuming a 50% estimated prevalence (to maximize sample size), a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. Using Open Epi online sample size calculation, 384 participants are required. To account for possible non-response or incomplete data, a 10% contingency was added, resulting in a final target sample size of approximately 422 participants.

A total of 467 individuals responded to the online questionnaire. After data screening, 27 responses were excluded due to participants being under 6 years of age or submitting incomplete questionnaires. The final analytical sample included 440 participants who met the inclusion criteria and provided complete, usable data.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered electronic questionnaire developed specifically for this study via Google Forms. The questionnaire was adapted from validated instruments and previous research on displaced populations in Gaza (Zinszer & Abuzerr, [23]). To minimize missing data, the Google Form was programmed so that submission was only possible once all questions had been answered. In addition, trained field assistants provided on-site support in shelters to ensure participants could complete the survey fully. These procedures contributed to the absence of missing values in the dataset.

The questionnaire was originally designed in English and later translated into Arabic. It was reviewed by a panel of public health experts and epidemiologists to ensure content validity. A pilot test was conducted with 20 individuals to assess clarity, coherence, and technical functionality; responses from the pilot were excluded from the final analysis.

Access to water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) was assessed using structured questions adapted from validated instruments and prior research in Gaza (Zinszer & Abuzerr,[23]). Items were recorded as categorical or Likert-scale responses (never, rarely, sometimes, always), with higher frequencies reflecting better WASH conditions. Pretesting confirmed clarity, cultural appropriateness, and reliability of the instrument.

Data analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms to Microsoft Excel and then analyzed using Stata version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, symptom profiles, and exposure-related variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Age was the only continuous independent variable in the analysis; its distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and both mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR) were reported to account for potential skewness.

Bivariate analyses were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables to assess associations with hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection status. Variables with p-values < 0.2 in bivariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of HAV infection. A backward stepwise elimination method was applied to derive the most stable model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.

Results

Participant characteristics and living situations

A total of 440 participants responded to the electronic survey. The mean age of respondents was 27.2 years (SD = 13.9), ranging from 6 to 80 years. Thus, age was skewed to the right, with a median of 24 (Interquartile Range (IQR): 18.5–33; n = 440). Of the total sample, the majority were female (n = 251, 57%) and 43% were male (n = 189). Participants characterstics (n = 440)

Hepatitis "A" exposure and diagnosis

Among the 440 study participants, 133 individuals (30.2%) reported being diagnosed with hepatitis A by a healthcare provider during the 2023–2025 Gaza conflict, indicating a high prevalence of infection and suggesting a substantial burden of infection during the conflict period. Of those diagnosed, 114 (25.9%) confirmed that their diagnosis had been verified through a laboratory test, while a small proportion (4.8%) were unsure about the confirmation status. Additionally, 64 participants (14.5%) reported having been diagnosed with hepatitis A more than once during the conflict, indicating possible reinfection or prolonged exposure to risk factors. Importantly, nearly half of the respondents (45.2%) reported that at least one family member had also been diagnosed with hepatitis A during the same period, highlighting the potential for intra-household transmission and shared environmental exposures. These findings emphasize the widespread impact of hepatitis A within affected communities and underscore the need for preventive interventions targeting both individual and household levels.

Month of hepatitis "A" infection onset

Among the 440 participants, 133 (30.2%) reported hepatitis A infection during the 2023–2025 Gaza conflict. Cases were reported throughout the period, with peaks in April–June 2024 and smaller clusters before and after these months. This pattern suggests the outbreak began around October 2023 and continued throughout the conflict, with notable increases in spring and early summer 2024 (Fig. 1). Clinical symptoms reported

Among participants diagnosed with hepatitis A (n = 133), the most commonly reported symptoms were fatigue (69.2%, n = 92), jaundice (66.9%, n = 89), loss of appetite (65.4%, n = 87), and nausea and/or vomiting (63.2%, n = 84). Other frequent symptoms included fever (60.9%, n = 81) and abdominal pain (58.6%, n = 78), while dark urine (39.1%, n = 52) and pale stool (34.6%, n = 46) were less common. Fatigue and jaundice were particularly prominent, emphasizing the significant clinical burden among affected individuals. Environmental and hygiene factors reported by participants (n = 440)

Regarding hygiene practices, less than 40% reported that water was always available for handwashing after using the toilet or before meals. Satisfaction with toilet cleanliness was also suboptimal, with only 35% reporting consistent satisfaction. These findings highlight significant challenges related to water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) during the conflict period, potentially contributing to the spread of infectious diseases such as hepatitis A. Moreover, the majority of participants (61.8%) reported relying on municipal tap water as their main source of drinking water during the conflict period. This was followed by trucked water, used by 24.8% of respondents, reflecting widespread dependence on alternative distribution systems. A smaller proportion of participants reported using well water (9.8%), while only 3.6% relied on desalinated bottled water, likely due to its limited availability and higher cost. These findings highlight a heavy reliance on insecure or potentially contaminated water sources, which may have contributed to the observed rates of hepatitis A infection in the affected population.

Governorate of residence was significantly associated with infection status (χ2 = 10.8, p = 0.013), with a higher proportion of infections reported in North Gaza (22.6%) compared to other regions. Educational level was also a key factor (χ2 = 14.1, p = 0.003), as participants with secondary or university education had lower infection rates compared to those with only primary or non-formal education.

Living and sanitation conditions during the war period were strongly associated with infection. Displaced individuals were significantly more likely to have contracted hepatitis A (90.2% vs. 79.1%, p = 0.005). Current living situation played a crucial role (χ2 = 28.9, p < 0.001); those residing in shelters (24.1%) and tent camps (39.1%) had significantly higher infection rates than those in permanent homes. Access to clean drinking water was a major predictor (χ2 = 26.2, p < 0.001), with infected individuals disproportionately reporting only rare (81.2%) or no access (14.3%). Source of drinking water also mattered (χ2 = 13.5, p = 0.004); those relying on trucked water were most frequently infected (69.9%).

Additional WASH-related factors were significantly associated with HAV infection, including poor access to functional toilets (χ2 = 32.6, p < 0.001), lack of water for handwashing after toilet use (χ2 = 27, p < 0.001) and before eating (χ2 = 69.8, p < 0.001), and low satisfaction with toilet hygiene (χ2 = 41, p < 0.001). These findings highlight the critical role of displacement-related WASH insecurity in facilitating the spread of hepatitis A during the conflict.

Living conditions emerged as a critical determinant, with individuals living in shelters facing markedly higher risk (AOR = 12.24; 95% CI: 4.66, 32.15; p < 0.001), underscoring the role of overcrowding and inadequate sanitation. Educational attainment was protective at the secondary level (AOR = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.12, 0.76; p = 0.011), suggesting that education may contribute to improved hygiene awareness and disease prevention.

Surprisingly, participants who reported rare access to clean drinking water had significantly lower odds of HAV infection compared to those with consistent access (AOR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.03, 0.45; p = 0.002). Those with no access also had lower odds (AOR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.20, 1.32; p = 0.66), though this was not statistically significant. These counterintuitive results may reflect reverse causality, where improved water access occurred after infection; or they may be influenced by recall bias or unmeasured confounders related to aid targeting or behavior changes. Alternatively, the "clean" water consistently accessed by some participants might itself have been contaminated, especially in a conflict setting with compromised infrastructure raising the possibility that access alone is not protective unless water safety is assured.

In terms of water source, reliance on trucked water as the main source for drinking was significantly associated with increased infection odds (AOR = 3.72; 95% CI: 1.19–11.60; p = 0.024), reinforcing concerns about its safety and quality. Furthermore, participants who rarely (AOR = 5.26; 95% CI: 1.21–22.85; p = 0.027) or never (AOR = 15.78; 95% CI: 6.24–39.89; p < 0.001) had water available for handwashing before eating exhibited significantly higher odds of infection compared to those with consistent access. This strongly suggests the protective role of hand hygiene in interrupting fecal–oral transmission. Finally, satisfaction with toilet hygiene was inversely associated with HAV infection. Participants who reported they were always satisfied with toilet hygiene had significantly lower odds of infection (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03–0.86; p = 0.033) than those who were never satisfied, emphasizing the importance of sanitation infrastructure in reducing disease burden.

Discussion

Key findings

Our study identified several independent risk factors for hepatitis A virus (HAV) infection during the 2023–2025 Gaza conflict. Residing in shelters emerged as the strongest predictor, followed by use of trucked water for drinking and constant availability of handwashing water before meals. Younger age and lower educational level were also associated with increased infection odds. These findings reinforce the critical role of overcrowding, environmental contamination, and inadequate water and sanitation infrastructure in driving hepatitis A outbreaks in Gaza.

Outbreak context and displacement conditions

Reports from Gaza health authorities and UN agencies indicate a dramatic surge in hepatitis A cases—from 85 pre-war cases to nearly 40,000 during the crisis—largely due to displacement, overcrowded shelters, deteriorated sanitation, and lack of clean water [18, 19]. A study of displaced individuals in southern Gaza (2023–2024) found a 32.8% self-reported HAV infection rate, associated with housing type, education, and poor hygiene conditions [2, 4]. The absence of routine hepatitis A vaccination leaves displaced children and young adults highly vulnerable, unlike in countries with systematic HAV immunization.

Regional and international evidence

Regional studies support our findings. In Jordan, use of filtered municipal water was linked to lower HAV prevalence, while lower educational levels increased risk, even outside conflict conditions [1]. In Uzbekistan, higher household access to “safe” water was paradoxically associated with rising HAV incidence [8], highlighting that water availability alone is insufficient without quality assurance and hygiene practices. These observations reinforce the importance of considering both water quality and behavior in outbreak prevention.

Age and education as protective factors

Younger age was associated with higher infection odds, consistent with HAV epidemiology: in populations with reduced early childhood exposure, older children and adolescents remain susceptible to symptomatic infection. Higher education appeared protective, likely reflecting better awareness of hygiene practices and ability to avoid unsafe food and water sources, in line with regional patterns [22].

Role of Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH)

Conflict-driven damage to Gaza’s water and sanitation infrastructure has been well documented [12],WHO EMRO, 2023; [11]. Over two-thirds of WASH facilities were destroyed by mid-2024, with sewage overflows exacerbating environmental contamination and viral transmission. Recent studies in humanitarian contexts consistently link WASH insecurity to increased incidence of enteric infections [23]. Our findings corroborate this, showing that inadequate water and sanitation, along with overcrowding, drive hepatitis A transmission.

Water access and handwashing behavior

Interestingly, participants reporting rare access to clean drinking water had lower odds of HAV infection, while “always available” handwashing water was associated with higher risk. These counterintuitive findings may reflect reverse causality (post-infection behavior changes), recall bias, or contamination of water sources. The results emphasize that water availability alone does not guarantee protection; both water quality and effective hygiene practices are essential.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include timely data collection amid an acute crisis, comprehensive multivariable analysis, and integration of clinical, environmental, and behavioral variables.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of a convenience sampling method and online data collection via Google Forms may have introduced selection bias, as not all residents of Gaza have reliable internet access. This could affect the generalizability of the findings, particularly for more vulnerable populations, such as the elderly or those living in marginalized or hard-to-reach areas.

Second, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between risk factors and HAV infection. While binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors, the model may be affected by residual confounding from unmeasured variables, and the assumption of linearity of the logit for continuous predictors (e.g., age) may not have been fully tested.

Third, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and reporting bias. This limitation is particularly relevant in a conflict setting, where participants may have difficulty accurately recalling exposures or may underreport certain behaviors due to stress, fear, or social desirability.

Fourth, the complex social, economic, and health dynamics in conflict-affected Gaza present challenges for data collection and interpretation. Barriers such as displacement, movement restrictions, and destruction of infrastructure may have limited access to certain populations, potentially leading to underrepresentation of specific groups. These contextual factors may also have affected response rates and the quality of the data collected.

Finally, the findings are context-specific, reflecting the unique humanitarian and epidemiological conditions of the 2023–2025 conflict in Gaza, and may not be generalizable to other settings or periods.

Future research should focus on longitudinal monitoring of HAV transmission in conflict settings, include more representative sampling of hard-to-reach populations, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted public health interventions, such as vaccination campaigns and WASH improvements. Studies incorporating laboratory-confirmed infection data and robust environmental assessments would strengthen causal inference and improve the reliability of findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates increased HAV infections in Gaza during the 2023–2025 conflict, with risk linked to displacement, overcrowding, and inadequate WASH services. Younger age, lower educational attainment, limited access to clean water, and substandard sanitation were independently associated with infection. Findings underscore the urgent need for targeted humanitarian interventions, including WASH restoration, hygiene promotion, and consideration of hepatitis A vaccination. Given the study’s cross-sectional and self-reported nature, causal interpretations should be cautious. Further research is required to explore sustainable preventive strategies and monitor long-term health impacts of conflict-driven environmental disruption.
