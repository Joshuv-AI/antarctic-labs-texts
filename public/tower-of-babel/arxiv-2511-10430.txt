Fuzzy Black Holes from Mass Generation in Matrix Compactification
Davide Laurenzano, and John F. Wheater

Abstract
We investigate a mechanism for generating mass terms in the IKKT and BFSS matrix theories through compactification on a torus and the derivation of a zero-mode effective theory, emphasizing the crucial role of fermionic boundary conditions. Extending a recent proposal developed for the IKKT model to the BFSS framework, we explore a broader class of mixed fermionic boundary conditions in both theories. This choice leads to a distinct effective theory with intermediate features, where a mass term is generated together with fermionic zero modes. In the BFSS case, this setup further allows for the construction of black hole solutions. The resulting geometry takes the form of a fuzzy sphere, with quantum excitations in the fermionic sector accounting for the corresponding black hole entropy.

Introduction

The search for a quantum framework which unifies all fundamental interactions, including gravity, has long been a central topic of research in Theoretical High Energy Physics. Indeed, the most promising candidate for such a framework is string theory. Perturbative formulations of String theory have, in research years, led to many theoretical breakthroughs. One of the main difficulties that ongoing efforts to derive testable predictions from String theory have to address is the fact that the space-time string theory lives in is constrained to be ten-dimensional. Hence, constructing observables proper to the four-dimensional space-time we live in requires dealing with compactification procedures.

Among the shortcomings of String theory is that it only provides a perturbative expansion in the string coupling
g_{s}
, and the theory is not well defined at strong coupling. Moreover, it assumes a fixed background, whereas we would like a quantum theory of gravity to be background independent; in fact, spacetime itself should be dynamical. Moreover, the presence of dualities connecting all five consistent superstring theories, as well as eleven-dimensional M-theory (the unknown theory which arises as the strong coupling limit of type IIA), hints at the existence of a single, more fundamental theory which encompasses all. With these motivations, non-perturbative formulations of string/M theory have been proposed, which take the form of matrix theories

We consider the case where the target space of the theory is compactified on a six-dimensional torus, where all directions have the same radius

3
IKKT with interchanging boundary conditions

In this section, we consider the Wilsonian effective action obtained by integrating out non-zero modes from the compact IKKT action with mixed boundary conditions. We expect mixed boundary conditions to result in an effective theory which is an intermediate case between the fully supersymmetric/no mass case and the effective theory with a mass term and no fermions. Our goal is to obtain a theory which, as a consequence of compactification, features fermionic degrees of freedom, while still giving rise to a mass term. We consider two types of interchanging boundary conditions: the first swaps the two components with the same chirality, and the other exchanges the chiralities.

S_{kin}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}}(2\pi n^{a})^{2}\mathrm{Tr}[A_{I}(-n^{a})A^{I}(n^{a})]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}(2\pi ir^{a})\mathrm{Tr}[\bar{\psi}_{+}^{\uparrow}(r^{a})\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\psi_{+}^{\uparrow}(r^{a})]\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}(2\pi ir^{a})\mathrm{Tr}[\bar{\psi}_{-}^{\uparrow}(r^{a})\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\psi_{-}^{\uparrow}(r^{a})]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}(2\pi is^{a})\mathrm{Tr}[\bar{\psi}_{+}^{\downarrow}(s^{a})\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\psi_{+}^{\downarrow}(s^{a})]\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}(2\pi is^{a})\mathrm{Tr}[\bar{\psi}_{-}^{\downarrow}(s^{a})\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\psi_{-}^{\downarrow}(s^{a})]+\sum_{{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}}(2\pi n^{a})^{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\bar{c}(n^{a})c(n^{a})]\,,

(20)

which shows that non-zero modes are massive. We will use this fact to integrate them out at one-loop, in order to obtain a Wilsonian effective action for the zero-modes. This is defined as

S^{eff}_{0}=-\ln\Bigl(\prod_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\nolimits^{\prime}\prod_{r^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\nolimits^{\prime}\prod_{s^{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}\int[\mathcal{D}A(n^{a})][\mathcal{D}\psi^{\uparrow}(r^{b})][\mathcal{D}\psi^{\downarrow}(s^{c})]e^{-S_{C}}\Bigr)\\
=S_{0}-\ln\langle e^{-S_{int}}\rangle-\ln Z_{kin}\,,

(21)

where
\ln Z_{kin}
is a constant, independent of the zero-modes, which we discard. The second term is the correction to the zero-mode effective action. The expectation value is computed in perturbation theory (we will evaluate it at one-loop) with respect to the kinetic action (
20
). The interaction can be decomposed as

S_{int}=\sum_{i=1}^{11}V_{i}\,,

(22)

where
V_{1},V_{2}
and
V_{11}
are purely bosonic or ghost terms, identical to the corresponding terms in
36

for

include fermions and differ from

We first examine the bosonic terms, which are given by

\displaystyle V_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\sum_{n^{a},m^{a},k^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[[A^{I}(-n^{a}-m^{a}-k^{a}),A^{J}(n^{a})][A_{I}(m^{a}),A_{J}(k^{a})]\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle V_{2}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{n^{a},m^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[2\pi(n^{a}+m^{a})A^{I}(-n^{a}-m^{a})[A_{a}(n^{a}),A_{I}(m^{a})]\Bigr]\,.

Note that at least one of the modes summed over is a zero-mode. The interaction term with ghosts reads

V_{11}=-\lambda\sum_{m^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}2\pi m^{a}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bar{c}(m^{a})[A_{a}(0),c(m^{a})]\Bigr]\,.

There are also interaction terms which only involve non-zero modes, but those are integrated over in the Wilsonian effective theory calculation, and since they do not depend on zero-modes, they contribute to the zero-modes effective action as a constant which can be ignored.

The interaction term for fermions is composed of a periodic and an anti-periodic component. These are completely disconnected from each other. We define
V_{3},...,V_{6}
to involve periodic spinors, given by

\displaystyle V_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\lambda\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{+}(r^{a})\Gamma^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{+}(r^{a})]\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle V_{4}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\lambda\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{-}(r^{a})\Gamma^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{-}(r^{a})]\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle V_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\lambda\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{+}(r^{a})\sigma^{\mu}_{\uparrow}[A_{\mu}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{-}(r_{a})]\Bigr]\,,

\displaystyle V_{6}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\lambda\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{-}(r^{a})\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}_{\uparrow}[A_{\mu}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{+}(r_{a})]\Bigr]\,.

The terms
V_{7},...,V_{10}
are obtained by swapping
\psi^{\uparrow}_{\pm}\leftrightarrow\psi^{\downarrow}_{\pm}
,
\sigma^{\mu}_{\uparrow}\leftrightarrow\sigma^{\mu}_{\downarrow}
and considering half-integers winding modes. Here

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu}_{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}\sigma^{\mu}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\sigma^{\mu}_{ij}=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(23)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu}_{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}\sigma^{\mu}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{ij=1}^{2}(-1)^{i+j}\sigma^{\mu}_{ij}=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,.

We can now compute the effective action for zero modes derived from the interaction terms above. Starting with bosons and ghosts, for which the computation proceeds analogously to
36
, we have

S_{0}^{eff}\supset\langle V_{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{11}^{2}\rangle

and expectation values are computed using the propagators

\displaystyle\langle A^{N}(n^{a})A^{M}(m^{a})\rangle=\frac{\delta^{MN}\delta_{n^{a}+m^{a},0}}{(2\pi n^{a})^{2}}

\displaystyle\langle\bar{c}(n^{a})c(m^{a})\rangle=\frac{\delta_{n^{a},m^{a}}}{(2\pi n^{a})^{2}}\,.

The result reads

\displaystyle\langle V_{1}\rangle=9\lambda^{2}NS_{B_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{I}(0)A_{I}(0)]

\displaystyle\langle V_{2}^{2}\rangle=2\lambda^{2}N\bigl((17S_{B_{2}}+S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]+S_{B_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{\mu}(0)A_{\mu}(0)]\bigr)

\displaystyle\langle V_{11}^{2}\rangle=-2\lambda^{2}NS_{B_{2}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]\,,

and the factors

S_{B_{1}}=\sum_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\nolimits^{\prime}\frac{1}{(2\pi n^{a})^{2}},\quad S_{B_{2}}=\sum_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\nolimits^{\prime}\frac{(2\pi n^{1})^{2}}{(2\pi n^{a})^{4}}\,,

(24)

come from propagators in the loop. Note that these sums are divergent, but the infinite part can be regularised, as shown in

, and separated from the finite component.

We now turn our attention to contributions to the effective action coming from fermions in the loop. These are obtained by integrating out fermions from interaction terms
V_{3},...V_{10}
. Fermionic propagators are inferred from (

\langle\bar{\psi}^{u}_{p,\alpha}(r^{a})\psi^{v}_{q,\beta}(s^{a})\rangle=-i\frac{2\pi r^{a}\Gamma^{a}_{\alpha\beta}\delta^{uv}\delta_{p,q}\delta_{r^{a},s^{a}}}{(2\pi r^{a})^{2}}\,,

where

,

and
\alpha,\beta=1,...,8
are

S_{0}^{eff}\supset-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{3}^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{4}^{2}\rangle-\langle V_{5}V_{6}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{7}^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle V_{8}^{2}\rangle-\langle V_{9}V_{10}\rangle\,.

(25)

Recalling that
\psi^{\uparrow}_{\pm}
has periodic boundary conditions and
\psi^{\downarrow}_{\pm}
has anti-periodic ones, a straightforward application of Wick’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\langle V_{3}^{2}\rangle=-4\lambda^{2}N(2S_{B_{2}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]

\displaystyle\langle V_{4}^{2}\rangle=-4\lambda^{2}N(2S_{B_{2}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]

\displaystyle\langle V_{7}^{2}\rangle=-4\lambda^{2}N(2S_{F_{2}}-S_{F_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]

\displaystyle\langle V_{8}^{2}\rangle=-4\lambda^{2}N(2S_{F_{2}}-S_{F_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{a}(0)A_{a}(0)]\,,

where
S_{F_{1}}
and
S_{F_{2}}
are propagators in the loop analogous to (

S_{F_{1}}=\sum_{s^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}\frac{1}{(2\pi s^{a})^{2}},\quad S_{F_{2}}=\sum_{s^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}\frac{(2\pi s^{1})^{2}}{(2\pi s^{a})^{4}}\,.

These terms are also divergent but can be regularised with the same procedure as the bosonic ones, and the divergent part is the same

. In particular, the difference
S_{F}-S_{B}
is a finite number which can be evaluated numerically.

Cross terms in (

\langle V_{5}V_{6}\rangle
. After contracting fermionic fields, we obtain

\langle V_{5}V_{6}\rangle=\lambda^{2}NS_{B_{1}}\sigma^{\mu}_{\uparrow}\bar{\sigma}^{\nu}_{\uparrow}\mathrm{Tr}[A_{\mu}(0)A_{\nu}(0)]\,,

which we can rearrange using (

\sigma^{\mu}_{\uparrow}\bar{\sigma}^{\nu}_{\uparrow}\mathrm{Tr}[A_{\mu}(0)A_{\nu}(0)]=4\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)+A^{2}_{1}(0)]\,,

and hence

\langle V_{5}V_{6}\rangle=4\lambda^{2}NS_{B_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)+A^{2}_{1}(0)]\,.

(26)

Analogously, one can compute

\langle V_{9}V_{10}\rangle=4\lambda^{2}NS_{F_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)+A^{2}_{1}(0)]\,.

(27)

Note that (
26
) and (
27
) imply that fermions in the loop only contribute to the mass of two of the components of bosonic matrices in the
SO(4)
sector, thus breaking the
SO(4)
symmetry.

We can now summarise all contributions to the zero modes effective action (
21
) to order
O(\lambda^{2})
in perturbation theory. First, the action
S_{0}
for zero-modes at tree level receives contributions from both the bosonic part and the periodic components of fermions, and it is given by

S_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}[A^{I}(0),A^{J}(0)]^{2}+i\bar{\psi}_{+}^{\uparrow}(0)\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi_{+}^{\uparrow}(0)]+i\bar{\psi}_{-}^{\uparrow}(0)\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi_{-}^{\uparrow}(0)]\\
-i\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{+}(0)[A_{0}(0)+iA_{1}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{-}(0)]+i\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{-}(0)[A_{0}(0)-iA_{1}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{+}(0)]\Bigr]\,.

Including the above one-loop corrections, we obtain the full effective action

S_{0}^{eff}=S_{0}-\lambda^{2}N\Bigl(4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)+A^{2}_{1}(0)]\\
-8S_{B_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{2}(0)+A^{2}_{3}(0)]+\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\Bigr)\,.

(28)

Note that the choice of interchanging boundary conditions (
15
) breaks the
SO(4)
symmetry subgroup in two ways: through the interaction term, which couples fermionic zero-modes to specific components of the bosonic field; and through the mass matrix, which is diagonal but takes different values for different components in the four-dimensional subspace. Moreover, these boundary conditions effectively reduce the number of fermionic degrees of freedom which contribute to loop diagrams, because of the decoupling of
\psi^{\uparrow}
and
\psi^{\downarrow}
modes. As a consequence, supersymmetry is broken at one-loop level and hence fails to cancel divergences. In particular, we find a divergent mass

M^{2}_{2}=M^{2}_{3}=-16\lambda^{2}NS_{B_{1}}

for two components of the bosonic field. This highlights a complication in generating a mass term alongside fermionic degrees of freedom from torus compactifications of IKKT with mixed boundary conditions. We will show in the next subsection that this issue also arises with different choices of boundary conditions. As we will see in section
5
, the same issue does not arise in BFSS theory, where we explicitly find a class of mixed boundary conditions which does not break supersymmetry at one-loop level and gives a finite result. This constitutes an interesting qualitative difference between the two models, which would be worth exploring further.

We can consider renormalising the divergence by introducing an
ad hoc
mass counterterm in the compact action on the torus (
22
). This is artificial from the point of view of the original IKKT model, where this term is forbidden by supersymmetry and global
SO(10)
. However, we explicitly showed how both these symmetries are broken in the compact action, so this term is allowed at the level of the EFT. We have freedom in choosing the renormalisation scheme. The choice we make is to cancel the divergence by restoring supersymmetry at one-loop level. This amounts to introducing a counterterm action given by

S_{CT}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int\frac{d^{6}\sigma}{(2\pi L)^{6}}16NS_{F_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{2}(0)+A^{2}_{3}(0)]\,,

such that

S^{eff}_{0}=S_{0}-\ln\langle e^{-S_{int}}\rangle-\ln Z_{kin}+S_{CT}\\
=S_{0}-\lambda^{2}N\Bigl(4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)+A^{2}_{1}(0)]\\
+8(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{2}(0)+A^{2}_{3}(0)]+\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\Bigr)\,.

This choice is natural in the sense that it reinserts the contribution coming from fermions in the loop, that have been lost due to the mixed boundary conditions. Of course, other choices are possible, and the finite value of the resulting mass is scheme dependent. We then rescale fields back by inverting (
19
), and Wick rotate to restore Lorentzian signature, resulting in

S_{0}=-\frac{1}{2g^{2}_{eff}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}[A^{I}(0),A^{J}(0)]^{2}+\bar{\psi}_{+}^{\uparrow}(0)\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi_{+}^{\uparrow}(0)]+\bar{\psi}_{-}^{\uparrow}(0)\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi_{-}^{\uparrow}(0)]\\
-\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{+}(0)[A_{0}(0)-A_{1}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{-}(0)]+\bar{\psi}^{\uparrow}_{-}(0)[A_{0}(0)+A_{1}(0),\psi^{\uparrow}_{+}(0)]\Bigr]

and

S_{0}^{eff}=S_{0}+\frac{N}{L^{2}}\Bigl(4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)-A^{2}_{1}(0)]\\
-8(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{2}(0)+A^{2}_{3}(0)]-\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\Bigr)\,.

As expected, the theory we obtain shows mixed qualitative features compared to the purely periodic or anti-periodic cases: here parts of the fermions are still present in the zero modes action, alongside a mass term. Both contributions break Lorentz symmetry explicitly.

Note that there are other options for boundary conditions, which also produce infinite mass terms in the effective action and require renormalisation. For example, we can give opposite boundary conditions to different components of the two-dimensional spinor or duplicate fermions and give each component different boundary conditions.

Another possibility would be to consider the theory to be compactified on a
\mathbb{T}^{4}
. Then, upon considering the decompactification limit with mixed boundary conditions, we can obtain an effective theory with a broken
SO(6)
subgroup and an unbroken
SO(4)
.

In the next subsection, we consider explicitly a second type of mixed boundary conditions, which swap around chiral components of the spinors.

3.2
Chiral Reversing boundary conditions

A further type of boundary condition that can be considered is swapping chiral components of fermions when cycling around the torus. These are implemented by

(U^{a})^{-1}\psi_{+}^{A}U^{a}=\psi_{-}^{A},\qquad(U^{a})^{-1}\psi_{-}^{A}U^{a}=\psi_{+}^{A}\,.

In other words, we decompose

\psi^{A}_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}+\psi^{A}_{\downarrow}),\quad\psi^{A}_{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}-\psi^{A}_{\downarrow})\,,

where
\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}
has periodic boundary conditions and
\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}
is anti-periodic, and their Hilbert space decomposition (
4
) reads

\displaystyle\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}(n^{b})\otimes e^{in^{b}p^{b}}

\displaystyle\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}(n^{b})\otimes e^{ir^{b}p^{b}}\,\,.

Substituting (
29
) into the compact action (
9
), we get the fermionic action

S_{F}^{CR}=\int\frac{d^{6}\sigma}{(2\pi L)^{6}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\uparrow}^{A}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\partial_{a}\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}+\frac{1}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\downarrow}^{A}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}\partial_{a}\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}-\frac{i}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\uparrow}^{A}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a},\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}]-\frac{i}{2}\bar{\psi}_{\downarrow}^{A}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a},\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}]\\
+\frac{i}{2}\bar{\psi}^{A}_{\uparrow}(\sigma^{j})_{AB}[A_{j},\psi^{B}_{\uparrow}]-\frac{i}{2}\bar{\psi}^{A}_{\downarrow}(\sigma^{j})_{AB}[A_{j},\psi^{B}_{\downarrow}]\Bigr]\,,

(29)

where the index
j=1,2,3
runs over the three space components of
SO(4)
and the superscript
CR
stand for
chiral reversing
boundary conditions. Note that the
A_{0}
component of the bosonic vector is not coupled to spinors, due to the relation (
8
) which forbids terms in the form
\bar{\psi}^{A}_{\downarrow}(\sigma^{0})_{AB}[A_{0},\psi^{B}_{\uparrow}]
. After expanding fermionic fields in modes such as

\displaystyle\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}(n^{b})e^{2\pi iLn^{b}\sigma^{b}}

\displaystyle\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}+1/2}\psi_{\downarrow}^{A}(r^{b})e^{2\pi iLr^{b}\sigma^{b}}\,\,,

we obtain the fermionic propagator

\langle\bar{\psi}^{A}_{u,\alpha}(r^{a})\psi^{B}_{v,\beta}(s^{a})\rangle=-i\frac{2\pi r^{a}\Gamma^{a}_{\alpha\beta}\delta^{A,B}\delta_{u,v}\delta_{r^{a},s^{a}}}{(2\pi r^{a})^{2}}\,,

where
u,v=\uparrow,\downarrow
. Integrating out non-zero fermionic modes, we get in this case

S_{0}^{eff}=S_{0}^{CR}-\lambda^{2}N\bigl(-8S_{B_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)]+4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{i}(0)]+\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\bigr)\,,

(30)

and

S^{CR}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}[A^{I}(0),A^{J}(0)]^{2}+i\bar{\psi}_{\uparrow}^{A}(0)\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}[A_{a}(0),\psi_{\uparrow}^{A}(0)]+i\bar{\psi}^{A}_{\uparrow}(0)(\sigma^{i})_{AB}[A_{i}(0),\psi^{B}_{\uparrow}(0)]\Bigr]\,.

Note that the zero-modes effective theory (
29
) is significantly different from (
28
). In particular, it features an explicit coupling of fermionic modes to all components of the bosonic vectors, except the zeroth one. The latter precisely corresponds to the bosonic component, which acquires a divergent mass, due to the cancellation of some of the fermionic interactions which contribute at one loop. We can add a counterterm to the compact action in order to renormalise this divergence, and our choice of renormalisation scheme at one loop is

S_{CT}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int\frac{d^{6}\sigma}{(2\pi L)^{6}}16N\,S_{F_{1}}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)]\,,

such that the renormalised effective action for zero modes reads

S_{0}^{eff,R}=S_{0}^{eff}+S_{CT}=S_{0}^{CR}-\lambda^{2}N\bigl(8(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)]\\
+4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{i}(0)]+\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\bigr)\,,

which is finite and preserves the
SO(3)
symmetry subgroup of the
SO(4)
. We can rescale fields back and go to Lorentzian signature

S_{0}^{eff,R}=S_{0}^{eff}+S_{CT}=S_{0}^{CR}+\frac{N}{L^{2}}\bigl(8(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{0}(0)]\\
-4(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{i}(0)]-\frac{8}{3}(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})\mathrm{Tr}[A^{2}_{a}(0)]\bigr)\,.

The conclusion is that mixed boundary conditions for fermions in IKKT theory dramatically change the zero-mode effective theory. They break part of the supersymmetries at one loop, thus causing divergent contributions to mass terms to appear in the effective action. These can be renormalised in order to obtain a finite result. As a result, a subgroup of
SO(1,3)\times SO(6)
is broken by the newly generated mass terms.

In the next two sections, we will turn our attention to BFSS theory. We will see that, in this case, the situation is different and, in particular, there is a class of mixed boundary conditions which do not break any subgroup of the global symmetry
SO(3)\times SO(6)
.

4
Compactification of BFSS theory

In this section, we extend the mechanism of torus compactification to the BFSS matrix theory. We will consider the cases of periodic and anti-periodic boundary conditions for fermions, and show that the result is qualitatively analogous to the corresponding cases in IKKT theory.

In BFSS theory, compactifications on a six-dimensional torus are ill-defined
41
;
42
. We therefore consider compactifications of the complementary coordinates on a three-dimensional square torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, where each of the dimensions has size
L
. We expect that, in the decompactification limit, the results should be qualitatively equivalent, still producing different mass terms for the two sets of directions. We will show that this is indeed the case.

BFSS matrix theory is a
SU(\mathcal{N})
gauge theory with gauge field
A
in the adjoint representation. The other degrees of freedom are a nine-component vector
X^{I}
,
I=1,...,9
of bosonic
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrices and a sixteen-component spinor
\psi_{\alpha}
,
\alpha=1,...,16
, where each component is a Grassmann-valued
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrix. It is a quantum mechanical theory, with time variable
t
, and action, in Lorentzian signature, given by

S_{BFSS}=\int dt\frac{1}{g^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{D}_{t}X^{I})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}[X^{I},X^{J}]^{2}+i\bar{\psi}\mathcal{D}_{t}\psi+\bar{\psi}\Gamma^{I}[X_{I},\psi]\Bigr]\,,

where
\Gamma^{I}
are gamma matrices in nine dimensions. This theory is maximally supersymmetric and has a global
SO(9)
symmetry, which can be interpreted as a subgroup of target space Lorentz symmetry, where the
X^{I}
matrices play the role of space coordinates. It has been conjectured
5
to be dual to M-theory in the Infinite Momentum Frame and, as such, it provides a non-perturbative definition of M-Theory, which makes it a very interesting candidate for a theory of quantum gravity. Analysing compactifications of matrix theory thus provides a potential path to a quantum gravity theory in the symmetry broken phase, where three large space dimensions emerge. Here we will reprise the discussion of
36
, taking into account the cases of anti-periodic and periodic boundary conditions for fermions. In the next section, we will extend the discussion to include mixed boundary conditions, analogously to what we did in section
3
for the IKKT model.

It will be convenient to work in Euclidean signature. To this end, we Wick rotate by means of

t\rightarrow it,\quad A\rightarrow iA,\quad\Gamma^{I}\rightarrow i\Gamma^{I}\,,

and we obtain the Euclidean action

S_{BFSS}^{E}=\int dt\frac{1}{g^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{D}_{t}X^{I})^{2}-\frac{1}{4}[X^{I},X^{J}]^{2}+\bar{\psi}\mathcal{D}_{t}\psi-i\bar{\psi}\Gamma^{I}[X_{I},\psi]\Bigr]\,.

(31)

Note that, in this signature, gamma matrices satisfy

\{\Gamma^{I},\Gamma^{J}\}=-2\delta^{IJ}

with a minus sign. The next step is to define a translation operator on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. This is done analogously to what we have seen in section
2
, by defining a Hilbert space acted upon by matrices, which is decomposed into two components, as in equation (
7

\displaystyle A(t)=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}A(t,n^{b})\otimes e^{in^{b}p^{b}}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle X^{\mu}(t)=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}X^{\mu}(t,n^{b})\otimes e^{in^{b}p^{b}}\,,

\displaystyle X^{a}(t)=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}X^{a}(t,n^{b})\otimes e^{in^{b}p^{b}}+2\pi L\mathbb{I}_{N}\otimes q^{a}\,,

\displaystyle\psi(t)=\sum_{r^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}+1/2}\psi(t,r^{b})\otimes e^{ir^{b}p^{b}}\,,

where we are considering the case of anti-periodic fermions explicitly, and we will comment on the periodic case later on. Here, indices are
\mu=4,...,9
and
a=1,2,3
, such that indices labelled by Latin letters
a,b,..
correspond to compact directions (note that this is different from previous sections). Note that all the degrees of freedom now have an explicit time-dependence. We substitute the decomposition (
32
) into the Euclidean action (
31
) and define the mode expansion of the fields

\displaystyle A(t,\sigma)=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}A(t,n^{b})e^{2\pi iLn^{b}\sigma^{b}}\,,

\displaystyle X^{\mu}(t,\sigma)=\sum_{n^{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}X^{\mu}(t,n^{b})e^{2\pi iLn^{b}\sigma^{b}}\,,

5
BFSS with Mixed Boundary Conditions

In this section, we consider a class of mixed boundary conditions for fermions which give rise to an effective theory with intermediate qualitative features. Namely, we will show that we can obtain a zero-mode effective theory where half the fermions are quenched, the remaining half being still present, and a mass term is generated.

In contrast to the IKKT case treated above, we will show that BFSS theory admits a class of mixed boundary conditions which preserves supersymmetry at one loop. As a consequence, the effective action is finite and does not require renormalisation. Most importantly, we will show that it features both a symmetry-breaking mass term and fermionic fields.

In the next section, we will leverage this construction to study black hole solutions in the theory we have obtained. We will see how these can be constructed explicitly in terms of the degrees of freedom of the theory, where both bosons and fermions play a key role.

6
Black hole in the zero-modes theory

In this section, we consider possible solutions of the theory (

Here, the compactification procedure of BFSS with mixed boundary conditions provides a top-down approach, where the theory we study is derived directly from BFSS theory.

6.1
Fuzzy sphere background

The key idea consists of considering classical solutions to the bosonic part of (

A=0,\quad c=0,\quad X^{a}=0\,,

Furthermore, we consider static solutions with

From a target space perspective, this amounts to saying that, for those solutions, six spatial coordinates have no extent, whereas the remaining three transverse directions are macroscopic. Note that the zero-mode effective action obtained as a consequence of torus compactification of the original matrix theory lives in a non-compact target space.
We can write the relevant part of the Hamiltonian obtained from (

The net effect is that of a solution living in five total dimensions, one of which is longitudinal and has size

A state of given energy is thus constructed by filling up some energy shells completely and partially filling up the highest contributing energy shell. The degeneracy

Recall that we are working in units such that the Planck length is one.

M_{5D}=r_{0}^{2}\,,

r_{0}
being the Schwarzschild radius. The bosonic part of the Hamiltonian yields, when evaluated on the fuzzy sphere background,

H_{bos}=-\frac{R\,k^{2}}{2}NR^{2}_{f}\,,

where
R_{f}=\frac{k\,N}{2}
is the physical fuzzy sphere radius in the large
N
limit, and the fermionic energy gives

H_{ferm}=\frac{2}{3}\bigl(1+y\bigr)RNk^{2}R_{f}^{2}\,.

Adding these together, the total energy, at leading order in
N
, reads

E_{LC}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{6}+\frac{4y}{6}\Bigr)RNk^{2}R^{2}_{f}\,,

and the condition that the fuzzy sphere sits behind its Schwarzschild radius,

r_{0}\geq R_{f}\,,

imposes

y\geq\frac{1}{8}\,.

Note that, when the bound is saturated, we have
r_{0}=R_{f}
and the Schwarzschild radius is exactly equal to the fuzzy sphere radius. Since, given the fuzzy sphere background (
46
), all the degeneracy is captured by the fermionic sector of the theory, the entropy of the configuration with
y=\frac{1}{8}
, which is given by

S=\log\Omega=\log{\binom{N-3}{y(N-3)}}\approx\alpha(N-3),\qquad\alpha\approx 0.38\,,

scales linearly with
N
in the large
N
limit. The coefficient
\alpha
comes from Stirling’s approximation. The appropriate entropy-radius relation for this configuration, namely

S\propto r_{0}^{3}\,,

is only satisfied if
r_{0}\sim N^{\frac{1}{3}}
, which requires
k\propto N^{-\frac{2}{3}}
. Note that the scaling of
k
follows from the mass term, which is generated through compactification, see equation (
44
). This, in turn, is related to the torus dimension
L
(eq. (
43
)). It follows that the compactification limit and the large
N
limit should be taken in a double-scaled fashion, such that

L\propto N^{\frac{7}{6}},\qquad N\rightarrow\infty\,.

(49)

The scaling of
S\propto N
is the one expected for the transition between a black hole localised in the longitudinal direction and a black string stretched along it
24
. Furthermore, if the scaling is
S\propto N^{\beta}
,
\beta\uparrow 1
, we are approaching the transition, corresponding to the Gregory–Laflamme instability from the black hole state to the black string. In our model, we can construct solutions with lower entropy by choosing a larger fraction of fermionic oscillators to be excited in the largest shell of the Fermi sea
y>\frac{1}{8}
. This, in turn, causes the Schwarzschild radius to be larger than the fuzzy sphere radius. The physical picture sees the black hole/black string transition occurring when the radius of the fuzzy sphere approaches the Schwarzschild radius.

We can, in particular, realise a black hole configuration with entropy
S\propto N^{\alpha},\,0<\alpha<1
by exciting a fraction
y
of oscillators in the leading shell, such that
y
tends to
1
parametrically with
N
. In this case, the degeneracy given by Stirling’s formula for large
N
is

\Omega\sim\frac{e^{NH(y)}}{2\pi Ny(1-y)},\quad H(y)=-y\log{y}-(1-y)\log{(1-y)},\quad 0<y<1\,.

Choosing

y=1-\frac{cN^{\alpha-1}}{\log{N}},\quad 0<\alpha<1\,,

c
being an arbitrary constant of order
1
, we obtain

S=\log{\Omega}=NH(y),\quad H(y)\sim(1-\alpha)cN^{\alpha-1}\,,

at leading order in
N
. Again, applying the entropy-radius relation and the relation between the Schwarzschild radius and the generated mass, we obtain the corresponding scaling of
k
as

k\propto N^{\frac{\alpha}{3}-1}\Rightarrow L\propto N^{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{3}}\,.

Our approach, therefore, shows that it is possible to construct a black hole solution as a fuzzy sphere background plus fermions in a state that generates the black hole entropy. We saw that, by taking the decompactification limit in the original theory in a specific way, see equation (
49
), we can approach the limiting case of the black hole/black string transition, represented by the entropy scaling like
S\sim N
. Moreover, we recover the known relations between physical quantities characterising a black hole.

The fuzzy sphere solution we found in this section is still valid even for different values of
L
and, therefore of the mass parameter
M_{X}
. These are solutions which do not saturate the Bekenstein bound on the entropy. Depending on the number of excited fermionic oscillators, the fuzzy sphere radius can be larger or smaller than the Schwarzschild radius. We leave a full characterisation of these solutions, possibly involving the analysis of bosonic quantum fluctuations, to future work.

6.3
Fermionic theory on the fuzzy sphere background

In this section, we prove that the fermionic Hamiltonian consists of a collection of harmonic oscillators when evaluated on the fuzzy sphere background. We consider the operator

\mathrm{X}:=\sigma_{i}[\tilde{X}^{i},\cdot],\quad\mathrm{X}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\times End(\mathcal{V}_{j})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}\times End(\mathcal{V}_{j})

which acts on the space of
N\times N
matrices as endomorphisms of the spin
j
representation. This shows up explicitly in the effective coupling
g^{2}_{eff}=\frac{g^{2}}{N}
. The
\mathbb{C}^{2}
factor is the space of two-dimensional complex spinors acted on by
\sigma^{i}
. We call this an adjoint-like representation, constructed from the spin
j
representation of
SU(2)
.

Let us now consider the square operator

\mathrm{X}^{2}=\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigl[\tilde{X}^{i},[\tilde{X}^{j},\cdot]\bigr]\,,

which, as a consequence of (
46
), satisfies

\mathrm{X}^{2}-\mathrm{X}-\bigl[\tilde{X}^{i},[\tilde{X}^{i},\cdot]\bigr]=0\,,

(50)

where the operator

\Delta:=\bigl[\tilde{X}^{i},[\tilde{X}^{i},\cdot]\bigr]

is the Casimir operator of the adjoint-like representation and defines a generalisation of the Laplacian to the non-commutative fuzzy sphere geometry. Indeed, this acts as the identity on the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
component. We can decompose

End(\mathcal{V}_{j})\cong\mathcal{V}_{j}\otimes\mathcal{V}^{*}_{j}\cong\mathcal{V}_{j}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{j}\,,

where the second isomorphism comes from the fact that
SU(2)
representations are real. It follows that we can carry out a Clebsch-Gordon decomposition

End(V_{j})\cong\bigoplus_{k=0}^{2j}\mathcal{V}_{k}\,,

(51)

such that the Casimir operator acts on each factor as

\Delta|_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}=k(k+1)\mathbb{I}_{2k+1}\,,

(52)

where the dimension of each Clebsch-Gordon factor is
dim(\mathcal{V}_{k})=2k+1
. It follows that, given an eigenvector
v_{\lambda}
of
\mathrm{X}
, equation (
50
) reads

\lambda^{2}v_{\lambda}-\lambda v_{\lambda}-\Delta v_{\lambda}=0\,.

We decompose
v_{\lambda}
consistently with (
51
)

v_{\lambda}=v_{0}+v_{1}+...+v_{2j}

and use (
52
). As a consequence of linear independence, we get

\bigl(\lambda^{2}-\lambda-k(k+1)\bigr)v_{k}=0,\quad\forall k

which implies that, for every
j
,
v_{\lambda}=v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalues

\lambda_{\pm,j}=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1+4j(j+1)}}{2}.

In particular, we can define

j=\frac{n-1}{2}

such that

\lambda_{\pm,n}=\frac{1\pm n}{2}

and the degeneracy of
\mathrm{X}|_{\mathcal{V}_{j}}
(the dimension of the Clebsh-Gordon factor) is
2n
. Moreover,

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{X}|_{\mathcal{V}_{n}})=0

due to the
\sigma
part of the operator, hence the sum of all eigenvalues is zero. It follows that, calling
r
the number of
\lambda_{+}
eigenvalues, we have

r\lambda_{+,n}+(2n-r)\lambda_{-,n}=0\Rightarrow r=\frac{2n\lambda_{-,n}}{\lambda_{-,n}-\lambda_{+,n}}=n-1\,.

Therefore, we can perform a spectral decomposition of
\mathrm{X}|_{\mathcal{V}_{n}}
in terms of eigenvalues and eigenvectors

\mathrm{X}|_{\mathcal{V}_{n}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{+,n}U^{j,n\dagger}_{a\alpha\gamma}U^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{-,n}V^{j,n\dagger}_{a\alpha\gamma}V^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}\,,

where
U^{j,n}
and
V^{j,n}
are eigenvectors corresponding to positive and negative eigenvalues respectively, obtained by representing
\mathcal{V}_{n}
in the space of
N\times N
matrices, such that
a,b=1,2
are spinor indices and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\sigma=1,...,N
are matrix indices. It follows from (
51
) that the full operator is

\mathrm{X}=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\mathrm{X}|_{\mathcal{V}_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{+,n}U^{j,n\dagger}_{a\alpha\gamma}U^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{-,n}V^{j,n\dagger}_{a\alpha\gamma}V^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}\Bigr)\,.

Having obtained a spectral decomposition for the operator, we substitute it back into the fermionic Lagrangian (
47
). This allows us to define fermionic oscillators

\xi^{j,n}:=U^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}\lambda^{\uparrow}_{b\beta\sigma},\quad\chi^{j,n\dagger}:=V^{j,n}_{b\beta\sigma}\lambda^{\uparrow}_{b\beta\sigma}

which satisfy canonical anti-commutation relations

\{\xi^{j,n},\xi^{k,m\dagger}\}=\delta^{jk}\delta^{n,m},\quad\{\chi^{j,n},\chi^{k,m\dagger}\}=\delta^{jk}\delta^{nm}.

As a consequence, the fermionic Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}_{ferm}=R\,k^{4}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{+,n}\xi^{j,n\dagger}\xi^{j,n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{-,n}\chi^{j,n\dagger}\chi^{j,n}-\frac{n^{2}-1}{2}\Bigr),

which proves the claim.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we analysed torus compactifications of IKKT and BFSS theories as a mechanism for the generation of a mass term in matrix theories. Building on recent results, we imposed a novel set of boundary conditions on fermionic degrees of freedom in IKKT theory. These amount to acting with a non-trivial operator on the spinor when winding around any of the torus dimensions. In the case of IKKT, we analysed two kinds of mixed boundary conditions. In both cases, the action on the spinor is not a symmetry of the compact theory. As a consequence, some fermionic degrees of freedom decouple and do not contribute to loop diagrams, thus failing to cancel bosonic divergences. Hence, the theory requires some
ad hoc
renormalisation. In the decompactification limit, non-zero modes become heavy and can be integrated out, thus obtaining an effective theory for zero modes. Such a theory breaks the original
SO(1,9)
symmetry to a subgroup of
SO(1,3)\times SO(6)
, where the
SO(1,3)
itself is broken to a subgroup.

We then analyse BFSS theory, firstly with anti-periodic boundary conditions, and show that the resulting effective theory generates a mass term and all fermions are quenched. Then, we considered mixed boundary conditions. In this case, we were able to act on spinors with a symmetry operator when winding around the torus. As a consequence, divergences cancel automatically and no renormalisation is required. The result is an effective theory with broken
SO(9)\rightarrow SO(3)\times SO(6)
, a mass term, and half the fermionic degrees of freedom compared to the initial theory.

In the last part of the paper, we take this top-down construction as a starting point for explicitly characterising black hole solutions using matrix theory degrees of freedom. We find black hole spacetime being described by a fuzzy two-sphere, while fermionic excitations and their degeneracies give rise to the black hole entropy. In this way, we provide a direct construction of a black hole in matrix theory, with features that are consistent with previous studies of Schwarzschild black holes in the M-theory region of BFSS theory. In particular, we reproduce the
S\leq N
scaling of the entropy. The entropy-to-radius relation is also reproduced, but this requires the torus dimension
L
to go to infinity as a power of the matrix size
N
, in the decompactification limit.

It would be interesting to investigate the saturated value of the entropy further, in order to assess whether the black hole/black string transition can be captured by this construction. In particular, a possible future direction would be the explicit construction of black string solutions from matrix degrees of freedom.

Fuzzy sphere solutions have been considered in IKKT in connection to the emergence of gravity from the matrix model, see e.g.
44
;
34
;
20
. As an interesting future direction, one could try to replicate the analysis we carried out in the context of BFSS theory to the zero-modes effective theory obtained through torus compactification with mixed fermionic boundary conditions of IKKT. The main difference to the polarised IKKT model
20
is that the mass deformation derived through compactification breaks supersymmetry of the compact action. It would be interesting to explore the consequences of this.

In this work, we have considered BFSS theory at zero temperature, in the M-theory region of parameter space. It would be interesting to repeat the analysis for generic temperature and to look at other regions of parameter space. For instance, one could consider the ’t Hooft limit of BFSS theory at higher temperature, where it is known to be dual to the black brane solution of type IIA supergravity
28
. The evaporation of the black brane has been fully characterised in matrix theory
8
and shown to reproduce a Page curve for the entanglement entropy
14
. It would be interesting to extend the construction presented in this paper to the supergravity regime of the parameter space. Achieving such a result would open the possibility of studying the black hole evaporation mechanism explicitly. This, in turn, would provide full theoretical control over the time-dependent dynamics of the process, offering valuable insight into the information paradox.

Appendix A
Loop propagator regularisation for BFSS

In this section, we extend the regularisation method of
36
for loop propagators to the case of coefficients arising from loops in the Wilsonian effective theory analysis of compact BFSS theory.

We begin by considering the bosonic sum

S_{B_{1}}=\sum_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\nolimits^{\prime}\int_{\infty}^{+\infty}dp\frac{1}{p^{2}+n^{a}n_{a}}\,,

and we introduce a regulator
\alpha
such that

S_{B_{1}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\sum_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\nolimits^{\prime}\int_{\infty}^{+\infty}dp\frac{e^{-\alpha^{2}n^{a}n_{a}-\alpha^{2}p^{2}}}{p^{2}+n^{a}n_{a}}\,.

We rewrite this as

S_{B_{1}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\sum_{n^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{6}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{0}^{+\infty}dte^{-(t+\alpha^{2})n^{a}n_{a}}e^{-(t+\alpha^{2})p^{2}}\,,

which can be rewritten in terms of the
\theta
-function

\theta(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\pi tn}\,,

such that

S_{B_{1}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{+\infty}dte^{-tp^{2}}(\theta^{3}(t)-1)\,.

Focusing on the divergent part of the integral, we have

\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\,e^{-tp^{2}}(\theta^{3}(t)-1)=\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\frac{e^{-tp^{2}}}{t^{3/2}}(\theta^{3}(1/t)-1)+F(\alpha;p)\,,

where

F(\alpha;,p)=\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\,e^{-tp^{2}}\bigl(\frac{1}{t^{3/2}}-1\bigr)

represents the divergent component of the integral. Here we made use of the property of the
\theta
-function

\theta(t)=\frac{1}{t^{1/2}}\theta(1/t)\,.

By rearranging the various terms, we obtain

S_{B_{1}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{1}^{+\infty}dt\bigl[\frac{e^{-p^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{t}}+e^{-p^{2}t}\bigr](\theta^{3}(t)-1)+\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dpF(\alpha;p)\,,

where the first integral is convergent, and we regularised the divergence to be in the second term.

We now consider the fermionic (in the case of anti-periodic boundary conditions) sum

S_{F_{1}}=\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}+1/2}\int_{\infty}^{+\infty}dp\frac{1}{p^{2}+r^{a}r_{a}}\,,

which we regulate as

S_{F_{1}}=\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\frac{e^{-\alpha^{2}((r^{a}+u^{a})^{2}+p^{2})}}{p^{2}+(r^{a}+u^{a})^{2}},\quad u^{a}=\frac{1}{2}(1,...,1)\,.

We can rewrite this as

S_{F_{1}}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{0}^{+\infty}dt\sum_{r^{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}e^{-(\alpha^{2}+t)(p^{2}+(r^{a}+u^{a})^{2})}=\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{+\infty}dt\,\theta^{3}(t|u)e^{-tp^{2}}\,,

where the generalized
\theta
-function takes the form

\theta(t|u)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-\pi t(n+u)^{2}}\,.

Again, we look at the divergent part, which reads

\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\,e^{-tp^{2}}\theta^{3}(t|\frac{1}{2})=\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\,\frac{e^{-tp^{2}}}{t^{3/2}}(e^{-\frac{3}{4}\pi t}\theta^{3}(\frac{1}{t}|\frac{it}{2})-1)+\tilde{F}(\alpha;p)\,,

where

\tilde{F}(\alpha;p)=\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}dt\,\frac{e^{-tp^{2}}}{t^{3/2}}

and we used the property

\theta(t|a)=\frac{e^{-\frac{\pi}{4}t}}{t^{1/2}}\theta(\frac{1}{t}|\frac{it}{2})\,.

It follows that

S_{F_{1}}=\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{1}^{+\infty}dt\bigl(\frac{e^{-p^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{t}}(e^{-\frac{3}{4}\pi/t}\theta^{3}(t|\frac{i}{2t})-1)+e^{-p^{2}t}\theta^{3}(t|\frac{1}{2})\bigr)+\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\,\tilde{F}(\alpha;p)\,,

where the second term is the divergent one.

As a consequence, when we consider the difference
(S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}})
, the divergent parts cancel out, giving

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\bigl(\tilde{F}(\alpha;p)-F(\alpha;p)\bigr)=\int_{\alpha^{2}/\pi}^{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\,e^{-tp^{2}}=2\sqrt{\pi}\,,

such that the difference reads

S_{F_{1}}-S_{B_{1}}=2\pi^{3/2}-\pi\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\int_{1}^{+\infty}dt\Bigl[\bigl(\frac{e^{-p^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{t}}+e^{-p^{2}t}\bigr)(\theta^{3}(t)-1)\\
-\bigl(\frac{e^{-p^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{t}}(e^{-\frac{3}{4\pi}t}\theta^{3}(t|\frac{i}{2t})-1)+e^{-p^{2}t}\theta^{3}(t|\frac{1}{2})\bigr)\Bigr]\,,

which is a finite number and can be evaluated numerically.

Appendix B
Filling up the Fermi sea

In this section, we focus on the fermionic Hamiltonian (
48
), which represents a collection of fermionic oscillators. Here we prove that filling up the shell with energy
\lambda^{+}_{N-2}
is required in order to interpret the fuzzy sphere solution as a black hole. To this end, it is enough to show that filling up the Fermi sea up to the previous shell leads to an inconsistency.

We begin by noting that
\lambda_{-,n}=\lambda_{+,n-2}
, so that for
\lambda_{-,2}=\frac{1}{2}<E_{n}<\lambda_{+,N-2}=\frac{N-1}{2}
the shell with energy
E_{n}
(ignoring the prefactor) is composed of the
n-3
oscillators
\xi^{j,n-2}
and the
n+1
oscillators
\chi^{j,n}
, giving total dimension
\#(E_{n})=2n-2
. It follows that the shell with the largest dimension is the one with energy
E_{N-1}=\lambda_{-,N-1}
, having dimension
\#(E_{N-1})=2N-4
. Moreover, the state with the largest degeneracy is the one for which such a shell is half-filled, which yields

\Omega=\binom{2N-4}{N-2}\propto\frac{2^{2N-4}}{\sqrt{N}}\,.

At leading order in the large
N
expansion, we can compute the fermionic energy contribution from the shell with energy
E_{N-1}
. This follows straightforwardly from (
48
). In particular, if we excite
j=(1+x)(N-2)
oscillators,
-1<x<1
being the fraction of excited oscillators, then the energy reads

H_{ferm}=\frac{R\,k^{4}}{6}xN^{3}+O(N^{2})=\frac{2R\,k^{2}}{3}xN\,R^{2}_{f}

where
R_{f}=\frac{k\,N}{2}
is the physical fuzzy sphere radius in the large
N
limit. At the same time, the bosonic part of the Hamiltonian yields, when evaluated on the fuzzy sphere background

H_{bos}=-\frac{R\,k^{2}}{2}NR^{2}_{f}

So the total energy reads

H=\frac{R\,k^{2}}{6}(4x-3)NR^{2}_{f}\,.

As explained in section
6.2
, the Hamiltonian gives the lightcone energy, and it is related to the invariant mass through

H=E_{LC}=\frac{R}{N}M^{2}\,.

The mass-radius relation

M_{5D}=r_{0}^{2}

then implies

r_{0}=\Bigl(\,\frac{2}{3}(4x-3)\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{4}}R_{f}\,.

In order for the fuzzy sphere geometry to represent a black hole, its radius must be smaller than the Schwarzschild radius. It follows that

x>\frac{9}{8}

which is larger than
1
, in contradiction with the Fermi sea being filled up to the
E_{N-1}
shell. Hence, collapsing the shell behind the Schwarzschild radius requires filling the next level with energy
\lambda^{+}_{N-2}
, which proves the claim.
