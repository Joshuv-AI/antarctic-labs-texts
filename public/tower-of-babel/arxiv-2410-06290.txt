Score Design for Multi-Criteria Incentivization
Anmol Kabra, Mina Karzand, Tosca Lechner, Nati Srebro, Serena Wang

Abstract
We present a framework for designing scores to summarize performance metrics. Our design has two multi-criteria objectives: (1) improving on scores should improve all performance metrics, and (2) achieving pareto-optimal scores should achieve pareto-optimal metrics. We formulate our design to minimize the dimensionality of scores while satisfying the objectives. We give algorithms to design scores, which are provably minimal under mild assumptions on the structure of performance metrics. This framework draws motivation from real-world practices in hospital rating systems, where misaligned scores and performance metrics lead to unintended consequences.



1
Introduction

The use of numerical metrics to evaluate performance and guide decision-making is common practice in healthcare, education, business, and public policy.
It is common for agencies to design
surrogate scores
that summarize performance metrics, in a way that aligns incentives with performance metrics.
Often the scored entities strategically optimize surrogates and end up degrading on metrics, a phenomenon commonly known as
unintended consequences
and pithily conveyed by Goodhart’s law
[
28
,
53
]
:

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

Agencies thus aim to ensure that optimizing scores leads to improved metrics.
As the number of performance metrics can be large in practice
[
58
,
43
]
, agencies must design
succinct
multi-dimensional surrogate scores.
We present a framework to study this
minimal design problem
, and propose score designs that prevent unintended consequences.

Our work is directly motivated by real-world examples in safety-critical domains such as healthcare and education, where manifestations of Goodhart’s law exemplify the serious ramifications of unintended consequences.
When Pacificare, a healthcare provider, incentivized hospitals in 2003 to perform certain medical procedures to improve quality of care, several unrepresented metrics deteriorated
[
37
]
.
Similar misalignment between performance metrics and score-based hospital ratings, used by the Medicare agency (CMS), has been widely critiqued
[
54
,
13
,
35
,
1
,
51
,
3
]
.
Even so, CMS uses these score-based ratings to incentivize hospital policies
[
19
,
20
]
.
Hence, it aims to design scores so that improving on scores also improves all performance metrics.
This goal motivates the
improvement objective
in our framework.
In a similar vein, rating agencies such as USNews aim to incentivize efficient use of hospital resources through published scores
[
56
]
.
On multi-dimensional metrics, the efficiency goal
[
47
]
naturally translates into the notion of pareto-efficiency, which motivates the
optimality objective
in our framework.

We present a framework for designing scores to summarize performance metrics.
We give three natural design restrictions that align with real-world interpretability desiderata
[
18
,
56
]
, and propose score designs that satisfy the multi-criteria objectives under these restrictions.
Striving for succinct scores, we formulate our design to minimize the dimensionality of scores.
We give polynomial-time algorithms to design these succinct scores, which are provably minimal under mild assumptions on the structure of performance metrics.
While existing work on score design for incentivization studies scalar scores
[
36
,
31
,
50
,
59
]
, we design scores of smallest dimensionality to satisfy the multi-criteria objectives.
These objectives are unsatisfiable with scalar scores in general.

1.1
Designing surrogate scores from performance metrics

In our model, the agency aims to design a surrogate score function
S:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{S}
given a set of performance metrics
\mathcal{F}
of hospitals.

Hospitals report to agencies like CMS and USNews on hundreds of performance metrics such as condition-specific death rates, readmission rates, and percentages of patients receiving satisfactory care
[
18
,
16
,
56
]
.
We can denote the values of
d
metrics of a hospital with a real-valued vector
\bm{f}\in\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since
d
is large and metrics can be related through confounding variables
[
6
,
39
]
, the agency wants to summarize the
d
metrics as
k
scores with values
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k
is small as possible.
For instance,
Example

3
suggests that, to summarize COVID and pneumonia death rate metrics, the agency can choose either of the two metrics as the score, so that
k=1
.
Whereas for pneumonia death rate and excess antibiotic use metrics,
Example

4
argues that selecting both metrics as scores is necessary, and so
k=2
.

Surrogate design objectives

Anticipating that the hospital would target the incentives by optimizing the score function
S
, the agency wants to design
S
in such a way that optimizing them ensures that the hospital does well on the performance metrics.
We formalize this goal with two design objectives, which utilize an ordering on the sets
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{S}
, denoted by
\succeq_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\succeq_{\mathcal{S}}
.
The two objectives are motivated from CMS and USNews hospital rating agencies
[
18
,
56
]
.

1.

Improvement objective.

Improving on surrogate scores should result in improving on performance metrics.
In particular,

\displaystyle\text{for }\bm{f},\bm{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F},\quad\text{ if }S(\bm{f}^{\prime})\succeq_{\mathcal{S}}S(\bm{f})\text{ then }\bm{f}^{\prime}\succeq_{\mathcal{F}}\bm{f}.

(1)

2.

Optimality objective.

Pareto-optimal points of surrogate scores should be pareto-optimal points of performance metrics.
In particular,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ParetoOpt}(S)\subseteq\mathrm{ParetoOpt}(\mathcal{F}).

(2)

Throughout the paper, we analyze the setting
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
and use elementwise order of vectors for
\succeq_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\succeq_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Surrogate design restrictions

Due to interpretability and public reporting obligations, rating agencies like CMS and USNews design scores by selecting subsets of the list of performance metrics or by taking weighted averages
[
16
,
18
,
17
,
15
,
56
]
.
Moreover, monotonicity of scores in performance metrics is a desirable property for CMS, as it ensures that a hospital striving to improve all performance metrics sees improved score values
[
16
,
15
]
.

We formulate these requirements as three different restrictions on
S
.
These restrictions impose a linear form on
S:\bm{f}\mapsto\bm{A}\bm{f}
with
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
satisfying certain structural constraints.

1.

Coordinate Selection (
Res-CS
).

Each of the
k
coordinates of scores are chosen from
d
coordinates of performance metrics.
That is, for all
i\in[k]
there exists
j\in[d]
such that
S(\bm{f})_{i}=\bm{f}_{j}
for all
\bm{f}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Equivalently,
S:\bm{f}\mapsto\bm{A}\bm{f}
where rows of
\bm{A}
are 1-hot vectors.

2.

Linear and Monotone (
Res-LM
).

The
k
coordinates of scores are linear combinations of
d
coordinates of performance metrics, and improving on performance metrics should result in improving on surrogate scores.
That is,
S:\bm{f}\mapsto\bm{A}\bm{f}
where for
\bm{f},\bm{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, if
\bm{f}^{\prime}\geq\bm{f}
then
\bm{A}\bm{f}^{\prime}\geq\bm{A}\bm{f}
.

3.

Linear (
Res-L
).

The coordinates of surrogate scores are linear combinations of coordinates of performance metrics.
That is,
S:\bm{f}\mapsto\bm{A}\bm{f}
without any further constraints on
\bm{A}
.

Minimal design problem

Since the number of performance metrics
d
can be large
[
16
,
18
,
56
]
,
a natural goal is to

summarize metrics with scores that are accessible to patients and policymakers.
This goal of succinctness translates into designing a multi-dimensional function

with
the smallest output dimension

k
.
For a combination of design objective and design restriction, the
minimal design problem
is determining the smallest dimensionality
k
and providing an algorithm outputs a surrogate score function

with this

Related work

Recent work has highlighted the plight of score-based incentivization when scores that do not align with performance metrics.

Such unintended consequences are prevalent in fields that use scores as an incentive mechanism

Our framework extends recent work on score design in principal-agent theory

Theorem

in the coefficient space

The respective improvement directions

Definition 16

(Three matrix ranks)
.

For a matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
whose rows generate cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, we define
\mathsf{ConeSubsetRank}\,(\mathsf{CSR}),\mathsf{ConeGeneratingRank}\,(\mathsf{CGR})
, and
\mathsf{ConeRank}\,(\mathsf{CR})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{CSR}(\bm{W})&\coloneqq\min_{k}\left\{k\mid\mathcal{K}_{W}=\mathcal{K}_{V}\text{ for some }\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\text{ such that }\bm{V}\subseteq\bm{W}\right\}.\\
\mathsf{CGR}(\bm{W})&\coloneqq\min_{k}\left\{k\mid\mathcal{K}_{W}=\mathcal{K}_{V}\text{ for some }\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\right\}.\\
\mathsf{CR}(\bm{W})&\coloneqq\min_{k}\left\{k\mid\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}\text{ for some }\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\right\}.\end{split}

(6)

The following relationship between the matrix ranks directly follows from the definitions, which place increasingly fewer restrictions on
\bm{V}
.

Proposition 17
.

For
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
m\geq\mathsf{CSR}(\bm{W})\geq\mathsf{CGR}(\bm{W})\geq\mathsf{CR}(\bm{W})\geq\rank\bm{W}
.

Lemma 21
.

\mathcal{K}_{W}

That is,

.
If

\mathcal{K}_{W}

D.4
tells us that, if there exists

with
\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}
, then

.
So
\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W})=r
if
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is pointed.
On the other hand when
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed,

10
finds
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
with
k=r+1
such that
\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}
.
And

D.5
tells us that, if there exists
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
with
\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}
, then

.

5.5
Comparing
\mathsf{ConeRank}
and
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}

In this section, we contrast
\mathsf{ConeRank}
with
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}
(often denoted as
\rank_{+}
), which is a widely-studied property of non-negative matrices
[
26
,
38
,
5
,
27
,
22
,
57
]
.
While
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}
is defined for a nonnegative matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we define
\mathsf{ConeRank}
for
any
matrix
\bm{W}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{k}\left\{k\mid\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}\text{ for some nonnegative matrix }\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{k}\left\{k\mid\mathcal{K}_{W}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V}\text{ for some matrix }\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}\right\}.

The definitions imply that
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W})\geq\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W})
for a nonnegative matrix
\bm{W}
.
Importantly,
\mathsf{ConeRank}
does not constrain
\bm{V}
to be nonnegative, resulting in these three main distinctions:

1.

Geometrically,
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}
is the minimum number of vectors (arranged as rows of
\bm{V}
) that generates cone
\mathcal{K}_{V}

nested
between
\mathcal{K}_{W}
and the nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}

[
26
]
.
In contrast,
\mathsf{ConeRank}
does not constrain
\mathcal{K}_{V}
to lie inside
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
—it only requires
\mathcal{K}_{V}
to
enclose

\mathcal{K}_{W}
.

2.

For non-negative matrix
\bm{W}
of rank
r\leq 2
, it is known that
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W})=r
.
However, for rank
r>2
, it is NP-hard to determine whether
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W})=r

[
57
,
26
]
.
In contrast,
Lemma

21
states that
\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W})=r
or
r+1
depending on the pointedness of
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, which can be determined in
{\mathrm{poly}}(m,n)
time with a Linear Program (
LP-1
in
Algorithm

3
).

3.

While
\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W})=\mathrm{NonNegativeRank}(\bm{W}^{\top})
, we might not generally have
\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W})=\mathsf{ConeRank}(\bm{W}^{\top})
.
This is because pointedness of cone generated by rows does not imply pointedness of cone generated by columns, or vice versa.

6
Conclusion

We propose a framework to design succinct scores to summarize performance metrics
\mathcal{F}
, and give polynomial-time algorithms that design scores that are provably minimal under mild assumptions on
\mathcal{F}
.
Two future directions are to design scores: (1) when metrics takes discrete high-dimensional values, (2) using incomplete, noisy high data from historical samples of metric values, and (3) when metrics have a non-linear structure.
On a technical note, it remains to identify structural properties of
\mathcal{F}
and corresponding minimal designs for the optimality objective.
Designing minimal scores for simultaneously satisfying both objectives under linear restriction is also an open direction.

Lemma A.4

Geometric interpretation.

This result follows from standard properties of extreme rays of pointed cones

In Border

, an

A pointed cone can be generated by different sets of generators.
Border
[

As a result, the number of extreme rays

.

\mathsf{CSR}
, set

if it is the smallest subset of
W
that generates
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Also, since
W
generates
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, the set of extreme rays of the pointed cone are always included in any of the generating sets of the cone.
That is, the set of extreme rays of
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is a subset of
W
.

[
11

also states that the set of extreme rays generates the cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Hence,
\mathsf{CSR}(W)\leq k
.

These two statements imply

17

With a larger angle between the extreme rays, the cone is still pointed as long as angle

But with a larger angle between the extreme rays, the cone

Appendix C
Preliminaries

C.1
Background on convex analysis

A convex set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a cone if for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K},\lambda\geq 0
, we have
\lambda\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}
.
The Minkowski-Weyl theorem states that a cone is polyhedral if and only if it is finitely generated.
The cone generated by vectors in
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted as
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Representing the elements of a cone as a conic combination of the generating vectors, as in
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, is called the Vertex-representation (V-representation).
Cones can equivalently be defined in terms of inequalities,
\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\bm{A}\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\right\}
for some
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
This representation is referred to as the Halfspace-representation (H-representation).

The dimension of a convex set
\mathcal{C}
is defined as the dimension of the affine hull of
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
\dim\mathcal{C}=\dim\affine(\mathcal{C})
where the affine hull is simply the set of all affine combinations of elements of a set.
The affine hull of a cone
\mathcal{K}
is the linear subspace spanned by the cone, and so
\dim\mathcal{K}=\dim\spanv(\mathcal{K})
.
It then follows that, for cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
generated by rows of
\bm{W}
, we have
\dim\mathcal{K}_{W}=\dim\spanv(\mathcal{K}_{W})=\rank\bm{W}
.

The dual cone of
\mathcal{K}
is
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\langle\bm{y},\bm{x}\right\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}\right\}
.
Thus, for cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
generated by rows of
\bm{W}=[\bm{w}_{1};\dots;\bm{w}_{m}]
, the dual cone is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{W}^{\ast}=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\text{for all }\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{W},\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{y}\right\rangle\geq 0\right\}=\left\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\bm{W}\bm{y}\geq\bm{0}\right\}

Lemma C.1

Lemma C.2
.

Let rows of
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
generate cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed if and only if there exists nonzero
\bm{\lambda}\geq\bm{0}
such that
\bm{0}=\bm{\lambda}\bm{W}
.

Proof.

We first prove that if
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed then there exists nonzero
\bm{\lambda}\geq\bm{0}
such that
\bm{0}=\bm{\lambda}\bm{W}
.
Cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed when there exists nonzero
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
such that
-\bm{x}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
That is, there exists nonzero and nonnegative
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}
such that
\bm{x}=\bm{\alpha}\bm{W}
and
-\bm{x}=\bm{\beta}\bm{W}
.
Adding the two equations, we get
\bm{0}=(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta})\bm{W}
.
Hence, there exists nonzero
\bm{\lambda}=(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{\beta})\geq\bm{0}
such that
\bm{0}=\bm{\lambda}\bm{W}
.

We now prove that if there exists nonzero
\bm{\lambda}\geq\bm{0}
such that
\bm{0}=\bm{\lambda}\bm{W}
then cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed.
W.l.o.g. assume
\bm{W}
does not contain any rows of all zeros and
\lambda_{1}>0
.
Let
\bm{x}=\lambda_{1}\bm{w}_{1}
.
Since
\bm{0}=\bm{\lambda}\bm{W}
, we have
\bm{x}=\lambda_{1}\bm{w}_{1}=-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}\bm{w}_{i}
.
Therefore, there exists nonzero
\bm{x}=\lambda_{1}\bm{w}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
such that
-\bm{x}=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
So cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is nonpointed.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

For two polyhedral cones

and

For any closed and convex cone

\mathcal{K}^{\ast\ast}=\mathcal{K}

The result then follows from the fact that for any two convex cones

D.1

Lemma D.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an
r
-dimensional linear subspace, and let columns of
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\mathcal{K}_{A_{1}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{A_{2}}
be cones in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
generated by rows of matrices
\bm{A}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times d}
and
\bm{A}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}\times d}
respectively.
With
\bm{V}_{1}=\bm{A}_{1}\bm{Z}
and
\bm{V}_{2}=\bm{A}_{2}\bm{Z}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{K}_{A_{1}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A_{2}}^{\ast}\iff\mathcal{K}_{V_{1}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V_{2}}^{\ast}\iff\mathcal{K}_{V_{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V_{1}}.

Proof.

We can simplify this condition
\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{K}_{A_{1}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A_{2}}^{\ast}
further by expressing vectors in the basis
\bm{Z}
.

First, every
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{L}
has a unique representation in the basis
\bm{Z}
.
That is,
\bm{x}=\bm{Z}\bm{c}
for some
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
Second, every
d
-dimensional row
\bm{a}
of
\bm{A}_{1}
and
\bm{A}_{2}
can be written as
\bm{a}^{\parallel}+\bm{a}^{\perp}
, where
\bm{a}^{\parallel}=\bm{a}\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top}\in\mathcal{L}
and
\bm{a}^{\perp}=\bm{a}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top})\in\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.
Therefore,
\bm{A}_{1}=\bm{A}_{1}^{\parallel}+\bm{A}_{1}^{\perp}
where
\bm{A}_{1}^{\parallel}=\bm{A}_{1}\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top}
and
\bm{A}_{1}^{\perp}=\bm{A}_{1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top})
.
Note that
\bm{A}_{1}^{\perp}\bm{Z}=\bm{0}_{m_{1}\times r}
.
Similarly we can decompose the matrix
\bm{A}_{2}=\bm{A}_{2}^{\parallel}+\bm{A}_{2}^{\perp}
.
Denote the coefficients as
\bm{V}_{1}=\bm{A}_{1}\bm{Z}
and
\bm{V}_{2}=\bm{A}_{2}\bm{Z}
.
Using these simplifications, we get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{K}_{A_{1}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A_{2}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\iff\text{for all }\bm{x}\in\mathcal{L},\bm{A}_{1}\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\implies\bm{A}_{2}\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}

(42)

\displaystyle\iff\text{for all }\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\bm{A}_{1}\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}\implies\bm{A}_{2}\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}

(43)

\displaystyle\iff\text{for all }\bm{c},(\bm{A}_{1}^{\parallel}+\bm{A}_{1}^{\perp})\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}\implies(\bm{A}_{2}^{\parallel}+\bm{A}_{2}^{\perp})\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}

(44)

\displaystyle\iff\text{for all }\bm{c},\bm{V}_{1}\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}\implies\bm{V}_{2}\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{Z}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}

(45)

\displaystyle\iff\text{for all }\bm{c},\bm{V}_{1}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}\implies\bm{V}_{2}\bm{c}\geq\bm{0}

(46)

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{K}_{V_{1}}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V_{2}}^{\ast}

(47)

\displaystyle\iff\mathcal{K}_{V_{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{V_{1}}.

(48)

where the last equivalence follows from
Lemma

C.3
.

Lemma D.2
.

\mathcal{L}
be the linear subspace corresponding to
\affine(X)
.
For any
\bm{x}^{\ast}
in the relative interior of

and any
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{L}

.

We use the definition of relative interior.

\bm{x}^{\ast}

\bm{x}^{\ast}

.

\mathcal{L}

\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{r}
, and
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{L}
.

Therefore,
k=r+1
vectors are sufficient to generate a cone equalling
r
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Lemma D.7
.

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector space and
W=\left\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\right\}
be a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\cone\left(\proj_{V}(W)\right)=\proj_{V}(\cone(W)).

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{k}
be an orthonormal basis of
V
.
We prove set inclusion in both directions.

(\subseteq)
. Let
\bm{x}\in\cone(\proj_{V}(W))
. Following definitions of projection, for some nonnegative
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}\right\rangle\bm{v}_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\bm{w}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}\right\rangle\bm{v}_{j}\in\proj_{V}(\cone(W)).

(51)

(\supseteq)
. Let
\bm{x}\in\proj_{V}(\cone(W))
. Again following definitions, for some nonnegative
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\bm{w}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}\right\rangle\bm{v}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle\bm{w}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}\right\rangle\bm{v}_{j}\right)\in\cone\left(\proj_{V}(W)\right).\qed

(52)

Lemma D.8
.

Let cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
generated by
W=\left\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\right\}
be pointed.
Let
U
be such that
U\subseteq W,\mathcal{K}_{U}=\mathcal{K}_{W}
, and the vectors
U
are positively independent.
Let
V
have the same properties.
Then
|U|=|V|
.

Proof.

We will show that (i) every vector in
V
is a positive multiple of some vector in
U
, and (ii) every vector in
U
is a positive multiple of some vector in
V
.
As
U,V
are sets of positively independent vectors, no
\bm{u}_{i}\in U
can be expressed as a nonnegative combination of
U\setminus\left\{\bm{u}_{i}\right\}
(and similarly for
V
).
These statements together imply that
V
and
U
are the same set upto positive scaling of vectors, implying that
|U|=|V|
.

Note that
U,V
do not contain the zero vector, as the zero vector would be a (trivial) nonnegative combination of other vectors in the set.
We now show that every vector in
V
is a positive multiple of some vector in
U
.
We prove this statement for vector
\bm{v}_{1}\in V
, the proof is the same for the other vectors in
\bm{V}
.

Let
|U|=s
and
|V|=t
.
Since
\mathcal{K}_{U}=\mathcal{K}_{V}
, let
\bm{v}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\bm{u}_{i}
for some
a_{i}\geq 0
that are not all zero.
Also, for all
i\in[s]
let
\bm{u}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{i,j}\bm{v}_{j}
for some
b_{i,j}\geq 0
such that
b_{i,1},\dots,b_{i,t}
are not all zero.
So,

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{i,j}\bm{v}_{j}

(53)

\displaystyle\implies\underbrace{\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}b_{i,1}\right]}_{\rho}\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{t}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}b_{i,j}\right)}_{c_{j}}\bm{v}_{j}.

(54)

Observe that the coefficient
c_{j}\geq 0
as
a_{i},b_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i\in[s],j\in\left\{2,\dots,t\right\}
.
Moreover, when
\rho\neq 0
, not all coefficients
c_{j}
are zero simultaneously.
This is because
c_{j}=0
for all
j\in\left\{2,\dots,t\right\}
would mean that
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{0}
, which contradicts our assumption about vectors in
V
.
Hence, if
\rho\neq 0
, then there exists
c_{j}>0
.

There are three cases:
\rho>0
,
\rho<0
, and
\rho=0
.

1.

Case
\rho>0
.
We can divide both sides of
Equation

54
by
\rho
to find that
\bm{v}_{1}
is a nonnegative combination of vectors
\bm{v}_{2},\dots,\bm{v}_{t}
.
This contradicts our assumption that vectors
V
are positively independent, and hence this case cannot happen.

2.

Case
\rho<0
.
We again divide both sides of
Equation

54
by
\rho
to find that
-\bm{v}_{1}
is a nonnegative combination of vectors
\bm{v}_{2},\dots,\bm{v}_{t}
.
This implies that
-\bm{v}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
and
\bm{v}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
, contradicting our assumption that
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is pointed.
So this case cannot happen.

3.

Case
\rho=0
.

Equation

54
simplifies to
0=\sum_{j=2}^{t}c_{j}\bm{v}_{j}
where
c_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}b_{i,j}
.
Since
\mathcal{K}_{W}
is pointed and
\bm{v}_{j}\neq\bm{0}
, we must have
c_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}b_{i,j}=0
for all
j\in\left\{2,\dots,t\right\}
.

Coefficient
c_{j}
is a sum of nonnegative terms
a_{i}b_{i,j}
, and for
c_{j}
to be exactly zero, each term must be exactly zero.
At least some
a_{i}>0
, as otherwise,
a_{i}=0
for all
i\in[s]
implies
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{0}
.
W.l.o.g. let
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}>0
for some
1<\ell\leq s
and
a_{\ell+1},\dots,a_{s}=0
.
This observation about
a_{1},\dots,a_{s}
says that
c_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}b_{i,j}=0
.
Hence, for all
j\in\left\{2,\dots,t\right\},i\in[\ell]
, we have
b_{i,j}=0
.

Using observation about
a_{i}
and
b_{i,j}
, we find that
\bm{v}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}\bm{u}_{i}
.
Moreover for
i\in[\ell]
, we have
\bm{u}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}b_{i,j}\bm{v}_{j}=b_{i,1}\bm{v}_{1}
.
Therefore,
\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{\ell}
are positive multiples of
\bm{v}_{1}
.
If
\ell>1
then
\bm{u}_{1},\dots,\bm{u}_{\ell}
would be positive multiples of
\bm{v}_{1}
, and thus of each other, contradicting assumption that vectors
U
are positively independent.
For the same reason,
\bm{u}_{\ell+1},\dots,\bm{u}_{s}
cannot be positive multiples of
\bm{v}_{1}
.
Thus, there exists exactly one vector
\bm{u}_{i_{1}}
that is a positive multiple of
\bm{v}_{1}
.

So, every vector
\bm{v}_{i}
in
V
is a positive multiple of some vector in
U
.
In fact, two vectors
\bm{v}_{i},\bm{v}_{j}
where
i\neq j
are positively multiples of different vectors in
U
, as otherwise
\bm{v}_{i}
would be a positive multiple of
\bm{v}_{j}
.
This means that
|V|\leq|U|
.
By a symmetric argument we can prove that
|V|\geq|U|
, implying that
|U|=|V|
.
∎

D.3
Decomposition of polyhedral cones

Lemma D.9
.

Let cone
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
have lineality space
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}\cap(-\mathcal{K})
.
Then
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{K})
.

Proof.

The lineality space
\mathcal{L}
is a linear subspace and so
\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{\perp}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is the Minkowski sum of
\mathcal{L}
and its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.
We can thus write
\mathcal{K}
as a Minkowski sum of two sets:
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}\cap(\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}^{\perp})=(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L})+(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp})
.
As
\mathcal{L}
is a linear subspace inside
\mathcal{K}
, we have
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}
, implying that
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}+(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp})
.
We now project
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
, and use the fact that
\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\cdot)
is a linear operator.
So,

\displaystyle\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{L}+(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}))

(55)

\displaystyle=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{L})+\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp})

(56)

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{0}\right\}+(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}).

(57)

where the last equality follows from
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
being orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{L}
, and
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
lying inside
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
so that projecting
\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
onto
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
has no effect.
We note that
\bm{0}\in\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
, which is the intersection of a cone and a linear subspace, and so
\left\{\bm{0}\right\}+(\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp})=\mathcal{K}_{W}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.
We thus get the desired result
\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{K})=\mathcal{K}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
.
∎

Lemma D.10
.

Let
W=\left\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
generate cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}=\lineality(\mathcal{K}_{W})
be the lineality space of
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, and denote
W_{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\bm{w}_{i}\in W\mid\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}\right\}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}
.

Proof.

This lemma is based on the following observation: since
\mathcal{L}
is the maximal linear subspace inside
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, for any point
\bm{w}_{i}
of
W
not in
\mathcal{L}
, we have
-\bm{w}_{i}\notin\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Hence, the lineality space
\mathcal{L}
can only be generated by the points of
W
that are inside
\mathcal{L}
.

As
W_{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}
is a linear subspace, we have
\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
.

We now show that
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}
.
Here
\mathcal{L}
is the lineality space of
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{K}_{W}\cap(-\mathcal{K}_{W})
.
Denote
W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\bm{w}_{i}\in W\mid\bm{w}_{i}\notin\mathcal{L}\right\}
, and so
\mathcal{K}_{W}=\mathcal{K}_{(W_{\mathcal{L}}\cup W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}})}=\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}+\mathcal{K}_{W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}
, which is the Minkowski sum of two cones.
Let
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{L}
.
As
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{W}=\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}+\mathcal{K}_{W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}
, we have
\bm{x}=\bm{y}+\bm{z}
for some
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}
and
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{K}_{W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}
.
From earlier we know that
\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, and so
\bm{x}-\bm{y}=\bm{z}\in\mathcal{L}
.
So
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{K}_{W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}
.
We next show that
\bm{z}
is exactly zero, implying that
\bm{x}=\bm{y}\in\mathcal{K}_{W_{\mathcal{L}}}
and completing the proof.

Suppose that
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{K}_{W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}
is such that
\bm{z}\neq\bm{0}
.
Then
\bm{z}=\sum_{\bm{w}_{i}\in W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}}\lambda_{i}\bm{w}_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
that are not all zero.
W.l.o.g. let
\lambda_{1}\neq 0,\bm{w}_{1}\neq\bm{0}
and rearrange to get:

\displaystyle-\bm{w}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\bm{z}}{\lambda_{1}}+\sum_{\bm{w}_{i}\in W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}},i\neq 1}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}\bm{w}_{i}.

(58)

Note that as
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{L}
, which is a linear subspace inside
\mathcal{K}_{W}
, we have
-\bm{z}/\lambda_{1}\in\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
As every
\bm{w}_{i}\in W_{\mathcal{L}}
is also in
W
, we get that
-\bm{w}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Hence,
\bm{w}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{W}\cap(-\mathcal{K}_{W})=\mathcal{L}
.
This contradicts the choice of
\bm{w}_{1}
, which is an element in the set
W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\bm{w}_{i}\in W\mid\bm{w}_{i}\notin\mathcal{L}\right\}
.
So our assumption that
\bm{z}\neq\bm{0}
was wrong.
∎

Lemma D.11
.

Let
W=\left\{\bm{w}_{1},\dots,\bm{w}_{m}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
generate cone
\mathcal{K}_{W}
.
Let
\mathcal{K}_{W}=\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{K}_{P}
be the decomposition such that
\mathcal{L}=\lineality(\mathcal{K}_{W})
is the lineality space of
\mathcal{K}_{W}
and
\mathcal{K}_{P}=\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{K}_{W}
is a pointed cone.
Denote
W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\bm{w}_{i}\in W\mid\bm{w}_{i}\notin\mathcal{L}\right\}
.
Then
\cone(\tilde{W})=\mathcal{K}_{P}
where
\tilde{W}=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

We denote
W_{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{\bm{w}_{i}\in W\mid\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}\right\}
, and note that
W=W_{\mathcal{L}}\cup W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}
.
Here
W_{\mathcal{L}}
only contains vectors that lie in
\mathcal{L}
, and
W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}
does not contain vectors that lie in
\mathcal{L}
.
Hence, we get

\displaystyle\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W)

\displaystyle=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W_{\mathcal{L}})\cup\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}})=\left\{\bm{0}\right\}\cup\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}}).

(59)

Lemma

D.9
states that
\mathcal{K}_{P}=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\mathcal{K}_{W})
, and thus we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{P}

\displaystyle=\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(\cone(W))\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=\joinrel=}}\;\cone\left(\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W)\right)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=\joinrel=}}\;\cone\left(\proj_{\mathcal{L}^{\perp}}(W_{\cdot\setminus\mathcal{L}})\right)=\cone(\tilde{W})

(60)

where equality
(a)
follows from
Lemma

D.7
, and
(b)
from the fact:
\cone(\left\{\bm{0}\right\}\cup X)=\cone(X)
for set
X
.
∎
