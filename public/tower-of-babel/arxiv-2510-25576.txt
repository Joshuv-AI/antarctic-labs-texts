Abstract
. We study the variational behavior of the total inverse mean curvature of curves with prescribed boundary in the half-plane. We characterize the existence of critical points with prescribed area. We show that such critical points are strongly stable. As an application, we prove a local minimality property.

1.
Introduction

The
Heintze-Karcher inequality
in the Euclidean space states that, given a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with smooth boundary and with positive mean curvature
H
, it holds that

(1.1)

|\Omega|\leqslant\frac{n-1}{n}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{H}.

Moreover, equality in (
1.1
) holds if and only if
\Omega
is an Euclidean ball. Using dilations, (
1.1
) is equivalent to the following optimization problem: spheres, and only spheres, minimize the total inverse mean curvature among closed, strictly mean-convex hypersurfaces enclosing the same volume. Inequality (
1.1
) was first proved by Heintze and Karcher
[
14
]
. Later on, Ros
[
26
]
gave another proof of (
1.1
) in spaces with nonnegative Ricci curvature by means of
Reilly’s formula

[
24
]
. Owing to (
1.1
), he provided a new proof of
Alexandrov theorem

[
1
,
2
]
, i.e. a closed, embedded, constant mean curvature hypersurface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a round sphere, highlighting a link between the Heintze-Karcher inequality and the isoperimetric problem.

Recently, various generalizations of the Heintze–Karcher inequality have been established. By means of the Aleksandrov-Bakelman-Pucci (ABP) method (cf. e.g. (
[
13
]
), Xia and Zhang
[
28
]
reproved (
1.1
) in spaces of nonnegative Ricci curvature, while Brendle
[
6
]
proved a Heintze-Karcher inequality in some
warped products
to obtain an Aleksandrov theorem. Similar results have been achieved by Li and Xia
[
21
]
in
sub-static manifolds
, and have been recently improved by Borghini, Fogagnolo and Pinamonti
[
5
,
12
]
. Other versions of (
1.1
) have been obtained in
weighted manifolds

[
23
]
and in
metric measure spaces

[
18
]
.

A relevant proof of the Heintze-Karcher inequality was given by Montiel and Ros
[
22
]
in space forms. Their approach, employing a clever fibration of
\Omega
by geodesic segments, provides another effective proof of the Alexandrov theorem. These ideas were adapted by Delgadino and Maggi
[
9
]
to extend the Alexandrov theorem to the measure-theoretic framework: connected sets of finite perimeter with finite volume and constant distributional mean curvature are Euclidean balls. As a by-product, the authors extend (
1.1
) to strictly mean convex sets in an appropriate
viscosity
sense, e.g. allowing the presence of outward corners.

Moreover, Montiel and Ros’ argument paved the way for studying
free-boundary
Heintze-Karcher inequalities and their applications in the setting of capillarity. Motivated by the study of the equilibrium shape assumed by a droplet of small volume on a flat homogeneous substrate, Delgadino and Weser
[
10
]
obtained suitable Heintze-Karcher inequalities for smooth hypersurfaces with free-boundary in the half-space, and characterized spherical caps as the optimal configurations.
A similar analysis has been recently performed in
[
8
]
in a non-smooth setting.
These examples (see also
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
) further highlight the connection between the Heintze–Karcher inequality and the isoperimetric problem.

In this paper, we show that the aforementioned symmetries between the the Heintze-Karcher inequality and the isoperimetric problem break down when we consider problems with
constrained boundary
.
Precisely, we consider the problem of minimizing the
total inverse curvature

\mathcal{F}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\frac{1}{H},

among positively curved curves
\gamma:[0,2L]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
with prescribed boundary
\{(-x_{0},0)\}\cup\{(x_{0},0)\}
and enclosing a prescribed area. We call them
admissible curves
. Here
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y):y>0\}
. Precisely, if
x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0}>0
are fixed, denoting by
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
the area enclosed by
\gamma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, our optimization problem reads as

(1.2)

\inf\mathcal{F}(\gamma),\qquad\gamma(0)=(x_{0},0),\qquad\gamma(2L)=(-x_{0},0)\qquad\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\mathcal{A}_{0}.

We propose a variational approach to the problem, with the aim of characterizing its equilibrium configurations and studying their stability properties.
The starting point is the identification of the correct notion of variation associated with (
1.2
). Accordingly, we say that a variation is
admissible
when it induces a deformation of admissible curves. Among all admissible variations, the natural class to consider in connection with (
1.2
) is that of
area-preserving admissible variations
, namely those which preserves the enclosed area.
Were (
1.2
) consistent with the corresponding isoperimetric problem under the same boundary constraints - as it is in the free-boundary setting - we would expect its critical points to be one-dimensional spherical caps, i.e. circular arcs. We show that this is not the case. Indeed, stationary curves under area-preserving admissible variations are given explicitly by
\gamma=(x,y):[0,2L]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, where

(1.3)

\begin{split}x(s)&=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right)\\
y(s)&=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{\pi x_{0}}{\sigma\tan\sigma}\end{split}

Here
L=L(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
is half the length of
\gamma
, and moreover

\sigma=\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}},\qquad\kappa=\frac{L}{\sin\left(\sigma\right)}.

These configurations are uniquely determined by the data of the problem, namely
x_{0}
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Even more surprisingly, the existence of such configurations is governed by a specific compatibility condition among the parameters. In fact, the existence and uniqueness of such configurations are equivalent to the relation

(1.4)

\mathcal{A}_{0}>\frac{3}{2}\pi x_{0}^{2}.

Heuristically, this threshold expresses the fact that equilibrium configurations cannot undergo excessive stretching.

The characterization of critical points, as well as the derivation of (
1.4
), follows by imposing the vanishing of the first variation of
\mathcal{F}
, which we compute in
Proposition

2.2
, under area-preserving admissible variations. This leads to the
Euler-Lagrange equation

(1.5)

2+\Delta^{\gamma}(H^{-2})=\lambda\qquad\text{ on $\gamma$},

where
\Delta^{\gamma}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\gamma
and
\lambda
is the Lagrange multiplier emerging by the area constraint, and to the first-order boundary condition

(1.6)

\gamma\text{ meets the horizontal axis tangentially}.

It is worth noting that, although circular arcs solve (
1.5
) for
\lambda=2
, they are ruled out by the boundary tangency condition prescribed by (
1.6
).
Moreover, the correct Lagrange multiplier is uniquely determined by the relation

(1.7)

\lambda=\frac{2L}{L+x_{0}},

and the first-order variational analysis of
\mathcal{F}
is equivalent to that of the
penalized functional

\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}

under arbitrary admissible variations, as we show in
Theorem

3.8
. This fact is consistent with what happens to the
n
-dimensional area functional under volume constraints (cf.
[
3
]
). The characterization of critical points further exploits a Steiner-type symmetrization argument, which we explain in
Section

3.2
: optimal configurations must be symmetric with respect to the vertical axis. Our critical points yield a class of counterexamples to the validity of (
1.1
) when inward corners are allowed. Indeed, the domain
\Omega
whose boundary consists of
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
and its reflection across the horizontal axis, satisfies

\mathcal{A}(\Omega)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{2(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})\sigma+(\pi^{2}+\sigma^{2})\sin 2\sigma}{2(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})\sigma+(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})\sin 2\sigma}\right)\int_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}\frac{1}{H}>\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{1}{H},

because (
1.7
) implies that
\lambda<2
(
Remark

3.12
). This fact prevents the generalization of (
1.1
) to sets with strictly positive mean curvature everywhere but in a discrete set.
Subsequently, we address the problem of the stability of critical points, that is, the second-order properties of the total inverse curvature. The starting point is the computation of the second variations of
\mathcal{F}
(
Proposition

4.1
) and of its penalized version
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
(
Corollary

4.4
), evaluated at the equilibrium configurations. Precisely, let
\mathcal{V}
be any admissible variation of a fixed critical point. We denote by

X:=\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{\prime}:=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{V}}{\partial t^{2}}\right|_{t=0}

its
velocity vector
and
acceleration vector
respectively. Assume that
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length. Let
\varphi,\,\varphi_{\tau},\,\psi,\,\psi_{\tau}
be such that

(1.8)

X=\varphi N+\varphi_{\tau}\dot{\gamma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{\prime}=\psi N+\psi_{\tau}\dot{\gamma},

where
N=\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}
. We show that the second variations of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
are given respectively by

(1.9)

\left.\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\lambda\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\psi+H\varphi_{\tau}^{2}+2\varphi\dot{\varphi}_{\tau}\right)\,ds+\left[\frac{\dot{\psi}}{H^{2}}\right]_{0}^{2L}

and

(1.10)

\left.\frac{d^{2}(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{0}^{2L}\left((2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\left[\frac{\dot{\psi}}{H^{2}}\right]_{0}^{2L}.

Remarkably, the second variation of the penalized functional, apart from the boundary contribution, depends solely on the normal component of the velocity
X
, in contrast to what happens for
\mathcal{F}
. This highlights a further analogy with the area functional (cf.
[
3
]
). The correct notions of stability for
\mathcal{F}
and for
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
are then formulated by means of (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) (
Definition

4.6
). Namely we impose, respectively, the non-negativity of (
1.9
) under area-preserving admissible variations, and of (
1.10
) under arbitrary admissible variations. Yet, as with the area functional (cf.
[
3
]
), the second-order behaviors of
\mathcal{F}
and

\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
are no longer equivalent. In fact, as we prove in
Proposition

4.7
, the stability of
\mathcal{F}
under area-preserving variations coincides with the stability of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
under admissible variations satisfying

\int_{0}^{2L}\varphi\,ds=0,

which we call
first-order area-preserving
admissible variations. As a byproduct of this equivalence, we deduce that the stability of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
under arbitrary variations is indeed a more restrictive condition. Nevertheless, we are able to show that the equilibrium configurations are stable in this strengthened sense (
Theorem

5.8
). This provides an interesting contrast with the behavior of the area functional under volume constraints, where circles are indeed
unstable
critical points for the relevant penalized functional (cf.
[
3
]
).
By (
1.10
), the study of the sign of the second variation of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
relies on the validity of a sharp weighted Hardy-type inequality of the form

(1.11)

\mu_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}\int_{0}^{2L}\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}\,ds\leqslant\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}+(2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}\right)\,ds,

for some
\mu_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}>0,
where
\varphi
belongs to the functional space

\mathcal{W}_{1}=\left\{\varphi\in W^{2,2}(0,2L)\,:\,\varphi(0)=\varphi(2L)=0,\,\dot{\varphi}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(2L)=0\right\}

We refer to
[
4
,
11
,
19
,
20
,
27
]
for some accounts on weighted Hardy-type inequalities. The space
\mathcal{W}_{1}
arises naturally imposing that variations are admissible. We prove the well-posedness of (
1.11
) in
Proposition

5.3
, and we reduce the problem to determine the smallest positive constant
\mu
for which the problem

(1.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{2}{H^{3}}\ddot{\varphi}\right)^{\prime\prime}+\mu\left(\frac{2}{H}\dot{\varphi}\right)^{\prime}+(2-\lambda)H\varphi=0\qquad\text{on }(0,2L),\\
&\varphi(0)=\varphi(2L)=0,\\
&\dot{\varphi}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(2L)=0\end{aligned}\right.

admits a non-trivial solution (
Proposition

5.7
). This equivalence follows from a variational analysis of (
1.11
), from which (
1.12
) emerges as the relevant Euler–Lagrange equation. In
Theorem

5.8
, we show that
\mu_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}>1
. The proof of this fact relies on the explicit characterization of the general solution to the first equation in (
1.12
) (
Section

5.3
). In turn, the behavior of a solution depends on whether
\mu

is less than, equal to, or greater than the threshold value

\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}+2\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}.

Consequently, the analysis of (
1.12
) must take these different cases into account. Combining (
1.10
) with (
1.11
), the fact that
\mu_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}>1
and the fact that
H
is strictly positive and bounded on
[0,2L]
, the second variation of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
admits the uniform lower bound

\left.\frac{d^{2}(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}\geqslant{\mathcal{C}}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)},

for some
C=C(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
. Our
results can therefore be summarized in the following statement.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0}>0
be fixed. The following facts hold.

(i)

\mathcal{F}
admits a critical point under area-preserving admissible variations if and only if (
1.4
) holds.
In this case, it is unique, and it is explicitly given by (
1.3
). We denote it by
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
.

(ii)

There exists
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}=\tilde{\mathcal{C}}(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
such that, if
\mathcal{V}
is an admissible variation of
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
, then

\left.\frac{d^{2}(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}\geqslant\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)},

where
\lambda
is as in (
1.7
) and
\varphi
is as in (
1.8
). In particular,
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
is a stable critical point of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
under arbitrary admissible variations.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{V}
is an area-preserving admissible variation of
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
and
\varphi
is as above, then

\left.\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}\geqslant\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)}.

In particular,
\gamma(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
is a stable critical point of
\mathcal{F}
under area-preserving admissible variations.

Finally, as an application of
Theorem

1.1
, we prove a local minimization property of equilibrium configurations when compared to sufficiently
C^{2}
-small normal graphs (
Theorem

6.1
).

Preliminaries

In the following, we let
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,y>0\}
and
\partial{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,y=0\}.

If
(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we set
(\xi,\eta)^{\perp}=(\eta,-\xi).
If
a>0
and
f:[0,a]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we may denote its derivative by
\dot{f}
,
f^{\prime}
,
f^{(1)}
or
\frac{df}{ds}
. We denote the right derivative of
f
at
0

\left.\frac{df}{ds}\right|_{s=0^{+}}

2.1.
Planar curves

Let
L>0
be fixed. We say that
\gamma=(x,y)\in C^{1}\left([0,2L],{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)
is a
regular parametrization
, or a
regular curve
, if
|\dot{\gamma}(s)|>0
for any
s\in[0,2L]
. The support of a regular parametrization,
\gamma([0,2L])
, is an immersed curve of class
C^{1}
. Notice that
\gamma
is an embedding if and only if it is injective. In the following, we shall identify a regular curve with its parametrization. A curve is
parametrized by arc-length
if
|\dot{\gamma}|\equiv 1
. The tangent and normal bundles of
\gamma
are spanned respectively by

(2.1)

\tau(s):=\frac{\dot{\gamma}(s)}{|\dot{\gamma}(s)|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad N(s):=\frac{\dot{\gamma}(s)^{\perp}}{|\dot{\gamma}(s)|}\qquad\text{for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

The
curvature
of
\gamma
is defined by

H(s):=\frac{\big\langle\dot{\gamma}(s),\ddot{\gamma}^{\perp}(s)\big\rangle}{|\dot{\gamma}(s)|^{3}}\qquad\text{for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

We say that a parametrization is
counterclockwise
if
H(s)\geqslant 0
for any
s\in[0,2L]
, and that it is
strictly counterclockwise
if
H(s)>0
for any
s\in[0,2L]
. We write
d\gamma
to denote integration with respect to the
1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. We recall that

(2.2)

\int_{\gamma}\left(f\circ\gamma\right)\,d\gamma=\int_{0}^{2L}f(s)|\dot{\gamma}(s)|\,ds\qquad\text{for any $f\in C^{1}[0,2L]$. }

The following lemma collects some formulas which will be needed in the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\gamma\in C^{3}([0,2L],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
be a regular curve parametrized by arc-length. Then, the following holds.

(2.3)

\ddot{\gamma}=-H\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dddot{\gamma}=-H^{2}\dot{\gamma}-\dot{H}\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}.

Proof.

As
|\dot{\gamma}|\equiv 1
, we recall that
\big\langle\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}\big\rangle=0
and
H=\big\langle\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}^{\perp}\big\rangle
. In particular,
\ddot{\gamma}=-H\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}
. Moreover,

\big\langle\dddot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma}\big\rangle=\frac{d}{ds}\big\langle\ddot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma}\big\rangle-|\ddot{\gamma}|^{2}=-|\ddot{\gamma}|^{2}=-H^{2}\qquad\text{and}\big\langle\dddot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}\big\rangle=\frac{d}{ds}\big\langle\ddot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma}^{\perp}\big\rangle-\big\langle\ddot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}^{\perp}\big\rangle=-\dot{H},

whence (
2.3
) follows.
∎

2.2.
A general first variation formula

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a smooth, embedded, two-sided hypersurface with boundary
\partial S
. We say that
S
is
strictly mean-convex
if
H>0
on
S
, where
H
is the non-averaged mean curvature of
S
. If
S
is strictly mean-convex, we denote by

\mathcal{F}(S)=\int_{S}\frac{1}{H}\,dS

the
inverse total mean curvature
of
S
, where
dS
denotes integration with respect to the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. We denote by
\nabla^{S}
and
\Delta^{S}
the Levi-Civita connection and the Laplace-Beltrami operator of
S
, respectively. When
S
encloses a bounded region
\Omega
, we denote by
N
its outer unit normal, and by
V(S)
the Lebesgue measure of
|\Omega|
. A smooth map
\mathcal{V}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is called a
variation
of
S
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\mathcal{V}(p,0)=p
for any
p\in S
;

(ii)

the set
S_{t}=\mathcal{V}(S,t)
is a smooth, embedded, two-sided hypersurface for any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
;

(iii)

\mathcal{V}(p,t)=p
for any
p\in\partial S
and any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
.

Here, a variation fixes the boundary. For any
p\in S
and any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
, we define

X(p)=\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}(p,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}.

Clearly,
X\equiv 0
on
\partial S
. If
S
encloses a bounded region, then also
S_{t}
encloses a bounded region for any sufficiently small
t
. In this case, it is well-known (cf.
[
25
]
) that

(2.4)

\left.\frac{dV(S_{t})}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{S}\langle X,N\rangle\,dS.

In particular, when
V(S_{t})\equiv V(S)
,

(2.5)

\int_{S}\langle X,N\rangle\,dS=0.

With respect to the first variation of
\mathcal{F}
, the following holds.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a smooth, embedded, two-sided, strictly mean-convex hypersurface with boundary
\partial S
. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variation of
S
. Then

(2.6)

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(S_{t})}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{S}\left(\Delta^{S}(H^{-2})+\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}+1\right)\langle X,N\rangle\,dS+\int_{\partial S}H^{-2}\langle\nabla^{S}\langle X,N\rangle,\eta\rangle\,d\partial S,

where
d\partial S
denotes integration with respect to the
(n-2)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
|h|
is the norm of the second fundamental form of
S
and
\eta
is the conormal outward unit vector of
S
.

Proof.

We know by
[
25
, Lemma 1.26]
that

(2.7)

\left.\frac{\partial H(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}=X^{T}H-\Delta^{S}\langle X,N\rangle-|h|^{2}\langle X,N\rangle,

where
X^{T}=X-\langle X,N\rangle N
. Denote by
\jac(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)|_{S})
the Jacobian of
\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)|_{S}
. Recall (cf.
[
25
]
) that

(2.8)

\left.\frac{\partial\jac(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)|_{S})}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}=\divv^{S}X,

where
\divv^{S}
is the tangential divergence of
S
.
The area formula and the divergence theorem yield

\begin{split}\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(S_{t})}{dt}\right|_{t=0}&=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\int_{S}\frac{\jac(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)|_{S})}{H(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}\,dS\\
&=\int_{S}\left(\left.\frac{\partial(H(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))^{-1})}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}+\frac{1}{H}\left.\frac{\partial(\jac(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)|_{S}))}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\right)\,dS\\
&\overset{\eqref{derijacgenfirstvar}}{=}\int_{S}\left(-\frac{1}{H^{2}}\left.\frac{\partial(H(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}+\frac{1}{H}\divv^{S}X\right)\,dS\\
&\overset{\eqref{derivativeofHinfirstgeneral}}{=}\int_{S}\left(-\frac{1}{H^{2}}X^{T}H+\frac{1}{H^{2}}\Delta^{S}\langle X,N\rangle+\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}\langle X,N\rangle+\frac{1}{H}\divv^{S}X\right)\,dS\\
&=\int_{S}\left(\divv^{S}\left(H^{-2}\nabla^{S}\langle X,N\rangle\right)-\langle\nabla^{S}(H^{-2}),\nabla^{S}\langle X,N\rangle\rangle+\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}\langle X,N\rangle\right)\,dS\\
&\quad+\int_{S}\left(X^{T}(H^{-1})+H^{-1}\divv^{S}X^{T}+H^{-1}\divv^{S}\left(\langle X,N\rangle N\right)\right)\,dS\\
&=\int_{\partial S}H^{-2}\langle\nabla^{S}\langle X,N\rangle,\eta\rangle\,d\partial S-\int_{S}\divv^{S}\left(\langle X,N\rangle\nabla^{S}(H^{-2})\right)\,dS\\
&\qquad+\int_{S}\left(\Delta^{S}(H^{-2})+\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}+1\right)\langle X,N\rangle\,dS+\int_{S}\divv^{S}\left(H^{-1}X^{T}\right)\,dS\\
&=\int_{S}\left(\Delta^{S}(H^{-2})+\frac{|h|^{2}}{H^{2}}+1\right)\langle X,N\rangle\,dS+\int_{\partial S}H^{-2}\langle\nabla^{S}\langle X,N\rangle,\eta\rangle\,d\partial S.\qed\end{split}

Remark 2.3
.

Recall that, when
n=2
,
|h|^{2}=H^{2}
. In particular, if
\gamma:[0,2L]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a a smooth, embedded curve parametrized by arc-length and with
H>0
, then (
2.6
) reads as

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\gamma_{t})}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\frac{d^{2}\left(H^{-2}\right)}{ds^{2}}+2\right)\langle X,N\rangle\,ds+H^{-2}(2L)\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=2L}-H^{-2}(0)\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=0}.

3.
Critical points

3.1.
Admissible curves and variations

Let
x_{0}>0
and
L>0
be fixed.
We say that a curve
\gamma=(x,y)
is
admissible
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\gamma\in C^{\infty}([0,2L],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
is a regular, strictly counterclockwise embedding;

(ii)

\gamma(0)=(x_{0},0)
and
\gamma(2L)=(-x_{0},0)
;

(iii)

y(s)>0
for any
s\in(0,2L)
.

Any admissible curve encloses a bounded, simply connected, open region in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, that we denote by
\Omega
. The boundary of
\Omega
,
\partial\Omega
, is the union of
\gamma
with the segment
\left(-x_{0},x_{0}\right)\times\{0\}
. As
\gamma
is counterclockwise, then
N
defined as in (
2.1
) is the outer unit normal to
\Omega\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, and
H(s)
is the curvature of
\partial\Omega
at
\gamma(s)
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
the area of
\Omega
. The divergence theorem implies that

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{2L}x(s)\dot{y}(s)\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2L}\left(x(s)\dot{y}(s)-\dot{x}(s)y(s)\right)\,ds.

Finally, we say that an admissible curve is
vertically symmetric
if it is symmetric with respect to the vertical axis. The notion of admissible curve carries a natural notion of admissible variation. Precisely, a smooth map
\mathcal{V}:[0,2L]\times[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is called an
admissible variation
of
\gamma
if the following properties hold:

(i)

\mathcal{V}(\cdot,0)=\gamma
;

(ii)

\gamma_{t}=(x_{t},y_{t}):=\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)
is an admissible curve for any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}].

Let
\mathcal{V}(s,t)
be an admissible variation. We define the
velocity
and
acceleration
of
\mathcal{V}
repectively by

X(s)=\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{V}(s,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0},\qquad X^{\prime}(s)=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{V}(s,t)}{\partial t^{2}}\right|_{t=0}\qquad\text{for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

We say that a variation is:

(i)

normal
if
X(s)=\langle X(s),N(s)\rangle N(s)
for any
s\in[0,2L]
;

(ii)

geodesic
if
\mathcal{V}(s,t)=\gamma(s)+tX(s)
;

(iii)

area-preserving
if
\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{t})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
for any
t
;

(iv)

first-order area-preserving
if
\int_{\partial\Omega}\langle X,N\rangle=0.

If in the above definitions the interval
[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
is replaced by
[0,t_{\mathcal{V}}]
, we call
\mathcal{V}
a
one-sided admissible variation
. One-sided variations are motivated by the fact that admissible curves must lie in the upper half-plane.
We introduce two relevant classes of admissible geodesic variations, which will suffice to characterize critical points of
\mathcal{F}
under area-preserving variations. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an admissible curve parametrized by arc-length. Let
t_{\mathcal{V}}>0
. Set

(3.2)

\mathcal{V}(s,t)=\gamma_{t}(s)=\gamma(s)+tX(s)\qquad\text{$s\in[0,2L]$, $t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]$},

where
X\in C^{\infty}([0,2L],R^{2})
satisfies
X(0)=X(2L)=0
. If
t_{\mathcal{V}}>0
is sufficiently small,
\mathcal{V}
is an admissible variation provided that
y_{t}>0
on
(0,2L)
for any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathcal{V}(0,t)=(x_{0},0)
and
\mathcal{V}(2L,t)=(-x_{0},0)
. Moreover,

(3.3)

|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}=\langle\dot{\gamma}+t\dot{X},\dot{\gamma}+t\dot{X}\rangle=1+2t\langle\dot{X},\dot{\gamma}\rangle+t^{2}|\dot{X}|^{2}

and

(3.4)

\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t},\ddot{\gamma}_{t}^{\perp}\rangle=\langle\dot{\gamma}+t\dot{X},\ddot{\gamma}^{\perp}+t\ddot{X}^{\perp}\rangle=H+t\left(\langle\dot{\gamma},\ddot{X}^{\perp}\rangle+\langle\ddot{\gamma}^{\perp},\dot{X}\rangle\right)+t^{2}\langle\dot{X},\ddot{X}^{\perp}\rangle.

In particular, if
t
is sufficiently small,
\gamma_{t}
is a regular, smooth, strictly counterclockwise parametrization.
Since
\gamma
is an embedding, then
\gamma_{t}
is an embedding when
t
is small.
Therefore,
\mathcal{V}
is an admissible variation provided that
y_{t}>0
on
(0,2L)
.
∎

In the above generality, it may happen that an arbitrary choice of
X
leads to non-admissible variations. There are two relevant choices that lead to admissible variations.

Example 3.2
.

Choose
X\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,2L)
. Then
\mathcal{V}
is admissible. By the above considerations, it suffices to show that
y_{t}>0
on
(0,2L)
. If
X\equiv 0
the claim is trivial. Assume
\|X\|_{L^{\infty}([0,2L],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}>0
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\supp X\subseteq[\varepsilon,2L-\varepsilon]
. Since
y
is continuous and positive on
(0,2L)
, there exists
c_{3}>0
such that
y(s)\geqslant c_{3}
for any
s\in[\varepsilon,2L-\varepsilon]
. Therefore

y_{t}=y+tX\geqslant c_{3}-|t|\|X\|_{L^{\infty}([0,2L],{\mathbb{R}}^{2})},

and the claim follows when
t
is small.

Example 3.3
.

Choose
X\equiv(f,0)
for any
f\in C^{2}[0,2L]
such that
f(0)=f(2L)=0
. Then
y_{t}=y>0
on
(0,2L)
, and
\mathcal{V}
is trivially admissible.

Following
[
3
]
, we can construct area-preserving variations sufficiently close to first-order area-preserving variations.
Although the following result is essentially
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
, we prove it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
x_{0}>0
and
L>0
. Let
\gamma:[0,2L]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be an admissible curve parametrized by arc-length. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a first-order area-preserving admissible variation of
\gamma
. Denote by
X
and
X^{\prime}
its velocity and acceleration respectively. There exists an area-preserving admissible variation
\mathcal{\tilde{V}}
, with velocity
\tilde{X}
and acceleration
\tilde{X}^{\prime}
, such that

(3.5)

\tilde{X}=X\text{ on $[0,2L]$}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{X}^{\prime}=X^{\prime}\text{ on }\left[0,\frac{L}{2}\right]\cup\left[\frac{3L}{2},2L\right].

Proof.

Denote by
\Omega
the open set enclosed by
\gamma
. Let
\hat{\psi}\in C^{\infty}_{c}\left[\frac{L}{2},\frac{3L}{2}\right]
be such that

\int_{0}^{2L}\hat{\psi}\,ds\neq 0
. For
t,u
small, set
\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t,u)=\gamma+tX+u\hat{\psi}N
. Notice that

\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t,u)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0,2L}\left\langle\gamma+tX+u\hat{\psi}N,N+t\dot{X}^{\perp}+u\frac{d}{ds}\left(\hat{\psi}N^{\perp}\right)\right\rangle\,ds,

whence

\mathcal{A}\left(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,0,0)\right)=|\Omega|
. Moreover, recalling that
X(0)=X(2L)=0
and since
\mathcal{V}
is first-order area-preserving,

\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t,u))}{\partial t}\right|_{(t,u)=(0,0)}=\int_{0}^{2L}\langle X,N\rangle\,ds=0

and

\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t,u))}{\partial u}\right|_{(t,u)=(0,0)}=\int_{0}^{2L}\hat{\psi}\,ds\neq 0.

Therefore, the implicit function theorem implies the existence of
\delta>0
and
g\in C^{\infty}[-\delta,\delta]
such that
g(0)=0
,
\dot{g}(0)=0
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t,g(t)))=|\Omega|
for any
t\in[-\delta,\delta]
. Set
\mathcal{\tilde{V}}(\cdot,t)=\gamma+tX+g(t)\hat{\psi}N
. By construction,
\mathcal{\tilde{V}}
is area-preserving. Moreover, (
3.5
) holds. Te proof that
\mathcal{\tilde{V}}
is admissible, up to choosing
t
small, follows arguing as in
Example

3.2
.
∎

3.2.
Total inverse curvature under Steiner’s symmetrization

In the following, we prove that we can reduce to consider vertically symmetric critical points.
Let
\gamma
be an admissible curve. Denote by
H_{\gamma}(x,y)
its curvature at
(x,y)
. We denote by
\Omega
the bounded domain enclosed by
\gamma
and set

\bar{y}=\max\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,(x,y)\in\overline{\Omega}\}.

Since
\gamma
is admissible,
\Omega
is convex. Therefore, there exist two functions
f,g\in C^{\infty}(0,\bar{y})
such that
f
is convex,
g
is concave and

\Omega=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,f(y)<x<g(y),\,0<y<\bar{y}\}.

We define the
Steiner’s symmetrization

\tilde{\Omega}
of
\Omega
by

\tilde{\Omega}=\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\,-h(y)<x<h(y),\,0<y<\bar{y}\right\},\qquad\text{ where $h=\frac{g-f}{2}$. }

Notice that
-h
is convex and
h
is concave, whence
\tilde{\Omega}
is convex. Let us consider a regular parametrization
\tilde{\gamma}
of
\partial\Omega\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
. By construction,
\tilde{\gamma}
is admissible provided that is strictly counterclockwise.
To this aim, let
(x,y)\in\gamma
. We assume first that
x>0
. Being
\tilde{\gamma}
a graph over the vertical axis,

H_{\tilde{\gamma}}(x,y)=\frac{\ddot{f}(y)-\ddot{g}(y)}{2\left(1+\left(\frac{\dot{f}(y)-\dot{g}(y)}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\ddot{f}(y)}{(1+\dot{f}(y)^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}},\frac{-\ddot{g}(y)}{\left(1+\dot{g}(y)^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right\}=\min\{H_{\gamma}(-x,y),H_{\gamma}(x,y)\},

and in particular
H_{\tilde{\gamma}}(0,\bar{y})\geqslant H_{\gamma}(0,\bar{y})
.
Arguing similarly when
x<0
, we conclude that
H_{\tilde{\gamma}}>0
, whence
\tilde{\gamma}
is an admissible curve. Moreover, it is vertically symmetric by construction.
We show that Steiner’s symmetrization preserves the volume and decreases the total inverse curvature.

Proposition 3.5
.

Fix
x_{0}>0
. Let
\gamma
be an admissible curve. Let
\tilde{\gamma}
be its Steiner’s symmetrization. Then
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\gamma})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
. Moreover,
\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\gamma})<\mathcal{F}(\gamma)
unless
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma
.

Proof.

By Fubini’s theorem,
\mathcal{A}(\tilde{\gamma})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
. To conclude, we are left to show that, if
\tilde{\gamma}\neq\gamma,
then

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\gamma})=\int_{0}^{\bar{y}}\frac{4}{\ddot{f}(y)-\ddot{g}(y)}\left(1+\left(\frac{\dot{f}(y)-\dot{g}(y)}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\,dy<\int_{0}^{\bar{y}}\left(\frac{(1+\dot{f}(y)^{2})^{2}}{\ddot{f}(y)}-\frac{(1+\dot{g}(y)^{2})^{2}}{\ddot{g}(y)}\right)\,dy=\mathcal{F}(\gamma).

To this aim, we consider the function
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\Phi(z,w)=\frac{(1+z^{2})^{2}}{w}\qquad\text{ for any $(z,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty)$.}

A simple computation reveals that, for any
(z,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,+\infty)
,

\trace D^{2}\Phi(z,w)=\frac{4+12z^{2}}{w}+\frac{2(1+z^{2})^{2}}{w^{3}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\det D^{2}\Phi(z,w)=\frac{8(1+z^{2})^{3}}{w^{4}}.

Since
w>0
,
D^{2}\Phi
is positive definite, and therefore
\Phi
is strictly convex. Hence, the thesis follows.
∎

3.3.
A family of candidate critical points

In this section we detect the candidate critical points. We begin with the following rigidity statement.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
x_{0}>0
and
L>0
be fixed. Then
there exist a unique
\lambda>0
and a unique vertically symmetric admissible curve
\gamma=(x,y)
parametrized by arc-length, such that
\dot{y}(0)=\dot{y}(2L)=0
and

(3.6)

2+\frac{d^{2}(H^{-2})}{ds^{2}}=\lambda\qquad\text{ on $[0,2L]$,}

if and only if
3x_{0}<L
.
In these cases,

(3.7)

\lambda=\lambda(x_{0},L)=\frac{2L}{L+x_{0}},

and moreover

\displaystyle x(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle y(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{\pi x_{0}}{\sigma\tan\sigma}

for any
s\in[0,2L]
, where

(3.8)

\kappa=\frac{L}{\sin\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma=\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}.

In the following, we denote such admissible curves by
\gamma(x_{0},L)
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into some steps.

Step 1.
Assume that there exist
\lambda
and
\gamma
as in the statement. Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}
be such that
2+2A=\lambda
.
Then

\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{H^{2}}\right)=2A,

whence there exist
B,C\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

H(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{As^{2}+Bs+C}}\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

By assumption,
\gamma
is vertically symmetric, so that

(3.9)

H(s)=H(2L-s)\qquad\text{for any $s\in[0,L]$}.

Notice that

\begin{split}H(s)^{-2}-H(2L-s)^{-2}&=As^{2}+Bs-A(2L-s)^{2}-B(2L-s)\\
&=A(4Ls-4L^{2})+B(2s-2L)\\
&=2AL(2s-2L)+B(2s-2L)\\
&=(2s-2L)(2AL+B).\end{split}

Therefore, (
3.9
) holds if and only if
2AL+B=0
. In this case,

H(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{As^{2}-2ALs+C}}.

Notice that
A\neq 0
,
since otherwise
\gamma
would be a part of a circle with center on the vertical axis, and in this case
\dot{y}(0)\neq 0
. Moreover,
C>0
, since
H(0)>0
.

Step 2.

We claim that
A<0
. Indeed, assume by contradiction that
A>0
. As
H(L)>0
, we have
C-AL^{2}>0.

Notice that

As^{2}-2ALs+C=A\left(s^{2}-2Ls+\frac{C}{A}\right)=A\left((s-L)^{2}+\frac{C-AL^{2}}{A}\right).

Since
\frac{C-AL^{2}}{A}>0
, there exists a unique
\kappa>0
such that
\kappa^{2}=\frac{C-AL^{2}}{A}
. Conversely, given
A>0
,
L>0
and
\kappa>0
, there exists a unique
C>0
such that
\kappa^{2}=\frac{C-AL^{2}}{A}
. Therefore we may adopt the parameters
(\kappa,A,L)
without loss of generality. In this way,

H(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{(s-L)^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$. }

Set

\theta(s)=\int_{0}^{s}H(l)\,dl.

Then

\theta(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\log\left(\frac{s-L+\sqrt{(s-L)^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}{-L+\sqrt{L^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\right)\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$. }

As
\gamma
is parametrized by arc-length, it is well-known that

(3.10)

\dot{x}(s)=\cos\theta(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{y}(s)=\sin\theta(s)\qquad\text{for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

Since
\gamma
is vertically symmetric, then
\dot{y}(L)=0
, so that
\theta(L)=\pi
.
In particular,

\pi=\theta(L)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\log\left(\frac{k}{-L+\sqrt{L^{2}+k^{2}}}\right),

whence

\begin{split}\theta(s)=\left(\frac{\log\left(s-L+\sqrt{(s-L)^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\right)-\log\left(-L+\sqrt{L^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\right)}{\log\kappa-\log\left(-L+\sqrt{L^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\right)}\right)\pi=\left(1+\frac{\arcsinh\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}{\arcsinh\left(\frac{L}{\kappa}\right)}\right)\pi\end{split}

for any
s\in[0,2L]
. Set
\tau=\arcsinh\left(\frac{L}{\kappa}\right).
Then

\cos\theta(s)=\cos\left(\pi+\frac{\pi}{\tau}\arcsinh\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)=-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{\tau}\arcsinh\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right).

Via the change of variable

(3.11)

\frac{s-L}{\kappa}=\sinh l,\qquad ds=\kappa\cosh l,

we infer that

\begin{split}x(2L)-x(0)&=\int_{0}^{2L}\cos\theta(s)\,ds\\
&\overset{\eqref{changevarpre}}{=}-\kappa\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{\tau}l\right)\cosh l\,dl\\
&=-\kappa\left[\frac{\frac{\pi}{\tau}\cosh(l)\sin(\frac{\pi}{\tau}l)}{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}+\frac{\cos(\frac{\pi}{\tau}l)\sinh(l)}{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}\right]_{-\tau}^{\tau}\\
&=\frac{2L}{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{\tau^{2}}}.\end{split}

On the other hand,
x(2L)-x(0)=-2x_{0}<0
, a contradiction.

Step 3.

By the previous step,
A<0
. Since
C>0
, then
\frac{AL^{2}-C}{A}>0
, and there exists a unique
\kappa>L
such that
\kappa^{2}=\frac{AL^{2}-C}{A}
. Conversely, given
A<0
,
L>0
and
\kappa>L
, there exists a unique
C>0
such that
\kappa^{2}=\frac{AL^{2}-C}{A}
. Arguing as above, we change parameters accordingly. Notice that

As^{2}-2ALs+C=A\left((s-L)^{2}+\frac{C-AL^{2}}{A}\right)=(-A)\left(k^{2}-(s-L)^{2}\right)\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

In this way,

H(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-A}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{k^{2}-(s-L)^{2}}}\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

Set again

\theta(s)=\int_{0}^{s}H(l)\,dl.

Then

\theta(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-A}}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{k}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{-A}}\arcsin\left(\frac{L}{k}\right)\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

Arguing as above,
\theta(L)=\pi
, whence

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-A}}=\frac{\pi}{\arcsin\left(\frac{L}{k}\right)}

and

\theta(s)=\left(1+\frac{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{k}\right)}{\arcsin\left(\frac{L}{k}\right)}\right)\pi\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$.}

Set
\sigma=\arcsin\left(\frac{L}{k}\right).
Then

\cos\theta(s)=\cos\left(\pi+\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)=-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right).

Since
\gamma
is vertically symmetric,
x(L)=0
. Recalling (
3.10
), and via the change of variable

(3.12)

\frac{s^{\prime}-L}{\kappa}=\sin l,\qquad ds^{\prime}=\kappa\cos l,

we infer that

\begin{split}x(s)&=\int_{L}^{s}\cos\theta(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\\
&=-\kappa\int_{0}^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}l\right)\cos l\,dl\\
&=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{0}^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}\cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}+1\right)l\right)\,dl-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int_{0}^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}\cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}-1\right)l\right)\,dl\\
&=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left[\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\right]_{0}^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}\\
&-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right).\end{split}

Therefore, imposing the boundary condition
x(0)=x_{0}
and noticing that

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sin\left(\pi+\sigma\right)=\frac{L}{\kappa},

\displaystyle\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\sin\left(\pi-\sigma\right)=-\frac{L}{\kappa},

we deduce that

(3.13)

x_{0}=x(0)=\frac{L\sigma^{2}}{\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2}}.

Since
0<L<k,
(
3.13
) is equivalent to

\arcsin\left(\frac{L}{\kappa}\right)=\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}},

which in turn admits a solution if and only if
3x_{0}<L
.
In such cases,

\kappa=\frac{L}{\sin\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)},\qquad\sigma=\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda=\frac{2L}{L+x_{0}}.

We proved that, assuming that
0<3x_{0}<L
, there exists at most one couple
\gamma
and
\lambda
as in the statement.

Step 4.
To prove existence it suffices to verify that the curve of the previous step is a solution. To this aim, we just need to find
y
. Notice that

\sin\theta(s)=\sin\left(\pi+\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)=-\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right).

Changing variables as in (
3.12
), and recalling that
y(0)=0
,

\begin{split}y(s)&=\int_{0}^{s}\sin\theta(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\\
&=-\kappa\int^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}_{-\sigma}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}l\right)\cos l\,dl\\
&=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}_{-\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}+1\right)l\right)\,dl-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}_{-\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}-1\right)l\right)\,dl\\
&=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left[\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\cos\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\right]_{-\sigma}^{\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)}\\
&=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\pi+\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{\sigma}{\pi-\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{\pi x_{0}}{\sigma\tan\sigma}.\end{split}

The remaining properties are straightforward, and the thesis follows.
∎

3.4.
Two equivalent notions of stationarity

Fix
x_{0}>0
and
L>0
. Let
\gamma
be an admissible curve. We recall that

\mathcal{F}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\frac{1}{H}\,d\gamma.

For any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we also introduce the penalized functional

\mathcal{F}(\gamma)-\lambda\mathcal{A}(\gamma).

Definition 3.7
.

Fix
x_{0}>0
. Let
\gamma
be an admissible curve.

1.

We say that
\gamma
is an
area-preserving critical point
of
\mathcal{F}
if

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=0\qquad(\text{respectively $\geqslant 0$})

for any area-preserving admissible (respectively one-sided admissible) variation
\mathcal{V}
of
\gamma
.

2.

We say that
\gamma
is a
critical point
of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
if

\left.\frac{d(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=0\qquad(\text{respectively $\geqslant 0$})

for any admissible (respectively one-sided admissible) variation
\mathcal{V}
of
\gamma
.

The next result shows that the admissible curves of
Proposition

3.6
are the unique critical points of both problems.

Theorem 3.8
.

Fix
x_{0}>0
and
L>0
. The following holds.

(i)

\mathcal{F}
admits a vertically symmetric area-preserving critical point
\gamma
of length
2L
if and only if
3x_{0}<L
. In these cases,
\gamma=\gamma(x_{0},L)
and it is unique.

(ii)

\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
admits a vertically symmetric critical point
\gamma
of length
2L
if and only if
3x_{0}<L
and
\lambda=\lambda(x_{0},L)
. In these cases,
\gamma=\gamma(x_{0},L)
and it is unique.

Proof.

We start proving (i). Assume that
3x_{0}<L
. Then we can consider the curve
\gamma(x_{0},L)
given by
Proposition

3.6
. Let
\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)=\gamma_{t}=(x_{t},y_{t})
be an area-preserving admissible variation of
\gamma(x_{0},L)
. By
Proposition

2.2
and
Proposition

3.6
, it holds that

\begin{split}\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}&=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2+\frac{d^{2}(H^{-2})}{ds^{2}}\right)\langle X,N\rangle\,ds+H^{-2}(2L)\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=2L}-H^{-2}(0)\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\\
&=\lambda(x_{0},L)\int_{0}^{2L}\langle X,N\rangle\,ds+H^{-2}(2L)\left(\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=2L}-\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{vpthenfovp}}{=}H^{-2}(2L)\left(\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=2L}-\left.\frac{d\langle X,N\rangle}{ds}\right|_{s=0}\right)\\
&\overset{X(0)=X(2L)=0}{=}H^{-2}(2L)\left(\left\langle\dot{X}(2L),N(2L)\right\rangle-\left\langle\dot{X}(0),N(0)\right\rangle\right)\\
&\overset{\dot{y}(0)=\dot{y}(2L)=0}{=}H^{-2}(2L)\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,0)}-\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,2L)}\right).\end{split}

Notice that
y_{t}(0)=y_{t}(2L)=0
and
y_{t}(s)>0
for any
s\in[0,2L]
and any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
. Therefore
\dot{y}_{t}(0)\geqslant 0
and
\dot{y}_{t}(2L)\leqslant 0
for any
t\in[-t_{\mathcal{V}},t_{\mathcal{V}}]
. Since
\dot{y}_{0}(0)=\dot{y}_{0}(2L)=0
, we conclude that

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,0)}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,2L)}=0,

whence

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=0.

If
\mathcal{V}
is just an area-preserving one-sided admissible variation, we proceed as above, noticing that in this case we can just conclude that

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,0)}\geqslant 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left.\frac{\partial^{2}y_{t}(s)}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,2L)}\leqslant 0,

so that

\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\geqslant 0.

Therefore,
\gamma(x_{0},L)
is an area-preserving critical point of
\mathcal{F}
. To show uniqueness, let
\gamma
be a vertically symmetric area-preserving critical point of length
2L
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,2L)
be such that
\int_{0}^{2L}\varphi\,ds=0
. By
Lemma

3.4
and
Example

3.2
, we find an area-preserving admissible variation such that
X=\varphi N
. Then

(3.14)

0=\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2+\frac{d^{2}(H^{-2})}{ds^{2}}\right)\varphi\,ds.

By (
3.14
), there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

2+\frac{d^{2}(H^{-2})}{ds^{2}}=\lambda

on
[0,2L]
. Let
f\in C^{2}[0,2L]
be such that
f(0)=0
,
f(2L)=0
,
\dot{f}(0)=1
and
\dot{f}(2L)=0
. By
Lemma

3.4
and
Example

3.3
, there exists an area-preserving admissible variation such that
X=(f,0)
. Then, arguing as above and by (
3.14
),

0=\left.\frac{d\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt}\right|_{t=0}=H^{-2}(2L)\left(\left\langle\dot{X}(2L),N(2L)\right\rangle-\left\langle\dot{X}(0),N(0)\right\rangle\right)=-\dot{y}(0).

Therefore
\dot{y}(0)=0
. Similarly,
\dot{y}(2L)=0
. By
Proposition

3.6
, we conclude that
\gamma=\gamma(x_{0},L)
and that

3x_{0}<L
is necessary, and (i) follows. The proof of (ii) is a straightforward adaptation of the above.
∎

3.5.
Uniqueness of critical points with prescribed area

The statement of
Theorem

3.8
ensures existence of critical points with prescribed length
2L
, provided that
0<3x_{0}<L
. In this subsection, we prove that it is equivalent to prescribe the enclosed area. Indeed, for critical points, these two quantities are in bijection, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 3.9
.

Fix
0<3x_{0}<L
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)
be the area enclosed by
\gamma(x_{0},L)
. Then

(3.15)

\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)=\frac{L+x_{0}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}}{\sin\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}\right)^{2}\left(L+(2L+x_{0})\frac{\sin\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}}\right).

In particular,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)}{\partial L}>0,\qquad\inf_{L>3x_{0}}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)=\lim_{L\to 3x_{0}}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)=\frac{3}{2}\pi x_{0}^{2}.

In conclusion, for any
\mathcal{A}_{0}>\frac{3}{2}\pi x_{0}^{2}
, there exists a unique
L=L(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})>3x_{0}
such that
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)
.

Proof.

First we prove (
3.15
).

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)&\overset{\eqref{volumediomegaincurva}}{=}\int_{0}^{2L}x(s)\dot{y}(s)\,ds\\
&=-\frac{\kappa\sigma}{2\pi+2\sigma}\int_{0}^{2L}\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\sin\left(\pi+\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\,ds\\
&\quad-\frac{\kappa\sigma}{2\pi-2\sigma}\int_{0}^{2L}\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\sin\left(\pi+\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\,ds\\
&=\frac{\kappa\sigma}{2\pi+2\sigma}\int_{0}^{2L}\sin\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\,ds\\
&\quad+\frac{\kappa\sigma}{2\pi-2\sigma}\int_{0}^{2L}\sin\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}\arcsin\left(\frac{s-L}{\kappa}\right)\right)\,ds,\\
\end{split}

where
\sigma
is defined in (
3.8
). We change variables as in (
3.12
), and obtain that

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)&=\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma}{2\pi+2\sigma}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}l\right)\cos l\,dl\\
&\quad+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma}{2\pi-2\sigma}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\sigma}l\right)\cos l\,dl\\
&=\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma}{4\pi+4\sigma}\left(\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl+\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma}{4\pi-4\sigma}\left(\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl+\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl\right)\\
&=\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2(\pi+\sigma)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi+\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\zeta\right)\,d\zeta+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2(\pi-\sigma)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi-\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\zeta\right)\,d\zeta\\
&\quad+\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma}{2(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl.\end{split}

First,

\begin{split}\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2(\pi+\sigma)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi+\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\zeta\right)\,d\zeta&=\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{4\pi+4\sigma}-\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{8(\pi+\sigma)^{2}}\sin 2\sigma\\
\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2(\pi-\sigma)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi-\sigma}\sin^{2}\left(\zeta\right)\,d\zeta&=\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{4\pi-4\sigma}+\frac{k^{2}\sigma^{2}}{8(\pi-\sigma)^{2}}\sin 2\sigma\\
\end{split}

Finally,

\begin{split}\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma}{2(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}&\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi+\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\sin\left(\left(\frac{\pi-\sigma}{\sigma}\right)l\right)\,dl\\
&=\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma}{4(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\cos 2l\,dl-\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma}{4(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{\sigma}l\right)\,dl\\
&=\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma}{4(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}\sin 2\sigma.\end{split}

In conclusion,

(3.16)

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)&=\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})}+\frac{\pi\kappa^{2}\sigma(\pi^{2}+\sigma^{2})}{4(\pi^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{2}}\sin 2\sigma\\
&=\frac{L+x_{0}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}}{\sin\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}\right)^{2}\left(L+(2L+x_{0})\frac{\sin\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}}\right).\end{split}

In particular,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)}{\partial L}=\frac{\left(4L\pi^{2}\frac{\cos\!\left(\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}{\sin\!\left(\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}+(3L+4x_{0})\left(\left(\frac{L+x_{0}}{x_{0}}\right)\sin\!\left(2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)+2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L+x_{0}}{x_{0}}}\right)\right)}{8\left(\frac{L+x_{0}}{x_{0}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\,\sin^{2}\!\left(\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)}.

By
L>3x_{0}
,
\sin\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)>0,

\cos\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)>0
and
\sin\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_{0}}{L+x_{0}}}\right)>0
, whence
\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)}{\partial L}>0
. Therefore,
\inf_{L>3x_{0}}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)=\lim_{L\to 3x_{0}}\mathcal{A}_{0}(x_{0},L)
, and the expression of the latter follows from (
3.15
). The last claim is clear from the above.
∎

4.1.
Second variation formulas

We begin computing the second variation formula for
\mathcal{F}
when evaluated at critical points.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
0<3x_{0}<L
. Let
\gamma=\gamma(x_{0},L)
be the unique critical point of
Theorem

3.8
. Let
\lambda=\lambda(x_{0},L)
be as in (
3.7
). Let
\mathcal{V}
be a one-sided admissible variation of
\gamma
. Let
\varphi,\varphi_{\tau},\psi,\psi_{\tau}\in C^{\infty}[0,2L]
be such that

(4.1)

X(s)=\varphi(s)N(s)+\varphi_{\tau}(s)\dot{\gamma}(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad X^{\prime}(s)=\psi(s)N(s)+\psi_{\tau}(s)\dot{\gamma}(s)\qquad\text{ for any $s\in[0,2L]$. }

Then

(4.2)

\begin{split}\left.\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0^{+}}&=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds\\
&\quad+\lambda\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\psi+H\varphi_{\tau}^{2}+2\varphi\dot{\varphi}_{\tau}\right)\,ds+\left[\frac{\dot{\psi}-2\dot{\varphi}\dot{\varphi}_{\tau}}{H^{2}}\right]_{0}^{2L}.\end{split}

If in addition
\mathcal{V}
is an admissible variation, then
\dot{\varphi}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(2L)=0
,
\dot{\psi}(0)\leqslant 0
and
\dot{\psi}(2L)\geqslant 0
, whence

(4.3)

\begin{split}\left.\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t))}{dt^{2}}\right|_{t=0}&=\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\lambda\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\psi+H\varphi_{\tau}^{2}+2\varphi\dot{\varphi}_{\tau}\right)\,ds+\left[\frac{\dot{\psi}}{H^{2}}\right]_{0}^{2L}\\
&\geqslant\int_{0}^{2L}\left(2H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\lambda\int_{0}^{2L}\left(\psi+H\varphi_{\tau}^{2}+2\varphi\dot{\varphi}_{\tau}\right)\,ds.\end{split}

Proof.

As usual, we set
\gamma_{t}=\mathcal{V}(\cdot,t)
for any
t\in[0,t_{\mathcal{V}}]
. As
\gamma_{t}(0)=(x_{0},0)
and
\gamma_{t}(2L)=(-x_{0},0)
,

(4.4)

\varphi(0)=\varphi_{\tau}(0)=\psi(0)=\psi_{\tau}(0)=\varphi(2L)=\varphi_{\tau}(2L)=\psi(2L)=\psi_{\tau}(2L)=0.

Define
\alpha(s,t)=\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle
and
\beta(s,t)=\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\ddot{\gamma}_{t}(s)^{\perp}\rangle
for any
t\in[0,t_{\mathcal{V}}]
. For the sake of notational simplicity, we set
\dot{\alpha}_{0}=\left.\frac{\partial\alpha(\cdot,t)}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0^{+}}
and
\ddot{\alpha}_{0}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha(\cdot,t)}{\partial t^{2}}\right|_{t=0^{+}}
, and accordingly for
\beta.

Notice that
\alpha(s,0)\equiv 1
and
\beta(s,0)=H(s)
for any
s\in[0,2L]
. Then

(4.5)

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\left(\alpha^{2}\beta^{-1}\right)}{\partial t^{2}}\right|_{t=0^{+}}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(2\alpha\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}\beta^{-1}-\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial t}\beta^{-2}\right)\right|_{t=0^{+}}\\
\begin{split}&=\left.\left(2\left(\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}\right)^{2}\beta^{-1}+2\alpha\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha}{\partial t^{2}}\beta^{-1}-4\alpha\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial t}\beta^{-2}-\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\beta}{\partial t^{2}}\beta^{-2}+2\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial t}\right)^{2}\beta^{-3}\right)\right|_{t=0^{+}}\\
&=\frac{2\dot{\alpha}_{0}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\alpha}_{0}}{H}-\frac{4\dot{\alpha}_{0}\dot{\beta}_{0}}{H^{2}}-\frac{\ddot{\beta}_{0}}{H^{2}}+\frac{2\dot{\beta}_{0}^{2}}{H^{3}}\\
&=\frac{2\ddot{\alpha}_{0}}{H}-\frac{\ddot{\beta}_{0}}{H^{2}}+\frac{2}{H}\left(\dot{\alpha}_{0}-\frac{\dot{\beta}_{0}}{H}\right)^{2},\end{split}

Proposition 5.3

Let

Proof.

Since

be a minimizing sequence. Since

We claim that there exists

Indeed, let

. Therefore, by Poincaré-Wirtinger’s inequality (cf.
[

) follows.
Therefore, there exists

\gamma_{t}

\mathscr{L}(q,z,r)=\dfrac{(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2})^{2}}{z_{1}r_{2}-z_{2}r_{1}}-\dfrac{\lambda}{2}(q_{1}z_{2}-q_{2}z_{1})\qquad\text{where}\qquad q=(q_{1},q_{2}),\,z=(z_{1},z_{2}),\,r=(r_{1},r_{2}).

By the definition of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
, we have

(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma_{t})=\int_{0}^{2L}\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\ddot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\,ds.

Let
q(s)
,
r(s)
and
z(s)
be such that

(6.1)

\gamma_{t}(s)=\gamma(s)+t\,q(s),\qquad\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)=\dot{\gamma}(s)+t\,r(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ddot{\gamma}_{t}(s)=\ddot{\gamma}(s)+t\,z(s).

From now on, we will omit the dependence on
s
for the functions that appear along the proof, when this does not create confusion. By the definition of
\gamma_{t}
,
q=\varphi N
. Then a simple computation allows us to find the components of
r
and
z
:

(6.2)

\begin{split}&r_{1}=\dot{\varphi}\dot{y}+\varphi\ddot{y}\hskip 54.06006ptr_{2}=-\dot{\varphi}\dot{x}-\varphi\ddot{x}\\
&z_{1}=\ddot{\varphi}\dot{y}+2\dot{\varphi}\ddot{y}+\varphi\dddot{y}\qquad z_{2}=-\ddot{\varphi}\dot{x}-2\dot{\varphi}\ddot{x}-\varphi\dddot{x}.\end{split}

Denote by
U:=(q,r,z)
. Thanks to (
6.1
), we can apply the Taylor expansion formula with Lagrange remainder to
\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t},\ddot{\gamma}_{t})
, for
t=1
, deducing that, for a suitable
\tau=\tau(s)\in(0,1)
,

(6.3)

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{1},\dot{\gamma}_{1},\ddot{\gamma}_{1})&=\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma})+\left\langle\nabla\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}),U\right\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{\tau},\dot{\gamma}_{\tau},\ddot{\gamma}_{\tau})U,U\right\rangle\\
&=\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma})+\left\langle\nabla\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}),U\right\rangle+\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma})U,U\right\rangle+\langle R_{\tau}U,U\rangle,\end{split}

where
R_{\tau}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{\tau},\dot{\gamma}_{\tau},\ddot{\gamma}_{\tau})-\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma})\right)
.
We now observe that

(6.4)

\int_{0}^{2L}\left\langle\nabla\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma}),U\right\rangle ds=0,

because
\gamma
is critical for
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
, and, by the expression for the second variation of
\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A}
at
\gamma
provided by (
4.18
), we also deduce that

(6.5)

\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2L}\left\langle\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma,\dot{\gamma},\ddot{\gamma})U,U\right\rangle ds\geqslant\int_{0}^{2L}\left((2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds.

Therefore, integrating (
6.3
) and applying (
6.4
) and (
6.5
), we infer that

(6.6)

(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma_{1})\geqslant(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma)+\int_{0}^{2L}\left((2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\int_{0}^{2L}\langle R_{\tau}U,U\rangle\,ds.

We estimate the second integral on the right hand side of (
6.6
). First we observe that

(6.7)

\left|\langle R_{\tau}U,U\rangle\right|\leqslant\left|R_{\tau}U\right|\left|U\right|\leqslant\left|R_{\tau}\right|\left|U\right|^{2}.

Moreover, by the definition of
U
, there exists
C_{1}=C_{1}(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})>0
with the property that, for every
s
,

(6.8)

|U(s)|\leqslant C_{1}\sup\{|\varphi(s)|,|\dot{\varphi}(s)|,|\ddot{\varphi}(s)|\},

and so in particular, for some
C_{2}=C_{2}(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})>0
,

(6.9)

\|U\|_{C^{0}[0,2L]}\leqslant C_{2}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}[0,2L]}\leqslant C_{2}\,\epsilon.

Consider

K:=\{(\gamma_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\ddot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\,:\,s\in[0,2L]\,\,\text{and}\,\,0\leqslant t\leqslant 1\}
.
Recalling that
(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s),\ddot{\gamma}(s))+t\,U(s)
, by the estimate (
6.9
), if we take
\epsilon>0
small enough, we can choose
C_{3}=C_{3}(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})>0
such that

\|\nabla^{3}\mathscr{L}\|_{C^{0}(K)}\leqslant C_{3}.

Hence, we have

(6.10)

|R_{\tau}(s)|=\dfrac{1}{2}|\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma_{\tau}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{\tau}(s),\ddot{\gamma}_{\tau}(s))-\nabla^{2}\mathscr{L}(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s),\ddot{\gamma}(s))|\leqslant\dfrac{C_{3}}{2}|U(s)|.

Thus, (
6.7
) in combination with (
6.10
) and (
6.8
) ensure that, for some
C_{4}=C_{4}(x_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})>0
,

(6.11)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2L}\langle R_{\tau}U,U\rangle\,ds\leqslant\dfrac{C_{1}^{3}\,C_{3}}{2}\int_{0}^{2L}\sup\{|\varphi|,|\dot{\varphi}|,|\ddot{\varphi}|\}^{3}ds\leqslant C_{4}\,\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}[0,2L]}\,\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)}^{2}.\end{split}

Expression (
6.11
) together with (
5.21
) gives that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2L}\left((2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\int_{0}^{2L}R_{\tau}(s)\,ds&\geqslant\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)}^{2}\left(1-\dfrac{C_{4}}{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}[0,2L]}\right),\end{split}

and consequently, if we pick
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,

(6.12)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2L}\left((2-\lambda)H\varphi^{2}-\frac{2\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{H}+\frac{2\ddot{\varphi}^{2}}{H^{3}}\right)\,ds+\int_{0}^{2L}R_{\tau}(s)\,ds&\geqslant\dfrac{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}{2}\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)}^{2}\geqslant 0.\end{split}

Loading (
6.12
) inside (
6.6
) we finally conclude that

(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma)\leqslant(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma_{1})=(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma+\varphi N)\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}[0,2L]}\leqslant\varepsilon.

Clearly, if
(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma)=(\mathcal{F}-\lambda\mathcal{A})(\gamma+\varphi N)
for some
\varphi
satisfying
\|\varphi\|_{C^{2}[0,2L]}\leqslant\varepsilon
, then (
6.6
) and (
6.12
) imply that
\|\varphi\|_{W^{2,2}(0,2L)}=0
, i.e.,
\varphi\equiv 0
.
∎
