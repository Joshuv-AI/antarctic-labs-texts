Knowledge and acceptability of the human papillomavirus vaccine and associated factors among adolescent girls in public primary schools in Dessie Town, South Wollo, Northeast Ethiopia, 2020: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yezbalem Negesse Simegn, Alemtsehay Wossen Samuel, Rediet Gebresilasie Kelelew, Lubaba Ahmed Mohammed, Atrsaw Dessie Liyew, Mengistu Abate Belay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess the knowledge and acceptability of the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine and the associated factors among adolescent girls in public primary schools in Dessie Town, South Wollo, Northeast Ethiopia in 2020.Design, participants and methodsThis was an institutional cross-sectional study conducted from 1 November to 30 November 2020 among 844 adolescent girls. A systematic random sampling method was used to select participants, who completed a pretested, self-administered questionnaire. Data were entered into EpiData V.4.6 and exported to SPSS V.25 for analysis. A binary logistic regression model identified the contributing factors to HPV vaccine knowledge and acceptance. Adjusted OR (AOR) and 95% CI computed at p<0.05 were used to assess significance levels.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcomes of interest were knowledge and acceptability of the HPV vaccine.ResultsThe HPV vaccine is known and acceptable to 47% and approximately 43% of the respondents, respectively. Age (AOR=3.51, 95% CI 1.62 to 7.6), father’s educational level (AOR=2.62, 95% CI 1.57 to 4.38) and parent–child communication about sex and reproductive health (AOR=1.94, 95% CI 1.36 to 2.76) were associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine. Acceptance of the HPV vaccine was significantly influenced by fathers’ educational level (AOR=1.88, 95% CI:1.02 to 3.29), knowledge of Pap smear tests (AOR=2.54, 95% CI 1.79 to 3.62) and knowledge of the HPV vaccine (AOR=5.30, 95% CI 3.77 to 7.73).ConclusionIn this study, there was generally good and high acceptance of the HPV vaccine, respectively, compared with a study conducted in Southwest Ethiopia. The study, however, had limitations in terms of recall bias, assessment of coverage/effectiveness over time and generalisability due to the institutional sample used.Keywords: Adolescent; Feasibility Studies; Factor Analysis, Statistical; Gynaecology; Public health; Obstetrics

Introduction

The non-enveloped, double-stranded papillomavirus family of viruses includes the human papillomavirus (HPV). There are more than 100 distinct HPV genotypes, and at least 13 of these contribute to the development of cervical cancer and are linked to oropharyngeal and other anogenital tumours. HPV types 16 and 18 account for more than 70% of all cervical cancer cases worldwide.1

The HPV vaccine was first developed by the University of Queensland in Australia, and the final form was created by the Georgetown University Medical Center, the University of Rochester and the US National Cancer Institute.2 The first preventative HPV vaccination, sold under the trade name Gardasil, was licensed by the Food and Drug Administration in 2006, and 64 countries initiated HPV immunisation programmes between June 2006 and October 2014.3

Empty protein shells, which resemble virus particles and are created by recombinant technology, are used to develop the HPV vaccines. There are currently three licensed HPV vaccines: Gardasil (a quadrivalent vaccine targeting HPV types 6, 11, 16 and 18), Cervarix (a bivalent vaccine targeting serotypes 16 and 18) and Gardasil 9 (a nonavalent vaccine targeting HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52 and 58).4

Currently, preventing cervical cancer is still the major goal of HPV vaccination. According to WHO, girls between the ages of 9 and 14 are the main target population for HPV vaccination, while boys and girls under the age of 15 are the secondary target population for immunisation. In December 2018, only a few African nations, including Ethiopia, introduced the HPV vaccine.5 Only 68% of high-income countries, 28% of middle-income countries and 2.7% of lower-middle-income countries had a global HPV vaccine coverage rate of 15%–20% among girls.6 In Ethiopia, there are 5000 annual deaths and 7000 new cases of cervical cancer, according to estimates,5 and approximately 84% of cases are caused by HPV.7 Effective cervical cancer screening programmes have been difficult to implement in low-resource settings8; therefore, the best way to protect girls from acquiring HPV infections is by enhancing the uptake of the HPV vaccine.

Cytology-based screening programmes, which serve as the primary method for identifying and treating precancerous abnormalities and early-stage cancer, have been used to prevent up to 80% of cervical cancers in affluent nations. However, in areas with limited resources, it has been challenging to implement effective screening programmes, which is one factor contributing to the significantly higher cervical cancer mortality rates in underdeveloped nations.9 In nations with limited resources, HPV vaccination is a better option to prevent cervical cancer-related illnesses and fatalities in women, compared with cytology, screening or DNA testing.10 In Africa, majority of cervical cancer research has focused on secondary prevention screening, whereas only approximately 23.4% of publications have focused on primary prevention, mainly HPV vaccination.11

In Ethiopia, HPV vaccination is implemented through a standard immunisation programme for girls aged 9–14 years. However, due to the global lack of HPV vaccines, the nation is presently launching single-dose vaccinations for 14-year-old girls.6 Vaccination is largely distributed through a school-based method to ensure that all qualified girls in both private and public schools, as well as out-of-school girls, have access to immunisation at any health institution across the nation.5 However, a study revealed that there is little information describing the issues experienced with vaccinations and the recipient’s awareness of and acceptance of the HPV vaccine.12

Although there is some evidence of HPV vaccine knowledge and acceptance in Ethiopia, these studies focused on parents’ knowledge and acceptance and not on adolescents. Therefore, there remains inadequate information about the knowledge and acceptance of the HPV vaccine.

As far as the literature search has shown, no study has yet been performed to assess the knowledge and acceptance of the HPV vaccine in the study area, and this study fills this information gap. This study aimed to pinpoint the factors that influence people’s understanding and acceptance of the HPV vaccine. It will also serve as guide to organise and improve the delivery of HPV vaccine services in Dessie Town in order to lower the incidence of cervical cancer and cancer-related deaths and the burden it places on women. As there has been no study of its kind in the study area, this study will also offer essential baseline data to health professionals and stakeholders to help them plan and monitor initiatives to eliminate cervical cancer as a significant public health problem. Planners, policymakers and the general public are also concerned about the issue. Thus, the results of this study will contribute to or develop new knowledge in the field of HPV immunisation, improving service quality and coverage. The results of this study will also assist adolescents in increasing their knowledge about the HPV vaccine, increasing their readiness to prevent HPV infections and decreasing the incidence of cervical cancer and its impact on cancer-related deaths in women. Additionally, this study will serve as a starting point for other scholars to continue research in the field.

Materials and methods

Study area and study period

The study was carried out from 1 November to 30 November 2020 in Dessie Town, the administrative centre of the South Wollo administrative zones located in the Amhara Regional State. Dessie is located 480 km from Bahir Dar, the capital of the Amhara Regional State, and 400 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The town has an area of 28 km2. According to the 2007 national census of the Central Statistical Agency in Ethiopia, Dessie Town has 120 095 residents overall, 52% of whom are women. 32 public and 9 private primary schools are located in Dessie Town. Additionally, it has 28 097 elementary students in total, 13 746 of whom are female.

Study design

An institution-based, cross-sectional study was conducted.

Source population

All adolescent girls in public primary schools, Dessie Town, South Wollo, Ethiopia were the source population.

Study population

All adolescent girls who attended education in the selected public primary schools during the data collection period comprised the study population.

Inclusion criteria

All adolescent girls in public primary schools in Dessie Town, South Wollo, Ethiopia were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Parents and adolescent girls who were severely ill and unable to give consent were excluded from the study.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the general public were not involved in determining the study design. All patients who participated were informed about the research aims and provided written consent to participate voluntarily. Their perspectives were not consulted when analysing the survey responses or reporting the findings. The results were not shared or communicated with the patients.

Sampling procedure

From 32 public primary schools in Dessie Town, 9 were selected via simple random sampling. On the basis of proportional allocation, the sample sizes selected from each health facility were as follows: 90 in Dawdo, 81 in Robit, 89 in Merha Tibeb, 70 in Adis Fana, 120 in Hote, 108 in Tosa, 85 in Kidame Gebeya and 106 in Etege Menen. Skip interval in Gerado (n=95) was determined for each school by dividing the estimated population by the respective sample size (ie, Kth=N/n). Hence, each participant was selected by systematic sampling with a skip interval of 2 at each institution. Using the lottery method, the first participant was selected and used as the starting number on the basis of the arrival order. Every other student was subsequently included until the desired sample size was achieved (figure 1).

Study variables

Knowledge and acceptance of the HPV vaccine were the dependent variables of interest in this study. Sociodemographic variables and reproductive health-related factors (age, grade level, parental educational status, parental occupation, religion, parental status and family income, monthly family history of cervical cancer, fear of future HPV infection, hearing about the HPV vaccine and information about Pap smear tests, and knowledge of the HPV vaccine) were the independent variables.

Operational definitions

Participants who answered 6 or more of the 11 questions that assess knowledge were considered to have good knowledge.13 High acceptability was considered among participants who had a mean score of 3.1 and above on the acceptance items.14

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire via pretested questionnaires adopted from the literature.13 The questionnaires included questions about sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge of the HPV vaccine, HPV vaccine acceptability and reproductive health. The questionnaires were prepared in English and then translated to the regional language Amharic to make them simple and understandable; the questionnaires were then translated back to English to maintain consistency. Four midwives who were diploma holders and were familiar with the local language and customs were recruited as data collectors. Midwives with two degrees were assigned as supervisors. Overall supervision was also provided by the investigator.

A 1-day training was given to the data collectors and supervisors on data collection procedures, content of the questionnaire and confidentiality of information obtained from the respondents. Before starting the data collection, the data collectors provided appropriate information about the filling procedures used by the participants each day.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured during data collection, entry and analysis. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of respondents (42 respondents) in Kombolcha Primary School (Goffa) for teenage girls to determine the content, wording and linguistic understandability of the questions prior to the actual data collection time of 1 month. Every day, every questionnaire was verified to determine if it had been completed correctly. Additionally, the primary investigator closely supervised the data collectors to ensure the quality of data collection.

Data processing and analysis

EpiData V.4.6 was used to validate, purge, code and input the data, which were then exported to SPSS V.25 for analysis. Frequencies, percentage means and SD were computed as descriptive statistics. Text, graphs and tables were used to portray the findings after analysis. Bivariable logistic regression analysis was used to test each independent variable’s relationship with the dependent variable, and all associated variables with a p value of less than 0.25 were added to the final multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent variables linked to the dependent variables. The Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the fitness of the models. To assess the existence and strength of the link, an adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% CI was calculated. A p value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Ethical clearance

Written assent was obtained from the participants and written informed consent was obtained from their parents after the purpose of the study was explained. The students were informed that all the data obtained would be kept confidential and anonymous. To ensure confidentiality, the names of the respondents were replaced by code numbers. Students had the right to withdraw at any point during the data collection.

Reproductive health-related factors

Of all participants, 608 (72.6%) and 632 (75.4%) had no history of cervical cancer and Sexually transmited diseases, respectively. Approximately half of the participants (51.3%) had heard about Pap smear tests, and most feared HPV infections (58.6%). Of the participants, 16 (2%) had previous sexual experience and only 31.3% freely communicate about sex and reproductive health with their parents.

Factors associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine

Bivariate analysis was performed to identify the factors associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine. Significantly associated factors identified were the respondent’s age, the respondent’s grade level, father’s educational level, mother’s educational level, fear of HPV infection, information about Pap smear tests and free communication about sex and reproduction with the parents (p<0.25).

Multivariate analysis

All variables that had a statistically significant association with knowledge of the HPV vaccine according to the bivariable analysis (p<0.25) were included in the multivariable analysis model. The multivariable analysis model revealed that the respondent’s age, father’s educational level, ever having heard about Pap smear tests and parental communication about sex and reproduction were significantly associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine (p<0.05) (table 4).

Discussion

An institution-based study was conducted to determine the magnitude of knowledge and acceptance of the HPV vaccine and the associated factors among adolescent girls in Dessie Town. It is becoming important to increase knowledge and acceptance of HPV vaccination to promote healthy behaviour and knowledge of important measures of HPV vaccination. Early knowledge of the benefits, particularly prevention of cervical cancer, is highly important in preventing diseases in developing countries such as Ethiopia. Several HPV vaccine-related studies have been conducted that have widely ignored HPV vaccinations among the target population.14 15

In this study, nearly half (47.3%, 95% CI 43.8% to 50.7%) of the respondents had good knowledge of the HPV vaccine. This finding was consistent with the studies performed in Uganda (44%), Ethiopia (43.8%) and southern India (44.8%).15,17 However, this percentage is greater than that reported in a study from Southwest Nigeria (21%).17 This difference might be due to variations in study area or differences in geographical location, which may be a significant factor in terms of accessing different sources of information to obtain knowledge about the HPV vaccine. On the other hand, the proportion of knowledge was lower than that reported in a study conducted in Hong Kong (59%).18 This difference might be due to population lifestyle, level of development and economic variations between countries.

Age, father’s educational level, hearing about Pap smear tests and parental communication about sex and reproduction were significantly associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine (p<0.05). In this study, age was associated with knowledge of HPV vaccination, where students aged 13–14 years were 3.5 times more likely to have good knowledge than those aged 9–10 years (AOR=3.51, 95% CI 1.62 to 7.6). This finding is supported by a study conducted in Hong Kong and Nigeria.13 16

This study also revealed that the likelihood of knowledge of the HPV vaccine was 2.6 times greater among respondents whose fathers had a diploma and above than among those whose fathers did not attend at all or had no formal education. This could be because with increasing level of education fathers might be more exposed to health information, particularly immunisation for children, and thus be educated about and be up-to-date with their children’s HPV vaccination to prevent cervical cancer.

Having heard about Pap tests was also significantly associated with knowledge about HPV vaccination. Participants who have heard about Pap tests were three times more likely to have good knowledge than those who have not heard about Pap tests. This finding is consistent with the findings of other studies in Morocco.19 This might be because students who have information about cervical cancer screening are able to obtain information about prevention methods for cervical cancer.

Lastly, this study revealed that those who freely communicated about sex and reproduction with their parents were two times more likely to have good knowledge (AOR=1.94, 95% CI 1.36 to 2.76). A possible reason for this finding may be that parents who communicate more openly with their adolescents regarding sexual topics and protective sexual health are more likely to discuss perceptions of being at risk for HPV infection, the benefits of immunisation, and the side effects, safety and effectiveness of the HPV vaccine, which will increase the knowledge level of adolescents with regard to the HPV vaccine.

Acceptability of the HPV vaccine

In this study, the proportion of adolescent girls who received the HPV vaccine was 42.7% (95% CI 39.3% to 46.2%). This finding shows that despite the problems associated with cervical cancer, many girls do not accept the HPV vaccine. This percentage was lower than that reported in a study performed in the USA, which is at 52%.20 This difference might be due to the sociodemographic and sociodevelopmental characteristics of the participants in developed countries, as developed countries have greater access to health-based information and are expected to have a higher level of health literacy than developing countries such as Ethiopia. The acceptance rate for this study is consistent with that of another study in Southwest Nigeria, which was at 40%.17 However, these findings are higher than those in Ethiopia, where the percentage of acceptance in Jimma Town was 36.8%13 and in Morocco 27%.19 The possible explanation for these differences could be study time variations. For example, the study in Ethiopia was conducted before 6 years, at which time HPV vaccine provision was not started in the country, and the current promotion and introduction of the HPV vaccine might increase the acceptance of the HPV vaccine. Similarly, the differences with Morocco might be due to differences in study time, study area or study participants.

In this study, fathers’ educational level was significantly associated with acceptance of the HPV vaccine among adolescents. Fathers of participants who completed primary education or who had a diploma or above were 3 and 1.88 times more likely to accept the HPV vaccine, respectively, than those who did not have a formal education. In addition, participants who had ever heard about Pap smear tests were 2.5 times more likely to accept the HPV vaccine than those who had no information about it. This finding is similar to that of a study performed in Morocco.19

These findings are in line with those of other studies conducted in Ethiopia,13 Nigeria,17 southern India21 and Teresina.22 Knowledge of the HPV vaccine was significantly associated with acceptability of the vaccine. The reason might be that those who have good knowledge of the benefits, side effects, safety, effectiveness and immunisation schedule of HPV vaccination could be more interested in accepting and getting themselves vaccinated.

The methodological approach used was cross-sectional; therefore, it gives a snapshot in time but does not show cause and effect interactions. With regard to this aspect, it is also more difficult to learn about change in acceptance and knowledge over time. Furthermore, self-completed questionnaires have proclivity for bias due to overestimation of acceptability or ability as a result of social desirability effects. In addition, the study focused on adolescents only, and while the focus is on adolescent girls, not placing enough emphasis on the parents, healthcare professionals and educators may limit the wider context that informs vaccine knowledge and acceptability.

Conclusions

In this study, knowledge and acceptability of the HPV vaccine were overall good and high, respectively, compared with those reported in a study conducted in Southwest Ethiopia. Age, fathers’ educational level, hearing about Pap smear tests and parents’ communication with their children about sex and reproductive health are associated with knowledge of the HPV vaccine. Meanwhile, fathers’ educational level, hearing about the Pap smear test and knowledge of the HPV vaccine are the factors associated with acceptability of the HPV vaccine. In general, increasing parents’ educational level and awareness of cervical cancer screening will improve the acceptance of and knowledge about the HPV vaccine.
