BCAA catabolism mediates POU2AF1 propionylation to enhance T-ALL development
Authors: Sijia Dan, Yilu Xu, Li Xie, Xiaoxiao He, Haotian Liu, Lu Zhao, Liyuan Cao, Bo Qu, Yan Lu, Chiqi Chen, Zhuo Yu, Jiangbo Wan, Pu Chen, Junke Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeT-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) is an aggressive, life-threatening hematological malignancy with limited therapeutic regimens. While metabolic reprogramming is known to play critical roles in leukemogenesis, how distinct metabolic pathways orchestrate T-ALL pathogenesis remains largely unknown. We herein aim to unravel how the branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism fine-tunes T-ALL cell fates.MethodsMetabolomic and transcriptomic analyses were performed to identify branched-chain amino acid transaminase 1 (BCAT1)-associated metabolic and molecular alterations. Functional studies in murine T-ALL models were conducted to evaluate the role of BCAT1 in leukemia development, including the assessment of leukemia burden by flow cytometry during T-ALL development. Biochemical and molecular assays were used to assess POU2AF1 propionylation and its downstream signaling. Clinical datasets were analyzed to investigate the clinical relevance of BCAT1 in T-ALL.ResultsWe revealed that BCAT1 could serve as a critical driver to sustain T-ALL initiation and progression. BCAT1 was highly expressed in both murine and human T-ALL cells and was critical for the self-renewal and homing capacities of leukemia cells. Mechanistically, BCAT1-mediated BCAA catabolism promoted the propionylation of transcriptional coactivator POU2AF1, which further enhanced its ability to transactivate SLC7A11 expression to suppress ferroptosis and support the proliferation of T-ALL cells. Moreover, BCAT1 expression levels were strongly correlated with poor prognosis in T-ALL patients. Combined treatment with dietary BCAA restriction and immune checkpoint blockade synergistically inhibited T-ALL progression.ConclusionOur findings reveal a novel mechanism whereby BCAA catabolism mediates ferroptosis resistance through POU2AF1 propionylation during T-ALL progression. Therapeutic blockade of the BCAT1-POU2AF1-SLC7A11 axis holds considerable translational potential in T-ALL treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01201-w.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common hematological malignancy in children, among which approximately 15–20% of cases involve T-cell lineage transformation [1]. T-ALL is a highly aggressive and genetically heterogeneous hematopoietic disorder caused by alterations in intrinsic factors and bone marrow niche components, including transcription factors, cell cycle regulators, and epigenetic modifications, during early T-cell development [2–4]. Current treatments for T-ALL mainly rely on chemotherapy or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Although these treatments have overall favorable clinical effects in children and youth, the 5-year overall survival rate in adult patients is still less than 50% and is even lower in patients older than 50 years [4, 5]. For example, combined chemotherapy regimens with vincristine, anthracyclines (e.g., daunorubicin), and cytarabine have been considered the standard of care [6], but the interindividual variability in treatment response often leads to relapse and therapeutic failure. Currently, several challenges limit the potential therapeutic outcomes, including dose-limiting toxicity, the emergence of acquired resistance associated with conventional chemotherapy, limited donor availability and risks of graft-versus-host disease in allo-HSCT [7, 8]. Therefore, it is urgent to identify novel targets and therapeutic strategies for T-ALL treatment.

Increasing evidence indicates that T-ALL cell fates are governed by a complex interplay between intrinsic factors (such as genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications and metabolism) and extrinsic signals from the microenvironment [9, 10]. Notably, metabolic reprogramming has been recognized as a hallmark of T-ALL development. For example, enhanced aerobic glycolysis and altered glutamine utilization support T-ALL cell proliferation through the provision of essential energy sources and biosynthetic molecules [11]. Furthermore, NOTCH1-driven metabolic rewiring promotes nucleotide biosynthesis, thereby facilitating leukemic cell expansion through increased intracellular nucleotide pools [12]. Mitochondrial metabolism also contributes to T-ALL progression, as disruptions in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity and redox homeostasis sustain cell growth and survival [13]. However, the roles of different nutrients in T-ALL development are largely unknown.

In recent years, amino acid metabolism has attracted much attention from researchers because of its multiple important roles in many physiological and pathological activities [14, 15]. In particular, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, have been reported to be involved in lipid metabolism, diabetes, cardiovascular disorders and cancers [16, 17]. BCAAs affect cell fates by modulating diverse cellular processes, including mTOR signaling [18], mitochondrial function [19], and DNA methylation [20]. BCAAs are essential amino acids and can be converted into branched-chain keto acids (BCKAs), catalyzed by branched-chain aminotransferases (BCATs) such as cytosolic BCAT1 and mitochondrial BCAT2 [21]. BCAT1 has been reported to serve as the rate-limiting enzyme in BCAA degradation and as a potent oncogene in several tumor types. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), BCAT1 accelerates BCAA catabolism to suppress TET2-dependent DNA demethylation and promote epigenetic reprogramming in AML cells [20]. Similarly, in blast crisis chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), BCAT1 is aberrantly reactivated and promotes disease progression by facilitating intracellular BCAA synthesis, blocking myeloid differentiation, and sustaining leukemia-initiating cell self-renewal [22]. We previously reported that BCAA catabolism enhances AML progression by maintaining high levels of glycolysis and reducing Meis1 ubiquitination [23]. Recent work has identified BCAT1 as a direct NOTCH1 target in T-ALL, focusing on how its activity limits the accumulation of the HDAC-inhibitory metabolite 3-hydroxybutyrate, thereby regulating protein acetylation and supporting leukemic proliferation [24].

We herein provide striking evidence showing that BCAT1 enhances the self-renewal and homing capacity of T-ALL cells. Notably, we demonstrate that propionyl-CoA, a key intermediate metabolite, is involved in POU2AF1 propionylation, thereby maintaining its protein stability and suppressing SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis. In addition, our findings also demonstrated a potential application for T-ALL treatment by combining BCAA restriction with immunotherapy. These findings provide new insights beyond previous reports by linking BCAT1-dependent metabolism to downstream transcriptional regulation in T-ALL. Our data not only provide unique insights into metabolic reprogramming in T-ALL cell fate determination, but also aid in the development of effective strategies to overcome the current limitations in T-ALL treatments.

Results

Deletion of BCAT1 suppresses the initiation and progression of T-ALL

To identify metabolic alterations associated with T-ALL development, we conducted untargeted metabolomic profiling of bone marrow (BM) cells derived from T-ALL mice and healthy controls. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct metabolic signatures between T-ALL and Lin− cells (Fig. 1A), with elevated BCAA metabolism in T-ALL (Fig. 1B), indicating that BCAA metabolism may be important for T-ALL development. To clarify its specific role in T-ALL, we treated murine cell lines with exogenous BCAAs or their downstream metabolites of BCKAs and found that both metabolites increased cell proliferation, whereas BCAAs had a more potent effect (sFig. 1A–B). These findings demonstrate a metabolic preference for BCAAs in T-ALL development. Interestingly, as the first rate-limiting enzyme for BCAA catabolism, BCAT1 was expressed at the highest level in T-ALL cells derived from a Notch1-induced murine T-ALL model compared with those in long-term/short-term hematopoietic stem cells (LT/ST-HSCs) and normal bulk bone marrow, as determined by quantitative RT‒PCR analysis (Fig. 1C). We then designed two short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) and demonstrated that knockdown of Bcat1 in murine cell lines (EL4 and L1210) markedly suppressed cell proliferation in vitro (sFig. 1C–E).

By crossing Bcat1flox/flox mice with Vav1-improved Cre (iCre) transgenic mice, we then generated conditional knockout mice (Bcat1fl/fl; Vav1-iCre) in which Bcat1 was specifically deleted in all hematopoietic cells, as verified by both RT‒PCR and Western blotting (Fig. 1D–E). We then established a T-ALL murine model by overexpressing Notch1 in Lin⁻ bone marrow cells isolated from Bcat1fl/fl (wild-type, WT) and Bcat1fl/fl; Vav1-iCre (knockout, KO) mice, followed by transplantation into lethally irradiated recipient mice as reported previously [25]. Interestingly, we found that Bcat1 deletion led to a significant delay in T-ALL development as evidenced by a reduced proportion of GFP⁺ leukemia cells in peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 1F–G and sFig. 1F), decreased leukemic infiltration in the liver, spleen and thymus (Fig. 1H–J and sFig. 1G–H) and a prolonged survival (Fig. 1K) in recipient mice transplanted with Bcat1-deficient Notch1-induced leukemia cells compared with those in WT mice.

Interestingly, compared with WT control cells, Bcat1-deficient T-ALL cells had a greater percentage of CD4⁺CD8⁺ (DP) cells and a lower percentage of CD4⁻CD8⁻ (DN) cells in the bone marrow, spleen and thymus, suggesting a trend toward enhanced maturation or differentiation [26] (sFig. 1I–K). Additionally, compared with WT leukemic cells, Bcat1-deficient leukemic cells contained fewer CD25⁺CD44⁻ cells (DN3 stage, sFig. 1L–N). Given that DN3 thymocytes are known to have potent leukemogenic potential [27], these results suggest that BCAT1 regulates the malignant transformation of T-cell lymphoid progenitors during leukemogenesis.

BCAT1 enhances the self-renewal and homing capacity of T-ALL cells

To further evaluate the roles of BCAT1 in other cell fates of T-ALL cells, including self-renewal, homing and apoptosis, we performed secondary transplantation and revealed that Bcat1 deletion markedly impaired the self-renewal capacity of T-ALL cells, as evidenced by the reduced T-ALL cell burden in peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 2A–B), attenuated leukemia cell infiltration (Fig. 2C–D) and prolonged survival (Fig. 2E) in leukemic mice transplanted with Bcat1-null T-ALL cells compared with those in control mice. In addition, we performed the tertiary transplantation and consistently observed similar phenotypes in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells, with a reduced leukemic cell burden in peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 2F–I). Moreover, Bcat1-null leukemic cells exhibited increased apoptosis (Fig. 2J–K) and decreased homing ability to the bone marrow (Fig. 2L–M), but showed no significant changes in cell cycle distribution (Fig. 2N–O). In addition, regarding that Bcat1 was expressed in LT-HSCs, B-ALL cells and AML cells (Fig. 1B), and Bcat1 is required for HSC repopulation and AML development as we previously reported [23]. We herein further established a N-Myc-induced murine B-ALL model and found that BCAT1 had no significant effect on the progression of B-ALL (sFig. 2A–D). To assess the impact of BCAT1 on normal T-cell development in the thymus, we also examined T-cell subsets at different developmental stages in thymus samples from Bcat1fl/fl and Bcat1fl/fl; Vav1-iCre mice. Our results revealed that BCAT1 had no effect on T-cell development (sFig. 2E–H). These findings suggest that BCAT1 specifically supports T lymphoid malignancy development without impairing normal T-cell lineage differentiation.

BCAT1-mediated BCAA catabolism promotes POU2AF1 propionylation to enhance T-ALL progression

To elucidate the downstream molecular mechanisms of BCAT1 in the regulation of T-ALL development, we performed RNA-seq with WT and Bcat1-null T-ALL cells. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that several pathways, including transcriptional misregulation in cancer, immune checkpoint signaling and ferroptosis, were notably downregulated in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells (Fig. 3A). Indeed, several genes related to transcriptional misregulation in cancer (Tfap4, Stat4, Runx2, and Pou2af1) and ferroptosis (Cybb and Slc7a11) were significantly decreased in Bcat1-null leukemia cells (sFig. 3A). Interestingly, among the differentially expressed genes, POU class 2 homeobox associated factor 1 (Pou2af1) and Slc7a11 (also commonly known as xCT) were ranked as the top 2 downregulated genes upon Bcat1 deletion (Fig. 3B). Consistently, quantitative RT‒PCR further confirmed markedly reduced Pou2af1 and Slc7a11 expression in Bcat1-null leukemia cells (Fig. 3C). POU2AF1 serves as a key transcription factor that plays important roles in cell proliferation, and accumulating evidence has demonstrated its oncogenic potential in multiple myeloma [28]. To determine whether POU2AF1 functions downstream of Bcat1, we compared its protein abundance and found that POU2AF1 expression was markedly lower in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells than in WT controls, falling to less than 5% of control levels (Fig. 3D). Given that the reduction in POU2AF1 protein level was much more dramatic than the decrease at the mRNA level, we expected that BCAT1 may regulate POU2AF1 expression mainly through post-translational modification. We then ectopically overexpressed Pou2af1 in L1210 and EL4 cells and demonstrated that it could reverse the proliferation defects caused by Bcat1 knockdown (sFig. 3B–C), suggesting that POU2AF1 plays a potential role in T-ALL development.

Emerging evidence identifies lysine residue propionylation (Kpr), a novel post-translational modification, as critical for regulating protein stability and function [29]. Notably, propionyl-CoA is one of the key metabolites generated during BCAA catabolism and contributes to the lysine propionylation of various proteins, ultimately modulating cellular functions and the pathogenesis of diseases [30, 31]. Based on this evidence, we speculated that POU2AF1 might undergo propionylation. To test this, we first performed immunoblotting, which revealed a marked decrease in global protein propionylation levels in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells relative to control cells (Fig. 3E). Treatment with BCAAs or BCKAs in Jurkat cells increased global protein propionylation levels (Fig. 3F). Notably, POU2AF1 lysine propionylation increased with increasing BCAA concentrations in HEK293T cells (Fig. 3G), indicating that the intermediate metabolite of propionyl-CoA derived from BCAA catabolism might play important roles in protein propionylation. We then performed mass spectrometry and identified that lysine residues at 5 and 25 (K5 and K25) were two propionylation sites of POU2AF1 (sFig. 3D–E) and both of which were highly conserved. (sFig. 3F). Mutation of K5 to arginine (K5R), but not K25 to arginine (K25R), abolished POU2AF1 propionylation and decreased protein stability in HEK293T cells, indicating that K5 propionylation fine-tunes the level of POU2AF1 protein (Fig. 3H–J). More importantly, in vivo functional assays with overexpression of POU2AF1 K5 mutant revealed that this mutation could not reverse the decreased cell proliferation capacity of Bcat1-null leukemic cells, as evidenced by a decreased leukemic cell percentage in peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation, extended overall survival and reduced infiltration in the spleen and liver (Fig. 3K–M and sFig. 3G–H). Consistently, the results of the homing assays further demonstrated that the POU2AF1 K5 mutant could not rescue the impaired homing ability to the bone marrow niche of T-ALL cells (Fig. 3N–O). Collectively, these findings provide unique evidence that the metabolite of propionyl-CoA derived from BCAA catabolism increases POU2AF1 K5 propionylation to maintain its stability and potential function.

POU2AF1 transactivates SLC7A11 to support T-ALL development

Since KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of the ferroptosis pathway and a marked decrease in the expression of the ferroptosis-related gene SLC7A11 in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells, we investigated the potential connection between POU2AF1 and SLC7A11. We found that knockdown of Bcat1 led to a dramatic reduction in SLC7A11 protein levels in murine cell lines (Fig. 4A). Moreover, compared with WT control cells, Bcat1-null T-ALL cells had much lower SLC7A11 protein levels (Fig. 4B). The overexpression of Pou2af1 in Bcat1-null T-ALL cells restored the protein level of SLC7A11 (Fig. 4C). To determine whether POU2AF1 directly regulates SLC7A11 expression, we performed Pou2af1 knockdown in murine EL4 T-ALL cell line (sFig. 4A). Notably, depletion of POU2AF1 markedly reduced SLC7A11 protein levels (sFig. 4B). Luciferase analysis further demonstrated that Pou2af1 overexpression effectively transactivated Slc7a11 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4D). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays revealed that POU2AF1 was enriched at the promoter region of Slc7a11. In contrast, compared with the POU2AF1 WT, the POU2AF1 K5R mutant failed to transcriptionally activate Slc7a11, indicating that this residue is critical for its transcriptional activity (Fig. 4E).

BCAT1 is important for human T-ALL cell proliferation

To elucidate the role of BCAA metabolism and the function of BCAT1 in human T-ALL development, we first conducted in vitro validation by treating human T-ALL cell lines with exogenous BCAAs or BCKAs. Both significantly enhanced T-ALL cell proliferation, with BCAAs exerting a more potent effect than their downstream metabolite BCKAs (sFig. 5A–B). Subsequently, to establish the clinical relevance of BCAA metabolism in T-ALL and further characterize the role of BCAT1, we analyzed the potential connection between BCAT1 and patient overall survival by the TCGA database. Interestingly, the BCAT1 mRNA levels in human T-ALL cells were much higher than those in normal bone marrow cells (Fig. 5A), which were inversely correlated with the overall survival of patients (Fig. 5B). We then knocked down the expression of BCAT1 in human T-ALL cell lines using several BCAT1-targeting shRNAs (sFig. 5C–D) and found that knockdown of BCAT1 expression could significantly inhibit the proliferation of human T-ALL cell lines (sFig. 5E–F). In vivo transplantation also revealed that leukemia progression in the recipient mice injected with BCAT1-knockdown Jurkat cells was much slower than that in the control mice, as indicated by the lower frequency of leukemia cells in the peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 5C–D), reduced infiltration in the spleen and liver (Fig. 5E–F), and prolonged overall survival (Fig. 5G).

Consistently, knockdown of BCAT1 in human primary T-ALL cells led to a notable decrease in cell proliferation in vitro (Fig. 5H–J). We then employed a PDX model to recapitulate T-ALL progression in vivo by injecting patient BCAT1-knockdown T-ALL cells into NOD-SCID mice (sFig. 5G). We found that the knockdown of BCAT1 in human primary T-ALL cells resulted in a notable delay in leukemia progression, as evidenced by a much lower leukemic cell frequency in the peripheral blood at 3 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. 5K–L), alleviated infiltration in the spleen and liver (Fig. 5M–N), and substantially extended survival of the recipient mice transplanted with BCAT1-knockdown leukemic cells (Fig. 5O). Taken together, these results suggest that BCAT1 is important for the progression of human T-ALL.

To determine whether the BCAT1-POU2AF1-SLC7A11 pathway identified in murine models is also functional in human T-ALL, we examined the regulatory pathways in human T-ALL cells. Consistent with our findings in mouse cells, knockdown of POU2AF1 in Jurkat cells reduced SLC7A11 expression, confirming that the POU2AF1-mediated regulation of SLC7A11 is conserved in human T-ALL (sFig. 5H–I). Furthermore, supplementation with combined valine and isoleucine or total BCAAs markedly increased global protein propionylation levels, and SLC7A11 protein expression was also significantly upregulated, especially in response to total BCAA treatment (sFig. 5J). These findings indicate that BCAA metabolism promotes POU2AF1 propionylation and enhances its ability to induce SLC7A11 expression in human T-ALL.

Given the critical role of SLC7A11 in suppressing ferroptosis, together with our observation that BCAT1 knockdown markedly downregulates SLC7A11 expression (Fig. 4A–B), we further investigated whether BCAT1 deficiency induces ferroptotic phenotypes in human T-ALL cells. We found that BCAT1 knockdown significantly increased lipid peroxidation compared with control cells, a hallmark of ferroptosis (sFig. 5K–L). Furthermore, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 [32]significantly restored cell viability in BCAT1-knockdown Jurkat cells (sFig. 5M). Consistently, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) [33], a ROS scavenger, also significantly rescued the reduced cell viability caused by BCAT1 knockdown (sFig. 5M). Together, these results demonstrate that BCAT1 sustains the POU2AF1-SLC7A11 axis to maintain redox homeostasis and suppress ferroptosis, thereby supporting the proliferation and progression of human T-ALL.

Combined treatment with BCAA restriction and PD-1 blockade suppresses T-ALL progression

To evaluate the potential therapeutic effect of targeting BCAT1 on T-ALL, we first used a BCAT1-specific inhibitor, gabapentin [34], to treat both murine and human T-ALL cell lines in vitro, and revealed that the treatment efficiently suppressed the proliferation of leukemia cells (Fig. 6A–D). As indicated in the KEGG analysis from our RNA-seq, the pathways related to “PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer” showed significant differences between these two groups; therefore, we further treated a Notch1-induced murine T-ALL model with a combination of a low-BCAA diet and PD-1–blocking antibodies, which synergistically inhibited leukemia cell infiltration (Fig. 6E–F) and extended the overall survival of recipient mice (Fig. 6G). In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that BCAT1 promotes T-ALL initiation and progression through the propionylation modification of POU2AF1, which further enhances its stability to transactivate the expression of SLC7A11 to suppress ferroptosis. Targeting BCAT1-mediated BCAA catabolism with or without immunotherapy may offer a promising new strategy for T-ALL treatment (Fig. 6H). Discussion

In the current study, we showed that BCAT1 plays important roles in the development of T-ALL. These findings may reflect the unique metabolic demands of T-ALL cells. In particular, we found that certain intermediate metabolites from BCAT1-mediated BCAA catabolism are important for post-translational modification, indicating that intermediate metabolites play additional roles in pathological activities. Given that BCAAs contain leucine, isoleucine and valine, it is still unclear which type of BCAAs and their downstream metabolites are mainly involved in the progression of T-ALL. Previous studies have shown that BCAA catabolism is also required for the regulatory mechanisms of other malignant hematopoietic disorders, including acute myeloid leukemia and chronic myeloid leukemia [20, 22]. Therefore, determining the unique regulatory roles of BCAAs in different physiological or pathological activities is urgent for precise treatments with these disorders.

Here, we identify a novel regulatory link between BCAA metabolism and protein propionylation that is involved in protein stability, which is totally distinct from the protein acetylation as reported in previous studies. Since both acetyl-CoA and propionyl-CoA are intermediates of BCAA metabolism, it indicates that these modifications may operate in parallel regulatory mechanisms. Our data demonstrate that BCAA supplementation markedly elevates global protein propionylation levels in T-ALL cells. Specifically, propionylation at the K5 residue significantly enhances POU2AF1 stabilization, thereby promoting its oncogenic activity during T-ALL development. As a transcriptional co-activator, POU2AF1 is regulated not only by classical modifications but also through metabolite-driven propionylation. This functions as a metabolic checkpoint, allowing POU2AF1 to execute its role only when BCAT1-dependent metabolic flux produces sufficient propionyl-CoA. These findings establish a tight connection between amino acid availability, metabolic signaling, and transcriptional regulation in T-ALL progression. Whether additional proteins modified by propionylation synergize with POU2AF1 to orchestrate leukemic programs remains unknown. Previous studies have shown that leucine is a potent activator of mTOR signaling for T-ALL cell proliferation [35], which is consistent with our preliminary findings that BCAT1 deletion disrupts mTOR activity. Nevertheless, how the mTOR pathway interacts with protein propionylation and which potential “writers” mediate this process to promote leukemogenesis remain unclear.

From a translational perspective, the high relapse rate and therapeutic resistance in T-ALL highlight the urgent need for novel interventions. In contrast to existing therapeutic approaches for T-ALL, we propose an innovative combined strategy for T-ALL treatment by coupling metabolic intervention with immunotherapy. Notably, dietary BCAA restriction synergized with PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade could suppress leukemic progression in vivo. We also showed that high BCAT1 expression inversely correlated with overall survival in T-ALL patients and that BCAT1 knockdown impaired the growth of human primary T-ALL cells. Owing to the limited availability of patient samples, more work will focus on elucidating the roles of BCAT1 in primary cells from different types of T-ALL patients. Moreover, we demonstrated that POU2AF1 positively regulates SLC7A11 expression, which is known to play a key role in controlling ferroptosis susceptibility in T-ALL cells. In particular, POU2AF1 could bind to the SLC7A11 promoter to transactivate its expression. However, both the correlation between POU2AF1 and SLC7A11 and the mechanisms by which SLC7A11 regulates T-ALL development remain elusive. It will be interesting to target T-ALL by manipulating ferroptosis pathways. These findings position BCAT1–mediated BCAA catabolism as a metabolically anchored vulnerability with therapeutic potential in T-ALL. Additional efforts are needed to elucidate the detailed regulatory networks of BCAAs in T-ALL development and optimize combined therapeutic regimens for potential clinical application.

Methods

Animals

Bcat1flox/flox (Bcat1fl/fl) mice were crossed with Vav1-iCre transgenic mice to generate hematopoietic-specific Bcat1 knockout mice (Bcat1fl/fl; Vav1-iCre). Littermate Bcat1fl/fl mice without Vav1-iCre served as controls. A normal BCAA diet (A11072001) and an isoenergetic low-BCAA (L-BCAA) diet (60% less BCAAs than the normal BCAA diet; A12030802) [36] were purchased from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-Engineering Co,Ltd. (Xietong Shengwu). Anti-mouse PD-1 antibody (BE0146, BioXcell) or IgG control (BE0089, BioXcell) was intraperitoneally injected at 3 mg per kg body weight for the indicated days. C57BL/6 and NOD-SCID mice were obtained from the Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Company and maintained at the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University under standard conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 5% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle). The mice had ad libitum access to food and water. 6–8-week-old mice from the same litter or batch were used to minimize variability. All animal procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee and followed institutional guidelines. Genotyping was performed by PCR on tail genomic DNA using primers specific for the Cre and Bcat1fl/fl alleles (see Table S1 for primer sequences).

Cell culture

HEK293T cells (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (BasalMedia, L110KJ) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell, FSD500) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (BasalMedia, S110JV). Cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (BasalMedia, S310JV). Cell lines (EL4, L1210, Jurkat, and MOLT-4) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (BasalMedia, L210KJ) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The cells were passaged every 2–3 days. Primary murine T-ALL cells were cultured in StemSpan™ SFEM (STEMCELL Technologies, 09650) supplemented with 20% FBS, recombinant mouse SCF (10 ng/mL), and IL-7 (10 ng/mL). Primary human T-ALL cells were maintained in α-MEM (Gibco, C12571500BT) supplemented with 20% FBS, recombinant human FLT3 ligand (5 ng/mL), IL-7 (2 ng/mL), and SCF (10 ng/mL).

Murine T-ALL model

The murine T-ALL model was established as described previously [37]. Briefly, the MSCV-Notch1(ΔE)-IRES-GFP retroviral plasmid and pCL-ECO packaging plasmid were cotransfected into HEK293T cells. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection. Donor C57BL/6 mice were pretreated with 5-fluorouracil (100 mg/kg). Bone marrow was harvested on Day 5.5. Lineage-negative (Lin−) bone marrow cells were isolated using a mouse lineage depletion kit (BD Biosciences, 558074) and transduced with viral supernatants (1000×g, 90 min, 37 °C) in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma). Infected cells were cultured for 48 h in StemSpan™ SFEM supplemented with SCF (10 ng/mL) and TPO (10 ng/mL). Subsequently, 2 × 10⁵ transduced Lin⁻ BM cells were injected intravenously by the retro-orbital sinus into lethally irradiated C57BL/6 recipient mice. GFP+ T-ALL cells from the primary recipients were sorted by flow cytometry (FACS Aria III, BD Biosciences) and transplanted (1 × 10⁴ cells) into secondary lethally irradiated mice. Leukemia progression was assessed via flow cytometry of GFP+ cells in peripheral blood. Survival and GFP+ cell infiltration in the spleen, liver, and thymus were monitored.

In certain experiments, shRNA constructs (pLKO.1-IRES-GFP) designed to specifically silence murine Bcat1 or human BCAT1 were co-transfected with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G (at a 4:3:1 ratio) into 293T cells to generate lentiviruses. These viral particles were subsequently used to infect NOTCH-GFP⁺ bone marrow leukemia cells, human cell lines Jurkat and MOLT-4 (ATCC), murine cell lines EL4 and L1210 (ATCC), as well as primary T-ALL patient samples, followed by assessment of in vitro cell proliferation. In parallel, FACS-sorted GFP⁺ murine spleen leukemia cells (1 × 10⁴) or human GFP⁺ Jurkat cells (2 × 10⁶) were transplanted into lethally irradiated C57BL/6 mice or sublethally irradiated NOD-SCID mice, respectively, for monitoring leukemia progression.

For rescue experiments, retroviral plasmids MSCV-HA-Pou2af1-mCherry, MSCV-HA-Slc7a11-mCherry, or MSCV-HA-Pou2af1-mutant-mCherry were co-transfected with the packaging plasmid pCL-ECO (2:1) into 293T cells. The harvested viral supernatants were used to infect WT and Bcat1-deficient BM leukemia cells, which were then transplanted into recipient mice for further evaluation.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model

Primary T-ALL cells were isolated from patient bone marrow aspirates by Ficoll gradient centrifugation and injected (1 × 107 cells/mouse) into sublethally irradiated (1 Gy) NOD-SCID mice via retro-orbital injection. Engraftment was monitored by flow cytometric detection of human CD45+ cells in peripheral blood. Leukemia cells from PDX bone marrow were collected for secondary transplantation.

mRNA isolation and quantitative RT‒PCR

Total RNA from T-ALL cells was extracted with the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, #RC112). The obtained RNA was treated with gDNA Wiper and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Vazyme, #R323). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was carried out with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q341-02), and fluorescence signals were measured on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. Gene expression was normalized to β-ACTIN, and primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing and analysis

T-ALL cells were sorted from the bone marrow of three leukemic mice per group (WT and Bcat1-KO) using a FACSAria II (BD Biosciences). Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen, #15596026), assessed for integrity and concentration, pooled within each group, and submitted for transcriptome sequencing at Novogene Co., Ltd. Low-quality reads were removed to obtain clean data, which were aligned to the reference genome and subjected to statistical analysis. Gene expression levels were quantified with the Subread feature Counts tool, and differential expression was determined based on fold change > 1.5 and P < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Functional enrichment was conducted using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, and significantly enriched pathways were visualized in RStudio.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from peripheral blood, bone marrow, spleen, thymus, and liver samples. Red blood cells were lysed using ACK lysis buffer (Gibco). The cells were subsequently washed and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% FBS), followed by incubation with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies on ice for 30 min. The antibodies used included anti-mouse CD3e-APC (clone 145-2C11; eBioscience, 17-0031-82), anti-mouse CD4-PE (clone GK1.5; eBioscience, 12-0041-82), anti-mouse CD8α-APC (clone 53 − 6.7; BioLegend, 10712), anti-mouse CD25-PE (clone PC61; BioLegend, 102007), anti-mouse CD44-APC (clone M1/70; BioLegend, 103012), anti-mouse Mac-1 (CD11b)-APC (clone M1/70; eBioscience, 14-0112-82), anti-mouse Gr-1 (Ly6G/C)-PE (clone RB6-8C5; eBioscience, 12-5931-82), anti-mouse B220-PE (clone RA3-6B2; eBioscience, 12-0452-85), and anti-human CD45-APC (clone HI30; BD Pharmingen, 555485). For intracellular staining (Ki-67), the cells were fixed and permeabilized using Fixation/Permeabilization Diluent Buffer Set (eBioscience, 00-5223-56) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by staining with anti-mouse Ki-67-PE (clone SolA15, eBioscience, 12-5698-82). Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC Apoptosis Detection Kit (MULTI SCIENCE, AP105) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell cycle analysis was performed by staining with Hoechst 33342 (5 µg/mL; Sigma, B2261) for 45 min at 37 °C. Data were acquired via a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman) and analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1; BD Biosciences).

Western blotting

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013C) supplemented with 1% SDS and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 µg) were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk (Sangon Biotech) in TBST (Tris-buffered saline + 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used included anti-BCAT1 (ABclonal, A16351), anti-POU2AF1 (HUABIO, JE47-78), anti-SLC7A11 (HUABIO, JE31-93), anti-β-ACTIN (HUABIO, JF53-10), anti-HA tag (HUABIO, PSH01-92), anti-FLAG tag (HUABIO, M1403-2), and anti-propionyllysine mouse mAb (PTM Biolabs, PTM-203). After being washed, the membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry

For immunoprecipitation, flag-tagged POU2AF1 (WT) or Flag-tagged K5R/K25R-mutant constructs were transfected into 293T cells cultured in the presence or absence of BCAAs. Cells were lysed in Pierce IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors, and whole-cell extracts were incubated with anti-Flag M2 affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C overnight. The beads were washed extensively, and bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by SDS–PAGE followed by immunoblotting with antibodies against propionyl-lysine to detect POU2AF1 propionylation.

For mass spectrometry, proteins were excised from Coomassie-stained gels, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by LC–MS/MS (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Spectra were searched against the IPI Human 3.87 database using SEQUEST, and propionylated proteins were confirmed by immunoblotting. All mass spectrometry analyses were performed by Shanghai Yingmu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Luciferase reporter assays

To assess the transcriptional regulation of Slc7a11 by POU2AF1, HEK293T cells were cotransfected with XZ201-Pou2af1-HA or the control vector and the pGL4.27-Slc7a11 promoter construct. After 24 h, luciferase activity was measured using the GloMax® Multi Detection System (Promega). Relative activity is expressed as the firefly/Renilla luciferase ratio.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime). HEK293T cells were cotransfected with XZ201-Pou2af1-HA and the pGL4.27-Slc7a11-promoter plasmid. Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde at 37 °C for 10 min. The chromatin was sheared and immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C using anti-HA (Sigma) or control rabbit IgG (Santa Cruz). The input controls used 1% sonicated chromatin. DNA was purified with a Nucleospin kit, and POU2AF1 binding to the Slc7a11 promoter was analyzed by semiquantitative PCR using the primers listed in Table S1.

Cell proliferation assay

T-ALL cell lines or primary cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5000–10,000 cells per well in complete medium. Cell numbers were quantified daily for 3–5 days using either manual counting with a hemocytometer (trypan blue exclusion) or a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism. The data are expressed as the means ± SEMs. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. Depending on the experimental setup, comparisons between groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, or two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. Differences in survival among groups were evaluated by Kaplan–Meier analysis with a log-rank test. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of prior power calculations. For in vivo studies, five mice per group were used per replicate. No blinding or randomization was applied during sample allocation. Gene expression and proliferation studies were conducted in triplicate (three technical replicates) across at least three independent experiments. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed with a minimum of three replicates. All the data obtained were included in the analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).
