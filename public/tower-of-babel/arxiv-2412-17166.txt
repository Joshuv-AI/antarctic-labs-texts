Abstract
Some classic second-order sufficient optimality conditions in the calculus of variations are shown to be equivalent, while also introducing a new equivalent second-order condition which is extremely easy to apply: simply integrate a linear second-order initial value problem and check that the solution is positive over the problem domain.

Introduction

This note concerns an efficient algorithm for showing that a given
stationary point in the calculus of variations is a strict local minimizer.
Our focus is second-order sufficient optimality conditions for
classic problems in the calculus of variations such as

\min~F(y):=\int_{0}^{1}f(y^{\prime}(x),y(x),x)~dx\quad\mbox{subject to }y\in{\cal{C}}_{0}^{1},

(1)

where
{\cal{C}}_{0}^{1}
denotes the space of continuously differentiable,
real-valued functions defined on
[0,1]
with
y(0)=y(1)=0
.
If
f\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
, then at a local minimizer
y^{*}
, Euler’s equation
is satisfied:

\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y^{\prime}}\left(({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x),x)\right)\right)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\left(({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x),x)\right).

(2)

This is a necessary optimality condition.

A trivial second-order sufficient optimality condition is formulated
in terms of the Hessian of the integrand of
f
along a solution
y^{*}
of
(
2
).
Suppose that
f\in{\cal{C}}^{2}
and
y^{*}
satisfies the Euler equation (
2
).
Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let us consider the
2
by
2
Hessian

\nabla^{2}_{z}f\left(z,x\right)\bigg|_{z=({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x))}.

(3)

If this Hessian is positive definite for all
x\in[0,1]
, then
y^{*}
is
a strict local minimizer of (
1
).
This follows from a second-order Taylor expansion of the integrand
of (
1
) around
({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x))
.
The first-order term in the Taylor expansion vanishes since
y^{*}
satisfies
the Euler equation (
2
), while the second-order term in the expansion
is nonnegative in a neighborhood of
({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x))
.
The requirement of positive definiteness of the Hessian (
3
)
for all
x\in[0,1]
is a rather strong condition which limits the
applicability of this second-order condition.

A second-order sufficient optimality condition that does not require
positive definiteness of the quadratic (
3
) on
[0,1]

was obtained by Legendre.
Let
\Omega
denote the quadratic form associated with the Hessian (
3
):

\Omega(h)=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+2R(x)h^{\prime}(x)h(x)+Q(x)h(x)^{2}\;dx,

where
h\in{\cal{C}}_{0}^{1}
and

P
,
Q
, and
R
correspond to the
(1,1)
,
(2,2)
, and
(1,2)

elements respectively of the Hessian (
3
).
If
f\in{\cal{C}}^{2}
, then
P
,
Q
, and
R\in{\cal{C}}^{0}
.
Any conditions that ensure a lower bound of the form

\Omega(h)\geq\gamma\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+h(x)^{2}\;dx

for some
\gamma>0
lead to a sufficient optimality condition for (
1
).
Since

\frac{d}{dx}h(x)^{2}=2h(x)h^{\prime}(x),

an integration by parts yields

\Omega(h)=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+[Q(x)-R^{\prime}(x)]h(x)^{2}\;dx

(4)

assuming
R\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
.
Hence, when
R\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
, there is no loss of generality in focusing on
quadratic forms with
R=0
.
Let
\Gamma(h)
denote the quadratic form
\Omega(h)
in the special case
where
R=0
:

\Gamma(h)=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+Q(x)h(x)^{2}\;dx,

(5)

where the
Q
term in (
5
) is identified with the

Q-R^{\prime}
term in (
4
).

If
\Omega(h)\geq 0
for all
h\in{\cal{C}}_{0}^{1}
, then

P\geq 0
on
[0,1]
;
consequently, Legendre focused on quadratic forms
\Omega
where

P(x)\geq\alpha>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
, and he completed
the square in
\Gamma
using any bounded solution on
[0,1]
to the
Ricatti equation

w^{\prime}(x)=\frac{w(x)^{2}}{P(x)}-Q(x).

(6)

Adding the identity

0=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dx}\left(w(x)h(x)^{2}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}\left[w^{\prime}(x)h(x)^{2}+2w(x)h(x)h^{\prime}(x)\right]\;dx

to
\Gamma
and substituting for
w^{\prime}
using the Ricatti equation yields a
perfect square:

\Gamma(h)=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)\left(h^{\prime}(x)+\frac{w(x)}{P(x)}h(x)\right)^{2}\;dx.

(7)

Since
P
is strictly positive,

\Gamma(h)\geq 0
and
\Gamma(h)=0
if and only if
h=0
,
or equivalently, the quadratic form
\Gamma
is positive definite.
In the next section, we show that
\Gamma
is positive definite
if and only if there exists a bounded solution of the
Ricatti equation (
6
) on
[0,1]
.

A different, but related view of second-order optimality,
is developed by Gelfand and Fomin
[
3
]

in the context of conjugate points.
The Euler equation associated with a stationary point of
\Gamma
is

\frac{d}{dx}(P(x)u^{\prime}(x))=Q(x)u(x).

(8)

It is assumed that
P\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
and there exists
\alpha
such
that
P(x)\geq\alpha>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Gelfand and Fomin consider (
8
) in the context of a boundary-value
problem with conditions

u(0)=u(a)=0

(9)

where
a\in(0,1]
.
The point
x=a
is said to be conjugate to
x=0
if
there exists a nonzero solution to (
8
) satisfying (
9
).
On pages 106–111 of
[
3
]
, Gelfand and Fomin show that

\Gamma
is positive definite over
C_{0}^{1}
if and only if

a
is never conjugate to
x=0
for any choice of
a\in(0,1]
.

We will also view (
8
) as an initial-value problem with initial conditions

u(0)=u_{0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad u^{\prime}(0)=u_{1}.

(10)

Since (
8
) is a linear equation,
there exists a unique solution on
[0,1]
to the initial-value problem
(
10
) for any choice of
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
.

The development of second-order conditions in optimization is an area of
intense research that continues today.
As an example of current research, the paper
[
2
]
of
Frankowska and Osmolovskii develops second-order optimality conditions for a
general set in a Banach space.
There has been particular interest in the development of second-order
conditions in optimal control;
a few references include
[
1
,
4
,
6
]
.
However, here our interest is not in the further development or extension of
second-order conditions, but rather in numerically checking whether
a second-order condition is satisfied.
In general, it may not be easy to use either the Ricatti equation
or the conjugacy conditions to check numerically whether a solution
of Euler’s equation (
2
) is a strict local minimizer.
With the Ricatti equation strategy, showing that the second-order
optimality condition holds amounts to finding an initial condition for which
the solution to the Ricatti equation (
6
) does not blow up on
[0,1]
.
With the conjugate point approach, showing that the second-order
optimality condition holds amounts to verifying
that there does not exist
a\in(0,1]
that is conjugate to
x=0
.
The goal of this paper is to establish the close connection between both these
approaches to second-order optimality, while also providing a simple numerical
test for a strict local minimizer.

2
Main Results

Our observations are contained in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that there exists
\alpha
such that
P(x)\geq\alpha>0
for
all
x\in[0,1]
, where
P\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
and
Q\in{\cal{C}}^{0}
in

(\ref{Gamma-def})
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(C1)

There exists a strictly positive solution
u
of the linear differential
equation
(
8
)
on
[0,1]
.

(C2)

There exists a uniformly bounded solution
w
of the Ricatti equation

(
6
)
on
[0,1]
.

(C3)

There exists
\gamma>0
such that

\Gamma(h)\geq\gamma\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+h(x)^{2}~dx\quad\mbox{for all }h\in{\cal{H}}_{0}^{1},

where
{\cal{H}}_{0}^{1}
is the space of function with
h^{\prime}
square integrable
over
[0,1]
and
h(0)=h(1)=0
.

(C4)

Whenever
u
is a solution of
(
8
)
with
u(0)=u(a)=0
for
some
0<a\leq 1
,
u
is identically zero on
[0,a]
;
thus there are no points
a\in(0,1]
conjugate to
x=0
.

(C5)

The solution of the initial-value problem for
(
8
)

with
u_{0}=0
and
u_{1}=1
is positive on
(0,1]
.

Condition (C5) is easily checked: simply integrate the linear, second-order
initial-value problem (
8
) forward from the initial condition

u(0)=0
and
u^{\prime}(0)=1
.
If the solution is positive on
(0,1]
, then (C3) holds.

Proof.

(C1) implies (C2):
Let
u_{0}=u(0)
and
u_{1}=u^{\prime}(0)
for the given solution of (
8
).
If the initial-value problem for (
8
) is written as a first-order system

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u^{\prime}\\
v^{\prime}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
Q/P&-P^{\prime}/P\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
v\end{array}\right],\quad u(0)=u_{0},\quad v(0)=u_{1},

then the coefficient matrix is continuous under the assumptions of the theorem.
Hence, by the standard existence theory
[
5
, Sect. 1.5]

for the solution of a linear system of differential equations,
the solution pair
(u,v)
is
{\cal{C}}^{1}
, which implies
that
u\in{\cal{C}}^{2}
since
u^{\prime}=v\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
.
Since
u
is strictly positive on
[0,1]
,
it follows that

w=-\frac{Pu^{\prime}}{u}\in{\cal{C}}^{1}\quad\mbox{on }[0,1].

(11)

Differentiating
w
and using (
8
) to simplify the derivative of the
numerator in (
11
) shows that
w
satisfies the Ricatti equation
(
6
).
Since
w\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
, it is clearly bounded uniformly.
The observation that
w=-Pu^{\prime}/u
satisfies the Ricatti equation (
6
)
appears in
[
3
, p. 108]
.

(C2) implies (C3):
As seen earlier, when there exists a solution
to the Ricatti equation (
6
),
\Gamma
reduces to a perfect square
(
7
).
Define
r=h^{\prime}+wh/P
.
If
h\in{\cal{H}}_{0}^{1}
, then
wh/P
is continuous and

r\in{\cal{L}}^{2}
, the space of square integrable functions on
[0,1]
.
Solving for
h^{\prime}
gives

h^{\prime}(x)=r(x)-\frac{w(x)h(x)}{P(x)}.

(12)

Using an integrating factor and the fact that
h(0)=0
, we have

h(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\mbox{exp}\left(\int_{t}^{x}-\frac{w(s)}{P(s)}~ds\right)r(t)~dt.

(13)

Take the
L^{2}
norm of (
13
).
Since the factor multiplying
r(t)
in (
13
)
is bounded uniformly in
x
and
t\in[0,x]
,
there exists a constant
c
, independent of
r
, such that

\|h\|\leq c\|r\|,

(14)

where
\|\cdot\|
is the
{\cal{L}}^{2}
norm.
Combining (
12
) and (
14
),
h^{\prime}
is also bounded in terms
of
r
.
Choose
c
larger if necessary to ensure that

\|h^{\prime}\|\leq c\|r\|.

(15)

Since
P(x)\geq\alpha
, we obtain

\Gamma(h)=\int_{0}^{1}P(x)r(x)^{2}~dx\geq\alpha\|r\|^{2}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|r\|^{2}+\|r\|^{2}\right)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2c^{2}}\left(\|h\|^{2}+\|h^{\prime}\|^{2}\right),

where (
14
) and (
15
) are used in the last step.
Hence, (C3) holds with
\gamma=\alpha/(2c^{2})
.

(C3) implies (C4):
Let
u
denote the solution of (
8
) given in (C4).
Since
u
is also the solution of initial-value problem for (
8
)
with
u_{0}=u(0)
and
u_{1}=u^{\prime}(0)
, it follows that
u\in{\cal{C}}^{2}
on

[0,a]
.
Extend
u
as zero on the interval
[a,1]
.
The extended
u
lies in
{\cal{H}}_{0}^{1}
.
Since
u
vanishes on
[a,1]
, we have

\Gamma(u)=\int_{0}^{a}P(x)u^{\prime}(x)^{2}+Q(x)u(x)^{2}\;dx.

(16)

Integrating by parts yields

\int_{0}^{a}P(x)u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\;dx=\int_{0}^{a}-\frac{d}{dx}(P(x)u^{\prime}(x))u(x)\;dx=\int_{0}^{1}-Q(x)u(x)^{2}\;dx,

where the last equality is due to (
8
).
Combining this with (
16
) yields
\Gamma(u)=0
, which implies that

u:=0
by (C3).

(C4) implies (C5):
If
u
is the solution of the initial value problem (
10
) for (
8
)
with
u_{0}=0
and
u_{1}=1
, then
u(x)>0
for
x>0
near zero.
If for some
a\in(0,1]
, we have
u(a)=0
, then (C4) is violated.
Hence,
u
is positive on
(0,a]
.

(C5) implies (C1):
Let
U_{i}
denote the solution of the initial-value problems
for (
8
) associated with
(u_{0},u_{1})=

(i,1-i)
,
i=0,1
.
Any linear combination of
U_{0}
and
U_{1}
is a solution of (
8
).
By (C5),
U_{0}
is positive on
(0,1]
.
Since
U_{1}(0)=1
, we will add to
U_{0}
a small positive multiple of
U_{1}

to achieve a solution of (
8
) that is strictly positive on the
closed interval
[0,1]
.
In detail, since
U_{1}(0)=1
and
U_{1}
is continuous, we can choose

0<\delta\leq 1
such that
U_{1}(x)\geq 1/2
on
[0,\delta]
.
By (C5),
U_{0}
is strictly positive on any closed interval
contained in
(0,1]
.
Consequently,

m=\min\{U_{0}(x):\delta\leq x\leq 1\}>0.

Finally, observe that

u(x)=U_{0}(x)+m\left(\frac{U_{1}(x)}{2\|U_{1}(x)\|_{\max}}\right),

(17)

where
\|U_{1}(x)\|_{\max}
is the absolute maximum of
U_{1}
over the
interval
[0,1]
, is strictly positive on
[0,1]
.
On
[0,\delta]
, the first term in (
17
) is nonnegative and
the second term is strictly positive.
On
[\delta,1]
,
U_{0}(x)\geq m>0
while the second term in
(
17
) is bounded in absolute value by
m/2
.
Hence,
u(x)\geq m/2
on
[\delta,1]
, which implies that
u(x)
is
strictly positive on
[0,1]
.
∎

3
Second-order sufficient optimality conditions

As a consequence of Theorem
1
, we have the following
second-order sufficient optimality conditions.

Corollary 2
.

The following conditions are sufficient for
y^{*}\in{\cal{C}}_{0}^{1}
to be a
strict local minimizer of
F
in
(\ref{P})
:

(S1)

f\in{\cal{C}}^{2}
and
y^{*}
satisfies the Euler equation
(\ref{E})
.

(S2)

We define

\displaystyle P(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{(\partial z_{1})^{2}}\right)f\left(z,x\right)\bigg|_{z=({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x))},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle Q(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{(\partial z_{2})^{2}}-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{1}\partial z_{2}}\right)f\left(z,x\right)\bigg|_{z=({y^{*}}^{\prime}(x),y^{*}(x))}.

P\in{\cal{C}}^{1}
with
P
strictly positive on
[0,1]
,
and the mixed partial derivative of
f
contained in
Q
is
{\cal{C}}^{1}
.

(S3)

The solution of the initial-value problem
(\ref{IVP})
for
(\ref{EE})
with

u_{0}=0
and
u_{1}=1
is positive on
(0,1]
.

Proof.

Expand the integrand of (
1
) in a second-order Taylor expansion around

(y^{*}(x),y^{*}(x))
using the intermediate value form for the remainder term.
The first derivative term in the expansion vanishes since
y^{*}
satisfies
the Euler equation (
2
) by (S1).
After deleting this first derivative term, the expansion has the form

F(y^{*}+h)=F(y^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\Omega}(h),

(18)

where

\overline{\Omega}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}\overline{P}(x)h^{\prime}(x)^{2}+2\overline{R}(x)h^{\prime}(x)h(x)+\overline{Q}(x)h(x)^{2}\;dx.

Here
\overline{P}
,
\overline{Q}
, and
\overline{R}
denote the
(1,1)
,
(2,2)
, and

(1,2)
elements of the Hessian

\nabla^{2}_{z}f\left(z,x\right)\bigg|_{z=(a(x),b(x))},

where

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a(x)\\
b(x)\end{array}\right]=(1-\alpha)\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{y^{*}}^{\prime}(x)+h^{\prime}(x)\\
{y^{*}}(x)+h(x)\end{array}\right]+\alpha\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{y^{*}}^{\prime}(x)\\
{y^{*}}(x)\end{array}\right]\quad\mbox{for some }\alpha\in[0,1].

Add and subtract
\Omega(h)
on the right side of (
18
)
and replace the second
\Omega
by
\Gamma
to obtain

F(y^{*}+h)=F(y^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\Omega}(h)-\Omega(h))+\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(h).

(19)

By (S3) it follows that (C3) is satisfied.
Hence, for
h
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of zero in the
max-norm for
{\cal{C}}^{1}
, the
\Gamma(h)
term in the expansion
(
19
) dominates the

(\overline{\Omega}(h)-\Omega(h))
term.
∎

4
Conclusions

Theorem
1
unifies the classic second-order sufficient
optimality conditions by both showing their equivalence, and by introducing
a new equivalent condition (C5) that is easy to check; we simply integrate a
second-order linear differential equation over the interval
[0,1]
.
If the solution is positive on
(0,1]
, then
y^{*}
is a strict local
minimizer within the framework of Corollary
2
.
