Potential of plasma metagenomic next-generation sequencing to guide antibiotic therapy in acute necrotizing pancreatitis with early fever: a prospective multicenter cohort study
Authors: Xiaoyue Hong, Dingcheng Shen, Chiayen Lin, Xintong Cao, Zefang Sun, Baiqi Liu, Rong Guo, Zhiyong Liu, Tao Luo, Yi Yu, Ming Yang, Bin Feng, Wenqian Zhang, Gengwen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndiscriminate antibiotic use remains common in febrile patients with acute necrotizing pancreatitis (ANP), particularly during the early phase. Metagenomic next‑generation sequencing (mNGS) has shown diagnostic utility for infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) and may offer a means to guide antimicrobial therapy. We aimed to explore whether mNGS could potentially improve the appropriateness of antibiotic use in ANP patients presenting with early fever.MethodsThis prospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at five hospitals in China, enrolling ANP patients who developed fever within two weeks of symptom onset. Antibiotic susceptibility was defined per local microbiology laboratory reports. The hypothetical impact of mNGS on reducing inappropriate antibiotic use was evaluated through a retrospective simulation using predefined criteria from the BGI China antimicrobial drug usage card, as mNGS results were not disclosed to the treating teams during the actual clinical course.ResultsBetween May 2023 and December 2024, 125 ANP patients with early fever were enrolled. Antibiotics were administered to 91.2% (114/125) of patients, whereas only 23.2% (29/125)were eventually confirmed to have IPN, and the rate of appropriate antibiotic use was 14.5% (17/117) based on conventional culture. In our simulated model, if therapy had been guided by plasma mNGS results, the estimated rate of appropriate antibiotic use could have increased to 71.8%.ConclusionsPlasma mNGS facilitates rapid pathogen identification and shows potential for improving antibiotic appropriateness in ANP patients with early fever.Keywords: Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Acute necrotizing pancreatitis, Infected pancreatic necrosis, Antibiotic stewardship

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is the most common pancreatic disease worldwide, with approximately 20% of patients developing pancreatic or peripancreatic necrosis [1, 2]. The clinical manifestations of acute necrotizing pancreatitis (ANP) are non-specific, including abdominal pain, nausea, vomit, fever and tachycardia. Early fever is quite common in ANP. However, it remains very difficult to differentiate between systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis due to infected necrosis, especially within 2 weeks [3]. Infection of pancreatic collection can be documented by the presence of gas on CT, or by positive culture from fine needle aspiration (FNA). Nevertheless, air bubble sign occurs in only a minority of cases (20% or less) with infected necrosis [4]. Routine FNA is also currently not recommended due to high rates of false negativity and the possibility of iatrogenic infection [1]. Delayed intervention is currently recommended by the guidelines and high-quality clinical trials [5, 6], making it difficult to obtain (peri)pancreatic specimens in the early course of the disease. Consequently, despite the lack of benefit, indiscriminate use of antibiotics is widespread in the early phase of ANP, occurring in 69% to 80.4% of cases [7, 8], which inevitably results in the emergence of resistant organisms, drug toxicities, and higher healthcare expenses.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is a promising approach for the diagnosis of infection by affording culture-independent detection of pathogens [9]. Its applications in respiratory infection, bloodstream infection, neurological infection and pancreatic infection have been reported [10–12]. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of mNGS in diagnosing infected pancreatic necrosis (IPN) and its potential ability to guide antimicrobial usage [13–15]. However, the clinical value of mNGS in ANP patients with early fever remains unclear, and whether mNGS could be a solution to the dilemma of inappropriate antibiotic use in the early stage of ANP.

Therefore, we designed this prospective multicenter cohort study specifically targeting mNGS application in ANP patients with early fever. In this study, the potential of blood mNGS to reduce antibiotic abuse was evaluated. Our hypothesis is that mNGS can tackle the problem of the ubiquitous and unnecessary usage of antibiotic in ANP patients with early fever.

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

From May 2023 to December 2024, the prospective multicenter observational clinical study was conducted at 5 hospitals in China (ChiCTR2300071292), including the Xiangya Hospital, Zhuzhou Hospital Affiliated to Xiangya School of Medicine, Changde Hospital, the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou City, and Liuyang People’s Hospital. The target condition was ANP accompanied with fever ≥ 38.5 °C within two weeks of onset in patients who had no confirmed diagnosis of infection at the time of enrollment. The index test was a mNGS assay of blood sample, and the reference standard was conventional pancreatic collection culture. The diagnostic performance of mNGS, antibiotic usage, and the potential of mNGS to optimize antibiotic usage were assessed.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (reference: 202304286) and registered by the ethics committees of all the participating hospitals, and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their representative for the study participation.

All hospitalized patients diagnosed with ANP according to the revised Atlanta classification [16] were prospectively and consecutively screened for this study. ANP patients who met the following criteria were enrolled: (1) age above 14 years; (2) experienced fever with a temperature of ≥38.5 °C at least once within two weeks of onset, and (3) had no prior abdominal or retroperitoneal invasive procedures (FNA, percutaneous catheter drainage, endoscopic transluminal drainage or necrosectomy, or open pancreatic necrosectomy, etc.). Patients were excluded if any of the following criteria was met: (1) presence of extra-pancreatic infectious complications at screening, such as pneumonia, biliary infection, or urinary tract infection; (2) inability of the patients or their representative to understand and implement the study procedures, or informed consent was withdrawn.

The data of clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, imaging results, antibiotic usage, and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records.

Definitions

The diagnosis and classification of AP and ANP were based on the revised Atlanta classification [16] and the American Gastroenterological Association guideline [1, 17]. IPN was defined as positive culture from the first retroperitoneal or peripancreatic surgical intervention. Sterile pancreatic necrosis (SPN) was defined as negative culture in pancreatic or peripancreatic specimens obtained from the initial surgical intervention, or as the absence of infection signs upon discharge or follow-up without the necessity of any further surgical intervention. Undetermined patients were defined as patients who died before any surgical intervention to obtain pancreatic or peripancreatic specimens as we described previously [15, 18].

Antibiotic susceptibility was defined as reported by the local microbiology laboratories. Multidrug-resistant bacteria were confirmed according to international expert proposal for interim standard definitions for acquired resistance [19]. Antibiotic overuse was defined as empiric use of antibiotic for SPN. Antibiotic misuse included two aspects: use of unsusceptible antibiotic or absence of any antibiotic for IPN. Correct use of antibiotic includes: use of pathogen-susceptible antibiotics for IPN or no antibiotic was used in SPN. The ability of mNGS to potentially guide antibiotic usage was assessed according to the recommendations from antimicrobial drug usage card by BGI China.

Sample collection and nucleic acid extraction

Blood samples were collected simultaneously for mNGS, procalcitonin, culture, and other routine biomarkers such as C-reaction protein during fever (T ≥ 38.5 °C). For patients who met surgical indications, peripancreatic specimens were collected for microbial culture during the initial surgical intervention. Blood samples for mNGS were transported to the Wuhan BGI Medical Laboratory via cold-chain transportation. Blood was placed in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube, stored at room temperature, and centrifuged at 1600 g for 10 min at 4 C for plasma separation. 300 μL plasma was transferred into a 2 mL sterile tube for DNA extraction. 600 μL of peripancreatic specimens was mixed with 7.2 mL Lyase (RT410, TIANGEN BIOTECH) at 30 C for 10 min. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 20 s, the sample was transferred to a FastPrep (FP) lysis tube for DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using TIANamp Micro DNA kit (DP316, TIANGEN BIOTECH) according to standard procedures.

Construction of DNA library and sequencing

DNA libraries were constructed through DNA-fragmentation, end-repair, adapter-ligation and PCR amplification. Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies) and Qubit 2.0 (Invitrogen, USA) were used for library quality control of the DNA libraries. Quality qualified libraries were pooled. DNA Nanoball (DNB) was made and sequenced by BGISEQ-50/MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI Genomics Co., Ltd.). The internal control was laboratory-constructed plasmid that was spiked into samples before DNA extraction and was tracked through whole steps. The negative control samples should be detected without any pathogens otherwise indicated DNA pollution from the environment. Positive control included specific Hela Cells and Acinetobacter baumannii.

Bioinformatic analysis

High-quality sequencing data were generated by removing low quality reads, followed by computational subtraction of human host sequences mapped to the human reference genome (hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment. The remaining data by removal of low-complexity reads were classified by simultaneously aligning to Pathogens Metagenomics Database, consisting of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites. The classification reference databases were downloaded from NCBI (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ genomes/). RefSeq contained 4945 whole genome sequence of viral taxa, 6350 bacterial genomes or scaffolds,1064 fungi related to human infection, and 234 parasites associated with human diseases. The coverage ratio and depth of each microorganism were calculated using BEDTools.

Metagenomic data interpretation

The microbial list from the above analysis was compared to the laboratory’s background microbiology database, an internal dynamic database that included microbes detected in more than 50% of the laboratory over the past three months. The blood of healthy volunteers was tested in the same batch as a negative control. Microorganisms with significantly different stringently mapped read number (SMRN) on species level were considered suspected pathogens compared with the negative control. After quality control, the detection of ≥ 1 read of bacteria or fungi by mNGS was considered positive. The clinician determined the pathogen based on the patient’s clinical symptoms, data analysis results, and microbiological characteristics. For the purpose of this simulation, mNGS‑detected organisms were considered as potential pathogens, except when the clinician clearly identified them as clinically insignificant contaminants.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range, and categorical variables were described as frequency and percentages (%). The Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher exact test was performed for comparative analysis of the categorical variables, and Student t -test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and diagnostic accuracy (the sum of true positives and true negatives divided by total) were calculated and used to examine the performance of blood mNGS. Data analyses and figures were done using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0) and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5). All analyses were two-tailed. Results with a type I error less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics and outcomes

From May 2023 through December 2024, 489 patients with ANP were assessed for eligibility across 5 hospitals in China. A total of 125 ANP patients with early fever were prospectively and consecutively enrolled for analysis (Fig. 1). The median age of the 125 patients (71.2% were male) was 46 years (Table 1). Hyperlipidemia was the predominant etiology (n = 74, 59.2%), followed by biliary (n = 37, 29.6%), idiopathic (n = 8, 6.4%), alcoholic (n = 5, 4.0%) and iatrogenic (n = 1, 0.8%). The median time from onset to fever manifestation was 4 days (2–8 d). According to the revised Atlanta classification, 53.6% (67/125) of the patients developed severe acute pancreatitis (SAP) and 58 (46.4%) patients developed moderate SAP. 54 patients (43.2%) required surgical intervention and antibiotics use rate was 91.2% (114/125). 29 patients (23.2%) were confirmed with IPN after a median of 19 days (12.5–28 d) following the disease onset. 88 patients (70.4%) were proven SPN eventually, while the other 8 patients (6.4%) who died before any surgical intervention was attempted were classified as undetermined. In the entire cohort, the median length of stay was 20 days (14–33 d). A total of 61.6% (77/125) patients were admitted to ICU. The overall mortality rate was 14.4% (18/125).

Antibiotic therapy

Broad spectrum antibiotics were prescribed in 114 patients (91.2%), after a median of 4 days (2–8.5 d) following the onset. Meropenem was the most prescribed antibiotic, accounting for 44.7% (51/114) of prescriptions. Twelve patients (10.5%) received more than one antibacterial agent at the initiation of antimicrobial treatment, while most of patients (110/114, 96.5%) did not have a proven infection. Antifungals were administered in 23 of 114 patients (20.2%), 82.6% (19/23) of which had no proven fungal infection.

Excluding 8 patients with undetermined infection status, the rate of correct use of antibiotic was 14.5% (17/117), including using of pathogen-susceptible antibiotics for 5 IPN patients and no antibiotic was used in 12 SPN patients. 65.0% (76/117) of patients experienced antibiotic overuse. Besides, the proportion of antibiotic misuse was 20.5% (24/117) (Fig. 3).

The potential of blood mNGS to reduce antibiotic overuse and misuse

This was a real-world observational study and the results of mNGS were blinded to the multidisciplinary teams. Thus, the rates of correct use, overuse, and misuse of antimicrobial therapy were estimated according to the results of mNGS (Fig. 3). In real-world management, antibiotic overuse was observed in 65.0% of patients, and misuse occurred in 20.5% of IPN patients. Under mNGS guidance, overuse could drop to 20.5%, and misuse to 7.7% (p < 0.001). The rate of correct use of antibiotics was assumed to improve greatly from 14.5% to 71.8% (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Turnaround time and cost

The turnaround time starts with the sample collection and ends at the time that the clinicians receive the final report. The average turnaround time was 48.2 ± 11.3 h for mNGS and 104.7 ± 8.5 h for the culture, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). The average cost of blood mNGS was 310.6 dollars per case, accounting for 1.4% of the average total hospitalization expenses ($22299.0).

Discussion

This is the first prospective multicenter study which specifically evaluates the value of plasma mNGS in reducing antibiotics overuse and misuse in ANP patients with early fever. This study observed that antibiotic overuse was quite common in ANP patients with early fever, which was a prevalent problem, and plasma mNGS could potentially significantly reduce the rate of overuse and thus improve the rate of correct use of antibiotics.

Pancreatic infection is less common in the early phase of AP and is typically considered a late complication [17]. Infection of necrosis was diagnosed at a median of 26 days after admission in a multicenter study [20]. However, early infection is not as rare as traditionally believed. In a prospective study of 114 patients with surgically treated necrotizing pancreatitis, the rate of necrosis infection was 23.8% during the first seven days of attack [21]. Infected necrosis was detected within the first 14 days of admission in 18% of 98 patients with IPN in another cohort study [22]. These studies demonstrated that not only did bacterial contamination of pancreatic necrosis occur early and frequently, but was also associated with a significant increase in morbidity and mortality. In fact, the infection of pancreatic necrosis may have occurred much earlier than the actual clinical diagnosis. In the present study, 23.2% of the patients with early fever were proven IPN, in accordance with previous studies [20–22]. However, it still remains very difficult to confirm the infection of pancreatic necrosis due to non-specific symptoms between SIRS and infection, and infrequent occurrence (20% or less) of air bubble sign on CT scan, especially in early phase [4]. Although the presence of bacteria can be detected by the FNA, it has not been recommended now because of the demand for high standard technique and the risk of introducing infection [1, 3, 17, 23].

Due to the challenge to clinically distinguish between SIRS and pancreatic infection in early phase of ANP, it was difficult to administer antibiotic regimens timely and correctly. Although under the most circumstances, fever within the first two weeks of disease onset was typically caused by SIRS rather than infection [24], antibiotics were still widely administered, which was not compatible with the current guidelines [1, 17]. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered in 80.4% of mild AP in a large Japanese cohort [25], and 74% of antibiotic therapy was initiated on the first day in another international study [26]. An observational multicenter study on necrotizing pancreatitis observed that 69% of infection was not proven by cultures at the initiation of antibiotics, and empirical antibiotics for infected necrosis provided insufficient coverage in 50% patients with a pancreatic culture [8]. Notably, 23 patients (20.2%) were given antifungal therapy empirically, despite only four having documented fungal infection in this study, reflecting additional unnecessary coverage that might be avoided with clearer infection identification. These findings were revealing a common problem of significant overuse and misuse of antibiotics, especially at the early phase of ANP. The potential drivers of this overprescribing pattern, including diagnostic uncertainty and the lack of rapid, reliable pathogen identification tools in routine practice, which might lead to the emergence of resistant organisms, drug toxicities, and higher healthcare expenses [26, 27].

As an efficient and precise pathogenic diagnostic method, mNGS is gradually being applied in the diagnosis of various infectious diseases [9], including pancreatic infection. This innovative method was assumed to be a key driver for precision diagnosis of infectious diseases, advancing precision medicine efforts to personalize patient care [11]. Previous studies have validated the advantages of blood mNGS in diagnosing IPN with high accuracy and sensitivity, given the noninvasive nature of testing and the potential to provide actionable diagnosis faster than conventional microbiologic methods [14, 15]. Additionally, blood mNGS has been shown to be superior to procalcitonin in guiding antibiotic therapy [18]. The value of mNGS for pathogen testing of peripancreatic specimens has also been evaluated, showing satisfactory diagnostic accuracy and the ability to broaden the range of identifiable infectious pathogens [28]. However, adding mNGS to the standard FNA procedure has not yet been proven to improve the diagnostic accuracy compared with culture-based FNA [29]. Therefore, peripancreatic mNGS was not performed in the present study. Based on these previous studies, it was hypothesized that blood mNGS might offer a solution to the ubiquitous problem of indiscriminate use of antibiotics in the early phase of ANP. To effectively address this problem, the solution must meet several key criteria: 1) precision; 2) efficiency; 3) the ability to identify the causative pathogen, preferably providing information on antibiotic resistance.

The diagnostic accuracy of blood mNGS was promising in ANP patients with early fever. Similar to previous study [18], the sensitivity and NPV of mNGS were also high in the present study. These results demonstrated the precision of blood mNGS as a diagnostic method of infected necrosis. Additionally, in line with previous research [15], the average elapsed time for mNGS detection was significantly faster than conventional diagnostic methods, illustrating the efficiency of mNGS.

Included patients exhibited extremely high rates of antibiotic use, and this result clearly contradicted the actual infection rate. The present study demonstrated that blood mNGS had the potential to significantly improve the accuracy of antibiotic therapy in ANP patients with early fever, which might be primarily attributed to the decline of the rate of antibiotic overuse. In patients with IPN, mNGS was also assumed to reduce antibiotic misuse, comparably less significant compared with overuse. This discrepancy might be attributed to the fact that metagenomic predictions of antimicrobial resistance rely on the detection of resistance genes, rather than direct drug susceptibility testing. A low antimicrobial resistance gene burden might lead to negative detection by mNGS [12]. However, targeted enrichment could potentially overcome this limitation by amplifying antimicrobial resistance gene signals, which might need further investigation.

The strength of this study lies in the focus on ANP patients with early fever, in whom the potential of blood mNGS to optimize antibiotic use was hypothesized and assessed. With high NPV of 94.1%, blood mNGS showed excellent performance in distinguishing SIRS and pancreatic infection in patients with early fever, demonstrating great potential to reduce antibiotic overuse. Moreover, with high concordance of pathogen identification identified by mNGS, physicians can find the responsible pathogen in time, enabling the prompt adjustment of appropriate targeted antibiotic therapy.

This study provides a real-life snapshot of antibiotic use in ANP patients with early fever across different hospitals, highlighting a situation where physicians show a strong inclination toward antibiotic therapy, which is incompatible with current guideline. Early diagnosis of infection is the key to appropriate antibiotic therapy. Given the promising results of this simulation, we suggest that clinicians consider plasma mNGS as a supplementary diagnostic tool in febrile ANP patients when available, particularly when conventional cultures are negative or delayed.

The result of this study should be interpreted in light of some limitations. First, the number of participating hospitals was limited, and these hospitals were primarily located in a single province of China, with some hospitals contributing a relatively small number of cases. Variation in empirical antibiotic policies across centres may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Second, although most of data has been prospectively and carefully collected and evaluated, a small section of the data regarding antimicrobial therapy was retrospectively collected from electronic record. Third, the observational nature of this study has inherent limitation in the ability to ascertain the role of mNGS in guiding antibiotic usage in ANP. This may over- or underestimate the real impact of the mNGS result. Randomized controlled clinical trials are needed to accurately quantify and compare clinical impact. Fourth, according to a nationwide prospective cohort conducted by the Dutch Pancreatitis Study Group [7], 86 out of 305 patients (28%) with suspected or proven infected necrotizing pancreatitis were successfully treated with antibiotics alone and did not require drainage. There also may be IPN patients who recover with antibiotic treatment alone and might have been misclassified as SPN in this study, which results in an underestimation of the true infection rate and, conversely, an overestimation of unnecessary antibiotic treatments. Fifth, the definition of correct antibiotic use was devised specifically for the present study. While this allowed for a standardized assessment, it inevitably oversimplifies actual clinical decision‑making, particularly in patients with severe ANP and suspected infection, where empirical broad‑spectrum antibiotics are commonly prescribed on clinical grounds prior to any microbiological result. Lastly, mNGS also has the possibility of confusing explanations of the reports. It remains unclear if the reported organisms are the underlying drivers of sepsis. A critical limitation of mNGS is its inability to differentiate viable from non-viable organisms, which may confound interpretation and increase false-positive results, particularly in patients receiving antibiotic therapy. It should be emphasized that clinical judgment remains paramount: antibiotic decisions should integrate the mNGS result with the patient’s clinical trajectory, inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, PCT), imaging features, and the likelihood of alternative diagnoses. We also caution against empirical broadening of antibiotic coverage based solely on a positive mNGS, as this could lead to overtreatment, adverse drug events, and antimicrobial resistance. We propose that in such scenarios, a repeat mNGS or close clinical monitoring may be more appropriate than immediate escalation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this was a prospective multicenter real-world study that described the current status of antibiotic overuse and misuse in clinical practice. It also emphasized the potential of mNGS to provide precise diagnosis and reduce the antibiotic overuse and misuse. Further randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate the potential benefit of an mNGS-based algorithm for guiding antibiotic use in ANP patients and to draw more comprehensive and definitive conclusions.
