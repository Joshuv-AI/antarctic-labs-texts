Coformer: a deep learning-based framework for cross-environment and multi-year cotton phenotype prediction and interpretation
Authors: Huan Liu, Longyu Huang, Jiale Li, Zikang He, Xiaoyan Zhao, Qingzhuo Chen, Jian Wang, Guomin Zhou, Jianhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of key agronomic traits in cotton is crucial for advancing its genetic improvement and enabling breeding-by-design. However, when using high-dimensional genomic data (e.g., massive SNP markers) for prediction, traditional models often suffer from overfitting and poor generalization. To address this, we propose an innovative deep learning model—Coformer. Coformer is a hybrid Transformer–autoencoder model: a self-attention Transformer encoder captures long-range SNP dependencies and compresses high-dimensional genotypes into a compact latent representation, which is then decoded by a supervised prediction head to the target phenotype. Through rigorous evaluation on a combined multi-environment, multi-year dataset, Coformer maintains outstanding predictive robustness even without explicitly modeling environmental factors. The model integrates a normalization module and a linear projection layer to enable adaptive input processing and end-to-end training, effectively improving generalization across varying data dimensionalities. Importantly, Coformer is interpretable: it can precisely pinpoint key genetic loci that influence target traits, providing a solid theoretical basis for deeper insight into the genetic underpinnings of phenotypes and for implementing precision breeding. In addition, to accelerate application and dissemination, we have concurrently developed a browser-based Cotton Phenotype Prediction System (CPPS) that seamlessly integrates the full workflow of “data preparation–model training–result interpretation” into a unified graphical interface. The system supports batch processing and result visualization, substantially lowering the barrier for non-specialists and offering an efficient, user-friendly solution to bridge genomics research and breeding practice.

Introduction

Thanks to the groundbreaking achievements of the Human Genome Project, genomics and genomic prediction have ushered in unprecedented development [1, 2]. Compared to the lengthy cycles caused by traditional breeding methods relying on phenotypic observation and screening, phenotype prediction technology based on genomic data has significantly improved breeding efficiency by utilizing genomic information such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). This technology enables researchers to conduct phenotype prediction experiments at the seedling stage or even the seed stage of crops, thereby quickly identifying excellent individuals with target traits. This accelerates the selection and promotion of superior varieties. Taking cotton breeding as an example, through phenotype prediction technology, researchers can rapidly screen out plants with high yield potential from a large number of seedlings, significantly enhancing breeding efficiency and providing robust technical support for the development of modern agriculture.

In recent years, artificial intelligence technology, especially machine learning and deep learning, has made significant progress in the field of genomic prediction and phenotypic prediction [3, 4]. Yan et al. proposed a model called VBS-ML and applied it to grain yield prediction [5]. The model improves the accuracy of genomic prediction by introducing variational Bayesian sparsity for feature selection. Compared with traditional LMM, BayesA and BayesB models, the prediction error of VBS-ML is reduced by nearly 0.2%. However, the model requires complex optimization and calculation during training, which will lead to high computing resource overhead and long training time when the data set is large. Ma et al. proposed a deep convolutional neural network (DeepGS) that can automatically learn and extract complex features from genotype data, reduce dependence on manual feature selection, and improve prediction efficiency [2, 6]. Compared with the traditional CNN model, DeepGS has a 40% higher accuracy in predicting rice plant height. However, the model is prone to overfitting risk on data sets with relatively small sample sizes. Xu et al. proposed a new method called metabolic marker-assisted genomic prediction (MM_GP) [7]. In hybrid populations of corn and rice, the prediction ability of the MM_GP method was 4.6% and 13.6% higher than that of the traditional genomic prediction (GP) method, respectively. The MM_GP method performed well in different crops such as corn and rice, indicating its wide applicability in hybrid breeding. Although the MM_GP method improves the prediction ability, the MM_GP method requires high-quality metabolomics data, which may increase the complexity and cost of data collection and processing. Yang et al. designed a DeepGOPlus for automatically identifying HKT genes from genomic data [8]. The model combines convolutional neural networks (CNN) and recurrent neural networks (RNN), which can effectively capture local and global features in gene sequences and achieve a mean square error (MSE) of 0.25 in the prediction of HKT genes. Using this model, researchers can quickly and accurately identify target genes, saving a lot of time and human resources. However, its decision-making process is difficult to explain and lacks interpretability. The above studies show that the combination of deep learning technology and genomic prediction methods has brought important progress in the field of plant breeding, especially in analyzing the complex genotype–phenotype association mechanism.

Deep learning, with its powerful ability to capture nonlinear features, has demonstrated great application potential in the field of phenotype prediction. At present, many deep learning methods mainly rely on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for agronomic phenotype prediction. However, the local receptive field of CNNs constrains information propagation, making it difficult to capture complex dependencies between genomic loci that are far apart (i.e., long-distance SNP dependencies, potentially across chromosomes). Here, “long-distance (long-range) SNP dependencies” refer to statistical correlations or interaction effects between genotypes at loci that are widely separated along genomic coordinates (e.g., across distant linkage blocks or different chromosomes). Such dependencies are difficult to model with local receptive-field methods (e.g., CNNs) but can be captured by global attention mechanisms. To address this limitation, we propose Coformer, whose key idea is to integrate a Transformer into an autoencoder framework: a self-attention–based Transformer is used as the encoder to learn global, long-range relationships among SNPs and produce compact latent representations, and a decoder then maps these latent representations to the target phenotypes, enabling effective compression of high-dimensional genotypes and supervised decoding of phenotypic signals. To clarify the architectural difference, the “Transformer” baseline in this study refers to a standard encoder-only Transformer, where SNPs are embedded (with positional encoding) and passed through stacked Transformer encoder layers, and the pooled encoder output is fed to a regression head. In contrast, Coformer adopts an autoencoder-inspired encoder–decoder design: the Transformer encoder produces a compact latent representation, and a dedicated decoder/prediction head maps this latent code to the target phenotype. Note that, unlike a classical autoencoder that reconstructs the input SNP sequence, Coformer’s decoder is supervised and predicts the phenotype from the latent representation.

In addition, positional encoding is incorporated to inject SNP order/position information, and Dropout together with L1 regularization is used to mitigate overfitting, thereby improving prediction stability and generalization across environments and years. In addition, agronomic phenotype data used in previous studies are mostly limited to the same location and year, with insufficient attention to phenotype prediction under cross-environment and multi-year conditions. At the same time, most existing phenotype prediction models are structurally complex, tend to exhibit “black-box” characteristics, and lack interpretability. To address these issues, this study employed multi-environment and multi-year cotton data for phenotype prediction and incorporated SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) for interpretability analysis[9]. We chose SHAP rather than relying solely on Transformer attention weights because attention maps are not guaranteed to be faithful feature attributions for the final prediction, while SHAP provides consistent, additive importance scores directly in the original SNP feature space. It should be emphasized that environmental variables were not explicitly included in the modeling; instead, only genotype data were used as input to explore their utility in phenotype prediction. To facilitate rapid reproduction and interpretation in practical breeding pipelines, we developed a web-based CPPS consistent with the methodological settings in this study, providing one-click training and visualization.

Materials and methods

Construction of the dataset

Cotton phenotype data

In order to systematically and accurately obtain cotton phenotypic data, we selected cotton varieties from 6 representative locations. These locations include: the Yellow River Basin, Liaohe River Basin, Hebei, Northwest Inland, Yangtze River Basin, and Texas in the United States. The selected cotton varieties include Tuzi cotton, Sizi cotton 2B, etc. (Fig. 1A). After collecting cotton seeds, we planted 273 cottons in each of the three regions of Hainan, Anyang and Xinjiang in 2023. At the same time, a total of 383 cottons were planted in Anyang between 2020 and 2023. These cotton materials are not only widely distributed geographically, but also diverse in variety types, providing a solid data foundation for cotton phenotypic prediction research under cross-environment and multi-year conditions. Acquisition and processing of genotype data

To obtain genotype data, we used liquid-phase probe sequencing technology to generate an SNP variation dataset of 1,203 accessions covering 7,779 loci from cotton tender leaves [10]. After quality control and matching genotype IDs with phenotype records, 1,202 samples with complete genotype–phenotype pairs were retained for subsequent analyses. To ensure the quality of SNP data, we have adopted strict filtering criteria. Specific measures include: setting a minimum allele frequency (MAF) threshold of 0.05 to eliminate low-frequency alleles; removing SNP sites with a missing rate exceeding 10% to improve data integrity. At the same time, the PLINK tool was used for linkage disequilibrium pruning to reduce the redundant information caused by highly correlated loci. The dataset is stored and processed in a standardized manner using VCF files [11]. In the experimental design, we pooled the AY/HN/XJ data and performed a stratified 70/30 train–test split by site to preserve site proportions (Fig. 2F) [12]. Given the limited sample size and the high dimensionality of SNP features (7,779 loci), we did not allocate a separate validation set in order to avoid further reducing the effective training data. We acknowledge that this choice may increase the risk of overfitting; therefore, we applied Dropout and L1 regularization during training to control model complexity and improve generalization. Experimental process for cotton phenotype prediction

Subsequently, we extracted SNPGT (SNP Genotype) data that affect cotton trait expression from the VCF file for phenotypic prediction. SNPGT is usually expressed in four forms: 0/0, 0/1, 1/1, and 1/0. In addition, “./.” indicates that the genotype of the site has not been successfully identified, usually due to missing sample data or low quality, which is a type of dirty data. In addition, the dataset also contains other types of dirty data, such as missing values and character data. In order to improve the stability of subsequent model training, we cleaned the SNPGT dataset. Specifically, we applied a k-nearest neighbors (kNN)-based imputation strategy to handle missing or low-quality genotype calls (“./.”) and other dirty entries in the SNPGT matrix. For each dirty entry, we identified the k most similar samples based on genotype distance and imputed the value using the observed genotypes of these neighbors [13]. During the data processing, we encoded SNPGT to convert it into a format that can be processed by deep learning. 0/0 is encoded as 0, 0/1 is encoded as 1, 1/0 is encoded as 1, and 1/1 is encoded as 2. This encoding scheme was adopted because both 0/1 and 1/0 represent heterozygous genotypes, which typically have similar genetic effects. By assigning them the same code of 1, we simplified the model input and reduced the risk of overfitting, ensuring more efficient and generalizable predictions. Subsequently, we used data processing libraries such as numpy and torch to convert these encoded data into data matrices. The rows of the matrix represent samples, the columns represent variant sites, the sample names are used as row indices, and the variant sites are used as column names (Fig. 1B). In this study, all samples were genotyped on the same SNP set, and the genotype matrix therefore shares an identical marker panel and fixed SNP order across environments/datasets. In this way, SNPGT data is effectively converted into a numerical format and can be directly input into the deep learning model for training and prediction, thereby ensuring that the model can efficiently process these data and achieve efficient prediction of complex cotton phenotypes (Fig. 2B).

Construction of models for phenotype prediction

In this study, we developed a deep learning model named Coformer for cotton phenotype prediction using 7,779 SNP loci. For comparison, the baseline Transformer used in this study follows the common encoder-only design: after embedding SNPs and adding positional encoding, representations are processed by the Transformer encoder, and a pooled representation (e.g., mean pooling across loci) is passed to a regression head. In contrast, Coformer retains the same Transformer encoder for global dependency modeling but introduces an autoencoder-inspired bottleneck-and-decoder prediction pathway, in which the encoder output is first compressed into a compact latent representation and then mapped to the phenotype by a dedicated decoding/prediction head. This encoder–decoder formulation explicitly separates representation learning from phenotype mapping and is intended to improve robustness in high-dimensional SNP settings. Coformer consists of three core modules: an embedding block, an encoding block, and a decoding block (Fig. 2C). The embedding block takes an SNP genotype vector of length L=7779 (one genotype value per locus) as input, applies normalization (StandardScaler), and maps the genotype representation into a fixed embedding space of dimension d=512 via a learnable linear projection (input_projection). To incorporate SNP order/position information, sinusoidal positional encoding is added to the embedded representation before it enters the encoder. The encoding block is a multi-layer Transformer encoder comprising four TransformerEncoderLayer blocks with four attention heads and a dropout rate of 0.1, which captures long-range dependencies among SNP genotypes through multi-head self-attention, feed-forward networks, and residual connections. The decoding block is a feed-forward prediction head that maps the encoded representation to a single continuous phenotype value (output dimension = 1), and the predicted value is inverse-transformed to the original phenotype scale when reporting results. During training, model parameters are optimized via backpropagation using the Adam optimizer, and L1 regularization is applied to improve generalization capability [14]

L1 regularization and dropout

To prevent overfitting, we incorporated L1 regularization and dropout into the model to improve its generalization ability [16, 17].

L1 regularization is an effective regularization method, particularly suitable for high-dimensional data and scenarios requiring feature selection. By imposing a penalty on the absolute value of the weights, it reduces model complexity, improves generalization ability, and generates sparse solutions, making it an essential tool for addressing overfitting and feature selection issues. Dropout can randomly discard some neurons in the neural network during the training process, preventing excessive dependency between neurons and thereby enhancing the model's generalization ability. Through the effective combination of the embedding, encoding, and decoding blocks, the Coformer model can efficiently learn patterns in sequential data and achieve high-performance prediction.

Training without a separate validation set. Because the sample size is limited, we did not allocate an additional validation set to avoid further reducing the effective training data. Instead, we controlled overfitting by combining Dropout in the Transformer layers and an L1 penalty in the training objective, which encourages sparse solutions and reduces model complexity in high-dimensional SNP settings. We acknowledge that the absence of a validation set may increase sensitivity to the particular train–test split, and we therefore emphasize regularization and strict separation of the held-out test set.

Experimental procedure and analysis

The hardware and software configurations used in this study are as follows: Python 3.6.11, PyTorch 1.10.2 + CUDA 11.3, Linux Ubuntu 20.04, and a GPU NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 with 10 GB of memory. The Coformer model runs in the Linux Ubuntu 20.04 environment, while the other six models run in the Windows environment.

Hyperparameter selection and evaluation metrics

The key hyperparameters of Coformer (number of Transformer encoder layers, embedding dimension, number of attention heads, and dropout rate) were selected based on a small empirical search conducted on the training set under the same site-stratified 70/30 split described above (random seed = 42). We evaluated a limited set of candidate configurations guided by common practice in Transformer-based models and prior work applying attention mechanisms to high-dimensional genomic prediction tasks. Specifically, we tested encoder depths in {2, 4, 6}, embedding dimensions in {128, 256, 512}, attention heads in {2, 4, 8}, and dropout rates in {0.0, 0.1, 0.2}. The final setting (4 encoder layers, embedding dimension d = 512, 4 attention heads, and dropout = 0.1) provided a good balance between predictive accuracy and model complexity, while maintaining stable optimization and reducing overfitting risk on the relatively small sample size. Unless otherwise stated, all other training settings follow standard practice and were kept fixed across comparisons.

To evaluate model performance, we used Average Relative Error (ARE) and Mean Squared Error (MSE) for regression, and Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1score for classification. Classification metrics. For the binary classification task, we define true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) in the standard manner. Accuracy is used to measure the correctness of the model's overall prediction and is one of the most commonly used evaluation indicators in classification tasks. It indicates the proportion of samples predicted correctly by the model to the total number of samples. In this study, accuracy is used to evaluate the model's overall judgment ability in distinguishing “excellent” and “non-excellent” cotton samples. Its calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\mathrm{Accuracy}} = \frac{TP + TN}{{TP + TN + FP + FN}} $$\end{document}

Among them, TP represents the number of samples correctly predicted as high-quality, TN represents the number of samples correctly predicted as non-quality, FP represents the number of non-quality samples misjudged as high-quality, and FN represents the number of high-quality samples misjudged as non-quality. The higher the accuracy, the stronger the overall classification ability of the model.

Precision mainly measures the accuracy of the model's recognition of positive samples. It calculates the proportion of samples that are actually positive among all samples predicted by the model to be positive (i.e. performing well). In this study, precision is used to measure how many of the cotton samples predicted by the model to be “good” are actually good materials. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\mathrm{Precision}} = \frac{TP}{{TP + FP}} $$\end{document}

among them, TP represents the number of samples correctly identified as performing well by the model, and FP represents the number of samples that are actually not performing well but are mistakenly identified as performing well by the model. The higher the precision, the fewer mistakes the model makes when identifying performing well samples, and the more credible the selected “performing well “ is.

The recall rate reflects the model's ability to identify actual positive samples (i.e., high-quality cotton) and is an important indicator for measuring missed judgments. It indicates the proportion of all real high-quality samples that are successfully identified by the model. In this study, the recall rate measures the proportion of all high-quality cotton samples that the model correctly finds. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\mathrm{Recall}} = \frac{TP}{{TP + FN}} $$\end{document}

Among them, TP represents the number of samples correctly predicted as high-quality by the model, and FN represents the number of high-quality samples misjudged as non-high-quality by the model. The higher the recall rate, the more comprehensive the model is in screening high-quality cotton materials, and the fewer missed detections.

The F1score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and is an indicator for comprehensively evaluating the classification performance of a model. In this study, the F1score is used to comprehensively measure the accuracy and coverage of the model in identifying high-quality samples. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ F1score = 2 \times \frac{{{\mathrm{Precision}} \times {\mathrm{Recall}}}}{{{\mathrm{Precision}} + {\mathrm{Recall}}}} $$\end{document}

When the F1score is close to 1, it means that the model has a good balance between precision and recall, and the model performs well. When the F1score is close to 0, it means that the model has poor precision and recall.

Binary classification task

To simulate the identification of high-performing cotton materials in practical breeding, we additionally designed a binary classification task. The phenotype values were discretized according to their distribution, where the top 25% of samples were defined as positive (high-performing) and the remaining samples were defined as negative. For model training, all models were optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. Model performance was evaluated using four common classification metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score.

Results

Cross-environment evaluation

In this cross-environment setting, we pooled AY/HN/XJ samples collected in 2023 and evaluated all models using a site-stratified 70/30 train–test split (random seed = 42). To systematically evaluate Coformer under cross-environment evaluation datasets, we conducted a series of phenotype prediction experiments based on the 2023 cross-environment cotton dataset collected. These cotton data from different locations have certain correlations. as shown in (Fig. 1D), we conducted an in-depth analysis of phenotypic correlations in each dataset across the three regions, revealing multiple significant phenotypic associations. The results showed that the correlation of fruit branch height (GZG) between Anyang and Xinjiang was significant, reaching 0.61. In the plant height (ZG) dataset, the correlation between Hainan and Xinjiang was 0.44, and the correlation between Hainan and Anyang was 0.52. These findings show that the same phenotype still has significant correlations under different environments, which provides an important reference for further understanding the relationship between different phenotype and their performance differences under multiple environmental conditions. The back end performs rigorous data validation, automated preprocessing, model training, and evaluation, and finally presents the results on a dedicated, visualization-rich page (Fig. 8). CPPS seamlessly integrates the three core stages—data preparation, model training, and result interpretation—on a single page, and is optimized for routine use in breeding and phenotype-analysis scenarios. The home page is intentionally simple: users need only upload two files—the feature matrix X (samples × SNP loci) and the label file Y (target traits). The system automatically performs intelligent alignment of sample IDs and format checks, promptly flagging potential issues. The platform currently assumes that user genotypes are provided on the same marker panel as the training data (i.e., an identical SNP list, fixed SNP order, and consistent allele coding); under this setting, external samples can be appended to the existing genotype matrix after basic format/QC checks. For training, we distilled the settings to two essentials—epochs and learning rate—and provide recommended ranges that cover common dataset scales while avoiding onerous hyperparameter tuning. With a single click on “Start Training,” the system completes preprocessing and training automatically, with no further user action required.

Upon completion, the system automatically navigates to the results page, where model performance is displayed via easy-to-read visual components. Prominent metric cards at the top present two key evaluation metrics—ARE and MSE—explicitly labeled “the smaller, the better,” helping non-technical users quickly judge the utility of the current run. Below, two chart types are provided: (i) a bar chart for metric visualization that supports horizontal comparison across metrics or historical comparison across multiple runs; and (ii) a “ground truth vs. prediction” comparison plot that draws paired bars per sample, enabling instant identification of outliers and precise localization of potential data-quality issues. To further support downstream decision-making, the system provides a downloadable CSV containing per-sample predictions on the test set, making it easy to feed suspicious samples or high-potential materials directly into the next round of screening and validation.

CPPS adheres to a “less is more” philosophy in interaction and presentation: inputs require only the X and Y files, with automatic sample-ID alignment; evaluation and visualization focus on “two numbers + two figures,” enabling users to quickly decide “Is this run reliable?” and “Which materials deserve follow-up?” without delving into algorithmic details. The entire workflow remains fully aligned with the methodology reported in this study—by default, it performs within-site evaluation and does not introduce environmental variables such as weather—ensuring that the system outputs are strictly consistent with the experimental protocol described in the paper.

Model interpretability

Deep learning models, especially complex architectures such as Coformer, face significant interpretability challenges due to their “black box” nature. This is mainly reflected in the lack of transparency of the decision-making mechanism and prediction process within the model, making it difficult for users to understand how it works [18]. To address this issue, we conducted model interpretability experiments.

In the 2022 fruit branch number (GZS) phenotypic prediction experiment in Anyang, the mean square error (MSE) of the model reached 3.17, indicating that its prediction effect was relatively ideal. To further explore the key factors affecting the prediction results, we used the GZS dataset of the area to perform model interpretability analysis, aiming to identify the features that contribute most to the prediction results (such as key SNP sites). Specifically, we selected 20 features from the total SNPs as model inputs and analyzed them using the SHAP method to generate bar charts and bee swarm charts to visualize the importance of the features and their impact on model predictions (Fig. 9). First, we calculated the mean SHAP value of each genotype feature and visualized the feature importance of the model (Fig. 9A). To provide biological context for the SHAP-identified loci, we mapped SNP18 and SNP15 to the Gossypium hirsutum reference genome using their physical coordinates in the VCF file. It should be noted that SHAP provides statistical feature attributions rather than direct mechanistic evidence; nevertheless, these annotations suggest that the highlighted loci may be associated with biological processes relevant to fruiting-branch development and plant architecture, and thus represent plausible candidates for follow-up functional validation and marker development.

In the bee swarm plot (Fig. 9B), each point represents a genotype, and the color reflects the magnitude of the feature value, with red indicating higher feature values and blue indicating lower feature values. The horizontal axis represents the SHAP value, which is used to measure the contribution of the feature to the GZS prediction—the larger the SHAP value, the more the feature tends to increase the predicted probability of GZS, and vice versa [19]. This analysis helps enhance the interpretability of the model, allowing researchers to intuitively understand the decision-making logic of the model and providing more reliable theoretical support for cotton phenotype prediction and breeding research.

Discussion

Coformer integrates multiple cutting-edge technologies such as linear projection, positional encoding, Dropout, and L1 regularization, achieving significant progress in cotton phenotype prediction [20]. By employing the Dropout mechanism, it effectively reduces the co-adaptation between neurons and, in combination with L1 regularization strategy, significantly enhances the model's generalization performance in complex genomic data environments, successfully alleviating the overfitting problem caused by high-dimensional data. Notably, position encoding technology can accurately capture the interaction relationships between long-range dependencies among SNP genotypes (SNPGT), giving it a unique advantage in handling genomic data with complex linkage disequilibrium structures [21].

Compared with traditional convolutional neural networks (CNN), Coformer breaks through the limitation of the receptive field imposed by the size of the convolutional kernel through the self-attention mechanism, solving the problem of capturing long-distance dependencies. The model creatively combines bottleneck-style feature compression (autoencoder-inspired) with the global dependency modeling advantages of Transformer, not only efficiently handling high-dimensional SNPGT data but also improving information transmission efficiency. In experiments of cross-environment and multi-year phenotype prediction, Coformer has performed outstandingly in processing large-scale high-dimensional SNP genotype data. Our experiments in Hainan, Xinjiang, and Anyang regions show that the model's average relative error (ARE) in phenotype prediction remains stable at around 0.1 under different geographical environments, demonstrating good adaptability. A limitation of this study is that we used a hold-out split without a separate validation set, and the performance may be sensitive to the specific train–test partition. Future work will include repeated splits or nested repeated hold-out and explicit environment-to-environment transfer tests (e.g., training on one site and testing on another) to provide more stable estimates of generalization.

In multi-year experiments,to evaluate the model's time series prediction ability, we designed a systematic time-recursive pretest experiment using a progressive prediction strategy: first, using the data from 2020 as the training set to predict the phenotypes of 2021, 2022, and 2023; then adding the data from 2021 to the training set to predict the phenotypes of 2022 and 2023; finally, using the data from 2022 to predict the phenotype of 2023. The results show that in the prediction of the number of fruit branches (GZS) trait from 2020 to 2022, the model achieved an excellent mean squared error (MSE) of 3.17. Additionally, to comprehensively evaluate the model's generalization ability and practical application value, we designed a classification task based on high-performance sample identification as a supplementary test to explore its ability to identify superior materials. Compared with the six mainstream models, Coformer performed excellently in multiple evaluation metrics, fully demonstrating its practical value and broad application potential in cotton breeding. In the study of model interpretability, we used the SHAP method to conduct an in-depth analysis of GZS in the Anyang region [22]. By constructing SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) bar charts and group charts, the contribution of each feature to the prediction results is quantitatively analyzed, providing a visual basis for explaining the decision-making mechanism of the model. The interpretability analysis not only deepened the understanding of the model's operational logic but also provided a solid theoretical foundation and direction guidance for subsequent model optimization and iteration. To enable researchers and breeders to use the Coformer model with ease, we developed a companion Cotton Phenotype Prediction System (CPPS). The system offers a user-friendly browser interface that automates tedious steps in data processing, model configuration, and result analysis. Through this platform, even users without a deep learning background can readily perform phenotype prediction and obtain intuitive visual reports, thereby accelerating the practical translation of genotype-to-phenotype (G2P) research.

Due to the reduction of arable land and climate change, how to increase the yield and quality of cotton has become one of the important issues in cotton production. Through phenotype prediction, breeders can screen out cotton varieties with excellent traits. These seeds not only can adapt to harsh climatic conditions but also achieve higher yields within limited cultivated land. Combined with modern agricultural technologies such as precision fertilization, intelligent irrigation, and climate-smart agriculture, phenotypic prediction can also assist cotton farmers in formulating more scientific planting management strategies, optimizing the growth environment of cotton, and further enhancing yield and quality [23]. Phenotypic prediction can also be combined with genomic data for genotype–phenotype association analysis. This precise prediction method not only improves the efficiency of cotton production but also provides more solid data support for the genetic improvement of cotton varieties. Through phenotypic prediction, breeding work can be more refined and personalized to meet the planting needs of different regions and promote the sustainable development of the cotton industry.

Although the results of this study demonstrate that Coformer performs well in multi-environment and multi-year cotton phenotype prediction, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study uses only genotype (SNP) data as input and does not explicitly model genotype-by-environment (G × E) interactions; therefore, when environmental effects are strong or pronounced G × E exists, the model’s generalization and predictive accuracy may be constrained. Second, the SHAP-based interpretability analysis provides statistical feature-attribution scores rather than direct physiological or mechanistic explanations. For SNPs ranked as highly influential, their links to candidate genes and biological pathways still require confirmation through functional annotation, existing biological evidence, and further experimental validation. Importantly, the top-ranked SNPs highlighted by SHAP can be prioritized for further functional annotation and cross-referenced with previously reported QTLs, thereby strengthening the link between predictive modeling and biological interpretation. These loci may also serve as practical molecular markers for screening high-performing materials; however, their causal roles require further experimental validation. Future work could extend this framework in several directions: incorporating environmental covariates or developing explicit G × E modeling to improve robustness in cross-environment prediction; adopting multi-task learning or transfer learning across sites/years to enhance adaptability to distribution shifts; and integrating genomic functional annotations and prior biological knowledge to further improve prediction performance and strengthen biological interpretability.

Conclusion

This study proposes Coformer, a model for accurate and interpretable prediction of cotton phenotypes across multiple environments and years. Built on a Transformer architecture and combined with L1 regularization and a Dropout mechanism, Coformer effectively mitigates the overfitting and limited generalization commonly associated with high-dimensional genomic data. Using only SNP genotypes and without explicitly modeling environmental factors, the model delivers robust predictive performance for key agronomic traits, including plant height (ZG), number of fruit branches (GZS), and height of the first fruit branch (GZG).

In cross-environment evaluations across Anyang, Hainan, and Xinjiang, Coformer produced stable and reliable predictions with low Average Relative Error (ARE). In multi-year prediction experiments, the model likewise demonstrated strong across-year generalization. Comparative analyses show that, in both regression tasks and classification tasks aimed at identifying high-performing materials, Coformer outperforms seven baseline models in the regression setting (Table 4), and achieves the best overall performance among six deep-learning and machine-learning baselines in the binary classification task (Fig. 6).A key contribution of this work is interpretability. Using the SHAP method, Coformer can identify genetic loci with substantial effects on target traits, turning the “black box” of deep learning into actionable genetic insight. To promote adoption, we also developed a browser-based Cotton Phenotype Prediction System (CPPS) that streamlines the entire workflow from data upload to result visualization, enabling breeders and researchers without specialized computational expertise to perform phenotype prediction with ease.

In summary, the Coformer framework and its companion CPPS platform provide a powerful, interpretable, and user-friendly solution to accelerate genetic improvement in cotton. By effectively bridging genomics research and practical breeding, this work offers strong support for developing superior cotton varieties to meet future agricultural demands.
