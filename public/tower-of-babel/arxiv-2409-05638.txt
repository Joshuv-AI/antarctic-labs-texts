1.
Introduction

A classical result in analysis concerns estimating size of sumsets of arbitrary subsets of Euclidean spaces. In particular, given positive integers
d,k
and non-empty, compact sets
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the well-known Brunn–Minkowski inequality states that the sumset

\mathcal{A}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}:a_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}\}

satisfies

(1.1)

\mu(\mathcal{A}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{A}_{k})\geq(\mu(\mathcal{A}_{1})^{1/d}+\dots+\mu(\mathcal{A}_{k})^{1/d})^{d},

where we denote
\mu
to be the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, such a result can be seen to be sharp, by considering the case when
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
are homothetic convex sets. It is natural to ask whether such an estimate remains true in the discrete setting. In this endeavour, we define for every finite, non-empty set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the dimension
\dim(A)
to be the dimension of the affine span of the set
A
. Analogously to the continuous setting, one may naively expect that whenever
\dim(A)=d
, then the set
kA=\{a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}:a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A\}
satisfies
|kA|\geq(k^{d}-o(1))|A|
. However, this fails to be true as evinced by the example

(1.2)

A_{N}=\{0,e_{2},\dots,e_{d}\}+\{e_{1},2\cdot e_{1},\dots,(N-d)\cdot e_{1}\},

where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular,
|kA_{N}|/|A_{N}|\leq(d+1)^{k-1}
, and so, one can expect the so-called doubling to grow at most polynomially in
d
.

It is natural to analyse the conditions that sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of cardinality
N
must satisfy so that
|A+B|\geq(C^{d}-o(1))N
holds for some constant
C>1
. One possible answer to this is given by Green–Tao
[
11
]
, who proved that whenever
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
contains
\{0,1\}^{d}
, then
|2A|\geq 2^{d/2}|A|
, see also
[
15
]
. A recent breakthrough result of Green–Gowers–Manners–Tao
[
8
]
on the polynomial Frieman–Ruzsa conjecture implies the existence of constants
C,C^{\prime}>1
such that any finite set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfying
|A+A|<C^{d}|A|
must contain
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
such that
|A^{\prime}|\geq|A|/C^{dC^{\prime}}
and
\dim(A^{\prime})\leq d-1
. Both these results have found various applications towards the sum-product conjecture and related topics, see
[
20
,
18
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we investigate a third such setting which has been explored in a conjecture of Conlon–Lim
[
5
]
, which itself is a revised version of a previous question raised by Bukh
[
2
, Problem
4
]
. This concerns sumsets of the form

\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)=\{\mathcal{L}_{1}(a_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(a_{k}):a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A\}

where
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite set. Following
[
5
]
, we denote matrices
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
to be
irreducible
if there are no non-trivial subspaces
U,V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
of the same dimension such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}(U)\subseteq V
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Furthermore, we write
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
to be
coprime
if there do not exist matrices
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{Q})
satisfying
0<|\det(\mathcal{P})\det(\mathcal{R})|<1
such that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{1}\mathcal{R},\dots,\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{k}\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
. With these definitions in hand, we state the following conjecture from
[
5
]
.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Given
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
and irreducible, coprime
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
and a finite, non-empty set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, one has

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\geq(\det(\mathcal{L}_{1})^{1/d}+\dots+\det(\mathcal{L}_{k})^{1/d})^{d}|A|-o(|A|).

Note that the analogous result holds in the continuous setting by simply applying
(
1.1
)
. Moreover, the hypothesis that
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
be coprime and irreducible is necessary, see
[
5
, §1]
.

When
d=1
, Conjecture
1.1
matches
[
2
, Problem
4
]
and
was resolved by Bukh
[
3
]
. On the other hand, when
d\geq 2
, even the simplest non-trivial case of this conjecture remained open until work of the third author
[
16
, Theorem 1.3]
and subsequent work of Krachun–Petrov
[
14
, Theorem 1]
.
More recently, in their very nice paper
[
5
]
, Conlon–Lim completely resolved the
k=2
case of Conjecture
1.1
. In particular, their main result implies that for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
and for any coprime, irreducible
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
, there exists some constant
\delta=\delta(d,\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2})>0
such that for any finite
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

(1.3)

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2}(A)|\geq(\det(\mathcal{L}_{1})^{1/d}+\det(\mathcal{L}_{2})^{1/d})^{d}|A|-O_{d,\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}}(|A|^{1-\delta})

Our main aim in this paper is to present a variety of sharp results in the
k\geq 3
regime of Conjecture
1.1
and related problems. In this endeavour, letting
\mathbb{F}
be either
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
, we denote matrices
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F})
to be
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
if there are no non-trivial subspaces
U,V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{d}
of the same dimension such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}(U)\subseteq V
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. With this in hand, we present our first result below.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be either
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
. Given
d,k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\delta=\delta(d,k)>0
such that for any
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
in
\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F})
and any finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(1.4)

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\geq k^{d}|A|-O_{d,k}(|A|^{1-\delta}).

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
and
k
is even and
\mathcal{L}_{2i-1},\mathcal{L}_{2i}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
are coprime and irreducible for every
1\leq i\leq k/2
, then there exists
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k})>0
such that for every
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(1.5)

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\geq(\det(\mathcal{L}_{1})^{1/d}+\dots+\det(\mathcal{L}_{k})^{1/d})^{d}|A|-O_{\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}}(|A|^{1-\delta^{\prime}}).

In particular, we are able to resolve Conjecture
1.1
in the more specific case when
k
is even and
\mathcal{L}_{2i-1},\mathcal{L}_{2i}
are coprime and irreducible for
1\leq i\leq k/2
, the latter being a more restrictive condition than just
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
being coprime and irreducible.

We further note that the main term in (
1.4
) is sharp for a variety of cases. For instance, we have the following example.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be the identity matrix, and for each
2\leq j\leq d
, let
\mathcal{L}_{j}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be the linear transformation satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{j}(e_{1})=e_{j}\ \ ,\ \ \mathcal{L}_{j}(e_{j})=-e_{1}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{L}_{j}(e_{i})=e_{i}\ \ \text{for every}\ \ i\in[d]\setminus\{1,j\}.

Then
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{d}
are
\mathbb{R}
-irreducible and coprime. Moreover, setting

A=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{d}|\leq N\},

we have that

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{d}(A)|=d^{d}|A|+O_{d}(|A|^{1-1/d}).

We now turn to another discrete Brunn–Minkowski result proved by Grynkiewicz–Serra
[
12
]
for sumsets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, let
A_{1},A_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be finite, non-empty sets and let
l
be a line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover, let
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
be the minimal number of translates of
l
required to cover
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
respectively. In this setting, Grynkiewicz–Serra
[
12
]
proved that

(1.6)

|A_{1}+A_{2}|\geq(|A_{1}|/r_{1}+|A_{2}|/r_{2}-1)(r_{1}+r_{2}-1),

consequently generalising earlier work of Freiman
[
6
]
who proved (
1.6
) when
A_{1}=A_{2}
. As a straightforward consequence of the methods involved in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we can prove a
k
-fold generalisation of the above result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given
k\geq 2
, let

A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be finite, non-empty sets, and let
l
be a line in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each
1\leq i\leq k
, let
r_{i}
be the minimal number of translates of
l
required to cover
A_{i}
. Then

(1.7)

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq\left(\frac{|A_{1}|}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{|A_{k}|}{r_{k}}-(k-1)\right)(r_{1}+\dots+r_{k}-(k-1)).

We firstly note that (
1.7
) is sharp. Indeed,
let
n_{1},\dots,n_{k},m_{1},\dots,m_{k}
be positive integers and let
A_{i}=\{1,2,\dots,n_{i}\}\times\{1,2,\dots,m_{i}\}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Let
l
be a line parallel to the
x
-axis, so that
r_{i}=m_{i}
and
|A_{i}|/r_{i}=n_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. In this case, one sees that

A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}=\Big\{k,k+1,\dots,\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}\Big\}\times\Big\{k,k+1,\dots,\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\Big\},

and therefore,

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-(k-1)\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}-(k-1)\Big).

This example shows that equality may hold in (
1.7
), and so, the bound is sharp.

We now return to our earlier example in (
1.2
) and note that

|kA_{N}|=\binom{k+d-1}{d}|A_{N}|-(k-1)\binom{k+d-1}{d-1},

see for instance Lemma
7.2
. This is known to be optimal when
k=2
. Indeed, the well-known Freiman’s lemma states that for any finite, non-empty set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim(A)=d
, one has

|2A|\geq(d+1)|A|-d(d+1)/2.

Combining our methods along with some ideas of Gardner–Gronchi
[
7
]
, we can further confirm that the above example is optimal for every
k\geq 3
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
d,k
be positive integers, let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite, non-empty set with
\dim(A)=d
. Then

|kA|\geq\binom{k+d-1}{d}|A|-(k-1)\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}.

Our result provides sharp lower bounds even in the regime when
|A|,d
are fixed and
k
becomes large, wherein, the lower bound maybe rewritten as

|kA|\geq{k+d-1\choose d-1}\Bigg(\frac{k(|A|-d)}{d}+1\Bigg).

Since
\dim(A)=d
, we have that
|A|\geq d+1
, whence the right hand side above looks like
Q_{A}(k)
, where
Q_{A}
is a polynomial of degree
d
with rational coefficients that depend on
d
and
|A|
. This is reminiscent of a classical result of Khovanskiĭ
[
13
]
which implies that once
k
is sufficiently large, then
|kA|=P_{A}(k)
, where
P_{A}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
is some polynomial of degree at most
d
. These polynomials can be quite difficult to describe explicitly and there has been quite a lot of interest recently in estimating the threshold value of
k
for which
|kA|=P_{A}(k)
, see
[
9
]
and the references therein. Our result implies that
P_{A}(k)\geq Q_{A}(k)
for
k
sufficiently large, and that equality holds whenever
A=A_{N}
.

Our proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.4
and
1.5
build on an assortment of ideas from
[
5
,
11
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
7
]
respectively. In particular, these involve an amalgamation of additive combinatorial results such as Freiman’s theorem and Plünnecke–Ruzsa type sumset inequalities, see §2 for further details, along with a key ingredient in the form of the the technique of compressions. The latter has been widely employed in the literature to analyse sumsets in higher dimensions, see for instance
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
17
]
. In §3, we will give a brief outline of this technique and then utilise it to prove a variety of sumset inequalities for
k
-fold sumsets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The latter consists of Theorem
1.4
as well as
k
-fold analogues of the discrete Brunn–Minkowski inequality proven by Conlon–Lim
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
. We devote §4 to presenting some further combinatorial and geometric maneuvers required for the proof of Theorem
1.2
, the latter being recorded in §5. We utilise §6 to record the proof of Proposition
1.3
. Finally, we use §7 to present the proof of Theorem
1.5
, following closely some ideas of Gardner–Gronchi
[
7
]
.

2.
Additive combinatorics preliminaries

In this section, we gather some preliminary results from additive combinatorics that we will employ
throughout our paper. We begin by noting that for any
d,k\geq 1
and any finite, non-empty sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

(2.1)

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq|A_{1}|+\dots+|A_{k}|-(k-1).

Moreover, this is sharp if one takes
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
to be arithmetic progressions with the same common difference.

Our next set of preliminary lemmas concern inverse result on sumsets, that is, these characterise finite sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfy
|A+A|\leq K|A|
for some fixed constant
K\geq 1
. This is the subject matter of a classic result in additive combinatorics known as Freiman’s theorem. In this endeavour, letting
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
, we define a
generalised arithmetic progression

P
in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
of
additive dimension

D
and size
L
to be a set of the form

P=\{\bm{u}_{0}+l_{1}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+l_{D}\cdot\bm{v}_{D}\ :\ l_{i}\in[L_{i}]\ (1\leq i\leq D)\},

where
\bm{v}_{0},\dots,\bm{v}_{D}
are elements of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
and
L_{1}\dots L_{D}=L
. We denote
P
to be
proper
if
|P|=L
. We remark that the additive dimension of
P
may not be the same as
\dim(P)
.

Note that
P
has a small sumset, that is,
|P+P|\leq 2^{D}|P|
. Freiman’s theorem
[
10
]
roughly states that these are essentially the only such examples.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
. For any
K\geq 1
, there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that if
A\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
is a finite, non-empty set with
|A+A|\leq K|A|
, then
A
is contained in a proper generalised arithmetic progression in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
of arithmetic dimension
s\leq C_{1}
and size
L\leq C_{2}|A|
.

We conclude this section by presenting various sumset inequalities which hold over arbitrary abelian groups. We start with the well-known Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality, see
[
21
]
for a streamlined proof.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A,B
be finite, non-empty subsets of some abelian group
G
such that
|A+B|\leq K|B|
for some
K\geq 1
. Then for every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, one has

|mA-nA|\leq K^{m+n}|B|.

Finally, we record the additive version of the so-called Ruzsa’s triangle inequality. In particular, given any finite, non-empty subsets
U,V,W
of some abelian group
G
, one has

(2.2)

|U||V+W|\leq|V+U||U+W|.

This can be deduced swiftly from
[
21
, Proposition 2.1]
.

3.
Compressions

We will utilise this section to give a brief introduction about the technique of compressions as well as develop the various necessary compression lemmas required in the proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.4
and
1.5
. Thus, let
H
be a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector not parallel to
H
. For any
\bm{u}\in H
and any finite, non-empty set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

A_{\bm{u}}=\{\bm{a}\in A\ :\ \bm{a}+x\cdot\bm{v}=\bm{u}\ \ \text{for some}\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}\}.

We now define the
compression
\mathcal{C}_{H,\bm{v}}(A)
of
A
onto
H
with respect to
\bm{v}
to be the set satisfying

\mathcal{C}_{H,\bm{v}}(A)_{\bm{u}}=\{\bm{u}+n\cdot\bm{v}\ :\ n\in\{0,1,\dots,|A_{\bm{u}}|-1\}\}

for every
\bm{u}\in H
. Whenever the hyperplane
H
is clear from context, we will write
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A)
.
Moreover, a finite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
(H,
\bm{v}
)-compressed
if
\mathcal{C}_{H,\bm{v}}(A)=A
. Finally, we denote a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be a
down set
if
A=\mathcal{C}_{H_{i},e_{i}}(A)
for every
1\leq i\leq d
, where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
denotes the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
H_{i}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{i}=0\}
.

It is clear from the definition that
|\mathcal{C}_{H,\bm{v}}(A)|=|A|
for every pair
(H,\bm{v})
. The key property that we will employ throughout our proofs is that sumsets do not increase in size under compressions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be finite, non-empty sets, let
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be some hyperplane containing
\bm{0}
and let
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector not parallel to
H
. Then we have that

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})\supseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{k}).

In particular, we get that

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq|\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{k})|.

Proof.

Note that the case
k=1
is trivial. We will prove this for the case when
k=2
, since the general case follows inductively. Indeed, supposing we have proved Lemma
3.1
for every
2\leq k\leq k_{0}
, then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1}+\ldots+A_{k_{0}+1})

\displaystyle\supseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k_{0}})+\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{k_{0}+1})

\displaystyle\supseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{k_{0}})+\mathcal{C}_{\bm{v}}(A_{k_{0}+1}),

where the above inclusions follow from the
k=2
and
k=k_{0}
case of Lemma
3.1
respectively.

Thus, we focus on the
k=2
case, and so, let
A,B
be finite non-empty subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For every
\bm{z}\in H
, note that

\displaystyle C_{\bm{v}}(A+B)_{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle\supseteq\cup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x},\bm{y}\in H\\
\bm{x}+\bm{y}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}\{\bm{z}+n\cdot\bm{v}:0\leq n\leq|A_{\bm{x}}+B_{\bm{y}}|-1\}

\displaystyle\supseteq\cup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x},\bm{y}\in H\\
\bm{x}+\bm{y}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}\{\bm{z}+n\cdot\bm{v}:0\leq n\leq|A_{\bm{x}}|+|B_{\bm{y}}|-2\}

\displaystyle=\cup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x},\bm{y}\in H\\
\bm{x}+\bm{y}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}(\{\bm{x}+m_{1}\cdot\bm{v}:0\leq m_{1}\leq|A_{\bm{x}}|-1\}+\{\bm{y}+m_{2}\cdot\bm{v}:0\leq m_{2}\leq|B_{\bm{y}}|-1\})

\displaystyle=(C_{\bm{v}}(A)+C_{\bm{v}}(B))_{\bm{z}},

where the second inclusion follows from (
2.1
). Taking a union over all
\bm{z}\in H
on both sides then delivers the desired result.
∎

If we drop the condition that
\bm{0}\in H
, then the inequality in the conclusion of Lemma
3.1
still holds since

\mathcal{C}_{H+\mu\cdot\bm{v},\bm{v}}(A)=\mathcal{C}_{H,\bm{v}}(A)+\mu\cdot\bm{v}\ \ \ \text{holds for any }\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

As a first application of this technique, we now present the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We prove this result by induction on
k
. Our base case is
k=2
which follows from (
1.6
), and so, we may assume that
k\geq 3
and proceed with the inductive step.

Let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since the conclusion is invariant under rotations, we may assume that
l
is parallel to the
e_{2}
. We first claim that it suffices to consider the case when
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are down sets. In order to see this, write
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
to be the projection map satisfying
\pi(\bm{x})=x_{1}
for every
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and note that
r_{i}=|\pi(A_{i})|
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Setting
H
to be the subspace spanned by
e_{1}
, we observe that
\pi(A_{i})=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{H_{2},e_{2}}(A_{i}))
for every
1\leq i\leq k
. Applying Lemma
3.1
, we get that

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq|\mathcal{C}_{H,e_{2}}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{H,e_{2}}(A_{k})|,

whence it suffices to assume that
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are
(H,e_{2})
–compressed. Let
H^{\prime}
be the subspace spanned by
e_{2}
. We will now compress
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
onto
H^{\prime}
with respect to
e_{2}
. For every
1\leq i\leq k
, since the set
A_{i}
was
(H_{2},e_{2})
–compressed, we have that
|\pi(A_{i})|=|\pi(\mathcal{C}_{H^{\prime},e_{1}}(A_{i}))|
. Combining this observation with Lemma
3.1
, we may further assume that the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are also
(H^{\prime},e_{1})
–compressed. In particular, the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are down sets.

Since
A_{k-1}
and
A_{k}
are down sets, we have that
|\pi(A_{k-1}+A_{k})|=r_{k-1}+r_{k}-1
. We may apply the inductive hypothesis for sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k-2},A_{k-1}+A_{k}
to deduce that

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq\left(\frac{|A_{1}|}{r_{1}}+\dots+\frac{|A_{k-2}|}{r_{k-2}}+\frac{|A_{k-1}+A_{k}|}{r_{k-1}+r_{k}-1}-(k-2)\right)(r_{1}+\dots+r_{k}-k+1).

Applying the base case for the sets
A_{k-1}
and
A_{k}
gives us

\frac{|A_{k-1}+A_{k}|}{r_{k-1}+r_{k}-1}\geq\frac{|A_{k-1}|}{r_{k-1}}+\frac{|A_{k}|}{r_{k}}-1,

which may now be substituted into the preceding estimate to deliver the desired bound. This concludes the inductive step, and so, we finish the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

For the rest of this section, we will use some specific notation in order to simplify the exposition. In particular, for every
1\leq i\leq d
, we will use
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to denote the compression operator
\mathcal{C}_{H_{i},e_{i}}
, where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
H_{i}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{i}=0\}.
Moreover, given a basis
\mathcal{B}=\{\bm{b}_{1},\dots,\bm{b}_{d}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
I\subseteq[d]
, we denote
\pi_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
to be the projection map satisfying

(3.1)

\pi_{I}(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{i})=\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}

for every
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
. While we will not explicitly write the particular basis
\mathcal{B}
in notation of
\pi_{I}
, it will be clear in all instances which
\mathcal{B}
we will be using.

With this notation in hand, we state our next lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for every
I\subseteq[d]
and
i\in[d]
and for all finite non-empty sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

(3.2)

|\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{k}))|\leq|\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})|

Proof.

The case when
i\notin I
is relatively straightforward, since in this case, one has

\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{k}))=\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}).

Thus, we may assume that
i\in I
. Writing
J=I\setminus\{i\}
, we define the projection map
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{|I|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
as
\pi(\sum_{j\in I}\lambda_{j}\cdot e_{j})=\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}\cdot e_{j}
for every sequence
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of real numbers. Note that
\pi_{J}=\pi\circ\pi_{I}
. As before, for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
and any non-empty set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
, we define
C_{\bm{x}}=\{\bm{c}\in C:\pi(\bm{c})=\bm{x}\}
. With this notation in hand, we observe that for every
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
and every
1\leq l\leq k
, the set
\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{l}))_{\bm{x}}
is an arithmetic progression of size at most
|\pi_{I}(A_{l})_{\bm{x}}|
and common difference
e_{i}
. Thus for any
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}
, one has that

\displaystyle|\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{k}))_{\bm{z}}|

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}\\
\bm{x}_{1}+\dots+\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}|\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{1}))_{\bm{x}_{1}}|+\dots+|\pi_{I}(\mathcal{C}_{i}(A_{k}))_{\bm{x}_{k}}|-(k-1)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}\\
\bm{x}_{1}+\dots+\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}|\pi_{I}(A_{1})_{\bm{x}_{1}}|+\dots+|\pi_{I}(A_{k})_{\bm{x}_{k}}|-(k-1)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|J|}\\
\bm{x}_{1}+\dots+\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{z}\end{subarray}}|\pi_{I}(A_{1})_{\bm{x}_{1}}+\dots+\pi_{I}(A_{k})_{\bm{x}_{k}}|

\displaystyle=|\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})_{\bm{z}}|,

where the second inequality follows from (
2.1
). The required estimate (
3.2
) may be deduced by summing the above inequality for all
\bm{z}\in\pi_{J}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})
.
∎

Our final aim in the section is to prove the following Brunn–Minkowski type inequality for
k
-fold discrete sumsets. The
k=2
case of this was proven by Conlon–Lim
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
, with a similar result appearing in earlier work of Green–Tao
[
11
, Lemma 2.8]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any finite, non-empty sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

|A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}|\geq(|A_{1}|^{1/d}+\dots+|A_{k}|^{1/d})^{d}-\sum_{I\subsetneq[d]}(k-1)^{d-|I|}|\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})|.

Proof.

We begin by noting that the above conclusion is invariant under the application of invertible linear maps, whence we may assume that
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, for each
i\in[d]
, noting Lemmata
3.1
and
3.2
, we may further assume that the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are
(H_{i},e_{i})
–compressed. In particular, this means that the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are down sets.

Given some
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a down set
A_{i}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define the sets

\mathcal{Q}_{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}+[-1,0]^{d}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{A}_{i}=\cup_{\bm{x}\in A_{i}}\mathcal{Q}_{\bm{x}}.

Since
A_{i}
is a finite, non-empty down set, we see that
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is connected and compact. Writing
\mu
to be the usual Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we note that
\mu(\mathcal{A}_{i})=|A_{i}|
, and so, we may apply the Brunn-Minkowski inequality, see (
1.1
), to deduce that

(3.3)

\mu(\mathcal{A}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{A}_{k})\geq(|A_{1}|^{1/d}+\dots+|A_{k}|^{1/d})^{d}.

Next, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{A}_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}+[-k,0]^{d}

\displaystyle=\cup_{\bm{x}\in A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}+\{-k+1,\dots,-1,0\}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}_{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\cup_{I\subseteq[d]}\cup_{\bm{x}\in S_{I}}\mathcal{Q}_{\bm{x}},

where for any
I\subseteq[d]
, we define

S_{I}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\ \ :\ \ \pi_{I}(\bm{x})\in\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})\ \ \text{and}\ \ -k+1\leq x_{i}\leq-1\ \ \text{if}\ \ i\notin I\}.

Noting the above definition along with the fact that if
\bm{x}
belongs to a down set in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
then
x_{j}\geq 0
for all
j\in[d]
, we see that for all distinct
I,I^{\prime}\subseteq[d]
, one has

|S_{I}|=(k-1)^{d-|I|}|\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})|\ \ \text{and}\ \ S_{I}\cap S_{I^{\prime}}=\emptyset.

Combining this with the preceding discussion, we get that

\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{A}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{A}_{k})=\sum_{I\subseteq[d]}|S_{I}|=\sum_{I\subseteq[d]}(k-1)^{d-|I|}|\pi_{I}(A_{1}+\dots+A_{k})|.

Putting this together with (
3.3
) delivers the required result.
∎

4.
Some further combinatorial and geometric maneuvers

In this section, we will establish various lemmas that will perform an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, with the first two being iterative Plünnecke–Ruzsa type inequalities. We begin by proving the following
k
-fold generalisation of a result of Krachun–Petrov
[
14
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k,N\geq 2
be integers, let
G
be an abelian group and let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\subseteq G
be sets such that
|A_{1}|=\dots=|A_{k}|=N
and let
X=A_{1}+\dots+A_{k}
. If
|X|\leq KN
for some
K\geq 1
, then
|X+X|\leq K^{7}N
.

Proof.

The
k=2
case of this was proven in
[
14
]
, but we provide a proof of this here for the sake of completeness. When
k=2
, we note that the hypothesis combines with Lemma
2.2
to deliver the bounds
|3A_{1}|,|3A_{2}|\leq K^{3}N
. We now apply (
2.2
) to deduce that

|A_{2}+2A_{1}|\leq\frac{|3A_{1}||A_{1}+A_{2}|}{|A_{1}|}\leq K|3A_{1}|\leq K^{4}N,

whereupon, we can apply (
2.2
) again to see that

|2A_{2}+2A_{1}|\leq\frac{|3A_{2}||A_{2}+2A_{1}|}{|A_{2}|}\leq K^{3}|A_{2}+2A_{1}|\leq K^{7}N.

We now turn to the
k\geq 3
case. Writing
X^{\prime}=A_{2}+\dots+A_{k}
, our hypothesis implies that
|X^{\prime}+A_{1}|\leq KN
, whence we may apply Lemma
2.2
to discern that
|3X^{\prime}|\leq K^{3}N
. Combining this with (
2.2
), we get that

|2X^{\prime}+A_{2}+A_{1}|\leq\frac{|2X^{\prime}+A_{2}+A_{3}||A_{1}+A_{3}|}{|A_{3}|}\leq K|3X^{\prime}|\leq K^{4}N,

where the second last inequality follows from the fact that
|A_{1}+A_{i}|/|A_{1}|\leq|X|/N\leq K
for every
2\leq i\leq k
. Similarly, we may amalgamate the preceding bound with another application of (
2.2
) to deduce that

|X+X|=|2X^{\prime}+2A_{1}|\leq\frac{|2X^{\prime}+A_{1}+A_{2}||A_{1}+A_{2}|}{|A_{2}|}\leq K|2X^{\prime}+A_{1}+A_{2}|\leq K^{5}N.\qed

This allows us to prove the following inequality on iterated sums of linear transformations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
k\geq 2
and
\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and some finite, non-empty set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, writing
X=A+\mathcal{L}_{2}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)
, if
|X|\leq K|A|
for some
K\geq 1
, then

|X+\mathcal{L}_{2}(X)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)|\leq K^{7k+1}|A|.

Proof.

We will prove this inductively, and so, for any
2\leq j\leq k
, define

T_{j}=X+\mathcal{L}_{2}(X)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)+\mathcal{L}_{j+1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A).

Moreover, set
T_{0}=X
and
T_{1}=X+\mathcal{L}_{2}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)
. Our aim is to prove inductively that
|T_{j}|\leq K^{7j+1}|A|
for every
0\leq j\leq k
. Our hypothesis covers the base case
j=0
, and so, we proceed to the inductive step. For any
j\in[k]
, we may apply Lemma
2.2
to get that

|T_{j}|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{j}(X)||T_{j-1}|}{|\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)|}\leq K^{7(j-1)+1}|A+X|\leq K^{7(j-1)+1}|X+X|\leq K^{7j+1}|A|,

where the second and final inequalities follow from the inductive hypothesis and Lemma
4.1
respectively. This concludes the inductive step, and consequently, we finish the proof of Lemma
4.2
.
∎

It is worth remarking that the conclusion of Lemma
4.2
does not hold if we set
X=\mathcal{L}_{1}A+\mathcal{L}_{2}A+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}A
for some arbitrary
\mathcal{L}_{1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{Q})
and consider upper bounds for
\mathcal{L}_{1}(X)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)
. Indeed, let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
and
A\subset\mathbb{Q}^{2}
be defined as

\mathcal{L}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{L}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad A=\{(0,i):i\in[N]\}.

Then one sees that

\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)=\mathcal{L}_{2}(A)=\{(i,i):i\in[N]\},

and so,

X=\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2}(A)=\{(i,i):2\leq i\leq 2N\}.

On the other hand, we have

\mathcal{L}_{1}(X)=\{(2i,i):2\leq i\leq 2N\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{L}_{2}(X)=\{(2i,0):2\leq i\leq 2N\}.

Hence, we get that

|X|=2N-1\leq 2|A|\quad\text{while}\quad|\mathcal{L}_{1}(X)+\mathcal{L}_{2}(X)|=|X|^{2}\geq|A|^{2},

which would contradict an upper bound of the shape
|\mathcal{L}_{1}(X)+\mathcal{L}_{2}(X)|\ll_{K}|A|
whenever
|A|
is sufficiently large.

We are now ready to prove the following geometric result, which roughly states that for any irreducible
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{Q})
, if
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies
|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\ll|A|
, then
A
can not have a significantly large subset lying in some lower dimensional subspace. The
k=2
case of this was proven by Conlon–Lim
[
5
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F})
be
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite, non-empty set with
|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\leq K|A|
for some
K\geq 1
. Then for any subspace
U
of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
of dimension
r<d
, one has

\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}|(\bm{x}+U)\cap A|\leq(K|A|)^{1-2^{-r}}.

Proof.

Our proof proceeds via induction on
r
. We first consider the base cases, that is, when
r=0
and
r=1
. The case when
r=0
is trivial since
|(\bm{x}+U)\cap A|=|\{\bm{x}\}\cap A|\in\{0,1\}
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
. On the other hand, for the
r=1
case, since
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
are invertible and
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible, there exist some
i<j
such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}(U)
and
\mathcal{L}_{j}(U)
are two distinct
1
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Writing
A^{\prime}=A\cap(\bm{x}+U)
, one has that

K|A|\geq|\mathcal{L}_{i}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)|\geq|\mathcal{L}_{i}(A^{\prime})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime})|=|A^{\prime}|^{2},

which concludes the base case analysis.

We now proceed with the inductive step, and so, let
U
be some subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
of dimension
r<d
. As before, let
\bm{x}
be some element of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
and let
A^{\prime}=A\cap(U+\bm{x})
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}
are invertible and irreducible, there exist some
i<j
such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}(U)
and
L_{j}(U)
are two distinct
r
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Thus, let
V=\mathcal{L}_{i}(U)\cap\mathcal{L}_{j}(U)
and let
s\geq 1
be the smallest integer such that there exist
\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{s}\in A^{\prime}
satisfying
A^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(V)+\{\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{s}\}
. Since
r^{\prime}=\dim(V)\leq r-1
, we may apply the inductive hypothesis to get that

(4.1)

|A^{\prime}|\leq s\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}|(\bm{x}+\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(V))\cap A^{\prime}|\leq s\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}|(\bm{x}+\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(V))\cap A|\leq s(K|A|)^{1-2^{-r^{\prime}}}.

Our next claim is that the sets
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{a}_{1})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime}),\dots,\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{a}_{s})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime})
are pairwise disjoint. Indeed, if, say,
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{a}_{1})-\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{a}_{2})=\mathcal{L}_{j}(\bm{a}^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}_{j}(\bm{a})
for some
\bm{a},\bm{a}^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
, then we have that

\bm{a}_{1}-\bm{a}_{2}\in(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime}-A^{\prime}))\subset U\cap\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(U))=\mathcal{L}_{i}^{-1}(V),

but this contradicts the minimality of
s
. We employ the aforementioned claim to discern that

s|A^{\prime}|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{s}|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{a}_{t})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime})|\leq|\mathcal{L}_{i}(A^{\prime})+\mathcal{L}_{j}(A^{\prime})|\leq K|A|.

Combining this with (
4.1
), we get that

|A^{\prime}|^{2}\leq(K|A|)^{2-2^{-r^{\prime}}}\leq(K|A|)^{2-2^{-r+1}},

which concludes our inductive step. This finishes the proof of Lemma
4.3
.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Our main aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
, and in this endeavour, we prove the following key proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F})
be
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible and let
A\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
be a finite, non-empty set such that
|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|\leq K|A|
for some
K\geq 1
. Then there exists some basis
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that for every
j\in[d]
, one has

(5.1)

|\pi_{[d]\setminus{j}}(\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A))|\ll_{d,k,K}|A|^{1-\sigma},

for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k,K}1
, where the map
\pi_{[d]\setminus{j}}
is defined as in (
3.1
).

Proof.

It is worth noting that if
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{F})
are
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible, then so are the matrices
\mathcal{I}_{d},\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{k}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Indeed, if there were some subspaces
U,V
of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that
U\subseteq V
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(U))\subseteq V
for every
2\leq j\leq k
, then
\mathcal{L}_{j}(U)\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1}(V)
for every
1\leq j\leq k
. Moreover, the hypothesis implies that the set

Y=A+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{2}(A))+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(A))

satisfies
|Y|\leq K|A|
.

Our aim will be to show that there exists some basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{\bm{b}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\bm{b}_{d}^{\prime}\}
of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
such that for every
j\in[d]
, the map
f_{j}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
satisfying

f_{j}(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}^{\prime})=\sum_{i\in[d]\setminus\{j\}}\lambda_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}^{\prime}\ \ \text{for every}\ \ \lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{F}

further satisfies

(5.2)

|f_{j}(Y)|\ll_{d,k,K}|A|^{1-\sigma}

for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k,K}1
. This will suffice to prove the desired result, indeed, define the set
X=\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)
and consider the basis
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{b}_{1}^{\prime}),\dots,\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{b}_{s}^{\prime})\}
of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Then for any
I\subseteq[d]
, one has

\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{i}^{\prime}\in Y\ \ \text{if and only if}\ \ \sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{b}_{i}^{\prime})\in X.

Moreover, since the sets
\mathcal{B}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}
are bases of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, we may use (
5.2
) to deduce that for every
j\in[d]
, one has

|\pi_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}(X)|=|f_{j}(Y)|\ll_{d,k,K}|A|^{1-\sigma},

for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k,K}1.

Hence, we will now proceed to construct some
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
such that one has (
5.2
). Note that
|Y|\leq K|A|
, and so, we may apply Lemma
4.1
to deduce that
|Y+Y|\leq K^{7}|A|
. We may now apply Lemma
2.1
to discern that
Y
is contained in some generalised arithmetic progression
P
where

P=\{\bm{v}_{0}+l_{1}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}+\dots+l_{s}\cdot\bm{v}_{s}\ :\ l_{i}\in[L_{i}]\ \ (1\leq i\leq s)\}

for some
\bm{v}_{0},\dots,\bm{v}_{s}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
and
s\ll_{K}1
and
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
L_{1}\geq\dots\geq L_{s}
and
L_{1}\dots L_{s}\ll_{K}|A|
. Since
|A|
is sufficiently large in terms of
d,k,K
, we may apply Lemma
4.3
to deduce that
A
can not be completely contained in a lower dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Moreover, since
P
contains a translate of
A
, we get that
\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}(\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{s}\})=\mathbb{F}^{d}
, where for any finite, non-empty set
T\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
we define

\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}(T)=\{\sum_{\bm{t}\in T}\lambda_{t}\cdot\bm{t}:\lambda_{\bm{t}}\in\mathbb{F}\}.

We now construct a basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{\bm{v}_{i_{1}},\dots,\bm{v}_{i_{d}}\}\subseteq\{\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{s}\}
greedily by letting
\bm{v}_{i_{1}}=\bm{v}_{1}
, and for
2\leq j\leq d
, letting

i_{j}=\min\{l:\bm{v}_{l}\not\in\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{\bm{v}_{i_{1}},\dots,\bm{v}_{i_{j-1}}\})\}.

As
Y\subseteq P
, we get that

|f_{j}(Y)|\leq|f_{j}(P)|\leq\frac{L_{1}\dots L_{s}}{L_{i_{j}}}\ll_{K}\frac{|A|}{L_{i_{j}}}.

Since
L_{1}\geq\dots\geq L_{s}
, our goal of proving (
5.2
) reduces to showing that
L_{i_{d}}\gg|A|^{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k,K}1
. Thus, let
H=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{B}\setminus\{\bm{v}_{i_{d}}\})
. Since
Y=A+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{2}(A))+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(A))
and
|Y|\leq K|A|
, we may apply Lemma
4.2
to deduce that

|Y+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{2}(Y))+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(Y))|\leq K^{7k+1}|Y|.

Moreover, as
\dim(H)=d-1
and
\mathcal{I}_{d},\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{1}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{k}
are
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible, we can apply Lemma
4.3
to get that

\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}|(H+\bm{x})\cap Y|\ll_{d,k,K}|A|^{1-2^{-d+1}}L_{i_{d}}^{s-d+1},.

Note that we can cover
P
, and hence
Y
, by at most
L_{i_{d}}\dots L_{s}
many translates of
H
, and so,

|A|\leq|Y|\leq L_{i_{d}}\dots L_{s}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}|(H+\bm{x})\cap Y|\ll_{d,k,K}|A|^{1-2^{-d+1}}L_{i_{d}}^{s}.

This simplifies to give
L_{i_{d}}\gg_{d,k,K}|A|^{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k,K}1
, and so, we are done.
∎

We are now ready to present the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We begin by deducing (
1.4
) from a combination of Lemma
3.3
and Proposition
5.1
. We begin by claiming that we may assume that
A\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
, which is trivial when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and which may be deduced from the discussion following
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
. We may further assume that
|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\cdots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|<2k^{d}|A|
, since otherwise we are done. We can now apply Proposition
5.1
with
K=2k^{d}
to get find some basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{F}^{d}
satisfying
(
5.1
)
. We now combine this with the conclusion of Lemma
3.3
to deduce that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)|

\displaystyle\geq k^{d}|A|-O_{d,k}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\pi_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}(\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A))|)

\displaystyle\geq k^{d}|A|-O_{d,k}(|A|^{1-\sigma})

for some
\sigma\gg_{d,k}1
, which is precisely the content of (
1.4
).

We now proceed to prove (
1.5
). As in the above proof, noting the discussion following
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
, it suffices to prove Theorem
1.2
for finite, non-empty sets
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Next, we can translate
A
so as to ensure that
0\in A
. Writing
X=\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A)
and
\Lambda=(\det(\mathcal{L}_{1})^{1/d}+\dots+\det(\mathcal{L}_{k})^{1/d})^{d}
, we may assume that
|X|\leq 2\Lambda|A|
, since otherwise we are done. Applying Proposition
5.1
, we find a basis
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
satisfying
(
5.1
)
. We now apply Lemma
3.3
with
A_{j}=\mathcal{L}_{2j-1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2j}(A)
for every
1\leq j\leq k/2
, whence we find that

\displaystyle|X|

\displaystyle\geq(\sum_{j=1}^{k/2}|\mathcal{L}_{2j-1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2j}(A)|^{1/d})^{d}-O_{d,k}(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\pi_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}(\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{k}(A))|)

(5.3)

\displaystyle\geq(\sum_{j=1}^{k/2}|\mathcal{L}_{2j-1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2j}(A)|^{1/d})^{d}-O_{d,k,\Lambda}(|A|^{1-\sigma}),

for some

. Noting the hypothesis that

[

, Theorem 1.5]

|\mathcal{L}_{2j-1}(A)+\mathcal{L}_{2j}(A)|\geq(\det(\mathcal{L}_{2j-1})^{1/d}+\det(\mathcal{L}_{2j})^{1/d})^{d}|A|-O_{d,k,\Lambda}(|A|^{1-\sigma_{j}})

\displaystyle|X|

\displaystyle\geq\Lambda|A|-O_{d,k,\Lambda}(|A|^{1-\delta}),

6.
Proof of Proposition
1.3

In this section, we will record the proof of Proposition
1.3
. We can immediately deduce that
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{d}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
are coprime since
\det(\mathcal{L}_{i})=1
for every
1\leq i\leq d
. We will now proceed to show that
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{d}
are
\mathbb{R}
-irreducible. The case when
d=2
is trivial, since in this case,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
rotates vectors anticlockwise by
\pi/2
, and so, we may assume that
d\geq 3
. Our proof of this case follows via contradiction, and so, suppose that
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{d}
are not
\mathbb{R}
-irreducible. Thus, there exist non-trivial subspaces
U,V
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the same dimension such that
\mathcal{L}_{j}(U)=V
for all
j\in[d]
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is the identity matrix, we must have
U=V
, that is,
V
is an invariant subspace of
\mathcal{L}_{j}
for every
j\in[d]
.

Now, choose some
\bm{a}\in V\setminus\{0\}
and let
S_{\bm{a}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the orbit of
\bm{a}
under the action of
\mathcal{L}_{j}
for every
j\in[d]
. Since
V
is invariant under
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{d}
, it follows that
S_{\bm{a}}\subset V
. Moreover, since
\dim V<d
by assumption, there must exist some hyperplane
H
defined by the equation
c_{1}x_{1}+\dots+c_{d}x_{d}=0
such that
S_{\bm{a}}\subset V\subset H
. We will now show that such a hyperplane can not exist.

For any
j,k,l\in[d]
such that
1<j
and
k<l
, we define
\bm{v}_{k,l}=\mathcal{L}_{l}(\mathcal{L}_{l}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{L}_{k}(\bm{a}))))
and
\bm{v}_{j}=\mathcal{L}_{j}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(\bm{a}))
. Note that both

\bm{v}_{j}=(-a_{1},\dots,-a_{j},\dots,a_{d})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \bm{v}_{k,l}=(a_{1},\dots,-a_{k},\dots,-a_{l},\dots,a_{d})

are elements of
S_{\bm{a}}
. Given
1\leq k<l\leq d
, since
\bm{a},\bm{v}_{k,l}\in S_{\bm{a}}\subset H
, we see that

c_{k}a_{k}+c_{l}a_{l}=0.

Thus, for any distinct
l,l^{\prime}\in[d]
, choosing some
k\in[d]\setminus\{l,l^{\prime}\}
we may apply the preceding equality twice to deduce that

c_{l}a_{l}=-c_{k}a_{k}=c_{l^{\prime}}a_{l^{\prime}}.

Combining this with the fact that
c_{l^{\prime}}a_{l^{\prime}}+c_{l}a_{l}=0
, we discern that

c_{l}a_{l}=0\ \ \text{for every}\ \ l\in[d].

Now, for any
1\leq j\neq k\leq d
, we see that the
k^{\text{th}}
coordinate of
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(\bm{a}))
is either
a_{j}
or
-a_{j}
. Thus, repeating the above procedure with
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathcal{L}_{j}(\bm{a}))
instead of
\bm{a}
, we may deduce that

c_{k}a_{j}=0\ \ \text{for any}\ \ 1\leq j\neq k\leq d.

Moreover, since there must exist some
k\in[d]
such that
c_{k}\neq 0
, the above implies that
a_{j}=0
for every
j\in[d]
. This contradicts the fact that
\bm{a}\in V\setminus\{0\}
, and so, we finish the proof of our claim.

Finally, let
A=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{d}|\leq N\}
. Note that
\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)=\dots=\mathcal{L}_{d}(A)=A
. Thus, we have that

|\mathcal{L}_{1}(A)+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{d}(A)|=|dA|=(2dN+1)^{d}=d^{d}|A|+O_{d}(|A|^{1-1/d}),

which is the desired estimate.

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

We begin this section by recording
[
7
, Lemma 3.7]
which states that for any finite, non-empty set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|A|=N
and
\dim(A)=d
, there exists a finite sequence of compressions which transform
A
into a long simplex of the form

A_{d,N}=\{0,e_{2},\dots,e_{d}\}\cup\{e_{1},2\cdot e_{1},\dots,(N-d)\cdot e_{1}\}.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any finite, non-empty set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|A|=N
and
\dim(A)=d
, there exists some
l\in\mathbb{N}
and vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and hyperplanes
H_{1},\dots,H_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where for every
1\leq i\leq l
we have
H_{i}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{j}=0\}
for some
1\leq j\leq d
, such that

\mathcal{C}_{H_{l},\bm{v}_{l}}(\dots(\mathcal{C}_{H_{2},\bm{v}_{2}}(\mathcal{C}_{H_{1},\bm{v}_{1}}(A)))\dots)=A_{d,N}.

The second ingredient we will require for our proof of Theorem
1.5
concerns estimating the size of iterated sumsets of these long simplices.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
k,N,d
be positive integers such that
N\geq d+1
. Then

|kA_{d,N}|=\binom{k+d-1}{d}(N-d)+\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}

Proof.

We will prove this via induction on
d
. The
d=1
case of this is trivial. Indeed, note that
kA_{1,N}=\{0,e_{1},\dots,kN-k\}
, which gives us
|kA_{1,N}|=k(N-1)+1.
We now move to the inductive step, and so, suppose that we have proved Lemma
7.2
for all
d\leq D-1
, for some integer
D\geq 2
. Now observe that

kA_{D,N}=\cup_{i=0}^{k}((k-i)\cdot e_{D}+iA_{D-1,N-1}),

whence,

|kA_{D,N}|=1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|iA_{D-1,N-1}|

Applying the inductive hypothesis for
|iA_{D-1,N-1}|
for every
1\leq i\leq k
, we get that

\displaystyle|kA_{D,N}|

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Bigg(\binom{i+D-2}{D-1}(N-D)+\binom{i+D-2}{D-2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\binom{k-1+D}{D}(N-D)+\binom{k-1+D}{D-1},

where the last step follows from applying the standard binominal identity

\binom{n+l}{l}=1+\binom{l}{l-1}+\dots+\binom{n+l-2}{l-1}+\binom{n+l-1}{l-1}

twice, once with
(n,l)=(k-1,D)
and the other time with
(n,l)=(k,D-1)
. This concludes the inductive step, and so, we have proved Lemma
7.2
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a set such that
\dim(A)=d
and
|A|=N
for some
N\geq d+1
. We begin by claiming that it suffices to consider the case when
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\{0,e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}\subseteq A
. In order to see this, first translate
A
so as to ensure that
0\in A
. We now consider the largest subset
S=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{r}\}\subseteq A
which is linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. Note that
r\geq d
since
\dim(A)=d
. Moreover, for any
\bm{a}\in A
, there exist
q_{1},\dots,q_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\bm{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}q_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}
. We may now consider the map
\psi:A\to\mathbb{Z}^{r}
which satisfies
\psi(\sum_{i=1}^{r}q_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{i})=(Mq_{1},\dots,Mq_{r})
, where
M
is a suitably chosen positive integer which ensures that
\psi(\bm{a})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for every
\bm{a}\in A
. Next, since
\{0,e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}\subseteq\psi(A)\subset\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is a finite set and
r\geq d
, we may find some suitably large integer
X>0
such that the map
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}^{r}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfying

\sigma((b_{1},\dots,b_{r}))=(b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1},b_{d}+Xb_{d+1}+\dots+X^{r-d}b_{r})

for every
(b_{1},\dots,b_{r})\in\psi(A)
further satisfies the fact that for every
\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{2k}\in A
, one has

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\psi(\bm{a}_{i})-\psi(\bm{a}_{k+i}))=0\ \ \text{if and only if}\ \ \sum_{i=1}^{k}(\sigma(\psi(\bm{a}_{i}))-\sigma(\psi(\bm{a}_{k+i}))=0.

In particular, writing
A^{\prime}=\sigma(\psi(A))
, we see that
\{0,e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}\subseteq A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
|A^{\prime}|=N
, and
|kA^{\prime}|=|kA|
, whence our aforementioned claim is justified.

Noting Lemma
7.1
, we may apply Lemma
3.1
iteratively to deduce that

|kA|\geq|k\mathcal{C}_{H_{1},\bm{v}_{1}}(A)|\geq\dots\geq|k\mathcal{C}_{H_{l},\bm{v}_{l}}(\dots(\mathcal{C}_{H_{2},\bm{v}_{2}}(\mathcal{C}_{H_{1},\bm{v}_{1}}(A)))\dots)|=|kA_{d,N}|.

We finish our proof by applying Lemma
7.2
which implies that

\displaystyle|kA|\geq|kA_{d,N}|

\displaystyle=\binom{k+d-1}{d}(N-d)+\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{k+d-1}{d}|A|-(k-1)\binom{k+d-1}{d-1}.\qed
