Harnessing Information in Incentive Design
Raj Kiriti Velicheti Subhonmesh Bose Tamer Başar

Abstract
Incentive design deals with interaction between a principal and an agent where the former can shape the latter’s utility through a policy commitment. It is well known that the principal faces an information rent when dealing with an agent that has informational advantage. In this work, we embark on a systematic study of the effect of information asymmetry in incentive design games. Specifically, we first demonstrate that it is in principal’s interest to decrease this information asymmetry. To mitigate this uncertainty, we let the principal gather information either by letting the agent shape her belief (aka Information Design ), or by paying to acquire it. Providing solutions to all these cases we show that while introduction of uncertainty increases the principal’s cost, letting the agent shape its belief can be advantageous. We study information asymmetry and information acquisition in both matrix games and quadratic Gaussian game setups.

3.2
Harnessing Information via
\mathfrak{G}_{3}
or

Should

about

can only benefit from more information.

1
.

J_{P,2}^{\star}:\Delta(\Theta)\to\mathbb{R}
is piecewise affine and concave.

Proof.

The optimization problem in (

as it takes the minimum over the aforementioned piecewise affine and concave functions, completing the proof.
∎

in

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{\tau}{\text{minimize}}\ \mathds{E}_{\tau}[J_{A,2}^{\star}(\mu_{s})],\;\text{subject to }\mathds{E}_{\mu_{s}\sim\tau}[\mu_{s}]=\mu_{0},\end{split}

is

For an arbitrary function

from an arbitrary subset

2

The feasible set of this problem for a specific

P
’s standpoint. One can consider a more general affine policy of the form
\gamma(v)=L_{1}v+L_{2}
and optimize over parameters
L_{1},L_{2}
. While this route does not yield closed-form solution, a numerical comparison of
P
’s performance with such policies and that with our mean-feedback policies is left for a future endeavor.

Restricting attention to affine mean feedback strategies for
P
, we now consider
\mathfrak{G}_{3}
, where
P
allows
A
to disclose information before committing to an incentive policy. Precisely,
P
observes a signal
s
sampled according to a signaling scheme
\pi
chosen by
A
. Restrict attention to signaling via an additive Gaussian noise channel, i.e.,
s=\theta+w
, where
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
. In essence,
P
only chooses
\sigma_{w}
to fix the signaling mechanism.

Given this signaling scheme, the posterior belief upon observing
s
is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{s}(s,\pi)=\mathcal{N}\left(\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}z_{0}+\sigma_{0}^{2}s}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}}_{:=z_{s}},\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}}_{:=\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right).

(37)

With this updated belief,
P
implements an affine mean feedback policy of the form (
32
) with
z_{0}
replaced by
z_{s}
to which
A
responds with
v^{\star}(\theta)
in (
33
) with again
z_{0}
replaced by
z_{s}
. Substituting the obtained affine mean feedback policy
\gamma(v)
and
v^{\star}(\theta)
in
c_{A}
then yields an expression quadratic in
\theta,z_{s}
.
A
must compute its expectation over
\theta\sim\mu_{0}
and
s\sim\pi(\cdot|\theta)
, where
z_{s}=\mathds{E}[\theta\mid s]
. Utilizing the relation,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\theta z_{s}]=\mathds{E}[[\theta z_{s}\mid s]]=\mathds{E}[z_{s}^{2}]=z_{0}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{4}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}

(38)

and
\mathds{E}[\theta^{2}]=z_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{0}^{2}
,
the expected cost faced by
A
in
\mathfrak{G}_{3}
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J_{A,3}^{\star}(\mu_{0},\pi)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}^{4}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)f(\beta)+\eta,\end{aligned}

(39)

where
\eta
does not depend on
\sigma_{w}
and

\displaystyle f(\beta):=\frac{4\left(\beta^{2}+1\right)}{9\beta^{2}+12\beta+4}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\beta^{2}+1}.

(40)

Thus,
A
either chooses
\sigma_{w}
to be zero or infinity, corresponding to fully revealing
\theta
or not revealing anything about
\theta
, depending on the sign of
f(\beta)
, a continuous function of
\beta
. When
f(\beta)>0
, set
\sigma_{w}=\infty
and choose
\sigma_{w}=0
, otherwise. Notice that
\lim_{\beta\to 0}f(\beta)=1

and
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}f(\beta)=-5/9
, implying that the choice of revelation depends on the value of
\beta
. Such a result is reminiscent of pure persuasion in scalar QG games studied in
[
Tam18
]
. Hence,
J_{P,3}^{\star}
is either
J_{P,2}^{\star}
or
J_{P,1}^{\star}
depending on no revelation or full revelation, respectively. Since
J_{P,1}^{\star}
is the team optimal cost for
P
in QG games, when full revelation is optimal for
A
, affine mean feedback strategies indeed are optimal for
P
. When
A
chooses not to disclose any information under mean feedback policies, however, there is scope for improvement with optimal affine or possibly nonlinear incentive policies–a topic we are keen to study in future work.

Finally, consider
\mathfrak{G}_{4}
where
P
can pay to construct a channel to acquire information about
\theta
. Specifically, let the channel
\pi
be of additive white Gaussian type that yields an observation
s=\theta+w
, where
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
. The posterior distribution, contingent on the observation
s
, is
\mu_{s}(s,\pi)
, described in (
37
). With the affine mean feedback policy in (
32
),
P
faces a cost of the form (
35
) with
(z_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2})
replaced by
(z_{s},\sigma_{s}^{2})
. Call this cost
J_{P,2}^{\star}(\mu_{s}(s,\pi))
.
P
must pay for the information channel she wishes to use. Similar to Section
3
, consider the channel cost to be equal to the decrease in entropy from a reference normal distribution
\mu^{\prime}_{0}
, which, for this case, we consider to be
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. This implies, any additive Gaussian noise channel maps the reference distribution to
\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}s}{1+\sigma_{w}^{2}},\frac{\sigma_{w}^{2}}{1+\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)
, where
s\sim\theta+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
. Consequently,
P
obtains the cost,

\displaystyle J_{P,4}^{\star}(\mu_{0},\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[J_{P,2}^{\star}(\mu_{s}(s,\pi))]+\frac{\kappa}{2}\log(1+\sigma_{w}^{-2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\beta}{3\beta+2}\left(z_{0}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{4}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\log(1+\sigma_{w}^{-2})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta^{4}+\beta^{3}+2\beta^{2}-4\beta+2}{\beta^{4}+2\beta^{2}+1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right).

In general, this is challenging to optimize. We plot this cost as a function of
\sigma_{w}
for various values of
\beta
in Figure

that reveals cases where noisy monitoring is optimal. For this figure, we chose
z_{0}=1,\sigma_{0}=2
. Notice that for
\beta=1
,
f(\beta)<0
in (
39
), implying that when left to
A
, the latter will choose
\sigma_{w}=0
(full revelation), implying that
P
will attain the cost in
\mathfrak{G}_{1}

P
invests in its information channel, her optimal choice from Figure

will yield a noisy estimate of
\theta
leading to a higher cost in
\mathfrak{G}_{4}

\mathfrak{G}_{3}

P
stands to gain by allowing
A
to signal. With
\beta=0.5

f(\beta)>0

A

\sigma_{w}=\infty

\mathfrak{G}_{4}

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have formulated and studied an incentive design problem between a principal (
P
) and an agent (
A
), where we have endowed
A
with informational advantage. Specifically, we have allowed
P
to know only the prior distribution of a cost-relevant random variable (the state), while letting
A
know the realization of the state. We have studied two types of information acquisition mechanisms. Namely, in one variant,
P
allowed
A
to reveal information about the state prior to the interaction, and in the other, invested in a channel to gain information about the state. We have studied these games within two setups–matrix games and quadratic Gaussian games. Our results reveal the trade-offs between these two types of information acquisition techniques. One direction for future work is to consider more general classes of policies for
P
and study a broader class of QG games. Another direction for future research concerns studying the same question in the context of repeated and dynamic games, where the state itself might evolve,
P
can update beliefs over time, and
A
can shape these beliefs.
