Subungual Glomus Tumor Managed with Nd: YAG Laser: A Case Report with Clinical and Ultrasonographic Follow-Up
Authors: Valeria Duque-Clavijo, Maria Alejandra Sánchez, Claudia González, Hernán Duque-Romero
Published: 2026

Introduction

Glomus tumors are rare benign mesenchymal neoplasms, accounting for less than 2% of soft tissue tumors; they arise from modified smooth muscle cells of the glomus body, most commonly in the subungual region. They characteristically present with severe, paroxysmal pain and cold sensitivity that may appear disproportionate to the subtle or absent visible nail changes. Although surgical excision remains the standard treatment, incomplete removal may result in recurrence and postoperative nail deformity. Here, we report a presumed subungual glomus tumor in an older adult managed with Nd:YAG laser, supported by clinical and ultrasonographic follow-up.

Case Presentation

An 83-year-old female presented with a 17-year history of severe, paroxysmal pain localized to a precise subungual point of the right fourth fingernail. The pain was sharp, point-specific, reproducible with minimal pressure, and exacerbated by cold exposure, impairing activities. Her medical history included hypertension treated with valsartan.

Physical examination revealed focal subungual tenderness without nail plate pigmentation or matrix-related dystrophy. Mild onychoschizia with limited distal onycholysis (~10% of the nail plate) and subtle localized erythema were observed (Figure 1A). Targeted pressure elicited severe pain, 9/10 on the visual analog scale (VAS). No sign of infection or inflammatory disease was present. This constellation was highly suggestive of a subungual glomus tumor.

A) Baseline clinical photograph of the fourth digit of the right hand showing onychoschizia and distal onycholysis involving approximately 10% of the nail plate, accompanied by erythema affecting the middle third of the nail; B) Clinical photograph at 8-month follow-up demonstrating complete resolution of the erythema. Onychoschizia and distal onycholysis remain unchanged.

High-frequency ultrasonography with Doppler analysis using an 18-MHz probe demonstrated a well-circumscribed, oval-shaped hypoechoic subungual nodule in the mid-to-distal nail bed, measuring 3.8 × 1.9 × 2.6 mm, with subtle scalloping of the distal phalanx (Figure 2A). Color and pulsed Doppler imaging revealed marked intralesional hypervascularity, with a peak systolic velocity of 60.0 cm/s, consistent with a high-flow vascular lesion characteristic of glomus tumors (Figure 2B–C). A) Pretreatment longitudinal high-frequency ultrasound of the nail bed demonstrating a well-defined hypoechoic nodule located in the distal portion of the nail bed, with subtle scalloping of the underlying distal phalanx. Yellow calipers indicate the dimensions of the lesion; B) Pretreatment color Doppler ultrasound showing marked intralesional hypervascularity within the subungual nodule (white arrow); C) Pre-treatment spectral Doppler analysis demonstrating a high-flow arterial pattern with a peak systolic velocity of 60.0 cm/s, a finding characteristic of glomus tumors; D) Follow-up longitudinal high-frequency ultrasound after Cynergy Multiplex Nd:YAG laser treatment demonstrating a small residual hypoechoic focus at the site of the previous lesion (white outline); E) Follow-up color Doppler ultrasound after treatment showing marked reduction in lesion size and vascularity, with only minimal residual flow (white arrow); F) Follow-up longitudinal high-frequency ultrasonography after treatment demonstrating markedly reduced intralesional flow on spectral Doppler analysis, with a peak systolic velocity of 7.95 cm/s, consistent with normalization of vascular dynamics.

Given the clinical-radiological suspicion, the patient’s age, and preference for a minimally invasive approach, histopathological confirmation was not pursued, and the lesion was managed as a presumed subungual glomus tumor. Treatment was performed under digital nerve block using Nd:YAG laser (7-mm spot size, 20-ms pulse duration, 85 mJ/cm2, four pulses).

At one month, the patient reported marked improvement, with VAS reduced to 1/10. At eight months, complete resolution of erythema and absence of pain on palpation were observed (Figure 1B). Follow-up ultrasonography showed near-complete resolution, with a residual hypoechoic focus (2 × 1 × 2 mm) and markedly reduced vascularity (peak systolic velocity 7.95 cm/s) without abnormal arterial waveforms (Figure 2D–F), correlating with sustained clinical remission.

Conclusion

Surgical excision remains the gold standard for glomus tumors; however, recurrence and nail deformity may occur, particularly in older patients [4,5]. Nd:YAG laser therapy represents a minimally invasive option that induces selective vascular coagulation while preserving structures and may be appropriate for selected patients who decline surgery or present increased surgical risk. [6]

In this case, dermatological ultrasound functioned as a supportive diagnostic and follow-up tool, demonstrating characteristic vascular features and enabling noninvasive monitoring of treatment response. [1,2] Although histopathological confirmation was not obtained, the clinical-radiological correlation and sustained remission support the presumptive diagnosis and therapeutic outcome. Further studies are needed to better define the role of laser-based therapies in subungual glomus tumors.
