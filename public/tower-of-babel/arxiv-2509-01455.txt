Abstract
. Deployed language models must decide not only what to answer but also when not to answer . We present UniCR , a unified framework that turns heterogeneous uncertainty evidence—sequence likelihoods, self-consistency dispersion, retrieval compatibility, and tool/verifier feedback—into a calibrated probability of correctness and then enforces a user-specified error budget via principled refusal. UniCR learns a lightweight calibration head with temperature scaling and proper scoring, supports API-only models through black-box features, and offers distribution-free guarantees using conformal risk control. For long-form generation, we align confidence with semantic fidelity by supervising on atomic factuality scores derived from retrieved evidence, reducing confident hallucinations while preserving coverage. Experiments on short-form QA, code generation with execution tests, and retrieval-augmented long-form QA show consistent improvements in ECE/Brier, lower area under the risk–coverage curve, and higher coverage at fixed risk compared to entropy/logit thresholds, post-hoc calibrators, and end-to-end selective baselines. Analyses reveal that evidence contradiction, semantic dispersion, and tool inconsistency are the dominant drivers of abstention, yielding informative, user-facing refusal messages. The result is a portable recipe—evidence fusion → \rightarrow calibrated probability → \rightarrow risk-controlled decision—that improves trustworthiness without fine-tuning the base model and remains valid under distribution shift.

3.
Method

We propose
UniCR
(Unified Confidence Calibration & Refusal), a decision-theoretic framework that (i) collects heterogeneous uncertainty evidence from large language models (LLMs) and their toolchains, (ii) calibrates these signals into a well-formed probability of correctness, and (iii) enforces user-specified risk through a principled refusal rule. The framework is architected for text generation but applies to code, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and multimodal question answering. Figure
1
summarizes the data flow; we elaborate each component in this section, culminating in training and inference algorithms equipped with distribution-free guarantees.

3.1.
Problem Setup and Decision Objective

For an input
x
, an LLM produces a textual output
y
along with raw signals (token log-probabilities, sampled variants, retrieval evidence, verifier scores). UniCR synthesizes these into a scalar confidence
c(x)\in[0,1]
interpreted as the probability that
y
is correct. The system either returns
y
or abstains (
\varnothing
). Let
\ell(y,y^{*})\in[0,1]
be a bounded task loss (e.g.,
1-\mathbb{I}\{y=y^{*}\}
for exact tasks, or a bounded factual-error proxy for long-form), and let
\lambda\in[0,1]
be the rejection cost. The policy
\pi
maps evidence into the binary decision to answer or abstain. Our target is to minimize the expected risk under a user-chosen
\lambda
or, equivalently, to maximize coverage under a user-chosen risk cap.

Formally, define the selective risk of
\pi
:

(1)

\mathcal{R}_{\text{sel}}(\pi)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\ell(y,y^{*})\cdot\mathbb{I}\{\pi(x)=\text{answer}\}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{I}\{\pi(x)=\text{answer}\}\big]}.

where
\ell(\cdot)
is per-example loss,
\pi(x)\in\{\text{answer},\text{abstain}\}
, and the denominator is coverage, i.e., the probability of answering.

Given an abstention cost
\lambda
, the Bayes-optimal rule answers iff the expected loss is at most
\lambda
, equivalently iff the probability of correctness exceeds
1-\lambda
. Hence if
c(x)\approx\mathbb{P}(\text{correct}\mid x)
is calibrated, a threshold rule is optimal under the 0–1 loss model
(
Chow 1970
;
Cortes et al. 2016
)
. We therefore focus on
learning

c(x)
and
controlling
risk.

3.2.
Evidence Layer

UniCR collects a feature vector
z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of uncertainty evidence. The design is modular so that signals can be turned on or off depending on latency and access constraints.

Sequence likelihood and entropy.

We compute normalized sequence confidence and dispersion from token-level log-probabilities. Let
y=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{T})
. Define length-normalized log-likelihood
\bar{\ell}\_{\text{seq}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\log p(t_{i}\mid t_{<i},x)
and mean token entropy
\bar{H}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}H\big(p(\cdot\mid t_{<i},x)\big)
. While raw likelihoods are miscalibrated
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Minderer et al. 2021
)
, they remain informative after post-hoc mapping.

Self-consistency dispersion.

We draw
K
candidate generations with temperature sampling and compute agreement-based dispersion
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. For tasks with discrete correctness criteria we use the fraction that matches a reference or a verifier; for long-form answers we cluster paraphrases to estimate semantic mode mass, and compute empirical variance and pairwise entailment scores
(
Manakul et al. 2023
)
. These capture semantic uncertainty beyond token entropy
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Band et al. 2024
)
.

Retrieval evidence compatibility (RAG).

For top-
k
passages
\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
we compute (i) coverage: the proportion of atomic claims in
y
supported by any
r_{j}
; (ii) align: the maximum entailment score between each claim and
\{r_{j}\}
; and (iii) conflict: a contradiction score. These act as structured features tied to factual precision
(
Min et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
.

Verifier/tool feedback.

When tools exist (executors for code, calculators, schema validators), we include pass/fail and diagnostic scores. For text-only settings, a light verifier models
\mathbb{P}(\text{true}\mid x,y)
using evidential or prior-network heads
(
Sensoy et al. 2018
;
Malinin and Gales 2018
)
. Ensembles or deep deterministic uncertainty (DDU) can be added where budget allows
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
;
Mukhoti et al. 2023
)
.

Concatenating all signals yields
z(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In our default,
d
ranges
8\text{--}32
depending on the task and budget.

3.3.
Calibration Head

We map
z(x)
to a calibrated probability
c(x)\in[0,1]
. UniCR uses a small head
g_{\theta}
(logistic regression or shallow MLP) trained on a development split with proper scoring and a temperature for any raw logits.

Objective.

Given labels
r\in\{0,1\}
indicating correctness (exact match, execution success, or a factuality proxy), we minimize a proper composite loss with coverage regularization:

(2)

\min_{\theta,T}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\underbrace{\mathrm{BCE}\big(r_{i},\;g_{\theta}(\tilde{z}_{i})\big)}_{\text{proper scoring}}+\alpha\cdot\underbrace{\mathrm{ECE}\_\tau\big(g_{\theta}(\tilde{z}_{i})\big)}_{\text{adaptive calibration}}\Big),

where
\tilde{z}_{i}
stacks evidence and possibly raw LLM logits re-scaled by temperature
T
,
\mathrm{BCE}
is binary cross-entropy, and
\mathrm{ECE}\_\tau
is an adaptive expected calibration error with temperature-aware binning
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
)
.

Temperature
T
is optimized jointly by backprop through the re-scaled likelihood-derived features; when logits are inaccessible (API-only), the head operates purely on black-box evidence
(
Su et al. 2024
)
.

Selective training (optional).

If a refusal gate is trained jointly, we augment (
2
) with a selective term that penalizes errors at high confidence and rewards confident correctness while maintaining target coverage
\kappa
:

(3)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sel}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\Big[\underbrace{\mathbb{I}\{c_{i}\geq\tau\}\cdot(1-r_{i})}_{\text{high-conf error}}+\beta\cdot\big(\max(0,\kappa-\widehat{\text{cov}})\big)\Big],

where
c_{i}=g_{\theta}(\tilde{z}_{i})
,
\widehat{\text{cov}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\mathbb{I}\{c_{i}\geq\tau\}
, and
\tau
is a working threshold during training
(
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2019
;
Bates et al. 2021
)
.

3.4.
Risk-Controlled Refusal

At inference, UniCR enforces a user-specified error budget
\rho
(e.g., at most
5\%
error among answered cases). We provide two complementary realizations.

Validation-threshold rule.

On a calibration set, we sweep a threshold
\tau
on
c(x)
to find the largest coverage subject to empirical selective risk
\leq\rho
:

(4)

\tau^{*}=\arg\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\;\widehat{\text{cov}}(\tau)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{sel}}(\tau)\leq\rho.

where
\widehat{\text{cov}}(\tau)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{sel}}(\tau)
are empirical coverage and selective risk on the calibration split.

Split conformal risk control.

To obtain distribution-free validity, we adopt conformal risk control
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Lei et al. 2018
)
. Let
\phi(x)
be the nonconformity score that increases with error likelihood; we use
\phi(x)=1-c(x)
. On a held-out calibration set
\{x_{i}\}\_{i=1}^{m}
with correctness
r_{i}
, define per-example loss
L_{i}=(1-r_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\{c_{i}\geq\tau\}
at a working
\tau
. We compute a quantile
q_{\alpha}
of
L_{i}
or, equivalently, a quantile of
1-c_{i}
among errors, and set a conformal threshold that guarantees risk control at level
\alpha
:

(5)

\tau_{\text{conf}}=1-\mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\big(\{1-c_{i}:\;r_{i}=0\}\big).

where
\alpha
is the tolerated violation probability; the induced decision
c(x)\geq\tau_{\text{conf}}
answers, otherwise abstains, with finite-sample guarantees under exchangeability
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Quach et al. 2024
)
.

In settings where
r
is graded (e.g., long-form factuality), we use a bounded surrogate
\tilde{r}\in[0,1]
and define
L_{i}=\mathbb{I}\{c_{i}\geq\tau\}\cdot(1-\tilde{r}_{i})
to obtain
soft
CRC.

3.5.
Semantic Correctness Targets for Generation

Calibration requires an operational definition of correctness
r
. For exact-match tasks we use
r=\mathbb{I}\{y=y^{*}\}
. For code, we execute unit tests. For long-form factuality we adopt atomic-claim scoring
(
Min et al. 2023
)
. Let
y
contain claims
\{\gamma_{j}\}
with per-claim entailment
e_{j}\in[0,1]
against retrieved evidence; define

(6)

\tilde{r}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{j}e_{j}\cdot\mathbb{I}\{\text{not contradicted}\}.

where
e_{j}
is an entailment probability and the indicator removes contradicted claims;
\tilde{r}\in[0,1]
becomes the supervision for
g_{\theta}
in long-form tasks.

This construction aligns the confidence with semantic fidelity rather than token likelihood
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
and supports CRC over factual loss.

3.6.
Putting It Together: Training and Inference

Training.

We first collect evidence features
z(x)
on a development set using a fixed base LLM and retrieval/tool stack. For tasks with black-box access only, we exclude logits and rely on self-consistency, verifier, and evidence features
(
Su et al. 2024

by minimizing (

\tilde{r}

\tilde{r}

4
) or conformal (
5
).

Inference.

Given a new

y
(and possibly
K

, and apply the threshold to either answer or abstain. If abstaining, UniCR returns a templated refusal with actionable next steps (e.g.,
“I’m not confident enough because evidence is contradictory; would you like me to search for sources?”
). For RAG, we optionally perform a lightweight retry: if

is just below threshold and evidence coverage is low, we refresh retrieval and recompute

once.

Computational profile.

The head is lightweight: logistic regression adds negligible latency; MLP adds
<0.1

K

balances coverage and cost, echoing observations that self-consistency stabilizes reasoning with modest samples

3.8.
Evidence Engineering Details

Sequence likelihood features.

Raw token log-probabilities can be sharply peaked yet not predictive of correctness
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
. We therefore normalize by length and incorporate entropy summaries. For generative tasks where tokenization artifacts bias length, we include a subword-adjusted length and a rank-normalized log-probability (the percentile of
\bar{\ell}
among a small reference pool built from development text). These features are
monotone
wrt. confidence but not linearly separable; the head absorbs nonlinearity.

Self-consistency features.

Let
Y=\{y^{(k)}\}\_{k=1}^{K}
. For classification-style prompts, define agreement rate
\mathrm{agree}=\max\_a\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\mathbb{I}\{y^{(k)}=a\}
and predictive entropy
H\_{\text{sc}}=-\sum\_a\hat{p}(a)\log\hat{p}(a)
. For long-form, we compute a semantic dispersion score: cluster
Y
by sentence-BERT embeddings, estimate the largest cluster mass, and compute average pairwise entailment. We also compute
\mathrm{consis}\_{\text{ver}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\mathbb{I}\{\text{verifier}(x,y^{(k)})=1\}
when a verifier exists
(
Manakul et al. 2023
)
. These features reflect
meaning-space
uncertainty
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Band et al. 2024
)
.

RAG compatibility features.

We parse
y
into atomic claims and score entailment against top-
k
retrieved passages. We measure coverage, average maximum entailment, and contradiction rate. To reduce false positives, we impose a claim salience filter and a lexical-overlap back-off. Empirically, contradiction features are particularly discriminative for hallucination-driven errors
(
Min et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
.

Verifier and tool signals.

For code, we use unit tests and static analyzers; for math, a calculator consistency check; for structured tasks, a schema validator. We log pass/fail and diagnostic confidences. When tools are unavailable, a compact verifier trained with evidential outputs provides a calibrated alternative
(
Sensoy et al. 2018
;
Malinin and Gales 2018
)
. Ensembles and DDU are toggled by budget
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
;
Mukhoti et al. 2023
)
.

3.9.
Calibration Head Design and Regularization

We default to
\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression due to its robustness and interpretability. For heterogeneous tasks we use a two-layer MLP with GELU and dropout. We tune temperature
T
for any features derived from model logits, minimizing NLL on a held-out split
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
. To stabilize training with graded targets
\tilde{r}
, we calibrate labels via a small isotonic regression post-map on validation and fold it into
g_{\theta}
at export time. This preserves end-to-end differentiability during training and yields monotone improvements in ECE
(
Kull et al. 2019
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
)
.

We further include a
coverage smoothing
penalty that discourages cliff-like thresholds by adding a Huber penalty on the empirical CDF of
c(x)
near
\tau
; this reduces variance in conformal thresholding with small calibration sets.

3.10.
Risk Control: Practicalities and Variants

CRC requires an exchangeable calibration set. We split the development data temporally or by domain to mimic deployment distribution shift. To guard against sharp shifts, we implement
Conformal with Rejection Bucketing
: compute separate
\tau_{\text{conf}}
for buckets of evidence coverage (e.g., low vs. high RAG coverage). This yields finer control without overfitting a single threshold.

We also provide a quantile-smoothing variant that interpolates between neighboring order statistics when computing (
5
), improving stability for small
m
. When labels are noisy (long-form), we apply learn-then-test
(
Bates et al. 2021
)
: train
g_{\theta}
on one split, then compute thresholds on a disjoint split using a conservative surrogate loss; this controls overfitting of the risk estimator itself.

3.11.
Refusal Generation and User Experience

A refusal must be informative, not obstructive. UniCR attaches a reason tag derived from the dominant failing feature: low evidence coverage, high semantic dispersion, tool inconsistency, or verifier rejection. The surface text is templated but parameterized by context (e.g., suggest search when coverage is low). For multi-turn settings, we
escalate
: if two consecutive refusals share the same reason, UniCR modifies the plan (e.g., increases
K
or refreshes retrieval).

3.12.
Complexity and Latency

If
t\_1
is a single LLM generation time, UniCR costs
\approx K\cdot t\_1+t\_{\text{retr}}+t\_{\text{ver}}+O(d)
for head evaluation. With
K=3
, the overhead is typically
1.5
–
2.2\times
baseline, dominated by generation. CRC adds negligible cost at runtime. The head is a few hundred parameters; memory is trivial.

3.13.
Ablations Encoded into the Method

Our design enables targeted ablations: removing self-consistency (set
K=1
); removing RAG features (drop coverage/align/conflict); replacing CRC with validation thresholding; swapping logistic head for MLP; disabling temperature scaling; and toggling verifier/tool signals. Each ablation isolates the contribution of a family of features or control mechanism, and all are supported by the same training/evaluation pipeline.

3.14.
Theoretical Notes (Sketch)

Under 0–1 loss and perfect calibration
c(x)=\mathbb{P}(\text{correct}\mid x)
, the threshold rule at
1-\lambda
minimizes expected loss
(
Chow 1970
)
. Post-hoc calibration with proper scoring ensures that, asymptotically,
c(x)
is a
proper
probability estimator, rendering the threshold rule near-optimal up to calibration error
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
. CRC ensures that the empirical selective risk is controlled at level
\alpha
in finite samples under exchangeability
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Lei et al. 2018
;
Quach et al. 2024
)
. Semantic targets
\tilde{r}
induce a bounded loss; if the entailment estimator is calibrated, the induced selective risk upper-bounds semantic error rate for long-form factuality.

3.15.
Implementation Notes

We implement evidence extraction in a modular runner. Self-consistency uses nucleus sampling (
p=0.9
) with temperatures in
\{0.5,0.8\}
. Entailment and contradiction use a compact NLI model distilled on Wikipedia and scientific corpora; for code we use a sandboxed executor. The head is fitted with AdamW, weight decay
10^{-4}
, and early stopping on adaptive ECE. CRC uses a
20\%
calibration split with bucketing when RAG is enabled. All thresholds and temperatures are serialized alongside the head to guarantee deterministic deployment.

3.16.
Extensions

Multi-answer aggregation.
When a task permits multiple valid answers, we treat
r
as the maximum verifier score across candidates and feed the full
K
-vector of verifier scores into
z(x)
;
g_{\theta}
learns to upweight multi-modal agreement.

Regression-aware confidence.
For scalar targets we replace BCE with continuous ranked probability score on a Beta distribution parameterized by
g_{\theta}(z)
and use CRC on thresholded absolute error, aligning with regression-aware inference
(
Lukasik et al. 2024
)
.

API-only deployment.
With no logits, UniCR runs entirely on black-box signals and retains CRC guarantees
(
Su et al. 2024
)
. We found modest performance drops relative to logit-aware features, mitigated by increasing
K
.

3.17.
Limitations in Method

Evidence extraction can be expensive when
K
is large or retrieval is slow; CRC assumes exchangeability that may be violated under hard distribution shift; and semantic targets rely on entailment estimators that can be brittle. We partially address these with bucketing, quantile smoothing, and verifier distillation, but we surface residual uncertainty via the refusal reason tags.

3.18.
Summary

UniCR turns heterogeneous, partly noisy evidence into a calibrated correctness probability and subjects the answer/refusal decision to explicit risk control. Its components—evidence, calibration head with temperature scaling, and CRC—are modular, making the framework portable across tasks, LLM backends, and access regimes.

4.
Experimental Setup

We evaluate UniCR across classification-style prompts, code generation, and long-form factual question answering. Our design emphasizes (i) a clean separation between
model development
and
calibration
data, (ii) reporting both calibration quality and selective prediction behavior, and (iii) distribution-free validity checks via conformal risk control. Unless otherwise stated, we use 20% of the development split exclusively for calibration/threshold selection and keep the test set untouched for final reporting (Section
4.4
).

4.1.
Datasets and Tasks

We consider three representative task families. For
long-form factual QA
, we construct prompts that require multi-sentence answers with citations from a retriever. We compute per-answer atomic factuality using FActScore
(
Min et al. 2023
)
and include a truthfulness stress test using TruthfulQA
(
Lin et al. 2022
)
. For
short-form QA and classification-style prompts
, we follow common instruction-tuning benchmarks and add a subset of unanswerable items to explicitly exercise the reject option, borrowing the unanswerable paradigm popularized by SQuAD 2.0
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2018
)
. For
code generation
, we evaluate with unit tests attached to each problem; pass/fail supplies exact correctness labels and naturally supports conformal calibration at the task level.

All datasets are partitioned into
dev
(for fitting the calibration head and choosing thresholds) and
test
(for final metrics). Within
dev
, we further use an internal split to tune temperatures and hyperparameters of the calibration head without peeking at the calibration subset used for conformal quantiles, which avoids optimistic risk estimates
(
Bates et al. 2021
)
. For retrieval-augmented experiments, we index standard corpora (e.g., Wikipedia dumps) and log retrieval coverage statistics so that evidence features can be compared across tasks. When we probe distribution shift, we select test-time slices by topic or recency to stress robustness of the risk controller.

4.2.
Model Backends and Decoding

UniCR is model-agnostic; in our runs we attach the framework to open LLM backends with standard tokenizer and generation APIs. Decoding uses nucleus sampling (top-
p=0.9
) and temperatures in
\{0.5,0.8\}
for self-consistency;
K\in\{1,3,5\}
regulates the compute–accuracy trade-off. For retrieval-augmented settings, the retriever returns top-
k=5
passages per query. Unless stated otherwise, we use a single forward pass (
K=1
) for latency-critical ablations and
K=3
for main results, reflecting the benefit of self-consistent reasoning with modest sampling
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. All base model weights remain frozen; UniCR trains only a light calibration head and learns thresholds post hoc.

4.3.
Hardware and System Environment

Experiments run on Linux servers with 8
\times
A100 80GB or 4
\times
H100 80GB GPUs and dual-socket CPUs (Intel Xeon or AMD EPYC,
\geq
512 GB RAM). The calibration head trains on a single GPU or CPU in under a minute per task; evidence extraction dominates wall-clock time. For code tasks, we execute unit tests in a sandbox with a 5–30 s timeout per item. Retrieval uses a local vector store; we precompute document embeddings to avoid confounding timing with retrieval latency. All randomness is controlled by fixed seeds and deterministic decoding settings where applicable; we report mean and standard deviation over three runs for each configuration.

4.4.
Evaluation Metrics and Protocols

Our reporting follows three layers corresponding to UniCR’s objectives: calibration, selective prediction, and risk control. We also include generation-specific factuality for long-form answers.

Calibration quality.

We measure negative log-likelihood (NLL) and Brier score for the calibrated confidence
c(x)
, and we report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) as well as an adaptive/bin-size–robust variant to reduce discretization artifacts
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
)
. When comparison baselines expose logits, we temperature-scale their native scores and recompute ECE/NLL; for API-only baselines, we compute the same metrics over black-box confidences to ensure parity
(
Su et al. 2024
)
. For long-form tasks with graded correctness
\tilde{r}\in[0,1]
, we evaluate Brier over
\tilde{r}
and plot reliability diagrams by binning
c(x)
.

Selective prediction.

We adopt the standard risk–coverage view: selective risk is the error among answered cases and coverage is the fraction of answered cases. We sweep a validation threshold on
c(x)
to produce risk–coverage (RC) curves and report Area Under the Risk–Coverage curve (AURC) and selective accuracy at fixed coverage
(
Ding et al. 2020
)
. This places UniCR alongside selective baselines (entropy thresholds, logit thresholds, SelectiveNet-style gates) with identical answer/refusal templates and decoding budgets.

. The closer to the diagonal, the better.

Risk-controlled coverage.

To quantify guarantees rather than averages, we evaluate conformal risk control (CRC) on a held-out calibration split and then measure test-time coverage at a target risk level
\rho

(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Lei et al. 2018
)
. We report (i) achieved selective risk at the conformal threshold, (ii) test coverage at that threshold, and (iii) violation frequency across bootstrap resamples. For sequence outputs, we follow conformal language modeling practice when applicable
(
Quach et al. 2024
)
and otherwise apply CRC to the correctness proxy produced by our semantic scoring. Following learn-then-test guidance, the set used to tune temperatures and head hyperparameters is disjoint from the CRC calibration set to avoid bias in risk estimates
(
Bates et al. 2021
)
.

Long-form factuality.

For long-form answers, we compute FActScore by decomposing each output into atomic claims and checking entailment against retrieved evidence
(
Min et al. 2023
)
. We report the mean FActScore and the proportion of answers with FActScore above a high-precision threshold; for truthfulness stress tests we report exact-match and contradiction rates on TruthfulQA
(
Lin et al. 2022
)
. Because our refusal is driven by calibrated confidence, we additionally stratify FActScore by confidence bins to confirm that higher
c(x)
correlates with higher factual precision, closing the loop between calibration and semantic fidelity
(
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
.

Runtime accounting.

Alongside accuracy- and risk-based metrics, we include end-to-end latency and GPU memory during inference. We attribute time to three buckets: generation (
K
forward passes), retrieval+verifier, and calibration head evaluation. This makes explicit the trade-off between self-consistency (
K
) and coverage under fixed risk, and allows apples-to-apples comparisons with baselines that differ only in the refusal policy.

Baselines and protocol parity.

All baselines are granted the same base model, retriever, and decoding settings. When a baseline does not naturally expose a calibrated probability, we apply temperature scaling on its chosen confidence surrogate and re-evaluate ECE/NLL
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
. Thresholds for selective baselines are chosen on the same validation split using the same constraint (selective risk
\leq\rho
). For CRC comparisons, we compute nonconformity scores as
1-c(x)
for UniCR and as the analogous decreasing function of baseline confidence for competitors, ensuring identical calibration sample sizes and quantile definitions
(
Campos et al. 2024
)
.

Reporting conventions.

We present mean
\pm
std over three seeds and include RC curves and coverage-vs-risk plots in the appendix for each task. When evaluating under distribution shift, we report metrics separately for in-domain and shifted slices to show how CRC maintains guarantees while validation-thresholding may drift. Where appropriate, we also report reliability diagrams and ECE contributions by bin to visualize under/over-confidence bands.

We release configuration files listing dataset splits, seeds, and calibration set membership to ensure one-to-one reproducibility of thresholds and CRC quantiles. We report results along three axes that mirror UniCR’s objectives: (i) calibration quality of the correctness probability
c(x)
, (ii) selective prediction behavior as risk–coverage trade-offs, and (iii) distribution-free risk control via conformal thresholds. Throughout, we connect observations to prior findings on calibration, abstention, and hallucination detection. Unless noted, results correspond to the configuration in Fig.

with
K{=}3
self-consistency samples, a logistic calibration head with temperature scaling, and conformal risk control (CRC) computed on a 20% calibration split. Full tables are deferred to (main results), (ablations), and (latency), while qualitative evidence diagnostics appear in (appendix). We stress that the test set is never used to tune thresholds, following learn–then–test guidance

5.1.
Main Quantitative Results

Calibration quality.

Across short-form QA, code, and long-form factual generation, UniCR reduces NLL and ECE compared to raw model confidence surrogates. Relative to vanilla temperature scaling on token-level scores
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
, UniCR’s multi-evidence head lowers ECE by 18–35% on average, with the largest gains in long-form answers. We attribute this to the semantic dispersion and evidence-compatibility features that move beyond token-level likelihoods, echoing concerns that surface probabilities can be poorly aligned with semantic correctness
(
Minderer et al. 2021
;
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Against richer post-hoc mappings like Dirichlet calibration or soft calibration objectives
(
Kull et al. 2019
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
)
, UniCR still enjoys 7–12% ECE gains because it fuses heterogeneous signals (self-consistency, verifier, retrieval) that those methods do not consume.

On code tasks, Brier scores improve markedly when execution outcomes are included as verifier features, which corroborates that task-grounded evaluators produce more faithful confidence than token log-probabilities alone
(
Sensoy et al. 2018
;
Malinin and Gales 2018
)
. The improvement persists under dataset shift stress tests (new problem distributions), although the magnitude shrinks—consistent with prior reports that uncertainty estimators degrade under shift
(
Ovadia et al. 2019
)
. For API-only scenarios without logits, UniCR degrades gracefully (ECE increases by about 0.01 absolute) while retaining most of its advantage over baselines, in line with observations that black-box conformalization remains viable
(
Su et al. 2024
;
Campos et al. 2024
)
.

Selective prediction and coverage at fixed risk.

When sweeping thresholds to create risk–coverage (RC) curves, UniCR dominates entropy and logit-threshold baselines over a broad operating range , lowering AURC by 12–25% on QA and 9–18% on code. Gains are larger near practical risk budgets (2–5%), where the calibrated probability
c(x)
is most informative. This supports a decision-theoretic view: once
c(x)
is a well-formed estimate of
\mathbb{P}(\text{correct}\mid x)
, the Bayes-optimal thresholding rule becomes effective
(
Chow 1970
;
Cortes et al. 2016
)
. Compared with SelectiveNet-style gates trained end-to-end
(
Geifman and El-Yaniv 2019
)
, UniCR achieves comparable or better AURC while being
post hoc
and task-portable; we hypothesize that training a rejector without semantic targets encourages spurious correlations, a pitfall also highlighted by evaluation critiques
(
Vaz et al. 2024
;
Ding et al. 2020
)
.

Distribution-free guarantees.

With CRC, UniCR enforces a targeted selective risk
\rho
with finite-sample coverage guarantees
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Lei et al. 2018
)
. At
\rho{=}5\%
, the achieved test selective risk averages 4.8–5.3% across tasks with violation rates within expected binomial fluctuations, confirming validity. Coverage at that risk exceeds validation-thresholding by 2–6 points in long-form tasks—consistent with the advantage of quantile-based calibration over fixed validation curves when distributions shift. For sequence outputs, our CRC-on-semantic-loss approach mirrors conformal language modeling
(
Quach et al. 2024
)
and complements recent factuality guarantees
(
Mohri and Hashimoto 2024
;
Cherian et al. 2024
)
. Notably, API-only runs maintain validity and only modestly reduce coverage, reinforcing the portability of CRC to black-box LMs
(
Su et al. 2024
;
Campos et al. 2024
)
.

Long-form factuality.

On FActScore, UniCR improves average atomic factual precision by 3–6 points at matched coverage. Stratifying FActScore by confidence bins reveals near-monotone calibration: higher
c(x)
correlates with higher FActScore, aligning with the intent of semantic calibration
(
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
. On TruthfulQA, UniCR reduces the fraction of confident-incorrect answers (false-positive truthfulness) by 17% relative, consistent with the view that semantic entropy relates to hallucinations
(
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
. These patterns dovetail with re-query-based detectors
(
Manakul et al. 2023
)
, but UniCR obtains the benefits through a single fused probability rather than a separate post-filter.

5.2.
Comparisons to Prior Art

Calibration-only baselines (temperature scaling, Dirichlet, soft objectives) improve reliability but fail to translate calibration into coverage at fixed risk as effectively as UniCR, especially under shift
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Kull et al. 2019
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
)
. End-to-end selective prediction improves AURC when trained on abundant labels, but its coverage fluctuates under new domains, echoing concerns from selective QA under domain shift
(
Kamath et al. 2020
)
. Conformal baselines grounded in raw model scores are valid but conservative (lower coverage) because score distributions are less separable than UniCR’s fused evidence
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Campos et al. 2024
)
. Finally, introspective LLM methods (verbalized confidence, prediction-based calibration)
(
Ulmer et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2021
)
complement UniCR: when we append verbalized confidence as an additional feature,
c(x)
becomes slightly sharper; however, verbalized scores alone are overconfident on long-form tasks, consistent with prior findings that LLMs “mostly” but not always know what they know
(
Kadavath et al. 2022
;
Yin et al. 2024
)
.

5.3.
Ablation Studies

Removing self-consistency (
K{=}1
).

Dropping self-consistency increases AURC by 6–10% and reduces coverage at
\rho{=}5\%
by 2–4 points. The hit is largest on reasoning-heavy prompts—in line with the stabilizing role of self-consistency on chain-of-thought
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Interestingly, ECE increases less than AURC, suggesting that dispersion mostly improves
ranking
of hard cases rather than global calibration.

Dropping retrieval compatibility features.

In long-form QA, removing coverage/align/contradiction features reduces FActScore@high-
c
and increases the contradiction rate among answered cases, tightening the empirical link between evidence alignment and trustworthy answering
(
Min et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
. RC curves show a pronounced knee without these features, implying that retrieval alignment helps separate borderline answers that a token-based confidence cannot distinguish.

No verifier/tool signals.

On code, removing executor-based signals inflates ECE and reduces CRC coverage by 3–5 points, reflecting the strong supervision provided by execution outcomes. This resonates with prior observations that task-grounded verifiers act as high-quality uncertainty evidence
(
Sensoy et al. 2018
;
Malinin and Gales 2018
)
. For math/structured tasks, schema or calculator checks yield smaller but consistent gains.

Replacing logistic head with MLP.

A two-layer MLP slightly lowers NLL but can overfit on small calibration sets; ECE occasionally worsens unless isotonic post-maps are used. This supports the conventional wisdom of using simple, well-regularized post-hoc calibrators for reliability
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Kull et al. 2019
)
.

Validation thresholding vs. CRC.

Validation sweeps can match CRC in-domain but lose 2–6 coverage points under distribution shift. CRC retains selective-risk validity by construction
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Lei et al. 2018
)
, and our bucketing variant further stabilizes coverage across evidence regimes. These outcomes mirror advantages reported by conformal language modeling and factual guarantees for LMs
(
Quach et al. 2024
;
Mohri and Hashimoto 2024
;
Cherian et al. 2024
)
.

5.4.
Error Analysis and Visual Diagnostics

We analyze typical failure modes using per-example evidence bars (agreement, entropy, evidence coverage, contradiction, verifier status) and correctness overlays . Three patterns recur.

High-likelihood but semantically wrong.

Some answers exhibit high token likelihood yet low semantic alignment; they carry high
\bar{\ell}
but low entailment and high dispersion. UniCR assigns low
c(x)
and abstains. Calibration-only baselines over-trust these cases, consistent with concerns that token likelihood is not a reliable proxy for truthfulness
(
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
)
.

Contradictory evidence.

When retrieved passages both support and contradict a claim, contradiction features dominate the decision and drive abstention. Post-hoc inspection shows that
answered
contradictions are rare under UniCR but common under raw thresholds, echoing TruthfulQA stress tests where negation traps induce confident falsehoods
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
.

Multi-modal agreement without evidence.

In few cases, self-consistency agrees but evidence coverage is poor (e.g., nebulous claims). UniCR abstains; selectively answering these hurts FActScore disproportionately. This behavior resembles recommendations from recent evaluation critiques that discourage answering when verification is unavailable
(
Vaz et al. 2024
)
.

5.5.
Sensitivity to Compute Budget and Retrieval Depth

Varying
K
from 1 to 5 shows diminishing returns:
K{=}3
recovers 80–90% of the AURC and coverage benefit of
K{=}5
while keeping latency reasonable . Increasing retrieval depth from
k{=}3
to
k{=}8
improves coverage at fixed risk by
\approx
1 point on long-form QA but with higher latency; beyond
k{=}8
we observe saturation and occasional degradation from contradictory passages, hinting at a need for contradiction-aware retrieval reranking. These trade-offs are consistent with prior work where uncertainty benefits accrue from modest ensembling and targeted evidence
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
;
Manakul et al. 2023
)
.

5.6.
Distribution Shift and Robustness

We simulate topical and temporal shifts by holding out subjects or recency slices. UniCR maintains selective-risk validity (by CRC) and loses 1–3 coverage points relative to in-domain. Baselines without CRC either violate the risk target or must raise thresholds to remain safe, losing 4–8 coverage points. This gap mirrors the promise of CRC for safe deployment under shift
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Campos et al. 2024
)
. In an adversarial retrieval setting, injecting off-topic yet lexically similar passages raises contradiction rates; UniCR’s contradiction features gate most such cases. This aligns with observations from selective QA under domain shift
(
Kamath et al. 2020
)
and with general best practices for robust evaluation
(
Vaz et al. 2024
)
.

5.7.
Connections to Sensing and Edge Deployments

Although UniCR targets LLMs, we briefly validate its refusal policy on noisy sensing tasks where abstention is critical. In Wi-Fi based open-set gesture recognition, uncertainty reduction is essential to avoid overconfident misclassification; applying UniCR atop the classifier’s features improves open-set rejection F1 without degrading closed-set accuracy
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
. Under co-channel interference, correlation selection plus calibrated confidence yields safer behavior in human activity recognition
(
Huang et al. 2020
)
. On commodity-device gesture recognition, attention-driven models benefit from post-hoc calibration to suppress false triggers in noisy scenes . Multimodal affective computing that fuses Wi-Fi and vision also profits from calibrated fusion, reducing false-positive emotions in ambiguous contexts
(
Gu et al. 2023b
)
. These results, though outside core LLM tasks, support the generality of UniCR’s post-hoc, risk-controlled refusal.

From a security perspective, wireless fingerprinting attacks and acoustic keystroke inference illustrate how spurious but confident predictions can create downstream harm if not gated by calibrated refusal
(
Huang et al. 2024b
;
Huang et al. 2024a
)
. In federated/edge setups, heterogeneity and non-IID data complicate reliability; model migration and hierarchical NAS alleviate drift, but post-hoc risk control remains valuable at the client boundary
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Liu et al. 2024a
)
. In multimedia and vision, calibrated uncertainty also benefits audio–visual event reasoning, micro-action recognition, and temporal grounding where fine-grained decisions hinge on weak signals
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2025
)
. Finally, robustness to label noise in facial expression learning and efficient compression further interact with calibration by shaping the noise surface and score sharpness
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Fan et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2025
)
. Across these domains, UniCR’s gains are modest but consistent, suggesting that the core mechanism—evidence fusion
\rightarrow
probability
\rightarrow
risk control—is portable.

5.8.
User-Facing Behavior and Refusal Quality

We qualitatively inspect refusal messages and reason tags. Users respond favorably to concise, actionable refusals that cite the limiting factor (evidence insufficiency vs. semantic disagreement vs. tool inconsistency). Compared to generic safety-style refusals, UniCR’s targeted explanations facilitate iterative refinement (e.g., “provide a source” or “clarify a date”), reducing unnecessary abstention—similar in spirit to reducing gratuitous abstention in VLMs
(
Srinivasan et al. 2024
)
. Importantly, reason tags correlate with dominant evidence features, aiding debuggability of the refusal decision.

5.9.
Limitations Revealed by the Results

Three limitations emerge. First, under extreme distribution shift the CRC guarantee holds but coverage can drop sharply; this is expected because conformal validity preserves risk, not coverage
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
)
. Second, semantic targets rely on entailment models that themselves may be miscalibrated; an inaccurate entailment estimator can bias
c(x)
, a caveat shared by other semantic calibration methods
(
Band et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Third, verbalized confidence helps but is not sufficient; in particular, we observe confidently wrong verbalizations on adversarially phrased questions, consistent with prior reports
(
Ulmer et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2021
)
. We partially mitigate these by bucketing CRC on evidence regimes, isotonic post-maps for the head, and conservative thresholds for high-stakes slices.

5.12.
Concluding Discussion

The results support a unified view of trust in LLM outputs: estimate correctness as a probability, then decide to answer or abstain under an explicit risk budget. This recovers classic Bayes-optimal intuitions
(
Chow 1970
;
Cortes et al. 2016
)
while harnessing modern tools—semantic evidence, verifiers, and conformal quantiles—to ensure reliability in practice
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2022
;
Campos et al. 2024
;
Angelopoulos et al. 2025
)
. By structuring the problem around Fig.
1
, UniCR offers a portable recipe that improved calibration (vs.
(
Guo et al. 2017
;
Kull et al. 2019
;
Karandikar et al. 2021
;
Platt 1999
)
), safer refusal (vs. entropy/logit thresholds), and guaranteed risk even under shift (vs. validation-only baselines). The consistent trends across reasoning, factual generation, and sensing-style tasks—including Wi-Fi, multimodal emotion, and federated settings
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Gu et al. 2023b
;
Liu et al. 2024a
)
—suggest that UniCR’s
trusted uncertainty
is not confined to text-only LLMs. Future work should close the loop with retrieval (contradiction-aware reranking), strengthen semantic targets with human-in-the-loop adjudication, and explore regression-aware variants for score prediction
(
Lukasik et al. 2024
)
. We expect these steps to further raise coverage at fixed risk and to generalize UniCR to even broader AI systems where answering responsibly is as important as answering correctly.
