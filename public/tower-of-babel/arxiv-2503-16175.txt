Abstract
. We study a random walk on the Lie algebra 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ⁡ ( 𝐅 p ) \slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p}) where new elements are produced by randomly applying adjoint operators of two generators. Focusing on the generic case where the generators are selected at random, we analyze the limiting distribution of the random walk and the speed at which it converges to this distribution. These questions reduce to the study of a random walk on a cyclic group. We show that, with high probability, the walk exhibits a pre-cutoff phenomenon after roughly p p steps. Notably, the limiting distribution need not be uniform and it depends on the prime divisors of p − 1 p-1 . Furthermore, we prove that by incorporating a simple random twist into the walk, we can embed a well-known affine random walk on 𝐅 p \mathbf{F}_{p} into the modified random Lie bracket, allowing us to show that the entire Lie algebra is covered in roughly log ⁡ p \log p steps in the generic case.

The vector space

Lemma 2.1

(compare with Proposition 6.2 in

For any two elements

[A,B,B]=2\langle B,B\rangle A-2\langle A,B\rangle B.

Proof.

The characteristic polynomial of

AB^{2}+BAB+B^{2}A-\frac{1}{2}(2\langle A,B\rangle B+\langle B,B\rangle A)=0.

Taking into account that

, we get

\langle B,B\rangle A-2\langle A,B\rangle B+2BAB=0.

The last term of the sum is
2BAB=2B[A,B]+2B^{2}A=2B[A,B]+\langle B,B\rangle A
, hence

(1)

2\langle B,B\rangle A-2\langle A,B\rangle B=-2B[A,B].

Finally, we have
B[A,B]=BAB-B^{2}A=BAB-AB^{2}=[B,A]B

[A,B,B]=[A,B]B-B[A,B]=-2B[A,B],

which is the right-hand side of (
1

∎

We clearly have

2.2.
Odd/even number of steps

The random walk
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
begins at
X_{0}=[A,B]
. After the first step,

is uniformly distributed among the elements
\ad_{A}X_{0}=-\alpha A+\beta B
and
\ad_{B}X_{0}=-\gamma A+\alpha B

is uniformly distributed among
\beta[A,B]

\alpha[A,B]
,
\alpha[A,B]

\gamma[A,B]

By induction, we observe that for all even indices,

[A,B]

on the line
\lin\{[A,B]\}

is distributed proportionally (scaled by a factor of

) across the lines
\lin\{-\alpha A+\beta B\}

\lin\{-\gamma A+\alpha B\}

Thus, to understand the behavior of our random walk, it suffices to focus on the distribution after an even number of steps. This allows us to reduce the problem to analyzing how the distribution evolves along the line
\lin\{[A,B]\}
.

The sequence
(X_{2k})_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
is equivalent to a random walk on
\mathbf{F}_{p}
, where we start at the element
1\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
and, at each step, multiply by
\alpha
with probability

\beta
or
\gamma

1/4

\alpha

\beta

are nonzero, this random walk occurs on the group
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}

3.
Distribution of the Gram matrix

In order to analyze our random walk, we need to determine how the parameters
\alpha=2\langle A,B\rangle
,
\beta=2\langle A,A\rangle
, and
\gamma=2\langle B,B\rangle

\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})

\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})

G_{A,B}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle A,A\rangle&\langle A,B\rangle\\
\langle A,B\rangle&\langle B,B\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\beta&\alpha\\
\alpha&\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

We thus consider the Gram map to the set of all symmetric matrices,

G\colon\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})^{2}\to\Sym_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p}),\quad(A,B)\mapsto G_{A,B}.

The orthogonal group

\mathbf{F}_{p}
acts naturally on the space

[

,

, and

\dim\langle A,B\rangle=2

4.
A brief overview of Fourier analysis on cyclic groups

We give a brief overview of Fourier analysis on cyclic groups, following the exposition in
[
Dia88
]
.

4.1.
Setup

Let
S
be a subset of a cyclic group
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
equipped with a probability measure
p\colon S\to\mathbf{C}
. Let
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbf{N}_{0}}
be the random walk on
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
starting at
0
with transition probabilities given by
p(x,y)=p(y-x)
. Let
\sigma_{k}
be the probability distribution of the walk after
k
steps, i.e.,
\sigma_{k}(x)=\mathbf{P}(Y_{k}=x)
for
x\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}
. This distribution is a function in the vector space
\lspace(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
of all complex valued functions on
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
.

4.2.
Markov operator

Let
M\colon\lspace(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\to\lspace(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
be the operator defined by

(Mf)(x)=\sum_{s\in S}p(s)f(x-s).

The distribution
\sigma_{k}
can be written as

\sigma_{k}(x)=\sum_{s\in S}p(s)\sigma_{k-1}(x-s)=(M\sigma_{k-1})(x),

and so
\sigma_{k}=M^{k}\sigma_{0}=M^{k}1_{0}
. In order to understand high powers of the Markov operator, we need to analyze its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. For any character
\chi
of the group
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, we have

(M\chi)(x)=\left(\sum_{s\in S}p(s)\chi(-s)\right)\chi(x),

so
\chi
is an eigenfunction of
M
with eigenvalue
\lambda=\sum_{s\in S}p(s)\chi(-s)
. Characters of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
are of the form
\chi_{j}(x)=\omega^{jx}
for
j\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}
, and they form a basis of
\lspace(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
.

4.3.
Limiting distribution

The initial distribution
1_{0}
can be expressed in terms of the basis of characters as
1_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\chi_{j}
. It follows that the distribution after
k
steps is given by

\sigma_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}^{k}\chi_{j},

where
\lambda_{j}=\sum_{s\in S}p(s)\chi_{j}(-s)
is the eigenvalue corresponding to
\chi_{j}
. We can decompose
\sigma_{k}
into two components
\sigma_{k}=\phi_{k}+\omega_{k}
, where

\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|=1}\lambda_{j}^{k}\chi_{j},\qquad\omega_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|<1}\lambda_{j}^{k}\chi_{j}.

As
k
grows to infinity, the contribution of
\omega_{k}
diminishes to zero, making
\sigma_{k}
increasingly close to
\phi_{k}
.
6
6

6

Note that
\phi_{k}
may not converge as
k\to\infty
, nor is it guaranteed to be uniform.
This proximity can be quantified in terms of the spectral radius
\rho=\max\{|\lambda_{j}|\mid|\lambda_{j}|<1\}
of the Markov operator.

Lemma 4.1

(Lemma 3 in
[
ABL+22
]
)
.

The distance to the limiting distribution satisfies

\rho^{2k}\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma_{k}-\phi_{k}\right\rVert}_{1}^{2}\leq\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|<1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}^{2k}.

Proof.

The characters
\chi_{j}
form an orthogonal basis of
\lspace(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
and have norm
\sqrt{n}
. Thus

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\omega_{k}\right\rVert}_{1}^{2}\leq n\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\omega_{k}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|<1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\lambda_{j}^{k}\chi_{j}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|<1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}^{2k},

proving the second inequality. For the first inequality, let
\chi
be a character of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
corresponding to an eigenvalue with absolute value
\rho
. Since
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\chi\right\rVert}_{\infty}=1
, we have

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\omega_{k}\right\rVert}_{1}\geq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle\omega_{k},\chi\rangle\right\rvert}=\frac{1}{n}\rho^{k}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle\chi,\chi\rangle\right\rvert}=\rho^{k}.\qed

5.
The limiting distribution

We are now ready to analyze our random Lie bracket in more detail. We have already seen that the distribution of the random walk after an even number of steps is equivalent to a random walk on the cyclic group
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}
starting at
1
and evolving by multiplication with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
(all nonzero) with probabilities
1/2,1/4,1/4
.

5.1.
Log

Fix an isomorphism
\log\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}\to\mathbf{Z}_{n}
and let
\alpha^{\prime}=\log\alpha
,
\beta^{\prime}=\log\beta
, and
\gamma^{\prime}=\log\gamma
. Under this mapping, the random walk on
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}
translates to a random walk on
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, starting at
0=\log 1\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}
. At each step, the walk adds
\alpha^{\prime}
with probability
1/2
, or
\beta^{\prime}
or
\gamma^{\prime}
each with probability
1/4
. Let
S^{\prime}=\{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\}
be the set of steps of the walk with probability measure
p(\alpha^{\prime})=1/2
,
p(\beta^{\prime})=p(\gamma^{\prime})=1/4
. The limiting distribution on
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is given by

\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|=1}\lambda_{j}^{k}\chi_{j}.

5.2.
Contributing characters

Let us examine which characters
\chi_{j}
appear in the sum
\phi_{k}
. Certainly the trivial character
\chi_{0}
will always contribute to the limiting distribution. The character
\chi_{j}
will contribute if and only if

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\sum_{s\in S^{\prime}}p(s)\chi_{j}(-s)\right\rvert}=1.

As
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\chi_{j}(-s)\right\rvert}=1
for each

, this holds if and only if
\chi_{j}(-\alpha^{\prime})=\chi_{j}(-\beta^{\prime})=\chi_{j}(-\gamma^{\prime})
, which is equivalent to
\chi_{j}(\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime})=\chi_{j}(\alpha^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime})=1
. Introduce parameters
a=\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}
and
b=\alpha^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime}
.
Hence a character
\chi_{j}
appears in the limiting distribution if and only if
a,b\in\ker\chi_{j}
, and the corresponding eigenvalue is
\lambda_{j}=\overline{\chi_{j}(\alpha^{\prime})}
. The random walk is thus essentially determined by the parameters

.

Lemma 5.1
.

\ker\chi_{j}=(n/\mathrm{gcd}(n,j))\mathbf{Z}_{n}
.

Proof.

An element
x\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}
satisfies

if and only if

. We can write

and

, where

and hence to

. Therefore
\ker\chi_{j}=\frac{n}{d}\mathbf{Z}_{n}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\ker\chi_{j}\right\rvert}=d
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
a=\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}
and
b=\alpha^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime}
. Then the characters appearing in

are precisely those

for which

Proof.

The character
\chi_{j}
contributes to the limiting distribution if and only if
a,b\in\ker\chi_{j}=(n/\mathrm{gcd}(n,j))\mathbf{Z}_{n}
. This is equivalent to

, which is the same as
n/\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)\mid\mathrm{gcd}(n,j)

.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

The limiting distribution
\phi_{k}
is uniform if and only if
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)=1
.

In fact, we always get a uniform distribution on
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)\mathbf{Z}_{n}
provided the number of steps

\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)
, and more generally a uniform distribution on a coset of
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)\mathbf{Z}_{n}

Lemma 5.4
.

The distribution
\phi_{k}
is uniform on the coset

.

Proof.

Let
d=\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)
. We have

\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\frac{n}{d}\mid j}\overline{\chi_{j}(\alpha^{\prime})}^{k}\chi_{j}.

Any

5.3.
Randomly chosen parameters

Suppose that the parameters
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}

. In this case, the parameters

contributes to the limiting distribution is equal to

contributes is

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)=1)=\prod_{q\mid n}\left(1-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\right),

For each prime

6.
Pre-cutoff

Let
A,B\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
be uniformly random. Let
\mu_{2k}
be the distribution of the random Lie bracket after
2k
steps, and let
\nu_{2k}
be the associated limiting distribution, supported on
\lin\{[A,B]\}
. We shall now prove that with high probability, the distance
\dist(\mu_{2k},\nu_{2k})
quickly transitions from
1
to
0
at around
k\approx p
. This is the phenomenon of pre-cutoff, and we establish it by providing sharp upper and lower bounds on
\dist(\mu_{2k},\nu_{2k})
in terms of
k
and the parameters
a,b
. We further inspect what happens for generic
a,b
.

The distribution of the random Lie bracket after
2k
steps is the same as the distribution of the random walk on
\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}
with parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
after
k
steps. Supposing these parameters are all nonzero, the random walk is equivalent to a random walk on
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
with parameters
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}
. Let
\sigma_{k}
be the distribution of this random walk after
k
steps. The Markov operator has eigenvalues

\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\chi_{j}(-\alpha^{\prime})+\frac{1}{4}\chi_{j}(-\beta^{\prime})+\frac{1}{4}\chi_{j}(-\gamma^{\prime})

of absolute value

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\chi_{j}(a)+\frac{1}{4}\chi_{j}(b)\right\rvert},

where
a=\alpha^{\prime}-\beta^{\prime}
and
b=\alpha^{\prime}-\gamma^{\prime}
.

6.1.
Upper bound

For
x\in\mathbf{R}
, let
\langle x\rangle
be the unique value in the interval
(-1/2,1/2]
such that
x-\langle x\rangle
is an integer. Let us associate to each eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
the vector

u_{j}=\left(\left\langle\frac{ja}{n}\right\rangle,\left\langle\frac{jb}{n}\right\rangle\right)\in\mathbf{R}^{2}.

The norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert}
controls
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}
in the following way.

Lemma 6.1
.

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

The triangle inequality gives

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1+\chi_{j}(a)\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert 1+\chi_{j}(b)\right\rvert}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\cos\left(\pi ja/n\right)\right\rvert}+\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\cos\left(\pi jb/n\right)\right\rvert}.

Note that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\cos(\pi x)\right\rvert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\cos(\pi\langle x\rangle)\right\rvert}
. Using the bound
\cos(\pi x)\leq\exp(-2x^{2})
, which holds for all
x\in[-3/2,3/2]
, we obtain

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\exp(-2\langle ja/n\rangle^{2})+\exp(-2\langle jb/n\rangle^{2})\right).

By Jensen’s inequality for the function
x\mapsto\exp(-2x^{2})
, which is concave on the interval
[-1/2,1/2]
, the latter is at most

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle ja/n\rangle\right\rvert}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle jb/n\rangle\right\rvert}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}\right)

and the proof is complete.
∎

The characters contributing to the limiting distribution
\phi_{k}
are those
\chi_{j}
for which
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}=1
. Note that this happens if and only if
a,b\in\ker\chi_{j}
, which is the same as
u_{j}=0
. In order to control the residual distribution
\sigma_{k}-\phi_{k}=\omega_{k}
, let

be the plane lattice

\Lambda=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbf{Z}_{n}}\left(u_{j}+\mathbf{Z}^{2}\right)=\left(\frac{a}{n},\frac{b}{n}\right)\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}^{2}\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{2}

and let
\minnorm
be the minimal distance between two distinct points in
\Lambda
. When
a,b\neq 0
, the lattice
\Lambda
is nontrivial, and we have

\minnorm=\min\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert}\mid j\in\mathbf{Z}_{n},\ u_{j}\neq 0\right\}.

For all

Use

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sigma_{k}-\phi_{k}\right\rVert}_{1}^{2}\leq\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|<1}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert}^{2k}\leq\sum_{u_{j}\neq 0}\exp\left(-k\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}\right).

Suppose the parameters

Lemma 6.4

T=\left\{(k-x-y,x,y)\in\mathbf{Z}^{3}\mid\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x-k/4\right\rvert},\mathinner{\!\left\lvert y-k/4\right\rvert}\leq\lambda\sqrt{k}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad S=\phi\left(T\right).

The distribution
\phi_{k}
is uniform on a subset of size
n/\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)
by
Lemma

5.4
. With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)\leq 1+1/\delta
by
Lemma

6.4
, and hence

\phi_{k}(S)\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\mathrm{gcd}(a,b,n)/n\leq\left(1+1/\delta\right)\left(2\lambda\sqrt{k}+1\right)^{2}/n<\epsilon

by the assumption on
n,k
. On the other hand, let
X,Y
be the number of times the generators
\beta^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}
are chosen in the random walk. Note that
X,Y
are distributed as a sum of
k
independent Bernoulli random variables with success probability
1/4
. Thus

\sigma_{k}(S)\geq\mathbf{P}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X-k/4\right\rvert}\leq\lambda\sqrt{k},\ \mathinner{\!\left\lvert Y-k/4\right\rvert}\leq\lambda\sqrt{k}\right)\geq 1-2\mathbf{P}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X-k/4\right\rvert}>\lambda\sqrt{k}\right).

By Hoeffding’s inequality, the last probability is at most
2\exp\left(-2\lambda^{2}\right)
. Let
\lambda
be such that this is less than
\epsilon
. Then
\sigma_{k}(S)>1-2\epsilon
, as required.
∎

Corollary 6.12
.

For every
\epsilon,\delta>0
there are constants
C,c>0
such that the following holds. Let
p>C
and let
A,B\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
be uniformly random. For all
k<cp
, we have

\mathbf{P}_{A,B}\left(\dist(\mu_{2k},\nu_{2k})>1-\epsilon\right)>1-\delta.

Proof.

For matrices
A,B\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
such that
G_{A,B}
has nonzero entries, the random Lie bracket satisfies
\dist(\mu_{2k},\nu_{2k})>1-\epsilon
if and only if the parameters
(a,b)=F(A,B)
satisfy
\dist(\sigma_{k},\phi_{k})>1-\epsilon
. By the previous theorem, we have
\mathbf{P}_{a,b}(\dist(\sigma_{k},\phi_{k})>1-\epsilon)>1-\delta/2
for
k<cp
. We can assume
C
is large enough so that
15/C<\delta/2
, so
Corollary

3.6
implies that
\mathbf{P}_{A,B}(\dist(\mu_{2k},\nu_{2k})<1-\epsilon)>1-\delta
.
∎

Corrollaries
6.8
and
6.12
together prove
Theorem

1.1
.

7.
Diameter

Here, we prove the diameter bound of
O(\log p)
for the random Lie bracket in
\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
, as stated in
Theorem

1.3
. The key point behind the argument is based on the analysis of the affine random walk on
\mathbf{F}_{p}
evolving according to
x_{n+1}=ax_{n}+b_{n}
for fixed
a\in\mathbf{F}_{p}^{\ast}
and i.i.d. random
b\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
. This walk has been studied many times in the literature, the starting point being
[
CDG87
]
. The most relevant for us are the strong results of
[
BV22
]
which exhibit rapid mixing of the walk for almost all values of
a
. In order to explain this more precisely, let, for any
a\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
and
n\in\mathbf{N}
,

P_{n}(a)=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}a^{i}\mid b_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}\right\}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{p}

be the set of all polynomials in
a
of degree at most
n
with coefficients in
\{-1,0,1\}
.

Theorem 7.1

(Proposition 13 together with Theorem 2 in
[
BV22
]
)
.

There is a (possibly empty) small set of primes
T
with the following property. For any
\epsilon>0
there is a
C
such that for all primes
p>C
not in
T
and at least
(1-\epsilon)p
values of
a\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
, we have
P_{n}(a)=\mathbf{F}_{p}
for some
n\leq 10\log p
.

Remark 7.2
.

Alternatively, we can use Theorem 1 from
[
BV22
]
instead of Theorem 2. This yields a weaker bound of
n\leq C_{\epsilon}\log p\log\log p
, but it holds for
all
primes
p
.

We now show how to do deduce the stated diameter bound from this. First of all, using basic properties of the random Lie bracket, we show that obtaining
P_{n}(a)
-multiples of
[A,B]\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
grows linearly with
n
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
A,B\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
and
S=\{0,\pm A,\pm B\}
. Let
\alpha=2\langle A,B\rangle
. Then for any
n\in\mathbf{N}
, we have

P_{n}(\alpha)\cdot[A,B]\subseteq S^{4n+2}.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
n
. The statement is trivial for
n=0
. Suppose it holds for
n
and take any
x\in P_{n+1}(\alpha)
. Then
x=\alpha y+z
for some
y\in P_{n}(\alpha)
and
z\in\{-1,0,1\}
. By the induction hypothesis, we have
y[A,B]\in S^{4n+2}
, and so
\alpha y[A,B]=[B,[A,y[A,B]]]\in S^{4n+4}
by
Lemma

2.1
. Then

x[A,B]=\alpha y[A,B]+z[A,B]\in S^{4n+6}=S^{4(n+1)+2}.\qed

Secondly, we show that, with high probability, the value
\alpha=2\langle A,B\rangle
belongs to any large enough set.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{p}
be a subset of
\mathbf{F}_{p}
with
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert}\geq(1-\epsilon)p
and let
A,B\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
be uniformly random. Then

\mathbf{P}_{A,B}\left(\alpha\notin\mathcal{A}\right)\leq 3\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\Sym_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
be the set of symmetric matrices
X
with
2X_{12}\notin\mathcal{A}
. By assumption, we have
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert}\leq\epsilon p^{3}
. By
Proposition

3.2
, we then get then bound
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert G^{-1}(\mathcal{X})\right\rvert}\leq 3\epsilon p^{6}
. The result follows.
∎

We are now ready to deduce
Theorem

1.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
T
be the exceptional set of primes from

7.1
. Take any
\epsilon>0
. Then there is a
C
such that for all primes
p>C
not in
T
and at least
(1-\epsilon)p
values of
a\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
, we have
P_{n}(a)=\mathbf{F}_{p}
for

.
Hence, by
Lemma

7.4
, for
A,B\in\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
uniformly random we have

with probability at least
1-3\epsilon

7.3
, that
\lin\{[A,B]\}\subseteq S^{4n+2}
.

We can assume that
C
(and therefore
p
) is large enough that with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, the matrices
A
,
B
and
[A,B]
span
\slfrak_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
as a vector space.

Write
A=(a_{ij})
,
B=(b_{ij})
. Then
A,B,[A,B]
span

if and only if the
3\times 4

.
In that case, we can write any element of
