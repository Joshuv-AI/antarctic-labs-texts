Non-invasive multiparametric characterization of essential thrombocythemia, premyelofibrosis, and overt myelofibrosis
Authors: Carole Mosnier, Marie-Christine Copin, Isabelle Quintin-Roué, Eric Lippert, Yunjiao Lu, Jérémie Riou, Simon Blanchard, Nathalie Mérillon, Bertille Pawlicki, Françoise Boyer, Joris Argentin, Léa Sureau, Anaïs Malinge, Julie Sevestre, Marine Demoy, Christophe Aubé, Noémi Reboux, Jonathan Farhi, Aurélien Giltat, Christopher Nunes Gomes, Mathilde Hunault, Yves Delneste, Valérie Ugo, Jean-Christophe Ianotto, Corentin Orvain, Damien Luque Paz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), prefibrotic myelofibrosis (preMF) was defined as a distinct entity from essential thrombocythemia (ET) and primary overt myelofibrosis (PMF) since the 2016 revision of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification, while precise characterization may be difficult in some instances. In that context, several non-invasive parameters have shown potential to differentiate ET from PMF. The aim of the study was to prospectively evaluate non-invasive parameters in 128 patients (44 ET, 53 preMF, and 21 PMF). The mutational landscape became increasingly complex across diagnostic from ET to preMF and PMF. We observed significant differential expression of inflammatory mediators (IL-1RA, IL-1β, IL-6, S100A8/S100A9, CCL4), immune activation markers (CD25 and CXCL10), matrix regulator (YLK-40 and TIMP1), and lower levels of EGF and CXCL4 between ET and PMF with similar results between preMF and PMF. The two-step Lasso regression strategy to classify patients with internal bootstrap validation reliably identified PMF (AUC: 0.909 [0.804–0.994]) and reasonable discrimination between ET and preMF (AUC: 0.762 [0.656–0.847]). The non-invasive parameters studied enable effective discrimination of PMF and help to distinguish preMF from ET, although the low discriminatory power of these parameters supports the hypothesis of a pathophysiological continuum between these conditions. Subject terms: Translational research, Myeloproliferative disease

Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) are chronic myeloid malignancies characterized by the acquisition of somatic mutations in driver genes leading to the proliferation of myeloid lineages and the overproduction of mature myeloid cells. These disorders, including essential thrombocythemia (ET), polycythemia vera (PV), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF), are a continuum of disease, as ET and PV can progress to secondary myelofibrosis. Prefibrotic myelofibrosis (preMF), an intermediate entity between ET and overt PMF, has been incorporated into the 2016 revision of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [1]. Initially described in the early 2000s as a histopathological entity distinct from ET [2], Barbui et al. later showed poorer prognosis due to a higher risk of hematological progression [3]. In the 2016 and 2022 WHO classifications, histological criteria were complemented by minor clinicobiological criteria for the diagnosis of preMF. Nevertheless, establishing a definitive diagnosis according to the WHO classification remains challenging in some cases. Several studies have reported difficulties in distinguishing ET from preMF on bone marrow biopsies (BMB), with consensus between expert pathologists in 53–88% of cases [4–10]. Furthermore, when minor criteria for preMF are absent, typical MPN diagnosis cannot be made, resulting in the MPN being categorized as unclassifiable myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN-U) in ~10% of cases [11].

Several non-invasive parameters have been explored in MPN and have shown potential to differentiate overt myelofibrosis from ET. These include the quantification of circulating CD34-positive cells [12], plasma cytokine levels [13], mutational profiling [14], and spleen stiffness assessment [15]. However, these parameters have not been extensively investigated in the framework of the recent WHO classifications and the introduction of preMF, with the exception of CD34-positive cell quantification. Although CD34-positive cell counts are slightly higher in preMF compared to ET, this parameter alone is insufficient to reliably distinguish these two diagnoses [16].

In the present study, we prospectively recruited 128 newly diagnosed patients in two French centers to evaluate the discriminatory potential of these non-invasive multimodal parameters across well-defined ET, preMF, and PMF, and to explore their ability to contribute to non-invasive disease classification as a basis for future score development.

Methods

Patients and samples

Adults diagnosed with a myeloproliferative neoplasm (ET, preMF, or PMF) at the University Hospital of Angers or Brest were prospectively included between 2019 and 2024. All analyses, including bones marrow biopsies (BMB) and specific research-related sampling, were performed prior to any cytoreductive treatment. Bone marrow histology was independently assessed by three hematopathologists with expertise in myeloid pathology (IR, MCC, BP). Cases with discordant interpretation were conjointly reviewed to reach a consensus diagnosis, according to criteria listed in the 5th edition of the WHO classification [17]. Minimal clinical information available at diagnosis (age, gender) was provided. Biopsies with fewer than five evaluable marrow trabeculae were deemed inadequate. The histological diagnosis (ET, preMF, or PMF) and fibrosis grade (0–3) were recorded. The final diagnosis of ET, preMF, or PMF, according to 2016/2022 WHO diagnostic criteria, was confirmed by an adjudication committee composed of two physicians (JCI and CO). This final adjudication was based on the BM histology combined with clinicobiological parameters. The adjudication committee was blinded to all non-invasive parameters assessed in the study. This study (NCT03869476) was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by French authorities (2019-A00375-51). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before inclusion. All patients were included in the “Hematological Malignancies” collections at University Hospital of Angers or Brest and in “FIMBANK”, a multicentric national French collection of myeloproliferative neoplasms (BCB INCa).

Molecular analysis

DNA samples obtained from peripheral blood at diagnosis were centralized at the University Hospital of Angers for next-generation sequencing (NGS). A panel covering all exons of 52 genes involved in myeloid malignancies was analyzed (Supplementary Table S1). The sensitivity was set at 2%, only pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations were retained for analysis. For all analyses, we considered the allelic burden of mutations and not only their presence or absence. The allele burden was derived from the NGS data except for CALR mutations measured by PCR fragment analysis.

Circulating CD34

Circulating CD34+ cells were quantified locally at University Hospital of Angers or Brest, by flow cytometry with a standardized simple platform technique, after CD45 gating (BD™ Stem Cell Enumeration Kit, BD Bioscience; Stem-Kit™ CD34 HPC Enumeration kit, Beckman Coulter).

Spleen ultrasound and elastography

Patients underwent spleen elastography locally at the University Hospital of Angers or Brest. Spleen stiffness was assessed using VTQ point shear wave elastography on Sequoia (Siemens), or 2D shear wave elastography on Supersonic Imaging (Aixplorer) in Angers and 2D shear wave elastography on Aplio a450 (Canon) in Brest. Values were expressed in kilopascal (kPa), estimated from the speed of the shear wave using the E = 3pVs2 formula (p = mass density, Vs = shear wave velocity) [18], automatically calculated by the device. Ten measurements were made for each patient, and the median value was retained. Operators were blinded to clinical characteristics. As shear wave speeds depend on the excitation frequency, which varies across ultrasound devices, results were expressed as z-scores after normalization within each center.

Cytokines

Blood samples collected at diagnosis in EDTA tubes were centrifugated (2000 × g, 10 min) to obtain plasma, which was conserved at −80 °C. Quantification of 29 cytokines was performed at University Hospital of Angers at the end of the study. S100A8/S100A9 heteroduplex and IL-34 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Quantikine ELISA Kit R&D® Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Human IFNɤ, IL-12, IL-15, CCL24, CCL4, CXCL1, CXCL10, IL-8, YKL-40, IL-10, IL-4, IL-1RA, IL-2RA, IL-1β, Osteopontin, Osteoprotegerin, Dkk-1, EGF, VEGF-A, GM-CSF, S100A8, S100A9, IL-6, IL-17, TIMP1, PF4 and FGF23 were quantified by multiplex fluorescent-bead-based technology (Luminex 200™, Austin, TX, USA) using 6 different custom magnetic Luminex® assays (R&D Systems), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. IL-1β was subsequently quantified using ultrasensitive ELISA (Quantikine HS ELISA Kit R&D® Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) due to indetectable levels in several samples with the Luminex® assay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Mann–Whitney pairwise comparisons; categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test; p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Multi-block data integration was performed using the DIABLO framework (mixOmics package), a supervised sparse PLS-DA approach applied to the 124 patients with available cytokine data after random forest imputation. To identify the most predictive non-invasive parameters for distinguishing PMF, then ET from preMF, we used a two-step LASSO regression model (glmnet package), with λ selected by cross-validation (lambda.min). Predictors were standardized internally by cv.glmnet prior to penalization, with coefficients back-transformed to the original scale in the output. Internal validation used the bootstrap .632 method (1000 resamples); AUC, sensitivity, and specificity were estimated on out-of-bag samples using per-sample Youden-optimal thresholds. The two steps were validated separately; the step 1 threshold defining step 2 cohort composition was derived from the full training set, potentially introducing residual optimism in step 2 estimates. Additional performance metrics (F1-score, Brier score) were estimated from the bootstrap .632 procedure and calibration curves were generated from full-model predictions. As a sensitivity analysis, repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation (10 repetitions) was performed. For descriptive purposes, variables selected in >70% of bootstrap iterations were considered to show high selection stability and are reported as the most consistently identified parameters across resamples. This threshold did not influence model fitting or performance estimation. This study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD methodology; the completed TRIPOD checklist is provided in the Supplemental data.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between October 2019 and January 2024, 133 patients were prospectively included. After study completion, five patients were finally excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Table S2): two patients had a final diagnosis of MPN/MDS (myeloproliferative neoplasm with ring sideroblast and thrombocytosis with SF3B1 mutation; chronic eosinophilic leukemia with t(5;12)(q22,p13) and ETV6::PDGFRβ fusion transcript) and 3 patients in whom a diagnostic of MPN was not confirmed after reviewing BMB, clinical, and laboratory characteristics by the adjudication committee. The centralized bone marrow review identified 22 ET, 49 preMF, and 21 PMF patients with typical histological aspects. Patients who lacked the full histological criteria for any of these diagnoses were categorized as MPN-U (n = 24) and 13 BMB were inconclusive due to a lower number of evaluable marrow trabeculae (Fig. 1). After integrating histological findings with clinical and biological data, the adjudication committee ultimately classified 44 patients as ET, 53 as preMF, 21 as PMF, and 10 as MPN-U. This resulted in an overall reclassification rate of 40% compared with the local diagnosis at inclusion, with a notably higher proportion of preMF cases (40% in the final classification vs 22% at the initial local diagnosis). Of note, among the 53 preMF patients, 15 had BMB suggestive of preMF but did not meet the WHO 2016 requirement for at least one minor criterion.

Mutational landscape

We characterized the mutational landscape of all patients using NGS with a targeted myeloid panel. The variant allele frequencies (VAF) of the JAK2V617F and CALR driver mutations progressively increased from ET to preMF and then to PMF (Fig. 2A). The median VAF for the JAK2V617F mutation was 9.5% [1.2–37.9%] in ET patients, 15.3% [3.6–64.5%] in preMF patients, and 37.9% [7.6–88.5%] in PMF patients (p = 0.0006 between PMF and preMF, p = 0.004 between preMF and ET and p = 0.00006 between ET and PMF). For CALR mutations, the median VAF was 19% [6.5–44.1%] in ET patients, 33.3% [13.7–64.6%] in preMF patients, and 44.7% [411–47.6%] in PMF patients (p = 0.063 for each pairwise comparison).

In our cohort, the mutational landscape became increasingly complex across the diagnostic groups with the proportion of patients harboring at least one additional mutation increasing from 34% in ET to 57% in preMF and 67% in PMF patients (p = 0.040 for ET vs preMF, p = 0.018 for ET vs PMF, and p = 0.600 for preMF vs PMF; Fig. 2B). The proportion of patients with 2 or more additional mutations was also significantly higher in PMF (57%) compared with preMF (19%, p = 0.002) and ET (9%, p < 0.001). The detailed mutational landscape of the cohort ordered by diagnosis is summarized by an oncoplot in Fig. 2C. The most commonly mutated gene in the whole cohort was TET2 (18% of patients) without any difference between the 3 main diagnoses (Supplementary Table S4). The second most frequently mutated gene was DNMT3A (9% of patients), and third ASXL1 (8% of patients), more often in PMF (33.3% of patients) than in ET (2.3%, p = 0.001) or preMF (3.8%, p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S4). In the same manner, mutations in genes carrying high-risk in myelofibrosis (HMR i.e., ASXL1, IDH1/2, EZH2, SRSF2 or U2AF1) were more frequent in PMF (48%) compared to ET (4.5%, p < 0.001) and preMF (7.5%, p < 0.001), and no difference was found between ET and preMF (p = 0.686) (Supplementary Table S4). Interestingly, we observed higher prevalence of DNMT3A mutations in preMF (17% vs 4.5% in ET and 0% in PMF), with no age difference in patients with or without DNMT3A mutations (p = 0.876). Patients categorized as MPN-U did not exhibit a specific molecular profile, except for a lower VAF of the driver mutation (Fig. 2A) and the high prevalence of mutations in CBL gene (3/10, 30%) (Supplementary Table S4). As the JAK2V617F mutation was more frequent in PMF patients, analyses of associations between driver genotype and additional mutations were performed in the combined ET and prePMF cohort. Among additional mutations, only TET2 mutations showed a differential distribution according to driver genotype, being more frequent in MPL-mutated cases (57.1%) than in CALR-mutated (9.1%, p = 0.018) or JAK2-mutated cases (14.3%, p = 0.019).

Among the 29 cytokines quantified, 24 yielded interpretable results. IL-34, GM-CSF, IL-12p70, S100A8, and IL-15 were not analyzed because their expression was detectable in only 0%, 2%, 19%, 30% and 36% of patients, respectively. All cytokine levels according to the diagnosis are depicted in Fig. 3 for cytokines with at least one significant difference and in Supplementary Fig. S2 for others.

When comparing PMF to ET, we observed significantly higher levels of the inflammatory mediators IL-1RA, IL-1β, S100A8/S100A9, IL-6, CCL4 and the immune activation markers CD25 and CXCL10. The matrix regulators TIMP1 and YLK-40 were also significantly increased. Conversely, the growth factor EGF and the platelet-derived chemokine CXCL4 were significantly lower in PMF than in ET. The comparison between PMF and preMF confirmed these findings, with significantly higher concentrations of CCL4, CXCL10, IL-1β, IL-6, S100A8/S100A9, CD25, YLK-40, and TIMP1 in PMF, together with significantly lower levels of EGF and CXCL4. Finally, when comparing ET with preMF, only CXCL4 differed significantly, being higher in ET than in preMF. Other cytokines, including IL-10, VEGFA, osteopontin, osteoprotegerin, and Dkk-1, were not significantly different between groups. Median values and results of multiple statistical testing are presented in Supplementary Table S5. No significant differences in cytokine levels were observed according to driver mutation status in ET and preMF patients.

Spleen evaluation and circulating CD34

Spleen size increased with progression of the disease severity, with a median size of 9.9 cm for ET, 11 cm for preMF, and 15.1 cm for PMF (p < 0.001 for PMF vs preMF, and p < 0.001 for PMF vs ET), with a significant increase in preMF compared to ET (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). There was however no difference in median spleen stiffness, as assessed by elastography, according to the diagnosis of ET, preMF, or PMF after normalization of values (Fig. 4B), nor when analyzed by center (Supplementary Table S6). Median circulating CD34 cell concentrations were higher in PMF (27.4 ×106 cells/L) than preMF (3.95 ×106 cells/L) and ET (3.30 ×106 cells/L) (p < 0.001 for PMF vs preMF, p < 0.001 for PMF vs ET, and p = 0.067 for preMF vs ET) (Fig. 4C).

Integration of parameters for distinguishing the 3 main diseases

Finally, we aimed to integrate all parameters to assess whether a combination of non-invasive markers could discriminate ET, preMF, and PMF. For this purpose, we first performed a supervised analysis (sPLS-DA), in which the parameters were grouped into three data blocks: clinical data (age, spleen size, complete blood count, LDH, CD34+ cell concentration), genomic data, and cytokine levels. Sparse variable selection was applied to identify a limited set of variables per block with the strongest discriminatory power among diagnostic groups. The graphical representation (Fig. 5A) showed that the three blocks together allowed clear discrimination of PMF on the first component. In contrast, separation between ET and preMF was less robust, with only few preMF cases being isolated on the second component based on clinical and genomic data. The parameters contributing most to the separation of PMF included spleen size, immature granulocytes, circulating CD34+ cell concentrations, low hemoglobin, high JAK2V617F allele burden, total number of additional mutations, presence of ASXL1 mutations and several cytokines (Fig. 5B). For the distinction between preMF and ET, the most discriminating parameter was the higher platelet count observed in preMF (Fig. 5B). The correlation structure and co-expression patterns across all three data blocks are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Based on these results, we applied a two-step Lasso regression strategy to classify patients based on non-invasive parameters. In the first step, the model aimed to distinguish PMF from non-PMF patients, with internal validation using 1’000 bootstrap iterations yielding a mean AUC of 0.909 (95% CI: 0.804–0.994) with means sensitivity and specificity of 76.2% and 95.5%, F1-score of 0.754 (95% CI: 0.526–0.900), and Brier score of 0.063 (95% CI: 0.032–0.107), respectively (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S4). In the second step, among patients predicted as non-PMF, a Lasso model was applied to differentiate ET from preMF and bootstrap validation yielding a mean AUC of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.656–0.847) with mean sensitivity and specificity of 70.9% and 74.1%, F1-score of 0.674 (95% CI: 0.533–0.794), and Brier score of 0.225 (95% CI: 0.162–0.310), respectively (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S4). Overall, this two-step Lasso identified the most stable non-invasive parameters contributing to group discrimination, with internal bootstrap validation supporting their relevance. A sensitivity analysis of internal validation was also performed using repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation (10 repetitions), yielding AUC consistent with bootstrap .632 for both step 1 and step 2 (Supplementary Fig. S5). We then examined the stability of variables contributing to class separation (Supplementary Table S7). In the first step (PMF vs. non-PMF), ASXL1 and JAK2V617F VAFs, LDH, spleen size and hemoglobin level showed consistent selection, supporting their strong discriminatory value. In the second step (ET vs. preMF), spleen size and platelet counts were almost always retained. In addition, beta-coefficients and SHAP values from the full model illustrating the direction and magnitude of each predictor’s contribution to the final classification are presented in Supplementary Fig. S6. Finally, we evaluated the contribution of each data layer to the two-step classification (Supplementary Table S8). Although the complete model achieved the best overall performance, all reduced models provided good identification of PMF in the first step. In contrast, for the second step, distinguishing ET from preMF among non-PMF patients, genomic and cytokine data alone showed substantially lower performance and only the combination of all three data types restored reasonable discriminatory capacity.

Discussion

This study provides a prospective description of extensive non-invasive parameters in ET, preMF, and PMF patients. This is the first complete analysis combining NGS results, elastography, circulating CD34+ cell counts, and circulating cytokines quantification in newly diagnosed MPN patients accurately classified by an adjudication committee with systematic BMB review.

Our results were consistent with the existing literature regarding the comparison between ET and overt PMF, particularly in terms of higher molecular complexity [14], inflammatory cytokine profile [13,19], and circulating CD34+ cell counts for PMF [20,21]. Beyond this concordance, our work provides new insights by precisely positioning preMF within the biological spectrum of MPNs. The mutational landscape of preMF displayed an intermediate degree of molecular complexity between ET and overt PMF. However, preMF did not exhibit a distinct specific mutational signature comparable to the high-risk mutations in PMF [22]. Interestingly, DNMT3A mutations emerged as the only variant preferentially enriched in preMF, suggesting a potential biologically meaningful association specific to this subgroup. This finding is in line with published Jak2V617F-Dnmt3a−/− mouse models showing an intermediate disease with splenomegaly and fibrosis [23], and an inflammatory signature in hematopoietic stem cells [24].

Our observation of a marked increase in inflammatory cytokines in overt PMF (IL-1RA, IL-1β, S100A8/S100A9) is in line with previous reports [13]. Recent mouse models have demonstrated that inflammatory cytokines actively contribute to the development of bone marrow fibrosis, and that their pharmacological inhibition may therefore represent a promising therapeutic opportunity [25,26]. In addition, our data highlight the relevance of markers associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, particularly YKL-40, already described in PMF [27,28], and TIMP1, a metalloprotease inhibitor, both elevated in PMF compared with ET and preMF. These cytokines are predominantly produced by monocytes and macrophages, cell populations that may derive from the mutated hematopoietic clone and already known to contribute to the pro-inflammatory phenotype in MPN [29]. While CXCL4 has been implicated in bone marrow fibrosis through remodeling of the bone marrow microenvironment [30–32], plasma levels do not necessarily reflect this role, as circulating CXCL4 is strongly influenced by platelet counts (Supplementary Fig. S7), likely explaining the lower levels observed in overt PMF patients with thrombocytopenia. Although statistically significant differences were detected only between PMF and the ET/preMF groups, without significant separation between ET and preMF, visual inspection of the cytokine distributions suggests a biological gradient for several markers, supporting the notion of a continuum of inflammatory states across MPN phenotypes.

Of note, we did not observe differences in spleen stiffness among diagnoses, whereas Iurlo and collaborators reported higher spleen stiffness associated with bone marrow fibrosis [15]. Several hypotheses may explain this result. First, Iurlo et al. used FibroScan, while we used ARFI technique. Second, spleen involvement in MPN is heterogenous: among 213 splenectomies from PMF patients, 56% showed a diffuse infiltrative pattern and 35% had nodular involvement [33], suggesting heterogeneity even within the same diagnostic category. Third, we observed a center effect, which was likely attributable to a different equipment. For these reasons, evaluation of spleen stiffness with ARFI seems difficult to reproduce and to apply reliably in clinical practice at least with the workflow we used.

As of now, bone marrow histology remains the only parameter capable of reliably distinguishing MPN subtypes. However, as demonstrated in other chronic diseases, most notably in liver fibrosis, non-invasive composite scores can accurately capture underlying tissue pathology and reduce or even replace the need for invasive procedures [34,35]. Similar tools would be highly valuable in MPNs, where histologic criteria may be challenging to interpret consistently and where a proportion of patients, such as those receiving anticoagulation or of advanced age, are less suitable candidates for bone marrow biopsy. In this context, our two-step LASSO regression model represents a promising framework for identifying the most discriminant non-invasive parameters. The model efficiently identified PMF with excellent sensitivity and specificity. Although the distinction between ET and preMF proved more challenging, the model still achieved an AUC of 0.762, virtually identical to the performance of the initial local diagnosis based on bone marrow biopsies (AUC of 0.756) when compared with the adjudication committee’s final classification.

Altogether, our data support the existence of a biological continuum across ET, preMF, and PMF [36–38]. Despite this continuum, PMF remains clearly distinguishable as a more advanced and biologically demarcated stage. The multilevel sPLS-DA analysis highlights this point particularly well: while ET and preMF partially overlap, PMF forms a separate cluster, reflecting the cumulative burden of molecular, inflammatory, and hematological abnormalities that characterize overt myelofibrosis. These findings suggest that, although the three entities are biologically connected, PMF corresponds to a later stage in which disease-driving mechanisms become sufficiently pronounced to generate a distinct and identifiable profile. Furthermore, this hypothesis is reinforced by the fact that clinical data alone allows a good identification of PMF while the distinction between ET and preMF requires the integration of all three biological layers.

This study has several limitations. First, although the diagnostic adjudication followed a rigorous methodology, the distinction between ET and preMF remains one of the most challenging issues in MPN classification. Our data are consistent with accumulating evidence suggesting that true ET may represent a rare subtype among MPN when WHO criteria are rigorously applied [39]. Only long-term clinical follow-up of this cohort will ultimately confirm the stability of the preMF classification and may even help identify prognostic biomarkers among those measured. Second, although our internal validation supports the stability of the selected variables, the model has not been externally validated and should not be interpreted as a ready-to-use clinical score. Its primary contribution is to highlight the most informative non-invasive parameters for future prospective validation in larger multicenter cohorts.

To conclude, the non-invasive parameters studied enable effective differentiation between PMF from ET and preMF, and help to distinguish preMF from ET, although the low discriminatory power of these parameters supports the hypothesis of a pathophysiological continuum between these conditions.
