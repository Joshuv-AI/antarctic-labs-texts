Brain-wide information flow dynamics during novel visual processing in humans
Authors: Wushuang Huang, Jianping Song, Jingru Huangfu, Shanshan Liang, Zhiji Wang, Xiang Liao, Chunqing Zhang, Xiaowei Chen, Rongdi Yuan, Lukang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human brain’s capacity for visual information processing is essential for higher-order cognitive functions, including facial recognition and spatial navigation. However, the millisecond-scale dynamics of cortico-subcortical information flow during novel visual stimulation remain unclear. To investigate this, we conducted intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) recordings in 22 patients with refractory epilepsy as they underwent a picture-viewing task. Our findings showed region-specific modulations in high-frequency broadband (HFB, 60–160 Hz) activity across multiple brain regions, such as the dorsal and ventral visual streams, the limbic system, and higher-order cortical areas. Initial activation followed a hierarchical, back-to-front propagation pattern, starting from the primary visual areas, progressing through the ventral and dorsal streams, and ultimately engaging high-order cortical and limbic structures. Extensive top-down connectivity from higher-order cortex to limbic areas further revealed their role in novel visual processing, providing a high-resolution spatiotemporal map of visual information flow across brain regions.Subject terms: Object vision, Pattern vision

Introduction

The human brain’s ability to process and integrate visual information is fundamental to environmental adaptation1,2. In particular, encoding novel visual stimuli upon their initial presentation is critical. Visual information processing is characterized by its speed, precision, and hierarchical distribution3. According to the classical visual dual-stream model, visual information reaches the visual cortex via the retina and is then transmitted through two distinct yet interconnected pathways: the dorsal and ventral streams1.

A large body of research using neuropsychological and neuroimaging methods suggests that, though anatomically distinct, these two pathways work in close coordination4–6. The dorsal “where” pathway, extending from the visual association cortex to the parietal cortex, plays a primary role in spatial awareness and action guidance. In contrast, the ventral “what” stream transmits visual input from primary and association cortices to the inferior and anterior temporal cortex, supporting hierarchical processing and facilitating object recognition by integrating attributes such as color and shape. Modern neuroimaging techniques, particularly those based on blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) signals, have provided insight into large-scale visual network function. Interestingly, research using resting-state functional magnetic resonance (rs-fMRI) has revealed that limbic structures are extensively connected to both visual and non-visual cortical regions7. However, the limited temporal resolution hinders the ability to observe the millisecond-scale interactions occurring between individual brain regions during visual processing.

Recent studies using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) have provided novel insights into the rapid, hierarchical processing of visual stimuli in the human brain. Current iEEG research has clearly identified category-specific (e.g., faces, objects, and words) activation patterns in multiple brain regions8–10. For example, the ventral temporal cortex exhibited lateral–medial response biases to visual stimuli, with face-selective activity concentrated in the fusiform gyrus (FG) and place-selective activity localized in the medial parahippocampal gyrus (PHC)11,12; among these, the FG displayed a posterior to anterior gradient in face-selectivity activity, consistent with the hierarchical structure of the ventral visual stream13,14. The medial parietal cortex showed category-selective interactions with the medial temporal lobe, exhibiting bilateral preferential responses to places and a right-lateralized response to faces in the subparietal sulcus15. It is noteworthy that visual processing was not only observed in the dorsal and ventral visual streams but also widely in higher-order cortical areas (e.g., temporal lobe, frontal cortices) and subcortical regions (e.g., the hippocampus) within the first few hundred milliseconds of stimulus presentation8,9,16, indicating a distributed brain network involved in visual perception rather than being confined to traditional visual stream regions. However, these iEEG studies provided only limited simultaneous coverage of brain regions; consequently, the propagation patterns and directional functional connectivity among visual regions, higher-order cortices, and limbic structures at the whole-brain scale remains unclear, leaving gaps in our understanding of how perception and higher-level cognition, such as memory systems, interact during novel visual processing.

To address these questions, we employed multi-site, high-spatiotemporal resolution iEEG recordings in combination with a picture-viewing task which involved novel pictures of faces and places. Specifically, we explored high-frequency broadband (HFB, 60–160 Hz) activity across both cortical and subcortical structures in response to novel visual stimuli (faces and places). Additionally, we analyzed the onset latency of HFB activity in visual response sites and conducted cross-correlation analyses to evaluate connectivity between these regions. Here, we uncovered a global landscape of evoked neural activation by revealing the spatiotemporal orchestrations of visual processing at millisecond resolution during exposure to novel stimuli. This visual hierarchy traveled through primary visual cortices, split into ventral and dorsal streams, and then reached high-order cortical and limbic regions with consistent posterior-to-anterior polarity in both face and place conditions. These patterns of functional connectivity also pointed to a deep integration among visual, high-order cortical, and limbic networks supporting novel visual processing.

Results

The demographics of the 22 epilepsy patients who received depth electrode implantation are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The experimental task design is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Each trial started with a fixation cross displayed at the center of the screen for 1, 1.5, or 2 s. Participants were then shown previously unseen images of faces or places, each displayed for 2 s. After a brief interstimulus interval (1, 1.5, or 2 s), a new image was presented. Participants were told to look closely at each image and remember its details. In the free-recall session, we asked participants to freely recall the images in order to gauge their level of concentration with the recall rate of 44.8% ± 4.7% (mean ± sem; Supplementary Table 1).

Throughout the experiment, iEEG signals were recorded using stereotactically implanted depth electrodes (10.9 ± 0.4 electrodes per patient) targeting brain regions functionally associated with visual information processing (see Fig. 1B for electrode distribution in a representative patient). During data preprocessing, raw iEEG signals were continuously recorded throughout the picture-viewing task. The signals were filtered into non-overlapping 10 Hz bands within the high-gamma range (60–160 Hz, segmented into 60–70, 70–80, 80–90, 90–100, 100–110, 110–120, 120–130, 130–140, 140–150, 150–160 Hz), and the absolute values of the analytic signals in each narrow band were extracted. The envelope of each band was normalized and subsequently averaged across all bands to generate a single HFB amplitude time series, which reflected localized neuronal population activity (Fig. 1C).

Of the 1008 contacts analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 1), we identified 234 visually responsive contacts, the spatial distribution of which is illustrated in Fig. 2A. Responsive contacts were predominantly located in key visual processing regions, including the occipital cortex (OC, n = 21), parietal cortex (PC, n = 53), and frontal cortex (FC, n = 33) along the dorsal visual pathway. They were also located in the temporal cortex (TC, n = 67), hippocampus (HIPP, n = 38), and amygdala (AMY, n = 22) within the ventral visual pathway. Since most OC-responsive contacts were positioned outside the V1/V2 regions (19/21), the OC was treated as a single entity in further analyses. Regarding the distribution of responsive contacts across different regions, the proportion of activated contacts in the OC accounted for 65.6% of the total (21/32), followed by the PC (33.8%, 53/157), AMY (30.6%, 22/72), HIPP (23.5%, 38/162), TC (20.4%, 67/328), and FC (12.8%, 33/257). This pattern indicated a gradual decline in the likelihood of electrode responsiveness to stimulation from posterior to anterior regions of the brain, observed in both the dorsal and ventral visual pathways.

Our analysis primarily examined HFB activity, a well-established marker of localized neuronal population activity. As depicted in Fig. 2B, when participants encountered a novel image, electrodes displayed robust, consistent HFB responses across multiple visual processing-related regions. While fMRI data delineated the key pathways involved in visual information processing17, the precise timing and sequential activation of brain regions during novel visual perception remain less understood. To investigate how different visual areas interact when perceiving a novel visual stimulus, we used intracranial depth electrodes to record iEEG signals, focusing on HFB activity in response to visual input.

According to the classical hierarchical model of visual perception, visual information is transmitted from the lateral geniculate nucleus to the primary visual cortex (V1) in the OC before diverging into two parallel pathways: the dorsal (“Where”) pathway, which processes spatial scenes and actions, and the ventral (“What”) pathway, which processes object recognition, including faces, gestures, and movements6. To test this model, response latencies across visual cortical regions were examined using simultaneous recordings from all contacts. A latency analysis was conducted on trial-level normalized HFB amplitude data for each region of interest (ROI). The baseline HFB amplitude was defined as the signal recorded 400 ms before stimulus onset. The response latency was determined by a significant and sustained amplitude increase (p < 0.05, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test) lasting at least 50 ms. Trial-averaged normalized HFB amplitude for a representative responsive contact within a given region is shown in Fig. 3A.

To refine the direction of information flow within each brain region, the TC, PC, and FC were subdivided into their respective subregions (e.g., temporal cortex into lateral temporal lobe, fusiform gyrus, and parahippocampal cortex; parietal cortex into inferior parietal lobe, superior parietal lobe, and precuneus cortex; frontal cortex into middle frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, and superior frontal gyrus). The fastest visual responses were recorded in the OC (median/25th to 75th percentiles: 75/48 to 116 ms, n = 20), followed by the precuneus cortex (PreC, 111/83 to 163 ms, n = 19) and the parahippocampal cortex and fusiform gyrus (PHC & FG, 121/66 to 200 ms, n = 39). These findings indicate near-simultaneous activation of the dorsal and ventral pathways. Within the dorsal pathway, response latencies increased progressively from the inferior parietal lobe (IPL, 125/83 to 164 ms, n = 13) to the middle frontal gyrus (MFG, 185/158 to 239 ms, n = 7), followed by the superior parietal lobe (SPL, 189/151 to 303 ms, n = 10), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG, 209/112 to 355 ms, n = 10), and superior frontal gyrus (SFG, 268/238 to 289 ms, n = 7). In the ventral pathway, following initial activation in the PHC and FG, responses were observed in the lateral temporal lobe (LTL, 137/112 to 207 ms, n = 10), HIPP (284/206 to 452 ms, n = 30), and AMY (404/326 to 458 ms, n = 12) (Fig. 3B, C). These findings revealed a posterior-to-anterior latency gradient, with responses progressing from the OC, PreC, PHC & FG, IPL, and SPL to the LTL, FC, and limbic regions. Additionally, similar propagation patterns emerged at the individual participant level (Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Data 1).

To explore whether propagation patterns differed between faces and places, we separately analyzed and identified face-active and place-active contacts (Fig. 4A). We analyzed the onset latencies of these two contacts types (see “Methods”). In face-active contacts, following an initial response in the OC (105/71 to 135 ms, n = 12), activations were recorded in parallel along the dorsal pathway, including the IPL (120/77 to 152 ms, n = 10), IFG (166/96 to 257 ms, n = 6), PreC (169/126 to 208 ms, n = 10), SPL (173/156 to 248 ms, n = 7), MFG (194/179 to 212 ms, n = 3), and SFG (274/273 to 277 ms, n = 3), and along the ventral pathway, including the PHC & FG (123/56 to 152 ms, n = 20), LTL (146/109 to 213 ms, n = 12), HIPP (290/251 to 425 ms, n = 10), and AMY (381/264 to 417 ms, n = 9) (Fig. 4B). In place-active contacts, after initial activation in the OC (60/33 to 96 ms, n = 12), parallel activations were recorded along the dorsal pathway, including the PreC (123/99 to 145 ms, n = 18), IPL (167/82 to 231 ms, n = 4), MFG (220/163 to 240 ms, n = 6), SPL (230/158 to 294 ms, n = 5), IFG (263/129 to 420 ms, n = 7), and SFG (316/306 to 326 ms, n = 2), and along the ventral pathway, including the PHC & FG (138/82 to 224 ms, n = 40), LTL (192/170 to 350 ms, n = 3), HIPP (251/181 to 416 ms, n = 16), and AMY (232 ms, n = 1) (Fig. 4C). Additionally, within-subject propagation patterns mirrored group-level findings for both face- and place-active contacts (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C and Supplementary Data 2, 3).

Collectively, the latency and cross-correlation analyses indicated that after the initial reception and organization of novel visual information in the OC, processing occurs via two parallel yet interconnected pathways. The ventral visual cortical stream, involving more complex routes, required longer for novel signals to propagate to the limbic regions, as evidenced by the delayed responses in corresponding electrodes.

Discussion

The study aimed to clarify the temporal dynamics and functional connectivity of visual processing across multiple brain regions. To accomplish this, we recruited epilepsy patients with intracranial electrodes and administered a well-established picture-viewing task. HFB activity was recorded across an extensive network of brain regions, revealing strong associations with visual perception, emotion, and memory. Three key findings emerged. First, we identified responsive sites in both the ventral and dorsal visual streams, as well as in higher-order cortical areas (LTL and FC) and limbic regions (Fig. 2). Second, hierarchical, back-to-front activation patterns were observed, beginning in primary visual regions and propagating toward higher-order cortical areas (Figs. 3, 4). Third, cross-correlation analysis revealed extensive functional connectivity and directional information flow both within visual regions and between these regions and limbic structures, supporting the interplay between visual perception and cognition (Figs. 5, 6).

The study focused on the HFB activity (60–160 Hz) in response to visual tasks, which corresponds to the broadband high-gamma responses. This activity has been shown to correlate strongly with both local spike activity and BOLD signals18,19. In the human primary visual cortex, high-gamma activity (HGA, 60–150 Hz) has been demonstrated as a reliable signal for estimating receptive field size, with a stronger correlation to local spiking activity than low-gamma activity (30–60 Hz)20. Rapid and pulsed HGA activity in both primary and higher visual cortices has been linked to visual perception16,21,22. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that sharply enhanced HFB activity was strongly associated with visual perception (Fig. 2). Beyond its role in sensory processing, HFB activity has been implicated in cognitive functions such as visual memory encoding8 and retrieval8,23, attentional processing24, and executive functions25, making it a valuable physiological marker of visual processing.

The double-stream model of visual processing proposes that visual information is processed hierarchically and in a distributed manner. The ventral visual stream, responsible for object recognition, extends from the primary visual cortex to the TC, while the dorsal visual stream, involved in spatial perception and action execution, extends to the PC1,26. In this study, we used the unique opportunity to directly examine the spatiotemporal distribution of neural responses to novel visual stimuli. Our findings demonstrated widespread neural activation, extending beyond the core areas typically associated with the conventional visual stream model (Fig. 2). These observations confirmed and expanded upon previous findings from iEEG8 and magnetoencephalography (MEG) studies27. Additionally, although a spatial gradient separation of the selectivity for faces and places was reported in the ventral temporal lobe15,28, we found that both the PHC and FG contained sites responsive to both faces and places, which was also consistent with recent iEEG research11. A neuroimaging study also pointed out that the PHC could be selective for both places and faces29. Future studies should systematically investigate the role of category-selective neurons in humans to further elucidate these findings.

To further investigate activation timing, we conducted an onset latency analysis of HFB activity in visually responsive cortical sites. This analysis confirmed two parallel visual information flows from the OC to the parietal and temporal cortices, followed by activation of frontal and limbic brain areas (hippocampus and amygdala) at later stages (Fig. 3B). A prior MEG study using a visual object recognition task similarly found that OC activity appeared about 50 ms after stimulus onset, with simultaneous activation of the dorsal and ventral visual stream regions occurring within 90–150 ms27,30. Additionally, an intracranial event-related potential study of visual recognition tasks also supported this activation sequence31. Another iEEG study on visually guided motor tasks showed that the OC was preferentially activated during visual cue presentation, followed by the sequential activation of the dorsal and ventral visual streams, and, later, the hippocampus, FC, and central areas32. Moreover, broadband neural oscillations (50–500 Hz) induced by novel picture presentation exhibited a similar processing architecture, progressing from the primary visual cortex to higher-order cortical regions via the ventral visual stream8. Our category-specific analysis uncovered further spatiotemporal response dynamics. After finer discrimination of contacts activated for faces and places respectively, we observed that in face-active contacts, the IFG preceded the PreC and SPL, with the hippocampus and amygdala showing the latest responses. In place-active contacts, the hippocampus and amygdala preceded the IFG and SFG (Fig. 4). Collectively, these studies and our findings provide a detailed account of the spatiotemporal dynamics of neural activity during the first few hundred milliseconds of novel visual information processing, characterized by the cascading activation of the ventral and dorsal visual pathways, leading to higher-order cortex and limbic areas.

Connectomic studies have demonstrated not only robust functional connectivity and cascade activation within the ventral and dorsal visual stream regions but also strong interconnectivity between these two pathways. This synergy is thought to play a crucial role in visual recognition by integrating object attributes (e.g., shape, markings, and color) with spatial location information5–7. Cross-correlation analysis during visual stimulation revealed that the IPL may serve as a crucial hub for place information processing, displaying robust functional connections and outward information flow to various regions within the PC, as well as to medial temporal visual regions, the FC, and limbic areas (Fig. 5). Prior studies have linked IPL to spatial object recognition, and damage to this region has been associated with spatial and object neglect, as well as agnosia33. Furthermore, the IPL has been shown to be closely connected to the superior temporal sulcus (STS), forming the “what” visual stream. This pathway is responsive to moving objects and facial expressions and is involved in emotional processing through its interactions with the orbitofrontal cortex34. Functional connectomics has also revealed that the IPL interacts with the PHC to update spatial representations based on self-motion17,35.

In addition, our results indicated that the visual information was flowing from the lateral frontal and temporal cortices to the limbic regions (Figs. 5, 6). These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that enhanced synchronization between higher-order cortex and hippocampus may be associated with attentional36 or other cognitive processes, such as semantic4 and memory processing37. Together, these results suggest that dynamic interactions and directed information flow among visual, cortical, and limbic regions are fundamental to novel visual processing.

Notably, there are a number of methodological limitations to take into account with our study. First, the number of participants was limited and each participant’s spatial coverage of intracranial electrodes was not uniform. This restricts our findings’ statistical power and generalizability, necessitating careful interpretation of group-level propagation and specific subject-level pattern analysis10. Second, we may have missed a wider spectral range that would have captured the entire complexity of neural dynamics during novel visual perception because our analysis was limited to the HFB range. In addition, the bivariate cross-correlation analysis cannot differentiate between direct and indirect functional connections, account for nonlinear neural interactions, or model complex network-level dynamics. Future research will better clarify the hierarchical structure of the visual processing stream using sophisticated multivariate techniques, such as graph-theoretic methods. Additionally, in this study, we did not correlate neural signals with behavioral data; however, this analysis will be carried out in subsequent research.

Methods

Participants

This study enrolled 22 patients (13 females) aged 16 to 65 years (mean ± SD: 28.4 ± 9.9 years). All participants underwent intracranial depth electrode implantation for presurgical evaluation of drug-resistant epilepsy, with electrode placement determined solely by clinical requirements. Each patient completed a visual task as part of the experimental protocol. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinqiao Hospital, Third Military Medical University (2021-Research No. 108-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Visual task paradigm

The visual task was adapted from our prior research38. For each participant, the experiment spanned two days. Each day included two runs of a picture-viewing task, which comprised 14 novel images (7 faces and 7 places; Fig. 1A), the order of which was randomized for each run. The selection of novel images was derived from two database39,40. Each image was shown for 2 s, with interstimulus intervals randomly set at 1, 1.5, or 2 s. Participants were first instructed to attentively observe and retain details of each image. They were then asked to freely recall the pictures, providing not only the name of the person or place but also at least three additional features. This was done to ensure participants maintained their focus on the novel images. We deemed a free-recall event unsuccessful if the participant was unable to recall at least 3 features of the picture.

Stimulus presentation was controlled using Psychtoolbox-3 (https://psychtoolbox.org/) implemented in MATLAB 2017b (MathWorks, Natick, USA). Images were displayed on a standard LCD screen positioned at a comfortable viewing distance in front of the participants. We made efforts to ensure that each participant’s studies were carried out at the same time of day. However, the tasks were postponed for half a day on the condition that the patients had just had an epileptic seizure within the previous 6 h. It should be noted that five participants experienced epileptic seizures during the task, which prevented them from completing all the tasks. The detailed number of pictures for each participant is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Electrode localization

Electrode localization was performed using the Fieldtrip toolbox41. Electrode locations were identified by coregistering preoperative MRI and postoperative CT scans, which were aligned to the AC-PC coordinate system using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The cortical surface and subcortical structures were reconstructed and segmented using FreeSurfer 7.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/), following the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas to determine the brain regions associated with electrode placement. Each contact was manually verified using the 3D Slicer software (https://www.slicer.org/), and a total of 1652 contacts (75.1 ± 4.3 per patient) were included in further analysis.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

iEEG data were recorded using a Nihon Kohden system (256-channel amplifier, model JE120A). Signals were analog-filtered above 0.01 Hz and digitized at 1000 Hz or 2000 Hz, then resampled to 1000 Hz for uniformity. The triggers were simultaneously recorded in DC channels to maintain accurate synchronization with the stimulus presentation. Data preprocessing steps included: (1) Exclusion of contacts that were located within seizure onset zones assessed by the clinical epileptology team; (2) Visual inspection for noisy channels, which were subsequently discarded from analysis; (3) Minimization of line noise interference (typically 50 Hz and harmonics) using a fourth-order Butterworth notch filter; (4) Application of bipolar referencing by pairing adjacent contacts along the same depth electrode.

High-frequency broadband signal

The high-frequency broadband (HFB) signal was defined as the mean normalized power within the 60–160 Hz frequency range. HFB power was calculated using the following steps42: (1) Band-pass filtering the signal into non-overlapping 10-Hz bands using a Kaiser windowed band-pass finite impulse response (FIR) filter; (2) Extracting the envelope of each narrow-band signal by computing the absolute value of the analytic signal derived via Hilbert transform; (3) Normalizing each amplitude time series by dividing it by its mean; (4) Averaging all normalized narrow-band envelopes; (5) Multiplying the averaged time series by the mean amplitude across all bands to restore signal units to volts.

Inter-ictal epileptic discharges (IEDs) and artifact detection protocols were implemented to identify and exclude potential interferences in HFB signals. Trials containing IEDs or artifacts were discarded. IED detection followed established approaches43 and consisted of: (1) Band-pass filtering the data in the 25–80 Hz range (zero-phase, fourth-order Butterworth); (2) Identifying events where Hilbert envelopes of filtered signals exceeded 2.5 times the baseline; (3) Eliminating events where the unfiltered envelope did not exceed 2.5 times the baseline. After visual inspection, 4.08% ± 1.05% trials contained false-positive IED events.

Artifact detection was performed using the absolute amplitude of the raw HFB signal, the gradient (difference between adjacent time points) of the raw HFB signal, and the amplitude of the signals after applying a 250-Hz high-pass filter. A time point was marked as an artifact if it exceeded either a z-score of 5 in any measure or a conjunction of a z-score of 3 in both gradient/high-frequency measures.

During the viewing stage, HFB signals from each electrode were epoched relative to stimulus onset (−1000 to 3000 ms post-stimulus) and expressed as a fractional change by normalizing each data point to the mean amplitude of a pre-stimulus baseline (−400 to 0 ms), averaged across all remaining trials. Before statistical analysis, amplitude ratios were transformed into decibel (dB) values using 10×log10 transformation.

Identification of visually responsive contacts

To identify visually responsive contacts, we evaluated the normalized HFB responses during the post-stimulus period (100−500 ms) relative to the pre-stimulus baseline (−400 to 0 ms) across all trials. This comparison was performed using a one-sided, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test42. Given that this test was conducted across all electrode contacts, all p-values (across contacts and patients) were pooled, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled (pFDR < 0.05). Contacts exhibiting significant HFB responses were classified as visually responsive44. A one-sided, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was also used to identify two classes of contacts: (1) face-active contacts, that is, those showing significant HFB responses across trials for faces (pFDR < 0.05); (2) place-active contacts, that is, those showing significant HFB responses across trials for places (pFDR < 0.05). Co-active contacts were defined as the overlap in these two contact types.

The following ROIs were included in the analysis: occipital cortex (cuneus cortex, lateral occipital cortex, lingual gyrus, pericalcarine cortex), parahippocampal cortex, fusiform gyrus, lateral temporal lobe (superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyrus, banks of superior temporal sulcus, transverse temporal gyrus, temporal pole), inferior parietal lobe, superior parietal lobe, precuneus cortex, inferior frontal gyrus (opercular, triangular, and orbital parts), superior frontal gyrus, and middle frontal gyrus (rostral middle frontal gyrus, caudal middle frontal gyrus). Therefore, a total of 1008 contacts (45.8 ± 3.0 per patient) were hence included in the analysis.

Latency analysis

To determine the exact response latency in each visually responsive contact, we compared the HFB amplitude at each time point post-stimulus to the pre-stimulus baseline (−400 to 0 ms) using a one-sided, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank. Response latency was defined as the first time point at which the HFB amplitude exceeded a p-value of 0.05 and remained significant for at least 50 ms42.

Cross-correlation

For each electrode pair, cross-correlation analysis was performed using the xcorr function in MATLAB, with a [−1, 1]-second maximum lag during the visual task45. The mean HFB power across all trials was calculated for each electrode, and cross-correlation for each participant was performed across all electrode pairs. The resulting values were then averaged across all electrode pairs spanning each brain region. Functionally connected regions were identified by high positive peak values (rpeak). If one region consistently preceded another in HFB activity, the peak lag (rlag) was expected to be negative. Only strongly connected electrode pairs, defined by a threshold rpeak > 0.1, were included in further analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB 2017b. To identify visually responsive contacts and confirm the posterior-to-anterior directionality, the one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate differences between two groups as well as comparisons within groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Summary values were reported as the median with 25th–75th percentiles or mean ± sem unless otherwise stated. Data collection and analyses were not performed in a blinded manner.

For the identification of visually responsive contacts, statistical testing was performed at the individual trial level. For all other analyses (e.g., latency analysis, cross-correlation), trials were first averaged within each contact, and then statistical comparisons were performed across independent contacts or participants. All experiments were performed on 22 independent participants. Electrode contacts from the same participant were treated as independent observations unless otherwise specified. The number of biologically independent samples for each analysis is specified in the figure legends and results sections. The reproducibility of the observed effects was confirmed at the individual participant level, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3. The sample size (n = 22 patients) was determined by clinical availability of patients undergoing intracranial electrode implantation for epilepsy monitoring. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes.
