Active shooters and gun-free zones: emotional versus legal motivations – author's reply
Authors: Paul M. Reeping
Published: 2024

Thank you Lane et al.1 for the letter.

You claim that a shooter chooses to attack a place due to an event, and not based on the presence of a single law.1 We agree, and in doing so, you have helped illuminate a major reason why gun-free zones may be protective.

Many active shootings or mass shootings occur after emotional or distressing events (i.e., the loss of a job, a domestic incident, bullying, harsh disagreements, or other trauma). If individuals are in a place where guns are already prohibited, then, regardless of the escalation of the event, an active shooting is less likely to occur at that moment. If someone had to leave a gun-free location to get a firearm and then return, they might have time to rethink–what would likely be–a passionate, impulsive action. In this way, being in a place where gun carrying is less common could prevent active shootings.

Consider a more specific example. A company has a policy that no guns are allowed in the office (e.g. carrying a firearm would result in immediate dismissal). A disgruntled employee is unexpectedly fired from his job, which he feels is unfair, and that day he does not have a gun on him because of the policy. Thus, an active shooting does not occur, even if the employee may feel passionate enough at the moment to commit one. If the same employee, who carries a firearm everywhere else, had been carrying it in the office, an active shooting might have occurred.

We also agree that many gun-free zones are not well-enforced, but some are. Movie theatres, stadiums, courtrooms, museums, and many schools have implemented specific tactics, e.g. metal detectors, to prevent firearms from being carried onto the premises as part of their gun-free zone policies. If other gun-free zones are completely ignored and, in effect, become gun-allowing, including them in the analysis would bias the results toward the null. This is because some of the treated units (the gun-free zones) would not actually be “treated.” In this way, our inclusion of all gun-free zones, regardless of their enforcement, is akin to the “intent-to-treat” principle utilized in randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

In RCTs, participants are analysed in the groups to which they were originally assigned, regardless of whether they fully complied with the treatment. This approach provides a more conservative estimate of the treatment effect, acknowledging real-world non-compliance. Similarly, by considering all gun-free zones (both enforced and unenforced), our analysis may dilute the true effect of gun-free zones in preventing shootings. Therefore, our results are conservative, and it is thus possible that strictly enforced gun-free zones would have an even greater preventive effect.

Finally, the statement, “criminals do not respect laws,” is cynical and superlative. If this were absolutely true, then why do any laws exist at all? Laws of course serve as a deterrent to crime, a way to keep people and places safer, and are an indication of societal norms, even if they are, at times, violated or unenforced. We considered this imperfection in explaining the mechanisms by which gun-free zones may prevent active shootings.
