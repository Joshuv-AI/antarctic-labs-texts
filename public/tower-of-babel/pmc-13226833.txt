Vitamin D status tracks inflammation perfusion and pregnancy in polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Kexin Wang, Ruifang Wang, Zhuhua Cai, Junxian Ma, Guosheng Li, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is characterized by metabolic and inflammatory heterogeneity, but the relative associations of vitamin D status and insulin resistance with inflammatory activation, uterine perfusion, and reproductive outcomes remain unclear. We analyzed a prospective discovery cohort of 580 affected women and 332 controls, followed by multicenter retrospective external validation in 750 additional affected women. Multivariable linear and logistic regression analyses showed that lower serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was more broadly and consistently associated than the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) with inflammatory markers, uterine artery perfusion indices, recurrent pregnancy loss, and live birth. In contrast, associations of HOMA-IR were narrower, mainly involving C-reactive protein and uterine perfusion, and were less stable after mutual adjustment and external validation. These findings suggest that vitamin D status may be a more informative marker than insulin resistance for inflammatory-vascular and reproductive risk stratification in women with PCOS.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine-metabolic disorders among women of reproductive age and is frequently accompanied by insulin resistance (IR) and systemic low-grade chronic inflammation. These metabolic and immune abnormalities are widely considered important features of PCOS and have been linked to both reproductive dysfunction and long-term metabolic risk.1,2,3 Extensive evidence indicates that IR is closely associated with several key manifestations of PCOS, including hyperandrogenism, impaired ovulatory function, and adverse metabolic profiles.4,5,6 However, inflammatory activation in PCOS is not fully explained by IR alone, and some women exhibit persistent inflammatory abnormalities even in the absence of overt IR, suggesting heterogeneity in the pathways underlying the inflammatory phenotype of PCOS.7

Vitamin D has also attracted considerable attention in PCOS because of its immunomodulatory and metabolic regulatory properties.8 Through binding to the vitamin D receptor, vitamin D can influence the differentiation and cytokine secretion of monocytes, macrophages, and T cells, thereby modulating pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory immune responses.9 Previous studies have shown that women with PCOS frequently have lower serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations, and reduced 25(OH)D levels have been associated with impaired insulin sensitivity, higher inflammatory cytokine levels, and reduced ovulatory function.10,11,12,13 Although both IR and vitamin D deficiency have been linked to the PCOS inflammatory phenotype, their relative associations with systemic inflammation, uterine perfusion, and pregnancy outcomes remain insufficiently characterized.14,15 In particular, few studies have directly compared 25(OH)D and IR within the same analytical framework across inflammatory, vascular, and reproductive domains.

Systemic low-grade chronic inflammation may not only accompany metabolic abnormalities in PCOS but may also be associated with reproductive outcomes.16,17 Increased levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) have been associated with impaired follicular development, poorer embryo quality, and a higher risk of pregnancy loss in women with PCOS.18 In addition, uterine artery Doppler indices, including the systolic/diastolic ratio (S/D), resistance index (RI), and pulsatility index (PI), together with peripheral natural killer cell percentage (NK%), may reflect the inflammatory-vascular milieu relevant to implantation and pregnancy maintenance.19,20 However, evidence regarding whether vitamin D levels or IR status show broader and more stable associations with these inflammatory and uterine perfusion parameters remains limited, particularly in studies with independent external validation.

Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the relative associations of serum 25(OH)D and the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) with inflammatory markers, uterine perfusion indices, and pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS. We further sought to validate the main findings in two independent external cohorts from different centers, in order to assess the robustness and cross-center consistency of the observed patterns.

In terms of inflammatory and immune markers, women with PCOS had higher CRP (7.11 vs. 6.28 mg/L) and IL-6 (4.23 vs. 2.70 pg/mL) levels and lower interleukin-10 (IL-10) (2.59 vs. 2.77 pg/mL) levels than controls, whereas TNF-α and NK% did not differ significantly between groups. Serum 25(OH)D is more broadly associated than HOMA-IR with inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers in PCOS

In women with PCOS, serum 25(OH)D, FINS, and HOMA-IR were correlated with multiple systemic inflammatory and uterine perfusion parameters (Figure S1). Overall, 25(OH)D showed inverse correlations with CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, NK%, and uterine artery Doppler indices, whereas it was positively correlated with IL-10. In contrast, FINS and HOMA-IR generally showed weaker correlations in the opposite direction.

To further examine the independent associations of these metabolic indicators with inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed (Table 2). Overall, the combined-exposure group exhibited the most unfavorable profile. Compared with the non-IR and IR-only groups, women in the combined-exposure group had the highest levels of IL-6, CRP, TNF-α, NK%, and uterine artery resistance indices, including PI, RI, and S/D, whereas IL-10 levels were the lowest.

By contrast, the IR-only group showed a more limited pattern of abnormalities. Although CRP and uterine artery Doppler indices were higher than those in the non-IR group, differences in several other inflammatory markers were less marked. Moreover, the differences between the non-IR and IR-only groups were not uniform across all markers, and some parameters in the IR-only group were comparable to or even lower than those in the non-IR group. In particular, the most pronounced deterioration across both inflammatory and uterine perfusion parameters was consistently observed in the combined-exposure group rather than in the IR-only group.

For RPL, higher serum 25(OH)D was consistently associated with a lower risk across all three models. In the unadjusted model, the odds ratio (OR) for 25(OH)D was 0.87 (95% CI: 0.85–0.89), and this association remained unchanged after adjustment for maternal age, BMI, blood pressure, lipid profile, and lifestyle factors [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.85–0.89]. After further mutual adjustment for HOMA-IR, the inverse association remained robust (aOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.86–0.90). By contrast, HOMA-IR was positively associated with RPL in the crude and partially adjusted models, but its effect was attenuated after mutual adjustment (aOR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.01–1.47).

A similar pattern was observed for live birth. Higher serum 25(OH)D was consistently associated with a greater likelihood of live birth across all three models, with OR ranging from 1.17 to 1.19 in the crude and partially adjusted models and remaining significant after mutual adjustment (aOR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.12–1.26). In contrast, HOMA-IR was inversely associated with live birth in the crude and partially adjusted models, but this association was attenuated after mutual adjustment for 25(OH)D and was no longer statistically significant (aOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.69–1.25).

The main associations of serum 25(OH)D are reproduced in independent external cohorts

An independent external validation cohort comprising 1624 women was assembled from two centers, including 1110 from the Luoyang cohort and 514 from the Wenzhou cohort. Overall clinical characteristics and pregnancy outcomes are summarized in Table 5. Discussion

In this multicenter study with external validation, serum 25(OH)D showed broader and more stable associations than HOMA-IR with inflammatory markers, uterine perfusion, and reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS.

PCOS is widely recognized as a heterogeneous endocrine-metabolic disorder in which low-grade chronic inflammation is closely linked to both metabolic abnormalities and impaired reproductive function.21,22 IR has traditionally been regarded as a key component of this process because of its associations with hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, adipokine imbalance, and oxidative stress.23,24 Prior studies have also shown that CRP is among the inflammatory markers most consistently associated with IR-related metabolic risk in PCOS.25,26 In the present study, however, HOMA-IR was independently associated mainly with CRP and uterine artery Doppler indices, whereas its associations with IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α were weak or absent after multivariable adjustment. This pattern suggests that the inflammatory phenotype of PCOS may not be fully captured by HOMA-IR alone and that the contribution of IR may vary across biological domains and patient subgroups.1,27,28,29

One plausible explanation for the weaker independent associations of HOMA-IR is that insulin dysregulation in PCOS is heterogeneous. Earlier mechanistic work suggested that hyperinsulinemia in PCOS may be influenced not only by peripheral metabolic dysfunction but also by neuroendocrine regulation. In particular, a classic naloxone study showed that opioid receptor blockade selectively reduced insulin responses in hyperinsulinemic women with polycystic ovary disease without affecting glycemia or gonadotropin secretion, suggesting that hyperinsulinemia in PCOS may not be purely peripheral in origin.30 Longer-term work with naltrexone further supported a modulatory role of endogenous opioid pathways in exaggerated insulin responses in at least a subset of women with PCOS.31,32 Thus, our findings do not argue against the importance of IR in PCOS. Rather, they suggest that HOMA-IR may not fully reflect the heterogeneous mechanisms through which insulin-related abnormalities are linked to inflammation.

By contrast, serum 25(OH)D demonstrated broader associations across inflammatory and vascular measures. In the discovery cohort, lower 25(OH)D was independently associated with higher CRP and IL-6, lower IL-10, and greater uterine artery resistance. This overall pattern was also supported in the external and pooled analyses of selected inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers. These findings are compatible with the known immunomodulatory functions of vitamin D and with prior literature linking lower 25(OH)D levels in PCOS to unfavorable metabolic and reproductive profiles.33,34,35 Mechanistically, vitamin D has been linked to the modulation of innate and adaptive immune responses, cytokine balance, and endothelial homeostasis, all of which may be relevant to the inflammatory-vascular phenotype observed in PCOS.33,34,36 At the same time, our data should be interpreted as showing stronger and more stable associations, rather than proving that vitamin D is the sole or primary causal origin of inflammation in PCOS. It is also possible that serum 25(OH)D partly reflects broader nutritional, behavioral, metabolic, or seasonal influences that are themselves linked to inflammatory status.

The phenotype-stratified analyses further supported the view that the coexistence of vitamin D deficiency and IR was associated with the least favorable inflammatory-vascular profile. Women in the combined-exposure group had the highest levels of CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, NK%, and uterine artery resistance indices, whereas IL-10 levels were lowest. At the same time, the differences between the non-IR and IR-only groups were not uniform across all markers, and some values in the IR-only group were comparable to or lower than those in the non-IR group. Taken together, these findings suggest an additive pattern when vitamin D deficiency and IR coexist, while also indicating that IR alone may not consistently correspond to a more adverse inflammatory phenotype.

The pregnancy outcome analyses yielded a similar pattern. In the discovery cohort, higher serum 25(OH)D was consistently associated with lower RPL risk and higher live birth across all three hierarchical models. These associations were further reproduced in the independent external cohorts and pooled analyses, strengthening the clinical relevance of the main findings. In contrast, although HOMA-IR was associated with RPL and live birth in crude models, these associations weakened after mutual adjustment and were not consistently retained in the external or pooled datasets. These results suggest that, within the present analytical framework, serum 25(OH)D was a more stable correlate of reproductive outcome variation than HOMA-IR. Previous studies have likewise linked vitamin D status to reproductive success in PCOS and related infertile populations.37,38 Interventional or secondary-analysis data have suggested associations with ovulation induction outcomes, clinical improvement, and pregnancy-related reproductive endpoints.39

The main strength of this study lies in its integrated comparison of 25(OH)D and HOMA-IR across inflammatory markers, uterine perfusion indices, and pregnancy outcomes within the same analytical framework, together with external validation in two independent centers. This design extends prior literature that has often examined these components separately and provides a more comparative view of the inflammatory-vascular phenotype of PCOS. In this sense, the present work is better interpreted as a refinement of existing concepts rather than a paradigm shift.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that serum vitamin D status may warrant consideration as a practical marker for inflammatory-vascular and reproductive risk stratification in women with PCOS. While causality cannot be inferred from the present study, routine assessment of vitamin D status may help identify women who could benefit from closer follow-up and more individualized preconception counseling. Future prospective multicenter studies, ideally incorporating repeated measurements, more detailed metabolic phenotyping, and interventional designs, are needed to clarify the temporal and biological relationships among vitamin D status, IR, inflammation, and reproductive outcomes in PCOS.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although the study now includes multicenter external validation, the discovery cohort was derived from a single center, and the external validation component was retrospective, which may introduce differences in data completeness and outcome ascertainment across centers. Second, HOMA-IR is a pragmatic surrogate of IR and may not fully capture dynamic or tissue-specific insulin signaling. Third, although we adjusted for major clinical and lifestyle covariates, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the observational design does not allow causal inference. Fourth, data on vitamin D supplementation, sun exposure, dietary intake, and seasonal variation were not uniformly available, which may have influenced serum 25(OH)D levels. Finally, not all inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers were equally available across external centers, so the cross-cohort validation of mechanistic markers was more limited than that of pregnancy outcomes.

Method details

Study design and participants

This study consisted of a prospective single-center discovery cohort and a multicenter retrospective external validation cohort.

The discovery cohort was established at the Reproductive Medicine Center of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University between January and December 2024. Women of reproductive age who underwent preconception evaluation or fertility-treatment screening during the study period were screened for eligibility. According to diagnostic criteria, they were classified into a PCOS group and a non-PCOS control group. PCOS was diagnosed based on the modified Rotterdam criteria (2023), requiring at least two of the following three features after exclusion of related disorders40: (1) clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism; (2) oligo-ovulation or anovulation; (3) polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound. The control group consisted of non-PCOS women undergoing routine preconception health assessment, characterized by regular menstruation, absence of hyperandrogenic manifestations, and normal ovarian morphology.

For external validation, an independent multicenter retrospective cohort was assembled from two centers: the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Science and Technology (Luoyang cohort; January 2021 to December 2025) and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou cohort; September 2023 to March 2025). In these external cohorts, eligible women were identified through hospital electronic medical record systems, and corresponding clinical, laboratory, ultrasound, and pregnancy outcome data were retrospectively extracted for validation analyses.

Across cohorts, the inclusion criteria were: (1) age 20–40 years; (2) available pregnancy outcome information or follow-up records; (3) available measurements of serum 25(OH)D, FINS, and fasting blood glucose (FBG) for calculation of the HOMA-IR; and (4) available inflammatory markers and uterine artery Doppler parameters for the discovery analyses and selected cross-cohort validation analyses. Exclusion criteria were: (1) diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, hepatic or renal dysfunction, autoimmune disease, or chronic inflammatory disease; (2) chromosomal abnormalities or hereditary disorders in either partner; (3) tubal obstruction, uterine malformations, intrauterine adhesions, or other structural reproductive abnormalities; (4) male-factor infertility; (5) ectopic pregnancy, hydatidiform mole, or other abnormal early pregnancy; and (6) incomplete key clinical data or loss to follow-up.

Clinical data collection, laboratory measurements, and data harmonization

For the present analysis, all clinical, biochemical, ultrasound, and pregnancy outcome data in the discovery cohort were retrieved from the hospital electronic medical record system and matched using unique patient identifiers. Pregnancy outcomes were additionally ascertained through electronic medical record review and telephone follow-up. All women in the discovery cohort were followed until delivery or pregnancy termination. Trained researchers conducted structured follow-up calls, with outcomes independently checked by two investigators and verified by the supervising clinician.

In the external validation cohort, the corresponding clinical, laboratory, and ultrasound data were retrospectively retrieved from hospital electronic medical record systems and harmonized to the same variable definitions and units used in the discovery cohort. Across all cohorts, two researchers independently checked the extracted data, and discrepancies were resolved against the original records. All data were anonymized before analysis.

Demographic and clinical variables included maternal age, BMI, age at menarche, reproductive history, and lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, and educational level. Women in the discovery cohort underwent fasting blood sampling and transabdominal ultrasound on menstrual cycle days 3–5 (natural cycle or pre-stimulation natural cycle). Biochemical and endocrine measurements included fasting blood glucose, FINS, serum 25(OH)D, lipid profile [total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)], AMH, and the LH/FSH ratio.

Inflammatory and immune markers in the discovery cohort included CRP, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and NK%. Uterine artery Doppler parameters included PI, RI, and the S/D, measured bilaterally and averaged for analysis.

For the external validation cohort, corresponding laboratory, ultrasound, and pregnancy outcome variables were extracted from routine clinical records. HOMA-IR was recalculated uniformly across centers, and pregnancy outcomes were coded according to the same predefined criteria used in the discovery cohort. Selected inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers available across cohorts were used for limited cross-cohort validation of inflammatory and vascular association patterns.

Definitions, metabolic phenotypes, and pregnancy outcomes

IR was defined using the HOMA-IR (HOMA-IR = FBG × FINS / 22.5), with HOMA-IR ≥ 2.5 indicating IR.41 Vitamin D status was categorized based on serum 25(OH)D levels: deficiency (<30 nmol/L), insufficiency (30–50 nmol/L), and sufficiency (≥50 nmol/L).42

To examine the combined associations of vitamin D deficiency and IR, women with PCOS in the discovery cohort were classified into three metabolic phenotypes for the primary phenotype analysis: (1) non-IR group, defined as HOMA-IR <2.5; (2) IR-only group, defined as HOMA-IR ≥2.5 and 25(OH)D ≥30 nmol/L; and (3) combined-exposure group, defined as HOMA-IR ≥2.5 and 25(OH)D <30 nmol/L. Because the vitamin D deficiency only subgroup [HOMA-IR <2.5 and 25(OH)D <30 nmol/L] was small (n=22), it was not retained as a separate category in the primary phenotype analysis and was merged into the broader non-IR group to improve subgroup stability.

Pregnancy outcomes were defined as follows. RPL was defined as two or more failed pregnancies; clinical pregnancy was defined as ultrasound visualization of a gestational sac; live birth was defined as delivery of a live infant at ≥20 gestational weeks; adverse pregnancy was defined as any occurrence of biochemical pregnancy loss, spontaneous miscarriage, stillbirth, or fetal malformation.43,44,45 In the discovery cohort, pregnancy outcomes were obtained from electronic medical record review and structured telephone follow-up. In the external validation cohort, pregnancy outcomes were retrospectively extracted from hospital electronic medical record systems. Live birth and adverse pregnancy analyses were restricted to women with clinical pregnancy and available final pregnancy outcome data.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.5.1. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons were performed using the independent-sample t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Exploratory Spearman correlation analyses were additionally performed to visualize the correlations of serum 25(OH)D, FINS, and HOMA-IR with inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers in the discovery cohort.

In the discovery cohort, multivariable linear regression models were used to evaluate the associations of serum 25(OH)D, FINS, and HOMA-IR with inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers. β, B, and 95% CI for B were reported.

Comparisons across the three predefined metabolic phenotypes were performed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons when applicable.

For RPL and live birth in women with PCOS, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed in the discovery cohort, the external validation cohort, and pooled datasets. Three models were prespecified: Model 1, unadjusted; Model 2, adjusted for maternal age, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, lipid profile, and lifestyle factors; and Model 3, further mutually adjusted for serum 25(OH)D and HOMA-IR on the basis of Model 2. OR or aOR with 95% CI were reported. In pooled analyses, cohort/center was additionally included as a covariate.

To assess cross-cohort consistency for selected inflammatory and uterine perfusion markers, multivariable linear regression models were fitted in the discovery, external, and pooled PCOS cohorts, and the resulting β were visualized in a heatmap.

All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
