Alcohol use and alcohol use disorders as a mediator between common mental disorders and antiretroviral therapy adherence among people living with HIV in Tanzania
Authors: Florian Emanuel Ghaimo, Neema Allen Ng’unda, Samuel Chacha, Kim Madundo, Frank Kiwango, Karino Kilaye, Blandina T Mmbaga, Catherine A Staton, Ester Steven Mzilangwe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommon mental disorders (CMDs) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) are significant barriers to effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence among people living with HIV (PLHIV), especially in sub-Saharan Africa. Suboptimal adherence to ART contributes to increased morbidity and mortality in this population. CMD and AUD frequently co-occur, with alcohol often serving as a maladaptive coping mechanism for psychological distress, thereby compounding the negative impact on treatment outcomes. Understanding the mediating role of AUD in the relationship between CMD and ART adherence is essential for designing targeted interventions aimed at improving HIV treatment success.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between August and October 2023 in Moshi Municipality, Kilimanjaro. A multistage systematic sampling technique was used to recruit participants. Data were collected using structured sociodemographic and interviewer-administered, validated assessment tools. Statistical analyses included one-way ANOVA for continuous variables, chi-square tests for categorical variables, and logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Mediation analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.2, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe study involved 532 participants, with an average age of 46.6 ± 13.3 years; 71.4% were female. The prevalence of depression, anxiety, and ART non-adherence was 14.8%, 12.4%, and 10.7%, respectively. Among men, depression and anxiety were both significantly associated with ART non-adherence in model1 and 2 respectively (depression OR = 5.38, 95% CI: 1.80-16.08; OR = 5.10, 95% CI: 1.55–16.82. Anxiety OR = 5.12, 95% CI: 1.63–16.12; OR = 5.30, 95% CI: 1.48–18.92). Among women, only depression significantly increased ART non-adherence in all models, respectively (OR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.16–5.36; OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.13–5.59; OR = 3.26, 95% CI: 1.34–7.95). Alcohol use disorder significantly mediated the relationship between depression and ART non-adherence more substantially than alcohol use alone, with mediation effects up to 45.5%.ConclusionDepression is significantly associated with ART non-adherence in both genders, and AUD is a key mediator, especially among male participants. Background

Common mental disorders (CMDs) and alcohol use disorders (AUDs) are increasingly acknowledged as major barriers to effective HIV care and treatment, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where the burden of HIV remains disproportionately high [1]. Among people living with HIV (PLHIV), mental health conditions are notably prevalent, with approximately 31% experiencing depression and 29% experiencing anxiety disorders in low- and middle-income countries [2, 3]. These comorbid mental health conditions have been strongly linked to suboptimal adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART), undermining efforts to achieve viral suppression, reducing treatment efficacy, and contributing to higher rates of disease progression and mortality [1, 4].

Co-occurrence of CMDs and AUDs among PLHIV is common and has a compounding effect on ART adherence [5, 6]. Alcohol use, in particular, is frequently employed as a coping mechanism for psychological distress [5, 6]. However, it impairs cognitive function, exacerbates psychiatric symptoms, and directly interferes with medication adherence either through forgetfulness or intentional non-adherence due to perceived interactions between alcohol and ART [7]. High levels of alcohol use have also been associated with missed clinic appointments, poor engagement in care, diminished quality of life, and unfavorable treatment outcomes [8].

Globally, alcohol consumption continues to pose a substantial public health burden. In 2019 alone, approximately 2.6 million deaths were attributable to alcohol use, and AUDs accounted for an estimated 3.8% of the total global burden of disease [1, 9]. Among PLHIV, the prevalence of AUDs is significantly higher than in the general population, estimated at 29.8% and has been correlated with worse clinical outcomes, including lower CD4 + cell counts and elevated viral loads [10]. Such deterioration in HIV disease status may further heighten vulnerability to emotional disorders such as depression, creating a vicious cycle that perpetuates poor mental health and substandard treatment adherence [11].

Evidence from both high- and low-resource settings supports the interplay between mental health disorders, alcohol use, and ART non-adherence. A study conducted in the United States highlighted the widespread burden of psychiatric disorders among PLHIV and their disruptive impact on ART adherence, particularly in cases of major depressive disorder [12]. Research from sub-Saharan Africa echoes these findings, though it also emphasizes the influence of cultural norms such as HIV related stigma, socioeconomic challenges, and limitations within health systems on these associations [13, 14]. For instance, studies conducted across Sub-Saharan Africa reported ART non-adherence pooled rate of 34% predominantly linked to heavy alcohol consumption [1]. These behaviors have been associated with more rapid disease progression, emergence of drug-resistant HIV strains, and increased transmission risk [15]. Similarly, a study in Tanzania found that AUD significantly reduced ART adherence, particularly among individuals exhibiting depressive symptoms [3].

In Tanzania, the prevalence of AUD in the general population is approximately 6.8%, nearly double the WHO African regional average of 3.7% [16]. Among PLHIV specifically, recent estimates place the prevalence of AUD at 28.2%, indicating a substantial public health concern [3]. Comparable figures have been observed in other African countries, such as 28.77% in South Africa and 22.8% in Nigeria [17]. Furthermore, recent studies in Tanzania report the prevalence of common mental disorders among PLHIV at 12.48% for anxiety and 41% for depression, underscoring the high burden of co-occurring mental health conditions in this population [18, 19]. Notably, these common mental disorders have been shown to contribute to two to threefold higher rates of ART non-adherence, further complicating HIV care and treatment outcomes [20, 21]. Despite the magnitude of the issue, targeted interventions aimed at addressing AUDs and CMDs among PLHIV remain limited. Moreover, the potential mediating role of alcohol use in the relationship between CMDs and ART adherence has not been sufficiently explored, particularly in low-resource settings such as Tanzania.

Understanding the mediating influence of alcohol use and AUDs on the association between common mental disorders and ART adherence is essential for developing integrated, evidence-based interventions. Such interventions should address both mental health and substance use disorders in a coordinated manner to enhance treatment adherence, improve clinical outcomes, and ultimately support the health and well-being of PLHIV. Thus, this study aimed to examine the mediating effects of alcohol use and alcohol use disorders on the association between common mental disorders (depression and anxiety) and ART adherence in Tanzania.

Methods

Study design

This was a hospital-based cross-sectional quantitative analytical study conducted from August to October 2023 in Moshi Municipality, Kilimanjaro, that aimed to determine the mediating role of AU and AUD in the association between CMD and ART adherence among PLHIV.

Setting

The study was conducted at HIV Care and Treatment Clinics (CTCs) located in Moshi, within the Kilimanjaro region. Moshi Municipal Council is one of the seven districts in the region, covering an area of 58 square kilometers and home to approximately 1.99 million people [22]. The municipality includes 21 wards across both urban and peri-urban settings and operates 19 HIV CTCs, which collectively serve between 800 and 1,200 clients each week [23]. CTC services are offered across all levels of the healthcare system, from primary care centers to tertiary hospital. These services are delivered by trained healthcare professionals and include HIV testing and treatment, counseling, community education, initiation of ART, management of opportunistic infections, and psychosocial support [24]. Four CTCs were selected as study sites to ensure a diverse representation of clients receiving HIV services across different levels of the healthcare system. These included Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Center (KCMC), a tertiary hospital; Mawenzi Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), a secondary-level facility; and two primary-level health centers, Pasua and Majengo. All four facilities are located within Moshi Municipality.

Study population

The study included all adults aged 18 and above who had been diagnosed with HIV and were receiving care at CTCs in Moshi Municipality, who provided written informed consent, and met the study’s eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: The study involved outpatient clients who were enrolled in CTC services and had been on ART for a minimum of six months.

Exclusion criteria: PLHIV who were too ill to take part were excluded from the study.

Sample size

A total sample size of 532 participants was determined using Cochran’s formula [N = Z²P(1 − P)/d²], where N represents the estimated sample size, Z is the standard value for a 95% confidence level (1.96), d is the margin of error set at 5%, and P is the estimated prevalence of alcohol use disorder (AUD) among people living with HIV (31.8%), based on findings from a study conducted in Ethiopia [25]. An additional 10% was added to the sample size to account for potential non-response. Since the study used a stratified random cluster sampling method, the sample size was further adjusted to account for the design effect, using an intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.005 [26, 27]. After adjustment, the final required sample was 532 participants.

Sampling procedure

A multistage cluster sampling method was used to select 4 out of the 19 CTCs in Moshi Municipality. The selection was done to ensure representation from all levels of Tanzania’s healthcare system: tertiary, secondary, and primary. Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Center (KCMC), the only tertiary facility in the area, was included, along with Mawenzi Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), the sole secondary-level facility. For the primary level, out of 17 health centers’ CTCs in the region, a subset of high-volume health centers was first purposively identified based on patient attendance data, to optimize participant recruitment within the constrained data collection period. From this subset, two facilities, Pasua and Majengo Health Centers, were randomly selected. Proportional sampling was used to determine how many participants to recruit from each clinic, based on the number of patients typically seen at each site. To ensure balanced gender representation across facilities, all male clients were included in the sample, while a systematic sampling approach was used to select every third female client. This intentional oversampling of men was done to address the usual gender imbalance in clinic attendance, where women typically outnumber men by about 3 to 1. This sampling method has been widely used in previous studies and is known for its validity and reliability [18, 27].

Data collection and management

The study was carried out over the course of two months, from August to October 2023, by a team of eight research assistants (RAs) with medical training, all of whom were medical doctors. Prior to data collection, the RAs received one week of training on the assessment tools from the principal investigator (PI).

Participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. To protect their identities, questionnaires were assigned unique identification numbers rather than names, ensuring that individual data could not be linked to participants in any reports. Consent forms were securely stored in a locked cabinet, while data collected via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software were entered on password-protected computers accessible exclusively to the research team. To further safeguard privacy, all interviews were conducted in private, unoccupied consultation rooms, and the data collection process was designed not to interfere with the routine clinical activities at the facilities.

The interviews were conducted in Kiswahili by the RAs and the PI, each lasting approximately thirty minutes, which allowed sufficient time to complete the questionnaire. Upon completion of each interview, the study team reviewed the questionnaires to ensure all items were properly filled out and expressed appreciation to each participant. Additionally, the team assisted participants in navigating clinic services as needed, and those who screened positive for AUD, clinically significant depressive and anxiety symptoms were referred to mental health clinics for further evaluation and care. No financial or material compensation was provided to participants for their involvement in the study.

Data collection tools

Demographic information was collected using a short questionnaire on participants’ age, sex, education level, marital status, and employment status. Medical records were reviewed to obtain recent viral load results and ART duration.

Perceived social support was assessed using the Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ), a 14-item tool measuring emotional, material, and social support.

HIV-related stigma was measured using the HIV/AIDS Stigma Instrument for People Living with HIV (HASI-P), a 12-item scale covering personalized stigma, self-image, disclosure concerns, and public attitudes.

Alcohol use and alcohol use disorder were evaluated with the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), a 10-item WHO tool widely used to screen for hazardous, harmful, and dependent drinking.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated 9-item screening tool for depression.

Anxiety symptoms were measured with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), a 7-item questionnaire screening for generalized anxiety.

ART adherence was assessed using the Center for Adherence Support Evaluation (CASE) Adherence Index, a brief 3-item measure of medication-taking behavior.

Further details on scoring, validation, and access link for each tool are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Pre-testing of data collection tools

Swahili versions of study instruments were sought from previous research in which tools had been culturally adapted and validated for use in Tanzania or the broader East African context. For instruments that had not yet been translated into Swahili, specifically the CASE Adherence Index, a formal translation process was undertaken three months prior to data collection. This involved a forward translation from English to Swahili, followed by an independent back translation into English by an individual who had no prior knowledge of the original tool. A review panel, composed of the PI, a mental health clinician, and a nurse, examined a discrepancy report comparing the original and back-translated English versions. This process helped identify any changes in meaning that may have occurred during translation. Items showing discrepancies were carefully reviewed, discussed, and revised to ensure that the Swahili version preserved the intended meaning of the original instrument.

The translated tools were then piloted with a sample of 30 adults PLHIV (15 male and 15 female) attending the Care and Treatment Clinic at Muhimbili National Hospital in the Dar es Salaam region. The pilot aimed to assess face validity and ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. Participants in the pilot study were excluded from the main study conducted in the Kilimanjaro region to avoid introducing potential bias. The pilot study provided valuable feedback that guided the refinement of the study instruments.

Statistical analyses

Participants were classified into two groups according to gender to present their characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Meanwhile, categorical variables were displayed as frequencies along with their respective percentages. Analysis of the data included either one-way ANOVA for continuous variables, along with chi-square tests for categorical variables. Logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios and the 95% confidence interval for associations of ART adherence with depression or anxiety. Three models were used for adjustment: Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 included only demographic, age, marital status, education level, and occupation. Model 3 was fully adjusted by the addition of duration in ART, recent viral load count, level of social support, and HIV stigma in model 2.

Additionally, a counterfactual-based mediation analysis was conducted to ascertain whether and to what extent AU and AUD mediated the total effect of depression or anxiety on ART non-adherence (Fig. 1) by utilizing the “mediator” R package. The mediation analysis models were adjusted similarly to the aforementioned logistic regression models. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2. Significance was indicated by a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Table 1 displays the data of 532 participants included in the study. The average age was 46.6 ± 13.3 years. Women comprised 71.4% of the sample. Significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics by gender, such as age, marital status, duration in ART treatment, AU, and AUD (P value < 0.05). Compared to men, women in the study were generally younger, more likely to be unemployed, and more often single or divorced. They had a shorter duration on ART, lower recent viral load counts, poorer levels of social support, and experienced less HIV-related stigma. Women also showed lower prevalence of alcohol use and alcohol use disorders, but higher rates of depression and anxiety. Despite these challenges, they demonstrated better adherence to ART than men. The prevalence of depression, anxiety, and ART non-adherence among all participants was 14.8%, 12.4%, and 10.7%, respectively.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Tanzania to investigate the mediating role of AU and AUD in the relationship between CMD and ART adherence among PLHIV. By examining gender differences in baseline characteristics and outcomes, our findings offer valuable insights into how CMDs affect ART adherence differently in men and women, and how alcohol related behaviors may mediate these effects.

Our analysis identified a significant association between CMDs and ART non-adherence, with notable gender-specific patterns. Among men, both depression and anxiety were significantly associated with ART non-adherence. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that men are generally less likely to seek psychological support and may turn to alcohol as a coping mechanism, thereby exacerbating non-adherence to ART [28]. In contrast, among women, only depression was significantly associated with poor adherence, a finding consistent with previous systematic reviews in sub-Saharan Africa [29]. This gender difference may be partially explained by contextual and structural factors, such as women’s increased vulnerability to gender-based violence and economic dependence, which heighten their risk for depressive symptoms. Depression-related symptoms such as fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and hopelessness can directly hinder adherence to ART regimens [30]. Furthermore, gender-based stigma around alcohol use among Tanzanian societies may have led to underreporting of alcohol use among women, potentially obscuring its true impact on ART adherence [31, 32].

Interestingly, anxiety was not significantly associated with ART adherence among women, consistent with other findings in the region [29, 30]. This may reflect a more complex or indirect relationship between anxiety and ART-taking behaviors in women, potentially moderated by unmeasured psychosocial, environmental, or cultural factors. Unlike depression, anxiety may have protective or neutral effects depending on the individual’s context and coping resources.

A key contribution of this study is the identification of AU and AUD as mediators in the CMD-ART adherence relationship. AUD, in particular, showed a strong mediating effect in the association between depression and ART non-adherence, accounting for 33.9% to 45.5% of the total effect across different models. This finding is consistent with studies from Vietnam and India, which also demonstrated that alcohol use can mediate the relationship between depressive symptoms and ART adherence among PLHIV [28, 33]. Our findings suggest that for some individuals, alcohol may be used as a coping strategy in response to depressive symptoms stemming from the chronic stress and socioeconomic challenges of living with HIV. However, problematic alcohol use ultimately undermines ART adherence, compounding the health risks for this population.

While AU also mediated the CMD-ART adherence relationship, its effect was less pronounced compared to AUD. This suggests that the severity of alcohol involvement plays a critical role in influencing ART adherence behaviors. Similar results have been reported in Ethiopia and Uganda, where higher levels of alcohol use were associated with greater challenges in ART adherence [4, 34]. Potential mechanisms include alcohol-induced cognitive impairment, through memory deficits and misconceptions about concurrent use, hence prioritization of alcohol use over ART [4].

These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated mental health and substance use interventions within HIV care programs in Tanzania. Addressing CMDs, particularly depression, and providing targeted support for reducing alcohol use, especially among those with AUD, could significantly improve ART adherence and overall health outcomes among PLHIV. Evidence from high-income countries suggests that brief negotiation interventions (BNI), using motivational interviewing principles, significantly reduce AUD in various populations [35, 36]. However, due to socio-cultural and health system differences between high- and lower-income countries, adaptation of BNIs is crucial to ensure they are effective, sustainable, acceptable, and feasible in low and middle-income countries.

Staton et al. have adapted and implemented a brief negotiation intervention to reduce harmful and hazardous alcohol use (the “Punguza Pombe Kwa Afya Yako” (PPKAY)) among injury patients in Tanzania. This is a single, 15-minute nurse-led brief negotiated interview (BNI) designed to promote responsible alcohol use and encourage behavioral changes related to alcohol consumption in the Kilimanjaro region of Tanzania (Staton et al. 2022). The intervention has been shown to reduce 26 binge drinking events per year compared to usual care [37]. This adapted intervention is important for Tanzania because it allows interventions typically delivered by professional and specialized staff to be provided by non-specialized staff, such as general nurses, an approach also supported by the World Health Organization (WHO) through the Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) [38].

By adapting and implementing these brief interventions such as PPKAY which has already shown impressive results among injury patients in our region we can enhance the integration of AUD care into HIV management, thereby reducing alcohol’s impact on treatment adherence, disease progression, transmission and improving their overall quality of life among PLHIV to support reaching our 95-95-95 goals [39]. However, future research should explore additional psychosocial and structural mediators to further elucidate these complex relationships and inform comprehensive, gender-responsive interventions.

Strength of the study

This study benefits from a robust sample size and the use of tools that have been previously used in our setting with good reliability for assessing CMDs, AUD, and ART adherence. Stratified analyses allowed for gender-specific insights, and mediation models offered an understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Limitation of the study

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and the reliance on self-reported data may introduce reporting bias, particularly regarding alcohol use and mental health symptoms. Additionally, unmeasured confounders, such as trauma history or adherence support systems, may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

Depression is significantly associated with ART non-adherence among both men and women living with HIV, with anxiety playing a more prominent role among men. AUD appears to be a key mediator in the relationship between depression and ART adherence, particularly in male participants than in female participants. These findings highlight the importance of integrating mental health and substance use interventions into HIV care, with a focus on gender-specific vulnerabilities and pathways to non-adherence.
