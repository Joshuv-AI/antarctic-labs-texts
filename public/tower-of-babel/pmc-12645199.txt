Gas-containing cystic intradural lumbar disc herniation at L1–2, a rare diagnosis confirmed by discography and review of the literature: illustrative case

Lumbar disc herniation is a common condition in spine surgery practice, most frequently occurring at the L4–5 and L5–S1 levels in approximately 95% of cases.1 In contrast, herniation at the L1–2 level is rare, accounting for 1%–2% of all lumbar disc herniation cases.2 An even more uncommon entity is intradural disc herniation (IDH), in which disc material penetrates the dura mater and enters the intradural space.3 This condition was first reported in 1942 by Dandy4 and accounts for only 0.26%–0.30% of all lumbar disc herniations.5,6 Given its rarity, IDH remains a relatively underrecognized and diagnostically challenging condition.7

The pathogenesis of IDH is thought to involve extrusion of disc material through the posterior longitudinal ligament followed by penetration of the dura mater into the intradural space. Strong adhesion between the posterior longitudinal ligament and the dura is thought to be necessary for this to occur. Such adhesions may arise secondary to spine surgery, infection, or inflammation or may result from advanced degenerative changes, such as formation of osteophytes at the vertebral endplate.8 However, cases have also been reported in which IDH develops in the absence of any apparent predisposing factors, suggesting a degree of variability in its pathophysiology.

Preoperative diagnosis of IDH is often difficult based solely on imaging studies, and only intraoperative findings can confirm an intradural disc extrusion.6 Distinguishing between extradural and intradural lesions can be challenging on conventional MRI. Even when an abnormal intradural mass is suspected, differentiating it from a tumor, hematoma, or cystic lesion may be difficult.9,10 In some cases, contrast-enhanced MRI may show enhancement of the herniated disc material, which can aid with diagnosis, although the absence of enhancement is not uncommon.11

In this report, we present a rare case of cystic IDH at L1–2 complicated by intradural gas accumulation. Resection was performed via dorsal durotomy. The rarity of this anatomical location, along with the successful preoperative identification by CT discography and prompt surgical intervention leading to clinical improvement, makes this a valuable case, which we report along with a review of the literature.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation

The patient was a 57-year-old man who presented to our hospital with worsening low back pain that had persisted for 2 years, followed by the development of bilateral buttock and anterior thigh pain that was more prominent on the right side. The patient had a history of mild intellectual disability, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, and posttraumatic epilepsy following a head injury. He also had a history of percutaneous coronary intervention for angina pectoris and was on regular aspirin therapy. Neurological examination revealed normal muscle strength in the lower extremities; however, the patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes were hyporeactive bilaterally. The femoral nerve stretch test was positive on both sides.

Preoperative Imaging

Conventional MRI demonstrated multilevel disc degeneration and disc space narrowing below T12–L1 but no evidence of spinal canal stenosis. However, both T1-weighted and T2-weighted images revealed a well-defined, low-intensity mass within the intradural space at L1–2 that was located slightly to the right of the midline (Fig. 1). The conus medullaris and cauda equina were displaced ventrally by this lesion. Furthermore, the intervertebral disc at L1–2 was bulging, and the boundary with the intradural mass was unclear.

CT myelography revealed a gas-containing lesion within the dura mater at L1–2 that corresponded to the MRI findings (Fig. 2). The cauda equina appeared to be adherent and pulled ventrally around the collection of gas (Fig. 2). A vacuum phenomenon was observed within the L1–2 intervertebral disc, raising suspicion of communication between the intradural gas and intradiscal gas.

Lumbar discography was then performed at L1–2. Contrast medium was seen to leak from the disc space into the area containing intradural gas, confirming the presence of a direct communication (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of IDH with associated accumulation of intradural gas was made, and surgical intervention was planned.

Surgical Findings and Postoperative Course

A partial laminectomy was performed at L1–2. On midline dorsal dural incision, a cystic intradural mass was observed. The mass was embedded among the cauda equina nerve roots (Fig. 4A), several of which were found to be adherent to the lesion and displaced ventrally along with the mass.

Intraoperative microscopic findings. A: Immediately after durotomy, a gas-containing cystic mass was visualized at the L1–2 disc level, with several cauda equina nerve roots adherent to it and displaced ventrally. B: The mass was removed by dissecting the plane between the herniated fragment and the surrounding structures, including the cauda equina and the ventral dura, using bipolar coagulation, and the lesion was resected down to the level of the ventral dura. Although no discrete herniation orifice was identified, the thinned ventral dura was coagulated with bipolar radiofrequency coagulation to reinforce the area.

After meticulous dissection to separate the mass from the adherent nerve roots, the cystic lesion was completely excised. The adhesions between the cauda equina and ventral dura mater were successfully released, confirming complete decompression (Fig. 4B). There was no intraoperative decrease in motor evoked potentials. The surgery was concluded without any complications, such as postoperative CSFA leak, surgical site infection, sensory disturbances, or bladder and bowel dysfunction.

Postoperatively, the patient’s bilateral buttock and anterior thigh numbness and pain improved without significant improvement of his low back pain. Histopathological examination revealed a small amount of fibrous cartilage, consistent with herniated nucleus pulposus and annular fibrosus tissue.

Follow-up nonenhanced MRI performed 1 year after surgery showed no evidence of recurrent disc herniation or displacement of the cauda equina (Fig. 5).

Follow-up lumbar MR scans obtained 1 year postoperatively. A: Midsagittal T2-weighted image. B:Axial T2-weighted image at the L1–2 level. No evidence of recurrent herniation is seen. The cauda equina is relaxed and evenly distributed within the thecal sac.

Discussion

Observations

IDH is often difficult to distinguish radiologically from other intradural pathologies, such as tumors, hematomas, or cystic lesions, making preoperative diagnosis particularly challenging.9,10 In this case, MRI revealed an intradural soft tissue mass at the L1–2 intervertebral level accompanied by ventral displacement of the cauda equina. This displacement represents a form of “nerve root deviation,” an indirect but relatively characteristic finding in IDH that is less commonly observed in intradural tumors.12,13 Furthermore, the appearance of a mass protruding intradurally at the level of disc bulging has been described as the “hawk-beak sign,” which is also considered relatively characteristic of IDH.14

CT myelography demonstrated an intradural gas collection corresponding to the mass with associated ventral traction and clustering of the cauda equina, suggesting adhesions. Gas in the intradural space is rarely observed in other pathologies, and its presence suggested the possibility that gas and disc material had extruded into the intradural space through a defect in the annulus fibrosus and dura.15 The definitive diagnosis of IDH in this case was made by discography. When contrast medium was injected into the L1–2 disc, leakage into the gas-containing intradural space was clearly visualized. This leakage strongly indicated direct communication between the disc and the intradural cavity, providing convincing preoperative evidence of IDH. Discography and CT discography have been reported to be reliable tools for the preoperative diagnosis of IDH. One of the earliest reports by Benyamin et al. described incidental radiographic diagnosis of IDH during discography, where contrast medium was observed spreading into the intrathecal space during injection at L4–5.17 More recently, Matsumoto et al. demonstrated that discography combined with CT discography provided definitive preoperative confirmation of IDH, enabling accurate surgical planning.16 Discography has become less frequently used in recent years due to its invasiveness and questions regarding its diagnostic value. However, in rare conditions like IDH, where MRI or CT alone may not yield a definitive diagnosis, discography remains a valuable adjunctive tool. When indirect findings such as intradural gas with nerve adhesion and nerve displacement are observed in combination, the addition of discography can significantly improve the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis.

The stepwise diagnostic approach taken in this case, starting with noncontrast MRI followed by CT myelography and culminating in discography, allowed for the accumulation of multiple supportive imaging findings and ultimately led to a definitive diagnosis of IDH. At the L1–2 level, where IDH is exceedingly rare and seldom suspected preoperatively, awareness of such imaging clues can lead to more accurate treatment planning and safer surgical outcomes.

IDH is an exceedingly rare condition, accounting for less than 0.3% of all disc herniations.4,5 Even more infrequent are cases involving the L1–2 level. According to the literature, the most common site of IDH is the L4–5 level (in more than 50% of cases), with L1–2 involvement representing only about 8% of cases, making its occurrence at this level particularly noteworthy from an anatomical perspective.18,19

Although the spinal canal is not anatomically narrow at L1–2, IDH can still occur as a result of local structural vulnerabilities. Cadaveric and histological studies have shown that the ventral dura mater at the lumbar levels, particularly around the conus medullaris, is thinner and structurally weaker than in other regions, making it more susceptible to penetration by herniated disc material.20 Furthermore, the posterior longitudinal ligament, although broad in the upper lumbar spine, tends to become thinner and less robust over the intervertebral disc space, especially at L1–2, which may facilitate disc extrusion toward the dural sac.21 When adhesions develop between the posterior longitudinal ligament and the ventral dura due to chronic inflammation, prior trauma, or degenerative changes, they can create a direct pathway for disc material to intrude into the intradural space.22 These combined anatomical factors likely contributed to the development of IDH at the L1–2 level in our case.

Based on a comprehensive review of the literature indexed in PubMed, we identified 12 cases of IDH at L1–2 reported since 2000.5,6,18,19,23–29 Among these, only 3 cases (25%) were diagnosed as IDH preoperatively.19,24,29 Furthermore, gas retention within the herniated disc was described in only 1 case (8%).29 Although various preoperative imaging modalities, including conventional MRI, contrast-enhanced MRI, and CT myelography, were used in these reports, neither discography nor CT discography was used in any of the cases. When there is suspicion of IDH in association with gas accumulation around or within the dura, especially in rare locations such as the upper lumbar spine, discography and CT discography may be valuable adjuncts to establish a definitive preoperative diagnosis. Although these studies do not change the surgical indication per se, they can influence intraoperative strategy and resource planning, for example, permitting direct progression to intradural management, limiting the extent of skin and laminar exposure, and ensuring readiness for watertight dural closure with appropriate microsurgical instruments and adjuncts. Accordingly, in selected patients with atypical or inconclusive MRI findings, or when preoperative confirmation would materially alter the planned approach, these studies may facilitate a more targeted, efficient, and potentially safer operation.

Lessons

We have encountered a rare case of cystic IDH at the L1–2 level that was accompanied by accumulation of intradural gas and surgically treated by posterior durotomy and removal of the herniation. Accurate preoperative diagnosis of IDH is essential for optimal surgical planning and implementation. When a gas-containing mass is observed in the intradural space, discography can be a valuable diagnostic tool to confirm continuity between the intervertebral disc and intradural lesion.
