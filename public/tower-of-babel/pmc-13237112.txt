Identification and validation of disulfidptosis-related gene signature revealed DKK1 as a therapeutic target in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Zhenzhen Li, Wenpin Xu, Zhiwei Li, Zijing Yu, Jian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) poses significant prognostic challenges due to recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance. Disulfidptosis, a newly identified cellular self-destruction pathway, is implicated in various diseases, including cancer. This study aimed to identify biomarkers by analyzing disulfidptosis-related gene expression. Recent studies have evaluated disulfidptosis in HNSCC; we therefore position this work by integrating multi‑cohort transcriptomic analysis with a focused evaluation of DKK1 and functional assays to clarify its potential clinical relevance. Utilizing transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas(TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus(GEO)datasets, unsupervised clustering grouped HNSCC patients, and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression pinpointed central genes to develop a prognostic and therapeutic prediction model. The integration of a nomogram enhanced prediction accuracy. Among 527 HNSCC patients, we identified 10 disulfidptosis-related genes with dysregulated expression, contributing to HNSCC progression. A risk score model incorporating clinical factors such as age and stage was developed. Analyses based on gender, stage, and risk score revealed significant differences in tumor mutation burden and immune landscape between risk categories. The model showed predictive utility predicted chemotherapy sensitivity and identified candidates for programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1(PD-1/L1)-targeted therapy. Integrating this with clinical features could guide personalized follow-up strategies. Experimental validation confirmed DKK1 upregulation in oral squamous cell carcinoma cell (OSCC) lines (CAL27 and SCC15), and its knockdown inhibited proliferation in vitro, supporting its potential involvement in OSCC development, however, mechanistic assays of β‑catenin activity/localization and cystine‑starvation–induced disulfidptosis (e.g., phalloidin staining, disulfide‑stress readouts) were not performed, so the DKK1–disulfidptosis link is interpreted as associative rather than causal. Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) encompasses malignancies of the oral cavity, tongue, nasopharynx, larynx, oropharynx, and hypopharyn1,2. Approximately 90% are histologically classified as Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma(HNSCC)3, with risk factors including genetic susceptibility, tobacco/alcohol use, and HPV/Epstein-Barr virus infections4,5. Due to its intricate origins, HNSCC demonstrates intrinsic diversity. Despite advances in multimodal therapies (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted agents), the prognosis for metastatic or recurrent HNSCC remains poor6,7. Thus, post-genomic era research must deepen understanding of genetic determinants in HNSCC.

Regulated cell death (RCD) is controlled by molecular pathways and is modifiable by genetic/pharmacological interventions8,9. It contributes to organismal homeostasis and disease pathogenesis, including cancer. Given cancer cell resistance to apoptosis, exploring alternative RCD mechanisms is crucial.

Disulfides stabilize protein structures through inter/intra-subunit bonds, conferring physicochemical stability10,11. Glucose starvation in SLC7A11-high cells induces aberrant disulfide bonds in actin cytoskeleton proteins, triggering disulfidptosis—distinct from apoptosis/ferroptosis12. This process involves SLC7A11-dependent F-actin collapse, suggesting a therapeutic target12,13. Additionally, disulfide accumulation induces cell death independent of ATP14.

Since 2024, several studies have investigated disulfidptosis in HNSCC, including subtype characterization and prognostic modeling15–19. Building on these advances, we investigated its regulatory modes in HNSCC by integrating The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) multi‑cohort analyses, identified dysregulation of 10 disulfidptosis-related genes. Notably, DKK1—a secreted protein that inhibits Wnt signaling by binding LRP5/6—is a negative prognostic indicator in HNSCC20. Our results suggested an increase in DKK1 protein levels was observed in CAL27 and SCC15 compared to HOK cells. Following siRNA‑mediated DKK1 knockdown in CAL27 and SCC15 cells, we observed reduced proliferation. Because β‑catenin activity and cystine‑starvation disulfidptosis phenotypes were not assessed, this observation should be interpreted as correlative rather than causal. In conclusion, we developed a forecasting model utilizing the distinctive cell death pathway, disulfidptosis, for HNSCC patients. The risk score demonstrated good predictive performance and held certain guiding significance for the comprehensive management of HNSCC patients, thus contributing to improving their prognosis.

Materials and methods

Data gathering and preprocessing

We acquired the gene expression data of TCGA-HNSC using “TCGAbiolinks” in R21 and transformed them using the log2 (FPKM + 1). We further excluded samples with primary sites annotated as “bones, joints and articular cartilage of other and unspecified sites” and “lip”. To gather precise survival data like overall survival (OS) and comprehensive clinical information including age, disease stage, sex, and tumor grade, TCGA officially corrected data were sourced from the Cell journal22. For model validation, the GSE65858 dataset23 was sourced from GEO. To standardize the processing of the GEO dataset, we converted probes into symbols according to the relevant relationship for each platform, eliminated probes with multiple genes, and averaged multiple probes corresponding to the same symbol. For both TCGA and GEO data, samples with incomplete clinical information, i.e., survival time and status, were filtered out. In total, 10 genes associated with disulfidptosis were chosen from a previously published article12.

Unsupervised grouping according to genes related to disulfidptosis

To ascertain the biological attributes of genes associated with disulfidptosis in HNSCC, the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package24(1000 iterations and an 80% re-sampling frequency) was employed using the pam method and Euclidean distance metric to classify HNSCC patients into distinct subtypes. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA)25 was conducted and the clustering results analyzed. Kaplan-Meier (KM) analysis was employed to create survival plots and assess variations among different categories. The clustering results were further analyzed for their correlations with clinical outcomes and other molecular features.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among distinct disulfidptosis subtypes

The DEGs between distinct disulfidptosis subtypes were identified using “limma”26. The screening criteria for DEGs were established as |log2 FC| > 0.585 and an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Further, gene set enrichment analysis(GSEA)was performed27 using “clusterProfiler”28 to identify enriched pathways. Pathways with an FDR < 0.05 were deemed meaningful for exploration of the potential functions of the selected DEGs.

Development of a prognostic model using genes associated with disulfidptosis

Univariate Cox regression screened prognostic DEGs. To prevent overfitting, LASSO-penalized Cox regression via “glmnet” selected genes29,30. The risk score was calculated as:

n: number of genes predictive of outcomes, expi: gene i’s expression, βi: regression coefficient associated with gene i.

Subjects were stratified into high and low-risk categories according to the median risk score, and the “survminer” tool was applied to assess their survival rates. Furthermore, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic(ROC)curves were analyzed using “survminer”31 and “timeROC”32 packages to determine the model’s predictive value. Further, the standalone prognostic significance of the risk score was evaluated through both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses. Finally, the validity of the risk score calculation was confirmed in the GSE65858 using identical methodology.

Nomogram for clinical utility evaluation

To identify significantly independent prognostic factors and develop a nomogram, we merged the outputs from the risk scoring model and clinical features of the TCGA-HNSC cohort into a multivariate Cox regression analysis through the “rms” package33. Subsequently, a calibration curve was generated to graphically determine the relatability among actual and predicted values. The clinical utility of the constructed nomogram was assessed by quantifying the net benefits at various threshold probabilities through decision curve analysis (DCA)34.

Genomic alteration

The Single Nucleotide Variation (SNV) and Copy Number Variations (CNV) information for HNC was obtained from the TCGA repository. Genomic variations were determined using GISTIC analysis, and high and low risk score CNVs and alteration peaks were derived via GISTIC 2.0 analysis (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp/pages/index.jsf). Data on somatic mutations were obtained from “TCGA biolinks” in R by Mutect2, and differences in SNV mutations among high and low risk score groups were evaluated through Fisher’s exact test.

Analysis of immune infiltration in tumor microenvironment (TME)

The relative abundance of types of immune cell infiltrating the tumor milieu of HNSCC patients was quantified by the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis(ssGSEA) algorithm35. The gene sets from Charoentong’s study were obtained to label cells, including activated CD8+ T, dendritic, macrophage, natural killer T, and regulatory T cells36. Their relative abundance was analyzed from enrichment scores calculated via ssGSEA. The “ESTIMATE” package in R was employed to determine stromal score, immune score, ESTIMATE score, and tumor purity.

Prediction of sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs

The disparities in responsiveness to chemotherapeutic agents among high and low risk score categories from TCGA-HNSC samples were analyzed through prediction of drugs’ half-maximal inhibitory concentration(IC50)values by “oncoPredict.” This analysis was based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer(GDSC)database37. To construct a ridge regression model, oncoPredict38 and drug data from GDSC and Clinical Trials Reporting Program(CTRP)were used. The correlation between genes used to develop DDscore and drug IC50 was determined using Spearman correlation analysis. In addition, boxplots of IC50 for drugs were generated to show partial differences between the groups.

Prediction of immune therapy response

The immunotherapy information for HNSCC patients was retrieved from the GEO repository. The GSE159067 dataset39 comprises 102 patients who received death-1/programmed death-ligand 1(PD1/L1) therapy and their OS data. Using the same algorithm, a risk assessment was computed for each sample within the dataset, and the survival differences among the two risk score groups were analyzed.

Cell culture cell culture reagents

Human normal oral epithelial keratinocytes (HOK), human tongue squamous cells SCC15, CAL27 were acquired from le TongPai Biotechnology Co., LTD. (Shang Hai, China). HOK cells were cultivated in MEM at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2, containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin, and streptomycin. CAL27 and SCC15 were maintained at 37 ℃ in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. Cell line authenticity was verified by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to functional assays, and the obtained profiles were consistent with the expected references for CAL27 and SCC15. STR profiling serves to confirm cell identity and does not establish biological representativeness across head and neck subsites; accordingly, our in vitro experiments should be interpreted as OSCC‑specific.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Cellular total RNA was extracted following the protocol provided by the miRNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The entire RNA was transformed into cDNA employing the PrimerScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq reagent kit (Takara, Japan). The sequences employed by the target genes were as delineated: DKK1, 5’ - ACACTAAACCAGCTATCC − 3’ and 5’ - GCCACACTGAGAATTTAC − 3’.

Knocking down DKK1 using siRNA

siRNAs designed against DKK1 (siDKK1) and a siRNA control were synthesized by Sango Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The nucleotide sequence of the siDKK1 was 5’-AAAAUGACCGUCACUUUGCAA-3’. In 6-well dishes, cells were transfected with 10 nM siRNA by Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Transfections were performed in 6‑well plates at ~ 60–70% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) under the manufacturer’s instructions. The final siRNA working concentration was 10 nM. Transfection complexes were prepared and added according to the vendor protocol; the culture medium was replaced after 4–6 h, and phenotypic readouts were collected 48 h post‑transfection. To address the known transfection challenge in CAL27 cells, we validated the protocol by transfecting a GFP reporter plasmid under identical conditions. Fluorescence microscopy and quantification revealed high transfection efficiency (70–85%) in CAL27 (Supplementary Fig. S1). The same optimized protocol was used for SCC15. Given that CAL27 is notably more difficult to transfect than many epithelial lines, the high efficiency achieved in CAL27 strongly supports the reliability of siRNA delivery in SCC15 for the subsequent functional assays.

Western blotting

The cells were disrupted using RIPA lysis solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, cat: P0013B), and the protein was heated at 100 degrees Celsius for 5 min for denaturation. The BCA Kit (Thermo Scientific, cat# 23225) was utilized to access the concentrations of the protein. Equal quantities of protein were isolated and moved to the PVDF membrane (GE health) by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The membrane was subsequently sealed with 5% non-fat milk for a duration of 1 h. After being incubated through the night with primary antibody (DKK1, Proteintech, USA, 1:8000 dilution and GAPDH, Abcam, USA,1:10000 dilution.). Secondary antibodies corresponding to the species (1:5000 dilution, Abcam, USA) was applied and left at ambient temperature for a duration of 1 h. Signal on the membrane was identified using a ChemiDoc™ MP E-Gel (Biorad, USA), and protein band intensity was quantified by ImageJ.

Tissue microarray and IHC

The tissue microarray was supplied by OUTDO BIOTECH CO., LTD (Shanghai, China). Immunohistochemical analysis of DKK1 was conducted on tissue microarrays containing samples of oral squamous cell carcinoma. The DKK1 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, USA) was diluted to a concentration of 1:500.

Cell growth assay

Cell proliferation was assessed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (K1018, APExBIO, USA). CAL27 and SCC15 cells were seeded into 96-well plates. After 48 h of transfection, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagant was pipetted to each well. The optical density at 450 nm was assessed using a Multiskan SkyHigh (Thermo, USA) two hours subsequent to the addition of the CCK-8 solution.

Matrigel penetration assay

The cell invasion experiment utilized a Transwell insert featuring 8.0 μm pores (Corning Corporation, USA). Based on a 1:8 ratio of Matrigel (356234, BD, USA) matrix gel to culture medium, dilute the Matrigel matrix gel with culture medium lacking fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a 1:8 ratios, and evenly distribute it onto the upper compartment of Transwell. The cells were subsequently resuspended in a medium without serum. A number of 5.0 × 104 cells in 100 µl of cell solutions were introduced into the upper chamber, while the lower compartment was loaded with 600 µl DMEM containing 20% FBS. After incubating for an additional 48 h, the medium in the upper compartment was removed and washed twice with PBS buffer before fixing with a solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde for half an hour and air-drying at ambient temperature. Subsequently, it was stained with a solution of crystal violet (1%) for approximately 20 min. Cells that did not migrate to the lower side were delicately removed using a cotton applicator and then washed twice again with PBS buffer before being dried at room temperature. Ultimately, five arbitrary regions within the insert were chosen using a microscope to capture and enumerate the cells.

Statistical analysis

All computations were performed using R software version 4.1.2. For comparing significance among different values between two groups, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was conducted. To analyze differences among multiple groups of samples, the Kruskal-Wallis’s test was utilized. “ns”, “*”, “**”, “***”, and “****” were employed to denote different levels of statistical significance: “ns” for p > 0.05, * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001, and **** for p < 0.0001.Survival plots were constructed using KM analysis, and distinctions between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test to assess significance.

Results

Molecular and clinical signatures of disulfidptosis-related genes

First, we identified the occurrence of somatic mutations and CNVs for the 10 shortlisted disulfidptosis-related genes in HNSCC. Among 503 TCGA-HNSC samples, 27 (5.37%) harbored somatic mutations in disulfidptosis-related genes, with LRPPRC exhibiting the highest mutation frequency (Fig. 1A). CNV analysis revealed prevalent deletions in NDUFS1 and NDUFA11, while SLC3A2 showed frequent amplification (Fig. 1B,C). The distinct variations in gene expression patterns enabled the clear differentiation of HNSCC samples from normal samples (Fig. 1D). We assessed the mRNA expression of genes associated with disulfidptosis in both normal and HNSCC samples to investigate how genetic variations affect their levels in patients with HNSCC. Our findings indicated that CNV alterations were prominent factors causing disturbances in disulfidptosis-related gene expression. Genes exhibiting CNV amplification showed markedly higher expression in HNSCC tissues in comparison with non-cancerous samples (Fig. 1E). Consequently, a high degree of heterogeneity in disulfidptosis-related gene expression patterns between normal and HNSCC samples was identified, potentially exerting a significant impact on the onset and advancement of HNSCC.

To provide a comprehensive understanding of the interaction among disulfidptosis-related genes and their significance in HNSCC prognosis, we constructed a comprehensive disulfidptosis gene regulatory network. Significant correlations were uncovered among disulfidptosis genes, including a clear inverse relationship between NCKAP1 and NDUFA11 and a positive association of NCKAP1 with SLC7A11, NDUFS1, and LRPPRC (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that the crosstalk among disulfidptosis genes is critically involved in the development of various disulfidptosis patterns and tumor milieu cell infiltration characteristics in each specific tumor.

We assessed the impact of these 10 genes on HNSCC prognosis through a univariate analysis and pinpointed NUBPL, SLC3A2, and SLC7A11, as significant prognosis-related risk factors (Fig. 1G). Notably, two out of the 10 disulfidptosis-related genes exhibited significant survival differences, as depicted by their Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves (Fig. 1H,I).

Identification of disulfidptosis subtypes

We employed the “ConsensusClusterPlus” tool to categorize HNSCC patients according to the expression profiles of the 10 selected genes related to disulfidptosis and identified two distinct modification patterns through unsupervised clustering. Cluster 1 comprising 383 cases and Cluster 2 comprising 113 cases (Fig. 2A). PCA findings clearly showed differentiation between the two subtypes (Fig. 2B), and their prognostic analysis revealed particularly significant survival advantages in Cluster 1 (Fig. 2C).

Differential expression examination was conducted to investigate the possible biological behaviors of the two disulfidptosis subtypes using the “limma” package. Considering a filtering criterion of |log2FC| > 0.585 and FDR < 0.05, we identified 371 genes that were differentially expressed between the two subtypes (Fig. 2D,E). Subsequent GSEA showed that Cluster 1 exhibited significantly enrichment in immune activation-associated pathways (Fig. 2F), including antigen handling and display, natural killer cell-driven cell destruction, T cell receptor signaling route, chemokine signaling route, NF-κB signaling route, and TNF signaling route. In contrast, Cluster 2 showed enrichment of genes involved in energy metabolism and carcinogenic activation pathways such as the citric acid cycle, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, ferroptosis, and signaling routes governing the pluripotency of stem cells (Fig. 2G).

Development and verification of risk prediction model for disulfidptosis

We discovered 188 DEGs between HNSCC and control samples, as well as between different subtypes of disulfidptosis (Fig. 3A). In the univariate analysis, 48 out of 188 DEGs were found to be associated with HNSCC prognosis (Fig. 3B).

To manage the substantial number of genes for analysis, we applied LASSO regression examination to further narrow the gene pool and clarify the alteration path of each independent variable (Fig. 3C). The count of independent variable coefficients progressively diminished to zero as the lambda value steadily increased. A ten-fold cross-validation was executed to construct this model, and the confidence intervals for each lambda are illustrated in Fig. 3D. The model demonstrated optimal performance when the number of genes was restricted to 10. Consequently, we selected 10 genes as candidates (Fig. 3E).

We fomulated a risk score using the coefficients and expression levels of these 10 selected genes, evaluating their impact on OS. By using the median value of the risk score, we divided the TCGA-HNSC cohort into categories with high and low scores (Fig. 4A,B), with a greater proportion of deceased patients belonging to the high-score group. KM curves showed markedly reduced OS for the high-score category in comparison with the low-score category (Fig. 4C). The risk score demonstrated good predictive ability for OS in the training cohort, with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.634, 0.700, and 0.663 at 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years (Fig. 4D).

We tested the stability of the risk score in this TCGA-HNSC by validating its prognostic performance in the validation set GSE65858 using the same algorithm. Consistent outcomes were noted in the training cohort, where the low-score category demonstrated a superior survival benefit (Fig. 4E–H).

Relationship between risk score and clinical features

We investigated the association of risk score with clinical attributes of HNSCC patients and identified significant differences based on patients’ age and clinical stage. In particular, the risk score was notably reduced in the subgroup of older individuals (≥60 years) than the younger age subgroup (Fig. 5A) and markedly elevated in the subgroup of advanced stage compared to subgroup at an early stage (Fig. 5B). No significant differences were noted between risk scores and other clinical characteristics.

Clinical features of risk score. (A,B) The risk score was associated with age and tumor grade. (C) Univariate Cox analysis of TCGA-HNSC cohort. (D) Multivariate Cox examination of TCGA-HNSC cohort. (E) Univariate Cox examination of GSE65858 cohort. (F) Multivariate Cox examination of GSE65858 cohort.

We determined whether risk score serves as an autonomous predictive factor for OS. Among both TCGA-HNSC and GSE65858 datasets, the risk score was significantly related to OS in the univariate analysis (TCGA-HNSC cohort: HR = 2.39, 95% CI = 1.80–3.17, P < 0.001; GSE65858 cohort: HR = 1.80, 95% CI = 1.19–2.73, P = 0.006) (Fig. 5C,D). In the multivariate analysis, after accounting for confounding factors, the risk score functioned as an autonomous indicator of OS (TCGA-HNSC cohort: HR = 2.43, 95% CI = 1.78–3.32, P < 0.001; GSE65858 cohort: HR = 1.76, 95% CI = 1.16–2.66, P = 0.008) (Fig. 5E,F).

Construction and validation of the nomogram

Multivariable analysis of the TCGA-HNSC cohort identified gender, stage, and risk score as independent predictive factors for OS in HNSCC. A nomogram integrating these three variables was developed to predict OS (Fig. 6A). Calibration curves at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals validated the accuracy of the nomogram for OS prediction (Fig. 6B). ROC analysis demonstrated that the nomogram outperformed individual predictors (gender, stage, risk score) in predictive value (Fig. 6C–E). DCA further confirmed the nomogram’s superior net benefit for forecasting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival compared to other prognostic variables (Fig. 6F–H).

Genomic alterations and immune infiltration analysis based on risk score

We examined the correlation between somatic mutations and risk score through performing SNV mutation examination. The low-hazard score category displayed a wider tumor mutation load compared to the high-hazard score category (Fig. 7A). In comparison with the high-hazard score category, the low-hazard score category displayed greater mutation trends in ADAMTS9, ARHGAP21, CNKSR3, OR2J2, and KIF14 genes. In addition, analysis of CNVs showed significant changes at multiple chromosomal loci in individuals with high or low hazard scores (Fig. 7B,C). For instance, the high-hazard score category exhibited more copy number amplification than the low-hazard score category on chromosome number 7. These findings offer a fresh perspective for investigating the role of hazard score in tumor mutations. To ascertain the association of hazard scores with the immune status of individuals with HNSCC from the TCGA-HNSC, the ssGSEA method was employed to detect the abundance of immune-infiltrating cell populations based on risk score (Fig. 7D). The presence of immune cell influx was greater in the low-hazard score category than in the high-hazard score category. Additionally, ESTIMATE analysis results demonstrated the significant increase in immune scores and decrease in tumor purity for the low-hazard score category (Fig. 7E). Finally, Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) findings confirmed that some important immune-associated routes (Fig. 7F) such as antigen handling and presentation, B cell receptor, VEGF, T cell receptor, and other signaling routes were more active in individuals with a low-hazard score. Meanwhile pathways related to tumor advancement, such as biosynthesis of N-glycans, cell cycle, and WNT signaling, were more prominent in those with a high-hazard score. These results suggest a strong connection between hazard score and the TME.

Relationship of risk score with chemotherapy and immunotherapy responses

GDSC project has identified clinically relevant genes as anticancer drug targets, with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) being a target for multiple anticancer drugs40. To evaluate the potential impact of the genes employed to construct risk core on drug reactions, we employed “oncoPredict” tool to forecast the IC50 values for the medications from the GDSC database against tumor specimens from the TCGA-HNSC cohort. Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant correlation between drug sensitivity (IC50 values) and transcriptional expression of 10 risk score genes, identifying 19 drugs that were correlated with at least three feature genes (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, we compared the drug sensitivity differences of the three frequently utilized chemotherapy medications for HNSCC treatment in elevated and reduced risk score groups. Paclitaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil showed significantly higher IC50 values in high-hazard score category than in low category (Fig. 8B–D). This indicates that HNSCC patients in the low-hazard score category might exhibit greater sensitivity to chemotherapy compared to those in the high-hazard score category. In the context of the latest advances in immunotherapy, especially PD-1 inhibitors, which have enhanced treatment results for HNSCC relapse and metastasis compared to traditional chemotherapy. Immunochemotherapy, which involves interaction between chemotherapy and immune cell mechanisms, is a strong candidate among the current cancer treatment strategies. We analyzed whether risk score can forecast the responsiveness of the anti-PD-1/L1 group (GSE159067) to immunotherapy. The results indicated that HNSCC patients with low-hazard score had experienced notable clinical advantages and extended survival compared to individuals with a high-hazard score (Fig. 8E). In contrast to the high-hazard score category, the low-hazard score category demonstrated enhanced clinical response to anti-PD-1/L1 therapy. (Fig. 8F,G). Thus, the hazard score is strongly linked to tumor response to anti-PD-1/L1 drug, and the developed hazard score model will facilitate the prediction of how HNSCC patients respond to anti-PD-1/L1 treatments.

By conducting single gene analysis of DKK1, it was observed that tumor tissues exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of DKK1 (Fig. 9A). Similarly, paired-like analysis revealed a substantial upregulation of DKK1 in cancerous tissues compared to near-by normal tissues (Fig. 9B). Notably, individuals with reduced DKK1 expression demonstrated prolonged survival rates (Fig. 9C). Therefore, the assessment of DKK1 holds potential as a strong prognostic marker for outcomes (Fig. 9D).

DKK1 was highly expressed in OSCC, and inhibition of DKK1 effectively inhibits the growth and spread of oral squamous cell carcinoma

Building upon the strong correlations identified in the preceding analyses, we further conducted experiments by extracting RNA from HOK, CAL27 and SCC15, and employed qRT-PCR to measure the expression of DKK1. The results illustrated elevated DKK1expression in OSCC cells (Fig. 10A). Likewise, the western blot analysis revealed that CAL27 and SCC15 OSCC cell lines exhibited significantly elevated concentrations of DKK1 protein relative to HOK cells (Fig. 10B). The immunohistochemical results demonstrated a strong expression of DKK1 in oral squamous cell carcinoma as opposed to normal oral tissue (Fig. 10C). Using an siRNA transfection protocol validated for high efficiency in CAL27 cells (Supplementary Fig. S1), we silenced DKK1 expression in both CAL27 and SCC15 cells. This led to a significant decrease in the growth and infiltration capacity of these OSCC cells, as demonstrated by CCK-8 and Transwell experiments (Fig. 10D-F).

Discussion

Disulfidptosis, a recently described cellular apoptosis process plays an essential role in tumor proliferation, infiltration, and spread41. Overexpression of genes related to disulfidptosis often indicates poor prognosis among cancer patients. Accumulating evidence suggests the applicability of disulfidptosis as an indicator to forecast tumor outcome and therapy response42,43. In this direction, researchers have constructed prediction models based on disulfidptosis that can also serve as a guide for tumor treatment. For instance, more invasive treatment modalities such as chemotherapy and surgery may be deemed necessary with high levels of disulfidptosis in tumor cells. Conversely, milder treatment options such as radiotherapy or immunotherapy may be appropriate when disulfidptosis level is low. In the context of HNSCC, early diagnosis is crucial44,45, and disulfidptosis can act as an indicator for forecasting prognosis and therapy response in HNSCC patients12.

Herein, we identified dysregulation in the levels of the 10 genes participating in disulfidptosis, contributing to HNSCC occurrence and progression. Unsupervised clustering revealed two subtypes with distinct biological differences in HNSCC cohort. Using the expression profiles of the DEGs between the groups, we created a risk prediction framework for HNSCC focusing on genes associated with disulfidptosis. This approach provides a new perspective and guidance for comprehensive management and treatment of HNSCC patients and improves their survival status. Our study revealed a significantly poorer OS for individuals with elevated hazard scores, which is consistent with previous study outcomes. Notably, the high-risk score group exhibited substantial variations in specific clinical attributes, such as advanced age and later staging. To enhance the accuracy of predictions, our study integrated the hazard score with additional clinical attributes, presenting the findings in a nomogram. The ROC curve and its AUC indicated excellent predictive performance. Moreover, discernible variations in genetic features and responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy were observed between the two patient subgroups. Immunotherapy emerged as a more appropriate choice for the low-hazard score category, characterized by its capacity to recruit more immune cells, thereby contributing to a more effective antitumor immune response.

DKK1 is the most important of the 10 genes in the predictive model. Our study revealed a substantial upregulation of DKK1 expression in OSCC cells, as determined by qRT-PCR. This finding is consistent with previous reports linking DKK1 to the pathogenesis of various cancers. Moreover, we observed higher levels of DKK1 protein in CAL27 and SCC15 cell lines compared to normal oral keratinocytes (HOK). These discoveries indicate a possible function for DKK1 in the malignant transformation of oral cells. By utilizing RNA interference to silence DKK1 expression, confirmed by efficient transfection in CAL27 cells (Supplementary Figure S1), we were able to investigate the functional implications of its upregulation in OSCC. Our results demonstrated that silencing DKK1 led to a significant decrease in the growth and infiltration capacity of OSCC cells, as demonstrated by CCK-8 and Transwell experiments. These data suggest that DKK1 expression is associated with proliferative and invasive phenotypes in OSCC models, possibly through modulation of key signaling pathways aberrantly activated during carcinogenesis.

DKK1 is a secreted protein that played a crucial role in the modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which is implicated in various biological functions such as cellular proliferation, differentiation, and programmed cell death46,47. Imbalance of the Wnt pathway has been linked to the onset and advancement of various cancer types48. In recent years, accumulating evidence has shed light on the intricate and context-dependent function of DKK1 in tumorigenesis.

In specific cellular contexts, DKK1 assumes the role of a cancer suppressor by counteracting the Wnt pathway, thereby exerting inhibitory effects on cancer cell proliferation. The intricate interplay between DKK1 and Wnt signaling pathways has been extensively studied in various cancer types, revealing DKK1’s multifaceted role in tumorigenesis. DKK1, as a released inhibitor of Wnt signaling, operates by binding with the Wnt co-receptor LRP5/6 and preventing the interaction between Wnt ligands and their receptors, thereby inhibiting downstream signaling events48. In our dataset, we did not directly test β‑catenin activity or nuclear translocation; consequently, references to Wnt/β‑catenin in this manuscript are interpretative and should be read as correlative.

Several studies have elucidated the tumor-suppressive effects of DKK1 accomplished through the suppression of Wnt signaling. For instance, Zhou et al.49 and Chen et al.50 identified DKK1 as a tumor suppressor gene in glioblastoma, increased DKK1 expression restrains the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, consequently impeding retinoblastoma progression. Similarly, in endometrial cancer (EC), DKK1 inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in endometrial cancer cells, thereby inhibiting the viability and stemness of endometrial cancer cells51. Additionally Ding et al. found that ZBTB38 binds to the DKK1 site and activates the production of DKK1 in prostate cancer cell lines, thereby suppressing its migration and proliferation52. Moreover, preclinical models and in vitro studies have provided further insights into the mechanisms underlying DKK1-mediated tumor suppression. For example, in breast cancer cell lines, overexpression of DKK1 has been shown to hinder cellular proliferation, promote programmed cell death, and restrain tumor growth both in laboratory settings and in living organisms53. Overall, the expanding body of research underscores the significance of DKK1 as a cancer-inhibiting factor in diverse types of cancer. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying DKK1-mediated inhibition of Wnt signaling provides valuable perspectives into possible therapeutic approaches for tumor therapy. Recent clinical research has identified that the atypical concentrations of DKK1 as an oncogenic factor is strongly correlated with tumor vascularization, metastasis, and other advanced invasions, and is linked with unfavorable prognosis54. Liu et al. discovered that heightened DKK1 levels in gastric cancer correlated with tumor vascularization, lymphatic invasion, and distant spreading, and patients exhibiting elevated DKK1 expression experienced reduced OS rates55. The increased serum levels of DKK-1 protein observed in individuals with cervical and endometrial carcinoma, associated with histological subtype, lymph node spread, and disease stage, make it a promising molecular indicator for detection and diagnostic purposes56. Several additional investigations have indicated that DKK1 is excessively expressed in various cancers, such as prostate cancer, liver cancer, bladder cancer, and lung cancer, indicating that DKK1 may function as a prospective indicator for detection and prediction56–59. Collectively, these studies provide compelling evidence for the oncogenic function of DKK1 across a spectrum of cancer types, underscoring its promise as a therapeutic focus in cancer therapy.

In conclusion, we developed a prediction model based on the distinctive cell death pathway, disulfidptosis, for HNSCC patients. The risk score demonstrated good predictive performance and held certain guiding significance for the comprehensive management of HNSCC patients, thus contributing to improving their prognosis. Nevertheless, it is crucial to recognize that this study has limitations, and additional analysis with experimental validation is necessary to confirm the model and improve the robustness and credibility of the hazard score.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Our analyses rely on retrospective transcriptomic datasets from TCGA‑HNSC and GEO; despite normalization and multi‑cohort evaluation, residual batch effects, platform differences, and selection bias may persist. While the overall sample size is moderate, effective sample numbers become limited after stratification by anatomical subsite and HPV/EBV status; such biological heterogeneity likely attenuates effect sizes and helps explain why only a small number of DEGs remain under a stringent threshold (|log2FC| ≥ 1.15). Accordingly, we retained |log2FC| ≥ 0.585 with FDR control as the primary criterion and supported the conclusions with full‑gene pre‑ranked GSEA and risk‑model performance. The risk signature was derived via univariate Cox pre‑screening followed by LASSO‑Cox; although internally cross‑validated and externally evaluated, potential overfitting cannot be excluded, and prospective, multi‑center validation with detailed treatment information is needed. Importantly, all functional assays were performed in two OSCC lines (CAL27 and SCC15), which supports a role for DKK1 in OSCC but does not establish representativeness for non‑oral HNSCC subtypes; STR profiling was used solely to authenticate cell line identity and does not imply biological applicability. We did not perform assays that constitute definitive evidence for disulfidptosis (e.g., cystine‑starvation with F‑actin collapse or disulfide‑stress readouts) or direct β‑catenin activity/localization measurements (e.g., TOP/FOP luciferase reporter or nuclear‑fraction western blot), nor rescue experiments. Consequently, statements linking DKK1 to disulfidptosis or canonical Wnt/β‑catenin signaling are framed as associations derived from transcriptomic patterns and OSCC‑cell phenotypes rather than causal demonstrations. In addition, CAL27 is known to be challenging to transfect, which may limit siRNA uptake efficiency; while Lipofectamine 2000 is commonly used in OSCC cell lines, we did not quantify DKK1 knockdown or perform fluorescent control siRNA, so transfection‑related effects cannot be excluded. These invitro results are therefore framed as associative observations and do not alter the main conclusions derived from full‑gene GSEA and survival modeling. Finally, transcript‑level inferences may not reflect protein abundance, activity, or spatial context; integration of proteomic/phospho‑proteomic, single‑cell, and spatial transcriptomic data will further strengthen mechanistic interpretation and clinical translatability.

Conclusion

We found that dysregulation of disulfidptosis-related genes contributes to HNSCC progression. Cross-talks among these genes may determine disulfidptosis subtypes and TME cell infiltration. The TCGA-HNSC dataset was categorized into two subtypes, with Cluster 1 showing notable survival advantages. DEGs in these subtypes were enriched in different pathways. Using 10 prognosis-related genes, we formulated a hazard score model that acted as an autonomous prognostic determinant and predicted chemotherapy sensitivity and suitability for anti-PD-1/L1 therapy. This model aids personalized therapy strategies. Additionally, elevated DKK1 expression in OSCC cells was confirmed, and its knockdown following efficient transfection inhibited cell proliferation and invasion, indicating its role in OSCC development. These discoveries offer a fresh, accurate predictive model for HNSCC prognosis and treatment response, supporting personalized chemotherapy and immunotherapy.
