Long-Term Survival After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Relative to an Age- and Comorbidity-Matched Population
Authors: Akiva Rosenzveig, Faysal Massad, Osamah Badwan, Ankit Agrawal, Samuel Reiss, James Yun, Milind Desai, Amar Krishnaswamy, Samir Kapadia, Grant W. Reed
Published: 2026

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is approved for patients across the surgical risk spectrum. Guidelines from the American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association recommend TAVR as the preferred approach for patients aged ≥80 years who are appropriate candidates, whereas surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) is preferred for patients ≤65 years with life expectancy ≥10 years.1

While TAVR has been compared with medical therapy in inoperable patients and with SAVR across surgical risk categories,2 it remains unclear how TAVR affects survival as compared to individuals without aortic stenosis (AS). In the SWEDEHEART (Swedish Web-system for Enhancement and Development of Evidence-based care in Heart disease Evaluated According to Recommended Therapies) study of SAVR patients, life expectancy was reduced by 1.5 years in those aged 70 to 79 and by 0.4 years in those ≥80 compared to age-matched population controls.3 Comparable data after TAVR are limited. What is the clinical question being addressed?

Does survival after transcatheter aortic valve replacement approximate that of age- and comorbidity-matched patients without aortic stenosis?

What is the main finding?

Among adults aged ≥65 years, long-term survival after TAVR approximated matched controls, whereas excess mortality persisted in patients aged 50 to 64 years.

We used the TriNetX platform, a federated network of deidentified electronic health records (EHRs) from 113 million patients across 64 health care organizations. The data set includes standardized data on demographics, diagnoses (the International Classification of Diseases-10), medications (RxNorm), procedures (Current Procedural Terminology), labs (LOINC), and socioeconomic information.

We identified patients aged ≥50 years with AS who underwent TAVR and GP controls without AS or TAVR between August 1, 2016, and August 1, 2024. Individuals with prior valve surgery or coronary artery bypass grafting were excluded.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 1, 3, and 5 years from the index date. Analyses were stratified by age: 50 to 64, 65 to 80, and ≥81 years. Propensity score matching was performed separately within each age stratum using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement through the TriNetX platform using a caliper of 0.1 pooled SDs. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 considered acceptable.

Follow-up continued until death, loss to follow-up, or 5 years. Mortality within TriNetX is derived from linked EHR and national death data sources, though out-of-network deaths may be incompletely captured. Cox proportional hazards models estimated HRs and 95% CIs based on the matched samples. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used for survival estimates. Significance was defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted on March 6, 2025, using built-in TriNetX tools powered by R (Survival v3.2-3), Python v3.7, and Java v11.

We identified 52,457 patients aged ≥50 years with AS who underwent TAVR and 4,074,283 GP controls without AS or TAVR during the study period. Among patients aged 50 to 64 years, median follow-up was 729 days (IQR: 1014) for the TAVR cohort and 743 days (IQR: 1,401) for the GP comparator. In those aged 65 to 80 years, median follow-up was 673 days (IQR: 957) for TAVR and 772 days (IQR: 1,389) for GP. Among patients aged ≥81 years, follow-up was shorter, with a median of 540 days (IQR: 822) for TAVR and 440 days (IQR: 805) for GP.

In the 50 to 64 age group, TAVR patients (N = 4,208) were older (60.0 vs 58.0 years) and more likely to be White (75.2% vs 51.5%) and male (64.4% vs 49.7%) compared to the GP (N = 2.3 million). Cardiovascular conditions were markedly more prevalent: ischemic heart disease (67.5% vs 7.2%), heart failure (57.7% vs 2.4%), and hypertension (76.8% vs 28.9%).

In the 65 to 80 group, TAVR patients were older (74.1 vs 70.8 years), predominantly White (80.6% vs 54.3%), and had higher rates of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and kidney disease compared to the GP. Notable differences included ischemic heart disease (73.6% vs 16.7%) and heart failure (52.6% vs 5.5%).

Among those aged ≥81, TAVR patients (N = 15,506) were older (83.6 vs 83.0 years) and showed greater prevalence of ischemic heart disease (74.8% vs 25.0%), heart failure (53.4% vs 11.2%), and other comorbidities than the GP (N = 221,105). After propensity score matching, age groups were well balanced.

In the 50 to 64 group, 5-year mortality was 16.2% for TAVR vs 10.6% for GP (P < 0.001). For ages 65 to 80, 5-year mortality was 14.9% vs 18.0% (P < 0.001). HRs at specific time points were 1-year HR = 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84-0.95), 3-year HR = 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88-0.96), and 5-year HR = 0.93 (95% CI: 0.89-0.96) (P < 0.001 for all) (Figure 1).

In the ≥81 group, 5-year mortality was 16.8% in TAVR vs 17.7% in GP (P = 0.05). HRs at specific time points were 1-year HR = 0.85 (95% CI: 0.78-0.93), 3-year HR = 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77-0.87), and 5-year HR = 0.83 (95% CI: 0.79-0.88) (P < 0.001 for all) (Figure 1).

Our findings demonstrate that TAVR mitigates the excess mortality associated with untreated AS and results in survival comparable to that of an age- and comorbidity-matched GP in patients aged ≥65 years. In contrast, in patients aged 50 to 64, TAVR did not lead to similar survival.

Data from our center show that SAVR outcomes have improved over time, outperforming the Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality.4 The SWEDEHEART study also reported only modest losses in life expectancy after SAVR: 1.5 years in those aged 70 to 79 and 0.4 years in those ≥80.3 In this context, our findings support the possibility that TAVR, with its less invasive approach and reduced perioperative morbidity, may offer even greater survival benefits for older patients ≥65 years.

Prior studies have shown similar age-dependent effects. For example, Martin et al analyzed 6,420 patients in the UK TAVI registry (2007-2014) and found that only those ≥85 had survival approximating the GP, while younger patients had worse outcomes.5

Our analysis, with robust propensity score matching and a significantly larger sample, shows improved survival beginning at age 65. Additionally, our cohort reflects patients treated from 2016 onward, representing more intermediate- and low-risk individuals and benefiting from advances in valve technology and operator expertise. Conversely, younger patients undergoing TAVR during the study period likely represented a highly selected cohort with substantial comorbidity burden, prior cardiac disease, frailty, or procedural contraindications to surgery that may not be fully captured within TriNetX.

Limitations of our study include potential residual confounding, limited socioeconomic data, and absence of cause-specific or functional outcomes along with the possibility of missing out-of-hospital deaths. Additionally, TriNetX lacks granular clinical variables including frailty measures, symptom severity, echocardiographic parameters, valve anatomy, Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk scores, procedural complications, and device-specific characteristics. Cause-specific mortality and functional outcomes were unavailable. As with all administrative/EHR-based analyses, the possibility of coding inaccuracies, residual confounding, and incomplete capture of out-of-network deaths remains. Additionally, differences in follow-up duration between age groups and potential informative censoring inherent to EHR-based data sets may have influenced observed outcomes. Also, formal testing of proportional hazards assumptions and advanced time-varying survival modeling were not feasible within the native TriNetX analytic environment. Nevertheless, our findings offer insights into the benefits of TAVR and can support shared decision-making. Future research should focus on understanding the incomplete benefit in younger patients and evaluating the long-term durability of transcatheter valves.
