Damping Effects on Global Existence and Scattering for an Inhomogeneous NLS Equation with Inverse-Square Potential
Makram Hamouda ID , Mohamed Majdoub ID & Tarek Saanouni ID

Abstract
. This work explores the global existence and scattering behavior of solutions to a damped, inhomogeneous nonlinear Schrödinger equation featuring a time-dependent damping term, an inverse-square potential, and an inhomogeneous nonlinearity. We establish global well-posedness in the energy space for subcritical, mass-critical, and energy-critical regimes, using Strichartz estimates, Hardy inequalities, and Gagliardo–Nirenberg-type estimates. For sufficiently large damping, we highlight how the interplay between damping, singular potentials, and inhomogeneity influences the dynamics. Our results extend existing studies and offer new insights into the long-time behavior of solutions in this more general setting. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to address the combined effects of inverse-square potential, inhomogeneous (or homogeneous) nonlinearity, and damping in the context of the NLS equation.

1.
Introduction and main results

We consider the Cauchy problem for the inhomogeneous Schrödinger (INLS) equation with inverse-square potential and an unsteady linear damping term, namely

(DINLS)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm i}\partial_{t}u-\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}\,u+{\rm i}\mathbf{a}(t)u=\mu|x|^{-b}|u|^{p-1}u,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{array}\right.

where the space dimension is
N\geq 3
, the inhomogeneous term exponent is
0<b<2
, the exponent of the source term is
p>1
,
\mu=\pm 1
and the linear free operator is
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}=-\Delta+\lambda|x|^{-2}
, where
\lambda>-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}
. The damping term is a time-dependent nonegative continuous function
\mathbf{a}:=\mathbf{a}(t)\geq 0
. The focusing case corresponds to
\mu=-1
and the defocusing one stands for
\mu=1
.

Based on the sharp Hardy inequality
[
4
]
,

(1.1)

\lambda_{N}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-2}\,|f(x)|^{2}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,dx,

where the optimal constant
\lambda_{N}
is given by

(1.2)

\lambda_{N}=\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4},

it is known that the operator
-\Delta+\lambda|x|^{-2}
admits a positive semi-definite symmetric extension
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}
for all
\lambda>-\lambda_{N}
. However, in the range
-\lambda_{N}<\lambda<1-\lambda_{N}
, this extension is not unique
[
33
,
53
]
. In such cases, one typically chooses the Friedrichs extension
[
33
,
47
]
, which is the canonical self-adjoint extension associated with the corresponding quadratic form:

(1.3)

\left<\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}f,f\right>_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigg[|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+\lambda|x|^{-2}\,|f(x)|^{2}\bigg]\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Big|\nabla f(x)+\kappa|x|^{-2}\,xf(x)\Big|^{2}\,dx,

for
\lambda>-\lambda_{N}
and for any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}\right)
. In the above identity (
1.3
), we introduced the following parameter:

(1.4)

\kappa:=\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{N}+\lambda},

which is of essential use regarding the norms’ equivalence.

Note that the operator
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}
is well-defined on the domain,

H^{1}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{N})=\{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\lambda}}<\infty\},

where

(1.5)

\|f\|_{H^{1}_{\lambda}}^{2}=\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\lambda|x|^{-2}\,|f(x)|^{2}\,dx=\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}_{\lambda}}^{2}.

It is straightforward, by (
1.1
), to see that we have the equivalence of the norms as follows:

(1.6)

\|f\|_{{H}^{1}_{\lambda}}\sim\|f\|_{H^{1}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ \lambda>-\lambda_{N}.

From the physical point of view, the equation (
DINLS
) arises in diverse physical contexts ranging from quantum gases to nonlinear optics. Specifically, this model incorporates an inverse-square potential which arises in quantum three-body problems, a spatially modulated nonlinearity that captures interaction inhomogeneities in trapped Bose-Einstein condensates, and a time-dependent damping term modeling energy gain/loss dissipation. The interplay of these features yields a mathematically rich framework for analyzing global existence and scattering. For further details, see
[
8
,
12
,
33
,
34
]
and the references therein.

The mathematical analysis of (
DINLS
) presents a nontrivial synthesis of three fundamental phenomena: the critical scaling behavior induced by the inverse-square potential, the spatial modulation of nonlinear interactions, and the non-autonomous effects of damping. Each component individually raises delicate analytical questions concerning functional settings, dispersive properties, and long-time dynamics. The transition between these regimes reveals a subtle competition.

Equation (
DINLS
) with
\mathbf{a}=0
is invariant under the following scaling transformation:

u_{\delta}(t,x):=\delta^{\frac{2-b}{p-1}}u(\delta^{2}t,\delta x),\hskip 10.00002pt\delta>0.

Associated with this scaling is the critical Sobolev exponent

(1.7)

s_{c}:=\frac{N}{2}-\frac{2-b}{p-1},

which leaves the homogeneous Sobolev norm invariant in the sense that

\|u_{\delta}(t)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\delta^{s-s_{c}}\|u(\delta^{2}t)\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

Two particular cases are of significant interest from a physical viewpoint. The first is the
mass-critical
case, corresponding to
s_{c}=0
, which occurs when
p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
. This regime is associated with the conservation of mass. The second is the
energy-critical
case, defined by
s_{c}=1
, which arises when
p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N-2}
.

1.1.
Main results

In this section, we present our main results on global existence and scattering for the equation (
DINLS
). We consider only the focusing case, corresponding to
\mu=-1
.

We begin with the following theorem which establishes global existence and scattering in both the mass-subcritical and the mass-critical settings.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\lambda>-\lambda_{N},\ 0\leq b<2
,
\displaystyle 1<p\leq 1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
, and
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Assume that
\underline{\mathbf{a}}>0
.

(a)

If
\displaystyle p<1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
, then the solution
u
to (
DINLS
) exists globally and scatters in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

(b)

If
\displaystyle p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
and

(1.11)

\|u_{0}\|<\left(\frac{N}{N+2-b}\right)^{\frac{N}{4-2b}}\|\mathcal{Q}\|,

then the solution
u
to (
DINLS
) exists globally and scatters in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Here,
\mathcal{Q}
is the ground state given by (
2.35
) below.

Remark 1.1
.

(i)

Assumption (
1.11
) imposes a stronger condition than the classical ground state criterion
\|u_{0}\|<\|\mathcal{Q}\|
.

(ii)

For
\displaystyle 1<p<1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
, the global existence can be proven as in
[
29
, Proposition B.1]
.

(iii)

In the mass-critical regime
\displaystyle p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
, the global existence is shown in
[
29
, Theorem 1.6]
for
\lambda=b=0
and for

being sufficiently large. Under Assumption (

(iv)

As mentioned before, for example, in
[
15

and
[
51

, scattering requires the nonlinearity to exhibit superlinear growth near zero. Specifically, the nonlinearity exponent must exceed a critical value,
p_{*}=p_{*}(N,\lambda,b)
, which marks the threshold between scattering and the nonexistence of asymptotically free solutions.

(v)

For the standard NLS (
\lambda=b=0
in (
DINLS
)), the critical value is
p_{*}(N,0,0)=1+\frac{2}{N}
; see
[
7
,
49

,

]
. Recently, in
[

it was shown that

for
0<b<1
. Alternatively, a partial result was obtained in
[
56
]
for
N=3,\lambda>0,b=0
, that is,
p_{*}(3,\lambda,0)=1+\frac{2}{3}
. Based on the aforementioned recall, we conjecture that
p_{*}(N,\lambda,b)=1+\frac{2-2b}{N}
for
\lambda>0
and
0\leq b<1
.

(vi)

We conjecture that the scattering result in Theorem
1.1
remains valid even when the damping function is merely nonnegative for sufficiently large times.

The following theorem deals with the global existence and scattering of (
DINLS

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

\lambda\geq 0

(ii)

(iii)

2.
Background and auxiliary results

This section is dedicated to introducing the necessary notation, along with several auxiliary results and useful estimates.

For simplicity, we will employ the following notations:

\|\cdot\|_{L^{r}}:=\|\cdot\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},\hskip 9.24994pt\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}},\hskip 9.24994pt\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}:=\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

Then, we define some energy-related quantities associated with equation (
DINLS
), namely

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}[u(t)]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx,

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[{u(t)}]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(t,x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx-\frac{2}{p+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|u(t,x)|^{p+1}\,dx,

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{I}}[u(t)]

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(t,x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|u(t,x)|^{p+1}\,dx.

Observe that the energy can be expressed as

(2.4)

{\mathbf{E}}[{u(t)}]=\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2}-\frac{2}{p+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|u(t,x)|^{p+1}\,dx.

Next, we introduce the following notation:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(t)=\int_{0}^{|t|}\,\mathbf{a}(s)ds,\hskip 9.24994ptt\in\mathbb{R},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{a}}=\inf_{t>0}\,\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{A}(t)}{t}\bigg),\hskip 9.24994pt\overline{\mathbf{a}}=\sup_{t>0}\,\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{A}(t)}{t}\bigg).

To the best of our knowledge, the quantities defined in (
2.5
)–(

) were first introduced in
[
29
]

\displaystyle{\mathbf{M}}(u(t))={\rm e}^{-2\mathbf{A}(t)}{\mathbf{M}}(u_{0}),\vskip 8.5359pt

Definition 2.1

Let

The following lemma will be essential to establish the completeness of a metric space that will be subsequently employed in our fixed point argument.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
1<q,r<\infty
, let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval, and let
M>0
. Define the space

\mathbf{Y}:=\big\{u\in L^{q}(I;W^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}));\;\;\|u\|_{L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})}\leq M\big\},

equipped with the metric

d(u,v):=\|u-v\|_{L^{q}(I;L^{r})}.

Then
(\mathbf{Y},d)
forms a complete metric space.

Proof of Lemma
2.11
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Cauchy sequence in
(\mathbf{Y},d)
. Since
L^{q}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
is a complete metric space with respect to the standard Lebesgue norm, there exists
u\in L^{q}(I;L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
such that

(2.41)

\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{q}(I;L^{r})}\to 0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as }n\to\infty.

To establish the completeness of
(\mathbf{Y},d)
, it suffices to show that
u\in\mathbf{Y}
.

First, observe that the strong convergence in Lebesgue spaces implies the convergence in the sense of distributions:

(2.42)

u_{n}\to u\hskip 9.24994pt\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Since
L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})
is a reflexive Banach space (due to the fact
1<q,r<\infty
) and
(u_{n})
is bounded in this space by definition of
\mathbf{Y}
, there exists (by weak sequential compactness) a subsequence
(u_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges weakly to some
v\in L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})
. Moreover, by the lower semicontinuity of the norm under weak convergence
[
10
, Proposition 3.5 (iii), p. 58]
, we have:

(2.43)

\|v\|_{L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|u_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})}\leq M.

The weak convergence in
L^{q}(I;W^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
implies convergence in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
:

(2.44)

u_{n_{k}}\to v\hskip 9.24994pt\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{N}).

From (
2.42
) and (
2.44
), we deduce by uniqueness of distributional limits that
u=v
. Therefore, we conclude that
u\in L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})
(inherited from
v
) with

\|u\|_{L^{q}(I;W^{1,r})}\leq M
by (
2.43
),
which establishes that
u\in\mathbf{Y}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.11
.
∎

3.
Mass-sub-critical regime

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We begin by defining the Hamiltonian for
v
:

(3.1)

\mathbf{H}(v(t)):=\|\nabla v(t)\|^{2}+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|v(t,x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx-\frac{2}{p+1}e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|v(t,x)|^{p+1}\,dx.

Through direct computation, we obtain its time derivative:

(3.2)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{H}(v(t))=\frac{2(p-1)}{p+1}\mathbf{a}(t)e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|v(t,x)|^{p+1}\,dx.

Integration over time yields the identity:

(3.3)

\mathbf{H}(v(t))=\mathbf{E}(u_{0})+\frac{2(p-1)}{p+1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(s)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|v(s,x)|^{p+1}\,dx\right)ds.

Similarly, we define the Hamiltonian for
u
as:

(3.4)

\mathcal{H}(u(t)):=e^{2\mathbf{A}(t)}\mathbf{E}(u(t))-\frac{2(p-1)}{p+1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)e^{2\mathbf{A}(s)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|u(s,x)|^{p+1}\,dx\right)\,ds.

From (
2.12
), we immediately deduce the conservation property:

(3.5)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(u(t))=0.

Combining (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we obtain the energy identity:

(3.6)

\mathbf{E}(u_{0})=e^{2\mathbf{A}(t)}\mathbf{E}(u(t))-\frac{2(p-1)}{p+1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)e^{2\mathbf{A}(s)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|x|^{-b}|u(s,x)|^{p+1}\,dx\right)\,ds,

where
\mathbf{A}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}(u(t))
are defined in (
2.5
) and (
2.2
), respectively.

First, consider the mass-subcritical regime, that is
p<1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
.
Using the energy functional
\mathbf{E}(u(t))
from (
2.4
) together with (
2.11
), Hardy’s inequality (
1.1
) and the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (
2.32
), we obtain the lower bound:

(3.7)

\mathbf{E}(u(t))\geq\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2}-\frac{2\mathtt{K}_{opt}}{1+p}\|u_{0}\|^{1+p-\nu}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{\nu},

where the exponent
\nu
is given by

(3.8)

\nu=b+\frac{N(p-1)}{2}.

Combining (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we derive the following estimate:

(3.9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}(u_{0})\geq&\ e^{2\mathbf{A}(t)}\left(\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2}-\frac{2\mathtt{K}_{opt}}{1+p}\|u_{0}\|^{1+p-\nu}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{\nu}\right)\\
&-\frac{2\mathtt{K}_{opt}(p-1)}{1+p}\|u_{0}\|^{1+p-\nu}\,\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)e^{2\mathbf{A}(s)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(s)\|^{\nu}\,ds.\end{split}

We can rewrite (
3.9
) in the form

(3.10)

f(t)\leq\mathbf{E}(u_{0})+g(t)(f(t))^{\beta}+\int_{0}^{t}h(s)(f(s))^{\beta}\,ds,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\,\,\;0\leq t<T_{max},

where we define:

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=e^{2\mathbf{A}(t)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2},

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathtt{K}_{opt}}{p+1}\|u_{0}\|^{p+1-\nu}e^{(2-\nu)\mathbf{A}(t)},

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(p-1)\mathtt{K}_{opt}}{p+1}\|u_{0}\|^{p+1-\nu}\,\mathbf{a}(t)e^{(2-\nu)\mathbf{A}(t)},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu}{2}=\frac{b}{2}+\frac{N(p-1)}{4}.

For the range
p<1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
(which implies that
\beta<1
), we verify that all conditions of
[
30
, Lemma 3.1]
are satisfied. Consequently, by norm equivalence (
2.14
), we obtain the uniform bound,

(3.11)

\sup_{0\leq t<T_{max}}e^{\mathbf{A}(t)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|<\infty.

The above estimate together with the blow-up alternative yield the global existence, that is
T_{max}=\infty
. Furthermore, the estimate (
3.11
) implies the global bound

(3.12)

\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|\lesssim e^{-\mathbf{A}(t)}\lesssim e^{-\underline{\mathbf{a}}t},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\ t\geq 0.

Therefore, the scattering for
p<1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
is obtained similarly as in
[
30
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Now, we consider the mass-critical case
p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N}
. In this setting, we clearly have
\nu=2
and
\mathtt{K}_{\text{opt}}=\frac{1+p}{2}\|\mathcal{Q}\|^{1-p}
. Thus, inequality (
3.9
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\left(\frac{\|u_{0}\|}{\|\mathcal{Q}\|}\right)^{p-1}\right)\left(e^{\mathbf{A}(t)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|\right)^{2}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt-(p-1)\left(\frac{\|u_{0}\|}{\|\mathcal{Q}\|}\right)^{p-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)\left(e^{\mathbf{A}(s)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(s)\|\right)^{2}\,ds.

Note that, by taking
t=0
in the RHS of (
3.13
) and using (
1.11
), one can easily see that
\mathbf{E}(u_{0})\geq 0
. Now, recalling assumption (
1.11
), we obtain

(3.14)

\left(e^{\mathbf{A}(t)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(u_{0})}{1-\varrho}+\frac{(p-1)\varrho}{1-\varrho}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{a}(s)\left(e^{\mathbf{A}(s)}\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(s)\|\right)^{2}\,ds,

where
\varrho:=\left(\frac{\|u_{0}\|}{\|\mathcal{Q}\|}\right)^{p-1}
.
Applying Gronwall’s lemma to (
3.14
) implies that

(3.15)

\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(u_{0})}{1-\varrho}\,\exp\left(\left(\frac{(p-1)\varrho}{1-\varrho}-2\right)\mathbf{A}(t)\right).

Consequently, scattering holds provided

(3.16)

\frac{(p-1)\varrho}{1-\varrho}<2.

Indeed, (
3.16
) combined with the fact that
\underline{\mathbf{a}}>0
gives

(3.17)

\|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u(t)\|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathbf{E}(u_{0})}{1-\varrho}\,\exp\left(-\left(2-\frac{(p-1)\varrho}{1-\varrho}\right)\underline{\mathbf{a}}t\right).

A direct computation reveals that (
3.16
) is equivalent to (
1.11
).
∎

4.
Inter-critical regime

In this section, we establish the proof of Theorem
1.2
which is essentially based on the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume the hypotheses in Theorem

hold true. Let
\theta,r
and

as defined in (

). Suppose further that
\underline{\mathbf{a}}>0
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon>0
independent of
\mathbf{a}(t)
such that the solution of (

(4.1)

\left\|U_{\mathbf{a},\lambda}(\cdot)u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\theta}(0,\infty;L^{r})}\leq\varepsilon.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Although we deal here with a time-dependent damping term, the proof of Proposition
4.1
follows the same lines as in
[
14
, Theorem 1.8]
. However, to facilitate the reading of the article, we will give the most important steps. First, recall the definition of
(q,r,\theta,\tilde{\theta})
as given by (

) and (

). Then, let us define

(4.2)

\Phi(u)(t)=U_{\mathbf{a},\lambda}(t)u_{0}+\,i\int_{0}^{t}\,U_{\mathbf{a},\lambda}(t-\tau)\left[|\cdot|^{-b}|u(\tau)|^{p-1}u(\tau)\right]d\tau,

(4.3)

\mathbf{X}=\left\{u:\,\|u\|_{L^{\theta}(0,\infty;L^{r})}\leq 2\varepsilon,\,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}_{\lambda})}\leq 2{\color[rgb]{0.39,0.58,0.93}C}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}},\ \|\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}_{\lambda}\|_{L^{q}(0,\infty;L^{r})}\leq 2{\color[rgb]{0.39,0.58,0.93}C}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\right\},

that we endow with the following distance

(4.4)

d(u,v)=\|u-v\|_{L^{q}(0,\infty;L^{r})}+\|u-v\|_{L^{\theta}(0,\infty;L^{r})}.

One can easily check that
(q,r)
is admissible,
(\theta,r)
is
s_{c}-
admissible and
(\tilde{\theta},r)
is
(-s_{c})-
admissible in the sense of Definition
2.1
, where
s_{c}
is defined by (
1.7
).

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem 1.8 in
[
14
]
and using Strichartz estimates together with Lemma
2.6
, the function
\Phi
defines a contraction on the complete metric space
(\mathbf{X},d)
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
is thus achieved via the Banach fixed-point theorem.
∎

Now, to conclude the proof of global existence in Theorem
1.2
, it remains to show that we can make
\left\|U_{\mathbf{a},\lambda}(\cdot)u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\theta}(0,\infty;L^{r})}
small enough for large
\underline{\mathbf{a}}
. To this end, we use the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{r}
and (
2.6
), and we obtain

(4.5)

\begin{split}\left\|U_{\mathbf{a},\lambda}(\cdot)u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\theta}(0,\infty;L^{r})}^{\theta}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,{\rm e}^{-\theta\mathbf{A}(t)}\,\|{\rm e}^{-it\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}}u_{0}\|_{L^{r}}^{\theta}\,dt\\
&\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{\theta}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\,{\rm e}^{-\theta{\underline{\mathbf{a}}}t}\,dt\lesssim\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{\theta}}{\theta\,{\underline{\mathbf{a}}}}.\end{split}

From (
4.5
) and Proposition
4.1
we easily deduce that
T^{*}=\infty
if
\underline{\mathbf{a}}\gtrsim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{\theta}
(
\mathbf{a}^{*}=C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{\theta}
).

This finishes the proof of small data global existence part of Theorem
1.2
. Furthermore, owing to the definition of the space
\mathbf{X}
, the global solution enjoys the bound
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;H^{1}_{\lambda})}\leq 2C_{s}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}
, which yields the scattering part of Theorem
1.2
.

5.
Energy-critical regime

This section focuses on proving Theorem
1.3
. The core of our argument relies on a key auxiliary result, which we present in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
N\geq 3
,
0<b<\frac{4}{N}
, and
p=1+\frac{4-2b}{N-2}
. Suppose that the parameter
\lambda
satisfies

\lambda>-\frac{(N+2-2b)^{2}-4}{(N+2-2b)^{2}}\lambda_{N},

where
\lambda_{N}
is defined in (
1.2
). For every initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, there exists
\mathbf{a}^{*}>0
such that if
\underline{\mathbf{a}}\geq\mathbf{a}^{*}
(with
\underline{\mathbf{a}}
given by (
2.6
)), the problem (
2.13
) has a unique global solution
v
such that

v\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))\cap L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})),

where
r=\frac{2N(N+2-2b)}{N^{2}-2Nb+4},

and the pair
(\alpha(r),r)
satisfies the admissibility condition in (
2.15
). Furthermore, the solution satisfies the uniform bound

(5.1)

\|v(t)\|_{H^{1}}\leq 2C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all \ }t\geq 0,

where
C>0
is a constant.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

We follow steps similar to those in the proof of
[
32
, Theorem 1.1]
with the necessary modifications. For
M:=2C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}
, we define

\mathcal{X}:=\left\{v\in L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r}):\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r})}\leq M\right\},

and

(5.2)

\Psi(v)(t):=U_{\lambda}(t)u_{0}+i\int_{0}^{t}\,e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(\tau)}U_{\lambda}(t-\tau)\left[|\cdot|^{-b}|v(\tau)|^{p-1}v(\tau)\right]d\tau.

We emphasize that, by Lemma
2.11
, the function space
\mathcal{X}
endowed with the metric

d(v_{1},v_{2}):=\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))}

forms a complete metric space. This completeness will be crucial for our subsequent fixed-point argument.

In the sequel, we will verify the conditions required in the application of the Banach fixed-point theorem. For that purpose, we show that
\Psi
maps
\mathcal{X}
to itself and it is a contraction for
\underline{\mathbf{a}}
large enough.

Note that the function
\tau\mapsto e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(\tau)}
is bounded for all
\tau\geq 0
. Hence, it will be ignored in the next estimates in the remainder of this proof.

For
v\in\mathcal{X}
, by Strichartz estimate and Lemma
2.10
, we obtain that

(5.3)

\begin{split}\|\Psi(v)\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r})}&\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+CM^{p-1}\|e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(t)}v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r})}\\
&\leq\frac{M}{2}+CM^{p-1}e^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}}\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r})}\\
&\leq\frac{M}{2}+CM^{p}e^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}}.\end{split}

If we choose
\mathbf{a}^{*}>0
such that
CM^{p-1}e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{a}^{*}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then for
\underline{\mathbf{a}}\geq\mathbf{a}^{*}
, we get by (
5.3
),

(5.4)

\|\Psi(v)\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};W^{1,r})}\leq\frac{M}{2}+\frac{M}{2}=M.

Now, let
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
. Again employing Strichartz estimate and Lemma
2.10
, we end up with the following estimate for
\underline{\mathbf{a}}\geq\mathbf{a}^{*}
:

(5.5)

\begin{split}\|\Psi(v_{1})-\Psi(v_{2})\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r})}&\leq Ce^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}}\left\||x|^{-b}\left(|v_{1}|^{p-1}v_{1}-|v_{2}|^{p-1}v_{2}\right)\right\|_{L^{\alpha(\sigma)^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{\sigma^{\prime}})}\\
&\leq Ce^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}}\left(\|v_{1}\|^{p-1}_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\dot{W}^{1,r})}+\|v_{2}\|^{p-1}_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\dot{W}^{1,r})}\right)\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r})}\\
&\leq Ce^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}}M^{p-1}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r})}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(\mathbb{R}_{+};L^{r})}.\end{split}

This guarantees the existence of a unique fixed point
v\in\mathcal{X}
for the mapping
\Psi
, that is,
\Psi(v)=v
.

The regularity
v\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1})
follows directly from the identity
\Psi(v)=v\in\mathcal{X}
and the application of Lemma
2.10
. To establish the estimate (
5.1
), we combine the Strichartz estimates, the definition of the space
\mathcal{X}
, the relation
M=2C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}
, and arguments similar to those used in the proof of (
5.3
).

This completes the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

∎

Remark 5.1
.

The damping term plays a crucial role here. Indeed, unlike the undamped case, which requires restricting the time interval to ensure contraction for local existence, our large damping assumption directly enables the fixed-point argument without shortening the existence time. This gives rise to global existence directly in the damped case.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
v
be the global solution of (
2.13
) given by Proposition
5.1
. The Duhamel formula for (
2.13
) yields

(5.6)

v(t)=U_{\lambda}(t)v(t_{0})+i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,e^{-(p-1)\mathbf{A}(\tau)}U_{\lambda}(t-\tau)\left[|\cdot|^{-b}|v(\tau)|^{p-1}v(\tau)\right]d\tau,

where
t_{0}>0
is arbitrary chosen. Let
T>t_{0}
. Using Strichartz inequality, Lemma
2.10
, (
5.1
) and (
2.6
), we infer that

(5.7)

\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(t_{0},T;\ W^{1,r})}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}+C{\rm e}^{-(p-1)\underline{\mathbf{a}}t_{0}}\,\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(t_{0},T;\ W^{1,r})}^{p}.

Applying Lemma
2.7
, we obtain that

(5.8)

\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(t_{0},T;\ W^{1,r})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}.

Letting
T\to\infty
in (
5.8
), we get

(5.9)

\|v\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(t_{0},\infty;\ W^{1,r})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}.

From (
5.9
), the relation
v(t,x)=e^{\mathbf{A}(t)}u(t,x)
and (
2.6
), we have

(5.10)

\|u\|_{L^{\alpha(r)}(t_{0},\infty;\ W^{1,r}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))}\lesssim{\rm e}^{-\underline{\mathbf{a}}t_{0}}\,\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}.

This yields the scattering for (
DINLS
), and hence the proof of Theorem
1.3
is complete.
∎

6.
Conclusion and open questions

The present work offers a new avenue in the study of the damped INLS equation with an inverse-square potential, advancing our understanding of nonlinear dispersive equations influenced by a damping term. Global existence of solutions is established for various regimes, where the damping term plays a key role in preventing blow-up. Scattering is shown to occur under sufficiently large damping, highlighting the dissipation effect. The analysis combines refined Strichartz estimates, Hardy-type inequalities, and a fixed-point argument. One of the key differences in this setting is that the standard ground state argument fails due to the presence of time-dependent damping.

Despite these advances, several important questions remain open including, but not limited to, optimizing the damping threshold, extending the results to non-radial data, opening to other singular (repulsive) potentials and investigating generalizations to fractional operators.
