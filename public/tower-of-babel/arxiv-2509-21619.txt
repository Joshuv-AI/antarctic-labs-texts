PreLoRA : Hybrid Pre-training of Vision Transformers with Full Training and Low-Rank Adapters
Krishu Kumar Thapa, Reet Barik, Krishna Teja Chitty-Venkata, Murali Emani, Venkatram Vishwanath

Abstract
Training large models ranging from millions to billions of parameters is highly resource-intensive, requiring significant time, compute, and memory. It is observed that most of the learning (higher change in weights) takes place in the earlier stage of the training loop. These changes stabilize as training continues enabling them to be captured by matrices of a low intrinsic rank. Therefore, we propose an approach to identify such states of partial convergence and dynamically switch from full parameter training to Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA) on the ViT-Large model. We introduce a flexible approach that leverages user-defined hyperparameters to determine the switching point and assign a rank specific to each module layer based on its level of convergence. Experimental results show that this approach preserves model accuracy while reducing the number of trainable parameters to 10% of its original size, resulting in a 3 × \times improvement in throughput, and a 1.5 × \times reduction in average training time per epoch while also reducing GPU memory consumption by 20%.

Introduction

In recent years, the field of machine learning has undergone significant advances in the development and deployment of large models with ever-increasing capacity across diverse domains
[
14
,
21
,
19
]
. The notion of large models has evolved from architectures with millions to those comprising billions of parameters, enabling significant improvement in performance and generalization
[
13
]
. However, the massive parameter count that strengthens model capacity in turn introduces substantial computational and memory bottlenecks, rendering training prohibitively expensive
[
22
,
25
]
.

Several parameter-efficient approaches have been explored to reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving model performance and generalization. Adapter methods
[
10
]
insert small trainable modules into each layer and keep the original parameters frozen. While this approach does reduce the number of trainable parameters, it introduces additional inference latency. The prefix
[
16
]
and the prompt tuning
[
15
]
techniques enable lightweight adaptation by optimizing a small set of learnable task-specific vectors that are added to the input sequence or intermediate activations. However, these methods often demonstrate limited expressivity when applied to complex tasks
[
24
]
. Recently, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
11
]
has gained widespread popularity because it leverages the flexibility of adapter methods with better expressivity than prompt-based methods, while maintaining minimal resource overhead. This is achieved by injecting low-rank trainable matrices into existing weight updates without adding any additional layers. While prior applications of LoRA have predominantly focused on fine-tuning, its integration into pretraining has the potential to accelerate training, reduce resource demands, and maintain model capacity.

Motivation:

During the initial training epochs, the model undergoes substantial weight updates; however, as training progresses, the intrinsic rank of the updates decreases
[
11
]
. Figure
1
illustrates this behavior for a representative large Vision Transformer model. Plot (a) on the left shows that the weight norm variations are pronounced across all the modules during the initial epochs but gradually stabilize in the later half of the training loop. Despite the stabilization of weight norms, the model loss (shown in plot (b)) continues to reduce significantly. Therefore, transitioning to LoRA at this stage has the potential to effectively capture the remaining low-rank variations in weight norms.

Challenges:

Identifying the optimal transition point from full-parameter to LoRA-based training is non-trivial. An early transition can yield substantial performance gains, but often at the expense of accuracy. In contrast, a late transition preserves accuracy, but limits potential reductions in training time. The central challenge, therefore, lies in identifying a dynamic switching strategy that balances model accuracy and training performance. Enabling explicit user control over this switching point allows them to choose an accuracy-speed trade-off, making the model adaptable to specific use-case requirements. Moreover, it is evident from Figure
1
that although weight norms across modules exhibit broadly consistent trends, their magnitudes differ considerably. Consequently, applying a uniform low rank across all modules may inadequately capture module-specific convergence dynamics.

4.

Related Work

In an earlier work, the adapter method
[
9
]
popularized the domain of parameter-efficient fine-tuning by introducing lightweight, trainable modules inserted into pretrained models, thereby minimizing the number of parameters updated during adaptation. Since its inception, various adapter variants have been developed and applied across diverse domains, including NLP, CV and speech recognition
[
9
,
1
,
8
,
20
]
. In parallel, prompt-tuning
[
15
]
and prefix-tuning
[
16
]
emerged as alternative approaches, which reduce trainable parameters by appending learnable prompts or prefixes to the input sequence, rather than modifying the model’s architecture. These methods have gained widespread adoption for fine-tuning LLMs
[
26
,
2
,
27
]
and vision models
[
30
,
29
,
12
]
, offering flexibility in scenarios with limited computational resources. Our work, however, centers on Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
11
]
, which provides a third paradigm by decomposing weight updates into low-rank matrices, thus achieving parameter efficiency without introducing new modules. LoRA stands out for its superior efficiency, stronger expressivity, and ease of integration, as evidenced by empirical studies
[
24
,
4
]
compared to adapters and prompt-tuning. Its applications span language models, vision transformers, multimodal systems, and even reinforcement learning frameworks
[
7
,
17
,
28
,
18
,
6
,
4
]
, with extensions like QLoRA further combining quantization for enhanced memory savings during fine-tuning.

Despite these advancements, most PEFT methods, including LoRA, have been predominantly explored in the context of fine-tuning pretrained models rather than full pretraining from scratch. This focus creates a notable gap, as pretraining large models demands immense computational resources, and efficient methods could democratize access to custom foundation models. Our work aims to bridge this gap by investigating LoRA’s efficacy in pretraining scenarios, potentially enabling scalable training on resource-constrained hardware. A recent study
[
3
]
applied LoRA to train a spatiotemporal attention-based model, accelerating it on processing-in-memory (PIM) architectures. Their approach monitors weight convergence during training and dynamically switches to LoRA upon detection, reducing overall training costs. However, it requires running two model copies in parallel (the full model and the LoRA variant) and performs a t-test on their losses for convergence checking. This dual-model setup is particularly inefficient for large models, as it doubles memory usage, hampers scalability, and introduces overhead that could be mitigated through more lightweight monitoring techniques, such as periodic loss sampling. Future directions might include hybrid PEFT strategies that combine LoRA with quantization or distillation to further optimize pretraining efficiency.

Our approach relies on two key strategies. First, to determine an appropriate point in the training loop at which transitioning from full-parameter to LoRA-based training can be performed safely. To this end, we introduce a convergence criterion that assesses partial convergence. Additionally, to add flexibility, hyperparameters allow users to adjust the strictness of this criterion. Second, when switching to LoRA, we employ a rank-assignment algorithm that dynamically assigns ranks to individual layers of target modules based on the extent of weight norm convergence. Training is subsequently continued using the LoRA-augmented model. A schematic overview of
PreLoRA
’s methodology can be found in Figure
2
. Each component is described in detail in the subsections that follow.

3.3
Warmup (Full model + LoRA)

The switch from the full parameter setting to the LoRA modules substantially reduces the number of trainable parameters. Since LoRA modules are randomly initialized, their updates may deviate from the expected optimization trajectory. This is particularly pronounced when employing LoRA while pre-training vs finetuning where the full parameter model has already converged. To mitigate this effect, the full model and LoRA modules are trained jointly for
w\in\mathbb{Z}
warmup epochs, allowing LoRA modules to get guidance from the full model. Following the warmup period, the full model is frozen, and training proceeds only on the LoRA modules. The warmup window
w
is a hyperparameter that regulates the influence of the full model on the initial learning of the LoRA modules.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Model and Data:

To showcase the advantages of our
PreLoRA
framework, we employ the
ViT-Large
architecture as a representative vision model, trained from scratch on the ImageNet-1k dataset. For an effective configuration to train ViT-Large from scratch on ImageNet-1K dataset, we adopt the training configuration prescribed by Steiner et al.
[
23
]
.

Hardware:

Training was conducted in a distributed setting on a supercomputing cluster. A total of 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs were used across 16 compute nodes connected via NVLink, to enable high-bandwidth and low-latency inter-GPU communication. Each GPU features 6912 CUDA cores and 432 Tensor cores, supporting FP32, FP16, BF16,
and Int8 precisions. They have 40 GiB of HBM2, offering a
memory bandwidth of 6.4 TB/s and thermal design power of 600 watts. The host is equipped with a 2.8 GHz AMD EPYC Milan 7543P 32-core CPU and 512 GB of DDR4 RAM.

Software and Code:

The HuggingFace library (v0.33.0) provided the model architecture, which was implemented in PyTorch (v2.7.1) for distributed training with LoRA support from PEFT (v0.15.2). The project is open-source, with code publicly available on GitHub:
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PreLoRA-7324
, which also contains the data processing and visualization scripts using multiple Python packages.

Hyperparameters:

In our
PreLoRA
approach, following Dahal et al.
[
3
]
we employed 3 consecutive windows of 3 epochs each for the partial convergence test (Algorithm
1
) by setting

=3 and
m
=3. Testing for convergence too frequently risks false positives from local minima, whereas overly large windows can prolong full-parameter training unnecessarily. We define
\alpha
= [query (q), key (k), value (v), dense (d), output (o)] as the set of target modules for training in the
PreLoRA
framework. Our experiments evaluate how the hyperparameters

and
\zeta

w
, which dictates how many epochs the LoRA modules are trained jointly with the full base model prior to freezing its weights. For the dynamic rank assignment algorithm, ranks were constrained between 8 and 64.

4.2.2
Time, compute, and memory

LoRA adaptation reduces the number of total trainable parameters in the model. This is expected to make the backpropagation process faster and more memory-efficient as the number of gradients to be computed and stored is substantially reduced. For instance, in our experimental setting, the number of trainable parameters reduces from 300M to approximately 10% of the full model size.
As a result, our implementation is expected to improve training efficiency in terms of time, computational cost, and memory utilization. Our results (Figure
7
) show a comparison of average time taken per epoch, throughput (images per second), and GPU memory utilization between full parameter and LoRA training.
We observe that
PreLoRA
achieved a 1.5
\times
reduction in average epoch time over the total training cycle. Accumulated over 300 epochs that translates to a total saving of
~9
hours. Faster epochs enables more images to be processed per second and that trend is reflected in the plot for throughput which shows a 3
\times
increase for
PreLoRA
over the baseline. The gains in memory utilization show a 20% improvement which has the potential to be capitalized on to accommodate larger batch sizes during training.

5
Conclusion

In summary, this work proposes a framework that leverages LoRA to reduce the number of trainable parameters during pre-training of a large vision model. It provides a methodology to identify a transition point between full-parameter and low-parameter training, enabling explicit control over the accuracy–speed trade-off. While evaluated on ViT-Large, the approach is generalizable to other deep learning architectures. As future work, we plan to investigate online hyperparameter optimization techniques to establish a more principled and generalizable method for defining the convergence criterion in the partial convergence test, thereby enhancing the robustness, scalability, and applicability of the approach across diverse domains.
