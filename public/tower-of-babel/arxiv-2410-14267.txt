Algebraic constructions of cubic minimal cones
Daniel J. F. Fox, Vladimir G. Tkachev

Abstract
. Hsiang algebras are a class of nonassociative algebra defined in terms of a relation quartic in elements of the algebra. This class arises naturally in relation to the construction of real algebraic minimal cones. Additionally, Hsiang algebras were crucial in the construction of singular (trulsy viscosity) solutions of nonlinear uniformly elliptic partial differential equations in a series of papers by Nikolai Nadirashvili and Serge Vlăduţ. The classification of Hsiang algebras is a challenging problems, based on a Peirce decomposition like that used to study Jordan algebras, although more complicated. This paper introduces two new tools for studying Hsiang algebras: a distinguished class of algebras called quasicomposition that generalize the Hurwitz algebras (the reals, complexes, quaternions, and octonions) and cross-product algebras and a tripling construction associating with a given algebra one of three times the dimension of the original algebra, that can be thought of as a kind of analogue of the Cayley-Dickson doubling process. One main result states that the triple is an exceptional Hsiang algebra if and only if the original algebra is quasicomposition. The quasicomposition and tripling notions are interesting in their own right and will be considered in a more general form elsewhere.

Introduction

In this paper, we discuss why the construction and classification of certain commutative nonassociative algebras called Hsiang and quasicomposition algebras are relevant and interesting from an analytic and differential geometric point of view. Nonassociative algebraic structures occur naturally in relation to the description of homogeneous and special model solutions in a wide variety of geometric and analytic problems.
A typical example is the relation between finite-dimensional Jordan algebras and the geometry of self-dual homogeneous cones and the associated tube domains and the analysis of functions on them

Quadratic minimal cones (the Clifford-Simons minimal cones) play a basic role in the resolution of the Bernstein problem for minimal hypersurfaces in

Recent developments in the structure of singular (truly viscosity) solutions of nonlinear elliptic partial differential equations initiated by N. Nadirashvili and S. Vlăduţ in

Although general algebraic minimal cones (of degree 3 and higher) have been the subject of considerable recent interest, still little is known about their structure.

A real algebraic minimal cone is determined by a homogeneous degree
k
polynomial solution of the equation

(1)

\displaystyle|Du|^{2}\Delta u-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\langle Du,D|Du|^{2}\rangle=B(x)u(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

\displaystyle|Du|^{2}\Delta u-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\langle Du,D|Du|^{2}\rangle=-\frac{3}{2}\theta h(x,x)u(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

By rescaling

Solutions of (

All known

By using invariant theory, Hsiang constructed four explicit examples of radial eigencubics for dimensions

and posed the problem of classifying radial eigencubics.
In
[
38

6

steps were taken toward the solution of this problem. This was achieved by translating the problem into one of pure algebra, the classification of Euclidean radial Hsiang algebras. This classification problem is viable because for such algebras there is a Peirce type decomposition associated with an idempotent, like that use in the classification of Jordan algebras, although somewhat more complicated. As is recounted below, there is an infinite family of radial eigencubics, occurring in arbitrarily large dimensions, that are constructed from Clifford systems, and there are finitely many dimensions in which there occur radial eigencubics, here denominated
exceptional
, not equivalent to ones obtained from Clifford systems. For the Hsiang algebras associated with exceptional radial eigencubics the trace-form, called here the
Killing form
, constructed by tracing the product of two left multiplication endomorphisms, is invariant. This Killing invariance condition almost distinguishes the exceptional radial Hsiang algebras; only four of the Hsiang algebras built from Clifford systems, those here called

72
, existence and uniqueness are not fully determined for some of the possible dimensions.
An important feature of the exceptional eigencubics is that they appear in a variety of geometric and analytic problemas.
This makes their characterization an attractive and interesting problem. One of the goals of the present paper is to provide an alternative approach to understanding of the algebraic nature of radial eigencubics, and, especially, exceptional eigencubics.

A phenomenological observation is that all of the exceptional radial eigencubics occur in a dimension divisible by

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
describes necessary background on algebraic minimal cones and the original Hsiang problem.
It also presents some particular solutions (radial eigencubics) that are revisited later in later sections. The general notion of a metrized algebra with involution is recalled in Section
3

4

4.18

5

7

2.
Hsiang eigencubics

2.1.
Minimal algebraic cones and Hsiang equations

A smoothly immersed submanifold of a Riemannian manifold
M
is said to be
minimal
if its mean curvature is zero (in particular, the qualifier
minimal
supposes no further variational restrictions on
M
).

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the unit sphere in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
To
M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
associate the cone
\mathsf{C}(M)=\{tp:(p,t)\in M\times[0,\infty)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A closed smoothly immersed submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is minimal if and only if
\mathsf{C}(M)\setminus\{0\}
is an immersed minimal submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
(real) algebraic
if it is the zero set of a polynomial,
u
. The nonsingular locus of a real algebraic set comprises the points where the differential
Du
is nonzero. A subset
M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is
algebraic (of degree
k
)
if the cone
\mathsf{C}(M)
is contained in the zero level set of a degree
k
homogeneous polynomial,
u
, on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A real algebraic subset
M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is
minimal
if its nonsingular locus is minimal.

Here as in the rest of the paper, for expository simplicity, all algebraic structures are supposed defined over the real field,
\mathbb{R}
, and real algebraic is abbreviated as algebraic.

For example, for
p\geq q
, the set of
p\times q
matrices
X
satisfying
\det X^{t}X=0
, is a minimal algebraic submanifold of the sphere
\{X:\operatorname{tr}X^{t}X=1\}

[
50
]
,
[
6
]
.

For sufficiently smooth
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
define a differential operator by

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{M}(F)&=|DF|^{2}\Delta F-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle DF,D|DF|^{2}\rangle=F^{p}F_{p}F_{q}\,^{q}-F_{pq}F^{p}F^{q}.&\end{aligned}

A straightforward calculation shows that at a regular point of a level set
F^{-1}(c)
,
c\in R{}
, the mean curvature equals
\tfrac{1}{n-1}|DF|^{-3}\mathscr{M}(F)
.

If
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is an irreducible homogeneous polynomial, it follows from some version of the real algebraic nullstellensatz (see
[
45
, Lemma
2.2
]
), that the cone
F^{-1}(0)
is minimal if and only if there is a homogeneous polynomial
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathscr{M}(F)=QF
. This relation can be summarized schematically as

(4)

\displaystyle\text{the cone $F^{-1}(0)$ is minimal}\iff\mathscr{M}(F)=0\,\,\text{mod}\,\,F.

Because
\mathscr{M}(GF)=G^{3}\mathscr{M}(F)\,\,\text{mod}\,\,F
, when looking for polynomial solutions to
\mathscr{M}(F)=QF
it is reasonable to restrict attention to irreducible polynomials.
The homogeneous linear solutions of (
4
) are simply hyperplanes, which are cones over equators in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
If
F
is homogeneous of degree
k\geq 2
then
\mathscr{M}(F)
is homogeneous of degree
3k-4
, so
Q
must be homogeneous of degree
2k-4
. In the case
k=2
,
Q
is a constant, and Hsiang showed in
[
29
]
that in this case any solution of (
4
) is equivalent to a multiple of

(5)

\displaystyle(q-1)(x_{1}^{2}+\dots x_{p}^{2})-(p-1)(x_{p+1}^{2}+\dots+x_{p+q})^{2},

\displaystyle p,q\geq 2,\quad p+q=n.

The corresponding minimal hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
are known as Clifford tori.

Because of the equivariance property,
\mathscr{M}(g\cdot F)=g\cdot\mathscr{M}(F)
for
g\in O(n)
(where
(g\cdot F)(x)=F(g^{-1}x)
)), a special case of
\mathscr{M}(F)=0\,\,(\text{mod}\,\,F)
is obtained by requiring
\mathscr{M}(F)/F
to have some prespecified form, for example that
\mathscr{M}(F)/F
equal some
orthogonally invariant
polynomial. When
k=3
, then
Q
must be a quadratic form. An orthogonally invariant quadratic form must be a multiple of the Euclidean quadratic form
h(x,x)
.

For cubic homogeneous polynomial solutions of (
4
) (the normalization factor is the same as in (
2
) will be explained latter) there result two variants of the
Hsiang
equations:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}b(x,x)\,u

nonradial Hsiang

(7)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\theta h(x,x)u

radial Hsiang

where
Q
is an arbitrary (undetermined) quadratic form and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant. Here
nonradial
means
not necessarily radial
, and evidently any solution to the radial Hsiang equations solves also the nonradial Hsiang equations.

The equations (
6
)-(
7
) are called

in honor of Wu-yi Hsiang who first found interesting cubic solutions in
[
29
]
. More precisely, Hsiang observed that a homogeneous minimal hypersurface in a sphere is real algebraic and constructed cubic solutions to (

4
) admits irreducible homogeneous polynomial solutions of arbitrary degree and to classify cubic homogeneous polynomial solutions. It is this last question that is broached here.

Definition 2.1
.

Following the earlier work
[
50
]
and
[
38
, Chap. 6]
, cubic solutions to the (radial) Hsiang equations are called
(radial) eigencubics
.
Two radial eigencubics are

if they are on the same orbit of the action of the conformal group

On
\mathbb{R}^{3}
any product of three linear forms solves (
6

7

Initially the radial case appears simply as a technical simplification that facilitates finding solutions.
It is not clear how big the gap between the nonradial and radial cases is. Comparison with the special case of pseudocomposition/eikonal algebras (see Example
4.13
below) suggests that it is not large. Moreover, every example we know of an

solution of the nonradial Hsiang equation in fact solves the radial Hsiang equation.

2.2.
Clifford type vs exceptional

A cubic polynomial solution

of (

In

In

.

A radial eigencubic has

Example 2.4
.

A
composition algebra
is an algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond)
equipped with a nondegenerate quadratic form

that is multiplicative, meaning

\displaystyle n(x\diamond y)=n(x)n(y).

A

is a

\bar{x}=n(e,x)e-x
, where

is the polar form obtained by linearizing

(so

). By the Hurwitz theorem

,

and is isomorphic to the base field

, a quadratic étale

-algebra, a quaternion

-algebra, or an octonion
\mathbb{F}
-algebra. In particular a real Hurwitz algebra with positive definite quadratic form is isomorphic to the real field

, the complex field

, the quaternions

, or the octonions

. Note that there do exist nonunital composition algebras; the simplest example is the

obtained by equipping a Hurwitz algebra

Proposition 2.7
.

The Cartan eigencubics are
not
of Clifford type.

Proof.

The squared norm of the gradient
|Du(x)|^{2}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is invariant under the action of the orthogonal group

. For a Clifford type eigencubic

, the quartic polynomial

has degree 2 as a polynomial in the variables

, whereas, by (

is a degree 4 polynomial in all its variables. Because

is fixed by the action of the orthogonal group it has degree four in every variable in any orthogonal coordinate system so cannot be equivalent to

∎

This proposition suggests a natural dichotomy for radial eigencubics.

Definition 2.8

A radial eigencubic is

3.
Metrized commutative algebras

The first systematic use of nonassociative algebra to approach the classification of cubic solutions of the Hsiang equations was made in
[
38
, Chap. 6]
. In
[
21
,
22
,
24
]
a similar algebraic approach was taken to finding cubic solutions of (
15
). Before describing (see Section
4
) this approach we outline the basis notions of nonassociative algebra that are needed.

3.1.
The general case

An
algebra
is a vector space
\mathbb{A}
equipped with a bilinear map
\diamond:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
. The product
\diamond
is
not
required to be unital, commutative, or associative.

The left and right multiplication endomorphisms
L_{\diamond},R_{\diamond}:\mathbb{A}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{A})
are defined by

L_{\diamond}(x)y=x\diamond y
and
R_{\diamond}(x)y=y\diamond x
, respectively. When it is obvious from context the subscript indicated the multiplication is omitted and there is written simply
L(x)
or
R(x)
.

A emphunit of
\mathbb{A}
is an element
e\in\mathbb{A}
such that
e\diamond x=x\diamond e=x
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. An algebra is
unital
if it has a unit, in which case the unit is unique.

An
involution

\sigma
of an algebra
\mathbb{A}=(V,\diamond)
is a linear endomorphism
\sigma\in\End(\mathbb{A})
satisfying
(x\diamond y)^{\sigma}=y^{\sigma}\diamond x^{\sigma}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
and squaring to the identity
\sigma\circ\sigma=1
. By definition, in an algebra with involution there hold

(16)

\displaystyle R(x^{\sigma})=\sigma\circ L(x)\circ\sigma,\qquad L(x^{\sigma})=\sigma\circ R(x)\circ\sigma.

In particular, for an algebra with involution, either of
L(x)
and
R(x)
determines the other.
The identity operator
\sigma=1
on a commutative algebra and the negative of the identity
\sigma=-1
on a anticommutative algebra are obviously involutions; these called the standard involutions. Note that there are both commutative and anticommutative algebras admitting nonstandard involutions and an algebra with involution need not be either commutative or anticommutative.

A bilinear form
h(x,y)
on a vector space
\mathbb{A}
is symmetric if
h(x,y)=h(y,x)
and a symmetric bilinear form
h
is nondegenerate if its radical is trivial, in other words,
h(x,y)=0
for any
y\in\mathbb{A}
implies
x=0
. A nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form is called a
metric
. A metric is
Euclidean
if it is positive definite.

Definition 3.1
.

A symmetric bilinear form
h(x,y)
on an algebra
\mathbb{A}
with involution
\sigma
is
involutively invariant
if

(17)

\displaystyle h(x^{\sigma},y^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=h(x,y),

(18)

\displaystyle h(x\diamond y,z)

\displaystyle=h(x,z\diamond y^{\sigma}),\quad\forall x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}.

The condition (
17
) states that
\sigma
is
h
-orthogonal. Under condition (
17
), the relation (
18
) is equivalent to

(19)

\displaystyle h(R(y)x,z)

\displaystyle=h(x\diamond y,z)=h(x,z\diamond y^{\sigma})=h(x,R(y^{\sigma})z),\quad\forall x,y,z\in\mathbb{A},

which states that the
h
-adjoint
R(y)^{\ast}
equals
R(y^{\sigma})
. Likewise, (
19
) together with (
16
) implies that the
h
-adjoint
L(x)^{\ast}
satisfies

(20)

\displaystyle L(x)^{\ast}=L(x^{\sigma})=\sigma\circ R(x)\circ\sigma.

Definition 3.2
.

An algebra with involution is
metrized
if it is equipped with an involutively invariant metric. A commutative algebra is said to be
metrized
if it is equipped with a metric involutively invariant with respect to the standard involution.

Metrized algebras with involution abound. Some interesting examples include:

•

A semisimple Lie algebra with its standard involution is metrized by its Killing form
h(x,y)=\trace(\mathrm{ad}(x)\mathrm{ad}(y))
.

•

Many solvable and nilpotent Lie algebras with vanishing Killing form nonetheless admit invariant metrics.

•

A Hurwitz algebra (a unital composition algebra) is metrized by the trace form
h(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}L(\bar{x})L(y)
where
x\to\bar{x}
is the canonical involution.

•

The commutative Griess algebras of certain vertex operator algebras, in particular
the
Griess algebra on which the Monster finite simple group acts by automorphisms, are naturally metrized, as are many related Norton and axial algebras
[
42
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
31
]
.

Example 3.3

(Jordan algebras)
.

Recall that a
Jordan algebra
is a commutative algebra
(A,\diamond)
satisfying the commutator relation

(21)

\displaystyle[L(x),L(x^{2})]=0.

This identity can be thought as a weakening of the associative law.
In particular, any Jordan algebra is power-associative (the subalgebra generated by a single element is always associative). On any semisimple Jordan algebra
(A,\diamond)
the bilinear form

h(x,y):=\trace L(x\diamond y),

is nondegenerate and invariant
[
34
, p. 60]
. In other words,
(A,\diamond)
is naturally metrized by
h
.

The fundamental paper
[
32
]
classified finite-dimensional semisimple Euclidean Jordan algebras and showed that any such is a direct sum of simple building blocks, namely, the spin factors (Jordan algebras satisfying a quadratic relation), Hermitian matrices of size
n\geq 3
over a Hurwitz algebra
\mathbb{F}_{d}
, for
d=1,2,4
, and the exceptional Albert algebra of
3\times 3
Hermitian matrices over octonions
\mathbb{F}_{8}
; in these cases the Jordan product is the symmetrization of the standard matrix product:
x\diamond y=\frac{1}{2}(xy+yx)
. This classification is an essential ingredient in the classification of radial eigencubics because these have a certain hidden Jordan algebra structure (see the remark following (
52
)).

3.2.
Commutative metrized algebras

This section considers metrized commutative algebras in more detail. Let
(\mathbb{A},\diamond)
be a commutative algebra equipped with the standard involution
\sigma=1
metrized by
h
. In this case (
17
) is trivial, while (
18
) is equivalent to the complete symmetry (invariance under permutations) of the trilinear form
t
defined by

(22)

\displaystyle t(x,y,z):=h(x\diamond y,z).

This complete symmetric reflects an important property of the multiplication operator in a metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
. Namely, by (
20
),
L(x)
is an
h
-
self-adjoint operator
.

Associate with
\mathbb{A}
the
cubic form

(23)

\displaystyle u(x):=\frac{1}{6}t(x,x,x):=\frac{1}{6}h(x\diamond x,x).

In the converse direction, given a metric cubic space
(\mathbb{A},h,u)
, meaning a cubic form
u(x)
on a vector space
\mathbb{A}
with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
h
, a commutative algebra structure is determined as follows: given a pair
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
, define
x\diamond y
as the unique vector satisfying

(24)

\displaystyle h(x\diamond y,z)=t_{u}(x,y,z),\qquad\forall z\in V,

where

(25)

\displaystyle t_{u}(x,y,z):=u(x+y+z)-u(x+y)-u(x+z)-u(y+z)+u(x)+u(y)+u(z)

is the complete polarization of
u(x)
. The product
\diamond
is evidently commutative, and the corresponding algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is metrized.

The correspondence between metrized commutative algebras
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
and cubic forms on an inner product vector space
(\mathbb{A},h)
is functorial with respect to the natural notions of morphisms. In particular, isometrically isomorphic metrized commutative algebras correspond to isomorphic metric cubic spaces. This means that the study of metric cubic spaces is equivalent to the study of commutative metrized algebras.

This correspondence means that analytic calculus of cubic forms translates in a nonassociative algebra language as follows. Observe that

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}t(x,x,y)=\partial_{y}u|_{x}

is the directional derivative of
u
in the direction
y
evaluated at
x
which is a quadratic with respect to
x
and linear with respect to
y
. On the other hand, the gradient of
u
(with respect to the inner metric
h
on
\mathbb{A}
) is defined as the unique vector
\nabla u(x)
such that
\partial_{y}u|_{x}=h(\nabla u(x),\,y)
holds for any
y\in\mathbb{A}
. Therefore the Euler homogeneity function theorem combined with (
22
) implies

(27)

\displaystyle\nabla u(x)=\frac{1}{2}x\diamond x,

and by linearization we obtain

(28)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hess}u(x)(y,z)=h(L_{\diamond}(x)y,z).

The condition that
u
be harmonic,
\Delta u=0
, is equivalent to the trace free condition

(29)

\displaystyle\Delta u(x)=\trace L_{\diamond}(x)=0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{A}.

Following
[
24
]
, algebras satisfying (
29
) are called
exact
(they were called
harmonic
in
[
38
]
).

The relations (
28
) and (
29
) determine the translation from the PDE setting to the nonassociative language, and vice versa. For example, by (
27
), the singular locus of the zero level set of
u
comprises the
2
-nilpotent elements (those satisfying
x\diamond x=0
) in the associated metrized commutative algebra. As will be seen later the different types of radial eigencubics reflected in the Diagram
1
correspond strongly with the structure of their singular loci.

Note that over an arbitrary field a metrized commutative algebra need not contain a nonzero idempotent. On the other hand, one can prove in a purely analytic manner that a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra always contains at least one nonzero idempotent (it cannot be guaranteed that there is more than one idempotent; an example is in
[
54
, section 3]
).

Proposition 3.4

(
[
54
]
)
.

Any nonzero Euclidean metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
contains a nonzero idempotent.

Proof.

Since
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is a nonzero algebra, its cubic form
u(x)=\frac{1}{6}h(x^{2},x)
is not identically zero. The sphere
S=\{x\in\mathbb{A}:\langle x,x\rangle=1\}
is compact in the Euclidean topology on
\mathbb{A}
, hence the cubic form
u(x)
attains its maximum on
S
, say at
z\in S
. Because
u
is not identically zero,
u(z)>0
. The Lagrange multiplier condition is
\nabla u(z)=k\nabla\langle z,z\rangle=2kz
for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, by the Euler homogeneous function theorem
u(z)=\frac{1}{3}\langle\nabla u(z),z\rangle=\frac{4}{3}k\langle z,z\rangle>0
implying
k\neq 0
. Using (
27
) this yields
z\diamond z=4kz
, hence
c:=z/4k
satisfies
c\diamond c=c
, that is
c
is a nonzero idempotent in
\mathbb{A}
, as desired.
∎

4.
From Hsiang eigencubics to Hsiang algebras

The correspondence between metrized cubic spaces and metrized commutative algebras described in section
3
is use to translate the Hsiang equation into the definition of Hsiang algebras.

4.1.
Definition of Hsiang algebras

In this section
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h(x,y)=\langle x,y\rangle
is the Euclidean scalar product. For readability,
L_{\diamond}(x)
is written
L(x)
and
\diamond
-powers of an element
x
are written as juxtapositions,

x^{2}=x\diamond x,\quad x^{3}=x^{2}\diamond x=(x\diamond x)\diamond x,\quad x^{2}x^{2}=(x\diamond x)\diamond(x\diamond x),\quad\text{ etc.}

This notation is unambiguous because powers of order 2 and 3 are well defined in any commutative algebra.

Suppose that the cubic form
P(x)=\tfrac{1}{6}h(x^{2},x)
of the metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
solves
\mathscr{M}(P)=0\,\,(\text{mod}\,\,P)
. Rewriting this using (
27
) and (
28
) yields

(30)

\displaystyle h(x^{2},x^{3})-h(x^{2},x^{2})\operatorname{tr}L(x)=0\,\,\text{mod}\,\,P.

If
P
is irreducible it follows that there is a symmetric bilinear form
b(x,y)
such that

(31)

\displaystyle h(x^{2},x^{3})-h(x^{2},x^{2})\operatorname{tr}L(x)=b(x,x)h(x,x^{2}).

The following definition generalizes one from
[
38
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A Euclidean metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is a
Hsiang algebra
if it satisfies (
31
) for some symmetric bilinear form
b
on
\mathbb{A}
. If there is a constant
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
b=\theta h
, then
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is said to be a
radial Hsiang algebra
.

The radial Hsiang algebras are characterized by the equation

(32)

\displaystyle h(x^{2},x^{3})-h(x^{2},x^{2})\operatorname{tr}L(x)=\theta h(x,x)h(x,x^{2}).

Remark 4.2
.

Replacing
h
by
r^{-1}h
replaces
\theta
by
r\theta
, so
\theta
can be normalized as one likes by rescaling
h
. In many applications, the choice
\theta=4/3
is natural. A radial Hsiang algebra satisfying
\theta=4/3
is said to be
normalized
.

Proposition 4.3
.

A cubic form
u(x)
solves (
2
) if and only if the commutative metrized algebra
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\diamond,\langle,\rangle)
with
\diamond
induced by
u
as in (
24
) is a radial Hsiang algebra.

If the cubic form of a Euclidean radial Hsiang algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
equals the cube of a linear form,
\omega(x)^{3}
, then the corresponding product
\diamond
satisfies
x\diamond y=6\omega(x)\omega(y)\alpha
for the element
w\in\mathbb{A}
uniquely determined by
\omega(x)=h(x,w)
. In this case
\operatorname{tr}L_{\diamond}(x)=6h(w,w)\omega(x)
. These conditions in fact characterize radial Hsiang algebras whose cubic forms are cubes of linear forms.

Theorem 4.4
.

For a Euclidean radial Hsiang algebra the following are equivalent.

(1)

It is not exact.

(2)

\dim(\mathbb{A}\diamond\mathbb{A})=1
.

(3)

Its cubic form
u(x)
equals the cube of a linear form.

The proof, which is more complicated than one might expect, is omitted (see however the proof in
[
38
, section 6.6.3]
). The key points are the following. First, by Proposition
3.4
, a Euclidean metrized commutative algebra contains a nonzero idempotent. Second, if a radial Hsiang algebra is not exact, there is a unique element
\ell
such that
h(\ell,x)=\operatorname{tr}L(x)
and it follows from (
32
) that any idempotent is a multiple of
\ell
. From this it can be deduced that
\mathbb{A}\diamond\mathbb{A}
is spanned by
\ell
.

Definition 4.5
.

A radial Hsiang algebra is
degenerate
if it satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
4.4
and
nondegenerate
otherwise.

Theorem
4.4
justifies the assertion in section
2
that for a nondegenerate solution
u(x)
of (
2
), there holds
\Delta u(x)=0=\trace L(x)
. By Theorem
4.4
, a nondegenerate radial Hsiang algebra is exact, and in this case (
32
) specializes to the equations
\operatorname{tr}L(x)=0
and

(33)

\displaystyle h(x^{2},\,x^{3})=\theta h(x,x)\,h(x^{2},\,x).

The linearization of (
33
) yields the identity

(34)

\displaystyle 4x^{3}x+x^{2}x^{2}-2\theta h(x,x)x^{2}-3\theta h(x^{2},\,x)x=0,

while via the invariance of
h
the identity (
34
) implies (
33
).

Corollary 4.6
.

A cubic form
u(x)
is a nondegenerate solution of (
2
) if and only if the commutative metrized algebra
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\diamond,\langle,\rangle)
with
\diamond
induced by
u
is an exact radial Hsiang algebra.

Let
c
be a nonzero idempotent in a nondegenerate Euclidean radial Hsiang algebra. Substituting
c
for
x
in (
33
) implies

(35)

\displaystyle h(c,c)=\frac{1}{\theta}.

In particular,
all idempotents in a radial Hsiang algebra have the same length
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta}}
.

Remark 4.7
.

There are
weak
versions of the equations (
31
) and (
32
) in which either of the symmetric bilinear forms
b
or
h
is allowed to be degenerate and interesting examples can be found. For simplicity such degenerate solutions are not discussed further here.

4.2.
Polar algebras

It is natural to identify the subclass of Hsiang algebras that corresponds with Clifford type eigencubics. This is explained next.

Definition 4.8
.

A
polar algebra
is a metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
equipped with a nontrivial
h
-orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus_{h}\mathbb{A}_{1}
(meaning that both
\mathbb{A}_{0}
and
\mathbb{A}_{1}
are nontrivial subspaces) such that

(i)

\mathbb{A}_{0}\diamond\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{0\}
,

(ii)

\mathbb{A}_{1}\diamond\mathbb{A}_{1}\subset\mathbb{A}_{0}
,

(iii)

(36)

x(xy)=h(x,x)y,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{A}_{0},\,\forall y\in\mathbb{A}_{1}.

(iv)

If
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}=1
, then there is required additionally that
\trace L_{\diamond}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{A}_{0}
.

The following result is a corollary of the definitions (see
[
38
, Proposition 6.5.1]
).

Theorem 4.9
.

A polar algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is a radial Hsiang algebra and its cubic form
u(x)=\frac{1}{6}h(x^{2},x)
is a Clifford type radial eigencubic satisfying (
33
) with
\theta=4/3
. Conversely, if
u(x)
is a Clifford type radial eigencubic satisfying (
33
) with
\theta=4/3
, then the metrized algebra
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\diamond,h)
is isomorphic to a polar algebra. In this case there holds

(37)

\displaystyle\trace L_{\diamond}(x)^{2}=h(x_{0},x_{0})\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}+2h(x_{1},x_{1})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0},

where
x={x_{0}}+{x_{1}}
in accord with the decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
.

Proof.

For completeness, the first identity in (
33
) is proved (the traceless property is proved similarly).
By (
36
) for all
x\in\mathbb{A}_{0}
and
y\in\mathbb{A}_{1}
,

(38)

\displaystyle h(L(y)^{2}x,x)=h(y(yx),x)=h(y,(yx)x)=h(y,y)h(x,x)=h(h(y,y)x,x).

By (
20
),
L(y)^{2}
is a self-adjoint operator, hence (
38
) implies the restriction of
L(y)^{2}
to
\mathbb{A}_{0}
equals
h(y,y)1_{\mathbb{A}_{0}}
on for any
y\in\mathbb{A}_{1}
. That is,

(39)

\displaystyle y(yx)=h(y,y)x,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{A}_{0},\,\forall y\in\mathbb{A}_{1}.

Decompose
x={x_{0}}+{x_{1}}
according to
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
. Because
\mathbb{A}_{0}\diamond\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{0\}
,
x^{2}=x_{1}x_{1}+2x_{0}x_{1},
where
x_{1}x_{1}\in V_{0}
because
\mathbb{A}_{1}\mathbb{A}_{1}\subset\mathbb{A}_{0}
. Since
h(V_{0},V_{1})=0
, using the invariance of
h
yields

(40)

h(x,x^{2})=h(x_{0},x_{1}x_{1})+2h(x_{1},x_{1}x_{0})=3h(x_{0},x_{1}x_{1}).

Because
\mathbb{A}_{0}\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{0\}
,
(x_{1}x_{1})x_{0}=0
and by (
39
)

\displaystyle x^{3}

\displaystyle=2x_{1}(x_{1}x_{0})+2x_{0}(x_{0}x_{1})+(x_{1}x_{1})x_{1}=2h(x_{1},x_{1})x_{0}+2h(x_{0},x_{0})x_{1}+(x_{1}x_{1})x_{1}.

Arguing similarly, one obtains

\displaystyle h(x^{2},\,x^{3})

\displaystyle=2h(x_{1},x_{1})h({x_{1}x_{1}},{{x_{0}}})+4h(x_{0},x_{0})h({x_{0}},{x_{1}x_{1}})+2h({x_{1}}{x_{0}},(x_{1}x_{1})x_{1})

\displaystyle=4h(x_{1},\mu)h({x_{1}x_{1}},{{x_{0}}})+4h(x_{0},x_{0})h({x_{0}},{x_{1}x_{1}})

\displaystyle=\begin{matrix}\frac{4}{3}\end{matrix}h(x,x)h(x,x^{2}),

implying the first identity in (
33
) with
\theta=4/3
. The proof of (
37
) is similar.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

By Theorem
4.9
, the radial eigencubic
u(x)=\frac{1}{6}h(x^{2},x)
associated with a polar algebra
\mathbb{A}
is of Clifford type with
q=\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}
and
2p=\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}
, as follows readily from (
40
).
By (
10
) this implies

(41)

\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}-1\leq\rho(\frac{1}{2}\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}).

where
\rho
is the Hurwitz-Radon function.

Remark 4.11
.

The Clifford type radial eigencubics always have nontrivial singular locus. Precisely, by (
27
), since
x^{2}=0
on
\mathbb{A}_{0}
, the subspace
\mathbb{A}_{0}
of a polar algebra is contained in the singular locus of the associated (Clifford type) radial eigencubic
u
.

Definition 4.12
.

A Hsiang algebra is
exceptional
if it is not (algebra) isomorphic to a polar algebra.

Example 4.13
.

The simplest examples of exceptional Hsiang algebras are given by pseudocomposition algebras. A commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is called a
pseudocomposition algebra

[
36
]
if it satisfies the identity

(42)

\displaystyle x^{3}=b(x,x)x

for some nonzero symmetric bilinear form
b
on
\mathbb{A}
. If a metrized commutative algebra satisfes (
42
), then
h(x^{3},\,x^{2})=b(x,x)h(x,x^{2})
, so that (
31
) holds provided the algebra is moreover exact. This shows that
a metrized exact pseudocomposition algebra is a nonradial Hsiang algebra
.

Pseudocomposition algebras naturally arise in the context of the eiconal equation and isoparametric hypersurfaces, see
[
52
]
. This motivates the following

Definition 4.14
.

A pseudocomposition algebra is an
eikonal
algebra if it satisfies (
42
) with
b=\theta h
for some nonzero
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and a Euclidean metric
h
.

By a theorem of Elduque and Okubo
[
16
, Theorem
12
]
, over a field of characteristic not dividing
6
the bilinear form
b
of a pseudocomposition algebra is automatically invariant. By the theorem of Elduque and Okubo, for an eikonal algebra
h
is invariant and there holds

(43)

\displaystyle h(x^{3},\,x^{2})=\theta h(x,x)h(x,x^{2}),

so that (
32
) holds provided the eikonal algebra is moreover exact. Therefore
an exact eikonal algebra is a radial Hsiang algebra
(by the preceding remarks, in this case metrizability need not be assumed).

Note that the distinction between pseudocomposition and eikonal algebras is formally parallel to (in fact a subcase of) the distinction between nonradial Hsiang algebras and radial Hsiang algebras.

Lemma 4.15
.

An exact eikonal algebra with Euclidean metric is an exceptional Hsiang algebra.

Proof.

Were the algebra of Clifford type there would exist a polar decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
with subspaces
\mathbb{A}_{0}
and
\mathbb{A}_{1}
having positive dimension. For any
x\in\mathbb{A}_{0}
there would hold
x^{2}=0
, so that
0=x^{3}=\theta h(x,x)x
. Because
\theta\neq 0
and
h
is Euclidean this implies
x=0
. This implies that
\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{0\}
is trivial, a contradiction.
∎

The simplest example of an exact pseudocomposition algebra is the paracomplex numbers defined in Example
2.4
(the paraquaternions do not give an example because they are not commutative).

Because by (
43
) an exact eikonal algebra contains no nonzero
2
-nilpotent element, by (
27
) the associated radial eigencubic has no singular locus, so is smooth.

4.3.
Types of polar algebras

By definition, a polar algebra is determined by a decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
. It is natural to ask whether this polar decomposition is unique in the sense that for any other polar decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{B}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{B}_{1}
satisfying Definition
4.8
there must hold
\mathbb{A}_{i}=\mathbb{B}_{i}
,
i=0,1
.
Because the
0
-graded part of the polar decomposition is contained in the singular locus of the associated radial eigencubic
u(x)=h(x,x^{2})
, this question is related to understanding the structure of the singular locus of the associated minimal cone
u(x)=0
.

It follows from the theory of Clifford systems (or the well-known properties of the Radon-Hurwitz function
\rho
and (
10
)) that
\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}=2\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}
implies that
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}\in\{1,2,4,8\}
. Such a polar algebra must be the algebra corresponding with one of the Hurwitz eigencubics defined in Example
2.4
. See Corollary
6.6
for the corresponding algebraic statement.

It follows from
[
38
, section 6.5.3]
that the uniqueness of the polar decomposition depends on the polar dimensions
(p,q)=(\dim\mathbb{A}_{0},\dim\mathbb{A}_{1})
. Namely, if
(p,q)\notin\{(d,2d):d=1,2,4,8\}
then the polar decomposition is unique, while for the remaining four pairs (Hurwitz triples), there are exactly
6=3!
different polar decompositions (the symmetric group
S_{3}
acts naturally permuting these decompositions).

The preceding motivates Definition
4.16
and justifies that it is well made.

Definition 4.16
.

A polar algebra is a
mutant
if it admits a polar decomposition so that
\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}=2\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}
and is
regular
otherwise.

The structure of the (uniquely determined) subspace
\mathbb{A}_{0}
of a regular polar algebra is related to the singular locus of the associated radial eigencubic
u(x)
. From this point of view, polar algebras are naturally partitioned into several classes:

•

mutants
with
(\dim\mathbb{A}_{0},\dim\mathbb{A}_{1})\in\{(d,2d):d=1,2,4,8\}
and having radial eigencubic
u(x)
equivalent to one of the four Hurwiz eigencubics.

•

regular
polar algebras.

–

It can be shown that the singular locus of
u(x)
equals
\mathbb{A}_{0}
if and only if
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}<2\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}
.

–

When
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}>2\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}
, then the singular locus of
u(x)
contains
\mathbb{A}_{0}
as a proper subset. Its precise structure is more complicated and will be described elsewhere.

That a mutant polar algebra admits several polar decompositions permuted by an
S_{3}
action is a kind of triality phenomenon that can be explained by the triple algebra construction; see Corollary
6.6
below.

4.4.
Killing-metrized algebras

Exceptional eigencubics share many properties with the mutant subclass of Clifford type radial eigencubics consisting of the four Hurwitz eigencubics, defined in Example
2.4
.
They are naturally united by the notion of Killing metrizability
[
25
]
.

Definition 4.17
.

Given a metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
, the symmetric bilinear form

\kappa(x,y):=\trace L(x)L(y)

is called the
Killing form
of
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
. A commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is
Killing metrized
if its Killing form
\kappa
is nondegenerate and invariant.

In general it is interesting that a commutative algebra admits an invariant metric.
It is a stronger condition that it be metrized by a specific trace-form, as such a form is determined by the underlying algebraic structure and so is not additional data. For an exact commutative algebra the only possibility is the Killing type form, for any nontrivial bilinear form constructed from traces of the multiplication endomorphisms and the multiplication itself must be a multiple of the Killing form.

Theorem
2.9
is clarified by Theorem
4.18
which is its reformulation in terms of Hsiang algebras.

Theorem 4.18
.

A Hsiang algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is Killing metrized if and only if it is exceptional or mutant.

Proof.

Suppose that a polar algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is Killing metrized. In this case, by (
37
) the Killing form has the expression

(44)

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y)=\trace L(x)L(y)=h(x_{0},y_{0})\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}+2h(x_{1},y_{1})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0},

where
x=(x_{0},x_{1})
,
y=(y_{0},y_{1})
according to the polar decomposition
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
. Next, the identity (
44
) is applied to
x=y=z_{0}z_{1}
, where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{A}_{i}
are nonzero elements. By the assumed invariance of
\kappa
and the relations (
36
) and (
39
),

(45)

\displaystyle\kappa(z_{0}z_{1},z_{0}z_{1})

\displaystyle=\kappa(z_{0}(z_{0}z_{1}),z_{1})=h(z_{0},z_{0})\kappa(z_{1},z_{1}),

(46)

\displaystyle\kappa(z_{0}z_{1},z_{0}z_{1})

\displaystyle=\kappa(z_{1}(z_{0}z_{1}),z_{0})=h(z_{1},z_{1})\kappa(z_{0},z_{0}).

On other hand, since
z_{0}z_{1}\in\mathbb{A}_{1}
, by (
44
),

(47)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \kappa(z_{0}z_{1},z_{0}z_{1})&=2h(z_{0}z_{1},z_{0}z_{1})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}\\
&=2h(z_{0}(z_{0}z_{1}),z_{1})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}=2h(z_{0},z_{0})h(z_{1},z_{1})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}.\end{aligned}

By assumption
h(z_{1},z_{1})\neq 0
, hence combining (
46
) and (
47
) yields

(48)

\displaystyle\kappa(z_{0},z_{0})=2h(z_{0},z_{0})\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}.

On the other hand, by (
44
),
\kappa(z_{0},z_{0})=h(x_{0},y_{0})\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}
, and in (
48
) this yields
\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}=2\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}
. Applying (
41
), we get
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}-1\leq\rho(\dim\mathbb{A}_{0})
implying by the properties of the Radon-Hurwitz function
\rho
that
\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}\in\{1,2,4,8\}
, and therefore
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
must be a mutant polar algebra.

This shows that if a normalized Hsiang algebra is Killing metrized then it must be exceptional or mutant. That a mutant algebra is Killing metrized follows from the definition and (
37
). That an exceptional Hsiang algebra is Killing metrized is a substantial claim proved in
[
38
, Proposition 6.11.1]
(see also
[
51
, Theorem 4]
), where the terminology is a bit different.
∎

Summarizing, there are two main classes of radial Hsiang algebras:

(i)

the polar algebras constructed from Clifford systems.

(ii)

the Killing metrized Hsiang algebras.

There are infinitely many nonisomorphic polar algebras (i), occuring in arbitrarily large dimensions, while results in
[
38
, Ch. 6]
establish that exceptional Hsiang algebras occur in at most finitely many dimensions no greater than
72
. The intersection of (i) and (ii) is small, consisting of the four mutant Hsiang algebras.

4.5.
The Peirce decomposition and the hidden Jordan algebra structure

The results recounted next are needed in Theorem
7.1
that shows a relation between two numerical invariants associated with a Killing metrized Hsiang algebra.

A principal tool in the structure theory of Hsiang algebras (in particular, in the proof of the finiteness of the isomorphism types of exceptional radial eigencubics) is the Peirce decomposition (
49
) of an idempotent (for more details see
[
38
, Ch. 6]
). This is like the Peirce decomposition associated with an idempotent in a Jordan algebra, although a bit more complicated.

By (
20
), for an idempotent
c
in a metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
, the multiplication operator
L(c)
is self-adjoint, hence
L(c)
naturally induces the
h
-orthogonal eigenspace decomposition, called the
Peirce decomposition
associated with
c
:

\mathbb{A}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{A}_{c}(\alpha_{i}),

where
L(c)=\alpha_{i}1_{\mathbb{A}_{c}(\alpha_{i})}
,
1\leq i\leq k
, and
\alpha_{i}
are (necessarily real) eigenvalues of
L(c)
, called the Peirce spectrum of
c
.

For any nonzero idempotent
c
in a Hsiang algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
, the Peirce spectrum is
\{1,-1,-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}
and the associated Peirce decomposition is

(49)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{c}(1)\oplus\mathbb{A}_{c}(-1)\oplus\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})\oplus\mathbb{A}_{c}(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}).

Moreover, the eigenvalue
1
has multiplicity one and it follows from the trace-free condition in (
33
) that

(50)

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{A}=3\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-1)+2\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})-1,

where the
Peirce dimensions

(51)

\displaystyle n_{1}=\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-1),\qquad n_{2}=\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}).

and

\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})=2\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-1)+\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})-2=2n_{1}+n_{2}-2

do not depend on the particular choice of
c
. The Peirce dimensions
(n_{1},n_{2})
for exceptional eigencubics are shown in Table
1
. For a polar algebra
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{A}_{1}
, the Peirce dimensions are given by

(n_{1},n_{2})=(\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}-1,\,\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\dim\mathbb{A}_{1}-\dim\mathbb{A}_{0}+2).

One can prove that the subspace
\mathbb{B}_{c}:=\mathbb{A}_{c}(1)\oplus\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\frac{1}{2})
is a subalgebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
, and moreover, with the modified multiplication

(52)

\displaystyle x\ast y=\frac{1}{2}x\diamond y+h(x,c)y+h(y,c)x-2h(x\diamond y,c)c,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{B}_{c},

\mathbb{B}_{c}
becomes a rank 3 Jordan algebra. A remarkable property is that this
Jordan algebra
(\mathbb{B}_{c},\ast,h)
is simple if and only if the original Hsiang algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is exceptional
. In the case when
n_{2}=\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})=0
, the subalgebra
\mathbb{B}_{c}=\mathbb{A}_{c}(1)\cong\mathbb{R}
is trivial. This corresponds exactly to the five the isoparametric eigencubics. By the Jordan-von Neumann-Wigner classification
[
32
]
, the simple formally real rank three Jordan algebras are exactly the
(3d+3)
-dimensional Jordan algebras of Hermitian
3\times 3
-matrices over Hurwitz algebras
\mathbb{F}_{d}
,
d=1,2,4,8
. This implies that when the Peirce dimension
n_{2}=\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})
is positive there holds

(53)

\displaystyle n_{2}=\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(-\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix})=\dim\mathbb{B}_{c}-\dim\mathbb{A}_{c}(1)=3d+2,

which combined with (
50
) implies that the dimension of
\mathbb{A}

is divisible by 3
:

(54)

\displaystyle\dim\mathbb{A}=3(2d+1+n_{1}).

This observation was what originally suggested a relation between exceptional Hsiang algebras and the tripling construction defined in section
6
.

In this notation, a Hsiang algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is mutant if and only if
n_{2}=2
, which formally corresponds to the case
d=0
in (
53
).
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.19
.

Given a Killing metrized algebra, the number
d=d(\mathbb{A})
such that (
53
) holds is called its
Hurwitz dimension
.

Corollary 4.20
.

If
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
is a Killing metrized Hsiang algebra distinct from a Cartan the relation (
53
) holds with
d\in\{0,1,2,4,8\}
.

Remark 4.21
.

The Hsiang algebras corresponding with the isoparametric eigencubics appear in the context of pseudocomposition algebras
[
36
]
. On the other hand, the Cartan eigencubics were used in
[
37
]
,
[
41
]
in the construction of non-classical and singular examples of truly viscosity solutions to fully nonlinear Hessian type uniformly elliptic equations; see also
[
55
, sec. 3]
for a friendly and detailed exposition of this construction. In
[
52
]
, a one-to-one correspondence between cubic solutions of the general eikonal equation similar to (
14
) and rank three Jordan algebras has been established.

Remark 4.22
.

By
[
38
, Theorem 6.11.1]
, a radial Hsiang algebra satisfies
n_{2}=0
if and only if it contains no nonzero
2
-nilpotent element. Via (
27
) it follows that the singular locus of a radial eigencubic is trivial if and only if the radial eigencubic is one of the five radial eigencubics of isoparametric type.

5.
Quasicomposition algebras

The quasicomposition algebras defined in this section generalize Hurwitz algebras and cross product algebras.
Hurwitz algebras were defined in Example
2.4
. Some basic facts about Hurwitz algebras needed here are recalled now. Their proofs can be found in
[
14
,
49
]
.
Let
e
be the unit in the Hurwitz algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond)
. The polar bilinear form

(55)

\displaystyle n(x,y)=n(x+y)-n(x)-n(y)

induces the trace form
t(x)=n(x,e)\in\mathbb{A}^{*}
such that any element
x\in\mathbb{A}
satisfies the quadratic relation

(56)

\displaystyle x^{2}-t(x)x+n(x)e=0,

and the norm recovered from
t
by
n(x):=t(x)^{2}-t(x^{2})
satisfies the composition law (
11
). It follows from (
11
) that
n(e)=1
and from (
55
) that
t(e)=2
.
Then

x^{\sigma}=t(x)e-x

is an involution on
\mathbb{A}
and the trace bilinear form
h(x,y)=t(x\diamond y)
is involutively invariant, in other words,
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a metrized algebra with involution.

Any Hurwitz algebra satisfies the
composition identity
(see Lemma 1.3.3 in
[
49
]
)

(57)

\displaystyle L(x^{\sigma})L(x)y=x^{\sigma}\diamond(x\diamond y)=n(x)y.

Definition 5.1
.

A metrized algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a
quasicomposition algebra
if

(58)

\displaystyle L(x)L(x^{\sigma})L(x)y=x\diamond(x^{\sigma}\diamond(x\diamond y)=h(x,x)(x\diamond y)

holds for any
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
, where
n(x)=h(x,x)
. A quasicomposition algebra over the real field with a positive definite form
h
is said to be Euclidean.

Note that (
57
) immediately implies (
58
).

Proposition 5.2
.

A unital composition algebra is a quasicomposition algebra.

However, a quasicomposition algebra need not be unital. Examples are given below.

An important invariant of a quasicomposition algebra is its deviation from the composition property. By (
57
), for any composition (Hurwitz) algebra

(59)

\displaystyle\trace L(x)L(x^{\sigma})=\dim\mathbb{A}\cdot h(x,x).

Such a trace identity holds true for any quasicomposition algebra with a certain integer factor in place of
\dim\mathbb{A}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a nonzero quasicomposition algebra. There is an integer
0\leq\delta(\mathbb{A})\leq\dim\mathbb{A}-1
such that

(60)

\displaystyle\trace L(x)L(x^{\sigma})=(\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A}))h(x,x)

for all
x\in\mathbb{A}
. Furthermore,
\delta=0
if and only if
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a composition algebra.

Proof.

Let
x\neq 0
.The defining identity (
58
) implies the operator identity

(61)

\displaystyle L(x^{\sigma})L(x)L(x^{\sigma})L(x)=h(x,x)L(x^{\sigma})L(x),

which can be rewritten as
P(x)^{2}=P(x)
, where
P(x)=\frac{1}{h(x,x)}L(x^{\sigma})L(x)
. It follows from (
18
) that

h(x,x)h(P(x)y,z)=h(L(x^{\sigma})L(x)y,z)=h(y,L(x^{\sigma})L(x)z)=h(x,x)h(y,P(x)z),

so that
P(x)
is self-adjoint with respect to
h
. This implies that
P(x)
is an orthogonal projection in
\mathbb{A}
, thus

\trace P(x)=\dim\im P(x)=:m(x)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
P(x)
is not identically zero, this implies
m(x)\in\{1,2,\ldots,\dim\mathbb{A}\}
. Moreover,
\trace L(x^{\sigma})L(x)-m(x)h(x,x)=0
identically on
\mathbb{A}
. A standard continuity (in the Zariski topology) argument implies that
m(x)
must be constant on
\mathbb{A}
, so that
m(x)=m
. Then
\delta:=\dim\mathbb{A}-m
satisfies (
60
).

By (
59
),
\delta(\mathbb{A})=0
for any Hurwitz algebra. In the converse direction, if
\delta(\mathbb{A})=0
then the above argument shows that
\dim\im P(x)=\dim\mathbb{A}
, therefore
\im P(x)=\im L(x^{\sigma})L(x)=\mathbb{A}
, hence
P(x)=1
on
\mathbb{A}
, in other words,
L(x^{\sigma})L(x)=h(x,x)1_{\mathbb{A}}
, implying (
57
), so that
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a composition algebra.
∎

The proof of Theorem
5.3
implies the following characterization of
\delta(\mathbb{A})
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Suppose
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a nonzero quasicomposition algebra. For any nonzero
x\in\mathbb{A}

\delta(\mathbb{A})=\dim\ker L(x^{\sigma})L(x)=\dim\ker L(x)L(x^{\sigma}).

Definition 5.5
.

The integer
\delta(\mathbb{A})
defined by Theorem
5.3
is the
defect
of the quasicomposition algebra
\mathbb{A}
.

Hurwitz algebras are quasicomposition algebras having defect
0
. The defect measures the deviation of a quasicomposition algebra from the composition property.
There follow some examples of quasicomposition algebras that are not Hurwitz algebras.

Example 5.6

(
\delta(\mathbb{A})=0
)
.

As in Example
2.4
, the
paracomplex
algebra
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is the
2
-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{C}
equipped with the commutative multiplication

x\diamond y=\overline{x}\overline{y},
where the juxtaposition
xy
is the standard multiplication in
\mathbb{C}
. Take
\sigma
to be the identity on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
\sigma
is an involution on
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
:

(x\diamond y)^{\sigma}=x\diamond y=y\diamond x=y^{\sigma}\diamond x^{\sigma}.

Because
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is exact, it is not unital.
Finally,

x\diamond(x^{\sigma}\diamond(x\diamond y)=x\diamond(x\diamond(x\diamond y)=x\diamond(x\diamond\bar{x}\bar{y})=x\diamond\bar{x}xy=n(x)x\diamond y,

implying the quasicomposition identity (
58
) with
\delta(\mathbb{A})=0
.

Note that the paraquaternions are not a quasicomposition algebra. The preceding proof uses the commutativity of the usual complex multiplication in an essential way.

Example 5.7

(
\delta(\mathbb{A})=1
)
.

A
cross product algebra
is a metrized anticommutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\times,\sigma,h)
equipped with the standard involuiont
x^{\sigma}:=-x
, such that

(62)

\displaystyle x\times(x\times y)=-h(x,x)y+h(x,y)x,

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{A}
. Nonzero cross product algebras exist only in dimensions
3
and
7

[
7
]
. Any simple
3
-dimensional Lie algebra is a cross-product algebra. The case of
\mathfrak{so}(3)
yields the usual cross product on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a
3
-dimensional real cross product algebra with positive definite
h
is isomorphic to this example. The imaginary octonions with the cross product equal to the commutator of the usual octonion product give an example of a
7
-dimensional cross-product algebra.

Since the multiplication is anticommutative, the identity (
62
) becomse
x^{\sigma}\times(x\times y)=h(x,x)y-h(x,y)x
which implies

x\times(x^{\sigma}\times(x\times y))=h(x,x)x\times y,

so that
(\mathbb{A},\times,\sigma,h)
is a quasicomposition algebra. Since
x\times x=0
, by Corollary
5.4
one has
\delta(\mathbb{A})=\dim\ker L(x^{\sigma})L(x)\geq 1
. On the other hand, if
x\neq 0
and
y\in\ker L(x^{\sigma})L(x)
is non-collinear to
x
then by (i) we have
-h(x,x)y+h(x,y)x=0
, implying
y=0
, so that
\delta(\mathbb{A})=1
.

Example 5.8

(
\delta(\mathbb{A})=2
)
.

In 1978 Domokos and Kövesi-Domokos

\circ
and showed that

presents it as

\displaystyle(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\diamond(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(x^{\prime}\times y^{\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}\times y^{\prime\prime},-x^{\prime}\times y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}\times y^{\prime}\right)

6.
Tripling construction and Hsiang algebras

This section shows that the quasicompostion algebras arise naturally as building blocks for exceptional Hsiang algebras. The bridge material between these different contexts is the tripling construction described next.

6.1.
The triple algebra construction

The classical Cayley-Dickson doubling construction produces from a Hurwitz algebra a new Hurwitz algebra having twice the dimension as the original algebra. The most well known proof of the classification of Hurwitz algebras is based on this process
[
49
]
. A. Albert extended the doubling construction to algebras with involution
[
1
]
. The tripling constrution described here has a similar flavor, associating with an algebra with involution a commutative algebra having three times the dimension of the original algebra. Just as the doubling construction can be used to classify Hurwitz algebras, the triple construction is useful for studying Hsiang algebras.

The motivation for the tripling construction, described in detail below, following its definition, is the correspondence between metrized commutative algebras and cubic spaces. Succinctly, the trilinear form of a metrized algebra with involution can be viewed as a cubic form on the threefold direct product of the algebra with itself, and via the correspondence this yields the triple product of Definition
6.1
.

Definition 6.1
.

Given an algebra with involution
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma)
, its
triple
is the vector space

(64)

\displaystyle{T}(\mathbb{A})=\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}

equipped with the evidently commutative multiplication given by

(65)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\ast(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=(x_{3}^{\sigma}y_{2}^{\sigma}+y_{3}^{\sigma}x_{2}^{\sigma},x_{1}^{\sigma}y_{3}^{\sigma}+y_{1}^{\sigma}x_{3}^{\sigma},x_{2}^{\sigma}y_{1}^{\sigma}+y_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}).\end{aligned}

(Here juxtaposition indicates the product
\diamond
.)
Alternatively, the
\ast
-multiplication by
{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is given by the endomorphism

\displaystyle L_{\ast}({x})=R_{\ast}({x})=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{3})&\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{2})\\
\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{3})&0&\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{1})\\
\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{2})&\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{1})&0\\
\end{array}\right).

It follows from (
6.1
) that the triple algebra is exact,

(69)

\displaystyle\trace L_{\ast}({x})=0,\qquad\forall x\in{T}(\mathbb{A}).

Although this paper treats only algebras over the real field, Definition
6.1
works as stated over general ground fields.

The triple algebra has the following motivation. Consider a metrized commutative algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,h)
. By definition the trilinear form
\omega(x,y,z)=h(xy,z)
is completely symmetric. This trilinear form can be viewed as a cubic form on the threefold product
\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}
. This threefold product can be endowed with the product metric
H=h\times h\times h
, and this makes
(\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A},H,\omega)
a cubic space. With respect to the metric
H
, the polarization of the cubic form
\omega
determines on
\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}
a commutative multiplication
\ast
, and this product is by definition the triple of the original multiplication. By construction the metric
H
is invariant with respect to the triple product. The first author found this construction in the context of building new metrized commutative algebras from old and described it in
[
21
,
22
,
24
]
.

Now consider a semisimple real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
metrized by its Killing form,
B
. The trilinear form
\omega(x,y,z)=B([x,y],z)
is completely antisymmetric, but still can be viewed as a cubic form on the threefold product of the Lie algebra, and the preceding construction goes through as written to produce a commutative multiplication on
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}
. This multiplication was introduced, with independent motivations, by M. Kinyon and A. Sagle in
[
33
]
, where it was called the
Nahm algebra
of the Lie algebra. Actually nothing about the Jacobi identity is used in the Nahm algebra construction and it makes sense for any metrized anticommutative algebra.

This shows that from a metrized commutative or anticommutative algebra there is a natural construction of a metrized commutative algebra having three times the dimension of the original algebra.

The tripling construction described here was found by both authors looking for a common generalization of the commutative and anticommutative cases. After the fact it is evident that the tripling construction has much in common with the construction of Albert algebras from Hurwitz algebras, but this will be discussed elsewhere.

The common construction realizes the triple algebra of a metrized algebra with involution
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
as the polarization of the twisted cubic form
h^{\sigma}(xy,z)=h(xy,z^{\sigma})
on
\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}
. It is straightforward to check that
h
is involutively invariant in the sense (
17
)-(
18
) if and only if the twisted bilinear form
h^{\sigma}
defined by
h^{\sigma}(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}(h(x,y^{\sigma})+h(x^{\sigma},y))
satisfies

(70)

\displaystyle h^{\sigma}(x^{\sigma},y^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=h^{\sigma}(x,y),

(71)

\displaystyle h^{\sigma}(xy,z)

\displaystyle=h^{\sigma}(x,zy),\quad\forall x,y,z\in\mathbb{A}.

In this case the trilinear form
h^{\sigma}(xy,z)
is completely symmetric. It determines a cubic form
u(x)
on
\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{A}
with formally the same expression,
u(x)=h^{\sigma}(x_{1}x_{2},x_{3})
. Polarizing this form with respect to the product metric
H=h\times h\times h
and using the invariance of
h^{\sigma}
yields

(72)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} h^{\sigma}&(x_{1}y_{2},z_{3})+h^{\sigma}(y_{1}z_{2},x_{3})+h^{\sigma}(z_{1}x_{2},y_{3})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+h^{\sigma}(y_{1}x_{2},z_{3})+h^{\sigma}(z_{1}y_{2},x_{3})+h^{\sigma}(x_{1}z_{2},y_{3})\\
&=h(z_{1},x_{3}^{\sigma}y_{2}^{\sigma}+y_{3}^{\sigma}x_{2}^{\sigma})+h(z_{2},x_{1}^{\sigma}y_{3}^{\sigma}+y_{1}^{\sigma}x_{3}^{\sigma})+h(z_{3},x_{2}^{\sigma}y_{1}^{\sigma}+y_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})\\
&=H(x\ast y,z),\end{aligned}

where
\ast
is the triple product defined in (
65
). In short, the cubic form of the triple product is the twisted trilinear form of the original algebra.

Proposition 6.2
.

If
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma)
carries an involutively invariant metric
h
then the commutative algebra
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast)
is metrized by
H
defined by
H(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{3}h(x_{i},y_{i})
. Moreover, the cubic form of
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast,H)
is the trilinear form of
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h^{\sigma})
.

Proof.

Because
h
is involutively invariant, the twisted bilinear form
h^{\sigma}
is invariant, so (
72
) shows that the trilinear form
H(x\ast y,z)
is completely symmetric, which shows the invariance of
H
with respect to
\ast
. Taking
x=y=z
in (
72
) yields
H(x\ast x,x)=6h^{\sigma}(x_{1}x_{2},x_{3})
, which is the final claim.
∎

The triple algebra
{T}(\mathbb{A})
decomposes naturally into an
H
-orthogonal direct sum of vector subspaces

(73)

\displaystyle{T}(\mathbb{A})=\mathbb{T}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{T}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{T}_{3},

where
\mathbb{T}_{1}=\mathbb{A}\times 0\times 0
,
\mathbb{T}_{2}=0\times\mathbb{A}\times 0
, and
\mathbb{T}_{3}=0\times 0\times\mathbb{A}
satisfy

(74)

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{i}\ast\mathbb{T}_{i}=\{0\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{i}\ast\mathbb{T}_{j}=\mathbb{T}_{k},\quad\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}.

If
{x}={v}_{1}+{v}_{2}+{v}_{3}\in{T}(\mathbb{A})
, where

(75)

{v}_{1}=(x_{1},0,0)\in\mathbb{T}_{1},\quad{v}_{2}=(0,x_{2},0)\in\mathbb{T}_{2},\quad{v}_{3}=(0,0,x_{3})\in\mathbb{T}_{3},

then

(76)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{v}_{1}\ast{v}_{1}&={v}_{2}\ast{v}_{2}={v}_{3}\ast{v}_{3}=0,\\
{v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3}&=(x_{3}^{\sigma}x_{2}^{\sigma},0,0)\in\mathbb{T}_{1},\\
{v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1}&=(0,x_{1}^{\sigma}x_{3}^{\sigma},0)\in\mathbb{T}_{2},\\
{v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2}&=(0,0,x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})\in\mathbb{T}_{3}.\end{split}

implying

{x}\ast{x}=2(x_{3}^{\sigma}x_{2}^{\sigma},x_{1}^{\sigma}x_{3}^{\sigma},x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}).

Moreover,

(77)

\displaystyle L_{\ast}({v}_{1})^{2}{v}_{2}={v}_{1}\ast({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2})=(0,x_{1}^{\sigma}(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})^{\sigma},0)=(0,x_{1}^{\sigma}(x_{1}x_{2}),0)\in\mathbb{T}_{2}

and similarly

(78)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{\ast}({v}_{1})^{3}{v}_{2}&=(0,0,((x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})x_{1})x_{1}^{\sigma})=(0,0,(x_{1}(x_{1}^{\sigma}(x_{1}x_{2})))^{\sigma})\\
&=(0,0,\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{1})L_{\diamond}(x_{1}^{\sigma})L_{\diamond}(x_{1})x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}_{3}.\end{split}

Similar relations hold for any pair of
{v}_{i}
and
{v}_{j}
with
i\neq j
. We record some useful identities needed in the proof of Theorem
6.3
.
By (
76
),

(79)

\displaystyle{x}\ast{x}=2({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3}+{v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1}+{v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2}),

and therefore, using the fact that
H
is involutively invariant (Proposition
6.2
) and (
76
),

(80)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H({x}\ast{x},{x})&=H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{v}_{1})+H({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1},{v}_{2})+2H({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2},{v}_{3})\\
&=6H({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2},{v}_{3}).\end{split}

Similarly,

(81)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{x}\ast({x}\ast{x})=&2\underbrace{({v}_{2}\ast({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{1})+{v}_{3}\ast({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1})}_{:={w}_{1}}+\underbrace{{v}_{1}\ast({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2})+{v}_{3}\ast({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{2})}_{:={w}_{2}}\\
&+\underbrace{{v}_{1}\ast({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{3})+{v}_{2}\ast({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3}))}_{:={w}_{3}}=:2{w}_{1}+2{w}_{2}+2{w}_{3},\qquad{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{i}.\end{split}

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
be an algebra with involution equipped with an involutively invariant metric
h
. Its triple
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast)
metrized by
H=h\times h\times h
is a Hsiang algebra if and only if
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
a Euclidean quasicomposition algebra. In this case
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast)
is normalized by
\theta=4/3
.

Proof.

If
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a quasicomposition algebra, then the identity
L_{\diamond}(x)L_{\diamond}(x^{\sigma})L_{\diamond}(x)=h(x,x)L_{\diamond}(x)
holds for any
x\in\mathbb{A}
, so (
78
) implies

(82)

\displaystyle L_{\ast}({v}_{i})^{3}{v}_{j}=H({v}_{i},{v}_{i}){v}_{i}\ast{v}_{j}.

Combining (
81
) with (
79
) and (
74
) yields

(83)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H({x}\ast{x},{x}\ast({x}\ast{x}))&=2H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{w}_{1})+2H({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1},{w}_{2})+2H({v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2},{w}_{3}).\end{split}

By (
82
) there hold

\displaystyle H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{w}_{1})

\displaystyle=H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{v}_{2}\ast({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{1}))+H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{v}_{3}\ast({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1})

\displaystyle=H({v}_{3},{v}_{2}\ast({v}_{2}\ast({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{1})))+H({v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3},{v}_{3}\ast({v}_{3}\ast({v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1}))

\displaystyle=H({v}_{2},{v}_{2})\,H({v}_{3},{v}_{2}\ast{v}_{1})+H({v}_{3},{v}_{3})\,H({v}_{2},{v}_{3}\ast{v}_{1})

\displaystyle=[H({v}_{2},{v}_{2})+H({v}_{3},{v}_{3})]\,H({v}_{1},{v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3}),

and the similar identities obtained by permuting the indices.
Substituting this in (
83
) and using (
80
) yields

(84)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H({x}\ast{x},{x}\ast({x}\ast{x}))&=8[H({v}_{1},{v}_{1})+H({v}_{2},{v}_{2})+H({v}_{3},{v}_{3})]\,H({v}_{1},{v}_{2}\ast{v}_{3})\\
&=\frac{4}{3}H({x},{x})\,H({x}\ast{x},{x}).\end{split}

Because
\ast
is exact, this last relation shows that
{T}(\mathbb{A})
satisfies the Hsiang equation (
33
) for
\theta=4/3
.

In the converse direction, suppose that
{T}(\mathbb{A})
satisfies the Hsiang equation (
33
) with
\theta=4/3
.
Suppose that
{x}={v}_{1}+{v}_{2}
,
{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{i}
. By (
76
)

{x}\ast{x}=2{v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2}=(0,0,2x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})\in\mathbb{T}_{3},

hence
H({x}\ast{x},{x})=0
and

({x}\ast{x})\ast({x}\ast{x})=0.

Also, using (
77
),

({x}\ast{x})\ast{x}=2{v}_{1}\ast{v}_{2}\ast({v}_{1}+{v}_{2})=(2(x_{1}x_{2})x_{2}^{\sigma},2x_{1}^{\sigma}(x_{1}x_{2}),0)\in\mathbb{T}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{T}_{2}

Applying (
34
) for
\theta=4/3
to
{x}={v}_{1}+{v}_{2}
yields

4(({x}\ast{x})\ast{x})\ast{x}-4H({x},{x}){x}\ast{x}=0.

Using

(({x}\ast{x})\ast{x})\ast{x}=2(0,\,0,\,x_{2}^{\sigma}(x_{2}(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}))+(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})x_{1})x_{1}^{\sigma})

and
H({x},{x})=h(x_{1},x_{1})+h(x_{2},x_{2})
yields

x_{2}^{\sigma}(x_{2}(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}))+(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})x_{1})x_{1}^{\sigma}=(h(x_{1},x_{1})+h(x_{2},x_{2}))x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}.

Setting
x_{1}\to tx_{1}
, and equating terms of equal degree in
t
yields htat

x_{2}^{\sigma}(x_{2}(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}))=h(x_{2},x_{2})x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma},\qquad(x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma})x_{1})x_{1}^{\sigma}=h(x_{1},x_{1})x_{2}^{\sigma}x_{1}^{\sigma}

holds for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{A}
, implying (
58
). The theorem is proved.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

There are
weak
versions of quasicomposition algebras in which the symmetric bilinear form
h
is allowed to be degenerate and interesting examples can be found. Via the tripling construction such degenerate quasicomposition algebras yield degenerate Hsiang algebras in the sense of Remark
4.7
.
An example is given by the
6
-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{A}=\mathfrak{gl}(2,\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the product
(X,u)(Y,v)=(XY,\bar{X}v+Yu)
where
\bar{X}=\operatorname{tr}(X)\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}-X
, the involution
(X,u)^{\sigma}=(\bar{X},-u)
, and the metric
h((X,u),(Y,v))=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\bar{X}Y+\bar{Y}X)
given by polarizing the quadratic form
q(X,u)=\det X=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\bar{X}X)
. Because
\bar{X}X=\det(X)\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
, this solves
L((X,u)^{\sigma})L(X,u)=\det(X)\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{A}}=h((X,u),(X,u)\operatorname{Id}_{\mathbb{A}}
, so solves (
58
), though
h
has rank
4
rather than
6
. Such weak quasicomposition algebras are not discussed further here.

7.
Realizability of exceptional Hsiang algebras as triples

The triple
{T}(\mathbb{A})
of a quasicomposition algebra
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
is a Hsiang algebra. However, because
\dim{T}(\mathbb{A})=3\dim\mathbb{A}
, the dimension of a triple algebra is divisible by
3
, so not every Hsiang algebra can be realized as the triple of a quasicomposition algebra. On the other hand, by (
54
) the dimension of an exceptional eigencubic that is not a Cartan isoparametric eigencubic is also divisible by 3. This suggests characterizing those Hsiang algebras
realizable
as triple algebras.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(\mathbb{A},\diamond,\sigma,h)
be a Euclidean quasicomposition algebra with defect
\delta(\mathbb{A})
defined by (
60
). Then the triple
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast,H)
is a Killing-metrized Hsiang algebra (so is exceptional or mutant). Furthermore,

(86)

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbb{A})=d({T}(\mathbb{A})),

where
d({T}(\mathbb{A}))
is the Hurwitz dimension of
{T}(\mathbb{A})
(see Definition
4.19
).

Proof.

Tracing the square of (
6.1
) and using the properties of the trace, the identities (
16
), and (
60
) of Theorem
5.3
yields

\displaystyle\trace L_{\ast}({x})^{2}

\displaystyle=\trace\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{3})&\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{2})\\
\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{3})&0&\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{1})\\
\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{2})&\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{1})&0\\
\end{array}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\trace(\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{3})\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{3})+\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{2})\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{2})+\sigma L_{\diamond}(x_{1})\sigma R_{\diamond}(x_{1}))

\displaystyle=2\trace(L_{\diamond}(x_{1}^{\sigma})L_{\diamond}(x_{1})+L_{\diamond}(x_{2}^{\sigma})L_{\diamond}(x_{2})+L_{\diamond}(x_{3}^{\sigma})L_{\diamond}(x_{3}))

\displaystyle=2(\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A}))\sum_{i=1}^{3}h(x_{i},x_{i})

\displaystyle=2(\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A}))H({x},{x}).

Thus, the Killing trace-form is proportional to the invariant metric
H
, hence
({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast,H)
is a Killing-metrized Hsiang algebra.

Next, let
c
be a nonzero idempotent in
\mathbb{T}:=({T}(\mathbb{A}),\ast,H)
. Then, by (
35
),

(87)

\displaystyle\trace L_{\ast}(c)^{2}=2(\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A}))H(c,c)=\frac{3}{2}(\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A})).

On the other hand, combining (
49
) with (
53
), yields directly

(88)

\begin{split}\trace L_{\ast}(c)^{2}&=1+\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1)+\frac{1}{4}(\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-\frac{1}{2})+\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(\frac{1}{2}))\\
&=\frac{3}{2}(\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1)+d+1).\end{split}

By (
54
),
3\dim\mathbb{A}=\dim\mathbb{T}=3(2d+1+\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1))
, hence

\dim\mathbb{A}=2d+1+\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1).

Equating (
87
) with (
88
) yields

\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1)+d+1=\dim\mathbb{A}-\delta(\mathbb{A})=2d+1+\dim\mathbb{T}_{c}(-1)-\delta(\mathbb{A}),

which implies (
86
).
∎

By Theorem
7.1
the defect of a quasicomposition algebra equals the Hurwitz dimension of its triple algebra which is a Killing metrized Hsiang algebra, hence we have

Corollary 7.2
.

The defect of a quasicomposition algebra
\delta(\mathbb{A})\in\{0,1,2,4,8\}
.

It should be possible to control the defect of a quasicomposition algebra directly by associating a Hurwitz algebra with the operator
L(x)L(x^{\sigma})
.

Concluding remarks

The definition of Hsiang algebras is motivated by a problem in differential geometry and this context leads to a focus on solutions over the real field metrized by a Euclidean metric. However the problem of classifying Hsiang algebras makes sense for real algebras with metrics of other signatures and over arbitrary fields. The differential geometric context motivates an initial focus on Euclidean metrics. Relaxing this restriction to include isotropic metrics is necessary for considering algebraically closed fields but is also relevant for the problem of finding algebraic mean curvature zero hypersurfaces in pseudo-Riemannian space forms.

Comparision with division algebras, composition algebras, and the like suggests that it is useful initially to focus on base fields that are algebraically closed or real so as to avoid issues that essentially arithmetic (rather than related to the structural properties of the class of algebras under consideration). For example, the classical Hurwitz theorem says that over the real field there are four unital composition algebras, while over the rationals there infinite families in each dimension. On the other hand, that there are only four dimensions in which there occur unital composition algebras is true independently of the base field. This is a genuinely structural statement that does not depend on arithmetic. The expectation is that the general structural features described here and summarized in Diagram
1
remain true over general fields.

A fundamental feature of quasicomposition algebras is the constant rank property, that the left and right multiplication endomorphisms have constant rank, equal to the dimension minus the defect. By itself such a constant rank condition defines a larger (probably much larger) class of algebras. Such a condition admits many interesting variations. It would be interesting to study such conditions in detail.
