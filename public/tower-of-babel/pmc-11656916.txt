FAM96B negatively regulates FOSL1 to modulate the osteogenic differentiation and regeneration of periodontal ligament stem cells via ferroptosis
Authors: Qianyi Qin, Haoqing Yang, Runzhi Guo, Yunfei Zheng, Yiping Huang, Luyuan Jin, Zhipeng Fan, Weiran Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontal ligament stem cell (PDLSC)-based therapy is one of the methods to assist bone regeneration. Understanding the functional regulation of PDLSCs and the mechanisms involved is a crucial issue in bone regeneration. This study aimed to explore the roles of the family with sequence similarity 96 member B (FAM96B) in the functional regulation of PDLSCs.MethodsTo assess the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, the alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay, Alizarin red staining, quantitative calcium analysis, and osteogenic marker detection were conducted. Transplantation PDLSCs under the dorsum of nude mice and into the rat calvarial defects were also performed. Then, FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs were used for RNA-sequencing and bioinformatic analysis. To evaluate the ferroptosis of PDLSCs, cytosolic reactive oxygen species (ROS), expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), mitochondrial morphology and functions including the mitochondrial ROS, mitochondria membrane potential, and mitochondrial respiration were detected.ResultsThe osteogenic indicators ALP activity, level of mineralization, and osteocalcin expression were decreased in PDLSCs by FAM96B, which demonstrated that FAM96B inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. FAM96B knockdown promoted the new bone formation of PDLSCs subcutaneously transplanted to the dorsum of nude mice. Then, related biological functions were detected by the RNA-sequencing and the ferroptosis was focused. FAM96B enhanced the cytosolic ROS level and inhibited the expression of GPX4 and mitochondrial functions in PDLSCs. Hence, FAM96B promoted the ferroptosis of PDLSCs. Meanwhile, we found that FAM96B inhibition upregulated the target gene FOS like 1, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (FOSL1) expression and FOSL1 promoted the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in vitro. FOSL1 also promoted the new bone formation of PDLSCs transplanted subcutaneously to the dorsum of nude mice and transplanted into rat calvarial defects. Then, the inhibitory effect of FOSL1 on the ferroptosis was confirmed.ConclusionsFAM96B depletion promoted the osteogenic differentiation and suppressed the ferroptosis of PDLSCs. FAM96B negatively regulated the downstream gene FOSL1 and FOSL1 promoted the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs via the ferroptosis. Hence, our findings provided a foundation for understanding the FAM96B-FOSL1 axis acting as a target for MSC mediated bone regeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-024-04083-7.

Background

There are various clinical conditions in which bone defects are created, such as inflammation, trauma, tumor resection, and skeletal abnormality. It is usually difficult to repair the defects through the body’s natural healing ability. Although the autologous bone graft is a common therapeutic method to repair defects, there are several disadvantages including a limited amount of graft tissue and donor site morbidity [1, 2]. Recently, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based therapy is considered as a favorable method of bone regeneration [3, 4]. MSC transplantation relies on good biological scaffold materials to provide biomechanical support and cytokines to provide a suitable biochemical environment, which can promote adhesion, migration, proliferation, osteogenesis, and angiogenesis of seed cells [5, 6]. Periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), one of the dental tissue-derived MSCs, have high self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential, which can create the bone-periodontal ligament-cementum complex. Implanting PDLSCs via cell sheet or local injection into alveolar bone defects can promote bone regeneration [7–11].

Studies have revealed that phenotypic and functional transformations of mitochondria play an essential role in the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [12, 13]. Iron–sulfur (Fe–S) clusters which participate in various cellular functions also have a significant impact on mitochondrial energy production [14]. The family with sequence similarity 96 member B (FAM96B), a subunit of cytosolic Fe–S cluster assembly (CIA), participates in assembling Fe–S clusters into cytosolic and nuclear proteins and maintaining cellular and mitochondrial iron homeostasis [15–19]. Our previous study demonstrated that FAM96B enhanced the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation capacities of dental pulp stem cells [20]. FAM96B is involved in various physiological and pathological processes, mainly binding to proteins to exert its functions. As a component of the MMXD complex, FAM96B interacted with the brain-type creatine kinase during mitosis which was essential to the proper formation of mitotic spindle [21]. It was confirmed that FAM96B also suppressed the proliferation and invasion of cancer cells [22]. Besides, FAM96B promoted the tube formation, proliferation, and migration of endothelial cells through the interaction with E2-2 protein [23]. FAM96B could interact with prelamin A, which provided some clues to the mechanisms of prelamin A in cellular senescence [24]. However, the effect and mechanism of FAM96B on bone regeneration by PDLSCs remain unclear.

Mechanisms of enhancing the osteogenic potential of PDLSCs have been widely studied, including improving cell proliferation and migration ability, targeting activation of important signalling pathways, and epigenetic regulation, etc. [25–27]. Cell senescence and death can greatly affect the regeneration of tissue defects. Ferroptosis, a kind of iron-dependent cell death caused by lipid peroxidation, is characterized by reactive oxygen species (ROS), shrunken mitochondria, and diminished antioxidant enzyme activity [28]. Previous studies have reported that iron overload could affect bone metabolism and contribute to bone resorption [29, 30]. Inhibiting ferroptosis has recently become a target for bone regeneration therapy [31, 32]. However, few studies concerned the influence of ferroptosis on the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs. Studies reported that PDLSC-based therapy via inhibiting ferroptosis could be a promising therapeutic strategy for periodontitis; however, they did not evaluate the bone regeneration [33, 34]. A recent study revealed that an osteoimmunomodulatory biopatch improved the osteogenesis of PDLSCs by regulating IL-17 signalling and ferroptosis [35].

This study aimed to figure out the effect of FAM96B on the osteogenesis of PDLSCs and underlying mechanism related to the ferroptosis. Moreover, we demonstrated the target gene of FAM96B and elucidated the therapeutic effect in bone regeneration.

Methods

Cell cultures

Human PDLSCs were acquired from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The PDLSCs were cultured in MEM Alpha medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) mixed with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 10,000 units/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 200 mM L-glutamine (Gibco). PDLSCs between passages 3 and 5 were applied.

To induce the ferroptosis, 5 μM erastin (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was used with 24 h incubation.

Transfection

The full-length human FAM96B and FOS like 1, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (FOSL1) constructs were built separately and corroborated by gene sequencing. For overexpression, LV5 lentiviral vector (GenePharma Company, Suzhou, China) was used to subclone the cDNA of FAM96B or FOSL1. Meanwhile, the LV3 vector was applied to build a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) of FAM96B or FOSL1. The virus transfection and cell selection were performed as in our previous study [36]. Short hairpin RNA sequences

Small-interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting FOSL1 (si-FOSL1) was purchased from GenePharma and the sequence was 5’-GTCGAAGGCCTTGTGAACAGA-3’. When the degree of cell fusion reached 70%-80%, PDLSCs were transfected with siRNAs using Opti-MEM and Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (real-time RT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used to extract RNA from PDLSCs. We performed real‐time RT‐PCR and determined the gene expression following the previous protocol [37]. The primers used are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

The PDLSCs were lysed and total protein was extracted on ice by RIPA lysis buffer (Applygen, Beijing, China). Western blot analysis was conducted as the method described in our previous research [38]. The primary antibodies applied were as below: FAM96B (Cat No. ab166607, Abcam), osteocalcin (OCN; Cat No. A20800, Abclonal), FOSL1 (Cat No. ab252421, Abcam), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4; Cat No. 67763-1-Ig, Proteintech), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Cat No. 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). Relative protein levels were quantified by using ImageJ analysis software (Wayne Rasband, NIH, USA).

Osteogenic induction, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay, and Alizarin red staining

PDLSCs were cultured in 6-well plates (2.0 × 105 cells/well) with osteogenic-inducing medium to induce the osteogenic differentiation. The composition of the medium is the same as that used in our previous study [36]. The in vitro osteogenic induction lasted for 14 days. On day 5, we used an ALP activity test kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to test the ALP activity. Moreover, after a 14-day osteogenic induction, Alizarin red staining and quantitative calcium analysis were performed to assess the mineralization level according to the protocols described in our previous research [36]. The Alizarin red staining was observed and photographed under a microscope (Olympus IX73, Olympus America, Center Valley, Pa, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

PDLSCs were cultured on round coverslips in 12-well plates (1.0 × 105 cells/well) with osteogenic-inducing medium for 1 week. Cells were fixed with 10% formalin for 10 min and permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Subsequent to block with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h, the coverslips were incubated with the primary antibody dilutions at 4 °C overnight. Then, the secondary antibody dilutions were used to incubate the coverslips for 60 min. The nucleuses were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The primary antibody applied was OCN (Cat No. A20800, Abclonal) and the secondary antibody was Alexa Fluor 488 (Cat No. A21206, Invitrogen). Cells were captured under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX61, Olympus America, Center Valley, Pa, USA). The relative fluorescence intensities were quantified by using ImageJ analysis software.

Bioinformatic analysis of RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA extracted from FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs was used for RNA-seq. RNA-seq and further bioinformatic analysis were conducted by Guangzhou Epibiotek Company (Guangzhou, China). RNA libraries were built up by the Epi™ mini long RNA-seq kit, and the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 was applied as the instrument model. The DESeq2 algorithm was used to filter the differentially expressed genes. The significant analysis and false discovery rate (FDR) analysis were performed according to the criteria below: |log2FC|> 1 (fold change > 2), FDR < 0.05. In addition, gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed to find out the biological functions of the differentially expressed genes. The GO annotations were downloaded from Gene Ontology (http://www.geneontology.org/), NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org/). Pathway analysis was carried out to identify the significant pathways of the differentially expressed genes on the basis of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Cytosolic and mitochondrial ROS level detection and mitochondria membrane potential (MMP) detection

The culture medium was removed from 6-well plates and 10 μM DCFH-DA working solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added. PDLSCs were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min followed by washing the cells three times with serum-free culture medium. PDLSCs were snapped under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73). Then, the cytosolic ROS levels were quantified through ImageJ software.

Mitochondrial ROS levels were tested by MitoSOX Red staining. After culturing PDLSCs on round coverslips in 12-well plates, PDLSCs were stained with 10 μM MitoSOX Red molecular probe working solution (MedChemExpress). The nucleuses were stained with Hoechst 33342. PDLSCs were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min followed by washing the cells three times, five minutes at a time, with serum-free culture medium. PDLSCs were photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX61). The mitochondrial ROS levels were quantified through ImageJ software.

MMPs were determined by tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) staining. After culturing PDLSCs on round coverslips in 12-well plates, PDLSCs were stained with 20 μM TMRE working solution (MedChemExpress). The nucleuses were stained with Hoechst 33342. PDLSCs were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min followed by washing the cells twice, five minutes at a time, with serum-free culture medium. PDLSCs were then captured and the MMPs were quantified same as above.

Mitochondrial respiration detection

Mitochondrial respiration detection was performed on Seahorse XFe24 Flux Analyzer with stress test kits (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Mitochondrial respiration capacity was determined by the oxygen consumption rate (OCR). In the “Mitostress” test, oligomycin (1.5 μM), carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (0.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM) were used as inhibitors of electron transport chain components by which mitochondrial and non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production were detected [39].

Mitochondria morphology observation

PDLSCs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for about 2 h and washed 3 times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). PDLSCs were fixed with 1% osmic acid at 4 °C for 2 h and subsequently washed with PBS for 3 times. Then, the cells were dehydrated by 50%, 70%, 90% ethanol respectively for 10 min and dehydrated by 100% ethanol for 15 min twice. Subsequently, the displacement and soaking were performed with propylene oxide and resin at room temperature, followed by embedding the cells. Semithin sections (1 μm) were cut and stained by methylene blue. Then, ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Mitochondria were observed and photographed by a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan).

Animal experiments

Protocols and procedures of animal studies were approved by the Animal Use and Care Committee, Ethics Committee of Peking University (PUIRB-LA2019315). Animals were given the maximum welfare and care in the whole process. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Subcutaneous transplantation to the dorsum of nude mice

Ten BALB/C nude mice (8-week-old, female) were used in this experiment. Sterile cryovials containing cell suspension with 2.0 × 106 cells and 20 mg of hydroxyapatite/beta-tricalcium phosphate (HA/TCP) scaffold material were shaken gently at 37 °C for 1 h, and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 6 min. The supernatant was discarded, and cell-scaffold complex was collected. In a specific pathogen free animal operating room, nude mice were firstly anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium. After making a longitudinal incision of 2 cm along the midline of the dorsum and separating bluntly on both sides of the subcutaneous tissue, Consh/FAM96B-depleted or Vector/FOSL1-overexpressed PDLSCs with HA-TCP were transplanted separately and the incisions were sutured. After euthanasia by intraperitoneal injection excessive pentobarbital sodium, samples were collected after 8 weeks of transplantation.

Rat calvarial defect model establishment and cell transplantation

Ten Sprague–Dawley rats (8-week-old, female) were used in this experiment. For the Vector/FOSL1-overexpressed PDLSC sheets induction, after seeding 2.0 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates, a complete medium with 20 μg/mL of vitamin C (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for 10 days. The transplantation procedure was performed under anesthesia by 3% pentobarbital sodium intraperitoneal injection. A ring drill (4 mm in diameter) was used to drill and generate calvarial defects on both sides of the center of the parietal area and the bone pieces were completely removed. Then, sizable Vector/FOSL1-overexpressed PDLSC sheets were transplanted to both sides separately and the incisions were sutured. After euthanasia by intraperitoneal injection excessive pentobarbital sodium, samples were collected after 12 weeks of transplantation.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

The harvested samples of rat calvaria were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and were scanned by Skyscan 1174 micro-CT system (Bruker, Belgium). A three-dimensional structure of each calvaria was established using CTvox software.

Histological examination

After fixing the samples from the dorsum of nude mice and rat calvaria samples, decalcification and dehydration were conducted. The samples were embedded in paraffin blocks and sections with 5 μm thick were cut. Then, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Masson staining were conducted following the manufacturer’s (Solarbio, Beijing, China) protocols. For immunofluorescence staining, after the deparaffinization, hydration, antigen retrieval, incubation in endogenous peroxidase inhibitor, and block with 5% goat serum, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. OCN (Cat No. A20800, Abclonal), bone sialoprotein (BSP; Cat No. bs-4729R, Bioss), and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2; Cat No. bs-1134R, Bioss) were applied. After rinsing three times with PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594 (Cat No. A21207, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 1 h. The nucleuses were stained with DAPI. Slides were captured under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX61). The data were quantified by ImageJ analysis software.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS version 22 software was used to analyze data. Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. P ≤ 0.05 was set statistically significant. A minimum of three independent experiments were conducted to acquire the results.

Results

After 5-day osteogenic induction, FAM96B-depleted PDLSCs showed higher ALP activity compared with that in the Consh group (Fig. 1B). In addition, the mineralization of PDLSCs was enhanced by FAM96B depletion after 2-week osteogenic induction which was demonstrated by the Alizarin red staining and calcium quantitative assessment (Fig. 1C, D). Then, PDLSCs were transfected with FAM96B-overexpressed lentivirus and an empty vector. The overexpression efficiency of FAM96B was confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 1H, I). On day 5 of osteogenic induction, the ALP activity was lower in the FAM96B overexpression group than that in the control group (Vector group) (Fig. 1J). Then, the FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs had a lower mineralization level after 2-week osteogenic induction (Fig. 1K, L). Furthermore, the expression of OCN was significantly downregulated after 1-week induction in FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs (Fig. 1M–O). Hence, FAM96B inhibited the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs.

To evaluate the effect of FAM96B on the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in vivo, experiment of subcutaneous transplantation Consh/FAM96B-depleted PDLSCs with HA-TCP to the dorsum of nude mice was conducted. HE staining showed that more new bone was formed in the FAM96B-depleted group compared to that in the Consh group (Fig. 1P, Q). Masson staining also confirmed that new bone area was larger in the FAM96B-depleted group (Fig. 1R, S). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the transplanted area with FAM96B-depleted PDLSCs expressed higher level of OCN than the Consh area (Fig. 1T, U).

Differential gene expression and GO and pathway analysis in FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs

The differentially expressed mRNAs and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) in FAM96B-overexpressed PDLSCs were detected. In all, we discovered 285 differentially expressed mRNAs, among which 84 were upregulated and 201 were downregulated (Table S2). The heatmap was shown in Fig. 2B. Meanwhile, we also detected 12 differentially expressed lncRNAs, among which 2 were upregulated and 10 were downregulated (Table S3). To determine the critical mechanisms by which FMA96B regulated the phenotypes of PDLSCs, GO and pathway analyses were carried out. GO analysis was performed from aspects including biological process, molecular function, and cellular component (Figure S1). In the biological process analysis, 182 upregulated and 623 downregulated GO terms were obtained (P < 0.05) (Table S4). The negative logarithm of the P value (–LgP) represented the relevance between biological processes and gene expression. The bubble diagram showed GO terms which might be related to FAM96B functions (Fig. 2C). GO terms including osteoblast proliferation, negative regulation of hydrogen peroxide metabolic process, ferrous iron transport, and positive regulation of mitochondrial DNA metabolic process were downregulated. Also, negative regulation of collagen biosynthetic process, negative regulation of cell development, regulation of fatty acid oxidation, and negative regulation of ATPase activity terms were upregulated. In addition, the significantly changed pathways were detected according to KEGG database. In total, we identified 7 upregulated pathways and 36 downregulated pathways (P < 0.05) (Table S5, Figure S2). The bubble diagram showed the top 20 enriched pathways (Fig. 2D). FAM96B depletion inhibited the ferroptosis of PDLSCs

As the GO analysis indicated that FAM96B overexpression might affect the peroxidation, ferrous iron transport, and mitochondrial metabolism, it was speculated that FAM96B was associated with the ferroptosis of PDLSCs. To test the hypothesis, firstly, the cytosolic ROS level was investigated by DCFH-DA and it was observed that ROS level was lower in the FAM96B-depleted group than that in the Consh group (Fig. 3A, B). Then, the expression of the key gene involved in ferroptosis, GPX4, was tested. The Western blot result showed that FAM96B depletion upregulated the GPX4 expression (Fig. 3C). In addition, the mitochondrial dysfunction always appeared with the ferroptosis. The MitoSOX Red staining revealed that the mitochondrial ROS level in the FAM96B-depleted group was lower than that in the Consh group (Fig. 3D, E). The TMRE staining showed that FAM96B-depleted PDLSCs existed a higher MMP than the Consh group (Fig. 3F, G). Discussion

To promote the bone regeneration, it is essential to find out the molecular regulation mechanisms of PDLSCs. In this research, we explored the effects of FAM96B on the osteogenic differentiation. First, FAM96B knockdown promoted the ALP activity, which was an early-stage marker of osteogenesis, and the mineralization, a late-stage marker of osteogenesis. FAM96B knockdown also enhanced the expression of OCN in PDLSCs. OCN, the most non-collagenous protein in the bone and specifically expressed in osteoblasts, is one of the osteogenic markers [40]. Meanwhile, FAM96B-depleted PDLSCs showed a good osteogenic potential in subcutaneous tissues of nude mice. These results indicated that FAM96B knockdown promoted the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in vitro and in vivo. FAM96B always exerted its functions by acting as a binding protein [21, 23, 24], hence we speculated that FAM96B probably activated other key genes or interacted with its interacting proteins which exerted strong osteogenic function to antagonize the effect of FAM96B.

To explore the mechanisms of FAM96B action, the RNA-seq and bioinformatic analysis were conducted. We found that hydrogen peroxide metabolic process, ferrous iron transport, mitochondrial DNA metabolic process, regulation of fatty acid oxidation, and regulation of ATPase activity were affected by FAM96B. Ferroptosis, a newly-discovered programmed cell death, varies from necrosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis, and has the characteristics of ROS accumulation, iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and mitochondria dysfunction [41]. GPX4, a central inhibitor of ferroptosis, plays a master role in blocking ferroptosis. It acted as a key enzyme that decreased peroxidized membrane lipid, and genetic or small-molecule-mediated inhibition of GPX4 could induce ferroptosis in various cell types [28, 42]. We supposed that FAM96B could affect the ferroptosis of PDLSCs and verified it. Firstly, FAM96B enhanced the cytosolic ROS level and inhibited the expression of GPX4. It was reported that GPX4 was localized in both cytoplasm and mitochondria and the inhibition of ferroptosis by mitochondria was also mainly through GPX4 [43]. Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles that play important roles in cellular metabolism, energy production, and cell death regulation in eukaryotic cells. We found that mitochondria functions including ROS clearance, MMP, and mitochondrial respiration were inhibited by FAM96B. Hence, although FAM96B was a component of CIA targeting complex which transferred Fe-S clusters and maintained the mitochondrial iron homeostasis, FAM96B was verified to promote the ferroptosis in this study. It was supposed that FAM96B acted independently or as a member of protein complexes, exerting different functions.

Further, among the differentially expressed genes from the RNA-seq data, FOSL1 was selected to be one of the target genes of FAM96B due to its known participation in cell metabolism and ferroptosis. FOSL1, a subunit of the transcriptional complex AP1, contributed to the bone and lipid metabolism regulations in cells by binding to the DNA or interacting with other transcription factors [44]. FAM96B overexpression downregulated the FOSL1 expression and FOSL1 promoted the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in our study. Previous studies have confirmed that FOSL1 is crucial for bone mineralization which is consistent with our findings. Conventional FOSL1 knockout caused embryonic lethality and FOSL1 knockout in embryonic cells caused decreased bone formation after birth [45]. One research indicated that inhibition of FOSL1 could reduce the osteogenic differentiation and vascular calcification of vascular smooth muscle cells [46]. Meanwhile, in vivo experiments in this study including transplantation FOSL1-overexpressed PDLSCs into the dorsum of nude mice and rat calvarial defects were performed to confirm the bone repair potential of FOSL1. Hence, it was speculated that FAM96B knockdown promoted the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs by upregulating FOSL1; however, the directed regulating relationship between FAM96B and FOSL1 needs to be further validated.

In this study, it was also verified that FOSL1 inhibited the ferroptosis of PDLSCs. After stimulating FOSL1-overexpressed PDLSCs with erastin, the upregulated GPX4 by FOSL1 was downregulated. Iron-dependent signallings and ROS were required for erastin-induced ferroptosis and one of the molecular targets of erastin was the mitochondrial voltage-dependent anion channel. Induction of ferroptosis in cells using erastin resulted in the reduced mitochondrial size and reduced mitochondrial ridges compared to normal cells [47]. Our study showed that FOSL1 could maintain the normal morphology of mitochondria; however, FOSL1 knockdown or erastin induction made the mitochondria small and short of ridges. This study suggested the positive role of FOSL1 in bone regeneration might be partly through inhibiting ferroptosis, but the mechanism needs to be further explored.

Conclusions

This study revealed that FAM96B inhibition promoted the osteogenic differentiation and suppressed the ferroptosis of PDLSCs. Meanwhile, FAM96B regulated the ROS level and mitochondria functions, both of them playing key roles in the ferroptosis. The downstream gene FOSL1, negatively regulated by FAM96B, enhanced the osteogenesis of PDLSCs through the ferroptosis. Therefore, targeting the FAM96B-FOSL1 axis offered a promising potential for developing new therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting bone regeneration through dental MSCs.
