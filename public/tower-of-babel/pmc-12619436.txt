Global, regional and national burden of lip and oral cavity cancer from 1990 to 2021 and projections to 2036
Authors: Xuan Liu, Bing Wang, Zhengyu Zhang, Hui Han, Zhaolu Liu, Suyun Li, Lianlong Yu, Panpan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the global trends in the incidence, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of lip and oral cavity cancer from 1990 to 2021, analyze regional, age and gender differences in lip and oral cavity cancer across the world, and predict future trends.MethodsThe data were all from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, calculating the global incidence, mortality and DALY rates of lip and oral cavity cancer per 100,000 population. The estimated annual percent change (EAPC) was calculated and a Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) analysis was conducted. The fitting curves of the disease burden indicators and the socio-demographic index (SDI) were analyzed.ResultsGlobally, incident cases of lip and oral cavity cancer in 2021 were 421,577 (95% UI, 389,878.79–449,782.06), number of deaths was 208,379 (95% UI, 191,287.97–224,162.08) and number of DALY was 5,874,070 (95% UI, 5,326,986.06-6,347,557.28). From 1990 to 2021, the incidence increased by 63.68%, the mortality rate increased by 44.6%, and the DALY rate increased by 35.21%. Among the five SDI regions, the incidence, mortality, and DALY rate in middle SDI regions have seen the largest increases, with EAPC of 2.83% (95% CI, 2.69–2.97), 1.99% (95% CI, 1.9–2.07), and 1. 71% (95%CI, 1.64–1.78). From the perspective of 21 regions, the incidence of lip and oral cavity cancer was highest in Australasia in 2021 (11.21 per 100,000; 95%UI, 9.97–12.4). At the national level, Palau has the highest incidence, mortality, and DALY rate globally (32.32 per 100,000; 95%UI, 24.64–41.33, 17.62 per 100,000; 95%UI, 13.37–22.83, 558.99 per 100,000; 95%UI, 420.48-735.74). BAPC forecasts a global rise in age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) for this cancer in the coming years.ConclusionFrom 1990 to 2021, global rates of lip and oral cavity cancer incidence, mortality, and DALY rates increased significantly, with particularly notable rises in low-middle and middle SDI regions. Introduction

Lip and oral cavity cancer have become increasingly serious public health issues globally, with a disproportionately rising disease burden observed in low-middle and middle-income countries. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study indicates that incidence of lip and oral cavity cancer have been consistently increasing between 1990 and 2017, with significant changes [1], especially in the loss of healthy life years and impacts on quality of life [2]. Lip and oral cavity cancer primarily exhibits pathological features of squamous cell carcinoma, marked by abnormal epithelial overgrowth and malignant invasion of the mucosal tissue. Smoking, high alcohol use, tobacco chewing and areca nut are known major risk factors, which significantly increase the risk of cancer [3]– [4]. Among these, smoking is the most frequent risk factor, evidence suggests that the risk of oral cancer is substantially higher for smokers than for non-smokers; whereas high alcohol use, especially when combined with smoking, significantly increases the risk of cancer [4]; in some regions, behaviors such as tobacco chewing and areca nut use are also considered important causes of oral cancer, for instance South and Southeast Asia [5–7]. The quality of life is greatly affected by lip and oral cavity cancer. Patients often face dual physical and psychological suffering during treatment, leading to social barriers and decreased quality of life [8]– [9]. Therefore, early diagnosis and prevention of this cancer are crucial.

In global epidemiological studies, the association between socio-demographic index and disease burden is a significant topic. Although some research has revealed associations between socioeconomic factors and oral health, comprehensive global analyses covering the long span from 1990 to 2021 remain relatively scarce [10]. Additionally, income inequality is closely linked to periodontal disease incidence in developed countries, highlighting the importance of relative income in disease burden [11]. Other studies have indicated that psychosocial factors might hold a pivotal position in oral health disparities [12].

There is currently a lack of comprehensive analysis regarding the global burden of lip and oral cavity cancer. Therefore, this study aims to deeply explore its global disease burden in different regions, while considering the influence of age and gender, and analyzing the impact of socioeconomic factors on oral health. Our goal is to offer crucial insights for developing more effective public health strategies, thereby alleviating the disease burden.

Methods

Overview and methodological details

The GBD dataset is widely acknowledged as one of the most thorough and organized epidemiological research resources globally. Led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, its primary goal is to measure health losses induced by various diseases, injuries, and risk factors [13]. In GBD analysis, three core metrics are used to measure the disease burden: incidence, mortality rate, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). DALY is computed as the total of years of life lost (YLL) and years lost due to disability (YLD). The specific formulas are as follows:

The disability weight ranges from 0 (representing full health) to 1 (representing death), assigned according to expert consensus. This all-encompassing approach provides a scientific comprehension of the global burden of disease and injury. The research obtained and analyzed data on lip and oral cavity cancer cases, incidence rate, mortality rate, and DALY rate across all age groups from 1990 to 2021. For our analysis, the GBD data were downloaded on July 3, 2024. This cross-sectional study involved time series and cross-regional analyses, without including any personal identifying information. Therefore, the Shandong Center for Disease Control and Prevention Ethics Committee approved the exemption of informed consent. The study strictly adhered to the guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology [14] (STROBE).

Demographic indicators

The Socio-demographic Index (SDI) is a comprehensive indicator measuring the socioeconomic development of countries or regions [15]. It encompasses multiple factors, including but not limited to economic structure, education level, social security, and social welfare. The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with greater values signifying better socioeconomic development. Based on the GBD database, countries and regions are divided into five SDI categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high. Meanwhile, considering both epidemiological similarity and geographical proximity, the GBD study also divides the world into 21 geographical regions. These categorizations aid in studying the relationship between geographical differences and social economy.

Statistical analysis

Using the data from the GBD database, we calculated the incidence rate, mortality rate and DALY rate per 100,000 population and their 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). Estimated annual percent changes (EAPC) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using a linear regression model with logarithmic transformation to assess the temporal trends of lip and oral cavity cancer incidence, mortality, and DALY rates from 1990 to 2021 [16](The UI is used to quantify the uncertainty associated with disease burden indicators, encompasses multi-source uncertainties, and adopts GBD-standardized methods (with 95% intervals) to support the comparison of disease burdens across regions; the CI is exclusively used to quantify the uncertainty of the EAPC, utilizes sampling theory and log-transformed regression methods (reflecting only sampling error), and serves to assess the significance of the EAPC while verifying the reliability of disease burden trends). If the EAPC value and its 95% CI lower limit are above 0, it indicates a rising trend in the indicator. In contrast, if the EAPC value and its 95% CI upper limit are below 0, it indicates a falling trend. Fitted curves were used to analyze the association between disease burden metrics and SDI. Future trends between 2021 and 2036 were forecasted using Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) analysis. The primary reason for this 15-year prediction to 2036 is that in long-term forecasts, the uncertainties around population structure and medical progress will rise significantly. Furthermore, a 15-year period ensures the feasibility of the forecast and provides valuable medium-term trends. All analyses in this study were conducted using R version 4.3.3, with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05.

The role of funding sources

The funding agency played no part in the design of the study, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation or report writing. All authors have access to all data related to this study and are responsible for the submission of manuscripts.

Results

Lip and oral cavity cancer: global burden trends

Incidence

The analysis shows that the global incidence trend of lip and oral cavity cancer exhibits a significant upward dynamic change (Fig. 1A). In 1990, the number of cases of lip and oral cavity cancer worldwide were 174,077 (95% UI, 167,404.23–181,621.61), which increased to 421,577 by 2021 (95% UI, 389,878.79–449,782.06), representing a total increase of 142.18%. Similarly, the incidence rate increased from 3.26 (95% UI, 3.14–3.41) per 100,000 in 1990 to 5.34 (95% UI, 4.94–5.7) per 100,000 in 2021, with a total increase of 63.68%. The EAPC was 1.6% (95% CI, 1.5–1.7) (Table 1; Figs. 2A-C). Regarding age and gender differences, the incidence is concentrated in individuals over 60 years old and gradually increases with age, peaking in the 90–94 age group (38.98 per 100,000; 95% UI, 30.89–43.34). The incidence rate was consistently greater in males than in females (Fig. 3A).

Mortality

The global mortality rate for lip and oral cavity cancer has also shown an upward trend (Fig. 1B). In 1990, the number of deaths from lip and oral cavity cancer worldwide was 97,402 (95% UI, 92,506.37–102,360.99), while in 2021, it increased to 208,379 (95% UI, 191,287.97–224,162.08), representing a total increase of 113.94%. Similarly, the mortality rate increased from 1.83 (95% UI, 1.73–1.92) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 2.64 (95% UI, 2.42–2.84) per 100,000 people in 2021, a growth of 44.6%. The EAPC was 1.15% (95% CI, 1.07–1.24) (Table S1). Regarding age and gender differences, in 2021, the death rate consistently increased with age, peaking in those aged 95+ (37.14 per 100,000; 95% UI, 27.09–42.46), and male mortality rate has consistently been higher than that of females (Fig. 3B).

DALYs

The DALY rate for this cancer has also broadly exhibited an upward trend (Fig. 1C). The DALY rate rose from 55.05 (95% UI, 52.38–57.97) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 74.44 (95% UI, 67.5–80.44.5.44) per 100,000 population in 2021, an increase of 35.21%. The EAPC was 0.93% (95% CI, 0.86–1.86) (Table S2). Regarding age and gender differences, in 2021, the female DALY rate was the highest among those over 95 years old (288.9/100,000), but for males, it peaked at 375.18 (95%UI, 330.47–411.97.47.97) per 100,000 in the 65–69 age group, and the DALY rate in males has always been higher than that of females (Fig. 3C).

Lip and oral cavity cancer: SDI regional trends

Between 1990 and 2021, the incidence, mortality, and DALY rates of lip and oral cavity cancer in five SDI regions all showed an upward trend (Figs. 1A and 4A). During this period, the middle SDI regions saw the highest increase in both number of cases and incidence rate, with the increases reaching 235.06% and 135.76%, respectively, and its EAPC was 2.83% (95% CI, 2.69–2.97). This region was followed by the low-middle SDI, high-middle SDI and high SDI regions in sequence in terms of the increase in EAPC. The EAPC in the low SDI region was the lowest, at only 0.36% (95% CI, 0.19–0.52) (Table 1; Figs. 1A and 4A). The middle SDI region also saw the largest increase in both death numbers and mortality rate, the EAPC was 1.99% (95% CI, 1.9–2.07), followed by low-middle SDI, high-middle SDI, high SDI and low SDI regions in sequence. The low SDI was only 0.04% (95% CI, −0.08–0.17) (Table S1; Figs. 1B and 4B). Similarly, The DALY rate increased the most in the middle SDI regions (69.03%), where the EAPC was 1.71% (95% CI, 1.64–1.78) (Table S2; Figs. 1C and 4C).

Lip and oral cavity cancer: 21 regional trends

Incidence

In 2021, South Asia led the world in lip and oral cavity cancer cases (152,646 cases; 95% UI, 131,077.13–170,703.14), while the highest incidence rate was observed in Australasia (11.21 per 100,000; 95% UI, 9.97–12.4). From 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate rose the most in East Asia (227.72%) with an EAPC of 4.26% (95% CI, 4.06–4.45) (Table 1). Analysis using the SDI revealed that the incidence in all regions showed an overall upward trend, increasing with higher SDI values (r = 0.794, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A).

Mortality

In 2021, South Asia had the highest number of deaths from lip and oral cavity cancer globally (97,154 cases; 95% UI, 84,355.1–108,404.61) and also the highest mortality rate (5.26 per 100,000; 95% UI, 4.57–5.87). From 1990 to 2021, the mortality rate in high-income Asia Pacific region increased the most (179.87%), the EAPC was (3.05%; 95% CI, 2.7–3.39) (Table S1). SDI analysis shows that the overall trend in mortality rates in 21 regions featured an initial increase followed by a decrease (r = 0.6171, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). In 2021, smoking was the primary risk factor for lip and oral cavity cancer death in East Asia, with a population mortality risk factor attribution ratio of 42%, followed by Eastern Europe and Central Europe (accounting for 36.2%, 33.9%); in South Asia, the risk attributable to chewing tobacco had the highest proportion (35.7%), significantly higher than other regions; in Central Europe, high alcohol use was the leading risk factor (37.8%) (Fig. 5).

DALYs

In 2021, the DALY rate was highest in South Asia (182.29 per 100,000; 95% UI, 157.06–203.68.06.68). From 1990 to 2021, High-income Asia Pacific region experienced the largest rise in the DALY rate (89.12%)(Table S2). SDI analysis shows that the DALY rate in 21 regions also rose first and then declined with the increase of SDI (r = 0.5275, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4C).

In 2021, India topped the world in cases of lip and oral cavity cancer worldwide (112,447 cases; 95% UI, 96,269.19–126,068.81). Palau had the highest incidence, mortality, and DALY rate globally (32.32 per 100,000; 95% UI, 24.64–41.33), (17.62 per 100,000; 95% UI, 13.37–22.83), (558.99 per 100,000; 95% UI, 420.48–735.74.48.74) (Figs. 2A-B). From 1990 to 2021, the incidence in Cape Verde increased the most (EAPC = 10.52%; 95% CI, 8.23–12.85) (Fig. 2C). SDI analysis shows that the overall trend of incidence in 204 countries was upward in 2021 (r = 0.7075, p < 0.001), while mortality (r = 0.5537, p < 0.001) and DALY rate (r = 4825, p < 0.001) initially increased, peaked when the SDI was approximately 0.8, and then declined (Fig. 6).

Projections based on the bayesian age-period-cohort model

The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of lip and oral cavity cancer in the global population is expected to rise from 4.88 (95% UI, 4.87–4.89) per 100,000 in 2021 to 5.09 (95% UI, 4.34–5.84) per 100,000 in 2036, representing a 4.3% growth rate. The age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) will increase slightly from 67.71 (95% UI, 67.68–67.74) per 100,000 in 2021 to 68.36 (95% UI, 58.82–77.9) per 100,000 in 2036, representing a 0.96% growth rate. The age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) is projected to decrease from 2.42 (95% UI, 2.41–2.43) per 100,000 to 2.35 (95% UI, 2.02–2.68) per 100,000, with a growth rate of −2.89% (Figure S1).

Discussion

This research illustrates the evolving trends of the global burden of lip and oral cavity cancer from 1990 to 2021. During these thirty years, the incidence, mortality and DALY rates all showed an upward trend. Notably, in middle SDI regions between 1990 and 2021, the growth rates of five key metrics—incident cases, incidence rate, death cases, mortality rate, and DALY rate—were all higher than those in low SDI and high SDI regions. This same trend also applied to the EAPCs of incidence, mortality, and DALY rates, making middle SDI regions bear the most significant disease burden. This discovery emphasizes the significance of this cancer as a global public health issue and highlights the differences among countries of different economic levels.

Age, gender and SDI regional differences

This study further reveals the differences in lip and oral cavity cancer among gender, age and region. Globally, the rates of incidence, mortality, and DALY are notably higher in males than in females. The main reason is that males have much higher exposure to risk factors than females, such as smoking, high alcohol use, chewing tobacco and areca nut, etc. In addition, the incidence of lip and oral cavity cancer increases with age, especially among the elderly, reaching its peak in the 90–94 age group. This discovery indicates that aging is an important factor contributing to the increased burden of this cancer.

Our research indicates that from 1990 to 2021, the growth rate of the incidence and mortality rates of lip and oral cavity cancer, as well as the EAPC of three indicators, in the middle SDI region were the highest among the 5SDI regions. Take India as an example, due to high incidence and limitations in medical resources, the mortality rate for oral cancer has remained high, severely impacting patient survival outcomes [17]. In the low-middle SDI region, the growth rate of incidence and mortality is second only to that in the middle SDI region. In Brazil, which is a high-middle SDI region, the burden of lip and oral cavity cancer has also increased. Reference shows that the mortality rate for most head and neck cancers showed a downward trend between 1990 and 2019, but the mortality rate for nasopharyngeal cancer increased in some populations [18]. In 2018, the estimated global mortality rate of lip, oral and salivary gland cancer was 0.5%, with significant differences among countries [19].

It is worth noting that according to the SDI analysis, the mortality rate and DALY rate of lip and oral cavity cancer show a downward trend when the SDI is about 0.8. An SDI of around 0.8 usually indicates highly developed regions, which have advanced equipment and treatment methods and can treat lip and oral cavity cancer efficiently, reducing the risk of death and disability. At the same time, multiple factors such as the shift in the disease spectrum in these regions jointly lead to a subsequent decrease in the proportion of lip and oral cavity cancer in the overall disease burden.

Geographic regional differences

From 1990 to 2017, the ASIR of lip and oral cavity cancer changed. In Australia, the overall trend of lip, oral and pharyngeal cancers shows a bimodal trend. The incidence rate among men increased by 0.9% from 1982 to 1992, and then decreased by 1.6% from 1992 to 2008. The incidence rate among women rose by 2.0% from 1982 to 1997, and then decreased by 2.8% from 1997 to 2008 [20]. Our study has shown that Palau, located in Oceania, exhibits an extreme burden of lip and oral cavity cancer. From 1990 to 2021, its incidence, mortality and DALY rates all ranked first globally. Studies have shown that the proportions of current drinking and marijuana use were higher in Palauan than in other ethnicities [21], and chewing areca nut has also been proven to be a key risk factor [22]. In addition, insufficient medical resources (which lead to limited access to treatment) and the potential genetic susceptibility of the population are multiple factors that collectively increase the disease burden.

The characteristics of incidence and mortality for lip and oral cavity cancer vary across the Asian region. The East Asia region has the highest EAPC in the incidence rate of lip and oral cavity cancer, while the high-income Asia Pacific region has the highest EAPC in the mortality rate of this cancer. For instance, from 2010 to 2019, lip and oral cavity cancer constituted a significant proportion of all cancer cases in Karachi, Pakistan, with males making up 67.8% and females 32.1%, and squamous cell carcinoma accounting for 97.7% [23]. The incidence of lip and oral cavity cancer is the highest in both males and females compared to any Asian population [24]. In Singapore, between 1968 and 2017, the ASIR and ASMR of oral cancer generally showed a downward trend. The ASIR for oral cancer decreased from 3.07 in 1968–1972 to 2.01 in 2008–2012, and the ASMR dropped from 2.06 in 1978–1982 to 1.21 in 2013-2017 [25].

There are significant differences in the epidemiologic trends of lip and oral cavity cancer in different regions of Europe. Research results show that the incidence rate in Western Europe has consistently been higher than that in Central and Eastern Europe from 1990 to 2021. As a high-income region, it is speculated that the possible reasons for Western Europe are long-term exposure to traditional risk factors such as smoking and drinking, as well as strong medical diagnostic capabilities and a high case capture rate. Similar to the results of a study in France: the combined consumption of tobacco and alcohol is a key factor contributing to the burden of oral cancer, with a population attributable risk reaching 69.9%, higher among males than females [26]. In Belgium, the mortality rate of this cancer in different regions shows certain geographical distribution characteristics. For example, the mortality rates are relatively high in parts of Wallonia and Brussels [27]. Overall trends show that in some countries, like Estonia, lip and laryngeal cancer rates are decreasing, whereas oropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal cancer rates are on the rise [28].

Regional differences in risk factors

Lip and oral cavity cancer incidence is closely linked to risk factors like smoking, high alcohol use, chewing tobacco and areca nut. The research results show that in East Asia and Eastern Europe, smoking represents a dominant risk factor; in South Asia, chewing smokeless tobacco is more common. Areca nut use is not included in the GBD data, although it is a known risk factor. In some countries, such as in South Asia and Southeast Asia, areca nut is often used with smokeless tobacco [29]. This difference indicates that the lip and oral cavity cancer incidence is not only influenced by traditional behavioral habits, but also by local culture and socio-economic factors.

Tobacco and high alcohol use are among the primary risk factors for lip and oral cavity cancer. Studies have shown that carcinogens in tobacco, like nitrosamines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, form DNA adducts in oral cells, which may lead to squamous cell carcinoma of the mouth and head and neck [30]. Additionally, the combined effect of smoking and drinking is considered to have a significant additive interaction with the risk of esophageal cancer, that may also apply to oral cancer [31]. Within a major case-control research in France, smoking and high alcohol use have been recognized as key risk factors for oral cancer. The risk attributable to smoking was 78.6%, compared to 7.3% for alcohol, and for both smoking and high alcohol use reaching up to 80.7% [32]. In a study in Italy, diet-related inflammation was also identified as a significant risk factor for laryngeal cancer, particularly among smokers and drinkers, further reinforces the link between tobacco, high alcohol use and oral cancer [33]. So, reducing or stopping tobacco and high alcohol use can significantly lower the risk of oral cancer [34]– [35].

Future trend projections

The predictive results show that from 2021 to 2036, the ASIR and ASDR of lip and oral cavity cancer will increase globally. The possible reasons for this are the future population growth, the intensification of aging and the continuous exposure to risk factors, which will lead to an increase in new cases. The increase in ASIR leads to an increase in the number of patients, and the increase in ASDR may be caused by long-term disability of survivors due to the disease. The decline in ASMR may be related to the advancement of medical technology. Nevertheless, early identification of high-risk individuals remains essential, as it facilitates timely intervention and reduces the disease burden of this cancer. Additionally, it is crucial to interpret these predictive results with caution, as the measures taken may extend life expectancy, potentially exacerbating population aging and increasing disease burden.

Early diagnosis technology

Early diagnosis of lip and oral cavity cancer is crucial for improving the prognosis of patients, and significant progress has been made in related technologies in recent years. Emerging non-invasive imaging techniques, such as tissue autofluorescence technology, assist in diagnosis by detecting fluorescence changes in oral tissues. It can produce positive results in 96.4% of the suspected cases, but its specificity is relatively low [36]. Narrow-band imaging, high-frequency ultrasound and optical coherence tomography also show promise, providing clearer views of tissue morphology and structure, thus enhancing the accuracy of early diagnosis. AI-based diagnostic methods are also gaining traction, such as using deep learning algorithms to analyze images of oral cancer, with classification accuracy reaching 96.76% in some studies, offering a new and efficient approach [37].

Prevention and management strategies

Preventive measures and public health policies are crucial for reducing the burden of diseases. Primary prevention focuses on modifiable factors like reducing tobacco, alcohol and areca nut use. Public awareness has been enhanced through health education activities. Secondary prevention emphasizes early screening and diagnosis, and improves the detection rate through regular dental check-ups and the use of diagnostic aids. The study has shown that early diagnosis of oral diseases is associated with better survival outcomes for patients who participate in screening [38]. In terms of public health policies, governments should strengthen the control of tobacco, alcohol and areca nut and increase investment in oral health care to improve access to dental services, especially in remote and low-income regions, with the aim of better prevent and intervene early.

Clinical and public health impact

From a public health perspective, the prevention and control of lip and oral cavity cancer should be tailored to specific risk factors in each region. In high-risk areas, especially those with low-to-middle SDI, preventive measures should be strengthened, including smoking bans, alcohol restrictions, and regulation of smokeless tobacco and areca nut. For the older population, early screening and diagnosis should be prioritized to improve patient survival rates and life quality. Additionally, there is a pressing requirement for enhanced education and awareness campaigns globally to raise public understanding of the disease and promote reforms in health policies to reduce its burden.

Future research direction

Future research should focus on the specific pathogenic mechanisms of lip and oral cavity cancer, early diagnostic methods, and the evaluation of treatment outcomes. Additionally, as global health challenges evolve, particularly with health inequalities and uneven socioeconomic development, the epidemiological characteristics of lip and oral cavity cancer in different regions still require further investigation. Future studies should also enhance comparisons between different countries and regions to explore more suitable prevention and treatment strategies for local conditions.

Limitations of the research methods and data

This study has certain limitations. Initially, the analyzed data mainly stems from the GBD Database. The accuracy of this database is limited by factors such as the completeness of national registration data, the number of undiagnosed cases of lip and oral cavity cancer, and the lack of data on other related risk factors. So, more in-depth causal analysis of risk factors remains the direction of future research. Secondly, data limitations prevent the exploration of the impact of race and ethnicity on the burden of this cancer. Furthermore, differences in public health systems, medical resource allocation, and disease reporting around the world may cause deviations in data integrity and accuracy.

Conclusion

This study reveals a significant upward trend in the incidence, mortality and DALY rates of global lip and oral cavity cancer, and this trend is closely related to age, gender and regional differences. The disease burden is notably greater in males than in females and increases significantly with age, especially among people over 65 years old. The increase in incidence and mortality rates is more significant in regions with low-middle and middle levels of socio-economic development. Therefore, prevention and early screening for high-risk populations remain key measures to reduce the global burden.
