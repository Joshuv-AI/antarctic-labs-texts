A covariate-adaptive test for replicability across multiple studies with false discovery rate control Thanks: This research was supported by The University of Melbourne’s Research Computing Services and the Petascale Campus Initiative.
Ninh Tran, Dennis Leung

Abstract
. Replicability is a lynchpin for credible discoveries. The partial conjunction (PC) p-value, which combines individual base p-values from multiple similar studies, can gauge whether a feature of interest exhibits replicated signals across studies. However, when a large set of features are examined as in high-throughput experiments, testing for their replicated signals simultaneously can pose a very underpowered problem, due to both the multiplicity burden and inherent limitations of PC p p -values. This power deficiency is markedly severe when replication is demanded for all studies under consideration, which is nonetheless the most natural and appealing benchmark for scientific generalizability a practitioner may request. We propose ParFilter, a general framework that marries the ideas of filtering and covariate-adaptiveness to power up large-scale testing for replicated signals as described above. It reduces the multiplicity burden by partitioning studies into smaller groups and borrowing the cross-group information to filter out unpromising features. Moreover, harnessing side information offered by auxiliary covariates whenever they are available, it can train informative hypothesis weights to encourage rejections of features more likely to exhibit replicated signals. We prove its finite-sample control on the false discovery rate, under both independence and arbitrary dependence among the base p p -values across features. In simulations as well as a real case study on autoimmunity based on RNA-Seq data obtained from thymic cells, the ParFilter has demonstrated competitive performance against other existing methods for such replicability analyses.

Introduction

The replicability crisis
(
Ioannidis,, 2005
)
has raised widespread concern about the credibility of contemporary scientific findings. A recent article of
Nosek et al., (2022)
has recounted how psychologists have been actively confronting this issue within their field over the past decade, as a case in point. To enhance the reliability of and assurance in scientific claims, the statistical community have responded by developing rigorous methods for
replicability analysis
, which aim to identify, in a principled manner, replicable discoveries that are supported by data from
multiple studies
of a similar nature. In an era of open science where experimental data from different research groups become more accessible, these statistical tools have been increasingly employed in different domains, such as immunology
(
Jaljuli et al.,, 2025
)
, natural language processing
(
Dror et al.,, 2017
)
and developmental neuroscience
(
Meltzoff et al.,, 2018
)
. In this paper, we will further contribute a timely method for such analyses of modern high-throughput experimental data.

Consider a typical meta-analytical framework that consists of
n
comparable studies, each examining the same set of
m
features. Adopting the shorthand
[\ell]\equiv\{1,\dots,\ell\}
for any natural number
\ell\in\mathbb{N}\equiv\{1,2,\dots\}
, suppose
\mu_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
is an unknown effect parameter corresponding to feature
i\in[m]
in study
j\in[n]
, and
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a common
null
parameter region across the
n
studies for feature
i
. The effect specific to study
j
is considered a scientifically interesting finding (i.e., a
signal
) if
\mu_{ij}
does not belong to
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Consequently, the query of whether
\mu_{ij}
is a signal can be formulated as testing the null hypothesis

(1.1)

H_{ij}:\mu_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}

against the alternative
\mu_{ij}\not\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
. In this paper, we exemplify the problem with a meta-analysis on thymic gene differential expression in relation to autoimmunity, the details of which will be given in Section
5
.

Example 1.1

(Meta-analysis on differential gene expression in thymic medulla for autoimmune disorder)
.

We consider a meta-analysis on RNA-Seq data obtained from medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTECs) for
m=6,587
genes compiled from
n=3
independent mouse studies. For each
(i,j)\in[6587]\times[3]
,
\mu_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
is the mean difference in expression level of gene
i
between healthy and autoimmune mice in study
j
. If
\mu_{ij}\neq 0
(corresponding to
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{0\}
), then gene
i
in study
j
is implicated as a potential marker for autoimmunity, as its expression differs between healthy and autoimmune mice, on average.

Testing the individual hypotheses
H_{ij}
is certainly central to each study. However, to reinforce the generalizability of scientific findings, one may ask whether a given feature
i
has
replicated
signals across studies. First advocated by
Friston et al., (2005)
,
Benjamini and Heller, (2008)
and
Benjamini et al., (2009)
, the following paradigm addresses the latter problem:
For a given number
u\in[n]
that represents the required
level of replicability
, declare the effect of feature
i
as being
u/[n]

replicated
if at least
u
of
\mu_{i1},\dots,\mu_{in}
are signals. This can be formulated as testing the
partial conjunction
(PC) null hypothesis

(1.2)

H^{u/[n]}_{i}:|\{j\in[n]:\mu_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\}|\geq n-u+1

which states that at least
n-u+1
of the effects
\mu_{i1},\dots,\mu_{in}
belong to the null set
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, or equivalently, no more than
u-1
of them are signals. Setting
u=1
amounts to testing the
global null hypothesis
that
H_{ij}
is true for all
j\in[n]
, an inferential goal pursued in many past meta-analyses, but one that is the least demanding in terms of replicability. At the other end, setting
u=n
, the maximum replicability level, offers the most conceptually appealing yet strictest benchmark for assessing the generalizability of scientifically interesting findings.

Assume the primary data available from the studies allow a statistician to directly access or otherwise construct a
p
-value
p_{ij}
for each
H_{ij}
. For meta-analyses like Example
1.1
, the gene expression data are often available on public repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus
1
1

1

https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/
(GEO) and can be processed into
p
-values using bioinformatics pipelines such as
limma

(
Ritchie et al.,, 2015
)
.
In such large-scale experiments with many features (e.g. the
6,587
genes in Example
1.1
), it is natural to aim for controlling the
false discovery rate
(FDR), i.e. the expected proportion of Type I errors among the discoveries, when simultaneously testing the PC nulls in (
1.2
) for all
i\in[m]
. In the present context, suppose
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq[m]
is a set of rejected PC nulls determined with the available data, the FDR for
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is then defined as

\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}=\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}})\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}}I\{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\}}{1\vee\sum_{i\in[m]}I\{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\}}\right],

where
a\vee b\equiv\max(a,b)
for any real numbers
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and

(1.3)

\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}\equiv\{i\in[m]:H_{i}^{u/[n]}\text{ is true}\}

is the set of all features whose PC null hypotheses are true.

A “vanilla” protocol for controlling the
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
is to apply the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure
(
Benjamini and Hochberg,, 1995
)
to a set of
m

p
-values for assessing the PC nulls, also commonly referred to as partial conjunction (PC)
p
-values. In the literature, a PC
p
-value for testing
H_{i}^{{}_{u/[n]}}
, denoted as
p^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{i}
, is typically constructed by combining the base
p
-values
p_{i1},\dots,p_{in}
following the formulation proposed by
Benjamini and Heller, (2008)
and later extended by
Wang and Owen, (2019)
. Although seemingly natural, this vanilla approach is known to have low power; this is not only due to the multiplicity correction required for the FDR control, but also due to the inherent limitations of PC p-values. In Section
2.1
, we will re-examine this issue with a particular focus on the maximum replicability level
u=n
, which represents the most natural benchmark for replicability of discoveries a typical practitioner may wish for, but unfortunately also poses the most power-deficient testing problem.

The ParFilter is our proposed method to powerfully test for replicated signals in the meta-analytical setting above with strong (finite-sample)
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control guarantees. In a nutshell, as the name suggests, it operates by first
par
titioning the
n
studies into different groups and borrowing the between-group information to
filter
out features that are unlikely to be
u/[n]
replicable; this reduces the multiplicity correction required and set the scene for high testing power. Moreover, it can incorporate useful
side information
provided by auxiliary covariates, on top of the
p
-values, to decide on the features that are
u/[n]
replicable. Specifically, for each study
j
, the statistician may optionally have access to a covariate
x_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
that is also informative for testing
H_{ij}
. For instance, in Example
1.1
,
x_{ij}
can be taken as the differential expression of gene
i
in cells from a different part of the thymus other than the medulla, such as the cortex (Section
5
).
This reflects the recent trend in the multiple testing literature of integrating side information to enhance power.

Therefore, the ParFilter seamlessly marries two lines of current research – replicability analysis and covariate-adaptive inference, and we highlight our contributions in context:

(i)

The ParFilter substantially generalizes the two
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
controlling procedures of
Bogomolov and Heller, (2018, Sec. 3.2 & 4.2)
, which were only designed for assessing
2/[2]
replicability using the filtering strategy. Precisely, in the special case where
n=u=2
and covariates are unavailable, our ParFilter boils down to them under particular tuning parameter settings; see Section
3.5
. Their powerful procedures provide finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control guarantees under standard assumptions
(
Bogomolov and Heller,, 2018
, Theorems 2 & 4)
, and can therefore be considered as gold standards to be pursued for further generalization.

Nonetheless, the original proofs for
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control in
Bogomolov and Heller, (2018)
involve bounding sums of probabilities over intricate intersecting events, which appears too difficult to be directly extended for more than two studies. As a technical innovation, we take a different route by harnessing techniques inspired by the recent developments in
Ramdas et al., (2019)
and
Katsevich et al., (2023)
for “dependency control”
(
Blanchard and Roquain,, 2008
)
, and provide relatively transparent proofs of ParFilter’s finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
guarantees (Section
3.6
) for arbitrary
u\leq n
.

(ii)

To our best knowledge, the ParFilter is the only covariate-adaptive procedure specialized for
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
controlling multi-study replicability analysis thus far. Due to their useful side information offered for testing, auxiliary covariates have been incorporated into multiple testing within a single study for some time. The related literature encompasses Type I error rates beyond the FDR and resists a succinct summary; we refer to
Lei and Fithian, (2018)
,
Ignatiadis and Huber, (2021)
,
Leung and Sun, (2022)
,
Ren and Candès, (2023)
,
Freestone et al., (2024)
for examples of covariate-adaptive testing procedures with finite-sample FDR guarantees that are most relevant to our present work.

To boost testing power while ensuring its finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
guarantees, the ParFilter trains informative hypothesis weights, which can encourage rejections of features more likely to contain replicated signals, from the covariate-augmented data
in a manner that naturally respects the partitioning structure of the studies in the filtering step (Section
3.4
). This novel approach shares a similar spirit as the “cross-weighting” approach in the single-study FDR work of
Ignatiadis and Huber, (2021)
, where hypothesis weights are cross-trained on randomly or manually defined
p
-value folds alongside the covariates.

We also compare with two existing methods that employ the general idea of filtering in different ways for power enhancement in
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
controlling testing. The AdaFilter-BH procedure of
Wang et al., (2022, Section 4.2)
first filters out features that are unlikely to exhibit
(u-1)/[n]
replicability and then computes a final rejection set for
u/[n]
replicability from the remaining features. While being powerful, since its rejection set is derived from the same
p
-values used for filtering, it only offers asymptotic
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control under limiting assumptions. On the other hand, the CoFilter of
Dickhaus et al., (2024)
, like the ParFilter, offers finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control for an arbitrary number of studies
n
. It forms a selection set
\mathcal{S}=\{i\in[m]:p^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{i}\leq\tau\}
using a pre-determined threshold
\tau\in(0,1)
, and then runs a BH procedure on the
selection-adjusted
PC
p
-values in
\{p^{u/[n]}_{i}/\tau:i\in\mathcal{S}\}
. While its power benefits from filtering, the gain is limited because selection-adjusted PC
p
-values are more conservative than ordinary PC
p
-values. Further comparisons will be explored in our simulation studies (Section
4
).

Other inferential methods for partial conjunction hypotheses not already discussed include those of
Bogomolov, (2023, Sec. 5.3)
and
Benjamini et al., (2009, Sec. 4(b))
, which provide lower bounds on the number of studies in which feature
i
shows an effect, while controlling the false coverage rate
(
Benjamini and and,, 2005
)
. The methods proposed in
Chung et al., (2014)
and
Heller et al., (2014)
provide replicability inferences specifically under the popular two-group modeling assumption of
Efron et al., (2001)
.
Li et al., (2021)
developed a method — building on the model-X framework of
Candès et al., (2018)
— to assess replicability in a regression setting, where the goal is to determine whether explanatory variables are consistently associated with a response variable across
n
sampled environments.
Sun et al., (2015, Sec. 3.2 & 4.2)
analyzed spatial signal clusters by formulating them as partial conjunction testing problems. These references are certainly not exhaustive, and we refer to
Bogomolov and Heller, (2023)
for a comprehensive review.

Our paper is structured as follows: Section
2
reviews PC
p
-values and re-examines their power limitations. Section
3
provides the layout of the ParFilter, outlining its implementation and finite-sample FDR guarantees (Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
). Section
4
demonstrates the competitiveness of the ParFilter against existing
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
procedures using simulated data. Section
5
explores the ParFilter’s performance in the replicability analysis of the real data from Example
1.1
. Finally, Section
6
concludes with a discussion on potential extensions. An R package implementing the ParFilter is available at
https://github.com/ninhtran02/Parfilter
.

2.
Multiple testing with PC
p
-values

We first review some fundamentals of partial conjunction testing. Consider a generic context where
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
are
n
parameters of interest and
\Theta_{j}
is a set of null values for
\theta_{j}
for each
j\in[n]
. Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in[0,1]
be the base
p
-values for testing the null hypothesis
\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}
,
\dots
,
\theta_{n}\in\Theta_{n}
respectively, and suppose these
p
-values are
valid
, i.e.,

(2.1)

\Pr(p_{j}\leq t)\leq t\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]\text{ if the null hypothesis }\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}\text{ is true}.

For a given replicability level
u\in[n]
, the partial conjunction (PC) null hypothesis that at least
n-u+1
parameters among
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n}
are null is denoted as

H^{u/[n]}:|\{j\in[n]:\theta_{j}\in\Theta_{j}\}|\geq n-u+1.

In the literature,
H^{u/[n]}
is typically tested by combining the base
p
-values
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
to create a
combined

p
-value that is both
valid
and
monotone
:

Definition 2.1

(Valid and monotone combined
p
-values for testing
H^{u/[n]}
)
.

For a given combining function
f:[0,1]^{n}\to[0,1]
, the combined
p
-value
f(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
is said to be

(a)

valid for testing
H^{u/[n]}
if

\Pr(f(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\leq t)\leq t\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]\text{ under $H^{u/[n]}$, and }

(b)

monotone if
f
is non-decreasing.

The combined
p
-value properties outlined in Definition
2.1
are minimal because validity is required for ensuring Type I error control, and monotonicity is sensible for scientific reasoning; a more nuanced discussion on monotonicity can be found in
Wang and Owen, (2019, Sec 3.2)
. For testing
H^{1/[n]}
, i.e., the
global null hypothesis
that all
\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}\in\Theta_{n}
hold true, perhaps the most well known combining function is

f_{\text{Bonferroni}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\equiv(n\cdot\min(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}))\wedge 1.

In words,
f_{\text{Bonferroni}}
applies a Bonferroni correction to the minimum base
p
-value, and the resulting combined
p
-value is usually a very conservative. More powerful functions for combining
p
-values to test global nulls are available, and a comprehensive list of such functions is provided in
Bogomolov, (2023, Section 2.1)
. Below, we present three common choices:

•

Fisher, (1973)
’s combining function:

f_{\text{Fisher}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\equiv 1-F_{\chi^{2}_{(2n)}}\left(-2\sum_{j\in[n]}\log(p_{j})\right),

where
F_{\chi^{2}_{(2n)}}
is the chi-squared distribution function with
2n
degrees of freedom.

•

Stouffer et al., (1949)
’s combining function:

f_{\text{Stouffer}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\equiv 1-\Phi\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}\Phi^{-1}(1-p_{j})}{\sqrt{n}}\right);

•

Simes, (1986)
’s combining function
:

f_{\text{Simes}}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\equiv\min_{j\in[n]}\frac{np_{(j)}}{j},

where
p_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq p_{(n)}
are the ordered statistics for
p_{1}\leq\dots\leq p_{n}
.

Assuming
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are independent and property (
2.1
) holds, all four combining functions above (i.e., Bonferroni, Fisher, Stouffer, and Simes) give valid combined
p
-values for testing
H^{1/[n]}
. Moreover, these combining functions are all evidently non-decreasing.

To test
H^{u/[n]}
for a general
u>1
, the corresponding combined
p
-value, commonly referred to as a PC
p
-value, is typically derived using the
generalized Benjamini-Heller
combining method
(
Benjamini and Heller,, 2008
,
Wang and Owen,, 2019
)
. The resulting PC
p
-value is known as a
GBHPC
(
g
eneralized
B
enjamini-
H
eller
p
artial
c
onjunction)
p
-value:

Definition 2.2

(GBHPC
p
-value for testing
H^{u/[n]}
)
.

Suppose
u\in[n]
is given.
For each subset
\mathcal{J}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n-u+1}\}\subseteq[n]
of
n-u+1
ordered elements
1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{n-u+1}\leq n
, let

\mathbf{p}_{\mathcal{J}}=(p_{j_{1}},\dots,p_{j_{n-u+1}})\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{J}}=(\theta_{j_{1}},\dots,\theta_{j_{n-u+1}})

be the vectors of
p
-values and parameters corresponding to
\mathcal{J}
. Moreover, let

f_{\mathcal{J}}:[0,1]^{n-u+1}\longrightarrow[0,1]

be a non-decreasing function where
f_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathcal{J}})
is valid for testing

H^{1/\mathcal{J}}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathcal{J}}\in\Theta_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{j_{n-u+1}},

the restricted global null hypothesis that all the base nulls corresponding to
\mathcal{J}
are true. Define the GBHPC
p
-value for testing
H^{u/[n]}
to be

(2.2)

f^{\star}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n};u)\equiv\max\{f_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathcal{J}}):\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n]\text{ such that }|\mathcal{J}|=n-u+1\},

i.e., the largest combined
p
-value
f_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathcal{J}})
for testing the restricted global nulls
H^{1/\mathcal{J}}
among all
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n]
with cardinality
|\mathcal{J}|=n-u+1
.

As a concrete example, if we take each
f_{\mathcal{J}}
in (
2.2
) to be Stouffer’s combining function in
n-u+1
arguments, then

(2.3)

f^{\star}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=f_{\text{Stouffer}}(p_{(u)},\dots,p_{(n)})=1-\Phi\left(\frac{\sum^{n}_{j=u}\Phi^{-1}(1-p_{(j)})}{\sqrt{n-u+1}}\right).

Intuitively, (
2.3
) makes sense as a
p
-value for testing
H^{u/[n]}
because it is a function of the largest
n-u+1
base

-values only. By

, GBHPC

-values (Definition
2.2
) are valid for testing
H^{u/[n]}
(Definition
2.1
(a)
) because every
f_{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathcal{J}})
in (
2.2
) is valid for testing their corresponding restricted global null
H^{1/\mathcal{J}}
; moreover, they are monotone (Definition
2.1
(b)
) because every
f_{\mathcal{J}}
in (
2.2
) is non-decreasing. Under mild conditions,
Wang and Owen, (2019, Theorem 2)
has also demonstrated that any admissible monotone combined

2.2
).

2.1.
The deprived power of partial conjunction test when
u=n

Maximum replicability (
u=n
) is the most natural and appealing requirement for ensuring the generalizability of scientific findings. However, it also posits the most power-deprived partial conjunction testing problem, because the underlying composite null parameter space of
H^{u/[n]}
becomes larger as
u
increases
(
Bogomolov and Heller,, 2023
, Sec. 2.1)
. What may be surprising is how disproportionately underpowered the case of
u=n
can be, relative to other cases with
u<n
, especially when multiple PC nulls
H_{1}^{{}_{u/[n]}},\dots,H_{m}^{{}_{u/[n]}}
are to be tested simultaneously as in Section
1
.

To illustrate, consider a simple scenario with
n=4
studies, where all the base null hypotheses
H_{ij}
are
false
with their corresponding
p
-values generated as

p_{ij}\overset{\tiny\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Beta}(0.26,7)\text{ for each }(i,j)\in[m]\times[4],

which are highly right-skewed and stochastically smaller than a uniform distribution. At a given replicability level
u=1,\dots,4
, we assess
H_{i}^{u/[4]}
by forming a PC
p
-value
p^{{}_{u/[4]}}_{i}
from
p_{i1},\dots,p_{i4}
for each feature
i
, using the Stouffer-GBHPC formulation given in (
2.3
). We then apply the BH procedure to the
m
PC
p
-values at a nominal FDR level of 0.05. For various numbers of hypotheses tested, i.e.,
m\in\{1,10,20,\dots,250\}
, Figure
2.1
shows the power of this standard testing procedure, estimated by the average proportion of rejected PC null hypotheses out of the total
m
over 500 repeated experiments.

For a single test at

, the power is
1.00
,
1.00
,
0.89
, and

u=1,2,3,4
, respectively; the significant drop in power from
u=3
to
u=4
highlights how highly conservative a single PC
p
-value can already be under
u=n
. As
m
increases, the power for testing
4/[4]
replicability further drops rapidly towards
0
, while the power for other replicability levels remains quite stable throughout. This illustrates that, in addition to their poor individual power, PC
p
-values at maximum replicability level
u=n
can be extremely sensitive to standard multiplicity correcting methods such as the BH procedure, and yield power too low to be detected.

In Appendix
D
, we repeat these simulations with
n
varying from 2 to 8, and also examine the power when Fisher’s combining function is used in place of Stouffer’s in the GBHPC
p
-value formulation of
p^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{1},\dots,p^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{m}
. The results
there
exhibit the same pattern: a sharp drop in power when moving from
u=n-1
to
u=n
, and a rapid decline to zero power as
m
increases when
u=n
. A general discussion on the low power of combined
p
-values in partial conjunction hypothesis testing can be found in
Liang et al., (2025, Sec. 1.3)
and the references therein. However, a complete theoretical explanation or lower bound for the sharp drop in power of a GBHPC
p
-value at
u=n
currently eludes us, though we suspect that the results of
Weiss, (1965)
,
Weiss, (1969)
on the distribution of sample spacings
p_{(j+1)}-p_{(j)}
for
j\in[n-1]
may prove useful in developing one.

3.
The ParFilter

In this section, we introduce our ParFilter for simultaneously testing the PC nulls in (
1.2
). In the sequel, we will use

\mathbf{P}\equiv(p_{ij})_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{X}\equiv(x_{ij})_{i\in[m],j\in[n]}

to respectively denote the
m\times n
matrices of
p
-values and covariates for the base hypotheses in (
1.1
), with the understanding that the covariates
x_{ij}
within the same study
j
have a common dimension
d_{j}\geq 1
. If covariates are unavailable for a given study
j
, the
j
-th column of
\mathbf{X}
is taken to contain entries of
0
’s as constants.

We also need to denote submatrices or subvectors of
\mathbf{P}
or
\mathbf{X}
. If we are given
i\in[m]
,
j\in[n]
,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[m]
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n]
as row or column indices, then
(-i)
,
(-j)
,
(-\mathcal{I})
and
(-\mathcal{J})
are shorthands for their complements
[m]\backslash\{i\}
,
[n]\backslash\{j\}
,
[m]\backslash\mathcal{I}
and
[n]\backslash\mathcal{J}
respectively, and “
\cdot
” represents either all the rows or all the columns. For examples,
\mathbf{P}_{i\cdot}\equiv(p_{i1},\cdots,p_{in})
denotes the
i
th row of
\mathbf{P}
as a vector,
\mathbf{P}_{\cdot\mathcal{J}}
denotes the whole matrix
\mathbf{P}
restricted to the columns in
\mathcal{J}
,
\mathbf{P}_{i\mathcal{J}}
denotes the
i
th row of
\mathbf{P}_{\cdot\mathcal{J}}
,
and
\mathbf{P}_{(-\mathcal{I})\mathcal{J}}
denotes
\mathbf{P}_{\cdot\mathcal{J}}
restricted to the rows in
[m]\backslash\mathcal{I}
. Other such submatrices or subvectors should be self-explanatory from their context.

These elementary assumptions will ground our theoretical results throughout:

Assumption 1

(Independence across studies conditional on
{\bf X}
)
.

\mathbf{P}_{\cdot 1},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{\cdot n}
are mutually independent
conditional on
\mathbf{X}
.

Assumption 2

(Valid base
p
-values)
.

Each base
p
-value in
\mathbf{P}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
satisfies

\Pr(p_{ij}\leq t)\leq t\quad\text{for all $\ t\in[0,1]$ whenever $H_{ij}$ is true}.

In other words,
p_{ij}
is stochastically no smaller than a uniform distribution (i.e. superuniform) when
H_{ij}
is true.

Assumption 3

(Independence between
p_{ij}
and
{\bf X}
under the null)
.

Given
H_{ij}
is true, then
p_{ij}
is independent of
{\bf X}
.

When the covariates can be considered as deterministic, such as our case study in Section
5
where the covariates are computed from external datasets, Assumption
1
is natural since meta-analyses typically consist of independently conducted studies. In other occasions, within each study, the covariates are induced from the same data used to compute the base
p
-values and are more appropriately treated as random. An example is
Bottomly et al., (2011)
’s well known dataset in the single-study FDR testing literature
(
Lei and Fithian,, 2018
,
Leung and Sun,, 2022
,
Zhang and Chen,, 2022
,
Ignatiadis et al.,, 2016
)
, where for each gene (a feature), the
p
-value corresponds to its differential expression between two mouse strains and the covariate is the mean normalized read counts across samples. With multiple studies, as long as the
n
covariate-augmented datasets
\{{\bf P}_{\cdot 1},{\bf X}_{\cdot 1}\},\cdots\{{\bf P}_{\cdot n},{\bf X}_{\cdot n}\}
are independent, Assumption
1
is obviously satisfied.

The superuniformity of null
p
-values (Assumption
2
) is a standard starting point for hypothesis testing. In virtually all covariate-adaptive testing literature in the single-study context, it has always been assumed that the covariates do not influence the distribution of a null
p
-value as a “principle for information extraction”
(
Cai et al.,, 2019
)
, and such covariates naturally exist in many applications

(
Ignatiadis et al.,, 2016
,
Boca and Leek,, 2018
)
. Assumption
3
is therefore a multi-study generalization of this principle, and is trivial if
{\bf X}
is deterministic. If
{\bf X}
is random and the covariate-augmented datasets
\{{\bf P}_{\cdot 1},{\bf X}_{\cdot 1}\},\cdots\{{\bf P}_{\cdot n},{\bf X}_{\cdot n}\}
are independent, Assumption
3
can be restated as “
p_{ij}
is independent of
{\bf X}_{\cdot j}
when
H_{ij}
is true
” as in the single-study case; see
Ignatiadis and Huber, (2021, Assumption 1(b) and 2(b))
for instance.

The ParFilter begins by specifying a
testing configuration for
u/[n]
replicability
.

Definition 3.1

(
Testing configuration for
u/[n]
replicability
)
.

For a given number
K\in[u]
, let

\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{K}\subseteq[n]

be disjoint non-empty subsets, referred to as groups, that form a partition of the set
[n]
.
Let
w_{1},\cdots,w_{K}\in(0,1]
be numbers, referred to as local error weights, that sum to one, i.e.,

\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{k}=1.

For each feature
i\in[m]
, let
\mathbf{u}_{i}\equiv(u_{i1},\dots,u_{iK})
be a vector of
K
natural numbers, referred to as local replicability levels, that satisfies

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{ik}\in[|\mathcal{G}_{k}|]\text{ for all }k\in[K]\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{k\in[K]}u_{ik}=u.

Then the combination

(3.2)

\{\mathcal{G}_{k},w_{k},\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{k\in[K],i\in[m]}

is known as a testing configuration for
u/[n]
replicability. This configuration can be fixed or dependent on the covariates
\mathbf{X}
, but must not be dependent on the base
p
-values
\mathbf{P}
.

With respect to a testing configuration (
3.2
), for each feature
i
in a given group
k
, one can define the
local
PC null hypothesis

(3.3)

H_{i}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}:|\{j\in\mathcal{G}_{k}:\mu_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\}|\geq|\mathcal{G}_{k}|-u_{ik}+1,

which asserts that there are no more than
u_{ik}-1
true signals among the
|\mathcal{G}_{k}|
effects, as well as its corresponding
local
PC
p
-value

(3.4)

p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}\equiv f_{ik}(\mathbf{P}_{i\mathcal{G}_{k}};u_{ik}),

where
f_{ik}
is a function that combines the base
p
-values in
\mathbf{P}_{i\mathcal{G}_{k}}
and also depends on
u_{ik}
. The ParFilter will leverage the property associated with a given testing configuration in the following lemma to control the
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
when testing the PC nulls in (
1.2
):

Lemma 3.1

(
\text{FDR}_{k}
control
\Rightarrow

\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control)
.

Suppose
\{\mathcal{G}_{k},w_{k},\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{k\in[K],i\in[m]}
is a testing configuration and

\mathcal{H}_{k}\equiv\{i\in[m]:\text{$H^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}$ is true}\}

is the set of true local PC nulls for each group
k\in[K]
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq[m]
be an arbitrary data-dependent rejection set and

\text{FDR}_{k}=\text{FDR}_{k}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}})\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}I\{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\}}{|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}|\vee 1}\right]

be the FDR entailed by rejecting the local PC nulls in group
k
for the features in
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
. For an
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
target
q\in(0,1)
, if
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is such that
\text{FDR}_{k}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}})\leq w_{k}\cdot q\text{ for each }k\in[K]
,
then
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}})\leq q
.

Lemma
3.1
is proved in Appendix
C.1
. In addition to the testing configuration, for each group
k
, the ParFilter employs two related functions
f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
and
f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}}
that take
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{X}
as inputs: The first function output a subset

(3.5)

\mathcal{S}_{k}\equiv f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{X})\subseteq[m]

that aims to have as few intersecting features with the set of null features
\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}
in (
1.3
) as possible, and the second function creates non-negative weights
\nu_{1k},\dots,\nu_{mk}\in[0,\infty)
by outputting a vector

(3.6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}=(\nu_{1k},\nu_{2k},\dots,\nu_{mk})\equiv f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{X})\quad\text{ that satisfies }\quad\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\nu_{\ell k}=|\mathcal{S}_{k}|.

Finally, for given fixed tuning parameters

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{K}\in(0,1),

the ParFilter considers the following
candidate
rejection set

(3.7)

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})\equiv\bigcap_{k\in[K]}\left\{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}:p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}\leq(\nu_{ik}\cdot t_{k})\wedge\lambda_{k}\right\}\\
\text{ for any vector of thresholds }{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{K})\in[0,\infty)^{K},

and decides on a data-dependent vector
\hat{\mathbf{t}}=(\hat{t}_{1},\dots,\hat{t}_{K})
, which is plugged into (
3.7
) to obtain a final rejection set
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
. Under suitable assumptions,
\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
will control
\text{FDR}_{k}(\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}}))
below
w_{k}\cdot q
for each
k\in[K]
, and therefore control
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}(\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}}))
below
q
by virtue of Lemma
3.1
.

In the following subsections, we will discuss the specifics of how the testing configuration, local PC
p
-values,
f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
,
f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
should be formed. But before getting into these downstream details, one can already observe from our overview above how the ParFilter derives its testing power:

(i)

The selected sets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{K}
serve to filter out unpromising features that have little hope of demonstrating
u/[n]
replicability. If the
selection rules

f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
’s can successfully exclude many features belonging to
\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}
, we can substantially reduce the amount of multiplicity correction required when determining the data-dependent threshold
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
for the rejection set.

(ii)

The
local PC weights

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{K}
rescale the rejection thresholds, as seen in (
3.7
). A simple choice is to set
\nu_{ik}=1
for every
i
and
k
. However, if the weights can be learned from the data, ideally also with the help of auxiliary covariates, in such a way that
\nu_{ik}
is large for
i\notin\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}
and small for
i\in\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}
, then the final reject set
\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
renders a more powerful procedure.

3.1.
Testing configuration for the ParFilter

Our suggestion for setting the testing configuration for maximum replicability is straightforward:

Example 3.1

(Simple testing configuration for
u=n
)
.

Let
K=n
. For each
k\in[n]
, let

\mathcal{G}_{k}=\{k\},\quad w_{k}=1/n,\quad\text{and}\quad u_{ik}=1\quad\text{for each $i\in[m]$ }.

Since we primarily focus on the power-derived case of
u=n
, we defer our suggested testing configuration for the case of
u<n
to Appendix
A.1
. Adapting a testing configuration to the covariates
\mathbf{X}
is permitted by Definition
3.1
and may lead to improved power. However, developing a general methodology for creating an adaptive testing configuration is challenging, as covariates can vary drastically in type (e.g., continuous, categorical, etc.), dimensionality, and level of informativeness across different scientific contexts. Based on our experiments, we find that the testing configuration provided in Example
3.1
performs well across various settings, particularly when paired with the selection rules and the local PC weights proposed in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

3.2.
Local PC
p
-values for the ParFilter

The local PC
p
-values in (
3.4
) should generally satisfy the following condition for our theoretical results established in Section
3.6
. It resembles the validity property in Definition
2.1
(a)
but additionally account for the covariates:

Condition 1

(Validity of
p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
conditional on
{\bf X}
)
.

For any given feature
i
and group
k
, its corresponding local PC
p
-value
p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
defined in (
3.4
) is said to be valid for testing
H_{i}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}
conditional on
{\bf X}
if

\Pr(p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}\leq t\mid\mathbf{X})\leq t\text{ for all }t\in[0,1]\ \text{when $H_{i}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}$ is true}.

Under our basic assumptions, defining the combining function
f_{ik}
in (
3.4
) with the generalized Benjamini-Heller combining method (Definition
2.2
) and the four examples of combining functions for testing global nulls mentioned in Section
2
can render conditionally valid local PC
p
-values.

Lemma 3.2

(Validity of local GBHPC
p
-values conditional on
{\bf X}
)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
3
hold.
Given a testing configuration for
u/[n]
replicability (Definition
3.1
) and
(i,k)\in[m]\times[K]
, let

(3.8)

p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}=\max\left\{f_{i,\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{P}_{i\mathcal{J}}):\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}\text{ and }|\mathcal{J}|=|\mathcal{G}_{k}|-u_{ik}+1\right\},

where each
f_{i,\mathcal{J}}:[0,1]^{|\mathcal{G}_{k}|-u_{ik}+1}\longrightarrow[0,1]
is one of the Bonferroni, Fisher, Stouffer, or Simes combining function in
|\mathcal{G}_{k}|-u_{ik}+1
arguments. Then
p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
meets Condition
1
, i.e. it is valid for testing
H_{i}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}
conditional on
{\bf X}
.

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is given in Appendix
C.2
. Note that the
p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
in (
3.8
) is also monotone as in Definition
2.1
(b)
, because the Bonferroni, Fisher, Stouffer and Simes combining functions are all non-decreasing.

3.3.
Selection rules for the ParFilter

The choice of the selection rule
f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
for each group
k
is flexible, provided it satisfies the following two conditions for establishing the finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control guarantees of the ParFilter in Section
3.6
.

Condition 2

(
\mathcal{S}_{k}
depends only on
\mathbf{P}_{\cdot(-\mathcal{G}_{k})}
and
{\bf X}
)
.

For each
k\in[K]
, the selected set
\mathcal{S}_{k}\equiv f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{X})
only depends on
{\bf P}
via
{\bf P}_{\cdot(-\mathcal{G}_{k})}
. In other words,
\mathcal{S}_{k}
cannot be determined using knowledge of the base
p
-values in the same group
k
.

Condition 3

(Stability of
f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
)
.

For each
k\in[K]
and
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\equiv f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{X})
, fixing
\mathbf{P}_{(-i)\cdot}
and
\mathbf{X}
and changing
\mathbf{P}_{i\cdot}
so that
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
still holds will not change
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. In other words, each selection rule
f_{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
is stable.

Many common multiple testing procedures are stable (Condition
3
) and can be employed as selection rules; see
Bogomolov, (2023)
for examples. For simplicity, we suggest the following thresholding selection rule which only depends on the
p
-values and can be easily verified to satisfy both Conditions
2
and
3
.

Example 3.2

(Selection by simple PC
p
-value thresholding)
.

A simple selection rule can be defined by taking

(3.9)

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\bigcap_{\ell\in[K]\backslash\{k\}}\left\{i\in[m]:p^{u_{i\ell}/\mathcal{G}_{\ell}}_{i}\leq\left(w_{\ell}\cdot q\right)\wedge\lambda_{\ell}\right\}\quad\text{for each }k\in[K].

This suggested selection rule is an extension of what was adopted by
Bogomolov and Heller, (2018, Sec. 5)
in their two-study special case where
n=u=K=2
,
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{1\}
,
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{2\}
, and
u_{i1}=u_{i2}=1
for all
i\in[m]
. The idea behind (
3.9
) is to select for each group
k
the features that are likely to be
u_{i\ell}/\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
replicated for all other groups
\ell\neq k
; such features are also more likely to be
u/[n]
replicated since
\sum_{k\in[K]}u_{ik}=u
.

3.4.
Local PC weights for the ParFilter

Given the form of a candidate rejection set in (
3.7
), for each group
k
, it suffices to only construct the local PC weights
\nu_{ik}
for those
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
. To ensure finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control, we require the local PC weight functions
f_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{k}}
’s to create weights that satisfy either Condition
4
(a)
or
(b)
below:

Condition 4

(Restrictions on the construction of
\nu_{ik}
)
.

For a given group
k\in[K]
and
i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, the local PC weight
\nu_{ik}
is a function of

(a)

\mathbf{P}_{(-\mathcal{S}_{k})\mathcal{G}_{k}}
,
\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
only.

(b)

\mathbf{P}_{\cdot(-\mathcal{G}_{k})}
,
\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{k}
only.

In other words,
both conditions allow
\nu_{ik}
to depend on all the covariates, where Condition
4
(a)
allows further dependence on those base
p
-values in the same group
k
that are not selected by
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, and Condition
4
(b)
allows further dependence on those base
p
-values not in the same group
k
. Under either condition,
our
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control in Section
3.6
is established in the present frequentist setting where the truth statuses of the base hypotheses
H_{ij}
’s in (
1.1
) are fixed.

To motivate our specific construction of the local PC weights, we will nonetheless borrow a working model for each study where the statuses of its base hypotheses can be
random
. Such Bayesian formulations are also common for multiple testing in the single-study context; see
Leung and Sun, (2022)
and
Heller and Rosset, (2020)
, as well as the older references therein for instance. Our working model is as follows: Conditional on
{\bf X}
, the duples
(p_{1j},H_{1j}),\dots,(p_{mj},H_{mj})
in study
j
are independent, and the distribution of each
p_{ij}
only depends on
x_{ij}
as

(3.10)

p_{ij}|x_{ij}\sim f_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})\equiv\pi_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}(x_{ij})+(1-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}(x_{ij}))f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})

with parameter vectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d_{j}}
, where

\pi_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}(x_{ij})\equiv\frac{1}{1+e^{-\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{T}_{j}(1,x_{ij})}}

models
\Pr(H_{ij}\text{ is true}\mid x_{ij})
, the conditional probability that
H_{ij}
is true given
x_{ij}
, and

f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})\equiv(1-k_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(x_{ij}))\cdot p^{-k_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(x_{ij})},

with
k_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(x_{ij})\equiv(1+e^{-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}^{T}(1,x_{ij})})^{-1}
, is a right-skewed beta density that models the conditional density of
p_{ij}
when
H_{ij}
is not true. Note that
p_{ij}
is modeled to be uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
given
x_{ij}
when
H_{ij}
is true, and (
3.10
) boils down to a marginal mixture model for
p_{ij}
with only intercept parameters if no covariates are available for study
j
. The model can also be generalized by incorporating splines to capture possible nonlinearities in
x_{ij}
.

Under the working assumption that
(p_{i1},H_{i1}),\dots,(p_{in},H_{in})
are independent conditional on
{\bf X}

2
2

2

A natural analog to Assumption
1
when the base hypotheses can be random.
, our approach to constructing sensible local PC weights revolves around estimating

\omega^{\upsilon/\mathcal{J}}_{i}\equiv\Pr(\text{$H^{u/\mathcal{J}}_{i}$ is false}\mid\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X})

for a given
i\in[m]
,
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n]
and
\upsilon\in|\mathcal{J}|
, where
H^{v/\mathcal{J}}_{i}
is the partial conjunction null hypothesis

H^{v/\mathcal{J}}_{i}:|\{j\in\mathcal{J}:\mu_{ij}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\}|\geq|\mathcal{J}|-v+1.

In Appendix
A.2.1
, we will derive that
\omega^{\upsilon/\mathcal{J}}_{i}
has the generic form

(3.11)

\omega^{\upsilon/\mathcal{J}}_{i}=\omega^{\upsilon/\mathcal{J}}_{i}\big(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},p_{ij}\big)=\sum_{\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{J}:|\mathcal{L}|\geq\upsilon}\Bigg(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{J}\backslash\mathcal{L}}\tfrac{\pi_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}(x_{ij})}{f_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})}\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\tfrac{(1-\pi_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j}}(x_{ij}))\cdot f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})}{f_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})}\Bigg),

which is treated as a function in
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},p_{ij}
.
We now illustrate how to leverage this expression to construct PC weights that satisfies Condition
4
(a)
. A similar way to construct such weights satisfying Condition
4
(b)
is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Example 3.3

(Forming
\nu_{ik}
that satisfies Condition
4
(a)
)
.

The general idea is to let

(3.12)

\nu_{ik}=\frac{{\footnotesize\tilde{\omega}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}}|\mathcal{S}_{k}|}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}{\footnotesize\tilde{\omega}^{u_{\ell k}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{\ell}}},

where
{\footnotesize\tilde{\omega}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}}
is an estimate of
\omega^{{}_{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}}_{i}
,
the probability that
H^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
is false conditional on
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{X}
. The local PC weights defined by (
3.12
) not only satisfy
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\nu_{\ell k}=|\mathcal{S}_{k}|
as required in (
3.6
), but also allows feature
i
to be more easily rejected if
H^{{}_{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}}_{i}
is false.
Therefore, to the end of estimating

(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j},\tilde{p}_{ij})
of
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j},p_{ij})
constructed only based on
(\mathbf{P}_{(-\mathcal{S}_{k})\mathcal{G}_{k}},\mathbf{X},\mathcal{S}_{k})
into (

) above, by taking

and

.

Let

be the group that study

belongs to, and

(\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j})\equiv\argmax_{(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})}\sum_{i\notin\mathcal{S}_{k(j)}}\log\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}}(p_{ij}|x_{ij})\right)

be estimators for
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{j},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j})
, obtained by maximizing the working likelihood of (

) on the subset of data from study

corresponding to the non-selected features in group

. Since non-selected features tend to exhibit sparse signals, we find that inflating

by a multiplicative factor of

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}=1.5\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}\text{ and }\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}.

Moreover, by substituting them into the expression of
\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}|x_{ij}\right]
in

derived in Appendix

, an estimate for

\tilde{p}_{ij}\equiv\frac{2\cdot(1-k_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(x_{ij}))+k_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(x_{ij})\cdot\pi_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}}(x_{ij})}{4-2\cdot k_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(x_{ij})}.

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}\equiv\sum_{\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{k}:|\mathcal{L}|\geq u_{ik}}

\displaystyle\Bigg(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{k}\backslash\mathcal{L}}\tfrac{\pi_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}}(x_{ij})}{f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(\tilde{p}_{ij}|x_{ij})}\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\tfrac{(1-\pi_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j}}(x_{ij}))\cdot f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(\tilde{p}_{ij}|x_{ij})}{f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}}(\tilde{p}_{ij}|x_{ij})}\Bigg).

3.5.
Determining the thresholds

For each group
k\in[K]
, let

(3.13)

\text{$\pi_{k}\equiv\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}}\nu_{ik}}{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\vee 1}$}\in[0,1]

be the
(post-selection) weighted proportion of true local PC nulls
.
Moreover, for a candidate rejection set
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})
, let

\displaystyle\text{FDP}_{k}(\mathbf{t})\equiv\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}I\left\{i\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})\right\}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1}

be the
false discovery proportion (FDP)
of local PC nulls in group
k
.
A natural data-driven estimator for
\text{FDP}_{k}(\mathbf{t})
can be taken as

(3.14)

\widehat{\text{FDP}}_{k}(\mathbf{t})\equiv\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\cdot\hat{\pi}_{k}\cdot t_{k}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1},

where
t_{k}
is the
k
-th coordinate of
\mathbf{t}
, and
\hat{\pi}_{k}\equiv f_{\hat{\pi}_{k}}(\mathbf{P};\mathbf{X},\lambda_{k})\in(0,\infty)
is an estimator for
\pi_{k}
; possible choices for the function
f_{\hat{\pi}_{k}}
are discussed in Section
3.6
. Heuristically, (
3.14
) provides a conservative estimate for
\text{FDP}_{k}(\mathbf{t})
because

\displaystyle\text{FDP}_{k}(\mathbf{t})\equiv\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}I\left\{i\in\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})\right\}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}}I\left\{p^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}\leq\nu_{ik}\cdot t_{k}\right\}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\cdot{\pi}_{k}\cdot t_{k}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1}\approx\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\cdot\hat{\pi}_{k}\cdot t_{k}}{|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|\vee 1}\equiv\widehat{\text{FDP}}_{k}(\mathbf{t}).

Above, the approximate inequality “
\lesssim
” follows from the fact that, under conditionally valid local PC
p
-values (Condition
1
(a)
), the number of rejected true local PC nulls is approximately
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}}(\nu_{ik}t_{k})\approx|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\cdot\pi_{k}\cdot t_{k}
from (
3.13
). As mentioned in our overview, the ParFilter aims to control
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
under level
q
, by making a data-driven choice of
\mathbf{t}
for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})
in (
3.7
) that controls each
\text{FDR}_{k}
under level
w_{k}\cdot q
. As such, it computes a data-driven threshold vector
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
that satisfies

(3.15)

\widehat{\text{FDP}}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})\leq w_{k}\cdot q\text{ for all }k\in[K],

because one can then expect that

\text{FDR}_{k}(\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}}))\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\text{FDP}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})\right]\lesssim\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\text{FDP}}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})\right]\leq w_{k}\cdot q\text{ for each }k.

Rearranging (
3.15
) into

\hat{t}_{k}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})|\vee 1}{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\cdot\hat{\pi}_{k}}\cdot w_{k}\cdot q

highlights how, as a result of filtering, the multiplicity correction associated with
\hat{t}_{k}
scales with
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|
, instead of with the possibly much larger
m
. Below, we define the set of all feasible values for
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
that satisfy all the constraints in (
3.15
):

Definition 3.2

(Set of all feasible values for
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
)
.

Given the false discovery proportion estimator in (
3.14
) for each group
k\in[K]
, define

\mathcal{T}\equiv\Big\{\mathbf{t}\in[0,\infty)^{K}:\widehat{\text{FDP}}_{k}(\mathbf{t})\leq w_{k}\cdot q\text{ for all }k\in[K]\Big\}

to be the set of possible values for
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
for which the constraints in (
3.15
) are all satisfied.

Now, among all the vectors in
\mathcal{T}
, which one should
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
be? Since the number of rejections
|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{t})|
is a non-decreasing function in
\mathbf{t}
, the intuition is to choose the coordinates of
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
to be as large as possible. Turns out, an unequivocally best choice of
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
exists:

Proposition 3.3

(Existence of an optimal vector
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
in
\mathcal{T}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be as in Definition
3.2
, and also define

(3.16)

\hat{t}_{k}\equiv\max\left\{t_{k}\in[0,\infty):\exists(\mathbf{t}_{[1:(k-1)]},t_{k},\mathbf{t}_{[(k+1):K]})\in\mathcal{T}\right\}

for each
k\in[K]
.
Then
\hat{\mathbf{t}}\equiv(\hat{t}_{1},\dots,\hat{t}_{K})\in\mathcal{T}
.
In particular, for any
{\bf t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{K})\in\mathcal{T}
, we have that
t_{k}\leq\hat{t}_{k}
for all
k\in[K]
;
in other words,
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
is the largest element-wise vector in
\mathcal{T}
.

To find this optimal
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
efficiently, we recommend using our iterative procedure in Algorithm
1
below. Its correctness and termination are ensured by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4

(Correctness and Termination of Algorithm
1
)
.

Algorithm

will

(i)

output the optimal vector of thresholds
\hat{\mathbf{t}}
in Proposition
3.3
, and

(ii)

terminate in finitely many steps.

Both the proofs of Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
involve interesting limiting arguments, and are provided in Appendices

4.
Simulation Studies

For
n\in\{2,3,4,5\}
studies,
we conduct simulations to assess the performance of the ParFilter in a maximum replicability analysis with
u=n
, where
H^{n/[n]}_{1},\dots,H^{n/[n]}_{m}
are tested simultaneously for
m=5000
features. The data are generated with the steps below:

(i)

We first let
x_{i}\overset{\tiny\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{N}(0,1)
for
i=1,\dots,5000
, and set

(4.1)

x_{i1}=x_{i2}=\cdots=x_{in}=x_{i}.

In other words, for each feature
i
, the covariates
x_{i1},\dots,x_{in}
are all equal and generated from the same standard normal distribution.

(ii)

Next, given all the covariates, the statuses of the base null hypotheses are decided as follows: For each
(i,j)\in[5000]\times[n]
, its corresponding base hypothesis only depend on
x_{ij}
and is generated as

I\{H_{ij}\text{ is true}\}|x_{ij}\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{\exp(\gamma_{0}(n)+\gamma_{1}\cdot x_{ij})}{1+\exp(\gamma_{0}(n)+\gamma_{1}\cdot x_{ij})}\right),

with the parameters
\gamma_{0}(n)=\log((0.01/n)^{-1/n}-1)
and
\gamma_{1}\in\{0,1,1.5\}
.

To shed light on our choices for the simulation parameters, note that when
x_{ij}=0
for all
(i,j)\in[5000]\times[n]
, the value of
\gamma_{0}(n)
ensures the proportion of false PC nulls is approximately
0.5\%
,
0.3\%
,
0.25\%
and
0.2\%
for
n=2,3,4
, and
5
respectively, as the probability of any
H_{i}^{n/[n]}
being false can be computed as
1/(1+\exp(\gamma_{0}(n)))^{n}
. When
\gamma_{1}=0
,
x_{ij}
has no influence on
H_{ij}
, but as
\gamma_{1}
increases above 0,
x_{ij}
becomes more correlated with
H_{ij}
.

(iii)

Lastly, given all the covariates and hypothesis statuses, the base
p
-values are independently generated as

p_{ij}\sim\begin{cases}\text{Unif}(0,1)&\text{if $H_{ij}$ is true}\\
\text{Beta}(1-\xi,7)&\text{if $H_{ij}$ is false}\end{cases},

where
\xi
is a signal size parameter ranging in
\{0.74,0.76,0.78,0.80,0.82\}
. As
\xi
increases from
0.74
to
0.82
,
\text{Beta}(1-\xi,7)
becomes increasingly right-skewed and stochastically smaller than a uniform distribution.

Under this data generating mechanism, the base
p
-values across features are independent given
{\bf X}
and Theorem
3.5
applies.
For each feature
i\in[5000]
, we also let

p^{n/[n]}_{i}=\max(\mathbf{P}_{i\cdot}),

be the PC

formed using the GBHPC method in Definition

) when

5.
Case Study

The immune system mounts an immune response to defend against foreign substances in the body (e.g., viruses and bacteria). An equally important task, called self-tolerance, is to recognize substances made by the body itself to prevent autoimmunity, where the immune system mistakenly attacks healthy cells and tissues. In medullary thymic epithelial (mTEC) cells expressing high Major Histocompatibility Complex class (MHC) II levels, commonly known as mTEChi cells, a protein called the autoimmune regulator (AIRE) drives the expression of genes to synthesize proteins critical for self-tolerance

6.
Discussion

It may be of interest to perform
directional
inference in some applications involving two-sided hypotheses where
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{0\}
for all
i\in[m]
in (
1.1
). Formally, one can define two base null hypotheses

\text{$H^{-}_{ij}:\mu_{ij}\geq 0\quad$ and $\quad H^{+}_{ij}:\mu_{ij}\leq 0\quad$ for each $(i,j)\in[m]\times[n]$},

and subsequently the directional PC null hypotheses

\displaystyle H^{u/[n],-}_{i}:

\displaystyle|\{j\in[n]:H^{-}_{ij}\text{ is false }\}|\geq n-u+1\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad\quad H^{u/[n],+}_{i}:|\{j\in[n]:H^{+}_{ij}\text{ is false }\}|\geq n-u+1\quad\text{ for each $i\in[m]$}.

Rejecting
H^{u/[n],-}_{i}
(resp.
H^{u/[n],+}_{i}
) declares there are at least
u
negative (resp. positive) signals for feature
i
. We can apply the ParFilter to test
\{H^{u/[n],-}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
and
\{H^{u/[n],+}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
under the
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
targets
q^{-}
and
q^{+}
separately, and the directional false discovery rate for the overall replicability analysis can be controlled under level
q^{-}+q^{+}
; we refer to
Wang et al., (2022, Sec. 4.4.3)
and
Bogomolov and Heller, (2023, Remark 5.1)
for more exposition on this approach.

In summary, the ParFilter is a highly customizable framework (with flexible choices of testing configuration, selection rules, local replicability levels, etc.) to test for replicated signals in large-scale settings with strong false discovery rate control in finite samples. Its adaptiveness to auxiliary covariate information has opened up new avenues to tackle partial conjunction testing, which is by nature very power-deficient for high replicability levels if the inference is based on
p
-values alone. There is certainly ample room for further improving our the current implementation, and we discuss some of these extensions and their related issues below.

6.1.
Future directions

One natural direction for future work is to develop more powerful data-driven strategies for constructing
\mathcal{G}_{k}
,
w_{k}
,
\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
p^{{}_{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}}_{i}
,
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, and
\nu_{ik}
than those proposed in this paper. In particular, we suspect that
\nu_{ik}
– the local PC weight for feature
i
in group
k
– may have an optimal construction under data generated from a two-group model
(
Efron,, 2008
)
, by using a weighting scheme similar to that in Proposition 3.2 of
Roquain and van de Wiel, (2008)
. Subsequently, it may be possible to derive an “oracle inequality” for the optimal power; see
Roquain and van de Wiel, (2008, Theorem 4.2)
.

Another direction is to convert the ParFilter from a
p
-value-based procedure to an
e
-value-based procedure, in an analogous spirit to how
Wang and Ramdas, (2022)
converts the BH procedure to an
e
-BH procedure. Interest in
e
-values has grown enormously in recent years as an alternative to
p
-values in hypothesis testing
(
Vovk and Wang,, 2021
)
. Based on the theoretical underpinnings of the e-BH procedure introduced in
Wang and Ramdas, (2022)
, we suspect that an explicit
e
-value version of the ParFilter could offer improved power when the data among the
m
features within a study are arbitrarily dependent, as in our Theorem
3.6
; we are actively investigating this extension at the time of writing.

6.2.
Current limitations when
u<n

Throughout, we have largely emphasized on the important case of maximum replicability level
u=n
. While this is the most natural level a practitioner may request, it is sometimes worthwhile to choose
u<n
, either because of power consideration or because maximum replicability is not demanded for some applications.

When assessing less-than-maximum replicability with
u<n
, our simulation studies in Appendix
E.2
suggest that our implementation of the ParFilter therein is less competitive than its performance demonstrated in Section
4
for testing maximum replicability. This is mostly due to the difficulty of selecting appropriate local replicability levels
\mathbf{u}_{i}
for
i\in[m]
in a way that makes
u/[n]
replicated features more detectable. In Appendix
A.1
, we have offered a detailed discussion of this issue, which arises from the “imbalance” of a testing configuration with respect to some
u/[n]
replicated features, and why the ParFilter does not encounter the same difficulty when
u=n
.

Given its state-of-the-art performance for
u=n
, the ParFilter in its current form is a much-needed addition to practitioners’ toolkits for conducting meta-analyses, and
complements other existing
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
controlling methods, such as the AdaFilter-BH procedure
(
Wang et al.,, 2022
)
, that have exhibited stronger performance for
u<n
as per our simulations in Appendix
E.2
. In the same appendix, we have also discussed potential directions for future research aimed at improving the power of the ParFilter when
u<n
.

6.3.
Post hoc inference for individual studies

The ParFilter can easily incorporate a
post hoc
FDR procedure for testing the base hypotheses in a given study
j
. Consider the following candidate rejection set

\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})\equiv\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})\cap\{i\in[m]:p_{ij}\leq\tau_{j}\}

for testing
H_{1j},\dots,H_{mj}
,
where
\tau_{j}\in[0,1]
is a rejection threshold for the base
p
-values in study
j
. Above, the base null
H_{ij}
can only be rejected by
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})
if the PC null
H^{u/[n]}_{i}
is already rejected by
\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
, the output of the ParFilter (Algorithm
2
). Thus,
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})
provides
post hoc
inferences by building upon the initial discoveries made by
\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
. Let

\text{FDR}_{j}\equiv\text{FDR}_{j}(\tau_{j})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})}I\left\{\text{$H_{ij}$ is true}\right\}}{|\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})|\vee 1}\right]

denote the false discovery rate associated with the base null hypotheses rejected by
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})
.
\text{FDR}_{j}
can be controlled by implementing the post hoc procedure outlined in the theorem below, whose proof provided in Appendix
A.5
is based on
Katsevich et al., (2023, Theorem 1)
:

Theorem 6.1

(Post hoc FDR control for an individual study)
.

Suppose the assumptions and conditions of either Theorem
3.5
(i)
or
(ii)
hold. For a given study
j\in[n]
and an
\text{FDR}_{j}
target
q_{j}\in(0,1)
, let

\hat{\tau}_{j}\equiv\max\left\{\tau_{j}\in\{p_{ij}:i\in[|\mathcal{S}_{k(j)}|]\}:\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{k(j)}|\cdot\tau_{j}}{|\mathcal{R}_{j}(\tau_{j})|\vee 1}\leq q_{j}\right\},

where
k(j)\in[K]
is the group in the testing configuration to which study
j
belongs. Then the post hoc rejection set
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\hat{\tau}_{j})
has
\text{FDR}_{j}(\hat{\tau}_{j})
control at level
q_{j}
, i.e.,
\text{FDR}_{j}(\hat{\tau}_{j})\leq q_{j}
.

We note that, if each group in the testing configuration contain only a single study as in Example
3.1
, such post hoc FDR inference is only meaningful if
q_{j}\leq w_{k(j)}q
, because Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
already ensure that each group-wise
\text{FDR}_{k(j)}
can be controlled under level
w_{k(j)}\cdot q
.

Appendix A
Miscellaneous implementational details

A.1.
Setting the testing configuration when
u<n

Generally speaking, one should aim to choose a testing configuration that is
balanced
for the vast majority of features. In other words, one should avoid choosing a configuration that is
imbalanced
for most features.

Definition A.1

(Imbalance and Balance)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{G}_{k},w_{k},\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\}_{k\in[K],\ell\in[m]}
be a testing configuration for
u/[n]
replicability, and
i
be a given
u/[n]
replicated feature, i.e., the PC null
H^{u/n}_{i}
is false. Then the testing configuration is said to be:

•

imbalanced for feature
i
if a local PC null
H^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
is true for some
k\in[K]
.

•

balanced for feature
i
if the local PC null
H^{u_{ik}/\mathcal{G}_{k}}_{i}
is false for all
k\in[K]
.

To illustrate why minimizing imbalance is important, suppose
every

u/[n]
replicated feature is imbalanced because of a chosen testing configuration. Thus, by Definition
A.1
, the set of non-nulls
[m]\backslash\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}
is also the set of all imbalanced features and satisfies

(A.1)

\displaystyle[m]\backslash\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}\subseteq\bigcup_{{}_{k\in[K]}}\mathcal{H}_{k}.

For each
k\in[K]
, the number of false discoveries made by the ParFilter among the local PC nulls in group
k
is
{|\tiny\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}|}
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}(\hat{\mathbf{t}})
is the output of Algorithm
2
. Since the ParFilter controls
\text{FDR}_{k}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}})
at level
w_{k}\cdot q
, where
q
is typically small (e.g.,
q=0.05
),
|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}|
is generally small for all
k
. It follows from (
A.1
) that the number of correct rejections
|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\cap([m]\backslash\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]})|
is small too, which means that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}
is unlikely to include many
u/[n]
replicated features.

When
u=n
, it is impossible for a testing configuration to be imbalanced for any feature. Hence, imbalance is not a concern for maximum replicability analyses, which explains the strong performance of the ParFilter in the simulations of Section
4
. Unfortunately, the same cannot be said for the case of
u<n
. For a testing configuration in the latter case, our recommendation is to use
K=2
groups with approximately equal sizes and local error weights, as using less groups makes the testing configuration less likely to be imbalanced for any
u/[n]
replicated feature. For instance, if
\text{mod}(a,b)
denotes the operation
a
modulo
b
for integers
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, letting

(A.2)

\mathcal{G}_{k}=\{j\in[n]:\text{mod}(j,2)+1=k\}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{k}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{k}|}{n}\quad\text{for each $k\in[2]$}

assigns the studies
\{1,3,5,\dots\}
to
\mathcal{G}_{2}
and the studies
\{2,4,6,\dots\}
to
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, with the two local error weights proportional to their group sizes. If possible, we recommend using expert knowledge to construct the local replicability levels by analyzing the covariates
x_{i1},\dots,x_{in}
to identify which base hypotheses among
H_{i1},\dots,H_{in}
are most likely false, and assigning
\mathbf{u}_{i}\equiv(u_{i1},\dots,u_{iK})
accordingly, while ensuring the constraints in (
3.1
) are satisfied.

It may be true that several testing configurations are considered equally effective in mitigating imbalance. In such scenarios, we recommend randomly sampling one such configuration to be used in the ParFilter to hedge against overly relying on a single configuration that may not yield powerful results. It is immediately clear that as long as the testing configuration is sampled independently of
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{X}

guarantees provided by Theorems
3.5
and

(

are

testing configurations, and the testing configuration used in the ParFilter (Algorithm

) is

Lemma B.7

(Equation (5.4) of
Ramdas et al., (2019)
)
.

Let
S\in\mathbb{N}
be a fixed natural number,
\lambda\in(0,1)
be a fixed tuning parameter,
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{S})\in[0,\infty)^{S}
be fixed non-negative weights where
\sum_{\ell\in[S]}\nu_{\ell}=S
, and
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq[S]
be a fixed subset of
[S]

\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{S})
be a vector of independent
p
-values where
\Pr(p_{\ell}\leq t)\leq t
for all
t\in[0,1]
if
\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(1-\lambda)\cdot S}{\nu_{\max}+\sum_{\ell\in[S]\backslash\{i\}}\nu_{\ell}\cdot I\left\{p_{\ell}>\lambda\right\}}\right]\leq\frac{S}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\nu_{\ell}}

for any

where

.

Proof of Lemma
B.7
.

For a vector of constants

where
d\in\mathbb{N}

, the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{1+\sum^{d}_{i=1}a_{i}\cdot b_{i}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)\cdot(1+\sum^{d}_{i=1}a_{i})}

holds by Lemma 3 of
Ramdas et al., (2019, p.2805)
where the right-hand-side is increasing in
\lambda
. Since
\Pr(p_{\ell}>\lambda)>1-\lambda
if
\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(1-\lambda)\cdot S}{\nu_{\max}+\sum_{\ell\in[S]\backslash\{i\}}\nu_{\ell}\cdot I\left\{p_{\ell}>\lambda\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{S}{\nu_{\max}}}{1+\sum_{\ell\in[S]\backslash\{i\}}\frac{\nu_{\ell}}{\nu_{\max}}\cdot I\left\{p_{\ell}>\lambda\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{S}{\nu_{\max}}}{1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\backslash\{i\}}\frac{\nu_{\ell}}{\nu_{\max}}\cdot I\left\{p_{\ell}>\lambda\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\tiny(a)}{\leq}\frac{(1-\lambda)\cdot\frac{S}{\nu_{\max}}}{(1-\lambda)\cdot(1+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\backslash\{i\}}\frac{\nu_{\ell}}{\nu_{\max}})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{S}{\nu_{\max}+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\backslash\{i\}}\nu_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{S}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}\nu_{\ell}}.

where
(a)
is a result of (
B.31
).

.

To show (

Appendix F
Implementation of the oracle procedure

The
optimal

FDR
procedure achieves the highest expected number of true discoveries among all procedures that control finite-sample
FDR

(
Heller and Rosset,, 2020
, Sec. 2.1)
. In this section, we describe the implementation of the oracle procedure used in Appendix
E
, which served as a computationally simpler alternative to the optimal FDR procedure. The oracle procedure follows a similar approach to that of
Sun and Cai, (2007)
and avoids the need to solve the complex optimization problem required by the optimal FDR procedure
(
Heller and Rosset,, 2020
, Sec. 2.4)
.

For each
i\in[m]
, let
\psi^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{i}\equiv\Pr(\text{$H^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{i}$ is true}|\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X})
denote the probability that
H^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{i}
is true given
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{X}
. Moreover, let
\psi^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\psi^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{(m)}
denote the order statistics of
\psi^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{1},\cdots,\psi^{{}_{u/[n]}}_{m}
. For a target
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
level
q
, the oracle procedure computes a rejection index

(F.1)

\hat{l}\equiv\max\left\{l\in[m]:\frac{\sum_{i\in[l]}\psi^{u/[n]}_{(i)}}{l\vee 1}\leq q\right\}

and returns the rejection set

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}\equiv\begin{cases}\{i\in[m]:\psi^{u/[n]}_{i}\leq\psi^{u/[n]}_{(\hat{l})}\}&\text{ if $\hat{l}$ exists};\\
\emptyset&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

By design,
|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}|=\hat{l}
if
\hat{l}
exists.

The oracle procedure has finite-sample
\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}
control at level
q
because:

\displaystyle\text{FDR}_{\text{rep}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}})

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}}I\left\{i\in\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}\right\}}{|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}|\vee 1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}}I\left\{i\in\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}\right\}}{|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}|\vee 1}\right]\Bigg|\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in[\hat{l}]}\psi^{u/[n]}_{(i)}}{\hat{l}\vee 1}\right]\Bigg|\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\tiny(a)}{\leq}q

where
(a)
follows from the definition of
\hat{l}
in (
F.1
). Note, the proof above assumes
\hat{l}
exists when
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{X}
are given. If
\hat{l}
does not exist, then the proof still holds because then

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}}I\left\{i\in\mathcal{H}^{u/[n]}\right\}}{|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{\text{Oracle}}|\vee 1}\right]\Bigg|\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{0}{|\emptyset|\vee 1}\Big|\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X}\right]\right]=0\leq q.
