Psychometric Validation of the Korean Preparedness to Help Scale for Assessing Adolescents’ Readiness to Support Peers at Risk of Suicide
Authors: Young-Ran Kweon, Youngmi Kwon, Hyunsoo Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdolescents are often the first to recognize emotional distress and suicidal warning signs among their peers, making their readiness to provide appropriate support an essential component of school-based suicide prevention. However, no validated Korean version of the Preparedness to Help Scale (PHS) is currently available for adolescents. This study aimed to translate and culturally adapt the PHS into Korean and evaluate its validity and reliability among Korean adolescents.Patients and MethodsA cross-sectional psychometric validation study was conducted with 434 middle and high school students in South Korea. Construct validity was examined using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Convergent validity was evaluated using standardized factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (CR). Criterion-related validity was examined through concurrent validity and known-groups validity. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω.ResultsCFA yielded fit indices consistent with the hypothesized unidimensional structure, although these indices should be interpreted cautiously given the model’s two degrees of freedom (χ2(2)=3.40, p=0.18; χ2/df=1.70; CFI=0.998; TLI=0.995; NFI =0.996; SRMR=0.012; RMSEA=0.040, 90% CI [0.000,0.111]). Standardized factor loadings ranged from.660 to.876, demonstrating satisfactory convergent validity (AVE=0.633; CR=0.872). Preparedness scores showed a small positive correlation with preventive suicide attitudes (r=0.221, p<0.001). Adolescents in the upper quartile of suicide attitude scores reported significantly higher preparedness than those in the lower quartile (t(196.19)=5.70, p<0.001; Hedges’ g=0.76), supporting known-groups validity. The Korean version of the PHS demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.868; McDonald’s ω=0.870).ConclusionThe Korean PHS demonstrated promising initial psychometric properties for assessing adolescents’ preparedness to support peers at risk of suicide. It may provide a brief and feasible measure for school-based suicide prevention and peer gatekeeper program evaluation, although further validation in diverse samples and against behavioral outcomes is warranted.

Introduction

Adolescent suicide is a major public health concern worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of death among young people.1 In South Korea, the problem is particularly severe. According to Statistics Korea, suicide remains the leading cause of death among adolescents, and the youth suicide rate reached 11.7 per 100,000 population in 2023, approximately twice the average rate reported across Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries.2 Moreover, the suicide rate among adolescents aged 15–19 years increased from 6.5 per 100,000 in 2015 to 12.3 in 2023, representing an increase of nearly 90%.3 These findings underscore the urgent need for effective and sustainable suicide prevention strategies targeting adolescents.

Since 2004, South Korea has implemented a series of national suicide prevention policies and established comprehensive legal and community-based delivery systems aimed at reducing suicide and strengthening early identification and intervention.4 Within this national framework, school-based suicide prevention programs have become a central strategy for promoting adolescents’ mental health and preventing suicide.5 Most school-based programs are delivered as universal interventions designed to improve students’ knowledge, awareness, and attitudes regarding suicide. Although these programs have consistently demonstrated positive effects on suicide-related knowledge and attitudes, their effectiveness in promoting actual helping behaviors or reducing suicidal behaviors remains inconsistent.6 In contrast, selective interventions targeting adolescents at elevated suicide risk, including peer counseling and peer gatekeeper programs, have demonstrated beneficial effects in reducing suicidal ideation among vulnerable students.7 International evidence similarly indicates that school-based gatekeeper training can improve suicide-related knowledge, skills, self-efficacy, and intervention-related outcomes.8,9 Peer-led programs have also demonstrated that trained adolescents can facilitate connections between peers at suicide risk and trusted adults.10 Because these interventions aim to prepare adolescents to recognize suicide warning signs, initiate supportive conversations, and facilitate timely referral to appropriate services, assessing adolescents’ preparedness to provide help has become an increasingly important outcome in school-based suicide prevention.

Although school-based suicide prevention programs commonly evaluate outcomes such as suicide knowledge, attitudes, or help-seeking intentions, these measures do not necessarily indicate whether adolescents are psychologically prepared to intervene when confronted with a peer at risk of suicide. Knowledge of suicide warning signs does not automatically translate into helping behavior during emotionally demanding situations. Conceptually, preparedness is related to but distinct from suicide-related attitudes, self-efficacy, behavioral intentions, and actual helping behavior. Attitudes reflect favorable or unfavorable evaluations of intervening, whereas self-efficacy concerns perceived capability to perform a particular action.11 Preparedness reflects perceived confidence and readiness to respond in a potential helping situation,12–14 whereas behavioral intention represents motivation or a plan to act and actual helping behavior represents the enacted response. Accordingly, preparedness may be conceptualized as a belief-based, action-oriented readiness construct that is theoretically related to, but not interchangeable with, these adjacent constructs.10,15 This distinction is consistent with gatekeeper training literature, which evaluates knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy, behavioral intentions, and behavioral change as related but distinct intervention outcomes.8,9 Thus, preparedness provides a conceptually relevant outcome for evaluating whether peer gatekeeper interventions enhance adolescents’ perceived readiness to respond to peers at risk of suicide.

The Preparedness to Help Scale (PHS), originally developed by Baber and Bean,15 is a brief four-item instrument designed to assess individuals’ perceived confidence and behavioral readiness to provide support to adolescents at risk of suicide. Unlike conventional evaluation measures that primarily assess suicide-related knowledge or attitudes, the PHS directly measures perceived preparedness to intervene, making it particularly suitable for evaluating interventions designed to strengthen adolescents’ helping behaviors, such as peer gatekeeper training and peer counseling programs. Although preparedness has increasingly been recognized as an important outcome of suicide prevention education, studies evaluating this construct have largely been conducted in adult populations, including professional gatekeeper instructors, middle-aged adults, and older adults.16–18 Although the PHS has previously been translated into Korean and used in suicide prevention studies,19,20 evidence regarding its psychometric properties remains limited, particularly among adolescents. To our knowledge, the factor structure and broader psychometric properties of the Korean PHS have not been systematically evaluated in an adolescent population. Thus, evaluating the PHS among Korean adolescents addresses an important measurement gap by examining the psychometric performance in a population in which peers may play an important role in identifying suicide risk and facilitating connections with appropriate support.10

Cross-cultural validation is particularly important because preparedness may be influenced by cultural norms surrounding interpersonal relationships and mental health. Cross-cultural adaptation involves more than linguistic translation and requires consideration of conceptual and cultural equivalence between the original and target populations.21 Accordingly, establishing the conceptual and psychometric equivalence of the PHS is important before applying it to Korean adolescents. South Korean adolescents develop within a collectivistic culture that emphasizes interpersonal harmony and social responsibility.22 In addition, concerns about social stigma and maintaining interpersonal relationships may influence adolescents’ perceived confidence and willingness to intervene when peers experience suicidal crises.23 Therefore, the psychometric properties of the PHS cannot be assumed to generalize directly to Korean adolescents without empirical evaluation.

Therefore, this study was undertaken to translate and culturally adapt the Preparedness to Help Scale into Korean and to conduct an initial psychometric evaluation among Korean adolescents. Specifically, this study sought to determine whether the Korean version of the Preparedness to Help Scale provides initial evidence of validity and reliability for assessing adolescents’ preparedness to support peers at risk of suicide. Based on the established structure of the original PHS and the theoretical distinction between preparedness and suicide attitudes, we hypothesized that (1) the original one-factor structure would be supported in Korean adolescents, (2) the scale would demonstrate adequate convergent validity and internal consistency, and (3) preparedness would be positively, but not strongly, associated with preventive suicide attitudes, with higher preparedness expected among adolescents with more preventive attitudes.

Material and Methods

Research Design and Participants

This methodological study employed a cross-sectional design to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically validate the Korean version of the Preparedness to Help Scale (PHS). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.24 The completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File S1.

Participants were recruited from six secondary schools (three middle schools and three high schools) in Gwangju, South Korea. Schools were selected using convenience sampling based on institutional willingness to participate, school size, and previous implementation of suicide prevention education. Sample size was determined according to recommendations for confirmatory factor analysis, which suggest that 200–400 participants provide stable parameter estimates.25 To account for incomplete responses, 440 students were recruited. Data were collected between December 10 and 31, 2025. Six questionnaires were excluded because of excessive missing responses, straight-lining, or implausibly short completion times, resulting in a final analytic sample of 434 adolescents (190 boys and 244 girls; 209 middle school students and 225 high school students).

Procedures

The study was conducted in two sequential phases: (1) translation and cultural adaptation of the Preparedness to Help Scale (PHS), followed by (2) psychometric validation of the finalized Korean version. Both phases followed internationally accepted guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation of patient-reported outcome measures.21,26

Translation and Cultural Adaptation

The PHS was translated using a functionalist approach that emphasizes conceptual and communicative equivalence over literal linguistic correspondence.27 The primary researcher and a bilingual mental health expert independently completed forward translations of the original instrument. The two versions were then compared and reconciled through consensus to produce a preliminary Korean version.

Content validity was evaluated by an expert committee consisting of two nursing researchers, two youth education specialists, two professional counselors, and one psychiatrist. Each expert evaluated the semantic, conceptual, and cultural appropriateness of each item for Korean adolescents using a 4-point scale. The I-CVI (item-level content validity index) values ranged from.86 to 1.00, and the S-CVI/Ave (scale-level content validity index using the average method) was.93. Based on the expert review, minor revisions were made to improve item clarity and linguistic naturalness.

The revised version was pilot-tested through cognitive interviews with six Korean adolescents aged 13–18 years, including one student from each grade of middle school (Grades 1–3) and one student from each grade of high school (Grades 1–3), following established cognitive interviewing and cross-cultural adaptation procedures.28,29 Participants were asked to explain each item in their own words and identify any wording that was unclear, unnatural, culturally inappropriate, or difficult to understand. The interviews confirmed that the translated items were comprehensible and culturally and developmentally appropriate. No substantive problems related to item comprehension, linguistic naturalness, or cultural relevance were identified, and therefore no further revisions were required following the cognitive interviews. Subsequently, an independent bilingual translator, blinded to the original instrument, performed back-translation into English. Any discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were resolved through discussion among the research team, resulting in the final Korean version of the PHS.

Psychometric Validation

Following cultural adaptation, the finalized Korean version of the PHS underwent psychometric validation. Because the original PHS was developed and validated as a unidimensional instrument,15 exploratory factor analysis was not performed. Instead, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to verify whether the original factor structure was supported in Korean adolescents, consistent with previous cross-cultural validation studies.30,31

Construct validity, convergent validity, criterion-related validity, and internal consistency reliability were subsequently evaluated using established psychometric methods. Details of the statistical analyses are described in the Data Analysis section.

Measures

Preparedness to Help Scale

The PHS is a brief four-item instrument designed to assess individuals’ perceived confidence and behavioral readiness to provide support to a peer at risk of suicide.15 A sample item is, “I would feel confident about my ability to effectively respond to threats or an attempt of suicide by a friend” Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater preparedness to provide support. In the present study, the Korean version of the PHS was administered to adolescents to evaluate its psychometric properties.

Attitudes Toward Suicide Scale

Criterion-related validity was evaluated using the Attitudes Toward Suicide Scale, originally developed by Domino et al32 and subsequently adapted for Korean adolescents by Kim and Chun.33 The instrument consists of 18 items assessing attitudes toward suicide on a continuum from permissive to preventive. Each item is scored dichotomously (2 = Agree; 1 = Disagree or Do not know), with higher scores indicating more preventive attitudes toward suicide. Suicide attitudes were selected as a concurrent criterion because they are theoretically related to preparedness to intervene while representing a conceptually distinct construct.8,9

Data Analysis

All Statistical Analyses Were Performed Using IBM SPSS and AMOS Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Construct validity was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) because the original Preparedness to Help Scale (PHS) was developed and validated as a unidimensional instrument.15 Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic (χ2), chi-square divided by degrees of freedom (χ2/df), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Model fit was considered acceptable if the recommended cutoff criteria:34 χ2/df ≤ 3.00, CFI and TLI ≥ .95, NFI ≥ .90, SRMR ≤.08, and RMSEA ≤ .06

Convergent validity was evaluated using standardized factor loadings (λ), average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (CR). Standardized factor loadings of at least.50 (preferably ≥.70), AVE values of.50 or greater, and CR values of.70 or greater were considered indicative of adequate convergent validity.25,35 Bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for standardized factor loadings were estimated using 2,000 bootstrap samples.

Concurrent validity and known-groups validity were examined as complementary evidence of validity. Concurrent validity was assessed by examining Pearson’s correlation coefficients between total PHS scores and scores on the Attitudes Toward Suicide Scale. Given that preparedness and suicide attitudes represent theoretically related but distinct constructs, a positive association was hypothesized without assuming a strong correlation. Known-groups validity was examined by comparing preparedness scores between participants in the upper and lower quartiles of suicide attitude scores using independent-samples t-tests. The upper and lower quartiles were selected to represent contrasting levels of suicide attitudes, whereas the full continuous attitude scores were retained in the concurrent validity analysis. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test, and Welch’s t-test was applied when the assumption of equal variances was violated. Effect sizes were calculated using Hedges’ g. Group differences in preparedness according to gender and school level were examined using independent-samples t-tests, with effect sizes reported as Cohen’s d.

Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (ω), and Spearman–Brown split-half reliability based on an odd-even split of the four items. Reliability coefficients of.70 or higher were considered acceptable.36

Finally, item- and scale-level descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis, were examined to characterize the score distribution of the PHS. Floor and ceiling effects were assessed at both the item and scale levels and were considered present when more than 15% of participants obtained the minimum or maximum possible score, respectively.37

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University (Approval No. 1040198-250724-HR-166-02) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating adolescents, and written assent was obtained from the students themselves. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty or disadvantage. All questionnaires were completed anonymously, and collected data were securely stored and used exclusively for research purposes to ensure participants’ confidentiality and privacy.

Results

Construct Validity

CFA demonstrated that the single-factor model provided satisfactory fit to the data: χ2(2) = 3.40, p =0.183; χ2/df = 1.70; CFI =0.998; TLI =0.995; NFI =0.996; SRMR =0.012; and RMSEA =0.040, 90% CI [0.000,0.111] (Table 1). These indices were consistent with an adequate fit of the hypothesized one-factor model; however, given the limited degrees of freedom (df = 2) associated with the four-item model, the excellent fit indices were interpreted cautiously rather than as strong evidence of structural validity in isolation.

Convergent Validity

Standardized factor loadings ranged from.660 to.876, with all items exceeding the recommended criterion of.50; all bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence intervals excluded zero (Table 2 and Figure 1). The average variance extracted (AVE) was.633 and the composite reliability (CR) was.872, both exceeding the recommended thresholds (AVE ≥.50; CR ≥.70), supporting convergent validity.

Concurrent and Known-Groups Validity

Concurrent Validity

Total PHS scores showed a small but statistically significant positive correlation with preventive suicide attitude scores (r =0.221, p <0.001; Supplementary Table S1).

Known-Groups Validity

Adolescents in the upper quartile of suicide attitude scores reported significantly higher preparedness (M = 3.86, SD = 1.02) than those in the lower quartile (M = 3.22, SD = 0.73). As Levene’s test indicated unequal variances, Welch’s t-test was used. The difference was statistically significant, t(196.19) = 5.70, p <0.001, with a substantial effect size (Hedges’ g = 0.76) (Table 3).

Group Differences by Gender and School Level

Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences in preparedness according to gender and school level (Table 4). No significant differences were observed between boys and girls, t(432) = −0.30, p =0.761, Cohen’s d = −0.03, or between middle and high school students, t(432) = −0.08, p =0.940, Cohen’s d = −0.01.

Reliability

Corrected item-total correlations ranged from.617 to.788 (Table 5). Deletion of any individual item did not improve reliability, with Cronbach’s α ranging from.807 to.872 and McDonald’s ω ranging from.813 to.872 across item-deletion analyses. Overall, the Korean version of the PHS demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α =0.868, McDonald’s ω =0.870, and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability =0.879.

Score Distribution and Floor and Ceiling Effects

Item-level descriptive statistics were examined to characterize the score distribution and potential floor and ceiling effects of the PHS (Table 6). Mean item scores ranged from 3.21 to 3.65 (SDs = 1.03–1.17), with skewness ranging from −0.364 to −0.062 and kurtosis from −0.720 to −0.459. No item-level floor effects were observed (2.8%–8.8%), whereas ceiling effects exceeding the 15% criterion were observed for all four items (18.0%–28.3%). The overall PHS mean score was 3.50 (SD = 0.94), with skewness of −0.061 and kurtosis of −0.380. At the scale level, 5 participants (1.2%) obtained the minimum possible score and 61 (14.1%) obtained the maximum possible score. Although the proportion attaining the maximum score approached the 15% threshold, neither a floor nor a ceiling effect was identified at the scale level according to the prespecified criterion.

Discussion

The present study translated and culturally adapted the PHS for use among South Korean adolescents and examined its psychometric properties. Overall, the findings provide initial evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the Korean version of the PHS. The principal findings and their implications are discussed below.

The present findings provide initial evidence regarding the structural validity of the Korean PHS. The CFA findings were consistent with a unidimensional representation of preparedness as an integrated, belief-based construct rather than separate cognitive, affective, or behavioral dimensions. This finding is consistent with the original conceptualization of preparedness proposed by Baber and Bean,15 suggesting that adolescents’ confidence, perceived knowledge, and comfort in responding to suicide risk represent a single latent construct. This interpretation is also consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior, which proposes that behavioral beliefs contribute to the formation of attitudes, while attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control contribute to behavioral intentions.38 From this perspective, preparedness may be conceptualized as an action-oriented belief reflecting adolescents’ perceived confidence and readiness to respond to a peer at risk of suicide, while remaining conceptually distinct from behavioral intention and actual helping behavior. Although the model contained only two degrees of freedom, contemporary psychometric guidelines recommend evaluating construct validity using multiple sources of evidence rather than relying solely on global model fit indices.39,40 In this study, satisfactory standardized factor loadings, AVE, CR, and consistency with the original theoretical framework provide converging evidence supporting the internal structure of the Korean PHS. This pattern is broadly consistent with psychometric studies of related belief- and readiness-oriented measures in youth populations.41,42 However, direct comparison with previous adolescent PHS validation studies is limited because published psychometric evaluations of the PHS in adolescent populations remain scarce. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as initial psychometric evidence, and further replication in independent adolescent samples across different countries and cultural contexts is needed to establish stronger evidence of structural validity.

Additional evidence supporting the validity of the Korean PHS was obtained from the concurrent and known-groups validity analyses. Preparedness demonstrated a modest but statistically significant positive association with preventive suicide attitudes, and this modest correlation should not be interpreted as evidence of weak validity. Preparedness and suicide attitudes are theoretically related but conceptually distinct constructs; therefore, a modest rather than strong association is conceptually expected.8,9,38 Specifically, suicide attitudes reflect evaluative beliefs concerning suicide,32 whereas preparedness reflects adolescents’ perceived confidence and readiness to respond when a peer is at risk.8,9,15 This distinction is consistent with gatekeeper training literature, which distinguishes attitudes, self-efficacy, behavioral intentions, and gatekeeper behaviors as related but distinct intervention outcomes.8,9 In the present study, adolescents with more preventive attitudes toward suicide demonstrated substantially higher preparedness than those with more permissive attitudes, yielding a substantial between-group effect (Hedges’ g = 0.76). This known-groups finding provides complementary evidence that the PHS discriminates between adolescents with differing levels of suicide-related attitudes. Although categorizing a continuous variable into quartiles may reduce statistical information, the known-groups analysis was intended to examine whether the PHS differentiated between adolescents with clearly contrasting levels of suicide attitudes, while the continuous attitude score was retained in the concurrent correlation analysis. Taken together, the modest correlation and substantial known-groups difference are consistent with the conceptualization of preparedness as a related but distinct construct rather than simply another measure of suicide-related attitudes. Accordingly, the Korean PHS may complement existing knowledge and attitude based measures by providing a direct assessment of adolescents’ preparedness to intervene when peers are at risk of suicide.

The Korean PHS also demonstrated satisfactory reliability despite consisting of only four items. Classical psychometric theory emphasizes that reliability reflects the extent to which observed scores consistently represent the underlying construct rather than measurement error.36 In the present study, corrected item-total correlations were satisfactory, and deletion of individual items did not improve reliability. Moreover, Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability all exceeded recommended thresholds, demonstrating stable internal consistency across multiple reliability indices. These findings are consistent with current psychometric recommendations advocating the use of multiple reliability estimates, particularly for brief or congeneric instruments.40,43 Together with the satisfactory factor loadings, AVE, and CR, these findings indicate that the four items consistently represent a common preparedness construct while maintaining adequate measurement precision. This balance between brevity and reliability is particularly advantageous for adolescent research, where lengthy questionnaires may increase participant burden and reduce response quality.41

Beyond its satisfactory psychometric performance, the Korean PHS has important practical implications for adolescent suicide prevention. Despite sustained national suicide prevention policies and substantial investments in school-based prevention, recent evidence suggests that the current national suicide reduction target is unlikely to be achieved under existing policy approaches.44 These findings highlight the need not only to expand suicide prevention programs but also to evaluate whether such programs effectively improve adolescents’ readiness to intervene when peers are at risk. School-based suicide prevention programs are increasingly shifting from knowledge-centered education toward interventions that encourage active helping behaviors among peers.9,45,46 As a belief-based psychological state that precedes helping behavior,15,38 preparedness may serve as a meaningful intermediate outcome for evaluating peer gatekeeper training and other school-based suicide prevention programs. Rather than evaluating only knowledge acquisition or attitude change, the Korean PHS enables researchers and educators to assess whether adolescents develop the confidence and readiness needed to intervene when peers are at risk of suicide.

Consequently, the scale may provide a more meaningful indicator of program effectiveness by capturing changes in behavioral readiness that are expected to precede actual helping behavior. The cultural adaptation of the PHS is particularly relevant because adolescents’ responses to suicide risk and help-seeking may be shaped by the sociocultural context in which peer intervention occurs.22,23 In South Korea, suicide stigma has been associated with culturally salient characteristics, including collectivism, chemyon (concern with maintaining socially acceptable face), conformity, and interdependent self-construal.47 In addition, research among Korean high school students indicates that cognitive and psychological factors are associated with intentions to seek help for mental health difficulties.48 These cultural and help-seeking contexts suggest that Korean adolescents’ perceived preparedness may involve not only confidence in recognizing and responding to suicide risk but also navigating concerns surrounding disclosure and seeking support from others. These findings provide initial support for assessing preparedness within the Korean cultural context and for the use of the Korean PHS in future suicide prevention research and program evaluation.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study has several strengths that should be acknowledged. To our knowledge, this is the first study to translate, culturally adapt, and conduct an initial psychometric evaluation of the PHS for use among Korean adolescents. The study employed a systematic cross-cultural adaptation process and evaluated the instrument using multiple sources of validity and reliability evidence. Nevertheless, the findings should be regarded as initial psychometric evidence rather than definitive validation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the CFA results were consistent with the proposed one-factor structure, the PHS consists of only four items and the model contained only two degrees of freedom. Consequently, the excellent model fit indices should be interpreted cautiously, and replication in independent and more heterogeneous samples is required to provide stronger evidence of structural validity. Second, the study employed a cross-sectional design and convenience sampling from six secondary schools in a single metropolitan area of South Korea. Although schools with similar school size and gender composition were selected, participation depended on institutional willingness, which may have introduced selection bias; students from participating schools may differ from adolescents in other regions or school environments. Accordingly, the generalizability of the findings to the broader Korean adolescent population is limited. Third, measurement invariance across gender, school level, and other relevant demographic groups was not examined. Although no significant mean differences in preparedness were observed according to gender or school level, measurement equivalence across these groups cannot be assumed. Future studies with sufficiently large subgroup samples should examine measurement invariance before broader group comparisons are made. Fourth, criterion-related validity was evaluated using suicide attitudes rather than actual helping intentions or observed helping behaviors. Although preparedness is theoretically positioned as an intermediate construct linking knowledge and attitudes to helping behavior, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether PHS scores predict subsequent peer intervention or referral behaviors. Fifth, ceiling effects were observed at the item level, and 14.1% of participants attained the maximum overall PHS score, suggesting a potential limitation in differentiating adolescents with very high levels of preparedness. Further validation in more heterogeneous samples is warranted. Finally, test–retest reliability and responsiveness to intervention were not examined. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate temporal stability, sensitivity to change, and predictive associations with actual helping behaviors.

Conclusion

This study provides initial psychometric evidence for the Korean version of the Preparedness to Help Scale among Korean adolescents. The findings were consistent with a unidimensional structure and demonstrated adequate convergent validity, theoretically expected associations with suicide attitudes, and good internal consistency. The Korean PHS may provide a brief and feasible measure of adolescents’ perceived confidence and readiness to intervene in school-based suicide prevention and peer gatekeeper programs. However, further validation using independent and nationally representative samples, longitudinal designs, test-retest assessment, and actual helping behaviors is needed before the Korean PHS can be considered a fully established assessment instrument.
