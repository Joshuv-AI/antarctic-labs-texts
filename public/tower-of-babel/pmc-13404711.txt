Adie’s syndrome in a recovered case of Bell’s palsy – A rare case report
Authors: Priyanka Gupta, Priyanka Dahiya, Supreet Rai, Sapana Yadav, Panku Baghla, Navdeep Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article describes a rare case where a patient developed Adie’s pupil after recovering from Bell’s palsy. Adie’s pupil, caused by parasympathetic involvement, presents as a dilated, poorly reactive pupil with reduced accommodation; Bell’s palsy involves facial nerve dysfunction leading to facial paralysis, usually resolving within 6 months. The patient, with a prior history of fully resolved Bell’s palsy, presented with right-sided Adie’s pupil and absent deep tendon reflexes. Diagnosis was confirmed with 0.125% pilocarpine. While Adie’s pupil has been linked to other conditions, its occurrence post-Bell’s palsy is unusual, underscoring the importance of long-term follow-up in such patients.

Introduction

Adie’s pupil is a rare neurological condition characterized by a dilated and poorly reactive pupil. It is caused by damage to the parasympathetic nerves that control the iris sphincter muscle. It typically affects young adults and is more common in females than in males. The exact etiology of Adie’s pupil is not fully understood, but it has been associated with several conditions, including viral infections, trauma, and autoimmune disorders. On the other hand, Bell’s palsy is a common neurological disorder that results in unilateral facial paralysis due to the dysfunction of the facial nerve. The majority of patients with Bell’s palsy undergo complete recovery within 6 months of onset. In this report, we present a case of Adie’s pupil appearing after an episode of Bell’s palsy, which might represent a co-occurrence.

Case Report

A 22-year-old female medical student presented to us after noticing a difference between the size of her pupils while looking in the mirror. Her right pupil was significantly larger than the left pupil and was barely reactive to light. She denied any associated symptoms such as pain, headache, nausea, or vomiting. She had no history of ocular trauma, prior ocular surgery, or use of ocular medications. She reported having had right-sided lower motor neuron Bell’s palsy 9 months back, which had resolved completely after treatment with oral steroids and acyclovir.

On examination, she was 6/6 with −3.5 Dsph in the right eye (OD) and −3.25 Dsph in the left eye (OS) and unaided N6 in both eyes (OU). Her intraocular pressure, contrast sensitivity, and color vision were normal in both the eyes. Her extraocular movements were full in all gazes, with no ptosis or proptosis. The corneal sensations were present in both eyes. Schirmer’s test measured values of 25 mm OD and 30 mm OS. The patient had right and left pupils measuring 7 mm and 3 mm, respectively, in photopic conditions. The swinging flashlight test yielded the absence of direct and indirect reflex in the right eye. In the left eye, both the pupillary reflexes were brisk and normal. However, in the right eye, on exposure to bright light, minute “vermiform movements” in the iris were observed at 6 o’ clock position. Her accommodation was assessed, and her accommodative facility was found to be reduced in the right eye [Table 1]. The rest of the anterior segment and posterior segment were unremarkable. Her systemic examination revealed the absence of deep tendon reflexes in both lower limbs.

Based on her symptoms and examination findings, a preliminary diagnosis of Adie’s syndrome was made. She was advised to undergo a complete neurological and ear, nose, and throat examination, which revealed no other abnormalities. Her blood investigations, including inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of her brain and orbits was also performed, which showed no significant abnormality. Since no other significant history could be found, and Bell’s palsy may have a viral etiology, our presumptive diagnosis was Adie’s pupil secondary to Bell’s palsy. To establish our diagnosis, we instilled 0.125% pilocarpine eye drops in both eyes, and the patient was examined every 15 min for 30 min. After 15 min, the affected pupil started reducing in size, although the size of the left pupil remained unchanged [Figure 1]. This established our diagnosis of Adie’s syndrome. The patient was asymptomatic, except for her concern regarding a dilated pupil on looking at a mirror, but had reduced accommodative facility in her right eye, so she was advised for Hart chart exercises and to remain under regular follow-up [Table 1].

Adie’s pupil has previously been reported in the past, associated with syphilis, immunologic conditions, Lyme’s disease, human immunodeficiency virus, and ischemia. However, it occurred after complete resolution of Bell’s palsy in our case.

Discussion

A symmetrical round pupil exists by virtue of the autonomic nervous system controlling the smooth muscles of the iris. Sphincter pupillae, which is more powerful is under parasympathetic control and dilator pupillae is under sympathetic control.[1] A tonic pupil or Holmes-Adie pupil (HAP) is caused by parasympathetic denervation of the iris. It is usually seen in females and is unilateral in 80% cases. It has the following features.[23]

Absent light reflex with segmental iris palsy

Pupillary light and near responses are under parasympathetic control. The afferent pathway extends from retinal photoreceptors to the lateral geniculate body and projects bilaterally in pretectal nuclei of the midbrain. The efferent pathway starts from Edinger–Westphal nuclei of the oculomotor nerve.[4] Absent light reflex with segmental paralysis of the iris sphincter is characteristic, though not pathognomic of HAP. The phrase “vermiform contraction of iris” was first used by Sattler in 1911 to describe these movements which are hippus of normal iris sectors, still wired for light reflex. When present, they rule out a central cause of pupillary abnormality and point to postganglionic oculoparasympathetic lesion.[5]

Accommodative paralysis and light near dissociation

As the efferent parasympathetic fibers travel with the third cranial nerve back toward the orbit, they synapse in the ciliary ganglion, with 3% of postganglionic fibers innervating the iris sphincter muscle (which allows for miosis) and the remaining 97% innervating the ciliary body (which allows for mydriasis). This relative abundance of accommodative fibers restores the near response postreinnervation, resulting in light near dissociation.[4]

Tonic pupil

This occurs because of aberrant regeneration of nerve fibers intended for the ciliary body, which end up targeting the pupil. Hence, a near response triggers an abnormal and prolonged tonic pupillary reaction.[6]

Cholinergic supersensitivity

As with denervated end organs, the iris sphincter develops denervation supersensitivity to acetylcholine secondary to upregulation of receptors in about a week. Diluted pilocarpine (0.125%), one-eighth of normal concentration, which has no effect on a normal pupil, causes constriction of a tonic pupil and is diagnostic for HAP.[7]

When HAP is associated with areflexia of lower limbs, it is called Holmes-Adie syndrome (HAS). When HAS is associated with hypohydrosis, it is called Ross syndrome, described by Ross in 1958.[8]

A young female presented to us with a complaint of a dilated right eye pupil. On detailed examination, she was found to have anisocoria in the right eye with absent light reflex and decreased accommodative facility. Vermiform movements of the iris, along with positive response to diluted pilocarpine, clinched the diagnosis of right eye HAP after other causes of light near dissociation such as aberrant third nerve, Argyll Robertson pupil, and perinaud syndrome were ruled out.[9]

The causes of tonic pupil include lesions of the ciliary ganglion or short posterior ciliary nerve within the orbit due to trauma, viral illness (herpes zoster virus), COVID-19, Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada, sarcoidosis, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and syphilis.[21011] It may also be associated with peripheral and autonomic neuropathies such as Miller Fisher Variant of Guillain Barré syndrome and Shy Drager syndrome.[1213] Postradiotherapy cases and association with head-and-neck cancers are also reported.[14]

Our patient underwent a detailed neurological evaluation and MRI of the brain and was found to have absent deep tendon reflexes with no other neurological deficit.

A final diagnosis of HAS was made. The patient had revealed a 9-month history of right-sided Bell’s palsy, which had recovered completely. We advised her to do Hart chart exercises for her reduced accommodative facility, and she continues to be on regular follow-up.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report of HAS in association with Bell’s palsy. It is thought that the same inflammatory or immune-mediated process that affects the facial nerve may also affect the ciliary ganglion or postganglionic fibres of the parasympathetic nerve supply to the iris sphincter muscle, leading to Adie’s pupil. Another possible explanation is that Bell’s palsy and Adie’s pupil may share a common viral etiology, such as infection with herpes simplex virus. The virus may infect both the facial nerve and the ciliary ganglion, leading to Bell’s palsy and Adie’s pupil, respectively.

Patients of Bell’s palsy are usually followed till recovery of facial and lid features, and long term sequalae may be missed. We recommend a long-term follow-up of patients with Bell’s palsy to detect rare, long-term sequelae like HAS.

It is important to note that the occurrence of Adie’s pupil in a resolved case of Bell’s palsy is rare, and further research is needed to fully understand the relationship between these two conditions.

To summarize, Adie’s syndrome is a rare neurological condition. While it has no serious consequences, it can be misdiagnosed as other conditions, such as Horner’s syndrome or third nerve palsy. A proper diagnosis can be confirmed with dilute pilocarpine eye drops as was done in this case. Although rare, Adie’s syndrome may develop after complete recovery from Bell’s palsy or as a co-occurrence, making long-term follow-up advisable.
