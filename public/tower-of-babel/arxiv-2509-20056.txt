An Overview of Meshfree Collocation Methods Tomas Halada 1,4 , Serhii Yaskovets 1,2,3 , Abhinav Singh 1,2,3 , Ludek Benes 4 , Pratik Suchde 5,6,† , and Ivo F. Sbalzarini 1,2,3,†,*


Abstract
We provide a comprehensive overview of meshfree collocation methods for numerically approximating differential operators on continuously labeled unstructured point clouds. Meshfree collocation methods do not require a computational grid or mesh. Instead, they approximate smooth functions and their derivatives at potentially irregularly distributed collocation points, often called particles , to a desired order of consistency. We review several meshfree collocation methods from the literature, trace the historical development of key concepts, and propose a classification of methods according to their principle of derivation. Although some of the methods reviewed are similar or identical, there are subtle yet important differences between many, which we highlight and discuss. We present a unifying formulation of meshfree collocation methods that renders these differences apparent and show how each method can be derived from this formulation. Finally, we propose a generalized derivation for meshfree collocation methods going forward.

Introduction

We present an overview and classification of meshfree collocation methods. Meshfree collocation methods are a class of numerical methods for approximating strong-form solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs). They do so without requiring a computational grid or mesh, by discretizing the unknown continuous functions on a set of irregularly placed collocation points. The collocation points therefore form a point cloud, on which the differential operators occurring in the PDE are approximated. In meshfree collocation methods, this is done in the strong sense by directly approximating the derivatives themselves from the discretized function values. Therefore, meshfree collocation methods can be seen as a generalization of finite differences to irregularly distributed collocation points, or nodes.

In addition to being a generalization of finite differences to scattered nodes, meshfree collocation methods are also a special sub-class of the larger algorithmic family of particle methods, although “meshfree method” and “particle method” are sometimes used synonymously in the literature. Particle methods are a family of numerical algorithms in which point-like objects—called particles—interact pairwise and then evolve as a result of these interactions, as has been rigorously defined
[
182
]
. They are amongh the oldest and most computationally powerful numerical algorithms
[

The labeled point cloud in meshfree (sometimes also referred to as meshless) methods is one specific case

Once the particle properties have been supplemented with appropriate boundary and/or initial conditions, the particles evolve in time so as to approximate the solution of the PDE. During time evolution, the particles can change either their positions, their properties, or both. Methods in which particle properties are dynamic, but their positions are constant, are referred to as
Eulerian particle methods
. If the particle positions (also) change over time, for example to track a flow field or a deforming domain geometry, particle methods are called
Lagrangian

Both Eulerian and Lagrangian particle methods are meshfree numerical methods for continuous PDE problems. They can be formulated to approximate the solution of the PDE in either the strong or the weak sense. Meshfree Galerkin methods discretize the weak form of PDE. Even though they are not the topic of this article, we briefly mention them here for completeness. Meshfree Galerkin methods originated from the Diffuse-Element Method (D-EM)

In this text, we provide an overview of meshfree collocation methods.
For this class of numerical methods, we provide a unifying mathematical definition and show how all reviewed methods adhere to this definition for certain design choices. This makes explicit the differences between methods, but also shows under what conditions they are equivalent. In doing so, we bridge diverse nomenclatures, as different methods were developed by different scientific communities—such as solid mechanics, fluid mechanics, or applied mathematics—each using different terminology. This also led to several methods being re-invented under different names over time, while others were independently derived using novel approaches. We hope we have succeeded in collecting most of the existing methods. We classify all reviewed methods into four groups according to the mathematical principle underlying their derivation. Thereby, we hope to clarify equivalence, differences, and characteristics of meshfree collocation methods.

To better understand the mathematical basis and commonalities of meshfree collocation methods, we start by giving an overview of their historic development.

1.1
A brief history of meshfree collocation methods

Meshfree collocation methods are historically rooted in works on function approximation and interpolation on scattered points from the early twentieth century
[
128
,
74
,
212
]
. The foundational reference specifically using these ideas for collocation methods is MacNeal, 1953
[
151
]
. Studying second-order boundary-value problems with curved boundary, MacNeal generalized finite differences to allow for determining weights for arbitrarily distributed nodes from a linear system of equations. This still is the basic concept underlying meshfree collocation methods today.

The concept of finite differences on scattered nodes was later discussed by Shepard in his work on interpolation of scattered data
[
204
]
. The function-approximation side of the problem was much later considered by Wendland in his seminal book on scattered data approximation
[
245
]
. Wendland’s book addresses both the theoretical foundation and the practical applications of meshfree approximation, spanning from numerical analysis to computer graphics.

Focusing on numerical analysis and the approximation of differential operators in meshfree collocation methods, MacNeal’s pioneering work
[
151
]
was generalized and formalized by Forsyth and Wasow
[
69
]
and by Swartz and Wendroff
[
224
]
. Meshfree collocation methods as we know them today were first formulated by Jensen
[
102
]
, where also the neighbor search procedure was given attention.

Indeed, an important early difficulty for meshfree collocation methods was to efficiently find the neighbors of a point when constricting and evaluating the stencils
[
187
]
. The
stencil
, also sometimes referred to as “star” is the set of all neighboring points a given point needs to know the values of in order to build its local approximation. Constructing the stencil without having to search over
all

[
93
]
and Verlet lists
[
239
]
. The problem remained, though, to choose for each point the correct number of neighbors such that the linear system of equations for the stencil weights is determined.

The constraint of working with determined linear systems was relaxed in the early 1980s with the introduction of Moving Least Squares (MLS) approximation methods
[
126

to considering more stencil points than the number of unknowns in the linear system, resulting in overdetermined stencil systems, as introduced by Liszka and Orkisz
[

The works by Liszka and Orkisz are therefore widely regarded as foundational for today’s meshfree collocation methods.

In parallel, and since the 1970s, alternative methods have been developed to approximate functions and their derivatives on scattered points.
Monaghan and Gingold
[
82
]
, and independently Lucy
[

A few years later, Chen and Liu elaborated on this concept, introducing the Reproducing Kernel Particle Method (RKPM)
[

, where radial basis functions are used to approximate the non-trivial domain integration problem.

Within the class of MLS-like methods, development continued with the Generalized Moving Least Squares Method (GMLS) introduced in the late 1990s
[

In summary, meshfree collocation methods have a long history. While mostly rooted in interpolation and approximation of scattered data, novel and original ideas have emerged over time. Some came from studying the numerical properties of the methods, some were inspired by particular applications. More recently, there is a clear trend to bridge across methods, study their mathematical connections, and generalize the theory. This is what we attempt in the remainder of this paper for meshfree collocation methods.

, where meshfree collocation approaches are often used to interpolate the function values from irregularly distributed particles to a regular Cartesian grid on which the derivatives are then approximated using finite differences
[
153
]
.
This remeshing approach is frequently used in Vortex-Particle methods for incompressible flows
[
119
,
38
]
, but also in SPH
[
36
,
91
]
and in Wavelet Particle methods
[
22
]
.

In addition to using meshfree collocation as a correction to ensure convergence of other particle methods, there is also a methodologically interesting connection with state-based peridynamics. State-based peridynamics presents an alternative formulation of continuum mechanics by introducing non-local forces in order to deal with large deformations and material separation
[
205
,
152
]
.
There, the divergence of the stress tensor is replaced with an integral formulation of the action potential of the forces. Upon discretizing the integral by a numerical quadrature, this leads to a meshfree collocation method for the force calculation.
It is therefore not surprising that there have been attempts to connect peridynamics operators with other, more canonical, meshfree collocation formulations
[
23
,
92
]
.

Taken together, there is a plethora of modern meshfree collocation methods in the literature today. While, as we have outlined in Section
1.1
, they all derive from a few common ancestors, they often diverged since then as they were developed by different scientific communities. For example, the methods used in computational fluid dyanmics (notably, GFDM, DC-PSE, LDD, LABFM) are disjoint from the methods used in solid mechanics (RKPM, GRKCM, MFDM).
While many of the methods are somewhat heuristic, rigorous numerical analysis is available for some of them, notably for GMLS, RKCM, and DC-PSE
3
3

3

For meshfree Galerkin methods, numerical analysis is more common in the literature than for meshfree collocation methods.
.
What stands out is that despite the diversity of methods presented in the literature, they are all based on common numerical principles and share many algorithmic properties. Understanding how all of these methods map onto one another could help translate algorithmic improvements and numerical analysis results from one method to the others. This is the main motivation for this paper. But before we present a unifying mathematical formulation for all meshfree collocation methods, we briefly highlight available software implementations and software libraries that can be used to implement meshfree collocation methods.

2
General formulation of meshfree collocation methods

All numerically consistent methods for approximating differential operators on scattered nodes follow a common scheme. In this scheme, the function approximation at any given point can be interpreted as an interpolation or convolution-like operation over the values at neighboring particles within a certain finite neighborhood, multiplied with suitable weights. In all consistent methods, the moments of the weight function must fulfill certain conditions. Different meshfree collocation methods mainly differ in how these conditions are enforced (e.g., by minimization, constrained minimization, or exactly), and what ansatz is used for the weight function. Different design choices lead to approximations with varying accuracy and stability properties.

We formulate the general definition of meshfree collocation methods for an open continuous domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing a set of
N
collocation points (nodes, particles)
\mathcal{X}\coloneq\{\bm{x}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\bm{x}_{N}:\bm{x}_{i}\in\overline{\Omega}\}
, where
\overline{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\partial\Omega
, at locations
\bm{x}_{i}\coloneq(x_{i,1},\,\ldots,\,x_{i,n})^{\top}=(x_{i,\beta})_{\beta=1}^{n}
.
Every point can be addressed by its index
\mathcal{P}\coloneq\{i:\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\}
.

Each particle
i\in\mathcal{P}
is surrounded by neighboring particles
\mathcal{N}_{i}\coloneq\{j\in\mathcal{P}:\lVert\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}<h_{i}\}
within a disk defined by the
stencil radius

h_{i}
, which is also sometimes called
smoothing length
,
cut-off radius
or
interaction radius
4
4

4

Note that
i\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
.
We similarly define neighboring particle sets
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{x}}
for any point
\bm{x}\in\overline{\Omega}
, not necessarily coinciding with a particle location.
Different particles
i
can have different
h_{i}
to allow for adaptive-resolution methods. Therefore, and because particle distributions can be non-uniform, different points
i
generally have different numbers of neighbors
N_{i}\coloneq\lvert\mathcal{N}_{i}\rvert
.
A characteristic length scale of the neighborhood can be defined for arbitrary node distributions as
\Delta x_{i}=\sqrt[n]{V_{n}(h_{i})/N_{i}}
, where
V_{n}(r)
is
n
-dimensional volume of a Euclidean ball of radius
r
.

Together with the stencil radius, this defines the
resolution ratio

h_{i}/\Delta x_{i}
, allowing to estimate
N_{i}\approx V_{n}(h_{i}/\Delta x_{i})
for locally homogeneous point distributions.

Now consider a sufficiently smooth continuous function
u(\bm{x}):\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. For notational simplicity, we take
u(\bm{x})
to be a scalar-valued function. For differential operators in flat spaces, all derivations apply element-wise for vector- and tensor-valued functions
5
5

5

For differential operators in curved spaces, such as Riemannian manifolds, additional metric terms appear
[
219
,
206
]
.
.
The function
u
is sampled on the particles in
\mathcal{X}
by assigning each particle
i
a continuous label
u_{i}\coloneq u(\bm{x}_{i})
.
For convenience, we write the difference between
u_{j}
and
u_{i}
as
u_{ji}\coloneq u_{j}-u_{i}
.
The goal of a meshfree collocation method is then twofold: (1) approximate the function
u(\bm{x})
at off-particle locations
\bm{x}\neq\bm{x}_{i}\,\forall i
; (2) approximate derivatives of the function.

For these approximations to exist, all methods assume that the neighborhood sets
\mathcal{N}_{i}
are
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
-unisolvent for the linear space
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
of real-valued polynomials in
n
variables of degree at most
k
.
Then, the local interpolant of
u(\bm{x})
over
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is unique, i.e., the zero polynomial is the only polynomial
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
that vanishes on
\mathcal{N}_{i}
,

\bm{p}(\bm{x}_{j})=0\quad\forall\,\bm{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\implies\bm{p}\equiv\bm{0}\,.

(1)

This is sufficient to ensure that the points do not all lie in some lower-dimensional manifold
[
29
]
.
Under this condition, the linear system of equations we define in the following has a unique solution. To ensure unisolvence in dimension
n=1
,
\bm{x}_{i}\neq\bm{x}_{j}\,\forall\,(i,j)
is a sufficient condition. For
n>1
, distinct particle positions are necessary but not sufficient, and more complex algebraic conditions apply
[
29
]
.
Indeed, unisolvence of the node set is equivalent with the Vandermonde matrix
\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{N}_{i})
having full rank, such that
\ker\mathbf{V}(\mathcal{N}_{i})=\bm{0}
. This ensures that there exists no hypersurface
H=Q^{-1}(\bm{0})
generated by a polynomial
\bm{0}\neq Q\in\mathbb{P}_{k}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq H
.
Unisolvence depends on the spatial distribution of points in the particle neighborhoods.
In particular, the points in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
must not lie on some lower-dimensional hypersurface
[
90
]
.

Assuming unisolvence in all neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, we define a discrete operator
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
at particle
i
as the weighted linear combination of function values within its neighborhood:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,,

(2)

where
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}
denote the particle weights. They are, in general, continuous functions of the distance between particles
i
and
j
and of the stencil radius, hence
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}=w^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i},\,h_{i})
. In some references, these weight functions are called “shape functions”. The sum is only over the neighbors
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, which is important for the computational efficiency of the method. Therefore, the weight functions must be compactly supported with
\mathrm{supp~}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}(\cdot,\,h)=\mathcal{N}_{i},\,\forall i\in\mathcal{P}
.

In a meshfree collocation method, we require the discrete operator
L_{i}^{d}
to be a consistent approximation of some derivative of
u(\bm{x})
, with the zeroth derivative being the function itself. Let

\bm{D}u\coloneq\left(\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\right)_{\bm{|\alpha|}=0}^{m}\text{~~~with~~~}\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\coloneq\partial^{\bm{|\alpha|}}u/\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\,,

(3)

be the vector of all derivatives up to and including the
m
-th derivative, indexed by the multi-index
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}=(\alpha_{1},\,\ldots,\,\alpha_{n})
with notation
|\bm{\alpha}|=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}
,
\bm{\alpha}!=\alpha_{1}!\ldots\alpha_{n}!
, and
\bm{x}^{\bm{\alpha}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.
In order to select a specific derivative, or a linear differential operator, from this vector, we introduce the
mapping vector

\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\coloneq\left(\delta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}\right)_{|\bm{\beta}|=0}^{m}\,,

(4)

where
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}
is the Kronecker symbol.
For example, the derivatives
\partial/\partial x
,
\partial/\partial y
, and
\nabla^{2}
in two dimensions correspond to:

\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}\left(0,1,0,0,0,0,0,0,0\dots\right)^{\top}&\text{for }\partial/\partial x\,,\\
\left(0,0,1,0,0,0,0,0,0\dots\right)^{\top}&\text{for }\partial/\partial y\,,\\
\left(0,0,0,1,0,1,0,0,0\dots\right)^{\top}&\text{for }\nabla^{2}\,.\end{cases}

(5)

We can then write:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,.

(6)

We require this approximation to have an order of consistency of
m
, i.e., the numerical error when using the method in stable numerical scheme should converge as
O(h_{i}^{m})
for all
h_{i}<1
.
Approximating an
|\bm{\alpha}|
-th derivative then requires
\bm{D}u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{p}
with

p=\sum_{k=0}^{k=m}(k+n-1)!/(k!(n-1)!)
in
n
dimensions.

To see the order of consistency of the approximation, we define the vector of scaled (by
1/\alpha_{i}!
) Taylor monomials

\bm{X}_{ji}\coloneq\left(x_{ji,1}^{\alpha_{1}},\,\ldots,\,x_{ji,n}^{\alpha_{n}}/\bm{\alpha}!\right)_{|\bm{\alpha}|=0}^{m},

(7)

with inter-particle distances
x_{ji}\coloneq x_{j}-x_{i}
.
Thus,
\bm{X}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{X}_{ji}\neq\bm{X}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i}
since
\bm{X}(\bm{x})
is nonlinear.
This allows us to write the Taylor expansion of
u(\bm{x})
over
\mathcal{N}_{i}
as:

u_{j}=\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}+e_{ji}^{m+1},

(8)

where
e_{ji}^{m+1}
denotes the approximation error
(Lagrange form of the remainder)
of leading order
m+1
.

To obtain the approximation, we first substitute the Taylor expansion for
u_{j}
from Eq. (
8
) into Eq. (
6
).
Dropping the error term, this yields:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left.\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,.

(9)

Introducing the vector of moments
\bm{B}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the weights as

\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,,

(10)

this becomes

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\cdot\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\,.

(11)

We assume the
w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}
to be linear combinations of basis functions. In meshfree collocation methods, the basis can be general
Anisotropic Basis Functions
(ABFs)
W_{ji}^{q}=W^{q}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i},\,h_{i})
. This is in contrast to
Radial Basis Functions
(RBFs)
\varphi_{ji}=\varphi_{ji}(\|\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2},\,h_{i})
used for example in SPH. The ABFs
W_{ji}^{q}
depend on
\bm{x}_{ji}
, whereas RBFs are radially symmetric and only depend on
\|\bm{x}_{ji}\|_{2}
.
We therefore have:

w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\coloneq\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=W^{0}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,0}+W^{1}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,1}+W^{2}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,2}+W^{3}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,3}+W^{4}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,4}+\dots+W^{n}_{ji}\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,p}\,,

(12)

where
\bm{W}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the vector of basis functions and the vector of unknown coefficients, respectively:

\bm{W}_{ji}\coloneq\left(W^{0}_{ji},\,W^{1}_{ji},\,W^{2}_{ji},\,W^{3}_{ji},\,\dots,\,W^{p}_{ji}\right)^{\top}

(13)

\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\coloneq\left(\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,0},\,\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,1},\,\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,2},\,\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,3},\,\dots,\,\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,p}\right)^{\top}.

(14)

The specific choice of ABFs is a pivotal design choice that distinguishes different meshfree collocation methods.
Most existing methods use a polynomial ABF basis
\bm{P}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
multiplied with a compactly supported (for computational efficiency) RBF
\varphi_{ji}
, thus
\bm{W}_{ji}\coloneq\bm{P}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
. Some references refer to the RBFs
\varphi
as “basis functions”, others confusingly call either the ABFs or the RBFs “weights”, which we here reserve for the stencil coefficients
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}
.

The goal of every meshfree collocation method is to find weight functions
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}
in all
\mathcal{N}_{i}
such that Eq. (
6
) is fulfilled for the discrete operator
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
from Eq. (
2
). This can be achieved in one of four ways.

The first way is based on assuming a particular parametric ansatz for the weight function, usually a linear combination of certain basis functions
[
55
,
63
,
200
]
. Consistency is then ensured by determining the unknown coefficients of the weight function. Substituting the ansatz for the weight function into the Taylor expansion of Eq. (
8
) leads to conditions on the moments
\sum_{j}\bm{X}_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}
of the weight function given a certain derivative
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\bm{D}u
. These moment conditions define a liner system of equations for the coefficients of the weight function. We refer to this approach as
approximation of moments
(AOM), and discuss it in more detail in Section
3
.

The second way to derive a consistent weight function starts with expressing the derivative values at the neighboring collocation points from the Taylor expansion in Eq. (
8
). This defines a linear system of equations where the system matrix depends on the positions of the neighboring particles
[
143
]
. The unknowns in this case are not the parameters of the weight function, like in AOM, but directly the values of the derivatives. There is one equation for each particle in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. These systems are usually over-determined, and the unknown derivatives are approximated as a least-squares solution. We therefore refer to this approach as
\ell_{2}
minimization of the discretization error
(
\ell_{2}(\mathcal{E})
), and discuss it in more detail in Section
4
.

The third way starts from Eq. (
6
) and formulates an objective functional over a certain space of weight functions, for example polynomials. This functional is then optimized by minimizing some norm of the weights
w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_{j})
at the neighboring points under the constraint of AOM-like moment conditions
[
221
,
216
,
220
]
.
We refer to this approach as
\ell_{2}
minimization of weights
(
\ell_{2}(\mathcal{P})
), and discuss it in more detail in Section
5
.

The fourth approach generalizes
\ell_{2}
minimization of weights by dropping the additional moment constraints. This is done by modifying the objective functional such that the resulting unconstrained solutions match the approximations obtained by AOM. While this approach is not found in the literature so far, and no methods exist that are explicitly based on it, we introduce it here to show the connection between AOM and minimization-based approaches. We call this the
generalized
\ell_{2}
minimization
approach (g
\ell_{2}(\mathcal{P})
), and discuss it in more detail in Section
6
.

2.1
Matrix notation

Meshfree collocation methods can be written more compactly when using matrix notation, where summations over neighboring particles become matrix multiplications.
This is achieved by
stacking
the individual particle pairs over rows, which requires defining an ordering over the neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
of each particle
i
. Recall the definition of the general discrete operator from Eq. (
6

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}u_{j,i}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,,

(15)

where
u_{j,i}

j

Since the sum is commutative, the ordering introduced in the second step is arbitrary. \mathbf{X}_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}&\bm{X}_{1i}&\rule[2.15277pt]{10.76385pt}{0.5pt}\\

3
Derivation by approximation of moments

Approximation of moments (AOM) methods find a weight function
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}=w^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i},\,h_{i})
of the discrete operator in Eq. (
6
) such that the moments of the desired derivatives in Eq. (
11
) are equal to one, and all others (up to and including order
m
) become zero.
For this, we substitute the ansatz for the weight function from Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
10
).
The moments then become

\bm{B}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,,

(23)

which defines a linear system of equations for the unknown coefficients
\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
with moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
:

\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\,,\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}\coloneq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,.

(24)

Imposing the values for the moments of a given target derivative
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
amounts to replacing the vector
\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
with the appropriate
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
.
This then yields the weight-function coefficients

\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,,

(25)

and thus the discrete operator over the neighborhood of each particle,

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right).

(26)

The operator in Eq. (
26
) is linear and amounts to a convolution operation with a matrix kernel. When fulfilling the moment conditions up to order
m
, it ensures polynomial consistency of order
m
. Therefore, the error when approximating an
l
-th derivative is
o(h_{i}^{-m-1+l})
, where
o
represents the standard Landau small-o notation.
For any resolution ratio
h_{i}/\Delta x_{i}\in O(1)
, this implies an error of
o(\Delta x_{i}^{-m-1+l})
, hence:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}w_{ji}^{d}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}+o(\Delta x_{i}^{-m-1+l})\iff L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u^{*}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u^{*}\right\rvert_{i}\quad\forall u^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{m}\,,

(27)

where
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is the ring of polynomials of degree at most
m
.
In other words, the discrete differential operator is exact for polynomials up to and including degree
m
.
The condition expressed in Eq. (
27
), more precisely the part
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u^{*}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u^{*}\right\rvert_{i}
, is sometimes referred to as
gradient reproducing condition

[
44
]
,
discrete reproducing condition

[
39
]
, or
discrete moment condition

[
200
]
. In Appendix
0.A
, the derivation is repeated in 2D with all components explicitly written out. This can help grasp the general concept of meshfree collocation methods.

The main difficulty in the AOM approach is the condition number of the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
when numerically solving the linear system in Eq. (
24
). In general, this condition number depends on the ABF and on the distribution of points in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. The closer the Vandermonde matrix from Section
2
is to singular, the worse the condition number of
\mathbf{M}_{i}
7
7

7

Finding bounds on the condition number depending on the algebraic set of the neighbor particles and the ABF is, to our knowledge, an open problem.
. In practice, this often requires the use of pre-conditioning schemes. The simplest form of pre-conditioning scales each row with a power of
h_{i}
, defining the diagonal pre-conditioning matrix

\mathbf{H}_{i}\coloneq\mathrm{diag}\left(h_{i}^{-|\bm{\alpha}|}\right)_{|\bm{\alpha}|=0}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\,.

(28)

We then define
\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{ji}\coloneq\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{X}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\widetilde{\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}}\coloneq\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Replacing
\bm{X}_{ji}
by
\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{ji}
and
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
by
\widetilde{\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}}
leaves the analytical solution of Eq. (
25
) unchanged, but improves the condition number of
\mathbf{M}_{i}
in a numerical solver.

3.1
Formulation without the zeroth moment

Following the original symmetric particle strength exchange (PSE) formulation
[
56
,
55
]
, some works
[
200
,
160
,
248
,
244
]

write the operator
L_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
from Eq. (
6
) as a function of the
differences of function values

u_{ji}=u_{j}-u_{i}
, rather than the values
u_{j}
themselves, thus:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,.

(29)

This allows using weight functions with a singularity at
w_{ii}^{\bm{\alpha}}

[
115
]
.
The indices in the vector of derivatives
\bm{D}u
, the mapping vector
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, and the vector of scaled monomials
\bm{X}_{ji}
then only start from 1:

\bm{D}u\coloneq\left(\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\right)_{|\bm{\alpha}|=1}^{m}\,,\quad\bm{C}^{(\bm{\alpha})}\coloneq\left(\delta_{\bm{\alpha}\bm{\beta}}\right)_{|\bm{\beta}|=1}^{m}\,,\quad\bm{X}_{ji}\coloneq\left(x_{1,ji}^{\alpha_{1}},\,\ldots,\,x_{n,ji}^{\alpha_{n}}/\bm{\alpha}!\right)_{|\bm{\alpha}|=1}^{m}\,.

(30)

This means that the zeroth moment of the weights is excluded and the length of the vectors is
p=\sum_{k=1}^{k=m}(k+n-1)!/(k!(n-1)!)
for an
m
-th order approximation in
n
dimensions.
This leads to an operator of the form:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(31)

The order of consistency remains as in Eq. (
27
). Excluding the zeroth moment slightly reduces (by 1 row) the cost of solving the linear system in Eq. (
24
). More importantly, though, it improves the condition number of the moment matrix by eliminating the
O(1)
entries
[
30
]
and improves the stability of the discrete operator in explicit time stepping
[
200
]
.

3.2
Matrix notation

Following the notation introduced in Section
2.1
, we see that
\mathbf{C}_{i}=\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
.
This defines a system of
p
linear equations as in Eq. (
24
) with moment matrix

\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\,.

(32)

The resulting form of the discrete differential operator then is:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,,

(33)

since
\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}
.

3.3
Methods derived by approximation of moments

A typical moment-approximation method is
Discretization-Corrected Particle Strength Exchange (DC-PSE)
. This method was originally introduced by Schrader et al.
[
200
]
and later revisited in several other works
[
201
,
259
,
208
]
and extended to differential operators on curved surfaces
[
206
]
, spatially adaptive
[
193
]
and anisotropic
[
87
]
resolution, and to PDEs with discontinuous coefficients
[
68
]
.
The original motivation for DC-PSE was to render the classic
Particle Strength Exchange (PSE)
operators
[
56
,
63
]
consistent on irregular particle distributions. This was achieved by replacing the continuous, integral moment conditions of PSE with discrete sums in the spirit of collocation methods as presented in Section
2
.
In DC-PSE, the discrete differential operator is written as:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u\coloneq\frac{1}{h_{i}^{|\bm{\alpha}|}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(u_{j}\pm u_{i}\right){w}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}v_{j}\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,,

(34)

with weights
{w}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\coloneq w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\left(\left(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i}\right)/h_{i}\right)
, and
v_{j}
the
volume
of particle
j
.
The sign between the function values is positive for odd
|\bm{\alpha}|
and negative for even
|\bm{\alpha}|
and renders the method conservative on symmetric point distributions
[
63
]
. The particle volume
v_{j}
appears as the integration element when deriving the above equation by quadrature from the continuous moment integrals of PSE
[
63
]
. As later noted
[
30
]
, however, this is identical to Eq. (
6
) when absorbing the unknown particle volumes into the ABF coefficients.
DC-PSE was derived for general ABFs
[
200
]
, in the same spirit as anisotropic-kernel PSE
[
87
]
. However, all works on DC-PSE so far
[
200
,
30
,
206
,
259
,
208
,
101
]
used ABFs that are
h
-scaled monomials multiplied with Gaussian RBFs:

\bm{W}_{ji}=\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}\,.

(35)

This choice allows writing the mapping vector as the differential operator
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}

applied to the monomials with the result evaluated at zero,
(\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{X}_{ji})|_{0}
.
The scaling factor
1/h_{i}^{|\bm{\alpha}|}
can be absorbed into the mapping vector, which amounts to scaling
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
by
\mathbf{H}_{i}
according to Eq. (
28
).
Thus expressing the mapping vector as

\widetilde{\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}}\coloneq(-1)^{\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rvert}\mathbf{H}_{i}(\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{X}_{ji})|_{0}\,.

(36)

Therefore, DC-PSE includes the pre-conditioning from Eq. (
28
), rendering it better conditioned than MLS
[
30
]
.
Due to its symmetric formulation w.r.t. the function values
u_{i,j}
, DC-PSE can either include or exclude the zeroth moment (see Section
3.1
). This can be exploited to improve its stability in explicit time stepping
[
200
,
202
]
. The final form of the DC-PSE operator then is:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(u_{j}\pm u_{i}\right)\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}}\right).

(37)

A similar approach, albeit formulated slightly differently, was used in deriving the
Reproducing Kernel Collocation Method (RKCM)
. Originally introduced by Aluru
[
4
]
, RKCM was inspired by the Reproducing Kernel Particle Method (RKPM)
[
146
,
41
]
. It aimed to eliminate the quadrature from RKPM in order to turn it into a collocation approach.
Following its RKPM lineage, RKCM is derived from
reproducing conditions
, which is the RKPM name for moment conditions. Just like DC-PSE replaced the continuous moment integrals of PSE with discrete sums, RKCM replaces the continuous reproducing conditions of RKPM with discrete variants as in Eq. (
27
).
This starts from approximating the function itself using Eq. (
6
) for
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
:

L^{\bm{0}}_{i}u\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{0}}_{ji}\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{0}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,.

(38)

and determining the resulting weight function
w_{ji}^{\bm{0}}
from the reproducing conditions.
The weight function for a differential operator
|\bm{\alpha}|>0
is then obtained as the derivative
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\!\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{0}}\right)
.
This approach is referred to as
direct derivatives
in the literature
[
40
]
.
Then:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}L^{\bm{0}}_{i}u=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\!\!\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{0}}_{ji}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}w^{\bm{0}}_{ji}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\!\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{0}})\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,.

(39)

RKCM, similarly to RKPM, uses ABFs in form of Taylor monomials multiplied by suitable compactly supported RBFs, hence
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.
This choice turns Eq. (
39
) into the RKCM approximation

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u\coloneq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\left(\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\!\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{0}}+\bm{X}_{ji}\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\varphi_{ji}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{0}}+\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}\cdot\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{0}}\right)\,.

(40)

The order of consistency is the same as in Eq. (
26
), and Eq. (
27
) holds (see Ref.
[
40
]
for details).

The
Gradient Reproducing Kernel Collocation Method (GRKCM)
introduced by Chi et al.
[
44
]
replaces the direct derivatives approximation in RKCM by Eq. (
26
) with the zeroth moment included.
Still, the ABFs are Taylor monomials multiplied by compact RBFs,
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.

The
Local Anistropic Basis Function Method (LABFM)
by King et al.
[
115
,
114
]
is one of the most recent moment-approximation methods.
Originally, the method was formulated without the pre-conditionig of Eq. (
28
)
[
115
]
, but this was added two years later
[
114
]
.
LABFM excludes the zeroth moment of the weight function and emphasizes the choice of ABFs
\bm{W}_{ji}
.
Since the elements of
\bm{X}_{ji}
scale as
h_{i},\,h_{i}^{2},\,h_{i}^{3},\,\ldots
, the ABFs should scale as
h_{i}^{-1},\,h_{i}^{-2},\,h_{i}^{-3},\,\ldots
.
Originally, the ABFs in LABFM were taken to be the partial derivatives of some suitably scaled RBF
\varphi_{ji}\coloneq\varphi\left(\|\bm{x}_{ji}\|_{2},\,h_{i}\right)
evaluated at
\bm{x}_{j}
, thus
[
115
]

\bm{W}_{ji}=\left.\bm{D}\varphi_{ji}\right\rvert_{\bm{x}_{j}}\,.

(41)

While this works in two dimensions (
n=2
), it is unable to ensure linear independence of the rows of the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
for higher dimensions and approximation orders. For example, for
n=2
and
m=4
, the moment matrix is always singular.
Therefore, the more recent formulation of LABFM
[
114
]
constructs ABFs from orthogonal polynomials, specifically Hermite or Legendre polynomials, multiplied by a compact RBF as:

\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{P}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}\,.

(42)

Several choices of
\bm{P}_{ji}
and
\varphi_{ji}
have been studied
[
115
,
114
]
.

A similar approach using ABFs generated by RBF derivatives as in Eq. (
41
) is used in
High-Order Consistent SPH (HOCSPH)
as introduced by Nasar et al.
[
170
]
.
Having roots in SPH, the method differs from LABFM in that it attributes finite volumes to the particles. However, this corresponds to direct node integration, rendering HOCSPH effectively a collocation scheme.

An independent and interesting view on moment approximation methods has resulted from studying the relationship between peridynamics
[
152
,
178
]
and meshfree collocation. This resulted in the formulation of
Generalized Reproducing Kernel Peridynamics (GRKP)
by Hillman et al.
[
92
]
.
Peridynamics is a non-local formulation of continuum mechanics based on the interaction potentials between material points.
The gradients of these potential functions then correspond to the forces acting in the material. They are approximated by weighed summation over neighboring points. This is akin to how meshfree collocation methods approximate derivatives.
GRKP assumes a differential operator of the form

\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}u(\bm{x}^{\prime})g_{m}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime}\,,

(43)

where the
g_{m}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}_{i})
are orthogonal functions that need to be constructed specifically for the approximation order
m
.
The usual ansatz for the
g_{m}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is a product of Taylor monomials
\bm{X}(\bm{x})
and a (normalized and compactly supported) scalar weight function
\psi(\bm{x})
,
thus
\bm{W}(\bm{x})=\bm{X}(\bm{x})\psi(\bm{x})
. With the vector of unknown coefficients

{\Psi}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
,

g_{m}^{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{x})=\bm{W}(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(44)

This resembles the general ABF in meshfree collocation methods as given in Eq. (
12
).
The connection with meshfree collocation becomes obvious when approximating the integral in Eq. (
43
) by direct nodal quadrature with an elementary volume associated with each point. Since the integration volumes (quadrature weights) can be absorbed into the weight functions, as is also done in DC-PSE, Eq. (
43
) becomes identical to the definition of the meshfree collocation operator in Eq. (
6
), replacing
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u_{i}
with
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u
.
Also, similarly to DC-PSE, the zeroth moment is optional, and both formulations are found in the literature
[
92
]
.
This results in an approximation akin to that of DC-PSE in Eq. (
37
), albeit without the pre-conditioning:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right).

(45)

The ABFs are usually chosen as Taylor monomials multiplied by a suitable RBF,
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.

Taken together, the different moment approximation methods mainly differ in the form of the ABFs used and in whether the zeroth moment of the weights is included in the derivation.
Each method’s derivation also brought its own mathematical contributions. For example, the DC-PSE paper
[
200
]
proved formal connections with (corrected) SPH, RKPM and, for the limit of particles lying on a Cartesian grid, compact finite differences. The LABFM papers
[
115
,
114
]
provided a detailed analysis of different ABF choices. RKCM introduced the concept of direct derivatives to strong-form collocation methods
[
4
]
, and GRKP provided a connection with peridynamics
[
92
]
, showing a direct link between meshfree collocation methods and physical systems.

4
Derivation by
\ell_{2}
minimization of the discretization error

An alternative way of obtaining the weights of the discrete operator in Eq. (
6
) is to minimize the truncation error
e_{ji}^{m+1}
of the Taylor expansion in Eq. (
8
) over
\mathcal{N}_{i}
for the desired derivatives
\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}
.
In general, the error can be weighted by a scalar function
\varphi_{ji}
.
Therefore, we solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{E},i}\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(e_{ji}^{m+1}\right)^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}-u_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{E},i}\right)\quad\text{with respect to}\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}.

(46)

The formal solution to this problem as derived in Appendix
0.B
is:

\displaystyle\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k}\varphi_{ki}\bm{X}_{ki}\bm{X}_{ki}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\!\!\!\bm{X}_{ji}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{X}_{ji}\,.

(47)

The moment matrix
\mathbf{M}
naturally appears in the solution of the optimization problem and does not depend on any choice of basis functions. Nevertheless, it is the same moment matrix as obtained in Eq. (
32
) for AOM methods:

\mathbf{M}_{i}\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}\varphi_{ji}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,,

(48)

with
\bm{W}_{ji}\coloneq\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
. This reveals that the error weights
\varphi
play the same role as the ABFs in AOM methods. In the present case, however, one is limited to radially symmetric functions, since the truncation error is a scalar.

The solution to the linear system of equations in Eq. (
47
) contains all derivatives up to and including the
m
-th derivative.
To obtain an approximation for a specific differential operator
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}
in the form of Eq. (
26
), we again use the mapping vector
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
to form the desired differential operator from
\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}
as

\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{X}_{ji}\right)\,.

(49)

Since in the present case the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
(and hence its inverse
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}
) is symmetric
8
8

8

\mathbf{M}=\sum\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\phi
, where
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
is the outer product of two identical vectors, resulting in a symmetric matrix with each term scaled by
\phi
.
, this yields the final approximation:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(50)

Formally, this is the same discrete operator as obtained in Eq. (
26
) from approximation of moments. There are two important differences, though. First, the basis functions
\bm{W}_{ji}
in Eq. (
50
) are given by the Taylor monomials and the error weighting
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
. They cannot be chosen freely. Second, the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
in Eq. (
50
) is symmetric, whereas it need not be symmetric in the AOM formulation (Section
3
).

The same derivation by error minimization can also be performed without the zeroth moment, following the procedure outlined in Section
3.1
.
In that case, the error
e_{ji}^{m+1}
in Eq. (
46
) takes the form
e_{ji}^{m+1}=\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}-u_{ji}
with
u_{ji}=u_{j}-u_{i}
. This replaces
u_{j}
with
u_{ji}
in Eq. (
50
), yielding the same result as in Eq. (
31
).

4.1
Matrix notation

Following the notation introduced in Section
2.1
, the
\ell_{2}
-error minimization approach can also be written in matrix notation.
For this, we additionally define the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{V}_{i}
of the error weights
\varphi_{ij}
for individual particle pairs
(i,j)
:

\mathbf{V}_{i}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\varphi_{ji}\right)_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{i}\times N_{i}}\,.

(51)

With this matrix, the minimization problem in Eq. (
46
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{E},i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(e_{ji}^{m+1}\right)^{2}=\bm{E}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\bm{E}_{i}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}-\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}-\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{E},i}\right)\quad\text{with respect to}\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}.

(52)

Formally solving this minimization problem (see Appendix
0.B.1
), we obtain:

\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}=\left[\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\right]\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\,.

(53)

With the definition in Eq. (
21
) (here for
\mathbf{W}_{i}=\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}
) and the definition of the moment matrix from Eq. (
32
), this yields the final operator:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right),

(54)

which is identical to Eq. (
50
).

4.2
Methods derived by
\ell_{2}
error minimization

The majority of meshfree collocation methods were derived by truncation error minimization. This notably includes the large class of methods based on moving least squares (MLS) minimization, and methods based on generalizing finite differences to scattered nodes. But it also encompasses more specialized methods, e.g., for advection problems or gradient and Laplacian approximation.

4.2.1
Moving least squares methods

The largest and most popular group of methods in this category are the
Moving Least Squares (MLS)
methods. These start from approximating the function itself using Eq. (
6
) for
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{0}
, as originally introduced by Lancester and Salkauskas
[
127
]
:

L_{i}^{\bm{0}}u=\bm{P}_{i}\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{P}_{j}\,.

(55)

Using relative coordinates with respect to
\bm{x}_{i}
leads to a formulation with better condition number:

L_{i}^{\bm{0}}u=\bm{P}(\bm{0})\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{P}_{ji}\,.

(56)

The differential operator
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}
is then approximated by taking the respective derivative of the basis,

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{P}(\mathbf{0})\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{j}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{P}_{ji}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,,

(57)

where we used
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{P}(\bm{0})
, the symmetry of
\mathbf{M}_{i}
, and
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{P}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.
The approximation in Eq. (
57
) is sometimes called
diffusive derivatives

[
172
,
113
,
96
,
247
,
40
]
, since it uses the derivative of the basis instead of directly computing the derivative of the function
u
itself (via the derivative of the
w_{ji}
in the discrete approximation), as is for example done for RKCM in Eq. (
40
).

The same derivative approximation is also used in the
Finite Point Method (FPM)
introduced by Oñate et al.
[
175
]
to describe a class of point-data interpolation schemes that includes Least Squares (LS), Weighted Least Squares (WLS), MLS, and RKPM.
The same authors later extended the MLS formalism by introducing
Multiple Fixed Least Squares (MFLS)

[
180
]
, allowing the

\varphi_{\bm{x}_{i}}
to have different shapes at different particles
\bm{x}_{i}
.
When evaluating
\varphi(\bm{x})
at an off-particle location, it is interpolated from the
\varphi_{\bm{x}_{i}}(\bm{x})
at the neighboring particles.

To our knowledge, every available variant of MLS or FPM used Taylor monomials in the ABFs, i.e.,
\bm{P}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}
and thus
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}

[
176
,
180
,
179
,
177
,
181
]
.
While FPM is usually presented in matrix notation, MLS is more commonly found in vector form.

A variant of the classic MLS approach is the
Least Squares Meshfree Method (LSMFM)
introduced by Youn and co-workers
[
185
,
257
]
. It differs from classic MLS in that it replaces the diffuse derivative with a direct derivative, similar to RKCM.
Starting from Eq. (
55
),
taking
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}(w_{ji}^{\bm{0}})
results in Eq. (
40
).
While this is formally the same approximation as in RKCM, LSMFM derives it via approximation-error minimization instead of moment approximation. The result, however, is identical, and LSMFM also uses Taylor monomials as basis functions.

Levin
[
133
]
generalized MLS approaches to arbitrary linear bounded operators in the
Generalized Moving Least Squares (GMLS)
method, which was further elaborated in several follow-up works
[
245
,
164
,
85
]
. It is based on a thorough mathematical formalism. Assume the function
u(\bm{x})
to be
m
times continuously differentiable,
u\in C^{m}(\Omega)
for some
m\geq 0
9
9

9

Here,
m=0
is allowed because the method approximates the sampled values at the particles and then computes derivatives of the approximation. No derivatives of
u
are computed.
.
In GMLS, the function
u
is approximated by a set of
sampling functionals

\Lambda(u)=\left\{\lambda_{j}(u)\right\}_{j=0}^{N}\in C^{m}(\Omega)^{*}
where
C^{m}(\Omega)^{*}
is the dual space to
C^{m}(\Omega)
.
In the simplest case, the sampling functionals are point-wise evaluations of
u
, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}(u)=\delta_{\bm{x}_{i}}(u)=u(\bm{x}_{i})=u_{i}
.
For a given
\lambda(u)\in C^{m}(\Omega)^{*}
, GMLS determines the approximation
\lambda_{i}(u)^{h}\approx\lambda(u)[\bm{x}_{i}]
at resolution
h
as a linear combination of the
\left\{\lambda_{j}(u)\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}
:

\lambda_{i}(u)^{h}\coloneq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{j}(u)w_{j}(\lambda)\,,

(58)

which is analogous to
L_{i}^{\bm{0}}u
.
Following Eq. (
58
), GMLS approximates any linear bounded operator
\tau_{i}
at
\bm{x}_{i}
as

\tau_{i}^{h}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{j}(u)\widetilde{w}_{j}(\lambda)\,.

(59)

In the GMLS literature, Eq. (
59
) is mostly written in its functional matrix form as

\lambda(u)^{h}=\lambda(u^{*})=\bm{P}(\bm{x})^{\!\top}\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}(u^{*})\,,

(60)

where
\bm{P}(\bm{x})
is the (stacked) vector of basis functions,
\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}(u^{*})
the (stacked) vector of coefficients, and
u^{*}
the solution to the local
\ell_{2}
-minimization problem

u^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\lambda_{j}(u)-\lambda_{j}(q)\right)^{2}\varphi_{ji}\,,

(61)

over the space
\mathcal{V}_{h}=\text{span}\{P_{1},\,\ldots,\,P_{p}\}
spanned by the basis
P_{i}\in\bm{P}
of dimension
\dim({\mathcal{V}_{h}})=p
(
p
depends on
m
and
n
as discussed after Eq. (
6
)).
This optimization problem is solved as in Appendix
0.B
, and the approximation
\tau_{i}^{h}
to the operator
\tau_{i}
is obtained by applying the operator to the basis functions while keeping the coefficients
\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}(u^{*})
constant.
In meshfree collocation methods, the operators of interest are differential operators at the locations of the collocation points
\bm{x}_{i}
, thus
\tau_{i}u=\left.\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\right\rvert_{i}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}
.
The GMLS procedure then yields the diffuse derivatives approximation
[
164
]

\tau_{i}^{h}u=(\tau_{i}\bm{P})^{\!\top}\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}(u^{*})=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{j}(u^{*})\widetilde{w}_{j}(\lambda)\,,

(62)

which amounts to a functional form of Eq. (
57
) as:

L_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}(u)=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{P}(\mathbf{0})\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\lambda_{j}(u)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\lambda_{j}(\bm{P}_{i})\,,

(63)

with symmetric moment matrix

\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\lambda_{j}(\bm{P}_{i})\lambda_{j}(\bm{P}_{i})^{\top}\varphi_{ji}\,.

(64)

For the simplest sampling functional
\lambda_{i}=\delta_{\bm{x}_{i}}
, this simplifies to
\lambda_{j}(\bm{P}_{i})=\bm{P}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})=\bm{P}_{ji}
and
\lambda_{j}(u)=u_{j}
.
The mapping vector appears as
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}\bm{P}(\mathbf{0})
with
\bm{P}(\mathbf{0})=\lambda_{i}(\bm{P}_{i})
.
Popular choice for the basis
\bm{P}_{ji}
are scaled Taylor monomials or Bernstein polynomials
[
122
]
.

Trask et al.
[
232
]
connected GMLS with compact finite differences in the
Compact Moving Least Squares (CMLS)
method. The goal was to derive more compactly supported meshfree stencils, taking inspiration from Compact Finite Difference Methods (CFDM)
[
131
,
62
]
.
For CFDM, E proposed using the expression of the truncation error for the very equation to be solved to improve the approximation accuracy without increasing the stencil size
[
62
]
.
Another class of CFDM schemes, proposed by Lele
[
131
]
, uses an implicit formulation in which the derivatives at grid points are not computed directly, but as part of a system of equations involving neighboring points.
Combining these two ideas, CMLS achieves compactly supported meshfree sencils by including an additional problem-dependent penalty into the optimization problem from Eq. (
61
) while keeping the moment matrix invertible.
This has been reported to halve the support
|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
of the MLS operators
[
232
]
.
To illustrate the CMLS approach, consider the linear boundary value problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}u

\displaystyle=f,\qquad\forall\bm{x}\in\Omega\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}u

\displaystyle=g,\qquad\forall\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\,.

(65)

In CMLS, Eq. (
61
) includes penalization terms reflecting the problem to be solved

u^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left[\left(\lambda_{j}(u)-\lambda_{j}(q)\right)^{2}+\varepsilon_{\Omega}\left(f_{j}-\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}q\right)^{2}+\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}\varepsilon_{\partial\Omega}\left(g_{j}-\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}q\right)^{2}\right]\varphi_{ji}\,,

(66)

where
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
and
\varepsilon_{\partial\Omega}
are interior and boundary regularization parameters, respectively, and
\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}
is the boundary indicator function
\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}=1
if
\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\Omega
and
0
otherwise.
The first term is the standard GMLS term responsible for the approximation quality.
The second and third terms are the solution and boundary residuals as in CFDM.
The optimization problem in Eq. (
66
) is solved as shown in Appendix
0.B
, resulting in

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}+\epsilon_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji}f_{i}\varphi_{ji}+\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}\epsilon_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji}g_{i}\varphi_{ji}\right)\cdot(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}})\,,

(67)

\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{P}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}+\epsilon_{\Omega}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji})(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji}^{\top})\varphi_{ji}+\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}\epsilon_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji})(\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji}^{\top})\varphi_{ji}\right).

(68)

Both the resulting operator approximation and the moment matrix include contributions from the additional penalty terms.
The regularization parameters are used to weight the terms. Since the penalties act as soft constraints, however, the boundary condition may not be exactly fulfilled. Therefore, for points located on the boundary, an additional hard constraint

\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}q_{i}=g_{i}\,,\qquad\forall i:\;\bm{x}_{i}\in\partial\Omega\,,

(69)

is used to exactly enforce the boundary condition
[
232
]
.
This ensures that the polynomial approximation constructed at the interior points, with the boundary condition imposed only as a soft constraint, fulfills the boundary condition exactly when evaluated at a boundary point.
This leads to an additional boundary constraint on the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
.
Following the notation introduced in Section
4
(in particular Eqs. (
47
) and (
49
)), we write Eq. (
67
) as

L_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}u\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,,

(70)

where the derivatives
\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}
are obtained by solving the linear system of equations
\mathbf{M}_{i}\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}=\bm{R}_{i}
with right-hand side

\bm{R}_{i}\coloneq\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}+\epsilon_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}\bm{P}_{ji}^{\top}f_{i}\varphi_{ji}+\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}\epsilon_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{j}^{\top}g_{i}\varphi_{ji}\right).

(71)

Including the boundary condition constraint, the problem takes the form

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}_{i}&\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{ij}\\
\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}\bm{P}_{ij}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert}_{i}\coloneq\left[\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i},\,\lambda\right],\quad\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i}\coloneq\left[\bm{R}_{i},\,g_{i}\right].

Solving the linear system of equations
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}\widetilde{\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert}_{i}=\widetilde{\bm{R}}_{i}
, we obtain the approximation

L_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}u\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\widetilde{\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert}_{i}\,,

(72)

for which
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}^{h}u_{i}=L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=f_{i}
for all
\{i:\bm{x}_{i}\in\Omega\}
and
L_{i}^{\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}}u=g_{i}
for all
\{i:\bm{x}_{i}\in\partial\Omega\}
.
We can therefore write the resulting CMLS scheme as:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}+\bm{Q}_{ji}\right)\cdot(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}})\,,\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{P}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}+\mathbf{Q}_{i}\,,

(73)

where
\bm{Q}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

and
\mathbf{Q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
represent the additional terms originating from the penalties.
Taylor monomials or Legendre polynomials are used as the basis functions
[
232
]
. For particles on a regular Cartesian grid, the CMLS approximation becomes identical to Lele CFDM
[
232
]
.

Relaxing the conditions on the number and distribution of neighbors is the aim of
Modified Moving Least Squares (MMLS)
as introduced by Joldes et al.
[
107
,
106
]
.
Like CMLS, MMLS also includes additional penalties but uses a process akin to
Tikhonov–Miller
regularization to do so.
Unlike standard
Tikhonov–Miller
regularization, though, MMLS can assign individual positive weights for the additional constraints.
This allows for higher-order approximations with less restrictive conditions on the size of the stencil.
MMLS also regularizes the moment matrix in cases where it becomes singular in classic MLS. For this, it uses sparsity to select the solution in which the most coefficients are zero.
This decreases the degree of the polynomial interpolation while preserving well-posedness of the problem.
In contrast to CMLS, the regularization in MMLS is problem-independent, as it directly acts on the coefficients of the polynomial basis functions.
This modifies Eq. (
61
) to:

u^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left[\left(\lambda_{j}(u)-\lambda_{j}(q)\right)^{2}\varphi_{ji}+\bm{\mu}\cdot(\hat{\bm{\Psi}}_{i})^{2}\right]\,,

(74)

where
(\mu_{i})_{i=1}^{s}\coloneq\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
is the vector of positive regularization weights for the subset of
s
coefficients
\hat{\bm{\Psi}}_{i}
of the leading-order terms
10
10

10

We illustrate this in an example. Using a second-order basis (
m=2
) in
n=2
Cartesian dimensions, we have

u^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\lambda_{j}(u)-\lambda_{j}(q)\right)^{2}\varphi_{ji}+\mu_{x^{2}}\left(\Psi_{i,x^{2}}\right)^{2}+\mu_{xy}\left(\Psi_{i,xy}\right)^{2}+\mu_{y^{2}}\left(\Psi_{i,y^{2}}\right)^{2}

for the leading-order (here second-order) terms
x^{2}
,
xy
, and
y^{2}
.

.
Using small positive
0<\mu_{i}\ll 1
ensures that if the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is close to singular, the coefficients for the leading-order terms become zero. Small regularization weights also ensure that the solution remains close to the standard MLS solution when the moment matrix is not singular. However, the important question of how to choose the regularization weights
\bm{\mu}
is rarely discussed in the literature. In the original publication
[
106
]
, the authors use a constant
\mu=10^{-7}
.
Solving then the optimization problem in Eq. (
74
) as in Appendix
0.B
, the MMLS moment matrix becomes:

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}+\mathbf{H}_{\mu}=\mathbf{M}_{i}+\mathbf{H}_{\mu}\,,

(75)

with
\mathbf{H}_{\mu}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{0}_{p-s},\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\bm{0}_{p-s}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-s}
a zero vector.
The resulting operator approximation is formally identical to Eq. (
50
) but with the above moment matrix.
The MMLS moment matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
generally has the same dimensions
as the GMLS moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
from Eq. (
64
) but requires fewer neighboring particles.
As ABFs, MMLS also mostly uses polynomials
\bm{P}_{ji}
multiplied by a RBF
\varphi_{ji}
.

Based on the general theme of MLS or regularized MLS, a large number of modified methods have been proposed.
The
Interpolating Moving Least Squares (IMLS
) method introduced by Lancester and Salkaukas
[
127
]
adds the soft constraint
w_{ji}\approx\delta_{ji}
to favor interpolating weight functions.
This allows for direct node-value assignment when imposing Dirichlet boundary condition.
This idea was later also extended to MMLS, resulting in
Interpolating Modified Moving Least Squares (IMMLS)

[
147
]
.
Another group of methods called
Improved Moving Least Squares (IMLS)

[
43
,
140
]
or
Improved Interpolating Moving Least Squares (IIMLS)

[
241
,
139
]
allows for the interpolating weight functions to be modified by any RBF
\varphi_{ji}
.
To avoid ill-conditioned moment matrices,
Adaptive Orthogonal IIMLS (AO-IIMLS)

[
243
]
uses an adaptive orthogonalization algorithm to remove small entries.
Slightly different regularization techniques were presented as
Regularized Moving Least Squares (RMLS)
and
Regularized Improved Interpolating Moving Least Squares (RIIMLS)
by Wang et al.
[
242
]
. RMLS and RIIMLS are closely related to MMLS and IIMLS, but use
direct Tikhonov–Miller regularization.
The
Least Squares Collocation Meshless Method (LSCM)

[
256
]
introduces auxiliary points where the approximation is not evaluated, but the solution must still be satisfied.
An attempt to unify the many MLS-type methods was made by introducing the
Meshless Local Strong Form Method (MLSM)

[
118
]
, which also highlighted the wide range of basis functions
\bm{P}_{ji}
that can be used in MLS-type approaches.

4.2.2
Generalized finite-difference methods

Besides MLS-type methods, another large class of meshfree collocation schemes derived by error minimization are
Meshless Finite Difference Methods (MFDM)
. Originally introduced by Milewsky
[
160
]
, MFDM take the viewpoint of finite difference stencils, which they generalize to irregular collocation point distributions.
Recent work in this direction focused on deriving higher-order approximations that only require inverting lower-order moment matrices
[
161
,
162
]
.
Instead of approximating
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
with some order of consistency
m
, this is done by first obtaining a lower-order approximation
L^{\bm{\alpha},L}_{i}
of order
L<m
, which is then extrapolated to the higher-order approximation

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=L^{\bm{\alpha},L}_{i}u-\Delta_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(76)

with a correction
\Delta_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
.
The correction is computed using Richardson extrapolation. For this, the Taylor expansion in Eq. (
8
) is split into terms of order
\leq L
and terms of order
>L
:

u_{j}=\bm{X}_{ji}^{\leq L}\cdot\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}+\bm{X}_{ji}^{>L}\cdot\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}+e_{ji}^{m+1},

(77)

where

\bm{X}_{ji}^{\leq L}=\left\{x_{1,ji}^{\alpha_{1}}/\bm{\alpha}!\ldots x_{n,ji}^{\alpha_{n}}/\bm{\alpha}!\right\}_{|\bm{\alpha}|=0}^{L},\quad\bm{X}_{ji}^{>L}=\left\{x_{1,ji}^{\alpha_{1}}/\bm{\alpha}!\ldots x_{n,ji}^{\alpha_{n}}/\bm{\alpha}!\right\}_{|\bm{\alpha}|>L}^{m},

(78)

\bm{D}^{\leq L}u=\left\{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\right\}_{\bm{|\alpha|}=0}^{L},\quad\bm{D}^{>L}u=\left\{\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}u\right\}_{\bm{|\alpha|}>L}^{m}.

(79)

A recommended choice for the split point is
L=|\bm{\alpha}|
for a target order of
m=2L

[
162
]
.
This then allows splitting the optimization problem in Eq. (
46
) into lower- and higher-order terms:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{E},i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(e_{ji}^{m+1}\right)^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(\bm{X}_{ji}^{\leq L}\cdot\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}+\bm{X}_{ji}^{>L}\cdot\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}-u_{ji}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{E},i}\right)\quad\text{with respect to}\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}.

(80)

Performing the minimization only with respect to the low-order terms results in

\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{ji}u_{j}-\bm{\Delta}_{i}\,,

(81)

where the first term on the right-hand side represents the low-order derivatives, and
\bm{\Delta}_{i}
is the higher-order correction

\bm{\Delta}_{i}=\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{ji}\left(\bm{X}_{ji}^{>L}\cdot\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}\right).

(82)

Following the general recipe of Richardson extrapolation, the unknown higher-order approximations
\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}
are expressed as nested low-order approximations:

\displaystyle\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{D}^{\leq L}\mathbf{P}_{t}\right)\left(\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}\mathbf{P}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{ji}\mathbf{P}_{t}\right)\left(\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{j}\mathbf{P}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{ji}\mathbf{P}_{t}\right)\left(\displaystyle\sum_{s}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{sj}u_{s}\mathbf{P}_{u}-\bm{\Delta}_{j}\mathbf{P}_{u}\right)\,,

which we expanded in the second and third lines by substituting the discrete operator. The matrices
\mathbf{P}_{t}
and
\mathbf{P}_{u}
are non-unique permutation matrices.
The recursive dependence on the correction
\bm{\Delta}
is approximated by performing a finite number of iterations
k=1,2,\ldots
of

\left(\left.\bm{D}^{>L}u\right\rvert_{i}\right)^{(k+1)}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{ji}\mathbf{P}_{t}\right)\left(\displaystyle\sum_{s}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{sj}u_{s}\mathbf{P}_{u}-\bm{\Delta}_{j}^{(k)}\mathbf{P}_{u}\right)\,,

(83)

with the lower-order approximation as a starting point,
\bm{\Delta}^{(0)}=\left.\bm{D}^{\leq L}u\right\rvert_{i}
.
This iteration converges for contractive maps. Whether the map above is contractive or not, howevever, depends on the distribution of particles in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
To select a particular derivative
\bm{\alpha}
, one again uses the mapping vector
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
as in Eq. (
49
).
For further details, as well as for higher-order corrections of boundary conditions, we refer to the original works
[
160
,
162
]
.

Several related variants of meshfree finite-difference methods exist. Among them the
Finite Difference Particle Method (FDPM
)
[
244
]
, which uses the same minimization procedure as MFDM but excludes the zeroth moment.
A variant that includes the zeroth moment is the
Particle Difference Method (PDM)

[
248
]
, also sometimes called
Particle Derivative Approximation (PDA)
. While essentially identical to MFDM, an important contribution of PDM was the extension of the approach to problems with discontinuities by introducing non-smooth RBFs
\varphi_{ji}
; the method is then also sometimes referred to as
Extended Particle Difference Method (EPDM)
.

In addition, one branch of the
Finite Pointset Method (FPsM)
has been derived by minimization of the approximation error. The name
Finite Pointset Method
has been used in the literature to refer to two distinct approaches. The first branch uses error minimization
[
124
,
227
,
228
]
, whereas the second branch follows from minimization of weights
[
59
,
235
,
149
,
1
]
and is discussed in Section
5
. Interestingly, both branches originate from the same authors.
The first branch of FPsM
[
125
]
, which falls into the category of this chapter, accounts for the specific problem to be solved by augmenting the optimization functional with additional penalty terms
[
195
,
199
,
78
]
. This is similar to the approach in CMLS as presented in Section
4.2.1
, but it predates CMLS.
Therefore, the FPsM was probably the first to introduce problem-specific corrections to meshfree collocation methods. However, the motivation for doing so was different from that of CMLS.
In FPsM, the penalty terms were motivated by solving a second-order boundary problem
[
125
]
, and not by reducing the stencil support.
FPsM therefore does not use the notion of stencil coefficients, but it directly assembles the global matrix of the system.
Considering again the model problem from Eq. (
65
), the minimization in FPsM is:

u^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left[\left(\lambda_{j}\left(u\right)-\lambda_{j}\left(q\right)\right)^{2}\varphi_{ji}+\left(f_{j}-\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}q\right)^{2}+\chi_{j,\partial\Omega}\left(g_{j}-\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}q\right)^{2}\right]\,,

(84)

where, in contrast to Eq. (
66
), the penalty terms do not have a relaxation prefactor and are independent of the weight function
\varphi_{j}
.
In some works, the problem-dependent penalty terms in Eq. (
84
) are understood to act globally
[
216
]
. This then results in a field-approximation method rather than a local differential operator, providing an interesting connection between meshfree collocation methods and global surrogate methods like
Optimizing a Discrete Loss (ODIL)

[
112
]
and global polynomial surrogates
[
214
,
215
]
.
Ultimately, this links meshfree collocation methods to
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN)

[
191
,
32
]
, a connection that might be worth exploring more deeply in the future.

Finally, we mention the
Generalized Finite Difference Method (GFDM)

[
143
,
224
]
, which can be derived by minimization of the approximation error
[
77
,
20
,
255
,
134
]
. However, more recent works derive it by minimization of weights
[
221
,
216
,
220
]
,
which is why we discuss it in Section
5
.

4.2.3
One-sided and upwind methods

A major limitation of collocation methods in Eulerian point clouds is that they are unconditionally unstable in explicit time stepping when discretizing the advection operator
\bm{v}\cdot\nabla
for some flow velocity field
\bm{v}(\bm{x})
. The three classic remedies are to either: (1) use a Lagrangian frame of reference, where the collocation points move with
\bm{v}
, (2) use one-sided neighborhoods
\mathcal{N}_{i}
containing only neighbors in the
upwind
direction, i.e., the direction from which the flow comes, or (3) use staggered grids where
\bm{v}
is discretized in-between grid points. Any meshfree collocation method can trivially be used in a Lagrangian setting. For upwinding and staggering, specific methods have been proposed.

An upwind meshfree collocation method is the
Least-Squares Kinetic Upwind Method (LSKUM)
introduced by Ghosh and Deshpande
[
79
,
81
]
and later extended to higher order as q-LSKUM
[
58
,
80
]
.
The method was originally designed to numerically solve the Euler equation of gas dynamics and the Navier–Stokes equations of compressible viscous fluids.
LSKUM exploits the physical relation between Boltzmann transport and the Navier–Stokes equations to solve compressible flow problems by kinetic flux vector splitting
[
57
,
154
]
. Hence, the method is also sometimes called q-KFVS referring to Kinetic Flux Vector Splitting
[
80
]
. The fluxes in the fluid-flow equations are the derivatives to be approximated.
LSKUM uses one-sided neighborhoods to compute stable approximations of the flux vector.
Following classic grid-based upwind schemes, only half of the neighbors of any given particle
i
are used.
We denote this truncated neighborhood by
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\beta\pm}
, where
\beta
stands for a direction in the coordinate system, e.g.,
\beta=x,\,y,\,z
, and the
\pm
denotes which half of the neighborhood is used.
A similar solution using one-sided particle neighborhoods was introduced by Eldredge et al.
[
63
]
for PSE.
The coordinate directions are chosen from the signs of the flow velocity components
\bm{v}=(v_{x},\,v_{y},\,\dots)
such that the flow propagates from the neighboring nodes
j
to the receiving node
i
, but not vice versa.
Apart from this difference, LSKUM uses the standard truncation-error minimization derivation with ABFs
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
excluding the zeroth moment.

Largely based on LSKUM, the
Kinetic Meshless Method (KMM)
was introduced by Praveen and Deshpande
[
34
,
188
]
to use staggering instead of one-sided kernels. This leads to smaller neighborhood radii and renders KMM rotationally invariant, simplifying boundary condition treatment. Since the neighborhoods are isotropic, and the condition number of the moment matrix is generally smaller than in LSKUM, the accuracy and robustness (wavenumber dissipation) of the method are also improved
[
188
]
.
KMM achieves this by replacing the Boltzmann-transport fluxes of LSKUM with a MLS-type approach to approximate derivatives over midpoint function values
u_{ji/2}\coloneq u((\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})/2)
. This advection stabilization is akin to classic staggered-grid finite differences and is sometimes referred to as
Dual Least-Squares (DLS) method

[
188
]
.
It is based on choosing the midpoint value from the appropriate side

u_{ij/2}=\begin{cases}u_{i},&\quad\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{e}}_{ji}\geq 0\\
u_{j},&\quad\bm{v}\cdot\hat{\bm{e}}_{ji}<0\,,\end{cases}

(85)

where
\bm{v}
is the flow velocity and
\hat{\bm{e}}_{ji}
is the unit vector pointing from
\bm{x}_{i}
to
\bm{x}_{j}
.
Evaluating the Taylor expansion in Eq. (
8
) at
u_{ji/2}
instead of
u_{j}
, the ABFs then become the Taylor monomials evaluated between
\bm{x}_{i}
and
(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})/2
, multiplied by the RBFs
\varphi_{ji}
. Hence, with
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji/2}\varphi_{ji}
, we obtain the final KMM approximation:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}(u_{ji/2}-u_{i})\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}})\,,\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji/2}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,.

(86)

Compared to the canonical approximation in Eq. (
50
), the values are supported at different locations, and the vector of Taylor monomials (and hence the moment matrix) is correspondingly modified.
Higher-order versions of KMM reconstruct
u_{ji/2}
using a linear scheme from the seminal five-paper series
“Towards the ultimate conservative difference scheme”

[
236
]
, forming a one-parameter family known as the
k-schemes
.
For
k=-1
, this includes a one-sided second-order scheme to interpolate variables from neighboring particles to the midpoints
(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{x}_{i})/2

before computing derivatives using Eq. (
86
)
[
237
]
.

4.2.4
Sepcialized gradient and Laplacian methods

The methods described so far provide approximations to arbitrary linear differential operators
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}
. In addition to these general numerical methods, there are also several contributions in the literature that target only specific derivatives, mostly the gradient or Laplace operator.
These are usually subsumed under the name
Lagrangian Differencing Dynamics (LDD)

[
12
,
186
]
and were originally proposed by Basic et al. under the name
Renormalized Meshless Operator (RMO)

[
11
]
.
LDD blends ideas from moment approximation (Section
3
) and MLS
with the aim of finding a compromise between SPH-type approximations, which do not provide sufficient accuracy, and accurate collocation schemes, which are computationally more expensive.
The central tool for this is a
correction matrix
, also known from several corrected SPH methods
[
192
,
28
]
.

We denote this correction matrix by
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{1}
, since it corresponds to the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
with only linear (
m=1
) terms present. The zeroth moment is excluded.
Together with the symmetry of the weight function
\varphi_{ji}
, this allows constructing compromise approximations. In the original work
[
11
]
,
absolutely normalized
weight functions
\psi_{ji}=\varphi_{ji}/\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}
were used, but in more recent works
[
12
,
186
,
13
]
the same authors use a canonical weight function
\varphi_{ji}
as introduced above in Section
2
.
While the derivation of LDD somewhat deviates from the derivation presented at the beginning of this section, it leads to the same approximation for the gradient
L_{i}^{\nabla}\approx\nabla
as in Eq. (
47
) with
m=1
.
For the Laplace operator
\partial^{\bm{\alpha}}=\nabla^{2}
, LDD presents three approximations.
The first, called
naïve approximation
, is:

L_{i}^{\nabla^{2}\text{naive}}u=2n\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{\varphi_{ji}}{\|\bm{x}_{ji}\|^{2}}\left(u_{ji}-\bm{x}_{ji}\cdot L_{i}^{\nabla}u\right).

(87)

The approximation error of Eq. (
87
) consists of the first-order error of the gradient approximation
L_{i}^{\nabla}u
and higher-order truncation contributions from the Taylor expansion
[
11
]
.
This is only accurate for large numbers of neighbors
|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
and if the points in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
are regularly distributed. Since these conditions are rather limiting in practice, the
sum approximation
has been proposed:

L_{i}^{\nabla^{2}\text{sum}}u=2n\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}u_{ji}\left(1-\bm{x}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{1}\right)^{-1}\bm{O}_{i}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\lVert\bm{x}_{ji}\rVert^{2}\left(1-\bm{x}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{1}\right)^{-1}\bm{O}_{i}\right)}\,,

(88)

where
\bm{O}_{i}
is the
offset vector
of particle
i

\bm{O}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\bm{x}_{ji}\,,

(89)

pointing from
\bm{x}_{i}
to the
weighted center
of the neighborhood. For locally regular and symmetric (around
\bm{x}_{i}
) neighbor distributions,
\bm{O}=\mathbf{0}
. Then, and when using only the nearest neighbors, Eq. (
88
) reduces to the compact second-order central finite difference stencil for the Laplacian.
Compared to Eq. (
87
), the sum approximation does not contain the first-order gradient error and does not require explicitly computing the gradient values. The resulting approximation error is an average of the error over the neighborhood. The third Laplacian approximation, called
least-squares approximation
, further improves accuracy by employing a MLS procedure over the offset vector.
This defines a modified moment matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
, which in LDD is called
renormalization matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\bm{Q}_{ji}\bm{Q}_{ji}^{\top}\,,

(90)

where

\bm{Q}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}-\bm{x}_{ji}^{\top}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{1}\right)^{-1}\bm{x}_{ji}\left(\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}\right)^{\top}\,,

(91)

and
\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}
are the square subcomponents of
\bm{X}_{ji}

\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}\coloneq\left\{x_{\beta,ji}^{2}\right\}_{\beta=1}^{n}\,.

(92)

From this, one obtains a Laplacian approximation of the form
[
11
]
:

L_{i}^{\nabla^{2}\text{LS}}u=\bm{I}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{i}^{-1}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}2\varphi_{ji}\bm{Q}_{ji}\left(u_{ji}-\bm{x}_{ji}\cdot L_{i}^{\nabla}u\right)\,,

(93)

with
\bm{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a vector of ones.
In LDD, this is referred to as
(full) inversion approximation
of the Laplace operator.
It is computationally inefficient, since several loops over the neighbors are required to compute all parts of the expression.
A computationally more efficient formulation of this approximation is obtained by modifying the moment matrix as

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\varphi_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}\left(\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}\right)^{\top}.

(94)

This leads to:

L_{i}^{\nabla^{2}\textrm{LS}}u=\bm{I}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}^{-1}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}2\varphi_{ji}\bm{Q}_{ji}\left(u_{ji}-\bm{x}_{ji}\cdot L_{i}^{\nabla}u\right).

(95)

Now, unlike in Eq. (
93
), both
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{1}
can be computed in the same loop, but the gradient
L_{i}^{\nabla}u
is again required. In LDD, this is referred to as
(basic) inversion approximation
.
Both least-squares approximations, Eqs. (
93
) and (
95
), run into problems with the condition number of the matrix when some elements of the vectors
\bm{Q}_{ji}
or
\bm{X}_{ji}^{2}
are close to zero
[
11
]
.

4.2.5
Summary

Summarizing the many variants of meshfree collocation methods derived by
\ell_{2}
minimization of the approximation error, we find that most of them build directly or indirectly on MLS
[
127
]
.
With the exception of LSMFM
[
185
,
257
]
, which uses direct derivatives,
they all first approximate the function
u
itself and then approximate derivatives of
u
by computing derivatives of this approximation, an approach known as
diffuse derivatives

[
40
]
.
Notable extensions to this recipe were provided by GMLS, CMLS, and MMLS.
GMLS
[
245
,
164
]

extended the approach to general linear bounded operators and provided a more solid mathematical foundation.
CMLS
[
232
]
and MMLS
[
107
,
106
]

introduced additional penalty terms that favor more compactly supported stencils and improve the condition number of the MLS system.
MFDM
[
160
]

introduced the idea of iterative Richardson extrapolation to construct higher-order approximations by inverting only lower-order moment matrices.
One branch of FPsM
[
124
,
227
,
228
]
introduced problem-specific penalties to solve second-order boundary problems, and some versions of GFDM were also derived by error minimization
[
77
,
20
,
255
,
134
]
. Upwind and staggering methods for Eulerian advection solvers were introduced by LSKUM
[
79
,
81
]

and KMM
[
34
,
188
]
,
respectively, and PDM
[
248
]

showed an extension to discontinuous solutions with non-smooth weight functions.
LDD
[
12
,
186
]
,
finally, presented a hybrid derivation for discretized gradient and Laplace operators following a conceptually different approach, which can avoid inversion of the moment matrix.

5
Derivation by
\ell_{2}
minimization of weights

A third way to derive the weights for the discrete operator in Eq. (
6
) is by applying
\ell_{2}
minimization directly to the weights.
This considers the optimization problem of finding the smallest weights such that the moment conditions from Section
3
are satisfied up to and including order
m
, hence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{ji}}\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u^{*}\right\rvert_{i}\quad\forall u^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{m}\,,

(96)

where
\mathbb{P}_{m}
is the linear space of polynomials of degree at most
m
with elements
u^{*}
.
The constraint enforcing derivative approximation can equivalently be written in terms of the scaled Taylor monomials, resulting in:

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{B}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(97)

The reason why we divide by
\varphi_{ji}
in Eq. (
96
), rather than multiplying, is purely notational, as this leads to the same form of the final approximation as in Section
4
.

The formal solution as derived in Appendix
0.C
is:

w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\varphi_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},\qquad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}\varphi_{ji}\,.

(98)

With this, we obtain the same discrete differential operator as in Section
4
:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right).

(99)

Also, as in Section
4
, the basis of the weight functions is fixed to
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
, and the moment matrix
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is symmetric. This makes the approach less general than the approximation of moments discussed in Section
3
.
A difference from Section
4
is that in the present case the form of the weights is determined by the choice of the functional
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}
, and they amount to inverse MLS weights.
This approach is sometimes referred to as
polynomial method

[
222
]
, and its connection with approximation-error minimization has been explored
[
216
]
.

5.1
Matrix notation

In the matrix formulation introduced in Section
2.1
, the optimization problem from Eq. (
96
) reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{X}^{\top}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},

(100)

where
\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}
is easy to compute, since
\mathbf{V}_{i}
is a diagonal matrix.
The formal solution to this minimization problem, as derived in Appendix
0.C
, is:

\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(101)

With these weights, the discretized differential operator

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)

(102)

is exactly the same as in Eq. (
99
), albeit in matrix notation.

5.2
Methods derived by
\ell_{2}
minimization of weights

The two methods discussed below were both originally derived by
\ell_{2}
error minimization (see Section
4.2
). However, they are nowadays more commonly presented by
\ell_{2}
minimization of weights, which is why we discuss them here.

Reaching all the way back to the very roots of strong form collocation methods, the
Generalized Finite Difference Method (GFDM)
was introduced by Liszka and Orkisz
[
143
,
141
]
. It still is a popular version of MFD as inspired by the work of Jensen
[
102
]
and of Swartz and Wendroff
[
224
]
.
Since its inception, various improvements have been made to GFDM in different applicaition areas.
The method was originally formulated using approximation-error minimization (Section
4
), but in recent years it was mostly presented according to the derivation in this section.
For recent formulations and applications of GFDM, we refer to Refs.
[
221
,
216
,
220
]
.
Therein, the method is derived exactly as presented above, with Taylor monomials multiplied by a suitable window function as ABFs
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.
The zeroth moment is typically excluded when deriving GFDM by approximation-error minimization but included when deriving the method by minimization of weights.

Since its introduction, GFDM has been extended in multiple directions. This included the possibility of using non-smooth basis functions in addition to polynomials (not RBFs)
[
220
]
, using
control cells

to enforce local flux conservation
[
221
]
or to address challenges arising from discontinuities in the coefficients of the PDE
[
120
]
. In addition, a central-weight control mechanism has been proposed to guarantee operator positivity
[
216
]
, establishing a connection between GFDM and FPsM
[
76
,
105
]
.
Finally, positive GFDM operators have been derived with better computational efficiency and stability
[
203
,
31
]
, and higher-order GFDM operators have been derived
[
121
]
.

The second method in the present category is one branch of the
Finite Pointset Method (FPsM
). The FPsM was initially introduced by Kuhnert and Tiwari
[
125
]
using error minimization (see Section
4
) and has since undergone numerous improvements and extensions
[
124
,
227
,
228
,
229
,
230
]
.
There are two distinct branches of the FPsM method: the first follows an error-minimization approach, while the second uses minimizaton of weights. We discussed the first branch in Section
4.2.2
. Here, we focus on the second branch of FPsM, derived by minimization of weights
[
59
,
235
,
149
,
1
]
.
This branch of FPsM, instead of including a problem-specific PDE penalty term, introduces a constraint requiring the weight function at the center particle
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ii}
to be negative
[
59
]
. This improves the condition number of the linear equation system for the weight coefficients.
The additional constraint is added to Eq. (
97
) as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{1}{\varphi_{ji}}\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{B}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},\;w_{ii}^{\bm{\alpha}}=-\frac{\epsilon}{h_{i}^{2}}\,,

(103)

where
\epsilon
is a small non-negative number, and
h_{i}
is the
stencil size
, i.e., the radius of the support of
\varphi_{ji}
.
To cover the additional constraint with the notation introduced above, we define an extended vector of Taylor monomials (which are the basis functions used in FPsM)
\bm{X}_{ji}^{+}
and an extended version of the mapping vector
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, as:

\bm{X}_{ji}^{+}=\left[\bm{X}_{ji},\,\delta_{ji}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1},\quad\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha},+}=\left[\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},\,\epsilon/h_{i}^{2}\right]\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{p+1},

(104)

where
\delta_{ji}
is the Kronecker delta.
Using these
extended
vectors, we rewrite the constraint in Eq. (
103
) as:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}^{+}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{d,+}.

(105)

Denoting
\bm{W}_{ji}^{+}=\bm{X}_{ji}^{+}\varphi_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
, this leads to the
extended
moment matrix

\mathbf{M}_{i}^{+}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}^{+}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}^{+}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1,p+1}.

(106)

The solution of the optimization problem then is:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{+}\cdot\left(\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{+}\right)^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha},+}\right)\,.

(107)

A similar constraint on the central particle has also been used to ensure positivity of the discrete operator, in particular of a discrete Laplace operator
[
220
]
.
This uses two stencil coefficients with different values. One satisfies polynomial exactness, while the other resides in the null space of polynomial exactness. Therefore, any linear combination of these coefficients satisfies polynomial exactness.
The method finds a particular linear combination such that the resulting discrete operator is positive, yet accurately approximates the target differential operator
[
216
]
.

The differences between this branch of FPsM and the branch derived by error minimization (Section
4.2.2
) have been highlighted in Refs.
[
222
,
98
]
.
The FPsM based on weight minimization is nowadays sometimes considered a special case of GFDM and referred to by this name
[
222
,
218
]
.

6
Derivation by generalized
\ell_{2}
minimization

The final way of deriving meshfree collocation methods is, at this point in time, purely theoretical. We are not aware of any methods in the literature that were derived or presented in this way. Nevertheless, it would be possible to do so, which is why we include this derivation here.

The derivation is motivated by the observation that the formulations obtained by
\ell_{2}
minimization (Eqs. (
50
) and (
99
) for error and weights minimization, respectively) are slightly different from what is obtained by approximating moment conditions (Eq. (
26
)).
Although the results are structurally alike, the minimization approaches led to a symmetric moment matrix composed of products of Taylor-monomial vectors
\bm{X}_{ji}
. This corresponds to the choice of basis functions
\bm{W}_{ji}=\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}
.
The result is therefore less general than Eq. (
26
), where the moment matrix need not be symmetric. This has been recognized by some authors, who posited that alternative choices of
\bm{W}_{ij}
could improve the accuracy of the method
[
232
,
115
,
114
]
.

In the case of
\ell_{2}
error minimization, the symmetry of the moment matrix is a consequence of the procedure and cannot be changed. The structure of the matrix results from minimizing the square of the approximation error. In the case of minimizing for the
best-fitting
weights with respect to the scaled moments, however, the structure of the moment matrix is a consequence of the formulation of the optimization problem, which can be generalized.
We generalize the formulation by allowing weight functions
w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}
with arbitrary ABFs
\bm{W}_{ji}
scaled by the weight coefficients
\bm{\Psi}_{i}
, as introduced in Eq. (
12
).
Formulating the optimization problem from Section
5
with such weight functions yields:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u^{*}\right\rvert_{i}\quad\forall u^{*}\in\mathbb{P}_{m}\,.

(108)

Due to the moment conditions in Eq. (
27
), this can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(109)

The optimal coefficients
\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
are found by solving this minimization problem as shown in Appendix
0.D
, leading to:

\displaystyle w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right),\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,,

where
\mathbf{M}_{i}
allows for arbitrary choices of
\bm{W}_{ji}
, in contrast to the formulations from Sections
4
and
5
.
The resulting discrete operator

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)

(110)

is identical to that found by moment approximation without the zeroth moment, cf. Eq. (
31
). This not only suggests a generalization of miminization-based approaches, but it also links them to moment-based approximations, showing how and when the two are equivalent.

6.1
Matrix notation

In the matrix notation from Section
2.1
, the optimization problem in Eq. (
108
) reads:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{W}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(111)

Solving this as shown in Appendix
0.D
, we find the optimal weight function

\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(112)

This has the same form as Eq. (
101
). But in the present form, the weight matrix

\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}

(113)

need not be symmetric and has the full flexibility of the definition in Eq. (
32
), without any particular form of ABFs
\mathbf{W}_{i}
(or
\bm{W}_{ji}
).
This leads to the same discrete operator as in Eq. (
110
) in matrix form:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(114)

7
Summary

We comprehensively surveyed meshfree collocation methods for consistently (with some desired order of accuracy
m
) approximating differential operators on continuously labeled unstructured point clouds. We presented a notation that can accommodate all methods reviewed in a common general formulation. This suggested classifying the methods according to how they were derived, or are usually presented, into three groups: methods derived by approximation of moments, methods derived by minimization of the approximation error, and methods derived by minimization of weights. For each group, we presented the derivation in detail, showed how it fits the present formulation in both vector and matrix notation, and reviewed methods from the literature that fall within that group. This allowed us to highlight similarities and differences between the methods.

Comparing methods, we found that all use similar discrete approximation operators, which can be formulated within the same framework. There are, however, also important differences, for example in whether the zeroth moment is explicitly accounted for, the structure of the moment matrix (symmetric or not), or if additional penalty terms are included when deriving the approximation. These differences shape the numerical properties of the methods, such as their computational cost, stability in explicitly time stepping, wavenumber dissipation, boundary condition handling, and robustness to noise. Therefore, each method has its niche, and different applications may benefit from different methods. It would have been beyond the scope of this paper to discuss all numerical consequences of the differences between methods or to give advice on which methods are well suited for what type of applications. Instead, we focused on the mathematical formulation of the methods, their formal relationships, and the historical evolution of ideas and concepts.

An important difference between the three groups of methods defined here is whether the moment matrix is allowed to be asymmetric, which makes a wider choice of basis functions accessible. Minimizing the approximation error reaches the same order of accuracy as moment-approximation methods, but only the Taylor mononials can be used as basis functions. This observation led us to formulate a fourth, hitherto unexplored derivation of meshfree collocation. We named it
generalized
\ell_{2}
minimization
, as it extends weight-minimization approaches to using arbitrary anisotropic basis functions. Interestingly, this yielded the exact same discrete operator as found by moment approximation. By showing that the same approximation can be derived in two conceptually different ways—by solving a generalized miminization problem or by canceling unwanted moments—we established a formal link between these groups of meshfree collocation methods. This hopefully allows theoretical results to be transfered between methods in the future.

Some of them are even identical and were re-discovered over the years and published under different names. Appendix 0.A
Component-wise example in 2D

We find that to understand meshfree collocation methods, it often helps to go through the derivation from Section
3
in a low-dimensional case with all components explicitly written out. We therefore present the derivation in two dimensions (2D), separately for when the zeroth moment is included and when it is not.

Consider a 2D domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
N
arbitrarily placed points (nodes, particles)
\mathcal{X}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}:\bm{x}_{i}\in\overline{\Omega}\}
, where
\overline{\Omega}=\Omega\cup\partial\Omega
, with positions
\bm{x}_{i}=[x_{i},\,y_{i}]^{\top}
.
Every particle has a unique index
\mathcal{P}=\{i:\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\}
.
Each particle interacts with other particles within a finite neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}
around it, defined by a cutoff radius. The number of neighbors
N_{i}
in
\mathcal{N}_{i}
in 2D can be estimated as
N_{i}\approx 4\pi(h_{i}/\Delta x_{i})^{2}
.

The particles collectively discretize a sufficiently smooth (at least
C^{m}
) function
u(x,\,y)
. The value of this function at each particle
i
is
u_{i}=u(x_{i},\,y_{i})
. We denote
u_{ji}=u_{j}-u_{i}
. Moreover, by
m
we denote the order of consistency of the approximation.

0.A.1
Derivation with the 0th moment

We define a general discrete operator
L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
at particle
i
, which approximates a specific partial derivative
\bm{\alpha}
as

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\approx\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\,,

(115)

where
w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}
are the operator weights, and the right-hand side
\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}
is just a notation for the multi-index derivative
\mathcal{D}^{|\bm{\alpha}|}u=\partial^{|\bm{\alpha}|}u/(\partial x^{\alpha_{1}}\partial y^{\alpha_{2}})
with
\bm{D}u
denoting the vector of all possible derivatives up to and including order
m
, which in 2D is

\bm{D}u=\left(u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial{x}},\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial{y}},\,\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{x}^{2}},\,\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{x}\partial{y}},\,\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{y}^{2}},\,\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}^{3}},\,\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}^{2}\partial{y}},\,\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}\partial{y}^{2}},\,\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{y}^{3}},\,\frac{\partial^{4}u}{\partial{x}^{4}},\,\dots,\,\frac{\partial^{m}u}{\partial{y}^{m}}\right)^{\!\!\top}.

(116)

\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is the
mapping vector
that
selects
the desired derivatives from the vector
\bm{D}\left(\cdot\right)
, as introduced in Eq.
5
.
The number of elements in
\bm{D}u
is
p=p_{\mathrm{2D}}=1+(m^{2}+3m)/2
.
Thus,
\bm{D}u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{C}^{d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{p}
.

The vector of 2D Taylor monomials is:

\bm{X}_{ji}=\left(1,\,x_{ji},\,y_{ji},\,\frac{x_{ji}^{2}}{2},\,x_{ji}y_{ji},\,\frac{y_{ji}^{2}}{2},\,\frac{x_{ji}^{3}}{6},\,\frac{x_{ji}^{2}y_{ji}}{2},\,\frac{x_{ji}y_{ji}^{2}}{2},\,\frac{y_{ji}^{3}}{6},\,\frac{x_{ji}^{4}}{24},\,\dots\,\frac{y_{ji}^{m}}{m!}\right)^{\!\!\top},

(117)

where
x_{ji}=x_{j}-x_{i}
and
y_{ji}=y_{j}-y_{i}
are distances between particles. Thus,
\bm{X}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\bm{X}_{ji}\neq\bm{X}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i}
.
This allows us to write the Taylor expansion of the function
u
in the neighborhood of particle
i
as

u_{j}=\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}+e_{ji}^{m+1}.

(118)

where
e_{ji}^{m+1}
is the approximation error
(Lagrange form of the remainder)
of orders
m+1
and higher.
Dropping the error
e_{ji}^{m+1}
and substituting the Taylor expansion into the operator approximation in Eq. (
115
), we find:

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left.\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,,

(119)

which expands to

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\left.u\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial{x}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}x_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial{y}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}y_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{x}^{2}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{x_{ji}^{2}}{2}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\\
+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{x}\partial{y}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}x_{ji}y_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial{y}^{2}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{y_{ji}^{2}}{2}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}^{3}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{x_{ji}^{3}}{6}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}^{2}\partial{y}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{x_{ji}^{2}y_{ji}}{2}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\\
+\left.\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{x}\partial{y}^{2}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{x_{ji}y_{ji}^{2}}{2}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial{y}^{3}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{y_{ji}^{3}}{6}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\left.\frac{\partial^{4}u}{\partial{x}^{4}}\right\rvert_{i}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\frac{x_{ji}^{4}}{24}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}+\dots.

(120)

The right-hand side thus corresponds to a sum of moments of the weight coefficients

.
We denote the vector of moments by

\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w^{\bm{\alpha}}_{ji}\,.

(121)

With the vector

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\left.\bm{D}u\right\rvert_{i}\cdot\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\,,

(122)

The goal is to find a weight functions

such that the moments associated with the desired derivatives are close to one, and all other moments (up to and including order

We make an ansatz for

to be a weighted sum of some anisotropic basis functions (ABFs)

(123)

where

\bm{W}_{ji}=\left(W^{0}_{ji},W^{1}_{ji},W^{2}_{ji},W^{3}_{ji},\dots,W^{p}_{ji}\right)^{\top}

(124)

\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\left(\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,0},\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,1},\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,2},\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,3},\dots,\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i,p}\right)^{\top}.

(125)

To complete the 2D formulation, we substitute the weight functions into Eq. (

\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\,,

(126)

which defines a system of linear equations for the weight coefficients

with system (moment) matrix

\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\bm{B}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\,,\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,.

(127)

By specifying the values of the moments, that is, by replacing the vector

with the vector

\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi^{\bm{\alpha}}}_{i}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(128)

The discrete operator

over the neighborhood of each paricle then becomes

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{j}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(129)

As a last step, we can optionally scale each row of the linear system with the corresponding power of the inter-particle spacing (spatial resolution)

This improves the condition number of the system and, in 2D, amounts to preconditioning with the matrix

\mathbf{H}_{i}=\text{diag}\left(1,\;h_{i}^{-1},h_{i}^{-1},h_{i}^{-2},h_{i}^{-2},h_{i}^{-2},h_{i}^{-3},h_{i}^{-3},h_{i}^{-3},h_{i}^{-3},\dots,h_{i}^{-m}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

(130)

and define
\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{ji}=\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{X}_{ji}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\widetilde{\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}}=\mathbf{H}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

24

-\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\varphi_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=-\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(149)

Solving for
\bm{\lambda}
yields:

\bm{\lambda}=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,,

(150)

which we substitute back into Eq. (
147
) to find the optimal solution:

w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\varphi_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},\qquad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{X}_{ji}^{\top}\varphi_{ji}\,.

(151)

0.C.1
Matrix notation

In matrix notation, the optimization problem from Eq. (
144
) reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{X}^{\top}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}},

(152)

where
\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}
is easy to compute, since
\mathbf{V}_{ji}
is a diagonal matrix.
In this notation, the Lagrange function from Eq. (
145
) becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{\top}\left(\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)

With this, we formally solve the minimization problem as:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)}{\partial\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}}=(\mathbf{V}_{i})^{-1}\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}+\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{0}\,,

(153)

\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=-\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\lambda}\,.

(154)

This vector of weight functions
\bm{\omega}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is then substituted into the constraint in Eq. (
152
) to find:

-\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(155)

This allows us to obtain

\bm{\lambda}=-\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\right)^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=-\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,,

(156)

where we have used that
\mathbf{W}_{i}=\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{i}
for the moment matrix from Eq. (
32
).
Substituting Eq. (
156
) back into Eq. (
155
) results in a vector containing weight for all neighboring particles:

\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{V}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(157)

Expanding the matrix multiplication corresponding to the summation of the neighboring particle, we can see that this is the same as in the original derivation in Section
5
,

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right).

(158)

Appendix 0.D
Formal generalized
\ell_{2}
minimization

We solve optimization problem

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}.

(159)

The Lagrange function for this problem is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{2}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,,

(160)

for which we find the derivative

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)}{\partial\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\bm{W}_{ji}+\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}\,.

(161)

Setting
\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)/\partial\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=0
leads to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\bm{W}_{ji}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}\,.

Using the constraint from Eq. (
159
), we substitute for
w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}
,

-\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(162)

This allows expressing the Lagrange multipliers
\bm{\lambda}
as:

\bm{\lambda}=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-T}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(163)

Substituting this back into the expression for the weights, we find:

\displaystyle w_{ji}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-T}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{W}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

This leads to the optimal solution

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}u_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}\cdot\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,,\qquad\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}\,.

(164)

0.D.1
Matrix notation

In the matrix notation introduced in Section
2.1
, the optimization problem from Eq. (
159
) reads:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{w}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{W}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\mathcal{P},i}\right)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}

(165)

The matrix form of the Lagrange function and its derivative are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{M}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}-\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{\lambda}\right)}{\partial\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{W}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}+\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{0}\,,

(166)

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}\,.

(167)

Substituting the weight coefficients
\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}
in the constraint in Eq. (
165
) yields:

-\mathbf{M}_{i}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(168)

From this, we find

\bm{\lambda}=-\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-T}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

(169)

Subsituting this back into the expression for the weights,

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{i}\bm{\Psi}_{i}^{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{i}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{i}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-T}\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\right)\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\,.

In contrast to the error or weights minimization approaches, the moment matrix here has a structure as in the definition in Eq. (
32
), without any particular form of anisotropic basis functions
\mathbf{W}_{i}
(or
\bm{W}_{ji}
). Therefore, the resulting discrete operator becomes:

\mathbf{M}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\bm{X}_{ji}\bm{W}_{ji}^{\top}=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{i}\,,

(170)

L^{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}u=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\bm{w}_{\bullet i}^{\bm{\alpha}}=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{C}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)=\bm{\bm{U}}_{i}\cdot\left(\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{M}_{i}^{-1}\bm{C}^{\bm{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(171)
