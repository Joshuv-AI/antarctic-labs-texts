Contribution and evolution of respiratory muscles function in weaning outcome of ventilator-dependent patients
Authors: Sara Virolle, Baptiste Duceau, Elise Morawiec, Quentin Fossé, Marie-Cécile Nierat, Mélodie Parfait, Maxens Decavèle, Alexandre Demoule, Julie Delemazure, Martin Dres
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe present study was designed to investigate the evolution and the impact of respiratory muscles function and limb muscles strength on weaning success in prolonged weaning of tracheotomized patients. The primary objective was to determine whether the change in respiratory muscles function and limb muscles strength over the time is or is not associated with weaning success.MethodsTracheotomized patients who were ventilator dependent upon admission at a weaning center were eligible. Diaphragm function was assessed with the phrenic nerve stimulation technique and with ultrasound to measure the diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) and diaphragm excursion (EXdi). Global respiratory muscle function was assessed with the maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and the forced vital capacity (FVC). Limb muscle strength was measured with the Medical Research Council Score (MRC). Measurements were made on a weekly basis. Patients were compared according to their outcome at discharge: complete weaning, partial weaning or death.ResultsAmong the 60 patients who were enrolled, 30 patients finally achieved complete weaning, 20 had partial weaning and 10 died. At 6 months, 6 patients were lost of follow-up, 33 achieved complete weaning, 10 had partial weaning and 11 died. In median, 2 (1–9) assessments were performed per patient. Diaphragm dysfunction was present in all patients with a median Ptr,stim of 5.5 cmH2O (3.0–7.5). Ptr,stim, MIP, TFdi and EXdi at admission were not different between patients who achieved complete weaning and their counterparts. At discharge of the weaning center, MIP, Ptr,stim and EXdi significantly increased in patients who achieved complete weaning. The MRC score significantly increased only in patients with complete weaning. At discharge, diaphragm dysfunction was highly prevalent even in patients with complete weaning (Ptr,stim < 11 cmH2O in n = 11 (37%)).ConclusionRespiratory muscle function and limb muscles strength are severely impaired in patients with prolonged weaning from mechanical ventilation. Significant improvement of diaphragm ultrasound indices was associated with successful weaning from mechanical ventilation and ICU-acquired weakness upon admission was significantly associated with good outcome suggesting that it was an amendable determinant of weaning failure in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-024-05172-y.

Introduction

Separation from the ventilator after recovery from acute respiratory failure is one of the most challenging task for caregivers working in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) [1]. Studies show that the majority of patients are usually extubated after a first attempt and that only 10% of them experience prolonged weaning from mechanical ventilation [2]. Nevertheless, with respect to the large number of patients who are exposed to mechanical ventilation each year, many patients experience prolonged weaning which has a major impact on the healthcare system [3] and on patients centered outcomes. In addition to their ventilator dependency, those patients often present severe complications of critical illness such as and not limited to: ICU-acquired weakness [4, 5], muscle wasting, neurocognitive dysfunction [6], swallowing disorders [7]. The constellation of clinical features occurring during or after the ICU stay has been referred to as chronic critical illness [8] of which the hallmark feature is ventilator dependence. Weaning failure is often viewed as an imbalance between the respiratory load and the respiratory capacity—namely the respiratory muscles [9]. Several studies highlighted the role of respiratory muscles dysfunction, especially diaphragm dysfunction [10–12], as a major risk factor of weaning failure. However, it is well established that several risk factors of weaning failure coexist in the context of prolonged weaning and the specific contribution of respiratory muscles function is unclear. In addition, whether the improvement of respiratory muscles function over the time influence or not weaning outcome is not clear. Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the role of respiratory muscles function on weaning outcome in prolonged weaning patients. Our objective was to determine whether the change in respiratory muscles function over the time is associated with weaning outcome. Our hypothesis was that complete weaning from the ventilator would be associated with an improvement in respiratory muscles function.

Methods

Study population

All patients admitted to the Weaning Center Facility of Pitié Salpêtrière Hospital were eligible if they were 18 years older, tracheostomized, dependent to the ventilator (as defined by clinical intolerance criteria under spontaneous breathing) and affiliated to the healthcare insurance. Patients with contraindication to perform the magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation technique (such as pacemakers, implantable defibrillators), those who were ventilator-dependent due to pre-existing neuromuscular disease, phrenic paralysis or upper spinal cord injuries, and pregnant women were not included. Informed consent was given to all patients who provided written consent to participate. The observational cohort study was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord Ouest III and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03676998) prior inclusion of the first patient. The study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines for observational studies.

Respiratory muscle function evaluation

Diaphragm function was assessed using the magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation technique and defined as its capacity to generate a negative intrathoracic pressure. In practice, tracheal pressure signal was used as a surrogate of the intrathoracic pressure and diaphragm function was measured as the change in tracheal pressure induced by bilateral phrenic nerve stimulation during an airway manual occlusion (Ptr,stim). Patients were studied by two operators while they were seated in semi recumbent position. Stimulation of the phrenic nerves was performed by bilateral magnetic stimulation using two coils connected to a pair of Magstim (The Magstim Company, Dyfed, UK) (Fig. 1). Coils were positioned at the level of the cricoid cartilage at both sides. Stimulations were triggered by an operator and delivered at the maximum intensity (100%) known to be associated with supramaximal diaphragm contraction. First, the patients were briefly disconnected from the ventilator and were allowed to breath quietly through the tracheostomy collar. Second, a manual occlusion of the tracheostomy collar was done at the end of expiration which was immediately followed by the magnetic stimulations. The change in tracheal pressure induced by the stimulation was measured via a linear differential pressure transducer (MP45 ± 100 cmH2O, Validyne, Northridge, California, USA), placed at the extremity of the tracheostomy tube with inflated cuff. The pressure transducer was connected to a computer equipped with pressure signal acquisition software (Labchart, ADInstruments). The tracheal pressure was sampled and digitized at 100 Hz (MP30, Biopac, Systems, Santa Barbara, Calif., USA) for offline analysis. Operators carefully checked the absence of active respiratory efforts and eliminated respiratory cycles that did not meet quality criteria. At least, three valid measurements were required at each assessment. During offline analysis, average of three measurements was calculated by operators blinded to the identity of the patients. Diaphragm dysfunction was defined by a Ptr,stim < 11 cmH2O as previously determined.Fig. 1Typical preparation for performing bilateral anterior magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation in a tracheostomized patient

Typical preparation for performing bilateral anterior magnetic phrenic nerve stimulation in a tracheostomized patient

In addition to the phrenic nerve stimulation technique, diaphragm ultrasound was performed on the same day than phrenic nerve stimulation. It was performed using an ultrasound device equipped with the Sparq system (Sparq ultrasound system, Phillips, Philips Healthcare, MA, USA). The patients were placed in a semi-sitting position to allow better identification of the diaphragm. Once the right hemi-diaphragm was identified, diaphragm ultrasound indices were collected while patients were disconnected from the ventilator and were receiving standard oxygen through the tracheostomy collar. Unilateral measurement of the diaphragm on the right side of the patient is an acceptable proxy for the whole diaphragm [13]. A standardized diaphragm ultrasound protocol was followed to measure the diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) and the diaphragm excursion (EXdi) during quiet breathing [14, 15]. Through the intercostal approach, diaphragm end expiratory and peak inspiratory thickness was measured using a 10–15 MHz linear array transducer between the mid-axillary or antero-axillary line, in the 8– 11th intercostal space and the TFdi was calculated as the difference between diaphragm end expiratory and peak inspiratory thickness, divided by diaphragm end expiratory thickness, expressed as %. Measurements were repeated on at least three separate tidal breaths and their average was reported. In addition, diaphragm excursion was obtained through the sub costal approach with a low frequency phased-array probe (2–5 MHz) positioned just below the costal arch at the midclavicular line while the sweep speed was adjusted around 10 mm/s to obtain at least three respiratory cycles. The diaphragm was identified as the hyperechoic linear structure cephalad to the liver, and EXdi was measured using M-mode, during quiet breathing.

Forced vital capacity (FVC) was collected using an electronic spirometer (World spirometer EasyOne) connected to the tracheostomy collar with inflated cuff. Patients were asked to breath in maximally and breath out as long as possible. Three measurements separated by one minute were attempted in each patient and the best value was retained in the analysis.

Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) was measured after a forced expiration against a manual occlusion of the respiratory circuit and held for 1 s. Three maneuvers were performed, and the highest value was used for analysis.

Limb muscle strength function

Limb muscles strength was measured using the Medical Research Council (MRC) score assessed by physiotherapists as previously reported in patients screened for awakening and understanding. Patients with an MRC score less than 48 were considered to have ICU-acquired weakness [5].

Weaning protocol

All patients were managed with the same weaning protocol developed and implemented in the weaning centre. This protocol that includes weaning from mechanical ventilation, decannulation, refeeding, motor rehabilitation, and global management of chronic critical illness has been previously published elsewhere [16] (see Figures E1 and E2 in the Additional File 1). The use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) was protocolized and was applied in patients with persistent hypercapnic (PaCO2 > 45 mmHg).

Protocol of the study

Upon admission, the following data were collected from the electronic medical chart of the patients: sex, age, medical and surgical history, reason for ICU admission, duration of ICU stay prior to the admission in the weaning center, date of weaning centre admission. Diaphragm function, MIP, FVC, MRC score and diaphragm ultrasound indices were measured during at least two visits: upon admission and at the time of decannulation or at the time of discharge if no decannulation was deemed not feasible. Whenever possible, additional visit occurred on a weekly basis between the admission visit and the last visit. Patients were followed up at 6 months after discharge, to determine their status death/alive and weaning outcome (need of non-invasive ventilation or spontaneous breathing).

Primary and secondary endpoints

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with complete weaning as defined by spontaneous breathing during at least 48 h. Secondary endpoints were the proportion of patients with partial weaning as defined by the possibility to sustain several hours of spontaneous breathing per day not longer than 24 h or spontaneous breathing during more than 48 h leading to decannulation but with dependence to non-invasive ventilation. Other secondary endpoints were Ptr,stim, MIP, FVC, TFdi, EXdi and MRC score.

Statistical analysis

No sample size was calculated as we aimed at enrolling all eligible patients over the duration of the study. The normality of continuous variables was verified by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables are reported as median with interquartile range [IQR]. Categorical variables are reported as count (percentage). For univariate analyses, continuous variables were compared by Mann–Whitney test or Student’s t-test adequately and categorical variables were compared by Fisher’s exact test.

Ptr,stim was analyzed as a continuous and discontinuous variable to determine the proportion of patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction defined by a Ptr,stim value < 11 cmH2O. Ultrasound measurements (TFdi, EXdi), MIP, MRC and FVC were analyzed as continuous variables. A first analysis compared respiratory muscle function indices and MRC score of two groups of patients: complete weaning on one side and a mixed cohort of patients with partial weaning and those who died on the other side. A secondary analysis compared respiratory muscle function indices and MRC score of three groups of patients: complete weaning, partial weaning and non-survivors. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the Rstudio interface.

At the end of the follow-up, patients with complete weaning had a greater MIP than patients with partial weaning and the non survivors (26 cmH2O [21–42] vs 10 [4–18], p = 0.003), a greater Ptr,stim (6.9 cmH2O [5.0–9. 1] vs 3.5 cmH2O [2.0–5.0], p = 0.010), and a greater EXdi (2.0 cm [1.1–4.5] vs 1.0 [0.7–1.8], p = 0.005). TFdi and vital capacity were not different between patients who achieved complete weaning and their counterparts. There were no differences between the two groups in terms of MRC scale (Table 2). At discharge of the weaning center, diaphragm dysfunction was highly frequent even in patients who achieved complete weaning (Ptr,stim < 11 cmH2O in n = 11 (37%)). A sensitivity analysis was performed between three groups of patients (complete weaning, partial weaning and death) and provided similar findings (Tables E3, E4 and Figure E3 in the additional file 1).

Changes in respiratory muscles function and limbs muscles and outcomes

In median, 2 (1–9) assessments were performed per patient. The changes in Ptr,stim, MIP, FVC, TFdi, EXdi and MRC between admission and the last available measurement over the weaning center stay are presented in Fig. 4 and Table 2. Ptr,stim and MIP did not change in patients with complete weaning whereas they decreased in patients with partial weaning and the non survivors. However, TFdi and MRC significantly increased in patients with complete weaning. A sensitivity analysis was performed between three groups of patients (complete weaning, partial weaning and death) and found no difference except for the change of MRC that was not significantly different between groups. Discussion

This study was designed to follow the time course of respiratory muscles function and limb muscles strength in patients with prolonged weaning admitted in a referral weaning center. Herein, we summarized the main results. First, a diaphragm dysfunction, a severe respiratory weakness and an ICU acquired weakness were found in all patients upon admission to the weaning center. Second, except the MRC score that was lower, the diaphragm function and the global respiratory muscles function upon inclusion were not different between patients who were eventually weaned on one hand and those who were partially weaned and who did not survive on the other hand. Over the stay in the weaning center, the increase in the MRC score and in diaphragm thickening fraction were the sole factors associated with complete weaning. The diaphragm function time course remained stable in the complete weaning group and was not associated with the weaning outcome.

Prolonged dependence on invasive mechanical ventilation is a problematic healthcare challenge. Studies show that prolonged weaning occurs in a minority of patients—around 10% of mechanically ventilated patients [2, 17]—but it is accountable for major healthcare resources and high proportion of bed occupancy. Patients with prolonged weaning undergo a complex and long process dedicated to the separation from the ventilator after several weeks of invasive mechanical ventilation and ICU stay. They often suffer from malnutrition, positive fluid balance, anemia and endocrinology disturbances [8, 18]. On the top of that, they are known to develop limb muscles weakness and respiratory muscles weakness that can act as contributors to weaning failure. In mechanically ventilated patients who enter into the weaning process only several days after intubation, the pressure generating capacity of the diaphragm on one hand and the maximal inspiratory pressure on the other hand have been associated with weaning failure [10, 11, 19]. Whether the respiratory muscle function at admission and its time course over the stay are critical determinants of weaning success in prolonged weaning patients is unclear. We found that all patients had a diaphragm dysfunction upon admission which may be explained by the notably long duration of ICU stay before admission to our facility (48 days in median). We observed a severe respiratory muscle weakness as shown by the low MIP (21 cmH2O) that is twofold lower than the MIP reported by Jubran et al. in a cohort of ventilator dependent patients [20]. This severe respiratory muscle weakness could be explained by a different case mix of our cohort as compared to the Jubran’s cohort (more acute respiratory failure and less postoperative patients). As in the former study, the global MIP did not change between admission and discharge, notably in the complete weaning group. However, MIP dropped in the non-survivors’ group and remained stable in the partial weaning group. Same patterns were observed for Ptr,stim. These results suggest that neither the diaphragm function nor the respiratory muscle function at admission seem to play as critical contributors of the weaning success. This is in contrast with the findings of Carlucci et al. who reported that patients who were eventually separated from the ventilator had an improvement in the diaphragm function as estimated by the transdiaphragmatic pressure [21]. It is likely that the characteristics of the patients and the reasons of prolonged weaning may explain these discrepancies with our results.

Another relevant finding in our study is the improvement in diaphragm thickening fraction in the complete weaning group. Diaphragm excursion seemed to mirror the same pattern as the thickening fraction despite the change was not significant (p = 0.059). Diaphragm thickening fraction and excursion are not per se markers of diaphragm function but rather surrogates of diaphragm activity which suggests that it may be useful to follow the progress of these patients. Interestingly, limb muscles function as evaluated by the MRC scale was lower in the complete weaning group as compared to the partial weaning and non survivor’s patients. In addition, the change in MRC score between admission and discharge was also higher in the complete weaning group as compared to their counterparts. This might reflect the fact that ICU-acquired weakness is an amendable key factor that is associated with weaning success in this specific population. This is a striking result suggesting that limb muscles weakness is probably a major determinant (maybe more critical than respiratory muscles weakness) in the prognosis of prolonged weaning patients. These results are in line with a previous study of our group that also identified ICU-acquires weakness has key factor of positive outcome in prolonged weaning patients suffering from SARS-CoV-2 related ARDS [22]. The link between limb muscles weakness and diaphragm weakness is not univocal. While the diaphragm is characterized by a permanent cyclic activity, other striated muscles such as limb muscles are not constantly active. Furthermore, although both diaphragm and limb muscle are striated muscles, their fibre type composition differs. Thus, the consequences of prolonged unloading of the diaphragm may be distinct from the consequences of unloading limb muscles [23]. At the time of weaning, diaphragm weakness is twice as frequent than limb muscle weakness and patients with diaphragm weakness are no more or less likely to have limb muscle weakness [11]. While they share common features, diaphragm and limb muscles weakness likely represent to two distinct entities with different pathophysiology and different consequences even on the long term prognosis [24].

Fifty percent of our patients were completely separated from the ventilator at discharge and 55% at 6 months. Six patients were lost of follow up without any record of death certificate, meaning that eventually 36 patients (65%) achieved complete weaning and 10 patients (17%) remained dependent of non-invasive invasive still at 6 months. This rate of successful weaning is higher than previously reported (50%) in a meta-analysis on 124 studies in 16 countries [25]. The inclusion of SARS-CoV-2 patients (50%) who are lacking of pre-ICU comorbidities as compared to non SARS-CoV-2 patients may certainly explain these results.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size is limited despite a long duration of observation. The limited number of patients may be explained by the long duration of stay in our facility and the relatively low rotation of patients along the year. Second, it is a monocentric study which impedes generalization. However, the monocentric design allowed to use a standardized weaning and decannulation protocol which would have otherwise been difficult to achieve. Third, limb muscle strength was assessed by using the MRC score and not an objective measurement such as the handgrip strength which would have provide more robust measurements and is easy to use. However, in previous studies [26], MRC score has shown to be reproducible and strongly related with weaning outcomes [27, 28]. Fourth, it would have been interesting to explore and follow respiratory mechanics over the study period but those data were not available. Last, complete weaning was defined after spontaneous breathing during at least 48 h which may be subject to discussion. A longer duration of spontaneous breathing has been suggested for this specific population. However, there is no clear consensus on this definition and we relied on our local protocol.

Conclusion

Respiratory muscle function and limb muscles strength are severely impaired in patients with prolonged weaning from mechanical ventilation. Significant improvement of diaphragm ultrasound indices was associated with successful weaning from mechanical ventilation and ICU-acquires weakness upon admission was significantly associated with good outcome suggesting that it was an amendable determinant of weaning failure in this population.
