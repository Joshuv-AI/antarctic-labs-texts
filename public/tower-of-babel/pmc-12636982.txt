Introduction

Background

The maternal health crisis is a significant public health issue in the USA. Despite the country’s status as a developed nation with significant healthcare resources, it suffers from persistently high rates of pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality and severe maternal morbidity (SMM), particularly affecting low-income women, women in rural areas1 2 and women of colour.3,5 SMM is defined as the “unexpected outcomes of labour and delivery that can result in significant short- or long-term health consequences”.6 Nearly 700 women die each year in the USA from pregnancy-related complications, approximately 80% of which are considered preventable, and another 50 000 women experience life-threatening events.7

Leading causes of SMM and mortality

The leading medical causes of pregnancy-related deaths in the USA include cardiovascular conditions, haemorrhage, infection or sepsis, thrombotic embolism, cardiomyopathy, mental health conditions including suicide and substance use disorders, hypertensive disorders (preeclampsia or eclampsia) and amniotic fluid embolism.8 9 The leading causes of pregnancy-related deaths vary by socio-economic and socio-demographic factors.10 11

Disparities

Rural areas often experience higher rates of poverty, higher rates of Medicaid insurance coverage or no insurance, lower educational attainment, insufficient prenatal care12 13 and a higher prevalence of chronic conditions like gestational diabetes, obesity and hypertension, which are known risk factors for SMM.14 15 For example, pregnant women residing in rural areas face a 9% higher likelihood of SMM compared with their urban counterparts,1 with pronounced disparities for non-Hispanic Black and American Indian/Alaska Native women in rural areas.10 Several factors contribute to the increased risk of SMM among rural women, including limited access to healthcare facilities with specialised obstetric care, longer travel distances to reach these facilities, shortage of obstetricians and gynaecologist and/or closure of rural hospitals.1 16

The primary causes of SMM among non-Hispanic Black women include cardiovascular conditions (ie, cardiomyopathy, haemorrhage), while mental health conditions (ie, substance use disorders) are the leading causes among non-Hispanic White and Hispanic women.8 17 For American Indian/Alaska Native women, the leading causes of SMM include haemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, cardiomyopathies and placental disorders.14 17 Hypertensive disorders are significant contributors to SMM across all racial and ethnic groups, with Black women experiencing higher incidence and more severe outcomes.18 These disparities persist across varied socio-demographic and economic factors, including location of residence, age, partner support, income status and education levels,3 4 19 and were exacerbated by cultural and structural factors, such as racism, lack of access to healthcare and biases within the healthcare system.20

Recent research using Medicaid claims data has further underscored the disproportionate burden of SMM among publicly insured populations. Admon et al (2023) documented nearly threefold state-level variation in SMM rates among Medicaid-financed births, with non-Hispanic Black enrollees experiencing significantly higher rates than their White counterparts.12 Yu et al (2021) found that neighbourhood deprivation was strongly associated with increased SMM risk in a statewide Medicaid population and that neighbourhood disadvantage accounted for nearly 40% of the Black–White disparity.21 Most recently, Auty et al (2025) showed that pregnant people with opioid-use disorder enrolled in Medicaid faced substantially elevated risks of SMM, particularly when Medicaid enrolment occurred later in pregnancy.22 Together, these findings illustrate both the value of Medicaid claims data for maternal health surveillance and the disproportionate burden of SMM borne by Medicaid-insured populations.

SMM in Arizona

Chronic diseases (such as cardiovascular conditions, diabetes and hypertensive disorders), mental health conditions, haemorrhage and infections remain significant causes of SMM in Arizona, disproportionately affecting women of colour.23 24 In Arizona, American Indian/Alaska Native women had the highest SMM rate at 303.0 per 10 000 deliveries, over 3.5 times that of non-Hispanic White women (83.3), followed by Black women (163.8), Hispanic/Latina women (133.0) and Asian/Pacific Islander women (132.3) between 2016 and 2019.25 With approximately half of Arizona’s population belonging to underserved marginalised communities,25 addressing the specific challenges concerning their maternal health outcomes is imperative.

Role of Medicaid and community health workers (CHWs)

In the USA and Arizona, Medicaid is the primary policy tool used to improve health of low-income high-risk populations. Arizona Medicaid eligibility is determined by factors such as income, household size and specific categories including children, pregnant women, elderly adults, individuals with disabilities and parents or caretaker relatives.26 Eligibility thresholds vary by state, but generally, individuals must have an income at or below 138% of the federal poverty level (FPL) to qualify. Approximately 30% of Arizona’s total population is insured by Medicaid, and among these beneficiaries, 53% are female, 45% are White, 8% are Black, 8% are American Indian/Alaska Native and approximately 42% are Hispanic/Latino.27 While Medicaid is critical in making healthcare services more accessible, health disparities persist among beneficiaries, including maternal chronic diseases and mental and behavioural health.

Previous research has shown that CHW programmes significantly enhance health outcomes and care utilisation among Medicaid populations.28,30 CHWs are crucial in providing culturally relevant education and support, addressing social determinants of health and enhancing healthcare access, thereby helping to reduce health disparities.30 31 These interventions are particularly important for improving maternal health among high-risk Medicaid beneficiaries. For example, Bigby et al (2020) reported that programmes offering community support and home visits have proven effective in enhancing maternal and child care quality and outcomes, particularly by increasing access to postnatal and well-child care for vulnerable groups.29 A recent study by Meghea et al (2023) found that the Strong Beginnings home visiting programme, involving CHWs, significantly improved birth outcomes and prenatal/postpartum care for Medicaid-insured individuals compared with standard prenatal care.28 Also, by improving access to prenatal care and addressing upstream risk factors, CHW interventions can potentially help manage gestational chronic conditions and prevent long-term complications.32 Le Roux et al (2020) also found that mothers who received the CHW home-visiting intervention reported fewer depressive symptoms.33 Our evaluation of Arizona’s Health Start Programme (HSP) builds on the literature of past studies examining CHW interventions’ impact on maternal chronic disease and mental health among Medicaid beneficiaries.

Objectives

The goal of this evaluation is to determine if, among Arizona Medicaid beneficiaries, participation in HSP improves physical and mental health outcomes for pregnant women compared with pregnant women who did not participate in HSP. We aim to evaluate the impact of HSP on (1) the risk of experiencing SMM, (2) the care management and outcomes of pregnant women diagnosed with chronic conditions (eg, diabetes and/or hypertension) and (3) the care management of pregnant women diagnosed with depression.

Design

To test our hypothesis that pregnant women enrolled in HSP during the study period (2008–2019) have reduced risk of SMM and improved chronic disease and mental healthcare management outcomes, we employ a quasi-experimental design using retrospective data. Propensity score matching (PSM) is used to establish comparison groups consisting of non-HSP mothers from the pool of Arizona Medicaid claims and enrolment records spanning the study period (2008–2019). HSP effectiveness in mitigating adverse maternal health outcomes will be measured as differences (if any) between HSP participants and these synthetic comparison groups in SMM incidence, care utilisation patterns and post-natal maternal depression diagnosis rates. PSM has been shown to produce results that are unbiased and robust.34 35 See Wightman et al (2022) for a description of PSM in the context of vital records and immunisations administrative data.36

No primary data will be collected. In the framework of the quasi-experimental PSM research design, the quality and volume of information collected in the Medicaid enrolment and claims records will provide context sufficient to detect meaningful intervention effects on low-frequency or limited-variation events. These include emergency department visits, hospitalisations, types of clinic encounters and medication use.

Methods and analysis

Study setting

Arizona is located in the Southwestern USA and, with a fast-growing population, diverse blend of cultures and formidable geography, offers a unique study setting. It shares an international border with Mexico and is also home to 22 federally recognised Native American Tribes.37 With a population of over seven million people, over half of Arizona’s residents belong to a racial or ethnic minority group. Over 16% of Arizona’s residents live below the FPL, with disproportionately higher rates among communities of colour (Black, Hispanic, American Indian/Alaska Native) compared with non-Hispanic Whites, as well as rural communities compared with urban, and almost 23% of Arizona children (under age 18) live in poverty (data from 2014 to 2018).24 Most low-income families in Arizona are insured by Medicaid.

Arizona’s Medicaid, also known as the Arizona Healthcare Cost Containment System (AHCCCS), provides health insurance to over two million low-income residents (approximately 30% of the state’s total population) and covers nearly 50% of all births in Arizona.27 This study will specifically examine outcomes among the Arizona Medicaid birthing population.

Intervention: Arizona’s HSP

Under the management of the Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS), HSP, established in 1984 and expanded statewide by 1994, aims to improve maternal and child health outcomes, particularly within underserved marginalised populations across 14 counties (figure 1).38 HSP is a comprehensive CHW-led intervention that provides essential perinatal education, community resource referrals and child health screening and assessments.38 Evidence from previous studies suggests HSP has been effective in increasing prenatal care attendance,39 reducing preterm birth and low birth weight births40 and increasing early childhood vaccine completion.36

The programme’s CHWs are recruited from the community in which they live, thereby reflecting the ethnic, cultural and socio-economic characteristics of each community served. Additional CHW prerequisites include English proficiency, high school diploma or general educational development and passing background check. All HSP CHWs complete an initial 40 hours of training and 8 hours of supervised home visiting, followed by annual continuing education.38 HSP CHWs have a caseload of approximately 30–40 clients, provide ongoing perinatal health education on various topics, facilitate clients’ continuity of care during and after pregnancy, promote early childhood immunisations and developmental assessments, connect clients to health and community resources and advocate for clients’ needs and well-being.38 Table 1 is a non-exhaustive list of education and screening topics provided by CHWs, specifically pertaining to the current evaluation aims (SMM, maternal chronic conditions, maternal depression).

Study population

HSP eligibility requirements include women who are pregnant or postpartum with children under age 2 years. While there are no maternal age or income requirements, participants must live in one of the 16 HSP service areas and have one or more medical or social risk factors.

The forthcoming evaluation’s intervention group consists of pregnant women insured by Arizona Medicaid who were enrolled in HSP between 2008 and 2019. Approximately 80% of HSP enrollees are insured by Arizona Medicaid.38 The comparison group consists of a 50% random sample of pregnant women insured by Arizona Medicaid who did not participate in HSP.

Outcomes

HSP aims to improve maternal and child health outcomes among high-risk, racially and ethnically diverse, rural and urban mothers and children of Arizona. The ADHS-managed HSP Enrollment Dataset and AHCCCS (Arizona Medicaid) Limited Data Set will be used to evaluate the impact of HSP on (1) risks for SMM, (2) maternal care management of chronic diseases and (3) maternal care management of mental health among Arizona Medicaid beneficiaries during the study period 2008 to 2019. Outcomes will be measured using utilisation (eg, category codes, place-of-service codes) and, where appropriate, medication fills/refills (table 2).

Timeline and recruitment

We are conducting a retrospective, quasi-experimental study using administrative data from 2008 to 2019. All data were accessed at one point in time in 2023. As such, no participants were directly recruited for this evaluation.

Sample (population) sizes and defining groups

By linking Arizona Medicaid and HSP data, we will identify mothers who self-selected into the HSP intervention group. PSM will be used to generate a comparison group with the same observable pre-treatment characteristics as the intervention group, from the same population of Medicaid-covered mothers who experienced a live birth (table 3). In order to generate an unbiased estimate of the treatment effect of HSP, the variables selected for the matching model will be based on their association with both treatment status (eg, HSP participation) and the outcomes of interest (eg, diabetes, hypertension, depression) prior to birth via the presence of relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes recorded on insurance claims. Given the time frame of the study, both ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes will be used. In order to identify and group individual ICD codes, we will use the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW) categorisations developed and maintained by the Centre for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), supplemented by the Clinical Classification Software (CCS) groupings developed and maintained by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.41 In addition, we will match on the timing (in relation to the birth) and types of encounters where these diagnoses are recorded (eg, clinic visits, hospitalisations).

Data collection and management

Arizona Medicaid data are accessed via data agreement between ADHS, Arizona State University (ASU) Centre for Health Information Research (CHiR), and the evaluation team from Northern Arizona University (NAU) and the University of Arizona (UA). CHiR serves as a repository and clearing house for Arizona Medicaid enrolment and claims records for research purposes (eg, public health surveillance, quality improvement). ADHS will securely transmit HSP participant information (identifying information and dates of enrolment) to ASU and CHiR, who will link the HSP participants to their Arizona Medicaid enrolment and claims records. This data will be appended to a 50% random sample (per year, during 2008–2019) of Arizona Medicaid beneficiaries with a live birth recorded in that given year. These are the candidate matches, from which the propensity-scored algorithm will identify the actual matches. The evaluation team and CHiR decided that limiting the pool to a 50% random sample would not significantly constrain our ability to identify sufficient matches because our analysis will use the entire claims histories of both the intervention and comparison groups, plus it would greatly reduce the logistical resources necessary to prepare the data (ie, processing time, size of the data transfer).

CHiR will create and provide a Limited Data Set to the NAU/UA evaluation team (including the HSP participation information) to analyse the impact of programme participation among Arizona Medicaid beneficiaries. The Limited Data Set will be securely transferred and accessed by study personnel via secure server and remote desktop environment hosted by NAU. The Limited Data Set created for the study will be destroyed when the study team confirms they are no longer needed or at the end of the study, whichever comes first. The evaluation team and their institutions do not own or have rights to distribute the Limited Data Set provided by CHiR.

The retrospective data will consist of two data transfers, accessed at two different points over the study period. The initial transfer will include the bulk of the analytic sample comprising enrolment records covering 2008–2019 and outcome (claims records) covering 2008–2021, to include a minimum 1 year postpartum follow-up period to account for the risk of SMM. These data will be used to establish cleaning and variable creation procedures and generate the initial descriptive statistics and analyses. Once these processes have been tested and verified, a second batch of claims and enrolment records will be added to the initial set, and all analyses will be updated. All analyses, including matching and post-matching comparisons, will be conducted in Stata, version 16 or higher.42

Statistical methods: matching models

We will use logistic regression to generate propensity scores to identify the comparison group of non-HSP participants most similar (statistically) to the HSP intervention group. The matching model will include available variables that correlate with characteristics associated with both HSP participation and the outcomes of interest, as well as other characteristics that have been shown to be relevant in previous empirical and theoretical work. Demographic information is limited to age and race/ethnicity. Because HSP eligibility criteria focus largely on social and medical risks, factors that are correlated with Arizona Medicaid populations, as proxies for the presence of socio-economic risks, we will include in the matching model that accounts for (1) Arizona Medicaid enrolment histories (period of current enrolment, number of previous enrolments, period of total enrolment), (2) the specific Arizona Medicaid programme (ie, rate code and funding source) under which the mother is enrolled at the time of birth and (3) the order of the birth event (first vs others, covered by Arizona Medicaid). The model will account for date (year of pregnancy outcome) and location of the birth (county). Matching on the date of birth will account for broad economic conditions and any potentially shifting programme parameters. We will also explore the effect of incorporating pre-delivery comorbidities using both individual conditions and the Obstetric Comorbidity Score,43 as well as care utilisation patterns.

Because propensity scores will often match many control cases to each intervention case (or group of cases), we will not limit the number of control cases matched to individual intervention cases, but instead weight characteristics based on the number of matches in order to mimic a one-on-one match.

Baseline equivalence, the statistical similarity of the intervention and matched-comparison groups, will be evaluated following What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) Standards Manual (version 4.1),44 using the usual metrics, including t-tests and standardised differences. Where necessary and possible, we will use interactions, variable transformations and other methods to refine the propensity-score estimation models until the criteria for acceptable equivalence are met. If unachievable, we will explore WWC-approved post-matching statistical adjustment techniques, for example, regression covariate adjustments and/or alternative matching strategies.

Statistical methods: programme evaluation

We will evaluate the effectiveness of HSP by comparing the claims-reported maternal health outcomes between the HSP intervention and comparison groups, focusing primarily on the incidences of SMM conditions and procedures (Aim 1), the onset and management of chronic conditions (diabetes and hypertension) (Aim 2) and the onset and treatment of depression (Aim 3). In all cases, we will use diagnoses (ICD codes) to identify and track the service utilisations and procedures associated with these conditions, comparing the claims histories of the HSP intervention to the comparison groups (table 4).

While our data specifications allow for at least 2 years post-delivery to observe the outcomes described here, our sample eligibility criteria do not include minimums on observed post-delivery utilisation. Our descriptive analyses will include measures of post-delivery activity, for example, average time between delivery and reported future disenrolment dates (from the enrolment records), the percentage of mothers with at least one observed claim at least 1 and 2 years post-delivery and the average time elapsed between delivery and the last claim observed in the data. We will also conduct sensitivity analyses that limit the samples to mothers with claims observed at least 1 and 2 years post-delivery.

Because these are administrative records collected for billing/payment purposes, we do not anticipate that missing data will be a major issue. Where we do encounter this issue, we will conduct robustness/sensitivity checks using different methods to account for missing/blank fields such as list-wise deletion, regression imputation and missing-value dummies and report on the impact these strategies have on the results.

All claims within the 2 year period following the birth of the child will be considered when constructing outcome measures. We will reference (match on) mothers’ entire claims histories in order to assess pre-existing or the onset of physical and mental health conditions.

Patient and public involvement

While patient and public were not involved in the development of this study protocol, the HSP CHWs will be involved in the dissemination of the forthcoming results of the study.

Discussion

The prevalence of chronic conditions like obesity, hypertension and diabetes, which are disproportionately higher among racial and ethnic minority groups, increases the risk for pregnancy-related complications.20 45 The persistent disparities in pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality stem from a complex interplay of factors, including systemic racism, inequitable access to quality healthcare and the compounding effects of social determinants of health.11 Institutional racism, manifested through discriminatory practices and policies, has been directly linked to poor maternal health outcomes,20 46 for example, policies that restrict access to insurance coverage and healthcare facilities for racial and ethnic minorities45 and implicit biases and stereotyping by healthcare providers that can lead to suboptimal treatment and communication.47 48 The historical practice of forced sterilisation and unethical medical experimentation on Black and American Indian/Alaska Native women has contributed to mistrust and hesitancy in seeking care,49 as well as the legacy of discriminatory practices continuing to impact the pregnancies and outcomes for women of colour.46

CHW interventions have been shown to be effective in addressing social and structural determinants of health that exacerbate adverse health outcomes.28,33 Considering these contexts, the proposed study will contribute to the body of evidence to demonstrate the effectiveness of a CHW-based programme working with a disadvantaged, medically vulnerable, racially diverse population to improve health outcomes of high-risk pregnant women. This study will leverage the unique advantages of insurance claims data, namely, comprehensive, detailed, longitudinal information on health conditions and healthcare utilisation.

Recent policy innovations make this topic especially timely. In 2023, CHW services were made eligible for Medicaid reimbursement in Arizona, specifically if the Arizona Medicaid beneficiary has or is at risk for a chronic condition or has ‘a documented barrier that is affecting the member’s health’.50 Measuring the impact HSP has on the health of Arizona Medicaid beneficiaries is important for improving our understanding of this specific programme, as well as broader implications for the Medicaid eligible population as a whole.

Public health implications

This study protocol highlights the vital role of CHW home visitation in advancing maternal health equity. By evaluating the Arizona HSP, it provides evidence on how CHW-delivered home visiting through health education, care navigation and advocacy can reduce SMM and improve chronic disease and mental health management among Medicaid-insured women. Because HSP participants are primarily low-income, high-risk mothers, findings will inform how CHW home visitation addresses social and structural determinants of maternal health specifically and could serve to strengthen continuity of perinatal care across clinical and community systems. Results can guide state Medicaid policy on CHW reimbursement at state and national levels and help scale CHW-led home visitation programmes that improve maternal outcomes, reduce health disparities and contribute to national strategies to prevent maternal morbidity and mortality.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

This work is supported through an inter-agency contract from ADHS and approved by the ADHS Human Subjects Review Board, Project #17–00010, determined as non-human subjects research. Evaluation of the proposed outcomes will be completed by June 2027, and findings will be disseminated to HSP directors, managers and CHWs, as well as through academic journals and conferences.

Confidentiality and access to data

No primary data will be collected. Confidentiality is maintained using a secure honest broker system. All data were de-identified by ASU CHiR and compiled into a Limited Data Set for the NAU/UA evaluation team. The Limited Data Set is securely stored within NAU-managed computer servers that maintain protected health information anonymisation and HIPAA compliance of data for 5 years post study period.
