Plasmon-induced indirect charge transfer and vibronic stabilization in 4-nitrobenzenethiol
Authors: Hyoungchul Ham, Jaeyoung Jeong, Zee Hwan Kim, Myounwoo Kim, Jin Yong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasmon-driven reduction of 4-nitrobenzenethiol (4-NBT) has been linked experimentally to transient negative ions and nonthermal multiquantum vibrational excitation, yet the molecular nature of the electronically excited anionic state involved in this process remains unclear. Here, we present a time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) study of the Au5−4-NBT complex to examine how hot-electron injection accesses a low-lying excited anionic manifold and how these states couple to vibrational motion. Our results identify a low-lying quartet excited anionic state, denoted Q1, that becomes energetically accessible in the Au5−4-NBT complex under plasmon-driven vertical electron attachment (VEA) conditions. Natural transition orbital analysis shows that Q1 has mixed local-excitation and charge-transfer character, while comparison with free 4-NBT indicates that gold coordination reorganizes and lowers the relevant excited-state manifold. Vibronically resolved one-photon absorption (OPA) calculations show that access to Q1 is strongly influenced by the NO2 symmetric stretching coordinate, and one-photon emission (OPE) calculations indicate that the same coordinate also plays an important role in a possible radiative decay channel from Q1. Potential-energy profile analysis further identifies low-energy crossing regions along the NO2 coordinate that are consistent with vibronically assisted access to the excited anionic manifold. Taken together, these results provide a molecular-level framework for interpreting the transient anionic state and nonthermal multiquantum vibrational excitation proposed experimentally for plasmon-driven 4-NBT chemistry.

Introduction

Plasmon-enhanced catalytic systems have garnered increasing attention as a promising route for harvesting solar energy to drive chemical transformations under mild conditions, supporting global efforts to transition to more sustainable energy sources1–3. In these systems, metallic nanostructures—often gold or silver—absorb visible light via localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPRs). Upon excitations, LSPRs decay and generate energetic hot electrons (and corresponding hot holes). These hot carriers can be transferred to adsorbed molecules, enabling chemical reactions that are otherwise challenging or inefficient under purely thermal conditions4–6. Consequently, plasmonic approaches have been explored for crucial reactions like water splitting for hydrogen production, CO2 reduction, and selective organic transformations. Despite their potential, plasmon-driven processes exhibit relatively low quantum yields (commonly around 1%), motivating ongoing research to improve efficiency and deepen mechanistic understanding7–9.

A cornerstone of plasmonic catalysis is the hypothesis that these hot carriers directly initiate chemical bond transformations, lowering activation barriers and allowing precise control over reaction pathways10–12. At the same time, inevitable photothermal heating, caused by the non-radiative decay of plasmons, contributes to the reactivity change. In some reactions, such effect dominates over the non-thermal activation mechanism13,14. Disentangling these two effects—hot-electron-driven processes vs. purely photothermal mechanisms—remains an active area of debate. More broadly, plasmon-driven chemistry may also be viewed from a molecular perspective in terms of electronically excited-state, charged-state, and vibrationally excited ground-state pathways, depending on how plasmon energy is transferred to the adsorbate15. In many cases, both effects operate simultaneously, complicating efforts to attribute observed reactivity to one mechanism or the other16–18. To probe plasmon-assisted chemical mechanisms, numerous studies have focused on the reduction of 4-nitrobenzenethiol (4-NBT) on illuminated noble-metal surfaces, typically gold or silver nanoparticles19–21. Under plasmonic excitation, 4-NBT has been observed to form both 4-aminobenzenethiol (4-ABT) and 4,4′-dimercaptoazobenzene (DMAB), with the latter arising from dimerization of two 4-NBT molecules. These transformations, first discovered through surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), are of particular interest because they appear to occur almost exclusively under light irradiation; purely thermal conditions fail to drive such reductions effectively17,22,23. Consequently, 4-NBT is widely regarded as an ideal testbed for plasmonic catalysis, helping probe fundamental questions of hot-carrier generation, vibrational activation, and molecule–metal interactions18,24,25.

To investigate how transient negative ions (TNIs) can contribute to molecular activation, we focus on how hot-electron attachment and vibronic coupling may promote access to electronically excited anionic states under plasmonic conditions. Recent experimental advances have identified additional mechanisms beyond hot-electron transfer, including nonthermal vibrational activation in which hot-electron energy is transferred into specific molecular vibrational modes26–28. This mode-selective behavior provides a new layer of control over plasmon-assisted chemistry. In 4-nitrobenzenethiol (4-NBT), transient anionic states formed by hot-electron injection have been linked experimentally to selective excitation of NO/NO2-related vibrational coordinates and to multiquantum vibrational excitation (v ≥ 2), suggesting that both electronic and vibrational dynamics are important in the reduction process26–28.

Previous theoretical studies on nitrothiophenol systems have provided important insight into plasmon-assisted and electrochemical reaction pathways, but they have focused mainly on ground-state reaction energetics, thermodynamic profiles, and vibrational spectral assignments. For example, DFT studies on Ag-cluster models examined the coupling mechanism of 4-NBT to DMAB through optimized intermediates, transition states, and vibrational analyses under irradiated and non-irradiated conditions29. Likewise, theoretical work on plasmon-enhanced surface catalysis emphasized thermodynamic and kinetic comparisons between plasmon-driven photocatalysis and surface-catalyzed reaction channels30. In electroreduction studies of PNTP on gold and silver surfaces, theoretical analysis was further combined with simulated surface Raman and IR spectra to assign intermediates and products on the basis of ground-state energetics31. Although these studies established valuable energetic and spectroscopic frameworks, they did not directly address how hot-electron injection accesses low-lying excited anionic states or how such states couple to molecular vibrations under plasmonic conditions.

In this work, we examine how plasmon decay can generate hot electrons with sufficient energy (≥0.75 eV above the ground state) to access a low-lying transient anionic state in 4-nitrobenzenethiol (4-NBT) through vertical electron attachment (VEA). Our focus is on a two-step process: (i) generation of energetic hot electrons within the gold cluster and (ii) their injection into the molecule’s LUMO-like manifold. Although the resulting transient anionic state lies in the visible-energy range (~1.96 eV; e.g., 633 nm excitation), we interpret its formation in the present system in terms of hot-electron-driven VEA rather than purely direct optical charge transfer. Building on recent SERS studies of plasmon-driven 4-NBT chemistry26–28, we employ a minimal Au5−4-NBT cluster model to capture key metal–molecule interactions that are often neglected in simpler frameworks. Through TD-DFT simulations, we examine transient anionic states and vibronic coupling, with particular attention to the symmetric N–O stretching coordinate of the nitro group. By analyzing how hot-electron attachment gives access to this low-lying transient anionic state under plasmonic conditions, we show that its formation is governed by the energetic distribution of hot carriers together with mode-selective vibronic coupling. In this sense, the present study provides a molecular-level framework for interpreting how hot electrons and molecular vibrations jointly influence plasmon-driven 4-NBT chemistry.

Results and discussion

Mechanistic insights into TNI formation in plasmonic systems

Raman/ESP analysis: identifying the NO2 vibrational hot spot

The CAM-B3LYP32 functional, in combination with the Def2TZVPP/Def2TZVP basis sets33, was selected based on the DFT comparison summarized in Table S1, in which the tested functionals were evaluated for their ability to reproduce the vibrational properties of the NO2 group. Among the methods examined, CAM-B3LYP32 showed the smallest deviation for the anionic-state frequencies and provided the most self-consistent description of the spectral changes induced by electron attachment. Although the neutral-state frequencies were overestimated by approximately 50 cm−1, this did not compromise the method’s ability to capture the relative vibrational trends and structural changes relevant to the present analysis. CAM-B3LYP32 therefore, provides an appropriate framework for examining the vibrational consequences of electron attachment in the Au5−4-NBT complex.

Our aim is to determine whether VEA under plasmonic conditions prepares a nonequilibrium anionic configuration that is spectroscopically and structurally distinct from the relaxed anion, and therefore potentially relevant to subsequent reactivity. To address this question, we first establish the vibrational baseline of the neutral complex and then examine how one-electron attachment modifies both the Raman response and the electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution. Rather than assigning a localized electron-trapping site, this analysis is intended to identify which vibrational coordinates respond most strongly to electron injection.

In the harmonic spectra, the symmetric NO2 stretching mode (νNO2) appears at 1379 cm−1 for the Neutral eq structure (Fig. 1b), shifts to 1369 cm−1 for the VEA structure at the fixed neutral geometry (Fig. 1c), and further red-shifts to 1358 cm−1 after full anionic relaxation (Fig. 1d). VEA therefore produces an initial red shift of 10 cm−1, followed by a further red shift of 11 cm−1 upon relaxation, giving a total shift of 21 cm−1 relative to the neutral state. Notably, the initial 10 cm−1 red shift at the VEA geometry is consistent in direction and broadly comparable in magnitude to the experimentally observed early red shifts (~6–7 cm−1) assigned to transient anionic intermediates during plasmon-driven and electrochemically induced reduction of 4-NBT28. This sequential red shift is consistent with progressive softening of the N–O bonds following one-electron addition and subsequent structural relaxation.

The Raman intensity of νNO2 also increases markedly after electron attachment. Together with the frequency red shift, this behavior indicates that the NO2 stretching mode is particularly sensitive to one-electron perturbation and becomes increasingly activated as the system evolves from Neutral eq to VEA and then to Anion eq.

The ESP maps in Fig. 1e provide complementary information on how the molecular electrostatic environment changes during this process. In the neutral equilibrium geometry, the nitro oxygens exhibit the most negative ESP values, whereas the nitrogen remains slightly positive, indicating a local dipolar character within an otherwise relatively uniform aromatic framework. Upon VEA, the pronounced electrostatic contrast around the NO2 group is substantially reduced, and the potential becomes more evenly distributed over the π-conjugated scaffold. After full anionic relaxation, the ESP surface becomes still more uniform. Taken together, these maps suggest that the excess electron is not statically trapped at the nitro group, but is redistributed over the conjugated framework both before and after structural relaxation.

Importantly, the delocalized character of the attached electron does not preclude selective vibrational activation. Rather, Fig. 1 shows that a globally delocalized one-electron perturbation can nevertheless produce a disproportionately strong structural and spectroscopic response at the NO2 unit. In this sense, the selectivity discussed here is vibrational rather than spatial: electron attachment does not generate persistent charge localization at the nitro group, but it does preferentially perturb the NO2-related vibrational coordinate.

Taken together, comparison of the Neutral eq, VEA, and Anion eq states shows that the NO2 mode responds immediately to VEA and becomes more strongly activated after anionic relaxation. Although only a modest red shift appears in the VEA structure at the fixed neutral geometry, the fully relaxed anion exhibits the largest shift and the strongest Raman enhancement. These results suggest that the most pronounced spectral and structural consequences of electron attachment arise not from statically localized excess charge, but from the evolution of a vertically prepared, vibrationally nonequilibrium anionic configuration toward its relaxed anionic structure. This interpretation is consistent with experimental Raman and IR studies showing that the NO2 stretching mode in 4-NBT is highly sensitive to changes in the electronic environment34,35. Accordingly, the NO2 vibration may be regarded as a key vibrational coordinate that is especially responsive to electron attachment in this system. In the context of plasmonic catalysis, this pronounced sensitivity suggests that vibronic coupling involving the NO2 stretching coordinate may contribute to transient anionic activation and may influence subsequent reaction pathways.

NTO analysis: transition to the excited anionic state (Q1)

To clarify the terminology used throughout this work, Q1 is defined here as the low-lying quartet excited state of the electron-attached Au5−4-NBT complex at the VEA geometry. We do not use Q1 to denote a state of free 4-NBT. Rather, Q1 refers specifically to a quartet state that becomes relevant in the metal-bound complex under plasmon-driven hot-electron injection conditions.

Calculations on free 4-NBT are included here only as a molecular reference to identify the intrinsic vibrational and excited-state features of the adsorbate in the absence of metal coordination. We do not regard free 4-NBT as a quantitative description of the plasmonic interface, because metal–molecule orbital mixing can substantially reorganize the interfacial excited-state manifold. Recent experimental and theoretical studies have likewise shown that free-molecule descriptions remain useful for identifying mode-selective vibrational responses, while the full interfacial charge-transfer problem requires explicit treatment of metal–molecule coupling26,27. To address this limitation, we additionally examine the Au5−4-NBT complex as a minimal interfacial model, which allows us to assess how gold coordination reorganizes and lowers the low-lying excited-state manifold relevant to hot-electron attachment.

A useful comparison is provided by free 4-NBT. Although free 4-NBT also supports quartet excited states, these states lie at substantially higher energies and are therefore less accessible within the hot-electron energy window relevant to 633 nm plasmonic excitation. Upon coordination to the Au5 cluster, the excited-state manifold is reorganized and lowered in energy, bringing a low-lying quartet state into a range that can be accessed through VEA. For this reason, the state discussed here is not simply a free 4-NBT quartet excitation, but a complex-specific low-energy quartet state of Au5−4-NBT. By contrast, Q1 is identified as a low-lying quartet excitation with mixed local-excitation and charge-transfer character. As shown in Fig. 2, the D0 → Q1 excitation is described by two major NTO pairs with comparable contributions (48.6% and 42.0%), indicating that Q1 is not dominated by a single, purely local or purely charge-transfer transition. Instead, the hole and particle distributions exhibit both local π-electronic reorganization and partial charge-transfer features within the Au5−4-NBT complex. This mixed LE/CT character, together with the moderate oscillator strength and appreciable transition dipole moment of Q1, supports its assignment as the most plausible electronically excited anionic state to consider in the Au5−4-NBT complex under plasmon-driven VEA conditions.

Hot-electron injection at 633 nm: accessing the excited anionic state

Further support for Q1 as the operative post-VEA excited state comes from the UV–Vis absorption profiles of the fully relaxed anionic equilibrium, shown in Fig. 3. In contrast to the neutral form (Fig. 3a), this anionic configuration (Fig. 3b) exhibits a pronounced absorption around 1.96 eV (633 nm), aligning directly with the red-laser excitation frequencies routinely employed in SERS experiments7. Importantly, we emphasize that this does not necessarily imply a one-step direct optical charge transfer; instead, our calculations indicate that hot electrons—with kinetic energies in the range of 0.75–2 eV—are capable of populating Q1 through a two-step mechanism (plasmon decay → hot electron → vertical electron attachment). Because the ~1.96 eV energy overlaps with the 633 nm excitation commonly used in plasmonic SERS experiments, our results support the plausibility of hot-electron-driven VEA into a low-lying excited anionic state of the Au5−4-NBT complex, consistent with prior reports of transient anionic intermediates and nonthermal hot-electron/TNI-mediated activation of NBT under 632.8–633 nm plasmonic excitation26–28. Taken together, our data suggest that, under plasmonic excitation, 4-NBT may access not only a relaxed ground-state anion but also an electronically excited anionic manifold represented here by Q1. This picture provides a refined framework for understanding hot-electron-driven processes, in which VEA can populate electronically excited anionic states that are coupled to vibrational motion. Moreover, our analysis suggests that such electronically excited anionic configurations may be relevant to subsequent reduction chemistry in addition to the fully relaxed ground-state anion. Mechanistic insight into hot-electron–driven catalysis

The accessibility and reactivity of the operative post-VEA excited state are intimately connected to molecular vibrations, especially those centered on the nitro group, which strongly influence the electronic and vibrational response of the system. Having established that hot-electron attachment can access a low-lying excited anionic state (Q1) in Au5−4-NBT, we next examine how specific vibrational modes shape access to Q1 and the vibronic pathways relevant to its subsequent relaxation and reactivity.

These findings underscore the important role of vibronic coupling in enabling electronic transitions that would otherwise remain weak in a purely electronic picture. Although the corresponding transition dipole strengths (D = 0.01–0.03 a.u.) are low—indicating that nuclear motions, especially those of the NO2 group, make a substantial contribution to the transition mechanism—these small values suggest that selective vibrational excitation plays an important role in promoting access to Q1. Typically, dipole-allowed transitions exceed D > 0.1 a.u.; thus, the lower transition dipole strengths observed here are consistent with a significant vibrational contribution to the transition. Moreover, coupling between the NO2 stretch (mode 42) and the symmetric ring stretch (mode 35) enhances access to Q1 under hot-electron conditions, suggesting that different mode combinations may influence the pathways by which the excited anionic manifold is reached. In this respect, vibrationally induced electron redistribution across the benzene ring and NO2 group can activate transitions that might otherwise remain too weak. Accordingly, mode 35 may serve as an additional coupling coordinate that facilitates access to Q1 in combination with the dominant NO2 stretching progression.

Brandt et al.’s38 ultrafast pump-probe measurements provide experimental evidence for strong vibronic coupling under plasmonic excitation in 4-NBT. In plasmonic hotspots, where hot-electron generation is enhanced, they observed transient Fano resonances arising from the interaction between vibrational scattering and hot-electron-driven photoluminescence38. These features indicate strong vibrational–electronic coupling and correlate with enhanced nitro-thiol dimerization yields. Although such Fano resonances do not by themselves identify a specific molecular intermediate, our TD-DFT results show that gold coordination reorganizes the excited-state manifold of 4-NBT and makes a comparable low-lying quartet anionic state accessible in the Au5−4-NBT complex. In this sense, the experimental observation of strong vibronic coupling and the calculated accessibility of Q1 are mutually consistent with a metal–molecule interfacial mechanism in which hot-electron attachment and vibrational motion act together. Thus, while the Fano resonance does not directly establish Q1, it supports the broader picture that mode-specific vibronic coupling at the metal–molecule interface can influence plasmon-driven catalytic pathways.

One-photon emission (OPE) analysis of a radiative decay channel from Q1

To complement the OPA analysis of access to Q1, we next evaluated the one-photon emission (OPE) spectrum associated with radiative relaxation from Q1 in the Au5−4-NBT complex. Whereas the OPA spectrum probes the vibronic pathways by which the excited anionic state is reached, the OPE spectrum provides a vibronically resolved description of a possible radiative decay channel from Q1 to the neutral ground-state manifold. In this sense, the OPE analysis is used here to characterize the radiative relaxation pathway itself, rather than to establish the overall decay dynamics of Q1 in the presence of competing non-radiative processes.

Strategic implications for catalyst design via engineering low-energy channels

Mechanistic role of PES crossings in plasmon-induced charge transfer

Building on these theoretical insights, we examine the mechanistic implications of low-energy vibronic access channels in Au5−4-NBT, particularly for strongly bound molecules such as 4-NBT. To bridge theory and application, we performed single-point energy scans along the NO2 symmetric stretching coordinate in both free 4-NBT and Au5−4-NBT. As depicted in Fig. 6, the resulting potential energy profiles (PEP) indicate multiple crossing points (within 0.75–1.13 eV) between the neutral ground state (S0) and low-lying excited states (including Q1, D6, and D7). Because hot electrons generated at 633 nm (1.96 eV) exceed these thresholds, they may access the excited anionic manifold, consistent with experimental reports of transient anionic intermediates and TNI-mediated nonthermal activation under 632.8–633 nm plasmonic excitation26–28. The low-energy barriers, combined with resonance in vibrational modes such as NO2, are consistent with a vibronically assisted VEA pathway into the Q1 state. These crossings are mechanistically significant because they identify regions where the neutral and low-lying anionic states come into close energetic proximity, thereby increasing the likelihood of vibronic coupling along the NO2 coordinate. In this sense, the PEPs do not provide a direct dynamical description of electron transfer, but they do indicate geometries at which access to the excited anionic manifold becomes energetically more favorable. Influence of gold coordination on electronic and vibrational structure

Comparing free 4-NBT with Au5−4-NBT suggests that gold coordination reorganizes and lowers the low-lying excited-state manifold relevant to hot-electron attachment. In free 4-NBT, the corresponding excited states remain at higher energy and the available vibronic access channels are comparatively limited. By contrast, in Au5−4-NBT, gold coordination shifts the relevant crossing energies into a range more compatible with 633 nm plasmonic excitation while also modifying the vibrational response of the NO2 coordinate. In particular, changes in the NO2 symmetric stretch are consistent with stronger metal–molecule coupling, which may influence access to Q1. These metal-induced modifications suggest that nanoparticle parameters, such as geometry, size, and composition, may influence how hot-carrier energies and molecular vibrations are coupled. Within the present Au5−4-NBT model, these results suggest that effective plasmonic design may benefit from aligning the hot-carrier energy distribution of the metal with molecular coordinates that are strongly involved in vibronic access to the excited anionic manifold.

Conclusion

In summary, our results support the presence of a low-lying excited anionic state, denoted Q1, in the Au5−4-NBT complex under plasmon-driven hot-electron injection conditions. Rather than a one-step optical excitation picture, the present TD-DFT analysis is consistent with access to Q1 through hot-electron-driven VEA into the coordination-modified excited-state manifold. The OPA analysis indicates that vibronic access to Q1 is strongly influenced by the NO2 symmetric stretching coordinate, whereas the OPE analysis shows that the same coordinate also plays an important role in a possible radiative decay channel from Q1. Comparison with free 4-NBT further suggests that gold coordination reorganizes and lowers the relevant excited-state manifold, bringing low-energy access channels into a range more compatible with plasmonic excitation. Taken together, these results provide theoretical support for the transient-negative-ion-mediated, nonthermal multiquantum vibrational excitation proposed experimentally for NBT under plasmonic conditions. In this sense, the present study provides a molecular-level framework for interpreting both the nature of the transient anionic state and the origin of mode-selective multiquantum vibrational excitation in Au5−4-NBT.

The present Au5−4-NBT model captures the essential metal-coordination effect while remaining in a regime where a clean, mode-resolved Franck-Condon analysis can be performed. Several directions extend naturally from this work. Larger gold clusters and extended-surface models, together with explicit time-resolved non-adiabatic dynamical treatments, will be needed to describe hot-electron injection in the actual plasmonic environment. Spin-orbit coupling, not treated in the present TD-DFT framework, may further refine the formation and decay channels of Q1. A careful treatment of solvent and interface effects will likewise be important for connecting the gas-phase mechanistic description established here with experimentally relevant conditions.

Methods

Computational models and software

All calculations were performed in Gaussian 16 (Revision A.03).39A planar Au₅ cluster was used as a minimal model of the metal–molecule interface in the Au5−4-NBT complex; the rationale for this choice and benchmarks against larger models are discussed in the Supplementary Methods.

Geometries and vibrational analysis

Geometries of the Au5−4-NBT complex and free 4-NBT were optimized at the B2PLYP-D3/Def2-TZVPP level40,41 (Def2-TZVP with an effective core potential for Au)33. Harmonic and anharmonic Raman spectra were obtained at the CAM-B3LYP/Def2-TZVPP32 level for the neutral equilibrium, VEA, and anionic equilibrium states. The choice of CAM-B3LYP32 and a comparison of seven density functionals for the NO₂-related vibrational modes are provided in the Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Excited-state and vibronic analysis

Excited-state properties were obtained from time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) at the B972/Def2-TZVPP level42. Vibronically resolved one-photon absorption (S0 → Q1) and one-photon emission (Q1 → S0) spectra were simulated using the Franck–Condon adiabatic Hessian method, with a low-frequency cutoff of 300 cm⁻¹ and a broadening of 135 cm−1. Potential-energy profiles (PEPs) along selected normal-mode coordinates were constructed by single-point TD-DFT scans of displaced geometries from the S₀ equilibrium. Full computational details for the OPA and OPE Franck–Condon simulations and for the PEP scans are provided in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Note 2, respectively, with general computational protocols described in the Supplementary Methods.
