Extracellular Vesicles Modulation by an Adiponectin Receptor Agonist Provides Cardioprotection for Myocardial Ischemic Injury
Authors: Jialing Tang, Wing Yan Chung, Serena M Pulente, Yubin Lei, Khang Nguyen, Eddie Tam, Nadya M Morrow, Ilka Lorenzen‐Schmidt, Fengxia Xiao, Augusto Zani, Dylan Burger, Hye Kyoung Sung, Erin E Mulvihill, Gary Sweeney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschemic heart disease is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, with more effective clinical therapies needed to mitigate cardiac ischemic injury. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) mediate intercellular communication in cardiac pathophysiology. Adiponectin mediates cardioprotective effects yet its relationship with EVs biology remains unexplored. We investigated whether ALY688, an adiponectin receptor agonist, modulates EV biogenesis and cargo to confer cardioprotection in a mouse model of myocardial infarction (MI). ALY688 (15 mg/kg daily for 28 days) significantly attenuated MI‐induced cardiac dysfunction, reduced infarct size, and decreased fibrotic scar in both lean mice on standard chow and obese mice fed a high‐fat diet. Plasma EVs were isolated and while MI reduced circulating EV numbers, ALY688 restored EV production and enhanced loading with bioactive adiponectin. In addition, proteomic analysis revealed ALY688‐shaped EVs were enriched in metabolism regulated proteins. The direct functional effects and mechanisms of EV action were examined in iPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes and H9c2 cells. EV from ALY688 treated mice (EVALY) reduced hypoxia‐induced apoptosis, cell death, and ROS accumulation while restoring mitochondrial function and enhancing autophagy flux, whereas EV from control mice did not. Importantly, intervention with systemic EVALY administration to mice reduced post‐MI plasma troponin I and LDH levels, decreased apoptosis, and improved mitochondrial dynamics. Thus, we show that ALY688 enhances EV‐mediated cardioprotection through multiple cellular mechanisms including adiponectin delivery, oxidative stress reduction, autophagy restoration, and mitochondrial recovery. These findings provide preclinical evidence supporting the feasibility of evidence for harnessing endogenous repair mechanisms through EVs modulation to mitigate acute cardiovascular injury.Keywords: adiponectin, cardiac protection, extracellular vesicles, myocardial ischemia

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease has emerged as the leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [1]. Myocardial ischemia results from coronary blood flow obstruction following rupture or erosion of coronary artery plaques [2]. The mechanisms underlying post‐ischemic cardiac remodeling are complex and multifaceted [3], involving intricate molecular pathways including inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress, metabolic reprogramming, and neurohormonal activation that collectively orchestrate the transition from compensatory adaptation to pathological dysfunction. These pathophysiological processes encompass myocyte loss, cardiac hypertrophy, extracellular matrix disruption, fibrosis, defective autophagy, and mitochondrial dysfunction, prompting intensive research efforts to identify new therapeutic targets [4].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are small, membrane‐bound particles surrounded by a lipid bilayer [5]. Different types of EVs are produced and released through distinct cellular processes. Exosomes (approximately 100 nm in diameter) are secreted when intracellular multivesicular bodies (MVBs) fuse with the plasma membrane [6]. Microvesicles (150–1000 nm in diameter) originate from the outward budding of cholesterol‐rich regions of the plasma membrane [6]. Other larger vesicles, such as apoptotic bodies, are enclosed by a membrane and can contain nuclear fragments or cellular organelles [7]. EVs functionally mediate intercellular communication under both physiological and pathological conditions by transferring bioactive cargo including proteins, lipids, mRNAs, and small non‐coding RNAs through various biological fluids [8]. EVs play pivotal roles in metabolic regulation pathways [9, 10], and serve as critical mediators of organ–organ crosstalk. EVs biogenesis is tightly regulated by multiple cellular signaling pathways, including endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) complexes, ceramide metabolism, and metabolic stress responses, such as AMPK activation, which can influence both EV production and cargo selection [11, 12]. However, whether ALY688‐induced AMPK/p38MAPK signaling directly regulates canonical EV biogenesis machinery (e.g., ESCRT complexes, ceramide‐dependent pathways, or MVB formation) remains to be experimentally established.

Recent advances in EVs research have revealed their therapeutic potential, with engineered EVs emerging as promising candidates for targeted cardiovascular therapeutics due to their inherent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to cross biological barriers [13]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that EV‐mediated communication is intimately involved in ischemic injury, exerting both beneficial and detrimental effects on cardiac outcomes [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)‐derived EVs, along with those from other sources, exert cardioprotective effects by modulating cell death, inflammation, and angiogenesis. Functionally, these EVs reduce infarct size, preserve ventricular function, and promote new vessel formation [17, 19, 20]. Several EVs types reprogram immune cells toward anti‐inflammatory phenotypes or deliver miRNAs that suppress apoptosis and oxidative damage [16]. Others, such as those from cardiomyocytes or endothelial progenitor cells, maintain mitochondrial integrity and stimulate endothelial proliferation [21, 22]. Collectively, compelling evidence demonstrates that EVs support cardiac repair after injury. Conversely, EVs can exacerbate myocardial injury through activation of pro‐inflammatory pathways. Specifically, post‐infarction cardiac EVs expressing cardiomyocyte and endothelial markers enhance monocyte secretion of IL‐6, CCL2, and CCL7 within the infarct zone [23]. Moreover, circulating inflammatory EVs carrying IL‐1α, IL‐1β, and RANTES trigger cardiomyocyte cytotoxicity through TLR4‐dependent NF‐κB activation [24]. Complementing this inflammatory response, M1 macrophage‐derived EVs activate pro‐inflammatory signaling cascades [25], while EVs from ischemic cardiomyocytes further impair macrophage cardioprotective functions, reducing phagocytic capacity during ischemia‐reperfusion [26]. The complex interplay between beneficial and detrimental EVs functions underscores their central importance in MI pathophysiology, positioning them as critical mediators that determine cardiac repair vs. maladaptive remodeling outcomes.

Adiponectin, a circulating anti‐inflammatory cytokine secreted from the adipose tissue, has been previously reported to have multiple cardioprotective effects, including in ischemic injury [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Induction of myocardial infarction in adiponectin knockout mice results in exacerbated left‐ventricular dilation and hypertrophy with increased apoptosis and fibrosis [29, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Robust evidence indicates that adiponectin regulates EV biogenesis in MSCs [36, 37]. The administration of adiponect in driven EVs derived from MSCs notably enhanced cardiac function in mice subjected to pressure overload [37]. Recent findings suggest that adipose tissue‐derived EVs are carriers of adiponectin with insulin‐sensitizing and anti‐inflammatory properties [38, 39]. ALY688 is a 10 amino acid peptide that has been shown to bind AdipoR1 and AdipoR2 and induce adiponectin‐like effects and cardiac protection in several cellular and animal models [40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. Mechanistically, ALY688 activates key signaling pathways including AMPK phosphorylation (T172) and p38MAPK activation, which are known regulators of cellular metabolism, stress responses, and endosomal trafficking, processes integral to EVs biogenesis and cargo loading [45]. Furthermore, AMPK activation has been implicated in the regulation of autophagy and multivesicular body formation, suggesting potential crosstalk between adiponectin receptor signaling and EV production pathways [46].

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of both adiponectin signaling and EVs in cardiovascular disease, the mechanistic intersection between adiponectin receptor activation and EVs biology remains unexplored. Given that metabolic reprogramming and cellular stress responses are central to both adiponectin signaling and EVs biogenesis and considering that ALY688‐induced AMPK activation could potentially influence endosomal trafficking and MVB formation, we hypothesized that ALY688 treatment might enhance EV production, modify EV cargo composition toward cardioprotective profiles, and improve EV‐mediated intercellular communication in the ischemic heart. Understanding this potential ALY688‐EV axis could reveal previously uncharacterized therapeutic mechanisms and provide a foundation for developing EV‐based cardiovascular therapeutics.

Here, we aimed to delineate the mechanistic role of ALY688 as a regulator of EV biogenesis and secretion and elucidate ALY688‐EV‐mediated cardiac protection in a myocardial ischemia mouse model.

Results

Result 1: ALY688 Improved MI Induced Cardiac Dysfunction and Reduced Fibrosis in Both SC and HFD Mice

To induce MI in vivo, we performed permanent left anterior descending artery (LAD) ligation in mice, a classic model for myocardial ischemia. Considering obesity is a predominant risk factor of coronary heart disease [47], mice were fed a 3‐week HFD before being subjected to surgery (Figure 1A and Figure S1A). ALY688 was administered to mice consecutively for 28 days at a dose of 15 mg/kg (Figure 1A). At terminal time point, heart weight (HW) was measured, and the ratio of HW and tibial length (TL), and HW and body weight (BW) was used as the index of cardiac hypertrophy. Results showed that MI induced hypertrophy was decreased by ALY688, in both SC and HFD groups (Figure S1B,C). To evaluate functional recovery and cardiac fibrosis following ALY688 treatment, mice underwent serial echocardiography over 4 weeks and terminal histological analysis of the heart. Notably, the dysregulation of ejection fraction and fractional shortening induced by MI was ameliorated following ALY688 intervention in both SC and HFD groups (Figure 1B–C, E–F, Table 1, and Figure S1D). Interestingly, while ALY688 provided cardiac function protection in both SC and HFD groups, there was no further exacerbation of ejection fraction, fractional shortening, end diastolic volume, and end systolic volume by HFD feeding in MI mice (Figure 1D,F and Figure S1E,F). Strain analysis showed that ALY688 significantly improved MI‐induced radial strain (RS) impairment in both SC and HFD groups (Figure S1G,H). Longitudinal strain (LS) was markedly altered by MI and partially restored by ALY688, regardless of diet (Figure S1I). Circumferential strain (CS) was significantly increased in HFD‐fed MI mice (Figure S1J), further supporting a beneficial effect of ALY688 on regional myocardial deformation under metabolic stress. MI size was evaluated using 2D akinetic length from the parasternal long‐axis view, and 4D volumetric measurement as described previously [48] at 4 weeks post‐MI (Figure 1G). ALY688 administered mice displayed a decreased akinetic length (Figure S2A), infarct size (Figure 1H) and infarct volume (Figure S2B). Additionally, MI increased end‑diastolic volume in both border and remote regions, while ALY688 significantly reduced border‑zone volume in the HFD group and remote volume in both SC and HFD groups, consistent with an early attenuation of adverse geometric remodeling (Figure S2C,D). Correspondingly, histological analysis of hearts by Masson trichrome and H&E staining demonstrating the increased collagen deposition induced by MI was decreased by ALY688, no further changes were observed in the HFD groups compared to SC (Figure S2E,F). Similarly, ⍺‐SMA, Timp1, and Col1a1 mRNA expression in infarct heart tissue, confirming that ALY688 reduced the fibrosis at 4 weeks post MI regardless of dietary intervention (Figure 1J–L). However, there was no consistent mRNA expression changes to Col3a1, Col4a1, MMP3, or MMP9 in infarcted hearts (Figure S2G–J).

Results 2: Characterization of Dysregulated EV Derived From MI Mice Plasma

Alterations in EVs has been reported to contribute to coronary heart disease [49, 50], and adiponectin is well‐documented to increase EVs biogenesis [37]. To investigate the impact of MI on circulating EVs and determine whether the adiponectin receptor agonist ALY688 affects EVs levels, plasma EVs from mice described above were isolated by size exclusion chromatography. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that ALY688 increased circulating number of EVs in both SC and HFD MI groups. Surprisingly, MI surgery significantly decreased EVs number, while this reduction was slightly improved by ALY688 treatment (Figure 2A,C and Figure S3A). Cryo‐EM confirmed lipid bilayers and electron‐dense cargo in isolated plasma EVs (Figure 2B). Regarding EVs size, MI increased EVs diameter in the SC group, and this enlargement was prevented by ALY688. However, this effect was not observed in the HFD group, although EVs from HFD MI were larger than those from SC MI mice (Figure S3B). All isolated vesicles were positive for the canonical EVs markers CD63, CD81, and Alix (Figure 2D,E) and negative for calnexin and Apoa1 (Figure S3C,D). Additionally, our previous comprehensive proteomics analysis of infarcted heart from ischemia reperfusion (IR) rat revealed that Rab8a expression is significantly decreased in IR compared to sham controls. Critically, ALY688 treatment restored Rab8a expression to baseline levels (Journal of Extracellular Vesicle, under revision). Given that Rab8a regulates vesicle trafficking and EV secretion across multiple cell types [10, 11, 12], we hypothesized that ALY688‐mediated Rab8a restoration may contribute to adiponectin receptor‐EV crosstalk during myocardial ischemia. Here we also added new data to prove that under MI, Rab8a expression was significantly decreased, while upon ALY688 administration, the level of Rab8a was improved in both SC and HDF mice heart tissue (Figure S3E,F). However, in the absence of functional manipulation of Rab8a (e.g., knockdown or overexpression experiments), this association remains correlative and requires future mechanistic validation to establish a causal relationship.

To elucidate the EV‐mediated mechanisms underlying the MI pathology, HFD effects, and the beneficial actions of ALY688 at the proteomic level, we performed quantitative proteomic analysis in the isolated plasma EVs. Partial least‐squares discriminant analysis (PLSDA) and hierarchical clustering demonstrated more distinct separation between treatment groups in the HFD condition compared to the SC condition (Figure 2F–I and Figure S4A–H). Analysis of EV proteomes by PLSDA revealed significant diet‐dependent proteomic alterations: HFD produced distinct, well‐separated group clusters (Component 1: 30.6%, Component 2: 31.0% variance explained) with clear treatment discrimination, whereas standard chow resulted in overlapping group distributions with substantially weaker discriminatory power (Component 1: 17.2%, Component 2: 14.8% variance explained) under both experimental conditions (Figure 2F–I). Whereas the gross cardioprotective effect of ALY688 indicated by echocardiography was observed under both diet conditions (Figure 1). Nevertheless, pathway enrichment analysis with the differentially expressed protein proteins (Padj. > 0.05, fold change ≥ 1.5) from the comparison of MI treated with ALY vs. PBS demonstrated fructose metabolism was enriched in SC context, while cholesterol metabolism and metabolic pathway were enriched in HFD context (Figure S4I,J). Comparative proteomic profiling showed that ALY688 or MI induced only a few significant changes under SC, while extensive remodeling occurred in the HFD background. Notably, there was almost no overlap in differentially expressed proteins between SC and HFD groups, with only two proteins shared (Figure 2J,L and Tables S1 and S2). This divergence indicated that HFD imposes a distinct EVs proteomic landscape. Pathway enrichment analysis (GO: Molecular Function) further highlighted the diet‐dependent divergence in EVs proteomes. In the comparison of SC sham ALY vs. HFD sham ALY, differentially expressed proteins were enriched for functions related to immune binding and regulation of protease activity, including antigen binding, peptidase inhibitor/regulator activity, enzyme inhibitor activity (Figure 2K). By contrast, in the comparison of SC MI ALY vs. HFD MI ALY, enriched terms were predominantly associated with lipid metabolism and enzymatic activity, such as lipid binding, cholesterol and sterol transfer activity, lipoprotein particle receptor binding, and phosphatidylcholine‐sterol O‐acyltransferase activity, along with antioxidant and peptidase‐related activities (Figure 2M). These distinct functional enrichments emphasize that HFD imposes a unique EVs proteomic landscape, particularly by engaging pathways linked to protease regulation and lipid handling, which were not prominent under SC conditions.

Results 3: ALY688 Enhanced EV‐Loading Adiponectin

Next, we tested adiponectin content in the isolated plasma EVs. Interestingly, EVs adiponectin levels were elevated following ALY688 injection in sham condition, with a trend toward further enhancement when comparing MI and sham groups, regardless of diet (Figure 3A–C). Upon MI injury, ALY688 intervention further increased the abundance of EV‐carried adiponectin in the SC group (Figure 3A–C). To determine whether the alterations of EV‐adiponectin reflect cardiac adiponectin status, we also examined the adiponectin level in infarcted heart tissue. Consistently, MI injury significantly enhanced cardiac adiponectin levels, with further increase observed following ALY688 treatment in both SC and HFD groups (Figure 3D–F). Under nonreducing conditions, low‐molecular‐weight forms of adiponectin, recognized as metabolically active forms, tended to be enriched in the SC MI mice injected with ALY688, though this difference was not statistically significant (Figure S5A–C). In parallel, these active forms also showed an increasing trend when comparing MI ALY vs. MI PBS groups, but only in the SC condition (Figure S5D–F). Correlation analysis revealed a weak but significant association between adiponectin levels in EVs and infarcted heart tissue following MI injury and ALY688 administration (Figure 3G,H). To further investigate ALY688's capacity to enhance EV‐adiponectin loading, we administered ALY688 to mice at 15 mg/kg for 3 days, then collected plasma for EVs isolation. NTA data demonstrated that ALY688 significantly increased the EVs number while particle size remained unchanged (Figure 3I–K), which is consistent with our previous observations. EVs characterization confirmed positivity typical markers (Figure 3L–P), and negative for calnexin and apoa1 (Figure S5G). Following ALY688 injection, EV‐loaded adiponectin was significantly enhanced (Figure 3L,Q). To validate the functional cardioprotective potency of EV‐carried adiponectin in cardiomyocytes, we treated rat cardiomyoblast H9c2 cell lines with EVs isolated from mice injected with either PBS (EVPBS) or ALY (EVALY) and examined adiponectin signaling activation. As presented, phospho‐AMPK and phosphor‐p38 were activated upon EVALY treatment, whereas no activation was observed with of EVPBS treatment (Figure 3R–T). Together, these findings suggest that ALY688 enhances EV‐carried adiponectin content, which maintains similar functional properties to native adiponectin. Because the subsequent mechanistic experiments including EVs functional assays and targeted validation required a consistent metabolic background, all follow‐up studies were conducted in the SC cohort to minimize diet‐driven confounding and allow clearer interpretation of ALY688‐ and MI‐related EVs changes.

Results 4: ALY688 Driven Plasma EVs Protective Effect in Hypoxia Cellular Model

In order to optimize the suitable dose of EVs treatment, in H9c2 cells exposed to normoxia or hypoxia, we evaluated a wide range of EV concentrations (1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL) for both EVPBS and EVALY groups. No significant cell death or cytotoxicity was observed under normoxic conditions at any concentration, even at 200 µg/mL, indicating a strong safety margin and absence of toxicity at doses far exceeding the therapeutic range. Under hypoxic conditions, we observed a clear dose‐dependent protective effect. A concentration of 10 µg/mL of EVALY emerged as the minimum effective dose capable of significantly reducing hypoxia‐induced cell death and restoring mitochondrial function. Lower doses produced inconsistent benefits, whereas higher doses (up to 200 µg/mL) did not yield additional improvements beyond those achieved with 10 µg/mL (Figure S6A).

Before examining how the ALY688‐shaped EVs elicit cardioprotective effects, we assessed the internalization efficacy of EVs in recipient cardiomyocytes. Based on our demonstration that HFD feeding did not exacerbate MI‐induced cardiac dysfunction and that ALY688 therapeutic effects were equivalent across dietary conditions (Figure 1), we focused our mechanistic studies on EVs derived from SC mice. This approach provides representative characterization of ALY688‐shaped EVs therapeutic potential, as both the pathological target (MI) and therapeutic response (ALY688 protection) appear independent of diet. H9c2 cells and induced pluripotent stem cell‐cardiomyocytes (iPSC‐CM) were incubated with DiR‐labeled plasma EVs isolated from SC mice (EVsham PBS, EVsham ALY, EVMI PBS, and EVMI ALY) as described in Figure 1, as well as the 3‐day PBS or ALY injected mice plasma derived EV (EVPBS and EVALY) as described in Figure 3 and cellular uptake was evaluated using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. Both confocal and flow cytometry data confirmed efficient internalization of all EVs types, and EVs from MI ALY treated mice showed higher uptake efficiency compared to MI PBS (Figure S6B,C). Next, we also evaluate the EVs uptake during hypoxia conditions. When H9c2 and iPSC‐CM treated with EVPBS and EVALY, we observed no significant differences in overall EV uptake efficiency between normoxic and hypoxic conditions for either EV preparation. Notably, EVALY consistently exhibited increased cellular uptake compared with EVPBS, independent of oxygen conditions (Figure S6D–G).

To better understand the functional effects of EVALY in ischemic injury, we employed a hypoxia system in H9c2 and induced pluripotent stem cell‐cardiomyocytes (iPSC‐CM) to simulate in vivo ischemia. We first measured apoptosis and cell death in the hypoxia model, as these represent the major event during ischemic remodeling [51]. Caspase 3/7 activity was used to evaluate apoptosis and propidium iodide (PI) staining and LDH release were used to measure cell death as described before [52]. In H9c2 cells, hypoxia induced apoptosis and cell death was significantly reduced by the EVALY treatment compared to control (Figure 4A and Figure S7A–C). Similar protective effects against cell death were observed in iPSC‐CM (Figure 4B). Mitochondrial dysfunction is the major cause of cellular ROS accumulation and subsequent cell death under ischemia conditions [53]. Next, we tested whether EVALY reduced mitochondrial damage and the consequent ROS production in cardiomyocytes. Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated by the index of networking, demonstrating that hypoxia impaired mitochondrial networking and the impairment was improve by EVALY in both H9c2 cells and iPSC‐CM (Figure 4C–E). Furthermore, IMARIS sphericity analysis, used as an index of mitochondrial fission, revealed increased mitochondrial fragmentation during hypoxia, which was reduced by EVALY in H9c2 cell (Figure 4F,G). ROS accumulation assessed by CellRox staining demonstrated that EVALY significantly decreased hypoxia‐induced ROS level in H9c2 cells and iPSC‐CM, whereas this protective effect was not observed with EVPBS treatment (Figure 4H–J).

Autophagy, a well‐characterized function of adiponectin, and an important regulatory mechanism for ischemic cardiac protection [35, 52, 54, 55]. We then investigated how the EVALY modulates autophagy under hypoxic conditions by performing quantitative analysis of autophagy flux using a genetically engineered H9c2 cell line expressing the LC3 HiBiT reporter [52]. Results 5: ALY688‑Driven Plasma EVs Reduced Acute Myocardial Injury and Early Post‑MI Responses in MI Mice

Circulating EVs have diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential in CVD [56]. Therefore, we sought to determine whether ALY688 shaped‐EVs mediate pro‐autophagy and mitochondrial protective actions in cardiomyocytes under physiological conditions of acute myocardial infarction. Mice were injected with injected EVALY or EVPBS, both previously characterized for standard EVs markers and confirmed to be enriched with adiponectin in the EVALY preparation (Figure 4). One hour following myocardial ischemia induced by LAD occlusion, EVALY or EVPBS were administered to mice via tail vein injection, and mice were sacrificed 2 days post‐MI (Figure 6A). Plasma Troponin I and LDH levels were measured to determine cell death. Following MI, both troponin I and LDH were significantly elevated, while only EVALY treatment suppressed the increase in these cardiac injury markers (Figure 6B,C). Apoptosis in infarcted hearts was then evaluated by caspase‐3 cleavage, demonstrating more abundant cleaved caspase‐3 in MI compared to sham conditions, which was reduced following EVALY treatment (Figure 6D,E). Additionally, TUNEL staining was performed in heart section combined with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) or immunofluorescent detection of the cardiomyocyte nuclear marker NKX2‐5 to identify apoptotic cardiomyocytes [57, 58, 59]. Quantification of TUNEL+/NKX2‐5+ nuclei in the peri‐infarct border zone and remote myocardium revealed significantly increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the MI and MI+EVPBS group compared with sham. Pearson correlation analysis further confirmed that most TUNEL‐positive nuclei localized to cardiomyocytes (Figure S8A,C). Collectively, these data evidenced that EVALY reduced apoptosis and cell death induced by acute MI. To further correspond with the previous recipient cell proteomics findings, autophagy flux was also measured in infarcted heart. EVALY treatment resulted in increased LC3‐II and ATG3 levels, along with enhanced p62 degradation (Figure 6L–O). As such, autophagic flux impaired by MI was relieved by EVALY.

In addition, histological analysis of H&E‐stained heart sections showed prominent inflammation in the infarct and border zones following MI. In contrast, EVALY‐treated hearts exhibited visibly reduced leukocytes infiltration and inflammatory foci compared with EVPBS‐treated MI controls (Figure S8E,F). Discussion

The present study shows that ALY688 significantly attenuates MI‐induced cardiac dysfunction, ventricular remodeling, and fibrosis in both SC and HFD‐fed mice, without worsening MI pathology by HFD. Circulating EVs are markedly downregulated in both lean and obese experimental MI models. Conversely, administration of ALY688 markedly increased EVs production, suggesting that EVs are an ALY688‐modulated protective factor in MI. Interestingly, EVs proteome was changes upon MI injury and ALY688 intervention was more significant differentially expressed protein number under HFD condition compared SC. These findings show that ALY688‐shaped EVs remain effective across different metabolic conditions, highlighting their potential as a candidate for further preclinical and translational investigation. Mechanistically, the bulk EVALY preparation is enriched in adiponectin and transports it to the injured heart; however, given the heterogeneous cellular origin of circulating plasma EVs, the contribution of adiponectin‐carrying vesicles relative to co‐isolated EVs from endothelial, immune, or other cell types to the observed cardioprotection remains to be established. In addition to increasing adiponectin signaling, ALY688‐shaped EVs also reduce ROS accumulation, cell death, and mitochondrial bioenergetic impairment in cardiomyocytes during hypoxic challenge. Importantly, cardiac cell death, disrupted autophagy, and impaired mitochondrial function in mice with acute MI were improved by intervention with ALY688‐shaped EVs. Consequently, our study broadens the understanding of EV‐dependent MI protection and the development of future experimental therapeutic strategies for acute MI, pending validation in additional preclinical models and longer‐term studies.

Given the established cardioprotective role of adiponectin and the emerging importance of EVs in cardiac repair, we investigated whether EVs serve as mediators of ALY688's therapeutic effects. Previous results have indicated that decreased circulating adiponectin levels are seen in several cardiovascular disorders, including atherosclerosis, diabetic cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, suggesting that restoring adiponectin levels is a potential strategy for protecting against cardiovascular disorders [60, 61, 62]. Various studies in mice have shown that MI injury is further exacerbated in adiponectin KO mice [32, 35]. ALY688, an adiponectin receptor agonist, has demonstrated adiponectin‐mimetic effects, including insulin sensitization, anti‐inflammatory activity, and autophagy activation, with protective effects shown in pressure overload mouse models [44, 63, 64, 65]. In this study, we showed that ALY688 significantly reduced cardiac dysfunction, cell death, and fibrosis post‐MI. Adiponectin receptors are predominantly expressed in adipose, liver and skeletal muscle [66, 67], and adiponectin levels were significantly elevated in the infarcted heart following ALY688 administration. While adipose tissue represents the primary production site for circulating adiponectin under normal physiological conditions, emerging evidence demonstrates that adiponectin can also be synthesized locally by other tissues, including cardiac myocytes themselves [68, 69, 70]. This local cardiac production of adiponectin has been observed in response to various stress conditions, including ischemia‐reperfusion injury [68, 69, 70]. However, the magnitude of adiponectin elevation observed in our study suggests that increased production from tissues with high adiponectin receptor density, particularly adipose tissue, likely represents the predominant source contributing to elevated cardiac adiponectin levels following ALY688 treatment. This interpretation aligns with the established principle that receptor distribution does not necessarily correspond to production sites. For instance, in the angiotensin II system, the peptide is synthesized in multiple tissues despite its receptors being predominantly expressed in target organs [71, 72]. Similarly, adipose tissue remains the principal quantitative source of systemic adiponectin under most physiological conditions [73, 74]. Furthermore, we hypothesize that ALY688 may enhance multiorgan crosstalk by stimulating adiponectin production in receptor‐rich tissues, which then contributes to the circulating pool that reaches the heart. To test whether this enhanced multiorgan communication involves extracellular vesicle‐mediated transport, we sought to determine whether EVs mediate the delivery of adiponectin from distant production sites to the cardiac tissue.

Emerging studies suggest dual pathophysiological roles for EVs in myocardial infarction, functioning as both pathogenic mediators and reparative messengers through complex intercellular communication networks [49]. Among all the sources, adipose‐derived EVs exhibit contrasting roles in myocardial infarction depending on their tissue source and metabolic state, functioning as either protective or detrimental mediators of cardiac outcomes. Therapeutic adipose‐derived stem cell EVs have been shown to reduce infarct size, improve left ventricular function, promote angiogenesis, and inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis by delivering beneficial microRNAs, such as miR‐205, miR‐31, and miR‐221/222 [75, 76, 77]. Brown adipose tissue‐derived EVs provide exercise‐mediated cardioprotection by delivering cardioprotective microRNAs (miR‐125b‐5p, miR‐128‐3p, miR‐30d‐5p) that suppress the pro‐apoptotic MAPK pathway, reduce ischemia‐reperfusion injury, and enhance cardiac survival mechanisms through targeted inhibition of apoptotic signaling cascades [17, 78]. Adiponectin has been shown to stimulate EVs production from healthy adipose‐derived stem cells and MSCs and amplify the cardioprotective effects of cell‐based therapies in heart failure models [37, 79, 80]. Mechanistically, adiponectin‐enhanced EVs carry protective cargo such as anti‐apoptotic factors, anti‐inflammatory mediators, and pro‐angiogenic molecules. They also lower harmful cellular ceramides by promoting their efflux in EVs. Together, these actions protect cells through AMPK‐dependent anti‐apoptotic and COX‐2‐dependent anti‐inflammatory pathways [37]. Here, we found that MI significantly impaired EVs production and that this was reversed upon ALY688 intervention. The magnitude of EV elevation observed systemically after ALY688 administration, combined with an adipocyte‐like proteome signature including high adiponectin content indicates that adipose tissue is the likely predominant source of the ALY688‐enhanced EVs [81, 82, 83]. The cardioprotective effects observed are mediated through delivery of these adipose‐derived EVs to cardiomyocytes expressing functional AdipoR1 and AdipoR2, though a contribution from co‐isolated EVs of other cellular origins cannot be excluded given the bulk, unfractionated nature of the plasma EV preparation. An important unresolved question is the precise cellular origin of the ALY688‐shaped protective EVs. Because we isolated EVs from whole plasma using size‐exclusion chromatography and did not perform EV source tracing, the isolated vesicles likely represent a mixed population derived from multiple tissues, including adipose tissue, endothelium, circulating immune cells, and cardiomyocytes. The adipocyte‐like proteomic signature and high adiponectin content of ALY688‐enhanced EVs are consistent with a predominant adipose origin; however, they do not exclude substantial contributions from other cell types. Thus, while our data support a model in which ALY688 augments adipose‐enriched, adiponectin‐enriched EVs that secondarily act on cardiomyocytes, the exact contribution of each tissue source remains to be defined. Future studies employing lineage‐tracing approaches and standardized EV subpopulation isolation will be required to mechanistically pinpoint the origin of the cardioprotective EVs and to improve translational reproducibility. It is therefore important to note that throughout this manuscript, the terms “ALY688‐shaped EVs” or “EVALY” refer operationally to the bulk plasma EV fraction isolated from ALY688‐treated mice, and not to a purified population of adipose‐derived or adiponectin‐carrying vesicles. While the adiponectin enrichment and adipocyte‐like proteomic signature of EVALY provide indirect evidence of a predominant adipose origin, the observed cardioprotective effects cannot be attributed exclusively to the adiponectin‐carrying subpopulation, as co‐isolated EVs from other cellular sources may contribute independently to the functional outcomes reported.

Activation of adiponectin receptor signaling triggers robust phosphorylation of AMPK and p38 MAPK, canonical downstream effectors known to orchestrate cardioprotective responses including autophagy restoration and mitochondrial dynamics recovery [84, 85, 86, 87]. Critically, the preservation of adiponectin receptor expression and function in cardiomyocytes during post‐MI recovery suggests sustained responsiveness to adipose‐derived signals, a feature that likely underpins the observed protective phenotype. These findings position adiponectin receptor signaling as essential for mediating the therapeutic benefits of ALY688 treatment in the context of myocardial injury. While the Introduction described that EV biogenesis is tightly regulated by ESCRT complexes, ceramide metabolism, and metabolic stress responses such as AMPK activation, our study did not employ pharmacological inhibitors or genetic manipulation of these canonical EV biogenesis pathways to definitively establish their involvement in ALY688‐mediated EV production. Therefore, the mechanistic link between ALY688‐induced AMPK/p38MAPK signaling and the observed changes in EV number, size, and cargo composition remains inferential. Future studies using ESCRT complex inhibitors, ceramide synthesis modulators, or AMPK‐specific inhibitors would be necessary to establish direct causality between ALY688 signaling and specific EV biogenesis machinery.

We next showed that ALY688 injection not only improved the production of circulating EVs in mice but importantly, the increased EVs also contained higher abundant active form of adiponectin. Beyond adiponectin, proteomic analysis of ALY688‐derived EVs in the MI context revealed enrichment in fructose metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, and metabolic pathways. The near‐complete absence of overlapping differentially expressed proteins between SC and HFD backgrounds (Figure 2J, L) indicates that HFD does not simply amplify the lean‐state EV proteomic response to ALY688, it fundamentally redirects it, engaging distinct molecular programs that reflect the altered cellular environment of obesity. This is consistent with prior work demonstrating that high‐fat feeding produces a distinct circulating EV proteomic landscape characterized by dyslipidemia‐driven remodeling and dysregulated intercellular communication [88, 89]. Under SC conditions, ALY688 treatment of MI mice enriched fructose metabolism pathways in plasma EVs (Figure S4I). This is biologically relevant in the post‐ischemic heart, where the cardiomyocyte can metabolize fructose as an alternative energy substrate bypassing the rate‐limiting glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase. EV‐mediated delivery of fructose‐metabolizing enzymes to recipient cardiomyocytes may therefore confer acute metabolic flexibility to sustain ATP production during ischemic energy crisis, consistent with the HIF‐1α‐fructokinase‐C axis as an adaptive anabolic response to cardiac stress [90, 91, 92].

Autophagy is an intracellular process responsible for the turnover of cellular constituents [93]. The critical role of autophagy in MI has been established, showing that activation of autophagy reduces infarct size [94, 95]. In this study, we demonstrated that acute MI impaired heart autophagic flux, while administration of ALY688‐EVs partially relieved this disruption. Similar findings were observed in hypoxic cardiomyocytes in vitro. ATG3 (Autophagy related 3) plays a central role in autophagosome formation [96]. Notably, recipient cell proteomic analysis (Figure 5), further revealed coordinated upregulation of ATG3 alongside mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I and V and fatty acid degradation enzymes. This molecular signature aligns with the observed restoration of mitochondrial respiration, reduction in ROS levels, and improvement in autophagic flux, supporting a functional link between EV‐mediated cargo delivery and cardiomyocyte stress adaptation. Oxidative stress is a central driver of myocardial ischemic injury, with excessive ROS originating from NADPH oxidases, uncoupled NOS, xanthine oxidase, and mitochondria, leading to cellular damage and microvascular dysfunction [97]. Among these, mitochondrial ROS are especially critical, causing ATP depletion, opening of the permeability transition pore, and mtDNA release that trigger sterile inflammation [97]. These interconnected ROS sources exacerbate cardiomyocyte injury, while mitochondrial dysfunction further accelerates cell death [98]. Here, we present evidence that induction of cardiomyocyte autophagy by ALY688‐shaped EVs in MI heart and hypoxia cardiomyocytes leads to a decrease in ROS levels, and inflammation. This, in turn, may result in a decrease in cell death, thus limiting the infarct size. Mitochondria are the primary source of ATP required to sustain cardiac function [99, 100] and their integrity is essential in the high‐energy‐demanding myocardium [101, 102, 103]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by impaired oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), is a hallmark of MI pathology [104]. Consistent with this, proteomic analysis showed that ALY688‐shaped EV treatment upregulated multiple ETC components under hypoxic conditions. Functional assays further confirmed restoration of mitochondrial respiratory capacity. Moreover, ALY688‐shaped EVs improved mitochondrial dynamics, as evidenced by enhanced fusion and reduced fission. Specifically, EV treatment increased fusion markers (Opa1, Mfn1, Mfn2) and decreased the fission marker p‐DRP1 (S616), thereby restoring the balance of mitochondrial networking critical for cardiomyocyte survival [105, 106, 107]. Importantly, the convergence of proteomic signatures and independent functional assays supports the concept that EV cargo is biologically active and capable of reprogramming cardiomyocyte metabolism and survival pathways. However, these findings remain associative, and cargo‐specific loss‐of‐function studies will be necessary to establish the causal roles of individual EV‐delivered proteins in mediating the observed cardioprotective effects. Importantly, aside from adiponectin and selected autophagy‐related proteins validated by Western blotting, the proteins identified by mass spectrometry have not been independently confirmed. Therefore, the proteomic dataset should be considered a hypothesis‐generating resource that guides future targeted investigations, rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

The 48 h EV‑intervention experiment was specifically designed to assess acute myocardial injury and early post‑MI responses, including apoptosis, mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy, and inflammation, which are anticipated to shape subsequent ventricular remodeling. However, this study does not directly evaluate scar formation or chronic fibrosis at this early time point, consistent with recent viewpoints on the temporal separation between acute injury and structural remodeling [108].

Limitations of the study: Although our data provide evidence of early cardioprotection and favorable remodeling based on strain and regional volumetric indices, a full diastolic characterization using Doppler‑derived parameters and regional wall‑thickness measurements remains an important objective for future studies. While in vitro models offer mechanistic resolution, they have inherent limitations. H9c2 cells are skeletal myoblast‐like, and iPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes remain metabolically immature compared with adult cardiomyocytes. While in vitro models offer mechanistic resolution, they have inherent limitations. H9c2 cells are skeletal myoblast‐like, and iPSC‐derived cardiomyocytes remain metabolically immature compared with adult cardiomyocytes. In this study, plasma EVs were isolated as a bulk population using size‐exclusion chromatography without cell‐type‐specific source tracing. As a result, the precise cellular origin of the cardioprotective ALY688‐modulated EVs remains unresolved, and the EV preparations likely contain heterogeneous vesicles from adipose tissue, blood cells, endothelium, and potentially cardiac cells. This limits mechanistic precision regarding the relative contribution of each tissue source and introduces variability that may affect translational reproducibility. Future work using standardized approaches to EV subpopulation characterization (including cell‐type‐specific labeling, immunocapture, and single‐vesicle profiling) will be necessary to rigorously define the source of protective EVs and to refine EV‐based therapeutic strategies.

The proteomic datasets in Figures 2 and 5 and Figure S4 are primarily exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Individual differentially expressed proteins identified by mass spectrometry were not independently validated by orthogonal methods, apart from adiponectin and the autophagy markers. Pathway enrichment conclusions, particularly regarding fructose metabolism, cholesterol handling, and protease inhibitor networks should therefore be interpreted as associative and directional rather than definitive, pending targeted protein‐level validation in future studies.

In conclusion, this study establishes ALY688 as a candidate therapeutic agent warranting further investigation in long‐term and translationally oriented preclinical models for MI through its ability to modulate EV production and cargo composition. The demonstrated cardioprotective effects of ALY688 across both lean and obese metabolic states highlight the robustness and translational potential of this therapeutic approach for diverse patient populations. The mechanistic insights revealing that ALY688‐shaped EVs deliver active adiponectin while simultaneously addressing multiple pathological processes, including oxidative stress reduction, autophagy restoration, and mitochondrial bioenergetic recovery, together underscoring the multifaceted protective effects of this intervention. We acknowledge that our immediate post‐MI treatment represents an idealized intervention window. Clinical translation will require testing efficacy at delayed, clinically relevant time points and evaluating potential prophylactic benefits in high‐risk populations. The 4‐week observation period also limits conclusions about durability, and longer‐term studies are needed to determine whether the acute benefits of EVALY translate into sustained functional improvement, reduced adverse remodeling, or improved survival. Key challenges for clinical translation include producing clinical‐grade EVs with consistent quality, the lack of standardized potency assays and regulatory‐grade controls, and the early post‐MI administration used in our model, which does not reflect typical STEMI presentation. Future studies should assess whether therapeutic benefit is maintained with delayed EV administration and longer follow‐up.

Experimental Section

Myocardial Infarction Model

Myocardial infarction was induced using a surgical model of heart failure involving the permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) as described before [48]. Standard chow (Research diet, D12450J) or high fat diet (Research diet, D12492) were fed to mice for 3 weeks prior to surgery intervention. Mice were treated pre‐operatively with 1.2 mg/kg buprenorphine (1.2 mg/kg; subcutaneous) and meloxicam (5 mg/kg) and treated with meloxicam (5 mg/kg) 24 and 48 h post‐surgery. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2%) and intubated by intra‐tracheal insertion of a 20G flexible catheter. The mice were maintained on a ventilator set to 130 breaths per minute throughout the procedure. The mouse was positioned supine on a heating pad on the surgical platform. The chest cavity was opened and maintained open with a chest retractor to expose the heart. The LAD was visualized underneath a surgical microscope and ligated 1–2 mm below the tip of the left atrium using a 7‐0 silk. Infarction was confirmed by the loss of color on the anterior wall of the left ventricle. The chest retractor was moved, and the muscles and skin were closed with 6‐0 absorbable nylon sutures. Sham mice underwent the same procedure without ligation of the LAD. Mice were moved to a 30°C recovery chamber and returned to housing rooms after recovery. After surgery, mice were administered daily injection of 15 mg/kg ALY688 (Alllysta Pharmaceutical) or equivalent sterile PBS for 4 weeks.

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic images were acquired using Vevo 3100 high‐frequency small animal ultrasound system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada), using the MX550D (Vevo 3100) linear array transducers. Images of the LV were taken at baseline, before thoracotomy and left coronary artery ligation, and at 2‐week post‐surgery using PLAX, SAX in 2D B‐mode. At 4‐week post‐surgery, images of the LV were acquired by using both PLAX, SAX in both 2D and 4D B‐mode. To acquire 4D mode images, a step motor was used to collect serial, ECG‐ and respiratory‐gated images along the SAX, with a step size of 80–130 µm and a frame rate of 300–400 Hz. During ECHO acquisition, mice were anesthetized with 2%–3% isoflurane and 1.5 L/min of oxygen and maintained on a heated (37°C) imaging platform as described before [48, 109]. Images were coded and analyzed in random order to maintain blinding throughout the analysis period.

Strain Analysis

Echocardiographic images in long‐axis view were analyzed for longitudinal and radial strain and strain rate using Vevo Strain software (Visualsonics). Three consecutive cardiac cycles free of respiratory artifacts were analyzed. Epi‐ and endocardium of the heart were manually traced at the time of end‐diastole in the first cycle and adjusted to ensure accurate tracking of the epi‐ and endocardial borders throughout the three cycles. The software determined an average time course of strain and strain rate at 49 evenly spaced points along the perimeter of the epi‐ and endocardium. Since it is difficult for technical reasons to detect strains accurately in the apical region, the apex was excluded from the analysis, leaving 34 points along the anterior and posterior myocardium, as previously described [110]. The average strain and strain rate time course was calculated from these points in each of the directions (longitudinal and radial) and the appropriate maxima/minima were chosen to represent systolic and diastolic strain and strain rate, respectively.

Histology Analysis

Paraffin embedded heart sections were subjected to H&E (Sigma, HT1079, HT110216), Masson Trichrome (Sigma, HT15) according to manufacturer's protocol and visualized using a bright field slid scanning microscopy (Leica, Aperio AT2) as reported before [35].

Immunofluorescence and TUNEL Staining

Paraffin embedded heart tissue sections were de‐paraffinized with serial rehydration, and antigen retrieval was performed in Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Heart sections were permeabilized and blocked with blocking buffer (3% BSA, 5% goat serum, 0.3% Triton X‐100) for 1 h and stained with primary antibody (NKX25, Abcam, ab97355) and incubated overnight at 4°. The slides were kept in secondary antibody (Alexa 594 goat anti‐rabbit, Thermo, A66787) at 1:1000 for 1 h at room temperature or WGA Alexa fluor 594 staining for 10 min at room temperature (Thermo, W11262) and followed with TUNEL staining (Promega, G3250) according to manufacturer's protocol. Sections cover with mounting medium contained DAPI (Vector Laboratories, H‐1200‐10). slides were visualized under bright field slid scanning microscopy (Leica, Aperio AT2).

Biochemistry Analysis

Commercially available kits were used to measure LDH (Abcam, ab102526 for animal samples), LDH (Biosciences, 786210 for cellular samples), Troponin I (novus, NBP3‐00456), ATP assay (Abcam, ab83355), and mouse Adiponectin ELISA (Antibody and Immunoassay Service, HongKong University, 32010) according to manufactural instructions.

Size‐Exclusion Chromatography Isolation of Plasma EVs

Size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) using the qEVsingle columns 70 nm (IZON, Cambridge, MA) was the primary method to isolate EVs from serum samples. In all experiments, serum was diluted in filtered PBS at a 1:3 ratio. Fractions 3–6 were collected and used for subsequent EVs characterization and downstream applications as per manufacturer's recommendations.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

To determine EVs sizes, 300 µL of EVs were analyzed by the NanoSight LM10 system (NanoSight Ltd, Salisbury, UK). 60‐second videos (number of experiments n = 3, number of videos n = 8) of EVs were collected, averaged, and analyzed using LM10 NTA equipped with a 65 mWatt 405 nm violet laser (NanoSight Ltd, Salisbury, UK). 100 nm polystyrene beads (Malvern Instruments, Saint‐Laurent, Canada) were used for size calibration, and data captured with sCMOS camera on NTA 3.1 (machine) with Build 3.1.46 (software) at the temperature of 22°C.

Cryogenic Electron Microscopy (Cryo‐EM)

Samples were vitrified in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Scientific). Liquid samples were pipetted onto Quantifoil R2/2 copper grids (Ted Pella Inc.) that had been glow discharged in air (Pelco easiGlow, Ted Pella, Inc.). Grids were transferred into a single tilt cryo TEM holder (Gatan). Grids were imaged with a Talos L120C TEM (Thermo Scientific) using a high tension of 120 kV with a 4k x 4k BM‐Ceta CMOS camera. At least 30 images of each sample were taken at magnifications 28 000x, 57 000x, and 120 000x yielding a pixel size of 510, 249, and 121 pm, respectively.

EV Storage and Handling

Following isolation by size‐exclusion chromatography, plasma EVs were immediately aliquoted into single‐use volumes (50–100 µL per aliquot) and stored at ‐80°C for ≤4 weeks in sterile Ca2 +/Mg2 +‐free PBS (pH 7.4, Gibco #10010023). Each EV aliquot underwent no more than one freeze‐thaw cycle to preserve structural integrity and biological activity. Prior to use, EVs were thawed at room temperature (22–25°C) for approximately 5–10 min and used within 30 min to minimize degradation. EVs were not subjected to filtration post‐isolation to preserve vesicle integrity and avoid loss of larger EV subpopulations. For in vivo administration, isolated EVs were resuspended in sterile Ca2 +/Mg2 +‐free PBS (pH 7.4) as the final injection vehicle immediately prior to tail vein injection. For in vitro experiments, EVs were resuspended in 0.5% EV‐depleted FBS‐supplemented Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) (for H9c2 cells) or RPMI medium (for iPSC‐CMs).

EVs Intervened Myocardial Infarction Model

Myocardial infarction was induced as described previously, followed by tail vein injection of 10 µg plasma EVs resuspended in 100 µL sterile PBS and isolated from mice treated with or without 15 mg/kg ALY688 for 3 days, or injection of 100 µL sterile PBS alone. EVs were administered at a dose of 10 µg total protein per injection measured by BCA assay, corresponding to ∼1 × 109 particles as determined by NTA, equivalent to ∼0.33–0.40 mg/kg or ∼3.3–4.0 × 1010 particles/kg body weight (based on 25–30 g mice). Isolated EVs were resuspended in sterile Ca2 +/Mg2 +‐free PBS (pH 7.4) immediately prior to administration to prevent divalent cation‐induced aggregation.

EVs Administration in Cellular Model

We administered EVs at 10 µg/mL immediately before hypoxia exposure. To prepare the treatment medium, EVs were resuspended in 0.5% EV free FBS DMEM medium for H9c2 or in 0.5% EV free FBS RPMI medium for iPSC‐CM to reduce background EV contamination from serum. Cells (H9c2 or iPSC‐CMs) were then incubated with this EV‐containing medium for 24 h under normoxic conditions (20% O2, 5% CO2, 37°C) or hypoxia (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 24 h.

Proteomics Analysis

Isolated EVs were lysed in 5% SDS (Fisher Chemical), 200 mM TRIS (Fisher BioReagnets) pH 8.5 with 10 mM TCEP (60C x 25 min). Free disulfides were alkylated with 40 mM IAA (RT, dark, 30 min) prior to proteolytic digestion by S‐TRAP micro (see attached for the SOP from the vendor that we use). Peptide containing eluates were lyophilized and rehydrated at 10 ng/µL prior to loading on EvoTips and analysis by library free DIA using an Evosep One system coupled to a Bruker timsTOF HT MS operated in DIA‐PASEF mode. Samples were separated on an EV1137 column at 30SPD. For recipient cell proteomics, H9c2 cells were seeded on 6‐well plate until reaching 80%–90% confluency. Confluent cells were treated with EVs isolated from mice plasma, at the concentration of 10 µg/mL, then subjected to 24 h hypoxia or normoxia incubation. After that, cells were trypsinized and centrifuged for 5 min at 2000 rpm. Cell pellets were collected and lysed in NP40 lysis buffer for 30 min on ice. Cell lysate was then subjected to in‐solution digestion following protocol by Promega (V5280). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed following the guidelines from the protocol using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2).39 KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the ClueGO plugin (version 2.5.10) in Cytoscape software. ClueGO was used to identify significantly enriched KEGG pathways, applying two‐sided hypergeometric test for pathway analysis. The p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using either the Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg correction, with an adjusted p‐value threshold of 0.05.

H9c2 Cell Culture

H9c2 rat embryonic cardiac myoblasts (ATCC CRL‐1446TM) were grown in DMEM (Gibco, 11885‐084) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo, 11885084) and 1% (vol/vol) streptomycin/penicillin (Gibco, 15070063) at 37°C and 5% CO2 as described previously [52].

Hypoxia and Normoxia Incubation

Hypoxia was achieved by placing cells in a hypoxic chamber (Onstage Incubator, ThermoFisher, NX7LIVE001) filled with a pre‐analyzed gas mixture of 1% O2 5% CO2. Normoxia were achieved by placing cells in the Onstage Incubator filled with 20% O2 and 5% CO2 as reported before [35, 52].

Genetic Modification of H9c2 Cell

CRISPR knock‐out lines were generated using guide RNAs cloned into the pX459 plasmid, as previously described [111]. Autophagy LC3 HiBiT reporter vector (Promega, GA2550) has a HiBiT tag and contains a sequence encoding the MAP1LC3B gene. H9c2 cells were transfected with the autophagy LC3 HiBiT reporter vector according to the manufacturer manual [112]. Nano‐Glo HiBiT lytic reagent (Promega, N3040) was added to the cells after each treatment and luminescence was measured on a Thermo Varioskan LUX microplate reader. To generate H9c2 lines stably expressing Venus‐based Caspase‐3 like protease activity indicator (VC3AI; a gift from Dr. Binghui Li, Addgene plasmid 78907), lentiviral particles were produced using pCDH‐puro‐CMV‐VC3AI and transduced cells were selected using 1 µg/mL puromycin. This genetically encoded biosensor consists of cyclized chimeras containing a caspase‐3 cleavage site as a switch. Upon cleavage by caspase‐3‐like proteases, the non‐fluorescent indicator rapidly becomes fluorescent and thus detects activation in real‐time [113].

iPSC culture and Cardiomyocyte Differentiation

iPSCs, PGPC14 line validated previously [114], were cultured on Matrigel (Corning, 354277) coated plates in mTeSR plus media (Stemcell Technologies, 100–0276) and passaged with ReLeSR (Stemcell Technologies, 100–0483) using a 1:6–1:12 dilution every 3–4 days, or upon reaching 80%–90% confluence. For cardiomyocyte differentiation, iPSCs were dissociated into single cells using gentle cell dissociation reagent (Stemcell Technologies, 100–0485), and seeded on Matrigel coated plates containing mTeSR plus media supplemented with 10 µM of Y‐27623 (Stemcell Technologies, 72304) for 24 h. Media was changed daily with mTeSR plus media without Y‐27623 until cells reached > 95% confluence (3–4 days post‐seeding), at which point differentiation was initiated using STEMdiff Ventricular Cardiomyocyte Differentiation Kit (Stemcell Technologies, 05010), following manufacturer protocol. Additionally, selection was performed on cardiomyocytes from day 12 to day 18 of differentiation with glucose‐free RPMI supplemented with 4 mM lactate and B27 containing insulin. Cells were allowed to recover for a minimum of 2 days, then dissociated using TrypLE Select (ThermoFisher, A1217701) for 30–45 min, pelleted by centrifugation, then seeded in Matrigel coated plates in STEMdiff Cardiomyocyte Support Medium (Stemcell Technologies, 05027) for 24 h. Cardiomyocytes were seeded at a density of 250 000–300 000 cells/cm2 for western blot, and 50 000–60 000 cells/cm2 for all other assays. Media change was performed every 2 days thereafter using RPMI media (Sigma, R8758) supplemented with B27 containing insulin (ThermoFisher, 17504044) for a minimum of 4 days. All cell treatments were carried out using RPMI media supplemented with B27 containing insulin.

RAW‐Dual Cells Culture and Assay

RAW‐Dual cells (InvivoGen, rawd‐ismip) [115] were harvested and seeded in 96 well plate 24 h before EVs treatment and hypoxia incubation. 40 µL of supernatants from treated reporter cells were mixed with 160 µL Quanti‐Blue reagent (InvivoGen, rep‐qbs) and incubated at 37°C for 6 h. The absorbance was measured at λ = 620 nm after 10 s shaking by a microplate reader. 20 µL of supernatants from treated reporter cells were mixed with 50 µL Quanti‐Luc reagent (InvivoGen, rep‐qlc4lg1). The luminescence was measured with a 4 s start time and 0.1 s reading time by a microplate reader. The readouts were blanked with negative control (i.e., sterile dH2O) and also subtracted.

Western Blot

Heart tissues were homogenized, H9c2 cells and EVs were lysed and suspended in RIPA lysis buffer. BCA (ThermoFisher, A55864) was performed to normalize protein concentration. Proteins were separated by reducing SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membrane. The following antibodies were used for western blot: GAPDH (1:2000, ThermoFisher, MA5‐15738), β‐Tubulin (1:2000, Cell Signaling, 2128, Rab8a (1:1000, BD Science, 610844), CD63 (1:1000, ThermoFisher, PA5‐92370), Alix (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 92880), Flotillin 1 (1:1000, Abcam, 133497), Calnexin (1:1000, Abcam, 22595), Apoa1 (1:1000, ThermoFisher, PA5‐29557), caspase 3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 9662), MFF (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 84580), p‐Drp1 s637 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 4867), p‐Drp1 s616 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 3455), OPA1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 80471), LC3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 2775), p62 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 5114), ATG3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling, 3415), anti‐rabbit (1:5000, Cell Signaling, 7074), and anti‐mouse (1:5000, Cell Signaling, 7076). The quantification of signals was performed by densitometry of scanned autoradiographs with the aid of ImageJ (version 1.4v).

Microscopic Imaging

H9c2 cells were seeded on glass coverslips, which were isolated as described previously [52]. Staining using different dyes including, CellEvent Caspase 3/7 Detection Reagent (ThermoFisher, C10423), ReadyProbe (ThermoFisher, R37609), CellRox DeepRed (ThermoFisher, C10422), MitoTracker Green (ThermoFisher, M7514), MagicRed (Immuno Chemistry, 938), MitoSox Red (ThermoFisher, M36008), JC1 (ThermoFisher, T3168) was performed according to manufacturer's protocols and DAPI (HCS NuclearMask Blue Stain, ThermoFisher, H10325) was added simultaneously. Cells were visualized under Nikon Eclipse Ti2 and for continuous measurements Cellnsight CX7 Platform (ThermoFisher, CX7A1110) was used. Co‐localization analysis was performed with ImageJ JACOP plug‐in.

EVs Uptake Assay

To confirm EVs uptake, EVs were stained with 2.5 µg/mL lipophilic near‐infrared indotricarbocyanine iodide dye DiR (Thermo, D12731) at 37°C for 1 h [116]. To remove excess staining, filtration using amicon ultra filters were applied as previously described [117]. In brief, amicon ultra filters (100 kDa MWCO, Millipore) were pre‐conditioned with 0.1% BSA in filtered PBS and washed twice with filtered PBS to maximize particle recovery; labeled EV samples were then loaded into the amicon ultra filter for subsequent centrifugation at 2000 x g for 5 min for labeled EV‐enriched retentate. Recipient cells treated with DiR‐labeled EVs were countered with PlasMem Bright Green (Dojindo, P504). Visualization was performed using a Nikon A1 confocal microscope with an incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). The uptake efficiency of DiR‐labeled EVs at varied timepoints was collected on all samples using a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer and analyzed in FlowJo.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Thermo, 12183018A) and cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo, K1691) to extract total RNA and synthesize first strand cDNA According to the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed in SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad, 1725274) method. Seahorse Flux Assay

Oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) were determined by a Seahorse XFe24 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent) as previously described [70]. Briefly, H9c2 cells were seeded in each well of a pre‐coated XFe24 cell culture plate (Agilent, 103015) and treated with 10 µg/mL EVPBS or EVALY prior hypoxia. After hypoxia, cells were washed with XF media twice and incubated in a CO2‐free incubator at 37°C for 1 h. The following reagents were used: Oligomycin (1 µM, Sigma, 75351), FCCP (carbonyl cyanide p‐trifluoromethoxy phenylhydrazone, 3 µM, Sigma, C2920), and Antimycin A (1 µM, Sigma, A8674) plus Rotenone (1 µM, Sigma, R8875).

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Student's t‐test was used for comparison of two groups and one‐way ANOVA, or two‐way ANOVA were used for comparison of more than two groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
