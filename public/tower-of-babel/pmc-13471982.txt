Expansion of Access to Rheumatology Care for Rural Veterans: Program Development and Initial Feasibility of the National Tele‐Rheumatology Program Using the RE‐AIM Framework
Authors: Gabriela Schmajuk, Cherish R. Wilson, Catherine Nasrallah, Alicia Hamblin, Gary Tarasovsky, Jing Li, Broderick Flynn, Asia Williams, Elizabeth Chang, John S. Richards, Bernard Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo describe and assess the initial feasibility of the National Tele‐Rheumatology Program (NTRP), a centrally managed hub‐and‐spoke telehealth model designed to expand access to rheumatology care for Veterans served by facilities with high proportions of rural Veteran populations.MethodsUsing the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance (RE‐AIM) framework, we analyzed Veterans Affairs (VA) program records, administrative and electronic health record data, Veteran questionnaires, and staff interviews from July 2024 through June 2025. Feasibility indicators included enrollment of spoke sites, delivery of care to Veterans in rural or highly rural areas, growth in encounter volume, changes in non‐VA care utilization, Veteran satisfaction, and identification of implementation barriers and adaptations.FindingsEleven spoke sites enrolled during NTRP's first year. NTRP reached 1227 Veterans (82% male, 81% White; mean age 62.8 years), including 47% from rural or highly rural areas, and delivered 2577 encounters, 45.3% to rural or highly rural Veterans. Among 149 Veterans with a non‐VA rheumatology care claim in the prior year, 89.3% had no further claims after their first NTRP visit. Veteran satisfaction was moderate to high and did not differ by rurality. Staff interviews identified technology access, reluctance to virtual care, and workflow variation as recurring challenges and described adaptations, including technology support and alternative clinic‐based video access.ConclusionsEarly feasibility indicators are promising. The NTRP reached its intended population, was acceptable to Veterans across rural and urban settings, and produced a preliminary signal of reduced non‐VA care utilization. Continued evaluation should assess sustainment, costs, and clinical outcomes, with particular attention to rural‐specific outcomes as the program grows.

Introduction

Access to rheumatology care in the United States, and particularly within the Veterans Affairs (VA) healthcare system, is increasingly strained by a national shortage of rheumatologists, a challenge that is especially acute in rural areas [1, 2, 3, 4]. Projections estimate that the rheumatology workforce may decrease by 25% by 2030, whereas the need for care is expected to rise by nearly 50% by 2040, potentially leading to a serious shortage if unaddressed [5]. On the other hand, Veterans face elevated rates of autoimmune and inflammatory conditions linked to military service, including inhalational exposures from burn pits and other airborne hazards associated with increased risk of systemic inflammatory disease [6]. Rheumatic diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and vasculitis, are complex, progressive conditions requiring timely diagnosis and ongoing specialist management—delays in initiating disease‐modifying therapy are associated with worse long‐term functional outcomes and higher rates of work disability. Access to rheumatology care is therefore particularly critical for this population, yet rural Veterans face significant barriers to timely specialist care.

Given the national shortage of rheumatologists, VA facilities in rural regions struggle to recruit and retain rheumatologists, often leaving Veterans without timely access to specialized care [7]. In response, the VA has established a system for “community care” wherein the VA pays for eligible Veterans to receive care from non‐VA community providers when certain access or eligibility criteria are met. However, this approach is problematic in rheumatology, as community rheumatologists face the same national workforce shortage as VA, meaning that wait times in the community are often no shorter than those within the VA [8]. When Veterans are seen by external providers, those providers may not have access to VA records at the time of the visit, creating gaps in medication monitoring and complicating management of complex conditions. This is especially concerning for rheumatology, where many treatments involve immunosuppressive medications that require regular laboratory monitoring for safety. When community providers prescribe these medications to be filled through VA pharmacies, standard VA safety checks may be bypassed, placing Veterans at increased risk of serious adverse events. In a recent meta‐analysis, clinical quality and safety outcomes of internal VA care were better than or equal to non‐VA care in most studies [9]. Implementation of a program that keeps rheumatology care internal to the VA thus has the potential to reduce care fragmentation, improve quality of care, while being more patient‐centered.

The VA Office of Rural Health (ORH) has been instrumental in expanding access to care for Veterans in geographically isolated areas. Since 2012, ORH has invested more than $3.1 billion to strengthen rural health infrastructure, inform delivery policy, and support Veteran well‐being [10]. A key strategy in these efforts has been the implementation of enterprise‐wide initiatives (EWIs), which fund and scale innovative care models to reduce barriers to care across the VA system. Among these, ORH‐funded EWIs are the National TeleNeurology Program [11], the National Cardiology Hub and Spoke Network [12], and the TeleSleep EWI [13, 14], For rheumatology, comparable telemedicine initiatives implemented in non‐VA settings at smaller scales have also shown promise [15, 16].

Building on the infrastructure and lessons learned from these earlier initiatives, the National Tele‐Rheumatology Program (NTRP) was launched in 2024 as an ORH‐funded EWI to address persistent gaps in rheumatology care for rural Veterans. By leveraging VA's telehealth infrastructure, NTRP aims to expand access to high‐quality, coordinated rheumatology services through a national, scalable care delivery model. Although prior VA telehealth initiatives have demonstrated improved access to subspecialty care, evidence on the national implementation of tele‐rheumatology programs remains limited. Guided by the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance (RE‐AIM) framework, we used a combination of quantitative and qualitative approaches to assess early feasibility and acceptability of the NTRP. This report is intended not as a formal outcome evaluation, but as an early account of how a centralized tele‐specialty model was built and began to function in practice.

Methods

Program Design and Implementation

NTRP is built around a hub‐and‐spoke model designed to extend rheumatology care to underserved areas. Clinical teams are based at the Lexington VA Medical Center (hub site), where they were hired and onboarded as remote tele‐care staff. This centralized staffing model allows a small number of clinicians to support Veterans across wide geographic areas. To enhance geographic coverage and accommodate national demand, each clinical team is situated in a different time zone. The value proposition for spoke site participation is to improve access to care and, additionally, to reduce expenses related to non‐VA care (community care).

NTRP care is provided through a team‐based model. Clinical teams consist of one rheumatologist; one telehealth coordinator (telehealth clinical technician); and shared access to a nurse and clinical pharmacist who support all teams. Telehealth coordinators support Veteran outreach and appointment scheduling, and “check‐in” tasks prior to the start of a rheumatologist visit. The nurses support Veteran education and care coordination, whereas the pharmacist offers medication management and education services. The main care modalities include clinic‐based video encounters (where Veterans travel to a convenient clinic and connect to the NTRP clinician via video) or home‐based video encounters (where Veterans connect to the NTRP clinician via video on their own device); other modalities of care include phone encounters or asynchronous e‐consults. For both types of video encounters, physical assessments are conducted virtually, where clinicians observe the Veteran's condition and assess joints for visible swelling, redness, or deformity via video. If a more detailed examination, imaging, or laboratory testing is needed, Veterans are referred to partnered VA facilities (spoke sites) for in‐person evaluation.

Veteran Recruitment and Enrollment for the NTRP

Primary care providers or other clinicians at spoke sites submit rheumatology consults through their standard interfacility consult process, which are reviewed by a spoke site referral coordination initiative (RCI) team. When a consult meets NTRP criteria (e.g., suspected inflammatory arthritis, vasculitis, or other immune‐mediated inflammatory disease such as systemic lupus erythematosus or myositis), it is routed to NTRP. Veterans are then contacted by a telehealth coordinator, who explains the program and schedules an initial visit. Veterans may still request to see a specialist through a VA interfacility consult or through Community Care if they do not prefer telehealth.

Feasibility Assessment Framework

The primary objective of this report is to describe the development of the NTRP and assess early feasibility indicators using the RE‐AIM framework [17]. Rather than serving as a formal outcome evaluation, RE‐AIM was used here as an organizing structure to systematically capture early program data across five domains: (R) reach to Veterans in rural or highly‐rural facilities, (E) preliminary effectiveness reflected by changes in non‐VA rheumatology care use and Veteran satisfaction, (A) adoption by participating sites as reflected by routing of consults to NTRP, (I) implementation fidelity and growth in encounter volume, and (M) identification of barriers and adaptations relevant to maintenance of the program.

This approach allowed for a structured account of how the program functioned in its first year across multiple sites and populations, generating implementation‐relevant findings that can inform continued program refinement and potential applicability to other tele‐specialty models. Below we describe the study populations, data sources, and the quantitative and qualitative analyses completed to address each RE‐AIM domain.

Study Populations

This evaluation included two study populations: (1) Veterans receiving care through NTRP and (2) NTRP program staff. The Veteran population consisted of individuals who had at least one encounter with the NTRP program between July 2024 and June 2025. The staff population included all NTRP clinical staff, including three rheumatologists, three telehealth coordinators, one nurse, and one clinical pharmacist, as well as operational staff, including one program manager and one program administrator.

Data Sources and Data Collection

Data sources included electronic health record (EHR) data, program records, two Veteran surveys, and staff individual and group interviews. A multi‐method approach was used because the RE‐AIM framework requires data across multiple domains and levels of analysis that cannot be adequately captured by any single source. Quantitative data from the EHR and program records addressed reach, adoption, and implementation volume, whereas Veteran surveys and staff interviews provided context for interpreting those findings and captured implementation barriers, adaptations, and stakeholder experiences that are not visible in administrative data alone.

EHR and Program Records

We used EHR data from the Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW, the VA's national repository of administrative, and EHR data) for information on Veteran demographics (age, gender, and race and ethnicity), NTRP encounter types and diagnoses (identified by ICD‐10 codes, available upon request), and patient‐level claims for non‐VA rheumatology care. Each Veteran's residential address was classified as urban or rural on the basis of RUCA codes, US Census tract‐based classifications that utilize the standard Bureau of Census Urbanized Area and Urban Cluster definitions (classified as urban (RUCA 1.0, 1.1), rural (RUCA 2–9, 10.1–10.3), or highly rural (RUCA 10.0)) [18, 19].

Staffing data were obtained from program records.

Staff Individual and Group Interviews

All program staff were invited via email to participate in virtual interviews to explore their experiences with the program, identify perceived barriers and opportunities, and discuss issues related to program maintenance and potential improvements over time. A team member trained in qualitative research methods (CN) conducted two group interviews, one with program physicians (n = 3) and the other with telehealth coordinators (n = 3), along with four individual interviews with the program manager, program administrator, pharmacist, and nurse. All interviews were conducted virtually via Microsoft TEAMs between February and April 2025 and lasted between 30 and 90 min. Notes were recorded during interviews by team member (CW). Both Individual and group interviews were guided by semi‐structured interview guides based on the RE‐AIM framework. The guides were pilot‐tested and refined by the evaluation team. Discussions were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim, and de‐identified transcripts were later checked against the recordings to ensure accuracy.

Reach

In order to understand which Veterans were reached by the program, (R1) demographic characteristics of Veterans with at least one NTRP encounter were summarized using descriptive statistics. ICD codes designated at NTRP encounters were grouped into diagnostic categories and the primary rheumatic diagnosis was assigned using an adaptation of a previously published hierarchy [22]. (R2) Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between Veterans residing in urban and rural or highly rural areas using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables.

(R3) Next, we calculated the number of Veterans with two or more NTRP encounters. We examined whether (R3a) Primary rheumatic diagnosis and (R3b) rurality (rural or highly rural vs. urban) was associated with two or more NTRP encounters using chi‐square tests. (R3c) To assess factors associated with repeat NTRP encounters, we fit a mixed‐effects logistic regression model with a random intercept for facility to account for within‐facility correlation. The binary outcome was having more than one NTRP encounter. Primary diagnoses were collapsed into three groups: inflammatory conditions, degenerative and other conditions, and none of these. Covariates included age (per 10 years), gender, rurality (urban vs. rural or highly rural), and primary diagnosis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF; all values <10) and none was detected. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals.

Effectiveness

Our long‐term goal is to assess clinical outcomes for Veterans in the program. However, given that this was a preliminary analysis of the initial implementation of this program, and longitudinal data were not available, we examined effectiveness in two ways:

(E1) Non‐VA Rheumatology Care Utilization

We examined how use of rheumatology community care changed for Veterans before and after participation in the NTRP. We reviewed all Standardized Episode of Care (SEOC) claims (professional, institutional, and other) from participating Veterans starting in FY23Q1 through FY25Q3. Claims for inpatient stays were excluded.

We identified Veterans in our study population with at least one non‐VA claim for rheumatology care in the 12 months before their first NTRP encounter. We excluded Veterans whose first NTRP encounter was after 3/1/2025 to ensure at least 3 months of follow‐up time. We reported the percentage of Veterans with a rheumatology community care claim subsequent to their initial NTRP encounter and repeated the mixed‐effects logistic regression model described above to assess factors associated with having a subsequent rheumatology community care claim after the initial NTRP encounter, using the same covariates and a random intercept for facility. Our hypothesis was that Veterans joining NTRP would reduce subsequent encounters with community care.

(E2) Veteran Satisfaction Surveys

We examined TSQ survey results and calculated descriptive statistics overall, by domain, and for two individual questions. On the basis of prior studies, an overall TSQ score >56 was considered a good experience for the patient [23]. Again, we conducted a mixed‐effects linear regression model to assess factors associated with the overall satisfaction score, using the same covariates described above and a random intercept for facility.

Adoption

To assess the adoption of NTRP by eligible VHA facilities, we tabulated (A1) the characteristics of spoke sites and (A2) the monthly percentage of new incoming rheumatology consults at all sites that were channeled to the NTRP program, non‐VA care, or local facility rheumatology services during program participation. (A3) We also visualized the monthly percentage of NTRP consults completed by rurality into a line graph over time. For this analysis only, the first and last months of each facility's consult records were excluded to avoid partial‐month observations; thus, the n was 10 facilities. (A4) Finally, in order to understand how many of the consults that were specifically submitted to NTRP were completed by the program, we reported the number and percentage of consults completed via NTRP clinicians.

Implementation

To examine how care through the NTRP was delivered, we (I1) described the number and roles of program staff positions. (I2) We tabulated the mean (SD) encounters at the Veteran‐level and by type: home‐based, clinic‐based, other (which included phone and e‐consult (chart review) encounters) overall, and stratified by rurality (rural or highly rural vs. urban), with differences compared using t‐test.

Qualitative Analysis Approach

Qualitative data from Veteran experience surveys and program staff were analyzed separately with the goal of understanding stakeholder perceptions of the NTRP across all RE‐AIM domains. In both cases, we used a multi‐stage thematic analysis that combined both deductive and inductive techniques to identify key themes and subthemes [24].

Veteran Experience Surveys

Data were first de‐identified and reviewed for clarity, relevance, and completeness to inform the development of a preliminary coding framework. Two team members (CN, AH) then independently summarized each response and applied inductive codes. An experienced qualitative researcher (CN) subsequently reviewed the coded data and used a grounded approach to generate overarching themes and subthemes. These themes were then organized within the domains of the RE‐AIM framework.

Interviews With Program Staff

First, transcripts were reviewed by a qualitative researcher (CN) for clarity, relevance, and completeness to support the development of a preliminary coding framework. Using Dedoose version 10.0.35, a set of deductive codes was applied on the basis of the RE‐AIM framework, along with a preliminary set of inductive codes to capture emergent ideas across interviews. Applied codes were discussed, refined, and organized into a structured codebook with definitions for each code. Two team members (CW, AH) independently applied the codes to each transcript, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings with a third coder (CN). Data were then analyzed by both deductive and inductive codes to identify emerging themes and subthemes, which were organized within the domains of the RE‐AIM framework. Finally, all coded excerpts were summarized and grouped by their corresponding themes and subthemes, with illustrative quotes selected for inclusion. Data from interviews were used to develop a roadmap of adaptations made by program staff with support from the evaluation team to generate information about the RE‐AIM “Maintenance” domain in particular.

This report adhered to the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology for cross‐sectional studies (STROBE) and consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklists. This work was determined to be a non‐research program evaluation activity by the VA Office of Research & Development and the VA National Rheumatology Program.

Results

Reach

Between July 2024 and June 2025, a total of 1227 unique Veterans received care through NTRP (Table 2). (R1) Most Veterans were male (82%) and White (81%), with a mean (SD) age of 62.8 (14.8) years. Nearly half (47%) of participating Veterans resided in rural or highly rural areas. (R2) Veterans residing in urban areas were demographically similar to those in rural or highly rural areas; however, the proportion who were Black was higher in urban areas (12% vs. 5%, p < 0.001), consistent with data from the National Veteran Health Equity Report—Black or African American Veteran Chartbook [25]. The number and types of NTRP encounters per Veteran were similar across both groups. The most common primary rheumatic diagnoses were inflammatory arthritis including rheumatoid arthritis (31%), followed by connective tissue diseases (including systemic sclerosis, vasculitis, and myositis) (14.4%). (R3) Overall, as of June 2025, 51% of Veterans with NTRP encounters had two or more encounters. (R3a) Veterans with psoriatic arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, and inflammatory arthritis (including RA) were more likely to have multiple encounters compared with veterans with soft tissue or other diagnoses (osteoporosis, back pain, and fibromyalgia and pain) (chi‐square, p < 0.001). (R3b) No significant differences in rurality were found to be associated with the number of NTRP encounters completed per patient (chi‐square, p = 0.39). (R3c) In the multivariable mixed‐effects logistic regression, inflammatory conditions were associated with higher odds of a repeat NTRP encounter compared to degenerative and other conditions (OR 3.90, 95% CI 2.95–5.15; p < 0.001). Age, gender, and rurality were not significantly associated with repeat encounters (p = 0.2, p = 0.06, and p = 0.98, respectively).

Effectiveness

(E1) Community Care Utilization

Among 149 Veterans with ≥1 non‐VA care claim in the 12 months before their first NTRP encounter, we found 89.3% (n = 133) had no subsequent non‐VA care claims following their first NTRP appointment during an average of 6.4 (SD = 1.6) months of follow‐up. In the mixed‐effects regression model, neither rurality nor inflammatory conditions (vs. degenerative and other conditions) were significantly associated with continued non‐VA care claims (OR 0.53, 95% CI (0.18–1.61), p = 0.26; OR 1.54, 95% CI (0.18–12.9), p = 0.69, respectively).

(E2) Veteran Satisfaction Surveys

Out of 151 unique respondents, 87.4% were male, with a mean (SD) age of 63.9 (14.8); and 46% were rural or highly rural. Overall satisfaction was moderate to high (median [IQR] total score 58 [53–66] out of possible 70; see Table 3). Veterans in rural or highly rural areas had TSQ scores identical to those in urban areas (both overall and by domain). In a multivariate model, there were no statistically significant differences in the overall satisfaction scores by rurality or diagnosis category. For the question “Overall, I am satisfied with the quality of services being provided via [NTRP] telemedicine,” 89% of responses were 4 or 5 (out of 5). Implementation

(I1) In its first year, the program staffed eight full‐time clinical positions at the hub site, forming three clinical teams supported by a shared nurse and pharmacist. Additional administrative staff included a director, two part‐time assistant directors, a program manager, a program administrator, and an evaluation team. (I2) As of June 2025, NTRP completed 2577 encounters, 45.3% of which were with Veterans from rural or highly rural areas. The mean (SD) number of NTRP encounters per Veteran was 2.1 (1.7) overall and did not differ significantly between rural or highly rural and urban Veterans (2.0 [1.4] vs. 2.2 [1.9]; p = 0.18). No significant differences were observed across encounter types by rurality. Home‐based video encounters averaged 1.4 (0.7), clinic‐based video 1.5 (0.7), and other encounter types 1.9 (1.9), with no significant rural or highly rural versus urban differences for any encounter type (p = 0.29, 0.55, and 0.52, respectively).

Veteran Experience Surveys

Out of 155 unique respondents, 81.3% were male, with a mean (SD) age 62.4 (14.2). Generated themes and subthemes with representative quotes are shown in Table 5. The most consistent themes were improved access to rheumatology care, positive treatment experiences, and convenience of virtual visits. They also emphasized strong collaboration among team members. Adoption

Structured onboarding for sites, standard enrollment processes, staff readiness (prior telehealth experience, experienced, and reliable staff), and standardized training and workflows were identified as facilitators of adoption. Leadership engagement and strong pre‐existing patient–provider relationships further supported new site adoption and Veteran acceptance of the telehealth model.

Implementation

Implementation was facilitated by streamlined workflows, real‐time coordination between physicians and staff during virtual sessions, and strong communication across team members. The involvement of a dedicated pharmacist played an important role in improving Veteran education and providing ongoing support outside of clinical encounters. Improved patient–provider relationship, including patient education and rapport facilitated trust and engagement, supporting care delivery comparable to in‐person visits (Table 6). The availability of clinic‐based video as an alternative to home‐based video was an important access pathway for Veterans with limited technology access at home. Staff interviews identified technology barriers as a recurring implementation challenge, with telehealth coordinators reporting that they coordinated VA loaned devices and conducted pre‐visit test calls to support Veteran participation. Other challenges included limitations in remote physical assessments and variation in EHR use across sites.

Maintenance

Staff reported that ongoing performance monitoring through automated reports was critical for maintaining operations and continuity of care. Continuous data monitoring through real‐time program dashboards was key elements in guiding continuous quality improvement of program operations. Staff engagement was supported by efforts to foster team connection, and workforce development was strengthened through structured training and onboarding materials (Table 6).

Program staff identified several challenges and adaptations taken to address them (Table 7). For example, program staff adapted implementation processes by developing standard operating procedure memos, clarifying roles and responsibilities, and modifying workflows to better align with existing practices. They also enhanced communication strategies within and across sites to improve engagement and support long‐term maintenance.

Discussion

In its first year, the NTRP demonstrated early feasibility as a national, hub‐and‐spoke model for expanding access to specialty care. The program was rapidly adopted across VA facilities and delivered more than 2500 encounters to over 1200 Veterans. Nearly half of participating Veterans resided in rural or highly rural areas, providing a preliminary signal that the model can engage the intended population within mixed rural–urban catchment areas. Veteran satisfaction was high across from those residing in both rural and urban areas, and qualitative feedback from both Veterans and staff highlighted improved access, coordinated workflows, and strong clinical relationships. Together, these early findings suggest the model is feasible to implement at scale and acceptable to rural Veterans, while also pointing to areas for continued refinement. This report contributes to the rural health literature by describing how a centralized telehealth model can be structured to reach Veterans who would otherwise face significant barriers to subspecialty care. Rural Veterans face a distinct set of challenges: longer travel distances, fewer local specialists, and limited broadband or device access for home‐based video visits. Our findings speak to several of these challenges: The program's centralized staffing model, with clinical teams distributed across time zones, helped extend coverage to geographically dispersed sites without requiring local rheumatology recruitment, which is particularly relevant given how difficult rural VA facilities find it to attract and retain subspecialists. The availability of clinic‐based video as an alternative to home‐based video visits was an important adaptation for rural Veterans with technology access barriers, and telehealth coordinators played an active role in supporting device access and building comfort with virtual care prior to appointments. Rural Veterans were just as likely as urban Veterans to complete repeat encounters and reported similar satisfaction scores, suggesting the model acceptable to this population. These findings offer practical guidance for other VA or non‐VA programs considering similar rural tele‐specialty models.

Our findings align with a growing body of work showing that virtual specialty care can expand access and sustain Veteran satisfaction in systems or specialties facing workforce shortages. Prior VA enterprise programs in neurology, cardiology, and sleep medicine used similar national infrastructure to shorten waits and maintain quality, and smaller non‐VA telerheumatology efforts have reported comparable gains in access [15, 16, 26]. The early challenges we observed (variable digital readiness, uneven site workflows, challenges with remote examination, and cross‐time‐zone coordination) are similar to implementation issues described in other telehealth roll‐outs [27, 28]. The diagnostic case mix seen by NTRP clinicians is typical for a rheumatology clinic, including inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis, connective tissue diseases such as vasculitis, lupus, axial spondyloarthritis, and crystal diseases along with some evaluations for raised antibody titers or inflammatory markers. Interestingly, although rheumatology is highly dependent on physical examination, Veterans with diagnoses for inflammatory conditions were more likely to have return encounters with NTRP, suggesting that Veterans and clinicians found the interactions effective.

Although less than half of participating Veterans were classified as rural, this proportion should be interpreted in the context of mixed rural–urban catchment areas at participating sites. The program was implemented at facilities selected for high rural Veteran populations rather than exclusively rural Veterans; therefore, reach reflects both access expansion, and the characteristics of the underlying referral populations. Future evaluations should assess whether the proportion of rural Veterans increases over time as referral pathways mature.

This report also extends prior work by linking specific operational design features to early feasibility outcomes. On the basis of our qualitative analysis, two features of the care model appear particularly important. First, centralized staffing across time zones enabled efficient coverage of geographically dispersed sites with limited local rheumatology capacity, suggesting a scalable approach for other non‐procedural specialties. Second, the team‐based model, particularly the integration of pharmacy support, supported care coordination, medication management, and patient engagement. Veterans frequently cited clear communication and continuity, while staff emphasized real‐time teamwork and pharmacist involvement as key facilitators of implementation. Prior studies have shown that pharmacist‐led telehealth interventions can improve medication optimization, chronic disease control, and medication safety, with outcomes comparable to or better than usual care, particularly in high‐risk and rural populations [29, 30]. These findings support the role of pharmacists as key contributors to telehealth‐based specialty care, where much of the clinical value is derived from longitudinal medication management and patient education. Given persistent rheumatology workforce shortages, embedding clinical pharmacists in tele‐Rheumatology teams may be a scalable way to extend capacity for medication education and monitoring in rural settings.

Program adoption was incomplete during the first year: Only 65% of consults submitted to NTRP were completed by the program, and non‐VA care continued to account for the largest share of completed rheumatology consults across participating sites. These patterns likely reflect several intersecting factors: some Veterans prefer face‐to‐face encounters or lack reliable access to the technology required for home‐based visits. Notably, incomplete adoption does not appear to reflect a mismatch between tele‐rheumatology and clinical complexity given the diagnostic case mix described above. Veterans with inflammatory conditions were more likely to return for follow‐up encounters, suggesting that both Veterans and clinicians found the virtual interactions clinically useful despite the absence of a physical exam. The proportion of NTRP encounters completed for rural versus urban Veterans reflected the underlying distribution of participating Veterans and remained stable after a brief ramp‐up at the beginning of the program. Taken together, these early findings suggest that the barriers to complete adoption were driven by patient preference and modality fit rather than by clinical unsuitability of the referred population, and that tele‐rheumatology is best seen as a complement to existing care pathways.

A preliminary signal of reductions in claims for non‐VA rheumatology care was observed among Veterans who had used community care in the year before joining the program. This is an encouraging early finding, particularly given the higher costs and care fragmentation associated with non‐VA care for Veterans. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Pre–post comparisons without a control group are susceptible to secular trends and can be confounded by a variety of factors. A full economic evaluation, including program costs, will be needed to determine the net economic value of the NTRP, and rural‐stratified cost analyses would be particularly informative given the higher per‐encounter costs often associated with community care in geographically isolated areas.

Several limitations of this report should be noted. First, this is a program development and initial feasibility report rather than a formal outcome evaluation. The findings reflect the program's first year of operation and should be interpreted accordingly. Longitudinal analyses comparing clinical outcomes, wait times, and costs between NTRP participants and comparable Veterans receiving care through other pathways, with stratifications based on rurality, are planned as the program matures and a sufficient dataset accumulates. Second, Veteran survey response rates were low, though typical for telephone surveys of this type, and satisfaction findings may not be representative of all Veterans served [31]. Although satisfaction survey results were generally favorable, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the modest number of respondents. Third, although the program was designed to prioritize sites with high proportion of rural Veterans, participating sites served mixed rural–urban populations, which limits conclusions about rural‐specific outcomes at this stage. Future evaluations should report rural‐stratified outcomes in more detail, including technology access rates, encounter completion rates by modality, and clinical outcomes, to better characterize how the model performs specifically for rural Veterans [32]. Finally, this evaluation did not include Veterans who declined to participate in NTRP, whose perspectives and characteristics would be valuable for understanding the full range of access barriers and program fit.

Conclusion

In summary, the NTRP demonstrates that a centralized telehealth model can be developed and implemented at scale to expand access to rheumatology care, including for rural Veterans who face significant barriers to in‐person subspecialty services. The workforce gap at rural VA facilities, the preliminary signal of reduced community care utilization, comparable satisfaction and return visit rates among rural Veterans, and the organizational precedent set by analogous VA telehealth programs collectively provide early justification for continued investment in the NTRP at rural sites, pending formal cost and outcomes evaluation. Continued evaluation will be critical to assess costs and clinical outcomes, long‐term sustainability, and to refine care pathways that match patients to the most appropriate modality based on clinical needs and access to technology. Rural‐specific analyses will be particularly important as the program grows, both to demonstrate its value for this population and to generate implementation guidance for other tele‐specialty programs serving geographically dispersed Veterans.
