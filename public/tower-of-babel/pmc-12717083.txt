Establishing long-lasting vaccine immunity: insights from mRNA and adjuvanted protein platforms
Authors: Andrea-Clemencia Pineda-Peña, Kohei Hasegawa, Vincent Pavot, Catherine Berry, Behazine Combadière, Guadalupe Cortes-Garcia, Adrian B. McDermott, Saranya Sridhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Durable vaccine-induced immunity is the basis of long-term protection against infection and disease. This review covers three topics. First, we describe the immunological foundations of durable response and its determinants (e.g., vaccine design, host, and pathogen factors). Second, we summarize the current evidence regarding the durable immune responses conferred by mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based vaccine platforms. Lastly, we discuss the challenges in developing durable mRNA vaccines and outline future considerations.

Introduction

The recognition of the durable protection provided by immunological memory predates our understanding of its molecular and cellular basis by millennia. During the Plague of Athens (430 BC), Thucydides recorded that survivors could care for the newly afflicted without falling ill, as the plague “never attacked the same person twice, at least not with fatal results”1. The principle—surviving an infection or being exposed to a less virulent or attenuated pathogen confers durable protection—laid the groundwork for Edward Jenner’s development of the variolae vaccinae in 17962. While this ancient wisdom remains foundational to modern vaccinology, contemporary challenges have emerged. Viral evolution, waning immunity, and the need for rapid-response platforms (exemplified by mRNA technology during COVID-19) highlight the critical importance of engineering durable immune responses.

Two centuries post-Jenner, the durability of protection—defined as maintaining protective immune responses against infection or disease over extended periods3—remains a critical aspect of vaccine development and public health strategies4–6. Certain conventional vaccines provide long-term immunity (e.g., empirical evidence estimating a half-life of 3014 years for measles vaccines and infections, 542 years for mumps vaccines and infections, and 11 years in average for tetanus vaccines)7. The COVID-19 pandemic and the continuing evolution of the SARS-CoV-2 have focused attention upon the importance of immune durability and other mechanisms such as immune dominance, antigenic sin (or imprinting), and antibody feedback4. What is becoming evident is an interplay between the pathogen, the antigen design, and the delivery platform, which influences the duration, the quality and magnitude of the immune response3,8. With the widespread use of mRNA vaccines for COVID-19 and the observations of waning humoral responses, the platform has been implicated with short-lived immune responses9,10. Therefore, to examine the implications of the platforms, we require comprehensive knowledge of the immunological mechanisms that govern the generation and the maintenance of effective, long-lived immunity. This will inform the vaccine field to engineer more effective, long-lasting vaccines and immunization approaches.

In this review, we first describe the immunological basis of durability and its determinants. Second, we summarize and contrast the existent evidence on the vaccine durability of two major vaccine platforms: mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based vaccines. Lastly, we review the evidence on the vaccine durability against SARS-CoV-2, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza, and cytomegalovirus (CMV), on which clinical data of both platforms are available in the literature. Lastly, we discuss the major challenges and future directions to engineer and develop durable vaccines.

Immunological mechanisms of vaccine durability

Vaccines elicit complex immune responses involving a coordinated effort of various immune cells from both the innate and adaptive immune systems, leading to the formation of immune memory. Immune memory is maintained in four major compartments: long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs), memory B cells, memory CD4+ T cells, and memory CD8+ T cells11,12. These memory compartments persist in a dynamic equilibrium, ensuring both immediate and recall protection upon re-exposure to the antigen. The cellular and molecular foundations of immune memory have been extensively reviewed elsewhere12–15.

B cell-mediated immunological memory

B cell immunity is a critical component of the adaptive immune system, providing protection against pathogens long after exposure. This immunological memory is primarily established through two mechanisms: long-lived plasma cells that produce protecting antibodies and memory B cells that respond to reinfection12,13. The development of B cell memory occurs in two main stages within secondary lymphoid organs, particularly in B cell follicles and germinal centers. In the first stage of the primary response, antigens encountered on dendritic cells activate naive B cells via their B cell receptors (BCRs) in the B cell follicles16. These antigens are then processed and presented to T helper (TH) cells that have been primed by dendric cells to differentiate to T follicular helper (TFH) cells at the border of T cell-B cell zone12,13,17,18. The interaction with pre-TFH cells stimulates B cells to proliferate and differentiate into short-lived plasma cells that rapidly produce low-affinity antibodies of unswitched and switched isotypes, germinal center B cells, and some memory B cells that develop independently of the germinal center19,20. In the second stage, germinal center B cells undergo clonal expansion and somatic hypermutation leading to BCR diversification12. In the germinal center light zone, these cells interact with follicular dendritic cells and germinal center TFH cells (GC-TFH). TFH cells play crucial roles in selecting high-affinity B cells. This selection process results in the differentiation of germinal center B cells into LLPCs and memory B cells and the reentry of B cells into the germinal center dark zone for additional rounds of population expansion, somatic hypermutation, and selection21–24.

LLPCs migrate to survival niches, primarily in the bone marrow, where they receive survival signals (e.g., from stromal cells, IL-6, APRIL) and continuously secrete high-affinity antibodies for months to years independently of antigen re-encounter25,26. These cells underpin the constitutive humoral memory, contributing to the maintenance of circulating antibody levels, providing immediate protection against encountered pathogens (Fig. 1A)27. In contrast, memory B cells, which persist in a quiescent state in secondary lymphoid organs, tissues, and peripheral blood, are poised for rapid reactivation upon antigen re-exposure—particularly when antibody levels wane or when variant pathogens partially evade neutralization12. Upon secondary exposure with the level of antibodies secreted by LLPCs being below the protective threshold or variants escaping recognition by pre-existing antibodies, these cells rapidly differentiate into plasma cells, producing a robust antibody response—i.e., reactive humoral memory (Fig. 1B)27. Some memory B cells can also re-enter germinal centers, undergoing further rounds of affinity maturation and diversification, proving more-potent antibodies. In addition to serving as the cellular source for the anamnestic antibody response, memory B cells also function as a repertoire library for potential future exposures to pathogen variants28.

T cell-mediated immunological memory

In addition to B cell immunity, T cells are important actors of protective immunity against pathogens29–31. CD4+ T cells play a crucial role in controlling and clearing viral infections through primarily indirect (e.g., TFH and TH cells) and direct (e.g., cytotoxic CD4 T cells) mechanisms12,32. These cells support B cell responses and exert direct antiviral functions, making them essential for robust and durable immunity33.

Memory CD4+ T cells are important in recall responses, where memory T cells specific for the previously-exposed antigen are present at a higher frequency than their naive counterparts, offering quantitative advantages. In addition, they rapidly respond to previously encountered antigens, providing quicker and more effective help to B cells and CD8+ T cells14. This rapid response is facilitated by their preformed CD40L and their ability to produce higher levels of cytokines compared to naive T cells, thereby promoting a more robust and efficient immune response14. Memory CD4+ T cells exhibit substantial functional heterogeneity, with subsets such as central memory (TCM), effector memory (TEM), and tissue-resident memory (TRM) cells, each contributing differently to immune surveillance and protection in various anatomical compartments. TRM cells reside at the port of pathogen entry (e.g., the airway, skin)14,34–36.

Memory CD8+ T cells are similarly heterogeneous, comprising central memory, effector memory, and tissue-resident subsets, each with distinct migratory and functional properties that enable broad immunosurveillance and local responses. They are critical for cellular immunity against intracellular infections. These cells are activated in the T cell zone by antigen peptide presentation via MHC class I molecules on dendritic cells. Upon activation, naive CD8+ T cells differentiate into short-lived effector CD8+ T cells or memory CD8+ T cells37. Effector CD8+ T cells are responsible for immediate cytolytic activity against infected cells. Most short-lived effector cells undergo programmed cell death at the end of the primary immune response38. In contrast, memory CD8+ T cells persist for years and can rapidly proliferate into a large wave of effector cells upon secondary exposure. This rapid proliferation ensures swift and effective control of reinfections11.

Vaccine formulations and differential immune durability

Different vaccine designs induce varying durability of immunity and protection by eliciting distinct sets of these immune components. For example, virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines against human papilloma virus (HPV) offer durable protection against infection and subsequent neoplastic diseases39. The protection is mainly driven by LLPCs that persistently produce L1-binding neutralizing antibodies—not by memory B or T cells, although they are part of the immune response40. The densely-ordered repetitive display structure of B cell epitopes and the particulate property of the vaccine lead to cross-linking of BCRs on naive B cells and robust germinal center reactions, generating greater activation and survival signals, thereby inducing LLPCs40.

In contrast, live attenuated vaccines, such as those against measles, induce broad and durable immunity through robust engagement of both humoral and cellular compartments—mimicking natural infection41. These vaccines elicit long-lived neutralizing antibodies, strong memory CD4⁺ T cell responses to sustain antibody production, and durable CD8⁺ T cell responses for cytotoxic protection42,43.

The longevity and effectiveness of immune memory conferred by vaccines vary depending on the specific pathogen or antigen and the nature of the immune response44. Despite the advancement of human immunology and vaccinology, the mechanisms governing the maintenance of protection and immune responses are not fully understood and remain an active area of research.

Vaccine-specific determinants

Antigen properties play a central role in determining the magnitude and persistence of immune responses. Repetitive, multimeric antigen displays (such as VLPs in HPV vaccines or synthetic nanoparticles) induce more persistent responses compared to soluble monomeric proteins or polysaccharides45. Higher antigen valency induces early B cell activation and persistent germinal center responses, thus leading to long-standing plasma cell differentiation and durable immune response46. Second, higher antigen doses increase immune response magnitude by enhancing antigen availability for uptake and presentation to B and T cells47. This mechanism generally enhances durability, while higher doses may also induce T regulatory cells48. Third, optimal early innate signals, such as synergistic activation of multiple pattern recognition receptors and proper inflammatory responses, are crucial49,50. Engagement of multiple pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)—such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), RIG-I-like receptors, or NOD-like receptors—enhances dendritic cell activation and cytokine production, providing the necessary cues for T and B cell priming51,52. Suboptimal inflammation, by contrast, may result in weak or short-lived memory formation. For protein-based vaccines, adjuvants can play a major role in achieving optimal innate response53. For example, advanced adjuvant systems combining multiple immunostimulatory components (e.g., TLR agonists with alum) outperform traditional adjuvants in inducing long-lasting responses54. These adjuvants augment germinal center reactions, diversify the B cell clonotype repertoire, and promote LLPCs and memory B cell generation54. Fourth, effector functions of IgG antibodies (e.g., antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, complement-dependent cytotoxicity) imparted by post-translational modifications also contribute to the durability of vaccine-induced immunity, as they enable antibodies to mediate pathogen clearance and sustain long-term protection even as neutralizing antibody titers wane55. Recent studies have shown between-vaccine differences in effector functions. For example, a third monovalent anti-SARS-CoV2 mRNA vaccination was shown to boost all isotypes (including IgG1 and IgG3) and robust Fc functional activity, while bivalent mRNA vaccine boosting led to primarily IgG2 and IgG4 responses with poor Fc functional activities56. By contrast, adjuvanted protein vaccines were reported to have lower IgG4, higher IgG1 and IgG3 levels, and greater Fc effector functions57. Fifth, the vaccination schedule, such as the number of doses and the duration of priming and boosting, impacts durability58,59. Optimal spacing between priming doses (typically 3–4 weeks) allows for the maturation and expansion of antigen-specific T and B cells without competitive interference58,60. Delaying the booster to 4–6 months post-priming allows for affinity maturation and differentiation into robust memory B cells within the germinal center61. Furthermore, heterologous prime-boost strategies, such as an mRNA prime followed by an adenoviral or adjuvanted protein boost, can enhance both the breadth and durability of immune responses by engaging complementary immune pathways62. Lastly, the route of vaccine administration affects the quality and persistence of the immune response63,64. For example, compared to intramuscular injection, intradermal administration may enhance antigen retention in draining lymph nodes, activate germinal center B and T cells, and promote more durable responses as suggested in preclinical models65. In addition, studies have suggested that ipsilateral boosting (same limb as priming) promotes immune responses, including enhanced SARS-CoV-2 neutralization, circulating spike-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses64, total germinal center B cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cells in the draining lymph nodes66, compared to contralateral boosting. Furthermore, delivering antigens slowly within the host, rather than through single large doses, may enhance long-lasting immunity. This could be due to the continuous presence of antigens, which helps maintain germinal center B cells and TFH cells59,67,68.

Host-specific determinants

Host factors—while they may not be modifiable—also influence vaccine durability69. For example, early-life immune immaturity and immunosenescence limit the induction and persistence of LLPCs70,71. Polymorphisms in genes encoding MHC molecules, cytokines, chemokines, chemokine receptors, and innate immune receptors influence the strength and quality of T and B cell responses70,72,73. These differences can affect the durability and breadth of immunity between individuals. In addition, pre-existing immunity through natural infection or prior vaccination can bias recall responses via mechanisms such as original antigenic sin or antibody-mediated feedback inhibition74. This has been particularly noted in influenza and SARS-CoV-2, where immune imprinting affects the response to variant antigens75–77. Furthermore, durability is negatively affected by comorbidities, such as immunocompromised status, end-stage renal disease, diabetes, and systemic corticosteroid use, due to impaired germinal center reactions and reduced memory cell persistence78.

Pathogen-specific determinants

The biology of the target pathogen shapes the required features of durable protection7. For example, pathogens with a stronger force of infection, shorter incubation period, and higher genetic variability (i.e., a greater number and variety of circulating strains and variants) negatively affect the generation of durable protection79,80. Indeed, infection by a variant with a shorter incubation period does not permit an effective recall response, which was widely observed during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic81. In addition, with antigenic drift or shift, neutralizing antibodies and memory B cell responses generated against historic mutable epitopes often lose efficacy, necessitating frequent reformulation of vaccines.

The durability of vaccine-induced immune responses is determined by a complex interplay of factors related to the vaccine design, host, and pathogen characteristics. Optimizing durability requires further understanding of factors, such as optimal early innate signals, exact mechanisms of these vaccine designs, and measurement of vaccine durability3, which will, in turn, inform the next generation of vaccine development.

Antibody responses

The durability of antibody responses following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination has been a major focus of research. The literature has suggested that both, mRNA vaccines and adjuvanted protein vaccines induce robust and durable antibody responses.

mRNA vaccines

mRNA vaccines (e.g., BNT162b2, mRNA-1273) induce strong initial antibody responses, with high titers of anti-spike and neutralizing antibodies peaking shortly after the primary series82–84. However, there have been concerns about waning immunity as antibody levels were observed to decline over time82–94. For example, a longitudinal study of BNT162b2 recipients in Israel has shown that the level of anti-receptor-binding domain (RBD) IgG declined ~16-fold over 6-month period post-vaccinations92. Similarly, studies of the Moderna mRNA-1273 vaccine have found a 10-fold decline in anti-RBD IgG levels and 5-fold decline in neutralizing antibody titers over 6 months after the second dose, with low but detectable neutralizing antibodies in nearly all study subjects83,95. Notably, with the use of a lower dose (25 μg) of the mRNA-1273 vaccine, a study has reported that, while the levels of RBD and neutralizing antibodies were ~50% lower compared to 100-μg dose, these levels were maintained with 7-fold to 10-fold reductions 6 months post vaccinations47—comparable to the fold changes reported in the 100-μg dose96. These observations collectively suggest that the antibody durability after 2-dose mRNA vaccines is consistent and its kinetics is independent of the dose, while the absolute magnitude of antibody response is dose-dependent. Comparative studies have indicated that mRNA-1273 elicits higher peak titers than BNT162b2, possibly due to differences in mRNA content and dosing intervals. However, antibody waning follows a biphasic decay model: an initial rapid decline (half-life ~30 days), followed by a plateau phase with relatively stable titers for 6–12 months, reflecting the transition from short-lived plasmablasts to long-lived plasma cell-derived antibody production68,97,98.

Because of declining neutralizing antibody titers and the emergence of variants of concern (e.g., Delta, Omicron), vaccine regimens incorporating booster doses have been implemented. Research has demonstrated that, compared to 2-dose regimens, boosters achieve more durable neutralizing antibody responses95,99–102, albeit with potential strain-specific differences in durability. For example, neutralizing antibody titers against wild-type USA-WA1/2020 and Omicron variants decreased only ~2-fold at 4 months after BNT162b2101. Studies with mRNA-1273 have shown that, 6 months after a booster dose, neutralizing titers declined ~2-folds against D614G and ~6-folds against Omicron B.1.1.52995. Furthermore, in a longitudinal study following healthcare workers for over three years (the PARIS study), modeling of spike-binding antibody durability demonstrated a biphasic pattern—an initial rapid decay phase followed by a stabilization phase103. Specifically, after the primary 2-dose mRNA vaccination series, spike-binding antibody titers peaked at 14 days post-second dose. The peak was followed by an initial decay with a half-life of ~30 days. However, rather than continuing to decline, spike-binding antibody levels stabilized for 6–12 months post-vaccination with a 3- to 5-fold decrease. In addition, the study showed that individuals with hybrid immunity (prior infection plus vaccination) maintained higher stable levels at 6–12 months post-vaccination compared to those with vaccine-only103–107 or infection-only108,109 immunity. This finding aligns with other studies—compared to subjects receiving 2-dose mRNA vaccines, those with hybrid immunity had 5- to 17-fold higher neutralizing antibody titers against different strains (e.g., Beta, Delta, Omicron) after 6 months88,110.

Self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA) platforms

Emerging saRNA-based vaccines have shown potential for enhanced antibody durability. Head-to-head studies comparing the Arcturus Therapeutics ARCT-154 with BNT162b2 have yielded compelling results. ARCT-154 contains 5 μg of mRNA drug substance comprising RNA coding for replicase proteins derived from the Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus and a transgene region that encodes the spike (S) glycoprotein of the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 S strain. When administered as a second booster, ARCT-154 generated significantly higher neutralizing antibody titers against both Wuhan-Hu-1 and Omicron BA.4/5 variants with superior durability observed from 1 to 6 months111–113 and extending to 12 months114 post-booster. These findings suggest saRNA technology’s capacity to extend antigen expression, potentially yielding more robust and durable immune responses compared to conventional mRNA platforms.

Adjuvanted protein vaccines

Limited data exist on antibody durability conferred by adjuvanted protein-based SARS-CoV2 vaccines, including NVX-CoV2373 (Novavax), VidPrevtyn Beta (Sanofi/GSK) and GSK/Medicago candidate115–119. Nevertheless, available evidence suggests that this platform can induce robust, durable antibody responses.

Two pivotal preclinical studies compared spike protein vaccines with a synthetic Toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist adjuvant against mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations in rhesus macaques. These investigations revealed higher peak of neutralizing antibodies in the adjuvanted protein vaccine group while maintaining equivalent antibody half-life120,121. Furthermore, the studies documented comparable levels of memory B cells in lymph nodes alongside more durable CD4+ T cell responses characterized by IFNγ, IL-17, and TNFα production120,121.

NVX-CoV2373—a nanoparticle spike protein vaccine incorporating the saponin-based adjuvant, Matrix-M adjuvant122—has demonstrated particularly promising durability in both preclinical and clinical settings. In rhesus macaques, three immunizations with NVX-CoV2373 elicited persistent antibody responses with high neutralizing titers and enhanced breadth for up to 7 months post-vaccination123. Importantly, the vaccine induced a stable pool of bone marrow-resident plasma cells, suggesting long-term immunity123.

Longitudinal analysis of NVX-CoV2373 further confirmed these findings, demonstrating neutralizing antibody titers at 6 months post-second dose comparable to those of mRNA vaccines, with similar decay kinetics (half-life of 52 days for NVX-CoV2373 versus 69 days for BNT162b2, and 68 days for mRNA-1273)124.

Memory B cells

In addition to circulating antibodies, the generation of memory B cells is crucial for durable humoral immunity. Studies on mRNA vaccines have consistently demonstrated the induction of long-lived and evolving memory B cell populations87,125. For example, two doses of mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273) were shown to elicit spike- and RBD-specific memory B cells, with frequencies increasing between 3- and 6-month post-vaccination88,126. These memory B cells accumulated somatic hypermutation at levels comparable to those observed following SARS-CoV-2 infection, at 5–6 months post-vaccination88,126. Notably, approximately 50% of RBD-specific memory B cells demonstrated cross-reactivity with multiple variants of concern88,124,127. Following a third (booster) dose of mRNA vaccines, memory B cells specific to the RBD further expended and matured, leading to increased antibody potency and breadth125. Moreover, persistent germinal center activity has been observed in draining lymph nodes for up to 6 months post-BNT162b2 vaccination61,128,129, accompanied by continued detection of vaccine-encoded mRNA and spike protein within the germinal centers130.

Although data on NVX-CoV2373 are more limited, studies suggest that this adjuvanted protein vaccine also induces spike- and RBD-specific memory B cells that increase over 3–6 months post-vaccination, albeit at lower frequencies compared to those generated by mRNA vaccines124. Overall, both vaccine platforms appear to generate memory B cells that are dependent on sustained germinal center activity.

Memory CD4+ T cells

Both mRNA and adjuvanted protein vaccines elicit robust and persistent memory CD4 T cell responses. Indeed, studies have demonstrated that, at 6 months post-vaccination, nearly all subjects retained detectable memory CD4+ T cells, with only a modest decline in frequency observed over time47,88,127,131. Compared to natural infection, vaccinations with mRNA-1273 were associated with higher frequencies of spike-specific memory CD4+ T cells and equivalent half-lives47,88,124. These memory CD4+ T cells include subsets such as circulating TFH, TH1, cytotoxic CD4+ T cells expressing granzyme B which were stably maintained for 6–11 months after vaccination, irrespective of the platform124,132,133. Specifically, vaccine-induced memory circulating TFH cells positively correlated with the level of neutralizing antibody titers134 and declined only modestly (less than threefold) over 6 months88,124,135. In parallel, germinal center TFH cells persisted in draining lymph nodes following mRNA vaccinations136, supporting the maintenance of germinal center reaction and contributing to durable antibody production128,137. TH1 memory cells expressing IFNγ, IL-2, or TNFα were also consistently maintained across vaccine platforms88,124,127,131.

Memory CD8+ T cells

SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, particularly mRNA-based formulations, can elicit durable memory CD8+ T cell responses. After two doses of mRNA vaccines, 40–70% of subjects had detectable spike-specific memory CD8+ T cells47,88,124,127,131. The BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273 vaccines induced similar frequencies of circulating spike-specific memory CD8+ T cells124. These populations were maintained in 60–70% of responders over 6 months, with an approximate 2-fold decline from peak levels, indicating the establishment of long-lived CD8+ T cell memory124. However, the magnitude of the CD8+ T cell response was generally lower (often <0.05% of total antigen-specific CD8+ T cells) than that of the spike-specific CD4+ T cell response, with the frequencies of these immune components being correlated47,88,124,127.

In contrast, for NVX-CoV2373, spike-specific memory CD8+ T cells were detectable in a smaller proportion of subjects (10–50%) with a lower frequency of multifunctional CD8+ T cells124. Memory CD8+ T cell responses were primarily characterized by IFNγ- and granzyme B production124.

Summary and link to vaccine efficacy

In summary, SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, including both mRNA and adjuvanted protein platforms, elicit durable immune responses that are generally maintained for at least 6 months following the primary immunization series (Fig. 3). Antibody responses exhibit a biphasic kinetic profile, characterized by an initial decline followed by a stabilization phase, consistent with the transition from short-lived plasmablasts-derived antibodies to those produced by LLPCs. Booster doses enhance both the magnitude and durability of humoral responses. In parallel, memory B cells continue to expend and undergo affinity maturation over time, with a substantial proportion capable of cross-recognizing diverse variants of concern.

Both vaccine platforms induce robust memory CD4+ T cell populations, including TH1, cytotoxic CD4⁺, and circulating TFH subsets, which remain relatively stable over time with only modest attrition. CD8+ T cell memory is also generated, particularly following mRNA vaccination, although at lower frequencies compared to CD4⁺ T cells. While the breadth and magnitude of memory B and CD8⁺ T cell responses appear lower with adjuvanted protein vaccines such as NVX-CoV2373, these vaccines still elicit durable antibody responses comparable to those of mRNA platforms.

The kinetics of these immune responses are broadly concordant with observed patterns of vaccine efficacy (VE) against SARS-CoV-2 infection and disease. Initial phase 3 clinical trials reported high VE against laboratory-confirmed symptomatic infection: 90–100% for BNT162b2138–141, 94–95% for mRNA-1273142,143, and 80–90% for NVX-CoV2373144–146. However, real-world effectiveness studies have consistently shown waning protection over time, particularly in the face of antigenically divergent variants such as Omicron9,10,147. For example, a meta-analysis of 40 studies estimated that VE against infection declined to 55% for the Delta variant and 21% for Omicron at 6 months post-vaccination9. A booster dose was associated with a partial restoration of the efficacy to 55% for Omicron, with an estimated half-life efficacy of 143 days9. The apparent discordance between the observed antibody responses and vaccine efficacy can be explained, at least in part, by the waning protective effects being a function of both the immune durability and the emergence of antigenically distinct variants148.

In contrast, protection against severe disease has remained more durable. Initial trials evaluating the mRNA and adjuvanted protein vaccines have demonstrated 95–100% vaccine efficacy139. In addition, a study using statewide data of 10+ million adults in North Carolina has shown the effectiveness of mRNA vaccines was ~70% against hospitalization and 80–90% against death over 10 months after the first dose149. Furthermore, for Omicron, the VE against hospitalization and death remained approximately 90% during the first 4 months following a booster dose149, although slightly lower protection has been reported in some studies, likely reflecting heterogeneity in prior infection rates, host factors, and circulating subvariants150,151. Nevertheless, the maintenance of protection against severe outcomes has been consistently greater and more sustained than for symptomatic infection10,152–157. These findings support a central role for memory T cell responses in long-term protection, particularly against severe disease, where rapid recall responses may compensate for reduced neutralizing antibody titers. Thus, T cell immunity represents a critical component of the protective immune landscape and may serve as a more stable correlate of protection across time and variants158.

Durability of immune responses to mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based RSV vaccines

RSV is a significant cause of lower respiratory tract disease in children and older adults, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality159–162. Recent advancements of RSV structures have led to the development of several RSV vaccines, including mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based vaccines. This section reviews the durability of immune responses elicited by these vaccines, such as Moderna mRNA-1345 and GSK’s RSVPreF3-AS01.

The mRNA-1345 vaccine that encodes the prefusion F protein of subtype A and is formulated in LNP, has demonstrated durable antibody responses. In a phase 1 trial conducted in young adults (aged 18–49 years), a single dose of the vaccine induced neutralizing and prefusion-F-binding antibodies targeting both the RSV-A and -B subtypes with titers remaining above the baseline for at least 6 months163. Consistent findings were reported in older adult populations (aged 60+ years and 65–79 years) where a single dose elicited similar antibody responses against RSV-A and -B with a modest decline over the subsequent 6 months164 and 12 months165. While these data support the durability of the antibody response, no published studies have yet characterized the contribution of memory B or T cells to long-term immunity. In a phase 2–3 trial examining clinical efficacy with a median follow-up of 112 days following the primary vaccination, the vaccine efficacy was 84% against RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease and 68% against RSV-associated acute respiratory disease166.

RSVPreF3-AS01—a combination of recombinant prefusion F protein from subtype A and a liposome-based adjuvant system containing two immunostimulants: 3-O-desacyl-4’-monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL) and the saponin QS-21—has shown durable antibody and CD4+ T cell responses. Studies on older adults (aged 60+ years) have reported that a single dose of the vaccine achieved prefusion-F-specific and RSV-A/B neutralizing antibody responses against contemporary RSV clinical isolates167—regardless of the adjuvant use—that remained above the baseline for 12 months post-vaccination168,169. They have also observed durable prefusion F-specific CD4+ T cell responses over 12 months post-vaccination169. The responses were significantly greater in the subjects with an adjuvant vaccine compared to those without168. However, no prefusion F-specific CD8+ T cell responses were detected168. A phase 3 trial demonstrated that the vaccine efficacy was 83% against RSV-related lower respiratory tract disease and 94% for severe disease with a median follow-up of ~7 months170. During the second RSV season following the vaccination, the efficacy for these outcomes decreased to 67% and 79%, respectively171.

Durability of immune responses to mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based influenza vaccines

Influenza remains a significant global health challenge, necessitating the development of effective and long-lasting vaccination strategies172,173. This section reviews current data on the durability of immune responses elicited by newly developed mRNA-based vaccines (e.g., Moderna mRNA-1010) and adjuvanted protein-based vaccines (e.g., MF59- and AS03-adjuvanted formulations). Given the limited published data on the durability of mRNA vaccines in the context of pandemic influenza, this section focuses on vaccines targeting seasonal influenza.

The mRNA-1010—a quadrivalent mRNA-based vaccine against seasonal influenza—has demonstrated the potential to elicit durable antibody response for up to 6 months. In a phase 1/2 clinical trial, a single dose mRNA-1010 induced hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titers that gradually declined over time but remained above the seroprotective threshold of 1:40 across all age groups (18–49, 50–64, and 65+ years), particularly for influenza A strains, over a ~6-month period following a peak response at day 29174. In contrast, the geometric mean titers (GMTs) for influenza B strains declined to the near-baseline levels during the same period174. Similarly, a phase 3 trial reported that HAI GMTs for all four vaccine strains peaked at day 29 and declined thereafter but remained above baseline through 6 months175. However, these data did not assess the durability of memory B cell or T cell responses, nor did they report vaccine efficacy data174,176.

Adjuvanted protein-based influenza vaccines have also shown promising durability of immune responses in both pediatric and older adult populations. In a study of young children (aged 6–72 months), the MF59-adjuvanted quadrivalent vaccines induced significantly higher HAI titers compared to a non-adjuvanted vaccine (Fluzone®) against homologous vaccine strains, with only modest reductions observed over 6 months post-vaccination177. Similarly, another study of MF59-adjuvanted trivalent vaccines reported sustained HAI responses superior to those elicited by non-adjuvated comparators (Agriflu® or Fluzone) over a 6-month period post-vaccination178. These findings are supported by additional studies demonstrating relative VE ranging from 31% to 75% against all influenza strains compared to non-adjuvanted vaccines over more than 6 months179,180. In older adults (aged 65+ years) MF59-adjuvanted trivalent vaccine (Fluad®) elicited durable HAI responses against homologous A/H3N2 strains over 6–12 months, although limited cross-reactivity was observed for heterologous strains when compared to non-adjuvated vaccines (Agriflu)181. These results are consistent with previous findings on MF59182 and AS03183 adjuvated vaccines. Moreover, a small single-center study in older adults found that the MF59-adjuvanted trivalent vaccine induced a durable increase in influenza-specific CD4+ T cells expressing IFNγ or CD107a for up to 6 months post-vaccination, while no significant CD8+ T cells responses were detected184. In addition, a post-hoc analysis of a randomized trial comparing AS03-adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted (Fluarix®) trivalent vaccines in older adults showed that the AS03 formulation conferred a 22% relative efficacy against A/H3N2 infection over a 6-month period185.

Durability of immune responses to mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based CMV vaccines

CMV is a significant cause of congenital infections and complications in patients with immunocompromised status186,187. Recent efforts to develop effective vaccines against CMV have focused on mRNA (Moderna’s mRNA-1647) and adjuvanted protein (e.g., GSK’s glycoprotein B [gB]/AS01, Sanofi/Novartis’ gB/MF59) vaccines.

The mRNA-1647 vaccine—encoding the CMV gB and pentametric complex (glycoproteins H and L, UL128, UL130, and UL131)—has demonstrated durable humoral and cellular responses. For example, data from phase 1 trials have shown that adults who received the vaccine had higher neutralizing anti-gB and anti-pentamer antibody titers against 14 different strains than the seropositive controls and that the titers were maintained for 12 months after the third dose188,189. This durable antibody response was accompanied by antigen-specific memory B cells188. In addition, the vaccine also induced robust polyfunctional CD4+ TH1 and effector CD8+ T cell responses as well as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis, regardless of CMV serostatus, which remained elevated for 6 months after the third dose189,190.

For gB/AS01, a study of seronegative adults (aged 18–40 years) has reported that three doses elicited durable neutralizing and anti-gB antibodies as well as gB-specific antibody-secreting plasma cells maintained for 18 months post-vaccination, comparable to natural infection191. Likewise, sustained frequencies of gB-specific CD4 T cells expressing IFNγ, IL-2, and TNFα were observed in peripheral blood191. In addition, gB/MF59—a combination of CMV envelope gB and an oil-in-water emulsion of squalene—had also demonstrated, in CMV seronegative women, a durable immune response with a ~50% efficacy against infection over 42 months192. For example, a study of seronegative adolescents (aged 12–17 years) had shown durable anti-gB response up to 2 years after the third dose193. Another study of seropositive women at a reproductive age had also found that durable neutralizing and gB-specific antibody response for 6 months after the third dose194. In contrast, gB-specific CD4+ T cell response initially proliferated after priming, which was not significantly maintained194. A comparative study with mRNA-1647 has observed that, while gB/MF59 elicited higher gB-specific IgG and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis response, mRNA-1647 achieved more potent and broader neutralizing antibody response in seronegative patients190.

Bringing the evidence together

Recent advances have significantly deepened our understanding of the immune responses elicited by mRNA- and adjuvanted protein-based vaccines, particularly in the context of SARS-CoV-2. However, notable heterogeneity exists across studies investigating the durability of these responses, especially when comparing different target pathogens. These discrepancies may arise from differences not only in the biological determinants of immune durability (as outlined in Fig. 2), but also in study-specific factors such as population demographics, baseline immune status, study design, and immunological assessment methodologies—or any combination thereof.

Moreover, the long-term durability of individual components of immune memory (e.g., LLPCs, memory B cells, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells) remains incompletely characterized and requires further empirical investigation. Despite these uncertainties, the collective body of evidence suggests that both mRNA and adjuvanted protein vaccines are capable of inducing robust and potentially long-lasting humoral and cellular immune responses, with durability that may extend over multiple years. These findings provide important insights and highlight ongoing challenges for the rational design and development of next-generation vaccines with optimized durability. They underscore the need for harmonized study designs and standardized immunological endpoints to enable more accurate cross-platform comparisons and inform future vaccine strategies.

Challenges and the way forward

Improving vaccine durability is fundamentally an engineering problem—one that can be addressed through advances in bioengineering once key immunological parameters are clearly defined. This endeavor requires a holistic understanding of the underlying immune mechanisms, optimization of vaccine platforms, and careful consideration of the heterogeneity in immune responses across diverse subpopulations. Below, we outline the major challenges and future directions toward enhancing vaccine durability.

Incomplete understanding of durable immunity and protection

A central obstacle to engineering durable vaccines lies in the incomplete understanding of the immunological mechanisms and correlates that underpin long-term protection against different pathogens. For example, we still lack sufficient insight into the factors governing the maintenance of germinal center activity, the enhancement of B cell affinity maturation, and the development of LLPCs3. Bridging this gap necessitates the use of in-depth sampling techniques and systems immunology approaches.

Vaccine studies have traditionally relied on peripheral blood samples, which often fail to capture the long-term immune responses relevant anatomical sites such as lymph nodes and bone marrow. In-depth sampling techniques, such as ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspirates136,195,196 now enable minimally invasive sampling from these compartments, offering a more complete picture of the immune landscape. For example, draining lymph node studies up to 29 weeks following BNT162b2 vaccination revealed persistent spike-binding germinal center B cells and plasmablasts, highlighting prolonged germinal center activity129. Similarly, bone marrow analyses have detected LLPCs with high levels of somatic hypermutations, correlating with enhanced antibody neutralization61. In contrast, another longitudinal study found that SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody-secreting cells resided mainly in the non-long-lived plasma cell compartment, illustrating variability across studies and individuals197.

Conventional immunological research has also chiefly relied on the reductionist approach—purifying and examining molecules and cells in isolation198. Yet, this approach does not capture the complexity of human immune systems that govern durable protection. Recent research has highlighted the systems biology approach that comprehensively examines interactions between components of a complex system both in the fields of infectious diseases, immunology, and vaccinology198–207. This approach aims to delineate the mechanisms and correlates of immunity by applying mechanistic challenge models (e.g., humanized mice, organoids, controlled human infection models) and by integrating computational science and high-throughput technologies (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics)208,209. The combination of in-depth sampling and systems biology approaches (e.g., spatial single-cell RNAseq210 and mass spectrometry211, CITE-seq202) should allow for the examination of the cellular interactions, kinetics of vaccine components, and microenvironment that are responsible for vaccine durability. For example, a recent study by Cortese et al. exemplifies this strategy, using a systems vaccinology framework to identify predictors and mechanisms underlying the durability of antibody responses across multiple human vaccines212. Furthermore, these efforts should enable the characterization of unique protective immune responses (beyond the antibody response) against different pathogens to help us build a unique target immune profile213, which guides the design of protective vaccine strategies.

Barriers to engineering and developing durable vaccine platforms

Once the immunological foundations of durable immunity are elucidated, the next challenge is translating this knowledge into optimized vaccine platforms. Several strategies can be employed to engineer durable vaccines. The major levers for mRNA platforms include the optimization of the formulations (e.g., LNP compositions, mRNA sequences and modifications)214–216, novel adjuvants that synergistically induce early innate signals3, and optimization of administration route and vaccine schedule59. In addition, for antigenically variable viruses (e.g., influenza viruses, SARS-CoV2), the use of highly conserved epitopes of antigens may contribute to the development of durable vaccines that maintain vaccine efficacy across evolving strains217. In particular, the induction of cross-reactive T cell responses, especially CD8+ T cells targeting conserved viral epitopes, is considered being correlated with protection against severe diseases, less affected by antigen variances, and more durable218. Compared to adjuvanted protein vaccines, the mRNA-based vaccine platform induces more robust responses as summarized above.

The development of vaccine platforms should be further accelerated and de-risked. The conventional framework of vaccine development follows a linear, unidirectional path, slowly progressing from preclinical testing in animal models, followed by eventual human trials in multiple phases. However, this framework can be hampered by the between-species differences in immune response, high reactogenicity profiles in some novel co-formulation solutions and admixing formulation using adjuvants, and the failure of late-stage clinical trials208,219. Rather, small-scale experimental human trials early (phase 0/1) in the developmental pipeline can be utilized to test new vaccine designs and decipher potential mechanisms of action208. This enables novel hypotheses that can be experimentally validated in preclinical models. With more iterative testing and validation in in vitro systems, animal models (e.g., mice, non-human primates) and humans, the new framework should not only accelerate but also de-risk the vaccine development. Furthermore, studies that directly compare the between-platform difference in effectiveness and underlying mechanisms remain limited in human124,220. While the lack of data is due to multiple reasons (e.g., logistical, proprietary), it is instrumental to develop rigorously designed and conducted “side-by-side” clinical studies that minimize potential selection bias, confounding, and measurement biases to advance the understanding.

Within-population heterogeneity in immune responses

The heterogeneity of immune responses across individuals presents a significant challenge to the development of universally durable vaccines221. The conventional development strategy often relies on a major assumption that the target population is homogeneous, while vaccine efficacy and durability are influenced by many factors (such as genetics and HLA polymorphism222, immune history223, and immunophenotypes205) that may vary within and across populations. For example, a study from the NIAID Human Immunology Project Consortium using the blood transcriptome data has derived pre-vaccination immunophenotypes (low, middle, and high inflammatory phenotypes) that are differentially enriched in TLR-related innate immunity pathways and associated with the magnitude of serum antibody response of 13 vaccines205.

To address the heterogeneity, it is instrumental to identify pre- and post-vaccination immunophenotypes that have differential durability through the integration of multimodal data (e.g., demographic, clinical, and omics data). These derived immunophenotypes must be rigorously validated in experimental models and humans, which are mostly inadequate in the current literature. In addition, to translate this knowledge into vaccine development and clinics, it is fundamental to develop analytically and clinically validated biomarkers (e.g., susceptibility, prognostic, and response biomarkers)224. These biomarkers can be used to accelerate vaccine development through optimal patient selection, enrichment, stratification, and dose selection, in addition to surrogate endpoints224. These approaches may open an avenue for precision vaccinology that aims to tailor vaccine strategy (e.g., adjuvant and vaccine administration optimization) to the specific needs of subpopulations221,225.

Conclusion

Both mRNA and adjuvanted protein-based vaccines have demonstrated the capacity to induce long-lived humoral and cellular immune responses, offering the potential for durable protection against infectious diseases. However, the extent and persistence of these responses are influenced by a complex interplay of host, pathogen, and vaccine design factors. Despite recent advances, several key challenges remain: the incomplete understanding of mechanisms underpinning durable immunity, translational barriers in platform optimization, and the heterogeneity in immune responses within populations. Addressing these challenges requires an integrated, systems-level approach that couples mechanistic insight with engineering innovation. By advancing our understanding of immune durability and leveraging this knowledge to inform platform design and clinical development, we can accelerate the creation of next-generation vaccines capable of providing long-lasting protection across diverse populations and emerging pathogens.
