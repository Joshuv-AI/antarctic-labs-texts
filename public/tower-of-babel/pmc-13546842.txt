Von Willebrand factor A2 domain inhibits A1-DNA binding while preserving A1-platelet interaction under flow: evidence from recombinant domain constructs
Authors: Zhitong Peng, Xiaoxi Sun, Guangzhou Qu, Jinhua Fang, Aiying Liu, Yuming Huang, Zhiwei Wu, Junxian Yang, Jie Ma, Jiangguo Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCirculating cell-free DNA levels increase in the circulation during inflammation and thrombosis. During immunothrombosis, neutrophil extracellular traps provide long DNA fibers with prothrombotic properties. Von Willebrand factor (VWF) has been reported to interact with DNA via its A1 domain; however, the consequences of this interaction and its regulation by the adjacent A2 domain remain poorly understood.ObjectivesUsing recombinant VWF domain constructs, we aimed to characterize VWF-A1–DNA interactions and to determine whether the VWF-A2 domain modulates A1–DNA binding and its impact on platelet adhesion under flow.MethodsVWF-A1–DNA interactions were examined using atomic force microscopy, magnetic tweezers, ELISAs, and microfluidic flow assays. Both recombinant A1 and mammalian-expressed A1A2 constructs were employed to assess physiological relevance.ResultsVWF-A1 bound to DNA and induced concentration–dependent DNA compaction. DNA binding to VWF-A1 significantly impaired platelet adhesion under flow conditions. In contrast, the A2 domain inhibited VWF-A1–DNA binding, as shown using both isolated A2 and A1A2 constructs, and mitigated DNA’s inhibitory effect on A1-mediated platelet adhesion.ConclusionThese findings suggest that, within recombinant VWF domain constructs, VWF-A1 compacts DNA and that the A2 domain negatively regulates A1–DNA interactions while preserving A1-mediated platelet binding under flow. Although further validation in native multimeric VWF and physiologically relevant models is required, these results provide new insights into how the A2 domain differentially regulates VWF-A1 interactions with DNA and platelets.

Introduction

Extracellular cell-free DNA originates from various biological processes, including cell turnover, apoptosis, necrosis, NETosis, and other forms of cell death [1]. During infection, neutrophils release DNA, forming a web-like structure known as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which function to capture and eliminate pathogens [2]. NETs are composed of an extracellular DNA network scaffold decorated with over 30 bioactive cellular components derived from the nucleus, cytoplasm, and cell membrane, such as histones, serine proteases, myeloperoxidase, and others. However, NETs are a double-edged sword. On one hand, they immobilize invading bacteria through the DNA network, preventing their dissemination, while the embedded antimicrobial factors kill pathogens, thereby contributing to host defense [[3], [4], [5]]. On the other hand, NETs can promote vascular occlusion [[6], [7], [8]] by providing a scaffold for platelets, red blood cells, extracellular vesicles, and procoagulant molecules, leading to immunothrombosis. This process can result in tissue damage, microvascular thrombosis, and even organ failure [7,[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is an extracellular adhesive protein secreted by damaged endothelial cells or activated platelets. VWF is a mechanoresponsive molecule activated by shear forces in blood flow, recruiting platelets at sites of vascular injury and promoting thrombus formation during primary hemostasis [13,14]. Under shear stress, VWF undergoes a reversible conformational change from a globular to an elongated state, exposing the cryptic binding site at the A1 domain [15]. This alteration enables VWF to interact with glycoprotein Ibα (GPIbα) on the platelet surface, mediating platelet adhesion and aggregation [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Electrostatic interactions enhance this binding through the positively charged A1 and negatively charged GPIbα interface [14]. Notably, the VWF-A1 domain can also bind to double-stranded DNA through electrostatic interactions independent of the deoxynucleotide sequence [20]. However, the structural organization of the VWF-A1–DNA complex remains unclear. In addition, the VWF-A1 domain is auto-inhibited by multiple structural elements, including the N-terminal D'D3 domain [21], the adjacent flanking regions [22,23], and the C-terminal A2 domain [24,25]. While these elements are known to regulate A1 accessibility for platelet binding, their roles in modulating VWF-A1–DNA interactions remain largely unexplored.

When NETs enter the bloodstream, they interact with platelets, red blood cells, or various plasma proteins, such as VWF and fibrin, to adhere to the vascular wall. Colocalization of NETs–DNA with VWF has been observed in deep vein thrombi [12]. Conversely, another study reported minimal association between VWF and extracellular DNA or citrullinated histone in endotoxemia-induced thrombi [26]. Thus, the molecular details of VWF–DNA interactions remain poorly understood. Early hypotheses suggested that VWF binds to NETs primarily through histones, and this binding does not affect VWF–platelet interactions [27]. Grässle et al. [28] demonstrated that the VWF-A1 domain can bind to DNA in a shear force-dependent manner, with this interaction being inhibited by heparin. Sandoval-Pérez et al. [20] proposed that shear stress promotes double-stranded DNA to interact with 3 positively charged arginines on an H4 helix of the VWF-A1 domain, independent of its nucleotide sequence. Additionally, this study noted that the DNA–VWF interaction indirectly affects platelet recruitment. However, the precise mechanisms by which A1–DNA binding influences platelet recruitment under shear remain largely unresolved.

In this study, using recombinant VWF domain constructs, we investigated the impact of VWF-A1 binding on DNA organization and the regulatory role of the A2 domain in A1–DNA interaction. We further assessed A1-mediated platelet recruitment under flow in the presence of DNA and how this process is regulated by the A2 domain.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The plasmids encoding VWF-A1 (amino acids Gln1238-Asp1472) and VWF-A1A2 (amino acids Gln1238-Gly1672) were constructed in our laboratory. Recombinant human VWF-A2 (amino acids Asp1498-Val1665, Catalog # 2764-WF-050) was purchased from R&D Systems. Luria-Bertani broth culture medium was purchased from HKM (028320). Isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside was obtained from Biofroxx Company (IPTG, 1122GR025). HisTrap HF columns were purchased from GE Healthcare (GM Healthcare). Polyethylenimine transfection reagent was purchased from Polysciences (24765). The restriction endonucleases Hind III and Xho I were purchased from New England Biolabs (NEB). The QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit was purchased from QIAGEN.

Methods

Expression and purification of VWF-A1 and VWF-A1A2

VWF-A1 was expressed in Escherichia coli strain M15 harboring a His-tagged plasmid. Cells were cultured in Luria-Bertani medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and kanamycin (25 μg/mL). Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM Isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside at 37 °C for 4 hours. Cells were harvested and washed twice with phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), then lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The VWF-A1 protein was purified using an ÄKTA Pure chromatography system (Cytiva) with HisTrap HF columns. Purity was confirmed by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Figure 2A). Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, 23227).

The recombinant VWF-A1A2 protein was expressed in HEK-293F cells using polyethylenimine-mediated transfection. After 24 hours of culture, fed-batch culture medium (4% v/v) was added, and the cells were cultured for 5 days. Supernatants were collected and purified using HisTrap columns. Protein purity was verified by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Supplementary Figure 4).

Atomic force microscopy experiments

To examine VWF-A1-induced changes in DNA organization, atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging was performed using an MFP-3D system (Asylum Research). DNA fragments were obtained by digesting the VWF-A1A2A3 plasmid with Hind III (NEB, R0104V) and Xho I (NEB, R0146V), followed by gel extraction using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, 28704). Short DNA fragments (∼2 kb) were used to ensure compatibility with AFM scan size and enable visualization of local DNA compaction. DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). For A1–DNA complex preparation, the VWF-A1 domain (100 or 1000 nM) and DNA (50 μg/mL) were incubated at room temperature for 3 hours. Samples were diluted in 10× AFM buffer (CH3COONa: 250 mM; Mg (CH3COO)2·4H2O: 100 mM; HEPES-KOH buffer: 250 mM) and deposited onto freshly cleaved mica. The samples were then washed with pure water and dried under a stream of nitrogen gas. AFM images were captured at a scan size of 1 μm × 1 μm, a scan rate of 1 Hz, and a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels with tapping mode. Images were analyzed using Asylum Research software. The length and height of DNA, the number of VWF proteins bound to DNA, and the distance from the adhesion position to the closed end were measured (Supplementary Figure 1A, B). Baseline measurements for VWF-A1 and bare DNA were obtained (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2). A bright spot was defined as a binding site if its height equaled or exceeded the combined height of VWF-A1 and DNA (Supplementary Figure 1B). To characterize DNA compaction, spot height (vertical), area (lateral), and derived volume (height × area) were quantified as proxies for local protein accumulation.

ELISAs

For protein-coated assays, VWF-A1 or VWF-A1A2 (0-2000 nM) was coated onto 96-well plates overnight at 4 °C, followed by blocking with 2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1.5 hours. λ-DNA (40 μg/mL) was added and incubated for 4 hours at room temperature. After washing, bound DNA was stained with Sytox Green for 15 minutes, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a plate reader (Tecan Group Ltd). DNA binding was quantified via fluorescence staining rather than His-tag detection unless otherwise specified.

For DNA titration assays, VWF-A1 or VWF-A1A2 (2000 nM) was immobilized as described above, followed by incubation with varying concentrations of λ-DNA (0-40 μg/mL). Bound DNA was detected using the same Sytox Green staining procedure.

To assess the role of VWF-A2, the DNA-binding assays were performed using VWF-A2 following the same procedure as VWF-A1 or VWF-A1A2. For inhibition experiments, VWF-A2 (0-2000 nM) was preincubated with VWF-A1-coated wells for 1 hour prior to the addition of λ-DNA. After washing, bound DNA was detected following the same staining procedure.

All fluorescence signals were normalized to BSA-coated controls to account for inter-assay variability due to automatic gain adjustment of the plate reader.

Magnetic tweezers experiments

Magnetic tweezers experiments were performed using 5.6 kb DNA double-strand molecules (60% GC content), modified with biotin at one end and digoxigenin (dig) at the other. DNA was tethered between an antidig-coated glass slide and a streptavidin-coated superparamagnetic microbead. NdFeB magnets generated a constant tensile force on the anchored molecule, adjusted by changing the magnet position. Force magnitude was calculated from bead Brownian motion [29]. The bead-to-surface distance reflected DNA extension. Dynabeads M-270 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2.8 μm diameter) were used to apply forces from 0 to 40 pN. DNA elongation was tracked in real time by a microscope with nanometer precision. Different concentrations of the VWF-A1 (10, 100, and 500 nM), VWF-A1A2 (10, 100, and 500 nM), and VWF-A2 (10, 100, and 500 nM) were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH = 7.5). Flow channels were sealed with Vaseline to prevent evaporation. Force-extension curves were fitted to the extensible worm-like chain model [30]: L=Lc[1−(kBT/FP)12/2+F/K], where L is the extension length, F is the applied force, Lc is the contour length, kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the absolute temperature, P is the persistence length, and K is the stretch modulus. At least 50 to 130 independent DNA tethers were analyzed for each condition.

Human platelet isolation

Human platelets were isolated as previously described [31]. Briefly, whole blood from healthy volunteers was collected into sodium citrate–containing tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 minutes at room temperature to obtain platelet-rich plasma, which was further supplemented with 0.2 μg/mL prostacyclin to prevent platelet activation and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS containing 5% platelet-poor plasma to achieve a final concentration of 4 × 107 cells/mL and rested for 10 minutes prior to experiments. It is worth noting that the concentration of VWF in 5% platelet-poor plasma is expected to be in the low nanomolar range (∼1-2 nM). Therefore, the influence of residual VWF is expected to be minimal.

Flow assay

Flow assays were performed using the Ibidi flow chamber system. Each channel was coated overnight at 4 °C with 30 μL of PBS containing 1% (w/v) BSA, 3000 nM VWF-A1, 3000 nM VWF-A2, 3000 nM VWF-A1A2, or 15 μg/mL λ-DNA. After washing 3 times with PBS, channels were blocked with 2% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. Platelets (4 × 107 cells/mL) labeled with 1 μM CFDA-SE (Beyotime Biotechnology), λ-DNA (15 μg/mL) stained with Sytox Green (1:100,000 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or platelet-λ-DNA mixtures were perfused into the chamber and imaged under static or flow conditions (0.4, 4, and 40 dyne/cm2) for 2 minutes. Images were acquired at 20 fps and analyzed using Nikon NIS-Elements and ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda). Platelet adhesion was analyzed from time-lapse recordings acquired at 20 fps over 2 minutes. A platelet was considered stably adhered if it remained adherent to the surface for at least 5 seconds without detachment. Transient tethering or rolling events were excluded from the analysis. Platelet adhesion was expressed as the number of adherent platelets per field of view. Time-integrated image stacks were generated for visualization of cumulative adhesion events. DNA adhesion was quantified from the integrated fluorescence intensity of the image stacks.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least 3 independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests, 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or 2-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests, as appropriate. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ∗ or $, P < .05; ∗∗ or $$, P < .01; ∗∗∗ or $$$, P < .001; and ∗∗∗∗ or $$$$, P < .0001; ns, not significant. Asterisks (∗) denote comparisons vs control (eg, BSA), whereas dollar signs ($) indicate within-group comparisons.

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the ethical committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval Number: KY2023-017-02). The participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

In addition, compact DNA-associated structures were occasionally observed at higher VWF-A1 concentrations (Figure 1C). To examine whether these compact appearances resulted from VWF-A1 self-assembly, VWF-A1 alone was imaged at low (10 nM) and high (100 nM) concentrations (Supplementary Figure 2B–E). VWF-A1 compacts DNA in a concentration-dependent manner

To further characterize the interaction between VWF-A1 and DNA, we analyzed DNA length changes and the distribution of VWF-A1-associated binding sites along DNA molecules. In the absence of VWF-A1, DNA displayed an average length of 590.9 ± 5.7 nm with a narrow unimodal distribution and only rare bright spots (4/255 molecules, Figure 2A). Incubation with 10 nM VWF-A1 shortened DNA to 535.5 ± 10.5 nm, accompanied by a broader distribution with some shorter fragments <400 nm (Figure 2C). Under this condition, 30.2% of DNA molecules contained 1 (25.5%) or 2 (4.7%) VWF-A1 binding sites (Figure 2D), which were randomly distributed along the DNA (Figure 2E). At 100 nM VWF-A1, DNA shortening became more pronounced, resulting in an apparent bimodal distribution (Figure 2F). Notably, 64.3% of DNA molecules contained VWF-A1 binding spots, with up to 10 binding sites observed per DNA molecule (Figure 2G). These sites remained randomly distributed (Figure 2H). VWF-A2 inhibits the binding of VWF-A1 to DNA

The function of VWF-A1 is known to be autoinhibited by the adjacent A2 domain [32], but its role in DNA binding remains unclear. Using ELISA, we found that VWF-A1 bound to λ-DNA in a concentration-dependent fashion (Figure 5A, B). In contrast, the isolated VWF-A2 domain showed no detectable DNA binding up to 2000 nM (Figure 5C). To investigate whether A2 modulates A1–DNA binding, immobilized VWF-A1 (2000 nM) was preincubated with various concentrations of VWF-A2 prior to DNA addition. DNA binding decreased progressively with increasing A2 concentrations (Figure 5D), demonstrating that A2 inhibits VWF-A1–DNA binding. DNA inhibits VWF-A1-mediated platelets adhesion under flow

The VWF-A1 domain mediates platelet adhesion by binding GPIbα. Activated VWF-A1 can also directly bind NET–DNA and anchor NETs to vessel walls [28]. To assess the functional consequence of A1–DNA interaction under flow, we quantified DNA or platelet adhesion to VWF-A1-functionalized surfaces at shear stresses of 0.4, 4, and 40 dyne/cm2 (Figure 7A–D). Both DNA and platelets adhered specifically to VWF-A1 surfaces, as indicated by significantly high DNA levels (Figure 7A, B) and number of platelets (Figure 7C, D) compared with BSA (1%) controls. Adhesion of DNA or platelets to VWF-A1 was enhanced by shear from 0.4 to 4 dyne/cm2 but then reduced from 4 to 40 dyne/cm2. When DNA and platelets were premixed, DNA adhesion was comparable with the DNA-only perfusion condition (Supplementary Figure 7), whereas platelet adhesion was markedly reduced (Figure 7E, F, Supplementary Figure 6). Platelets did not adhere to DNA-coated surfaces under flow (Supplementary Figure 5A, B), indicating that the reduction is not due to DNA-mediated platelet capture. (E) Representative time-integrated images of firmly adhered DNA (green) and platelets (red) after perfusion of a DNA–platelet mixture (15 μg/mL DNA; 4 × 107 cells/mL platelets) for 2 minutes at the indicated shear stresses. (F) Quantification of firmly adhered platelets in the presence of DNA. Scale bar: 50 μm. Data represent the mean ± SEM from 3 independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined by 2-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Significance levels: ∗, P < .05; ∗∗, P < .01; ∗∗∗, P < .001; ∗∗∗∗, P < .0001.

We next assessed whether VWF-A2 modulates VWF-A1 binding to DNA and, consequently, to platelets under flow. VWF-A2 alone did not support adhesion of either DNA or platelets (Supplementary Figure 5C–E). Preincubation of VWF-A1 with VWF-A2 significantly reduced both DNA and platelet adhesion across all shear conditions (Figure 7A–D). VWF-A1A2-coated surfaces showed minimal DNA binding while supporting platelet adhesion at levels comparable to VWF-A1 pretreated with VWF-A2. Notably, when DNA–platelet mixtures were perfused, platelet adhesion to VWF-A1-coated surfaces pretreated with VWF-A2 or to VWF-A1A2-coated surfaces remained comparable or increased in the presence of DNA (Figure 7E–F). Collectively, these results indicate that VWF-A2 inhibits A1–DNA interaction and mitigates the inhibitory effect of DNA on A1-mediated platelet adhesion under flow.

Discussion

This study investigated the interaction between VWF-A1 and DNA using complementary biophysical approaches and evaluated VWF-A1–dependent DNA association and compaction under defined experimental conditions. We found that VWF-A1 associated with DNA in a concentration-dependent manner and induced DNA compaction. Additionally, we evaluated whether VWF-A2 influences VWF-A1–DNA binding using ELISA and magnetic tweezers assays. The results consistently showed that VWF-A2 inhibits VWF-A1 binding to DNA. Finally, platelet adhesion assays performed under flow revealed that DNA competes with platelets for binding to VWF-A1, whereas the blocking effect of VWF-A2 on A1–DNA interaction preserves A1-mediated platelet adhesion.

Previous studies have shown that VWF regulates NETosis under ischemic conditions. VWF-deficient mice exhibit a marked reduction of NET formation 24 hours after ischemic stroke [9]. In a 24-hour myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (MI/R) mouse model, increased plasma nucleosomes, significant neutrophil infiltration, and the presence of citrullinated histone H3 at the site of injury were observed [33]. Although VWF-A1 has been reported to bind to DNA in a sequence- and length-independent manner [20], the consequences of VWF-A1–DNA interactions on DNA organization remain incompletely characterized. In the present study, we observed a concentration-dependent reduction in apparent DNA length with increasing VWF-A1 concentrations. At high VWF-A1 concentrations, the height, area, and volume of VWF-A1 spots exhibited strong negative correlations with DNA length. Complementary magnetic tweezers experiments support that VWF-A1 induces DNA compaction in a concentration-dependent manner. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly demonstrate that VWF-A1 compacts DNA. Notably, the extent of compaction is substantially weaker than that induced by canonical DNA-condensing proteins such as histone and protamine [[34], [35], [36]], which generate highly ordered wrapping or looping structures. This suggests that VWF-A1-induced DNA compaction likely arises from multivalent binding, bridging, or local bending rather than defined structured wrapping. Such a mechanism is consistent with the intrinsically adhesive and multivalent nature of VWF and may facilitate the local organization of extracellular DNA in thromboinflammatory environments. Notably, although compact structures indicative of local protein accumulation were occasionally observed at higher concentrations, such events were relatively infrequent and therefore did not significantly influence the overall distribution of spot size parameters.

The functional consequences of A1–DNA interaction remain an area of active debate. Grässle et al. [28] reported that activated VWF-A1 directly binds to NET–DNA, reducing platelet adhesion. In contrast, Sandoval-Pérez et al. [20] reported that the DNA-binding interface of A1 does not overlap with the platelet GPIbα-binding site and that DNA binding to full-length VWF increases under high shear (4000 s−1). In our flow assays, the presence of DNA markedly reduced platelet adhesion to VWF-A1 functionalized surfaces. Importantly, our data do not distinguish between direct competition at overlapping binding sites and indirect effects such as steric hindrance or altered surface accessibility. Given that prior structural studies indicate spatial separation of DNA- and GPIbα-binding interfaces, our results are more consistent with an indirect mechanism, such as steric interference or DNA-induced conformational constraints on A1.

Interestingly, we observed reduced DNA adhered to VWF-A1 functionalized surfaces at 40 dyne/cm2, which differs from previous studies using full-length VWF [20]. This discrepancy may be attributed to different VWF proteins used in the 2 studies. In full-length VWF, A1 accessibility is tightly regulated by adjacent domains and shear-induced unfolding, whereas the isolated A1 domain used here lacks these regulatory constraints. Under high shear, reduced contact duration between DNA and VWF-A1 may further limit binding. These observations highlight the importance of domain context and mechanical regulation in VWF function.

A key finding of this study is that the VWF-A2 domain inhibits A1–DNA interaction. Using both isolated A2 and a linked A1A2 construct, we consistently observed reduced DNA binding and diminished DNA-induced effects in the presence of A2. Notably, this inhibition preserved VWF-A1-mediated platelet adhesion in the presence of DNA. This interesting finding may help explain the lack of VWF-NET colocalization observed in some thrombi. VWF is considered a hub molecule, mediating interactions across hemostasis and inflammation [37], and tight regulation of A1 domain conformation is critical to prevent excessive activation. Because both VWF and NETs are highly prothrombotic, inhibition of A1–DNA interaction by A2 may represent a protective mechanism to limit pathologic thrombus formation. Unexpectedly, platelet adhesion to VWF-A1-coated surfaces pretreated with VWF-A2 increased in the presence of DNA at 0.4 and 4.0 dyne/cm2. However, this effect was absent on VWF-A1A2-coated surfaces under all flow conditions. One possible explanation is that long DNA molecules mechanically perturb or partially displace weakly associated A2 molecules from the surface, thereby increasing the accessibility of A1 for platelet binding. However, further experiments will be required to address this possibility.

At the sites of vascular injury, endothelial cells release ultralarge VWF, which binds to platelet GPIbα [38,39]. Under shear, the A2 domain unfolds, exposing its ADAMTS-13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motifs, member 13) cleavage site, allowing ultralarge VWF to be enzymatically cleaved into smaller, less active forms [38,39]. Dysregulation of the VWF–ADAMTS-13 axis is associated with enhanced NET formation and inflammation. Martens et al. [40] showed that Staphylococcus aureus infection promotes NET formation alongside elevated VWF and reduced ADAMTS-13 activity. Mang et al. [41] demonstrated that VWF and ADAMTS-13 regulate NET formation mediated by the neutrophil choline transporter SLC44A2 and that NETs induce cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction leading to heart failure; sacubitril/valsartan disrupts this VWF–SLC44A2–NET axis. Future research should investigate how ADAMTS-13 regulates A1–DNA binding in vitro and in vivo and how posttranslational modifications or conformational states of the VWF-A2 domain influence its regulation of VWF-A1–DNA interactions. These studies may yield insights into the broader roles of VWF in NET formation and thrombosis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the use of isolated domains and recombinant constructs represents a reductionist system that does not fully recapitulate the structural and mechanical complexity of full-length VWF multimers. Although the VWF-A1A2 construct partially restores native domain organization and supports our conclusion, shear-dependent conformational changes in full-length VWF are likely to further regulate A1 accessibility. Second, the constructs used here include the N-terminal flanking region of A1, which has been shown to modulate GPIbα binding [[42], [43], [44]] and may influence the interactions observed. Future studies using full-length, mammalian-expressed VWF multimers will be necessary to validate these findings under physiological conditions. Third, AFM sample preparation, including Mg2+-assisted adsorption, washing, and imaging under dry conditions, may affect the morphology of VWF-A1–DNA complexes and the measured AFM heights. Therefore, these AFM observations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Importantly, the DNA-compacting activity of VWF-A1 was independently supported by magnetic tweezers experiments performed entirely in solution.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into the interaction between VWF-A1 and extracellular DNA and demonstrates that VWF-A1 can induce modest DNA compaction in a concentration-dependent manner. Importantly, the VWF-A2 domain acts as a regulatory module that suppresses A1–DNA binding while preserving platelet adhesion under flow. These findings suggest that VWF domains may coordinate interactions with DNA and platelets during immunothrombosis. Further investigation of these interactions in physiologically relevant systems may reveal new potential therapeutic strategies targeting VWF-mediated immunothrombosis.
