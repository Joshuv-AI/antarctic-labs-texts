Undetected falls among older adults attending medical clinics in four tertiary care centres in Sri Lanka; the need of a comprehensive geriatric assessment
Authors: Warsha De Zoysa, Nirmala Rathnayake, Dhammika Palangasinghe, Shehan Silva, Priyamali Jayasekera, Chamila Mettananda, Thilina Abeygunasekara, Sarath Lekamwasam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFalls take a high priority among the prevalent medical conditions in old age. Despite this, a history of falls or the risk of future falls is not routinely assessed or properly managed in medical clinics in Sri Lanka. This study was done to evaluate the prevalence and factors associated with falls and recurrent falls among older adults attending medical clinics in four selected tertiary care centres in the country.MethodsA cross-sectional study was carried out at four centres (Teaching Hospital Karapitiya, Colombo South Teaching Hospital, Colombo North Teaching Hospital and University Hospital-Kotelawala Defence University) with 704 older adults, aged 65 years and above, attending medical clinics for more than six consecutive months. Information related to falls and possible associated factors (socio-demographic, behavioural, environmental and biological) were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire.ResultsThe Mean (SD) age of the participants was 72.5(5.5) years and 58.7% were females. Of the 704 total sample, 220 (31.3%, 95% CI 28–35%) participants experienced at least one fall after the age of 65, and 12.8% (95% CI 10–15%) (n = 90) experienced recurrent falls (two or more falls within the last 12 months). Falls were associated with gender, level of education, marital status, and physical dependence (p < 0.01). For those who had at least one fall, multiple logistic regression (MLR) revealed being single (p = 0.03, OR = 2.12, 95% CI; 1.052–4.304), being widowed/divorced/separated (p = 0.03, OR = 1.47, 95% CI; 1.039–2.093) compared to living with a spouse, presence of moderate (p = 0.007, OR = 1.72, 95% CI; 1.160–2.577) and severe (p = 0.001, OR = 2.98, 95% CI; 1.563–5.688) physical dependency compared to mild physical dependency as risk factors for falls. Having secondary education (p = 0.01, OR = 0.55, 0.350–0.876) was a protective factor for falls. For those with recurrent falls, MLR showed moderate physical dependency (p = 0.001, OR = 2.34, 95% CI; 1.442–3.821) compared to slight physical dependency as a risk factor.ConclusionsApproximately one-third of the older adults attending medical clinics had experienced at least a single fall, and one-eighth have had recurrent falls, which were mostly unrecorded and not clinically assessed. Physical dependency was the major contributing factor to falls and recurrent falls. Falls assessment should be included in the routine clinical assessment of older adults attending outdoor medical clinics. Health professionals should be educated to detect and assess those at risk of falling and take appropriate measures to prevent or minimize falls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05477-1.

Introduction

Falls and fall-related injuries are common, particularly among older adults and those with physical disabilities. Around one-third of older people living in the community fall at least once a year, and approximately one-sixth recurrently [1, 2]. A multitude of factors such as medication, co-morbidity, decreased mobility, female gender and advanced age are linked with an increased risk of falls. A fall is defined as “in-advertently coming to rest on the ground, floor or other lower level, excluding intentional change in position to rest in furniture, wall or other objects during last 12-months period, occurring of two or more falls within a year is considered as recurrent falls [1, 3]. Recurrent fallers have a distinct risk factor profile when compared with older adults with a single fall and recurrent fallers are more likely to sustain serious injuries [4].

Approximately a third of older fallers sustain fall-related injuries, which require medical treatment [2]. Falls result in fractures and they are seen mainly in the hip, spine, arms, ankles, and legs [5]. The Public Health Agency of Canada showed that among falls-related hospitalizations of older adults, 38% of admissions are due to hip fractures and 39% present with other fracture types [6]. Following an injurious fall, a significant proportion of older adults are unable to return to their pre-fall activities. Apart from pain and disability associated with injuries, fear of falling, anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, loss of independence and admission to residential care also contribute to the adverse clinical outcomes [7]. Furthermore, falls in older people can cause disabilities, resulting increasing burden on their families, the health sector, and the economy of the country [6].

Falls are associated with high and increasing demand for health and social care resources. Watson et al. reported that older adults with falls-related injuries, particularly hip and other fractures, consume a disproportionate share of hospital resources compared to those who sustain injuries due to other causes, such as road trauma [7]. In the United States, there are 2.7 million fall-related emergency department visits and 1.1 million in-patient visits per year, with a total annual average health cost of $22.9 billion due to falls in older adults [8]. In the Netherlands, falls-related injuries in the older population account for €675.4 million annually, with fractures being the main contributor to this healthcare cost [2]. The national healthcare cost for falls among older adults is $66.1 million in Australia while in Italy, the cost of hospitalization due to domestic falls is €395 million per year [9]. According to the International Osteoporosis Foundation, there are 4.3 million fragility fractures in older adults, with healthcare costs exceeding €56 billion annually in Europe [10].

The population of older adults has shown a rapid expansion worldwide, especially in Asia, during the recent past. According to the World Health Organization, by 2030, 1 in 6 people in the world will be aged 60 years or over. It is predicted that the proportion of older adults, aged 60 years or more, will increase from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion. The world’s population of people aged 60 and over will double (2.1 billion) by 2050 [11]. The number of people aged 80 years or more is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050. By 2050, 80% of the older adults will live in low- and middle-income countries [11]. Asia makes up 60% of the world’s population and is ageing faster than any other region. The proportion of people aged 60 years or more in South-East Asia was 9.8% in 2017 and it is expected to rise to 13.7% by 2030 and 20.3% by 2050 [12]. In Sri Lanka, the population over the age of 65 years was 9.67% in 2018 and it is expected to triple in 2050 [13].

Sri Lankan studies on falls and related factors are limited and a hospital-based study of older adults in the Colombo district found 23% of subjects to experience a fall in a year [14]. Another community-based study reported a fall incidence of 492 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 448–536) which is higher than the WHO reported 28-35% fall incidence, worldwide [5]. The same analysis found that the elimination of house risk level was effective in preventing 40% of falls among study subjects. Furthermore, a community-based study from Southern Sri Lanka revealed a 34.3% prevalence of falls and 9.6% of recurrent falls among community-dwelling older adults [15].

Older adults with chronic diseases have a higher tendency for falls and this is partly explained by prevalent diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease and heart failure and multiple medications used for such conditions [16, 17]. Among medications that increase the risk of falls (falls-increasing drugs: FRIDs), anticholinergic medications and poly-pharmacy are particularly associated with the increased risk of falls [18]. Assessing the risk of falls and steps to minimize the risk should be an integral part of the management of older patients with multiple comorbidities [4]. Therefore, it is important to know the prevalence of recurrent falls among older adults with medical diseases since falls and medical diseases are related.

Geriatrics is not a well-established field at present in Sri Lanka. As a result, older adults are managed in busy general medical clinics, manned by medical officers who are not well-trained to handle age-related disorders. Furthermore, in Sri Lanka, another key challenge in the proper assessment of common geriatric conditions such as falls, balance issues, and functional decline is the lack of specialized professionals like physiotherapists and occupational therapists in clinic settings. Therefore, falls and other age-related medical problems are likely to remain undetected. The identification of falls, especially recurrent falls and factors that are associated with them among ambulatory clinic patients are imperative both in preventing further falls among them and highlighting the need to educate the health staff on assessing falls in older adults regularly. This study assessed the prevalence of falls, including recurrent falls and associated factors among the older people attending medical clinics of four main tertiary healthcare institutions in Sri Lanka.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and sample

A multi-centre cross-sectional survey was conducted in medical clinics at four tertiary care hospitals situated in two districts in Sri Lanka. The four centres were Teaching Hospital Karapitiya, Galle (THK), Colombo South Teaching Hospital (CSTH), Colombo North Teaching Hospital (CNTH) and Teaching Hospital- Kotelawala Defence University Medical Unit (TH-KDU).

Older adults, aged 65 years or more, attending medical clinics of selected four tertiary care centres for more than 6 months were included in the study. To retain the external validity of data, only those who did not consent to participate were excluded. When patients admitted that they had recall limitations, collateral information was collected from caregivers who accompanied those patients. This was done in nearly 90% of patients, especially those with physical and mental function impairment.

The minimum required sample size was calculated using n = p (1-p) × z2/d2 equation [19], with 34.3% of falls prevalence among community-dwelling older people in Galle District Sri Lanka [15]. However, we collected data from 720 older people from all four sites while 16 were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete questionnaires. Only 704 were taken for the analysis. Every consecutive older adult attending the medical clinics was recruited for the study from January – March 2023.

Data collection and tools

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire that included questions on age, gender, level of education and income status.

A detailed analysis of falls during the previous 12 months was done. Participants were identified as fallers if they had at least a single fall during the previous 12-month period [20]. Recurrent fallers were identified if they had two or more falls during the previous 112-month period [21].

Physical dependency was assessed using the Barthel index (BI) [22], validated in the local language earlier [23]. The BI measures the degree of assistance required for 10 selected personal activities; feeding, personal toileting, bathing, dressing and undressing, getting on and off a toilet, controlling bladder, controlling bowel, moving from wheelchair to bed and returning, walking on level surface (or propelling a wheelchair if unable to walk) and ascending and descending stairs and the total score ranges from 0 (total physical dependency) to 100 (physically independent) [22].

Cognitive impairment was evaluated using the Sinhala version mini-cognitive test (Mini-Cog TM) which is only a content-validated version [24]. It assesses cognitive domains including cognitive function, memory, language, comprehension, visual motor skills and executive functions and further provides a visible record of both normal and impaired performance. The total score of Mini-Cog TM ranges from 0 to 5. A score of 0–2 indicates a higher likelihood of cognitive impairment while a score ≥ 3 indicates a lower likelihood of cognitive impairment [24].

Medical records were viewed, and current comorbidities and medications were identified. Further, the presence of ≥ 2 diseases was considered as multi-morbidity [25] while the use of ≥ 6 medications was considered as poly-pharmacy [26]. The use of assistive devices for walking, use of aids for vision and hearing, use of animal protein, sleeping pattern and consumption of alcohol were also inquired. The materials used for the floor and the presence of steps within the house were also recorded.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics: means (SD) or frequency (%), were used to describe the data. The prevalence of falls, recurrent falls and related injuries were calculated as frequency (%). The prevalence of selected risk factors was compared with the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test between fallers and non-fallers as well as between recurrent fallers and non-recurrent fallers. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed with unadjusted (crude) OR at 95% CI to confirm the factors that were associated with falls/recurrent falls in the Chi-square test. Multiple logistic regression (backward-conditional) was applied for the variables that were significant in the Univariate analysis for retaining only the most significant associated risk factors for falls/recurrent falls and presented with adjusted OR and 95% CI.

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package of Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0. P value < 0.05 was regarded as acceptable.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Ruhuna, Sri Lanka (Reference number; 2021.P.075). Before the participation, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. From the participants, those with cognitive impairment, the informed consent was obtained from a legal guardian who was available with the patient at the time of data collection. All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Basic characteristics of the participants

Of the 704 total sample, the majority of the participants were young older adults (65–75 years) (58%), females (58.8%), married (58.8%), unemployed (74.9%) and with monthly income below 30 USD (60.8%). Supplementary Table 1 demonstrates the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants in detail.

Seventy-seven (77%) had multi-morbidity with the majority having hypertension (74.1%), while 54.7% and 53.6% had dyslipidaemia and diabetes mellitus, respectively. While 31% were affected with ischaemic heart disease, 13.9% had osteoarthritis. Furthermore, 2.6% had chronic kidney disease and 1.6% had a history of malignancy.

Medications used by the participants included statins (68%), losartan (61.2%), metformin (42.5%), aspirin (36.5%), gliclazide (20%), clopidogrel (19.2%), hydrochlorothiazide (13.6%) and captopril (7%). The prevalence of polypharmacy (> 6 medications) was 12.6%. Participants who had undergone cataract surgery comprised 63.8% while 1.1% had sought treatment for hearing impairment. Based on the Barthel index, only 6.3% had severe dependency while none had total physical dependency. On the Mini-Cog assessment, 27% had a higher likelihood of cognitive impairment. Characteristics of falls

Among the study subjects, 43.5%% (CI 39–47%) (n = 306) had one or more falls after the age of 65 years and 31.3% (95% CI 28–35%) (n = 220) had experienced one or more falls during the previous 12 months. While 18.5% (n = 130) had only a single fall during the previous 12 months, 12.8% (95% CI 10–15%) (n = 90) had recurrent falls during the same period. Furthermore, the majority of fallers had injuries (75.9%, CI 70–81%, n = 167).

Discussion

This multi-centre study involving 704 older adults with comorbidities attending medical outpatient clinics revealed the prevalence of falls, either single or recurrent, and associated factors. One in three older adults in this study group (31.3%, n = 220) experienced a fall. While 18.5% (n = 130) had a single fall, 12.8% (n = 90) reported recurrent falls. Female gender, living single (unmarried, widowed, divorced, or separated), and moderate to severe physical dependency were found to be associated with an increased risk of falls. Among those risk factors, only moderate physical dependency was found to be associated with recurrent falls. Secondary education as well as living with the spouse were found to be protective factors for falls.

According to Ha V-AT et al. in Vietnam, the prevalence of falls among geriatric outpatients during the previous 12 months was 23.7% (single fall 17.9% and multiple falls 5.8%) [27]. Similar observations were made in an Indian study in 2019 [28]. Similar to our findings, IH Susilowati et al. also observed that the prevalence of falls among Indonesian older adults over 60 years was 29% [29]. According to the WHO Global Report on Fall Prevention in Older Age (2008), approximately 28–35% of people aged 65 and over fall each year, increasing to 32–42% for those over 70 years of age [1]. Although the prevalence of falls that were observed in this study is comparable with previous literature, the prevalence of recurrent falls was twice as high as that of the Vietnam study in 2021 [27]. However, the figure we report is consistent with studies where one in every six older adults was found to have recurrent falls [1, 2, 30].

Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that female gender, physical dependency, and multi-morbidity increase the risk of falls [15, 16, 31–33]. The authors discovered that living with the spouse and having higher levels of education were protective factors. These factors may have either direct or indirect links with falls. Higher levels of education may be associated with better cognitive functions, higher income, better nutrition, and physical activity, which may in turn lead to a lower risk of falls. Having a spouse may also lead to improved nutrition and increased physical and outdoor activities. In support of our findings, Engin et al. also found that living with a spouse reduced the risk of falls [34].

In general, studies have identified polypharmacy as an independent risk factor for falls, but this study failed to find such an association. This may be partially linked to the comparatively lower rates of sedative medication prescriptions, which are often associated with falls among older adults. Although sleep disturbances are common, impacting nearly 50% of the older population, sedative or hypnotic medications, known to elevate the risk of falls, seem to be prescribed less often in Sri Lanka. Likewise, the administration of potent pain relief medications, like narcotics, which can adversely affect balance and cognitive function, is also relatively rare. These elements may provide insight into the noted lack of correlation between polypharmacy and falls. A systematic review investigating the link between polypharmacy and health outcomes among older adults has revealed inconsistent data [35]. Furthermore, Zia et al. demonstrate that the intake of two or more FRIDs, as opposed to poly-pharmacy, correlates with falls among older adults [36]. Furthermore, the current study did not reveal a significant link between cognitive impairment and fall risk [37] among our study subjects. This observation is consistent with Roh HW et al. who also found no association between cognitive impairment and the risk of falls in older adults [38].

The medico-social problems of falls in older adults extend beyond the high incidence. The high susceptibility for both fatal and non-fatal injuries makes even a relatively minor fall particularly hazardous. Previous studies as well as the current study support the high prevalence of injuries among older fallers [1, 4, 6]. About one-eighth of subjects in our study reported either fracture or dislocation after falling. Falls in older adults result in fractures, mainly in the hip, spine, arms, ankles, and legs [1, 6]. Post-fall anxiety syndrome is a complication where an individual reduces activity due to an exaggerated fear of falling. This, in turn, contributes to deconditioning, weakness, abnormal gait, and anxiety that lead to an increased risk of falls [39, 40]. Regardless of co-morbidities and multiple risk factors, falls and their harmful effects are usually preventable. Adequate medical evaluation and treatment for underlying medical risk factors, such as unstable gait and disabling medical conditions, could prevent many medically related falls.

With an ageing population in Sri Lanka, a comprehensive geriatric assessment, including an assessment of fall risk, is required, especially for those with multi-morbidity, physical disability, and previous falls. Most of the falls we recorded were unrecognized and not documented. The majority of patients had not undergone an assessment of previous falls or the risk of future falls. This data indicates that an assessment of falls and risk factors should be an integral part of the routine clinical evaluation of these patients. Considering the rapid turnover of patients and the limitation of both physical and human resources in medical outpatient clinics, it is logical to educate the health staff and provide rapid screening tools to detect high-fall-risk patients. They should be referred for specialized care where multimode interventions should be introduced. The effectiveness of these preventive strategies has been documented in many studies [41–43]. Many programs (e.g., assessment, risk factor reduction, exercise, environmental modification, and education) have been tested, and recent meta-analyses have shown the effectiveness of several methods [43]. Among them, the reduction of fall risk through regular exercises to improve muscle strength, gait, and balance has shown promising outcomes.

Strength and limitations

This is a multi-centre study conducted in two provinces involving a relatively large number of older adults, and it should be considered the strength of the study. The demographic characteristics and lifestyle characteristics of the community dwellers in these two provinces roughly represent those of the entire country. These factors allow us to generalize our findings to the larger population in the country. There are several noteworthy limitations to our study. Subjects had to recall the information on falls, and we made sure that a caregiver was present at the time of the interview. This was particularly done for those with the impairment of mental functions. The possibility of recall bias and recall limitations, however, cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the authors could not validate the mini-cognitive test for our population. Older individuals with severe morbidity who are dependent frequently struggle to reach the outpatient clinic because of their disabilities, resulting in their consistent exclusion from the study. This aspect should also be regarded as a limitation of clinic-based study.

Conclusions

Among older adults attending medical clinics in selected four hospitals in Sri Lanka, nearly one in three reported a fall after the age of 65 years. Furthermore, one in eight experienced recurrent falls. Most of these falls were unnoticed by healthcare teams and these patients were not properly assessed concerning falls. This highlights the gaps in current care programs for older adults in busy medical clinics in the country. This study identified physical dependency as the strongest risk factor for both single and recurrent falls. It is essential to incorporate routine fall assessment in geriatric care protocols to provide holistic care for older adults. Furthermore, healthcare professionals should be trained to identify and manage falls effectively.
