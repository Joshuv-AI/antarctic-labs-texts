Prediction of prolonged mechanical ventilation in the intensive care unit via machine learning: a COVID-19 perspective
Authors: Marianna Weaver, Dylan A Goodin, Hunter A Miller, Dipan Karmali, Apurv A Agarwal, Hermann B Frieboes, Sally A Suliman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly recognition of risk factors for prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV) could allow for early clinical interventions, prevention of secondary complications such as nosocomial infections, and effective triage of hospital resources. This study tested the hypothesis that an ensemble machine learning (ML) analysis of clinical data at time of intubation could identify patients at risk of PMV, using a COVID-19 dataset to classify patients into PMV (> 14 days) and non-PMV (≤ 14 days) groups. While several factors are known to cause PMV, including acid-base, weakness, and delirium, lesser-utilized but routinely measured parameters such as platelet count, glucose levels and fevers may also be relevant. Patient data from a single University Hospital were analyzed via the ML workflow to predict patients at risk of PMV and identify key clinical markers. Model performance was evaluated on a chronologically distinct cohort. The ML workflow identified patients at risk of PMV with AUROCTRAIN=0.960 (F1TRAIN = 0.935) and AUROCTEST=0.804 (F1TEST = 0.800). Top key features for classification included glucose, platelet count, temperature, LVEF, bicarbonate (arterial blood gas), and BMI. Data analysis at intubation time via the proposed workflow offers the potential to accurately predict patients at risk of PMV, with the goal to improve patient management and triage of hospital resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81980-0.Keywords: Mechanical intubation, Predictive modeling, Machine learning, COVID-19Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases

Introduction

The proportion of ICU patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) has varied significantly across COVID-19 studies, ranging from 29 to 70% 1–4. Patients who require mechanical ventilation are also at risk for prolonged ICU and hospital stays5. This places patients at risk for adverse ventilator-associated events such as nosocomial infections and poor outcomes, and limits healthcare ICU bed utilization6. Prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV) has been associated with increased critical care interventions and post-discharge care relative to shorter-term intubation for COVID-19 patients5. Furthermore, patients with ICU stay ≥ 7 days often develop post-intensive care syndrome7, suggesting that PMV may be particularly vulnerable to longer-term complications. Consequently, intubated patients are at risk for poor clinical outcomes that contribute to higher hospital load. Additionally, ICU daily care cost for IMV increases by ~ 26% compared to non-IMV8. Thus, identifying patients at risk of PMV would improve patient outcomes and reduce hospital load.

Previous work has applied machine learning (ML) to predict outcomes for COVID-19 patients in the ICU (e.g., see recent studies9,10). Although the risk of intubation has been evaluated, patients at risk of PMV have not been specifically identified. For example, a methodology for selecting the best features and developing ML-based models to predict intubation risk of hospitalized COVID-19 patients achieved area under receiver operator characteristic curve (AUROC) of AUROCTEST=0.82 in the test (hold-out) set using laboratory and vitals data11. Interestingly, performance of a ML model trained on non-COVID-19 patients was improved using COVID-19 adaptation training, increasing from AUROC = 0.80 to 0.97 12, suggesting that COVID-19 IMV patients may possess specific intubation risk characteristics.

Efforts to predict PMV at time of intubation are ongoing. Using a definition of > 21 days, PMV was predicted in 20 COVID-19 patients using lung ultrasound (LUS), attaining an AUROC = 0.98 from LUS score data from day 7 post-intubation13. The low sample size precluded using a test set to confirm findings and LUS data was obtained after intubation had occurred. Akin to12, identification of a PMV threshold was recently evaluated by validating a predictive model using a variety of thresholds definitions for PMV, 3, 5, 7, 10, 14, and 23 days, in an ICU patient cohort spanning from 2016 to 2022 14. In doing so, a threshold of 14 days yielded the highest ML model performance (accuracy = 85.14%, F1 = 84.68%), suggesting that this threshold may accurately distinguish PMV-specific patient characteristics. Although including COVID-19 patients in14, it is unknown if the predictive potential would vary when considering only COVID-19 patients. Evaluating only COVID-19 patients, the study in15 found a median IMV duration of 10 days, which contrasts with the recommendation from 2005 defining PMV as ≥ 21 days16, suggesting that using a lower PMV threshold for COVID-19 such as ≥ 14 days may improve clinical relevance of PMV. Although previous efforts have contributed to better identifying and determining PMV in the context of COVID-19, it remains unclear whether recent findings would translate to a patient cohort with a disease-specific PMV definition.

This study tested the hypothesis that COVID-19 patients could be classified as either ≤ 14 days (non-PMV) or > 14 days (PMV) intubation duration by implementing an ensemble ML workflow validated with data obtained before or at patient intubation. While several factors are known to cause PMV, including acid-base, weakness, and delirium, this work includes lesser-known parameters such as platelet count, glucose levels and fevers. Although routinely measured clinically, these parameters are not typically utilized to determine readiness for ventilator liberation in a clinical setting. The ultimate goal is to provide an accurate predictive tool to help tackle secondary complications to ventilation usage and facilitate effective triage of limited resources in the ICU setting.

Methods

Study design and patient characteristics

Data were collected retrospectively from a single academic center with approval by the medical ethics committee and Internal Review Board (IRB #20.0404). Patient data were maintained in a REDCap database compliant with all Protected Health Information (PHI) regulations. De-identified clinical data of adult patients (n = 1371) treated at the University of Louisville Hospital from March 2020 to December 2021 were retrospectively evaluated for inclusion. No patient consent was required, as the IRB provided a waiver given the nature of the study with limited risk to patients. The de-identified data could not be traced to particular patients. Subjects included were symptomatic for COVID-19 and were on-site for the entire duration of intubation (n = 112), with no other inclusion or exclusion criteria. Data from both men and women were included. Trained residents, possessing a M.D. degree, performed chart review and data entry into pre-defined data fields under the supervision of the clinician in charge of the study. Building upon the findings in14 for prediction of PMV across a disease-unspecific cohort, a 14-day threshold was chosen to define long and short-term intubation. To avoid biasing the study, the clinical team was blinded to the data analysis, while the analysis team was blinded to the clinical data collection.

Study outcome

Prediction of PMV (> 14 days) and non-PMV (≤ 14 days) was the primary study outcome.

Method of patient weaning

Patients underwent a daily sedation vacation and if demonstrating clinical stability as well as good neurological function, would undergo a daily spontaneous breathing trial for approximately 30–60 min. If they were able to tolerate this trial, respiratory therapists would obtain ventilator parameters that if within normal limits would prompt the physicians to consider extubation. If at any time patients were unable to tolerate any of these steps, they would be placed back on control ventilator settings and the weaning trial would be tried again the next day.

Data preprocessing

Data were checked for entry errors by the clinical team to ensure accurate interpretation. The dataset was processed with a data wrangling pipeline to identify the subset of patients to be included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Asymptomatic patients were excluded from the analysis. Categorical features were factored and checked for consistency among factor levels. Categorical features with missing data were removed, followed by removing patients with missing data. The thresholds for categorical feature removal and patient removal were chosen to be 50%. A complete case dataset was created from the categorical features of intubated clinical patients, including hospital admission and discharge details, types of medications used during treatment, properties of microbiology cultures, use of certain hospital equipment, mental health evaluation, blood clotting disorders, oxygen requirements and saturation levels, and drugs used for COVID-19 treatment. Continuous clinical features with missing data were excluded, where the threshold for exclusion was chosen to be 35%, including measurements of magnesium, phosphorous, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), D-Dimer, ferritin, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), maximum procalcitonin, interleukin-6 (IL-6), mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), tidal volume, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), troponin, and C-reactive protein. To only use data known before the intubation event to predict PMV, recordings concerning acute patient events (e.g., myocardial infarction, renal failure, initial heart rhythm after cardiac arrest, etc.), symptoms developed during hospital stay, ventilator settings, ICU length of stay, diagnostic and treatment strategies, living status, and intubation duration were also excluded as features. In addition, patient social history was not considered in this study. Duration of intubation was measured from intubation to extubation time independent of patient outcome. For features measured periodically, only values measured within 24 h before or on the day of intubation were retained for analysis: FiO2, white blood cell count, respiratory rate, pH, PaCO2, PaO2, HCO3, and temperature.

Train-test split

To train and validate the ensemble ML model, the dataset was chronologically split 70% and 30% into training (n = 78) and test sets (n = 34), respectively, with earlier patients (March 2020 to March 2021) being in the training set and later patients (March 2021 to December 2021) being in the test set. This enabled a non-random split of the data into training and test sets, which according to the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis (TRIPOD) checklist17 is a preferred method for validating such models. Furthermore, dividing training and test data into a 70/30 split is consistent with ML modelling of severe COVID-19 patients in18, and chronological splitting has been used to validate ensemble ML model predictions of COVID-19 patient ICU admission and mortality19. Following the recommendation in20 for application of imputation while preserving hypothesis testing, all continuous features were confirmed to contain ≤ 40% missing values in both training and test sets. Statistics between training and test sets prior to imputation show that patient covariates including weight, body mass index (BMI), race, sex, and proportion of PMV and non-PMV patients did not significantly differ between the two sets (Supplementary Table 1).

Handling of missing values

Missing Forest, an imputation method recognized for accurate imputation of clinical data21 was used to impute continuous features in the training and test sets separately via the missForest function. No patient had ≥ 70% missing data, confirming that imputation would not inordinately contribute to any individual patient’s data.

Dataset oversampling

To mitigate the imbalance of PMV patients in the training set, Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique-Nominal Continuous (SMOTE-NC) was applied to the training set after imputation using the smotenc function with default settings to create artificial patient entries based on existing patient entries. SMOTE-NC generates new positive (PMV) class continuous entries using a line created from a randomly selected PMV entry to one of its PMV k-nearest neighbors, whereas categorical entries are set to the factor level taken by the majority of the entry’s k-nearest neighbors22. To ensure that trained models did not overfit to artificial entries, oversampling was not applied to the test set.

Base-learner machine learning methods

To evaluate PMV classifications, feature selection was employed for each machine learning model using varImp function. Some models, such as random forest, use custom varImp implementations, whereas others, such as support vector machines, rely on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Cohen’s Kappa was used as the optimization metric during training. Models were trained across feature subsets, adding clinical features one-by-one as ranked by varImp. Within the training set, validation subsets were generated as in23 to ensure model robustness using k-fold cross validation with 10 folds and 10 repeats for a total of 100 iterations using repeatedcv. To quantify model performance, a combination of area under ROC (AUROC) curve and F1 was used. F1 has been previously used to evaluate model performance in concert with AUROC24. During cross-validation, AUROC and F1 values were obtained by averaging performance metrics across all 100 iterations.

Ensemble machine learning approach

The ensemble ML model consisted of a meta-learner model that leverages the predictions of multiple base-learner models to increase classification performance over individual models alone25. In this study, a stacked generalized ensemble approach was employed from caretStack. Models were included as base-learners when maximum validation subset AUROC was ≥ 0.8 during feature selection. As in25, forward feature selection was performed by computing the weighted sum of feature ranks across all base-learners, with the weight of model () being 0 for model ’s maximum validation subset AUROC () of 0.5 and 1 for . Linear regression

Linear regression was performed by the lm function to predict intubation duration using all clinical characteristics. Homoscedasticity of regression residuals was achieved by log-transforming intubation duration and was confirmed by Breusch-Pagan test. Normality of residuals was evaluated by Shapiro-Wilks test. Outliers were identified using Cook’s distance ≥ 1 and excluded. Because models with a minimized Akaike information criterion (AIC) value better describe the connection between the dependent variable (intubation duration) and key data features (clinical characteristics), stepwise regression using AIC was performed using backward elimination via the stepAIC function.

Statistics

Statistics were performed using add_p in R 4.2.2. T-test and Wilcoxon Rank Sum tests were applied on parametric and non-parametric numeric variables, respectively, as determined by Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were calculated using Fisher’s exact and Chi-Squared tests. Correlations were calculated for Pearson (for parametric numeric data) and Spearman correlations (for non-parametric numeric data) using cor.test. Significant correlations were defined by p < 0.05. To prevent imputation from biasing statistical and correlation analyses, results were calculated using pre-imputed intubation patient data entries.

Classification of PMV

Performance of the ensemble ML model is quantified in Fig. 3, showing ROC and Precision-Recall (PR) metrics on the training and test sets, as well as corresponding AUROC and F1 values. The model achieved AUROC of 0.960 and 0.804 on the training subset and test set, respectively, and PRAUC of 0.981 and 0.810, respectively, for 14-day intubation duration thresholds. The F1 characteristic and balanced accuracy (BA) were also calculated: F1 = 0.935 and 0.800 and BA = 0.933 and 0.863 for training set and test set, respectively. Missing value imputation did not contribute significantly to the patterns found by the ensemble ML model, as described in Supplementary Information. Altogether, these performance metrics indicate that COVID-19 patient PMV could be accurately predicted using the proposed ML workflow.

Correlation of features to intubation duration

Comparison of characteristics for patients intubated ≤ 14 days vs. >14 days shows that chronic kidney disease, diabetes, glucose, and LVEF differed significantly between PMV and non-PMV patients (Supplementary Table 2). Of note, mortality did not significantly differ between PMV and non-PMV patients. To elucidate what may lead to PMV, a statistical analysis was performed on each clinical feature (Supplementary Table 3). All features were compared with the number of days each patient was intubated. Higher partial pressure of oxygen in alveoli (PaO2) at time of intubation was weakly correlated with shorter ventilation duration (Spearman’s ρ = -0.266; p = 0.011). Higher BMI was weakly correlated with longer intubation (ρ = 0.277; p = 0.003), as was weight. A medical history of diabetes or hypertension was also associated with a median increase in intubation duration (p = 0.010 or p = 0.001, respectively). Lastly, higher sodium was weakly correlated with shorter intubation (ρ = -0.260; p = 0.006).

Comparison to linear regression analysis

The clinical characteristics selected by the ensemble ML model were compared to those obtained by a linear regression analysis of the training set data (without oversampling). Residuals of the regression model before and after backwards elimination using log-transformed intubation duration were normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.597 and 0.674, respectively) and homoscedastic (Breusch-Pagan test p = 0.514 and 0.413, respectively). The regression model selected 14 out of 20 patient characteristics (Supplementary Table 4) used by the ensemble ML model (Table 2), indicating substantial agreement between the two models, with 9 out of 14 characteristics having significant regression coefficients (p < 0.05). However, the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) of the regression model were high (9.23 days and 12.4 days, respectively, on training set data), indicating that a linear regression of intubated patient characteristics would be insufficient to classify PMV in this cohort.

Discussion

Patients who require IMV are susceptible to poorer outcomes and longer ICU stays and represent a significant increase in ICU cost-of-care. Identifying ICU patients at risk of PMV could improve outcomes, allow families to participate in informed clinical decision making, and potentially aid in managing healthcare costs. Classifying patients as PMV or non-PMV has remained an ongoing challenge, with recent years focusing on predicting intubation of COVID-19 patients. This study tested the hypothesis that an ensemble ML methodology using clinical data at time of intubation could identify patients at risk of PMV, using a COVID-19 dataset as a representative example to classify patients into PMV (> 14 days) and non-PMV (≤ 14 days) groups, and could identify the associated key clinical markers. This study included lesser-known parameters such as platelet count, glucose levels and fevers, with the goal to use routinely measured clinical parameters to determine readiness for ventilator liberation in a clinical setting. The results highlight the need to consider such parameters and ideally optimize them where possible to minimize PMV. A majority of the key features selected for PMV prediction by the ensemble ML model were consistent with those obtained via a linear regression analysis, validating their significance. Longer term, these parameters could be further studied to stratify patients at risk for PMV.

The results show that patients at risk of PMV could be identified with AUROCTRAIN=0.960 and AUROCTEST=0.804, indicating strong performance26 and confirming that the ML ensemble model trained on oversampled training set data could be generalized to a chronologically distinct test set data. This study is consistent with findings that ML models have been successful in predicting ICU admission of COVID-19 patients27, extubation failure risk in IMV patients28, and diagnosis and classification of disease severity in COVID-19 patients29. The methodology is consistent with prediction of COVID-19 ICU admission and mortality using an ensemble ML model in which model performance was validated using a chronologically distinct test set19. The methodology in this study could also contribute to a generalized model applicable to other respiratory diseases, similar to an artificial neural network that predicted 14-day PMV in a disease-unspecific cohort14. Furthermore, the results support the findings in12 suggesting that COVID-19 patients on IMV may possess specific intubation risk characteristics.

Key features selected for PMV prediction by the ensemble ML model as listed in Table 2 are consistent with COVID-19 literature. Dysregulation of glucose concentration (the most important feature) has been associated with patient mortality30, as has elevated temperature31 (third most important feature). Moreover, glucose was significantly elevated in the PMV cohort relative to the non-PMV cohort (p = 0.044; Supplementary Table 22. Hyperglycemia often occurs during critical illness32 and has been attributed to insulin resistance and decreased insulin secretion33. Related to hyperglycemia, the proportion of patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease in the PMV cohort was significantly higher than in non-PMV patients (Supplementary Table 2). Platelet count, the second most important feature (Table 2), has been found to decrease in intermediate and severe COVID-19 cases34 and has been attributed to sepsis-induced coagulation cascades35. LVEF was higher in PMV patients (Supplementary Table 2); byperdynamic LVEF has been associated with increased mortality in COVID-19 patients36. Bicarbonate (ABG) has been linked to moderately severe COVID-19 patients, with its dysregulation as a predictor of prognosis37; here, bicarbonate trended higher with increased IMV duration (Supplementary Table 3).

Significant correlations with prolonged IMV (Supplementary Table 3) included increased BMI or weight, decreased sodium or PaO2 (at intubation), the PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio, and suffering from diabetes or hypertension. While these correlations were weak (ρ < 0.3), they are consistent with previous findings with patients with moderate to severe COVID-19 symptoms. BMI (p = 0.003) and weight (p = 0.005) were weakly correlated with IMV duration (Supplementary Table 3), consistent with literature prior to38 and during the pandemic39 indicating that elevated BMI is associated with complications in IMV patients. Diabetes was found to increase intubation duration in this patient cohort (p = 0.010; Supplementary Table 3), consistent with recent literature that type 1 diabetes mellitus in COVID-19 patients increased risk of ICU and IMV40. Likewise, hypertension increased the duration of intubation (p = 0.001; Supplementary Table 3). The finding that PaO2 was weakly negatively correlated with IMV duration (p = 0.011; Supplementary Table 3) is consistent with previous observations41, which found that P/F (partial pressure of oxygen / fraction of inspired oxygen) 24 h after admission predicted intubation in COVID-19 patients. Lower P/F by itself was also correlated with IMV duration (p = 0.013, Supplementary Table 3) as previously noted42, although the ratio was not used for prediction by the ensemble ML model (Table 2). Higher NLR trended with increased IMV duration, consistent with recent observations that elevated NLR suggests worse survival of COVID-19 patients43. Lastly, among key features, lower sodium was correlated with longer IMV duration (p = 0.006, Supplementary Table 3), also consistent with hyponatremia being linked to more severe disease44.

The key features for PMV prediction could correspond to underlying clinical pathology, with the caveat that several experimental therapies were being utilized at the time that have since fallen out of favor, and their impact remains incompletely understood. It is possible that platelet abnormalities could have been reflective of overwhelming infections resulting in PMV. Ongoing persistent fevers could have reflected a superimposed secondary infection, or of a more severe COVID-19 infection further prolonging IMV. Patients with known or acquired illnesses such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and transaminitis are likely to experienced more severe COVID-19, which could have worsened underlying conditions and made it difficult to liberate them from IMV. Additionally, more hypoxic as well as overweight patients would have likely had severe infections and poor lung compliance making it more difficult to ventilate. While several of these parameters have been found to cause more severe cases of COVID-19, the underlying pathophysiology of how they caused PMV remains poorly understood. The results of this study highlight that factors that are less well utilized when deciding to extubate (such as platelet and glucose levels) may have a larger role in PMV in this patient cohort.

This study highlights the difficulty in generalizing the definition of PMV across multiple conditions. A recommendation from 2005 to define PMV as ≥ 21 days was consistent with the observation that patients with ≥ 21-day ventilation composing most long-term acute care (LTAC) IMV referrals16. However, median IMV duration for COVID-19 patients was recently reported at 10 days15. In this study, median IMV duration was 8 days (Table 1). Moreover, many conditions often have ventilation durations < 21 days, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), bacterial pneumonia, and severe sepsis15. Therefore, ICU treatment of IMV patients may benefit from disease specific PMV definitions, which could potentially be informed by PMV thresholds identified via ML approaches as highlighted here.

A limitation of this study is that samples were retrospectively obtained at a single site; COVID-19 spread has been linked to local population density and pollution levels45. Future work will validate the model with data from other sites to evaluate potential environment-related confounding factors, with attention given to avoid collider bias noted in COVID-19 literature46. While age differed between train and test sets (p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table 1), the ensemble ML model achieved consistent performance across train and test sets and did not identify age as a key clinical feature. Future work could incorporate younger patients to confirm ML model generalizability across differing age populations. The predictive potential of pro-inflammatory cytokines associated with COVID-19 patient mortality47 could also be investigated. We further anticipate that training the ML model with non-COVID-19 data could provide prediction of PMV for other respiratory conditions.

The data were evaluated on the day of intubation only to predict PMV vs. periodically during IMV, which could limit the impact of other clinical factors on PMV. Provider preference and varied criteria for intubation may be confounding factors. Another limitation is that the majority of the data were collected during the early phases of the pandemic when there was limited guidance and therapeutic options available and considerable variability in how ICU’s were managing these cases. There is a possibility that intubation criteria were contingent on ICU admission criteria known to have varied between the first and second waves of the pandemic. Moreover, early glucocorticoid administration and thromboprophylaxis upon ICU admission in48 may have contributed to lower intubation proportions among second wave patients relative to the first wave.

From a technical standpoint, it has been noted in49 that AIC favors linear models with more parameters when the number of selectable features (model coefficients) approaches the number of data samples. Future work could explore other methods of isolating key clinical characteristics, such as the corrected AIC (AICc)49. Subsequent studies should control for demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, BMI, and race, as well as comorbidities such as asthma, in order to further validate the proposed approach. Because clinical parameters for the care of critically-ill COVID-19 patients, such as intubation criteria, and the COVID-19 virus itself have evolved across waves of the pandemic48, training and testing of ML models will remain an ongoing process to maximize clinical applicability. Future work could evaluate different approaches to mitigate imbalances in clinical datasets, such as resampling SMOTE (RSMOTE)50, or self-inspected adaptive SMOTE (SASMOTE)51 .

Conclusions

This study implemented an ensemble ML approach to identify patients with either ≤ 14 days (non-PMV) or > 14 days (PMV) intubation duration, using a COVID-19 patient dataset as a representative example. The model used data obtained before or at patient intubation to make the predictions, achieving strong performance in terms of AUROC and F1 metrics across training and test sets. Key features for classification included glucose, platelet count, temperature, LVEF, bicarbonate (arterial blood gas), BMI, history of diabetes, PaO2, NLR, history of chronic kidney disease, weight, INR, ALT, hypertension, blood urea nitrogen, AST, respiratory rate at intubation, sodium, procalcitonin, and race. Despite limitations, this study show that the proposed ML workflow offers the potential to accurately predict patients at risk of PMV. This workflow could assist in improving clinical decision-making as well as ventilator allocation in times of higher demand, potentially improving patient outcomes and reducing hospital cost-burden. We envision that a user-friendly interface could be developed for clinicians to enter patient information, including ICU-related parameters, and access the tool to evaluate potential outcomes. Future work will focus on building a predictive model to risk stratify patients at risk for PMV in the COVID-19 population and potentially with ARDS from other causes.
