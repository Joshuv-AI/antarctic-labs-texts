A young girl with partial chromosome 15q11.2 microduplication: a case report in Cameroon

Background

Various copy number variants (CNVs) can affect the 15q11 chromosomal region, leading to deletions or duplications with distinct clinical consequences. Deletion of this region is more frequently reported than duplication [1]. Deletions of the 15q11-q13 region cause either Prader-Willi syndrome or Angelman syndrome, depending on the parental origin of the deletion. When the deletion occurs on the paternal chromosome, it causes Prader-Willi syndrome ; when it occurs on the maternal chromosome, it causes Angelman syndrome [1]. The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication is a distinct and smaller CNV that does not encompass the full Prader-Willi/Angelman critical region (PWACR). It involves a 300 kb segment between breakpoints BP1 and BP2, containing four genes : NIPA1 (OMIM 608145), NIPA2 (OMIM 608146), CYFIP1 (OMIM 606322), and TUBGCP5 (OMIM 608147). These genes are expressed in the brain and their altered expression may explain the neurological signs reported in affected individuals [1, 2].

This condition is clinically associated with developmental delay, speech delay, learning disabilities, and memory problems [2]. It is also associated with mild to moderate dysmorphic features, seizures, and autism spectrum disorders. The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication displays incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity [3, 4]. The incidence of this disorder is estimated at 1 in 500 individuals referred for genomic testing [5]. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), and Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) are used to identify the 15q11.2 microduplication. To our knowledge, few cases of 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication have been reported in sub-Saharan Africa. We report a case of a nine-year-old girl who presented with failure to thrive and intellectual disabilities associated with a 15q11.2 microduplication in Cameroon.

Case presentation

Patient demographic details and medical history

Our patient was a nine-year-old girl evaluated at the pediatric endocrinology unit for short stature and feeding difficulties. She had a birth weight of 2450 g and a height of 47 cm, consistent with small for gestational age. She was admitted for early neonatal sepsis for ten days. She presented with feeding difficulties since birth and failure to thrive. She was previously followed by a pediatric endocrinologist for central hypothyroidism for at least one year. She was receiving levothyroxine 2 µg/kg/day. She had mild speech delay and intellectual disabilities at school. No seizures or autistic features were reported. She was the third child of non-consanguineous parents. Her two elder sisters were healthy with normal development. There was no family history of genetic disorders, intellectual disability, or autism spectrum disorders.

Clinical findings

At physical examination, she had a weight of 15.5 kg (− 4 SDS), a height of 110 cm (− 4 SDS) with a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 12.8 kg/m² (− 2.5 SDS). Craniofacial features included prominent forehead, triangular face, flattened nasal bridge, low-set ears, and full lips (Fig. 1). She had brachymetatarsia of the 4th metacarpals and clinodactyly (Fig. 2). She had prepubertal genitalia. Neurological examination revealed normal tone and reflexes.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory tests revealed normal full blood count, serum electrolytes, and urine dipstick. Hormonal assays showed an Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) level of 142.9 ng/ml (− 1 SDS). Thyroid function tests confirmed central hypothyroidism with low free T4 and inappropriately normal TSH levels. Bone age was 7 years, indicating a 2-year delay compared to chronological age.

As a differential diagnosis, Silver-Russell syndrome was initially considered based on failure to thrive, dysmorphic signs, and feeding difficulties. Given the constellation of growth failure, dysmorphic features, and developmental delay, a chromosomal microarray analysis was performed. Comparative Genomic Hybridization (CGH) array was performed using an Agilent 44 K oligonucleotide microarray platform, with a mean genome-wide resolution of approximately 200 kb. Analysis was performed using Agilent Feature Extraction v10.5 and CGH Analytics 3.5.14 software, according to the UCSC Genome Browser database GRCh37/hg19 (2009). The DRLSpread quality parameter was 0.15 (below 0.2, indicating excellent quality). The result was reported in accordance with ISCN 2020 nomenclature as: arr[GRCh37] 15q11.2(22784523_23085096)x3. This correspond to an interstitial microduplication of 300.5 kb at the 15q11.2 region between breakpoints BP1 and BP2 (Fig. 3). Parental genetic testing confirmed that this microduplication was of maternal origin. According to ACMG/ClinGen guidelines, this CNV was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) with likely pathogenic contribution, given its association with neurodevelopmental disorders in published literature and the incomplete penetrance of this locus. The combination of neurodevelopmental delay, dysmorphic features, and failure to thrive, confirmed by CGH-array findings, established the diagnosis of 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient received nutritional support with fortified soy porridge, levothyroxine therapy at a dose of 5 µg/kg/day, and regular follow-up at the pediatric endocrinology unit.

Outcomes

Our patient is currently 12 years old. She has a height of 120 cm (− 4 SDS) with a BMI of 15.27 kg/m² (− 1 SDS). She continues to receive L-thyroxine and is improving at school. Over the three-year follow-up period, she has shown a 10 cm gain in height and improvement in BMI from − 2.5 to − 1 SDS, indicating better nutritional status.

Discussion

The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication is a rare copy number variant distinct from complete trisomy 15. It results from a 300 kb interstitial duplication of the proximal long arm of chromosome 15. The incidence of this genetic disorder is not well established. More than 50 cases have been published worldwide, but few have been reported in Africa. Van der Zwaag et al. in 2010 reported three individuals with duplication of the BP1-BP2 region [5]. Burnside et al. in 2011 reported a series of 146 individuals with a copy number alteration of the BP1-BP2 region of chromosome 15 [1]. The prevalence of duplication was 0.45%. In their study, males were more affected than females, with a mean age of 7.9 years [1]. Our patient was a girl aged 9 years at the time of diagnosis.

The 15q region is particularly susceptible to genomic rearrangements due to the presence of five breakpoints. The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication should be distinguished from larger duplications encompassing the full Prader-Willi/Angelman critical region (PWACR), which extend from BP2 to BP3 and carry a different clinical significance. The BP1-BP2 region contains four genes including NIPA1, NIPA2, CYFIP1, and TUBGCP5. These genes are involved in central nervous system development. NIPA1 encodes a magnesium transporter important for neuronal function. NIPA2 is involved in magnesium homeostasis. CYFIP1 plays a role in synaptic plasticity and is implicated in fragile X syndrome pathway. TUBGCP5 is involved in centrosome function and microtubule organization. Altered expression of these genes has been related to impaired behavior and developmental delay [2, 3, 6]. This microduplication displays incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, as reported by Picinelli et al. [6].

The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication can be suspected clinically in a child presenting with moderate to severe hypotonia in early childhood, developmental delay, autism spectrum disorders, and seizures [3]. Patients may also present with moderate dysmorphic signs. Our patient presented with mild speech delay and intellectual disabilities but without autistic features. She also had dysmorphic signs. The main clinical findings were feeding difficulties and failure to thrive, which were also described by Burnside et al. in one patient [1]. Feeding difficulties are usually related to hypotonia, especially in newborns and infants. There is great variability in the clinical presentation of this condition. Burnside et al. reported various signs in their study including speech delay followed by developmental and motor delays (50%), autistic features (41%), and attention deficit disorder [1]. Some reports suggest that phenotype variability is related to the underlying mechanism of duplication [2].

The initial clinical suspicion of Silver-Russell syndrome in our patient underscores the phenotypic overlap among diverse genetic conditions manifesting as growth failure and dysmorphic features. This underscores the significance of genetic testing in identifying the underlying etiology of the extensive spectrum of genetic disorders resulting in growth failure.

Diagnosis of the 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication is established by identification of the copy number gain using genomic testing methods such as chromosomal microarray analysis or targeted duplication analysis. The diagnosis of our patient was made by CGH-Array at the Cytogenetic Laboratory of the Hôpitaux Universitaires de Genève (HUG), Switzerland. Two main mechanisms can lead to 15q11 duplications. The most reported mechanism is a maternal isodicentric chromosome 15 (idic(15)), which contains two extra copies of the 15q11-q13 region, leading to tetrasomy. In approximately 20% of cases, the duplication is due to an interstitial 15q11.2 duplication containing one extra copy, leading to trisomy. In our patient, the duplication was an interstitial maternal microduplication of the BP1-BP2 region of 300.5 kb, consistent with trisomy of this specific locus.

This case is particularly significant as it represents one of the few documented cases of 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication from sub-Saharan Africa. The few reports from sub-Saharan Africa suggest limited access to genetic testing rather than true differences in prevalence. Our patient’s diagnosis was only possible through collaboration with a genetics laboratory in Geneva, highlighting the need for increased local capacity for genetic testing in African countries.

Conclusion

The 15q11.2 BP1-BP2 microduplication is a rare copy number variant associated with variable neurodevelopmental and dysmorphic features. It should be clearly distinguished from complete trisomy 15 and from larger duplications encompassing the full PWACR. It can be suspected in children with developmental delay and behavioral difficulties. Genetic tests such as CGH-array should be performed for suspected cases. There is an urgent need to improve access to genetic testing in sub-Saharan Africa to enable accurate diagnosis and appropriate management of rare genetic disorders.
