Abstract
. The classical Stone duality associates to each Boolean algebra a topological space consisting of ultrafilters. Lawson’s generalisation constructs a dual equivalence of categories of Boolean inverse ∧ \land -semigroups and Hausdorff ample topological groupoids. We further generalise the duality to the graded analogues of those categories, and provide various illustrations.

1.
Introduction

Stone’s representation theorem for Boolean algebras
[
30
]
famously associates to each Boolean algebra a topological space consisting of ultrafilters. That general idea has been extended over the years to construct dual equivalences of various categories, collectively referred to as
Stone dualities
. In particular, (building on the work of Kellendonk
[
15
,
16
]
, Paterson
[
26
]
, and Lenz
[
24
]
) Lawson
[
20
,
21
]
established a duality between certain categories of
Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups
and
Hausdorff ample topological groupoids
. (See also
[
6
,
10
,
11
,
23
]
.)

Lawson’s duality results have received significant attention, in part because inverse semigroups and topological groupoids have played a fundamental role in constructing widely studied noncommutative algebras and
C^{*}
-algebras. One typically builds the relevant inverse semigroups and topological groupoids from some combinatorial object, such as a graph. From the resulting inverse semigroups one can then build enveloping algebras
[
31
]
, while from the resulting topological groupoids one can construct convolution algebras (e.g., Steinberg algebras
[
7
]
and groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras
[
27
]
). In certain circumstances these enveloping and convolution algebras are (graded) isomorphic
[
29
]
. The aforementioned connections are summarised in the following diagram.

graded inverse semigroups
\scriptstyle{Lawson-Stone\,duality}
envoloping algebras
\scriptstyle{graded\,isomorphism}
combinatorial data
graded ample groupoids
convolution algebras

It was noticed a long time ago that the algebras described above are graded, and the grading has played a crucial role in their study. For one thing, the zero-component subalgebras are typically rich and well-behaved. For another, one can lift properties from the homogeneous components to the whole algebras. More concretely, the gradings on Leavitt path algebras (which constitute a class of Steinberg algebras) played a fundamental role in the characterisation of their ideals
[
1
]
. Moreover, a central open question in the area is to describe the extent to which
graded/equivariant

K
-theory classifies the algebras
[
13
]
.

The gradings of the aforementioned enveloping and convolution algebras arise from the discrete nature of the combinatorial objects from which they are built. For example, in the case of a graph, the algebra grading ultimately results from path lengths. Thus it is natural to study the gradings on the level of semigroups and groupoids, most immediately constructed from the combinatorial objects, rather than on the higher level of resulting algebras. Gradings on both (inverse) semigroups
[
12
]
and groupoids
[
2
,
8
]
have indeed been systematically explored recently. The purpose of this note is to connect the relevant graded
inverse semigroups and graded topological groupoids, by generalising Lawson’s duality to the graded context. (See
[
4
,
5
,
9
,
17
,
18
,
22
,
28
]
for generalisations of that duality in other directions.)

In Sections
2
and
3
we provide definitions of, and basic observations about, our main objects of study, namely graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups and graded Hausdorff ample groupoids. In Section
4
we give several natural constructions of such semigroups, including graded symmetric inverse monoids and distributive completions of graph inverse semigroups. Section
5
is devoted to proving our main result (Theorem
5.12
), that the categories of graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups and graded Hausdorff ample groupoids (with appropriate morphisms) are dually equivalent. Finally, in Section
6
we show that given a graded Hausdorff ample groupoid
\mathscr{G}
and a ring
R
, we have
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\cong R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
, where
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is the graded inverse semigroup associated to
\mathscr{G}
by our duality,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
is its non-graded counterpart from Lawson’s duality, and
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
and
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
are corresponding enveloping rings.

2.
Basic Definitions

We begin by recalling and defining the various objects required for a graded version of Lawson’s duality, namely, Boolean algebras, gradings, (Boolean) inverse semigroups, and (ample) topological groupoids.

2.1.
Partially Ordered Sets

Recall that a partially-ordered set
(X,\leq)
is a
lattice
, if for all
x,y\in X
there exists in
X
a meet (i.e., greatest lower bound)
x\wedge y
and a join (i.e., least upper bound)
x\vee y
. A lattice
X
is
distributive
if
x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)
and
x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z)
, for all
x,y,z\in X
. A
Boolean algebra
is a distributive lattice
X
, with top and bottom elements (
1
and
0
, respectively), along with a unary
complement
operator
\lnot
, satisfying
x\lor\lnot x=1
and
x\land\lnot x=0
, for all
x\in X
.

Let
(X,\leq)
be a partially ordered set with bottom element
0
, and let
Y\subseteq X
. We set

Y^{\uparrow}:=\{x\in X\mid\exists y\in Y\text{ such that }y\leq x\},

and

Y^{\downarrow}:=\{x\in X\mid\exists y\in Y\text{ such that }y\geq x\}.

We say that
Y
is
downward directed
if it is nonempty, and for all
x,y\in Y
there exists
z\in X
such that
z\leq x
and
z\leq y
. If
Y
is downward directed,
Y=Y^{\uparrow}
, and
0\notin Y
, then
Y
is a
filter
. A maximal filter is called an
ultrafilter
. If
Y=Y^{\downarrow}
, then
Y
is an
order ideal
. An order ideal of the form
\{x\}^{\downarrow}
(
x\in X
) is called
principal
.

2.2.
Graded Semigroups

Recall that a
semigroup
is a nonempty set equipped with an associative binary operation. A
monoid
is a semigroup with an identity element
1
. We assume, throughout, that every semigroup has a zero element
0
. Given a semigroup
S
, we denote by
E(S)
the set of idempotents of
S
. If for each
s\in S
there is a unique
s^{-1}\in S
satisfying
s=ss^{-1}s
and
s^{-1}=s^{-1}ss^{-1}
, then
S
is an
inverse semigroup
. In this case
(st)^{-1}=t^{-1}s^{-1}
for all
s,t\in S
. It is well-known that if
S
is an inverse semigroup, then the elements of
E(S)
commute. See
[
14
, §5]
or
[
19
]
for more details about inverse semigroup fundamentals.

Given a semigroup
S
and a group
\Gamma
, we say that
S
is
\Gamma
-
graded
if there is a map
\phi:S\setminus\{0\}\to\Gamma
such that
\phi(st)=\phi(s)\phi(t)
, whenever
st\not=0
. For each
\alpha\in\Gamma
, we set
S_{\alpha}:=\phi^{-1}(\alpha)\cup\{0\}
. Equivalently,
S
is
\Gamma
-
graded
if for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
there exists
S_{\alpha}\subseteq S
such that

S=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}S_{\alpha},

where
S_{\alpha}S_{\beta}\subseteq S_{\alpha\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
, and
S_{\alpha}\cap S_{\beta}=\{0\}
for all distinct
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
.

Let
S
be a
\Gamma
-graded semigroup. For each
\alpha\in\Gamma
and
s\in S_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
, we say that the
degree
of
s
is
\alpha
, and write
\deg(s):=\alpha
. If
T\subseteq S
is such that
T\subseteq S_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
, then we say that
T
is
homogeneous
(
of degree
\alpha
). Note that
\deg(s)=\varepsilon
(the identity element of
\Gamma
) for all
s\in E(S)\setminus\{0\}
, and
\deg(s^{-1})=\deg(s)^{-1}
for all
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
, in the case where
S
is an inverse semigroup. We say that
S
is
trivially
graded if
S_{\varepsilon}=S
. It is easy to see that
S_{\varepsilon}
is a semigroup (with zero), and that
S_{\varepsilon}
is an inverse semigroup whenever
S
is. A homomorphism
\phi:S\rightarrow T
of
\Gamma
-graded semigroups is
graded
if
\phi(S_{\alpha})\subseteq T_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha\in\Gamma
. See
[
12
]
for more information on graded semigroups.

2.3.
Boolean Inverse Semigroups

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup. Elements
s,t\in S
are said to be
compatible
if
s^{-1}t,st^{-1}\in E(S)
(the set of idempotents), and to be
orthogonal
if
s^{-1}t=0=st^{-1}
. We say that subset of
S
is
compatible
if each pair of elements in that subset is compatible.

The
natural partial order

\leq
on
S
is defined by
s\leq t
(
s,t\in S
) if
s=tu
for some
u\in E(S)
. Equivalently,
s\leq t
if
s=ut
for some
u\in E(S)
. In particular, if
u,v\in E(S)
, then
u\leq v
amounts to
u=uv=vu
. Also if
s\leq t
for some
s,t\in S
, then
s^{-1}\leq t^{-1}
, and
rs\leq rt
,
sr\leq tr
for all
r\in S
. See
[
14
, §5.2]
for more details.

It is well-known that
E(S)
forms a lower semilattice (i.e., a partially ordered set where every pair of elements has a meet), with
e\land f=ef
for all
e,f\in E(S)
. If each pair of elements in
S
has a meet, relative to
\leq
, then
S
is called an
inverse
\land
-semigroup
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a
\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup, for some group
\Gamma
. Then
S
is
\Gamma
-
graded-Boolean
, or simply
graded-Boolean
, if it satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

For all
\alpha\in\Gamma
, every nonempty finite compatible subset of
S_{\alpha}
has a join in
S
.

(2)

For all
\alpha\in\Gamma
, all nonempty compatible
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\subseteq S_{\alpha}
, and all
s\in S
, the joins
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}s)
and
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}(ss_{i})
exist,
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}s)=\big(\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\big)s
, and
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}(ss_{i})=s\big(\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\big)
.

(3)

E(S)
is a distributive lattice, and each principal order ideal of
E(S)
is a Boolean algebra.

This definition is a graded version of one from
[
21
]
. More specifically, Lawson defines an inverse semigroup
S
to be
Boolean
if it satisfies the three conditions above, with each instance of
S_{\alpha}
in (1) and (2) replaced with
S
.

We say that a homomorphism
\phi:S\to T
of inverse semigroups is
proper
if
\phi^{-1}(X)
is an ultrafilter of
S
for every ultrafilter
X
of
T
. Fixing a group
\Gamma
, we denote by
\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
the category of
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups, having as its morphisms proper graded homomorphisms
\phi:S\to T
that preserve meets, and restrict to lattice homomorphisms
E(S)\to E(T)
that preserve complements in principal order ideals.

2.4.
Graded Topological Groupoids

A
groupoid

\mathscr{G}
is a small category in which every morphism is invertible. We denote the set of objects of
\mathscr{G}
, also known as the
unit space
, by
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
, and we identify these objects with the corresponding identity morphisms. For each morphism
x\in\mathscr{G}
, the object
\bm{d}(x):=x^{-1}x
is the
domain
of
x
, and the object
\bm{r}(x):=xx^{-1}
is its
range
. (Thus, two morphisms
x,y\in\mathscr{G}
are composable as
xy
if and only if
\bm{d}(x)=\bm{r}(y)
.) Define

\mathscr{G}^{(2)}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathscr{G}\times\mathscr{G}\mid\bm{d}(x)=\bm{r}(y)\}.

For subsets
X,Y\subseteq\mathscr{G}
of morphisms, we define

XY:=\big\{xy\mid x\in X,y\in Y,\bm{d}(x)=\bm{r}(y)\big\},

(2.1)

and

X^{-1}:=\big\{x^{-1}\mid x\in X\big\}.

(2.2)

A
topological groupoid
is a groupoid (whose set of morphisms is) equipped with a topology making inversion and composition continuous. A topological groupoid
\mathscr{G}
is
étale
if
\bm{d}:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
is a local homeomorphism, where
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
is viewed as a topological space in the topology induced by that on
\mathscr{G}
. (I.e., for all
x\in\mathscr{G}
there is an open
X\subseteq\mathscr{G}
such that
x\in X
,
\bm{d}(X)
is open, and the restriction
\bm{d}|_{X}:X\to\bm{d}(X)
is a homeomorphism with respect to the relevant subspace topologies.) An open subset
X
of an étale groupoid
\mathscr{G}
is called a
slice
or
local bisection
if the restrictions
\bm{d}|_{X}
and
\bm{r}|_{X}
are injective. An étale groupoid
\mathscr{G}
is
ample
or
Boolean
if the compact slices form a base for its topology, and
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}
is Hausdorff in the induced topology.

Given a topological groupoid
\mathscr{G}
and a group
\Gamma
, we say that
\mathscr{G}
is
\Gamma
-
graded
if there is a continuous functor
\phi:\mathscr{G}\rightarrow\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is endowed with the discrete topology. (Such a functor can be viewed as a continuous function from the set of all morphisms of
\mathscr{G}
to
\Gamma
, such that
\phi(x)\phi(y)=\phi(xy)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathscr{G}^{(2)}
.) Setting
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}:=\phi^{-1}(\alpha)
for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
, we have

\mathscr{G}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\mathscr{G}_{\alpha},

where
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}\mathscr{G}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{\alpha\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
, and
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}\cap\mathscr{G}_{\beta}=\emptyset
for all distinct
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
. Note that
\mathscr{G}^{(0)}\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}
. As with semigroups, we say that each
x\in\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
has
degree

\alpha
, and write
\deg(x):=\alpha
.

A functor
\phi:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{H}
of groupoids is called a
covering
functor if it is
star-injective
(i.e., if
\phi(x)=\phi(y)
and
\bm{d}(x)=\bm{d}(y)
, then
x=y
), and
star-surjective
(i.e., if
\bm{d}(y)=\phi(e)
, for some identity morphism
e
, then
\bm{d}(x)=e
and
\phi(x)=y
for some
x\in\mathscr{G}
). Additionally, a continuous functor
\phi:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{H}
of topological groupoids is
proper
if the inverse image of every compact set under
\phi
is compact. Fixing a group
\Gamma
, we denote by
\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
the category of
\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoids with
graded
proper continuous covering functors as its morphisms, that is proper continuous covering functors
\phi:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{H}
with the property that
\phi(\mathscr{G}_{\alpha})\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\Gamma
.

3.
Graded-Boolean Basics

In this section we record some preliminary observations about graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups, that will be used extensively.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup.

(1)

If
s,t\in S
are compatible but not orthogonal, then
\,\deg(s)=\deg(t)
.

(2)

If
s,t\in S\setminus\{0\}
are comparable in the natural partial order, then
\,\deg(s)=\deg(t)
.

(3)

If
X\subseteq S
is a filter, then
\,\deg(s)=\deg(t)
for all
s,t\in X
.

(4)

If
\alpha\in\Gamma
, and
s\in S
is the join or meet for some nonempty subset of
S_{\alpha}

(
in the natural partial order on
S
)
, then
s\in S_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
s,t\in S
are compatible but not orthogonal. Then
s^{-1}t\in E(S)\setminus\{0\}
, and so

\deg(s)^{-1}\deg(t)=\deg(s^{-1}t)=\varepsilon,

which implies that
\deg(s)=\deg(t)
.

(2) Taking comparable
s,t\in S\setminus\{0\}
, we may assume that
s=tu
for some
u\in E(S)
. Since
s\neq 0
, we have

\deg(s)=\deg(t)\deg(u)=\deg(t).

The statement also follows from (1).

(3) Suppose that
s,t\in X
. Since
X
is downward directed,
r\leq s
and
r\leq t
for some
r\in X
. Since
r,s,t\neq 0
, as elements of a filter, the statement follows from (1).

(4) Suppose that
s\in S
is the join or meet for some nonempty
T\subseteq S_{\alpha}
. Since
0\in S_{\alpha}
, we may assume that
s\neq 0
, and hence also that
T\neq\{0\}
. Then
t\leq s
or
s\leq t
for some
t\in T\setminus\{0\}\subseteq S_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus
s\in S_{\alpha}
, by (2).
∎

Lemma 3.2

(cf.
[
20
]
, Lemma 2.2(2,3))
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, and
s,t\in S
.

(1)

If
s\leq t
, then there is an element
t\setminus s
of
S
such that
t\setminus s\leq t
,
s
is orthogonal to
t\setminus s
, and
t=s\lor(t\setminus s)
.

(2)

If
s\not\leq t
, then there exists
r\in S\setminus\{0\}
such that
r\leq s
and
r\land t=0
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
s\leq t
, and write
s=tv
, for some
v\in E(S)
. We may assume that
t\neq 0
, since otherwise
s=0
, and we can take
t\setminus s=0
.

Let
X=\{t^{-1}t\}^{\downarrow}\subseteq E(S)
be the principal order ideal of
E(S)
generated by
t^{-1}t
. Then
X
is a Boolean algebra, since
S
is graded-Boolean. Since
E(S)
is commutative, we have

s^{-1}st^{-1}t=vt^{-1}tvt^{-1}t=vt^{-1}tv=s^{-1}s,

and so
s^{-1}s\leq t^{-1}t
. Thus
s^{-1}s\in X
, and therefore there exists
e\in X
such that
s^{-1}s\lor e=t^{-1}t
and
s^{-1}s\land e=0
. Setting
t\setminus s=te
, we claim that this element has the desired properties.

Clearly,
t\setminus s\leq t
. Next, note that
0=s^{-1}s\land e=es^{-1}s
implies that
es^{-1}=es^{-1}ss^{-1}=0
, and hence also
se=0
. Thus
(t\setminus s)s^{-1}=tes^{-1}=0
, and

(t\setminus s)^{-1}s=et^{-1}s=et^{-1}tv=t^{-1}tve=t^{-1}se=0.

Therefore
s
and
t\setminus s
are orthogonal.

Since
s\leq t
and
t\setminus s\leq t
, and since
t\neq 0
, we have
s,t\setminus s\in S_{\deg(t)}
, by Lemma
3.1
(2). Therefore
r=s\lor(t\setminus s)\in S
exists, given that
S
is graded-Boolean, and clearly
r\leq t
. We also have
r^{-1}r=s^{-1}s\lor(t\setminus s)^{-1}(t\setminus s)
–see
[
19
, Section 1.4]
. Now,
(t\setminus s)^{-1}(t\setminus s)=et^{-1}te=e(et^{-1}te)
, and so
(t\setminus s)^{-1}(t\setminus s)\leq e
. Since
e\leq t^{-1}t
implies that
e\leq et^{-1}te=(t\setminus s)^{-1}(t\setminus s)
, it follows that
e=(t\setminus s)^{-1}(t\setminus s)
. Therefore
r^{-1}r=s^{-1}s\lor e=t^{-1}t
. This, together with the fact that
r\leq t
, implies that
t=r=s\lor(t\setminus s)
, as desired. (Writing
r=tu
for some
u\in E(S)
, we have
t^{-1}t=r^{-1}r=ut^{-1}tu=t^{-1}tu
, and so
t^{-1}t\leq u
. Thus
t=tt^{-1}t\leq tu=r
, which implies that
r=t
.)

(2) Suppose that
s\not\leq t
. Since
s\land t\leq s
, we may form
r=s\setminus(s\land t)
, by (1), where
r\leq s
. It cannot be the case that
r=0
, since then we would have
s=(s\land t)\lor r=s\land t
, and so
s\leq t
, contrary to hypothesis. Next, note that since, by (1),
r
and
s\land t
are orthogonal, it must be the case that
r\land(s\land t)=0
. (Given orthogonal
a,b\in S
, setting
c=a\land b
, and writing
c=ag=bf
for
g,f\in E(S)
, we have
a^{-1}ag=a^{-1}bf=0
. Thus
c=ag=aa^{-1}ag=0
.) Therefore, using the fact that
r\leq s
, we conclude that
0=(r\land s)\land t=r\land t
.
∎

The next lemma collects some basic well-known facts about filters on inverse semigroups. We give proofs, in cases where we could not locate them in the literature, for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup, and let
X
be a filter on
S
.

(1)

If
s\in S
is such that
0\notin sX
, then
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is a filter on
S
.

(2)

Y=(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
is a filter on and an inverse subsemigroup of
S
, and
X=(xY)^{\uparrow}
for all
x\in X
.

(3)

If
X
is an ultrafilter, then
(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
is an ultrafilter on
S
, and
E((X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow})
is an ultrafilter on
E(S)
.

(4)

If
s\in S
is such that
s^{-1}s\in X
and
X
is an ultrafilter, then
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is an ultrafilter on
S
.

Proof.

(1) Let
s\in S
is such that
0\notin sX
, and let
r,t\in X
. Since
X
is a filter, there exists
x\in X
such that
x\leq r
and
x\leq t
, and so
sx\leq sr
and
sx\leq st
. Since
sx\in sX
, this shows that
sX
is downward directed, which implies that
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is downward directed as well. Given that
0\notin sX
, it follows that
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is a filter.

(2) This is
[
21
, Lemma 2.8(1)]
.

(3) Suppose that
X
is an ultrafilter, and write
Y=(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
. Then
Y
is a filter, by (2). Now suppose that
Y\subseteq Z
for some filter
Z
on
S
. Taking any
x\in X
, we have
X=(xY)^{\uparrow}\subseteq(xZ)^{\uparrow}
, by (2). Given that
X
is an ultrafilter,
(xY)^{\uparrow}=(xZ)^{\uparrow}
. Then letting
z\in Z
be arbitrary, we have
xz\geq xy
for some
y\in Y
, and so
z\geq x^{-1}xz\geq x^{-1}xy
. Since
Y
is semigroup, by (2), and
x^{-1}x\in Y
, we have
x^{-1}xy\in Y
. Since
Y
is a filter, it follows that
z\in Y
, and so we conclude that
Y=Z
. Therefore
Y
is an ultrafilter on
S
.

E((X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow})
being an ultrafilter on
E(S)
is a special case of
[
21
, Lemma 2.18(3)]
.

(4) Suppose that
s\in S
,
s^{-1}s\in X
, and
X
is an ultrafilter. Taking
t\in X
, there exists
r\in X
such that
r\leq t
and
r\leq s^{-1}s
. Write
r=s^{-1}su=tv
for some
u,v\in E(S)
. Then
su=ss^{-1}su=stv
. Since
r\neq 0
, as an element of the filter
X
, it follows that
st\neq 0
, and so
0\notin sX
. Thus
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is a filter, by (1).

To show that
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is an ultrafilter, suppose that
(sX)^{\uparrow}\subseteq Z
for some filter
Z
on
S
. Note that
0\notin s^{-1}Z
, and so
(s^{-1}Z)^{\uparrow}
is a filter, by (1). (For any
z\in Z
there exists
t\in Z
such that
t\leq s
and
t\leq z
. Writing
t=su=zv
for some
u,v\in E(S)
, gives
su=ss^{-1}su=ss^{-1}zv
, and so
s^{-1}z\neq 0
.) Now,
s^{-1}sX\subseteq s^{-1}Z
, and so
(s^{-1}sX)^{\uparrow}\subseteq(s^{-1}Z)^{\uparrow}
. According to
[
21
, Lemma 2.8(2)]
, since
X
contains an idempotent,
X=(XX)^{\uparrow}
, and
X
is an inverse subsemigroup of
S
. Thus,
X=(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
, and so
(s^{-1}sX)^{\uparrow}=X
, by (2). Given that
X
is an ultrafilter, we conclude that
X=(s^{-1}Z)^{\uparrow}
. Finally, letting
z\in Z
be any element, we have
s^{-1}z\in X
, and so
z\geq ss^{-1}z\in sX
. Thus
(sX)^{\uparrow}=Z
, and so
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is an ultrafilter.
∎

Next we record some concepts, first introduced in
[
24
]
, and used extensively in
[
21
]
. Let
S
be an inverse
\land
-semigroup. Given
s,t\in S
, write
s\to t
if for all
r\in S\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
r\leq s
, we have
r\land t\neq 0
. If
s\to t
and
t\to s
, then we write
s\leftrightarrow t
. It is shown in
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
that
\leftrightarrow
is a
0
-restricted congruence on
S
, and so one can form the quotient semigroup
S/\leftrightarrow
. (Here,
0
-restricted
refers to the property that for all
s\in S
, we have
s\leftrightarrow 0
if and only if
s=0
.) Note that if
s\leq t
for some
s,t\in S
, then
r\land t=r
for all
r\in S\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
r\leq s
, and so
s\to t
. The semigroup
S
is said to be
separative
provided that
s\leq t
if and only if
s\to t
, for all
s,t\in S
, in which case
\leftrightarrow
is simply equality. According to
[
21
, Proposition 2.4]
,
S/\leftrightarrow
is separative.

More generally, given
s_{1},\dots,s_{m},t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in S
write
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}\to\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
if for each
1\leq i\leq m
and
r\in S\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
r\leq s_{i}
, there exists
1\leq j\leq n
such that
r\land t_{j}\neq 0
. The notation
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}\leftrightarrow\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
is defined in the obvious way.

Lemma 3.4

(cf.
[
20
]
, Lemma 2.14)
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, and let
X
be an ultrafilter on
S
. If
s\lor t\in X
, then
s\in X
or
t\in X
, for all
s,t\in S
.

Proof.

Let
Y=(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
, and suppose that
r=s\lor t\in X
, for some
s,t\in S
. Then
r^{-1}r=(s^{-1}s)\lor(t^{-1}t)
–see
[
19
, Section 1.4]
. Since
r^{-1}r\in Y
, we have
(s^{-1}s)\lor(t^{-1}t)\in E(Y)
. Note that
E(Y)
is an ultrafilter on
E(S)
, by Lemma
3.3
(3).

According to
[
21
, Lemma 2.19(1)]
, since
E(Y)
is an ultrafilter on the inverse semigroup
E(S)
, it is a
tight
filter (i.e., if
x\in E(Y)
and
\{x\}\to\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
for some
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in E(S)
, then
y_{i}\in E(Y)
for some
i
), and by
[
21
, Lemma 2.41]
,
E(Y)
is a prime filter (i.e., if
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\in E(Y)
for some
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in E(S)
, then
y_{i}\in E(Y)
for some
i
). Thus, in particular,
s^{-1}s\in E(Y)
or
t^{-1}t\in E(Y)
. We may assume that
s^{-1}s\in E(Y)
, and let
Z=(sY)^{\uparrow}
. Note that, by Lemma
3.3
(2),
ry\neq 0
for all
y\in Y
, and so
sy\neq 0
for all
y\in Y
. Thus
Z
is a filter on
S
, by Lemma
3.3
(1). Now, for all
y\in Y
we have
ry\geq sy
, from which it follows that
(rY)^{\uparrow}\subseteq(sY)^{\uparrow}=Z
. But
X=(rY)^{\uparrow}
, by Lemma
3.3
(2), and so
X=Z
, since
X
is an ultrafilter. In particular,
s=ss^{-1}s\in sY\subseteq X
.
∎

Given an inverse
\land
-semigroup
S
, let
\mathsf{FC}(S)
denote the set of all finitely-generated compatible order ideals of
S
, where “compatible” means compatible as subsets of
S
. It is shown in
[
21
, Proposition 2.5]
that
\mathsf{FC}(S)
is an inverse semigroup (with the obvious subset multiplication operation). Let
I,J\in\mathsf{FC}(S)
, and let
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}
and
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
be generating sets for
I
and
J
, respectively, as order ideals (so
I=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}^{\downarrow}
and
J=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}^{\downarrow}
). Write
I\equiv J
if
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}\leftrightarrow\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}
. It is shown in
[
21
, Lemma 2.14]
that
\equiv
is a congruence on
\mathsf{FC}(S)
, and so one can form the quotient semigroup
\mathsf{FC}(S)/\equiv
. Now, define the
distributive completion
of
S
to be
\mathsf{D}(S):=\mathsf{FC}(S/\leftrightarrow)/\equiv
, and define
S
to be
pre-Boolean
if
\mathsf{D}(S)
is Boolean.

Proposition 3.5
.

Every graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup is pre-Boolean and separative.

Proof.

Let
S
be a
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup. It follows immediately from Definition
2.1
that
E(S)
is a Boolean
\land
-semigroup. It is shown in the proof of the Duality Theorem
[
21
, Section 2.4]
that every Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup is isomorphic to its distributive completion, and so
E(S)\cong\mathsf{D}(E(S))
. Thus
\mathsf{D}(E(S))
is Boolean, and so
E(S)
is pre-Boolean. It follows that
S
is pre-Boolean, by
[
21
, Theorem 1.5(1)]
, which says that an inverse
\land
-semigroup is pre-Boolean if and only if its semilattice of idempotents is.

To prove that
S
is separative, let
s,t\in S
. As noted above,
s\leq t
implies that
s\to t
. For the other direction, suppose that
s\to t
and
s\not\leq t
. Then, by Lemma
3.2
(2), there exists
r\in S\setminus\{0\}
such that
r\leq s
and
r\land t=0
, contradicting
s\to t
. Thus
s\to t
implies that
s\leq t
, and so
s\leq t
if and only if
s\to t
.
∎

4.
Examples of Graded-Boolean Inverse Semigroups

Let us next discuss a few ways to construct graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups. If
S
is a Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups that happens to be
\Gamma
-graded, for some group
\Gamma
, then
S
is certainly
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean. However, a graded-Boolean
\land
-inverse semigroup need not be Boolean, as we show in Proposition
4.1
. To state that result we first recall (graded) symmetric inverse monoids.

Let
X
be a nonempty set. For any
A,B\subseteq X
, a bijective function
\phi:A\rightarrow B
is called a
partial symmetry
of
X
. Here we let
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi):=A
and
\operatorname{Im}(\phi):=B
. The set
\mathcal{I}(X)
of all partial symmetries of
X
is an inverse semigroup, with respect to composition of relations, known as the
symmetric inverse monoid
. Specifically, for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
,
\phi\psi
is taken to be the composite of
\phi
and
\psi
as functions, restricted to the domain
\psi^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}(\psi)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\phi))
. The empty function plays the role of the zero element in
\mathcal{I}(X)
. It is easy to see that
\phi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
is idempotent if and only if
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)=\operatorname{Im}(\phi)
and
\phi(x)=x
for all
x\in\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)
. Moreover, for all
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
we have
\phi\leq\psi
if and only if
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)\subseteq\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)
, and
\phi(x)=\psi(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)
. It is noted in
[
20
, Example 2.25]
that
\mathcal{I}(X)
is a Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup. However, the only possible grading on
\mathcal{I}(X)
is the trivial one, provided
|X|\geq 3
, where
|X|
denotes the cardinality of
X
(see
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
), and so
\mathcal{I}(X)
is generally not graded-Boolean in any interesting way. However, there are natural graded-Boolean subsemigroups of
\mathcal{I}(X)
, which we describe next.

Given a set
X
and a group
\Gamma
, we say that
X
is
\Gamma
-
graded
if there is a map
\phi:X\to\Gamma
, and let
X_{\alpha}:=\phi^{-1}(\alpha)
for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
. We say that a grading
\phi:X\to\Gamma
on
X
is
trivial
if
\phi(x)=\phi(y)
for all
x,y\in X
. For each nonempty
Y\subseteq X
and
\alpha\in\Gamma
, let
Y_{\alpha}:=Y\cap X_{\alpha}
. Also, for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
let

\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}:=\big\{\phi\in\mathcal{I}(X)\,\big|\,\phi(\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)_{\beta})\subseteq X_{\alpha\beta}\text{ for all }\beta\in\Gamma\big\},

(4.1)

and set

\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X):=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}.

(4.2)

According to
[
12
, Proposition 5.3]
,
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is a
\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup, with respect to the above grading. Moreover, it is shown in
[
12
, Proposition 5.4]
that every
\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup can be embedded in
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
, for some
\Gamma
-graded set
X
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a nonempty
\,\Gamma
-graded set.

(1)

\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup.

(2)

If
\,|X|\geq 3
, then
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is Boolean if and only if the grading on
X
is trivial.

Proof.

(1) Let
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
be nonzero, let

Y=\{x\in\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)\mid\phi(x)=\psi(x)\},

and let
\chi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
be such that
\operatorname{Dom}(\chi)=Y=\operatorname{Im}(\chi)
and
\chi(x)=\phi(x)=\psi(x)
for all
x\in Y
. Then clearly
\chi=\phi\land\psi
. Moreover, if
Y\neq\emptyset
(in which case
\chi\neq 0
), then necessarily
\deg(\phi)=\deg(\psi)
and
\chi\in\mathcal{I}(X)_{\deg(\phi)}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
(e.g., by Lemma
3.1
(2)). It follows that
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is an inverse
\land
-semigroup.

To show that
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean, we first note that for a pair of elements
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
, being compatible means that
\phi
and
\psi
agree on
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)
, while
\phi^{-1}
and
\psi^{-1}
agree on
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\cap\operatorname{Im}(\psi)
. In this situation, the join
\chi\in\mathcal{I}(X)
of
\phi
and
\psi
is defined by
\operatorname{Dom}(\chi)=\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)\cup\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)
,
\operatorname{Im}(\chi)=\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\cup\operatorname{Im}(\psi)
,
\chi(x)=\phi(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)
, and
\chi(x)=\psi(x)
for all
x\in\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)
. Now, if
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}
are compatible, for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
, then clearly
\phi\lor\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}
. It follows that
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
has joins for nonempty finite compatible subsets of
\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in\Gamma
. Since meets and joins in
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
, and hence also in
E(\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X))
, correspond to taking intersections and unions of domains, respectively, it follows that
E(\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X))
is a Boolean algebra (with the identity function
X\to X
as the top element), as is each of its principal order ideals. Finally, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
satisfies the conditions in Definition
2.1
(2), and is therefore
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean.

(2) Suppose that the grading on
X
is trivial. Then
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)=\mathcal{I}(X)_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{I}(X)
, and so
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is Boolean by
[
20
, Example 2.25]
. The same conclusion also follows from (1).

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
is Boolean and
|X|\geq 3
. Suppose further that
X_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
and
X_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
for distinct
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
. Let
x\in X_{\alpha}
,
y\in X_{\beta}
, and
z\in X\setminus\{x,y\}
be arbitrary, with
z\in X_{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Interchanging the roles of
x
and
y
, if necessary, we may assume that
\gamma\neq\beta
. Now let
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
be the unique functions such that
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)=\{x\}
,
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)=\{x\}
,
\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)=\{y\}
, and
\operatorname{Im}(\psi)=\{z\}
. Then
\phi\in\mathcal{I}(X)_{\varepsilon}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{I}(X)_{\gamma\beta^{-1}}
. Since
\operatorname{Dom}(\phi)\cap\operatorname{Dom}(\psi)=\emptyset
and
\operatorname{Im}(\phi)\cap\operatorname{Im}(\psi)=\emptyset
,
\phi
and
\psi
are compatible, and so must have a join
\phi\lor\psi
in
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
, since we assumed it to be Boolean. Then, by Lemma
3.1
(2),

\varepsilon=\deg(\phi)=\deg(\phi\lor\psi)=\deg(\psi)=\gamma\beta^{-1},

and so
\gamma=\beta
, contrary to hypothesis. Therefore, it cannot be the case that
X_{\alpha}\neq\emptyset
for more than one
\alpha\in\Gamma
, and so the grading on
X
is trivial.
∎

The previous proposition describes Boolean
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
when
|X|\geq 3
; let us briefly discuss the other cases. If
|X|=1
, then only trivial gradings are possible, and so
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)=\mathcal{I}(X)
is Boolean. If
|X|=2
, then it is possible for
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
to be both Boolean and non-trivially graded. We omit the details here, however.

We can build further examples of graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups using the distributive completion (see Section
3
for the notation), as well as a graded version thereof.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded inverse
\land
-semigroup. Let
\,\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
denote the set of all homogeneous elements of
\,\mathsf{FC}(S)
, let
\,\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
denote the restriction of
\,\equiv
to
\,\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
, and let
\,\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S):=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S/\leftrightarrow)/\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
.

(1)

\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
and
\,\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
are
\,\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroups.

(2)

If
S
has no zero-divisors, then
\,\mathsf{FC}(S)=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
and
\,\mathsf{D}(S)=\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
.

(3)

If
S
is pre-Boolean, then
\,\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup.

Proof.

(1) Clearly,
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is closed under multiplication and inverses, and hence is an inverse subsemigroup of
\mathsf{FC}(S)
. Also,
I\mapsto\alpha
, where
I\neq\{0\}
, and
\deg(s)=\alpha\in\Gamma
for any nonzero
s\in I
, defines a
\Gamma
-grading on
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
. Since
\equiv
is a congruence on
\mathsf{FC}(S)
, it is easy to see that
\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
is also a congruence, and so
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)/\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
is an inverse semigroup.

Next, we show that
S^{\prime}:=S/\leftrightarrow
is a
\Gamma
-graded semigroup. If
s,t\in S\setminus\{0\}
are such that
s\to t
, then, in particular,
s\land t\neq 0
. Hence, by Lemma
3.1
(2),
\deg(s)=\deg(s\land t)=\deg(t)
. Thus if we denote by
[s]
the congruence class of
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
under
\leftrightarrow
, then every element of
[s]
has the same degree. Therefore, we can turn
S^{\prime}
into a
\Gamma
-graded semigroup via
[s]\mapsto\alpha
, where
s\neq 0
, and
\deg(t)=\alpha\in\Gamma
for any
t\in[s]
.

Since
S^{\prime}
is
\Gamma
-graded,
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})
is
\Gamma
-graded as well (as shown in the first paragraph). Now, let
I,J\in\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})
be such that
I\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}J
. Then, either
I
and
J
are both
\{0\}
, or both nonzero. In the latter case, by the argument in the previous paragraph,
\deg(I)=\deg(J)
. This, once again, allows us turn
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})/\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
into a
\Gamma
-graded semigroup, as above.

(2) Suppose that
S
has no zero-divisors, and let
I\in\mathsf{FC}(S)\setminus\{\{0\}\}
. Since
S
has no zero-divisors, it also has no nonzero orthogonal elements. Thus every nonzero element of
I
has the same degree, by Lemma
3.1
(1). That is,
I
is homogeneous, and hence
I\in\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
. Since
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is a subsemigroup of
\mathsf{FC}(S)
, we conclude that
\mathsf{FC}(S)=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
. Given that
S
has no zero-divisors, neither does
S^{\prime}
, and so our argument also shows that
\mathsf{FC}(S^{\prime})=\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})
. It follows that
\mathsf{D}(S)=\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
as well.

(3) Suppose that
S
is pre-Boolean. Since
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})
is a subsemigroup of
\mathsf{FC}(S^{\prime})
, and since
\equiv^{\operatorname{gr}}
is the restriction of
\equiv
from
\mathsf{FC}(S^{\prime})
to
\mathsf{FC}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S^{\prime})
, we can view
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
as a subsemigroup of
\mathsf{D}(S)
.

Now let
\alpha\in\Gamma
, and let
X\subseteq\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)_{\alpha}
be a nonempty finite compatible subset. By
[
21
, Lemma 2.10]
(see also
[
21
, Lemma 2.17]
), the natural partial order on
\mathsf{D}(S)
is simply set inclusion. Hence the meet of
X
in
\mathsf{D}(S)
is
\bigcap X
, while join is
\bigcup X
. Both are elements of
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)_{\alpha}
, since
X\subseteq\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)_{\alpha}
, and therefore, necessarily,
\bigcap X
and
\bigcup X
are the meet and join, respectively, of
X
in
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
. Thus
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is an inverse
\land
-semigroup and satisfies condition (1) in Definition
2.1
. It also follows easily that
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
satisfies condition (2) in Definition
2.1
, since
\mathsf{D}(S)
does. Since
E(\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S))
can be viewed as a subsemigroup of
E(\mathsf{D}(S))
, considerations analogous to those above show that the properties in condition (3) of Definition
2.1
pass from
E(\mathsf{D}(S))
to
E(\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S))
. (Note that, by Lemma
3.1
(2), the principal order ideals of
\mathsf{D}(S)
and
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
, and hence also of
E(\mathsf{D}(S))
and
E(\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S))
, coincide.) Therefore
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean.
∎

Graph inverse semigroups, first introduced in
[
3
]
, constitute a rich and well-studied class of inverse semigroups, constructed from directed graphs (see
[
25
]
for an overview). According to
[
21
, Theorem 4.10]
, graph inverse semigroups, over graphs in which each vertex has finite in-degree, are pre-Boolean. These semigroups are also
\mathbb{Z}
-graded, where
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the group of the integers (see
[
12
, Section 8.1]
for the details). Hence, by Proposition
4.2
(3),
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, for any graph inverse semigroup
S
constructed from a graph where each vertex has finite in-degree.

Generally speaking, a graph inverse semigroup has zero-divisors, and so
\mathsf{D}(S)
need not coincide with
\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(S)
, for such a semigroup
S
. However, the graph inverse semigroup corresponding to the graph with one vertex and one loop does not have zero-divisors. More specifically, this particular semigroup, known as the
bicyclic semigroup
, can be presented by generators and relations as the monoid

P_{1}:=\langle x,y\mid xy=1\rangle\cup\{0\},

though it is usually viewed in the literature as an inverse monoid without zero. By Proposition
4.2
(2,3),
\mathsf{D}(P_{1})=\mathsf{D}^{\operatorname{gr}}(P_{1})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup.

We conclude this section by recording a (fairly trivial) way of creating graded-Boolean semigroups from existing ones.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup.

(1)

If
S
is an inverse
\land
-semigroup, then so is
S_{\varepsilon}
.

(2)

If
S
is
(
\Gamma
-graded-
)
Boolean, then
S_{\varepsilon}
is both Boolean and
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
S
is an inverse
\land
-semigroup. Since, as noted in Section
2.2
,
S_{\varepsilon}
is an inverse semigroup, it suffices to show that each pair of elements in
S_{\varepsilon}
has a meet. Letting
s,t\in S_{\varepsilon}
, we have
s\land t\in S
. If
s\land t=0
, then
s\land t\in S_{\varepsilon}
. If
s\land t\neq 0
, then
s\neq 0\neq t
, and so Lemma
3.1
(2) implies that
s\land t\in S_{\varepsilon}
. In either case
s\land t
is necessarily the meet of
s
and
t
in
S_{\varepsilon}
. It follows that
S_{\varepsilon}
is an inverse
\land
-semigroup.

(2) Suppose that
S
is either Boolean or
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean. Then
S_{\varepsilon}
has joins for nonempty finite compatible subsets, by Lemma
3.1
(4). Since
S_{\varepsilon}
is a (homogeneous) subsemigroup of
S
, it certainly satisfies the conditions in Definition
2.1
(2). Since
E(S_{\varepsilon})=E(S)
, we conclude that
S_{\varepsilon}
is Boolean, and hence also
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean.
∎

5.
Duality

This section is devoted to proving our main result, that the categories
\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
and
\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
, of
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroups and
\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoids, respectively (see Sections
2.3
and
2.4
for the details), are dually equivalent. The construction is divided into a number of lemmas and propositions. We begin by defining the main ingredients.

Given an inverse semigroup
S
, let

\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S):=\{X\mid X\text{~is an ultrafilter on~}(S,\leq)\},

where
\leq
is the natural partial order. For all
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
define

X^{-1}:=\{x^{-1}\mid x\in X\},

XY:=\{xy\mid x\in X,y\in Y\},

and

X\cdot Y:=(XY)^{\uparrow}.

According to
[
21
, Section 2.1]
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is a groupoid with respect to the operation
\cdot
, where
(X,Y)\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)^{(2)}
provided that
X^{-1}\cdot X=Y\cdot Y^{-1}
, and where elements of the form
X^{-1}\cdot X
(
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S))
constitute the unit space.

Given an ample groupoid
\mathscr{G}
, let

\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G}):=\{X\mid X\text{~is a compact slice of~}\mathscr{G}\}.

Then
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
is an inverse semigroup under the operations given in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) (see
[
26
, Proposition 2.2.4]
), with
\emptyset
as the zero element. Supposing that
\mathscr{G}
is
\Gamma
-graded, for some group
\Gamma
, we say that a slice
X\subseteq\mathscr{G}
is
homogeneous
if
X\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
, and set

\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G}):=\{X\mid X\text{~is a homogeneous compact slice of~}\mathscr{G}\}.

Since
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha^{-1}}=\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}^{-1}
for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
, it follows that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is an inverse subsemigroup of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
. Moreover, it is easy to see that defining
\phi:\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\backslash\{\emptyset\}\rightarrow\Gamma
by
\phi(X)=\alpha
, whenever
X\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
, turns
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
into a
\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroup. The natural partial order on
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
is simply set inclusion (see
[
26
, Section 2.2]
). Since the idempotent slices of
\mathscr{G}
are subsets of
\mathscr{G}_{\epsilon}
, and are therefore homogeneous, the natural partial order on
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is also set inclusion. Finally, note that since each
\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
is clopen, and since
\mathscr{G}
is ample, the elements of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
form a base for the topology on
\mathscr{G}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoid. Then
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, with intersection as the meet and union as the join.

Proof.

According to
[
21
, Lemma 2.32(4)]
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
is a Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, with intersection as the meet and union as the join. Since the intersection of two homogeneous (compact) slices of
\mathscr{G}
is clearly homogeneous, it follows that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is an inverse
\land
-subsemigroup of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
. Since
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
has joins of nonempty finite compatible sets,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
must have joins of nonempty finite compatible homogenous sets, by Lemma
3.1
(4). Finally, since
E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G}))=E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G}))
, we see, from Definition
2.1
, that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is a
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(cf.
[
21
]
, Lemma 2.36)
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoid, and for each
y\in\mathscr{G}
let
\mathcal{X}_{y}:=\{Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\mid y\in Y\}
.

(1)

\mathcal{X}_{y}
is an ultrafilter for each
y\in\mathscr{G}
.

(2)

Every ultrafilter in
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is of the form
\mathcal{X}_{y}
, for some
y\in\mathscr{G}
.

(3)

\mathcal{X}_{y}^{-1}=\mathcal{X}_{y^{-1}}
for all
y\in\mathscr{G}
.

(4)

For all
x,y\in\mathscr{G}
, if
(x,y)\in\mathscr{G}^{(2)}
, then
\mathcal{X}_{x}\cdot\mathcal{X}_{y}=\mathcal{X}_{xy}
.

(5)

\mathcal{X}_{x}=\mathcal{X}_{y}
if and only if
x=y
, for all
x,y\in\mathscr{G}
.

Proof.

These statements can be proved in essentially the same way as their non-graded analogues in
[
21
, Lemma 2.36]
, but we provide the arguments for completeness.

(1) By Lemma
5.1
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is closed under finite intersections. Note also that
\mathcal{X}_{y}\neq\emptyset
for each
y\in\mathscr{G}
, since the elements of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
form a base for the topology on
\mathscr{G}
. Then it follows, from the fact that the natural partial order on this inverse semigroup is set inclusion, that
\mathcal{X}_{y}
is a filter for each
y\in\mathscr{G}
. Now, according to
[
21
, Lemma 2.6(2)]
, a filter
F
in an inverse
\land
-semigroup
S
is an ultrafilter if and only if
F
contains all
s\in S
such that
s\land t\neq 0
for all
t\in F
. We shall use this criterion on
\mathcal{X}_{y}
.

Thus suppose that
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is such that
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
for all
Y\in\mathcal{X}_{y}
. Note that since
X
is compact and
\mathscr{G}
is Hausdorff,
X
is necessarily closed. Thus, to conclude that
X\in\mathcal{X}_{y}
, it suffices to show that
X\cap N\neq\emptyset
for all open neighbourhoods
N
of
y
. Since the elements of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
form a base for the topology on
\mathscr{G}
, there exists
Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
such that
y\in Y\subseteq N
. But then
Y\in\mathcal{X}_{y}
, and so
X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
, by hypothesis. Hence
X\cap N\neq\emptyset
, as desired.

(2) Let
\mathcal{F}
be an ultrafilter on
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
, let
X\in\mathcal{F}
, and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{X\cap Y\mid Y\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is a filter,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, and any finite intersection of elements of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is nonempty. Moreover, each element of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is closed, as a compact subset of a Hausdorff space. Thus
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
consists of closed subsets of the compact set
X
, and has the finite intersection property. It follows that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
has a nonempty intersection. Taking
y
to be any element of that intersection, we see that
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{y}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is an ultrafilter, we have
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{X}_{y}
.

(3) Let
y\in\mathscr{G}
. According to
[
21
, Lemma 2.36(3)]
,

\{Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\mid y\in Y\}^{-1}=\{Y^{-1}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\mid y\in Y\}=\{Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\mid y^{-1}\in Y\}.

Restricting to homogeneous compact slices gives
\mathcal{X}_{y}^{-1}=\mathcal{X}_{y^{-1}}
.

(4) Let
x,y\in\mathscr{G}
be such that
(x,y)\in\mathscr{G}^{(2)}
, and let
X\in\mathcal{X}_{x}
and
Y\in\mathcal{X}_{y}
. Then
xy\in XY\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
, and so
XY\in\mathcal{X}_{xy}
. It follows that

\mathcal{X}_{x}\mathcal{X}_{y}=\{XY\mid X\in\mathcal{X}_{x},Y\in\mathcal{X}_{y}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{xy},

and hence
\mathcal{X}_{x}\cdot\mathcal{X}_{y}=(\mathcal{X}_{x}\mathcal{X}_{y})^{\uparrow}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{xy}
, since
\mathcal{X}_{xy}
is a filter, by (1). Finally, since
\mathcal{X}_{x}\cdot\mathcal{X}_{y}
is an ultrafilter, we conclude that
\mathcal{X}_{x}\cdot\mathcal{X}_{y}=\mathcal{X}_{xy}
.

(5) Certainly, if
x=y
, then
\mathcal{X}_{x}=\mathcal{X}_{y}
. For the converse, suppose that
x,y\in\mathscr{G}
are distinct. Since
\mathscr{G}
is Hausdorff, and since the elements of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
form a base for the topology on
\mathscr{G}
, there exist disjoint
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
such that
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. Then
X\in\mathcal{X}_{x}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{y}
, and so
\mathcal{X}_{x}\neq\mathcal{X}_{y}
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\phi:\mathscr{G}\to\mathscr{H}
be a morphism in
\,\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
. Then
\phi^{-1}:\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is a morphism in
\,\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
.

Proof.

According to
[
21
, Proposition 2.37]
,
\phi^{-1}:\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{H})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
is a proper homomorphism that preserves meets and restricts to a homomorphism
E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{H}))\to E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G}))
, and hence also
E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H}))\to E(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G}))
, that preserves complements in principal order ideals. Now, let
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H})
, and suppose that
X\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
. Since
\phi
is graded, we must have
\phi^{-1}(X)\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
. It follows that
\phi^{-1}
restricts to a graded homomorphism
\phi^{-1}:\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
that preserves meets. Thus, to conclude that
\phi^{-1}
is a morphism in
\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
, it suffices to show that it is proper.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an ultrafilter on
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
(1,2),
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{X}_{y}
, for some
y\in\mathscr{G}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{\phi(y)}
is an ultrafilter on
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H})
. The inverse image of
\mathcal{F}
under
\phi^{-1}
consists of all
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{H})
such that
\phi^{-1}(X)\in\mathcal{F}
. But
\phi^{-1}(X)\in\mathcal{F}
if and only if
y\in\phi^{-1}(X)
if and only if
\phi(y)\in X
if and only if
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\phi(y)}
. Thus the inverse image of
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{X}_{y}
under
\phi^{-1}
is the ultrafilter
\mathcal{X}_{\phi(y)}
, from which it follows that
\phi^{-1}
is proper.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Sending each object
\mathscr{G}
to
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
and each morphism
\phi
to
\phi^{-1}
defines a contravariant functor
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}:\operatorname{\bf GrGp}\to\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
.

Proof.

The mapping in question clearly respects identity morphisms and composition of morphisms. Thus the desired conclusion follows from Lemmas
5.1
and
5.3
.
∎

Lemma 5.5

Lemma 5.6

Let
S

x\in X
is arbitrary.

(1)

\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is a Hausdorff étale groupoid in the topology generated by basic open sets of the form
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, as in Lemma
5.5
.

(2)

\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is a
\,\Gamma
-graded groupoid, with respect to

.

(3)

\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S_{\varepsilon})
.

(4)

If

\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)

Proof.

(1) According to
[
21
, Proposition 2.33]
, for each inverse
\land

S
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is a Hausdorff étale groupoid in the topology described in the statement.

(2) We first note that
\psi
is well-defined, by Lemma

(3). Now, let
(X,Y)\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)^{(2)}

x\in X
and

be arbitrary. Then

, and

is an ultrafilter. Thus,

is open for all

(3) Since
\phi(x)=\varepsilon
for all

such that

, we have

, from which it follows that
X
is an ultrafilter on

, and so
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S_{\varepsilon})
. For the opposite inclusion, suppose that
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S_{\varepsilon})
. Since, by Lemma

(3), any filter on

must consist of elements of

, we conclude that

is an ultrafilter on

\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)_{\varepsilon}\supseteq\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S_{\varepsilon})
, and so
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S_{\varepsilon})

(4) According to

, Theorem 2.34]
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
a Hausdorff ample groupoid for any pre-Boolean
\land
-semigroup
S
(with respect to the topology described in (1)). The desired conclusion now follows from Proposition

.
∎

Lemma 5.7

(cf.
[
20
]
, Lemma 2.21(5,7,9-12))
.

Let
S
be a
\,\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse
\land
-semigroup, and view
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
as a topological groupoid in the topology generated by basic open sets of the form
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, as in Lemma
5.5
.

(1)

\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}
if and only if
s\leq t
, for all
s,t\in S
.

(2)

If
s\lor t
exists, then
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\mathcal{Y}_{s\lor t}
, for all
s,t\in S
.

(3)

Let
s,t_{i}\in S
be such that each
t_{i}\leq s
, with
i\in I
, for some indexing set
I
. Then
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
if and only if
\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
.

(4)

Let
u,v_{i}\in E(S)
, with
i\in I
, for some indexing set
I
. If
\mathcal{Y}_{u}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
, then
\mathcal{Y}_{u}=\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
for some finite
J\subseteq I
.

(5)

\mathcal{Y}_{s}
is compact for each
s\in S
.

(6)

Each nonempty homogeneous compact slice of
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is of the form
\,\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Y}_{s_{i}}
, for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
and compatible
\,\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\subseteq S_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

The proofs of most of these statements are nearly identical to their (non-graded) monoid analogues in
[
20
, Lemma 2.21]
, but we include them for completeness.

(1) Since
S
is separative (see Section
3
for the definition), by Proposition
3.5
, this follows from
[
21
, Lemma 2.11(1)]
, which says that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{t}
if and only if
s\to t
, for all
s,t\in S
.

(2) Let
s,t\in S
, and suppose that
s\lor t
exists. Then
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s\lor t}
, by (1). Also, by Lemma
3.4
, each
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s\lor t}
contains either
s
or
t
, and so
\mathcal{Y}_{s\lor t}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{t}
.

(3) Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
. Since
t_{i}\leq s
for any
i\in I
, if
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
, then
s\in X
, and so
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, showing that
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. For the opposite inclusion, suppose that
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. Then
s\in X
, and so
s^{-1}s\in(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
, which is an ultrafilter, by Lemma
3.3
(3). Thus
(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}
, and so, by hypothesis,
(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
for some
i\in I
. It follows that
s^{-1}t_{i}\in(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow}
. Now,
(s(X^{-1}X)^{\uparrow})^{\uparrow}=X
, by Lemma
3.3
(2), and hence
ss^{-1}t_{i}\in X
. But
ss^{-1}t_{i}\leq t_{i}
, and so
t_{i}\in X
, showing that
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
. Therefore
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
. Let
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
for some
i\in I
. Then
s^{-1}t_{i}\in X
. Since
t_{i}\leq s
, we have
s^{-1}t_{i}\leq s^{-1}s
, and hence
s^{-1}s\in X
. Therefore
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}
, and so
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}
. For the opposite inclusion, let
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}
. Then
s^{-1}s\in X
. It follows, by Lemma
3.3
(4), that
(sX)^{\uparrow}
is an ultrafilter, containing
s
, and so
(sX)^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
. Hence
(sX)^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
for some
i\in I
, and therefore
sx\leq t_{i}
for some
x\in X
. Then
s^{-1}sx\leq s^{-1}t_{i}
. Since
s^{-1}s,x\in X
, and
X
is a semigroup, by
[
21
, Lemma 2.8(2)]
, we have
s^{-1}sx\in X
, and so
s^{-1}t_{i}\in X
. Thus
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
, and so
\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}t_{i}}
.

(4) Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}_{u}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
. Then
v_{i}\leq u
for each
i\in I
, by (1). Since each principal order ideal of
E(S)
is a Boolean algebra, for each
i\in I
there exists
v_{i}^{\prime}\in\{u\}^{\downarrow}
such that
v_{i}\land v_{i}^{\prime}=0
and
v_{i}\lor v_{i}^{\prime}=u
. We claim that
\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}=\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}^{\prime}}
. Any ultrafilter containing
{v_{i}^{\prime}}
must contain
u
, since
v_{i}^{\prime}\leq u
, but not
v_{i}
, since otherwise it would contain
0
. Thus
\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
. For the opposite inclusion, suppose that
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
. Then
v_{i}\lor v_{i}^{\prime}\in X
but
v_{i}\notin X
, and so
v_{i}^{\prime}\in X
, by Lemma
3.4
. Thus
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}^{\prime}}
, and so
\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}=\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}^{\prime}}
.

Now, seeking a contradiction, suppose that for every finite
J\subseteq I
we have
\mathcal{Y}_{u}\neq\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
. Let
J\subseteq I
be finite and nonempty. Then

\emptyset\neq\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\bigg(\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}\bigg)=\bigcap_{i\in J}(\mathcal{Y}_{u}\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}})=\bigcap_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{Y}_{w},

where
w=\bigwedge_{i\in J}v_{i}^{\prime}
, by Lemma
5.5
(1), and so
\bigwedge_{i\in J}v_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
. Thus every finite nonempty subset of
\{v_{i}^{\prime}\mid i\in I\}
has a nonzero meet. It follows that
\{v_{i}^{\prime}\mid i\in I\}
is contained in an ultrafilter
Z
on
S
. As in the previous paragraph, we must have
u\in Z
, but
v_{i}\notin Z
for all
i\in I
. Thus
Z\in\mathcal{Y}_{u}
but
Z\notin\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
, contrary to hypothesis. Therefore
\mathcal{Y}_{u}=\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{v_{i}}
for some finite
J\subseteq I
.

(5) Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
, for some
s,t_{i}\in S
and indexing set
I
. Then

\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}(\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}})=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s\land t_{i}},

by Lemma
5.5
(1). Hence,
\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}(s\land t_{i})}
, by (3). Since
s\land t_{i}\leq s
, it follows that each
s^{-1}(s\land t_{i})
is an idempotent. Thus, by (4),
\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}s}=\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{s^{-1}(s\land t_{i})}
for some finite
J\subseteq I
. Hence
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{s\land t_{i}}
, by (3). Using Lemma
5.5
(1) once more, we have
\mathcal{Y}_{s}=\bigcup_{i\in J}(\mathcal{Y}_{s}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}})
, and so
\mathcal{Y}_{s}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in J}\mathcal{Y}_{t_{i}}
. It follows that
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
is compact for each
s\in S
.

(6) Let
X
be a nonempty homogeneous compact slice of
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
, where the grading on
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is as in Lemma
5.6
. Since
X
is an open and compact subset of
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
, we have
X=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Y}_{s_{i}}
for some
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in S
. Since
X\neq\emptyset
, we may assume that each of the
s_{i}
is nonzero. Since
X
is a slice,
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
is compatible, by Lemma
5.5
(5). (Recall that
S
is separative, by Proposition
3.5
.) Now, for each
i\in I
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{s_{i}}
, we have
\deg(Y)=\deg(s_{i})
, by Lemma
5.6
. Since
X
is homogenous, it follows that
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\subseteq S_{\alpha}\setminus\{0\}
for some
\alpha\in\Gamma
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\phi:S\to T
be a morphism in
\,\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
. Then
\phi^{-1}:\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)\to\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is a morphism in
\,\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is a proper morphism,
\phi^{-1}
, by definition, takes ultrafilters of
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)
to ultrafilters of
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
, and therefore the function
\phi^{-1}:\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)\to\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is well-defined.

Next, we show that
\phi^{-1}
is graded. If
S
is the zero semigroup, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)=\{\emptyset\}
, and so
\phi^{-1}
is graded, vacuously. The same is true if
T
is the zero semigroup. We may therefore assume that both
S
and
T
are nonzero. Now let
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)
, let
\alpha=\deg(X)
(see Lemma
5.6
), and let
s\in\phi^{-1}(X)
(which is necessarily nonzero). Since
\phi
is graded, and
\phi(s)\in X
, it must be the case that
\deg(s)=\alpha
. Thus, by Lemma
5.6
, we have
\deg(\phi^{-1}(X))=\alpha
. It follows that
\phi^{-1}
is a graded map.

To show that
\phi^{-1}
is continuous, let
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
(
s\in S
) be a basic open set in
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
(see Lemma
5.6
). Then
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)
is in the inverse image of
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
under
\phi^{-1}
if and only if
\phi^{-1}(X)\in\mathcal{Y}_{s}
if and only if

s\in\phi^{-1}(X)
if and only if
\phi(s)\in X
if and only if
X\in\mathcal{Y}_{\phi(s)}
. It follows that
\phi^{-1}
is continuous.

To show that
\phi^{-1}
is proper, let
X
be a compact subset of
\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
, and let
Y\subseteq\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(T)
be the inverse image of
X
under
\phi^{-1}
. We shall show that
Y
is compact. We have
X\subseteq\bigcup_{s\in S}\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, and so
X\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Y}_{s_{i}}
for some (finite list)
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in S
, since
X
is compact. As shown in the previous paragraph, the inverse image of each
\mathcal{Y}_{s_{i}}
under
\phi^{-1}
is
\mathcal{Y}_{\phi(s_{i})}
, and so
Y\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Y}_{\phi(s_{i})}
. As a compact subset of a Hausdorff space,
X
is closed, and so
Y
is closed as well, since
\phi^{-1}
is continuous. Also, as a finite union of compact subsets (see Lemma
5.7
(5)),
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Y}_{\phi(s_{i})}
is compact. Therefore, being a closed subset of a compact set,
Y
is compact.

To conclude that
\phi^{-1}
is a morphism in
\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
, it now suffices to show that it is a covering functor. This is shown in
[
20
, Proposition 2.15]
, in the case where
S
and
T
are Boolean inverse monoids. However, the proof, word-for-word, applies in our situation as well.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Sending each object
S
to
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
and each morphism
\phi
to
\phi^{-1}
defines a contravariant functor
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}:\operatorname{\bf GrIS}\to\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
. Here each
\,\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S)
is viewed as a topological groupoid in the topology generated by basic open sets of the form
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
, as in Lemma
5.5
.

Proof.

The mapping in question clearly respects identity morphisms and composition of morphisms. Thus the desired conclusion follows from Lemmas
5.6
(1,2,4) and
5.8
.
∎

Now that we have constructed the desired functors between the categories
\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
and
\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
, to conclude that they are dually equivalent, it remains to supply the relevant natural isomorphisms.

Lemma 5.10
.

Defining a function

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathscr{G}}:\mathscr{G}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G}))

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathcal{X}_{x}

\,1_{\operatorname{\bf GrGp}}
denotes the identity functor on

Proof.

Lemma

implies that each

is a bijective covering functor. Also, for each

and each

such that

, we have
\deg(X)=\deg(x)
, since

is homogeneous. Then, by Lemma
5.6
,
\deg(\mathcal{X}_{x})=\deg(x)
, and so
\mu_{\mathscr{G}}
is graded. Thus, to conclude that

is an isomorphism in
\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
it suffices to prove that it is open and continuous.

To show that
\mu_{\mathscr{G}}
is open, let

be a homogeneous compact slice of
\mathscr{G}
(i.e., a basic open set). Then

. Now,

for an ultrafilter

on

. That is,

To show that

is continuous.

Finally, for all morphisms

\operatorname{\bf GrGp}

and

Lemma 5.11
.

Defining a function

\displaystyle\nu_{S}:S

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\operatorname{\mathsf{G}}(S))

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathcal{Y}_{s}

.

Theorem 5.12
.

The categories
\,\operatorname{\bf GrIS}
and
\,\operatorname{\bf GrGp}
are dually equivalent, via the functors defined in Propositions

and

, and natural transformations defined in Lemmas
5.10
and

.

Let us next give an illustration of this theorem.

Example 5.13
.

For any set
X
, we can view
X\times X
as a groupoid, with unit space
X
, and morphism set
X\times X
. Here
\bm{d}((x,y)):=y
and
\bm{r}((x,y)):=x
for all
(x,y)\in X\times X
, and
(x,y)(y,z):=(x,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

According to
[
20
, Example 2.25]
, for a finite nonempty set
X
, the discrete (Hausdorff ample) groupoid
X\times X
corresponds to the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}(X)
, in the Lawson-Stone duality (see
[
20
]
or
[
21
, Theorem 1.4]
). More precisely, (compact) slices of
X\times X
are subsets
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in I\}
of
X\times X
, such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
and
y_{i}\neq y_{j}
for distinct
i,j\in I
. Each slice
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in I\}
can be viewed as a partial symmetry
\phi:\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\to\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
, with
\phi(x_{i})=y_{i}
for all
i\in I
, and the multiplication in
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(X\times X)
corresponds precisely to that in
\mathcal{I}(X)
. Thus
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(X\times X)
can be identified with
\mathcal{I}(X)
.

Now suppose that
X
is a
\Gamma
-graded finite nonempty set, for some group
\Gamma
, via
\phi:X\to\Gamma
. Then it is easy to see that
X\times X
becomes a discrete
\Gamma
-graded groupoid, via
(x,y)\mapsto\phi(x)^{-1}\phi(y)
. In the duality of Theorem
5.12
,
X\times X
corresponds to
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
(see Proposition
4.1
). To see this, note that
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X\times X)
consists of slices
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in I\}
of
X\times X
, such that
\deg(x_{i})^{-1}\deg(y_{i})=\deg(x_{j})^{-1}\deg(y_{j})
for all
i,j\in I
. It follows that each element
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i\in I\}
of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X\times X)
corresponds to an element of
\mathcal{I}(X)_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha=\deg(x_{i})^{-1}\deg(y_{i})
for all

, in the above identification. So
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X\times X)\cong\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{gr}}(X)
as
\Gamma
-graded inverse semigroups.

6.
Rings

This final section is devoted to relating certain enveloping rings constructed from the semigroups
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
, discussed in Section
5
. We begin by recalling graded semigroup rings.

Let
R
be a (unital) ring, and
\Gamma
a group. We say that
R
is
\Gamma
-
graded
if
R=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Gamma}R_{\alpha}
, where the
R_{\alpha}
are additive subgroups of
R
, and
R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}\subseteq R_{\alpha\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
(with
R_{\alpha}R_{\beta}
denoting the set of all sums of elements of the form
rp
, for
r\in R_{\alpha}
and
p\in R_{\beta}
).

Next, given a semigroup
S
, we denote by
R[S]
the
contracted
semigroup ring of
S
(where the zero of
S
is identified with the zero of
RS
). We denote an arbitrary element of
R[S]
by
\sum_{s\in S}r^{(s)}s
, where
r^{(s)}\in R
, and all but finitely many of the
r^{(s)}
are zero. If
S
is
\Gamma
-graded, then it is easy to check that defining

R[S]_{\alpha}:=R[S_{\alpha}]=\Big\{\sum_{s\in S}r^{(s)}s\mid s\in S_{\alpha}\text{ whenever }r^{(s)}\neq 0\Big\},

for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
, turns
R[S]
into a
\Gamma
-graded ring. See
[
12
, Section 7]
for additional details.

Supposing that
S
is a
\Gamma
-graded-Boolean inverse semigroup, we define the
enveloping
R
-ring of
S
(or
R
-algebra
, in case
R
is commutative) as

R\langle S\rangle:=R[S]\big/\big\langle u+v-u\vee v\mid u,v\in S_{\alpha}\text{ orthogonal, }\alpha\in\Gamma\big\rangle.

The
\Gamma
-grading on
R[S]
induces a grading on
R\langle S\rangle
. The non-graded version of
R\langle S\rangle
(i.e., where the grading on
S
, and hence also on
R\langle S\rangle
, is trivial) is discussed in
[
31
, §6.3]
.

Given a
\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoid
\mathscr{G}
, the graded inverse semigroup
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is generally a proper subsemigroup of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
, whenever the grading on
\mathscr{G}
is nontrivial (i.e.,
\mathscr{G}\neq\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon}
). Our next result shows, however, that those two semigroups result in isomorphic enveloping rings.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
R
be a ring and
\mathscr{G}
a
\,\Gamma
-graded Hausdorff ample groupoid. Then

R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\cong R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle.

Proof.

We can define
\phi:R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})]\rightarrow R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})]
by letting
\phi(X)=X
for each
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
, and extending
R
-linearly to all of
R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})]
. Since
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is a subsemigroup of
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
, the map
\phi
is clearly an injective ring homomorphism. Since the join operation in both
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
is simply set union (see Lemma
5.1
),
\phi
reduces to a ring homomorphism
\phi:R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\rightarrow R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
.

Next, for each
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
and
\alpha\in\Gamma
, let
X_{\alpha}:=X\cap\mathscr{G}_{\alpha}
. Then
X=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}X_{\alpha}
, where the union is disjoint, and
X_{\alpha}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})
for each
\alpha\in\Gamma
. Since
X
is compact, only finitely many of the
X_{\alpha}
are nonempty. Define
\psi:R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})]\rightarrow R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})]
by letting
\psi(X)=\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}X_{\alpha}
for each
X\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
, and extending
R
-linearly to all of
R[\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})]
. We shall next show that
\psi
reduces to a function
\psi:R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\rightarrow R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
.

Let
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})
be orthogonal slices. If
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
are distinct, then
X
being a slice implies that
X_{\alpha}
and
X_{\beta}
have disjoint domain sets, and hence
X_{\alpha}^{-1}X_{\beta}=\emptyset
. Thus in
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
we have

\overline{X}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\overline{X_{\alpha}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\overline{X_{\alpha}},

where
\overline{X}
denotes the image of
X
in
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
, and likewise for
\overline{X_{\alpha}}
. The same considerations apply to
Y
and
X\cup Y
. Therefore in
R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
we have

\overline{X}+\overline{Y}-\overline{X\cup Y}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\big(\overline{X_{\alpha}}+\overline{Y_{\alpha}}-\overline{X_{\alpha}\cup Y_{\alpha}}\big).

It follows that
\psi
reduces to a well-defined function
\psi:R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\rightarrow R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
.

It is easy to see that both
\phi\psi:R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\rightarrow R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
and
\psi\phi:R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle\rightarrow R\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{S}}^{\operatorname{gr}}(\mathscr{G})\rangle
