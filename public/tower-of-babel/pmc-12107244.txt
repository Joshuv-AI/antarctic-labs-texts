Enhanced amino acid biosynthesis in Phaffia rhodozyma via herbicide-induced selection
Authors: Svetlana Raita, Iveta Kuzmika, Taras Mika, Zane Geiba, Kriss Spalvins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract According to the Food and Agricultural Organisation 2024 statement, developing single-cell protein technology is important to reduce the burden on conventional feed protein production sectors. In this regard, improved commercial strains rich in amino acids, especially Lys and Met, may provide a sustainable alternative source of protein in aquaculture diets. The developed and laboratory-validated methodology for creating protein-synthesizing mutants will strengthen the competitiveness of SCP production technology. The present work provides unique results on improving the protein-producing properties of wild-type Phaffia rhodozyma DSM 5626 by mutagenesis and screening on herbicide-containing medium as a selective agent for amino acid biosynthesis inhibition. Inhibitory concentrations of pure herbicide actives were determined for S-(2-aminoethyl)-L-cysteine (AEC) hydrochloride and glufosinate-ammonium (GA) for complete inhibition and strong inhibition of the DSM 5626 strain. GA at a concentration of 50 mM and 100 mM and AEC at 0.5 mM and 2.5 mM were chosen for mutant selection after chemical mutagenesis. The use of herbicides resulted in the selection of mutants with significantly improved synthesis of Met and Lys. Specifically, mutants GA6/4 and GA7/5 exhibited 37% and 26% higher Met levels, respectively, while GA6/3 had a 14% increase in Lys compared to the wild-type strain. The AEC3/9 mutant demonstrated a 35% increase in Met, 24% in Lys, 8% in Ile, and 6% in Phe, underscoring the efficacy of this screening approach in enhancing essential amino acid content. The protein quality parameters essential amino acid index and amino acid score of these mutants became higher compared with commercial strains of SCP yeast such as C. utilis, S. cerevisiae, K. marxianus, etc.One-Sentence SummaryMutagenesis combined with selective screening using herbicides is an effective approach to enhancing amino acid biosynthesis in yeast.

Introduction

Aquaculture plays a critical role in providing a sustainable food source as wild fisheries are declining due to overfishing and adverse climate and environmental changes. Nowadays, aquaculture supplies more than half of the world's fish for human consumption (Mair et al., 2023). To ensure high aquaculture productivity, it must be provided with a balanced feed rich in high-quality protein (Felix et al., 2023; Margareth & Anders, 2016). The quality of a protein source is assessed by amino acid composition, although its suitability primarily depends on the essential amino acid requirements of the target species (Cho & Kim, 2011; Kurpad, 2012; Wolfe et al., 2018). According to the Food and Agricultural Organisation (FAO), the diet of Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout should contain at least 42%–50% crude protein in dry feed, depending on the life stage. An important requirement for a salmon and trout diet is the content and balance of essential amino acids in the protein sources. These are histidine (His), threonine (Thr), arginine (Arg), valine (Val), methionine (Met), tryptophan (Trp), phenylalanine (Phe), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), and lysine (Lys) (FAO, 2013a, 2013b). Fishmeal is considered the most nutritious and most digestible source of protein for aquaculture feed (Cottrell et al., 2020; FAO, 2022; Janbakhsh et al., 2018; Jannathulla et al., 2021).

Fishmeal was originally used as an effective and inexpensive protein-rich feed ingredient for farmed aquatic species. However, decreased fishing productivity and increased demand for fishmeal caused significant price changes (Cottrell et al., 2020). The price of fishmeal has increased over the past 10 years, from 599 EUR/t in 2013 to 1673 EUR/t in 2023 and is expected to continue to rise (FAO, 2022; IndexMundi, n.d.). More than 80% of fishmeal is used to produce feed for high-value aquaculture species, mainly for farming shrimp, salmonids, and marine fish like seabass (FAO, 2022). The expansion of the aquaculture sector requires the development of additional economically and environmentally viable solutions to meet the growing demand for feed and less dependence on conventional agricultural and marine ingredients. Therefore, for sustainable aquaculture farming, it is necessary to reduce dependence on fishmeal and replace it with alternative protein sources (FAO, 2022; Gasco et al., 2018; Jannathulla et al., 2019).

Single-cell protein (SCP) is a promising alternative to fishmeal and plant-based protein (Bratosin et al., 2021; Øverland et al., 2013). It has less negative impact on the environment and requires fewer resources compared to crops (Nyyssölä et al., 2022; Salazar-López et al., 2022; Sharif et al., 2021). Moreover, agro-industrial waste is successfully used to produce SCP, which ensures optimal management of food and agricultural organic waste (Spalvins, Ivanovs et al., 2018; Spalvins, Raita et al., 2018). Nowadays, SCP in particular biomass of Chlorella vulgaris, Nannochloropsis gaditana, Schizochyrium sp., Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Candida utilis, Kluyveromyces marxianus, Wickerhamomyces anomalus, Spirulina maxima, Methylococcus capsulatus, Methylophilus methylotrophus, and Methylobacterium extorguens is successfully used by some companies in the formulation of fish feed, albeit in small volumes (Glencross et al., 2020; Nyyssölä et al., 2022). Recent experimental feeding trials have shown that SCP can replace between 15% and 60% of fishmeal, corresponding up to 345 g of yeast per kg of fish feed without compromising growth performance, nutrient absorption, or health (Agboola et al., 2022; Huyben et al., 2017; Øverland et al., 2013; Vidakovic et al., 2020).

The present study proposes a novel approach to create SCP-rich mutants with improved essential amino acid profiles. In this approach, amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors are used as a tool to select mutants with desirable properties created through random mutagenesis. This concept is well known in the selection of mutant microorganisms with improved biosynthesis of fatty acids and carotenoids, where inhibition of the biosynthetic pathway of the target metabolite allowed the collection of overproducers (Atzmüller et al., 2019; Ducrey Sanpietro & Kula, 1998; Luna-Flores et al., 2022). Interestingly, the widely used random mutagenesis to improve the desired properties of strains is not used to create protein-synthesized mutants. Although, there is evidence of the effect of random mutagenesis on increasing the protein content in microalgae biomass (Liu et al., 2015). Several herbicides are amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors, which can be a tool for selecting improved SCP-producing mutants. It is assumed that microbial cells that have undergone mutagenesis and are capable of growing in medium in the presence of a herbicide concentration that inhibits about 100% of the cells of the wild-type strain have a high probability of being mutants with an improved amino acid profile (Raita et al., 2024; Spalvins et al., 2021).

The present study used the red yeast Phaffia rhodozyma to evaluate the potential of amino acid inhibitors in screening mutants with improved synthesis of essential amino acids. Phaffia rhodozyma is a well-known carotenoid-synthesizing yeast used primarily as a natural astaxanthin supplement in salmonids feed formulations (e.g. Aquasta®, RedStar®) (Bjerkeng et al., 2007; Vidakovic et al., 2020). This yeast has been studied for the last 50 years to develop competitive technology for natural astaxanthin production (Raita et al., 2023). Interestingly, P. rhodozyma biomass is exclusively a source of astaxanthin but does not consider the protein content. The protein content and amino acid composition of P. rhodozyma have been poorly studied. There are several studies from the 80–90 s reported that P. rhodozyma biomass contains about 25%–47% proteins, a balanced amino acid composition, and a high number of fatty acids (Acheampong, 1993; Johnson et al., 1980; Martin et al., 1993). The present study compares the total protein content, amino acid profile, and protein quality in the biomass of created P. rhodozyma mutants with the wild-type P. rhodozyma strain and conventional SCP-producing yeast species.

Materials and Methods

Media and Solutions Preparation

Yeast medium (YM) broth contained glucose 10 g/L, peptone 5 g/L, malt extract 3 g/L, and yeast extract 3 g/L was used for inoculum preparation. For yeast inhibition tests and mutant screening, P. rhodozyma was cultivated on glycerine-salt medium (GSM) that contained 40 g/L glycerol, 4.83 g/L NH4Cl, 1 g/L K2HPO4·3H2O, 0.88 MgSO4·7H2O, 0.2 g/L CaCl2·2H2O, 20 g/L C8H5KO4, and 1.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base without amino acid (YNB), pH 6.0 ± 0.05. Glycerine-peptone-rich medium (GPM) contained 70 g/L glycerol, 32 g/L soy peptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 1.7 g/L YNB, pH 6.0 ± 0.1 was used for flask experiments. YNB was dissolved in sterile distilled water and filtersterilized through a syringe filter with 0.22 µm pore size. Sterile YNB stock solution was added at a rate of 10% to autoclaved media before adjusting the pH under sterile conditions.

Herbicide stock solutions were prepared before experiments by dissolving in sterile water and filtersterilizing through a syringe filter with 0.22 µm pore size. Glufosinate-ammonium (GA, CAS 77182-82-2) was purchased from Combi-Blocks and S-(2-aminoethyl)-L-cysteine hydrochloride (AEC, CAS 4099-35-8) from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) (Sigma Aldrich) was used for P. rhodozyma cell mutagenesis at 4% concentration. EMS solution preparation and mutagenesis protocol is described in Raita et al. (2024).

Microorganism Strain

Yeast culture P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 was purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ) at the Leibniz Institute. It is a type strain isolated from Japanese beech (Fagus crenata) slime flux. The alternative strain numbers in other collections are ATCC 24202, CBS 5905, NBRC 10129, NRRL Y-10921, and IFO 10129. Phaffia rhodozyma DSM 5626 strain is an anamorph strain reproducing asexually. This process results in genetically identical offspring. Because an anamorphic organism reproduces without genetic recombination, it tends to maintain stable traits across generations. This stability is beneficial when a specific strain with desired traits is used, ensuring these traits are maintained across successive cultures (Gerrish & Lenski, 1998). The yeast culture was maintained on a YM agar plate containing 10 g/L glucose, 5 g/L peptone, 3 g/L malt extract, 3 g/L yeast extract, and 15 g/L agar. For all experiments, the inoculums were grown on YM broth at 22°C on a rotary shaker at 250 rpm for 48 hr.

Microplate Cultivation

The GSM medium with 1.0 × 106 cells/mL of inoculum and an inhibitor at the appropriate concentration was used. Microplate cultivation lasted 116 hr for yeast inhibition tests and up to 150 hr for mutant screening at a specific inhibitor concentration. At the end of microplate cultivation, the cells from all wells were counted.

Determination of Herbicide Inhibitory Concentration

Two amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors, AEC and GA, were selected as the best herbicides for yeast inhibition based on multicriteria decision analysis results in our previous study (Berzina et al., 2024). GA is an inhibitor of glutamine, a precursor to the biosynthesis of the seven amino acids: arginine (Arg), proline (Pro), aspartate (Asp), lysine (Lys), methionine (Met), threonine (Thr), and isoleucine (Ile) (Gong et al., 2020; Joo et al., 2018). AEC is an inhibitor of the biosynthesis of the four amino acids Met, Lys, Thr, and Ile (Pearce et al., 2017). In the present study, wild-type yeast was grown on GSM broth containing these herbicides separately to determine inhibition concentrations of about 100% for each. GSM with an inorganic nitrogen source was selected for P. rhodozyma because an organic nitrogen source could counteract the inhibitory effects of herbicides (Raita et al., 2024).

Seven concentrations of the AEC ranged from 0.1 to 50 mM (0.1, 0.5, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, and 50 mM) and 12 concentrations of the GA ranged from 0.1 to 350 mM (0.1, 0.5, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 50 100, 150 200, 250 300, and 350 mM) were tested for wild-type strain inhibition. The yeast inhibition test was conducted at different time points for GA and AEC inhibitors under identical conditions. The tests were performed in 48-well microplates with a working volume of 0.5 mL. The GSM medium was supplemented with inhibitors and 1.0 × 106 cells/mL inoculum and incubated for 116 hr. At the end of the test, the cells were microscopically examined and counted. The concentrations of amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors causing complete inhibition of wild-type strain growth were selected for further screening of mutants.

Mutants Screening

Screening of mutagenized P. rhodozyma cells at a concentration of 2.0 × 106 cells/mL was conducted in GSM medium containing four concentrations of inhibitors: 2.5 mM AEC (AEC2), 5 mM AEC (AEC3), 50 mM GA (GA6), and 100 mM GA (GA7). Control samples were: (1) a wild-type strain in a medium without an inhibitor (control-1), (2) a wild-type strain in a medium with an inhibitor (control-2), and (3) mutagenized cells in a medium without an inhibitor (control-3). Microscopy and cell counting were performed for all samples. Mutants exhibiting superior growth compared to control-2 were selected for subculture. Control-1 and control-3 were included to ensure consistent culturing conditions across different microplates and time points.

Then subculturing was performed to strengthen the potential mutants in the selective medium and minimize the risk of nonmutated wild-type cell proliferation. A 0.1 mL suspension of mutant cells was inoculated into 0.4 mL of GSM medium containing the corresponding concentration of inhibitor. Samples GA6 and GA7 were subcultured three times, AEC2 twice, and AEC3 once.

Cells were counted at the end of each subcultivation. Samples GA6, GA7, and AEC2 showed improved growth after each subculture, and at the end of the third and second subcultures, the cell concentration in the microplate wells increased more than 10 or 100 times. AEC3 samples after subculture showed a decrease in cell numbers, leading to a stop at one subculture. The mutants with higher cell concentration from microplate wells were transferred into 50 mL Erlenmeyer flasks with 15 mL YM broth without inhibitors and cultivated for up to 4 days. Cultures that reached cell concentrations of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL and higher were selected as inoculum for mutant shake flasks cultivation tests.

Shake Flasks Cultivation

The shake flask tests were conducted using inoculants of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL in a 25 mL working volume of GPM broth and incubated for 7 days. Inoculants were collected and washed twice in sterile water before each test. All shake flask tests were cultured in 250 mL baffled flasks in triplets at 22°C on a rotary shaker at 250 rpm. The biomass collected from the flask tests was subsequently used in the analytical measurements described in the next section.

Analytical Methods

Determination of yeast growth

Yeast growth was assessed by dry cell weight, culture optical density, and cell concentration. Dry biomass was determined by thermogravimetric analysis. Yeast biomass samples were collected daily from the third to the seventh day of the experiment and calculated as dry cell weight per liter of medium (g/L) for flask culture.

Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured every hour at six points per well during cultivation, using a microplate reader (TECAN Spark®, Switzerland) equipped with an integrated cooling module and compatible 48-well clear-bottom microplate (SARSTEDT, Germany). To prevent medium evaporation, the microplate was cultured in a humidity cassette (TECAN Spark®, Switzerland).

Cell concentration was determined for inoculants and samples at the end of microplate cultivation by counting from all wells using a hemocytometer (Assistant, Neubauer improved, 0.1 mm depth camera, Germany) and light microscope (Micros Austria, MCX100LCD, Austria) at 400× magnification.

Determination of total protein content in biomass

Total protein content in dry yeast biomass was determined by Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Merck, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance measurements were performed at a wavelength of 562 nm in a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, BioMateTM 160). The dry cell lysis procedure was adapted from a previously described method (Guerlava et al., 1998).

Determination of amino acid profile

Twenty to 50 mg of dry yeast biomass were hydrolyzed in 6 M HCl and 0.1% phenol solution at 110 ± 2°C for 24 hr in sealed tubes. Determination of methionine (as a methionine sulfone) and cysteine and cystine amino acids content (as a cysteic acid) was done using preoxidation of the samples for 16 hr at 0°C by performic acid before hydrolysis using the protocol (European Commission, 2009) with minor modification.

Identification and quantification of amino acids was done using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade reagents: amino acid standard mix (Supelco) and individual methionine sulfone and cysteic acid standards. An automated in-needle precolumn derivatization of protein hydrolysates was done by orthophthalic aldehyde in the presence of mercaptopropionic acid (Merck, Germany) and 9-fluorenylmethyl chloroformate (Soma et al., 2022).

HPLC analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu Nexera Series HPLC system (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a YMC-Triart C18 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm, 3 µm) and a guard column of the same type. The chromatography was performed in the gradient mode described in the protocol (GL Sciences, n.d.). Tryptophane was not determined in this study. Chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, USA unless otherwise noted.

Anchovy fishmeal was used as a reference protein (Agboola et al., 2021) in AAS and EAAI calculations (including for comparative studies) because it is recognized as the best protein source for fish diets, including salmon (Bunda et al., 2015; Felix et al., 2023; Tibbetts et al., 2017). Determination of astaxanthin content in biomass

The carotenoid extraction method was adapted from Luna-Flores et al. (2022) and the determination of astaxanthin and total carotenoid content followed the protocol described in Feldmane (2023). The measurement procedure for astaxanthin and total carotenoid concentration was based on Ni et al. (2005). For absorbance measurements, a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, BioMate™ 160) was used with the ‘multiwavelength’ detection program set to 474 nm and 452 nm.

Data processing

The data for biomass dry weight, total protein, astaxanthin, and total carotenoid content are presented as the means of three replicates. The amino acid composition of yeast protein is calculated as the mean of two replicates. The astaxanthin concentrations, protein content, and amino acid profiles shown in the results and discussion chapter are based on samples collected on the seventh day of cultivation.

Statistical analysis of biomass, protein, astaxanthin, and amino acid values was performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test in SPSS software (version 29.0). OD600 value results were statistically analyzed by ANOVA, followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < .05.

Results and Discussion

Wild-Type Phaffia rhodozyma Growth Inhibition

AEC caused a strong inhibitory effect on wild-type strain growth. At the lowest tested concentration (0.1 mM), it significantly inhibited yeast growth. Increasing the concentration to 0.5 mM resulted in a slight cell increase compared to the initial cell concentration from the inoculum. AEC concentrations of 2.5 mM and higher resulted in complete cell inhibition, accompanied by cell autolysis at the end of cultivation. These findings are consistent with a study by Sano and Shiio (1970) that reported about 100% inhibition of Bacillus subtilis and Escherichia coli at 5 mM AEC, whereas 50% inhibition of Brevibacterium flavum required 15 mM AEC (Sano & Shiio, 1970).

Phaffia rhodozyma showed less sensitivity to GA compared to the AEC inhibitor, moreover, at 0.1–10 mM, growth stimulation was observed. The growth-promoting effect of herbicides on some microorganisms has been described in numerous studies. Glyphosate, chlorsulfuron, imazaquin, and imazapyr have been reported to stimulate microbial growth at certain concentrations (Araújo et al., 2003; Łozowicka et al., 2021; Petrovickij-Angerer, 2009; Wang et al., 2007; Xuedong et al., 2005), but there is limited data on GA. The strong inhibition was observed at 50 mM GA concentration and complete cell growth inhibition was determined from 100 mM and higher. In the present study, P. rhodozyma demonstrated a higher tolerance to GA compared to S. cerevisiae and Aspergillus niger, where significant growth inhibition was observed at concentrations of 0.05 mM and 0.15 mM GA, respectively (Ahuja & Punekar, 2008; Vallejo et al., 2017).

0.5 mM AEC, 2.5 mM AEC, 50 mM GA, and 100 mM GA concentrations were selected for mutant screening as they represent strong inhibitory doses necessary for selecting mutants with enhanced target amino acid synthesis.

Selected Mutant Analysis

In the present study was conducted two flask experiments for six mutants selected from GA-contained media (hereafter referred to as GA mutants) and nine mutants selected from AEC-contained media (AEC mutants). P. rhodozyma wild-type strain on seventh day cultivation reached a 38.95–39.43 g/L biomass containing about 30.75%–30.98% and 0.029%–0.032% protein and astaxanthin content, respectively. GA mutants showed no statistically significant difference in biomass, protein, astaxanthin and total carotenoid values compared to the wild strain (Table 1). However, a significant difference was observed between some mutants in biomass values and total protein concentration.

The AEC mutants did not have significantly higher biomass parameters compared to the wild-type strain (Table 2). Moreover, the AEC3/4 mutant showed significantly lower biomass concentrations, total carotenoid and astaxanthin contents. The AEC3/9 mutant had a twofold decrease in astaxanthin in the biomass compared to the wild-type strain. The observed significant reduction in astaxanthin content in some AEC mutants may be explained by multiple factors related to the metabolic and regulatory consequences of EMS mutagenesis. EMS causes random point mutations that could reduce the activity of phytoene synthase, phytoene desaturase, β-carotene hydroxylase or β-carotene ketolase, which are essential enzymes in carotenoid biosynthesis (Sandesh Kamath et al., 2008). Another reason for decreased astaxanthin biosynthesis may be competition for metabolic precursors and cofactors. Astaxanthin biosynthesis depends on acetyl-CoA as a precursor in the mevalonate pathway, but it is also critical for amino acid and lipid biosynthesis, making it a central node of metabolic flux. S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) serves as a universal methyl group donor in Met biosynthesis and modulates Lys biosynthesis through feedback inhibition of aspartate kinase. SAM also participates in carotenoid biosynthesis by modifying pathway intermediates or regulating gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms (Kirschning, 2022; Zhao et al., 2024). Therefore, mutants with enhanced essential amino acid biosynthesis may redirect metabolic fluxes toward protein biosynthesis and increase the consumption of shared intermediates and cofactors. This redistribution of resources could limit the availability of essential components for astaxanthin biosynthesis, thereby reducing its accumulation. The P. rhodozyma AEC3/9 mutant exhibited enhanced synthesis of Met and Lys (Table 4), supporting the hypothesis of competition with astaxanthin for shared metabolic precursors and cofactors. These findings highlight the need for further transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to uncover the genetic and metabolic alterations responsible for reduced astaxanthin production in AEC-derived mutants.

According to the literature, GA inhibits the biosynthesis of seven amino acids, of which five are essential for fish (Arg, Lys, Met, Thr, and Ile) (Gong et al., 2020; Joo et al., 2018; Mowbray et al., 2014). Interestingly, in the study of Vallejo et al., the treatment of wine yeast with 0.05 mM GA significantly increased the synthesis of Met, Ile, Leu, Phe, Trp, Tyr compared to untreated cells (Vallejo et al., 2017). At the same time, Phe, Trp, Tyr are not targets for this inhibitor, which may indicate the switching-on side effects in the microorganism cells in the presence of the herbicide (Spalvins et al., 2021). In the present study, three mutants showed a significant improvement of GA-specific amino acids. GA6/3 had a higher amount of Lys by 14%, GA6/4 and GA7/5 had a higher amount of Met by 37% and 25%, respectively (Table 3). The amino acid pattern of P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 was found to be more consistent with SCP compared to the pattern of other strains of this yeast species. Aquaculture feed generally contains a combination of several protein sources to maintain the required amino acid balance for the target animal, and adding pure amino acids can also be used if necessary (Glencross et al., 2020). Aquaculture's feed formulations use fishmeal, legumes, oilseed crops, animal by-products, crustacean meal, and algal meal. Each of these sources separately has limiting essential amino acids. Plant-based proteins typically have less methionine, tryptophan, lysine, and cysteine, which is below 30% of the mean fish requirement. Proteins of animal origin except fishmeal are usually the limiting are Thr, Met, Cys, Ile, and Tyr. The SCP quality of the amino acids depends on the microorganism type. The limiting amino acid for S. cerevisiae, C. utilis is Met, for Spirulina maxima is Met, Cys, and Lys, and for Methylophilus methylotrophus is Cys (FAO, 2013a; Tacon, 1987). Algal protein contains the lowest concentration of His and Met (Astorga-España et al., 2016). In turn, fishmeal is considered a more balanced source of essential amino acids for the fish diet. Fishmeal is produced from small pelagic forage fish such as mackerel, herring, sardine, anchovy, and only a small portion from fish by-products and wastes (Cottrell et al., 2020; FAO, 2022; Janbakhsh et al., 2018; Jannathulla et al., 2021).

The first limiting amino acid in all P. rhodozyma strains was Met, which is typical for yeasts and algae (FAO, 2013a; Tacon, 1987). The EAAI of P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 protein indicated moderate quality from 70% to 89%. This contrasts with many commercial SCP-yeast proteins, which generally exhibit low EAAI values (<70%), reflecting suboptimal protein quality for fish feed applications (Tables 5 and 6). Notably, the GA6/4 and AEC3/9 mutant strains demonstrated the highest EAAI values among the tested mutants, reaching levels approaching high-quality protein, which requires an EAAI of ≥90% (Bunda et al., 2015; Machado et al., 2020). An EAA with an AAS below 100% is considered limiting (Kurpad, 2012). However, it is worth noting that there is no EU regulation clearly defining the quality of fishmeal protein used for fish feed, and there is also no classification of protein quality based on the amount of 10 EAAs contained. In general, fishmeal can be of varying quality depending on the species of fish used, whether whole fish or by-products are used (FAO, 2022; Janbakhsh et al., 2018; Jannathulla et al., 2021). For example, fishmeal from sprat caught in the Caspian Sea contained on average 0.73 g Arg, 1.97 g Met, 1.53 g Phe, 2.80 g Leu, and 5.50 g Lys per 100 g crude protein (Janbakhsh et al., 2018), which is much lower than that of anchovy fishmeal (Agboola et al., 2021) used as a reference protein in our study.

Similarly, other studies reported that fishmeal can have lower levels of all nine EAAs except for Trp, which may exceed reference values by 0.2 g per 100 g crude protein (Felix et al., 2023). These variations highlight the potential of P. rhodozyma mutants to replace lower-quality fishmeal, particularly when the reference protein is not of the highest standard. The suitability of SCP protein quality can vary depending on the reference protein chosen. For example, using the essential amino acid requirements of salmonids as an etalon standard (Agboola et al., 2021; Hua & Bureau, 2019), the EAAI of P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 protein could approach 100%. In this study, the AAS values for His, Arg, Val, Phe, Ile, and Leu were consistently higher in all P. rhodozyma strains compared to three commercial SCP yeast strains. Among the mutants, AEC3/9 exhibited the most improved Lys and Met content (Fig. 1), making it the best-performing strain. The Lys AAS of 95.59% was significantly higher than values reported for other yeasts such as Yarrowia lipolytica (Michalik et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2022), Cyberlindnera jadinii, Blastobotrys adeninivorans (Agboola et al., 2022), Wickerhamomyces anomalus (Agboola et al., 2022; Lapeña et al., 2020), S. cerevisiae (Michalik et al., 2014; Øverland et al., 2013; Vidakovic et al., 2020), C. utilis (Marius et al., 2018; Øverland et al., 2013), Kluyveromyces marxianus (Øverland et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2022), and fishmeal of not the highest quality (Felix et al., 2023), (Janbakhsh et al., 2018). The results suggest that the genetic modifications in mutant AEC3/9 successfully enhanced its amino acid profile making it a promising strain for SCP applications.

Prospects for Industrial Application of Phaffia rhodozyma

Regulatory documents confirming the use of P. rhodozyma biomass in the formulation of aquaculture feeds indicate permissible concentrations of astaxanthin and safety criteria for the additive. According to European regulation 2015/1415, the astaxanthin content should not exceed 100 mg/kg of finished feed, while the concentration in the P. rhodozyma biomass should be higher than 0.5% (Bampidis et al., 2022). Based on these conditions, the biomass of the yeast P. rhodozyma can be used presumably in an amount of about 20 g per 1 kg of finished feed as a coloring component. No studies indicate that this yeast's biomass harms the health of salmonids or other animals. Experimental fish-feeding trials for P. rhodozyma or X. dendrorhous biomass are limited above the recommended levels for an astaxanthin-rich supplement. However, it was reported that 100 g/kg of P. rhodozyma biomass in the diet provided similar growth rates for rainbow trout compared to an equivalent dose of a brewer's yeast-supplemented diet (Sanderson & Jolly, 1994). Theoretically, if the inclusion of P. rhodozyma biomass at levels of 100–350 g/kg of feed is confirmed to be safe, the required astaxanthin concentration could be reduced to 0.03%–0.1%. In this way, P. rhodozyma may serve as an excellent source of feed protein, partially replacing conventional ingredients while still providing the necessary astaxanthin levels in salmonid diets. This approach to SCP production from P. rhodozyma enhances the competitiveness of the technology and supports the development of a novel product for the feed market

Although this study is limited to laboratory-scale mutant screening, it is important to note that large-scale fermentation of P. rhodozyma, like other microbial systems, would face multiple scale-up challenges. A key difficulty in scaling up yeast fermentation lies in maintaining consistent mass and oxygen transfer throughout large bioreactor volumes (typically 1 000–10 000 L) (Wang et al., 2020). Changes in oxygen and nutrient gradients, shear stress, and mixing efficiency often lead to physiological stress in microbial cells, which can in turn reduce biomass productivity, metabolite yield, and batch-to-batch reproducibility (Glencross et al., 2020). These issues are particularly relevant in aerobic high-density fermentations typical for SCP production. In the specific case of P. rhodozyma, simultaneous production of both SCP and astaxanthin introduces a particular challenge, as the optimal fermentation conditions for biomass growth and protein synthesis differ significantly from those required to induce astaxanthin biosynthesis (Raita et al., 2022, 2023). To address this, a two-stage fermentation strategy could help to overcome this challenge. In the first phase, high nitrogen availability and optimal conditions (temperature, pH, C/N ratio, aeration, and trace element supplementation) are provided to promote yeast growth and protein accumulation. In the second phase, nitrogen limitation is applied, along with supplementation with ionic stress inducers (e.g. NaCl, H₂O₂, TiO₂), high dissolved oxygen concentrations, and lowered pH and temperature relative to the optimal values for yeast growth, to stimulate astaxanthin biosynthesis (Raita et al., 2023). In this way, by carefully managing the fermentation conditions for P. rhodozyma, it is theoretically possible to achieve balanced accumulation of both SCP and astaxanthin.

While this strategy offers a conceptual solution for balanced SCP and astaxanthin accumulation, its implementation at an industrial scale remains challenging. Transitioning between metabolic phases requires precise process control and real-time monitoring of physiological parameters. Fed-batch bioreactors equipped with biosensors for dissolved oxygen, pH, nutrient levels, and redox state would be necessary to ensure phase transitions are well-timed and that the culture remains metabolically balanced throughout the process (Vojinović et al., 2006). Achieving such control in large-scale systems is technically demanding, and further optimization and pilot-scale studies would be required to validate this approach.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates the potential of amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors in the screening of protein-improved mutants. Using a selective medium reduced the number of mutants studied, allowing for compact tests in flasks. Although the analysis of the mutant biomass did not show an increase in the synthesis of total protein, an increase in the synthesis of essential amino acids was achieved. Screening of mutagenized yeast cells on GA selective media resulted in selecting two mutants with significantly improved Met synthesis and one mutant with significantly improved Lys synthesis. Screening of mutagenized yeast cells on an AEC selective media resulted in selecting three mutants with significantly improved synthesis of two to four amino acids. The best mutant in this study, AEC3/9, had significantly improved synthesis of four essential amino acids for fish diets: Met, Phe, Ile, and Lys. The increase in synthesizing the first two limiting amino acids in yeast – Met and Lys – was especially valuable. This proves that amino acid biosynthesis inhibitors are a good tool for selecting improved protein-quality mutants.

An important aspect of this study is disclosing the potential of P. rhodozyma as an SCP producer. Phaffia rhodozyma DSM 5626 biomass has about 31% protein and 0.029% astaxanthin when cultivated in glycerol-peptone-rich medium with pH 5 in shaken flasks at 22°C for 7 days. Under these conditions, P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 has a moderate quality protein similar to fishmeal and an essential amino acid profile, except for the limiting content of Met and Lys. The P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 protein is superior in essential amino acid content to other SCP producers such as C. jadinii, W. anomalus, B. adeninivorans, or low-quality fishmeal. This was unexpected since P. rhodozyma had not been previously considered as an SCP producer. Further research is required to optimize culture conditions to increase metabolite concentration in the yeast biomass and to assess the competitiveness of both P. rhodozyma DSM 5626 and the best mutants as simultaneous producers of protein and astaxanthin.
