Effects of chemical and mechanical weed control strategies on bulb yield, physiological traits, and antioxidant enzyme activities in Persian shallot (Allium hirtifolium Boiss.)
Authors: Zohrab Adavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A two-year field experiment (2021–2023) was conducted using a randomized complete block design to evaluate the effects of mechanical and chemical weed control strategies on weed suppression, yield, and physiological responses of Persian shallot (Allium hirtifolium Boiss.) under semi-arid conditions. Treatments included season-long hand weeding, post-emergence application of the commercial formulations of ioxynil (22% EC), oxyfluorfen (24% EC), and oxadiazon (25% EC), each applied at 2 L ha⁻¹ of formulated product, and an untreated control. Results showed that the untreated control had the highest weed biomass and the lowest bulb yield. Oxyfluorfen provided the most effective chemical control, reducing weed biomass by 54%. However, hand weeding resulted in superior crop performance, producing the highest leaf dry weight, leaf area index, and bulb yield. Herbicide treatments reduced growth-related traits and photosynthetic pigments, particularly under oxadiazon. Physiological analyses indicated that stress markers, including proline content and antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and POD), were highest in the untreated control and herbicide-treated plants, while the lowest values were observed under hand weeding. In contrast, soluble sugars, relative water content, electrolyte leakage, and total phenolics were not significantly affected by treatments. Year effects were significant for several growth traits, but the interaction between year and treatment was tested and found to be non-significant for all measured traits. Overall, although oxyfluorfen was the most effective herbicide for weed suppression, hand weeding provided the best balance between yield and physiological stability. These findings highlight a trade-off between chemical weed control efficiency and crop physiological integrity in semi-arid production systems.

Introduction

Weeds compete with crops for water, nutrients, and light, often causing substantial yield losses in many cropping systems1,2. In Allium species, including Persian shallot (Allium hirtifolium Boiss.), a medicinal and edible plant native to the mountainous regions of Iran, uncontrolled weed interference can reduce bulb yield by up to 50%3,4. This species has traditionally been harvested from natural habitats but is increasingly cultivated to meet demand for its antioxidant-rich bulbs, which are valued in traditional medicine for their anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties5,6. In the semi-arid agroecosystems of central Iran, where A. Hirtifolium is grown, intense weed pressure exacerbates abiotic stresses and triggers physiological responses such as oxidative damage and activation of antioxidant defence systems7–9.

Effective weed management is essential for sustainable A. hirtifolium production, as it must balance effective weed suppression with minimal phytotoxicity to the crop10–12. Hand weeding provides precise control but is labour-intensive and impractical for large-scale farming13–15. Post-emergence herbicides offer a practical alternative for managing the broadleaf and grassy weeds common in Allium fields (Chenopodium album and Sorghum halepense)16–18. However, herbicide selectivity remains a concern; non-optimal applications can induce stress in Allium crops, leading to chlorosis, reduced chlorophyll content, and impaired photosynthesis19,20. Inhibitors of protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO), such as oxyfluorfen, have shown efficacy against weeds in related Allium crops but may promote porphyrin precursor accumulation and oxidative stress in sensitive tissues21,22.

In contrast, ioxynil acts as a photosystem II (PSII) inhibitor, disrupting electron transport, whereas oxadiazon, although also classified as a PPO inhibitor, differs in its selectivity and persistence. Therefore, these herbicides were selected to represent distinct physiological modes of action, enabling a more robust comparative evaluation of their effects on weed suppression and crop physiological responses.

Herbicide-induced stress often increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can damage membranes and biomolecules unless countered by antioxidant defences9. In Allium species, key components of these defences include enzymes such as catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD), as well as non-enzymatic compounds including proline and phenolics1,8,23. While preliminary studies suggest that selective post-emergence herbicides (ioxynil, oxyfluorfen, and oxadiazon) can reduce weed biomass by 40–60% in A. hirtifolium, their specific effects on crop physiology and antioxidant enzyme modulation under rainfed semi-arid conditions remain incompletely understood3,24,25. In addition, the interaction between weed control methods and inter-annual variation in rainfall (typical range 300–400 mm in the study area) requires clarification for reliable recommendations26–28.

This study therefore evaluated the effects of season-long hand weeding versus three post-emergence herbicides (ioxynil, oxyfluorfen, and oxadiazon) on weed density and biomass, bulb yield, and key physiological and biochemical traits of A. hirtifolium in a two-year field trial (2021–2022 and 2022–2023) at the research farm of Payame Noor University in Fereydunshahr, Iran. By integrating agronomic, physiological, and biochemical assessments, the work aimed to identify weed control strategies that optimise productivity while minimising physiological disruption29.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted over two consecutive growing seasons (2021–2022 and 2022–2023) at the research farm of Payame Noor University, Fereydunshahr, Iran (50°15′ E, 32°57′ N; 2500 m a.s.l.). The site is characterized by a semi-arid climate with an average annual rainfall of approximately 350 mm6,27.

In each growing season, the experiment was arranged as a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with four replications. Weed-control treatments were randomly assigned within each block. The experimental treatments included: (1) season-long hand weeding, (2) post-emergence application of ioxynil (Totrill 22% EC, 2 L ha⁻¹), (3) oxyfluorfen (Goal 24% EC, 2 L ha⁻¹), (4) oxadiazon (Ronstar 25% EC, 2 L ha⁻¹), and (5) an untreated weedy control16,21.

Bulbs of a locally adapted ecotype of Allium hirtifolium Boiss. were planted in November of each year at a density of 20 plants m⁻² (20 cm within rows and 30 cm between rows) on calcareous soil (pH 7.5–8.0; organic matter 1.2%)3. Each plot measured 2 m × 2 m and was separated by 0.5 m buffer zones to minimize edge effects and spray drift.

Herbicides were applied post-emergence at the 4–6 leaf stage of the crop, coinciding with early weed emergence. Applications were carried out using a calibrated backpack sprayer delivering 300 L ha⁻¹ at a pressure of 2.5 bar with flat-fan nozzles. Spraying was conducted under favorable environmental conditions (15–20 °C temperature and 60–70% relative humidity), with no rainfall occurring within 24 h after application24,30. Because the two growing seasons differed in climatic conditions (e.g., rainfall and temperature) but were not experimentally imposed treatments, year was treated as a random environmental (temporal) factor rather than a fixed effect. This approach allows generalization across seasons while accounting for inter-annual variability. Homogeneity of variances was confirmed prior to combined analysis (P > 0.05).

Agronomic traits

Weed dry biomass was determined by harvesting all weeds from a 1 m² quadrat per plot at crop maturity, oven-drying at 70 °C to constant weight, and expressing as g m⁻². Crop leaf dry weight (g plant⁻¹) and leaf area index (LAI) were measured on five randomly selected plants per plot one week after herbicide application; leaf area was recorded with a portable leaf-area meter, and LAI was calculated as total leaf area per unit ground area9,29. Bulb yield (g m⁻²) was obtained by harvesting the central 1 m² of each plot at maturity (10 June 2022 and 9 June 2023), separating bulbs from foliage, cleaning, and weighing fresh bulbs5,6,14. All agronomic measurements followed standard procedures for Allium crops31,32.

Physiological and biochemical traits

Physiological and biochemical traits were assessed one week after herbicide application using five randomly selected plants per plot (excluding border plants). This sampling time was selected to capture early physiological and biochemical responses during the peak phase of herbicide-induced stress, prior to recovery processes or the onset of secondary stress interactions20. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure analytical precision.

Photosynthetic pigments, including chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids, were quantified spectrophotometrically following extraction in 80% acetone according to Lichtenthaler (1987)33. Proline content was determined using the ninhydrin-based method described by Bates et al. (1973)34. Relative water content (RWC) was calculated based on fresh, turgid, and dry weights following the method of Barrs and Weatherley (1962)35. Electrolyte leakage (EL) was measured to assess membrane stability using the protocol of Lutts et al. (1996)36.

Soluble sugars were quantified using the anthrone method (Yemm and Willis, 1954)37, while soluble protein content was determined according to the Bradford (1976)38 assay.

For antioxidant enzyme analysis, fresh leaf tissues were homogenized in an appropriate extraction buffer under cold conditions. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide following Aebi (1984)39, whereas peroxidase (POD) activity was determined based on substrate oxidation using the method of Chance and Maehly (1955)40. Total phenolic content was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu method (Singleton and Rossi, 1965)41. All physiological and biochemical assays were conducted using standardized and widely accepted protocols as referenced above.

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SAS software (version 9.4). For each growing season, data were initially analyzed based on a randomized complete block design (RCBD). Before the combined analysis, homogeneity of error variances across years was tested. As no significant heterogeneity was detected, a combined ANOVA across years was performed. In the combined analysis, year was treated as a random factor representing environmental variation, while weed-control treatments were considered fixed effects. The interaction between year and treatment was also tested. Mean comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at P ≤ 0.05. Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and data transformations were applied where necessary to meet ANOVA assumptions. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using pooled plot means across all treatments and years (n = 40) to evaluate relationships among agronomic, physiological, and biochemical traits. All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Weed species in the A. hirtifolium field

A total of 26 weed species from 17 families were identified across the experimental plots over the two growing seasons. The Asteraceae and Poaceae families each contributed four species and were the most represented, followed by Boraginaceae, Brassicaceae, Amaranthaceae, and Polygonaceae (two species each). Annual species comprised 73% (19 species) and perennials 27% (7 species). Year effects were non-significant (P > 0.05) for species richness. Weed dry weight

Treatment effects on weed dry weight were significant (P < 0.01), while the year effect was not significant (P = 0.06). The interaction between year and treatment was not significant for any of the measured traits (P > 0.05; Table 2). The highest weed dry biomass was recorded in the untreated control (51.3 g m⁻²), followed by ioxynil (34.4 g m⁻²). The lowest biomass was obtained with oxyfluorfen (23.8 g m⁻²), while oxadiazon gave 32.4 g m⁻² and hand weeding gave 0 g m⁻² (Table 3).

Leaf dry weight

Treatments significantly influenced leaf dry weight (P < 0.01). A significant year effect was also detected (P < 0.05), with no year × treatment interaction (P > 0.05; Table 2). Hand weeding produced the highest leaf dry weight (46.4 g plant⁻¹). All herbicide treatments reduced leaf dry weight by 27–38% relative to hand weeding. The untreated control was intermediate (38.5 g plant⁻¹). Leaf dry weight differed between years (P < 0.05; Fig. 1).

Significant treatment (P < 0.01) and year effects (P < 0.05) were detected for LAI, with no interaction (P > 0.10; Table 2). The highest LAI was recorded under hand weeding (1.42). Bulb yield

Bulb yield responded significantly to treatments (P < 0.01), with a significant year effect (P < 0.05) and no interaction (P > 0.05; Table 2). The maximum yield was achieved with hand weeding (164 g m⁻²). Yields under all herbicide treatments (119–123 g m⁻²) and the untreated control (131 g m⁻²) were statistically similar (P > 0.05; Table 3). Bulb yield differed between years (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). Despite the substantial reduction in weed biomass under herbicide treatments, the absence of a corresponding increase in bulb yield suggests that weed competition may not have reached a threshold sufficient to limit yield under the experimental conditions. Alternatively, compensatory growth responses or partial phytotoxic effects of herbicides may have offset the benefits of weed suppression, resulting in comparable yield levels between treated and untreated plots.

Photosynthetic pigments

Treatment effects on Chl a, Chl b, total Chl, and carotenoids were highly significant (P < 0.01). Year effects were significant for Chl a, total Chl, and carotenoids (P < 0.05), with no interactions (P > 0.05; Table 2). Hand weeding produced the highest values (Chl a 1.05 mg g⁻¹ FW, total Chl 1.35 mg g⁻¹ FW, carotenoids 0.40 mg g⁻¹ FW). Oxadiazon gave the lowest values (Chl a 0.66 mg g⁻¹ FW, total Chl 0.84 mg g⁻¹ FW). Significant year effects were observed for pigment concentrations (P < 0.05) (Table 3).

Proline content

Proline content showed strong treatment dependence (P < 0.01) and a significant year effect (P < 0.05), with no interaction (Table 2). The untreated control exhibited the highest proline (9.46 µmol g⁻¹ FW). Hand weeding gave the lowest value (2.03 µmol g⁻¹ FW). Oxyfluorfen produced an intermediate level (4.98 µmol g⁻¹ FW). Proline was 6% lower in 2021–2022 (Table 3).

Soluble protein content and peroxidase (POD) activity

Treatments significantly affected soluble protein content (P < 0.01), with no year effect or interaction (P > 0.05; Table 2). Hand weeding supported the highest protein concentration (10.47 mg g⁻¹ FW). All herbicide treatments reduced protein content relative to hand weeding (Table 3). POD activity varied significantly with treatments (P < 0.01) and showed no year effects (P > 0.10). The highest POD activity was recorded in the untreated control (0.13 U mg⁻¹ protein), which was not significantly different from oxadiazon (0.12 U mg⁻¹ protein; P > 0.05), but both were significantly higher than hand weeding and oxyfluorfen (P < 0.05, LSD test) (Table 4).

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity responded significantly to treatments (P < 0.01), with no year or interaction effects (Table 2). The highest CAT activity occurred in the control (0.71 U mg⁻¹ protein). Hand weeding produced the lowest value (0.18 U mg⁻¹ protein) (Table 3).

Soluble sugars

Treatments had no significant effect on soluble sugar concentration (P = 0.12), and no significant year or interaction effects were observed (P > 0.05; Table 2). Pooled means ranged from 16.5 to 18.2 mg g⁻¹ DW (Table 3).

Electrolyte leakage (EL)

Electrolyte leakage was not significantly affected by treatments (P = 0.08), and no significant year or interaction effects were detected (Table 2). Values ranged from 12.3% to 18.7% across treatments (Table 3).

Relative water content (RWC) and total phenolics

Relative water content (RWC) showed no significant treatment effect (P = 0.09), with no significant year or interaction effects (Table 2). Values ranged from 81.2% to 85.4%. Total phenolic content was also not significantly influenced by treatments (P = 0.11), and no significant year or interaction effects were observed (Table 2).

Correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis performed on pooled plot means (n = 40) across all treatments and both growing seasons revealed biologically coherent interrelationships among the measured traits (Table 5). Weed dry biomass exhibited strong positive correlations with the stress markers proline content (r = 0.72, P < 0.01), POD activity (r = 0.68, P < 0.01), and CAT activity (r = 0.70, P < 0.01), while showing significant negative correlations with growth- and yield-related parameters, including leaf dry weight (r = − 0.71, P < 0.01), LAI (r = − 0.68, P < 0.01), bulb yield (r = − 0.65, P < 0.01), chlorophyll a (r = − 0.62, P < 0.01), and soluble protein content (r = − 0.59, P < 0.01). Proline content was positively correlated with both POD and CAT activities (r = 0.65 and 0.67, respectively; P < 0.01), indicating a coordinated osmotic and enzymatic antioxidant response under weed-induced stress. In contrast, the growth-related traits (leaf dry weight, LAI, bulb yield, chlorophyll a, and soluble protein) were strongly and positively intercorrelated (r = 0.72–0.82, P < 0.01), demonstrating that maintenance of vegetative growth and photosynthetic capacity was directly associated with higher bulb productivity.

Discussion

The weed community in this study was dominated by annual species, mainly from the Asteraceae and Poaceae families. This pattern aligns closely with what has been documented in other semi-arid, rainfed farming systems3,5,18. The prevalence of short-cycle weeds makes perfect sense given their ability to establish quickly and compete aggressively when moisture is limited and unpredictable. These results underscore the need for timely weed intervention, especially in the early stages of crop growth when resource competition is most intense10,42.

Among the herbicides we tested, oxyfluorfen stood out by delivering the strongest reduction in weed biomass, clearly outperforming ioxynil and oxadiazon. This superior performance likely stems from its action as a protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO) inhibitor, which triggers the buildup of phototoxic porphyrins and the production of reactive oxygen species compounds that can harm both weeds and, to some extent, crop tissues. Comparable weed control with oxyfluorfen has been noted in other Allium crops21,24,30. By contrast, hand weeding achieved total weed removal, reinforcing its reliability as a non-chemical option11,13,14. Interestingly, however, even though the herbicides suppressed weeds effectively, they did not translate into better yields. These findings indicate that the physiological costs associated with herbicide application may offset the benefits of weed suppression, thereby limiting improvements in crop performance under the present conditions.

The noticeable drops in leaf dry weight and leaf area index (LAI) under herbicide treatments point to some physiological drag on crop growth. Earlier work has described similar setbacks, where post-emergence herbicides can temporarily slow vegetative development through off-target effects or mild phytotoxicity19,31,32. Hand weeding, on the other hand, produced the highest values for these traits, showing that removing both weed pressure and chemical stress helps the canopy develop more fully43,44. The fact that LAI under hand weeding was fairly close to the untreated control further implies that both weed competition and herbicide exposure can shape canopy architecture45.

Bulb yield reached its peak with hand weeding, while the herbicide treatments and the untreated control showed no meaningful differences among themselves. It appears that any gains from chemical weed control were largely cancelled out by the negative effects on growth parameters. This kind of trade-off between weed suppression and overall crop output has been reported in other Allium species as well14,31,32,46. A noteworthy and initially counterintuitive result was that bulb yield under herbicide treatments did not differ significantly from the untreated control despite reduced weed biomass. This may indicate that weed pressure, although reduced, remained above the threshold required to limit yield formation. In addition, the observed reductions in chlorophyll content and protein levels, together with increased antioxidant activity, suggest that herbicide-induced physiological stress partially offset the benefits of weed suppression. Such trade-offs between weed control efficiency and crop physiological performance have also been reported in other Allium systems under moderate weed pressure31,32.

Photosynthetic pigment levels were lower in all herbicide plots, especially with oxadiazon, compared with hand weeding47. These declines probably resulted from disruptions in chlorophyll synthesis or faster pigment breakdown, a pattern seen in previous research20–22. Higher pigment concentrations under hand weeding, in turn, reflected more favorable conditions for photosynthesis9,48.

Proline levels were highest in the untreated control and lowest with hand weeding, with herbicides falling in between. This fits the well-established role of proline as a reliable marker of plant stress, whether osmotic or oxidative8,9. We also saw elevated activities of antioxidant enzymes (CAT and POD) in the control and some herbicide treatments, signaling that the plants were ramping up their defenses against oxidative stress20,29. The lower enzyme activity under hand weeding clearly indicated reduced overall stress when both weeds and chemicals were kept in check.

Soluble protein content was highest with hand weeding and lower under herbicide use, hinting at possible changes in protein metabolism caused by stress. Comparable drops in protein levels after herbicide application have appeared in earlier studies31,32. When viewed alongside the antioxidant enzyme data, these results suggest that both weed competition and chemical treatments can shift the plant’s physiological balance49,50. This reduction may be associated with stress-induced protein degradation, inhibition of protein synthesis, or reallocation of nitrogen toward defense-related metabolism, as reported under herbicide-induced oxidative stress conditions8,9,20.

Notably, we found no significant treatment effects on soluble sugars, electrolyte leakage (EL), relative water content (RWC), or total phenolics. These parameters stayed relatively stable across the board, and we could not detect any clear physiological response tied to the treatments.

Year-to-year differences affected several traits, including leaf dry weight, LAI, bulb yield, and proline content. Although weed biomass itself did not differ statistically between years, we did see some numerical variation; the lack of consistent significance suggests that annual weather shifts had only a modest impact on how the treatments performed. This could be linked to lighter weed pressure and changed resource competition during drier seasons6,27,51. Importantly, the absence of any year × treatment interactions confirmed that the effects of each strategy were consistent across both growing seasons.

Correlation analysis backed up these observations. Weed biomass was negatively linked to growth and yield traits but positively associated with proline and antioxidant enzyme activity relationships that match earlier reports on weed-induced stress1,9,52. At the same time, the strong positive correlations among leaf dry weight, LAI, and bulb yield highlighted how these performance indicators tend to move together.

From a grower’s point of view, the data show that hand weeding is still the most reliable way to maximize Persian shallot performance under semi-arid conditions. Among the herbicides, oxyfluorfen gave reasonably good weed control while causing comparatively milder physiological side effects. Even so, the lack of yield gains with any chemical treatment means farmers should think carefully before relying solely on herbicides44.

In summary, these results emphasize the need to balance strong weed suppression with keeping crop stress as low as possible in semi-arid systems. Herbicides certainly bring practical benefits, but their potential to affect plant physiology should not be overlooked53. More multi-year and multi-site studies will help strengthen these conclusions and refine weed management advice for the future.

These insights are especially valuable for smallholder farmers in semi-arid regions, where labor shortages and high costs often make intensive hand weeding difficult to sustain.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated clear trade-offs between mechanical and chemical weed control strategies in Allium hirtifolium under semi-arid conditions. Season-long hand weeding provided complete weed suppression and consistently resulted in superior crop performance, including higher bulb yield, leaf development, and photosynthetic capacity, alongside reduced physiological stress indicators such as proline accumulation and antioxidant enzyme activity. Among the herbicides evaluated, oxyfluorfen achieved the highest level of weed suppression and exhibited comparatively lower negative effects on crop physiological traits than ioxynil and oxadiazon, making it the most balanced chemical option. However, the absence of yield improvement under herbicide treatments suggests that gains in weed control efficiency may be offset by herbicide-induced physiological constraints. The stability of traits such as soluble sugars, electrolyte leakage, relative water content, and total phenolics indicates a degree of physiological resilience in A. hirtifolium under both weed competition and chemical exposure. Although inter-annual variation influenced some growth parameters, treatment responses remained consistent across years. From an applied perspective, hand weeding remains the most reliable strategy for maximizing yield and maintaining physiological stability, particularly in smallholder systems. Where labor limitations exist, oxyfluorfen may be considered within integrated weed management programs, provided that its potential physiological impacts are carefully managed. Further multi-location and long-term studies are recommended to refine these findings and support the development of sustainable weed management strategies.
