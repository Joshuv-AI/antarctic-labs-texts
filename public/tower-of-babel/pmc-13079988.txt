Conformational equilibria in the activation of cystine-knot hormone receptors
Authors: Zhen Gong, Wayne A. Hendrickson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCystine-knot hormones such as thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) act through cognate receptors (TSHR here) to effect responses (cyclic AMP production here). Despite substantial evidence that TSHR acts as a dimer in cells, it is monomeric as detergent solubilized. The homologous nematode receptor remains dimeric as extracted from cell membranes, and its atomic structure provides a basis for understanding the conformational equilibria associated with hormone activation in this receptor family. These equilibria and models provide testable hypotheses for how hormones elicit their responses.

Glycoprotein hormones (GpHs) and their receptors (GpHRs) together constitute neuroendocrine systems that are present in all vertebrates, but their evolutionary origins go back to the earliest metazoan animals (e.g. sponges and comb jellies) (1–3). The human GpHs comprise the pituitary hormones thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) as well as chorionic gonadotropin (CG) from the placenta. The gonadotropins FSH, LH, and CG act on FSHR and LHCGR receptors expressed in the gonads, whereas TSH acts on TSHR in the thyroid gland (4–6). The vertebrate receptors and their ligands coevolved in two rounds of whole genome duplication (WGD) at the outset of vertebrate evolution, when agnatha (lampreys and hagfishes) appeared, probably with TSH and TSHR coming in WGD1 and FSH and LH and their receptors following in WGD2 (2).

CKHs and LGRs.

Many of the invertebrate predecessors of GpHs are unglycosylated, but they all have cystine knots (7) distinct from those of other cystine-knot proteins, which are largely transcription factors such as the equivalently ancient transforming growth factor β (TGFβ). Since GpH is a misnomer for the family as a whole, we adopt cystine-knot hormone [CKH; (3)] as the generic descriptor for these hormones. Human GpHs are heterodimers composed of a common α-chain and one of four hormone-specific β-chains. The α and β chains are alike and both include the eponymous cystine-knot three disulfide bonds and three associated loops. The invertebrate homologs, which persist in vertebrates but were discovered later, are called α2 and β5.

The GpHRs are in a family of G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) called leucine-rich-repeat-containing GPCRs [LGRs; (8)]. An LGR is characterized by a large extracellular domain (ECD) with multiple leucine-rich repeats (LRRs), to which the hormone binds, and a rhodopsin-like transmembrane domain (TMD) of seven transmembrane (TM) helices, with which heterotrimeric G proteins interact. The subfamily of type A LGRs comprises the GpHRs and the invertebrate CKH receptors. Other types of LGRs are activated by distantly related hormones (bursicon) or unrelated ligands (R-spondin, relaxin) (9).

Physiology.

CKH/GpH signaling systems are essential for human reproduction and metabolism, and they are well studied in humans and other mammals; however, less is known about their roles in invertebrates or other vertebrates. Genes for CKH and LGR proteins are present throughout metazoan life (3), and there is appreciable genetic characterization for this neuroendocrine system in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, which has a single LGR gene fshr-1 and the CKH genes glpa-1/glpb-1 encoding α2/β5. Diverse phenotypes have been defined by mutational disruption of these genes, including significant impact on germline development and fertility (10) and on growth and gut formation (11, 12). Both the C. elegans hormone (Ceα2β5) and its receptor (CeLGR) have been characterized structurally and biochemically (13, 14).

Dimerization.

Many GPCRs form functionally relevant dimers (15–18). For GpHRs, a particularly impressive demonstration of dimer functionality came in the restoration of biological function in vivo when LHCGR receptors deficient in binding and ones deficient in signaling are coproduced in mice (19), and a model for such transactivation is provided by that occurring naturally in human GABAB receptors (20–23).

Several lines of evidence, including such functional complementarity, establish the property of GpHR dimerization: 1) Associations as dimers and other oligomers are found in studies of Förster and bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (FRET and BRET) between labels on receptors in membranes (24–27) and by superresolution microscopy (28). 2) Negative cooperativity in hormone binding (29–31) implies functional association in complexes with asymmetric receptor dimers having 1:2 hormone:receptor stoichiometry (26, 32, 33). 3) The switch in TSH:TSHR signaling, at higher TSH levels, from Gαs engagement to produce cAMP to Gαq engagement to produce inositol phosphates requires 2:2 hormone:receptor complexes (32), and similarly, the switch to Gαi/Gαo usage in cAMP signaling is thought to entail 2:2 complexes (34, 35). And 4) in vitro complementation restores lost activity through coexpression of binding-deficient and signaling-deficient receptor mutants restore signaling activity (26, 36–38).

Structure.

Despite evidence for a role of dimers in cellular activity, many GPCRs are monomeric as isolated for biochemical and structural analysis. In particular, all of the human GpHRs are monomers as detergent extracted for cryo-EM structural analysis (39–43). Although in vitro biochemical signaling activity has been demonstrated for nanodisc-isolated β2AR monomers (44), such tests have not been reported for the GpHR monomers. It may be that the functional dimerization of human GpHRs requires the localization to biological membranes and their association with lipids, which are removed by detergents. GPCR dimers may be transient and concentration dependent in general (18), as is also shown for TSHR dimers in particular (34).

Unlike the human GpHRs, the CeLGR counterpart is a stable dimer as solubilized for biochemical and structural analysis; however, each of its protomers is highly similar to the apo-state human GpHR monomers (13). Properties of the dimer interface, at which TMD residues predominate, may explain the CeLGR dimer stability. Although the interfacial sequence identity between CeLGR and the human GpHRs is highly conserved, seven residues at the CeLGR interface are distinct from ones identical among the human GpHRs and Drosophila LGR1 (45), which like the GpHRs also purifies as a monomer (13).

Motivation.

By coupling the CeLGR dimer structure with the several structures of monomeric human GpHRs and GpH:GpHR complexes, we have the opportunity to gain insight into the several conformational states of CKH family dimeric receptors. We have previously discussed such modeling (2, 13). Here, we elaborate a theory for the conformational equilibria that govern the elicited activities, we define and quantify the transitions of receptor activation, and we develop and refine atomic models for each of the conformational states. Ultimately, these models need to be tested against experimental validations; in the interim, the models provide a framework for understanding the biochemical and physiological evidence for dimers in cellular signaling by CKH receptors.

Results

Equilibrium Theory.

The action of CKH receptors involves multiple equilibria between states (Fig. 1). Dimers must equilibrate with monomers; both protomers of the dimer as well as the monomer may each equilibrate between “down” and “up” ECD orientations, in principle either with or without a bound hormone molecule, and in the dimers, intrinsically asymmetric 1:2 hormone:receptor complexes must equilibrate with 2:2 complexes, which likely will be at least quasisymmetric. Additionally, there are equilibria to consider between hormones and receptors and between heterotrimeric G proteins and activated receptors, whether hormone-bound or constitutively active. Physiologically, these equilibrations take place in situ for receptors localized to biological membranes; however, many biochemical characterizations require solubilization into detergent micelles, nanodiscs, or proteoliposomes. The characteristics of equilibration will likely differ depending on environment.

Although properties of the three vertebrate GpHRs are similar to one another and also to their invertebrate predecessors, TSHR is the one most closely related in structure and evolution to CeLGR, the best studied invertebrate CKH receptor (13). Moreover, the greatest body of evidence on the role of dimers in CKH activation of LGRs is that from the TSH-TSHR system. Therefore, our attention here is focused on CeLGR and human TSHR (HsTSHR).

Considered in its totality, these CKH-LGR systems are highly complex. Moreover, the characterization of reactions that occur within membranes (receptor dimerization), at membrane surfaces to membrane-tethered ligands (heterotrimeric G proteins), or at membrane surfaces to ligands free in solution (hormone binding) is not entirely straightforward, neither theoretically nor experimentally. Nevertheless, we proceed with a hypothetical analysis for these receptors in natural biological membranes, making simplifications based on observed properties of GpHRs in cellular experiments.

Equilibria of dimerization and hormone binding by monomeric receptors.

There must exist a monomer-dimer equilibrium for membrane-bound receptors, which we assume to be asymmetric in keeping with the negative cooperativity and half of the sites binding of TSH to TSHR (26, 33) and the CeLGR structure comprising distinctive protomers A and B (13). Furthermore, based on the monomeric cryo-EM structures, we expect the ECD-down conformation of the inactive state to predominate in the absence of hormone. where Md, Ad, and Bd refer respectively to the monomer and protomers A and B, all in the ECD-down state, and Kd2Ddimer designates the 2D dissociation constant defined by the relative concentration of monomer and dimer components in terms of molecules per unit area. The latter contrasts with molecules per unit volume for the conventional Kd3Ddimer constant, and since receptors in the membrane are preoriented relative to the membrane surface and diffusion is limited to movement within the bilayer, we expect receptor dimers to prevail more readily in the membrane than when detergent-solubilized (46).

In principle, the monomeric receptor in the membrane should bind the hormone, and this reaction can be formulated as[2]Md⇌KEqMMu→KidMHMu·H,

where KEqM is the equilibrium constant between the ECD-up (Mu) and ECD-down (Md) states, and KidM is the intrinsic hormone (H) binding constant to Mu to form the Mu·H complex.

Whether dimer equilibration occurs within a physiologically relevant range can be questioned since HsTSHR and other GpHRs purify as monomers (39–43) and little of the TSHR in primary cultures of human thyrocytes is dimeric (34); however, as Boutin et al. (34) also show, homodimers are prevalent when the level of TSHR mRNA in the cultured thyrocytes is promoted to that found in human thyroid tissue. Thereby, the Gs → Gi switch in cAMP production (35) proceeds normally. This switch is thought to require 2H:2R complexes as for the Gs → Gq switch that accompanies IP3 production (32). Similarly, Urizar et al. (26) showed that negative cooperativity (dimer dependent) in TSH binding to TSHR is the same over four levels of HsTSHR expression in COS-7 cells, which is as in porcine thyroid membranes. We conclude that physiologic dimers prevail in functioning tissue, and we proceed here to treat properties of the dimeric population.

Equilibria of conformation and hormone binding by dimeric receptors.

Based on observations of distinctive binding equilibria for a first (Kd1) and a second TSH binding (Kd2) to TSHR and the coincidence of TSH concentration for the second binding event (26, 33) with the TSH concentration needed for the switch from Gαs to Gαq usage for inositol phosphate production (32), three distinct states exist for the TSHR dimer: complexes bearing zero (0H:2R), one (1H:2R), or two (2H:2R) hormone molecules per receptor subunit [figure 7 of (13)]. As above for hormone binding to monomers, we now separate the binding to each protomer into an equilibrium to the hormone-receptive conformation and an intrinsic, essentially rigid-body binding of the hormone to that conformation. where, as for the Md and Mu monomers, Ad, Au, Bd, and Bu designate the down and up ECD orientations for the A and B protomers, respectively.

Dissociation constants (5 and 6) are a curious admixture of receptor concentrations measured as receptor dimers per unit area and hormone concentrations measured in hormone molecules per unit volume; nevertheless, the ultimate Kd values are in the 3D units of the hormone. Indeed, such is the case for the dissociation constants obtained biochemically from the kinetics of TSH binding to TSHR receptor molecules as produced in cultured cells (33).

The model of Eqs. 3–6 postulates that protomer A is ECD-up and protomer B is ECD-down in the asymmetric 1H:2R state (Au·H/Bd). We suggested previously that both the Au·H/Bd and Au·H/Bu conformations should be feasible [SI Appendix, Fig. S12 of (13)]; however, the Au·H/Bu alternative fails to explain the observed anticorrelation between constitutive activity by TSHR mutants and the Kd2 values for these mutants. Whereas affinity at the first site stays essentially the same independent of constitutive activity, affinity at the second site asymptotically approaches that of the first site as the constitutive activity increases [SI Appendix, Fig. S1; data of (33)]. Mutants showing greatest Kd2 sensitivity have their mutations at the ECD–TMD interface of TSHR when in its TSH-activated, ECDup state. Thereupon, activating mutant ECD residue S281I contacts TMD ECL1 residue I486 (40), and its Y279 neighbor is apposed to 486 such that aromatic rings would be stacked in the mutant I486F to stabilize the ECDup conformation (42).

When at full activation, as presumed for TSH:TSHR at high [TSH] or for a fully constitutive TSHR mutant (S281I and I486F), intrinsic activities are the same at the two sites, Kid2′ = Kid1′ ≈ Kid1 ≈ Kd1, and this functional equivalence implies structural symmetry for the ECDs in the Au·H/Bu and Au·H/Bu·H states. Somehow, the required symmetrization happens in coordination with the ECD down-to-up activation of protomer B; thus making Kd2 sensitive to the ECDup:TMD interface in Au·H/Bu whereas Kd1 in Au/Bd is not sensitive to constitutive mutants.

Receptor-Activating Transitions.

CKH binding to a cognate LGR entails a large, quasi-rigid rotation of its ECD relative to its TMD, both of which remain little changed within. In turn, the activating transition generates significant changes in TMD conformation whereby the heterotrimeric G protein can be engaged for signal transmission to a second messenger, cAMP or IP3. The activating transition can be quantified by first superimposing TMDholo of the hormone-bound receptor onto TMDapo in the apo-state frame of reference r0, and then transforming ECDapo (ECDdown) onto ECDholo (ECDup) of the holo structure as so superimposed. This transformation can be effected by[7]rA=RAr0+tA

The activating rotation matrix, RA, can be evaluated in Euler angles or in polar coordinates (φ, ψ, χ) for the single rotation of χactivation about the axis oriented by φ and ψ (47) and by translation tχ along that axis, whereby tA = tχ (48).

Similar shifts in ECD orientation were observed between the inactive apo and active hormone-bound conformations for LHCGR with CG (39), for FSHR with FSH (41) and for TSHR with TSH in two independent studies (40, 42). Invariably, these transitions are described as being from a “down” to an “up” or “upright” orientation of the ECD relative to the hypothetical membrane layer. Both protomers of the CeLGR homolog are also similarly down in its apo state structure (13); however, we noticed in studying CeLGR that the ECD-activating rotations are actually more complex than a simple down-to-up rotation. In fact, the azimuthal component, rotation about the membrane surface, is about double the change in elevation above the surface.

Molecular frame of reference.

In order to resolve the rotational components and to treat all CKH/LGR systems in a uniform manner, we define a molecular Cartesian frame referred to axes intrinsic to the ECD and TMD and we introduce an orthogonal transformation for the activating transition. The local frame of reference is established by two axes in the apo state: 1) a vector L along the LRR axis of the ECD and 2) a vector s roughly parallel to the TMD helices and normal to the putative membrane surface. In our case, we define the LRR-axis L by Cα positions in conserved and ordered features at either end of the LRR, and we set s to be the screw axis that relates protomer A to protomer B in CeLGR, even though this axis is inclined slightly from the normal to the membrane plane defined by hydrophobic belt of the CeLGR dimer (13). The human GpHRs are referred to this s axis by superposition of their TMDs onto that of CeLGR protomer A.

As defined in a specified laboratory frame, such as in a PDB file, the LRR-axes are designated LI0 for the ECDapo, the inactive state and LA0 for the ECDholo, the active state, and the s-axis is designated s0 in this laboratory frame of reference r0 (x0, y0, z0). Transformation to our molecular frame r (x, y, z) is accomplished by rotation matrix RM:[8]r=RMr0.

The resulting molecular frame has z along s, y along n=L1 ×s=LI0 × s0, and × mutually perpendicular (n × s) (Fig. 2A). These specifications define rotation matrix RM, which also brings LI0 to L1, LA0 to LA, and s0 to s.

Resolution of activating transitions into orthogonal components.

Our transformation to resolve the single rotation χactivation into orthogonal components of azimuthal rotation and altitudinal elevation is accomplished in the molecular Cartesian frame after a succession of orthogonal and physically meaningful rotations (Fig. 2B). These rotations are described fully in SI Appendix, Methods, and they are simply summarized here with particular reference to the disposition of LRR-axis L. This axis, and with it the whole of ECD, is first brought from LI0 in the laboratory frame to L1 in the molecular frame. Then, the first rotation R1 of α1 about the z axis along s moves ECD azimuthally about the membrane surface and takes L1 with it to L2 while maintaining the same altitude, θ0. This extends an intrinsic motion evident in the 7° distinction between protomers A and B of CeLGR at their TMD–ECD junctions (13). The second rotation R2 is by α2 about y′ to raise L2 to L3 and into alignment with LA, the LRR axis of the activated state.

Angles α1 and α2 derive from the dispositions of L1 and LA relative to s in the molecular frame. The inclinations α0 and αA, as well as the respective complementary altitude angles θ0 and θA (Fig. 2), are specified by the cross products L1 x s and LA x s. Atomic Models for CKH Receptor Dimers.

We wish to obtain experimental structures for CeLGR and for HsTSHR at an atomic level in each of the conformational states expected from the equilibria of Eq. 3, and progress toward this goal has been made by us and others. Taken in its entirety, this is a challenging aspiration; however, rigorous modeling can usefully complement the known structures of CeLGR and HsTSHR. Our previously proposed models for CeLGR in alternative conformational states (13), while only preliminary, provided an outline for these conformational equilibria. Here we refine those and related models with more sophisticated optimization procedures; we critically assess the models with validating procedures, and we develop corresponding models for dimeric HsTSHR as well as for CeLGR.

For the most part, this model building could be initiated from known cryo-EM structures and rigid-body transformations of domains from these structures. For the TMDs, the distinctions between apo inactive-state and holo active-state structures could be taken from known structures in the respective states, assuming conservation of conformation between paralogs. For the ECD, where conformational changes are larger, this is not so readily accomplished; however, we realized that the ECD can be dissected usefully into three modules, whereby the modeling is facilitated (Fig. 4A): 1) the quasi-rigid extracellular domain (QED), the switch activation segment (SAS), and the hinge variable segment (HVS).

Mechanistic dissection of ECD structure.

QED, the largest portion of the ECD as so divided into operational modules, moves as an essentially rigid body from ECDdown of the inactive state to ECDup of the active state. It comprises the N-terminal segment (NTS) and the entire leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, excepting that the HVS is inserted between hinge helix 1 (HH1) and the final repeat strand (LRR12). Although the successive strands of many LRR proteins are connected by α helices, most interstrand connections in the LRRs of LGR proteins are extended strands without regular structure but similar to one another. The connection between LRR11 and LRR12 is exceptional in that this return includes not only the HH1 helix but also the HVS insertion. The SAS is a conformational switch that communicates hormone binding at the ECD to the TMD and into G-protein activation. It comprises 15 highly conserved residues that link LRR12 of the QED to TM1 of the TMD and make a concerted change of extended-strand conformation as the QED moves from the inactive ECDdown to the active ECDup state. The HVS insertion rides with the QED as it rotates on activation, but it also changes conformation in the process, generating contacts with the bound hormone. Most of the HVS is disordered in inactive-state GpHR monomers (39–42); however, much of it is ordered in the inactive-state protomers of the CeLGR dimer (13).

Besides informing the model building, dissection of the ECD into these modules fosters insight into the mechanism of activation. Hinge helix HH1 is central to the activation mechanism. Its vicinal cysteine residues, C311 and C312 in CeLGR, are disulfide linked respectively to LRR12 and to the P10 segment. HH1 and LRR12 move quite rigidly with the QED module as it rotates by ~49° (Table 1) about the rQ origin, located just outside the vicinal cysteine residues; however, P10 and the entire SAS module move independently. This results in the superposition of Fig. 4B. As HH1 elevates on activation, it draws the P10 segment into an altered conformation—so that the SAS switches from the inactive-state (Fig. 4C) to the active-state conformation (Fig. 4D). Being tethered to the QED (at its N-terminal end via LRR12 and in the middle via disulfide linkage to the second cysteine of HH1), the SAS thereby redirects TM1 at its N-terminal end as the QED moves about its vicinal-cysteine fulcrum. Concomitantly, TM1 and associated TMD elements (notably TM7) necessarily shift in concert (Fig. 4E). These movements must be transmitted through the TMD to complete signal transmission from hormone binding to G-protein activation.

The HVS module is not directly in signal communication, but it does change during activation to become involved in hormone contacts. There is little in common among the active-state structures observed for these modules except for variable lengths of disordering beyond disulfide-linked and QED-associated ends of the HVS. Despite the structural differences, there are HVS contacts to the bound hormone in each case—to α for the HH1-linked outgoing segment and to β for the LRR12-linked returning segment. Fig. 5 A and B illustrate these interactions for FSHR and TSHR, respectively; the HVS of LHCGR is distinct and less ordered, but like FSHR in lacking long helices. Although the HVS is entirely disordered for each inactive-state GpHR, its terminal segments are ordered in both protomers of the CeLGR dimer (Fig. 5C). Active-state structures are not yet known for CeLGR and AlphaFold fails to predict an ECDup conformation; however, expecting hormone interactions as for the GpHRs, substantial change in HVS conformation must occur. Such change is beyond the reach of available modeling techniques, but we did prepare a model for the Au·H/Bd state with disulfide bridging and HVS:α2β5 contacts (Fig. 5D) reminiscent of those in TSHR:TSH (Fig. 5B).

Model building challenges and procedures.

The molecular architecture is essentially the same for all receptors of the family, at least as represented by the human GpHRs and the CKH receptors of bilaterian animals. As shown in the structure-based sequence alignment in SI Appendix, Fig. S1 of (13) and in Table 2 here, all of these proteins have very similar domain sizes except for variations in the NCR (NTS and first LRR) of the QEDs, the HVS insertion, and the C-terminal extension (CTE). To large extent, different states of activation relate to differences in the dispositions or conformations of principal domains, notably ECDup vs ECDdown and TMDactive vs TMDinactive. This leaves many alternatives to consider; however, as so simplified and with only single asymmetric and symmetric TMD dimer states considered, these are manageable.

Domain sizes of CKH receptors

Besides dealing with the rather complicated activating changes in both ECD and TMD conformation, other challenges needed to be addressed: 1) the mode of association of Ceα2β5 with CeLGR remains unknown; 2) the mode of TMD association in putative symmetric CeLGR dimers of 2H:2R complexes is unknown; and 3) the mode of TMD association in TSHR dimers is unknown. The approaches taken to address these and other challenges in the model building are described more fully in SI Appendix, Methods, but we summarize them here: 1) three alternative approaches for preparing a model of Ceα2β5:CeLGR association were tested, of which the AlphaFold starting point performed best; 2) AlphaFold models of CeLGR dimers, including those with bound Ceα2β5 hormones proved to be symmetric, and one of these showed well packed interfaces; and 3) presuming that similarities between CeLGR and TSHR extend to their dimer associations, we superimposed the TMD of TSHR onto the TM1-TM5 of both CeLGR protomers in each state with adjustments in the fittings of TM6 and TM7.

Bearing these considerations in mind, we proceeded to build and optimize atomic models for each conformational state in the hormone activations of CeLGR and HsTSHR. In the process, we also consider alternatives and intermediate states.

Models for the inactive apo 0H:2R states.

The reported cryo-EM structure of CeLGR as an asymmetric dimer in the apo state (13) provides the foundational model for CKH receptor dimers. This became our Ad/Bd CeLGR model. The corresponding apo-state model for TSHR (Ad/Bd) was generated by superposition of the TMD and ECD from an inactive-state structure of HsTSHR onto those of CeLGR. As for all of these nonexperimental models, this one for an inactive TSHR dimer was then relaxed by Rosetta procedures (49, 50).

Models for asymmetric 1H:2R activated states.

We presume that the hormone-bound protomer will have an ECDup conformation similar to that in the human GpHR complexes and that the asymmetry of dimeric TMD association will be retained as for apo CeLGR. To avoid bias to a chosen GpHR, we built the active state by rotating the LRR domain in protomer A through averages of transition angles α1, α2, and α3 from the four pairs of GpHR structures (Table 1). This Au/Bd conformation readily accepts Ceα2β5 to produce the Au·H/Bd state. We adopted a mode of hormone association based on the AlphaFold model of Ceα2β5:CeLGR, as described in Methods. This complex is stabilized by hormone contacts from the HVS module as repositioned such as in Fig. 5D.

A similar “active-state” conformation can be built for protomer B (Ad/Bu); however, as described previously (13), only the raised protomer A can accommodate Ceα2β5 since the binding site on protomer B is blocked by the CCR hinge region from protomer A. In addition, partly because of hormone contacts made to the B protomer when Bd but not when Bu, the buried surface area and interface energy favor the Au·H/Bd complex over the Au·H/Bu alternative (SI Appendix, Table S1). Moreover, were the Au·H/Bu conformation already formed before second hormone binding, there would be no basis for the effect of constitutive activation mutants on the affinity for second hormone binding.

As for other GPCRs, the TMDs of activated LGRs are distinguished from inactive counterparts by distinctive conformations, the most salient feature of which is a kinked TM6 preparing the domain for Gα engagement. Because TM6 and TM7 predominate in the LGR dimer interface, a kinked TM6 proves to be readily feasible only for an activated protomer A, which is also the subunit that can accommodate hormone binding.

Model for the symmetric 2H:2R activated states.

Our modeling for the 2H:2R state is inspired by the observation that Kd2, for second hormone binding, is inversely correlated with the potency of constitutive activation mutants, such that the second hormone binds with the same affinity as the first when at high constitutive activity [SI Appendix, Fig. S1; data of (33)]. This equivalence implies structural symmetry, including hormone binding accessibility at Bu being the same for symmetric Au·H/Bu as initially at Au for asymmetric Au/Bd. We achieved this symmetry in our modeling for Au·H/Bu·H by use of AlphaFold as described in Methods. Whereas two hormones would collide if modeled into the asymmetric CeLGR dimer, symmetric models for both CeLGR and HsTSHR accommodate two bound hormone molecules without collision provided that αelevation < ~21.5°; indeed, stabilizing hormone–hormone contacts form at appropriate ECD elevations (SI Appendix, Table S1). The most potent of constitutive activation mutations (e.g. S281L and I486F) are engaged at the ECDup–TMD interface (40, 42), and they also promote equivalence of first and second hormone binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We take this to mean that the ECDdown-to-ECDup transition in protomer B occurs concomitantly with the symmetrization needed to accommodate the second hormone.

Here, as for the TMD interface of an asymmetric LGR dimer, only one of the TM6 helices can readily be in a kinked conformation. Moreover, as previously noted (13), the asymmetric CeLGR dimer can only accommodate a single heteromeric G protein, and this remains the case for our symmetric 2H:2R models of both CeLGR and HsTSHR.

Discussion

In this study, we develop a comprehensive model for the activation of CKH receptors, synthesizing structural information from the human GpHRs and the CeLGR dimer in the light of biochemical and physiological evidence on GpHR activity. Our analysis entails a theory for the conformational equilibria involved in the activations, a quantifiable geometric model for the activating transitions, and energetically feasible atomic models for both CeLGR and HsTSHR in their major conformational states. These developments have implications for understanding of cellular signaling by CKH receptors.

Conformational Equilibria.

It is perhaps a tautology to relate activity to the equilibria between the conformational states of CKH receptors since we established these equilibria to describe the observed activities. Nevertheless, because GpHRs exhibit negative cooperativity and other complexities in their binding and signaling activities, the equilibria described by Eqs. 3–6 provide useful descriptions. We contemplate each hormone binding event as an initial equilibration step to acquire a hormone-accessible conformation, KEq1 for the first and KEq2 for the second, followed by intrinsic binding steps governed by Kid1 and Kid2. Thereby, Kd1 = KEq1 × Kid1 and Kd2 = KEq2 × Kid2. In essence, then, KEq1 and KEq2 characterize energy barriers due to conformational changes that must accompany hormone binding.

The substantially asymmetric structure of the CeLGR dimer (χB→A = 142°, tχB→A = 4.1Å; 13) inspires a structural hypothesis for the 1H:2R hormone:receptor state expected from the negative cooperativity observed for TSHR and other GpHRs (26, 33). In a 1H:2R complex that remains asymmetric in an Au·H/Bd conformation (Fig. 6 [middle]), the prospective second hormone-binding site is blocked. Therefore, a conformational change is needed to make it accessible, and that change will generate an energy barrier reflected in KEq2.

This barrier to second-hormone binding is encountered in the switch from TSHR Gs to Gq signaling for IP3 rather than cAMP production, and it is overcome as 2H:2R complexes form at higher TSH levels (32) as is also thought to occur in Gs-activating to Gi/o-inhibitory switch (34, 35). A hint into the nature of such 2H:2R TSH:TSHR complexes is provided by the study of Zoenen et al. (33) into the correspondence of negative cooperativity with specific constitutive activity (SCA). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1, Kd1 is independent of the SCA level, whereas Kd2 depends strongly on SCA, such that for SCA above some level (~20 in SI Appendix, Fig. S1) Kd2 = Kd1. In terms of our equilibria (Eq. 6), KEq2 is reduced at high SCA such that Kd2 = KEq2 x Kid2 → Kd1, which implies structural symmetry and Kid2 = Kid1. The conformational change defined by KEq2 entails both ECDB elevation, facilitated by interface mutations such as S281L and I486F, and concurrent rotation from the asymmetric Au·H/Bd state to a symmetric Au·H/Bu·H receptor state.

Since Kd1 is independent of SCA, it seems that the KEq1 barrier is insensitive to SCA, despite the role of SCA mutations at the activated ECD–TMD interface. The strong sensitivity of Kd2 to SCA is then somewhat surprising, and the molecular basis for the observed coupling of the ECDdown-to-ECDup transition with the symmetrization is a puzzle.

Activating Transitions.

Although functionally significant conformational change does occur within both the ECD and the TMD portions of CKH receptors, the most striking conformational change in the receptor from hormone activation is the movement of most of the ECD as a quasi-rigid body relative to the TMD. Whether our dissection of the ECD movement into its orthogonal azimuthal, elevation and twist components has utility and physical meaning may be less clear.

We argue that the breakdown into components does provide meaningful distinctions. First, in keeping with elevation, these GpHR transitions have been described from the outset as rotations of ECD from a “down” to an “up” or “upright” orientation, which are needed to avert hormone-membrane collision (39, 42); however, such descriptions overly simplify the motion. As we show (Table 1), activation moves the LRR domain horizontally about the membrane normal as well as vertically away from the membrane surface. An intrinsic azimuthal motion is evident from the 7° distinction between ECD–TMD orientations of protomers A and B of CeLGR (13). Moreover, the azimuthal component actually measures to be approximately twice the elevation (averaging −54° vs. 27°; Table 1), which is in accord with the intrinsic dynamics inherent to the CeLGR dimer as revealed by 3D variability analysis (3DVA). Extremes of movement from the 3DVA show azimuthal and elevational deviations of 18.0° and 9.7°, respectively (13).

The third orthogonal component, αtwist about the LRR axis of the ECD, is not so readily noticed on visual inspection even as hormone-bound; a model for the asymmetric 1H:2R state of CeLGR (Au·H/Bd) could be accommodated whether the twist is applied (Fig. 6, Middle and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) or not (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). In contrast, importance of the twist is clearly evident for its symmetric 2H:2R state (Au·H/Bu·H), where the twist needs to be applied to achieve an acceptable model (Fig. 6, Right and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), but where profound collision would occur in absence of the twist (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).

Atomic Models.

We built our models for CeLGR and HsTSHR dimers in apo, one-hormone, and two-hormone states by the redisposition of domains from experimental structures or from validated AlphaFold models, and we then used the sophisticated modeling tools of Rosetta (47, 48) to optimize these models to energetic plausibility. Structures within the domains have a sound foundation as does the dimer interface for the CeLGR apo (0H:2R). The suppositions of TMD interface equivalence for the one-hormone (1H:2R) state and symmetry for the two-hormone (2H:2R) state seem plausible. Nonetheless, because of general limitations of the modeling procedures and untested assumptions in formulating the complexes, these are only models and necessarily lack the imprimatur that comes from experiment.

Results from comprehensive validation tests are reassuring (SI Appendix, Table S1): The Rosetta optimizations assure an appropriate energetic balance in each case; the MolProbity clash scores are excellent in all cases, assuring that the models are essentially clash-free both within and between domains; each domain–domain interface is energetically favorable; most of the interfaces have substantial buried surface area, and the shape complementarity is excellent (> 0.6) for all but a few of the interfaces. Thus, we expect that these models will be useful for structure-based hypotheses in studies of receptor action.

Functional Implications.

Our model for CKH receptor activation assimilates atomic models for receptor dimers in their relevant states into the conformational equilibria among these states and descriptions of the transitions between states. This formulation provides a foundation for addressing pertinent questions on mechanism of biochemical action, potentially in a quantifiable manner. Here we pose just a few of such questions:

How does hormone binding evoke receptor activation? It seems most likely that CKH binding is a matter of conformational selection; i.e., we presume that, even if the equilibrium governed by KEq1 (Eq. 3) only rarely populates the hormone-receptive AuBd state, this suffices for hormone capture in the Kid1 step. A first challenge is to understand the structural consequences and sequence of events entailed in the Ad → Au transition, which we break down as illustrated in Figs. 2 and 4. How does the HVS module refold to engage hormone tips? How does the reconfigured SAS/P10 segment impact the TM segments? Even the classic questions of GPCR mechanism have CKH-receptor-specific embodiments—how the impulse of binding propagates into the further TMD changes including TM6 kinking, G-protein engagement, and GDP → GTP exchange depend on characteristics of the dimer.

Why dimers and how do they work? Conventional class A GPCRs such as β adrenergic receptors operate normally as monomers (44); however, the activity of CKH receptors such as TSHR is modulated by negative cooperativity (26, 33) and switching of G isotype usage (32–34)—properties that are linked inextricably to dimerization. CeLGR is a markedly asymmetric dimer, in keeping with the negative cooperativity in TSHR, and we invoke symmetrization to explain the effect of mutations that promote second-hormone binding as they impart constitutive activity. It remains a mechanistic puzzle as to how it happens that second-hormone binding (Au·H/Bd → Au·H/Bu·H) entangles the ECDdown-to-ECDup transition with the 38° rotation of symmetrization. There also remains the concomitant puzzle of how this second-hormone binding is coordinated with the TMD changes required to switch from Gs usage for cAMP production to Gq usage for IP3 production.

What is the mode of G-protein activation in CKH receptor dimers? This question relates to TMD activations in general, but, in particular, it concerns functional complementation experiments, which suggest signaling in trans in a 2R:1G complex—the hormone must bind to the signaling-deficient subunit and the “signal” must pass through the binding-deficient but G-associated subunit. Our models for the binding of G heterotrimers to CKH receptor dimers also impose a similar asymmetry since only one Gαβγ can fit per dimer; however, opposite from the complementation case, here we find that only the A protomer in our Au·H/Bd complex can have TM6 kinked for G binding, which suggests signaling in cis. This puzzle on signal transfer within TMD dimers is one to which ongoing work on class C receptors might contribute since these are natural in-trans signaling systems—the GABAB receptor binds GABA at subunit B1 but signals to Gα through B2 (21–23).

Experimental Testing.

Being explicit and atomic detailed, our model for CKH receptor activation has potential for discriminating tests. A comprehensive research plan is beyond feasible scope for here; however, we can contemplate tests to resolve predominate puzzles and ambiguities. The hypothesis that HsTSHR is dimeric in natural membranes but simply fails to survive detergent extraction might be tested by cryo-EM analysis of HsTSHR in native or reconstituted membrane vesicles (52) or by alternative experiments. The asymmetric-to-symmetric conformational change proposed to accompany second hormone binding might be tested by appropriate single-molecule FRET experiments using hormone-initiated reactions on native thyroid membranes or CeLGR-transfected HEK293 cells. Functional complementation of binding-defective and signaling-defective CeLGR proteins can be tested in vivo with phenotypic assays (10, 11) in appropriately selected heterozygotic C. elegans worms. And, of course, the atomic models offer many opportunities for incisive point-mutation tests.

Methods

The methods employed in this paper are described in full in the SI Appendix, Methods section of the accompanying supporting information.

Receptor-Activating Transitions.

The mathematical foundations for the rigid-body transformations involved in receptor activation Eqs. 7–16 are elaborated fully in SI Appendix, Methods, Eqs. 17–26. This first entails defining rotation matrix RM and the molecular frame of reference. The orthogonal transformation for the activating transition of ECD relative to TMD is then defined in terms of rotation of the ECD about s by αazimuth (α1), about the reoriented n by αelevation (α2), and then about the elevated L by αtwist (α3). Next, the transformation of the LRR axis from the inactive state LI to the active state LA is dissected into the α1 rotation to L2, the α2 rotation to L3, and finally the α3 twist about L3 to complete the superposition of ECDapo onto ECDholo. We also define the point of intersection or midpoint of closest approach, rQ, of two axes, Li and Lj, and the deviation at closest approach, Δi,j, and the angular deviation, δi,j, of the two axes.

Generation and Refinement of Atomic Models.

The model-building procedures used to generate atomic models of CeLGR and HsTSHR in each relevant conformational state are defined in full in SI Appendix, Methods. This entailed manual building of initial models, often testing several alternatives, followed by geometry minimization using the phenix.geometry_minimization tool, and then by energy relaxation with Rosetta FastRelax (49, 50). The alternative minimizing to the lowest converged FastRelax score was selected as the final model and further evaluated using Rosetta InterfaceAnalyzer (SI Appendix, Table S1).

For the symmetric two-hormone state, four of five AlphaFold models for CeLGR had TM6 and TM7 at the TMD interface as expected, and the model with the largest buried surface area and highest shape complementarity value (model 3 in SI Appendix, Table S2) was selected to build the CeLGR Au·H/Bu·H model.

Since experimental structures of ECD:hormone complexes are not yet known for CeLGR·H complexes, we tested three alternative procedures. Of these, the AlphaFold-predicted complex was selected because it yielded the lowest separation energy and a favorable buried surface area and shape complementarity value (model 3 in SI Appendix, Table S3).
