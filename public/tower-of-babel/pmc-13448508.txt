Long-term major histocompatibility complex class I suppression in astronauts and clinical implications
Authors: Erik LeRoy, JangKeun Kim, Helena Ruland, Nathan Schanzer, Anurag Sakharkar, Lucinda Innes, Zachary Feinstein, Daniel Zuniga Gamboa, Robert Chen, Mohith Reddy Arikatla, Kubra Can Kurt, Brian E Crucian, Michael A Schmidt, Christopher E Mason
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpaceflight can disrupt astronaut immunity, including sustained downregulation of Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I genes across missions ranging from the three-day Inspiration4 flight to long-duration International Space Station stays. MHC class I downregulation is a known mechanism of immune evasion in cancer and viral infection, potentially increasing health risks during and after spaceflight. The underlying mechanisms, clinical implications and therapeutic options remain underexplored. This Perspective discusses long-term risks associated with astronaut MHC class I suppression, proposes future research directions and outlines therapeutic approaches with broader relevance to related health challenges on Earth.Subject terms: Immunology, Biomarkers

Introduction

The space environment—encompassing hazards such as space radiation, microgravity, isolation and confinement, the enclosed spacecraft environment, and the distance from Earth—poses potential risks to human health and performance1,2. Of particular concern is the dysregulation of the immune system, the persistent reactivation of latent herpesviruses, and, in select crew members, immune-related clinical symptoms during and after spaceflight. Although not a pervasive clinical problem for orbital flight, these symptoms encompass a range of phenotypes, including generalized inflammation, atopic dermatitis, atypical allergy, and mild infectious diseases3,4. Indeed, clinical symptoms associated with immune dysregulation were reported on an average of 3.40 events per flight year, representing 46% of crew members4. During the construction era of the International Space Station (ISS), up to 61% of ISS astronauts were reported to have shed one or more herpes viruses in saliva4,5. The latent virus reactivation rates have dropped in more recent crews, a finding commonly attributed to the deployment of multiple biomedical countermeasures5. Recently, however, a case report detailed the first case of laboratory-confirmed herpes zoster onboard the ISS, with disease correlating with peak magnitude in both stress and immune compromise6.

Another component of the space environment is space radiation, composed of high-energy protons and heavy ions, which has shown carcinogenic properties7,8. While it has been reported that the spaceflight environment affects viral reactivation, inflammation, and cancer risk, a research gap exists in the underlying mechanisms behind these issues, as well as future research directions and therapeutic interventions, underscoring the need for further studies. These needs are heightened when considering that most of the biomedical countermeasures onboard ISS that have benefited immune systems rely on frequent resupply and large power/volume requirements and do not readily transfer to the operational constraints of a deep space vehicle design9,10. For that reason, novel immune-restorative countermeasures for deep space missions must be considered9–11.

Interestingly, a recent astronaut multi-omic study reported that MHC class I genes were downregulated in a long-term (over 6 months) manner in multiple biospecimens (blood, plasma, immune cells) from different missions (NASA Twins Study, Inspiration4, JAXA cell-free epigenomics Study)12 (Fig. 1). MHC class I downregulation is known to be one of the immune evasion mechanisms employed during viral activation and cancer progression13. In this Perspective, we address the long-term risks of MHC class I downregulation, provide a guide to future research directions, and discuss potential therapeutic approaches (Fig. 1).

The role of MHC class I

Vertebrates initiate an immune response to defend the body after an infection or exposure to a foreign body14. T cells play a crucial role in this process by recognizing infected cells, often killing them, and sending out activation signals to other immune cells14. Additionally, natural killer (NK) cells play an important role in the immune system, particularly in viral infections and tumor cell destruction15. Molecules of the MHC class I complex, located on the cell surface16, are essential for this recognition. The MHC class I system is designed to sample intracellular, cytosolic proteins to detect tumors or intracellular pathogens, such as viruses and intracellular bacteria. These molecules then migrate to the cell surface to present antigens, which can be identified by T-cell receptors (TCRs)16 and killer Ig-like receptors (KIRs) of NK cells15,17.

The MHC class I molecule, found on the cell surface of nucleated cells, is composed of two polypeptide chains: a heavy α-chain and an invariant light chain, called β2-microglobulin (B2M). The α-chain consists of three domains (α1 α3), with the α1 and α2 domains form a peptide-binding groove that interacts with the T-cell receptor itself18,19, and an α3, subunit that interacts with the T cell co-receptor, CD8, during T cell recognition18,19. The heavy chains are encoded by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes, with each subgroup (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) having multiple alleles18. This polygeny and polymorphism enable a wide variety of possible ligands to be bound and presented on the cell surface18.

Antigen presentation by MHC class I is essential to the process of T-cell differentiation. The process of endogenous antigen presentation begins with antigens being broken down into peptides by proteasomes in the cytosol20. These antigenic peptides are then transported to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) by the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), where they are further trimmed to lengths ranging from 8 to 10 amino acids18. A peptide-loading complex assists in binding the peptide to MHC class I molecules, resulting in the formation of a peptide-MHC-I (pMHC-I) complex18. Finally, the complex is released from the ER and is transported to the plasma membrane, where it can be recognized by CD8+ T cells or NK cells18. Simultaneously, naïve (not-activated) T lymphocytes circulate throughout the lymphatic system, scanning MHC class I complexes of cells21. When they encounter “self” peptides presented by MHC class I, they initiate the release of tonic signals, ensuring the survival of naïve CD8+ cells18. Conversely, “non-self” antigens activate T cells, triggering an immune response19.

There are three different types of effector cells21. Regulatory T cells inhibit the development of autoimmunity, maintaining immune system balance21. Helper T cells activate other components of the immune system21. They stimulate macrophages to kill bacteria in intracellular vesicles as well as signal B cells to produce antibodies against extracellular pathogens. Finally, cytotoxic T cells directly kill infected cells in a contact-dependent mechanism. Cytotoxic T cells recognize foreign peptides of the target cell in the context of host MHC class I by the cytotoxic TCR, leading to the formation of a conjugate with an immunologic synapse. Within minutes, the cytotoxic T cell activates apoptotic cell death in the target cell. This process is mediated by rapid mobilization of cytotoxic T cell granules to the synapse and exocytosis of granule contents19, including granzymes and perforin16. Granzymes are serine proteases that target various proteins in the host cell, leading to the activation of apoptosis. In a parallel apoptotic pathway, TCR activation in the immune synapse drives expression of Fas ligand on the cytotoxic T cell22. This, in turn, engages Fas (CD95) on the target cell membrane, again triggering apoptosis. Together, these mechanisms defend the body by combating infections effectively21.

Alongside T-cells, NK cells show a strong response against physiologically stressed cells, such as tumor cells and virus-infected cells. NK cells express several activating and inhibitory receptors, such as KIRs, that respond to altered expression of proteins on target cells and initiate pathways for cell death (apoptosis) and control of physiologic stress15. MHC class I molecules play a crucial role in displaying proteins on the surface of target cells and are involved in the activation and regulation of NK cells. Normal, healthy cells express MHC class I molecules on their surface, which act as ligands for inhibitory receptors and contribute to the self-tolerance of NK cells15.

Inhibitory receptors on the NK cells include (i) KIRs, which belong to the Ig superfamily and are specific for determinants shared by groups of HLA-A, HLA-B or HLA-C allotypes, and (ii) CD94/NKG2A, a heterodimer related to C-type lectins that recognizes HLA-E17. Both types of inhibitory receptors (KIRs and CD94/NKG2A) display immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motifs (ITIMs), which are tyrosine-phosphorylated upon crosslinking, and recruit tyrosine phosphatases that dephosphorylate activating adaptor molecules and shut down activation pathways leading to NK cell-mediated cell death17.

However, virus-infected cells or tumor cells lose surface MHC class I expression, resulting in lower inhibitory signals in NK cells15. Viral infections and tumor development can induce cellular stress responses, including DNA damage, senescence, and activation of tumor suppressor pathways, which in turn enhance the expression of ligands that activate immune receptors on affected cells15. A lack of MHC class I on the cell surface results in inadequate triggering of NK cell activation. Complete NK cell activation also requires recognition of stress-induced molecules by NK cell-activating receptors. The activating receptor NKG2D is a C-type lectin-like type II transmembrane protein expressed as a homodimer on the surface of all human NK cells. NKG2D ligands are expressed at low levels in healthy murine cells; however, they are upregulated and widely expressed in tumors of diverse tissue origin (i.e., lymphoma cells, lung, colon, rectal and prostate cancer)15. Other types of activating receptors include: (i) activating KIRs15, (ii) natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs) which recognize a variety of ligands on target cells ranging from viral, bacterial and parasite proteins to molecules from tumor cells and other host cells15, (iii) CD244 (2B4) which can act as both an activating and inhibitory transducer depending on environmental influences15, and (iv) CD38 which is an enzyme that has been shown to promote the cytotoxic activity of NK cells against tumor cells by promoting the degranulation of NK cells15,22–24. NKG2D, activating KIRs, and NCRs require phosphorylation of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAM) by the Src family of tyrosine kinases. Src activation triggers a molecular cascade that results in the elevation of calcium levels, reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton, release of cytolytic granules containing perforin and granzymes, and transcription of cytokine and chemokine genes15. Overall, the signal from activating receptors in NK cells shifts the balance toward NK cell activation and elimination of target cells directly through NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity or indirectly through secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. However, to induce activation of NK cells, there needs to be a synergistic activation of either NKG2D and 2B4 (CD244) or NKG2D and NCRs15,25,26. This synergistic activation of several receptors leads to convergence of signals toward a central signaling pathway so that even low effector molecule levels can reach the threshold required for NK cell activation.

The antigen presentation pathway, mediated by MHC class I, is also essential for the recognition of damaged or infected cells, differentiating self from non-self-antigens, and training of immune cells to mount an immune response27. This process begins with the degradation of antigenic peptides during the cell’s routine protein catabolism28. Within the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, 20S and 26S proteasomes break down endogenously synthesized proteins into oligopeptides28. Specialized forms of proteasomes, such as immunoproteasomes, differ in their active site subunits29. These immunoproteasomes exhibit distinct catalytic properties, producing a variety of peptides, some of which have a higher affinity for binding to MHC class I molecules compared to those generated by standard proteasomes30,31. The production of immunoproteasomes is induced by inflammatory signals, such as cytokines31. Some peptides generated by proteasomes and immunoproteasomes bind to the TAP32. TAP then translocates these peptides into the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum32.

Simultaneously, the co-translational transport of heavy and light chains of MHC class I molecules into the ER occurs, followed by their folding into the MHC class I heterodimer33. The peptide-loading complex loads high-affinity peptides into the binding groove of the empty MHC class I molecule33–35. The coordination of peptide translocation and loading is facilitated by the interplay of seven different subunits33. After proofreading by a multivalent chaperone network, stable peptide-MHC-I complexes are released to the cell surface and present antigens to CD8 + T cells or NK cells, thereby initiating an immune response33.

MHC class I downregulation in disease

Virus-infected and cancer cells have evolved various mechanisms to evade the immune surveillance of the host organism36. The adaptive immune response is initiated when cytotoxic CD8 + T-lymphocytes recognize ‘non-self’ antigen peptides presented by MHC class I molecules on the surface of cells, as reviewed previously21. As a countermeasure, virus-infected and cancer cells have developed strategies to downregulate their expression of the MHC class I molecules, thereby avoiding detection and destruction by the adaptive immune system37,38. This immune evasion facilitates the progression of infectious diseases and malignant transformations36.

While the immune evasion mechanisms of virus-infected and cancer cells can be used to bypass T cell-mediated cell destruction, NK cells are also part of the innate immune system and can mount an immune response against both virus-infected and cancer cells—especially in cases where critical functional proteins and enzymes in the antigen presentation pathways have been downregulated, mutated, or deleted. For example, cytotoxic (CD8 + ) T cells require presentation of neoantigens by cancer cells on their cell surface, utilizing MHC class I in order to create immunological synapses that result in a cascade of molecular signals leading to cancer cell death39. On the other hand, NK cells can detect and rapidly destroy tumor cells with downregulated MHC class I expression, a phenomenon known as “missing self” recognition39. NK cells do not require any priming or prior activation, which makes them effective for eliminating virus-infected cells when antigen presentation pathways have been compromised39. As mentioned previously, virus-infected or tumor cells lose surface MHC class I expression, leading to lower inhibitory signals in NK cells. Because MHC-class antigen presentation is not essential for cell growth or survival, many cancers or viruses will inactivate this pathway and escape CD8 + T cell-mediated destruction. Such immune evasion allows cancers or viruses to progress and also become resistant to CD8 + T-cell-based immunotherapies, such as checkpoint blockade40. Summary of strategies to target disruptions to MHC Class I normal expression

As MHC class I molecules are inherently unstable in the absence of bound peptides, defects in any component of the antigen presentation pathway can result in the loss of surface MHC class I expression37. Notably, the expression of MHC class I molecules is dispensable for cellular growth and survival37. Consequently, downregulation of this pathway leads to a selective advantage for these cells, allowing unrestrained proliferation while evading immune surveillance37. This downregulation can occur through various mechanisms, which differ between cancer and virus-infected cells.

MHC class I genes are regulated through multiple mechanisms. Their promoters contain three key cis-regulatory elements: the Enhancer A locus, which is bound by nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB); the Interferon Stimulated Response Element (ISRE), targeted by interferon regulatory factors (IRFs); and the SXY module, which interacts with NOD-like receptor family CARD domain containing 5 (NLRC5) to activate transcription41–43. These regulatory elements can be disrupted in various disease states. Beyond direct transcription factor binding, MHC-I gene expression is influenced by epigenetic factors such as histone and DNA modifications.

In cancer cells, immune evasion mechanisms include: genomic mutations combined with selection pressure of tumor-associated antigens44; mutations or deletions in genes encoding crucial components of the antigen presentation pathway; transcriptional dysregulation; reduced stability of mRNAs or proteins involved in the pathway; and alterations in signaling cascades that regulate MHC class I expression37. Virus-infected cells, on the other hand, employ viral immune evasion proteins to inhibit critical functions of the MHC class I antigen presentation pathway, thereby evading recognition by CD8 + T cells45.

MHC class I downregulation in cancer

The downregulation of MHC class I has been purported to occur as a primary means for solid tumors to circumvent anti-tumor protection (Fig. 2A, B)13. There are several mechanisms responsible for downregulating MHC class I. Understanding these mechanisms and their protean nature can enable the development of mitigation or reversal strategies, leading to improved prognosis for cancer and tumors.

The NF-κB family modulates MHC class I expression during normal cellular function. When either the canonical or non-canonical NF-κB pathways are activated, NF-κB factors can translocate to the nucleus and influence gene expression by binding to the Enhancer A locus within MHC-I gene promoters. Different tumors can affect NF-κB expression, and thus, the expression of MHC class I complexes. Several types of cancer affect the expression of MHC class I by upregulating or downregulating proteins that influence the NF-κB pathway. For example, the pediatric tumor neuroblastoma is well-known for having characteristically low MHC class I expression, and two major negative regulators have been identified to influence the low expression of MHC class I in this tumor13. These include Nedd4 Binding Protein 1 (N4BP1) and TNFα-induced protein 3 interacting protein 1 (TNIP1). N4BP1 exerts its effect on both the canonical and non-canonical NF-κB pathways, and TNIP1 exerts its effect on the canonical pathway alone, decreasing expression of MHC class I13.

NLRC5 is a critical trans-activator of basal MHC class I expression in immune cells, as well as other genes in the antigen presentation pathway, such as TAP1 and beta-2 microglobulin (B2M)43,46. NLRC5 binds the SXY module, a conserved element in all MHC class I promoters, to activate transcription. Some tumors specifically target NLRC5 pathways to suppress MHC class I expression and evade immune detection47. Interferon signaling is essential for inducible MHC-I expression, particularly through interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). IFN-γ signaling leads to phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), which promotes the formation of IRF complexes42. These complexes localize to the nucleus and bind the interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE) in MHC class I promoters to activate transcription. Tumors frequently downregulate this pathway, reducing MHC class I expression48.

In addition to cis-regulatory mechanisms, tumors also disrupt epigenetic modulation of MHC class I genes. Hypermethylation of MHC class I loci and NLRC5 has been observed in several cancers47,49. Histone deacetylation at MHC class I promoters is another mechanism by which tumors suppress gene expression, with histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors demonstrating the ability to restore MHC class I expression50. Dysregulation of Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) components, such as enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), leads to increased trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me3) at MHC class I loci, further silencing expression51,52. Recent studies also implicate Polycomb Repressive Complex 1 (PRC1), which deposits a repressive ubiquitin marker on histone H2A, in repressing MHC class I expression in cancers such as Merkel cell carcinoma and cutaneous melanoma53,54. Moreover, loss of BRCA1-associated protein 1 (BAP1), a histone H2A deubiquitinase, has been shown to reduce MHC class I expression in these cancers54.

Beyond these pathways, there are several oncogenic pathways that can provide more insight into the nature of MHC class I downregulation, including MAPK-, EGFR, HER2, c-MYC, and n-MYC pathways55. MHC class I impairment has been proposed as a result of the MAPK pathway, which negatively influences MHC class I expression via decreased IRF1 activity and STAT1 expression. IRF1 and STAT1 play an important role in the interferon type I and II-mediated upregulation of MHC class I13. Therefore, tumors that reduce IRF1 and STAT1 expression can result in decreased MHC class I antigen presentation. In certain cancers, such as breast, melanoma, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, MHC class I activity has been found to be negatively correlated with the HER2 protein, a member of the EGFR family13. MHC class I downregulation has been shown to be linked to cancer-related genes, including c- and n-MYC. Suppression of NF-κB protein p50 in the setting of rodent neuroblastoma allows for n-MYC to downregulate MHC class I13. The Wnt/B-catenin pathway has been proposed as another catalyst for MHC class I downregulation through the expression of the c-MYC gene13.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are known to have the capacity to quell activation of T cells in humans and rodents. One of the primary contributors to certain T cell immunity is the activation of MHC class I resistance brought on by MDSCs. Congruently, individuals with varying cancer types showed elevated blood MDSC prevalence. STAT3 transcription factor is believed to be a primary influencer of the activation and behavior of MDSCs. Rodents with known tumors showed elevated amounts of STAT3 within MDSCs relative to cancer-free control subjects56. STAT3’s involvement in MHC class I downregulation in cancer can be described in two pathways, which are illustrated in the graphic below (Fig. 3)57.

The first pathway involves the STAT3-mediated negative regulation of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Kida et al. suppressed STAT3 expression in various NSCLC cells using specific small-interfering RNAs (siRNA) and showed that upregulated STAT3 in cancer cells negatively regulates chemokines, including CCL5 and CXCL1057. These specific chemokines play an important role in cancer-induced inflammation by activating subpopulations of immune effector cells to mount an immune response against the tumor cells. STAT3 reduces the production of these chemokines, thereby hindering immune effector cells from fulfilling their function. Furthermore, Kida et al. demonstrated that STAT3-depleted non-small cell lung cancer cells exhibited elevated chemotaxis compared to cells that were not manipulated57. Overall, hyperactivated STAT3 in cancer cells decreases the expression of immune-stimulating factors, including interferons (IFNs), pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-12, TNF-α), and chemokines (CCL5, CXCL10), while increasing the expression of certain cytokines and growth factors (IL-6, IL-10, TGFβ, and VEGF) and can influence tumor development and progression58. STAT3 is also able to inhibit activation of the IKK-complex and prevent the phosphorylation and degradation of IkB, which sequesters NF-κB transcription factors in the cytosol. Because STAT3 can inhibit immune activation and NF-κB signaling, it can also significantly reduce MHC class I expression and allow tumor cells to avoid immune effectors13.

The second STAT3-dependent pathway can be characterized as enabling a cancer cell to circumvent the intervention by natural killer cells. Generally, NK-cell activation relies upon the balance between inhibitory and activating signals from surface receptors on the target cell. One of the major inhibitory receptor ligands is MHC class I57. On the other hand, MHC class I chain-related gene A/B (MICA/B) is a primary ligand for the engagement of natural killer cell receptors. Evidence of STAT3’s influence includes greater MICA/B expression in non-small cell lung cancer cells when STAT3 is downregulated. These same cells have a heightened sensitivity to natural killer cell cytotoxicity when STAT3 small interfering RNA is present57. Thus, hyperactivation of STAT3 in certain cancers decreases MICA/B expression and reduces NK cell activation signaling. This affords the tumor the ability to avoid killing by NK cells and circumvents an important method of immune intervention.

Dhatchinamoorthy et al. revealed that common cancers, such as melanoma, colorectal, breast, and prostate, have been associated with individuals presenting with reduced MHC class I expression37. MHC class I expression may differ because of the type of cancer, duration, and location in the body. The absence of, or a decrease in, levels of TAP and tapasin can cause significant reductions in the levels and expression of MHC class I. In addition, cells that lack ERAP1 can experience a wide range of MHC class I level variations. Endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) is a process carried out by certain viruses during which MHC class I molecules are displaced from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cytoplasm. Once in the cytoplasm, the virus is able to target the MHC class I molecule37.

The downregulation of MHC class I may have implications separate from the progression of cancer itself. It is believed that MHC class I downregulation can be linked to certain forms of cancer becoming resistant to cancer therapies. Disturbances in the behavior of MHC class I and its expression have been linked to certain cancers becoming resistant to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies (Fig. 4)59. Activation of genes involved in cancer cell growth and proliferation has been linked to MHC class I downregulation in individuals receiving PD-1 inhibitors (an immune checkpoint inhibitor) (Fig. 5)59. Individuals with melanoma responded positively to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, during which NF-κB expression was present. Conflictingly, MHC class I expression has been shown to be hindered as a result of certain tumors’ ability to impair NF-κB communication13. These findings have implications for future investigations on cancer treatment and immune evasion.

MHC class I downregulation in viral activation and its mechanism

Virus-infected cells employ diverse strategies to evade detection by CD8 + T-cells, including inhibiting peptide generation, blocking peptide transport, retaining MHC class I in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and promoting degradation of MHC class I molecules. Virus-infected cells utilize viral proteins to inhibit the MHC class I antigen presentation pathway, thereby evading recognition by CD8 + T cells. Some viruses prevent viral epitope generation without affecting proteasome function, like the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) EBNA-1 epitope and the Kaposi sarcoma herpes virus (KSHV) LANA-1 epitope, which resist degradation due to unique repeat regions60,61. EBNA-1 blocks antigen presentation through a repeat region of glycine and alanine, which interferes with the proteasomal degradation of EBNA-160. The LANA-1 repeat has a strongly acidic region of around 580 amino acids, which consists of aspartic acid, glutamine, and glutamic acid residues. It is hypothesized that the acidity of this region could inhibit antigen processing60. Murine cytomegalovirus (mCMV)‘s m27 protein inhibits IFN-gamma signaling, disrupting immunoproteasome formation and antigenic peptide generation62. Rhesus Cytomegalovirus’s Rh178 protein disrupts MHC class I heavy chain synthesis by binding the signal peptide, blocking ER insertion63,64. TAP-inhibiting epitopes such as HSV-1’s ICP47, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV)‘s US6, varicellovirus UL49.5, and EBV’s BNLF2a prevent peptide transport into the ER65–72.

Tapasin is a key component of the peptide-loading complex that stabilizes peptide-receptive MHC class I molecules and facilitates high-affinity peptide loading33–35. HCMV’s US3 protein and adenovirus’s E3-19K protein disrupt this process, hindering MHC class I from participating in the complex73–75. Cowpox virus’s CPXV203 protein and mCMV’s M152 gene product retain MHC class I in the ER and ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, respectively, preventing surface expression76–80. US2 and US11 proteins in HCMV, and mK3 protein in mouse herpesvirus 68 (MHV68), facilitate MHC class I heavy chain retrotranslocation and degradation via the ERAD pathway81–84. K3 and K5 in KSHV tag MHC class I for endocytosis and lysosomal degradation, reducing surface molecule expression45,85–91. mCMV’s m06 protein and immune evasions such as HHV-7’s U21 protein and EBV’s BILF1 protein, also direct MHC class I to lysosomal degradation92–94.

The immune system employs various methods to counter these evasion strategies. NK cells detect infected cells lacking MHC class I expression through the “missing self” process, balancing activating and inhibitory signals via specific receptors on the target cell surface. For example, in mouse models, mCMV’s m04 protein prevents complete MHC class I downregulation, allowing detection by inhibitory Ly49 receptors on NK cell surface (in humans these receptors are called KIRs) and avoiding NK cell activation95. Finally, viral infections can also cause host shutoff, suppressing cellular protein synthesis by reducing mRNA levels and impairing translation, leading to MHC class I downregulation, and rendering these cells resistant to lysis by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells96–98. Responsible viral gene products are encoded in HSV’s UL41 and BGLF5 proteins and KSHV’s ORF37 protein96–98.

Clinical implications of MHC class I downregulation

Therapeutic approaches developed for diseases can be repurposed for MHC class I long-term depression countermeasures. The strategies outlined here are broadly categorized into treatments, therapies, and biological targeting. Treatments include traditional cancer medications and preventative measures. Therapies are strategies that manipulate the genetic code or involve adoptive cell therapy approaches. Biological targeting describes any strategy that involves modulating a specific component of a known pathway.

Treatments

Some chemotherapies at IC50 values increase MHC class I protein expression in colon cancer cells99, while methylseleninic acid restores MHC class I antigen processing in melanoma cells by mimicking IFN-gamma signaling100. Radiation and photodynamic therapy (PDT) further enhance MHC class I expression, likely by boosting cytotoxic T cell and NK cell responses across various cancers101–103. Additionally, chemotherapy and radiation induce MHC class I expression through pathways including NF-κB, interferon-β, and mTOR, which may support combination treatment strategies13,101,104. Cytokine therapy offers another avenue, with IL-2 and IL-10 enhancing or restoring MHC class I expression in papillary thyroid cancer cell lines105,106. The transcription factor NF-IL6 also promotes MHC class I gene expression in mice107.

Therapies

Emerging approaches like gene therapy, adoptive cell therapy, and vaccination strategies aim to enhance MHC class I expression and improve anti-tumor immunity. Gene editing tools, such as CRISPR/Cas9, have been applied to correct or enhance MHC class I genes in tumors, including non-small cell lung cancer108, while viral vectors deliver these genes directly to tumor cells109. In adoptive cell therapy, T cells engineered to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) or T cell receptors (TCRs) specific to MHC class I-presented antigens have shown effectiveness against various cancers, with FDA-approved CAR-T therapies for conditions like acute lymphoblastic leukemia110 and multiple myeloma111,112. Vaccination strategies, including peptide vaccines and dendritic cell vaccines, leverage tumor-associated antigens presented by MHC class I molecules to elicit strong cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses113,114, while vaccines for gene-modified tumors have shown promise in preclinical models115. Interferon-gamma promotes MHC class I HLA gene (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) expression by interacting with Interferon Regulatory Factor 1 and Interferon Sensitive Response Elements in gene promoters116, an effect observed across various cancer models, including prostate cancer117, pancreatic beta cells118, and thymic epithelial cells119. This upregulation can also be observed with tumor necrosis factor, which overcomes repression mechanisms in adenovirus-transformed cells120.

Immunotherapy and targeted therapy

Additional inhibitors, such as the proteasome inhibitors, restore the STAT1 pathway, leading to increased MHC class I and PD-L1 expression121, while EGFR inhibition boosts MHC class I and II gene expression, with noted increases in MHCI protein and both MHCI and MHCII RNA levels in cancer patients122. Inhibiting geranylgeranyl transferase stimulates anti-melanoma immune responses, crucial for MHC class I expression123, and BET inhibition targets BRD4 to elevate MHC class I levels in head and neck cancers124. Moreover, the topoisomerase I inhibitor irinotecan enhances MHC class I and PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, depletes regulatory T cells, and boosts the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapies125. Other strategies include PIKfyve inhibition to increase MHC class I and CD8 + T cells in tumors126, HER2 depletion to upregulate MHC class I in breast and esophageal cancers127,128, and immune checkpoint inhibitors to restore T cell function against low MHC class I-expressing cells129. Additionally, certain oncogenes are involved in the downregulation of MHC class I molecules55, and targeting these oncogenes, such as DUX4130 and PIN1131, with specific inhibitors can restore MHC class I expression.

Compensation for low MHC class I expression

Several studies have revealed diverse mechanisms that upregulate or compensate for low MHC class I expression across various cellular contexts. In neurons, MHC class I expression is regulated by calcium-driven signaling pathways involving protein kinase C, the MAPK pathway, and the phosphorylation of CREB and NF-κB p65, which upregulates IRF-1132. Microglial secretion of TNF-α in response to amyloid-beta elevates MHC class I expression on brain endothelium, facilitating T cell migration across the blood-brain barrier133. The tumor suppressor Fhit also regulates MHC class I by modulating antigen-processing machinery components, with its restoration in tumor cells reinstating MHC class I surface expression134. Modifiers like BAP1 can prevent the silencing of MHC class I expression by removing ubiquitin modifications at MHC class I promoters54. Additionally, oxidative phosphorylation in tumor cells induces MHC class I expression via the ERK5 pathway135, while RUNX1-containing transcription complexes in T cells activate MHC class I gene expression136.

Prognostic markers

MHC class I expression serves as a crucial prognostic marker across various contexts. In viral infections, SARS-CoV-2 variants and HPV influence MHC class I expression, affecting immune recognition and disease progression, with treatments like IFN-gamma and 5-azacytidine modulating these effects137–141. Changes in MHC class I during measles virus and HIV/SIV infections impact viral dynamics and disease outcomes142–146. In cancers, high MHC class I expression is generally associated with better prognostic outcomes; for instance, in head and neck cancers, it correlates with improved survival when coupled with CD8 + T cells and PD-L1 expression147–149. In colorectal cancer, MHC class I expression enhances prognostic value, particularly in liver metastases150–152. Conversely, in lung cancer, MHC class I downregulation may be linked to prolonged survival, while in pancreatic cancer, MHC class I/II-restricted peptide vaccinations show potential as prognostic markers153–155. Urothelial carcinoma studies suggest that MHC class I predicts response to checkpoint inhibitors in metastatic cases but lacks prognostic value in localized disease156. Multiple myeloma progression and hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence are also associated with MHC class I-related markers157,158. In autoimmune diseases, MHC class I deficiencies impact conditions like myasthenia gravis and type 1 diabetes159,160.

Potential risks from MHC class I downregulation in astronauts

Long-duration space flights pose major risks to the health of astronauts. One of the most affected systems during spaceflight is the immune system. Studies show that astronauts’ immune systems are weakened, impaired, and susceptible to many viral and bacterial infections3,4. The hemostasis of the immune system is thought to be impaired due to factors such as radiation, gravitational change, disturbances in circadian rhythm, and psychological stress. However, the long-term consequences for astronauts of these complex mechanisms are unclear161,162.

Interestingly, during recent government and private spaceflight missions, MHC class I post-flight downregulation was consistently reported in astronauts, irrespective of spaceflight duration and orbital altitude12. Specifically, Inspiration4 (I4), a 3-day spaceflight reaching altitudes of 575 km, reported the long-term suppression of MHC class I genes (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, B2M) over 3 months in the I4 astronauts’ immune cells and over 6 months in plasma cfRNA. Consistently, JAXA plasma cfRNA profiles, derived from astronauts residing more than 120 days on the ISS, reported the long-term suppression of MHC class I genes over 6 months. Moreover, the NASA Twins Study, a year-long mission to the ISS, showed a consistent decrease of MHC class I genes (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, B2M) genes in immune cells12 (Fig. 6).

MHC class I long-term depression after spaceflight has, to date, not been studied extensively. However, there have been several studies addressing the phenomenon in the context of antigen presentation, regarding IFN-γ and in vitro culture under ground-based simulated microgravity (e.g., using clinostats, random positioning machines, or rotating-wall vessel bioreactors, as summarized), which reproduce altered gravitational loading but not the full spectrum of spaceflight stressors. Lv et al. reported that microgravity inhibits the antigen presentation ability of dendritic cells163 though it is still not clear whether MHC class I long-term depression is a factor contributing to this finding. In a 2015 study, Crucian et al. observed a significant decrease in IFN-γ levels in blood samples of astronauts participating in long-term spaceflights, which is notable since IFN-γ signaling should increase MHC class I and MHC class II production164. Hicks et al. reported that the expression of MHC class I, MHC class II, CD80, and CD86 was all up-regulated in dendritic cells cultured for 14 days in a microgravity environment165. However, MHC class I expression itself has not been the major focus of this research, nor its clinical implications.

As MHC class I downregulation has been implicated in the immune evasion mechanism of viral infection and cancer cells, its long-term depression (e.g., over 6 months in I4 plasma cfRNA profiles) could potentially result in increased post-flight viral infection, endogenous viral reactivation, and long-term risk of carcinogenesis. Though MHC class I long-term depression has been recently reported in multiple astronauts, biospecimens, and missions, it has not been studied in detail across various mission parameters, model organisms, and model systems. Clarification is required regarding whether the long-term downregulation of MHC class I indeed coincides with any significant clinical risks for astronauts. If future research validates this theory, underlying molecular mechanisms need to be elucidated, and clinical interventions need to be developed to mitigate astronaut health risks and improve performance during long-term spaceflight missions.

Additionally, astronauts are expected to experience altered stress hormone levels during and after spaceflight, although evidence regarding salivary stress markers remains mixed166,167. Several studies have reported elevated glucocorticoids in in-flight and post-flight plasma and urine samples167–169. Chronic stress has been associated with reductions in T cell numbers170, blunted beta-adrenergic receptor signaling, eventually lowering cAMP levels and exhausting CD8 + T cells171 and, in murine models of psychological stress such as restraint-induced stress, both upregulation of PD-L1 and reduced IFN-γ, which facilitate tumor immune evasion172,173. However, the specific contribution of spaceflight-associated chronic stress to MHC class I regulation remains uncertain. Future studies should clarify whether stress-related pathways interact with the observed long-term depression of MHC class I and evaluate potential implications for post-flight immune function and disease susceptibility.

Future research approaches: study design and therapeutic approaches to MHC class I downregulation in astronauts

To fully understand and address the breadth and depth of the impacts of MHC class I long-term depression, several mechanistic, clinical implications, and therapeutic research approaches can be considered (Fig. 7). One prong of this research strategy is meta-analysis. A thorough review of astronaut data repositories, such as the NASA Open Science Data Repository (OSDR), would allow for (i) the identification of consistent patterns of MHC class I downregulation across missions of varying durations and altitudes, (ii) correlate MHC class I levels with other documented physiological changes, and (iii) explore potential genetic predispositions by analyzing astronaut data for polymorphisms in MHC class I genes and related pathways. In addition, applications of MHC profiling technologies, such as TCR mapping of antigenic peptides (TCR-MAP)174 or immunopeptidomics of astronaut samples, could provide more insight into the mechanism’s function in the context of spaceflight.

Mechanistic studies

Identifying the underlying mechanisms regulating MHC class I levels will also prove vital to developing treatments for astronaut health. Systematic comparisons between the signaling pathways implicated in MHC class I downregulation in astronauts and those known from cancer and viral infection research would allow for the identification of shared pathways, common regulators, and spaceflight-specific factors. Cell culture and animal models could then be leveraged to validate the involvement of identified pathways. Similar treatment strategies to cancer and viral disease research could be explored as countermeasures for the negative effects of spaceflight. Leveraging advanced technologies like single-cell spatial proteomics, such as the molecular pixelation technique175, can provide unprecedented insights into MHC class I abundance, polarization, and co-localization with other immune markers at a single-cell resolution. Therefore, offering a deeper understanding of the spatial organization and functional consequences of MHC downregulation within tissues.

Biomarker monitoring

Mechanistic studies such as the ones described above will also pinpoint potential biomarkers indicative of pathway dysregulation. Biomarker discovery will enable early detection, as monitoring biomarkers before and during spaceflight will identify individuals at a higher risk of MHC class I suppression. These kinds of measurements will also lead to personalized countermeasures; tailoring interventions based on an astronaut’s individual pathway profile will maximize drug effectiveness and minimize potential side effects.

Although the previously described astronaut transcriptomics studies report that MHC class I downregulation persisted for over 3 to 6 months12, these studies were also unclear about how long the depression persisted and whether, and how, it recovered later. These findings suggest the need for the longitudinal monitoring of the MHC class I expression in astronauts for at least 6 months. Longitudinal monitoring should be integrated with clinical symptom monitoring to understand the broader impact on astronauts over the same amount of time.

Future astronaut and analog mission campaigns will offer valuable opportunities to expand upon these initial findings, validate their generalizability across varying mission profiles and spaceflight environments, and investigate potential countermeasures based on personalized biomarker discovery. Axiom, with its focus on private astronaut missions to the ISS and beyond, can provide data on MHC class I expression in a wider population of astronauts, potentially revealing individual susceptibility factors. NASA, JAXA, ESA, and other agencies have dedicated teams investigating the impact of long-duration spaceflight on various physiological systems, including the immune system, and they can incorporate MHC class I monitoring to contribute valuable longitudinal data and insights into potential countermeasures. While currently focused on suborbital flights, Blue Origin’s New Shepard missions can help determine if even short-duration exposure to spaceflight triggers MHC class I downregulation, potentially revealing the initial stages of this phenomenon. Finally, NASA’s Complement of Integrated Protocols for Human Exploration Research (CIPHER) program, a comprehensive approach spanning increasingly longer mission durations and involving as many as 30 astronauts, presents an opportunity to investigate how multiple systems in the body respond to spaceflight, including MHC class I profiles. By strategically integrating MHC class I monitoring and research into these diverse missions, humanity can gain a comprehensive understanding of this critical aspect of spaceflight-induced immune dysregulation and develop effective strategies to protect astronauts’ health.

Therapeutic intervention

Earlier, we described some of the more generalized countermeasures deployed to ISS that seem to benefit the immune system, including improved aerobic and resistive exercise, specific nutritional supplementation, and more frequent resupply5. NASA is testing a multifaceted immune countermeasure using Antarctica’s winter over as a suitable ground analog10. These, however, are all clearly interdisciplinary or multisystem countermeasures. All mission-associated stressors will increase during deep space missions, and our ability to implement countermeasures will be diminished. Currently, there is a lack of specific and targeted countermeasures that can rectify a specific cellular defect responsible for diminished immunocyte function during spaceflight.

We suggest that possible specific therapeutic approaches include agents that would directly target identified pathways to increase MHC class I gene expression, protein synthesis, and/or localization at the cell surface. Epigenetic modifiers, cytokine therapy, and small molecule activators that reverse silencing of MHC class I140, directly induce MHC class I expression176, or specifically activate transcription factors, fall under this category. To counteract the long-term downregulation of MHC class I observed in astronauts, a comprehensive drug-target library can be screened to identify potential therapeutic agents. This approach would involve assessing the library for compounds that can specifically target the pathways responsible for MHC class I downregulation, such as those involving inflammatory cytokines or cellular stress responses, ultimately aiming to restore MHC I expression to normal levels and maintain function during prolonged spaceflight.

Cytokine-based therapies are among the primary approaches aimed at increasing MHC class I expression. In particular, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) strongly stimulates the transcription of MHC class I genes, increasing the expression of HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C177. Long-term spaceflight studies conducted by Crucian et al. have shown a significant decrease in IFN-γ levels in astronauts; this suggests that interventions targeting interferon may be clinically meaningful178. However, it is noted that long-term suppression of MHC class I genes in space could be associated with epigenetic mechanisms. Epigenetic modulators such as HDAC inhibitors or DNA methyltransferase inhibitors have been shown to reactivate the expression of MHC class I genes179. In this context, epigenetic therapies can be considered among the potential agents that could reverse MHC class I silencing.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which have proven highly effective in delivering mRNA vaccines, can also be adapted to MHC class I mRNA delivery, enabling cellular production of these proteins while bypassing transcriptional or translational blocks. LNPs could also be functionalized with specific ligands to target them to immune cells, increasing delivery efficiency and minimizing off-target effects. Recent studies have also shown that mRNA-based therapies delivered by lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) can be used to directly increase MHC class I levels. The clinical efficacy of mRNA vaccines has demonstrated that LNP platforms have high potential for efficient delivery of targeted mRNA to immune cells180.

MHC I expression restoration has previously been explored as a potential treatment method for cancers, and treatment with interferons has been identified as the most promising candidate for this use case59. Studies have shown an increase in MHC I levels through treatment with interferon-gamma181, which is also known to increase the quantity and quality of peptides for MHC class I loading182. Considering these therapeutic approaches together, it is clear that strategies aimed at regulating MHC class I suppression should be addressed within a multidimensional framework encompassing epigenetic, transcriptional, environmental, and mechanical levels, rather than focusing on a single biological target. This multidimensional approach is critical both for the safety of future space missions and for mechanistically understanding the long-term immune abnormalities observed in astronauts.

Precision medicine considerations in MHC I modulation

Precision medicine (PM) in space is defined as the science and practice of providing methods of treatment and prevention tailored to an individual’s molecular, physiological, morphological, and behavioral characteristics, including specific attention to the spaceflight environment183. The field of PM applied to the modulation of MHC I and the associated phenotype is currently a nascent field, though it shares similar research and clinical questions with other applications of PM in space. In general, it requires specific attention to how food, micronutrients, macronutrients, environmental exposures, lifestyle, behavioral dynamics, and drug therapeutics might be examined to address the MHC I phenomenon in space.

Precision medicine applied to MHC I dynamics in space can be illustrated by exploring a brief introduction to one class of dietary molecules—lipids. For instance, palmitic acid (PA; 16:0) and oleic acid (OA; 18:1n9) have been studied in vitro for their impact on MHC I expression. In a concentration-dependent manner, PA inhibited MHC I surface expression and antigen presentation, whereas OA did not. Of note, pretreatment with OA had a protective effect against the PA impact on MHC I. This preliminary work suggests dietary composition and cell membrane lipid composition may be important in regulating MHC I184. Ketones have also been explored for their impact on MHC I, via their role as known histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDAC). Specifically, both ketogenic diets and exogenous ketones (as beta-hydroxybutyrate; BHB) have demonstrated upregulation of MHC class I molecules in Pca cells and animals, rendering them more visible to the immune system185. Long-chain fatty acids have also been studied for their impact on MHC I, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; 20:5n3) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n3). Both DHA and EPA treatment have been shown to significantly increase the surface levels of MHC class I molecules and antibody binding in EL4 cells (though not in primary activated B cells)186.

When four phosphatidylcholine (PC) forms were compared (stearic acid in the sn-1 position and either oleic, alpha-linolenic, arachidonic, or DHA in the sn-2 position), the greatest binding of monoclonal antibody AF6-88.5 (which detects a conformationally sensitive epitope in the extracellular region of the MHC I K-chain) was achieved with PC-DHA. DHA, in particular, may act by promoting changes in lipid rafts and possibly form DHA-rich nanoscale domains to alter MHC I protein lateral organization and conformation187. Since the effect of EPA and DHA on cell membrane composition and membrane architecture is thought to be one primary means of their impact on MHC I, assessment of RBC fatty acid status in individual astronauts for research and clinical care may become of central value in the PM approach (noting that RBC omega-3 measures are useful in predicting lymphocyte omega-3 levels)188.

Beyond dietary and cell membrane composition, understanding astronaut genotypes will also become important. For example, fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1) governs the desaturation of polyunsaturated fatty acids to their more highly unsaturated metabolites (e.g., conversion of alpha-linolenic acid to EPA and DHA by sequential addition of a double bond)189. Those possessing variants in the FADS1 gene (particularly rs174537) and the attendant change in the FADS1 enzyme activity have been consistently associated with lower serum/plasma EPA and DHA. Thus, should DHA be shown to be a consistent regulator of MHC I, understanding both DHA status and genetic limitations to endogenous DHA synthesis in astronauts will become essential clinical knowledge.

Adding further to the complexity and to the potential value of PM, one can consider the converging variables of the APOE genotype and fatty acid metabolism, just noted. For example, Tomaszewski et al. studied APOE genotype status in adults ingesting 2 g DHA daily for 18 months. Non-APOE4 carriers experienced a greater increase in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid DHA/AA. APOE ε2/ε3 carriers ingesting DHA also exhibited a 10% less decline in left hippocampal volume than APOE ε4/ε4 carriers190. Meanwhile, APOE ε2/ε3 carriers and APOE ε2/ε4 carriers experienced significantly less decline in right hippocampal volume than APOE ε4/ε4 carriers.

The convergence between the APOE4 genotype and MHC I activation in the CNS (part of a causal chain driving tau pathology and neuronal loss) also provides a note of caution191. As we might seek to upregulate MHC I from a suppressed state in a general approach, careful attention to overactivation may be warranted in specific populations, such as people with elevated genetic risk. Moreover, the lipids noted above possess a similar capacity to raise or lower MHC I, which appears concentration-dependent. While the above examples are only limited, they are intended to illustrate the importance of deep molecular phenotyping as we seek to understand the influences on MHC I, our growing attention to precision medicine, and the value of personalizing care to individual astronauts192,193.

Limitations

The examination of long-term MHC I downregulation in astronauts is limited by gaps in understanding of the mechanism of action, sparse longitudinal data, and small sample sizes inherent to human spaceflight studies. Although multiple missions have reported consistent suppression of MHC class I genes, the duration of this depression, its variability across individuals, and its direct clinical impact remain to be fully elucidated. Existing findings are largely correlational, with no definitive evidence linking MHC class I suppression to specific post-flight symptoms, viral reaction events, or cancer risk. Moreover, parallels drawn from cancer and virology research provide useful hypotheses but may not fully represent spaceflight-specific mechanisms such as microgravity, radiation mixtures, or stress-related endocrine shifts. Current countermeasure strategies are also theoretical, with no targeted interventions yet validated in astronaut populations or analog environments. Together, these limitations underscore the need for mechanistic studies, controlled model-system experiments, and systematic longitudinal monitoring to establish causality and develop effective, personalized countermeasures.

Conclusions

This Perspective highlights the concerning phenomenon of long-term MHC class I downregulation observed in astronauts following spaceflight. Mirroring immune evasion strategies employed by viruses and cancer cells, this downregulation potentially poses significant risks to astronaut health, including increased susceptibility to infections, viral reactivation, and even possibly carcinogenesis. While previous research has explored aspects of immune dysfunction in space, the persistent suppression of MHC class I genes across multiple missions and biospecimens necessitates a focused investigation into their long-term implications.

Priorities of future research should include longitudinal monitoring of MHC class I expression in astronauts, coupled with detailed clinical assessments to identify associated health risks. Delineating the underlying mechanisms of this downregulation, along with comparative analyses of pathways involved in cancer and viral immune evasion, is crucial. This understanding could pave the way for the development of targeted therapeutic interventions. Exploring strategies like MHC class I boosters, lipid nanoparticle delivery systems, or tailored vaccine approaches to mitigate these risks and enhance astronaut health during longer missions is also crucial.

The development of comprehensive monitoring systems, particularly for the burgeoning field of commercial spaceflight, is also essential. Addressing the challenge of MHC class I suppression is not only vital for ensuring astronaut well-being and mission success but also has broader implications for understanding and managing related health challenges on Earth, including in elderly or immunocompromised populations. Further research in this area will be critical in the new space age as humanity ventures farther into space and pushes the boundaries of human exploration.
