Stability analysis and numerical investigation of fractional SIR model for childhood disease transmission with vaccination
Authors: Jignesh P Chauhan, Samaruddin Jebran, Sagar R Khirsariya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents a fractional-order SIR-type framework to investigate the transmission dynamics of childhood infectious diseases under the influence of vaccination. The classical epidemic model is generalized through the Caputo fractional derivative to incorporate memory-dependent effects commonly observed in biological systems. The population is partitioned into susceptible, infected, and recovered compartments, and the impact of key epidemiological parameters such as vaccination rate, transmission coefficient, and recovery rate is examined. The well-posedness of the proposed model is established by proving the existence and uniqueness of solutions using fixed-point arguments in an appropriate Banach space. The local stability of both the disease-free and endemic equilibrium states is analyzed via the Jacobian matrix and the basic reproduction number. For numerical approximation, a fractional Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor-corrector scheme is employed, and its performance is assessed. Numerical simulations are carried out for scenarios with and without vaccination to highlight the role of immunization in controlling disease spread. The findings demonstrate that increased vaccination coverage significantly lowers the basic reproduction number and enhances the stability of the disease-free state. Furthermore, comparative analysis indicates that the proposed numerical method yields stable and accurate solutions relative to existing approaches such as the fractional Euler and implicit schemes. The study emphasizes the importance of incorporating memory effects in epidemic modeling and provides insights relevant to effective disease control strategies.Keywords: Fractional epidemic model, Caputo derivative, Stability analysis, Vaccination dynamics, Adams–Bashforth–Moulton methodSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Childhood infectious diseases continue to represent a persistent challenge for global public health systems. Due to frequent interactions in schools, playgrounds, and social environments, children are particularly vulnerable to rapid disease transmission. Epidemic outbreaks such as measles, influenza, mumps, and chickenpox demonstrate how quickly infections can spread when preventive strategies are insufficient. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms governing disease transmission is essential for designing effective control measures and improving vaccination policies.

Protecting children from infectious diseases remains a primary objective of healthcare systems, and vaccination plays a central role in achieving this goal. The development of vaccines has been one of the most significant milestones in medical science1. Although vaccination is widely recognized as an effective preventive measure, determining the optimal vaccination coverage required to suppress or eliminate outbreaks remains an important research problem2. From an epidemiological perspective, vaccination plays a central role in reducing disease burden and limiting outbreak size. Mathematical models incorporating vaccination strategies are extensively used to evaluate immunization programs and estimate herd immunity thresholds3,4. These models also provide insights into long-term disease dynamics and help identify critical conditions for disease eradication.

Mathematical models provide a structured approach to understanding disease transmission and evaluating intervention strategies. Compartmental models divide the population into distinct groups such as susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals, enabling systematic analysis of disease spread. Classical epidemic models based on ordinary differential equations have been widely applied to examine transmission patterns and control mechanisms5,6. Among these, the SIR model remains one of the most commonly used frameworks for analyzing epidemic behavior7–10.

Despite their effectiveness, classical integer-order models assume that system evolution depends solely on the present state. However, many biological processes exhibit memory and hereditary characteristics. Factors such as immune response delays, incubation periods, and accumulated exposure history significantly influence disease progression. These effects cannot be adequately captured by traditional models. Fractional calculus offers a powerful alternative by introducing derivatives of non-integer order, allowing the incorporation of memory-dependent behavior11,12.

In recent years, fractional-order epidemic models have attracted increasing attention due to their ability to provide a more realistic description of disease dynamics. By accounting for historical effects, these models improve predictive accuracy and better represent real-world phenomena13,14. Applications of fractional models span a wide range of infectious diseases, including dengue, COVID-19, tuberculosis, and influenza. Recent fractional-order models have also been applied to various biological and epidemiological systems, including chlamydia transmission15, dental infection models16, bacterial disease propagation17, and rat bite fever dynamics18. These studies demonstrate the flexibility of fractional frameworks in capturing complex system behavior.

Classical models have limitations in capturing memory-dependent processes in disease dynamics. Several recent contributions have extended classical epidemic models using fractional derivatives. For example, a fractional SIR model with treatment effects was analyzed in19, demonstrating improved stability characteristics. Rit et al.20 investigated nonlinear fractional models and reported complex dynamical behaviors such as bifurcation phenomena. In the context of COVID-19, Kang and Li21 showed that fractional models provide better agreement with observed data. Additional studies22–25 further highlight the importance of memory effects and the flexibility of fractional frameworks in capturing epidemic dynamics. Recent studies have also explored computational efficiency, control strategies, and data-driven approaches in epidemic modeling. For instance, optimal control strategies for pandemic models have been investigated in26, while deep learning approaches for fractional epidemic systems are discussed in27. Further developments on complex disease dynamics and intervention strategies can be found in28,29. In addition, numerical investigations of epidemic models with vaccination and multi-dose strategies have been reported in30,31, highlighting the importance of accurate numerical schemes in understanding disease spread.

Various analytical and numerical techniques have been developed for solving fractional differential equations. These include the quadratic finite element method32, Adomian decomposition approaches33, homotopy perturbation methods34, spectral techniques35, homotopy analysis methods36, and spectral collocation schemes37. These methods provide efficient tools for analyzing complex fractional systems. However, many of these studies do not simultaneously address vaccination effects, stability analysis, and high-accuracy numerical schemes within a unified fractional framework. Furthermore, fractional modeling approaches have been extended to other applied problems such as infertility treatment dynamics38, which further supports the applicability of fractional calculus in real-world systems.

Motivated by these developments, this study introduces a fractional-order SIR model for childhood disease transmission incorporating vaccination effects. The model captures interactions among susceptible, infected, and recovered populations while accounting for memory effects through the Caputo fractional derivative. In addition to theoretical analysis, a numerical scheme based on the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor-corrector method is employed39,40. This method is well known for its stability and efficiency and has been successfully applied to various real-world problems, including dengue transmission41, vehicle simulations42, cancer treatment models43, glucose-insulin systems44, HIV-1 infection dynamics45, and fluid-structure interaction problems46. The work focuses on stability analysis, numerical approximation using the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton method, and the influence of key epidemiological parameters. The aim is to provide a clearer understanding of disease dynamics under memory-dependent effects and support improved control strategies.

This study differs from existing fractional SIR models in several aspects. The model incorporates vaccination directly into the fractional framework, allowing the analysis of immunization effects together with memory-dependent dynamics. In addition, the work combines stability analysis with a numerical scheme based on the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton method, providing both theoretical and computational insights. This combination offers a more complete understanding of system behavior compared to approaches that focus on only one aspect.

Model formulation

In this section, we introduce the mathematical model used to describe the transmission dynamics of childhood infectious diseases. In this work, a compartmental epidemic model is proposed to study how infectious diseases spread among children. The total population is divided into three distinct groups: susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals. The susceptible group includes children who are at risk of infection but are not yet infected. The infected group consists of individuals who are currently carrying the disease and can transmit it to others. The recovered group includes individuals who have developed immunity either after recovery or through vaccination.

To better represent memory effects and hereditary characteristics present in epidemiological systems, the classical integer-order model is extended using fractional calculus. Fractional derivatives provide a more realistic description because the rate of change depends not only on the current state but also on the past behavior of the system.

To extend the model, the Caputo fractional derivative of order \varepsilon is used. Applying this operator leads to the following fractional-order model:

Here, denotes the Caputo fractional derivative of order \varepsilon. When \varepsilon = 1, the model becomes the classical integer-order system. For 0< \varepsilon < 1, the model incorporates memory effects, meaning that past states influence the current dynamics of the disease.

From a biological point of view, the parameter \varepsilon reflects the strength of memory in the system. Smaller values of \varepsilon indicate stronger memory effects, implying that past infection history has a greater impact on present dynamics. This allows the model to capture delays, incubation periods, and long-term immunity more effectively than classical models.

Thus, the fractional-order childhood disease model provides a more general framework that can describe both present and past influences in the spread of infectious diseases among children.

When \varepsilon = 1, the model reduces to the classical integer-order system, where the evolution depends only on the current state of the system. In contrast, for 0< \varepsilon < 1, the fractional model incorporates memory effects, allowing past states to influence present dynamics. This results in a slower evolution of the system and provides a more realistic representation of processes such as delayed response and accumulated exposure in disease transmission.

Fixed-point framework

The fixed-point approach is used to establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions. To establish existence results, we utilize the Banach contraction principle. Consider an operator X: B → B defined on a closed and convex subset of B. If X is contractive and continuous, then it admits a unique fixed point in that set. For the application of the fixed-point theorem, it is assumed that the nonlinear function satisfies continuity and Lipschitz conditions with respect to the state variables. These conditions ensure that the associated operator is well-defined and suitable for establishing existence and uniqueness of solutions.

Results and discussion

The model incorporates the Caputo fractional derivative to capture memory effects in the transmission dynamics. Numerical simulations are conducted for two epidemiological situations: the vaccinated population scenario (Case I) and the non-vaccinated population scenario (Case II). The results demonstrate how vaccination coverage, infection transmission rate, recovery rate, and fractional order influence the evolution of susceptible, infected, and recovered populations. These observations are consistent with findings reported in recent epidemic modeling studies26,28.

Conclusion

In this section, we summarize the main findings of the study. This study presents a fractional-order childhood disease model incorporating vaccination effects and solves it numerically using the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton predictor-corrector technique. The model is formulated using the Caputo fractional derivative to account for memory effects that influence disease transmission dynamics.

The stability analysis demonstrates that the disease-free equilibrium remains locally asymptotically stable when the basic reproduction number is less than unity, whereas the endemic equilibrium emerges when exceeds unity. Numerical simulations further confirm that vaccination significantly reduces infection levels and accelerates recovery within the population.

Graphical analysis shows that the susceptible population decreases over time while the recovered population increases steadily in the vaccinated system, as illustrated in Figs. 6 and 8. In contrast, the non-vaccinated system experiences a higher infection peak before approaching equilibrium, as observed in Fig. 10. Parameter sensitivity analysis also reveals that the transmission rate β plays a dominant role in disease spread (Fig. 12), whereas an increased recovery rate η promotes faster recovery and epidemic control (Fig. 13).

The numerical comparison indicates that the Adams-Bashforth-Moulton scheme provides stable and accurate solutions compared with the fractional Euler and implicit methods, as demonstrated in Table 2 and Fig. 14, 15, 16. The convergence study confirms that the proposed numerical technique approaches second-order accuracy as the step size decreases (Table 3, Fig. 17).

Overall, the fractional framework enhances the modeling capability of epidemiological systems by incorporating memory effects, improving the realism of disease dynamics. The results demonstrate that vaccination strategies and effective recovery mechanisms are essential for controlling childhood infectious diseases.

Future scope

Several directions may be considered to extend the present study. One possible extension is the incorporation of additional epidemiological compartments, such as exposed or quarantined individuals, which would provide a more detailed representation of disease progression. Another improvement involves integrating time-dependent vaccination rates or treatment strategies to capture dynamic public health interventions.

Future research may also consider stochastic fractional models to investigate the influence of random environmental variations on disease transmission. Furthermore, the integration of optimal control strategies within the fractional modeling framework could assist policymakers in designing efficient vaccination and treatment policies.

Finally, the proposed fractional modeling approach can be applied to other infectious diseases such as dengue, influenza, or COVID-19 to evaluate the effectiveness of intervention strategies under memory-dependent dynamics. Future work may also explore the integration of advanced computational techniques such as deep learning and optimal control methods, as discussed in26,27, to further enhance predictive accuracy and decision-making capabilities.
