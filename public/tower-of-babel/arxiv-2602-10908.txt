SoftMatcha 2: A Fast and Soft Pattern Matcher for Trillion-Scale Corpora
Masataka Yoneda, Yusuke Matsushita, Go Kamoda, Kohei Suenaga, [.2em] Takuya Akiba, Masaki Waga, Sho Yokoi, [.5em] University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan National Institute of Informatics, Tokyo, Japan Kyoto University, Kyoto, Japan

Abstract
We present an ultra-fast and flexible search algorithm that enables search over trillion-scale natural language corpora in under 0.3 seconds while handling semantic variations (substitution, insertion, and deletion). Our approach employs string matching based on suffix arrays that scales well with corpus size. To mitigate the combinatorial explosion induced by the semantic relaxation of queries, our method is built on two key algorithmic ideas: fast exact lookup enabled by a disk-aware design, and dynamic corpus-aware pruning. We theoretically show that the proposed method suppresses exponential growth in the search space with respect to query length by leveraging statistical properties of natural language. In experiments on FineWeb-Edu Lozhkov et al. 2024 (1.4T tokens), we show that our method achieves significantly lower search latency than existing methods: infini-gram Liu et al. 2024 , infini-gram mini Xu et al. 2025 , and SoftMatcha Deguchi et al. 2025 . As a practical application, we demonstrate that our method identifies benchmark contamination in training corpora, unidentified by existing approaches. We also provide an online demo of fast, soft search across corpora in seven languages. 1 1 1 Project Page & Web Interface : https://softmatcha.github.io/v2/ ​, 2 2 2 Source Code : https://github.com/softmatcha/softmatcha2

Corpus Search for NLP and Language Models

In corpus linguistics and quantitative linguistics, contextually grounded examples of words or phrases, known as KWIC

(
Luhn 1960

(

;
Berlin-Brandenburgische Akademie der
Wissenschaften 2026

In traditional natural language processing, corpus searching serves as a general subroutine for extracting world knowledge from text corpora (information extraction) or as a preprocessing step for analyzing product reviews (sentiment analysis).
In addition, language models are fundamentally built from large-scale corpora, making the study of language models inseparable from corpus research
(
Dodge et al. 2021
)
.
For instance, crucial studies that identify harmful biases and inappropriate expressions in training corpora, or detect benchmark contamination, are grounded in corpus search
Guo et al. 2022
;
Ippolito et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2024
.
Moreover, with the discovery of scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
and the exponential growth of training corpora

(
Gokaslan et al. 2019
;
Raffel et al. 2019
;
Penedo et al. 2024b
)
, corpora have reached sizes that are no longer manually verifiable, thereby increasing the necessity for efficient corpus searching methods in recent years
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
;
Deguchi et al. 2025
)
.

2.2
String Matching Algorithms

String matching
, or matching a pattern string against a target string, has been a fundamental research topic in computer science with a long history; see, e.g.,
(
Adjeroh et al. 2008
)
for the details.
Traditionally, string matching has been designed to match strings at the letter level; however, most of the string-matching algorithms can be naturally applied to token-level search rather than letter-level search.

A popular approach for scalable string matching is to build an index of the target text in advance and use it at runtime.

Suffix arrays

(
Manber and Myers 1990
)
and
inverted indexing

(
Zobel and Moffat 2006
)
are representative indexing schemes.
Using a suffix array, one can locate the occurrences of the query text in
O(\log\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert)
time, where
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
is the corpus size.
In contrast, a search with an inverted index takes linear time w.r.t. the frequency of the rarest letter in the target text—prohibitively costly for a massive corpus.

String matching is generalized for
approximate
matching (i.e., matching a pattern to a target text modulo substitution, insertions, and deletions of letters), where the distance from the query text is measured by an edit distance
(
Wagner and Fischer 1974
)
.
In addition to relaxation based on the
semantic
distance, our method supports matching with word insertions and deletions, inspired by this line of work.

Text Search for Massive Corpora

(

)

(

Our work follows the research line, significantly improving exact-match search speed (

SoftMatcha

(

Note that, due to the characteristics of the inverted index

Our research uses suffix arrays, which are robust to increases in corpus size, and employs powerful pruning algorithms (

We examine the differences between our work and these three prior studies in experimental section.

It is also worth mentioning methods that share the objective of “retrieving documents from queries” but differ somewhat in their goals and primary purposes.
In corpus linguistics and classical natural language processing, regular expressions have been used as versatile and powerful pattern matchers

Manning and

Dense vector search
(
Karpukhin et al. 2020
)
is a more modern approach for finding “related” documents to a query.
Technically, it uses text vectors
(

)
and approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search
Indyk and
Motwani 1998
;
Andoni and

3
SoftMatcha 2: Our Soft and Fast Corpus Search Algorithm

Our direct predecessor SoftMatcha
Deguchi et al. 2025
, which realizes soft search on billion-scale corpora, uses a “soft” inverted index and enumerated
positions in the corpus
directly.
However, as the inverted index runs in linear time in the corpus size, it is unsuitable for trillion-scale corpora. Therefore, we take a new approach, inverting the perspective: our tool enumerates
patterns
(token sequences) similar to the query and checks whether each occurs in the corpus via exact lookup in a
suffix array
.

There are two challenges to this approach: (i) lookup in a naive suffix array is slow due to many random disk accesses; and (ii) with naive search, the number of candidates increases exponentially with the query length. We solve (i) by speeding up exact lookups using a new
disk-aware suffix array

(
Section

3.2
).
Moreover, we address (ii) pruning the search space by dynamically exploiting the power-law distribution of
n
-grams in the corpus (
Section

3.3
).
The combination of the two gives a blazingly fast soft search for large corpora (see
Section

5
for the experimental results).

3.1
Problem Setting

Our basic problem setting is as follows.
The input is a
query

\bar{q}=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}
, a sequence of tokens, and a
similarity threshold

\alpha
.
The output is a set of patterns
\bar{p}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{n}
that are semantically similar to the query
\bar{q}
with similarity
\alpha
or more.
The similarity between patterns is calculated based on word (or token) embeddings.
At a high level, this problem setting inherits that of SoftMatcha
Deguchi et al. 2025
, but we introduce the following new twists.

Adaptive Similarity Thresholding.

Practically, it is often hard to find a similarity threshold
\alpha
that is not too tight or loose.
To mitigate that, our tool sets the similarity threshold adaptively as follows.
First, we set the number
K
of the matching patterns we want.
Our tool first enumerates the soft matches (i.e., solves the above problem) with some pre-determined initial
\alpha
.
If fewer than
K
soft matches are found, our tool relaxes the threshold
\alpha
(i.e., decreases its value) by some amount and enumerates the soft matches again.
The tool repeats this until more than
K
soft matches are obtained or the threshold
\alpha
becomes too low.

Definition of Similarity.

We propose a new definition of pattern similarity
\mathop{\mathsf{sim}}(\bar{q},\bar{p})
to improve search quality. First, the basic similarity (without deletion or insertion) between a query
\bar{q}=q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}
and a pattern
p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}
is defined as follows, letting
c_{i}
(
\leq 1
) be the cosine similarity between the word embeddings of
q_{i}
and
p_{i}
:

1-\log_{\beta}\left(\sum_{i}(\beta^{1-c_{i}}-1)+1\right).

This is a
smooth
minimum of
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m}
, with a parameter
\beta
, which is set to
\beta=10^{4}
in our experiments.
This improves over the plain minimum
\min(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
(the limit
\beta\to\infty
) used by SoftMatcha, which problematically ignores the similarities among all words except the one with the lowest score.
3
3

3

For example, the similarity between “gold medal” and “bronze medals” is approximately 0.62, if the cosine similarity of “gold” and “bronze” is 0.63 and that of “medal” and “medals” is 0.85.

We extend the similarity above to allow insertions and deletions by scaling the similarity by some factor less than
1
for each insertion or deletion.
To keep the penalty for low-information words like “the” and “of” small, we use the word vector norm after Zipfian whitening
Yokoi et al. 2024
, which correlates strongly with information entropy
Oyama et al. 2023
.
Letting
v
be the squared norm of the inserted or deleted word, the scaling factor is
\exp\,(-v/\gamma)
.
Here,
m
is the length of the query.
In our experiments, we set
\gamma=m\gamma^{\prime}
for
\gamma^{\prime}
such that the scaling factor equals
1/e
when
m=5
and the word with the 50th-lowest norm is inserted (e.g.,
\gamma^{\prime}\approx 21.70
for FineWeb-Edu).
4
4

4

Precisely speaking, we take the maximum similarity over the multiple ways to perform insertions and deletions.

3.2
Fast Disk-Aware Suffix Array

Our soft search method is built on top of exact corpus lookup, and its efficiency is essential for scaling our method to a trillion-scale corpus.
A naive corpus lookup implementation based on a suffix array faces a critical bottleneck in index access: the index for a trillion-scale corpus is enormous (
>$5\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}$
), which prevents storing it entirely in RAM and requires storing it on disk (e.g., SSD).
Since disk access latency is significantly slower (
\approx\times 10^{3}
) than RAM, reducing the number of disk accesses is vital.

To achieve this key goal, we newly design a
disk-aware, staged suffix array
.
See
Appendix

H
for the details.
Our approach uses a two-staged indexing scheme, similar to Google’s BigTable
Chang et al. 2008
: (1) identifying the range of potential matches in the first stage and (2) determining the precise match in the second stage, focusing on the identified area.
This scheme requires only
one
disk access per exact match query, and the search time is about 33 times faster than infini-gram
Liu et al. 2024
, a well-known corpus search engine based on suffix arrays.
Moreover, we reduce the index size via
run-length compression
; in our experience, this compression reduced the overall index size from
56.0\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}
to
21.6\text{\,}\mathrm{TB}
for the FineWeb-Edu corpus.

3.3
Dynamic Corpus-Aware Pruning of Search Space

To reduce the exponential growth of the search space in soft pattern matching,
we employ
iterative pruning
, which drastically reduces the number of
candidate patterns by exploiting the power-law distribution of
n
-grams
in the corpus.
Below, we describe our algorithm (shown in
Algorithm

1
), through an example of searching for “olympics gold medal” with a given initial value of
\alpha
(see
Figure

2

Step 1.
Enumerate all the patterns
L^{\prime}_{1}
whose similarity to the first query token, “olympics”, is at least
\alpha
. This step works only using the dictionary, without referencing the corpus. Then, filter
L^{\prime}_{1}
into the patterns
L_{1}
that occur in the corpus, using exact lookup by the fast suffix array (
Section

3.2
).

Step 2.
For each
w\in L_{1}
, enumerate all the patterns
w\cdot w^{\prime}
that have
w
as a prefix and whose similarity to “olympics gold” is at least
\alpha
. We denote this set by
L^{\prime}_{2}
. As before, filter
L^{\prime}_{2}
into those that appear in the corpus
L_{2}
.

Step 3.
Compute
L^{\prime}_{3}
and
L_{3}
analogously using the similarity to “olympics gold medal”. The final result is
L_{3}
.

To further improve the performance, we also introduce the following two techniques.

Appendix

I
.
(i)
k
-gram pruning
: For the 2-grams and 3-grams consisting only of high-frequency words, we check if they appear in the corpus beforehand and keep this information in RAM; at runtime, we determine if they appear without any extra disk access.
(ii)
Last-bits pruning

Theoretical Analysis

Why can our algorithm (
Section

3
) work so effectively, as observed in
Section

5
?
How robustly is it efficient, across a number of parameters (language, corpus
\mathcal{C}
, threshold
\alpha
, query
\bar{q}
, constant
\gamma
, etc.)?
To answer this, we here analyze our algorithm theoretically. See
Appendix

J
for details.

To begin with, let
S_{i}
be
\{w\in\mathcal{V}^{*}\mid\mathop{\mathsf{sim}}(q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{i},w)\geq\alpha\}
, the set of patterns that are similar to the
i
-token prefix of the query
\bar{q}
.
Note
L_{i}=\{w\in S_{i}\mid w\ \text{occurs in}\ \mathcal{C}\}
.
Also, let
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}
be the set of
i
-grams in the corpus.
For analysis, we assume the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

(Corpus occurrence ratio of similar words)
.

The corpus occurrence ratio
\lvert L_{i}\rvert/\lvert S_{i}\rvert
over query-similar patterns on average is approximately order of
\lvert\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}\rvert/\lvert\mathcal{V}^{i}\rvert
.

Note that the ratio is very small for large
i
, since
\lvert\mathcal{C}^{*}_{i}\rvert\leq\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
.
We believe that this hypothesis is valid from a linguistic perspective.
The occurrence probability of
n
-grams depends on grammatical correctness and collocations.
The set
S_{i}
is roughly the direct product of the sets of words similar enough to each word in the query, which should show an occurrence tendency similar to the direct product
\mathcal{V}^{i}
.
Assuming the hypothesis, we enjoy the following.

Theorem (Informal) 1

(No exponential explosion over
m
)
.

The total number of exact lookups in
Algorithm

1
on average is approximately bounded by a constant over the query length
m
, for fixed
\alpha
and moderate
\gamma
.
5
5

5

Beware that this analysis is for fixed
\alpha
and
\gamma
.
Practically,
\alpha
is adaptively relaxed so that
\lvert L_{m}\rvert\approx K
holds.
Also,
\gamma
is made proportional to
K
in our experiments.
This explains the mild exponentiality we experimentally observed (
Figure

5
,
Section

5.3
).

Assuming Zipf’s law of the
n
-gram distribution, we can further analyze the effect of the corpus size
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
.

Theorem (Informal) 2

(Sub-linearity over the corpus size
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
)
.

Assume that, for each
n
, the occurrence probability of the
k
-th most frequent
n
-gram is
O\left\lparen k^{-\delta}\right\rparen
for
\delta>1
.
Then the total number of exact lookups in
Algorithm

1
on average is approximately
O\left\lparen\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert^{1/\delta}\right\rparen
with respect to
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
.
6
6

6

Again, beware that this analysis is for fixed
\alpha
.
For larger
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
,
\alpha
tends to be adaptively made smaller, as
\lvert L_{m}\rvert
tends to be larger.
This explains the scalability we observed (
Figure

4
,
Section

5.3
).

Empirically,
\delta
is typically
1.5
or so for many languages
Bellina and Servedio 2025
;
Silva and Cunha 2020
.

6
Application: Contamination Detection

Evaluating LLMs intrinsically is extremely difficult due to their complexity; therefore, extrinsic evaluation using a benchmark dataset of hard problems is essential for the research and development of LLMs. Therefore, contamination of benchmark (i.e., test set) data by information from the pre-training corpus (i.e., training set) is harmful for the research and development of LLMs.

As a practical case study, this section demonstrates the effectiveness of our method in benchmark contamination detection.
Although infini-gram mini
Xu et al. 2025
has also been applied for the same purpose and identified contaminations in 24 popular benchmarks, it solely relies on exact matching. By applying soft search, we can detect two critical forms of leakage that are overlooked by exact matching: (1)
Semantic contamination
, in which a token sequence that is semantically similar to a benchmark task is found in a pre-training corpus; and (2)
Template leakage
, in which a token sequence that is obtained by substituting different numerical expression to a benchmark task is found in a pre-training corpus (e.g., “Alice has 8 pens” and “Alice has 5 pens”).

Contamination Detection Method.

Inspired by the experiments in infini-gram mini
Xu et al. 2025
, we extract all substrings of a stride of 10-tokens (instead of 50 characters in infini-gram mini), compute

\eta=\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert}\sum_{s\in S}\mathrm{softmatch}(s),

where
\mathrm{softmatch}(s)
is 1 if a substring with similarity
\geq 0.6
exists in the corpus, and 0 otherwise.
If
\eta\geq 0.8
, then a benchmark task is marked
dirty
.

Benchmarks and Corpus.
We analyze 7 benchmarks that are also used in the experiments of infini-gram
Xu et al. 2025
. The benchmark consists of problems taken from various categories: (1) knowledge and reasoning, (2) math, (3) coding, and (4) common-sense understanding. The full list of benchmarks is in
Appendix

K
.
For large test sets, we randomly sampled 400 entries. For benchmarks with questions and answers, we only use the question part as patterns. As a pre-trained corpus, we used the full FineWeb-Edu dataset (1.4T tokens).

Verifying True Contamination.
We manually inspected each problem flagged as “dirty” by the soft search but not by the exact match (i.e., maximum similarity
<1.0
but
\geq 0.6
), whether the problem is truly contaminated or not. Specifically, we classified each problem as (1) semantic contamination, (2) template leakage, or (3) false positive.

Quantitative Results.

Figure

6
shows the number of problems flagged as dirty by exact match and soft search, along with the breakdown of semantic contamination. In total, the standard exact match flagged 338 out of 2,564 samples (13.2%) as dirty. Our method detected an additional 36 samples (1.4%) that the exact match overlooked. Importantly, this manageable volume allowed for exhaustive manual inspection,
which revealed a high precision rate of 81% (29/36); 18 were classified as semantic contamination (e.g., comma insertions), and 11 were classified as template leakage (e.g., numerical substitution). The complete list, including the classification results is shown in
Appendix

K
.

Below, we show two different interesting discovered examples of contamination.

Example 1
: In the MMLU benchmark, one question asks “The difference between dc and ac in electric circuits is that in dc, charges flow”. In the corpus, we found the text “The difference between DC and AC
in
electric circuits is that in DC, charges flow in one direction.” appears in the corpus, where “in” is simply deleted. This is an example of semantic contamination that may affect the fairness of evaluation.

Example 2
: In the MATH benchmark, one question reads “The sum of two numbers is 25 and their difference is 11. What is the larger of the two numbers?”. In the corpus, we found the text “The sum of two numbers is
139
while their difference is
19
. What is the larger of two numbers?” exists in the corpus; one is obtained by numerical substitution from the other.
Furthermore, the solution to this problem immediately follows this text in the pre-trained corpus. This is an example of template leakage.

7
Conclusion

We developed an ultra-fast and flexible algorithm for matching queries against trillion-scale natural language corpora under semantic variations, centered on dynamic pruning for semantic query relaxation, built on top of a newly designed, disk-aware suffix-array index. We theoretically analyze how statistical properties of natural language suppress the exponential growth of query variants as query length increases. Empirically, our method achieves soft matching at the 0.1-second level and substantially reduces latency compared with infini-gram, infini-gram mini, and SoftMatcha. As a practical outcome, we show that the proposed method enables the detection of contamination between training corpora and evaluation data.

We also note several limitations of the proposed method. First, it operates at the word level and cannot capture semantic variations that span multiple words, such as the similarity between “U.S.” and “United States.” The theoretical analysis in
Section

4
presupposes statistical regularities of natural language to effectively suppress the combinatorial explosion induced by semantic query relaxation. In addition, the proposed method requires memory and disk resources that scale with corpus size, which can make deployment and system setup demanding.

An important future direction is to strengthen the theoretical grounding of our analysis by collecting broader empirical evidence and relating it to known statistical regularities and universals of natural language. Another promising direction is to incorporate compositional representations, such as sums of word vectors, to better handle multi-word semantic variations; however, doing so would substantially increase the number of substitution candidates, making it challenging to preserve low-latency search.
Supporting more general queries, such as wildcards, is also a future direction.
Finally, we plan to extend the online demo to additional languages and corpora, enabling broader analysis of large-scale training data and facilitating community use.

Appendix H
Details of the Fast Disk-Aware Suffix Array in
Section

3.2

As explained in
Section

3.2
, we also improve the suffix array to further improve the efficiency of exact lookup. The core ideas are (1) to exploit the fact that queries are usually short (10 tokens or fewer) and (2) to store some indices in RAM to reduce the number of random disk accesses.

Let
L
be the maximum query length (in our experiments, we used
L=12
). The first step is to record a sorted array of all contiguous
L
patterns in the corpus (i.e., the array has length
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert-L+1
), instead of the suffix array; we denote this array by
X
. By binary search on
X
, we can count the number of occurrences of the query string
\bar{q}
in the corpus. However, it still requires
O(\log\,\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert)
random disk accesses. We further reduce the number of accesses using a two-level indexing strategy, similar to Google’s BigTable
Chang et al. 2008
, described below.

Let
B
be a constant.
14
14

14

In our experiments, we use
B
between 128–256.
In addition to
X
, we construct a sparse array
Y=[X_{0},X_{B},X_{2B},\dots]
, where
X_{i}
is the
i
-th element of the array
X
. This array is stored in RAM. When we search for the position of an occurrence of query
\bar{q}
, we first find the approximate position (i.e., such
i
that
\bar{q}
occurs in
Z=[X_{iB},X_{iB+1},\dots,X_{(i+1)B-1}]
if it exists in
X
) using
Y
, and then find the exact position in
X
using
Z
. Surprisingly, only a single random disk access is required, significantly fewer than the
O(\log\,\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert)
accesses required by a standard suffix array if
B
is set appropriately so that
B/2
is smaller than the page size of operating systems. This is because the range of the array
X
examined (in the second step of binary search) is at most
B/2
entries.

Example.
An example is shown in
Figure

14
. To find the position of “pattern match”, we first search
Y
and determine that the position lies between indices 3 and 6. We then search
X
and find that the exact position is index 4.

Figure 14:
The sketch of our fast suffix array for the text “pattern match for a trillion scale corpus”, with
L=2
and
B=3
.

Compression.
This method requires substantial disk space. Let
V
be the vocabulary size; then the size of
X
is
n\lceil(L\log_{2}V)/8\rceil
bytes (e.g.,
29n
bytes when
L=12
and
V=400\text{K}
). To address this problem, we apply run-length compression to
X

Appendix I
Details of Dynamic Corpus-Aware Pruning in
Section

3.3

As explained in
Section

3.3
, we introduced two additional techniques, in addition to iterative pruning.

I.1
k
-gram Pruning: Caching 2-gram and 3-gram Existence

To reduce the number of corpus-existence check that requires disk access, we store precomputed 2-gram and 3-gram existence tables in RAM. Specifically, we record (1) all 2-grams both of whose constituent words rank among the top 100K most frequent words and (2) all 3-grams whose three constituent words have frequency ranks that sum to 10K or less. When calculating
L_{1},L_{2},\dots
(see
Section

3.3
), if a 2-gram or 3-gram meets the frequency-rank conditions above (and thus would appear in the table if it existed in the corpus), we skip the corpus lookup when it is absent from the tables.

I.2
Last-Bits Pruning: For Patterns with Low Frequencies

Suppose the situation that in
Algorithm

1
, the current pattern
w\in L_{i-1}
appears only once in the corpus. In this case, it is inefficient to search for
w\cdot w^{\prime}
for all
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
with similarity
\alpha
or more. Instead, it is better to look at the corpus directly and find the next tokens. We applied this method when the number of occurrences (after run-length compression of array
X
in
Appendix
