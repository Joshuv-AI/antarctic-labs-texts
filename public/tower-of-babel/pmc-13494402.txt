Pacing-Induced Transient Severe Mitral Regurgitation during Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Functional mitral regurgitation (MR) arises when left ventricular (LV) dysfunction alters papillary muscle tension and annular contraction, leading to incomplete leaflet closure despite a structurally normal valve.1,2 Myocardial stunning represents a transient, reversible loss of contractility after brief ischemic or hemodynamic stress, which can trigger acute MR through temporary papillary dysfunction and reduced closing force.3 Prior reports have described reversible MR secondary to coronary vasospasm or postischemic stunning,4, 5, 6 but this dynamic phenomenon is underrecognized in clinical practice. Related entities such as eclipsed or paroxysmal MR illustrate how transient mechanical discoordination, arising from vasospasm, rate-dependent dyssynchrony, or abrupt changes in loading conditions, can displace the papillary muscles and restrict leaflet coaptation, producing marked but fully reversible MR once systolic synchrony and papillary tension are restored.7 Eclipsed MR refers specifically to episodes of transient massive MR occurring in patients with normal LV function and no fixed structural valve disease, often triggered by acute hemodynamic or autonomic perturbations; unlike the present case, eclipsed MR has been described predominantly in the nonprocedural setting and is frequently associated with coronary vasospasm or abrupt changes in loading conditions.7

Acute MR during transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is a rare but clinically significant complication with multiple reported mechanisms, including guidewire entanglement, prosthesis impingement on the mitral leaflet, and coronary obstruction.8, 9, 10 However, transient MR resulting from pacing-induced mitral apparatus dysfunction, in which rapid ventricular pacing during valve deployment produces reversible leaflet tethering and malcoaptation without structural injury, remains underexplored.11,12 We report a case of acute severe MR that developed immediately after rapid ventricular pacing during TAVI, with complete resolution within minutes, and discuss the role of echocardiography in distinguishing this reversible phenomenon from structural complications requiring intervention.

Case Presentation

A 69-year-old patient with hyperlipidemia and secondary hypopituitarism after pituitary tumor resection, maintained on chronic steroid replacement therapy, presented with exertional chest pain and dyspnea consistent with New York Heart Association class II symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed 6 weeks before the procedure (blood pressure [BP], 131/80 mm Hg) demonstrated a heavily calcified trileaflet aortic valve with severe aortic stenosis (AS; peak velocity 4.4 m/s, mean gradient 47 mm Hg, valve area 0.8 cm2) and moderate aortic regurgitation (AR). Left ventricular systolic function was normal (LV ejection fraction [LVEF] 65%) with normal regional wall motion, and there was mild MR by color-flow Doppler. The left atrium was severely dilated (left atrial volume index, 54.0 mL/m2). Invasive coronary angiography showed a right-dominant system without obstructive disease. Right heart catheterization demonstrated normal filling pressures. Following multidisciplinary heart team evaluation and shared decision-making, the patient was scheduled for transfemoral TAVI.

A limited intraprocedural TTE performed in the catheterization laboratory before valve deployment confirmed normal global LV systolic function (LVEF, 65%), normal regional wall motion, mild MR, and severe AS with moderate AR. Baseline aortic pressure was 116/65 mm Hg (mean 87 mm Hg) with a heart rate of 69 beats per minute (bpm). A 26 mm balloon-expandable transcatheter aortic valve was deployed via transfemoral approach under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm.

Immediately after valve deployment, the patient developed acute hypotension (aortic pressure, 87/45 mm Hg; mean, 51 mm Hg), tachycardia (heart rate, 158-168 bpm), pulmonary edema, and hypoxemia requiring high-flow nasal cannula at 40 L/min. Intraprocedural echocardiographic assessment demonstrated acutely tethered and restricted mitral valve leaflets, particularly the posterior leaflet, with incomplete coaptation. There was qualitatively severe MR based on the two-dimensional systolic malcoaptation and the visualized regurgitant jet area with color-flow Doppler (Figure 1A and 1B, Videos 1 and 2). The transcatheter aortic bioprosthesis was well seated with normal gradients (peak/mean 9/6 mm Hg) and no paravalvular regurgitation. There was no evidence of structural disruption of the mitral valve apparatus, systolic anterior motion, or pericardial effusion. Regional wall motion appeared normal. The patient was treated with intravenous vasopressors and diuretics. Within minutes of hemodynamic stabilization, continued echocardiographic monitoring demonstrated recovery of mitral leaflet coaptation with only mild residual MR by color-flow Doppler (Figure 1C and 1D, Videos 3 and 4). Aortic pressure recovered to 124/71 mm Hg (mean, 88 mm Hg) with a heart rate of 86 bpm, approximately 16 minutes after the onset of acute hypotension. The patient was transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit on supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula, which was weaned to room air by the following afternoon.

A formal TTE performed the following morning (BP, 125/75 mm Hg) confirmed a normal-sized left ventricle (LV internal dimension in diastole 3.9 cm, in systole 2.8 cm) with mildly increased wall thickness, LVEF 60% to 65%, and no regional wall motion abnormalities. There was mild MR. The transcatheter aortic bioprosthesis demonstrated normal gradients (peak/mean 10/5 mm Hg) with no AR. At 1-month follow-up (BP, 110/80 mm Hg), TTE confirmed normal bioprosthetic aortic valve function (peak velocity 256 cm/s, peak/mean gradient 26/12 mm Hg, no AR), normal LV systolic function (LVEF, 55%-60%), and no significant residual MR.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but important complication of TAVI: transient severe MR resulting from pacing-induced mitral apparatus dysfunction, with rapid and complete resolution documented by serial echocardiography. The dramatic transition from mild MR at baseline to severe MR immediately after rapid ventricular pacing, and back to mild MR within minutes, highlights both the clinical significance and the reversibility of this phenomenon.

Transient MR developed immediately after rapid pacing during TAVI. The acute MR was attributed to papillary muscle displacement and the abrupt fall in afterload after valve deployment.1, 2, 3 High-rate pacing disrupts the coordinated sequence of ventricular contraction and reduces LV filling, temporarily diminishing stroke volume and transmitral closing pressure.1,2 The resulting loss of coordinated papillary traction produced tethering of the posterior mitral leaflet with incomplete coaptation and severe MR, permitting transient regurgitant flow until native contractility recovered within minutes. Notably, regional wall motion appeared normal and LVEF remained normal both during and after the episode, with no regional wall motion abnormalities identified on the postprocedural TTE. Because LV systolic function was normal throughout, the mechanism in this case is better characterized as transient mitral apparatus dysfunction rather than conventional myocardial stunning, which classically refers to reversible LV systolic dysfunction after brief ischemia or hemodynamic stress.3 The rapid pacing likely disrupted the finely coordinated timing of papillary muscle contraction relative to LV systole without producing detectable global or regional contractile impairment, consistent with observations that pacing-induced dyssynchrony can alter mitral valve coaptation independently of changes in global LV function.12,13 Although pacing-induced LV dysfunction has been reported in experimental studies, its clinical expression as acute MR in humans is exceptionally uncommon.4, 5, 6 Recognizing this reversible mechanism is crucial to avoid misinterpreting acute postprocedural MR as structural valve injury and to guide conservative management in hemodynamically stable patients.7,8

Several mechanisms may contribute to acute MR during TAVI. Structural causes include guidewire entanglement in the chordae or papillary muscles, direct impingement of the prosthesis on the anterior mitral leaflet, and chordal or papillary muscle rupture.8, 9, 10 Functional causes include new-onset left bundle branch block producing LV dyssynchrony, relative myocardial ischemia from rapid ventricular pacing leading to papillary muscle dysfunction, systolic anterior motion of the mitral leaflet, and the hemodynamic effects of abrupt afterload reduction after relief of AS.8,10,14 In this case, structural causes were excluded by echocardiographic demonstration of an intact mitral apparatus without chordal disruption, the absence of guidewire interference, and normal coronary perfusion on invasive angiography. The rapid resolution of MR further supports a functional rather than structural mechanism. Both acute afterload reduction after relief of severe AS and pacing-induced dyssynchrony likely contributed to the observed transient severe MR.

Caro-Codón et al.11 described a similar case of massive MR during TAVI in which rapid ventricular pacing produced severe biventricular dysfunction with restricted mitral leaflet motion and incomplete coaptation, resolving with hemodynamic stabilization. In that case, preexisting LV systolic impairment (LVEF, 50%) was identified as a possible risk factor. More recently, Manasrah et al.15 reported a case of acute severe MR after TAVI attributed to transient ischemic dysfunction of the posteromedial papillary muscle secondary to intraprocedural hypotension, with improvement over several days and only mild MR at 1-year follow-up. Our case differs in several important respects: LV systolic function was normal throughout, recovery occurred within minutes rather than days, and patent coronary arteries on invasive angiography supported a nonischemic mechanism. Additional reports have described transient MR during TAVI secondary to conduction disturbances14 and LV dyssynchrony from new left bundle branch block.12 Taken together, these cases suggest that acute MR during TAVI can arise from multiple mechanisms but is often reversible once the underlying hemodynamic or electrical disturbance resolves.

Echocardiography played a central diagnostic and management role throughout this case. Intraprocedural TTE enabled rapid identification of the mechanism of hemodynamic deterioration, exclusion of structural complications (paravalvular regurgitation, pericardial effusion, prosthesis malposition), and real-time documentation of leaflet tethering and malcoaptation as the cause of acute MR. Serial imaging then confirmed recovery of coaptation and resolution of MR, providing the clinical confidence to pursue conservative management rather than emergent intervention. This case illustrates the value of immediate echocardiographic assessment during TAVI when unexpected hemodynamic instability occurs and highlights the importance of serial imaging before attributing postprocedural MR to structural pathology requiring surgical or transcatheter correction.8,9,16

In an era of expanding TAVI indications across all risk categories, awareness of pacing-induced transient MR is important. Transient severe MR due to mitral apparatus dysfunction can closely mimic structural valve injury in the acute periprocedural setting and may prompt unnecessary intervention if its transient nature is not recognized. Conclusion

This case demonstrates that rapid ventricular pacing during TAVI can produce transient severe MR through acute mitral apparatus dysfunction, with complete resolution within minutes after hemodynamic stabilization. Serial echocardiography was essential in distinguishing this reversible phenomenon from structural valve injury. Clinicians performing TAVI should be aware that acute severe MR in this setting may represent a self-limited complication that responds to conservative management and that serial imaging before pursuing intervention is critical to avoid unnecessary procedures.
