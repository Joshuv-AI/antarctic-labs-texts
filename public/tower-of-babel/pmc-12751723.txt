Impact of laparoscopic surgical proficiency on survival outcomes in laparoscopic radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer: a multi-center cohort study
Authors: Rongjia Su, Dongjian Yang, Sibang Chen, Xiaoyi Ding, Tao Su, Jin Peng, Jiaxi Wang, Xinyu Ni, Roufei Lu, Fengshuang Li, Jiandong Wang, Jiangtao Fan, Shiqian Zhang, Yudong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the impact of gynecologic oncologists' laparoscopic proficiency on survival outcomes in cervical cancer patients.MethodsA cohort of 1,965 cervical cancer cases from four clinical centers in China was analyzed, including abdominal radical hysterectomy (ARH), laparoscopic radical hysterectomy (LRH), and robotic radical hysterectomy (RRH). The median operative time (MOT) for LRH was used as a measure of surgical proficiency. Survival outcomes of ARH vs. LRH were compared in early-stage cervical cancer patients without adjuvant therapy to identify a critical MOT threshold. Below this threshold, no significant differences in prognosis were observed between ARH and LRH. Propensity score matching and mixed-effects Cox regression were used to adjust for baseline risk factors and random effects, validating the finding across all LRH cases.ResultsThe Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that improved prognosis was associated with reduced MOT. When gynecologic oncologists had an MOT within 210 min, LRH vs ARH was no longer a significant risk factor (HR 1.1998; 95% CI: 0.9785–1.4713; p = 0.07998). Propensity score matching and mixed-effects Cox regression were used to further clarify the significant prognostic differences in LRH performed by different level surgeons.ConclusionMOT reflects surgical efficiency and serves as a key indicator of the operative proficiency of gynecologic oncologists, which is pivotal in determining the survival prognosis of cervical cancer patients undergoing LRH. For surgeons with rigorous laparoscopic training, the survival outcomes of LRH are expected to be comparable to those of ARH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15220-7.

Introduction

Surgery is undoubtedly a vital approach for the treatment of early-stage cervical cancer [1–3]. In patients with early-stage cervical cancer characterized by tumors less than 4 cm in greatest dimension and confined to the cervix, radical hysterectomy achieves cure rates exceeding 80% [4]. In recent years, the selection of the surgical approach has become a subject of significant debate. LACC (Laparoscopic Approach to Cervical Cancer) clinical research has demonstrated that Laparoscopic Radical Hysterectomy (LRH) is associated with lower disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) rates compared to open surgery [5, 6]. However, we must not overlook another crucial factor that impacts patient prognosis: the operating surgeons [7, 8]. The design of this study is based on a straightforward hypothesis: patients undergoing surgery performed by a surgeon with rigorous training and extensive clinical experience are expected to achieve superior outcomes compared to those operated on by a surgeon with limited experience in radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer.

The dexterity is a critical reflection of surgical skills, particularly in minimally invasive surgery [9]. The greater the surgeon's dexterity, the more efficiently the surgery can be performed. Furthermore, the anatomical considerations of a radical hysterectomy are well-characterized [10, 11], resulting in relatively standardized surgical steps. According to the Querleu-Morrow (QM) classification [11] in this multicenter retrospective clinical study, type C radical hysterectomy serves as the primary surgical approach for cervical cancer, specifically indicated for stages IB1 to IIIC2. In contrast, type B radical hysterectomy is generally recommended for stage IA2 disease. Performing a radical hysterectomy can be likened to an assembly line process, following a consistent and methodical sequence. As surgeons' operative skills improve and their familiarity with local anatomy increases with experience, the time required to complete a radical hysterectomy is expected to gradually decrease. Therefore, the time required to complete the procedure can serve as an indicator of the surgeon's technical proficiency. We have selected the median operative time (MOT) for all LRH performed by each surgeon, rather than the duration of an individual surgery, as an indicator of the surgeon's proficiency.

Another key difference from the LACC clinical study is that we selected early-stage cervical cancer patients who did not require adjuvant therapy based on their postoperative pathology reports to identify the MOT threshold between junior and senior surgeons. This selection ensured that surgical factors primarily determined patient prognosis. Finally, we further validated that the surgical proficiency of gynecologic oncologists was a critical determinant of LRH prognosis by adjusting for baseline differences between junior and senior surgeons using propensity score matching (PSM) and accounting for random effects in surgical performance through mixed effects Cox regression in all LRH cervical cancer cases with available survival data.

Methods

Study design and study population

This multicenter retrospective clinical study aimed to evaluate the hypothesis that the proficiency level of gynecologic oncologists is crucial in determining the survival prognosis after LRH, and that when performed by systematically trained gynecologic oncologists, LRH provides survival outcomes comparable to those of open radical hysterectomy. All patients were recruited from four clinical centers in China from January 2012 to January 2022.

Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older and had histologically confirmed primary cervical squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma (AC), or adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC) of the uterine cervix, classified according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO 2018) stages IA2 to IB3.

In the step to identify the MOT threshold between junior and senior surgeons, the initial exclusion criteria of the study aimed to exclude cervical cancer patients who require adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy post-surgery, confirming that surgical factors play a critical role in patient prognosis. For SCC, exclusion followed the Sedlis [12] criteria, as detailed in Table 1. For cervical AC/ASC, the four-factor model [13] was referenced. Since adenocarcinoma itself was considered an intermediate-risk factor, the presence of any additional intermediate-risk factors—such as a lesion size greater than 3 cm, positive lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), or deep stromal invasion—led to exclusion. Ethics approval and consent to participate

This clinical study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital. This clinical trial (Registration ID: ChiCTR2400095020) was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn/) on December 31, 2024. The study is under the responsibility of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Identification of the MOT threshold between junior and senior surgeons

Unlike the LACC trial, the surgical skills of gynecologic oncologists performing LRH were not assumed to be uniformly equivalent. Instead, their proficiency was assessed based on the MOT of all LRH procedures they personal performed. The primary purpose of collecting the maximum number of cases was to determine the MOT required by different surgeons to perform LRH. In addition, if a surgeon had performed fewer than ten LRH procedures, all of their cases were excluded from the analysis.

To specifically examine the impact of surgical factor on the prognosis of patients with early-stage cervical cancer, patients with locally early-stage cervical cancer, classified as IA2 to IB3, were selected. Additionally, only those cases in which, based on postoperative pathology, no adjuvant therapy was required were included in the analysis.

ARH, as the recommended standard surgical approach for cervical cancer [1], serves as the benchmark for patient outcomes, which remain consistent. Surgeries performed by surgeons with lower clinical proficiency, as indicated by longer MOT, were progressively excluded, and a univariate Cox regression model was reconstructed each time. This process continued until laparoscopic surgery was no longer identified as a risk factor for cervical cancer patients compared to open surgery. This threshold MOT, serving as an indicator of surgical proficiency and its influence on patient prognosis, will be further validated through survival analysis using all available LRH data. To balance the baseline characteristics between LRH procedures performed by senior and junior gynecologic oncologists and to reduce the impact of confounding factors, this study applied propensity score matching (PSM) to all available LRH data to clarify the differences in patient prognosis between the two groups. Finally, the study employed mixed effects Cox regression, incorporating surgeons as a random effect and MOT as a fixed effect, to further validate the accuracy of the aforementioned conclusions. Statistical analysis

This study primarily employed a dynamic, stratified analysis approach, progressively excluding LRH cases performed by surgeons with the longest MOT in survival analyses, aiming to demonstrate a gradual improvement in prognosis for cervical cancer patients following LRH. The primary endpoint was overall survival, defined as the interval from surgery to death attributable to cervical cancer. All analyses adhered to the intention-to-treat principle. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, while proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to evaluate the impact of treatment on OS. PSM and mixed effects Cox regression were used as sensitivity analysis methods to further validate the preliminary results. Statistical analyses were conducted using a two-sided significance level of 0.05 (p-value ≤ 0.05), with R 4.13 software utilized for all computations.

Results

Of these, 569 patients with early-stage cervical cancer who neither required neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to surgery nor received any adjuvant therapy postoperatively were included in the survival analysis. Discussion

Compared to open surgery, minimally invasive operation is associated with a lower risk of infection and faster recovery, and has therefore been widely adopted in gynecologic procedures [15]. Several clinical studies have confirmed that cervical cancer patients who undergo open surgery have better prognoses compared to those who undergo minimally invasive surgery [5, 6, 16, 17]. However, the LACC trial was not intended to elucidate the underlying causes of the inferior outcomes observed in the minimally invasive surgery cohort [18]. Several confounding variables were not adequately accounted for, including the type of minimally invasive surgery performed, lesion size, the effects of CO2 pneumoperitoneum, prior conization, the use of a uterine manipulator, the application of protective measures, and the influence of surgical expertise and the learning curve [19]. By addressing and correcting these factors, minimally invasive surgery has the potential to achieve outcomes comparable to those of open surgery. It would be irresponsible and unethical to initiate a prospective randomized controlled trial without understanding which factors in laparoscopic surgery are the fundamental causes of poor prognosis in cervical cancer patients. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective clinical study, employing stratified analysis, to identify the underlying factors contributing to the poor outcomes associated with laparoscopic radical hysterectomy. Taliento C et al. compared simple hysterectomy versus radical hysterectomy in early-stage cervical cancer and found no significant differences in recurrence rates or overall survival between the two surgical approaches [20]. This study focused on the impact of surgeon variability on patient survival outcomes in radical hysterectomy, demonstrating that with increasing surgical proficiency in laparoscopic radical hysterectomy, the prognosis of cervical cancer patients progressively improves, ultimately achieving comparable outcomes to standard open surgery.

It is evident that the outcomes of surgeries performed by different surgeons can vary significantly; however, selecting an appropriate objective metric to accurately reflect a surgeon's skill level remains a challenge [21]. The dexterity and minimization of unnecessary movements are both indicators of a surgeon's technical skill. Improvements in these areas undoubtedly enhance the surgeon's operative speed which has long been used as a metric for evaluating surgical skills [9]. For procedures like ovarian cystectomy and myomectomy, the variability in the morphology and number of tumors leads to significant inconsistency in surgical difficulty and operative duration. In contrast, for procedures like hysterectomy and radical hysterectomy [10, 11], which follow well-established steps based on fascial anatomy [22], the surgeon's proficiency is a crucial factor influencing both the speed and quality of the surgery. Ultimately, we chose the MOT for all LRHs performed by a particular surgeon as an indicator of that surgeon's skill level. The use of median helps to mitigate outliers caused by surgical challenges, such as anatomical variations or severe pelvic adhesions from prior surgeries, which can lead to unusually long or short operative durations. The MOT can be conceptually viewed as a composite indicator that inherently integrates multiple intraoperative factors, including anatomical variations, team coordination, operational errors leading to iatrogenic injury, and slowed procedure due to a poor surgical field. Specifically, unfamiliarity with anatomical variations increases the risk of intraoperative injury, and the subsequent repair inevitably prolongs operative time—a scenario that skilled surgeons are more likely to avoid. Similarly, poor team coordination is a well-established factor contributing to prolonged MOT. In essence, regardless of whether prolonged MOT stems from external factors or the surgeon’s technical limitations, it is generally associated with unfavorable surgical outcomes and compromised long-term survival. Conversely, proficient surgeons tend to mitigate these time-extending incidents, thereby achieving a lower MOT. Surgeons with fewer than 10 documented LRH procedures were excluded from the analysis to ensure the stability and reliability of the MOT.

Stratified analysis based on this MOT demonstrated that shorter MOT were associated with better outcomes for LRH. Our findings suggest that minimally invasive surgery was associated with a higher risk of death compared to open surgery for early-stage cervical cancer, particularly for junior surgeons performing laparoscopic radical hysterectomy with a MOT exceeding 210 min. However, for senior surgeons with a MOT of < 210 min, the laparoscopic approach showed no statistically significant difference in prognosis compared to the abdominal approach for cervical cancer patients. Through an in-depth analysis of the cervical cancer composition between the two groups, we found that cases operated on by junior surgeons were more likely to be in the early stages, whereas those operated on by senior surgeons tended to be in more advanced stages. In other words, although junior surgeons performed LRH on cervical cancer patients with earlier-stage disease, their patients had worse prognoses compared to those treated by senior surgeons. The impact of differences in surgical proficiency among gynecologic oncologists on cervical cancer prognosis was not limited to patients with early-stage disease. When applying the same analysis to all cervical cancer patients, stratified by surgeon experience, prognostic outcomes consistently showed that patients operated on by senior surgeons had significantly better survival outcomes compared to those operated on by junior surgeons. This highlights the critical role of surgical skill in influencing the prognosis of cervical cancer patients.

Robotic surgery does not fundamentally differ from laparoscopic surgery in terms of the surgical approach. It has been suggested that the unique attributes of robotic surgery make it particularly well-suited for complex procedures within the deep and narrow pelvic cavity in cervical cancer, potentially reducing the risk of complications [23]. Reports suggest that RRH represents a safe and effective alternative to the abdominal approach for early-stage cervical cancer [24]. The number of RRH cases included in our clinical study was limited, and, according to reports, did not reach the number required to complete the learning curve for laparoscopy [25]. This resulted in the poorest prognosis, indirectly highlighting the critical role of surgical skill in the prognosis of cervical cancer patients.

The LACC trial included patients with stages IA1 (with lymph-vascular invasion), IA2, or IB1 (tumor ≤ 4 cm; no evidence of nodal involvement) [5]. At the initial stage of the study, we selected IA2 and IB-stage cervical cancer patients who required no adjuvant therapy based on postoperative pathology reports. This approach was used to isolate the impact of surgical factors on patient prognosis, excluding the influence of adjuvant chemo-radiotherapy. Finally, building on the findings of prior research, we conducted survival analyses on all LRH patients to further validate the critical impact of surgical factors on patient prognosis.

Conclusion

The median operative time (MOT) reflects surgical efficiency and serves as a key indicator of the operative proficiency of gynecologic oncologists, which is pivotal in determining the survival prognosis of cervical cancer patients undergoing LRH. As surgical proficiency improves, the MOT required to complete LRH gradually decreases, and the prognosis of cervical cancer patients correspondingly improves. For senior surgeons with rigorous laparoscopic training, the survival outcomes of LRH are expected to be comparable to those of ARH.
