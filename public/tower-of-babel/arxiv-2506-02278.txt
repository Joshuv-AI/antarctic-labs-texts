Separable motions for self-gravitating hyperelastic matter
Juhi Jang, Trevor M. Leslie

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove the existence of separable solutions to the equations of motion for self-gravitating hyperelastic matter, under an appropriate class of constitutive assumptions on the strain-energy function. Our framework includes both global-in-time solutions which expand and also solutions which collapse to a point in finite time. Other authors have constructed expanding solutions in similar settings, but to the best of our knowledge, the collapsing solutions we construct are completely new.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the dynamics of a self-gravitating elastic body
\mathcal{B}(t)
, surrounded by vacuum, which obeys the following equations of motion:

(1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)+\diver(\rho{\bf u})(t,x)=0,\qquad x\in\mathcal{B}(t)=\supp\rho(t),\\
\rho(\partial_{t}{\bf u}+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla{\bf u})(t,x)=\diver\mathsf{T}(t,x)-G\rho(t,x)\nabla_{x}\Phi[\rho(t)](x),\qquad x\in\mathcal{B}(t),\\
\Delta\Phi[\varrho]=4\pi\varrho,\qquad\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\Phi[\varrho](x)=0\\
\mathsf{T}(t,x){\bf n}(x)=0,\qquad x\in\partial\mathcal{B}(t).\end{cases}

Here
\rho\geq 0
and
{\bf u}
are the density and velocity of the body,
\mathsf{T}
is the Cauchy stress tensor, and
{\bf n}(x)
denotes the unit normal vector to the boundary of
\mathcal{B}(t)=\supp\rho(t)
. The constant
G
is Newton’s gravitational constant. In order to determine the evolution of the quantities
\rho
and
{\bf u}
, the system must be supplemented with a constitutive relation that describes how the Cauchy stress
\mathsf{T}
responds to the dynamics; specifying a suitable class of constitutive hypotheses is an important and nontrivial part of formulating and studying problems related to (
1
). We discuss our choice of constitutive assumptions in Sections
1.2
and
1.5
.

Our main goal is to construct a family of special spherically symmetric, ‘separable’ solutions to the system (
1
), under an appropriate set of constitutive assumptions. Our framework is in large part inspired by a recent paper of Sideris
[
18
]
, who constructed separable solutions in the absence of a gravitational term. Calogero
[
3
]
has also studied separable solutions of (
1
), though there are some important differences between his framework and ours. We will discuss the relationship between our work and the papers
[
18
]
,
[
3
]
in Section
1.5
, after providing more details about the setup of our problem.

1.1.
Reference Configuration and Separable Solutions

Our constitutive assumptions and the separable solutions we will construct are most naturally described in Lagrangian rather than Eulerian coordinates. We fix the following reference domain
\mathcal{B}
and (constant) reference density
\bar{\rho}
:

(2)

\mathcal{B}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|X|\leq 1\},

(3)

\bar{\rho}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}(0)|}\int_{\mathcal{B}(0)}\rho(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Here
|\mathcal{B}(0)|
denotes Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{B}(0)
. When we refer to a ‘flow map’ associated to a classical solution
(\rho,{\bf u})
of (
1
) on the time interval
[0,T)
(assumed to be maximal), we mean a function
x~:~[0,T)\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}(t)
that satisfies
\dot{x}(t,X)={\bf u}(t,x(t,X))
and
\rho(t,x(t,X))\det Dx(t,X)=\bar{\rho}
, for all
t\in[0,T)
and all
X\in\mathcal{B}
. We also require that
x(t,\cdot)
is an orientation-preserving
C^{1}
diffeomorphism for each
t\in[0,T)
and that
x
is at least
C^{2}
in time.

Any classical spherically symmetric solution will possess a flow map satisfying
x(t,X)=\phi(t,|X|)\frac{X}{|X|}
for
t\in[0,T)
and
X\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\{0\}
, for some function
\phi:[0,T)\times[0,1]\to[0,+\infty)
. In order for
x(t,\cdot)
to be an orientation-preserving,
C^{1}
diffeomorphism for each
t
, we require that
\phi(t,0)=\phi_{RR}(t,0)=0
for all
t\in[0,T)
and
\phi_{R}(t,R)>0
on
[0,T)\times[0,1]
(c.f.
[
18
]
, Section 5). (Here
\phi_{R}
and
\phi_{RR}
denote partial derivatives of
\phi
.)

When we refer to a
separable solution
, we mean a spherically symmetric solution such that the function
\phi(t,R)
from the previous paragraph has the form
\phi(t,R)=q(t)\varphi(R)
, where
q:[0,T)\to[0,\infty)
and
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
are both
C^{2}
. We will also sometimes use the terminology
homologous
synonymously with ‘separable.’

Remark 1.1
.

Separable motions, by nature, allow for the separation of time and spatial dynamics, rendering them amenable to detailed study. Their admissibility as solutions to (
1
) is tied to the symmetry of the system, or more precisely the constitutive assumptions that we make in Section
1.2
below. Separable motions also have a long history in the context of fluid and gas dynamics, which has influenced some of the more recent literature on elastic matter that is relevant to our work. We will discuss this below, especially in Section
1.5
.

1.2.
Basic Constitutive Assumptions

Let
x(t,X)
denote a flow map as defined in the previous subsection; let
Dx(t,X)
denote its deformation tensor and
Jx(t,X)=\det Dx(t,X)
its Jacobian determinant. The
Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor

\mathsf{S}
associated to the reference configuration
(\mathcal{B},\bar{\rho})
is defined by

(4)

\mathsf{S}(t,X)=Jx(t,X)\mathsf{T}(t,x(t,X))Dx(t,X)^{-T},\qquad(t,X)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{B}.

Our constitutive assumptions will be stated in terms of
\mathsf{S}
and related functions. We need the following terminology, most of which can be found in standard references on mathematical elasticity theory, e.g.,
[
6
,
14
,
13
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that the body
\mathcal{B}(t)
is
elastic
if the Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor
\mathsf{S}
depends on time only through the deformation tensor, and if furthermore there exists a continuously differentiable ‘constitutive function’
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}:~GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R})\times~\mathcal{B}\to~GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R})
satisfying

\mathsf{S}(t,X)=\widehat{\mathsf{S}}(Dx(t,X),X),\qquad X\in\mathcal{B},t\in[0,T).

(Here
GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of invertible
3\times 3
matrices with real entries that have positive determinant. We will also use the notation
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
to denote the subspace of
GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R})
consisting of those matrices with determinant
1
.) We say that
\mathcal{B}(t)
is
hyperelastic
if it is elastic and, additionally, there exists a
strain-energy function

W:GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
associated to
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}
, in the sense that

\widehat{\mathsf{S}}(F,X)=\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial F}W(F,X),\qquad F\in GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R}),\;X\in\mathcal{B}.

We say that a hyperelastic body
\mathcal{B}(t)
is
homogeneous
if the strain-energy function
W
does not explicitly depend on
X
:

W(F,X)=W(F).

Definition 1.3
.

We say that a strain-energy function
W
is (i) objective or (ii) isotropic if

W(F,X)=W(OF,X),\text{ for each }F\in GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R}),\;O\in SO(3,\mathbb{R}),

W(F,X)=W(FO,X),\text{ for each }F\in GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R}),\;O\in SO(3,\mathbb{R}).

We say that
W
is homogeneous of degree
h
if

W(\sigma F)=\sigma^{h}W(F),\qquad\sigma>0,\;F\in GL_{+}(3,\mathbb{R}).

For the rest of the manuscript, we work with a hyperelastic, homogeneous body with a strain-energy function that is objective, isotropic, and homogeneous of degree
-1
.

Remark 1.4
.

Hyperelasticity and homogeneity of the body, as well as objectivity and isotropy of
W
are all mild assumptions. Degree
-1
homogeneity of
W
is on the other hand a rather strong assumption. However, as already remarked in Sideris’s work
[
18
]
(Section 2), some homogeneity assumption is necessary in order to produce separable solutions—even in the absence of the gravitational term. The gravitational force compels us to choose the degree of homogeneity to be equal to
-1
.

Remark 1.5

(Comparison with Mass-Critical Euler–Poisson)
.

It is instructive to compare (
1
) with the Euler–Poisson system for a polytropic gas, particularly in the ‘mass-critical’ case of polytropic exponent
\gamma=\frac{4}{3}
. This will be a frequent theme of the introduction.

If we set
W(F)=3\kappa\big(\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\det F}\big)^{\frac{1}{3}}
(which satisfies the constitutive hypotheses above for any
\kappa>0
), then the resulting Cauchy stress satisfies
\diver\mathsf{T}(t,x)=-\nabla(\kappa\rho(t,x)^{\frac{4}{3}})
, in which case the system (
1
) becomes the Euler–Poisson equations for the case of a mass-critical polytropic gas, except for the boundary conditions, which are different than those typically imposed for Euler–Poisson system. We refer to the boundary conditions (
1
)
4
as ‘solid vacuum boundary conditions,’ to contrast with the ‘gaseous vacuum boundary conditions’ where the pressure (and the density) is required to vanish at the boundary.

In order to give
\diver\mathsf{T}
the required units of force density, we assume that the constant
\kappa
from the previous remark has the units of
\frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Length}^{2}\cdot\text{Mass Density}^{\frac{4}{3}}}
. We normalize our (general, not-necessarily polytropic)
W
and assume without loss of generality that

(5)

W(I)=\bar{\rho}^{\frac{1}{3}}.

The apparent discrepancy in units between the stress term and the other terms in the equations of motion we will write down later can be resolved by taking into account the implicit units of
\kappa
.

1.3.
The Function
g

We now introduce a function that will be fundamental to the rest of the manuscript. Under our working constitutive assumptions, we will show below in Section
2.2.1

(c.f. also Lemma 6.1 of
[
18
]
) that the function

(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{S}^{2})\ni(y,\omega)\mapsto W\big(I+(y-1)(\omega\otimes\omega)\big)

is independent of
\omega
. (Here
I
denotes the
3\times 3
identity matrix, and
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is the
2
-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.) This motivates us to define
g:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
via

(6)

g(y)=\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{1}{3}}W\big(I+(y-1)(\omega\otimes\omega)\big).

Sideris’s analysis makes extensive use of a related quantity
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, which (under the assumption of degree
-1
homogeneity of
W
) is related to our
g
via
f(y)=y^{\frac{1}{3}}g(y)
. There is of course not much difference between
f
and
g
in terms of regularity, but working with
g
rather than
f
will allow us to write the equations of motion for a spherically symmetric flow map in a particularly compact form and will also simplify our derivation of this system.

The constitutive hypotheses we have stated above will be sufficient to derive the equations of motion for spherically symmetric or
separable solutions. However, in order to actually prove the existence of our family of separable solutions, we will impose one additional constitutive assumption—on
g
rather than on
W
directly (c.f. (
14
) below). Theorem 11.1 of
[
18
]
guarantees that this is mathematically legitimate. More specifically, it states that if
f
is strictly positive and sufficiently smooth, with
f^{\prime}(1)=0
(equivalent to
g^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{1}{3}
), then there exists a strain-energy function
W
that satisfies our other constitutive assumptions and such that (an identity that implies) (
6
) holds.

Remark 1.6
.

The case of a mass-critical polytropic gas corresponds (again, except for the boundary conditions) to the situation where
f(1)\equiv 1
, or equivalently,
g(y)=y^{-\frac{1}{3}}
. (This will become clear from the discussion in Section
2.2.1
.)

The additional assumption (

Remark 1.11

In what follows, our main focus will be Equations (

1.5.
Main Result and Relation to Existing Literature

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1.12
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
1.7

The most closely related results in the literature to our Theorem

[

Sideris
[

]
considers separable dynamics
in the absence of self-gravitation

Whether Sideris’s solutions expand or collapse depends entirely on the sign of the residual pressure; positive residual pressure corresponds to expansion, while negative residual pressure corresponds to finite-time collapse. Adding gravity to the system in our present work provides a mechanism to compete with the positive residual pressure and allows for the possibility of collapsing solutions in a qualitatively new parameter range. The reader familiar with
[
18
]
will notice a great deal of similarity between the framework outlined in Sideris’s paper and the one we use in our work, and our analysis indeed owes a great debt to Sideris’s. Nonetheless, we still feel it is valuable to develop our framework from the ground up, because our use of the function

In
[
3

[
3
]
is set entirely in the Eulerian formulation. As an application of his analysis, Calogero considers homologous dynamics similar to those treated in our paper. Some of his constitutive assumptions are quite different from ours, and the results of
[

do not appear to be directly comparable to those of the present work. However,

w^{\prime}(0)=0,\;\;w(1)=0.\end{cases}

(The boundary conditions (

[

, in which case

drops out. Note also the difference in the boundary conditions between the two systems. It would be interesting to carry out an investigation for self-gravitating hyperelastic matter analogous to what has been done for (
16
) in the context of self-gravitating gas, for instance as in
[
4
]
.

Our analysis below takes a very different approach, which is essentially a perturbative argument: it is based on the fixed point theorem for the integral reformulation of (
12
) (cf. (
34
)–(
36
)) for suitable range of
(\mu,\bar{\rho})
and a continuity argument for matching the boundary condition.
We make no attempt to obtain a
maximal
curve of solutions in
(\mu,\bar{\rho})
parameter space.

As a final note, we mention that in the fluid and gas literature (see for example
[
10
,
15
]
), the admissible separable dynamics include spherically symmetric expansion/collapse as well as more general affine motions. In the context of isolated moving gases, Sideris in
[
17
]
constructed affine solutions to the vacuum free boundary Euler equations representing moving ellipsoids that expand and spread to infinity with a linear rate, and moreover in
[
7
,
16
]
, Sideris’s solutions were shown to be nonlinearly stable.

2.
Derivation of the Equations of Motion for Separable Solutions

2.1.
General Lagrangian Formulation

Given a solution
(\rho,{\bf u})
, we define the flow map
x
as before, namely a function
x:[0,T)\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}(t)
that satisfies the relations
\dot{x}(t,X)={\bf u}(t,x(t,X))
and
\rho(t,x(t,X))\det Dx(t,X)=\bar{\rho}
, for all
t\in[0,T)
and all
X\in\mathcal{B}
. We set the following notation:

\begin{split}\Psi(t,X)&=\Phi[\rho(t)](t,x(t,X)),\\
A(t,X)&=Dx(t,X)^{-1}.\end{split}

We use the notation
a_{,k}
to denote the derivative of a scalar quantity
a(t,\cdot)
with respect to its
k
th argument, excluding time.

Setting
x=x(t,X)
in (
1
)
2
and multiplying by
Jx(t,X)
, we obtain

(18)

\bar{\rho}\ddot{x}^{i}=\mathsf{S}^{i}_{j,j}-G\bar{\rho}A_{i}^{j}\Psi_{,j},\qquad\text{ in }\mathcal{B}.

Here we have used

\mathsf{S}^{i}_{j,j}(t,X)=Jx(t,X)\mathsf{T}^{i}_{j,j}(t,x(t,X)),

which follows from the definition (
4
) of
\mathsf{S}
and the Piola identity

(19)

(A^{j}_{k}Jx)_{,j}=0.

The full system in these Lagrangian coordinates thus becomes

(20)

\begin{cases}\bar{\rho}\ddot{x}^{i}=\mathsf{S}^{i}_{j,j}-G\bar{\rho}A_{i}^{j}\Psi_{,j},&\qquad\text{ in }[0,T)\times\mathcal{B}.\\
A^{k}_{i}(A^{\ell}_{i}\,\Psi_{,\ell})_{,k}=4\pi\bar{\rho}(Jx)^{-1},&\qquad\text{ in }[0,T)\times\mathcal{B},\\
\mathsf{S}(t,X){\bf n}(X)=0,&\qquad X\in\partial\mathcal{B}.\end{cases}

2.2.
Lagrangian Formulation for Spherically Symmetric Solutions

Under the assumption of spherical symmetry (and using our constitutive hypotheses), we can simplify (
20
) substantially.
We write
x(t,0)~=~0
and

(21)

x(t,X)=\phi(t,R)\omega,\qquad R=|X|,\;\;\omega=\frac{X}{R},\qquad X\neq 0.

We also assume (as above) that

\phi(t,0)=\phi_{RR}(t,0)=0,\text{ for }t\geq 0;\qquad\phi_{R}(t,R)>0\text{ for }t\geq 0,\,R\in[0,1],

which guarantees that
x(t,\cdot)
is
C^{2}
even at the origin.

2.2.1.
Deformation Tensor and Strain-Energy Function

A short computation shows that the deformation tensor takes the form

(22)

Dx(t,X)=\Lambda(t,R)\big(I+(y(t,R)-1)(\omega\otimes\omega)\big),

where
\Lambda(t,R)
and
y(t,R)
are defined as in (
7
).
Note that the eigenvalues of
Dx(t,X)
are
\phi_{R}(t,R)
(with 1-dimensional eigenspace
\Span\{\omega\}
) and
\Lambda(t,R)
(with 2-dimensional eigenspace
(\Span\{\omega\})^{\perp}
). Motivated by (
22
), we introduce the notation

F(y,\Lambda,\omega)=\Lambda\big(I+(y-1)(\omega\otimes\omega)\big),\qquad y,\Lambda>0,\;\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Note that if
O\in SO(3,\mathbb{R})
, then clearly

OF(y,\Lambda,\omega)O^{T}=F(y,\Lambda,O\omega).

Therefore, objectivity and isotropy of
W
guarantee that

(23)

W(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))=W(F(y,\Lambda,O\omega)),\qquad O\in SO(3,\mathbb{R}).

It follows that
W(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))
is independent of
\omega=\frac{X}{R}
, so we may define

g(y)=\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{1}{3}}W\big(F(y,1,\omega)\big),\qquad y>0,

as claimed in Section
1.3
.

2.2.2.
The Piola–Kirchoff Stress Tensor

As pointed out by Sideris
[
18
]
, it is well-known (c.f.
[
14
]
, Theorem 4.2.5) that for a hyperelastic, homogeneous material with an objective, isotropic strain-energy function
W
, one has

\mathsf{T}(t,x(t,X))\in\Span\{I,\;(Dx(t,X)Dx(t,X)^{T})^{\frac{1}{2}},\;Dx(t,X)Dx(t,X)^{T}\}.

Under our assumptions of spherical symmetry, together with the relationship between
\mathsf{S}
and
\mathsf{T}
, it follows that

\widehat{\mathsf{S}}(Dx(t,X))\in\Span\{P(\omega),Q(\omega)\},

where
P(\omega)=\omega\otimes\omega
and
Q(\omega)=I-P(\omega)
. We may therefore write

(24)

\widehat{\mathsf{S}}(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))=c_{1}(y,\Lambda,\omega)P(\omega)+c_{2}(y,\Lambda,\omega)Q(\omega),

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are determined as follows. Noting that
P^{i}_{j}P^{i}_{j}=1
,
P^{i}_{j}Q^{i}_{j}=0
, and
Q^{i}_{j}Q^{i}_{j}=2
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{\frac{4}{3}}\Lambda^{-1}{g}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}{W}(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))={\mathsf{S}}^{i}_{j}\Lambda P^{i}_{j}=\Lambda c_{1},

\displaystyle-\bar{\rho}^{\frac{4}{3}}\Lambda^{-2}{g}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}{W}(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))={\mathsf{S}}^{i}_{j}(yP^{i}_{j}+Q^{i}_{j})=yc_{1}+2c_{2}.

Thus

(25)

c_{1}(y,\Lambda,\omega)=\bar{\rho}^{\frac{4}{3}}\Lambda^{-2}{g}^{\prime}(y),\qquad-2c_{2}(y,\Lambda,\omega)=\bar{\rho}^{\frac{4}{3}}\Lambda^{-2}\big(y{g}^{\prime}(y)+{g}(y)\big).

Notice that both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are independent of
\omega
. We record the formula for
\widehat{\mathsf{S}}
for further use below:

(26)

\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{4}{3}}\widehat{\mathsf{S}}(F(y,\Lambda,\omega))=\Lambda^{-2}{g}^{\prime}(y)P(\omega)-\frac{1}{2}\Lambda^{-2}\big(y{g}^{\prime}(y)+{g}(y)\big)Q(\omega).

2.2.3.
Divergence of the Piola–Kirchhoff Stress

We now substitute
y={y}(t,R)
,
\Lambda={\Lambda}(t,R)
into (
24
) and take the divergence (in
X
), recalling that
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are independent of
\omega
. We obtain

\displaystyle\omega_{i}\mathsf{S}^{i}_{j,j}(t,X)

\displaystyle=\partial_{R}(c_{1}({y},{\Lambda}))+\frac{2}{R}(c_{1}({y},{\Lambda})-c_{2}({y},{\Lambda}))=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\partial_{R}\big(R^{2}c_{1}({y},{\Lambda})\big)-\frac{1}{R}\cdot 2c_{2}({y},{\Lambda}).

Substituting in the formulas for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{4}{3}}\omega_{i}\mathsf{S}^{i}_{j,j}(t,X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R^{2}}\partial_{R}\bigg(\frac{R^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}{g}^{\prime}({y})\bigg)+\frac{1}{R\Lambda^{2}}({y}{g}^{\prime}({y})+{g}({y}))=\frac{1}{R^{3}\Lambda}\partial_{R}\bigg(\frac{R^{3}}{\Lambda}{g}^{\prime}({y})\bigg)+\frac{1}{R\Lambda^{2}}{g}({y}).

Finally, substituting
\Lambda(t,R)=\frac{\phi(t,R)}{R}
, we obtain the following expression (with the understanding that
\phi(R)/R
should be replaced by
\phi^{\prime}(0)
when

(27)

\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{4}{3}}\omega_{i}{\mathsf{S}}^{i}_{j,j}(t,X)=\frac{1}{R^{2}\phi}\partial_{R}\bigg(\frac{R^{4}}{\phi}{g}^{\prime}({y})\bigg)+\frac{R}{\phi^{2}}{g}({y})

2.2.4.
The Potential Term

Following (for example)
[

(28)

\omega^{i}A^{i}_{j}\Psi_{,j}=\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}\int_{0}^{R}4\pi\bar{\rho}s^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{4\pi}{3}R^{3}\bar{\rho}\phi^{-2}.

2.2.5.

which reduces to (

2.2.6.

We now work with a separable solution and write

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.12

The rest of the paper is dedicated to the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.12
. We provide a brief outline of our proof: In Section
3.1
, we write the eigenvalue problem (
12
)
1
as a fixed point problem of a certain operator, parametrized by the putative eigenvalue
\mu
and reference density
\bar{\rho}
. In Section
3.2
, we restrict the domain of
g
to guarantee that
g^{\prime\prime}(y)>0
(so that the use of (
12
) is justified) and provide some preliminary estimates. In Section
3.3
, we give conditions under which the operator introduced in Section
3.1
is a contraction mapping and thus has a unique fixed point. In Section
3.4
, we fix
\mu
and show that we can tune
\bar{\rho}
to obtain a fixed point of our operator that
also satisfies the boundary conditions
; the latter are not fully taken into account in the previous steps.

3.1.
Fixed Point Problem

Our starting point is Equation (
12
), which we rewrite as

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\bigg(\varphi_{RR}+\frac{2}{R}\big(\varphi_{R}-\frac{\varphi}{R}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\big(\varphi_{R}-\frac{\varphi}{R}\big)U(y)+\frac{V(\lambda)}{g^{\prime\prime}(y)},

where

(32)

U(y)=2(y-1)+\frac{E(y)}{g^{\prime\prime}(y)},\qquad V(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\rho}^{\frac{1}{3}}}\bigg(\frac{4\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}+\mu\lambda^{3}\bigg).

Note that
U(1)=0
, and that
U
and
V
are differentiable. We will provide estimates on their derivatives later on. First, we give one last reformulation of (
31
).

In order to enforce
\varphi(0)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
, we make the ansatz
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(R)=R\zeta(R)
, where
\zeta\in C([0,1])
. By a short computation, we thus have

(33)

\varphi(R)=R\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{R}(R-\tau)\tau\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

The normalization
\varphi(1)=1
forces

\varphi^{\prime}(0)=1-\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)\tau\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

However, we will continue to write
\varphi^{\prime}(0)
for brevity. We also have

\varphi(R)\equiv R\qquad\text{ when }\qquad\zeta\equiv 0.

The following are also useful:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(R)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{R}\tau\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(R)-\lambda(R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{2}\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\bigg(\varphi_{RR}+\frac{2}{R}\big(\varphi_{R}-\lambda\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\zeta(R)+\frac{2}{R^{3}}\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{2}\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

We may thus rewrite our entire system as

(34)

L\zeta=\mathcal{F}[\zeta],

where

(35)

L\zeta(R)=\zeta(R)+\frac{2}{R^{3}}\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{2}\zeta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\zeta](R)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\big(\varphi_{R}-\lambda\big)U(y)+\frac{V(\lambda)}{g^{\prime\prime}(y)}.

Note that
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
(and thus
\lambda
and
y
) are determined by
\zeta
, so the right side of (
36
) is determined by
\zeta
as well. Note furthermore that
L:C([0,1])\to C([0,1])
is invertible, and we can find its inverse explicitly. (Write
L\zeta=\eta
, multiply by
R^{3}
, differentiate, and solve for
\zeta
.) The formula is:

L^{-1}\eta(R)=\eta(R)-\frac{2}{R^{5}}\int_{0}^{R}\tau^{4}\eta(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Our task of finding a solution
\varphi
of (
10
)
1
is now reduced to that of finding a fixed point
\zeta
of
\zeta\mapsto L^{-1}\mathcal{F}[\zeta]
. For use below, we record that

(37)

L^{-1}[1]=\frac{3}{5},\qquad\|L^{-1}\|_{op}=\frac{7}{5}.

(It is clear that
\|L^{-1}\|_{op}\leq\frac{7}{5}
; to see the opposite inequality, consider the sequence of functions defined by
\eta_{n}(R)=-1+2R^{n}
.)

3.2.
Restricted Domain of
g

The equations (
34
), (
35
), (
36
) constitute a valid reformulation of (
10
)
1
if we can guarantee that
g^{\prime\prime}(y(R))>0
for all
R\in[0,1]
. We now restrict the domain of
g
to an interval
I(\delta)
on which
g^{\prime\prime}
is positive, justifying later that
y(R)
actually belongs to this interval for the solutions we consider. Under the assumption (
14
), we define

(38)

\delta=\frac{10}{g^{\prime\prime}(1)}.

Then

(39)

\frac{9}{10}g^{\prime\prime}(1)\leq g^{\prime\prime}(y)\leq\frac{11}{10}g^{\prime\prime}(1)\qquad\text{ whenever }\qquad|y-1|\leq\delta.

We will work on the fixed interval
I(\delta)=[1-\delta,1+\delta]
. It is not difficult to obtain the following estimates. (Here
M
is as defined in (
15
).)

\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}\leq 7\big(\|g^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}+g^{\prime\prime}(1)\big),\qquad\|E^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}\leq 2\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}.

(40)

\|U^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}\leq 2+\frac{2\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}}{\frac{9}{10}g^{\prime\prime}(1)}+\frac{2\|h^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}|y-1|M}{\frac{9}{10}g^{\prime\prime}(1)}\leq 22(1+M^{2}).

Note that we also have

(41)

|V(\lambda)|\leq(1+|\lambda-1|)^{4}K(\bar{\rho},\mu),\qquad|V^{\prime}(\lambda)|\leq 4(1+|\lambda-1|)^{3}K(\bar{\rho},\mu),

where

(42)

K(\bar{\rho},\mu):=\bar{\rho}^{-\frac{1}{3}}\big(\frac{4\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}+|\mu|\big).

3.3.
The Contraction Mapping

We show that, under additional assumptions on
\bar{\rho}
and
\mu
, the operator
L^{-1}\mathcal{F}
is a contraction on
N(\delta)
, where

N(\delta):=\{\zeta\in C([0,1]):\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\leq\delta\},

and where
\delta>0
is defined as in (

).

3.3.1.
Basic Estimates

Given
\zeta\in N(\delta)
, let
\varphi
be defined via (
33
), and let
\lambda
,
y
be defined as in (
9
). Let
\widetilde{\zeta}
be another element of
N(\delta)
and define
\widetilde{\varphi}
,
\widetilde{\lambda}
,
\widetilde{y}
in the analogous way. Then after a few short computations, we may estimate (for

):

(43)

|\varphi_{R}(R)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{R}(R)|\leq\frac{R^{2}}{2}\|\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta}\|_{\infty},\qquad|\lambda(R)-\widetilde{\lambda}(R)|\leq\frac{R^{2}}{6}\|\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta}\|_{\infty},\qquad\zeta,\widetilde{\zeta}\in N(\delta).

(44)

|y(R)-\widetilde{y}(R)|\leq R^{2}\|\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta}\|_{\infty},\qquad\zeta,\widetilde{\zeta}\in N(\delta).

Of course, if
\widetilde{\zeta}\equiv 0
, these reduce to

(45)

|\varphi_{R}(R)-1|\leq\frac{R^{2}\delta}{2},\qquad|\lambda(R)-1|\leq\frac{R^{2}\delta}{6},\qquad|y(R)-1|\leq R^{2}\delta,\qquad\qquad\qquad\zeta\in N(\delta).

The last estimate on the right confirms that

whenever

.

3.3.2.
Range of
\mu

In order to obtain proper estimates on
\mathcal{F}
, we restrict the range of
\bar{\rho}
and
\mu
.

First, fix
\bar{\rho}_{+}>0
satisfying
\frac{4\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}_{+}^{\frac{2}{3}}=10
. In other words, put

(46)

\bar{\rho}_{+}=\bigg(\frac{10}{\frac{4\pi}{3}G}\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Next, note that for fixed
\mu
, the function
\bar{\varrho}\mapsto K(\bar{\varrho},\mu)
is minimized when
\bar{\varrho}=\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu)
, where

(47)

\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu):=\frac{|\mu|}{\frac{8\pi}{3}G}.

(Note: When
\mu=0
, (
47
) implies that
\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu)=0
. We stress that
\bar{\varrho}=0
is a valid input for the function
K(\cdot,\mu)
, even as we continue to insist that the parameter
\bar{\rho}
that appears in our Lagrangian formulation is always strictly positive.) Furthermore, the minimum value
K(\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu),\mu)
is equal to some absolute constant times
|\mu|^{\frac{2}{3}}G^{\frac{1}{3}}
.

With the above in mind, we may pick
\mu_{0}>0
small enough so that both the following conditions are satisfied:

•

First, we require that
\mu_{0}<\min\{\frac{1}{2}\bar{\rho}_{+}^{\frac{1}{3}},\frac{8\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}_{+}\}
. This has the following three implications:

–

The fact that
\mu_{0}<\frac{8\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}_{+}
implies that

(48)

\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu)<\bar{\rho}_{+},\qquad\qquad\text{ whenever }|\mu|\leq\mu_{0}.

Thus
\bar{\rho}\mapsto K(\bar{\rho},\mu)
is increasing in
\bar{\rho}
for
\bar{\rho}\in[\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu),\bar{\rho}_{+}]
.

–

The fact that
\mu_{0}<\frac{1}{2}\bar{\rho}_{+}^{\frac{1}{3}}
implies that

(49)

0\leq K(\bar{\rho},\mu)\leq K(\bar{\rho}_{+},\mu)<\frac{21}{2}\qquad\text{ whenever }|\mu|\leq\mu_{0}\text{ and }\bar{\rho}\in[\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu),\bar{\rho}_{+}].

The fact that
\mu_{0}<\frac{1}{2}\bar{\rho}_{+}^{\frac{1}{3}}
also implies that

(50)

\epsilon(\bar{\rho}_{+},\mu):=\frac{4\pi}{3}G\bar{\rho}_{+}^{\frac{2}{3}}+\mu\bar{\rho}_{+}^{-\frac{1}{3}}>\frac{19}{2},\qquad\text{ whenever }|\mu|\leq\mu_{0}.

The first two of these implications will be put to almost immediate use; the utility of (
50
) will become apparent when we discuss boundary conditions.

•

Second, we require that

(51)

K(\bar{\rho}_{-}(\mu),\mu)<\frac{1}{20},\qquad\text{ whenever }|\mu|\leq\mu_{0}.

This requirement will also be used only when we discuss boundary conditions.

Remark 3.1
.

The reader may observe that it is possible to expand the range of
\mu
beyond the interval
[-\mu_{0},\mu_{0}]
that we cover. For example, more precise bounds on
V
and
V^{\prime}
than (
41
) are available for negative values of
\mu
, and by using such bounds and modifying the argument below, existence of separable solutions can be established for certain values of
\mu
outside
[-\mu_{0},\mu_{0}]
. However, finding an optimal range of
\mu
seems to be out of reach for our argument, even if we sharpen our estimates, and we therefore prioritize making our argument as simple as possible rather than introducing additional technicalities to squeeze out a marginally more general result.

3.3.3.
Estimates on
\mathcal{F}

For distinct
\zeta,\widetilde{\zeta}\in N(\delta)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{F}[\zeta](R)-\mathcal{F}[\widetilde{\zeta}](R)|}{\|\zeta-\widetilde{\zeta}\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\bigg[\frac{R^{2}}{3}\|U\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}+\frac{R^{2}\delta}{3}\|U^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I(\delta))}\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\bigg[\frac{4\|g^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}}{g^{\prime\prime}(1)^{2}}|V(\lambda)|+\frac{1}{3g^{\prime\prime}(1)}\sup_{|\lambda-1|<\frac{\delta}{6}}|V^{\prime}(\lambda)|\bigg]

|\mu|\leq\mu_{0}
