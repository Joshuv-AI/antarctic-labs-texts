Nurse-led behavioural support for lifestyle self-management in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus: an integrative review
Authors: Mingyang Han, YanLing Pei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) requires sustained self-management across diet, physical activity, body weight, glucose monitoring, and medication-taking behaviours. Although lifestyle recommendations are central to diabetes care, patients often need professional support to translate advice into feasible, culturally acceptable, and safe daily routines.AimTo synthesize evidence on lifestyle interventions and nurse-led behavioural support for adults with T2DM, and to clarify implications for nursing self-management support and service delivery.MethodsAn integrative review with narrative synthesis was conducted. We searched Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, WanFang Data, VIP, PubMed, and CINAHL for literature published between 2006 and 2026. To strengthen currency and nursing relevance, we added high-relevance, peer-reviewed studies on nurse-led education, behavioural coaching, medication adherence, technology-enabled support, and self-management. Twenty articles were included, comprising randomized and quasi-experimental studies, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, narrative reviews, position statements, and analytic studies. Data were summarized using inductive content analysis and appraised narratively for methodological clarity, relevance, and nursing implications; no design-specific formal risk-of-bias assessment, GRADE assessment, or quantitative synthesis was undertaken.ResultsFour interrelated themes were identified: physical activity and dietary habits; weight management and obesity control; structured patient education, behavioural coaching and empowerment; and medication-related support, glucose-monitoring guidance and technology-enabled follow-up. Lifestyle interventions provided the behavioural foundation for T2DM management. Nurse-led support may strengthen implementation by helping patients set realistic goals, interpret glucose data, address medication concerns, adapt recommendations to cultural and family routines, and maintain follow-up contact.ConclusionThis integrative synthesis suggests that structured lifestyle interventions and nurse-led behavioural support may be useful complementary components of T2DM care. Nurses may help translate lifestyle recommendations into feasible self-management routines, but the review does not establish comparative effectiveness or superiority over other delivery models. Findings should therefore inform practice-oriented nursing support rather than be interpreted as a graded clinical recommendation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05304-5.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major non-communicable disease and an important contributor to premature mortality, disability, complications, and healthcare expenditure worldwide. Its management requires continuous care and sustained self-management rather than isolated clinical encounters [1, 2].

Global diabetes prevalence continues to rise, highlighting the need for scalable, nurse-delivered self-management support [3].

Patients with T2DM are expected to make daily decisions about diet, physical activity, weight control, glucose monitoring, and medication use. These behaviors are shaped by family routines, financial resources, cultural food practices, comorbidities, health literacy, and motivation. As a result, lifestyle advice that appears simple in clinical guidance may be difficult to maintain in real life [4, 5].

Lifestyle interventions are widely recognized as a foundation of T2DM prevention and management. Physical activity can improve insulin sensitivity and cardiometabolic health, dietary adjustment can support glycaemic control and weight management, and gradual weight reduction can reduce risk among overweight or obese adults [6–8]. However, information alone is rarely sufficient to sustain behaviour change.

Nurses are well positioned to support self-management because they often provide repeated contact across community, outpatient, primary care, and inpatient settings. Their contribution is not limited to delivering information. Nurses assess barriers, translate medical recommendations into achievable goals, reinforce medication safety, interpret monitoring data with patients, and identify problems requiring escalation [9–12].

Previous reviews have examined lifestyle interventions, diabetes self-management education, medication adherence, digital diabetes care, or nurse-led programmes separately [13–16]. The rationale for another integrative review is not that these earlier reviews are absent or inadequate, nor that this review is the first to study nurse-led diabetes support. Its specific contribution is a nursing-oriented implementation map: it brings together behavior domains, intervention intensity, follow-up modalities, theoretical lenses, and nursing actions, while explicitly distinguishing primary intervention evidence from synthesis-level, mechanistic, and guidance evidence. This focus is intended to clarify how lifestyle recommendations may be operationalized in routine care and where the evidence remains uncertain. Therefore, this review focuses on how nurse-led behavioural support may connect lifestyle advice and daily self-management in adults with T2DM.

Aim

The aim of this review was to synthesize evidence on lifestyle interventions and nurse-led behavioural support for adults with T2DM, with particular attention to implications for nursing practice and service delivery.

Review question

The review question was: In adults with T2DM, how do lifestyle interventions and nurse-led behavioural support contribute to daily self-management, and what practical implications do these contributions have for nursing care delivery? The question was intentionally framed around processes and implementation, rather than comparative effectiveness alone, because the expected evidence included heterogeneous intervention and synthesis designs.

This review treats lifestyle self-management as a behavioural translation process. The emphasis is not only on whether diet, physical activity, or weight management is beneficial, but also on how nurses can operationalize these recommendations through assessment, goal-setting, monitoring interpretation, medication safety support, follow-up, and escalation pathways.

Behavioural translation is situated within a wider social and health-system context. Food access, income, transport, digital access, reimbursement, service organization, family support, and multidisciplinary coordination can enable or constrain what a patient and nurse can achieve. Nursing is therefore treated as one mechanism within a complex care system rather than as the sole determinant of self-management [17, 18].

Methods

Design

This study was conducted as an integrative review with narrative synthesis.

An integrative review is a structured evidence-synthesis method that allows empirical and non-empirical literature from diverse methodological designs to be combined to develop a broader understanding of a clinical or practice phenomenon [19, 20]. In this review, integration means comparing the role and relevance of each evidence type; it does not mean treating a randomized trial, a systematic review, a position statement, and an analytic study as equivalent evidence.

Narrative synthesis is a transparent and structured method for bringing together findings through textual description, tabulation, thematic organization, and interpretation of patterns, relationships, differences, and evidence limitations when statistical pooling is inappropriate [21].

Integrative review with narrative synthesis was selected because the review question concerns how different lifestyle and nursing-support components are translated into daily self-management, not only whether one standardized intervention changes one outcome. The included evidence was heterogeneous in design, population, intervention dose, follow-up, and outcome measurement. A conventional narrative review would provide less explicit searching, eligibility, extraction, and appraisal; a systematic review or meta-analysis would be inappropriate for pooling non-comparable outcomes and would narrow the question toward a single intervention-effect estimate; and a scoping review would map the field but provide less interpretive comparison of mechanisms and nursing actions. The chosen design therefore provides breadth while preserving transparent, practice-oriented interpretation [19–21].

The review was positioned as an integrative review rather than a full systematic review because the evidence base is heterogeneous and because the updated evidence expansion used a targeted supplementary search rather than a fully reproducible multi-database systematic search. This transparent positioning avoids overclaiming and supports a practice-oriented synthesis for nursing readers. The synthesis therefore focuses on patterns, mechanisms, and implications for nursing care delivery rather than on pooled estimates of effectiveness.

Conceptual definitions

The principal concept of this review is nurse-led behavioural support for lifestyle self-management in adults with T2DM. To avoid ambiguity, the terms below were operationalized before screening and synthesis. Lifestyle advice refers to general information or recommendations; a lifestyle intervention refers to a structured programme with planned content, behavioural techniques, and follow-up; lifestyle self-management refers to the patient’s ongoing daily actions; and nurse-led behavioural support refers to nursing activities that help patients translate recommendations into safe, feasible, and maintainable routines. The review uses “nurse-led behavioural support” consistently as its main nursing concept. Table 1 provides the definitions and objective examples used in the review.

Theoretical framework

The Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW), including its COM-B model, was used as a sensitizing framework after the initial inductive coding. Capability refers to the knowledge and skills needed to act; opportunity refers to physical and social conditions; and motivation refers to reflective and automatic processes that energize behaviour [22]. The framework was not used to force codes before reading. After the first coding cycle, it helped interpret how structured education may strengthen capability, how family and digital follow-up may create opportunities, and how goal-setting, feedback, and empowerment may support motivation. This provides a theoretical contribution while preserving the inductive and narrative character of the synthesis.

Protocol registration

No protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO or another public registry before this review. The review was initiated as a single-author, practice-oriented integrative synthesis, and retrospective registration was not attempted. This status is now reported explicitly as a transparency limitation.

Conceptual definitions and operational examples used in this review

Search strategy

A search strategy is a planned and reproducible combination of controlled vocabulary, free-text terms, database fields, Boolean operators, and limits used to identify records relevant to a review question [33]. We searched Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, WanFang Data, VIP, PubMed, and CINAHL for English-language, peer-reviewed literature published from 1 January 2006 to 31 July 2026. Database-specific yields are reported in Sect. 4.1, and the complete database-specific search strategies are provided in the supplementary search-strategy file. The exact calendar day of execution and the record-level screening log were not retained, and are acknowledged as limitations.

The three concept blocks in Table 2 were selected from the review question: population terms identify adults with T2DM; self-management terms capture the behaviours and outcomes of interest; and nursing-support terms capture professional mechanisms that translate recommendations into action. Synonyms were combined with OR within each block and the blocks with AND to balance sensitivity and relevance [33]. The reconstructed core Boolean structure was: (“type 2 diabetes” OR “type 2 diabetes mellitus” OR T2DM) AND (“lifestyle intervention” OR diet OR exercise OR “physical activity” OR “weight management” OR obesity OR “self-management”) AND (“nursing intervention” OR “nurse-led” OR “patient education” OR “medication adherence” OR “glucose monitoring” OR telehealth OR digital OR coaching).

Table 2 should be read as the conceptual map for the search, while the following reconstructed syntax makes the main limitations explicit. For PubMed, the population block was paired with MeSH/free-text terms for lifestyle and nursing support and limited to English publications dated 2006–2026.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria in Table 3 were derived from the review question and the conceptual definitions in Table 1. They were selected to retain evidence that could inform adult T2DM lifestyle self-management or the nursing mechanisms supporting it, while excluding evidence that could not be interpreted within this clinical and professional scope.

Type 1 diabetes was excluded because its classification, insulin deficiency, treatment requirements, and safety priorities differ materially from T2DM. Mixing the two conditions would introduce clinical heterogeneity and could make a lifestyle or nursing-support finding appear applicable when the underlying self-management tasks and medication risks are different [2, 25]. Articles with mixed diabetes populations were retained only when T2DM findings were separately identifiable or directly relevant to the review question.

Because the eligibility criteria permitted heterogeneous evidence, each source was assigned an evidence role before synthesis: primary intervention evidence; secondary synthesis evidence; contextual, epidemiological, or mechanistic evidence; or guidance/position evidence. Secondary reviews were used to identify cross-study patterns and contextualize findings, not as independent replications of every primary study they contained. Primary studies were used for direct intervention claims when their design and outcomes supported that interpretation. The review did not mathematically pool results, treat the number of articles mentioning a theme as an effect estimate, or add duplicate effect counts from reviews. Nevertheless, because the historical extraction record did not always permit complete study-level de-duplication across secondary sources, residual double-counting cannot be completely excluded.

The resulting evidence roles should be read together with Table 5; Fig. 2. This distinction limits amplification of expert opinion or review-level conclusions, but it does not convert the narrative synthesis into a formal certainty assessment.

Eligibility criteria

Study selection

The eligibility criteria in Table 3 were applied during title/abstract and full-text screening. Screening was conducted by one author using a structured screening process based on the predefined eligibility criteria. Records identified as potentially eligible after the initial screening were assessed in full text. The study-selection flow and the numbers of records at each stage are presented in Additional file 2.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted into a structured matrix containing author/year, country, design/type, intervention or phenomenon, population/context, sample or setting when reported, intervention duration or follow-up, behaviour targeted, nursing action, primary outcome or finding, limitations, and relevance to the review question. Inductive content analysis was selected because the included evidence used different designs and vocabularies, and the review sought to discover recurring concepts without imposing a single intervention taxonomy in advance [34]. This approach is consistent with the preparation, open coding, grouping, categorization, and abstraction stages described by Elo and Kyngäs [34]. The synthesis was also reported narratively because the evidence was heterogeneous and not suitable for statistical pooling [21].

First, the author repeatedly read each included article and recorded meaning units relevant to lifestyle self-management or nursing support. Example: the description of Yu et al. [35] that nurse-led structured education improved self-management behaviours and self-efficacy was recorded as a meaning unit rather than immediately labelled as an outcome of all nurse-led care.

All screening, data extraction, quality appraisal, and initial coding were conducted by one author. To improve credibility in the absence of independent dual review, the author used a structured extraction matrix, retained article-to-theme traceability, recorded supporting and contrasting evidence, applied the same Hawker domains to all included articles, and separated recurring patterns from causal claims. These safeguards reduce but do not remove single-reviewer selection and interpretation bias.

Second, meaning units were condensed and coded using short, close-to-the-data labels. The Yu et al. example generated “structured nurse education”, “repeated explanation”, “self-management behaviour” and “self-efficacy”. A medication-related example from Farmer et al. [13] generated “adherence check,” “medication concern” and “consultation-based support.”

Third, codes with a shared meaning were grouped into subthemes. “Structured nurse education,” “repeated explanation,” “goal negotiation” and “self-efficacy” were grouped as “structured education and empowerment”; “adherence check,” “side-effect recognition,” “glucose-pattern interpretation” and “referral” were grouped as “medication and monitoring safety support.”

Fourth, related subthemes were abstracted into broader themes. Walking, aerobic activity, dietary modification, and self-monitoring were grouped as “physical activity and dietary habits”; goal-setting, education, coaching, and empowerment were grouped as “structured patient education, behavioural coaching and empowerment”; and adherence, medication safety, monitoring interpretation, and digital contact were grouped as “medication-related support, glucose-monitoring guidance and technology-enabled follow-up”.

Fifth, the author compared the draft themes against every included article, recorded supporting and contrasting evidence, and retained a traceable link from each theme to the relevant article(s). For example, the “structured education and empowerment” subtheme was checked against the RCTs, quasi-experimental studies, and reviews rather than being inferred from a single study. Themes were revised when a code did not fit the evidence or when two themes substantially overlapped.

Finally, the themes were written as a narrative synthesis that distinguishes recurring patterns from causal claims. The resulting synthesis links each theme to a nursing action and a practice implication; it does not treat the frequency of a concept as evidence of effectiveness. Table 6 reports the final theme-to-action mapping, and Fig. 1 shows the proposed mechanism by which recommendations are translated into self-management.

Quality appraisal

Study quality and relevance were appraised narratively using the structured critical appraisal approach of Hawker et al. [36]. The appraisal considered nine domains: title/abstract clarity; introduction and aims; method and data; sampling; data analysis; ethics and bias; results; transferability or generalizability; and implications or usefulness. Each domain was considered using the Hawker four-point descriptors, but the review did not sum the descriptors into a single exclusion threshold.

Step 1—design fit: the design was compared with the question being asked. For example, a randomized trial such as Yu et al. [35] was relevant for whether a structured nurse-led programme changed self-management outcomes, whereas a position statement such as Colberg et al. [10] was relevant for the clinical rationale for exercise but was not treated as trial evidence.

Step 2—methodological clarity: the author recorded whether aims, participants, intervention components, comparison, follow-up, and outcomes were described sufficiently to interpret the finding. A study with clear intervention content but a single-site sample could be considered practically relevant while having limited transferability.

Step 3—Risk and reporting: sampling, missing data, analytic approach, ethics, and possible bias were noted. For example, a self-report education study may provide useful information about patient-reported confidence but may be vulnerable to response and performance bias.

Step 4—Confidence and relevance statement: each article was assigned a narrative statement such as “higher confidence for the described outcome, moderate transferability” or “high practice relevance, lower empirical confidence.” This explains the meaning of the appraisal for readers without implying that a numerical score is a universal measure across RCTs, reviews, guidance, and analytic studies.

Step 5—use in synthesis: evidence was used in proportion to its design and confidence. Mixed designs were not automatically discarded because they address different parts of the review question: trials inform observed changes, reviews identify patterns across studies, guidance informs clinical safety, and position statements clarify practice rationale. Thus, “the appraisal described confidence and relevance rather than excluding studies solely by score” means that weaker or less transferable evidence could still illuminate a mechanism or implementation issue, but it was not used to make a strong causal claim. The distribution of evidence types is shown in Fig. 2. Article-level appraisal summaries and evidence-use judgments are reported in Table 5.

The narrative appraisal should not be interpreted as a formal design-specific risk-of-bias or certainty rating. RoB 2 is an example of a tool designed for randomized trials, while GRADE provides a structured approach to certainty and recommendation strength; neither was applied here because the evidence set also contained reviews, position statements, narrative reviews, and analytic studies [37, 38]. A future systematic review should use design-appropriate risk-of-bias tools and a formal certainty framework. In the present review, Hawker-based judgments are used to make the synthesis more cautious and traceable, not to claim that the evidence has been formally graded.

Results

Characteristics of included articles

The database searches identified a total of 220,621 records, including 30,025 from PubMed, 65,901 from Embase, 16,102 from CINAHL, 11,508 from the Cochrane Library, 51,189 from Scopus, 19,251 from Web of Science, 16,763 from Wanfang Data, and 9,882 from VIP. Before screening, 70,034 duplicate records and 134,982 records identified as ineligible using automated screening procedures were removed, leaving 15,605 records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 8,918 records were excluded, including 3,788 that were unrelated to the review question and 5,130 conference abstracts. The full texts of 6,687 reports were subsequently assessed for eligibility, of which 6,667 were excluded because of insufficient data (n = 1,314), an inappropriate study population (n = 2,687), or the absence of an appropriate control group (n = 2,666). Ultimately, 20 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review. The evidence base included randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, meta-reviews, position statements, narrative reviews, and one analytic causal study. The literature provided evidence from North America, Europe, Asia, the Middle East, and Australia. The characteristics of the 20 included articles are presented in Table 4, which should be read alongside Table 5; Fig. 2 when interpreting the balance between intervention studies and evidence syntheses. They were retained because they established the behavioral and epidemiological rationale for lifestyle self-management and early intervention mechanisms. More contemporary studies from 2006 to 2026 were given greater interpretive weight for current nurse-led education, coaching, empowerment, and technology-enabled practice. The age range nevertheless means that some technologies, service models, and standards of care may not reflect current practice. Compared with the original manuscript, the expanded evidence base increased the representation of nurse-led education, nurse coaching, empowerment, medication adherence, and technology-enabled nursing support.

Study-level details, Hawker appraisal, and use in synthesis

Physical activity and dietary habits

Physical activity and dietary modification were the most frequently described lifestyle strategies. Walking, aerobic exercise, and increased daily movement were commonly recommended because they are practical and can be incorporated into daily routines. Across the included literature, regular activity was linked with improved glucose control, reduced cardiometabolic risk, and better self-management behaviour. Exercise recommendations appeared most useful when they were realistic, progressive, and adapted to the patient’s age, weight, comorbidities, and confidence level. The relationship between recommendations, nurse-led assessment, goal-setting, and daily self-management is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Dietary intervention was usually presented together with physical activity rather than as an isolated strategy. Common dietary messages included reducing excessive sugar, saturated fat, and calorie intake while increasing vegetables, fibre-rich foods, and healthier carbohydrate sources. The evidence did not support one single ideal diet for all patients. Instead, dietary change appeared more feasible when advice was culturally acceptable, affordable, and compatible with family eating routines.

Self-monitoring of blood glucose was identified as a supportive strategy within lifestyle management. Its value depended on whether patients understood the meaning of glucose readings and knew how to respond. Monitoring without explanation may increase confusion or anxiety, whereas monitoring combined with nurse-led guidance can help patients recognize how food, activity, and medication affect glucose control.

Weight management and obesity control

Weight management was a central theme because obesity and excess body weight were repeatedly described as modifiable factors associated with T2DM risk and poorer glycaemic control. Interventions targeting weight usually combined dietary adjustment, physical activity, self-monitoring, and goal-setting. The included literature supported gradual and sustainable weight reduction rather than rapid or restrictive dieting.

Concrete goals appeared more useful than vague advice. Examples include setting weekly walking targets, reducing sugary drinks, controlling portion size, monitoring body weight, and aiming for modest weight reduction over a defined period. Nurses can support this process by reviewing weight change, discussing barriers, reinforcing progress, and helping patients adjust goals when life circumstances change.

Structured patient education, behavioural coaching, and empowerment

Structured patient education was the most prominent nursing intervention. Education usually covered basic understanding of T2DM, diet, physical activity, medication use, glucose monitoring, warning signs, and prevention of complications. Group education can efficiently deliver standard information, whereas individual counselling allows nurses to address barriers such as poor adherence, low confidence, limited health literacy, fear of side effects, or difficulty interpreting glucose readings. The components and nursing implications of this theme are summarized in Table 6.

The expanded evidence base strengthened the nursing relevance of this theme. Nurse-led structured education, holistic nurse coaching, theory-based goal-attainment interventions, and empowerment-focused programmes all emphasized that education works best when it is interactive, repeated, culturally adapted, and linked to the patient’s own priorities. These studies suggest that nurses may function as behavioural translators who help patients convert general recommendations into daily routines, although the evidence does not establish that this role is superior to other delivery models.

Education should therefore be regarded as an ongoing process rather than a single teaching event. Repeated contact allows nurses to check understanding, identify medication concerns, reinforce behaviour change, and adjust goals. Remote follow-up, telephone contact, or digital communication may also support continuity, but these tools should be integrated into clinical workflows so that they do not increase workload without clear benefit.

Medication-related support, glucose-monitoring guidance, and technology-enabled follow-up

Medication-related support was another important nursing activity. The nursing role in the reviewed evidence was not independent prescribing. Instead, nurses supported safe use by explaining medication purpose, reinforcing correct administration, identifying side effects, and encouraging patients to report problems rather than changing treatment on their own. These actions form part of the nurse-led behavioural-support pathway summarized in Table 6; Fig. 1.

Medication adherence was closely connected to self-management. Some patients may stop or reduce medication when symptoms improve, when adverse effects occur, or when they do not understand the need for long-term treatment. Nurses can identify these problems during follow-up and escalate concerns to physicians when treatment adjustment is needed.

Technology-enabled nursing support emerged more clearly after the evidence base was expanded. Digital tools, mobile applications, telehealth, reminders, and remote monitoring may extend nursing contact beyond the clinic. However, digital tools are most useful when nurses use them to provide interpretation, feedback, behavioural reinforcement, and safety escalation rather than treating technology as a substitute for nursing support.

Across the evidence, intervention setting and intensity appeared to shape what nurses could contribute. A single consultation in primary care addressed a specific adherence problem, whereas multi-session education, coaching, or goal-attainment programmes created repeated opportunities for assessment, feedback, and reinforcement [13, 35, 40, 47]. Digital and telehealth approaches extended contact but varied in platform, human interpretation, and escalation arrangements [1, 43]. The populations also differed, including people with newly diagnosed T2DM, adults with obesity, and broader adult T2DM samples. Where theory was explicit, King’s theory of goal attainment and self-efficacy-informed education provided different explanations for how nurse–patient interaction, goal negotiation, and confidence may support behaviour [35, 47, 48]. These contrasts suggest that the themes are analytic patterns across contexts rather than universal effects. They also indicate that future studies should report setting, population, intervention dose, theoretical basis, follow-up, and organizational conditions in a comparable way.

The heterogeneity of objectives, intervention components, follow-up periods, healthcare systems, and outcomes prevents direct comparison of all 20 sources. For example, a trial measuring short-term self-management confidence cannot be treated as equivalent to a meta-review of supported self-management or a position statement about exercise. The synthesis therefore uses convergence of mechanisms and practice implications, not a common effect size, as its organizing principle.

Discussion

Principal findings

This expanded integrative review maps a plausible relationship between lifestyle self-management and nurse-led behavioural support in T2DM care. Physical activity, dietary modification, and weight management provide the behavioural basis for improving glycaemic and cardiometabolic outcomes, while nurse-led education, behavioural coaching, adherence support, monitoring guidance, and technology-enabled follow-up may help patients apply these behaviours safely and consistently. The evidence does not establish that nurse-led support is superior to other healthcare delivery models or that it should be treated as a universally required core component. Its most defensible contribution is a practice-oriented account of potential mechanisms and implementation tasks.

The findings are consistent with earlier evidence that supported self-management requires more than generic advice and that diabetes self-management education can improve glycaemic outcomes when it is structured and sustained [7, 9, 12, 26]. Evidence was weighted by design fit, methodological clarity, outcome proximity, and transferability: randomized trials informed observed intervention changes; systematic reviews and meta-analyses informed recurring patterns; epidemiological and Mendelian-randomization studies informed risk or mechanism; and position statements informed clinical rationale and safety. These categories were not treated as equivalent evidence of effectiveness. Farzaei et al. [49] reported that nurses had positive attitudes but only moderate knowledge and practice regarding nutritional management of diabetes; opportunities to provide diabetes education and perceived competence were associated with more favourable practice. This comparison supports the interpretation that the nursing contribution depends on workforce preparation and organizational opportunity as well as on patient motivation. It also supports explicit training in diabetes nutrition, behaviour change, teach-back, culturally responsive behavioural guidance, and escalation boundaries.

Lifestyle intervention as the behavioural foundation

Conceptually, this theme combines three related constructs. Physical activity is body movement that increases energy expenditure, including planned exercise and movement integrated into daily life [10]. Dietary habits are recurrent patterns of food and drink selection, portioning, timing, and social context [23]. Weight management is individualized support to achieve or maintain clinically appropriate weight through nutrition, activity, behavioural, and, where indicated, medical strategies [2]. Operational examples in the included literature were walking or aerobic activity, resistance exercise or reduced sedentary time; reducing sugary drinks or energy-dense portions while retaining culturally acceptable staple foods; and monitoring body weight with gradual, measurable goals.

These operational examples are consistent with Colberg et al. [10] and Bassuk and Manson [4], who position activity as a foundation for diabetes and cardiometabolic risk management, and with dietary intervention evidence from Wattanakorn et al. [40] and Shrestha et al. [44]. However, those articles vary in population and delivery setting, and their findings do not support a universal diet or a single nurse-led dose. The interpretation from this review is therefore that the nursing contribution lies in contextualizing and monitoring lifestyle actions—linking the patient’s food and activity pattern to glucose feedback and revising goals—rather than prescribing one standard lifestyle package.

The physical-activity, dietary, and weight-management literature should be interpreted as the behavioural foundation on which nursing support operates, rather than as evidence that one universal lifestyle prescription works for every patient. Activity and dietary goals must be adapted to family routines, financial resources, culture, physical ability, and health literacy. The BCW/COM-B lens helps explain this implementation problem: education and skills address capability, service access, and social support address opportunity, and negotiated goals and feedback address motivation [22]. Nurses may therefore help translate recommendations into feasible actions, but the feasibility and effect of that translation depend on wider patient and system conditions.

For clarity, a barrier is a relatively persistent personal, social, organizational, or structural condition that limits access to or continuation of self-management; an obstacle is a specific challenge or event encountered while carrying out a plan. The terms overlap in ordinary language, but this distinction helps describe nursing action. For example, the cost of healthy food, unsafe neighbourhoods, low health literacy, or lack of transport may be barriers; a night shift, knee flare, family celebration, or temporary medication side effect may be an obstacle. A barrier may require service or resource change, whereas an obstacle may be addressed by problem-solving, adaptation, or a shorter follow-up interval. In T2DM care, nurses should assess both without attributing non-adherence to lack of willpower [7, 12, 26].

Nurse-led support as a potential bridge between recommendation and action

Conceptually, nurse-led behavioural support is nursing-led or nursing-coordinated support that moves beyond information delivery to help a person perform and maintain a behaviour through assessment, goal-setting, education, coaching, feedback, reinforcement, self-monitoring, and follow-up [22, 26, 27]. Operational examples include assessing readiness and food access; using teach-back; agreeing a measurable walking or dietary goal; reviewing a glucose or medication log; arranging telephone or digital follow-up; and escalating symptoms or persistent abnormal patterns.

This interpretation agrees with Yu et al. [35], Noroozi et al. [47], Ibrahim et al. [15], and Bilgehan and Vardar Inkaya [5], whose structured, theory-based, empowerment, or coaching programmes used repeated interaction and goal negotiation. It also contrasts with Farmer et al. [13], where a single nurse consultation targeted medication adherence, and with technology-focused syntheses [1, 43], where contact intensity and human interpretation varied. Together, the comparison suggests that repeated, tailored interaction is a plausible implementation mechanism, but not proof that nurse-led delivery is superior; staffing, preparation, and organizational opportunity remain necessary conditions, consistent with Farzaei et al. [49].

The main potential nursing contribution identified in this review is the possibility of connecting clinical recommendations with patient behaviour. Nurses can assess whether patients understand the condition, whether they can perform recommended self-care tasks, and whether barriers are emerging. This role may be particularly useful for patients with low health literacy, multiple comorbidities, obesity, poor glycaemic control, or limited confidence in self-management. In this context, confidence means a person’s general sense that they can approach a task; self-management confidence is task- and condition-specific confidence that they can perform diabetes behaviours such as planning a meal, walking safely, taking medication as prescribed, interpreting a glucose pattern, and seeking help when needed [26, 48]. Self-management confidence is therefore close to task-specific self-efficacy, not a global personality trait. It differs from self-esteem, which refers to a person’s broad evaluation of their own worth or value [50]. For example, an adult with T2DM may value themselves highly (high self-esteem) but feel unsure about adjusting a meal plan (low self-management confidence); conversely, they may feel confident recording glucose readings while still experiencing low general self-worth. Nursing support should strengthen task-specific capability and confidence without implying that glycaemic outcomes reflect personal worth. Because this review did not formally compare nurse-led care with other delivery models, the “bridge” formulation is an interpretive mechanism rather than evidence of comparative effectiveness.

The added evidence provides convergent implementation signals. Nurse-led structured education, theory-based education, nurse coaching, empowerment interventions, and digital nursing support suggest that repeated contact, goal negotiation, and feedback may be important mechanisms. A nursing intervention is more interpretable when it specifies what nurses assess, what behaviours they target, how often they follow up, and how they respond to poor adherence or safety concerns. These interpretations remain provisional because the evidence base contains observational studies, narrative reviews, and position statements alongside trials.

Nursing actions also operate within broader health-system conditions. Socioeconomic inequality, food and medication affordability, transport, healthcare accessibility, reimbursement, staffing, organizational leadership, multidisciplinary collaboration, continuity between primary and specialist care, digital infrastructure, and family or community support can all influence whether a recommended behaviour is possible and sustained [17, 18]. A nurse may identify and escalate these constraints, but cannot resolve all of them through education alone. This context limits any interpretation that attributes self-management outcomes to nursing support in isolation.

Accordingly, the practice pathway in Fig. 3 should be adapted to the local scope of practice, staffing, referral pathways, and available resources. It is a service-delivery aid rather than a validated intervention model.

Medication support and role boundaries

Conceptually, medication-related support means nursing actions that help a patient understand, use, and safely continue glucose-lowering treatment, recognize possible adverse effects, and communicate concerns. Glucose-monitoring guidance means helping the patient obtain, interpret, and respond to readings within an agreed safety plan. Technology-enabled follow-up means ongoing nurse–patient contact supported by telephone, messaging, mobile applications, telehealth, or remote monitoring, with human interpretation and escalation rather than data collection alone [13, 26, 32]. Operational examples include adherence checks, medication reconciliation, side-effect screening, explanation of when to seek help, teaching pattern recognition, reviewing remote data, and documenting referral.

The one-consultation model evaluated by Farmer et al. [13] illustrates focused adherence support, whereas Alhaiti [1] and Joiner et al. [43] describe broader technology-enabled possibilities; neither establishes that digital contact alone changes outcomes. The current synthesis interprets nursing contribution as the safety and continuity layer connecting medication-taking, monitoring, and lifestyle routines. This is distinct from independent prescribing, and it depends on local regulation, referral pathways, digital access, and workload.

Medication-related support was present in the literature, particularly in relation to adherence and long-term treatment continuity. It is important to describe the nursing role accurately. The evidence reviewed here supports nurses as educators, monitors, and facilitators of medication safety, not as independent prescribers unless local regulations explicitly allow that role. Nurses can help patients understand why medication is needed, how it should be taken, and what adverse effects require attention. They can also refer patients to physicians when glucose readings, side effects, or adherence problems suggest that the treatment plan may need review.

Contribution to nursing service delivery

For nursing readership, the main contribution of this review is the clarification of how nurses operationalize lifestyle self-management in routine care. A “contribution” is the identifiable value added by an activity, person, or role to a process, setting, or outcome; a “nursing contribution” is the value added through nursing assessment, education, coaching, monitoring, coordination, teaching, and timely referral within scope of practice [9–12, 27, 29–31]. In this review, contribution is deliberately used as a process-level concept: it describes how nursing activity helps a recommendation become understandable, feasible, observable, and reviewable, without claiming that each activity independently causes a clinical outcome.

Four objective examples clarify the concept in diabetes service delivery. First, for dietary translation, a nurse can assess food access and cultural routines, use teach-back, agree on one measurable change, and review the resulting glucose pattern. Second, for physical activity and weight management, a nurse can identify a safety or access barrier, adapt the activity target, monitor progress, and shorten follow-up after a setback. Third, for medication safety, a nurse can ask about missed doses and adverse effects, reinforce correct use, document the concern, and escalate to the prescribing clinician when treatment review may be needed. Fourth, for continuity and coordination, a nurse can use a scheduled telephone or digital contact to review self-monitoring data, reinforce the agreed plan, identify warning signs, and communicate unresolved problems to the wider team. These are observable nursing processes and expected practice values; patient-level outcomes remain influenced by patient, family, organizational, and health-system conditions.

These examples are consistent with the contribution literature cited by the reviewer. Xu et al. [29] show that nursing-student contribution to clinical settings can be measured as a multidimensional, context-sensitive value perceived by clinical nurses; Matsumura et al. [30] describe both benefits and challenges of student participation, including mentoring and effects on professional work; and Akbıyık [31] highlights the contribution of nurses to practice teaching from both nurse and student perspectives. Together, these studies support viewing contribution as observable value added to care, learning, coordination, and service improvement, while recognizing that role clarity, supervision, work environment, and workload shape whether the value is realized. Applied to diabetes service delivery, nursing contribution should therefore be specified in terms of the action performed, the expected patient capability, the safety boundary, and the outcome indicator rather than inferred from the nurse’s job title alone.

Implications for nursing education

The findings have direct implications for pre-registration and continuing nursing education. Diabetes education should teach students to distinguish advice, guidance, behavioural support, and nursing contribution. Case-based learning can require students to assess capability, opportunity, and motivation; formulate a measurable goal; use teach-back; interpret a glucose, activity, or medication record; identify boundaries for referral; and document follow-up. This approach converts the abstract idea of contribution into an assessable sequence of nursing actions [22, 26, 29–31].

Simulation and clinical assessment can evaluate not only knowledge but also observable performance: culturally tailored dietary counselling, safe activity planning, medication-adherence assessment, digital follow-up, and escalation. Educators and practice partners should make learning objectives, supervision, feedback, and service expectations explicit, because contribution is shaped by the clinical environment and teaching conditions [29–31, 49].

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. It integrates evidence on lifestyle and nursing interventions rather than treating them as unrelated strategies. It also expands the evidence base from ten to twenty articles and adds more recent studies focused on nurse-led education, empowerment, coaching, and digital support. This strengthens the relevance of the synthesis for nursing service delivery.

The review was biased despite the structured audit safeguards. Because “high relevance” was not prospectively operationalized and the update was designed to strengthen nursing relevance, confirmation bias cannot be excluded. Publication bias and language bias are possible because only English-language peer-reviewed literature was included; no grey-literature or unpublished-study search was conducted, and no formal statistical assessment of publication bias was possible. Citation checking was relevance-driven and was not targeted to BMC Nursing, but this safeguard was not prospectively logged. The protocol was not prospectively registered. No design-specific formal risk-of-bias tool such as RoB 2 and no GRADE framework were applied; the Hawker-based narrative appraisal supports proportional interpretation but cannot produce a formal certainty rating [37, 38]. No quantitative synthesis or formal comparison with other healthcare delivery models was performed. The included articles varied in design, intervention intensity, follow-up length, healthcare system, and outcomes, preventing meta-analysis. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as a practice-oriented integrative synthesis rather than a comprehensive systematic review of effectiveness.

Implications for nursing practice and research

These findings support considering nurse-led behavioural support and lifestyle self-management support as part of routine diabetes care where local scope, staffing, referral arrangements, and resources permit. Nurses may help patients set achievable goals, understand glucose-monitoring results, manage medication concerns, and identify warning signs. Education should be individualized to literacy level, cultural background, comorbidities, daily routine, and material circumstances. These are practice implications from a narrative integrative synthesis, not graded clinical recommendations.

Future research should use rigorous designs, longer follow-up, and clearer reporting of intervention components. Studies should examine which elements of nurse-led support are most effective, for whom, and at what cost, while reporting setting, intervention dose, theoretical basis, staffing, reimbursement, and multidisciplinary coordination. More attention is needed to culturally adapted dietary education, family involvement, digital follow-up, health inequities, and workload implications for nurses. A future systematic review should search multiple databases and grey literature, use design-specific risk-of-bias tools, and apply a formal certainty framework such as GRADE [37, 38], with PRISMA 2020 reporting [51, 52].

Conclusion

This integrative review maps a plausible and practice-relevant nursing role in supporting lifestyle self-management among adults with T2DM. Physical activity, dietary change, and weight management provide the behavioural foundation, while nurses may help translate recommendations into practical routines through education, goal-setting, monitoring guidance, medication-related support, digital or telephone follow-up, and escalation pathways. Because the review included heterogeneous primary and non-primary evidence, used a targeted update with incomplete record-level documentation, and did not conduct formal risk-of-bias grading, quantitative synthesis, or comparative evaluation, it does not establish intervention superiority or certainty of effect. Future studies should use contemporary, rigorous, and transparently reported designs that account for patient, organizational, and health-system context.
