Three-coordinated Ce(III) complexes with long wavelength d–f emissions
Authors: Ziyu Liu, Yihu Yang, Wenliang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lanthanide luminescent materials have found a wide range of use in various fields. While most applications utilize intrashell f–f transitions, f–d transitions have recently emerged as a novel approach to develop molecular luminescent materials. Unlike Laporte-forbidden f–f transitions, f–d transitions are Laporte-allowed and thus not dependent on chromophoric ligands. Moreover, the involvement of 5d orbitals renders it possible to modulate the emission wavelength by tuning the lanthanide–ligand interaction. Herein, we report a series of three-coordinated cerium(III) complexes with a trigonal planar geometry supported by bulky 2,6-disubstituted phenoxide ligands. These complexes were fully characterized by X-ray crystallography and 1H NMR spectroscopy. In addition, we investigated the photophysical properties of these compounds that reveal strong d–f emissions with the photoluminescent quantum yield up to 62% and the CIE value of (0.46, 0.51). This work opens a new avenue to regulate the emission wavelength of lanthanide luminescent molecules through ligand field engineering.

Introduction

Lanthanides exhibit unparalleled luminescent and magnetic properties that are largely owing to their unique 4f valence orbitals. For trivalent lanthanide ions, the 4f orbitals are shielded by 5s and 5p orbitals and can hardly overlap with ligand-based orbitals. Such core-like characteristics of 4f orbitals dominates the physical and chemical properties of lanthanides. The intrashell f−f transitions of lanthanide complexes have been widely studied and utilized as luminescent materials in applications such as lighting and display1–5. However, the core-like character of 4f orbitals poses significant challenges for developing luminescent materials based on f−f transitions. While the parity-forbidden problem can be overcome by introducing strongly absorbing chromophore groups in the ligand manifolds that is known as the antenna effect6–8, the atomic-like emission for f−f transitions still limits the tunability of emission wavelengths. Unlike 4f orbitals bearing negligible orbital mixing with ligand-based orbitals, 5d orbitals of lanthanides are spatially available to interact with ligand orbitals and thus their energy levels can be readily modulated by the coordination environment. Therefore, compared to f−f transitions, f−d transitions offer several advantages: (1) Laporte-allowed transitions not relying on chromophore groups; (2) potentially tunable excitation and emission wavelengths; (3) short excited state lifetimes9–17.

While most lanthanides exhibit f−f emissions in the visible to near-infrared (NIR) region, only a few lanthanide ions may possess d−f emissions in this region due to intrinsic large energy difference between 4f and 5d orbitals18. For molecular lanthanide compounds, Ce(III), Eu(II), Yb(II) and Sm(II) complexes have shown 5d−4f emissions in the visible to NIR region due to their suitable energy difference between 4f and 5d orbitals18–20. Among these lanthanide ions, Ce(III) complexes have been mostly studied and hold the best promise because their relatively good stability and low cost21–32. However, since the energy difference between 4f and 5d orbitals is up to 6.35 eV for a free Ce(III) ion22, the 5d−4f emissions of Ce(III) complexes are usually in the ultra-violet (UV) to blue light region (Fig. 1a)21,22,28,33–39. Typical strategy to achieve d−f emissions with long wavelength is employing ligands with stronger ligand fields in order to reduce the energy gap between 4f and 5d orbitals. This approach usually requires the use of soft donors like carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur atoms. For example, Rausch and co-workers reported that (η5-C5Me5)2CeCl2Li(OEt2)2 exhibits 5d−4f emissions at 560/612 nm40. Bruno and co-workers also studied the luminescent properties of Ce(III) cyclopentadienyl complexes, including Li(C5Me5)CeMe2 with emission peaks at 544 nm and Li(C5Me5)CeCl2 with emission peaks at 538/615 nm41,42. In 2018, Edelmann and co-workers reported that [(CptBu2)2Ce(μ-Cl)]2 (CptBu2 = 1,3-di(tert-butyl)cyclopentadienyl) shows emission peaks at 560/620 nm with a photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 61% in solid state (Fig. 1b)43. Other than cyclopentadienyl ligands, Schelter and co-workers studied the luminescent properties of a series of Ce(III) amido and guanidinato complexes, including Ce[N(SiMe3)2]3 with emission peaks at 545/592 nm and PLQY of 3% (Fig. 1b)44. More recently, Liu and co-workers report a series of Ce(III) complexes supported by dithiobiuret ligands45. The use of soft S-donors significantly reduces the energy gap between 5d and 4f orbitals, which results in deep-red emission with PL maxima at 725 nm and PLQY of 31% (Fig. 1b). However, the number of Ce(III) complexes possessing long wavelength d−f emission with PL maxima beyond 600 nm and high PLQY is still very limited. In addition to using soft donors to reduce the energy gap via increasing the ligand field strength, enforcing a specific coordination geometry around the central lanthanide ion may also narrow the 5d−4f energy splitting (Fig. 1c). Inspired by the strategy to stabilize the 4f n5d1 electronic configuration of unconventional low-valent rare-earth metal complexes with a pseudo-D3h symmetry46–49, we reasoned that a trigonal planar geometry around the Ce(III) ion should lead to a significantly large splitting in energy levels for 5d orbitals with the 5dz2 orbital lying low enough to induce long wavelength d−f emissions. However, since trivalent lanthanide ions with large ionic radii and high positive charges usually exhibit high coordination numbers, three-coordinated Ce(III) complexes are very rare and usually adopt pyramidal geometry50. In order to realize a trigonal planar ligand field on a Ce(III) center, we consider bulky 2,6-disubstitute phenoxide ligands as suitable candidates. Notably, Schelter and co-workers reported PL data for Ce(OC6H4-2,6-tBu2)3 with PL maxima at 513/551 nm and PLQY of 18%51. We anticipate that more steric demanding phenoxide ligands should favor further bathochromic shift and improved PLQY for Ce(III) d−f emissions by enforcing a trigonal planar geometry and rigid structural framework. Recently, Evans and co-workers reported the synthesis of unconventional divalent rare-earth metal complexes supported by bulky aryloxide ligands 2,6-diadmantanyl-4-methylphenoxide and 2,6-diadmantanyl-4-tert-butylphenoxide52,53. The adamantyl substituents were found to provide additional steric hindrance compared to tert-butyl groups, which results in higher stability. Encouraged by these results, we set to explore the use of these aryloxide ligands in synthesizing three-coordinated Ce(III) complexes. Herein, we report the synthesis and PL properties of a series of Ce(III) complexes supported by 2,6-diadamantylphenoxide ligands with different substituents at the para-position. All compounds show a trigonal planar geometry with a coordination number of three. They exhibit strong d−f emissions in the yellow to orange light region with PLQYs up to 62% in solid state.

Results and discussion

Following modified literature procedures54,55, a series of 2,6-diadamantylphenol ligands (L1–L4) could be prepared in moderate to high yields (Fig. 2). L1–L4 differ by the substituent at the para-position: H for L1, Me for L2, tBu for L3, and 1-Ad for L4 (for NMR spectra see Figs. S3–S7 in Supplementary Note 2). L1 could readily react with Ce[N(SiMe3)2]3 in cyclohexane to yield the product Ce[OC6H4-2,6-di(1-admantyl)]3 (Ce1) in 67% yield. However, this protocol cannot be applied to the synthesis of Ce2–Ce4 from the reaction between Ce[N(SiMe3)2]3 and L2–L4, probably due to the increased steric hindrance. On the other hand, the deprotonation of L2–L4 with a strong base potassium hydride (KH) followed by the salt metathesis reaction with CeBr3(THF)4 could afford the targeted compounds Ce2–Ce4 in good yields (51–82%) (Fig. 2). The 1H NMR spectra of Ce1–Ce4 exhibit paramagnetic shifts typical for Ce(III) complexes with a three-fold symmetry (Figs. S8–S11 in Supplementary Note 2). For example, while the proton resonances of the 2,6-adamantly groups are split into five broad peaks, with the most upfield one shifted to about −4 ppm, the signals of the H atoms on the aromatic rings were downfield shifted to around 11 ppm. The molecular structures of Ce2 and Ce4 adopt a pseudo-C3 symmetry (Fig. 3), consistent with the 1H NMR spectra. The average of Ce–O distances for Ce2 (2.158(2) Å) and Ce4 (2.155(4) Å) are comparable to that of Ce3 (2.149(2) Å). To further analyze the steric effects of this series of bulky aryloxide ligands, we calculated the steric topographic maps and buried volumes for Ce1–Ce4 (Fig. 3). The space filling models and the red zones on the steric topographic maps clearly show that the cerium ion is well-shielded by the aryloxide ligands for all four compounds. The calculated buried volumes for Ce1–Ce4 are within a narrow range of 83.4–84.2% (Table S1 in Supplementary Note 4), indicating extremely bulky coordination environment around the cerium center37,56,57. The structural features and calculated parameters suggest that the substituent on the para-position only has a minor effect on the steric hindrance at the cerium ion, probably due to the para-position being relatively far from the cerium center. However, since lanthanide d–f emissions can be readily coupled with the vibration modes of ligands51,58,59, the rigidity of the ligand framework may play a key role in determining the PLQY as well as the emission wavelength. The effect of para-substitution on photoluminescent properties will be discussed later.

Ultraviolet-visible absorption (UV-vis) spectra of Ce1–Ce4 measured at room temperature in THF solution are shown in Fig. 4a. While Ce1 has absorption bands at 343 nm, 416 nm and 436 nm, Ce2–Ce4 have absorption bands slightly red shifted to about 360 nm and 460 nm. These absorption bands with molar absorption coefficient (ε) around 102 M–1cm–1 can be attributed to the 4f–5d transitions of the Ce3+ ions. Additional absorption bands with ε > 103 M–1cm–1 corresponding to ligand-based or charge transfer transitions can be observed at wavelength below 330 nm. The difference between Ce1 and Ce2–Ce4 may be resulted from larger 5d orbitals splitting caused by the fact that alkyl groups are more electron donating than hydrogen at the para-position, which leads to smaller energy gaps between 5d and 4f orbitals. The absorption bands of Ce2–Ce4 are very similar, indicating that the steric hindrance of different alkyl groups at the para-position has little influence on the energy levels of the cerium ion.Fig. 4Photophysical data of Ce1–Ce4.a Absorption spectra in THF solution (1.0 mM for Ce1–Ce3 and 0.6 mM for Ce4) at room temperature; b Excitation spectra in solid state; c Emission spectra in solid state; d CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram in solid state.

All of these four complexes exhibit strong yellow to orange emission under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation. The excitation spectra in solid state as powder are shown in Fig. 4b. Two main broad excitation peaks at around 370 nm and 460 nm are in line with the absorption spectra. The multiple broad excitation peaks correspond to multiple energy levels of the 5d orbitals of the cerium(III) ion. The maximum of excitation of Ce1 are slightly blue shifted from those of Ce2–Ce4, consistent with the findings in absorption spectra. The excitation spectra for Ce1–Ce4 in THF solution show similar peaks to the corresponding solid state excitation spectra (Figs. S16, S28, S40, S52 in Supplementary Note 3). The similar absorption and excitation spectra in solution and solid state for Ce1–Ce4 indicates that these cerium(III) complexes adopt mononuclear three-coordinated structures in both THF solution and solid state. The lack of coordination by THF suggests that the ligand framework is bulky and rigid enough to shield the cerium ion and enforce a trigonal planar geometry even in solution.

To further investigate the photophysical properties of Ce1–Ce4, room temperature steady state luminescence spectra and transient decays were characterized (see Supplementary Note 3 for details). The emission spectra of Ce1–Ce4 in solid state feature typical doublet emissions that are characteristic for 5d–4f emissions of Ce(III) complexes due to the 2F7/2 and 2F5/2 final states (Fig. 4c). Ce1 exhibits yellow emission under 367 nm UV irradiation, featuring a broad emission peak at 535 nm with a shoulder at 581 nm when measure at room temperature in solid state. The energy difference between the two peaks is 1480 cm−1, in line with the energy difference between the 2F7/2 and 2F5/2 states33,34,36,60. The CIE coordinates of the emission spectrum are (0.43, 0.54) in the yellow light region (Fig. 4d) and the PLQY in solid state is 62%, higher than most d–f emission cerium(III) complexes beyond yellow light. The transient PL decay of Ce1 in solid state yields a typical Ce(III) single exponential decay with a short lifetime of 89.9 ns (Fig. S65), consistent with the Laporte-allowed d–f emission. Compared to Ce1, the emission spectrum of Ce2 is slightly red shifted to 547 nm with a shoulder at 602 nm. The red shift phenomenon is consistent with the UV-vis spectra and excitation spectra. A stronger ligand field provided by more electron donating methyl groups at the para-position leads to a larger 5d orbital splitting and hence results in the red-shifted d–f emission for Ce2, in comparison to Ce1. The CIE coordinates of Ce2 also red-shift to orange light with a CIE value of (0.46, 0.51). The lifetime of the PL decay changes to 70.9 ns and PLQY deceases to 28%, probably due to smaller energy difference between the excited state and the ground state as evidenced by the red-shifted emission. Ce3 and Ce4 exhibit nearly identical emission spectra to Ce2, emitting orange light with PL maxima at 544/600 nm and 546/602 nm, and CIE coordinates of (0.45, 0.52) and (0.46, 0.52), respectively. While the decay lifetimes for Ce3 and Ce4 at 75.6 ns and 67.2 ns, respectively, are comparable to Ce2, the PLQYs significantly increase to 61% and 53%, respectively. These results indicate that bulky substituents, such as tert-butyl and 1-admantyl, at the para-position may lead to a more rigid ligand backbone, which in turn results in increased PLQYs with similar emission wavelength.

Conclusion

In this work, we designed and synthesized a series of three-coordinated Ce(III) complexes supported by bulky 2,6-diadamantylphenoxide ligands. Both the solution and solid-state structures are consistent with a trigonal planar geometry enforced by large steric hindrance of the aryloxide ligands. The photoluminescent properties of these compounds show yellow to orange emissions with prominent Ce(III) d–f emission characters. The PLQYs of these complexes are up to 62% in solid state with a CIE value of (0.46, 0.51). The study on the structure-property relationship reveals that the introduction of electron donating and bulky substituents at the para-position has a positive impact on the bathochromic shift of emission wavelength and PLQY. The current work provides a new approach to develop molecular d–f transition lanthanide luminescent materials with long wavelength emissions and sheds light on the design principle on future research in this field.

Methods

General methods

All experiments were performed under a dry argon atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques or in a nitrogen filled glove box unless otherwise specified. Solvents including n-pentane, hexanes, diethyl ether (Et2O), toluene, and methylene chloride (CH2Cl2) were collected from a Vigor YJC-5 Solvent Purification System under argon, transferred to the glove box without exposure to air, and stored over activated molecular sieves. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) was refluxed over sodium hydride for 72 h and then distilled from sodium-benzophenone ketyl before use. Benzene-d6 (C6D6) and chloroform-d1 (CDCl3) were obtained from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories or J&K Scientific, degassed three times and stored over activated molecular sieves for 1 week prior to use. CeBr3(THF)4 and Ce(N(SiMe3)2)3 were prepared following published procedures61,62. 1-Adamantanol was purchased from Bidepharm. 4-Tert-butylphenol and 4-bromophenol were purchased form Maclin. p-Cresol was purchased from Aladin. 1-Bromoadamantane was purchased from Konoscience. Phenol was purchased from Energy Chemical. Wet palladium on carbon was purchased from J&K Scientific. These chemicals were used directly without further purification. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer at room temperature in C6D6 or CDCl3 unless otherwise specified. Chemical shifts are reported with respect to internal solvent (C6D6 at 7.16 ppm or CDCl3 at 7.26 ppm). The diffraction data were collected using a Rigaku Oxford diffractometer equipped with a CCD collector using Mo Kα radiation at 180 K. CHN analyses were performed on a Vario EL elemental analyzer at the Analytical Center of Peking University. 10 mm pathlength quartz cells fused with a J-Young valve were used to contain solutions of air and moisture sensitive compounds for UV-vis and luminescence characterization. Electronic absorption spectra (UV-Vis) were collected on a Shimadzu UV-3600Plus UV-Vis-NIR Spectrometer. Excitation and emission spectra were recorded on an Edinburgh FLS980 fluorescence spectrophotometer. Luminescence decay lifetimes were obtained on a single photon counting spectrometer from Edinburgh FLS980 with micro-second pulse lamp as the excitation source. Absolute photoluminescence quantum yields were measured using Hamamatsu C9920-02 PL quantum yield measurement system with integrating sphere, with maximum errors of ±5%. The CIE coordinates were obtained using a Chromaticity Diagram plug-in of OriginPro 2022, after obtaining the emission spectra.

Synthetic details and characterization

Synthesis of 2,6-diadamantanylphenol (Ad2PhOH)

Synthesis of 2,6-diadamantanyl-4-bromophenol (Ad2BrPhOH)

Ad2BrPhOH was synthesized from a slightly modified literature protocol55. 4-bromophenol (3.75 g, 21.7 mmol, 1.0 equiv) was mixed with 1-adamantanol (6.60 g, 43.4 mmol, 2.0 equiv) and 1-bromoadamantane (0.98 g, 4.56 mmol, 0.2 equiv) in a glass pressure vessel and heated for 24 h at 160 °C. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was purified by column chromatography on silica gel, eluted with n-heptane (Rf = 0.3) to give a white solid. Yield: 1.45 g, 15%. 1H NMR (CDCl3, 400 MHz, 298 K) δ, ppm: 7.19 (s, 2H, CH of aryl), 5.33 (s, 1H, OH), 2.09 (s, 18H, γ-CH and β-CH2, Ad), 1.78 (s, 12H, δ-CH2, Ad). 1H NMR data match the literature55.

Synthesis of Ad2PhOH (L1)

Ad2PhOH was synthesized using a slightly modified literature protocol.55 Ad2BrPhOH (262 mg, 0.593 mmol) was mixed with 10 wt% Pd/C (132 mg) with 30 mL of CH2Cl2 and 30 mL of MeOH as solvents in a 350 mL Schlenk tube and placed in a dry ice/acetone bath for 30 min. The tube was degassed and refilled by H2 (about 1 atm) and then taken out of the dry ice/acetone bath and stirred for about 16 h. The reaction mixture was then filtered through Celite and a white solid was obtained after the volatiles were removed under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by column chromatography on silica gel, eluted with n-heptane (Rf = 0.4) to give a white solid. Yield: 94 mg, 44%.1H NMR (CDCl3, 400 MHz, 298 K) δ, ppm: 7.12 (d, 3JHH = 7.8 Hz, 2H, m-CH of aryl), 6.91–6.84 (m, 1H, p-CH of aryl), 5.35 (s, 1H, OH), 2.14 (m, 18H, γ-CH and β-CH2, Ad), 1.79 (s, 12H, δ-CH2, Ad). 1H NMR data match the literature55.

Synthesis of Ad2RPhOH (R = Me, tBu and Ad)

Synthesis of 2,6-diadamantanyl-4-methylphenol (Ad2MePhOH, L2)

Ad2MePhOH was synthesized using a slightly modified literature protocol54. To a stirred solution of p-cresol (2.60 g, 24.0 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and 1-adamantanol (8.10 g, 53.2 mmol, 2.2 equiv) in CH2Cl2 (50 mL) was added concentrated H2SO4 (18 M, 2.7 mL, 2.0 equiv) dropwise. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, and H2O (50 mL) was added. The mixture was neutralized with a saturated solution of NaHCO3 (about 100 mL). The mixture was extracted into CH2Cl2 (100 mL X 3). The organic phases were combined and dried over Na2SO4. After removal of the solvent, the residue was triturated with boiling MeOH (100 mL) to afford a white solid. The resulting solid was filtered off and washed with MeOH (50 mL) to give an analytically pure solid of Ad2MePhOH. Yield: 2.61 g, 29%. 1H NMR (C6D6, 400 MHz, 298 K) δ, ppm: 7.01 (s, 2H, CH of aryl), 5.02 (s, 1H, OH), 2.35 (s, 3H, CH3), 2.14 (d, 3JHH = 2.8 Hz, 12H, β-CH2, Ad), 2.01 (s, 6H, γ-CH), 1.72 (s, 12H, δ-CH2, Ad). 1H NMR data match the literature54.
