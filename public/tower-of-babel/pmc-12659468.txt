Oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among dental students in a Palestinian dental school: a cross-sectional study using the HU-DBI
Authors: Naji Arandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to assess oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (KABs) among dental students at Arab American University (AAUP), Palestine, and to examine variations in these factors on the basis of gender and clinical level.Materials and methodsA quantitative cross-sectional survey was conducted among students from all academic years at the Faculty of Dentistry, AAUP, using the Hiroshima University–Dental Behavioral Inventory (HU-DBI). The HU-DBI includes 12 scored items (maximum score = 12), with higher scores indicating greater preventive awareness and self-care practices. Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess associations between demographic variables and HU-DBI scores, and multiple linear regression analyses were performed to examine the independent effects of gender and clinical level (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong the 366 respondents, 57.7% were female, and 54.4% were clinical students. The mean HU-DBI score was 7.23 ± 1.77. Compared with male students, female students achieved significantly higher total scores (7.54 vs. 6.83; p = 0.0001) and behavior scores (2.29 vs. 1.81; p = 0.0001), with no significant bivariate differences in knowledge (p = 0.1000) or attitude (p = 0.5871) scores. Compared with preclinical students, clinical students scored significantly higher in total (7.70 vs. 6.68; p < 0.0001), knowledge (3.60 vs. 3.25; p = 0.0168), and behavior (2.46 vs. 1.65; p < 0.0001) domains, with no significant bivariate difference in attitudes (p = 0.1533). However, regression analyses demonstrated that clinical level was independently associated with more positive attitudes.ConclusionDental students at the AAUP generally demonstrated positive oral health awareness. Female gender and clinical training were associated with significantly better preventive behaviors and higher overall HU-DBI scores, while regression analysis revealed that clinical exposure also contributed to more positive attitudes. These findings underscore the value of reinforcing preventive dentistry concepts and practical oral hygiene training early in the preclinical years to support consistent self-care and a smooth transition to clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08216-1.

Introduction

Oral health is integral to overall health, well-being, and quality of life [1]. Poor oral health can lead to various adverse consequences, including infections, pain, tooth loss, difficulties with eating and speaking, systemic health issues, and limited social interactions [2–5].

Dentists play a pivotal role in the prevention of oral diseases and the promotion of general health through initiatives such as smoking cessation, healthy eating, physical activity, and immunization [6–8]. Furthermore, they are well positioned to model health-promoting behaviors for patients, colleagues, families, and the broader community [9].

Given that today’s dental students are future practitioners responsible for oral health education and promotion, it is essential to assess their knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (KAB) related to oral health [10]. Their personal oral health practices serve as indicators of their understanding of preventive dental care and its role in achieving optimal oral health outcomes [11]. Importantly, in professional practice, adequate knowledge and positive attitudes and behaviors among dental professionals are strongly associated with the implementation of preventive strategies in clinical settings, ultimately enhancing patient care and quality of life [12].

Monitoring dental students’ oral health-related KAB throughout their academic progression is essential for evaluating the effectiveness of dental education curricula. Establishing baseline data across different academic stages allows dental schools to assess current curricular outcomes and identify areas for enhancement. These insights can guide the development of targeted interventions aimed at fostering essential preventive behaviors and positive attitudes, ultimately contributing to improved patient care by future practitioners [13].

The Hiroshima University-Dental Behavioral Inventory (HU-DBI), developed by Makoto Kawamura in 1988 [14], is a validated psychometric tool widely used for assessing undergraduate dental students’ oral health-related KAB. It evaluates three distinct domains—knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors—using 20 agree/disagree items, with 12 contributing to a composite score that reflects preventive awareness and self-care practices. Its translation and cultural adaptation in various countries [15] have facilitated meaningful cross-cultural comparisons [16]. For example, European studies have reported relatively high HU-DBI scores, indicating strong preventive attitudes among dental students in countries such as Estonia, Germany, Slovakia, and the Czech Republic [17–19]. In contrast, lower scores have been observed in several Middle Eastern and North African (MENA) countries, including Turkey, Egypt, Lebanon, Syria, and Tunisia, which are often attributed to curricular gaps or limited resources [13, 20, 21]. Studies utilizing the HU-DBI have also revealed notable differences in KAB among dental students across institutions worldwide [19, 21–24], between clinical and preclinical cohorts [13, 17, 25–33], and across gender groups [17–19, 21, 23, 26, 30, 31, 33–37].

Understanding dental students’ oral health-related KAB provides valuable insight into the effectiveness of dental education in promoting preventive mindsets. These findings can inform curriculum enhancements, address educational gaps, and support the development of prevention-oriented practitioners. At the national level, the data enable cross-cultural comparisons and contribute to international benchmarking and policy discussions. These insights can guide the development of targeted national oral health initiatives to address country-specific challenges. Ultimately, strengthening students’ KAB may lead to improved patient counseling, earlier disease prevention, and better oral health outcomes in clinical settings.

However, data on the oral health-related KAB of undergraduate dental students in Palestine remain scarce. The influence of gender and clinical training on these behaviors has yet to be fully understood within the local context. Given the cultural, educational, and socioeconomic context of Palestine—as well as differences in healthcare systems and access to dental care—findings may differ from those reported in other countries. This study aims to address this gap by assessing oral health-related KAB among dental students across all academic levels at the Faculty of Dentistry, Arab American University, Palestine (AAUP), via the HU-DBI. Additionally, it seeks to examine the influence of gender and clinical experience on students’ oral health-related KAB.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April and May 2025 and included all full-time dental students (N = 810) enrolled across the five academic years at the Faculty of Dentistry, AAUP.

Sample size calculation and sampling method

The required sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for finite populations, assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution, yielding a minimum of 261 participants. To account for potential nonresponses and improve representativeness, 25% was added, resulting in a target of 326 participants. A stratified convenience sampling approach was employed, with academic year used as the stratification variable to ensure proportional representation in the final sample. The survey link was distributed to all eligible students via class-specific social media groups (WhatsApp and Facebook), and responses were tracked by academic year to maintain approximate proportional targets. Given that a nonprobability sampling method was employed, potential self-selection bias is acknowledged.

Recruitment and data collection

The questionnaire was self-administered and completed online through a secure, encrypted survey link. Invitations were shared via class-specific WhatsApp and Facebook groups, with student representatives from each year helping to disseminate the link and promote participation. Two reminder messages were sent at one-week intervals. To reduce social desirability bias, the questionnaire ensured full anonymity; no personally identifiable information (e.g., names, emails, IP addresses) was collected. In line with this privacy-focused approach, cookies and duplicate response detection tools were intentionally not used to avoid collecting any potentially identifying metadata. While this could theoretically allow duplicate responses, the survey’s closed distribution within class-specific groups and the academic relevance of the topic were expected to minimize this risk. The participants were also reminded to complete the survey only once. All the items were neutrally worded, and the data were stored on password-protected servers accessible only to the research team. A consent statement outlining the study purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality assurances, and data handling protocols was presented at the beginning of the survey. These measures align with key elements of the Checklist for Reporting Results of internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) guidelines to ensure data quality, privacy, and methodological transparency [38].

Questionnaire instrument and Language adaptation

A previously validated questionnaire, widely used in earlier studies, was employed in this research without modification (Appendix A) [17, 21]. The HU-DBI was administered in English, which is the official language of instruction at the Faculty of Dentistry, Arab American University. To enhance clarity and cultural relevance, we adopted the previously validated Arabic translation of the HU-DBI developed by Khalid et al. [39] and placed each Arabic translation alongside its corresponding English item in the survey. The bilingual questionnaire was then pilot tested with 20 dental students (four from each academic year) to confirm clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness. All the items were reported as clear and contextually appropriate, and no changes to the validated wording were needed. All participants were full-time students; no part-time interns were included in the clinical group. Only fully completed surveys were included in the analysis; therefore, no missing data were present.

Survey content and scoring

The survey comprised two sections: the first collected demographic data (age, gender, and academic year), and the second included 20 agree/disagree statements assessing oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. Scoring followed the HU-DBI system developed by Kawamura [14], with 12 of the 20 items contributing to the total score (maximum score = 12), with each correct response earning one point. Correct answers were defined as “agree” for items 4, 9, 11, 12, 16, and 19 and “disagree” for items 2, 6, 8, 10, 14, and 15. The 12 scored items were grouped into three domains: knowledge (Q2, Q8, Q10, Q15, Q19; maximum = 5), attitude (Q6, Q11, Q14; maximum = 3), and behavior (Q4, Q9, Q12, Q16; maximum = 4), with higher scores indicating more favorable oral health-related attitudes and behaviors [17–19, 21].

Statistical analysis

The data were compiled in Microsoft Excel and analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics V28.0. Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to examine associations between gender, clinical level, and HU-DBI domain scores, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The HU-DBI total and domain scores were analyzed as continuous variables; however, owing to significant deviation from normality (confirmed via Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests), nonparametric tests were applied. The clinical level variable was dichotomized into ‘preclinical’ (years 1–3) and ‘clinical’ (years 4–5) to reflect the progression from didactic instruction to patient-based training. In addition to p values, effect sizes (r) were calculated via Mann–Whitney U tests to quantify the magnitude of differences. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.1 ≤ r < 0.3), medium (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5), or large (r ≥ 0.5).

To further assess the independence of effects, multiple linear regression analyses were performed with the HU-DBI total and domain scores as continuous dependent variables and gender and clinical level as predictors. Additional potential covariates, such as age and academic performance, were not collected and therefore could not be included. No interaction terms or sensitivity analyses were performed, which is consistent with the exploratory design and relatively homogeneous population.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of AAUP (Approval No. J-2025/A/15/N). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the survey. All the responses were anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was collected to ensure confidentiality. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [40].

Results

Participant demographics

Among the 810 dental students invited to participate, 366 completed the survey in full, yielding a response rate of 45.2%. Although the sample size calculation incorporated stratification to ensure proportional representation, the survey was distributed to all eligible students to maximize participation. As participation was voluntary, the final sample represents a self-selected group with proportional representation across all five academic years. Among the respondents, 57.7% were female (n = 211), and 42.3% were male (n = 155). In terms of academic level, 54.4% (n = 199) were clinical students (years 4–5), and 45.6% (n = 167) were preclinical students (years 1–3). Only fully completed surveys were analyzed; therefore, there were no missing data for any variables.

Item-Level HU-DBI responses

Overall, the students demonstrated positive oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. In the knowledge domain (Q2, Q8, Q10, Q15, and Q19), 75.4% disagreed with Q2 (“My gum tend to bleed when I brush”), indicating a favorable response. Additionally, 75.4% disagreed with Q8 (“I think my teeth are getting worse despite my daily brushing”), which, under HU-DBI scoring, is favorable. For Q10 (“I have never received professional instruction on brushing”), 84.4% disagreed. Furthermore, 71.3% disagreed with Q15 (“I put off going to dentist until I have a toothache”). Finally, 37.7% agreed with Q19 (“I feel I sometimes take too much time to brush my teeth”).

In the attitude domain (Q6, Q11, and Q14), 81.7% disagreed with Q6 (“I think I cannot help having false teeth when I am old”). For Q11 (“I think I can clean my teeth well without using toothpaste”), 26.2% agreed. For Q14 (“It is impossible to prevent gum disease with just tooth brushing”), 62.3% disagreed.

In the behavior domain (Q4, Q9, Q12, and Q16), 74.6% agreed with Q9 (“I brush each of my teeth carefully”), and 80.1% agreed with Q12 (“I often check my teeth in the mirror after brushing”). A total of 21.9% reported using plaque-disclosing agents (Q16), and 32.5% agreed with Q4 (“I have noticed some white sticky deposits on my teeth”). Additionally, 19.4% reported using a child-sized toothbrush (Q5). Gender-based differences in HU-DBI item responses

Item-based gender comparisons revealed that female students were significantly more likely to agree with Q3 (“I worry about the color of my teeth”) and to agree with Q9 (“I brush each of my teeth carefully”). Females also had a higher rate of disagreement with Q1 (“I do not worry much about visiting the dentist”). Male students were significantly more likely to agree with Q5 (“I use a child-sized toothbrush”) and with Q19 (“I feel I sometimes take too much time to brush my teeth”). Females agreed more often with Q4 (“I have noticed some white sticky deposits on my teeth”). No statistically significant gender differences were found for Q2, Q8, Q10, Q15, Q6, Q7, Q11, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q16–Q18, or Q20. Discussion

Overview of key findings

This study assessed oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors among dental students, with a focus on differences by gender and clinical level. Overall, the findings indicate a generally positive level of oral health awareness. Compared with preclinical students, female students achieved significantly higher HU-DBI total and behavior scores, whereas clinical students scored higher overall, knowledge, and behavior domains. Regression analyses confirmed these differences, demonstrating that gender and clinical level exert independent effects on HU-DBI outcomes.

The strength of this study lies in its inclusion of students from all academic years, offering valuable insights for enhancing preventive education from the earliest stages of dental education. The HU-DBI was chosen for its well-documented validity, reliability, and cross-cultural applicability, along with its ease of scoring and extensive use in dental student populations worldwide [17–19, 21]. Its structured domain-based format facilitates targeted curriculum evaluation and supports meaningful international comparisons, particularly given its previous application in Arabic-speaking contexts.

Although the mean HU-DBI score was somewhat lower than those reported in some European countries—such as the Czech Republic (9.34) [19], Estonia (8.09) [17], Germany (7.67) [18], and Slovakia (7.56) [19], —it remains comparable to regional findings, including Saudi Arabia (7.09) [41] and Iraq (7.06) [42], and higher than the scores reported in Greece (6.9) [35], Egypt (6.77) [13], Lebanon (6.67) [21], Syria (6.38) [21], Morocco (6.33) [43], Turkey (6.3) [20], and Tunisia (6.05) [21]. These results place the cohort in a favorable position within the regional context while also underscoring opportunities to further align with international benchmarks.

Knowledge domain

Regarding knowledge, 75.4% of the respondents demonstrated an understanding of gingival health by disagreeing with the statement “My gum tend to bleed when I brush my teeth.” This reflects awareness that gum bleeding during brushing is not normal and may indicate gingival inflammation or disease. This rate is higher than that reported in several Middle Eastern and North African (MENA) countries, including Tunisia [21], Syria [21], Egypt [13], and Turkey [20], but lower than that reported in Lebanon [21] and Sudan [39] and is lower than the rates reported among European counterparts [17–19].

A similar proportion (75.4%) disagreed with the statement ‘I feel my teeth are getting worse despite my daily brushing’ (Q8), which HU-DBI codes as favorable. This proportion aligns with findings from several MENA countries [13, 21] but remains lower than the higher favorable-response rates reported in European countries [17–19]. The remaining 24.6% who perceived a decline in their oral health may benefit from the reinforcement of effective brushing techniques and preventive care strategies. Only 15.6% of the students reported never receiving professional brushing instruction—a proportion lower than those reported in Syria [21] and Sudan [39]. This figure is comparable to those in Germany [18] and Estonia [17] and slightly better than those in Tunisia [21], Slovakia [19], and Lebanon [21], although still behind the Czech Republic [19]. Awareness of the importance of regular preventive dental visits was reported by 71.3% of respondents, again exceeding most MENA countries [21, 39] but remaining below leading European benchmarks [17–19]. With respect to brushing duration, 37.7% of the respondents agreed with the item “I feel I sometimes take too much time to brush my teeth,” which HU-DBI codes as favorable. While this could reflect thoroughness, it might also indicate uncertainty or inefficiency in technique, particularly among less experienced students. This rate is comparable to those in Estonia [17], Lebanon [21], and Tunisia [21]; lower than those in Syria [21] and the Czech Republic [19]; and higher than those in Slovakia [19] and Germany [18]. Integrating structured, evidence-based instruction with practical training and greater access to diagnostic tools could further strengthen students’ knowledge and better align local education with international standards.

Attitudes domain

In terms of attitudes, 81.7% of the respondents disagreed with the statement “I think I cannot help having false teeth when I am old” (Q6), indicating confidence in maintaining natural dentition into older age. This proportion is comparable to those of Estonia [17] and Egypt [13] and exceeds those of several MENA countries, including Lebanon [21], Syria [21], Sudan [39], Tunisia [21], and Slovakia [19]. However, it remains slightly lower than the more optimistic figures reported in Turkey [20], Germany [18], and the Czech Republic [19].

For the HU-DBI item “I think I can clean my teeth well without using toothpaste” (Q11), 26.2% agreed. Under HU-DBI scoring, this is considered favorable because it reflects self-efficacy in mechanical plaque removal. This proportion is similar to that in Tunisia [21] and higher than that in Germany [18], Sudan [39], Estonia [17], Lebanon [21], and Syria [21] but lower than that in Slovakia [19] and the Czech Republic [19]. While this confidence is a positive attribute, it should be accompanied by an understanding of the additional caries-preventive benefits of fluoride-containing toothpaste; thus, a “favorable” Q11 response is best interpreted as confidence in brushing ability rather than opposition to fluoride.

For Q14 (“It is impossible to prevent gum disease with just tooth brushing”), 62.3% disagreed, indicating recognition of the role of tooth brushing in periodontal disease prevention. This proportion exceeds that reported in several MENA countries [20, 21, 39], Germany [18], and Estonia [17] and is comparable to that reported in Slovakia [19] but still below that reported in the Czech Republic [19].

Overall, the participants demonstrated constructive attitudes toward oral health, particularly regarding the preservation of natural dentition and understanding of the role of brushing in periodontal disease prevention. However, targeted educational reinforcement is warranted to ensure that students’ confidence in their ability to remove plaque through brushing is consistent with their knowledge of the chemical and preventive benefits of dentifrices, especially fluoride-containing products. Although bivariate analyses did not reveal significant differences in attitude scores between clinical and preclinical students, regression analyses demonstrated that clinical level was independently associated with more positive attitudes, suggesting that clinical exposure may contribute to attitudinal development.

Behavior domain

In the behavior domain, 32.5% of the respondents agreed with the HU-DBI item “I have noticed some white sticky deposits on my teeth” (Q4), which reflects self-reported plaque recognition. This proportion is higher than those reported in Slovakia [19] and Lebanon [21] but lower than those reported in Syria [21], Tunisia [21], Egypt [13], and Sudan [39]. This figure is notably higher than the figures reported by Estonia [17], the Czech Republic [19], and Germany [18]. Differences across countries may reflect variations in oral hygiene practices, plaque awareness, or self-perception of cleanliness.

A strong commitment to daily oral hygiene was indicated by 74.6% agreeing with “I brush each of my teeth carefully” (Q9), aligning closely with Lebanon [21], Turkey [20], the Czech Republic [19], and Slovakia [19]. Although lower than the rates reported in Germany [18], Estonia [17], and Sudan [39], this finding still points to generally thorough brushing habits.

Mirror checking after brushing (Q12) was reported by 80.1% of the students, a rate similar to that reported in Egypt [13], Slovakia [19], Germany [18], and Estonia [17] and higher than rates reported in the Czech Republic [19] and Sudan [39]. This figure is slightly lower than those in Lebanon [21], Syria [21], Turkey [20], and Tunisia [21]. Such self-assessment behaviors can support early detection of oral hygiene issues, although their effectiveness depends on the accuracy of students’ recognition skills.

The use of plaque-disclosing agents (Q16) was limited, with only 21.9% reporting this practice. This proportion exceeds that in Tunisia (3.61%) [21] but is lower than those in Syria (36.7%) [21] and Lebanon (25.6%) [21] and considerably lower than the higher rates reported in European contexts [17–19]. The low adoption of plaque-disclosing agents among Palestinian dental students mirrors regional trends and may reflect broader socioeconomic factors. These products may be perceived as less essential than core items, such as toothpaste and toothbrushes, are not consistently available owing to limited market penetration and import barriers, and fall outside cultural hygiene norms, where emphasis is placed on brushing or mouthwash use rather than adjunctive products.

Overall, the respondents demonstrated strong engagement in key oral hygiene behaviors, particularly tooth brushing and visual self-assessment. However, the relatively low rates of plaque recognition and limited use of plaque-disclosing agents highlight areas for improvement. The incorporation of more hands-on training in plaque detection, the provision of targeted guidance to refine brushing techniques, and the integration of clinical demonstrations could strengthen students’ practical skills, better align their behaviors with international standards, and promote more comprehensive oral health care.

Gender differences

Compared with male students, female dental students achieved significantly higher total HU-DBI scores (7.54 ± 1.71 vs. 6.83 ± 1.77; p = 0.0001), a difference driven primarily by higher behavior scores (2.29 ± 1.13 vs. 1.81 ± 1.14; p = 0.0001). No statistically significant differences were observed in knowledge (p = 0.1000) or attitude scores (p = 0.5871).

At the item level, females reported higher rates of careful tooth brushing (Q9; p < 0.001), expressed greater concern about tooth color (Q3; p = 0.0002), were less likely to use a child-sized toothbrush (Q5; p = 0.0116), and more often rejected the belief that tooth loss in old age is inevitable (Q6; p = 0.0262). They also disagreed more frequently with the statement “I do not worry much about visiting the dentist” (Q1; p = 0.0436), indicating greater engagement with preventive dental care. In contrast, males more frequently endorsed Q6 (23.9% vs. 14.2%), an unfavorable HU-DBI response indicating a more pessimistic view of long-term oral health outcomes. No significant gender differences were found for the use of plaque-disclosing agents (Q16) or post-brushing mirror checks (Q12).

The stronger behavioral engagement among female students may reflect heightened health responsibility, greater aesthetic motivation, or broader societal and cultural influences [44, 45]. Similarly, previous studies have reported higher HU-DBI scores among females [25, 30, 31, 35, 36, 46], particularly in the behavior domain, although some studies have reported no significant gender differences [17, 19, 21, 26, 33, 34]. Given these mixed findings, curriculum design could incorporate gender-sensitive strategies—such as targeted motivational interviewing, peer-led skill-building workshops, and personalized feedback—to encourage consistent preventive behaviors among male students and reduce gender-based disparities. These results were consistent with the regression analyses, which confirmed that female students maintained higher HU-DBI total and behavior scores, independent of the clinical level.

Clinical level differences

Compared with preclinical students, clinical students achieved significantly higher total HU-DBI scores (7.70 ± 1.62 vs. 6.68 ± 1.77; p < 0.0001), with notable advantages in the knowledge (3.60 ± 0.84 vs. 3.25 ± 1.26; p = 0.0168) and behavior (2.46 ± 1.21 vs. 1.65 ± 0.93; p < 0.0001) domains. The attitude scores did not differ significantly (p = 0.1533), suggesting that core attitudes toward oral health may be established early and remain stable throughout training.

At the item level, clinical students more frequently responded to key knowledge and behavior items, including rejecting gingival bleeding as normal (Q2; p < 0.001), disagreeing that their teeth deteriorate despite daily brushing (Q8; p = 0.0215), reporting prior professional brushing instructions (Q10; p < 0.001), recognizing the role of tooth brushing in preventing periodontal disease (Q14; p < 0.001), and rejecting delayed care until symptoms occur (Q15; p = 0.0463). They also demonstrated greater plaque recognition (Q4; 41.2% vs. 22.2%; p = 0.0002), more meticulous brushing (Q9; p = 0.0289), and greater use of post-brushing mirror checks (Q12; p < 0.001).

One area of divergence was Q11 (‘I think I can clean my teeth well without using toothpaste’), where clinical students agreed less often than preclinical students did (9.5% vs. 46.1%; p < 0.001). While HU-DBI codes agreement as favorable, reflecting self-efficacy in mechanical plaque removal, modern preventive principles emphasize that such confidence should coexist with recognition of the benefits of fluoride toothpaste [47, 48]. In contrast, preclinical students more often reported taking ‘too much time’ to brush (Q19; p < 0.0001), a favorable response that may reflect enthusiasm or caution in forming routines.

The comparatively stronger performance of clinical students aligns with patterns observed in many MENA contexts and some European contexts [18, 19, 21, 26, 28, 30, 34, 46], where increased patient contact and clinical exposure appear to strengthen preventive knowledge and behaviors. However, findings from Estonia, the Czech Republic, and India [17, 19, 33] indicate that such differences are not universal, highlighting the influence of curriculum design, preventive emphasis, and cultural context. These insights reinforce the need for context-specific educational strategies that integrate early clinical exposure, reinforce practical skills, and ensure that theoretical knowledge is consistently applied in daily patient care. Regression analyses further demonstrated that clinical training was independently associated with higher HU-DBI total, knowledge, behavior, and attitude scores, reinforcing the importance of experiential learning in shaping oral health behaviors and attitudes.

Educational implications

Building on the institution’s well-established strengths, this study highlights opportunities to further enrich dental education and continue supporting the development of students’ oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. The observation that female students scored significantly higher in preventive behaviors suggests a potential benefit in reducing gender disparities through targeted strategies—such as peer-led skill-building workshops, personalized feedback, and motivational interviewing—that can help male students more effectively translate their knowledge into consistent preventive habits.

For preclinical students whose HU-DBI knowledge and behavior scores are lower than those of their clinical counterparts, providing additional early support for preventive dentistry could be advantageous. This may include structured oral hygiene training with hands-on demonstrations, a stronger focus on plaque detection, and guided practice in using plaque-disclosing agents, given their current low usage. Offering these skills early can help build confidence and competence before students progress to clinical care.

Within clinical training, integrating targeted chairside coaching could help reinforce practices that align with current evidence-based recommendations. While factors influencing self-care behaviors during this stage may vary, reinforcing supportive measures—such as promoting well-being and work–life balance—can contribute positively to student development. The school is already engaged in benchmarking its curriculum against international standards, reflecting its commitment to evidence-based and sustainable educational enhancement. In addition, continued benchmarking of student outcomes against international HU-DBI data offers a valuable way to monitor progress in oral health knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors over time. Comparative insights from regional dental schools can further contextualize local achievements and guide culturally and educationally relevant innovations.

Limitations and future directions

The primary limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which offers only a snapshot of dental students’ oral health KAB at a single point in time. This design limits the ability to observe temporal changes or assess how students’ KAB evolves throughout their academic and clinical training, thereby preventing causal inferences regarding the influence of specific educational milestones or curricular interventions. With a 45.2% response rate, the potential for nonresponse bias cannot be ruled out, as students who did not participate may have had different KAB profiles from those who responded. No formal data were collected on reasons for nonparticipation; however, potential factors may include lack of interest, academic workload, or limited engagement with online surveys. Because demographic data for nonrespondents were not available, comparisons between respondents and the total student population could not be performed, which limits the ability to quantify the extent of nonresponse bias. In addition, the use of a nonprobability, convenience-based sampling method raises the possibility of selection bias. More academically motivated students or those with greater interest in oral health may have been more likely to participate, potentially inflating positive KAB responses.

The use of a self-administered questionnaire presents another limitation, as it introduces the potential for social desirability bias. Students may have overstated positive behaviors due to awareness of professional norms, even though anonymity was assured, questions were neutrally worded, and data were stored securely. While these measures likely reduce bias, some degree of response distortion remains possible. Although regression analyses were conducted to assess the independent contributions of gender and clinical level, additional covariates, such as age and academic performance, were not collected and therefore could not be included. This approach limits the extent of multivariable adjustment and leaves open the possibility of residual confounding. No sensitivity analyses were performed, as the study relied on complete-case analysis of fully completed surveys and used validated measures without variable transformations.

Another important limitation is that the study was confined to a single institution. Although it achieved comprehensive participation across all academic years at the AAUP—the only private dental school in Palestine—the findings cannot be generalized to all Palestinian dental students. Other dental schools in Palestine, which are public institutions, may differ in curriculum design, institutional priorities, available resources, and student demographics. These differences could influence students’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these results to the national level or to other cultural and educational contexts. Future research should expand to include other Palestinian dental schools to provide a more representative picture while also adopting longitudinal designs—extending into the postgraduate period—to better capture how the KAB evolves during and after dental training. Additionally, incorporating qualitative approaches could offer richer insights into contextual and cultural factors that shape students’ oral health-related KAB, as well as whether these attributes persist in professional practice.

Conclusion

Dental students play a pivotal role as role models for oral health behaviors, influencing both their future patients and the wider community. This study revealed that dental students at the Faculty of Dentistry, AAUP in Palestine demonstrated a generally positive level of oral health awareness, as reflected in their HU-DBI scores, placing them in a favorable position within the regional context. Clinical training and female gender were significantly associated with higher HU-DBI total and behavior scores, underscoring the impact of patient-based experience and possible gender-related behavioral tendencies on preventive practices. These findings highlight the potential value of reinforcing preventive dentistry concepts and hands-on oral hygiene training early in the preclinical years to further strengthen skills before clinical transition. While the results are encouraging, they should be interpreted in light of the study’s cross-sectional design, self-reported responses, and single-institution scope, which may limit generalizability. Although the findings are derived from a single-institution context, they provide valuable baseline data that can guide curriculum development locally and inform future multi-institutional studies across Palestine.
