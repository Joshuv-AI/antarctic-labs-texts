Molecular evolution of starch structure and sugar supply dynamics during the initial fermentation stages of Jiangxiangxing Baijiu
Authors: Bohan Zhang, Yubo Yang, Derang Ni, Yang Xu, Chenchen Zhuang, Xiangli Kong, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enzymatic hydrolysis of sorghum starch is fundamental to Jiangxiangxing Baijiu production. This study investigated the molecular-level degradation of starch during the initial fermentation phases by integrating GPC-MALLS-RI and HPAEC-PAD analyses. We quantified a progressive reduction in starch molecular weight and a structural shift from high- to low-degree-of-polymerization materials. Sugar profiling revealed a distinct temporal transition: heap fermentation served as the primary saccharification hotspot, predominantly accumulating glucose alongside maltose and maltotriose, while pit fermentation was dominated by glucose consumption for ethanol production. Mass balance analysis quantified substrate conversion efficiency, showing a net consumption of 18.20 g/100 g carbohydrates (dry basis) from an initial 72.67 g/100 g, with an ethanol yield of 2.34 g/100 g. Notably, proteomic detection of key amylolytic enzymes provided evidence consistent with a major contribution of the α-amylase and glucoamylase pair. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding starch saccharification, offering a scientific basis for optimizing traditional Baijiu fermentation.Keywords: Jiangxiangxing baijiu, Starch hydrolysis, Molecular weight distribution, Chain-length distribution, Saccharification dynamics, Fermentation process optimization

Introduction

Jiangxiangxing Baijiu is distinguished among Chinese Baijiu for its complex flavor profile (Wang et al., 2024b), achieved through a unique multi-round solid-state fermentation process (Niu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2021). This traditional process relies heavily on the sequential microbial and enzymatic breakdown of starch-rich raw materials, primarily sorghum grains (Li et al., 2024b). The initial phase—encompassing grain feeding (Xiasha and Zaosha) through the first-round fermentation—is particularly critical (Wang et al., 2024a). It establishes essential microbiological and biochemical conditions required for subsequent fermentations, particularly the third to fifth rounds (Da Hui Jiu), which are most productive for ethanol and characteristic aroma (Liu et al., 2025). Therefore, a molecular-level understanding of this primary fermentation phase is vital for elucidating the fundamental mechanisms governing the entire Jiangxiangxing Baijiu production system.

Starch, the principal carbon source, is enzymatically hydrolyzed into fermentable sugars to fuel microbial growth, ethanol production, and flavor synthesis (Ren et al., 2023). In Jiangxiangxing Baijiu, this occurs via a complex simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) system involving Daqu fermentation, heap fermentation, and pit fermentation (Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025b; Yuan et al., 2025). A critical challenge in the foundational Xiasha and Zaosha stages is balancing this hydrolysis: sufficient sugars must be released to initiate vigorous microbial metabolism, yet certain polymeric structures must be retained to support prolonged fermentation in subsequent rounds. However, current production practices remain largely empirical due to a lack of comprehensive, molecular-level datasets mapping the degradation process from native granules to fermentable sugars. On a scientific level, although major hydrolytic enzymes, such as α-amylase, β-amylase, and glucoamylase, have been identified (Bai et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2022), and recent studies have revealed complex microbial-enzyme interplays (Yang et al., 2024b; Zhu et al., 2023a), as well as advanced our understanding of static fine structural changes in starch (Li et al., 2024b; Yang et al., 2024a), previous research has primarily focused on bulk compositional analyses (Wang et al., 2021) or microbial community dynamics in isolation (Li et al., 2024a). How dynamic structural shifts are spatiotemporally coupled with enzymatic hydrolysis remains to be fully established.

To bridge the gap between empirical practice and mechanistic understanding, this study elucidates the structural evolution and hydrolysis dynamics of starch during the initial fermentation phases (Xiasha to the first round) of Jiangxiangxing Baijiu. Integrating GPC-MALLS-RI and HPAEC-PAD analyses, our specific objectives are to: (1) quantitatively track temporal changes in starch molecular weight and chain-length distribution; (2) correlate structural transformations with the kinetics of fermentable sugar generation; (3) identify key transitional phases in saccharification; and (4) establish a mass balance for substrate conversion efficiency. This approach provides novel molecular insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of starch degradation, establishing a theoretical basis for the precise optimization of this traditional technology.

Materials and methods

Samples

Jiupei samples were collected from a Jiangxiangxing Baijiu distillery in Renhuai City, Guizhou Province, during the 2025 production season. To ensure representative coverage of the production facility, one independent production group was selected from each of three different workshops to serve as biological replicates. Crucially, to maintain data consistency, all subsequent physicochemical, chromatographic, and proteomic analyses discussed in this manuscript were exclusively performed on these three cross-workshop groups, without mixing with any other plant-wide datasets. Sampling was conducted across key stages: Xiasha, Zaosha, and the first fermentation cycle. For each cycle, samples were collected both pre- and post-steaming. Heap fermentation samples were obtained by collecting material from the top, middle, and bottom layers, followed by thorough homogenization, on days 4 and 7. Pit fermentation samples were collected at a depth of 1.5 m on days 4, 7, 14, and at the endpoint of fermentation. All samples were immediately frozen at −40 °C and transported to the laboratory on dry ice until analysis.

Chemicals and reagents

Methanol (chromatographic grade), glucose (≥99.9%), sodium acetate, and sodium hydroxide (IC grade) were purchased from Merck (Germany). Standards of maltose (≥99.9%), maltotriose (≥99.9%), maltotetraose (≥99.9%), and maltopentaose (≥99.9%) were obtained from Shanghai YuanYe Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). Ultra-pure water was prepared using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Molecular weight distribution analysis by GPC-MALLS-RI

The molecular weight distributions of starch and its degradation products were analyzed using gel permeation chromatography coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering and refractive index detection (GPC-MALLS-RI), following an established method (Xu et al., 2025b). The column temperature was maintained at 80 °C, and the mobile phase consisted of 7 mg/mL LiBr in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to ensure complete dissolution. Briefly, samples were dissolved in the mobile phase, heated in a boiling water bath with stirring for 2 h, and then stirred continuously at room temperature overnight. The solution was centrifuged at 14,000 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm nylon membrane before injection.

The system was equipped with an 18-angle laser light scattering detector (DAWN HELEOS II, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA) and a differential refractive index detector (Optilab T-rEX) (Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA). Separation was achieved using two PSS GRAM analytical columns (100 Å and 10,000 Å, PSS GmbH, Mainz, Germany) connected in series and maintained at 80 °C. The mobile phase was 7 mg/mL LiBr in DMSO, filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon membrane, and delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using ASTRA software (version 7.0, Wyatt Technology Corporation, USA). The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) was determined using the second-order Berry fitting method, and the polydispersity (Ð) was calculated accordingly. A specific refractive index increment (dn/dc) value of 0.074 mL/g was used for starch in DMSO.

Sugar extraction and HPAEC-PAD analysis

Sugar extraction

Sugars were extracted from the jiupei matrix by boiling twice with 45% (v/v) aqueous methanol at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:4 (g mL−1) for 10 min per cycle.

HPAEC-PAD analysis

For the optimized procedure, 5 g of jiupei was homogenized with 20 mL of 45% (v/v) aqueous methanol and extracted for 20 min at room temperature with shaking. The extract was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered (0.45 μm nylon membrane) before analysis.

Sugar analysis was conducted on an ICS-5000+ HPAEC-PAD system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following a method from the literature (Rohrer, 2021) with modifications. Separation was achieved on a CarboPac PA100 analytical column (4 × 250 mm) and guard column at 30 °C. The injection volume was 20 μL. Elution was performed using a gradient program with two eluents: (A) 150 mM NaOH and (B) 1 M sodium acetate in 150 mM NaOH. The gradient was as follows: 0–1.3 min, 93% A + 7% B; 1.3–10 min, linear gradient to 82% A + 18% B; 10–19 min, linear gradient to 78% A + 22% B; 19–20 min, return to initial conditions, followed by a 10 min re-equilibration. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. Quantification was based on external calibration curves of authentic standards. The calibration parameters for all saccharide species satisfy the requirements for quantitative analysis: glucose, maltotetraose, and maltopentaose exhibited correlation coefficients of 0.999, while maltose and maltotriose achieved 0.998, demonstrating excellent linearity. The limits of detection (0.9–7.0 μg/L) and quantification (2.9–23.4 μg/L) confirm adequate sensitivity for trace-level determination. Furthermore, spike recovery experiments yielded satisfactory recovery rates ranging from 99.0% to 109.5%, with intra-day relative standard deviations (RSDs) of 1.04%–3.58% and inter-day RSDs of 1.30%–6.35%, confirming the accuracy and precision of the method.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences among means were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test (p < 0.05) using SPSS Au (https://spssau.com/). For time-series-related analyses, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient is employed to mitigate assumptions of linearity and independence. Figures were generated using Origin 9.0 (OriginLab Inc., USA).

Proteomic analysis of Amylolytic enzymes

Protein extraction and digestion

Frozen Jiupei samples were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Proteins were extracted using a lysis buffer containing 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 8 M urea, followed by sonication in an ice-water bath. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. For digestion, 100 μg of protein was reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 37 °C for 1 h and alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide (IAM) in the dark at room temperature for 45 min. The sample was diluted with ammonium bicarbonate to adjust the pH to 8.0 and digested with trypsin at an enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:50 (w/w) at 37 °C overnight. Digestion was terminated by adding 0.1% formic acid (FA).

Peptide desalting and fractionation

Peptides were desalted using a C18 solid-phase extraction cartridge. The cartridge was activated with 100% acetonitrile and equilibrated with 0.1% FA. After sample loading and washing, peptides were eluted with 70% acetonitrile and lyophilized. The dried peptides were fractionated by high-pH reverse-phase HPLC using a Waters BEH C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) on an L-3000 HPLC system. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (2% acetonitrile, 98% water, pH 10 adjusted with ammonia) and solvent B (98% acetonitrile, 2% water, pH 10). Fractions were collected every minute and pooled into six fractions.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Peptides were reconstituted in solvent A (100% water, 0.1% FA) and centrifuged at 14,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. A 1 μg aliquot of the supernatant was injected for analysis. LC-MS/MS was performed on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer coupled with a nano-LC system. Peptides were separated on a C18 column using a gradient of solvent A (100% water, 0.1% FA) and solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% FA) [refer to Table S1 for gradient details]. Mass spectra were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. Full MS scans were recorded at a resolution of 120,000 with a scan range of m/z 350–2000, an AGC target of Standard, and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. MS/MS spectra were acquired in “Top Speed” mode with a normalized collision energy of 30% and an AGC target of Standard. The maximum injection time for MS/MS was set to Auto. Raw data files were generated for subsequent analysis.

Database search, functional annotation, and differential protein identification

Raw data files were processed using MaxQuant (version 1.6.17.0). Data acquisition was performed via a data-independent acquisition (DIA) strategy, in which the full m/z range was systematically partitioned into overlapping isolation windows; all precursor ions within each window were co-fragmented and their corresponding fragment ion spectra recorded to enable comprehensive protein identification and label-free quantification. A spectral library was constructed by integrating data-dependent acquisition (DDA) runs—acquired from pooled sample aliquots—with DIA datasets, and this integrated library was subsequently employed for peptide-spectrum matching and quantitative analysis in MaxQuant. Database searches were conducted against a custom metaproteomic database generated de novo from assembled metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequences derived exclusively from the experimental samples. Search parameters were rigorously defined as follows: trypsin/P was specified as the digestion enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages; false discovery rates (FDRs) were controlled at ≤1% at both the peptide-spectrum match (PSM) and protein levels; minimum peptide length was set to seven amino acids; carbamidomethylation of cysteine was designated as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation as a variable modification. This workflow yielded confident identification of 3358 unique peptides mapping to 935 non-redundant proteins (FDR ≤ 1%). Protein relative abundance was estimated using MaxQuant's label-free quantification (LFQ) intensity algorithm. Functional annotation was performed by BLASTP alignment of identified protein sequences against multiple reference databases—including KEGG, eggNOG, and the CAZy (Carbohydrate-Active enZymes) database—with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−5 and coverage ≥50%. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using statistical criteria of FDR ≤ 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1.2. For the scope of this study, we focused our discussion on the dynamics of the two primary starch-hydrolyzing enzymes, α-amylase and glucoamylase, to directly address the saccharification pathway.

Results and discussion

Progressive reduction in starch molecular weight during fermentation

The consistent reduction in weight-average molecular weight (Mw) and the shift in molecular weight distribution across all stages (Table 1, Fig. 1) clearly demonstrate the progressive enzymatic hydrolysis of starch into lower molecular weight fragments throughout Jiangxiangxing Baijiu fermentation (Yang et al., 2024a). In the Xiasha stage, raw sorghum exhibited an initial Mw of 6.78 × 107 Da. From a mass balance perspective, the subsequent increase in Mw to 8.30 × 107 Da after grain soaking is driven by the selective leaching of low-molecular-weight (LMw) soluble components. During this process, the absolute mass of high-molecular-weight (HMw) starch polymers remains conserved in the solid matrix, while the total dry weight of the solid sample decreases. Because the weight-average molecular weight is mathematically defined as a mass-weighted average relative to the total dry mass, the proportional distribution inherently changes: the mass fraction of the HMw polymers increases strictly corresponding to the equivalent decrease in the mass fraction of the leached LMw components. A subsequent decrease was observed before steaming (Mw = 7.62 × 107 Da), and a further decrease after steaming (44.96%, Mw = 5.96 × 107 Da), concomitant with a rise in medium molecular weight substances (1 × 107–5 × 108 Da) from 14.46% to 39.84%. Since Daqu has not yet been introduced at this stage, this initial decrease cannot be attributed to enzymatic hydrolysis. Instead, it is driven by thermal degradation: the high-temperature steam treatment disrupts the crystalline structure of the starch granules (gelatinization) and simultaneously induces non-enzymatic hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds under moist heat, directly cleaving the high-Mw starch chains into medium-Mw fragments. As demonstrated in previous studies on Baijiu fermentation, starch undergoes a progressive and sequential degradation process throughout fermentation (Ren et al., 2023). Subsequently, Mw continued to decrease significantly during heap and pit fermentation, reaching 2.04 × 107 Da upon completion. This sustained reduction is driven by sustained microbial enzymatic activity, reflecting the fine structural evolution of sorghum starch under solid-state conditions (Li et al., 2024b; Yang et al., 2024a).

Restructuring of starch architecture and chain-length distribution

The temporal evolution of the chain-length profile (Fig. 2) provides evidence for the coordinated action of a complex enzymatic consortium. The subtle, progressive reduction in high-DP chains (DP > 20)—with changes of only 1–5 percentage points across these stages (Figs. 2c-e)—accompanied by the sequential rise and fall of medium-DP components (DP 6–20), indicates a highly regulated and gradual hydrolysis mechanism.

Changes in starch degradation products' degree of polymerization (DP) distribution during Baijiu fermentation. The stacked bar chart shows the relative abundance of high- (DP > 20), medium- (DP 6–20), and low-DP (DP ≤ 5) materials across fermentation stages (Xiasha, Zaosha, First Round).

The initial prevalence and subsequent decline of medium-DP dextrins are highly consistent with the action pattern of endo-acting enzyme activity, most likely mediated by α-amylase (Wei et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2026; Zinck et al., 2026). Recent studies employing metagenomic and metabolomic approaches have elucidated the complex relationship between microbial succession and flavor formation. Specifically, heap fermentation is characterized by the enrichment of Weissella, Pichia, and Aspergillus, which actively secrete amylases and proteases to drive the hydrolysis of starch and proteins (Xu et al., 2025a). This proteolytic activity is critical because, in sorghum grains, endogenous protein components form a dense matrix that encapsulates starch granules, creating a physical barrier that restricts enzymatic accessibility (Huang et al., 2025). Consequently, the efficiency of starch hydrolysis is intrinsically linked to the degradation of this protein-starch complex, where proteases play a synergistic role in exposing starch substrates to amylolytic attack. Fundamentally, the catalytic mechanisms of endo-glycosidases are structurally constrained to recognize and cleave internal α-1,4 glycosidic bonds within the amorphous regions of starch, a process that inherently generates intermediate-sized dextrins rather than monosaccharides (Morais et al., 2023). This endo-hydrolytic pattern rapidly reduces polymer size, generating intermediate dextrins (Ali et al., 2025). Their subsequent decrease indicates further hydrolysis as fermentation progresses.

The marked accumulation of low-DP sugars (DP ≤ 5) in later fermentation stages clearly demonstrates the increasing involvement of exo-acting enzymes. In contrast to the internal cleavage of endo-acting enzymes, the mechanistic signature of exo-glycosidases involves sequential hydrolysis from the non-reducing chain ends, a reactivity pattern that directly dictates the release of low-DP sugars such as glucose or maltose (Morais et al., 2023). In pure enzyme systems, this is often attributed to β-amylase, which specifically produces maltose (DP2) from the non-reducing ends of starch chains, alongside a reported allosteric synergy with α-amylase (Wei et al., 2024b). Consistent with recent metaproteomic profiling of Jiangxiangxing Daqu, our data also lacked evidence for β-amylase (Xia et al., 2022). This indicates that, in situ, the classic α/β-amylase synergy model is likely superseded by other exo-acting mechanisms. Beyond the absent β-amylase, interactions between different types of exo-acting enzymes may further reshape the starch architecture. For instance, studies on combined enzymatic modification have suggested a potential synergy involving α-glucosidase (Gui et al., 2021). While α-glucosidase can hydrolyze α-1,4 linkages to generate glucose, it may also exhibit transglycosylation activity, transferring a glucose unit to form new α-1,6 glycosidic branches (Jia et al., 2024). Nevertheless, without the contribution of β-amylase, the massive accumulation of low-DP sugars must be predominantly driven by another highly active exo-enzyme: glucoamylase. Classically, α-amylase exhibits strict substrate specificity for α-1,4 linkages, theoretically leaving α-1,6-linked branch points intact and necessitating auxiliary debranching enzymes like pullulanase to prevent limit dextrin accumulation (Yang et al., 2026). However, contrary to this theoretical expectation of branch-point steric hindrance, our DP profiles demonstrated a continuous and dramatic reduction in high-DP materials without significant limit dextrin accumulation (Fig. 2).

Notably, the reduction in high-DP debranched samples reflects the effective breakdown of the starch backbone itself. This suggests that the primary drivers of starch degradation are the endo-acting activity of α-amylase and the overwhelmingly exo-acting activity of glucoamylase. The synergy between these two detected enzymes—α-amylase rapidly creating numerous new chain ends, and glucoamylase processively releasing glucose from these ends—creates an efficient system for starch utilization, functionally compensating for the absence of β-amylase.

This sequential enzymatic activity, reflected in changing DP profiles, appears optimized for the fermentation environment. Initial endo-hydrolysis sustains microbial growth, while subsequent exo-hydrolysis provides specific sugars for alcohol and flavor formation. Crucially, this hypothesized temporal regulation is supported by our targeted proteomic analysis of key hydrolytic enzymes, indicating a shift towards a predominant α-amylase–glucoamylase pathway rather than the classic α/β-amylase synergy. During the Xiasha stage, the abundance of key amylolytic enzymes exhibited distinct dynamics. Specifically, the LFQ intensity of glucoamylase peaked at (1.30 ± 0.49) × 109, while α-amylase reached (1.00 ± 0.01) × 108 (individual data points of the three biological replicates are provided in Table S2). Subsequently, to evaluate the functional outcome of these enzymatic activities, we tracked sugar profiles from Xiasha to the first round (Fig. 3). This robust expression of the α-amylase/glucoamylase pair aligns perfectly with the vigorous “saccharification-dominant” environment, explaining how low-DP sugars accumulate efficiently without β-amylase. Conversely, upon entering the pit fermentation stage, the abundances of these critical enzymes exhibited a sharp decline that dropping to ∼4.80 × 108 for glucoamylase and ∼ 4.10 × 107 for α-amylase in the 1st-round 4-day pit. This significant downregulation is consistent with validates the metabolic transition from active starch degradation to a “fermentation-dominant” phase, where the previously accumulated sugars are consumed rather than newly generated.

Dynamic abundance profiles of core amylolytic enzymes during early fermentation.

Therefore, by directly linking the observed structural remodeling of starch to the quantified proteomic expression of glucoamylase and α-amylase, this study bridges the gap between substrate architecture and microbial functionality. The proteomic evidence suggests that the α-amylase/glucoamylase axis acts as the dominant functional driver, functionally compensating for the absence of β-amylase. However, we acknowledge that proteomic heatmap analysis reflects relative abundance rather than absolute enzymatic activity; thus, the current data cannot entirely exclude the auxiliary contributions of low-abundance exo-acting or debranching enzymes—such as pullulanase, isoamylase, or maltogenic amylase—in facilitating α-1,6 linkage cleavage. Consequently, the observed starch degradation dynamics in this ecosystem are best described as primarily driven by the highly expressed α-amylase and glucoamylase pair, rather than definitively excluding the involvement of minor, synergistic enzymatic pathways.

Dynamics of enzymatic Saccharification and metabolic shift

Using HPAEC-PAD, we tracked sugar profiles from Xiasha to the first round (Fig. 4). Starch hydrolysis yielded oligosaccharides, primarily glucose, maltose, and maltotriose, with smaller amounts of maltotetraose and maltopentaose. Glucose was predominant (70–80% of the total detected small sugars). This specific sugar profile is known to critically regulate the microbial metabolic diversity in Baijiu fermentation (Wang et al., 2021). Total soluble sugar content increased across stages: first round > Zaosha > Xiasha, indicating progressive starch degradation and utilization over successive cycles.

Dynamics of sugar profiles and metabolic shifts across Xiasha, Zaosha, and the first fermentation round.

These observations align with the molecular weight and chain-length distribution data, which showed a rapid reduction in high- Mw starch (Fig. 1) and a concurrent increase-then-decrease in medium- Mw /DP fragments (Fig. 2). The initial rise in oligosaccharides is consistent with α-amylase action, a major endo-amylase in Daqu (Zhu et al., 2023b). We infer that α-amylase and glucoamylase act simultaneously during heap fermentation. However, direct hydrolysis of native starch by exo-acting glucoamylase is inefficient. The main process likely involves initial degradation by α-amylase, generating dextrins and oligosaccharides that create abundant non-reducing ends, thereby enhancing subsequent glucoamylase efficiency (Bai et al., 2024). This coordination explains efficient fermentable sugar generation, with the high glucose proportion further supporting the crucial role of glucoamylase.

As fermentation transitioned to the anaerobic pit stage, a distinct metabolic shift occurred. Consistent with the proteomic evidence of maximal enzyme expression, Fig. 4 demonstrates that glucose and oligosaccharide contents reached their absolute peak immediately prior to pit entry. This marked accumulation definitively validates the heap stage as the “saccharification peak” of the process. The subsequent significant decrease in these sugars during pit fermentation does not contradict this peak; rather, it represents the expected metabolic transition into the “fermentation-dominant” phase, where the accumulated sugars are rapidly consumed by microorganisms (Tu et al., 2022)(e.g., yeast, lactic acid bacteria) for ethanol and flavor precursor synthesis. This shift underscores the dynamic nature of Jiangxiangxing Baijiu fermentation and is critical for balanced ethanol and flavor production.

These sugar profile dynamics reflect enzymatic degradation patterns and illustrate the metabolic adaptation of microbial community to changing conditions. The sequential, synergistic actions of amylolytic enzymes ensure efficient substrate utilization.

Heap fermentation: The Saccharification hotspot

In the heap fermentation stage, correlation analysis revealed moderate positive correlations among individual sugars (e.g., glucose-maltose: 0.39; glucose-maltotriose: 0.29), reflecting their synchronous accumulation. Interestingly, while maltose showed negligible correlation with starch (−0.06), glucose exhibited a distinct negative correlation with starch (−0.52), reflecting the expected substrate-product dynamic where starch depletion drives glucose accumulation. Contrary to typical fermentation profiles, sugars showed negligible to weak correlations with ethanol (e.g., glucose-ethanol: −0.01; total sugar-ethanol: −0.25). This lack of a positive coupling indicates that sugar accumulation had not yet driven massive ethanol production, firmly establishing heap fermentation as a “saccharification-dominant” phase, which aligns with the specific metabolic optimization strategies identified in recent heap fermentation mode studies (Yuan et al., 2025). Furthermore, the most striking correlations involved acid metabolism: total acid exhibited a remarkably strong negative correlation with starch (−0.87) and an extremely strong positive correlation with acetic acid (0.96), suggesting that early organic acid accumulation is closely coupled with starch degradation activity.

Pit fermentation: The metabolic shift to fermentation dominance

In the pit fermentation stage, correlation analysis reveals a significant metabolic shift from the previous heap phase, characterized by a transition from “saccharification-dominant” to “ethanol and acid co-production” dynamics. Unlike the strong positive correlations observed in heap fermentation, the correlations between individual sugars and starch weakened into slight negative territories (e.g., glucose-starch: −0.30; maltose-starch: −0.30). This indicates that starch hydrolysis is no longer the primary driver of sugar accumulation; instead, sugars are being rapidly consumed as fast as they are produced. Confirming this active conversion, starch exhibited a strong negative correlation with ethanol (−0.89), reflecting the continuous “starch → sugar → ethanol” consumption pathway. A particularly noteworthy finding is the profound metabolic coupling within the conventional parameters: ethanol exhibited an exceptionally strong positive correlation with total acid (0.97) and acetic acid (0.91). This directly contradicts the traditional view of metabolic competition between alcohol and acid production; rather, it suggests a highly synergistic relationship within the sealed pit environment. Conversely, both residual starch and total sugars showed weak negative correlations with acetic acid, implying that acetic acid accumulation is closely associated with the active fermentation phase rather than residual unfermented substrates. Overall, these specific correlation characteristics clearly demonstrate that pit fermentation is a complex phase defined by rapid substrate consumption, tightly coupled ethanol-acid generation, and a distinct metabolic network from the heap stage.

From molecular insights to process understanding

The correlation analysis links the precise molecular transformations—starch polymer degradation, oligosaccharide dynamics, glucose predominance—directly to macroscopic fermentation parameters. It confirms that the proposed sequential enzymatic mechanism is the fundamental driver of the entire process. These findings provide a scientific basis for process optimization using molecular indicators. For instance, monitoring the oligosaccharide-to-glucose ratio during heap fermentation could serve as a precise indicator of saccharification efficiency, potentially improving consistency and yield.

Starch mass balance and conversion efficiency

To elucidate the industrial relevance of the fermentation process, a mass balance analysis was conducted to trace the metabolic fate of starch and saccharides (Table 2). Quantifying carbon conversion and tracking substrate fate via mass balance is widely recognized as a critical metric for characterizing the industrial viability of solid-state fermentation processes (Li et al., 2021). Based on the dry matter analysis, the total carbohydrate content (starch and reducing sugars) decreased significantly from 72.67 g/100 g at the pit-entry stage to 54.23 g/100 g at the final stage. The results reveal a two-stage conversion profile: initially, a substantial pool of fermentable sugars (glucose and oligosaccharides) served as the primary substrate, while starch was continuously hydrolyzed to replenish the sugar pool. By the end of fermentation, the net consumption of starch and sugars was 18.44 g/100 g (dry basis). This consumed substrate was primarily converted into ethanol (yield: 2.34 g/100 g) and lactic acid (yield: 2.83 g/100 g), accompanied by an estimated CO₂ evolution of ∼2.25 g/100 g associated with ethanol synthesis. Despite active fermentation, a considerable fraction of the initial starch (∼52.1 g/100 g dry basis, representing 71.7% of the initial total) remained in the residue as unhydrolyzed starch. While this high residual starch level is traditionally associated with the multi-round fermentation strategy of Jiangxiangxing Baijiu, in the absence of data from subsequent rounds, this should be stated as an indicative observation regarding a characteristic feature of the process, which requires further verification through multi-round follow-up studies. This mass balance profile quantitatively demonstrates that while the hydrolysis of starch drives the fermentation, the residual starch represents a significant untapped potential for yield optimization, providing critical data for bioprocess characterization.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the molecular degradation kinetics of sorghum starch during the initial stages of Jiangxiangxing Baijiu fermentation. Proteomic analysis provided evidence consistent with a predominant α-amylase and glucoamylase network, whose sharp downregulation is temporally consistent with the metabolic shift from “saccharification-dominant” heap fermentation to “fermentation-dominant” pit fermentation. Beyond mechanistic insights, mass balance quantified significant residual starch as untapped potential, while correlation analysis identified the oligosaccharide-to-glucose ratio as a reliable molecular reference for monitoring industrial process optimization. These findings bridge starch structural remodeling with microbial functionality, providing a solid theoretical basis for the precise regulation of traditional Baijiu brewing.
