Experiences of social participation and inclusion among primary caregivers of children with cerebral palsy in China: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Hui Liu, Xiaojia Wu, Fang Wen, Lanxia Meng, Chunying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary caregivers of children with cerebral palsy (CP) may experience challenges affecting their social participation and inclusion. However, these experiences remain insufficiently understood in the Chinese context. Understanding these experiences is important for family-centred nursing and rehabilitation support.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted with 26 primary caregivers recruited from a tertiary rehabilitation hospital in China. Semi-structured interviews were conducted face-to-face, via WeChat video call, or by telephone. Data were analysed inductively using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes, each comprising two subthemes, were developed: (1) Emotional distress and withdrawal from social life; (2) Caregiving demands restricting opportunities for participation; (3) Role expectations narrowing social identities and everyday roles; and (4) Stigma and exclusion undermining belonging and acceptance.ConclusionsIn this study, primary caregivers of children with CP described reduced social participation and limited inclusion associated with overlapping emotional, caregiving, role-related, and social influences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05314-3.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Caregivers, Social participation, Social inclusion, Nursing, Rehabilitation

Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition characterised by disorders of movement and posture. It is often accompanied by sensory, cognitive, communication, and behavioural difficulties. Children with CP frequently require ongoing rehabilitation, daily assistance, and repeated contact with health and support services. In most families, primary caregivers assume the main responsibility for coordinating treatment and providing day-to-day care. These responsibilities can affect caregivers’ emotional well-being, employment, daily routines, leisure, and social relationships.

The World Health Organization’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides a useful framework for understanding these experiences [1]. In the ICF, participation refers to involvement in life situations, and functioning is shaped within physical, social, and attitudinal environments. At the caregiver level, reduced employment, leisure, relationships, and community involvement are participation restrictions experienced by the caregiver. Although caregiver social participation is not classified as an environmental factor within the child’s ICF profile, caregiver availability, social resources, family support, and engagement with rehabilitation services may function as important contextual influences within the child’s rehabilitation environment. Through these pathways, reduced caregiver participation may indirectly affect the conditions that support children’s rehabilitation and everyday participation. However, this study did not assess child outcomes, and any relationship between caregiver participation and the child’s rehabilitation should therefore be understood as indirect and potential rather than demonstrated.

In this study, social participation refers to caregivers’ involvement in valued social roles, relationships, work, leisure, and community activities. Social inclusion refers to feeling accepted, recognised, respected, and able to belong in those settings. The two constructs overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A caregiver may be present in a social setting and still feel excluded because of stigma, judgement, or a lack of reciprocal social relationships.

Occupational balance offers a complementary caregiver-centred perspective [2]. It refers to a person’s perceived balance in the amount and variety of everyday occupations. For caregivers, these occupations may include paid work, caregiving, domestic responsibilities, rest, leisure, and social engagement. When caregiving persistently dominates daily life, valued roles related to employment, leisure, social relationships, and personal development may become interrupted or narrowed. In this study, role disruption refers to the restriction or reorganisation of valued roles as caregiving responsibilities become central to everyday life. It does not imply that caregiving inevitably replaces all other identities, but rather highlights how sustained caregiving demands may limit opportunities to maintain a balanced occupational pattern.

In mainland China, caregivers’ participation and inclusion may be shaped by sociocultural and structural conditions. Expectations concerning parental responsibility, intergenerational obligation, and self-sacrifice may encourage caregivers to prioritise the child’s needs over employment, rest, leisure, and social life [3]. Caregiving responsibilities are also frequently gendered, with mothers often assuming the primary caregiving role. Access to rehabilitation and community support varies across settings, and rural families may face particular service-related and structural barriers. Caregivers of children with disabilities may also experience public stigma, affiliate discrimination, and negative social attitudes [4]. These conditions can restrict opportunities for participation and weaken feelings of acceptance and belonging.

Existing research in China has provided important evidence on caregiver burden, fatigue, psychological distress, quality of life, social support, general caregiving experiences, service access, and perceived discrimination [5]. However, this evidence remains conceptually fragmented. Quantitative studies have largely examined burden, distress, coping, and quality of life. Existing qualitative studies of caregivers of children with CP have addressed the broader experience of caregiving. Occupational studies have mainly focused on mothers or included children with different types of disabilities. Research on discrimination has generally examined caregivers of children with disabilities as a broader group rather than caregivers of children with CP specifically. It therefore remains insufficiently understood how primary caregivers of children with CP in mainland China experience both social participation and social inclusion, and how emotional strain, caregiving demands, role disruption, and stigma interact in everyday life. This study aimed to explore these experiences and identify the factors that caregivers perceived as shaping them.

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative descriptive design to explore how primary caregivers of children with CP in China experienced social participation and social inclusion in everyday life. A qualitative descriptive design was appropriate because the study sought a clear, practice-oriented account of caregivers’ experiences and the factors shaping those experiences, grounded in participants’ own descriptions rather than in the development of a formal theory [6]. This design was well suited to generating findings that could inform family-centred nursing and rehabilitation support for caregivers in paediatric rehabilitation settings. This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist [7].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the Neuropsychiatric Rehabilitation Institute of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University, a tertiary rehabilitation hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China. The centre provides rehabilitation services for children with CP and related neurodevelopmental conditions and receives families from Wuhan and surrounding areas. Because participants were recruited through a tertiary rehabilitation setting, the sample may over-represent caregivers who were connected to specialist services and able to access hospital-based rehabilitation.

Participants were primary caregivers of children aged 2–16 years with a confirmed diagnosis of CP. Eligible participants were those who provided most of the child’s daily care and were able to participate in an interview in Mandarin. Paid caregivers and individuals with self-reported cognitive impairment or severe mental illness that would have prevented meaningful participation in an interview were excluded.

Sampling and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to recruit primary caregivers with a range of caregiving circumstances relevant to the study aim. Variation was sought in caregiver relationship to the child (for example, mother, father, or grandparent), place of residence, employment status, and child functional status, in order to capture a range of experiences of social participation and social inclusion. Potential participants were identified during routine rehabilitation visits and approached by members of the research team, who explained the study and provided verbal and written information. A total of 34 potential participants were approached. Two declined participation and six withdrew before final inclusion. The reasons given for declining or withdrawing mainly concerned privacy and the practical burden of repeated travel to the hospital. Two of the six participants who withdrew had taken part in pilot testing of the interview guide; their pilot interview data were not included in the final dataset. The final sample comprised 26 primary caregivers.

Recruitment and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, and interviews continued until the dataset was considered sufficiently rich and diverse to address the study aim and no substantially new issues relevant to caregivers’ participation and inclusion were emerging from the interviews. The decision to stop recruitment was made through regular discussion within the research team, with attention to the depth, variation, and relevance of participants’ accounts.

Data collection

Data were collected from February to May 2025. The 26 initial interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private room at the rehabilitation centre (n = 10), via WeChat video call (n = 12), or by telephone (n = 4), according to participants’ preferences and practical feasibility. Participants interviewed remotely were asked to choose a quiet location where they could speak privately. No family members or other non-research persons were present during any interview session. Face-to-face interviews allowed fuller observation of non-verbal cues, whereas WeChat video interviews provided partial visual information and telephone interviews relied primarily on vocal cues and verbal clarification. Telephone interviews provided fewer opportunities to observe non-verbal expressions and contextual information; however, participants were encouraged to elaborate through additional verbal probing and clarification prompts. Across the three interview modalities, participants were able to describe their experiences in sufficient depth to address the study aims. Potential differences related to interview modality were considered during reflexive discussions and interpretation of the data.

A semi-structured interview guide comprising open-ended questions was developed specifically for this study based on the study aim, relevant literature, and discussions within the research team. The guide explored the effects of caregiving on daily routines and social life, changes in social relationships and activities, experiences in public and community settings, barriers and facilitators to social participation, experiences of judgement or differential treatment, and perceived support needs. The guide was pilot tested with two caregivers who subsequently withdrew from the study; their pilot interview data were excluded from the final dataset. An English-language version of the interview guide is provided in Supplementary File 2.

The guide comprised six core questions that formed the basis of all initial interviews. The order of the questions was adapted to the conversational flow, and participants were encouraged to introduce issues that they considered important to their own experiences. The six core questions were: (1) How has caring for your child affected your daily routines and social life? (2) Can you describe any changes in your social relationships or activities since your child’s diagnosis? (3) How do you feel when you are in public or community settings with your child? (4) What makes it easier or more difficult for you to participate in social activities? (5) Have you experienced any situations in which you felt judged or treated differently because of your child’s condition? and (6) What kinds of support or help would be most useful for you?

Open-ended exploratory probes and clarification prompts were tailored to participants’ responses to encourage elaboration, elicit concrete examples, and clarify their intended meanings. Examples included “Could you tell me more about that?”, “Can you give a specific example?”, “What happened next?”, and “How did that experience affect your daily activities or relationships?” The same six core questions were used in all initial interviews across the three interview modes, whereas follow-up probes were adapted to each participant’s account and the flow of the conversation.

All interviews were conducted in Mandarin and audio-recorded with participants’ written informed consent. Initial interviews lasted approximately 25–40 min. Four of the 26 participants completed one follow-up interview because the research team considered that information relevant to the study aim had not been sufficiently explored during the initial interview; these follow-up interviews were used to clarify and elaborate their accounts. Although interview duration varied according to participants’ availability and willingness to elaborate, the interview material generated sufficient narrative detail relevant to the study aims. Draft transcripts were generated using the speech-to-text function in WPS Office. The speech-to-text processing was conducted locally, and interview audio files were not uploaded to an external cloud-based transcription service. Each draft transcript was subsequently checked line by line against the corresponding audio recording and corrected where necessary.

Because the analysis focused on the semantic content of participants’ accounts rather than conversational or interactional features, non-substantive fillers, repeated words, prolonged pauses, and incomplete utterances were removed where their removal did not alter the substantive meaning. Sentence structure was also standardised for readability. All editorial changes were checked against the original audio recording to retain participants’ intended meanings. Paralinguistic and conversational features were not treated as analytic data. Identifying information was removed, and each participant was assigned a study code before the cleaned Mandarin transcripts were analysed.

For the English-language manuscript, selected quotations were translated from Mandarin into English by a bilingual member of the research team and checked against the original Mandarin transcript by a second bilingual team member for semantic equivalence and readability.

At the time of interview, caregivers provided sociodemographic information, including gender, relationship to the child, age, educational level, employment status, current residence, household registration (hukou) status, and monthly household income. The child’s age was reported by the caregiver, whereas the recorded CP subtype and Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) level were extracted from hospital clinical records.

Research team and reflexivity

All initial and follow-up interviews were led by HL, with XW present at each interview as a co-researcher. XW’s role was to observe the interaction and document contextual and non-verbal information. Both HL and XW were women.

Both researchers held intermediate-level professional nursing titles in China. Both were enrolled in master’s programmes in nursing. HL had 11 years of clinical nursing experience. XW had 9 years of clinical nursing experience. Their clinical experience included neurology and paediatric rehabilitation. Both had worked with children with cerebral palsy and their families.

Both researchers had completed a national training programme in qualitative research, including qualitative interviewing and thematic analysis. Both had previous experience conducting semi-structured qualitative interviews. HL’s master’s research project also used a qualitative design.

The wider research team included FW and LM, both physicians, and CL, a nurse who served as the study supervisor and contributed to reflexive discussions during theme development. The multidisciplinary composition of the team brought both nursing and medical perspectives to reflexive discussions and interpretation of the data.

Neither HL nor XW had a prior personal, nursing, or therapeutic relationship with any participant, and neither was involved in participant recruitment. HL and XW were not responsible for the clinical care or treatment decisions of the participating children. Before each interview, participants were informed of HL’s and XW’s nursing backgrounds, their roles as researchers in the study, and the purpose of the research. Participants were also informed that their decision to participate and the content of their responses would not affect their child’s treatment or rehabilitation services.

HL and XW were familiar with rehabilitation terminology and caregiving contexts through their clinical work. However, this familiarity could have influenced both the questions asked and the interpretation of participants’ accounts. To address this risk, HL adopted a reflexive interviewing approach and used neutral, open-ended probes grounded in participants’ own descriptions rather than clinical assumptions. During interviews, the depth and direction of probing were adjusted according to participants’ emotional responses, narrative completeness, and the contextual relevance of their experiences. Participants were given sufficient time to reflect and were encouraged to provide specific examples, explain the personal meanings of their experiences, and describe how these experiences affected their everyday lives. Paraphrasing and summary checks were used where clarification was required. Clinical advice, reassurance based on professional expertise, and evaluative comments regarding participants’ caregiving decisions were avoided during interviews.

XW documented contextual and interactional observations during each interview. In face-to-face and WeChat video interviews, field notes included salient pauses, facial expressions, and gestures. During telephone interviews, observations were limited to vocal and interactional cues, including changes in tone, hesitation, and silence. These observations were used to contextualise participants’ accounts and inform subsequent reflexive discussions within the research team. They were not coded or analysed as an independent data source. After each interview, HL and XW completed field and reflexive notes. The notes documented the interview context, initial impressions, issues requiring further clarification, and emerging analytic ideas. During data collection and preliminary analysis, the research team met regularly. The team reviewed the notes and examined assumptions arising from the researchers’ clinical and professional backgrounds. The team also considered alternative interpretations. These discussions informed subsequent probes and the interpretation and refinement of codes.

Data analysis

Data were analysed inductively using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to reflexive thematic analysis [8, 9]. The analysis was recursive rather than strictly linear, with movement between phases as interpretations developed and themes were refined.

In phase 1, familiarisation with the data, HL and XW repeatedly read all cleaned Mandarin interview transcripts to develop an overall sense of individual accounts and the dataset as a whole. Field and reflexive notes were also reviewed to retain awareness of the interview context. Follow-up interview material from four participants was considered alongside the corresponding participants’ initial interview material.

In phase 2, generating initial codes, HL and XW both engaged in data-driven coding across the dataset. Coding focused on features of participants’ accounts relevant to social participation and social inclusion while remaining open to unanticipated meanings and experiences. The researchers initially worked separately on coding and then compared their interpretations. Differences in coding emphasis or specificity were used to prompt reflexive discussion and closer engagement with the data rather than being treated as coding errors requiring a statistical measure of agreement.

In phase 3, generating initial themes, conceptually related codes were examined for broader patterns of shared meaning and organised into candidate subthemes and themes. The researchers considered how coded extracts related to one another, how candidate subthemes contributed to broader themes, and how these patterns addressed the study aim.

In phase 4, reviewing and developing themes, candidate subthemes and themes were checked against both the coded extracts and the complete dataset. HL and XW discussed alternative interpretations with the wider research team, including CL as the study supervisor. Candidate themes or subthemes were revised, combined, separated, or discarded where necessary to improve internal coherence and distinction between themes.

In phase 5, refining, defining, and naming themes, the central organising concept and scope of each theme and subtheme were clarified. Theme and subtheme names were revised to reflect participants’ accounts and the study focus on social participation and inclusion. This process resulted in four themes, each comprising two subthemes.

In phase 6, producing the report, representative quotations were selected to illustrate the relationship between participants’ accounts and the analytic interpretation. NVivo version 12.0, Microsoft Excel, and Microsoft Word were used to support data organisation and analytic documentation. Records of coding decisions, reflexive discussions, and theme development were retained as an audit trail. Trustworthiness

Several procedures were used to support credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [10]. Credibility was supported through concurrent data collection and preliminary analysis, repeated engagement with the Mandarin transcripts, and regular reflexive discussions within the research team. Each draft transcript was checked line by line against the corresponding audio recording before analysis. Theme and subtheme development considered both recurring patterns and divergent accounts, and representative quotations were included to illustrate the connection between participants’ accounts and the analytic interpretations.

Dependability was supported by documenting the analytic process across Braun and Clarke’s six phases. Records of coding decisions, reflexive discussions, field and reflexive notes, and successive revisions to subthemes and themes were retained as an audit trail. Both HL and XW engaged in coding and theme development, and differences in interpretation were explored through discussion and return to the relevant data rather than through formal inter-coder reliability testing.

Confirmability was supported through reflexive attention to how the researchers’ clinical and professional backgrounds could shape interpretation. Candidate subthemes and themes were repeatedly checked against coded extracts and the complete dataset, and the wider research team considered alternative interpretations during theme development.

Full interview transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction. During the interviews, paraphrasing and verbal summary checks were used where clarification of meaning was required. Post-interview participant feedback was obtained from five participants, who reviewed a summary of the preliminary themes and the draft manuscript. They were invited to comment on whether the themes, findings, and interpretations reflected their experiences. Their feedback provided an additional perspective on the developing interpretation, and no substantive concerns were raised regarding how their experiences were represented.

Transferability was supported by detailed reporting of the study setting, recruitment procedures, interview context, and caregiver and child characteristics. Purposive sampling sought variation in caregiver relationship, place of residence, employment status, and child functional status. These details allow readers to judge the relevance of the findings to other caregiver and rehabilitation contexts.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. WDRY2025-K292) prior to participant recruitment and data collection. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations and with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants provided written informed consent before participation, including consent for audio recording. Participation was voluntary, confidentiality was protected, and all identifying information was removed from transcripts before analysis.

Results

Participant characteristics

Twenty-six primary caregivers of children with CP participated in this study. Participants varied in age, educational background, employment status, place of residence, and household registration. Most participants were mothers, and many had reduced or left employment to assume primary caregiving responsibilities. Most children were classified as ambulatory (GMFCS I–III), although a smaller number had greater mobility limitations. Because all participants were recruited through a tertiary rehabilitation hospital, the sample mainly reflected the experiences of caregivers who were connected to specialist rehabilitation services. These characteristics provide context for interpreting the accounts, particularly those relating to employment disruption, reduced participation, and stigma. Overview of themes

Analysis generated four interrelated themes, each comprising two subthemes. Theme 1, emotional distress and withdrawal from social life, comprised (1.1) emotional shock and ongoing psychological strain and (1.2) withdrawal and reduced confidence in social interaction. Theme 2, caregiving demands restricting opportunities for participation, comprised (2.1) time, physical demands, and loss of personal flexibility and (2.2) treatment-related financial and logistical strain. Theme 3, role expectations narrowing social identities and everyday roles, comprised (3.1) loss of self and narrowing of valued identities and (3.2) caregiving duty, gendered roles, and self-sacrifice. Theme 4, stigma and exclusion undermining belonging and acceptance, comprised (4.1) overt stigma and public judgement and (4.2) anticipated stigma, avoidance, and threatened belonging.

Theme 1: Emotional distress and withdrawal from social life

Caregivers described emotional distress as closely intertwined with withdrawal from social life. Two subthemes captured this pattern: emotional shock and ongoing psychological strain, and withdrawal and reduced confidence in social interaction.

Subtheme 1.1: Emotional shock and ongoing psychological strain

Caregivers described their child’s diagnosis and ongoing care needs as emotionally overwhelming. Feelings of shock, sadness, anxiety, uncertainty, and emotional exhaustion were not limited to the early period after diagnosis but could continue throughout daily caregiving.

Participants described the diagnosis as a major disruption that left them feeling hopeless or emotionally numb:

It felt like the sky was falling. I’m completely speechless, I think there’s no hope left. (Participant 1)

I stayed alone for 1 day without eating or drinking, as if I had thought a lot, but in fact my head was blank, and realized that I had no other choice but to accept reality. (Participant 8)

Subtheme 1.2: Withdrawal and reduced confidence in social interaction

Emotional strain also shaped caregivers’ willingness and confidence to engage with other people. Participants described becoming less willing to respond to invitations, initiate conversations, attend family or community activities, or remain engaged in social interactions that had once felt routine.

For some, ongoing emotional exhaustion made social interaction feel difficult or exhausting:

I just keep going every day. If I pause and think about it … I don’t think I’d have the courage to deal with it anymore. (Participant 11)

These accounts suggested that distress did not remain a private emotional experience. It also reduced confidence in social interaction and encouraged retreat from wider social life.

Theme 2: Caregiving demands restricting opportunities for participation

Caregivers described practical caregiving demands as restricting opportunities for work, rest, leisure, and social contact. Two subthemes reflected these constraints: time, physical demands, and loss of personal flexibility, and treatment-related financial and logistical strain.

Subtheme 2.1: Time, physical demands, and loss of personal flexibility

Daily care was described as highly time-consuming and physically demanding. Intensive rehabilitation routines, constant supervision, disrupted sleep, and the child’s dependence reduced caregivers’ time, energy, and flexibility for employment, rest, friendships, family visits, and social activities.

Participants described having almost no personal time because their child depended on them for nearly everything:

It’s like every minute is a fight. My child needs me for everything, and I don’t even get a second to catch my breath. (Participant 13)

Subtheme 2.2: Treatment-related financial and logistical strain

Travel for rehabilitation, treatment-related relocation, and financial pressure created additional barriers to participation. Some families had to reorganise everyday routines around treatment access, which made it more difficult to maintain employment, social ties, and ordinary activities.

One caregiver described the combined effects of relocation, isolation, and financial pressure:

We moved to the city for my child’s treatment, but I was alone all day while my husband worked multiple jobs. The stress and financial pressure have been crushing. (Participant 20)

Together, these accounts suggested that participation was often reorganised around the labour and logistics of long-term care, with social activities becoming postponed, shortened, or avoided.

Theme 3: Role expectations narrowing social identities and everyday roles

Caregivers, especially mothers, described caregiving as gradually becoming central to their everyday roles and identities. Two subthemes captured this process: loss of self and narrowing of valued identities, and caregiving duty, gendered roles, and self-sacrifice.

Subtheme 3.1: Loss of self and narrowing of valued identities

Participants described difficulty maintaining previous work, social, and personal roles as caregiving came to dominate everyday life. For some, this was experienced as a loss or narrowing of their former sense of self.

It felt like my life as ‘me’ ended all of a sudden. I just started learning to take care of my child the way my parents took care of me. And somehow, we stopped being ourselves and became just ‘someone’s mom’ or ‘someone’s dad’. (Participant 9)

Others described how caregiving displaced personal priorities and time for themselves:

I had to figure out everything—cooking, rehab exercises … you name it. I had no time for myself; it was all for my child. (Participant 18)

Subtheme 3.2: Caregiving duty, gendered roles, and self-sacrifice

Participants also described strong family and moral expectations to place the child’s needs above employment, leisure, and personal interests. For some mothers, leaving work was experienced as both a practical necessity and a socially reinforced expectation of responsible caregiving.

I had no choice but to quit my job and focus entirely on caring for our child, while my husband took on all the financial responsibilities. It felt like this division of roles was necessary, even though it meant sacrificing my own career and personal life. (Participant 23)

These accounts suggested that reduced participation was shaped not only by time pressures but also by socially and familially reinforced expectations about caregiving responsibility and self-sacrifice.

Theme 4: Stigma and exclusion undermining belonging and acceptance

Caregivers described social inclusion as being undermined by both direct negative responses and the anticipation of judgement. Two subthemes captured these experiences: overt stigma and public judgement, and anticipated stigma, avoidance, and threatened belonging.

Subtheme 4.1: Overt stigma and public judgement

Participants described staring, whispering, avoidance, and derogatory labels directed at their child. These encounters made caregivers feel scrutinised and unwelcome in public and community settings.

People keep staring at my child like he’s some kind of freak. I’ve even overheard them calling him ‘stupid’ or ‘crazy.’ After a while, I just stopped reacting—I guess I became numb. (Participant 11)

Subtheme 4.2: Anticipated stigma, avoidance, and threatened belonging

Negative social responses also shaped caregivers’ expectations of future encounters. Some described feeling invisible or out of place, while others avoided public spaces because they anticipated judgement even before a direct negative interaction occurred.

Whenever we go out, people’s looks and whispers make me feel invisible. They label my child, and somehow I feel like I don’t belong anywhere. (Participant 15)

I notice people avoiding us or whispering about my child. It’s like the world thinks we’re different, and it makes me just want to stay home. (Participant 22)

These accounts suggested that inclusion involved more than physical presence in the community. For caregivers, it also involved being treated with respect and being able to enter social spaces without fear of judgement or humiliation.

Discussion

Overview of key findings

This study explored how primary caregivers of children with cerebral palsy in China experienced social participation and social inclusion in everyday life. The findings suggest that participants’ experiences of participation and inclusion were shaped by intersecting emotional, material, normative, and social conditions. The main contribution of this study is not simply that caregiving is burdensome, but that caregiving can restrict opportunities for participation and weaken a sense of belonging in everyday family, community, and public life.

Emotional distress and social withdrawal

Emotional distress was closely linked to withdrawal from social life. Participants described ongoing sadness, anxiety, uncertainty, and emotional exhaustion following their child’s diagnosis and throughout the caregiving process. This pattern is consistent with previous research showing that caregivers of children with CP often experience persistent psychological distress [11]. The present study adds to this literature by suggesting that participants experienced emotional distress as a barrier to social participation. When caregivers felt overwhelmed, they were less willing to maintain relationships, respond to invitations, or take part in everyday community life. These findings suggest that emotional support may also be relevant to efforts to support caregivers’ social participation.

Caregiving demands as barriers to participation

Participants described caregiving demands as restricting participation through material constraints. Time pressure, disrupted sleep, travel for treatment, and financial burden reduced opportunities for work, rest, leisure, and social contact [5]. In occupational balance terms, caregiving compressed the range and rhythm of daily occupations and reorganised everyday life around the child’s care schedule. Participants often described reduced participation as arising from the labour and logistics of long-term care rather than from personal preference. These findings are consistent with earlier evidence that caregiving for children with CP can reshape family routines, reduce work participation, and intensify economic stress.

Family role expectations and caregiver identity

Participants described role expectations as shaping participation through normative and identity-based constraints. Participants described caregiving as something a devoted parent was expected to prioritise above work and personal interests. These expectations made self-sacrifice appear responsible and appropriate, but they also narrowed caregivers’ social worlds and everyday roles. The findings therefore suggest that reduced participation was shaped not only by practical demands, but also by socially reinforced ideas about what a responsible caregiver should do [12]. From this perspective, role disruption refers not to the disappearance of all identities, but to the interruption or narrowing of valued roles as caregiving becomes dominant.

Stigma, exclusion, and the sense of belonging

Stigma and exclusion were central to caregivers’ experiences of social inclusion. Participants described staring, whispering, avoidance, and derogatory labels that made them feel unwelcome in public and community settings. These accounts suggest that, for participants, inclusion involved more than physical access and depended on recognition, respect, and a sense of belonging [13]. Anticipated judgement was sometimes enough to discourage outings even before any direct negative interaction occurred. In this sense, stigma affected not only whether caregivers participated socially, but also whether they felt they belonged when they did participate.

Interconnected influences on participation and inclusion

Taken together, the findings suggest that caregivers’ social participation and inclusion were shaped by the combined effects of emotional strain, intensive caregiving demands, role expectations, and stigma. These influences overlapped in everyday life. Caregivers who were already emotionally exhausted and burdened by care demands could become even less willing to enter public spaces when they anticipated negative judgement from others. At the same time, stepping back from work and social activities could reduce access to support and further narrow caregivers’ social worlds.

From an ICF-informed perspective, the caregivers’ reduced employment, leisure, and community involvement can be understood as participation restrictions at the caregiver level. These restrictions may also shape the child’s surrounding context through caregiver availability, family support, social attitudes, and access to services. However, the present study did not assess child rehabilitation outcomes, so any influence on the child should be interpreted as indirect and potential rather than demonstrated.

The findings can also be interpreted through occupational balance. Participants’ accounts suggest that caregiving compressed time for paid work, rest, leisure, and social engagement. Over time, this compression narrowed occupational variety and reduced the sense of a balanced everyday life. Rather than viewing participation and inclusion as fixed outcomes, it may be more useful to understand them as dynamic experiences shaped by the everyday demands of caregiving and by the social environment in which disability is encountered.

Implications for family-centred nursing and rehabilitation practice

These findings suggest several implications for family-centred nursing and rehabilitation practice. First, caregiver support should extend beyond child-focused rehabilitation to include attention to emotional strain, social withdrawal, and participation needs [5]. In paediatric rehabilitation settings, nurses may consider brief, routine conversations or screening to identify caregiver distress and social isolation, with referral to psychosocial support when needed.

Second, nursing practice should address the practical barriers that restrict caregivers’ participation. Nurses may help families connect with community-based rehabilitation, coordinated care pathways, respite options, peer networks, and social support resources.

Third, nursing encounters represent a key opportunity to address stigma and promote inclusion. Respectful communication, inclusive language, and acknowledgement of caregivers’ experiences may help reduce anticipated judgement and reinforce caregivers’ sense of legitimacy in clinical and community settings.

Fourth, culturally responsive care should recognise family caregiving obligations while also legitimising caregivers’ own needs for rest, social connection, and roles outside caregiving. Support for returning to social life should be gradual and individualised, rather than prescriptive.

These issues may also merit greater attention in nursing education in China, particularly in relation to disability stigma, social isolation, and the pressures placed on caregivers.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It addresses an underexplored aspect of caregiving for children with cerebral palsy in China by focusing on caregivers’ experiences of social participation and social inclusion rather than on caregiver burden alone. Drawing on primary caregivers’ accounts from a tertiary rehabilitation context, the study illustrates how emotional strain, caregiving demands, role expectations, and stigma were experienced as shaping participation and inclusion in everyday life. The study also offers nursing-relevant insights into how family-centred rehabilitation support may better respond to caregivers’ emotional, practical, and social needs.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary rehabilitation hospital in Wuhan, and the findings may therefore over-represent the experiences of caregivers who were connected to specialist services. Second, most participants were mothers, and only a very small number of fathers were included. The findings therefore primarily reflect mothers’ and other female caregivers’ perspectives, and the experiences of fathers may be underrepresented. Third, most children in the sample were ambulatory (GMFCS I–III), which may have influenced the forms of participation restriction and social exclusion most evident in the data. Caregivers of children with more severe mobility limitations may face additional barriers related to physical caregiving demands and environmental accessibility. Fourth, the use of three interview modalities may have introduced variation in access to non-verbal and contextual information. Although telephone interviews provided fewer opportunities to observe non-verbal expressions, participants were encouraged to elaborate through verbal probing. Nevertheless, differences related to interview modality should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, the transcripts were cleaned for readability, meaning that non-substantive fillers, repetitions, pauses, and incomplete utterances were not retained as analytic data. Finally, although four participants completed a follow-up interview for clarification and elaboration, the study was not designed longitudinally and therefore could not examine how caregivers’ experiences of participation and inclusion change over time or assess any relationship with child rehabilitation outcomes.

Conclusion

Primary caregivers of children with cerebral palsy in this study described their social participation and social inclusion as constrained by overlapping emotional distress, intensive caregiving demands, culturally influenced role expectations, and stigma in everyday public and community settings. Participants described these factors as limiting their opportunities to remain socially connected and as reducing their sense of belonging and acceptance. The findings highlight the importance of recognising caregivers’ participation and inclusion as part of family-centred support in rehabilitation settings. They also suggest that caregiver well-being, social connectedness, and stigma deserve greater attention in nursing and rehabilitation practice. However, the study does not provide evidence that changes in caregiver participation will directly improve child rehabilitation outcomes.
