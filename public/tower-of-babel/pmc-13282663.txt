In vivo visualization of bioactive iron and oxygen using LiON, the labile iron and oxygen notifier
Authors: Ayato Maeda, Akihiro Nita, Shoko Sashiyama, Suzu Yoshitomo, Komen Joan Jepkosgei, Yuqing Xu, Yuichiro Arima, Keiichi I. Nakayama, Kimi Araki, Toshiro Moroishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIron and oxygen are essential for numerous biological processes, yet monitoring their bioactive intracellular dynamics in vivo remains challenging due to the limitations of current detection methods. Here, we introduce the labile iron and oxygen notifier (LiON), a genetically encoded, ratiometric fluorescent reporter enabling single-cell-resolution monitoring of intracellular iron and oxygen levels in vivo. In cultured cells, LiON responds dynamically to physiologically relevant changes in iron and oxygen levels. Using LiON-knockin mice, we revealed heterogeneous distribution of iron and oxygen across tissues, cell types, and even among individual cells of the same type. Thus, LiON offers a platform for studying iron and oxygen metabolism in living organisms, providing insights into the cellular diversity of iron and oxygen utilization and their roles in physiology and disease.

Introduction

Although iron constitutes approximately 0.005% of human body weight, it plays a fundamentally important physiological role.1 In mammals, iron is essential for oxygen transport, energy production, hormone synthesis, DNA replication and repair, cell cycle regulation, and immune function.2 These functions highlight the widespread biological significance of iron, which is indispensable for survival. Notably, approximately 2% of human genes encode iron-containing proteins, and 6.5% of human enzymes are iron dependent, with iron serving as an essential cofactor.3 Additionally, cellular iron not only supports enzymatic activity but also dynamically modulates the activity of certain enzymes. For example, KDM3B, a Jumonji-C domain-containing lysine demethylase (JmjC-KDM), has relatively low affinity for iron, thus acting as an intracellular iron sensor that adjusts its function based on iron availability.4 This highlights the role of iron as an essential cofactor and regulatory element in biological processes. In addition to enzymatic regulation, iron is also involved in ferroptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and is implicated in pathological conditions such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and cancers.5 Although ferroptosis is influenced by multiple factors, iron is a key driver of this process.5 A complete understanding of intracellular iron dynamics could provide critical insights into ferroptosis and its physiological significance.6 However, one of the greatest challenges in studying iron biology is the difficulty of visualizing intracellular bioactive iron in vivo.

Currently available iron detection techniques include Perls’ stain,7 mass spectrometry (MS) imaging,8 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI),9 and fluorescence-based chemical probes.10,11 Although each method offers advantages, each has specific limitations. Perls’ staining, a histological technique that has been used for over 150 years, has low sensitivity and is restricted to fixed tissue sections.12 MS imaging provides high spatial resolution but cannot capture real-time dynamics.13 MRI is valuable in clinical contexts but lacks the resolution necessary to study iron-related processes at the cellular level.14 Fluorescent probes, such as calcein-AM10 and RhoNox-4,11 improve sensitivity, but their application is limited because of their limited compatibility with time-course imaging. In addition, these tools often face challenges related to cellular delivery, tissue permeability, and metabolic stability. Another key limitation is that most current detection tools (except fluorescent probes) measure total iron without distinguishing between biologically active and inactive (stored) forms. In biological systems, iron exists predominantly in two oxidation states: ferrous (Fe2+, reduced) and ferric (Fe3+, oxidized).15 Intracellularly, iron is primarily stored in ferritin as Fe3+, rendering it inaccessible to iron-dependent enzymes. To be utilized in cellular processes, the stored Fe3+ must be reduced to Fe2+.16 Therefore, an accurate assessment of physiologically active iron requires in vivo visualization of labile Fe2+, which is easily oxidized upon oxygen exposure and difficult to detect.

Iron is transported systemically by transferrin as Fe3+ and internalized by cells via endocytosis through interaction with the transferrin receptor (TfR).15 Once internalized, it is released from transferrin and enters the labile iron pool (LIP) as Fe2+. This dynamic iron pool is continuously utilized for iron storage in ferritin, iron-sulfur cluster, and heme synthesis and exported through the iron transporter ferroportin.17 Therefore, the LIP serves as a central hub for intracellular iron homeostasis. The cellular LIP levels are predominantly regulated by the mRNA-binding proteins iron-regulatory protein 1 (IRP1, also known as ACO1) and iron-regulatory protein 2 (IRP2, also known as IREB2). Although IRP1 and IRP2 are orthologs with overlapping and distinct functions, IRP2 plays more prominent roles in controlling systemic iron homeostasis in vivo.18 These proteins regulate the expression of key iron metabolism-related proteins, including TfR, ferritin, and ferroportin, by modulating the stability and translational efficiency of their mRNAs.19 IRP2 abundances are tightly linked to intracellular iron availability and controlled by the iron-sensing ubiquitin ligase F box and leucine-rich repeat protein 5 (FBXL5).20,21,22,23 Under iron-deficient conditions, FBXL5 becomes unstable and is degraded by proteasomes.24 This allows IRP2 accumulation, thereby increasing LIP levels. Conversely, under iron-sufficient conditions, FBXL5 binds to iron, stabilizes, and promotes the ubiquitin-mediated IRP2 degradation, thereby reducing LIP levels. Thus, the FBXL5-dependent IRP2 degradation in iron-replete cells is a key event in maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis. In mice, FBXL5 deficiency causes IRP2 accumulation in multiple tissues, leading to iron overload.22,25,26,27 Therefore, FBXL5 acts as a crucial sensor and regulator of bioactive iron by responding to fluctuations in the LIP.

In addition to iron, FBXL5 stability is also regulated by oxygen availability. Under hypoxic condition, FBXL5 is degraded in a proteasome-dependent manner.24 A regulatory element that renders FBXL5 stability sensitive to iron and oxygen resides within its N-terminal hemerythrin-like (Hr) domain.24 Hemerythrin belongs to a family of iron- and oxygen-binding proteins found in bacteria and invertebrates.28 Previous studies have demonstrated that in the absence of iron binding, a degron within the Hr domain becomes accessible to an as-yet-unidentified ubiquitin ligase, thus degrading FBXL5 via the ubiquitin-proteasome system under iron-deficient conditions.29 A FBXL5 fragment containing the Hr domain displayed similar iron-dependent stability changes, whereas a mutant lacking this domain did not.30 Thus, the Hr domain is considered both necessary and sufficient for the iron-dependent regulation of FBXL5 stability. In this study, we utilized the Hr domain of FBXL5 to develop a dual iron and oxygen reporter, termed labile iron and oxygen notifier (LiON), designed for in vivo monitoring of iron and oxygen levels at the single-cell level. Using LiON, we uncovered the diverse iron distributions across organs, cell types, and even within the same cell type. Additionally, we observed a physiological iron distribution pattern along the liver zone, which reflects the functional heterogeneity of hepatocytes. This iron gradient suggests that iron metabolism plays a role in liver functional specialization, providing insights into iron regulation under physiological conditions.

Results

Development of LiON for monitoring intracellular iron and oxygen levels

To enable time-course sensing of intracellular iron and oxygen levels, we developed a dual-function fluorescent reporter by fusing the Hr domain of human FBXL5 with the red fluorescent protein tdTomato (tdT) (Figure 1A). To normalize the baseline expression across the cells, the Hr-tdT fusion was linked to mNeonGreen (mNG) via a self-cleaving porcine teschovirus-1 2A (P2A) peptide, which allowed the stoichiometric expression of separate proteins from a single transcript in eukaryotic cells.31 The resulting construct, Hr-tdT-P2A-mNG, was introduced into HEK293A cells via lentiviral transduction. To evaluate the cleavage efficiency of the P2A sequence, we performed immunoblot analysis and calculated the ratio of truncated Hr-tdT to total Hr-tdT (truncated Hr-tdT plus uncleaved Hr-tdT-mNG) (Figure S1A). As expected, the control construct lacking the P2A sequence (Hr-tdT-mNG) expressed only the full-length fusion protein. We next examined the sensitivity of the reporter to the changes in cellular iron and oxygen levels. Immunoblotting revealed that Hr-tdT was stabilized upon ferric ammonium citrate (FAC)-induced iron overload but degraded following iron chelation with deferoxamine (DFO) or exposure to hypoxia (1% O2) (Figure 1B). Importantly, mNG expression remained constant under these conditions, with a slight reduction under hypoxia, likely due to the oxygen requirement for fluorophore maturation.32 However, this modest decrease did not affect interpretation of the ratio of tdT to mNG fluorescence intensity (tdT/mNG), as the reduction in Hr-tdT fluorescence was substantially greater. Consequently, the tdT/mNG ratio revealed statistically significant differences between PBS-treated controls and the cells subjected to iron or oxygen perturbations (Figure 1B). Fluorescence microscopy corroborated these results: tdT/mNG, displayed in pseudo-color, increased following FAC treatment and decreased with DFO treatment (Figure 1C). Similarly, tdT/mNG decreased under hypoxic conditions compared with normoxia (Figure 1C), and these observations were further supported by the statistical analyses (Figure 1D). Notably, the cell population exhibited heterogeneity in iron and oxygen levels, as reflected by the broad distributions in the violin plots, regardless of treatment. The reporter’s sensitivity to iron was further validated using additional modulators—hemin, 2,2′-bipyridyl (Bpy), and deferasirox (DFX)—which induced predictable changes in the tdT/mNG ratio (Figure S1B). Collectively, these data indicated that HEK293A cells expressing Hr-tdT-P2A-mNG can reliably report intracellular iron and oxygen status through fluorescence signals.

To quantify the reporter’s dynamic range, the Hr-tdT-P2A-mNG-expressing cells were treated with varying FAC or DFO concentrations, and fluorescence was measured via flow cytometry (Figure S1C). Increasing FAC or DFO concentrations (0–100 μg/mL and 0–100 μM, respectively) dose dependently increased or decreased the tdT/mNG fluorescence ratio. These changes correspond to the concentrations commonly used to experimentally modulate intracellular iron,24,30 indicating that the reporter effectively captures biologically relevant fluctuations. The reporter’s ability to detect oxygen levels was similarly assessed: tdT/mNG progressively decreased under 21%, 10%, and 1% O2 (Figure S1D). Because oxygen is required for chromophore maturation of DsRed-derived proteins,33 we examined the oxygen depletion effects on tdT itself, using a tdT-P2A-mNG control lacking the Hr domain. Although this control showed a slight reduction in tdT/mNG under hypoxia (Figure S1D), the decrease was substantially smaller than that observed with the Hr-containing reporter, highlighting the importance of the Hr fusion for oxygen sensing. To investigate the combined effects, reporter-expressing cells were treated with FAC or DFO under three different oxygen conditions (Figure 1E). Under identical oxygen levels, the reporter primarily reflected the cellular iron content, suggesting that the sensing mechanisms for iron and oxygen act independently.

The reporter also enabled time-course monitoring of intracellular iron dynamics. Time-lapse imaging revealed that tdT/mNG gradually increased within 6 h of FAC treatment (Figure 1F and Video S1) and decreased during DFO treatment (Figure 1G and Video S1). While mNG intensity remained stable, tdT intensity changed in response to iron availability. Interestingly, the response kinetics to FAC and DFO varied among individual cells (Figure S1E), suggesting cell-to-cell heterogeneity in adaptation to iron fluctuations. Because tdT forms tandem dimers, its maturation and degradation kinetics may differ from those of monomeric fluorescent proteins.34 We therefore compared the accumulation (Figure S1F) and degradation rates (Figure S1G) of dimeric tdT and monomeric mNG when fused with the Hr domain. During FAC treatment, the accumulation rates were similar between Hr-tdT (9.42%/h) and Hr-mNG (9.12%/h). The degradation rate during DFO treatment, measured from 0 to 4 h, was also comparable between Hr-tdT (−7.74%/h) and Hr-mNG (−6.13%/h), with Hr-tdT showing a slightly faster decay. These results suggest that the Hr domain modulates the maturation and degradation properties of fused proteins in response to iron, enhancing their suitability as sensors. Collectively, these results established that the Hr-tdT-P2A-mNG construct functions as an effective ratiometric reporter of intracellular labile iron and oxygen. Owing to its sensitivity, dynamic range, and temporal resolution, we designated this construct a “labile iron and oxygen notifier” (LiON) and defined it as the ratio of tdT to mNG fluorescence intensity for the quantitative assessment of intracellular iron and oxygen status.

LiON detects intracellular bioactive iron in vitro

To validate the use of LiON to monitor intracellular bioactive iron levels, we conducted two complementary experiments. First, we tested whether a small amino acid deletion in the Hr domain impairs the ability of LiON to sense the cellular iron status. Previous studies have reported that FBXL5 lacking residues 77–81 (YNVHS) within its 159-amino acid Hr domain loses responsiveness to iron.29 Structural prediction using AlphaFold335 showed that wild-type (WT) Hr coordinates two iron atoms using three α helices, whereas deleting residues 77–81 (Δ77–81) results in a structure with four α helices coordinating the iron atoms (Figure 2A). This conformational alteration likely disrupts the iron-sensing ability of FBXL5. Indeed, immunoblot analysis confirmed that the Δ77–81 mutant remained unchanged regardless of iron status, while WT Hr was stabilized and degraded upon FAC and DFO treatment, respectively (Figure 2B). Consistently, LiON harboring the Δ77–81 Hr mutant (a five-amino acid deletion in an 893-amino acid construct) lost its responsiveness to changes in iron status (Figure 2C), indicating that the LiON signal depends on iron-sensitive Hr domain degradation. Second, to clearly understand the source of iron sensed by LiON, we used the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas9 system to delete key genes involved in iron metabolism and assessed their impact on LiON signaling (Figure 2D). Previous studies have convincingly established that FBXL5 deletion leads to increased LIP via IRP2 accumulation, whereas IRP2 deletion reduces LIP.36 FTH1 acts as a cytoplasmic iron reservoir by storing iron as Fe3+, and its deletion is, therefore, expected to increase LIP.37,38,39 PCBP1 facilitates iron transport from endosomes to ferritin, and its loss enhances iron efflux via ferroportin, resulting in reduced LIP.40 Gene depletion in LiON-expressing HEK293A cells was achieved by transient transfection with CRISPR-Cas9 plasmids, followed by hygromycin selection. Drug-resistant cells were used as pooled populations without single-cell cloning. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the effective deletion of FBXL5, IRP2, FTH1, and PCBP1 (Figures 2E–2H). Flow cytometry revealed that FBXL5 or FTH1 deletion increased the LiON signal (defined as the ratio of tdT/mNG fluorescence intensity), whereas IRP2 or PCBP1 deletion decreased it (Figure 2I). These findings demonstrated that LiON can detect changes in the LIP resulting from the genetic disruption of key iron-regulatory genes. LiON detects cellular diversity in iron and oxygen levels in vivo

To visualize the intracellular iron and oxygen levels in vivo, we generated knockin mice expressing the LiON reporter. The LiON reporter cassette was inserted into the Gt(ROSA)26Sor locus (also known as ROSA26) of mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells via homologous recombination (Figure 3A). The target site, located between exons 1 and 2, was chosen owing to its suitability for stable and ubiquitous transgene expression.41 The targeting construct sequentially contained a splice acceptor sequence; a bovine growth hormone (bGH) polyadenylation (polyA) signal to terminate endogenous ROSA26 transcription; a strong synthetic CAG promoter to drive transgene expression; a floxed SV40 polyA cassette (loxP-SV40polyA-loxP, also known as LSL cassette) to enable conditional transgene expression via Cre-mediated excision; a puromycin resistance gene driven by a phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK) promoter flanked by flippase recognition target (FRT) sites for positive selection; the LiON cassette (Hr-tdT-P2A-mNG); and a second bGH polyA signal to terminate transgene transcription. The construct was flanked by short and long homology arms (SA and LA, respectively) and included a diphtheria toxin A (DTA) cassette outside the homology arms for negative selection against random integration. The targeting vector was transfected into the ES cells, followed by puromycin selection. The puromycin resistance cassette was subsequently excised by the transient expression of flippase (Flp) recombinase, resulting in a knockin allele suitable for downstream applications. Correct targeting of the construct to the ROSA26 locus was confirmed by PCR analysis at both the 5′ and 3′ junctions and further validated by restriction enzyme digestion (Figures S2A and S2B). The correctly targeted ES cells were injected into C57BL/6 blastocysts, and the resulting chimeras were mated with C57BL/6 mice to generate heterozygous offspring (ROSA26CAG-LSL-LiON/+ mice). To achieve systemic expression of the LiON reporter, ROSA26 CAG-LSL-LiON/+ mice were crossed with Fbxo17-Cre mice expressing Cre recombinase in fertilized eggs, thereby enabling germline excision of the LSL cassette. The resulting Fbxo17-Cre/ROSA26CAG-LSL-LiON/+ mice were crossed with C57BL/6 mice to establish the ROSA26CAG-LiON/+ line, which constitutively expressed LiON in all tissues. Systemic expression of the LiON reporter was confirmed in ROSA26CAG–LiON/+ embryos on embryonic day 13.5 (E13.5) by fluorescence microscopy of the whole embryo sections (Figure 3B). Notably, LiON fluorescence varied among tissues and exhibited substantial heterogeneity not only between tissues but also among cells within the same tissue. In the small intestine, the luminal surface of the villi exhibited lower LiON signals than the inner regions (Figure 3C). Because the luminal surface is known to experience lower oxygen levels than the inner layer—due to countercurrent blood flow and the presence of commensal bacteria42—the observed LiON gradient likely reflects this hypoxic environment. In the liver, LiON signal displayed a clear spatial gradient, suggesting zonal differences in iron or oxygen availability. To further investigate this pattern, we crossed ROSA26CAG-LSL-LiON/+ mice with Alb-Cre mice, which express Cre under the control of the albumin (Alb) promoter, resulting in liver-specific expression of the LiON reporter. Liver sections from Alb-Cre/ROSA26CAG-LSL-LiON/CAG-LSL-LiON mice displayed a LiON gradient extending from the portal vein to the central vein regions (Figure 3D), consistent with the known liver zonation pattern that reflects metabolic heterogeneity along the porto-central axis.43 Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that zone 1 hepatocytes (periportal region, marked by cadherin-1 [CDH1]) exhibited relatively high LiON signals—indicative of elevated intracellular iron and oxygen levels—compared with zone 3 hepatocytes (pericentral region, marked by glutamate-ammonia ligase [GLUL]) (Figure 3E). To determine whether iron or oxygen primarily drove the LiON pattern in Alb-Cre/ROSA26CAG-LSL-LiON/CAG-LSL-LiON livers, we established an ex vivo liver slice culture system (Figure 4A). Livers were harvested from the mice and sectioned into 100-μm slices by using a vibratome, followed by culture on transwell inserts. Because oxygen levels were evenly distributed in this ex vivo system, these observations suggest that zone 1 hepatocytes were more prone to iron accumulation than those in the other zones. Indeed, LiON signal increased following iron overload induced by FAC, whereas treatment with the iron chelator DFX decreased the signal (Figure 4B). To investigate the effects of iron accumulation in zone 1 hepatocytes, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) to compare the gene expression patterns in hepatocytes from WT and Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F mice, a previously characterized hepatic iron overload model22 (Figure 4E). Zone 1 and 3 hepatocytes were annotated using established zonation marker sets. For each nucleus, a zonal signature score was calculated based on the integrated expression of either zone 1 marker genes (Hal, Saa2, Sds, and Hamp) or zone 3 markers (Cyp2e1, Igfbp1, and Baat).44 The resulting uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization revealed distinct spatial segregation, with zone 1 hepatocytes clustering predominantly in the lower-right region and zone 3 hepatocytes in the upper-left region (Figure 4F). To identify pathways differentially regulated between Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F and WT control mice within each zonal subset, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the genes ranked by log2 fold change (Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F vs. WT), using the WikiPathways collection (version 2024.1) as the reference. Enrichment scores were normalized, and statistical significance was evaluated using q values. Among canonical pathways, the oxidative stress-related “WP_NRF2_PATHWAY” was significantly enriched in zone 1 hepatocytes (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.80, p < 0.01, q < 0.01), but not in zone 3 hepatocytes (NES = −0.88, p = 0.75, q = 0.99) (Figure 4G). These results indicated that zone 1 hepatocytes were more susceptible to iron-induced oxidative stress than zone 3 hepatocytes, likely reflecting their higher basal iron content. Altogether, our findings demonstrated that LiON faithfully reports spatial heterogeneity in hepatic iron distribution and reveals zone-specific vulnerability to iron toxicity.

Discussion

In this study, we developed LiON, a highly sensitive fluorescent reporter capable of monitoring intracellular iron and oxygen dynamics over time in vivo. Unlike conventional iron detection methods, LiON allows continuous live observation of intracellular iron dynamics at single-cell resolution. The dynamic range of LiON can detect biologically relevant changes in iron levels induced by commonly used modulators such as FAC and DFO (Figure S1C). In vivo LiON application revealed heterogeneous iron distribution across organs, cell types, and within the same cell type. Particularly, LiON uncovered a distinct iron distribution pattern along the liver zonation axis (Figure 3E).

In terms of temporal resolution, LiON responds more slowly than fast-acting probes such as calcein-AM and RhoNox-4 because its signal depends on iron- and oxygen-dependent protein degradation. This mechanism introduces a delay relative to real-time fluctuations but allows for the integration of changes over longer time frames. Because biological responses to iron and oxygen fluctuations often occur over hours, LiON’s ability to provide a stable, cumulative readout represents a key advantage for biologically relevant long-term monitoring.

LiON requires both iron and oxygen to generate a robust signal, enabling selective visualization of environments enriched in both cofactors—conditions that support the activity of numerous iron- and oxygen-dependent enzymes, including prolyl hydroxylases,45 cytochrome P450s,46 lipoxygenases,47 α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases,48 and iron-containing superoxide dismutases.49 This dual-sensing capability provides an integrated view of iron-dependent cellular processes. For example, ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, relies on the Fenton reaction, which requires both Fe2+ and oxygen. The sensitivity of LiON to both cofactors makes it particularly well suited for studying ferroptosis and other oxidative stress-related mechanisms.

Using LiON both in vivo and ex vivo, we identified a physiological pattern of iron distribution in the liver, with zone 1 hepatocytes exhibiting a high propensity for iron accumulation. This suggests that zone 1 cells possess unique functional properties compared with hepatocytes in other zones. Our findings are consistent with previous observations in hereditary hemochromatosis (Hfe-deficient) mouse models, where iron overload predominantly affects zone 1 hepatocytes.50 Additionally, zone 1 hepatocytes exhibit greater oxidative stress, potentially explaining their increased susceptibility to iron-induced toxicity. This zonal bias in iron accumulation likely contributes to the increased vulnerability of zone 1 hepatocytes to liver injury.51 Liver zonation is shaped by multiple factors, including Wnt/β-catenin signaling,52 glucagon signaling,53 and oxygen gradients.54 Although attributing the LiON signal distribution solely to iron levels would be an oversimplification, our ex vivo liver slice experiments, in which oxygen was uniformly distributed, support the idea that iron plays a significant role in shaping zonal patterns. Future studies should investigate how iron distribution intersects with key regulators of liver zonation and whether perturbations in iron/oxygen homeostasis influence metabolic specialization or vulnerability to disease across different hepatic zones.

During the preparation of this study, two conceptually similar reporters were described: IronFist55 and FEOX.56 IronFist is a ratiometric fluorescent biosensor for labile iron, consisting of the Hr domain fused to mNG, with mCherry serving as an internal control. FEOX uses mTagBFP2 for Hr fusion and mCherry for normalization. Both of these reporters employ monomeric fluorescent proteins for Hr fusion, as dimeric fluorophores typically display slower maturation kinetics57 and may influence degradation rates.34 In contrast, we fused the Hr domain to the dimeric red fluorescent protein tdTomato, which offers superior tissue penetration for in vivo imaging.58 Our comparative analyses showed that Hr-tdT and Hr-mNG responded promptly to the changes in iron availability, with no apparent differences in maturation (Figure S1F) or degradation kinetics (Figure S1G). It is possible that the Hr domain modulates these kinetic properties in the context of iron sensing, enhancing their suitability as biosensors. Thus, our Hr-tdT fusion functions effectively as a sensor of intracellular labile iron and oxygen.

Interestingly, Akyol et al. reported substantial cell-to-cell heterogeneity in iron responses, using IronFist, in HeLa cells.55 Consistent with this, we observed pronounced cellular diversity in iron status both in HEK293A cells (Figure S1E) and in vivo (Figure 3), suggesting cell type- and cell state-specific regulation of iron homeostasis. Future studies aimed at elucidating the functional significance of this heterogeneity will advance our understanding of iron biology. The major distinctions of our study from previous reports are the detailed characterization of oxygen sensitivity in Hr-based reporters and the comprehensive in vivo application of this system. We demonstrate that LiON exhibits both iron- and oxygen-dependent fluorescence changes, enabling visualization of the spatial distribution of these cofactors in vivo. This expands the applicability of hemerythrin-based reporters and complements the findings obtained with IronFist and FEOX.

In summary, LiON represents a useful fluorescent biosensor that enables quantitative, spatiotemporal visualization of intracellular labile iron and oxygen in living systems. This tool not only illuminates the spatial heterogeneity of iron and oxygen levels across tissues and cell types but also provides a framework for investigating how these essential elements coordinate to regulate cellular function, stress responses, and disease pathogenesis. The versatility of LiON makes it broadly applicable to diverse research areas, including redox biology, metabolic regulation, aging, cancer, and neurodegeneration. With future optimization and adaptation to other model organisms or imaging platforms, LiON is poised to serve as a foundational technology for dissecting the interplay between iron, oxygen, and cellular homeostasis in both health and disease.

Limitations of the study

A key strength of LiON—its ability to detect both iron and oxygen—is also a limitation. LiON cannot distinguish whether the reduced signal is owing to low iron or oxygen, as both factors influence its stability (Figure 1F). Although we used the Δ77–81 mutant Hr as a non-responder control (Figure 2B), the molecular basis of iron- and oxygen-sensing by the Hr domain has not been fully elucidated. Because the mechanisms by which the Hr domain senses iron and oxygen appear to be distinct, further mutational analyses could yield variants with iron- or oxygen-specific responsiveness. Another limitation is that LiON is not suitable for absolute iron quantification. As with most fluorescence-based tools, the quantitative interpretation of LiON signals requires standard calibration. Additional limitations for in vivo use include potential interference from tissue autofluorescence and non-uniform reporter expression driven by the CAG promoter. These technical considerations should be carefully addressed when designing and interpreting experiments involving LiON.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+ mice and Fbxo17-Cre transgenic mice were generated for this study (see below). Alb-Cre transgenic mice (Stock No. 003574)68 were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and Fbxl5F/F mice were generated as previously described.22 These strains were crossed to generate the following lines: Fbxo17-Cre/ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+, ROSA26CAG–LiON/+, Alb-Cre/ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+, Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F/ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+, and Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F. Age- (8–15 weeks) and sex-matched littermates were used for the experiments. Unless otherwise noted in the figure legends, all mouse strains were backcrossed to a C57BL/6 background for more than seven generations. Animals were group-housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a temperature-controlled environment (22 ± 2°C) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water. All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University and Institute of Science Tokyo.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293A cells were cultured in DMEM (044–29765, Wako or 08459-64, nacalai) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 173012, Lot: 0001638293, Sigma) and penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; 168–23191, Wako or 26252-94, nacalai) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and then exposed to hypoxia (10%, 1% O2) in a hypoxic incubator (#APM-30DR, ASTEC). HEK293A cells expressing Hr–tdT–mNG, Hr-tdT, Hr-mNG, Hr (WT), Hr (Δ77–81) were generated by transient transfection using PolyJet transfection reagent (SL100688; SignaGen Laboratories) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each construct was subcloned into a pcDNA3.1(+) vector for transient expression.

Method details

Lentiviral infection

HEK293A cells stably expressing Hr-tdT–P2A–mNG (LiON [WT]), tdT-P2A-mNG (LiON [ΔHr]), or Hr(Δ77–81)-tdT–P2A–mNG (LiON [Δ77–81 Hr]) were generated via lentiviral transduction, subcloned into the pLVSIN-CMV Pur vector (6183; Takara Bio, Inc.), and used for lentiviral production. To produce lentiviral particles, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pLVSIN expression plasmids and packaging plasmids pLenti-P2A and pLenti-P2B (LV003, Applied Biological Materials Inc.). Twelve hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with a fresh culture medium. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the lentiviral supernatant was collected into conical tubes, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane (SLGVR33RB, Merck), and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 90,000 g for 90 min at 4°C (concentration factor: 25- to 100-fold). The concentrated viral particles were then used to infect HEK293A cells in the presence of 5 μg/mL polybrene (TR-1003-G, Merck). Forty-eight hours post-infection, the cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (A1113803, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the culture medium for additional 48 h. The resulting puromycin-resistant pooled populations were subjected to single-cell sorting using an SH800S cell sorter (SONY) and expanded as clonal lines for subsequent experiments.

Generation of ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+ mouse

To generate ROSA26CAG–LSL–LiON/+ mice, a targeting vector was constructed by inserting a LiON reporter cassette (Hr-tdT–P2A–mNG) into the ROSA26 locus of mouse ES cells via homologous recombination. The targeting construct includes a splice acceptor, a bGH polyA signal, a synthetic CAG promoter (comprising the CMV early enhancer and chicken β-actin promoter), a floxed SV40 polyA cassette (loxP–SV40 polyA–loxP, forming an LSL element), an FRT-flanked puromycin resistance gene driven by a PGK promoter, the LiON cassette (Hr-tdT–P2A–mNG), a second bGH polyA signal, and a DTA. The bGH polyA sequence was subcloned from pcDNA3.1(+) (#V79020; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Other components were subcloned from LSL–Cas9–Rosa26TV (#61408, Addgene) or introduced by incorporating short sequences into the overhangs of the PCR primers used for amplification. The 5′ and 3′ regions of homology for ROSA26 consisted of approximately 4.3 and 1.1 kb fragments, respectively, spanning from exon 1 to intron 1. These fragments were subcloned into the pBS SK(−) plasmid along with the components described above. The targeting vector was transfected into C57BL/6N JM8A3 ES cells (European Mouse Mutant Cell Repository, EuMMCR) using the X-tremeGENE9 DNA transfection reagent (#06365779001, Merck). The transfected cells were cultured in mitomycin C (#139–18711, Wako)-treated mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) feeders in ES medium supplemented with leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF; ESG1106, Sigma-Aldrich). The ES cell medium was composed of StemSure D-MEM (High Glucose) with Phenol Red and Sodium Pyruvate (Wako: 197–16275) supplemented with 15% StemSure Serum Replacement (SSR) (191–18375, Wako), 2 mM L-glutamine (25030081, Thermo), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (11140050, Thermo), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (168–23198, Wako), and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME; ES-007-E, MERCK). The components were thoroughly mixed and stored at 4°C until further use. ES cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After transfection, positive selection was carried out with 1 μg/mL puromycin. After puromycin selection, the puromycin resistance cassette was excised by transiently expressing Flp recombinase. The ES clones were screened by PCR to confirm correct homologous recombination. The primers used for screening were as follows: long arm 1 (Forward: CCATAGAGCCCACCGCATC, Reverse: CAACAACATGGCCGTCATCAAA); short arm 1 (Forward: TGGTGGCATCGATATAACTTCGT, Reverse: TTGCTCTCCCAAAGTCGCTC); long arm 2 (Forward: CAGTGTTGAGGGCAATCTGG, Reverse: GTTGCCAGCCATCTGTTGTTT); and short arm 2 (forward: CCTAAAGAAGAGGCTGTGCTTTGG, Reverse: CATCAAGGAAACCCTGGACTACTG). The correctly targeted ES clone (clone #1; Figure S1) were expanded and microinjected into the C57BL/6 blastocysts, which were transferred into pseudopregnant females to generate chimeric mice, which were subsequently bred into C57BL/6 mice to confirm germline transmission of the knock-in allele.

Generation of Fbxo17-Cre mouse

To generate Fbxo17-Cre mice, the exchangeable gene trap ES clone Ayu21-T57 (https://egtc.jp/clones/21-T57),69 in which the trap vector carrying a mutant lox71 site was inserted into the Fbxo17 gene, was used. A replacement vector was constructed to replace the βgeo gene in the trap vector with a splicing acceptor–Cre–mPGK polyA signal (SA–Cre–polyA) cassette via Cre-mediated site-specific recombination. The replacement vectors included the loxKR3,70 SA–Cre–polyA cassette, mPGK promoter, puromycin N-acetyltransferase (Pac) gene, and loxP sequence. Cassette exchange by Cre-mediated site-specific recombination in Ayu21-T57 ES clone cells was performed as described previously.71,72 Chimeric mice were produced by aggregating replaced ES clones with eight-cell embryos from ICR mice (Nippon Clea). Chimeric male mice were mated with C57BL/6N females (Nippon Clea) to obtain F1 heterozygotes, which were backcrossed nine times.

Gene deletion by CRISPR/Cas9 system

FBXL5-, IRP2-, FTH1-, and PCBP1-deficient HEK293A cells were generated using a transient CRISPR/Cas9 approach to minimize the potential off-target effects associated with stable Cas9/sgRNA genome integration. To generate knockout cells, single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting each gene were designed using CRISPOR59 (https://crispor.org) and cloned into a modified PX459 vector (Addgene plasmid # 62988) that co-expresses Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 and a hygromycin resistance cassette. This enabled transient hygromycin resistance in the CRISPR-transfected cells. The sgRNA target sequences used were FBXL5 (TCCTTAGAGGTCTTAGCCTA), IRP2 (AACAAGACCCGTATTGAGTA), FTH1 (CATGGACAGGTAAACGTAGG), and PCBP1 (CGCTATGATCATCGACAAGC). HEK293A cells stably expressing Hr-tdT–P2A–mNG (LiON) were seeded in 6-well plates (3.0×105 cells/well) and transfected the next day with 1 μg sgRNA–Cas9 plasmid using PolyJet transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Eight hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM containing 10% FBS. After another 16 h, the cells were passed to 10 cm dishes. Six hours later, 1 mg/mL hygromycin B (#09287-84, Nacalai) was added to the medium for transient selection for 3 days. Following selection, the cells were allowed to recover in antibiotic-free medium for 7 days. The control cells were transfected with a sgRNA(−) PX459 plasmid lacking a guide RNA sequence. Knockout efficiency was validated by immunoblotting using FBXL5-, IRP2-, FTH1-, and PCBP1-specific antibodies. The pooled cell populations were subsequently used without single-cell cloning.

Time-lapse imaging

Time-lapse fluorescence imaging was performed using a BZ-X800 all-in-one microscope (KEYENCE) equipped with a live cell incubation chamber. The cells (5×104 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well glass-bottom plates (5826-024, IWAKI) without coating and cultured overnight. The next day, the culture medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing the indicated concentrations of the stimuli and imaged in a humidified chamber maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 every 1 h for 6 h using 10× PlanApoλ, fluorescence settings. Image acquisition and stitching were performed using BZ-X800 Analyzer software (KEYENCE). Raw image data were exported as TIFF files and processed using Fiji software60 (ImageJ2, version 2.14.0/1.54f) for quantification and contrast adjustment. For single-cell analysis, Cellpose66 (version 3.1.1.2) was used for cell segmentation with the cyto model and Trackpy (version 0.7) for cell tracking with default parameters unless otherwise specified.

Immunoblot analysis

The cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitor [10 μM Leupeptin (#336–40413, Wako), 1 μM Pepstatin A (#330–43973, Wako) and 1 mM PMSF (#160–12183, Wako)]. The lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 5 min at 4°C and the protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay (#T9310A, TaKaRa) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of total protein (30–60 μg per lane) were separated on 6–12% SDS polyacrylamide gels under reducing conditions with 2% 2-ME (#21438-82, Nacalai). The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore) using a wet transfer system (380 mA, 120 min at 4°C) and blocked in 5% skim milk (#190–12865, Wako) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 30 min at room temperature. For analyses involving a single target protein, membranes were cut after transfer and separately probed for the target protein and the corresponding loading control. When multiple target proteins were analyzed from the same sample set, independent SDS–PAGE gels were run to avoid potential loss of target proteins during membrane cutting. In all cases, identical lysate preparations were used for the respective targets. The following primary antibodies were used for specific protein detection: anti-mCherry (tdTomato) (#AB0040-200, SICGEN), anti-HSP90 (#610418, BD Transduction), anti-β-tubulin (#5346, CST), anti-β-actin (#F0012, Selleckchem), anti-mNeonGreen (#32f6, Proteintech), anti-FLAG (#015–22391, Wako), anti-FBXL5 (#672604, BioLegend), anti-IRP2 (#37135, CST), anti-FTH1 (#ab65080, Abcam), and anti-PCBP1 (#8534, CST). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% skim milk/TBS-T overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies: anti-goat IgG (#PA1-28664, Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti-mouse IgG2c (#ab97255, Abcam), and anti-mouse IgG (#7076, CST). The signals were detected using a chemiluminescent substrate (#WBKLS0500, Millipore) and visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Flow cytometry

The cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS), and stained with 0.1 μg/mL DAPI (#340–07971, Wako) in FACS buffer for 5 min on ice to exclude dead cells. The fluorescence was measured using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences). The fluorescence intensities of mNeonGreen and tdTomato were detected using FITC and PE channels, respectively. Doublets were excluded based on FSC-A versus FSC-W gating and dead cells were excluded using DAPI staining.

Quantification of LiON signal

The LiON signal was calculated as the ratio of tdTomato (tdT) fluorescence intensity to mNeonGreen (mNG) fluorescence intensity (tdT/mNG). The fluorescence intensities were measured using CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) or BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE), depending on the experiment. For flow cytometry analysis, tdTomato and mNeonGreen fluorescence were collected using the PE and FITC channels, respectively. The mean fluorescence intensities (MFI) were extracted from single live cell populations gated based on FSC-A vs. FSC-W and DAPI exclusion. For imaging-based quantification, the regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as independent cells in Fiji (ImageJ) and the average pixel intensities for each fluorophore were extracted. To normalize across conditions, the tdT/mNG ratios were divided by the mean tdT/mNG ratio of the control group, which was set at 1. To clearly visualize the changes in tdT/mNG ratios, the normalized values were transformed using the natural logarithm (ln) and multiplied by a scaling factor (100 or 1,000 depending on the experiment). Prediction of the protein structure

Structural predictions of the Hr domain were generated using AlphaFold3 on the AlphaFold Server (alphafoldserver.com) with default parameters. The full-length amino acid sequence of the Hr domain used in the LiON construct or that of Δ77–81 Hr domain was input for prediction. The resulting structural models were downloaded in the PDB format and visualized using PyMOL (v2.5.4, Schrödinger). The predicted structures were assessed for folding topology and domain orientation, and representative models were used for figure generation.

Histological analysis and immunostaining

For immunostaining, mouse livers or E13.5 embryos were harvested, briefly rinsed in PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS at 4°C overnight. The fixed tissues were cryoprotected in 30% (w/v) sucrose in PBS at 4°C for at least 12 h or until they were fully equilibrated. The tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound (#4583, Sakura Finetek Japan), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Cryosections (10 μm thick) were prepared using a cryostat (CM1950, Leica) and mounted on MAS-GP Type A-coated slides (#S9901, Matsunami). The slides were air-dried at room temperature for 60 min and rehydrated in PBS. For samples without antibody staining, the sections were incubated with DAPI (1:500, #D523, Dojindo) in PBS for 10 min, rinsed, mounted in Fluoromount (#K024, Diagnostic BioSystems), and cover-slipped. For immunostaining, the sections were blocked in 10% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer [0.3% Triton X-100 and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; #018–15154, Wako) in PBS]: anti-CDH1 (1:200, #13–1900, Invitrogen). For GLUL staining, an M.O.M. Immunodetection Kit (#BMK-2202, Vector) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions with an anti-GLUL (#610517, BD Biosciences) antibody. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with DAPI (1:500) and Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rat IgG Alexa Fluor 633, 1:500; A-21094, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 633, 1:500; A-21052, Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in blocking buffer. After the final wash, the slides were mounted on Fluoromount and covered with coverslips. Fluorescent images were acquired using a BZ-X800 microscope (KEYENCE) with a 20× objective lens.

Ex vivo liver slice culture

Krebs buffer was prepared by dissolving 2.0 g/L D-glucose, 0.288 g/L magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, 0.16 g/L potassium phosphate monobasic, 0.35 g/L potassium chloride, 6.9 g/L sodium chloride, 0.282 g/L calcium chloride, and 2.1 g/L sodium bicarbonate in deionized water to 1 L. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 using 1 N HCl and the solution was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (#SCGPS05RE, Millipore). The buffer was supplemented with 1% P/S and stored at 4°C until use. The mouse livers were harvested and immediately immersed in ice-cold Krebs buffer. The tissues were trimmed and sectioned into 100 μm-thick slices using a vibratome (Neo LinearSlicer NLS-MT, Dosaka EM). The liver slices were transferred to 0.4 μm pore size cell culture inserts (#3460, Corning) placed in 24-well plates, with 400 μL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S added to the lower chamber. Iron-perturbing agents were added directly to the lower chamber: 100 μg/mL FAC or 100 μM DFX, as indicated. After incubation at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 6 h, fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (FV3000, EVIDENT) with a 10× objective.

Nuclei isolation

Livers were harvested from three WT or Alb-Cre/Fbxl5F/F mice. For each group, the livers from three individual mice were pooled. Approximately 20–40 mg frozen liver tissue was minced on ice using a sterile razor blade and transferred to a pre-chilled Potter-type Teflon-glass homogenizer (RW20 digital; #45–0459, IKA). The pestle was positioned just below the liquid surface, and the tissue was homogenized with five strokes at 1,000 rpm to disrupt the cells while preserving nuclear integrity. The homogenate was immediately diluted with cold sucrose buffer and adjusted to 27% (w/v) final sucrose concentration. Each sample was carefully layered in an ultracentrifuge tube and preloaded with a sucrose step gradient composed of 12%, 27% (sample layer), 31%, and 35% sucrose solutions and ultracentrifuged at 24,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C using an Optima MAX-XP Ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter). The nuclear fraction was collected from the 35% sucrose layer at the bottom of the tube. To remove debris and non-nuclear material, the nuclei were sorted by lamin staining using a MoFlo cell sorter (Beckman Coulter).

Library preparation for snRNA-seq

The sorted nuclei harvested from the liver were counted using a hemocytometer, and their concentration was adjusted for loading on a 10× Chromium chip. All libraries were prepared using the 10× Genomics Chromium system with the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3 Kit v3.1, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The sorted nuclei were loaded on a chromium chip and encapsulated with gel beads in emulsion (GEMs), followed by reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and library construction as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was outsourced to Macrogen Japan (Tokyo, Japan) and performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina), generating approximately 500 million paired-end reads per library. Raw FASTQ files were processed using the Cell Ranger software (v7.2.0, 10× Genomics). The “cellranger count” pipeline was run with default settings and aligned to the mouse reference genome (gex-mm10-2020-A). Gene barcode matrices were analyzed using Seurat61 (v5.0.3). Cells with >200 genes and <5% mitochondrial content were retained. The data were normalized using SCTransform, followed by dimensionality reduction and clustering using a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell type annotation was performed using SingleR62 (v2.0.0) with the Celldex reference expression dataset (v1.8.0, MouseRNAseqData). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using ClusterProfiler63 (v4.6.2), and visualizations were generated using Enrichplot (v1.18.4). For cross-species comparisons, human gene symbols were converted to mouse orthologs using gprofiler265 (v0.2.3).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (v4.3.3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For physiological parameter- or mouse tissue-derived datasets, the sample size (n) represents the number of individual animals included in each group. Data distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test for comparisons between two groups. For comparisons involving more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, where applicable. All statistical tests were two-sided.
