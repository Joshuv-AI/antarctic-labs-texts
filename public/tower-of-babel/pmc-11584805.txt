Double plating and iliac crest bone graft can safely fix femoral shaft nonunion
Authors: Afshin Taheriazam, AsraSadat Mir ahmadi, Ahmad Abbaszadeh, Mohammad Soleimani, Rezvan Darabi, Mehdi Samberani, Erfan Poursaleh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction Femoral shaft nonunion can be extremely vexing and result in significant morbidity. We aimed to evaluate the outcomes of patients with nonunion of the femoral shaft fracture undergoing double plating and bone grafts from the iliac crest and femoral canal. Methods This retrospective study included 44 patients with femoral shaft nonunion between March 2020 to March 2022. Patients underwent dual plating, bone grafting from the iliac crest, and the utilization of the femoral canal. Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), the two surgical interventions interval, union time after the second surgery, hip range of motion (ROM), limb length discrepancy (LLD), infections, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were evaluated. Results Patients comprised 21 males (47.7%) and 23 females (52.3%), with a mean age of 42.3 ± 15.2 years. The mean BMI was 26.7 ± 3.77 kg/m2. The mean duration between the two surgical interventions for the patients was 17.07 ± 6.6 months. The patients were followed for ≥ 12 months. All patients achieved successful union and fracture healing, with an average time of 5 months. The mean hip flexion and extension were 112.84 ± 7.7˚ and 14.8 ± 5.2˚ degrees, respectively. Thirteen patients showed LLD after the first surgery, with a mean LLD of 7.15 ± 5.04 millimeters. LLDs were successfully rectified in all patients following the surgical intervention. No patients experienced postoperative infections or DVT. Conclusion According to our findings, the utilization of double-plate fixation in combination with iliac crest and femoral canal bone graft has proven to be a secure, productive, and straightforward surgical alternative for the management of femoral nonunion.

Introduction

Femoral shaft nonunion presents a debilitating issue, and the occurrence of nonunion following a fracture of a long bone can be extremely vexing and result in significant morbidity1,2. It is discovered that the healing of bone fractures fails in approximately 5 to 10% of all patients3,4. This not only places a financial burden on the patients and their families but also carries psychological ramifications for them5,6.

Various treatment options are accessible to manage nonunion in long bones. These options encompass plate and intramedullary fixation with bone graft, reamed nailing, external fixation, dynamization, ultrasound, and membrane induction for the correction of long bone defects7–10. Nevertheless, there is currently no consensus regarding the most efficacious approach in this specific context.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the outcomes of patients with nonunion of the femoral shaft fracture undergoing double plating and bone grafts from the iliac crest and femoral canal. It was postulated that this surgical technique would yield a significant therapeutic impact on the union rate in this situation.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study has been approved by our Institutional Review Board and Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University (IR.IAU.TMU.REC.1402.092). This research was in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants completed and signed the informed consent form. This retrospective study investigated a total of 44 patients who experienced nonunion of the femoral shaft [Fig. 1]. These patients underwent a procedure involving dual plating, bone grafting from the iliac crest, and the utilization of the femoral canal. The study period spanned from March 2020 to March 2022 in our affiliated hospitals (Farhikhtegan and Erfan Niayesh Hospitals). The inclusion criteria for this study consisted of age > 18 years, ≥ 12 months of follow-up, and a confirmed diagnosis of femoral shaft nonunion based on their medical history, physical examination, and radiological findings. Patients with pathological fractures, infectious nonunion, and < 12 months of follow-up were excluded from the study.

Nonunion can be delineated as the cessation of the customary biological reparative mechanism of osseous tissue, reaching a critical stage where it is deemed medically unsuitable to defer additional medical intervention11.

Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), duration between the two surgical interventions, time to the bony union after the second surgery, the range of motion (ROM) in hip joints, postoperative lower limb shortening, infections, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) were evaluated in the patients.

Surgical technique

All of the surgeries were performed by a single surgeon. Following the administration of general or spinal anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine, and the area of nonunion was exposed. Specimens were obtained from both the bone and soft tissue for bacterial culture. The medullary canal was then opened, and a bone graft was obtained using a reamer. Furthermore, bone graft was also obtained from the iliac crest. The previous hardware was removed, and the site of nonunion, along with the surrounding area, was copiously debrided and irrigated. Subsequently, slotting was performed on both ends of the nonunion site in the femur and then fixed using two plates on the lateral and anterior sides of the femoral shaft [Fig. 2]. An autologous bone graft from the iliac crest and femoral canal was applied to the nonunion site. Finally, the wound was closed in multiple layers, and a drainage tube was inserted.

Postoperative care

Anticoagulant agents were initiated postoperatively and extended for two weeks. Patients commenced a regimen of physical therapy involving both active and passive motion while under the careful guidance of a specialized practitioner, permitting assisted weight-bearing. Antibiotic administration ceased following the 24-hour mark. As part of the customary protocol, anteroposterior and lateral X-ray images were obtained [Fig. 3]. We routinely visited the patients two weeks, six weeks, three months, six months, and 12 months after surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data is described using the mean (standard deviation), while categorical data is conveyed through cell count and percentage. Parametric data for continuous variables underwent comparison through independent samples t-tests, and nonparametric data was analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical data was compared using Chi-square tests. The statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (Version 3.6.3, Vienna, Austria), and significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 44 patients diagnosed with nonunion of femoral bone fractures underwent double plating and bone grafting. The patient age ranged from 19 to 75 years (Table 1). All patients initially presented with closed fractures, and no instances of open fractures were observed. The interval between the two surgical interventions for the patients was 6 to 27 months.

The patients were monitored for a minimum duration of one year, during which it was noted that all patients achieved successful bone union and appropriate fracture healing. The average time to the bony union after the second surgery was 5.6 ± 0.6 months (range, 4–7).

In terms of hip joint flexion, the mean range observed in the patients was 112.84 ± 7.7 (range, 95–120) degrees. Additionally, the patients were assessed for hip joint extension, with an average range of 14.8 ± 5.2 (range, 5–20) degrees.

A total of 13 patients exhibited limb shortening after index surgery. The mean limb shortening among these 13 patients was 7.15 ± 5.04 (range, 3–20) millimeters. Notably, limb length discrepancies were in all patients successfully rectified following the surgical intervention. Also, none of the patients experienced postoperative infections or DVT. Eleven patients had anterior hip paresthesia and had a problem with using waist belts. The paresthesia and pain were resolved in all patients at the final follow up. No patient showed any signs of hematoma, permanent pain, infection, or abdominal hernia.

Discussion

Femur shaft nonunion is a common occurrence, often resulting from suboptimal surgical methods and infections12. Various techniques address nonunions, but the choice between nails and plates for fixation has been a subject of controversy13–15. Nails offer advantages such as minimal invasiveness, procedural simplicity, and shorter operation times. However, they provide only relative stability and non-anatomical reduction, particularly for certain fractures involving bone defects or fragment displacement, leading to an increased risk of nonunion12. On the contrary, plate fixation, combined with bone grafting, has shown excellent results by facilitating adequate anatomic reduction and reducing the fracture gap16. Despite these benefits, conventional techniques may not effectively treat certain nonunions. In such cases, we strongly believe that our double-locking plate construct represents a viable alternative.

Biomechanical and clinical studies have shown that dual plating is a successful method in managing comminuted fractures such as distal femoral fractures17–19. Nonetheless, there has been an interest in using double plates to address nonunion after femoral fractures. Rabea et al. achieved remarkable outcomes by employing fibular and iliac crest bone grafts in conjunction with double plate fixation to treat femoral shaft nonunion20. While our study also yielded positive results, it took a different approach by not incorporating a fibular graft, potentially making it a less invasive alternative. Moreover, our study benefited from a larger sample size compared to theirs (44 versus. 21). Another investigation by Maimaitiyiming et al. involving 14 patients, demonstrated favorable outcomes using the double plate technique alongside iliac crest bone grafts for femoral shaft nonunion treatment21. These results were consistent with our findings. Additionally, in a 2020 study, authors reported a 100% union rate by employing a similar methodology for treating supracondylar, subtrochanteric, and femoral shaft nonunion22. It is important to note that our study specifically focused on femoral shaft nonunion, distinguishing it from the aforementioned studies. Moreover, Mardani-Kivi et al. showed that using double-plating along with bridging bone grafts is effective in achieving union in the femoral shaft, subtrochanteric, and supracondylar nonunions22. They used iliac graft in their case series of 41 patients, of which 32 had femoral shaft fractures. All of their patients achieved union, which is consistent with our findings. Another study by Wang et al. documented various complications post-surgery, including DVT, nonunion, and surgical site infection23. In contrast, our study observed no such complications. Additionally, limb shortening was resolved after double plating and bone graft in our series. In comparison, Rabea et al. reported that average lower limb shortening was 5 mm in their study20. To the best of our knowledge, our study demonstrates the least amount of limb shortening when compared to previous investigations.

In our study, the average time for bone formation was 5 months. Notably, this union time was shorter than in previous studies conducted by Peng et al. (5.3 months), Wang et al. (7.6 months), and Chapman et al. (15 months)7,16,23. Moreover, the range of motion of the hip joint observed in our study is within the normal range of motion.

This study was not without limitations. First, we did not benefit from a control group. Second, we included all patients with femoral nonunion except those with infectious nonunion, which might have introduced some bias into the study. Also, this study was of retrospective nature. Moreover, we did not evaluate past medical history or history of smoking or substance abuse, which are significant factors.

Conclusion

According to our findings, the utilization of double-plate fixation in combination with iliac crest and femoral canal bone graft has proven to be a secure, productive, and straightforward surgical alternative for the management of femoral nonunion.
