The impact of community engaged healthcare education on undergraduate students’ empathy and their views towards social accountability; a mixed methods systematic review
Authors: Anastasios Plessas, Martha Paisi, Nilufar Ahmed, Zoe Brookes, Lorna Burns, Robert Witton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany non-communicable diseases are rooted in social factors that determine health outcomes. Complex topics such as the social determinants of health are difficult to teach through traditional didactic methods. Since the introduction of the social accountability of medical schools’ framework in 1995 by the World Health Organisation, healthcare education institutions are encouraged to shift their traditional education models towards a socially accountable approach. Community engagement can facilitate a deeper understanding of health inequity, barriers to health care, and the social determinants of health whilst enabling institutions to meet their obligation to the communities they serve. This systematic review aimed to answer the following question: ‘‘What is the impact of community engaged healthcare education on undergraduate healthcare students' empathy and their views towards social accountability?’’.MethodsThis is a mixed-methods systematic review. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42022306181). The following databases were searched: Scopus, MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, ERIC, BEI for primary research studies published from 1995 to September 2024. Grey literature was also searched via EThOS and Google. Studies recruiting undergraduate students of regulated healthcare professions in countries of very high development were included. A convergent integrated approach to synthesis was followed.ResultsOut of the 19,590 papers yielded from the literature search, 76 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The following subthemes were generated under three overarching themes: Social accountability (advocacy for action; giving back to the community; bigger picture—identifying social determinants of health and barriers to healthcare; barriers to social accountability), Empathy (humanise community members—everybody has a story; overcome bias and challenge attitudes, assumptions and stereotypes; cultural competence and sensitivity; empathetic communication and interpersonal skills; feeling sympathy and sadness), and Shaping the future workforce (future professional demeanour; professional interests; interprofessional education and collaboration).ConclusionsCommunity engaged education can help students better understand community needs, social determinants of health, improve empathy and cultural sensitivity, and build advocacy for social justice and change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06367-1.

Background

Poverty and social inequity are important determinants of ill health [1]. Many non-communicable diseases are rooted in social determinants of health which are known to increase health inequities and hinder access to health care [1]. Traditional teaching and learning about social determinants of health and public health have been criticised due to its failure to examine issues such as power and privilege or promote critical thinking about justice and inequity [2]. Within healthcare education, in order to promote health equity and respond to the global and societal health inequalities, the concept of ‘‘social accountability’’ has gained increased popularity [3]. Social accountability for health professional schools was first defined in 1995 by WHO as ‘‘the obligation to direct [health professional] education, research, and service activities towards addressing the priority health concerns of the community, the region, and the nations they have a mandate to serve’’ [4]. The United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development Goals highlighted the crucial role healthcare institutions have in the provision of inclusive and equitable quality education and the promotion of social learning opportunities [5], shifting healthcare education towards socially accountable approaches [5, 6].

Community engaged education has been advocated as a form of experiential pedagogy which can produce healthcare graduates who are responsive to population needs, are socially accountable, and person and population-centred [5, 7–9]. Such graduates are committed to the health of the community by identifying the social determinants of health, developing empathy towards the communities they serve, and advocating for community needs to be met [5, 7, 8, 10]. Empathy has been suggested as a prosocial behaviour, which may be essential if a sense of social accountability is to be cultivated [11, 12]. Empathy in a healthcare context is defined as one’s ability to understand patients’ experiences, concerns, and perspectives, combined with a capacity to communicate this understanding and intention to help [13].

A lack of consistent definition of terms to describe community engaged education complicates its conceptualisation and contextualisation [8]. Different terms have been used including community engaged education, community engagement, community-based education, community-oriented education, equity oriented education, service learning and community service learning, with service learning dominating the US healthcare literature [5, 7, 8, 10, 14]. In this review we adopt the term community engaged education and community engagement educational activities. The taxonomy of community based healthcare education developed by Magzoub and Schmidt was used to frame the systematic review [15]. Based on this taxonomy, community engaged education is considered to be a branch of community-based education and is conceptualised as a form of experiential education which utilises community exposure educational programmes (i.e., students are exposed to the population/community of interest usually in a non-clinical setting) aiming to address community needs. It draws on the learning theory of experiential learning, and it involves learning activities that take place in and with the community to educate the learner and serve the community [5, 10, 16].

To the best of our knowledge, there has not been an attempt to systematically synthesise the available evidence on community engaged healthcare education in terms of its impact on students’ empathy and views towards social accountability. Therefore, this systematic review aims to answer the following research question: ‘‘What is the impact of community engaged education on undergraduate healthcare students' empathy and their views towards social accountability?

Methods

The systematic review protocol was registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42022306181. The review adheres to the PRISMA [17] and ENTREQ [18] guidelines. A peer review process for developing the search strategy, coordinated by an information specialist (LB), was followed [19].

The following databases were searched to achieve optimal coverage of healthcare and subject focus of education: Scopus, MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, ERIC and BEI [23]. The searches were supplemented by forward and backward citation searching against the included studies [24]. Grey literature was searched via EThOS and Google. A search date limit of the year of 1995 was used, which reflects the year that the concept of social accountability was defined by WHO [4]. Only articles in the English language were included. Study selection and data extraction

Search results were uploaded to Rayyan systematic review web application, for screening [25]. A single reviewer (AP) applied the selection criteria to all references using the information available in the title and abstract. An inter-rater reliability process was followed at this stage, whereby a random selection of 5% of articles was assessed for relevance by a second reviewer (RW) independently. Since the inter-rater agreement rate was very high (97.8%), and following calibration it was not deemed necessary for the second reviewer to independently screen the titles and abstracts for relevance. Any uncertainty for inclusion of studies however thereafter was resolved by discussion between the first and second reviewers (AP and RW). The two reviewers screened the full text of the eligible papers independently. The inter-rater agreement was 98.2%. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer (MP) and consensus was reached. The data were extracted by the first reviewer (AP) and checked for accuracy by the second reviewer (RW).

Quality assessment

The Mixed-Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used as it allows quality assessment of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods studies [26]. It focuses on methodological criteria and includes five core quality criteria for different study designs. The appraisal was carried out by AP and RW independently and any disagreements were resolved through consensus. Percentages descriptors [27] were used to indicate the proportion of the quality criteria fulfilled. All studies regardless of methodological quality were included in the review [26]. However, studies of high quality contributed the most in our synthesis.

Data synthesis

A convergent integrated mixed methods approach to synthesis was followed [28, 29]. The convergent integrated approach refers to a process of combining extracted data from quantitative studies (including data from the quantitative component of mixed methods studies) and qualitative studies (including data from the qualitative component of mixed methods studies) and involves data transformation. For data transformation Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) recommends that quantitative data be qualitised, as codifying quantitative data is less error-prone than attributing numerical values to qualitative data [28]. Therefore, three stages during data synthesis were followed: 1. Data transformation, 2. Coding 3. Thematic synthesis.

Initially quantitative data were transformed (qualitised) into narrative interpretations by the first reviewer (AP) and this was verified by a second reviewer (RW). An iterative approach for the coding of qualitative data was followed [30]. The coding of qualitative data was focused on primary data or conclusions/authors' interpretations stemming tightly from the primary data/ student quotes. The qualitised data were also coded accordingly. A pool of 5 coded papers were discussed in a team meeting where the authors exchanged opinions about the interpretation of the data and the code names given to the extracted data. After the initial coding was completed, the codes were reviewed and some of the codes were abandoned or combined and renamed. Following a thematic synthesis approach, codes were then combined to generate themes and subthemes [31]. These decisions were discussed and interrogated in team meetings, to validate interpretations and minimise personal bias, ensuring the trustworthiness and confirmability of our synthesis findings [32].

The sample size in the studies ranged from 8 students to 580, with a median number of students 54. The majority of the included studies were conducted in North America (n = 56) followed by Europe (n = 8), Asia (n = 8) and Australia (n = 4). Studies recruited predominantly nursing (n = 25) and medical (n = 23) students. Ten of the educational interventions adopted an interprofessional learning model recruiting students from more than one discipline. In the remaining 18 studies, participants were from the following disciplines: dentistry (n = 6), social work (n = 4), pharmacy (n = 2), physiotherapy (n = 2), physician assistant (n = 2), paramedicine (n = 1) and chiropractic medicine (n = 1).

The included papers adopted a variety of study designs. The majority of studies adopted a qualitative (n = 38) and mixed methods (n = 20) research design. There were 12 non-randomised quantitative studies and 2 quantitative descriptive studies whilst 4 studies presented programme evaluation data. The most prevalent data collection method among the studies was the distribution of a survey (n = 41), followed by student reflections (n = 31), interviews (n = 14), focus groups (n = 11), and student feedback (n = 6). Thirteen of the studies used one or more validated quantitative instruments to measure outcomes and constructs such as empathy [50, 99], cultural competence [33, 66], attitudes towards aging and older people [61, 76, 79, 98], attitudes towards people experiencing homelessness [52, 88, 99], attitudes towards the underserved [67, 68, 82] and attitudes towards peoples experiencing disability [97]. A description of these validated instruments and a summary of the results can be found in Additional File 3.

There was a great diversity in the nature of the community engagement educational activities and projects employed in the included studies. The activities were categorised as follows: health education, health promotion and disease prevention (n = 33); health themed activities or projects (n = 24); non-health themed activities or projects (n = 31); needs assessment (n = 8) and community observation (n = 6). In 24 of the studies, the educational experience consisted of more than one of the above types of activities. A description of each category and an illustrative example is provided in Table 4, whilst a more detailed description of the educational activities employed in each of the included studies can be found in Additional File 3. Regarding the setting in which these activities took place, the vast majority of the activities were situated within the community (e.g., community centres, community organisations, homeless shelters etc.) (n = 50), followed by residential settings (e.g., home visits) (n = 13), schools (n = 9), rural communities (n = 2), and prisons (n = 2). Social accountability

Advocacy for action

Community engagement activities deepened the students’ sense of social responsibility and concern about social affairs. The student perception of social responsibility evolved from an abstract sense that social accountability is something generally important, to a sense that it is their professional and moral obligation to personally serve the needs of all individuals in society [39, 55, 59, 71, 95, 106]; a notion which was also supported by quantitative and mixed methods studies [86, 87, 96, 99, 105].

Students realised the ‘‘value of community health’’ [59] and expressed the desire to advocate for the needs of their communities [33, 43, 47, 53, 55, 101, 104, 105], particularly for ‘‘people who don’t have resources’’ [104] and for ‘‘people who fall through the cracks’’ [47]. The experiences the students gained inspired them to influence social change through activism and advocate for social justice [36, 47, 55, 63, 69, 75, 109].

The above findings were in line with findings from quantitative studies. Praksash et al. [99] found a statistically significant increase in personal and social advocacy among paramedic students towards people experiencing homelessness (P < 0.0001). Similarly, Belkowitz et al. [90] showed a statistically significant increase in self-rated advocacy skills (p < 0.01) after designing and implementing a community-level health advocacy project in partnership with various local community-based organisations compared to a control group of medical students who were not exposed to service learning. In another study by Suresh et al. [82] 85% of medical students participating in the study reported an interest in advocating for the underserved and engaging in social activism after participating in a hybrid model of community engagement consisting of virtual and face-to-face community engagement events. A weak positive correlation, albeit not statistically significant, was found between hours of participation in service learning and commitment to social responsibility, as well as the intention to engage in civic action across US Nursing programmes [84].

Giving back to the community

Students saw community engagement within the curriculum as an opportunity to ‘‘give back to the community’’ [42, 47, 62, 102]. Participating in these educational activities was, for some students, the first opportunity to form a ‘‘service identity’’ [105] and serve the needs of the community which hosts and facilitates their healthcare education. Students reported that they observed their projects making a positive difference in the community and people’s lives [33, 47, 49, 52, 59, 61, 64, 65, 74, 75, 101]. Witnessing this impact filled the students with pride and a sense of accomplishment and satisfaction [52, 57, 61, 64, 101]. Students notably recognised that the benefit of engaging and serving the community was reciprocal [62, 106].

Students felt that their efforts and impact to the community could be sustained via cascading health knowledge down to community organisations and members [34, 44, 49, 74]. This ongoing knowledge transfer was described by students as a ‘‘domino effect’’ [49] and a ‘‘trickledown effect’’ [34].

The community projects, also helped students to learn about the variety of services and resources available to the community, of which they were previously unaware. Students felt that they could utilise this knowledge during clinical encounters to help their patients by directing them to the appropriate services and/or resources [43, 47, 74, 82, 83, 99, 101, 104, 106]. A statistically significant increase in knowledge of population health needs [78, 86, 90, 103] and community resources [78, 82, 83, 90, 92, 96] was also demonstrated by quantitative and mixed methods studies.

The students appreciated the role of community organisations and were impressed by the power of volunteerism which further deepened their desire for advocacy and their sense of giving back to the community [36, 43, 46, 47, 57, 64, 75, 80, 87, 94]. In many studies, students continued working for their host organisation in a voluntary capacity beyond their mandatory curriculum commitment [36, 94], whilst in others the students expressed the willingness to do so in the future [43, 46, 47, 57, 59, 75, 87]. In a mixed methods study by Suresh et al. [82]. 71% of the medical students surveyed expressed an interest in volunteering with underserved populations in the future and nearly two thirds (67%) indicated a continued interest in working with community organisations throughout medical school. Similarly, a statistically significant improvement in dental students’ attitudes towards volunteering in the community (p = 0.036) was shown in a study by Gadbury-Amyot et al. [71]. In contrast, Brown et al. [78] found no statistically significant change in pharmacy students’ attitudes and their willingness towards volunteering.

Bigger picture—identifying social determinants of health and barriers to healthcare

The students described the community engagement educational experiences as ‘‘eye opening’’ [33, 64, 107], as these generated a greater understanding and appreciation of the complex factors that influence health behaviours and outcomes. The students reflected that sometimes they can be ‘‘blind’’ to poverty and deprivation in their local communities as it is ‘‘easy to be trapped in the student bubble’’ [81]. They acknowledged that reading about determinants of health and access to care ‘‘does not have the same impact as witnessing for oneself’’ [71] and hence experiential learning through community engagement can bring the concept of social determinants to life.

Community projects helped students to ‘‘learn and expand their understanding of the space and geography in which they are living and influencing’’ [100]. Students appreciated how the built environment, transportation links, and the lack or type of local amenities can influence health behaviours and the opportunity to make healthy choices and access care [40, 49, 56, 60, 65, 69, 75].

The students came to realise that there are no quick fixes or easy solutions to the multifaceted and complex needs and challenges faced by different vulnerable groups and underserved communities [101]. The intersectional interplay of politics, culture, religion, societal factors, socioeconomic status and system barriers all influence individual health decisions and outcomes. Students recognised that barriers to accessing services are often beyond the control of the individual [55, 83, 108] and they showed understanding of the reasons why ‘‘mistrust in health care system’’ may exist [33]. Students highlighted the difficulty they observed in navigating the healthcare system, services, and resources [41, 107] and the ‘‘obstacles and hoops people have to jump through’’ [104]. Quantitative and mixed methods studies, similarly, showed a statistically significant improvement in student knowledge and understanding of the impact of socioeconomic status on health and illness (p < 0.001) [78], the impact of healthcare delivery systems in community (p < 0.001) [78] and barriers to care (p < 0.001) [78]. They also demonstrated a better understanding of the impact of social determinants of health on individual health, health behaviours and access to care (p = 0.001) [92], confirming the findings of qualitative studies. However, interestingly, Suresh et al. [82] suggested that there was not a similar increase in student comfort discussing social determinants with patients.

Barriers to social accountability

Students described factors that hindered their ability to feel socially responsible both during their studies as well as in the future when they enter their careers. Some students felt that due to their social or racial background and their upbringing, they were not in a position to understand or empathise with underserved populations and hence felt unable to help these communities [52, 69]. Due to preconceived ideas regarding deprived areas and communities, some students expressed a fear towards their safety in public transport, public spaces and community settings [44, 48, 58, 60]. For some of them this fear hindered their ability to fully engage with the community engagement educational activities [60]. Students described feelings of hopelessness, guilt and frustration when they were confronted with macro-social issues such as poverty and recognised that they are not in a position of power to change the circumstances that underserved communities may face [53, 57, 60, 69, 92]. Besides, some students considered community engagement activities of limited value to the communities they targeted, especially in the long run [42, 49, 57, 60, 87, 100].

Some students could not see the educational value of community engagement in relation to developing their clinical skills and competences [58–60, 66, 83, 94]. The presence of these educational activities in the curriculum were considered ‘‘an unnecessary stress’’ [42] and student time would be ‘‘better spent developing clinical experience’’ [94]. Organisational issues around the delivery of these projects hindered further the ability of students to appreciate the value of community engagement [70, 94].

No shows and low attendance in community engagement activities of community members for whom these activities were designed negatively influenced student views towards underserved communities [53, 71]. Students felt that ‘‘the underserved population wasted that donation’’ [71]. Reflecting on the business model of dentistry and the incurring student debt, some dental students explained how they may not be able to help underserved populations as much as they would have liked to after graduation [71].

Empathy

Humanise community members—everybody has a story

Through community engagement students met people from all walks of life, and they had the opportunity to gain exposure and a deeper understanding of the living conditions of the communities which were different from their own [33, 35, 36, 38–41, 43, 48, 49, 53, 56, 65, 70, 78, 101, 106], and ‘‘learn what it is like to be’’ them [78]. Hearing about people’s different stories, journeys and about their past made the students more empathetic towards them [33, 38, 39, 41, 49]. Some students acknowledged that although they found it hard to imagine being ‘‘in their shoes’’ [39, 40], they appreciated people’s struggles and feelings and made them want to help the people and communities they encountered through their projects.

Students had the opportunity to get to know people from their communities, make connections and build relationships and ‘‘bonds’’ [40, 57, 65] with the individuals they met which they found humbling and rewarding [33, 34, 40, 42, 51, 52, 54, 57, 61, 64, 65, 72, 80, 100].

As a result, community engagement enabled students to humanise community members and see ‘‘individuals for individuals and not as a group of people’’ [101]. Reflecting on their experiences and preconceptions, students expressed how interacting with disadvantaged members of the community, helped see them as ‘‘humans’’ [43, 53, 88], who have the same ‘‘hopes and dreams’’ [65] as they and who deserve ‘‘respect, compassion and love like everyone else’’ [88].

Similarly, a positive impact of community engagement on student empathy was also demonstrated in quantitative and mixed method studies. A statistically significant improvement in paramedic students’ empathy, measured by the Medical Condition Regard Scale (MCRS), was shown by Prakash et al. [99]. In another mixed method study which utilised the Interpersonal Reactive Index (IRI) to assess empathy levels of physiotherapy students, a statistically significant difference was observed in one of the emotional domains of empathy (personal distress), but no statistically significant difference was detected in the cognitive domains of empathy [50]. Furthermore, a statistically significant improvement in sense of altruism in social work students was shown in a quantitative study which used a 20-item self -reported scale for altruism [85].

Overcome bias and challenge attitudes, assumptions and stereotypes

Community engagement helped students to challenge their assumptions and preconceptions [38, 51, 54, 55, 58, 61, 65, 73, 83, 95, 104] and recognise their inherent conscious or unconscious bias towards different individuals and communities [37, 39, 43, 52, 86, 88, 99, 106, 108]. Students also described how these experiences helped them to broaden their horizons [74] and get past their fears and misconceptions regarding different settings and interacting with people experiencing homelessness, older people, people with AIDS, and other marginalised groups [36, 44, 46, 48, 74].

Moreover, students gave examples of how getting to know different individuals and hearing their stories compelled them to confront and overcome the stereotypes and biases they held [44, 47, 53, 101]. Students reflected on their own privilege [40, 47, 49, 55, 65, 69, 71, 75, 83] and the fact they felt blessed for things that they took ‘‘for granted’’ [71, 75, 83]. As a result, students recognised that they have ‘‘no ground to stand on to judge people’’ [49] and recognised the importance of appreciating the effects of social background on health behaviours and outcomes and not being judgmental [43, 49, 69, 78, 82, 93, 94, 101].

The above qualitative findings were also confirmed by quantitative and mixed methods studies using validated scales measuring attitudes towards aging and older people [61, 76, 79, 98], people experiencing homelessness [52, 88, 99], and people experiencing disabilities [97]. In all four studies measuring pre and post student attitudes towards aging and older people, there was a statistically significant improvement in student scores, indicating a positive change against ageist biases. In a study by Beauvais et al. [76] for example, nursing students’ positive attitudes significantly increased in the intervention group (p = 0.001), and their negative attitudes significantly declined (p = 0.004) using the Kogan’s Attitudes Toward Old People Scale. Similarly, a statistically significant improvement in empathetic attitudes towards people experiencing homelessness was found in three studies using different validated scales [52, 88, 99]. In a study of first year dental students, students that entered dental school with idealist attitudes about oral health care for the underserved, maintained these throughout their first year [45]. Interestingly, dental students who had previously volunteered for community work showed more positive attitudes towards the underserved than their peers who had no previous volunteering experience [45]. On the other hand, contrary to the above positive outcomes, in three studies which utilised the Medical Student Attitudes Toward the Underserved scale (MSATU), there was no statistically significant change in attitudes towards caring for the underserved after the different community educational interventions [67, 68, 82].

Cultural competence and sensitivity

Engaging with the community allowed students to learn about other cultures and ‘‘how to provide culturally appropriate care’’ [104]. Students identified the importance of taking into consideration cultural nuances and religious practices when interacting and providing care for people of diverse backgrounds.

Students recognised the importance of considering different cultures’ views and beliefs about healthcare during patients encounters and when determining a treatment plan [41, 56, 78, 92, 104, 107]. Quantitative and mixed methods studies also supported an increase in self-perceived cultural competence [33, 43, 103, 105]. In a mixed methods study, 24 of the 32 students demonstrated evidence of cultural consciousness in their post-experience reflections, which was confirmed by a statistically significant improvement in their post-experience scores on a validated cultural sensitivity scale: White Racial Identity Attitude Scale (WRIAS) (p < 0.0001) [66]. Similarly, Knecht et al. [33] demonstrated a statistically significant increase in cultural competence of nursing students assessed by the Inventory for Assessing the Process of Cultural Competence Among Healthcare Professionals-Student Version (IAPCC-SV) validated tool (p < 0.001).

Empathetic communication and interpersonal skills

Students enhanced their social skills and increased their confidence by interacting with groups of people they had not interacted with before. Students reported enhanced communication and interpersonal skills and ability to use empathetic speech in their interactions which helped establish rapport with community members [53, 56, 61, 75, 81, 99, 101]. They recognised the importance of adjusting their tone, pace, language and style of communication when interacting with different audiences such as children [64, 72, 81], older people [54, 61], and people with disabilities [57, 70, 82, 97], and being sensitive to the individual’s specific needs and circumstances [40]. Students appreciated the need to use lay language and avoid using jargon when communicating health information [77, 95, 107]. The students recognised active listening as paramount when interacting with community members and patients [34, 39, 42, 52, 64, 95] and understood there are times when it is appropriate to be silent [39, 52]. They also came to realise the power of non-verbal communication [33, 52, 101] and eye contact in particular [39, 72, 107] when it comes to creating caring relationships. Students concluded that effective communication is a two-way street and highlighted the importance of taking into consideration people's knowledge attitudes and behaviours when communicating [40, 51, 65, 72].

Feeling sympathy and sadness

Students described the emotional impact the community engagement activities had on them. Students expressed feelings of sympathy and sadness towards peoples’ difficult situations and vulnerability [43, 46, 52, 65, 69, 70, 95, 104]. Students used for example terms such as ‘‘devastating’’ [65] and ‘‘my heart broke’’ [52] referring to their experiences. Some students recognised the difference between sympathy and empathy and highlighted their difficulty in discriminating between the two [39, 49].

Shaping the future workforce

Future professional demeanour

Students described and recognised attributes and behaviours that they will need to become better healthcare professionals [36, 44, 52, 80, 94]. Compassion was frequently quoted [36, 44, 94] as an essential quality, alongside being ‘‘caring’’ [35, 43, 80], and ‘‘understanding’’ [36]. Treating patients ‘‘holistically’’ [43], being a good communicator [35, 36, 52, 55], investing time to know patients as people and finding out about their lives and their social context [40, 43, 77] were also considered important in order to build ‘‘trusting relationships’’ [43] with the patients and empower them so ‘‘ they can help themselves’’ [53].

Community engagement was described as ‘‘refreshing’’ [49] as it enabled students to think about the role of healthcare in the wider community and reinforced the belief that healthcare professionals have an active role outside of the clinical setting in shaping policy and being a positive force for improving health in the community [34, 42, 49, 55, 69, 92]. Students often highlighted the importance of their future professional roles as becoming advocates for community health [34, 59, 93] and ‘‘social inclusion’’ [48].

Professional interests

Student’s future professional interests were influenced by their experiences through community engagement. Students met and got to know different populations they had not interacted with beforehand and reported that they would consider pursuing a career devoted to caring for these populations. Examples included working with older adults [46, 58, 61, 79], children [36] and underserved or vulnerable populations [82, 101]. Equally, some students expressed an interest in practicing in rural and medically underserved areas [56, 78, 82] and in primary care [92] or community-based settings [47, 53, 62] in the future.

Interprofessional education and collaboration

Through observing different healthcare and social work professionals during their community placements and through interacting with other health and social care students in interprofessional activities, students were able to appreciate the importance of interprofessional teamworking and collaboration in patient care and health outcomes. These activities helped students to learn about the roles and responsibilities of other healthcare professionals, understand different perspectives and develop respect and appreciation for the expertise of the various professions [42, 43, 55, 58, 77, 78, 83, 86, 89, 91, 92, 95, 103]. However, in a survey study by Brown et al. [78] although student’s knowledge and understanding about the responsibilities of interdisciplinary teams significantly improved (p < 0.001), their willingness to be part of a multi-disciplinary team in their future careers did not change significantly after a service-learning elective.

Discussion

Reorienting healthcare education towards social accountability has been widely advocated internationally by different educational and regulatory organisations [110, 111]. Community engaged education offers multiple benefits that can enable healthcare institutions to fulfil their social contract and address their societal commitment to the communities they serve. The present systematic review showed that community engagement can improve healthcare profession students’ understanding of community needs and social determinants of health, improve their empathy and cultural sensitivity, and build advocacy for social justice and change.

Community engagement was reported to be an eye-opening experience to many students, as it helped them to engage with individuals from diverse backgrounds and life experiences and exposed them to disadvantaged socioeconomic situations. Students may enter their training with preconceived ideas about different populations. Many students may not have any prior experiences with vulnerable populations and may hold beliefs and possibly fears that could interfere with the quality of care they provide. Our review showed that students who partook in community engagement developed cultural sensitivity and an appreciation of the health disparities that exist within the communities they serve. Students who understand social determinants of health may likely be better equipped to appreciate the factors that contribute to health disparities for vulnerable populations and engage in efforts to minimise them. Through community engagement, students developed a sense of social and moral responsibility towards their communities and felt responsible to advocate for their right to health. These learning opportunities situated within the community helped many students to confront their biases and resulted in developing empathy for others who they realise in the end, are not very different from themselves.

Empathy is an important quality of all healthcare professionals and an important non-technical skill for healthcare students to develop during their training [12]. Empathy has a cognitive and emotional element. Cognitive empathy refers to understanding another’s experience from a mental perspective, whilst emotional empathy encompasses being able to share another’s experiences and feelings. In the healthcare context, understanding the patients’ emotions and needs is more important than vicariously experiencing them [13]. The included studies collectively showed an increase in student empathy and an improvement in attitudes towards different populations and appreciation of community members’ experiences and situations, upon participation in community engagement activities, which is in line with a recent systematic review in medical education [10].

Community engagement allows students to move beyond the biomedical model of healthcare and conceptualise their practice within a holistic and inclusive model of health by appreciating the effects of social determinants of health. Thus, allowing students to develop their professional identity alongside developing a broader understanding of their future role in the community, both professionally and personally. Reeves et al. [10] similarly suggested that professional identity transformation occurs as a result of service-learning placements in medical students who study in medical schools with a social accountability mandated curriculum. In addition, the present review suggested that students recognised that in terms of their professional identity, they are a part of a bigger network of professionals and as such, interprofessional collaboration is paramount to the delivery of safe, effective and patient centered care.

Implications for educational practice and policy

The role of reflection in developing a sense of social responsibility and empathy cannot be overlooked. In some studies, guided reflection was an integral part of the community engagement educational activities [33, 39, 47, 52, 65, 66, 69, 71, 76, 82, 85, 87, 98, 101, 106, 107]. The use of guided reflection is particularly valuable when the goal is to identify and assess the cognitive and affective domain of student beliefs and emotional reactions in relation to personal experiences [112, 113]. This is even more relevant when students may have established false preconceptions and biases about the community participants. Simply situating students in community settings may not have the same effect as unpicking the complex interplay between health behaviours and attitudes and social determinants such as poverty, privilege, and power through a structured reflection led by competent faculty staff. Helping students to make sense of and interpret an experience is in line with the transformative learning theory; which suggests that exposing a learner to a disorienting situation triggers a reflection process that leads to the learner’s transformation and reassessment of their assumptions and perspectives [114]. This reflection element of community engagement differentiates community engaged education from other educational activities such as outreach clinical placements or volunteerism.

Contrary to a recent definition of community engaged education put forth by the Innovation Collaborative on Learning through Community Engagement ‘‘Health professional education is community engaged when community-academic partnerships are sustained, and they focus on the collaborative design, delivery, and evaluation of programs in order to improve the health of the people and communities the programs serve’’ [5], active and reciprocal involvement of community partners was only evident or described/reported in some of the included studies [33, 38, 46, 48, 49, 52, 62, 66, 70, 74, 82, 88, 90, 93, 95, 101, 104–107]. This observation is in line with a systematic review by Hunt et al. [8] on community based medical education, which concluded that medical schools do not routinely involve community members in the identification of local health priorities. Community engaged education requires the engagement of individuals (students, teachers, community members), institutions (from academia and the community), and their leadership; and collaborative development of the design, delivery, and evaluation of the learning activities [5, 14]. Healthcare educational institutions should utilise the expertise of community partners in the curriculum design and delivery and make community true partners and collaborators. Future community engagement educational programmes should consider the active involvement of community partners, following a co-creation or community participatory approach, so that the educational activities are not merely situated within the community but are designed and developed with and for the community.

Our review findings suggested social responsibility and empathy within healthcare education could be categorised into three intersecting domains: the individual, the institutional, and the structural. The individual domain refers to personal attributes whereby some students may by nature or nurture be more empathetic and altruistic and therefore more engaged with this type of pedagogy. The institutional domain captures local educational strategies and policies such as current UK Higher Education aspirations to become civic universities that can facilitate support and resources for community engaged teaching and community engaged educational activities that aim to build social accountability. The structural domain applies to wider policies and healthcare systems. For example, when professional standard requirements expect demonstrable social accountability and empathy in teaching programmes, there is greater likelihood of curricula being modified to accommodate pedagogy aligned to developing empathy, social justice, and social accountability; or when national level health polices embed expectations of health professionals, training courses adapt to meet new standards. Variations in these domains across contexts could account for differences across study findings in our review. Whilst any one of these domains may be positively associated with empathy and social justice, we propose the greatest impact would come from a positive attitude towards community engaged education across all three domains.

The findings of the present review are in line with previous work highlighting the diversity in approaches and aims of community engaged education across different institutions and nations [8, 10, 115]. Thus, there is an urgent call for bringing together interdisciplinary expertise to develop a framework for community engaged education. This would assist healthcare institutions in developing clear strategies for its effective implementation, thereby allowing space for creativity, innovation, and scholarship. These intersectional collaborative efforts could harmonize the identified fragmentation and align goals, approaches, and optimal delivery of healthcare community engaged education across healthcare disciplines internationally.

Implications for future research

The present review focused on countries of very high development as assessed by the UN Human Development Index (HDI) [22]. Therefore, our findings may not be able to be extrapolated and directly applied to educational settings of less developed countries due to the different educational, economic and societal infrastructures. Valuable lessons could have been learned from incorporating studies from less developed countries, which we accept as a limitation of our work. Further research is warranted across different international contexts to provide more universal recommendations. The role of prior community work and volunteering, as a confounding factor should also be accounted for in future studies. Well-structured longitudinal studies exploring the cost-effectiveness of community engagement activities will better inform policy and may encourage universal adoption of community engaged educational models across all healthcare educational sectors. Furthermore, the long-term impact of community engaged activities or programmes through student’s undergraduate training years and after graduation needs further exploration, especially since a decline in empathy over time during training and transition to clinical practice has been observed in several studies across various healthcare disciplines [116–119].

Conclusions

Community engaged education can help students appreciate community needs and the role of social determinants in health behaviours and outcomes; improve their empathy; and turn them into advocates of social justice and health equity; and drivers for change. By students overcoming their own biases and misconceptions and becoming more culturally sensitive, their future patients may also experience less stigma, blame, and discrimination within the health care system. Incorporating community engagement in the undergraduate curriculum may support healthcare educational institutions in producing socially accountable graduates competent to meet community needs at national and global level. The role of partnership and collaboration among relevant stakeholders is paramount for the successful implementation of community engaged education to ensure an authentic experience for students. The long-term impact of community engagement beyond graduation and its cost-effectiveness warrants further exploration.
