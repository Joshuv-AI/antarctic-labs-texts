Risk‐Stratified Bilateral Tonsillectomy for Unilateral HPV‐Associated Tonsillar SCC: Oncologic and Functional Outcomes From a Single Institution
Authors: Tereza Vitkovska, Crystal Z. Zhou, Ishna Pandey, Maxwell L. Weng, Lauran K. Evans, Song Hon Hwang, Christian Wooten, Dipti Sajed, Maie A. St. John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo characterize synchronous and metachronous contralateral second primary malignancy (SPM) rates across preoperatively defined risk strata in patients with HPV‐positive tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) undergoing unilateral versus bilateral tonsillectomy and to evaluate associated postoperative morbidity and functional outcomes.MethodsAll patients with HPV‐positive TSCC who underwent surgery between 2000 and 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Fisher's exact test, Kaplan–Meier analysis, and log‐rank tests were used to compare morbidity and survival, and multivariable regression models were employed to control for confounders, including patient demographics, substance use, tumor staging, and adjuvant therapy.ResultsFrom 163 patients, 123 (75.5%) underwent unilateral tonsillectomy, 30 (18.4%) low‐risk bilateral tonsillectomy, and 10 (6.1%) high‐risk bilateral tonsillectomy. Across all patients undergoing bilateral tonsillectomy, synchronous tonsillar SPMs were identified in 10.0%, with rates of 6.7% in the low‐risk bilateral group and 20.0% in the high‐risk bilateral group. Of the 123 patients who underwent unilateral tonsillectomy, 0.8% developed a metachronous contralateral TSCC at 181.6 months. Among low‐risk bilateral patients undergoing prophylactic contralateral tonsillectomy, the NNT for identification of one occult synchronous SPM was 14 (95% CI: 5–50). Bilateral tonsillectomy did not significantly alter discharge diet, postoperative length of stay, hemorrhage rates, return to the operating room, or 30‐day emergency department visits. Disease‐free survival did not differ significantly across risk‐stratified groups.ConclusionContralateral second primary TSCC clustered disproportionately among high‐risk patients. Bilateral tonsillectomy was not associated with significantly increased postoperative morbidity. These findings support a selective, risk‐stratified approach to bilateral tonsillectomy in HPV‐associated TSCC, balancing the potential benefit of occult contralateral disease detection against procedure‐related morbidity through shared decision‐making.Level of EvidenceIII.

Introduction

The incidence of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma continues to rise globally, with an estimated 106,400 new cases reported in 2022 [1, 2]. Tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is the most common oropharyngeal subsite, and HPV‐positive disease carries substantially better oncologic outcomes than HPV‐negative disease [3, 4]. Historically, second primary malignancies (SPM) from field cancerization—the diffuse mucosal effect of tobacco and alcohol exposure—were the leading cause of long‐term mortality in head and neck cancer [5, 6]. The emergence of HPV as the driver of oropharyngeal cancer has fundamentally altered this landscape: oropharyngeal cancers now carry the lowest risk of SPM among all head and neck subsites [7, 8]. These favorable outcomes have fueled interest in treatment de‐escalation and renewed attention to surgical decision‐making, including whether bilateral tonsillectomy is warranted for clinically unilateral disease.

The management of the contralateral tonsil in unilateral HPV‐positive TSCC remains one of the most debated questions in head and neck surgical oncology: an occult synchronous contralateral malignancy may be missed by unilateral surgery, yet bilateral tonsillectomy carries added risks of increased surgical morbidity including hemorrhage and swallowing dysfunction [9, 10]. A 2018 commentary by Kim and St. John brought early attention to this question, recommending routine contralateral tonsillectomy in suspected or known unilateral HPV‐positive TSCC and highlighting the oncologic consequences of occult contralateral disease [9]. As the evidence base has grown, subsequent authors called for larger studies to better characterize this risk–benefit profile, particularly regarding surgical morbidity in patients without preoperative evidence of bilateral disease [11]. Reported synchronous contralateral SPM rates vary widely with preoperative risk features, from 2.3%–2.7% without clinical concern for bilateral disease to 15%–33% with bilateral lymphadenopathy or suspicious imaging, while metachronous SPM rates after unilateral surgery with surveillance have remained low (0.3%) [12, 13, 14, 15].

The most comprehensive synthesis to date is the meta‐analysis by Peterson et al., pooling 11 studies and 1486 patients [16]. They reported a pooled synchronous contralateral SPM rate of 4.0% with bilateral surgery and a metachronous rate of 0.1% with unilateral surgery, with no between‐group differences in survival, hemorrhage rates, or gastrostomy tube dependence, concluding that omission of contralateral tonsillectomy is safe and should be guided by shared decision‐making. A key limitation, however, is that included studies did not stratify patients by preoperative risk, pooling those with bilateral lymphadenopathy or suspicious PET findings together with those with entirely unilateral imaging, and postoperative complication data were available in only 3 of 11 studies [16]. A separate meta‐analysis by Charlton et al. (13 studies, 2623 patients) reported a concordant synchronous prevalence of 4% and found that clinical and imaging evaluation often missed contralateral disease preoperatively, further supporting the need for risk‐stratified investigation [17].

While some prior studies have applied preoperative risk stratification [12, 13, 14, 15], this approach has not been consistently used across the literature. Here we present our single‐institution experience comparing unilateral and bilateral tonsillectomy in HPV‐positive TSCC, characterizing synchronous and metachronous contralateral SPM rates across preoperatively defined risk strata and evaluating associated postoperative morbidity and functional outcomes.

Methods

Patient Selection

This study is a single institution, retrospective cohort study approved by the UCLA Institutional Review Board. We identified all patients with primary tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) diagnosed between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2023 at a single tertiary care academic hospital. Only patients with confirmed p16‐positive testing at initial diagnosis and treated with primary surgical resection, with or without adjuvant radiation or chemotherapy, were included. p16 immunohistochemistry (> 70%) was used as a surrogate marker for HPV‐associated disease per institutional practice during the study period. Patients with an unknown primary subsequently diagnosed with HPV+ TSCC during workup were also included.

Patients underwent unilateral or bilateral tonsillectomy as primary surgical management for TSCC. Bilateral tonsillectomy patients were sub‐classified as high‐risk or low‐risk based on preoperative clinical and imaging suspicion for contralateral disease. High‐risk patients (“high‐risk bilateral”) had bilateral lymphadenopathy, HPV‐positive cervical nodal metastases from an unknown primary with imaging (FDG‐PET/CT or CT) concerning for bilateral tonsillar disease, or tongue base involvement concerning for synchronous oropharyngeal disease. Low‐risk patients (“low‐risk bilateral”) had physical exam and imaging suggestive of unilateral disease without concern for contralateral involvement, but underwent bilateral tonsillectomy by patient or surgeon preference. Patients with carcinoma of unknown primary underwent bilateral tonsillectomy as part of diagnostic workup for primary tumor localization (without prior tonsillar biopsy, as tonsillectomy itself served as the diagnostic procedure) and were classified as high‐ or low‐risk using the same criteria.

To differentiate persistent disease, recurrence, and second primary malignancies (SPM), the following definitions were applied. Persistent disease was residual tumor at the index site within 6 months of completing primary treatment. Recurrence was return of disease at the index site after a disease‐free interval of 6–60 months after treatment completion. A synchronous contralateral tonsil SPM was an independent contralateral carcinoma identified at diagnosis or within 6 months of treatment completion; a metachronous contralateral tonsil SPM, one arising more than 6 months after treatment completion. A metachronous SPM of the upper aerodigestive tract (UADT) was an independent second primary arising more than 6 months after treatment completion at a site other than the index tonsil, or any UADT tumor arising more than 60 months after treatment regardless of location. These definitions were adapted from established criteria by Warren and Gates, consistent with prior studies [13, 18, 19].

Tonsils with clinical, radiological, or pathological features concerning for malignancy were excised as a radical tonsillectomy as a permanent section specimen to obtain final pathology, with circumferential frozen sections taken to verify margins intraoperatively. In high‐risk bilateral cases, the contralateral tonsil was excised as a radical tonsillectomy when intraoperative findings were concerning for malignancy, and as a standard tonsillectomy otherwise. In low‐risk bilateral cases, the contralateral tonsil was excised in a standard tonsillectomy as a permanent section specimen to obtain final pathology. Ipsilateral and contralateral neck dissection were performed for clinical, radiological, or pathological concern for nodal metastasis on the corresponding side.

Patients were admitted overnight postoperatively, given analgesia (oxycodone and acetaminophen or ibuprofen, with or without stellate ganglion blockade per surgeon preference), and advanced from a liquid diet as tolerated. Postoperative complications reviewed included primary and secondary hemorrhage, 30‐day emergency department visits, 30‐day return to the operating room, and unplanned ICU admission.

Data collected from electronic medical records included demographics, smoking and alcohol use history, surgical approach, adjuvant treatment, postoperative length of stay and diet, and dates of recurrence, last follow‐up, and death. Pathologic T, N, M classification and the presence of extracapsular spread, extranodal extension, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion were extracted from pathology reports. Adjuvant treatment was categorized as none, radiation alone, or concurrent chemoradiation.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± SD. Age was compared across groups using the Kruskal‐Wallis test; other continuous variables using Fisher's exact test and ANOVA, with pairwise comparisons against the unilateral reference group. Unless otherwise specified, comparisons were adjusted for age, smoking, alcohol use, pT, pN, adjuvant radiation, and contralateral tonsil treatment. Disease‐free survival (time from primary surgery to recurrence or second primary malignancy) was modeled by Kaplan–Meier analysis with pairwise log‐rank tests, and by adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression, both referenced to the unilateral group. Discharge diet was analyzed by adjusted logistic regression with a Wald test for overall cohort effect; postoperative complications by unadjusted Fisher's exact test given low event counts, which also precluded tonsillar second primary and cancer‐specific survival analysis. The number needed to treat (NNT) for the low‐risk bilateral cohort was calculated using the Wilson score method (95% CI). Patients without an event were censored at last follow‐up. All analyses were performed in R (v4.4.2; R Core Team 2024) at a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

Most patients were never or former smokers (95.7% combined), and half reported alcohol use (50.3%). TSCC and Second Primary Malignancy Characteristics

Pathological T classification differed significantly across groups (p = 0.01). No significant differences were observed between groups with respect to pathological N classification, M classification, extranodal extension, perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, or extracapsular spread (Table 1).

Second primary malignancy rates are summarized in Table 2, with site‐level detail in Table S1. Among 123 patients who underwent unilateral tonsillectomy, 9 had locoregional recurrence, 1 had nodal recurrence, 4 developed metachronous SPM, including 1 tonsillar primary at 181.6 months and 3 other UADT primaries (15.5–203.8 months). To evaluate the diagnostic yield of contralateral tonsillectomy absent preoperative suspicion, NNT was calculated for the low‐risk bilateral cohort alone (n = 28), excluding 2 patients who underwent bilateral tonsillectomy for unknown primary workup—an independent clinical indication rather than a prophylactic one. Based on a 7.1% (2/28) synchronous SPM detection rate, the NNT was 14 (95% CI: 5–50), corresponding to one occult contralateral malignancy identified per 14 low‐risk bilateral procedures. The wide confidence interval reflects the current sample size, and the true clinical NNT may range from highly favorable to more modest benefit.

Treatment Characteristics

Adjuvant treatment rates did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.45, Table 1): radiation alone was given to 50.0% of low‐risk bilateral, 30.0% of high‐risk bilateral, and 45.5% of unilateral patients, and chemoradiation to 36.7%, 50.0%, and 29.3%, respectively. Contralateral tonsil treatment (chemoradiation or bilateral radiation) was similarly non‐significant across groups (50.0%, 70.0%, 40.7%; p = 0.16).

Median follow‐up was shorter for the low‐risk bilateral group (2.1 years, IQR = 4.1) than the high‐risk bilateral group (6.9 years, IQR = 5.5) or unilateral group (5.5 years, IQR = 3.4; p < 0.01, Table 3).

Survival Analysis

Disease‐Free Survival (DFS)

Neither the low‐risk (p = 0.824) nor high‐risk bilateral group (p = 0.811) showed a significant DFS difference from the unilateral reference on adjusted Cox analysis. Recurrence rates were 6.7% (n = 2), 10.0% (n = 1), and 9.8% (n = 12), respectively (Table 2). Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 1) showed comparable survival trajectories, with DFS remaining above 85% throughout follow‐up. Persistent disease occurred in 1 low‐risk bilateral (3.3%) and 2 unilateral patients (1.6%), with none in the high‐risk bilateral group.

Kaplan–Meier curves for disease‐free survival by surgical cohort. Tick marks denote censored patients. Axes truncated (x, 10 years; y, 75%) for visualization; statistics reflect complete follow‐up.

Cancer‐Specific Survival (CSS)

Cancer‐specific deaths occurred in 1 (3.3%) low‐risk bilateral, 0 high‐risk bilateral, and 5 (4.1%) unilateral patients (Table 2). Formal multivariable CSS analysis was not performed given the absence of high‐risk bilateral deaths and overall low event counts across groups.

Functional Outcomes

No significant differences were observed in length of stay or discharge diet (Table 3). Mean length of stay was 4.0 ± 2.0 days (low‐risk bilateral), 2.8 ± 2.8 days (high‐risk bilateral), and 3.9 ± 2.8 days (unilateral; p = 0.967 and p = 0.756 vs. unilateral). Combined bilateral groups did not differ significantly from unilateral (3.7 ± 2.3 vs. 3.9 ± 2.8 days; p = 0.607).

Postoperative Complications

Postoperative complication rates are summarized in Table 4. No statistically significant differences were observed between either bilateral group and the unilateral group in any category. Hemorrhage was the most common complication overall. Primary hemorrhage was not observed in any of the patients in the bilateral tonsillectomy group compared to 2 patients (1.7%) in the unilateral cohort (p = 1.00 for both comparisons). Secondary hemorrhage occurred in 1 low‐risk bilateral (3.3%), 0 high‐risk bilateral, and 12 unilateral patients (10.0%; p = 0.47, p = 0.60, respectively). Any hemorrhagic event occurred in 1 low‐risk bilateral (3.3%), 0 high‐risk bilateral, and 14 unilateral (11.7%) patients (p = 0.31, p = 0.60, respectively).

Thirty‐day emergency department visits occurred in 2 (6.7%) low‐risk bilateral, 0 high‐risk bilateral, and 15 (12.5%) unilateral patients (p = 0.53, p = 0.60, respectively). Hemorrhage was the leading cause (n = 10), followed by pain, dehydration, a manic episode, and hypertension with neurological symptoms (n = 1 each). All 7 unplanned interventional radiology procedures were for hemorrhage control. Of the 3 unplanned returns to the operating room, 2 were for surgical bleeding control and 1 for palatoplasty addressing dysphonia and granulation tissue at 2 weeks postoperatively.

Post‐surgical ICU admission for airway observation or inability to extubate occurred in 0 low‐risk bilateral, 1 (10.0%) high‐risk bilateral, and 5 (4.2%) unilateral patients (p = 0.58, p = 0.39, respectively). Four patients required monitoring for hemodynamic instability and two required persistent intubation.

Discussion

This study characterized the prevalence of synchronous and metachronous second primary TSCC following surgical management of unilateral HPV‐positive TSCC and evaluated whether preoperative clinical and radiographic features could identify patients at higher risk for contralateral disease. In this cohort, contralateral second primary disease was uncommon overall but disproportionately concentrated among patients with high‐risk features.

Among patients with known unilateral HPV+ TSCC, the overall prevalence of synchronous or metachronous second primary TSCC was 3.1%. All synchronous contralateral TSCCs were identified at the time of bilateral tonsillectomy, supporting a role for bilateral surgical evaluation in detecting occult contralateral disease at presentation. The prevalence of synchronous second primary TSCC was substantially higher in the high‐risk bilateral group (20.0%) than the low‐risk bilateral group (6.7%). Among unilateral patients, the rate of second primary TSCC was 0.8%, with only one metachronous case, arising 181.6 months after initial treatment—given this latency, it is uncertain whether this represented a true second primary or late recurrence. Collectively, these findings suggest the principal benefit of bilateral tonsillectomy may lie in identifying synchronous occult disease at presentation, while its impact on preventing future contralateral malignancy remains less clear.

Among patients without preoperative suspicion for contralateral disease, the number needed to treat for one occult synchronous contralateral tonsillar SPM was 14 (95% CI: 5.0–50.0). The wide interval reflects the current sample size, though the point estimate suggests a potentially meaningful diagnostic yield, supporting further investigation in larger cohorts. These findings are consistent with the Charlton et al. meta‐analysis of 2623 patients, which found that clinical and imaging evaluations often missed synchronous contralateral tonsil carcinoma preoperatively, reinforcing that preoperative workup alone may be insufficient to exclude occult disease [17]. Thus, bilateral tonsillectomy may remain appropriate even among clinically low‐risk patients.

Our findings are consistent with the meta‐analysis by Peterson et al. [16] which reported a pooled synchronous contralateral tonsillar second primary rate of 4.0% with bilateral tonsillectomy and a metachronous rate of 0.1% with unilateral tonsillectomy, concluding that omission of routine contralateral tonsillectomy is safe and should be addressed through shared decision‐making. Population‐level analysis from the SEER database demonstrated low overall rates of multiple HPV+ head and neck primary malignancies [20]. However, several studies have suggested higher synchronous contralateral TSCC rates among patients with clinical or radiographic concern for bilateral involvement. Parhar et al. reported synchronous rate of 33% with high‐risk imaging features versus 0% without suspicion for bilateral disease [12]. McMillan et al. reported 15% among patients with clinical concern for bilateral disease versus 2.7% with workup suggestive of unilateral disease [13]. Our findings support the low overall incidence of metachronous contralateral TSCC after unilateral tonsillectomy, while additionally showing increased synchronous rates among patients with high‐risk clinical features.

Bilateral HPV exposure may be more common than bilateral carcinogenesis. Prior studies have demonstrated active HPV infection in non‐neoplastic tonsillar tissue, and multifocal HPV DNA has been identified in both palatine and pharyngeal tonsils in HPV‐positive oropharyngeal cancer patients [21, 22]. Despite evidence of bilateral viral exposure, progression to contralateral carcinoma appears relatively uncommon, suggesting that local immune surveillance and other host factors may limit malignant transformation. Nevertheless, 6.7% of low‐risk patients in our cohort harbored occult synchronous contralateral carcinoma undetected by preoperative imaging, supporting consideration of selective bilateral tonsillectomy in appropriately risk‐stratified patients.

Current NCCN Head and Neck Cancer Guidelines recommend a structured diagnostic approach for HPV‐positive SCC of unknown primary, including examination under anesthesia with directed biopsies and tonsillectomy with or without lingual tonsillectomy when clinical and radiographic workup does not identify a primary site [23]. In this setting, bilateral tonsillectomy is performed to localize an occult oropharyngeal primary rather than to evaluate contralateral second primary disease risk—stratifying these patients by risk features may still clarify whether the diagnostic yield of bilateral tonsillectomy differs across groups. In our cohort, 5 patients with unknown primary in the high‐risk category and 2 unknown primary patients in the low‐risk category had a positive tonsillar primary identified, suggesting that risk features were associated with increased diagnostic yield in this subgroup.

Given the low overall incidence of second primary TSCC, the potential morbidity associated with bilateral tonsillectomy is an important consideration in surgical decision‐making. In a related single‐institution study focused specifically on transoral robotic surgery (TORS) outcomes, Peterson et al. [24] reported higher rates of oropharyngeal hemorrhage, 30‐day emergency department (ED) visits or hospitalization, and longer postoperative length of stay among patients undergoing bilateral versus unilateral TORS, without significant differences in disease‐free or overall survival. Based on these findings, the authors concluded that prophylactic contralateral tonsillectomy may increase patient harm without clear oncologic benefit. In contrast, our cohort did not demonstrate significant differences in progression‐free survival, postoperative length of stay, hemorrhage rates, discharge diet, gastrostomy tube utilization, return to OR, hemorrhage‐related interventional radiology procedures, or post‐discharge hospital utilization between low‐risk bilateral and unilateral tonsillectomy cohorts. Though not statistically significant, the unilateral cohort represented 11.7% of observed hemorrhagic events compared to 2.5% in the bilateral cohort. Bilateral tonsillectomy may therefore remain an appropriate consideration in patients at elevated risk for synchronous contralateral disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective review, this study was limited by the available clinical documentation, introducing potential for misclassification. Patients undergoing bilateral tonsillectomy were selected based on clinical and radiographic concern for contralateral disease, which may limit the generalizability of subgroup prevalence estimates. Additionally, the relatively small number of synchronous and metachronous tonsillar second primary malignancies, particularly within the high‐risk bilateral subgroup, limited statistical power and the precision of subgroup analyses. The low number of cancer‐specific deaths limited survival analyses. Comparisons of postoperative morbidity may be confounded by heterogeneity in concurrent oncologic procedures, including neck dissection and extent of primary tumor resection. Future studies should focus on prospective validation of risk‐stratification criteria, including radiographic and molecular predictors, as well as rates of synchronous and metachronous HPV‐positive tonsillar SCC, to refine surgical evaluation strategies.

Conclusion

Contralateral second primary HPV‐positive TSCC was uncommon overall but clustered disproportionately among patients with high‐risk clinical and radiographic features, with synchronous disease identified primarily at the time of bilateral tonsillectomy rather than as metachronous events following unilateral surgery. Bilateral tonsillectomy was not associated with significantly increased postoperative morbidity. These findings support an individualized, risk‐stratified approach to bilateral tonsillectomy in HPV‐associated TSCC, balancing the potential benefit of occult contralateral disease detection against procedure‐related morbidity through shared decision‐making. Prospective validation of preoperative risk‐stratification criteria in larger, multi‐institutional cohorts will be essential to refine patient selection.
