Beyond Depression and Chronic Pain: Toward Understanding the Complex Developmental Pathways to Self-Harm
Authors: Kaili Rimfeld, Alice M Gregory
Published: 2026

Self-harm is a substantial public health challenge, shaped by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors (1,2). Despite substantial progress in psychiatric genetics, the mechanisms linking self-harm to broader dimensions of mental and physical health remain poorly understood. In a recent study published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, Liu et al. (3) address this question by integrating epidemiological analyses, polygenic risk scores, linkage disequilibrium score regression, Mendelian randomization, genomic structural equation modeling (SEM), and multivariate genome-wide association analyses to investigate the relationships between chronic pain, depression, lifetime self-harm ideation, and self-harm behavior. Their findings demonstrate that depression has a substantially stronger relationship with self-harm compared with chronic pain. Their findings also suggest that the association between chronic pain and self-harm may be mediated, at least in part, by depression, and highlight biological pathways involved in synaptic signaling and neurodevelopment. Building on these findings, we focus on 2 areas that we think will be particularly important for future research: understanding these relationships at a more detailed phenotypic level, going beyond broad diagnostic categories, and examining how they emerge and change across development.

One of the strengths of Liu et al. (3) study is the authors’ decision to begin with phenotypic analyses before progressing to increasingly sophisticated genetic approaches. Although this progression may seem intuitive, increasingly sophisticated genetic analyses are not always accompanied by equally careful phenotypic characterization. The authors make careful use of the available data, combining self-report measures, hospital records, exploratory analyses of temporal ordering, age- and sex-stratified analyses, interaction analyses, and extensive sensitivity analyses with a range of genetically informed methods. Importantly, these approaches also allow the authors to move beyond simple associations and begin to examine the possible directionality and temporal ordering of these relationships. This progression is important because genetic methods cannot compensate for limitations in phenotypic measurement. Rather, their interpretation depends critically on the quality, precision, and developmental relevance of the phenotypes being studied. Liu et al. (3) provide an important framework for future research while raising a broader question: how can we better characterize these pathways across development?

Self-harm rarely occurs in isolation. However, depression and chronic pain are undoubtedly important parts of the wider picture, and Liu et al. (3) already take a broader approach, considering psychiatric comorbidities such as anxiety, sleep disorders, and substance use in their analyses and extending their genetic analyses to a range of psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions. Their findings show substantial genetic overlap between self-harm and other psychiatric traits. Therefore, an important next step may not simply be to include an even greater number of conditions but to examine these phenotypes in greater detail and understand how different symptoms, experiences, and risk factors come together to increase the risk of self-harm. Trauma exposure, emotional dysregulation, social difficulties, educational problems, and generally low well-being may all form part of these broader developmental pathways. The challenge is to understand which combinations are important, how they change over time, and whether they represent different pathways toward self-harm.

Increasing evidence suggests that psychiatric disorders share substantial genetic overlap and that many conditions reflect combinations of general and disorder-specific liabilities (4,5). At the same time, there is growing recognition that individuals may arrive at similar outcomes through very different developmental and etiological pathways (in line with the notion of equifinality), whereas similar symptoms and experiences may cut across traditional diagnostic boundaries. Although Liu et al. (3) focus primarily on relationships between defined phenotypes and their shared genetic influences, we argue that an important next step is to investigate how symptoms, behaviors, biological processes, and environmental experiences combine within individuals and change over time. This requires understanding both the differences between individuals in the pathways leading to self-harm and the changes within individuals as these pathways unfold (6). With increasingly rich longitudinal data from diverse populations and geographical settings, and with multivariate approaches, cluster analyses, and phenotypic and genetic network analyses, there is an opportunity to investigate these processes in much greater detail than was previously possible. Such a multilevel approach may also help clarify how genetic and environmental differences operate through intermediate biological, psychological, and developmental processes to shape the risk of self-harm.

The increasing availability of large-scale cohorts offers an opportunity to move beyond broad diagnostic categories and investigate specific symptoms and experiences that contribute to self-harm risk. Liu et al. (3) used UK Biobank data, with approximately 215,000 participants contributing to the self-harm analyses. Participants were recruited between the ages of 40 and 70 years, and the genetic analyses were predominantly based on British White and European-ancestry data. The scale of such resources provides sufficient power to examine heterogeneity, less common symptom profiles, and developmental trajectories that are often difficult to investigate in smaller studies. When combined with repeated prospective assessments, large longitudinal cohorts can also be used to characterize developmental trajectories. This is particularly relevant in the case of chronic pain and depression, both of which encompass considerable heterogeneity. Chronic pain differs in duration, severity, location, functional consequences, associated sleep disruption, underlying biological mechanisms, and the degree of accompanying social isolation (7). Similarly, depression comprises a broad range of symptoms, including low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, and changes in sleep and appetite. Individuals who meet the same diagnostic criteria may therefore differ substantially in their symptom profiles, experiences, and outcomes, whereas similar symptoms may cut across traditional diagnostic boundaries (8).

Better phenotypic characterization will also require methods that can make use of this complexity. Symptom-level data utilizing longitudinal cohorts, primary and secondary care data, together with advances in statistical genetics, provide an opportunity to address this challenge. Genome-wide association studies of individual symptoms, factor-analytic approaches, network models, and genomic SEM may help identify more homogeneous dimensions of risk and distinguish shared liabilities from more specific pathways. Liu et al. (3) take an important step in this direction by identifying shared genetic influences across chronic pain, depression, and self-harm. However, an important next step will be to identify the specific biological, psychological, and environmental mechanisms through which these liabilities influence later outcomes.

The second area we think is particularly important is development. Although Liu et al. (3) provide important insights into the phenotypic and genetic relationships between chronic pain, depression, and self-harm, future studies should adopt a more explicit developmental perspective. For many individuals, self-harm is preceded by difficulties that may begin years earlier and change over time. This is consistent with transdiagnostic clinical staging models, which conceptualize psychopathology developmentally, with early, often nonspecific difficulties potentially preceding a range of later, more persistent or severe outcomes (9). Importantly, earlier onset is often associated with greater symptom severity, increased persistence, and poorer outcomes later in life. This perspective also highlights the importance of environmental exposures and gene-environment interplay. Individuals both shape and are shaped by the environments they experience, with genetic predisposition potentially influencing exposure to or selection of environments; these processes are likely to change across development and may be particularly important at some developmental stages.

Large biobanks have transformed our ability to investigate complex traits; however, cross-sectional studies conducted in adulthood cannot fully capture the timing and sequence of experiences that precede self-harm. This limitation is compounded by the fact that retrospective reports of symptoms and diagnoses are often incomplete and may provide an inaccurate picture of earlier difficulties. Prospective longitudinal studies beginning in childhood, combined with repeated assessments across multiple domains of functioning, will therefore be essential. However, identifying earlier developmental risk factors does not necessarily mean that self-harm can be predicted accurately over long periods. More proximal experiences, such as illness, adversity, or changes in relationships, may also alter individual trajectories and increase the risk of self-harm. Longitudinal studies therefore need to capture both longer-term developmental vulnerabilities and more immediate changes in risk. The central question may be not only why self-harm occurs but also when these developmental pathways emerge, how they change over time, and why they differ between individuals. A related challenge is to move beyond diagnoses and toward understanding the processes that connect genetic predisposition with later behavioral outcomes. Examining these characteristics may help explain why individuals with apparently similar levels of genetic predisposition follow markedly different developmental trajectories and may highlight the role of protective factors and environmental experiences in shaping later outcomes (10).

By integrating rich phenotypic information across development with advances in statistical genetics, we now have an unprecedented opportunity to investigate how genes, environments, and experiences combine across development to influence later outcomes. Liu et al. (3) present an impressive integration of epidemiological and genetically informed methods to investigate the relationships between chronic pain, depression, and self-harm. Their study highlights the value of combining multiple approaches to investigate complex behavioral traits and points to important directions for future research. Self-harm is unlikely to arise from a single pathway, a single diagnosis, or a single point in time. Rather, understanding self-harm will require greater attention to symptom-level variation, transdiagnostic processes, developmental trajectories, and the biological, psychological, and social mechanisms through which genetic influences shape later outcomes. The challenge is to understand which pathways matter, when they emerge, and why they lead to self-harm in some individuals but not others.
