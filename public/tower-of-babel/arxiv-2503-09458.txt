Abstract
. A k k -star decomposition of a graph is a partition of its edges into k k -stars (i.e., k k edges with a common vertex). The paper studies the following problem: for what values of k > d / 2 k>d/2 does the random d d -regular graph have a k k -star decomposition (asymptotically almost surely, provided that the number of edges is divisible by k k )? Delcourt, Greenhill, Isaev, Lidický, and Postle proposed the following conjecture. It is easy to see that a k k -star decomposition necessitates the existence of an independent set of density 1 − d / ( 2 ​ k ) 1-d/(2k) . So let k d ind k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d} be the largest k k for which the random d d -regular graph a.a.s. contains an independent set of this density. Clearly, k k -star decompositions cannot exist for k > k d ind k>k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d} . The conjecture suggests that this is essentially the only restriction: there is a threshold k d ⋆ k^{\star}_{d} such that k k -star decompositions exist if and only if k ≤ k d ⋆ k\leq k^{\star}_{d} , and it (basically) coincides with the other threshold, i.e., k d ⋆ ≈ k d ind k^{\star}_{d}\approx k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d} . We confirm this conjecture for sufficiently large d d by showing that a k k -star decomposition exists if d / 2 < k < k d ind d/2<k<k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d} . In fact, we prove the existence even if k = k d ind k=k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d} for degrees d d with asymptotic density 1 1 .

Proof outline

In
[
6
]
the so-called small subgraph conditioning method was used. (Actually,
[
6
]
is an extension of an earlier paper
[
7
]
, where
\mathcal{G}_{N,4}
was decomposed into
3
-stars.) At the heart of that approach lies a second moment calculation, the completely rigorous treatment of which leads to very technical computations.

In comparison, our approach only relies on first moment calculations. This is due to the fact that our method produces star decompositions from independent sets using
in-degree-regular orientations
along the way. Next we give a brief outline of the proof.

For a fixed
d
and
k>d/2
, the following are equivalent objects to consider for a
d
-regular simple graph
G
:

•

a
k
-star decomposition of
G
;

•

an orientation of
G
whereby each out-degree is
0
or
k
;

•

an independent set
A
of density
\alpha_{d,k}=1-d/(2k)
along with a
(d-k)
-in-regular orientation
of the induced subgraph
G[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
, that is, an orientation of
G[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
for which each in-degree is exactly
d-k
, where
A^{\mathrm{c}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=V(G)\setminus A
.

Note that a graph
H
has an
\ell
-in-regular orientation if and only if its average degree is
2\ell
and every induced subgraph of
H
has average degree at most
2\ell
(see Proposition
2.2
). So we need to find an independent set
A
in
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
for which
H=\mathcal{G}[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
satisfies this condition.

First we try to find an
A
with the property that any vertex outside
A
has a limited number of neighbors in
A
(at most
\hat{d}\approx\tau d
neighbors for some constant
0<\tau<1/2
). We will call such independent sets
\hat{d}
-thin
, see Definition
2.4
. We start from an independent set
A
with density
\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\varepsilon
. Then we keep removing vertices from
A
that are neighbors of outside vertices with too many edges going to
A
. Here we need delicate first moment bounds in
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
to show that the total number of removed vertices is small (with high probability). Specifically, in Section
4
we prove for large
d
that
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has a
\tau d
-thin independent set of density at least
\alpha^{\star}_{d}-(\log d)^{3}/d^{2}
; see Lemma
4.1
(b). Then we can choose
k
in such a way that
\alpha_{d,k}
is below this density.

Once we have a thin independent set of appropriate density, we will use Lemma
2.5
. It is a (deterministic) result about
d
-regular graphs that produces star decompositions from thin independent sets. In order to check the conditions of the lemma in our setting, we need an upper bound on the average degree of induced subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
. For the purposes of Theorem
1.2
a very basic bound (see Proposition
5.1
) will suffice.

2.
Star decompositions from thin independent sets

We fix a degree
d\geq 3
and an integer
k>d/2
. The main result of this section is Lemma
2.5
that provides a sufficient condition for the existence of
k
-star decompositions in (deterministic)
d
-regular graphs.

We will need the notion of
in-regular orientation
.

Definition 2.1
.

An
in-regular orientation
of an undirected graph
H
is an orientation with the property that each in-degree of the resulting directed graph is the same.

Note that if this in-degree is
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, then
H
needs to have average degree precisely
2\ell
.

Whether a graph has an in-regular orientation can be phrased as a flow problem, and
(the integral version of) the max flow min cut theorem
provides the following characterization for the existence. It is an immediate consequence of e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, where orientations with general degree bounds were studied.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that
H
has average degree
2\ell
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
; i.e.,
e(H)=\ell|V(H)|
. Then
H
has an in-regular orientation if and only if every induced subgraph of
H
has average degree at most
2\ell
, that is, for every
U\subseteq V(H)
:

(5)

e[U]\leq\ell|U|.

Note that an equivalent way of writing (
5
) in graphs with
e(H)=\ell|V(H)|
is:

(6)

e\big[V(H)\setminus U\big]+e\big[U,V(H)\setminus U\big]\geq\ell\big|V(H)\setminus U\big|.

Next we state a simple observation that was already pointed out in the introduction.

Observation 2.3
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular graph. For a given integer
k>d/2
, the following objects are clearly equivalent:

•

a
k
-star decomposition of the edge set
E(G)
;

•

an orientation of
G
in such a way that each out-degree is
0
or
k
;

•

an independent set
A
of density
\displaystyle\alpha_{d,k}=1-\frac{d}{2k}
along with a
(d-k)
-in-regular orientation of the remaining graph
G[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
(so that each in-degree is
d-k
).

Note that the condition that the density of
A
is
\alpha_{d,k}
implies that
\frac{d|V(G)|}{2k}
is an integer, which is indeed necessary if we want to decompose
|E(G)|=\frac{d|V(G)|}{2}
edges into
k
-stars.

Clearly, such a
(d-k)
-in-regular orientation can exist only if
A

has the property that every degree of
G[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
is at least
d-k
. In other words, every vertex outside
A
can have at most
k
neighbors in
A
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a positive integer
\hat{d}<d
, we say that an independent set
A\subset V(G)
is
\hat{d}
-thin
if every vertex of
G
has at most
\hat{d}
neighbors in
A
:

\forall v\notin A:\;e[v,A]\leq\hat{d}.

Now we are ready to state our sufficient condition for the existence of a
k
-star decomposition.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices and let
k>d/2
. Let
\alpha_{d,k}
be as in (
1
). Suppose that
G
has an independent set
A
satisfying the following conditions for a positive integer
\hat{d}<k
and a real number
0<c<1
.

(i)

A
is
\hat{d}
-thin with density
\alpha_{d,k}
.

(ii)

For any set
U\subseteq A^{\mathrm{c}}=V(G)\setminus A
with
|U|\leq cN
we have

e[U]\leq(d-k)|U|.

(iii)

For any set
W\subseteq A^{\mathrm{c}}
with
|W|<(1-\alpha_{d,k}-c)N
we have

e[W]\leq(k-\hat{d})|W|.

Then
G
has a
k
-star decomposition.

Proof.

We use Proposition
2.2
with
\ell=d-k
to show that
H\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=G[A^{\mathrm{c}}]
has an
\ell
-in-regular orientation. This would prove the statement, according to Observation
2.3
.
Let
U\sqcup W=A^{\mathrm{c}}
be any partition of
V(H)=A^{\mathrm{c}}
into two sets. We need to check condition (
5
). This is immediate from (ii) for every set
U
with
|U|\leq cN
. So we may assume that
|U|>cN
. Then

|W|=|A^{\mathrm{c}}|-|U|<\big(1-\alpha_{d,k}-c\big)N,

and hence we can use (iii). Since
A
is
\hat{d}
-thin, we have for any
v\in A^{\mathrm{c}}
that

\deg_{H}(v)=d-e[v,A]\geq d-\hat{d}.

Using this and (iii), we get

e[W]+e[W,U]=\left(\sum_{v\in W}\deg_{H}(v)\right)-e[W]\geq(d-\hat{d})|W|-(k-\hat{d})|W|=(d-k)|W|,

meaning that (

) holds for
\ell=d-k
. Recall that (
6
) is just an equivalent formulation of (

), so the condition of Proposition

holds for every

, and the proof is complete.
∎

The reason why this lemma will be useful in the context of random regular graphs is that (for large

Remark 2.6
.

The condition that

Indeed, in this case

3.
Independent sets and counting

In this section we review the known facts and techniques we will need in our proofs in subsequent sections. We start with a brief overview of the various results (exact formulas, upper bounds) regarding the independence ratio of random regular graphs.

3.1.
Bounds on the independence ratio

As mentioned in the introduction, for each degree
d\geq 3
, there exists a constant
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
such that the independence ratio of the random
d
-regular graph
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
converges in probability to
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
as
N\to\infty
. This was proved in
[
3
]
.

The first upper bound on
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
was given by Bollobás
[
4
]
. We denote this bound by
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
and will refer to it as the
first moment bound
. Let

(7)

\varphi_{d}(\alpha)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=h(\alpha)+\frac{d}{2}\,h(1-2\alpha)-(d-1)h(1-\alpha)

and let
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
be the unique root of
\varphi_{d}
on
(0,1/2)
. Then
\alpha^{\star}_{d}\leq\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
for every
d\geq 3
. Asymptotically, as
d\to\infty
, we have
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}=\big(1+o_{d}(1)\big)\frac{2\log d}{d}
as
d\to\infty
. A more precise approximation can be found in
[
8
, formula (4)]
:

(8)

\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}=\frac{2}{d}\left(\log d-\log\log d+1-\log 2+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\log d}{\log d}\right)\right).

A decade after Bollobás’s bound, it was shown by Łuczak and Frieze
[
10
]
that

(9)

\alpha^{\star}_{d}\geq\frac{2}{d}\bigg(\log d-\log\log d+1-\log 2-o_{d}(1)\bigg).

So the first moment bound
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
is asymptotically optimal. However, it is not sharp for any given
d
. The reason behind this is that independent sets form clusters. Such a cluster may contain a large number of independent sets that differ little from each other. This leads to the phenomenon that, although most
d
-regular graphs do not contain independent sets of density
\alpha=\alpha^{\star}_{d}+\varepsilon
, a relatively small number of
d
-regular graphs contain exponentially many, causing the expected number to be above
1
, and hence the first moment bound fails to be sharp. These clusters can be described by the so-called
frozen configurations
. In their breakthrough paper
[
8
]
Ding, Sun, and Sly determined the expected number of such frozen configurations for large
d
and obtained the improved upper bound
\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
. In fact, they proved that

(10)

\alpha^{\star}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}\quad\text{for sufficiently large }d.

See
[
8
, Theorem 1 and formulas (1) and (2)]
for the exact result and the actual definition of
\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
. The improvement compared to the first moment bound is as follows (see
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
):

(11)

\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}-\left(\frac{2}{e}\,\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{2}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\log d}{\log d}\right)\right).

Although not needed in this paper, we say a few words about another approach—rooted in statistical physics—to determining
\alpha^{\star}_{d}
. There is a non-rigorous technique called the
cavity method
that was already used in
[
2
]
to predict the Ding–Sly–Sun result. More precisely, a 1-RSB (1-step replica symmetric breaking) formula
\alpha^{\mathrm{1-RSB}}_{d}
was obtained through the cavity method, and it was conjectured that
\alpha^{\star}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{1-RSB}}_{d}
for every
d\geq 20
. (This is still widely expected to hold true.) In fact, there is a rigorous technique called the
interpolation method
that proves one direction (see
[
13
,
11
]
for details):

\alpha^{\star}_{d}\leq\alpha^{\mathrm{1-RSB}}_{d}\quad\text{for every }d\geq 3.

There is a phase transition below
d=20
, and a so-called full-RSB picture is expected in that range; see
[
11
]
for improved RSB bounds for
d<20
.

As for concrete (implicit) formulas, see
[
11
, Formula (1)]
for the “replica symmetric bound”
\alpha^{\mathrm{RS}}_{d}
and
[
11
, Formula (2)]
for the 1-RSB bound
\alpha^{\mathrm{1-RSB}}_{d}
. These formulas—although they come from a very different approach—can be shown to be the same as
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
and
\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
:

\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{RS}}_{d}\;(\forall d\geq 3)\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{1-RSB}}_{d}\;(\forall d\geq 20).

3.2.
First moment method in random regular graphs

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
stands for a random
d
-regular graph on
N
vertices, that is, a uniform random graph among all
d
-regular simple graphs on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Next we review the connection of
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
to the so-called
configuration model
. Given
N
vertices, each with
d
“half-edges”, the configuration model picks a random matching/pairing of these
Nd
half-edges, resulting in
Nd/2
edges. We denote the corresponding random graph as
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. Note that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
is
d
-regular but it may have loops and multiple edges. A well-known fact is that if
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
is conditioned to be simple, then we get back
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
. Moreover, for any
d
, the probability that
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
is simple converges to a positive
p_{d}
as
N\to\infty
. It follows that if
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has a certain property, then so does
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
.

Suppose that we want to show that
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. does not contain a certain object, that is,
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
contains this object with probability
o_{N}(1)
. This probability is often exponentially small in
N
, and in many cases this can be proved by a standard first moment argument.

Let
Z_{N}
denote the number of certain objects in
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. For instance, the
object
may be an independent set of a given (approximate) density
\alpha
. The expectation
\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
often grows or decays exponentially in
N
. By the exponential rate of growth we mean

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log(\mathbb{E}Z_{N})}{N}.

If this limit exists, it may be regarded as the
entropy of the object
. When negative, we can conclude that
\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
is exponentially small in
N
, and hence so is
\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}>0)\leq\mathbb{E}Z_{N}
. Consequently,
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
, and hence
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
, a.a.s. does not contain such an object.

Actually, for a general class of objects, the limit can be expressed using Shannon entropy. Recall that the Shannon entropy of a discrete distribution
\mu
(over some finite set
S
) is

H(\mu)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{s\in S}h\big(\mu(\{s\})\big),

where
4
4

4

Throughout the paper
\log
means natural logarithm.

h(x)=\begin{cases}-x\log x&\text{if }x\in(0,1];\\
0&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

For our purposes, the following simple setting will be sufficient. Suppose that we have finitely many vertex labels, and our object is a vertex-labeling with constraints describing which pairs of labels (and with what frequency) we may see on an edge. In essence, if
Z_{N}
is the number of vertex-labelings of
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
with a prescribed (approximate) vertex and edge distribution (
\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
, respectively), then

(12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log(\mathbb{E}Z_{N})}{N}=\frac{d}{2}H(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})-(d-1)H(\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}}).

More precisely, suppose that
G
is a (deterministic)
d
-regular graph with a vertex labeling
\ell:V(G)\to L
for some finite set
L
of labels. The label
\ell(v)
of a uniform random vertex
v
of
G
has a discrete distribution
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{vertex}}
on
L
. Similarly, if we take a uniform random directed edge (i.e., an ordered pair
(u,v)
of neighboring vertices), then the (joint) distribution of
\big(\ell(u),\ell(v)\big)
is a discrete distribution
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{edge}}
on
L\times L
. Note that both marginals of
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{edge}}
are equal to
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{vertex}}
. We want to consider labelings for which
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{edge}}
is close to some prescribed distribution
\mu_{\textrm{edge}}
on
L\times L
. By “close” we mean that, say, their total variation distance is less than
\delta_{N}
for some positive sequence
\delta_{N}\gg 1/N
converging to
0
, such as
\delta_{N}=N^{-1/2}
or
\delta_{N}=1/\log N
. (Then the same is automatically true for their marginals
\mu^{G,\ell}_{\textrm{vertex}}
and
\mu_{\textrm{vertex}}
.)

Suppose that
\mu_{\textrm{edge}}
is a disrtibution on
L\times L
described by the probabilities
p_{ij}\;(i,j\in L)
such that
p_{ij}=p_{ji}
. Then the marginal distribution
\mu_{\textrm{vertex}}
has probabilities
p_{i}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{j\in L}p_{ij}
for
i\in L
. We define the random variable
Z_{N}
as the number of labelings
\ell
on
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
such that
\dist_{\textrm{TV}}\big(\mu^{\mathbb{G},\ell}_{\textrm{edge}},\mu_{\textrm{edge}}\big)<\delta_{N}
. Then standard counting arguments show that

(13)

\mathbb{E}Z_{N}=N^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\,\exp\left(N\left(\frac{d}{2}H(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})-(d-1)H(\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}})\right)\right),

and (
12
) follows. For a rigorous proof see
[
1
, Lemma 4.1 and the proof of Theorem 4]
.

For a specific example, let us consider independent sets
A
of density
\alpha
. Such an
A
can be described by a
\{0,1\}
-labeling with vertex distribution

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\;\alpha;

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\;1-\alpha,

and edge distribution

\displaystyle p_{00}

\displaystyle=\;0;

\displaystyle p_{01}=p_{10}

\displaystyle=\;\alpha;

\displaystyle p_{11}

\displaystyle=\;1-2\alpha.

Then the entropy of this object, as in (
12
), is the following:

\displaystyle\varphi_{d}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\bigg(2h(\alpha)+h(1-2\alpha)\bigg)-(d-1)\bigg(h(\alpha)+h(1-\alpha)\bigg)

\displaystyle=h(\alpha)+\frac{d}{2}\,h(1-2\alpha)-(d-1)h(1-\alpha).

It follows that if
\varphi(\alpha)<0
, then the independence ratio of
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
is a.a.s. less than
\alpha
. Note that this is the same function that we introduced in Section
3.1
. Recall that we defined
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
as the unique root of
\varphi_{d}
on
(0,1/2)
so that
\varphi_{d}(\alpha)<0
for any
\alpha>\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
. This proves the first moment bound
\alpha^{\star}_{d}\leq\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
, due to Bollobás
[
4
]
, mentioned in Section
3.1
.

4.
Thin independent sets

In this section we prove the a.a.s. existence of
\hat{d}
-thin independent sets in random
d
-regular graphs, where
\hat{d}=\tau d
for some positive constant
\tau
. To this end, given an independent set
A
, we need to bound the number of vertices with many neighbors in
A
. Our exact result is the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix a constant
0<\tau\leq 1
and set
\hat{d}=\lceil\tau d\rceil
. Furthermore, let

(14)

\beta_{d}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{3}.

Then for sufficiently large
d\geq d_{0}(\tau)
the following hold a.a.s. for
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
.

(a)

Suppose that
A
is an independent set of
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
with density
\alpha
in the range

(15)

\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}-\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{2}<\alpha<\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}.

Then there are at most
\beta_{d}N
vertices
v\notin A
such that

e[v,A]\geq\hat{d}
.

(b)

There exists a
\hat{d}
-thin independent set in
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
with density at least

\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}.

Note that the bounds in the statements do not depend on
\tau
but the threshold
d_{0}(\tau)
does.

The proof considers the expected number of the following objects in
\mathcal{G}_{N.d}
: an independent set
A
of density
\alpha
along with a set
B\subset A^{\mathrm{c}}
of density
\beta
such that
e[B,A]=\tau d|B|
, that is, a vertex in
B
has
\tau d
neighbors in
A
on average. As we will see in Lemma
4.3
, the exponential rate of this expectation is given by the following function.

Definition 4.2
.

For

\alpha\in[0,1/2]
,

\beta\in[0,1-2\alpha]
and

\tau\in[0,1]
let

\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{d}{2}\bigg(2h(\beta)+2\beta\big(h(\tau)+h(1-\tau)\big)+2h(\alpha-\tau\beta)+2h(1-2\alpha-(1-\tau)\beta)-h(1-2\alpha)\bigg)\\
-(d-1)\bigg(h(\alpha)+h(\beta)+h(1-\alpha-\beta)\bigg).

Note that for
\beta=0
we get back
\varphi_{d}
:

\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,0,\tau)=\varphi_{d}(\alpha)\quad\text{for every }\tau.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau)<0
. Then
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. does not contain a pair
A,B
, where
A
is an independent set of density
\alpha+o(1)
,
B\subset A^{c}
has density
\beta+o(1)
, and
e[B,A]=\tau d|B|+o(N)
.

Proof.

We use the method outlined in Section
3.2
. Labeling the vertices in
A
,
B
,
(A\cup B)^{\mathrm{c}}
with
0,1,2
, respectively, we get the following vertex distribution
\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}}
:

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\;\alpha;

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\;\beta;

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\;1-\alpha-\beta.

Its Shannon entropy is

H(\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}})=h(\alpha)+h(\beta)+h(1-\alpha-\beta).

The edge distribution is not completely determined by our constraints. The probabilities we know for sure are the following:

\displaystyle p_{00}

\displaystyle=\;0;

\displaystyle p_{01}=p_{10}

\displaystyle=\;\tau\beta;

\displaystyle p_{02}=p_{20}

\displaystyle=\;\alpha-\tau\beta.

The rest of the distribution has one degree of freedom:
p_{11}
,
p_{22}
,
p_{12}=p_{21}
can be chosen in such a way that

p_{11}+p_{12}=p_{1}-p_{10}=(1-\tau)\beta\quad\text{and}\quad p_{11}+p_{12}+p_{21}+p_{22}=1-2\alpha.

A well-known fact is that the “independent coupling” gives the largest entropy in such situations. More precisely, if

p_{11}+p_{12}=a_{1}\;\text{ and }\;p_{21}+p_{22}=a_{2}\;\text{ with }\;p_{12}=p_{21},

then

h(p_{11})+h(p_{12})+h(p_{21})+h(p_{22})\leq 2h(a_{1})+2h(a_{2})-h(a_{1}+a_{2}),

with equality for
p_{ij}=a_{i}a_{j}/A
, where
i,j\in\{1,2\}
and
A=a_{1}+a_{2}
. (Indeed, since
\log
is a concave function, we have

h(A)+\sum_{i,j}h(p_{ij})-2\sum_{i}h(a_{i})=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\log\left(\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{p_{ij}A}\right)\geq A\log\left(\sum_{i,j}\frac{a_{i}a_{j}}{A^{2}}\right)=0,

where we used that
\sum p_{ij}=A
and
\sum a_{i}a_{j}=A^{2}
.)

In our setting we have
a_{1}=(1-\tau)\beta
and
A=a_{1}+a_{2}=1-2\alpha
. Therefore,

\displaystyle H(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq 2}h(p_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq 2h(\tau\beta)+2h(\alpha-\tau\beta)+2h((1-\tau)\beta)+2h(1-2\alpha-(1-\tau)\beta)-h(1-2\alpha).

Since

h(\tau\beta)+h((1-\tau)\beta)=h(\beta)+\beta\big(h(\tau)+h(1-\tau)\big),

we can write

H(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})\leq 2h(\beta)+2\beta\big(h(\tau)+h(1-\tau)\big)+2h(\alpha-\tau\beta)+2h(1-2\alpha-(1-\tau)\beta)-h(1-2\alpha).

We conclude that

\frac{d}{2}H(\mu_{\mathrm{edge}})-(d-1)H(\mu_{\mathrm{vertex}})\leq\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau).

Recall that
\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
has one degree of freedom. Suppose that
\eta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau)<0
. According to Section
3.2
(and (
13
) in particular) it follows that the expected number of labelings is at most
N^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\exp(N\eta)
for any possible
\mu_{\mathrm{edge}}
. Since the number of terms is
\mathcal{O}(N)
, their sum is still exponentially small, and the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

The main step is showing that
\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta_{d},\tau)<0
. Heuristically, one expects
\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta_{d},\tau)
to be somewhat smaller than
\varphi_{d}(\alpha)
, which, in turn, is only slightly above
\varphi_{d}(\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d})=0
provided that
\alpha
is close to the first moment bound
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
. The precise estimates are as follows.

First note that
h^{\prime}(x)=-\log(x)-1\geq-1
is a monotone decreasing function on
(0,1)
. It follows that

(b-a)h^{\prime}(a)\geq h(b)-h(a)\geq(b-a)h^{\prime}(b)\geq-(b-a)\quad\text{for any }0<a\leq b\leq 1.

This observation yields the following inequalities:

\displaystyle h(1-\alpha-\beta)

\displaystyle\geq h(1-\alpha)+\beta\,h^{\prime}(1-\alpha-\beta);

\displaystyle h(1-2\alpha-(1-\tau)\beta)

\displaystyle\leq h(1-2\alpha)+(1-\tau)\beta;

\displaystyle h(\alpha-\tau\beta)

\displaystyle\leq h(\alpha)-\tau\beta h^{\prime}(\alpha)=h(\alpha)+\tau\beta(\log(\alpha)+1).

Then, if
0<\tau\leq 1
is a constant (not depending on
d
), we get

(16)

\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau)\leq\varphi_{d}(\alpha)+h(\beta)+\tau\beta d\log(\alpha)+\mathcal{O}(\beta d).

Next we bound
\varphi_{d}
near
\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}
. Recall that
\varphi_{d}(\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d})=0
by definition. Differentiating (
7
) gives

-\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(\alpha)=\log(\alpha)-d\log(1-2\alpha)+(d-1)\log(1-\alpha)\leq-d\log(1-2\alpha)\leq 2d\alpha.

Since
\alpha<\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}<(2\log d)/d
, it follows that
-\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(\alpha)<4\log d
. (In fact,
-\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(\alpha)\sim\log d
.) We conclude that for any
\alpha
in the range (
15
) we have

(17)

\varphi_{d}(\alpha)=\varphi_{d}(\alpha)-\underbrace{\varphi_{d}(\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d})}_{=0}=-\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}<(\alpha^{\mathrm{FM}}_{d}-\alpha)4\log d<\frac{4(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}.

Furthermore,
\alpha<(2\log d)/d
gives

(18)

\log(\alpha)<\log(2)+\log\log d-\log d<-\frac{\log d}{2}.

Setting
\beta=\beta_{d}
as in (
14
) and using (
16
), (
17
), (
18
)
we get

\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta_{d},\tau)\leq\frac{4(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}+3\frac{(\log d)^{4}}{d^{3}}-\frac{\tau}{2}\,\frac{(\log d)^{4}}{d^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}\right)<0

for sufficiently large
d
.

Then, by Lemma
4.3
, we conclude that for any independent set
A
with density
\alpha
in the range (
15
), there must be at most
\beta_{d}N
vertices with at least
\hat{d}=\lceil\tau d\rceil
neighbors in
A
, and the proof of part (a) is complete.

For (b), we first note that, due to (
11
),
\alpha=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
falls into the range (
15
) for large enough
d
. Then the same is true for
\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}-\varepsilon
if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough.

According to the Ding–Sly–Sun result (
10
),
we have
\alpha^{\star}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
for sufficiently large
d
. So
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has an independent set
A
of density
\alpha<\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}=\alpha^{\star}_{d}
. Take such an
A
, and for any vertex
v\notin A
with
e[v,A]\geq\hat{d}+1
, remove
e[v,A]-\hat{d}
neighbors of
v
from
A
. For each
v
, we remove at most
d-\hat{d}\leq d-\tau d=(1-\tau)d
vertices. By part (a), the number of
v
’s is at most
\beta_{d}N
. In total, we removed at most

(1-\tau)d\beta_{d}N\leq d\beta_{d}N=\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}N

vertices. We claim that the remaining independent set
A^{\prime}
is
\hat{d}
-thin. On the one hand,
e[u,A^{\prime}]=0
for any removed vertex
u\in A\setminus A^{\prime}
. On the other hand, for
v\notin A
, either
e[v,A^{\prime}]\leq e[v,A]\leq\hat{d}
in the first place, or if
e[v,A]>\hat{d}
, then after removals we have
e[v,A^{\prime}]\leq e[v,A]-\big(e[v,A]-\hat{d}\big)=\hat{d}
. Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get a
\hat{d}
-thin independent set
A^{\prime}
with the claimed density.
∎

Although not needed for our main result Theorem
1.2
, we include here a lemma that we will use in Section

, where specific degrees are considered.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
d
and
\alpha

\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. contains an independent set of density
\alpha

\text{for }\tau_{+}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\hat{d}+1}{d}\text{ and }\beta_{\mathrm{max}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\inf\big\{\beta>0\,:\,\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau_{+})<0\big\}

(d-\hat{d})\beta_{\mathrm{max}}<\alpha-\alpha_{d,k}

(\tau d-\hat{d})\beta<\alpha-\alpha_{d,k}\quad\text{for any $\beta\leq\beta_{\mathrm{max}}$ and $\tau\geq\tau_{+}$ with }\hat{\varphi}_{d}(\alpha,\beta,\tau)\geq 0.

Let
A

neighbors of every

5.
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we present the proof of our main result (Theorem
1.2
). It is based on Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
4.1
. Additionally, we will need an upper bound on the average degree of an induced subgraph (with a given size) in random regular graphs. Later we will consider this problem in more detail; see Lemma
6.1
for sharper bounds. For the purposes of Theorem
1.2
, the following (rather weak) bound will suffice.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists
\varrho<1
such that, for sufficiently large
d
, it holds a.a.s. for
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
that

e[U]\leq\frac{\varrho d|U|}{2}\quad\text{for every }U\subset V(\mathcal{G})\text{ of size at most $N/2$,}

that is, the average degree of
\mathcal{G}[U]
is at most
\varrho d
.

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of a result of Bollobás: in
[
5
]
a lower bound
i^{\star}_{d}
was given on the isoperimetric number (i.e., the Cheeger constant) of random
d
-regular graphs. Specifically, it holds a.a.s. for
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
that for any
U
with
|U|\leq N/2
we have
e[U,U^{\mathrm{c}}]\geq i^{\star}_{d}|U|
, where
i^{\star}_{d}=d/2-\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{d}\big)
. Then

e[U]=\frac{1}{2}\big(d|U|-e[U,U^{\mathrm{c}}]\big)\leq\frac{d-i^{\star}_{d}}{2}|U|\leq\frac{d-d/2+\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{d}\big)}{2}|U|,

and the claim follows for any
1/2<\varrho<1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\hat{d}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lceil\tau d\rceil<\tau d+1
for a small enough
\tau>0
to be specified later. According to Lemma
4.1

\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. contains a
\hat{d}

\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}.

We claim that a
k
-star decomposition a.a.s. exists for any

with
\alpha_{d,k}\leq\alpha
. Fix such a

. Then
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
contains a
\hat{d}

provided that
2k

Nd
. Therefore, condition (i) of Lemma
2.5
is satisfied. We claim that conditions (ii) and (iii) are satisfied as well.

Choose
\varrho<1
as in Proposition
5.1
. Note that
k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}\sim d/2+\log d
so we certainly have
d/2<k\leq k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}<d/2+2\log d
, hence

d-k>\frac{d}{2}-2\log d\quad\text{and}\quad k-\hat{d}>\frac{d}{2}-\tau d-1.

Both are larger than
\varrho d/2
if we choose

in Lemma

. Therefore, the

, we can use Remark

to conclude that all but at most

\alpha_{d,k}<\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{2}}.

Recall that

\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d})=\kappa^{\star}_{d}=\lfloor\kappa^{\star}_{d}\rfloor+\{\kappa^{\star}_{d}\}=k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}+\{\kappa^{\star}_{d}\},

where

; see (
2
). Then

\kappa_{d}(\alpha)-\kappa_{d}(\alpha-\varepsilon)=\frac{d}{2}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\alpha)(1-\alpha+\varepsilon)}<\frac{d\varepsilon}{2(1-\alpha)^{2}}.

Setting
\alpha=\alpha^{\star}_{d}
and
\varepsilon=(\log d)^{3}/d^{2}
, for sufficiently large
d
we get

\kappa^{\star}_{d}-\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\varepsilon)<\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{2d\big(1-(2\log d)/d\big)^{2}}=\frac{(\log d)^{3}d}{2\big(d-2\log d\big)^{2}}<\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d}<1.

In conclusion,
\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\varepsilon)>k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}-1
and it is even greater than
k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}
in case
\{\kappa^{\star}_{d}\}>(\log d)^{3}/d
, which is precisely condition (
4
) of the theorem. Since

\alpha_{d,k}<\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\varepsilon\;\Longleftrightarrow\;k<\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d}-\varepsilon),

it follows that
k
-star decompositions exist for all
k\leq k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}-1
, and even for
k=k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}
under condition (
4
).

Finally, we show that the degrees
d
for which condition (
4
) fails have asymptotic density
0
. Let
\gamma_{d}
be such that

\alpha^{\star}_{d}=\frac{2}{d}\big(\log d-\log\log d+1-\log 2+\gamma_{d}\big)=\frac{2}{d}\left(\log\left(\frac{d}{2\log d}\right)+1+\gamma_{d}\right).

We know from (
8
) and (
9
) or (
11
) that
\gamma_{d}\to 0
as
d\to\infty
. Then

\kappa^{\star}_{d}=\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d})=\frac{d}{2}\big(1+\alpha^{\star}_{d}+\mathcal{O}((\alpha^{\star}_{d})^{2})\big)=\frac{d}{2}+\log\left(\frac{d}{2\log d}\right)+1+\gamma_{d}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\log d)^{2}}{d}\right).

Suppose that (
4
) fails for some
d
, that is,
\{\kappa^{\star}_{d}\}\leq(\log d)^{3}/d
. Setting
m=2\lfloor\kappa^{\star}_{d}\rfloor-d-2
we get

\left|\frac{m}{2}-\log\left(\frac{d}{2\log d}\right)\right|<\varepsilon_{m},

where
\varepsilon_{m}\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. So every degree
d
for which condition (
4
) fails must satisfy

\frac{d}{2\log d}\in\left(e^{m/2-\varepsilon_{m}},e^{m/2+\varepsilon_{m}}\right)\quad\text{for some }m\in\mathbb{Z}.

It clearly follows that such degrees have asymptotic density
0
. The proof of Theorem
1.2
is now complete.
∎

6.
Star decompositions for specific degrees

What result does our approach yield for specific values of
d
? In the proof of Theorem
1.2
we chose
\hat{d}\approx\tau d
for some small positive constant
\tau
. What if
\hat{d}\approx d/2
so that
\hat{d}
is only slightly smaller than
k
itself? For larger
\hat{d}
, condition (iii) of Lemma
2.5
becomes more difficult to satisfy, and we need to use more delicate estimates than the basic bound of Proposition
5.1
. The payoff is that condition (i) becomes easier to satisfy.

The more delicate estimates that we need to use will be stated precisely in Lemma
6.1
below. It provides a bound on the average degree of induced subgraphs that holds a.a.s. for
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
. For now, in order to keep the discussion as general as possible, we do not use the specific bound. Instead, we assume that we have a (continuous and strictly monotone increasing) function
g_{d}\colon(0,1)\to(2/d,1)
with the following property:

(20)

\text{for every $x_{0}\in(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon>0$ it holds a.a.s.~for $\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{N,d}$ that}\\
\text{for any $U\subset V(\mathcal{G})$ of density at most $x_{0}$,}\\
\text{the average degree of $\mathcal{G}[U]$ is less than $g_{d}(x_{0})\cdot d+\varepsilon$.}

(Such a function
g_{d}
will be given in Lemma
6.1
soon.)

Now we consider the following question: given a triple
d,k,\alpha
,
can our method guarantee a
k
-star decomposition
in random
d
-regular graphs
provided that an independent set of density
\alpha
is guaranteed to exist?
We can decide this by the following procedure:

•

set
\displaystyle t_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{2(d-k)}{d}=1-\frac{2k-d}{d}
and

x_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=g^{-1}_{d}(t_{1})
;

•

set
x_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1-\alpha_{d,k}-x_{1}
and

t_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=g_{d}(x_{2})
;

•

set
\hat{d}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lfloor k-t_{2}d/2\rfloor
so that
k-\hat{d}\geq t_{2}d/2
;

•

check condition (
19
) of Lemma
4.4
.

If the condition at the last step is verified, then our method guarantees the existence of a
k
-star decomposition. This follows immediately by combining Lemma
4.1
, Lemma
2.5
and our assumption (
20
).

To get an idea of the strength of our approach for specific degrees, we used a computer to run the above procedure for
\alpha=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
and
k=\lfloor\kappa_{d}(\alpha)\rfloor
. If
\alpha^{\star}_{d}=\alpha^{\mathrm{FrC}}_{d}
indeed holds for
d\geq 20
as conjectured, then the above
k
is actually equal to
k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}
. Under this assumption the computer check confirmed the existence of a
k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}
-star decomposition for every
30\leq d\leq 3000
except for the following degrees:

31,46,48,87,89,164,166,301,303,305,550,552,554,995,997,999,1001,1788,1790,1792.

For these exceptional degrees
\kappa^{\star}_{d}=\kappa_{d}(\alpha^{\star}_{d})
is too close to its integral part
k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}
and our approach only guarantees a
k
-star decomposition for
k=k^{\mathrm{ind}}_{d}-1
.

The average degree of induced subgraphs

Now we give the specific
g_{d}
that we used. This result is essentially a reformulation of
[
12
, Theorem 3]
, where it is stated as a lower bound on the generalized
isoperimetric number/Cheeger constant
. Although the result in
[
12
]
concerns the case
x_{0}\in(0,1/2]
, it holds true for every
x_{0}\in(0,1)
due to a symmetry around
1/2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
0\leq x\leq t\leq 1
, let

F_{d}(x,t)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=h(tx)+2h((1-t)x)+h(1-(2-t)x)-\left(2-\frac{2}{d}\right)\big(h(x)+h(1-x)\big).

For any fixed
x\in(0,1)
the function
t\mapsto F_{d}(x,t)
is continuous and strictly monotone decreasing on
[x,1]
with a unique root that we denote by
g_{d}(t)
. Furthermore,
g_{d}
continuously and strictly monotone increasingly maps
(0,1)
onto
\left(\frac{2}{d},1\right)
.

Suppose that

x_{0}\in(0,1)\quad\text{and}\quad t_{0}\in\left(\frac{2}{d},1\right)\quad\text{with}\quad g_{d}(x_{0})<t_{0}.

Then it holds a.a.s. for
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
that the average degree of the induced subgraph
\mathcal{G}[U]
is less than
t_{0}d
for every
U\subset V(\mathcal{G})
with density
|U|\big/N\leq x_{0}
.

Equivalently, we have the following lower bound on the “generalized Cheeger constant”:

(21)

\min\left\{\frac{e[U,U^{\mathrm{c}}]}{|U|}\,:\,U\subset V(\mathcal{G});\,0<|U|\leq x_{0}N\right\}>(1-t_{0})d.

For a complete proof we refer the reader to
[
12
]
. Here we only include a brief outline. This is essentially a first moment bound, where one needs to count the subsets
U\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
for which
|U|\approx xN
and the average degree of
G[U]
is approximately
td
, that is,
e[U]\approx td|U|/2
. In the language of Section
3.2
, this corresponds to a
\{0,1\}
-labeling of the vertices with the following vertex and edge distributions
\mu_{\textrm{vertex}}
and
\mu_{\textrm{edge}}
:

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\;x;

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\;1-x;

and

\displaystyle p_{00}

\displaystyle=\;tx;

\displaystyle p_{01}=p_{10}

\displaystyle=\;(1-t)x;

\displaystyle p_{11}

\displaystyle=\;1-(2-t)x.

We get

\displaystyle H(\mu_{\textrm{vertex}})

\displaystyle=h(x)+h(1-x);

\displaystyle H(\mu_{\textrm{edge}})

\displaystyle=h(tx)+2h((1-t)x)+h(1-(2-t)x).

Now we see where the function
F_{d}
comes from:

\frac{d}{2}F_{d}(x,t)=\frac{d}{2}H(\mu_{\textrm{edge}})-(d-1)H(\mu_{\textrm{vertex}}).

According to (
12
),
\frac{d}{2}F_{d}(x,t)
is equal to the exponential rate of growth of the expected number of subsets
U
(with the given properties) in
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
. In particular, if
g_{d}(x)<t
, then
F_{d}(x,t)<0
, so
\mathbb{G}_{N,d}
a.a.s. has no such subset, and hence the same is true for
\mathcal{G}_{N,d}
.

To turn this argument into a rigorous proof, one basically needs two things. First, since this argument only works for subsets of linear size (when
x>0
), a little extra attention is needed to deal with subsets
U
of sublinear size as well. Second, it is crucial that
g_{d}
is increasing. (Otherwise we would need to replace
g_{d}(x_{0})
with
\max_{x\in(0,x_{0}]}g_{d}(x)
in the bound.) One can prove this by showing that for fixed
t>2/d
the function
x\mapsto F_{d}(x,t)
has a unique root on
(0,t]
.
