Individualized circulating tumor DNA monitoring in two dogs with malignant melanoma
Authors: Michihito TAGAWA, Ryohei YOSHITAKE, Kohei SAEKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) consists of genomic DNA fragments shed by tumor cells into the bloodstream that contain the same mutations present in the primary tumor. In human medicine, ctDNA has been utilized as a liquid biopsy tool and a noninvasive biomarker for the early diagnosis, prognosis, and management of several tumors. This study explored the utility of ctDNA monitoring using digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) during follow-up in two dogs with oral malignant melanoma. Whole-exome sequencing was performed to identify patient-specific tumor mutations, and dPCR probes designed for these mutations were then used to measure ctDNA levels in plasma samples obtained during follow-up. In two patients, ctDNAs were detected at the time of diagnosis, and in one patient, complete remission was attained via radiation; the disappearance of ctDNA was observed, followed by a re-elevation, indicating lymph node metastasis. In another patient in whom radiation therapy was ineffective, persistent ctDNA was noted during follow-up. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify patient-specific somatic mutations and monitor ctDNA using dPCR.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a tumor-derived fragmented DNA in the bloodstream that includes genetic alterations, copy number variations, and DNA methylation in primary and metastatic tumors. ctDNA detection is a promising liquid-biopsy approach for cancer diagnosis [13]. Recent progress in genomic and molecular methods, including next-generation sequencing (NGS) and digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR), has broadened the role of liquid biopsy by allowing detection of tumor somatic mutations and assessment of treatment response, recurrence, and metastasis [15, 23, 27]. ctDNA sampling is a noninvasive biopsy approach that permits repeated collection at multiple time points, thereby offering greater clinical utility than conventional methods such as tissue biopsy [18]. In human medicine, individualized ctDNA analysis contributes to the development of precision medicine and has also been employed to drug selection and treatment discontinuation/restart decisions [20].

However, there are no useful biomarkers for evaluating treatment response and early relapse in canine tumors. Recently, non-specific cell-free DNA (cfDNA) containing nucleosomes in blood samples have been used for tumor detection and disease monitoring; however, limited data are available in veterinary medicine regarding tumor-specific liquid biopsies that reflect abnormal methylation patterns and genetic alterations [30, 32, 36]. The utility of blood-based cancer detection using advanced genetic analytical techniques such as next-generation sequencing has recently been reported, and this approach now allows early cancer detection and precise identification of tumor type with a high level of accuracy [11, 12, 28]. However, ctDNA monitoring using advanced genomic techniques has not yet been established in dogs. Therefore, we conducted NGS analysis of tumor tissue and corresponding peripheral blood DNA to identify candidate tumor-specific mutations and then applied case-specific dPCR to detect ctDNA and assess the feasibility of tumor monitoring in two dogs with oral melanoma.

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (21-124), and patient characteristics are summarized in Supplementary File 1 [4]. Tumor biopsy samples were obtained from the patients, and paired PBMC samples were collected before any tumor-specific treatment. The tumor was histologically diagnosed as a malignant melanoma. Genomic DNA from tumor and PBMC samples was extracted using the QIAGEN QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To determine the ctDNA fluctuations, multiple blood samples were collected at diagnosis, post-treatment, and during follow-up. Approximately 2 mL of peripheral blood in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid was collected from each dog, and plasma was then separated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, transferred to new tubes and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell debris within 2 hr of blood collection. Plasma was visually inspected for hemolysis and stored at −30°C prior to DNA extraction. cfDNA was extracted and purified from 500 µL of plasma samples using MagMAX Cell-Free DNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cfDNA was eluted in a final volume of 15 µL of elution buffer and stored at−30°C until use. cfDNA concentrations at each time point in the two cases are illustrated in Supplementary File 2. Treatment response and relapse criteria at each visit were evaluated according to the guidelines described for solid tumors according to physical examination and additional tests [26].

Genomic DNA from matched tumor and PBMC samples from each dog was subjected to whole-exome sequencing (WES) using KOTAI Biotechnologies (Osaka, Japan). The library was prepared using the Twist Alliance Canine Exome panel (Twist Bioscience, San Francisco, CA, USA), and the 150-nucleotide paired-end sequences were sequenced using the NovaSeq™ 6000 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) for over 50 million read pairs for PBMC or 100 million read pairs for tumor samples. After evaluating the quality using FastQC [2] and removing low-quality reads using fastp (version 0.23.2) [7], the filtered reads were aligned to the ROS_Cfam_1.0 dog reference genome (GCA_014441545.1) using BWA mem (version 0.7.17-r1188) [21]. The resulting aligned reads were preprocessed using SAMtools (version 1.14) and Picard tools (version 2.26.6) (Picard SortSam, MarkDuplicates, AddOrReplaceReadGroups) before variant calling for somatic mutations in tumor samples compared to matched PBMC samples using the gatk (version 4.3.0.0) Mutect2 function [34]. To reduce false positives, variants were compared against a Panel of Normal built from the four matched PBMC samples using gatk tools (Mutect2, GenomicsDBImport, and CreateSomaticPanelOfNormals with default settings) [22, 35]. Additional filtering of the Mutect2 results (gatk FilterMutectCalls [34]) selected approximately 3% of the variants. To predict the biological significance of the identified variants, they were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (version 107) [24], and variants within introns or intergenic regions were not explored in further analyses.

From the ctDNA candidates with a variant allele frequency (VAF) greater than 3%, we selected genes associated with human and canine melanoma and designed case-specific primer probes targeting these tumor-specific mutations for dPCR analysis. Patient-specific primers and probes labeled for wild-type (HEX-conjugated) and mutant (FAM-conjugated) alleles were specifically designed using the Bio-Rad droplet digital PCR technology (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) to investigate ctDNA by dPCR. The dPCR was performed using a Bio-Rad QX200 ddPCR system (Bio-Rad). Prior to ctDNA monitoring, the synthesized primer and probe sets for each mutation were validated using the corresponding primary tumor DNA in a commercial laboratory (Cancer Precision Medicine, Kawasaki, Japan).

We demonstrate the clinical validity of ctDNA monitoring in two dogs with oral melanoma using individualized dPCR primer probes targeting tumor-specific mutations. In human medicine, ctDNA monitoring in patients with cancer has emerged as a promising tool for real-time disease assessment, and the detection of ctDNA in the plasma is associated with recurrence rates and survival times [3]. Furthermore, ctDNA monitoring-guided treatment is being used in humans, and molecular residual disease detection is beginning to be utilized as a criterion for evaluating treatment response and deciding whether to resume or discontinue treatment [1]. In this study, we conducted WES to identify tumor-specific mutations that are not found in normal cells and designed probes. In this study, the candidate genes to be analyzed were selected based on the conditions that they were genes related to tumorigenesis and that they were associated with human malignant melanoma. ARID1A encodes AT-Rich Interactive Domain-containing protein 1A, a well-known key component of the chromatin remodeling switching/sucrose nonfermentable (SWI/SNF) complex [14]. ARID1A mutations have been commonly identified in tumor samples from patients with ovarian, gastric, bladder, and colorectal cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma and melanoma [16]. Non-erythrocytic spectrin beta 1 (SPTBN1) is an important cytoskeletal protein involved in normal cell growth and is related to many types of human cancers, including melanoma [6, 33]. The gene mutations detected have not been previously reported in canine melanoma, and how these mutations affect tumor development remains unclear. ARID1A is a tumor suppressor gene that is frequently mutated in several tumor types [17]. SPTBN1 also be a potential mutation driver gene in human hepatocellular carcinoma [9]. Oncogenic driver genes are frequently detected in patients’ ctDNA [19], and in this study, patient-derived ctDNA demonstrated a certain correlation with treatment progress. In case 1, an increase in ctDNA was observed 2 months before the identification of lymph node metastasis, demonstrating its usefulness as an early marker of disease progression. In case 2, although the tumor was macroscopically stable, an increase in ctDNA was noted, followed by clear tumor progression. While mutation levels in plasma samples reflect the dynamics of subclones estimated from tumor biopsy sequencing, ctDNA levels fluctuate due to changes in subclones caused by disease progression and response to treatment [8, 25]. In this study, although the genes used reflected the clinical dynamics of tumors, the relationship between tumor dynamics and ctDNA in dogs needs to be clarified in more detail by monitoring ctDNA using multiple genes and evaluating the emergence of subclones through repeat biopsies. Few studies have clarified the utility of ctDNA in veterinary medicine; thus, further analyses are warranted to understand its properties and expand its clinical use.

ctDNA is a fragmented DNA, and its abundance is extremely low, accounting for only 0.01% of the total cell-free DNA. Hence, various techniques have been used to detect ctDNAs, including qPCR, BEAMing, Safe-SeqS, CAPP-Seq, and TAmSeq [29]. Digital PCR is a robust method for detecting point mutations in ctDNAs at low allele fractions. Currently, digital PCR is commonly used to monitor ctDNAs, allowing highly sensitive detection [31]. Digital PCR allows absolute quantification of ctDNA with a detection limit of 0.01% compared to real-time PCR, NGS (0.1%), and Sanger sequencing (10%) [5]. In our case, we were able to detect 0.01% VAF, showing that mutation-specific dPCR is an achievable tool for detecting ctDNA, even in the veterinary field.

This study had several limitations. First, this preliminary study only included two cases, and larger studies are required to clarify the usefulness of ctDNA monitoring in veterinary medicine. Furthermore, the monitoring intervals varied and the post-treatment monitoring methods were not standardized. A major issue is the limited amount of blood that can be obtained, rendering it difficult to secure sufficient DNA. Therefore, the analysis was limited to one gene per case. A sufficient amount of DNA is required for ctDNA analysis, and further studies are warranted to determine the appropriate amount of blood to be collected and the methods for DNA extraction.

In this study, we identified patient-specific mutations in two cases of canine melanoma using advanced genomic analysis. Furthermore, individual ctDNA monitoring correlated with the clinical behavior of the patients. This was a preliminary study of only two cases, and the clinical utility of individual ctDNA monitoring needs to be confirmed in larger cohorts with longer follow-up periods.
