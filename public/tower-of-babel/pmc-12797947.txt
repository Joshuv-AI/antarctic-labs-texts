Quercetin nanoemulsion ameliorates tremor and neuroinflammatory dysregulation: behavioral and molecular insights in a mouse model of essential tremor
Authors: Zeynab Pirmoradi, Monavareh Soti, Kristi A Kohlmeier, Mohammad Shabani, Fatemeh Shahsavari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEssential tremor (ET) is a common movement disorder characterized by persistent limb tremors. Currently, no effective treatment for ET exists. Natural plant-derived compounds, like the flavonoid, quercetin may provide therapeutic benefits, particularly when delivered in nanoemulsion formulations that enhance bioavailability and efficacy. This study evaluated the neuroprotective potential of quercetin nanoemulsion (Que-NE) in a harmaline-induced mouse model of ET.MethodsThirty-two male Swiss mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 each): Control, Harmaline (10 mg/kg, i.p., on days 3, 5, and 7), Que-NE (20 mg/kg, i.p., for 7 days), and Harmaline + Que-NE. Harmaline was used to reliably induce tremor via olivocerebellar hyperexcitability. Behavioral performance was assessed using the open field, elevated plus maze, tail suspension, wire grip, rotarod, and passive avoidance tests. Expression of NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NMDA receptor, and Lingo-1 was determined by RT-PCR.ResultsQue-NE significantly reduced harmaline-induced tremor severity (p < 0.0001), decreased immobility time in the tail suspension test (p = 0.0003), and improved open field anxiety-like behaviors compared with harmaline alone (P = 0.0012). Que-NE downregulated pro-inflammatory mediators (P < 0.0001) and reduced Lingo-1 gene expression (P < 0.0001). However, Que-NE showed limited efficacy in severe motor coordination tasks (rotarod, wire grip) and passive avoidance memory.ConclusionsQue-NE exerts measurable anti-inflammatory, anxiolytic, and antidepressant-like effects in the harmaline model of ET. The impact of Que-NE on improving motor deficits, reducing inflammatory markers, and suppressing inhibitors of synaptic plasticity highlights the potential of Que-NE as a disease-modifying strategy. However, dose-response, protein-level, and long-term studies are needed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of Que-NE for ET management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-025-00310-0.Keywords: Quercetin nanoemulsion, Essential tremor, Neuroinflammation, Lingo-1, Mice

Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is one of the most prevalent neurological movement disorders, with a global prevalence ranging from 0.32% in the general population to 5.79% among individuals over 65 years old. Despite its high frequency, ET is often misdiagnosed [1, 2], and remains under-recognized in clinical practice. ET is believed to originate from dysfunction in the cerebello-thalamo-cortical network, involving the cerebellum, thalamus, and inferior olive [3, 4]. Pathophysiological mechanisms include cerebellar neurodegeneration, Purkinje cell loss or remodeling, abnormal oscillatory activity, and altered GABAergic and glutamatergic neurotransmission [5–7]. Clinically, ET is diagnosed when bilateral upper limb action tremor persists for at least three years, with or without head, voice, or lower limb involvement, and when other motor-related neurological conditions such as dystonia, ataxia, or Parkinsonism are absent [8]. While primarily a motor disease, non-motor features can be present in ET, likely due to the role of the cerebello-thalamo cortical network in cognitive function and progressive neurodegenerative processes.

Current pharmacological treatments for ET are limited in efficacy and are often associated with adverse effects, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic strategies. Animal models of ET have been essential for studying ET mechanisms and evaluating potential therapeutics. One of the most robust models of ET is the harmaline model, which recapitulates dysfunction within the olivocerebellar circuitry. Harmaline, a β-carboline alkaloid, is widely used to induce ET-like tremors in rodents by activating the olivocerebellar pathway [9], resulting in rhythmic burst firing in inferior olivary neurons and synchronous activation of cerebellar Purkinje cells and deep cerebellar nuclei [10, 11]. This model closely mimics the clinical and neurophysiological features of human ET and has been useful for preclinical drug screening [9, 12]. Indeed, studies using the harmaline model have contributed to important advances in understanding ET pathophysiology and evaluating several pharmacological agents. However, many of these agents have demonstrated only partial tremor suppression or limited translational success, underscoring the need for more effective and better-tolerated therapies.

Quercetin, a dietary polyphenolic flavonoid abundant in fruits and vegetables, exhibits strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties [13], however, it has not been evaluated in the context of ET. Demonstrated beneficial effects of quercetin include improvement of cognitive, emotional, and motor function in animals [14, 15], and neuroprotective actions have been shown in preclinical studies using models of diseases exhibiting motor and cognitive dysfunctions, including Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases [16–19]. However, despite these findings, no published research has directly evaluated the actions of quercetin on motor and cognitive function in ET, or whether quercetin could ameliorate neurogenerative processes in this disorder. Likely hindering such explorations is the poor solubility, absorption, and bioavailability of quercetin [20, 21]. However, nanotechnology offers strategies to overcome these limitations, with nanoemulsion-based delivery systems—comprising oil, water, and surfactants that form droplets smaller than 100 nm—emerging as a novel approach for enhancing quercetin delivery to the brain as nanoemulsions enhance solubility, stability, gastrointestinal absorption and facilitate transport across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [22] of poorly bioavailable compounds. Therefore, the application of nanoemulsions for quercetin in ET could overcome pharmacokinetic barriers and enable anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective therapeutic actions. However, while alternative nanoformulations such as solid lipid nanoparticles and liposomes have shown promise in neurodegenerative models [19, 23] and nanoemulsions have been shown to not only enhance drug accumulation in the brain but also improve therapeutic efficacy [24, 25], no comparative or systematic study have examined nanoemulsion-based quercetin delivery (Que-NE) specifically in ET or in the harmaline model, leaving a clear gap in the literature.

To address this gap, we hypothesized that enhanced brain delivery of quercetin through use of Que-NE would reduce motor and cognitive dysfunctions associated with ET and alter expression of proteins implicated to play a role in ET pathology. Specifically, we evaluated whether Que-NE reduces harmaline-induced tremors and cognitive impairments when evaluated in behavioral testing. Further, we determined whether alterations in behavioral testing in the harmaline ET model were associated with suppression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-κB known to be linked to neuroinflammation in ET [26–29], alterations in expression of NMDA receptor that could play a role in tremor, and decreases in levels of Lingo-1, a protein that interferes with neuronal differentiation contributing to ET motor dysfunction [30]. Taken together, our data indicate that quercetin’s anti-inflammatory properties when delivered via a nanoemulsion system may represent a targeted therapeutic approach to mitigate ET-associated tremor, cognitive impairments, neuroinflammatory dysregulation and dysfunctions in neuroplasticity.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Swiss mice (25–35 g) were obtained from the Kerman Neuroscience Center and housed under standard conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle, food and water ad libitum). Male mice were selected to avoid the effects of estrous cycle on behavioral tests. To minimize stress, animals were handled daily and acclimated for two weeks before experiments.

Experimental design and drugs

Animals were assigned to one of four experimental groups (n = 8 per group) using a computer-generated random number sequence to ensure unbiased allocation. The groups were designated as follows: Control (Cont), Harmaline (Harm), Quercetin Nanoemulsion (Que-NE), and Harmaline combined with Quercetin Nanoemulsion (Harm + Que-NE). A control group with nanoemulsions without quercetin was not included due to lack of reporting that nanoemulsions induce adverse effects or biological activity in prior studies [31]. Harmaline hydrochloride dihydrate (10 mg/kg, i.p.; cat. no. H1392, Sigma, USA) was administered on the 3rd, 5th, and 7th days to induce essential tremor through olivocerebellar hyperexcitability [11, 27]. In the Harm + Que-NE group, animals received both Harmaline injections on those same days and daily Que-NE treatment (20 mg/kg, i.p.) from day 1 to day 7. On days when both compounds were administered, Que-NE was injected 60 min before Harmaline.

The Que-NE group was treated with daily Que-NE (20 mg/kg, i.p.) [32] for seven days, while the control group received daily saline injections (0.3 mL of 0.9% saline solution). To ensure consistent injection schedules across groups, animals receiving only one drug were given a saline injection 60 min before their main treatment on days 3, 5, and 7. Tremor assessments were conducted on days 3, 5, and 7. The selection of a 60-minute interval between quercetin administration and harmaline challenge was based on available pharmacokinetic evidence for improved oral quercetin formulations. Several preclinical and clinical studies consistently report that optimized delivery systems (nanoemulsions, SNEDDS/SEDDS, phytosome, or LipoMicel® formulations) achieve peak plasma concentrations (Tmax) within approximately 0.5 to 2.7 h, in contrast to unformulated quercetin which typically peaks later (≈ 3–4 h) [33, 34]. Additional behavioral tests took place on days 7 and 8, and molecular analyses were performed on day 8. Testing on Day 7 was necessary to capture acute behavioral responses within the harmaline model’s peak effect window (20–180 min post-injection), following established preclinical tremor research protocols that require dense data collection to observe dynamic changes. Experimental design and study timeline

Preparation of Que-NE

The Que-NE formulation was freshly prepared each day to ensure stability and prevent degradation of nanoemulsion components. Throughout the treatment period, the nanoemulsion showed no visible signs of phase separation, turbidity changes, or precipitation. Quercetin nanoemulsion was prepared using nonionic surfactants (Tween 40 and Aspen 40) and cholesterol in an organic phase containing chloroform. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation and drying, and the lipid layer was hydrated in distilled water at 25 °C for 30 min. Sonication at 20 kHz for 5 min produced a smaller-sized nanoemulsion. Anionic surfactants SDS and Tween 80 were also used in the initial preparation step under microwave irradiation. The particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of nanoemulsions are analyzed using photon correlation spectroscopy (PCS) with a Malvern Zetasizer, which monitors variations in light scattering caused by Brownian motion of particles over time. PCS is based on the principle that smaller particles move faster than larger ones. The laser beam diffracts off the sub-micron particles in the solution. Due to diffusion, rapid fluctuations in laser scattering intensity occur around a mean value at a fixed angle, depending on particle size. The calculated photon time-correlation function generates a histogram of the line width distribution, which relates to particle size. To measure particle size, a weighed amount of formulation is dispersed in double-distilled water to create a homogeneous dispersion, which must be used immediately for particle size and PDI measurement. The particle size and PDI of risperidone were evaluated using this method, and the particle size of a primaquine nanoemulsion was found to be in the range of 50–120 nm [35].

Behavioral Assessments

All behavioral tests were conducted by an experimenter blinded to the group assignments. The blinding was maintained until all data collection and analysis were completed. To minimize potential fatigue from multiple tests conducted on Day 7, rest intervals of 15–30 min were provided, and procedures were organized from least to most invasive. Behavioral assessments were sequenced based on increasing physical and psychological demands in the following fixed order: Open Field, Elevated Plus Maze, Footprint, Wire Grip, Rotarod, Tail Suspension, and Passive Avoidance. After each test, animals were returned to their home cages and given a rest interval (15 min for the less demanding tasks, Open Field, Elevated Plus Maze, Footprint, and 30 min for the more strenuous or cognitively challenging tasks, Wire Grip, Rotarod, Tail Suspension, and Passive Avoidance) to facilitate recovery and reduce carry-over effects.

Tremor scoring

Tremor occurrence and severity were evaluated by a blinded observer 20 min after drug administration. Tremor severity was rated on a scale from 0 to 4: 0 = no tremor, 1 = occasional head and neck tremor, 2 = intermittent full-body tremor, 3 = persistent full-body tremor, and 4 = severe tremor preventing standing or walking [27].

Open field test

Locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were assessed using a 90 × 90 × 45 [H] Plexiglas open field divided into 16 squares (4 central and 12 peripheral). Mice were placed in the center of the field, and activity was recorded for 5 min. Parameters measured included total distance moved (TDM), velocity, grooming frequently, rearing episodes, and time spent in central versus peripheral zones. Data were analyzed offline using Ethovision software (v7.1, Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands). The apparatus was cleaned with alcohol between sessions [36].

Elevated plus maze test

Anxiety-like behavior was also evaluated using an elevated plus maze with two open and two closed arms. Mice were placed in the maze center facing an open arm and observed for 5 min. The time spent in open arms and the numbers of entries into open and closed arms were recorded [37].

Footprint test

Gait and walking patterns were assessed by marking hind paws with non-toxic ink and allowing the animals to walk through a 100 × 10 × 10 cm Plexiglas tunnel onto white absorbent paper. Stride length was determined by measuring distances between consecutive footprints on the same side, while stride width was measured between footprints on opposite sides. First and last steps were excluded [38].

Wire grip test

Muscle strength was tested by suspending mice on a horizontal steel wire (80 cm long, 7 mm thick) between two platforms. Each mouse underwent three sessions with a 5-minute rest between trials. The latency to fall was recorded in each trial [39].

Rotarod performance test

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using an accelerating rotarod (10 to 60 rpm). Mice received pre-training one day prior to testing. Each animal completed three trials (maximum 300 s per trial) with 5-minute rest intervals. The average time on the rod was recorded [39].

Tail suspension test

The tail suspension test was conducted to assess depressive-like behavior. Mice were suspended by their tails in a testing chamber for a duration of six minutes. Immobility time was recorded as an indicator of behavioral despair, with longer periods of immobility reflecting depressive-like states. Typically, animals exhibiting such behavior remain immobile between the 2- and 4-minute intervals. This method is commonly used to evaluate antidepressant efficacy, as reduced immobility time is interpreted as a positive response to treatment [40].

Passive avoidance test

Passive avoidance test

Learning and memory were evaluated using a shuttle-box with illuminated and dark compartments. During training, after the mouse entered the dark chamber, it received a mild foot shock (0.5 mA, 2 s) [41]. The number of shocks needed to avoid re-entry was recorded. Memory was assessed 24 h later by measuring the step-through latency (STL), and the time taken by the mouse to enter the dark compartment (also referred to as the Total Dark Compartment, TDC) [42].

Molecular analyses

Following behavioral testing, animals were euthanized by rapid decapitation, and cerebellar tissue was collected, frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C. RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR analyses were performed by a laboratory technician blinded to the experimental groups to prevent any bias in sample processing and data interpretation. Total RNA was extracted from cerebella of four randomly selected animals per group using Trizol reagent (Zaver Zist Azma, Iran). RNA quality and concentration were determined via nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the Easy cDNA Synthesis Kit (Pars Tous, Iran). Gene expression analyses for TNF-α, NFKB, IL-1β, IL-6, Lingo-1, and NMDA receptor were conducted using SYBR Green-based real-time qPCR (Qiagen, Germany), with GAPDH as the reference gene. Amplification efficiency was verified using standard curves, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data with normal distribution (p > 0.05) were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed by one-way ANOVA; non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Tremor scores across time points and groups were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A post-hoc power analysis, performed using G*Power software (v3.1.9.7) on the primary outcome confirmed that the sample size was adequate, demonstrating a statistical power of 0.95 at a significance level of α = 0.05. F-values and degrees of freedom (df) ranges are presented alongside p-values to aid clear interpretation of the results.

Results

Que-NE attenuates harmaline-induced tremor

Tremor score analysis revealed a significant interaction between treatments and days (F (6, 83) = 9.058, p < 0.0001). Tremor scores significantly increased on day 3 following harmaline administration, as both the Harm and Harm + Que-NE groups exhibited significantly higher tremor scores compared with the Control and Que-NE groups (p < 0.001). By days 5 and 7, the Harm + Que-NE group demonstrated a significant reduction in tremor scores relative to the Harm group (p < 0.05), indicating that treatment with Que-NE effectively alleviated harmaline-induced tremors (Fig. 2).

Effects of Que-NE on locomotor, anxiety-like, and depressive-like behaviors

Behavioral assessments were performed using the Open Field Test (OFT), Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), and Tail Suspension Test (TST). In the OFT, the Harm + Que-NE group showed significantly more rearings than the number seen in the Harm group (F (3, 28) = 25.8, p = 0.0001, Fig. 3A). Grooming behavior was significantly elevated in the Harm group relative to all other groups (F (3, 28) = 7.000, p = 0.0012, Fig. 3B). The lower number of grooming events in the Que-NE group was significantly different than that seen in the Harm group (p < 0.05; Fig. 3B), suggesting an anxiolytic effect of Que-NE treatment. No significant group differences were detected in total distance moved (Fig. 3C). Harmaline exposure decreased the time spent in the central zone while increasing time in the periphery (F (3, 28) = 6.941, p = 0.0012, and F (3, 28) = 6.957, p = 0.0012 Fig. 3D–E), whereas Que-NE treatment normalized these parameters. Mobility time did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. 3F). In the EPM, Harm + Que-NE animals exhibited a significant increase in open-arm entries compared to Harm (F (3, 28) = 18.04, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3G), whereas no group differences were observed in the number of closed-arm entries (Fig. 3H). In the TST, harmaline significantly prolonged immobility time relative to the Control (p < 0.0001) Que-NE groups, while co-treatment with Que-NE significantly reduced immobility compared to Harm, indicative of an antidepressant-like effect (F (3, 28) = 8.866, p = 0.0003, Fig. 3I).

Effects of harmaline and Que-NE on gait, balance, and muscle strength

Harmaline administration did not significantly alter step length in either paw (Fig. 4A–B), but it increased step width compared with Control and Que-NE groups (F (3, 28) = 3.478, p = 0.029, Fig. 4C), indicating gait disturbances. However, Que-NE did not significantly ameliorate this alteration. In the wire grip test, harmaline significantly reduced the grip time when compared to control indicating a reduction in grip strength. However, quercetin treatment did not change the grip time (F (3, 28) = 4.712, p = 0.008, Fig. 4D). Similarly, rotarod performance was impaired (F (3, 28) = 11.43, p < 0.0001, Fig. 4E) in both Harm and Harm + Que-NE groups compared with Control and Que-NE, demonstrating deficits in motor coordination and balance that were not improved by quercetin treatment.

Modulation of passive avoidance learning and memory by harmaline and Que-NE

In the passive avoidance test, the Que-NE group required more shocks to reach learning criterion compared with Control (F (3, 28) = 2.644, p = 0.068, Fig. 5A). Both Harm and Harm + Que-NE animals exhibited significantly shorter step-through latency relative to Control (p < 0.005) and Que-NE groups (F (3, 28) = 15.81, p < 0.0001, Fig. 5B), indicating impaired memory retention. Harmaline also significantly increased time spent in the dark compartment compared to Control and Que-NE (p < 0.0001). Co-treatment with Que-NE notably decreased the time spent in the dark compartment compared to the Harm group (F (3, 28) = 14.15, p < 0.0001, Fig. 5C), indicating a 63.3% decrease in dark-compartment retention time, suggestive of improved memory performance (Harm vs. Harm + Que-NE: 103 ± 14.3 vs. 37.7 ± 16.03).

Modulation of neuroinflammation and neuroplasticity gene expression

Harmaline administration markedly upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes including NF-κB (F (3, 28) = 43.13, p < 0.0001), TNF-α (F (3, 28) = 32.30, p < 0.0001), IL-1β (F (3, 28) = 34.31, p < 0.0001), and IL-6 (F (3, 28) = 59.42, p < 0.0001) compared with Control and Que-NE (Fig. 6A–D). NMDA receptor expression was significantly elevated in both Harm and Harm + Que-NE groups relative to Control and Que-NE (F (3, 28) = 58.17, p < 0.0001, Fig. 6E). However, Harm + Que-NE did not alter the NMDA receptor gene expression compared to Harm. The neuroplasticity-inhibiting gene Lingo-1 was significantly upregulated in the Harm group compared to Control and Que-NE, while Que-NE co-treatment significantly downregulated Lingo-1 compared with Harm (F (3, 28) = 34.21, p < 0.0001, Fig. 6F). These results suggest that Que-NE suppresses harmaline-induced neuroinflammatory signaling pathways as well as reduces a protein that negatively regulates neuronal repair, which may underlie its behavioral protective effects.

Discussion

The present study investigated the neuroprotective potential of Que-NE in a harmaline-induced mouse model of ET. Our central hypothesis was that Que-NE would protect against harmaline-induced tremor, associated neurobehavioral alterations, suppression of neuronal plasticity, and neuroinflammatory changes. The results demonstrated that Que-NE significantly reduced tremor severity, ameliorated anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, and attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression as well as inhibited a plasticity-suppressing pathway. However, Que-NE provided only partial protection against passive avoidance memory deficits and failed to significantly restore locomotor/coordination performance. Thus, while the primary hypothesis regarding neuroprotection against tremor, cognitive dysfunction, neuronal plasticity and neuroinflammation was supported, full recovery of cognition and motor coordination was only partially achieved.

Our findings align with observations that harmaline administration induces motor deficits including tremor, and impaired locomotor and motor coordination, which provides further evidence to confirm the face validity of the harmaline model for study of ET [27, 38, 38, 43]. While we did not conduct histological studies, our data support the interpretation that harmaline administration disrupts the olivocerebellar pathway by increasing neuronal firing in the inferior olive and cerebellar cortex, resulting in transient generalized tremor and motor incoordination [38, 43]. Importantly, treatment with Que-NE reduced tremor severity in agreement with previous evidence that quercetin attenuates muscle tremor in Parkinson’s disease models [44]. While quercetin has shown beneficial effects on motor deficits in other neurodegenerative models, including Huntington’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and ALS [15, 45, 46], Que-NE demonstrated only partial efficacy on improving motor coordination in the ET model. Que-NE did not significantly improve performance in rotarod or wire-grip tests. This dissociation suggests that tremor suppression and motor coordination recovery may involve distinct mechanisms. This limited effect may be related to quercetin’s low oral bioavailability and extensive first-pass metabolism, which are only partially overcome by nanoemulsion formulations [47–49]. In addition, our single-dose, short-term treatment schedule may not have been sufficient to counteract acute harmaline-induced cerebellar dysfunction, as recovery of motor coordination often requires longer interventions [50, 51]. Furthermore, harmaline-induced tremor and neuroinflammation typically peak within 24–72 h and subside within 5–7 days [43], thereby narrowing the therapeutic window and potentially contributing to the partial efficacy observed. Taken together, these findings suggest that motor recovery in ET may require multimodal interventions, prolonged treatment, or optimized dosing strategies.

Previous reports demonstrated that harmaline increases exploratory and anxiety-like behaviors while impairing affective states in ET models [39, 52, 53]. In agreement with these findings, our results showed that Que-NE significantly reduced anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. Que-NE increased open-arm entries in the elevated plus maze and reduced immobility in the tail suspension test. Mechanistically, quercetin’s anxiolytic and antidepressant effects have been attributed to modulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [54, 55], inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines [56, 57], and regulation of NMDA receptor and BDNF signaling [58, 59]. We also showed that harmaline induced cognitive deficits which are consistent with prior reports [38, 52, 53]. In our study, harmaline treatment impaired memory performance in the passive avoidance task, and Que-NE partially prevented memory deficits by reducing time spent in the dark compartment. Previous studies have shown that quercetin enhances cognition in models of neurodegeneration such as Alzheimer’s disease [17, 60], which was suggested to involve NMDA receptor regulation, promotion of BDNF expression, and inhibition of inflammatory mediators [61–63]. In future, additional cognitive paradigms (light–dark box, sucrose preference, forced swim test) should be utilized to confirm effects of Que-NE on ET-associated cognitive dysfunctions. Further, similar to actions seen on motor activity, as effects on cognition were only partial, improvements in nano emulsions to enhance bioavailability, optimization of Que-NE dosing schedules and determination of the best therapeutic windows should be evaluated to identify the most effective parameters to reverse cognitive deficits of the harmaline ET model.

The mechanistic underpinnings of Que-NE’s therapeutic actions on motor and cognitive dysfunctions in ET remain to be determined. However, we saw effects of Que-NE on levels of Lingo-1 and neuroinflammatory markers, which could play a role in neuroprotection in the harmaline ET model. Lingo-1 is involved in ET pathogenesis. While the role played by the overexpression of Lingo-1 in ET is not fully elucidated, Lingo-1 decreases calcium channel activity [64] and activates the Nogo-66 receptor (NgR1), leading to inhibition of myelination. Additionally, Lingo-1 downregulates the EGFR–PI3K–Akt pathway, reducing Purkinje cell survival and enhancing tremor susceptibility [30]. The downregulation of Lingo-1 mRNA expression by Que-NE, could have resulted in enhanced remyelination and suppression of NF-κB/JAK2–STAT3-mediated inflammation. Furthermore, harmaline was shown in our study to activate NF-κB and upregulate the cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 which supports previous work that has suggested these neuroinflammatory modulators disrupt cerebellar function [65, 66]. Que-NE was shown to significantly reduce IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and NF-κB expression. These findings corroborate previous reports of quercetin as an NF-κB inhibitor [67, 68]. While NMDA receptor antagonists ameliorate tremor in ET and we noted an increase in NMDA receptor in the harmaline treated groups, we did not see evidence for an alteration in NMDA receptor gene expression by Que-NE, suggesting that Que-NE would not reverse potential ET-related dysfunctions in signaling mediated by this receptor. Taken together, we show therapeutic efficacy with Que-NE that was associated with alterations in levels of mRNA for proteins associated with inflammation and synaptic plasticity, which could have played a role in motor and cognitive therapeutic actions in the harmaline ET model. Despite our mechanistic insights, the roles played by the altered levels of mRNA in improving motor and cognitive function remain speculative, as we did not assess corresponding protein level expression. Further, because histopathological evaluations were not conducted, the changes observed at the mRNA level for these proteins cannot be interpreted as causative, but rather as potential correlates of the behavioral improvements. Protein-level validation (e.g., Western blot/ELISA for Lingo-1, phospho-Akt, and NF-κB) and functional assays (Purkinje cell histology, cerebellar electrophysiology) are necessary to confirm mechanistic causality. In addition, in future studies, alterations in the other molecular pathways suggested to play a role in quercetin’s anxiolytic, antidepressant and cognitive enhancing effects should be explored.

Current ET treatments such as propranolol and primidone primarily reduce tremor amplitude by targeting peripheral and central oscillatory circuits without addressing inflammatory or Lingo-1–mediated pathways [69]. Unlike propranolol, which primarily modulates peripheral β-adrenergic activity, and primidone, which enhances central GABAergic inhibition [69], Que-NE appears to exert broader neuroprotective and anti-oxidative effects [70] that directly target olivocerebellar dysfunction. These mechanistic features may complement the limitations of current ET therapies and highlight the potential clinical relevance of Que-NE. While speculative, Que-NE’s dual anti-inflammatory and Lingo-1-modulating properties suggest a potential disease-modifying role. Similarly, experimental compounds such as resveratrol that modulate Lingo-1/Sirt1 signaling or reduce excitotoxicity have shown benefits in harmaline models [53]. Thus, Que-NE aligns more closely with mechanism-based therapeutic strategies than with purely symptomatic treatments. Translation to clinical practice, however, will require longer preclinical studies, pharmacokinetic profiling (e.g., plasma/brain LC-MS/MS), protein-level validations, and ultimately, clinical trials.

In summary, Que-NE demonstrated partial efficacy in reducing tremor severity, modulating neuroinflammation, and improving affective disturbances in harmaline-induced ET. These effects could be mediated by the interplay of Lingo-1 suppression, and inhibition of pro-inflammatory pathways including the NF-κB/JAK–STAT pathway, which have all been shown to play a role in ET pathology. Partial benefits were noted on cognition with limited effects on some aspects of motor coordination.

Limitations and future directions

The key limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, to avoid confounds of estrus cycle on tremor, only male subjects were used, which precludes the assessment of potential sex-dependent differences in response to Que-NE treatment. The relatively small sample size and short treatment duration may also limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, while the selected dose of 20 mg/kg was based on prior reports demonstrating therapeutic or neuroprotective effects of quercetin and quercetin-based nanoformulations in a rodent model, and our data add to the literature by showing effects with a 7-day treatment protocol, dose–response experiments are warranted to identify the optimal therapeutic window and to better characterize the efficacy range of Que-NE intervention. Furthermore, while animal health was monitored diligently, and we noted no overt signs of toxicity, we did not directly assess peripheral actions, and future studies should be conducted to evaluate liver enzymes, and metabolic markers as well as evaluate histopathological effects on peripheral organs. Moreover, the absence of protein-level assays, and histological analyses constrains the interpretation of the mechanistic aspects of our results. In particular, without corresponding protein or histopathological data, the observed changes in mRNA expression cannot be considered causative but rather indicative of potential molecular correlates underlying the behavioral outcomes associated with Que-NE treatment in the harmaline-ET model.
