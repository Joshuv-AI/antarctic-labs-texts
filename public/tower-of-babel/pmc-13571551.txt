Bundibugyo virus disease evidence map: a rapid research needs appraisal
Authors: Louise Sigfrid, Dijana Spasenoska, Sinead O’Dwyer, Marieke de Swart, Eli Harriss, Thomas Mendy, Gemma Villanueva, Nicholas Henschke, Alice Norton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) outbreak in DRC and Uganda underscores that filoviruses remain an unpredictable global health threat, particularly in resource-limited settings. The sporadic occurrence of outbreaks has constrained evidence-generation. We implemented a Rapid Research Needs Appraisal (RRNA) to rapidly and systematically synthesise published research evidence and identify gaps in the evidence base to support management and research strategies.MethodsWe searched Ovid Medline, Embase and Scopus for primary research studies published between Jan 1, 1999, and Feb 6, 2026 by combining keywords and thesaurus headings for Bundibugyo and humans. This search was supplemented by an updated search in PubMed and MedRxiv up to June 25, 2026. Searches were conducted in English, but without language restrictions. Eligible studies including humans or human samples focused on at least one of the RRNA domains (clinical characteristics, immune response, transmission, risk factors, medical countermeasures and associated social and behavioural factors). Two reviewers screened records for inclusion, one reviewer extracted data with a second reviewer checking a proportion of the data. Data were analysed descriptively.Findings29 primary studies were eligible for inclusion, accounting for 1593 probable or confirmed BVD cases. Of these 29 studies, most were observational (n = 28, 96.6%) and were most commonly conducted in Uganda (n = 15, 51.7%) and the DRC (n = 10, 34.5%). Only one interventional study was included, a non-randomized interventional study exploring EBOV, SUDV and BDBV cross-reactivity following EBOV vaccination. Among studies that reported age, most (58.6%, 17/29) included adults (18+ years), eight (27.6%) included children (0–18 years), and only two (6.9%) included pregnant women. Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, fatigue, dysphagia, anorexia, dyspnoea and abdominal pain were the most commonly reported symptoms. Haemorrhagic manifestations ranged from 10.4 to 54.0%, with a meta-analysis estimating a CFR of 32.8% (CI 25.8–40.2) based on past outbreaks. Evidence on cross-reactivity to previous Ebola exposure following natural infection of vaccination was sparse and conflicting. One study identified low levels of cross-reactivity in EBOV vaccinated participants, another among EBOV survivors, whereas a third did not detect cross-reactivity following EBOV vaccination. Long-term sequelae, persistent BVD survivor stigma and limited community awareness were also reported.InterpretationWhilst acknowledging the limitations of such a rapid appraisal, our findings show that urgent, coordinated investment is needed in countermeasure trials inclusive of pregnant women and children, supported by observational studies community engagement and co-production.FundingThe National Institute for Health Research (NIHR) and the Wellcome Trust.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Bundibugyo virus (BDBV), a member of the Filoviridae family, is one of four Ebola viruses detected to infect humans and is associated with high morbidity and mortality. An outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Uganda was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the WHO in May 2026. Two previous outbreaks have been reported in Uganda (2007) and the Democratic Republic of Congo (2012), with estimated case fatality ratios ranging from 25 to 54%. There are no licenced BDBV therapeutics or vaccines.

Added value of this study

We activated our Rapid Research Needs Appraisal (RRNA) protocol to systematically synthesis existing published evidence on BDBV to support outbreak preparedness, prevention and response strategies. We searched Ovid Medline, Embase, Scopus and MedRxiv up to 25 June 2026 for studies including human or human samples focused on one of nine key research domains. We identified 29 studies, most observational (96.6%) and conducted in Uganda and the DRC. The most commonly presenting symptoms were fever, fatigue, headache, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Studies reported risk of acute and long-term sequelae and survivor stigma. Evidence regarding cross-reactivity was sparse and conflicted. One study reported detectable cross-reactive response against BDBV in participants vaccinated with EBOV vaccines, whereas another study detected cross-reactivity to SUDV but not BDBV. There were no BDBV vaccine effectiveness or therapeutic trials identified.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings highlight the need for coordinated investment in standardised observational and adaptive trials to increase capacity to generate evidence that informs detection, prevention, clinical management and outbreak control. Considering the sporadic nature of BDBV outbreaks research protocols should be pre-planned, pre-positioned and supported by community engagement, co-design and co-delivery to enable effective implementation as outbreak emerges. Our findings provide a comprehensive overview of existing BDBV evidence base to support management and research prioritisation strategies to generate evidence to protect communities and improve outcomes.

Introduction

Bundibugyo virus (Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense, BDBV) is one of the four Ebola viruses that has been detected in humans alongside Orthoebolavirus zairense (Ebola virus, EBOV), Orthoebolavirus sudanense (Sudan virus, SUDV), and Orthoebolavirus taiense (Taï Forest virus, TAFV). They are part of the Filoviridae family, and are classified as class 4 pathogens requiring BSL4 laboratories, and high priority pathogens for research and development by the World Health Organisation (WHO).1 BDBV, EBOV and SUDV have caused outbreaks of viral haemorrhagic fever (VHF) in humans, resulting in high morbidity and mortality. EBOV was first detected in 1976, causing sporadic outbreaks, with the largest one in 2014–2016 in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, resulting in more than 28,000 cases and 11,000 deaths.2 EBOV is estimated to be the most lethal, with a case fatality ratio (CFR) of up to 67% reported in countries in Africa, followed by SUDV and BDBV, whereas there has only been one documented case of Taï Forest disease which was non-fatal.3

On 15 May 2026, the Ministry of Public Health, Hygiene and Social Welfare officially declared the second Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The first suspected case, a health worker, reported the onset of symptoms including fever, haemorrhaging, vomiting and malaise on 24 April 2026. As of 2 July 2026, 1481 confirmed cases (DRC n = 1460, Uganda n = 20, France [n = 1, linked to DRC]) and 454 deaths (DRC n = 452, Uganda n = 2) have been reported.4 Transmission is thought to be driven by population movement, insecurity and gaps in surveillance.5

Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) was first detected in western Uganda in August 2007, followed by a second outbreak detected in the DRC in 2012. A meta-analysis by the WHO reported 149 cases and 37 deaths (CFR: 24.8%) in the first outbreak, and 57 cases and 34 deaths (CFR: 54.8%) in the second outbreak in the DRC.6,7 The onset of BVD is associated with nonspecific signs and symptoms, and survivors may suffer from long-term sequelae, with headaches and vision disturbances commonly reported.8 Filovirus infections during pregnancy for other Ebola viruses have been associated with maternal haemorrhage, preterm labour, miscarriage, and maternal and neonatal death. A systematic review about Ebola virus diseases in pregnancy estimated the associated absolute risk of maternal death at 67.8%.9 Bundibugyo virus can spread from person-to-person through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a person who is sick with or has died from the disease or from contact with objects or surfaces that have been contaminated with fluids from an infected person.7 Bats are considered a likely reservoir and are implicated in zoonotic human transmission, although evidence remains limited.4 The incubation period is estimated to range from two to 21 days.7

Unlike for the Ebola-Zaire strain, there are currently no approved Bundibugyo virus-specific therapeutics or vaccines, but there are some candidates in preclinical testing, and a CORE protocol for clinical trials, which was activated in the DRC in June 2026.10 There are limited data about whether existing FDA medical countermeasures approved for EBOV will provide cross-reactivity. One study noted that BDBV differs from previously characterized Ebola virus species by 32–42% in the genome sequence, which indicates that EBOV vaccines may not provide complete protection against this divergent species.11 Despite a high mortality risk, knowledge of BDBV is limited due to its sporadic emergence and detection, compounded by competing priorities and the challenges of implementing research in endemic regions. This poses a challenge to affected communities and frontline workers in the affected regions. To support management and research prioritisation strategies, we carried out a rapid research needs appraisal (RRNA) as part of our Pandemic PACT program12 to identify existing evidence across key research domain topics relevant to detection, protection, management and outbreak response.

Methods

Study design and ethics

We implemented a RRNA13 focused on Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) to identify studies including humans or human samples focused on studying at least one of our research domain topics: clinical characteristics, immune response, seroprevalence, transmission (human-to-human), risk factors for disease, severe disease/death/long-term sequalae, diagnostics, vaccines and therapeutic prophylaxis, therapeutics, supportive care, and associated social and behavioural factors. The RRNA utilises modified rapid evidence review methodologies, a relay of reviewers, a systematic review software and a data prioritisation step to facilitate timely completion (Fig. 1). It has been developed in collaboration with Cochrane South Africa, Cochrane Response and international multidisciplinary experts including clinicians, priority disease researchers, methodologists, social, behavioural and public health professionals.14 We followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. The protocol is registered online at https://osf.io/twfnr/.13Fig. 1Rapid research needs appraisal process. The RRNA utilises a relay of trained systematic reviewers across different time-zones supported by review software to optimise the process for quality and timely completion.

Rapid research needs appraisal process. The RRNA utilises a relay of trained systematic reviewers across different time-zones supported by review software to optimise the process for quality and timely completion.

Ethics approval was not required as the focus is on secondary data analysis. The requirement for written informed consent was waived, owing to the secondary data analysis of deidentified published data.

Search strategy

We searched Ovid Embase, Ovid MEDLINE and Scopus between Jan 1, 1999, and Feb 6, 2026 for human studies about BDBV and specific domain terms by combining keywords and thesaurus headings for Bundibugyo virus and humans (Appendix 1: Search strategy). The search was updated to May 19, 2026 in PubMed and MedRxiv using terms for Bundibugyo AND human to identify new studies published during the ongoing outbreak. PubMed was used as it is a comprehensive database with rapid publication of new studies including pre-prints. We did not include searches for grey literature, as the focus is on identifying primary research studies.

Eligibility

We included primary studies, including recent preprints from the 2026 outbreak including human or human samples and focused on studying at least one of the RRNA research domain topics (Appendix 2: RRNA domains). We defined cases as laboratory or clinically confirmed and probable cases or serologically confirmed past infection. The case ascertainment followed the case classification reported by study authors. We excluded opinion pieces (not presenting primary data), narrative, scoping and mapping reviews, conference abstracts, and book chapters. We excluded pure outbreak reports, modelling, animal, and cell studies. The search was conducted in English, but there was no language restriction.

Screening, data extraction and analysis

Title and abstract and full text screening was carried out by two reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving any conflicts. Primary studies eligible for data extraction were extracted by one reviewer, with a second reviewer checking the data. Full text records not identified via EndNote, PubMed, Google, or Oxford University online library access were excluded due to inaccessibility. We did not conduct a formal risk of bias analysis or critical appraisal nor research prioritisation as this is out of scope for the RRNA, since the focus is on rapidly and systematically identify all existing published primary study evidence and identify where there is gaps in the evidence-base to support management and research prioritisation strategies. The data were analysed descriptively and presented narratively.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no involvement in the study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Clinical characteristics

Eleven studies presented on clinical characteristics and CFRs: two meta-analyses, one cohort study, five cross-sectional studies and three case-series/reports.3,6,7,15,25,30,34,39 Five reported the inclusion of children (<18 yrs).7,15,25,30,39 One case report included a pregnant mother and neonate,30 one a postpartum female.34 Two cross-sectional studies presented meta-analyses but did not report demographics.3,6 Seven studies presented signs, symptoms and complications. The most commonly reported symptoms were fever, fatigue, headache, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain and dysphagia (Table 2, Fig. 6). The incubation times ranged from 2 to 20 days. A study set in the DRC (2026) presented data on 505 patients with laboratory confirmed BVD (determined by Altona Diagnostics and RADIONE PCR tests) included between 3 May and 8 June 2026. A study set in Uganda (2007–2008) including 116 confirmed or probable cases, reported a median duration from symptom onset to recovery of 10 days (range 2–26 days) and to death: 10 days (range 3–21 days). 59% of those who died experienced haemorrhagic symptoms.39 A study set in the DRC (2012) including 52 patients, reported a median disease duration of 18 days. They did not identify significant differences in mortality between people experiencing different signs or symptoms.25 However, an earlier study set in Uganda (2007–2008) analysing data from 26 cases reported a 12 times higher risk of dying in patients with haemorrhagic symptoms compared to those without (86% vs. 33%; OR 12.0).34

The neonate developed complications and died 7 days post-delivery. BDBV antigen was seen by immunohistochemical analysis within large atypical maternal mononuclear cells and in multiple foci within the villous syncytiotrophoblast.30

Long-term sequelae

A case-control study set in Uganda, including 40 confirmed BDBV survivors and 23 age-matched controls reported that post-disease sequelae were common and diverse. Headaches were the most reported symptom, experienced by 35% (14/40) of survivors, followed by vision problems (22.5%, 9/40). Fatigue, joint pain, and neurological issues, such as paraesthesia or cognitive disturbances (15%, 6/40 each), musculoskeletal weakness (5%, 2/40), other symptoms (10%, 4/40). Laboratory findings showed elevated basophils (40%) and urinary ketones (5%), with respiratory rates significantly reduced in survivors (p < 0.001). 57.5% reported persistent stigma.8 Another study including 49 probable and confirmed BVD survivors from the 2007 outbreak in Uganda and 157 seronegative contacts, controlled for age and sex, identified significantly increased risk of ocular deficits (retro-orbital pain (RR 4.3, 95% CI 1.9–9.6; p < 0.0001), blurred vision (RR 1.9, 95% CI 1.1–3.2; p = 0.018), hearing loss (RR 2.3, 95% CI 1.2–4.5; p = 0.010), difficulty swallowing (RR 2.1, 95% CI 1.1–3.9; p = 0.017), difficulty sleeping (RR 1.9, 95% CI 1.3–2.8; p = 0.001), arthralgias (RR 2.0, 95% CI 1.1–3.6; p = 0.020), chronic health problems (prevalence ratio [PR] 2.1, 95% CI 1.2–3.6; p = 0.008), limitations due to memory loss or confusion (PR 5.8, 1.5–22.4; p = 0.010) and other constitutional symptoms.19

Immune response

A study set in the DRC (2015) including 15 survivors identified a degree of reactivity to multiple filovirus species in 33.3% (n = 5). Three survivors showed serologic responses to EBOV, BDBV and SUDV, and three had plasma antibody responses to Marburg virus glycoprotein (GP).17 A study set in the DRC in 2018 which screened healthcare workers for Ebola virus antigens identified seroreactivity to BDBV GP in 13 participants, ten showed reactivity to both EBOV and BDBV GPs, and one to SUDV and BDBV GP. Community health volunteers were more likely to be seroreactive compared to nurses.36 Another study including EBOV survivors in Guinea identified a decline in EBOV antibody levels over time. At 60 months after discharge from the Ebola treatment centre, the probability of retaining antibodies against EBOV GPs was 76.2% (95% CI 67.2–83.3). The study identified low levels of cross-reactivity between EBOV, SUDV, and BDBV.20 A study using dried blood spots from 878 individuals in the Madina Oula region of Guinea identified BDBV GP antibody positivity in 5.0% (44/878) and BDBV VP40 antibody positivity in 3.8% (30/878) of samples.22 Immune response following Ebola vaccination is presented further below.

Transmission

The studies on human transmission were limited to contact tracing studies, and a case report of vertical transmission during pregnancy.7,22,23,30,40 One outbreak dynamic study (2007) including 56 confirmed BVD cases identified a peak in secondary cases related to a single case with prominent community status. Twenty-seven contacts became unwell, accounting for 21% of the cases in the Bundibugyo district. There were only two tertiary cases identified from this cluster.7 A case-report from DRC documented vertical transmission, with viral antigen identified in the syncytiotrophoblast and placental maternal mononuclear cells by immunohistochemical analysis.30 One study explored use of environmental sampling to detect shedding from BVD cases, by taking swabs from hospital wards and a home. The samples were analysed using RT-PCR. This identified low levels of BDBV genomic RNA in a hospital ward and a house where BVD cases had been cared for. The latter leading to the identification of the last chain of active transmission, which resulted in the safe burial of the last laboratory-confirmed case of this outbreak, demonstrating the use of environmental sampling in outbreak control.23

Risk factors

We identified four studies reporting on risk factors.7,30,36,39 A study set in Uganda (2007–2008) identified an overall attack rate (AR) of 43 cases/100,000 population, with a higher AR for men compared to females (64 vs. 47/100,000 population), and for people aged 41–50 years compared to 51–60-year-olds (146 vs. 122/100,000 population). Patients with confirmed disease were significantly more likely to have visited sick persons or the hospital three weeks before becoming sick (OR 8.71, 95% confidence interval [CI] 3.03–26.30). Multivariate analysis, controlling for confounding, identified funeral participation as the sole significant risk factor associated with disease (adjusted OR 3.83, 95% CI 1.78–8.23),39 whereas there was no significant association observed with consulting with a traditional healer within 3 weeks before illness (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.01–1.15).39 A study set in Uganda (2007) reported an overall CFR of 40% (17/43), and a significantly higher mean age in those who died compared to survivors (42 years, range: 20–70 years vs.33 years, range: 12–50 years; p = 0.039). No gender bias was observed, and no significant differences in symptoms between survivors compared to those who died.7

Diagnostics

We identified five studies exploring diagnostics.18,24,27,35,38 One of those reported that ReEBOV RDT, the first antigen-based assay to receive FDA and WHO emergency use authorisation in February 2015 showed sensitivity to EBOV, SUDV, and BDBV, with no cross-reactivity with non-EBOV febrile illness. It demonstrated specificity of 95% for serum panels and 97% for whole blood specimens.35 A study published in 2026 presented on the validation of a point-of-care closed PCR test (RADIONE) which was developed for rapid tests (65 min) to provide accurate BDBV diagnostics in settings with insufficient healthcare infrastructure. They reported an overall agreement with a RealStar Filovirus Screen kit of 95%.38

Other studies were limited mainly to cross-sectional, laboratory-based proof-of concept studies. A single probe panel was reported to distinguish between ebolaviruses, including BDBV, allowing for rapid screening for multiple antigens in a single sample.24 Another study presented a one-step RT FRET-PCR for differential detection,27 and a third study presented a serological multiplex point of care test for detection of IgG antibodies against SUDV, BDBV and EBOV.18

Vaccines

There were no BDBV vaccine trials including humans or human samples identified, but two studies assessing cross-reactivity to multiple filoviruses following EBOV vaccination.26,29 One cohort study nested within an EBOV vaccine RCT (pre-print, 2026) set in Guinea, Liberia, Mali and Sierra Leone explored cross-reactive BDBV antibody responses after licenced Ebola vaccines.26 They assessed samples from 179 participants against GPs for EBOV Kikwit, EBOV Mayinga and BDBV at day 28 and 90 post-vaccination with rVSVΔG-ZEBOV-GP or Ad26.ZEBOV/MVA-BN-Filo vaccines. They reported detectable cross-reactive antibody responses against BDBV across vaccine groups, timepoints, and age categories. However, BDBV responses remained substantially lower than homologous EBOV responses.26 A non-randomized interventional study set in the US and Africa explored cross-reactive antibodies following vaccination with MVA-BN-Filo followed by Ad26.ZEBOV 14 days later or Ad26.ZEBOV followed by MVA-BN-Filo 28 days later. 13% (26/205) of participants had limited positive IgG responses to SUDV GP compared to EBOV GP (Median (IQR) fold increase: 1.1 (1.0–2.1) vs. 63.7 (28.1–138.6)). No responses were detected against BDBV or MARV GP in this study.29

Supportive care

One study set in Uganda in 2007–2008 includin adults (20–70 yrs) presented data on the care provided to 19 hospitalised patients with confirmed BDBV infection. Most (95%) were administered paracetamol, 84% oral rehydration solution, 21% IV-fluids, and 1% cimetidine for dyspepsia. 37% (n = 7) received antibiotics for potential concomitant infections. Of 58% (n = 11) receiving antimalarials, 73% died. With the exception of antimalarial treatment, there was no significant difference in clinical outcome for any treatment component (Fisher’s exact p value range 0.33–1.00). The overall mortality rate was 42%, with care found to be challenging due to the resource-poor setting of outbreaks and the infection-control procedures required.34

Social and behavioural studies

We identified three observational studies set in the Bundibugyo district in Uganda.8,31,37 A case-control study set in 2023 following up 40 adult BVD survivors reported that most (57.5%, 23/40) reported persistent stigma 16 years after disease onset.8 A cross-sectional study set in the Bundibugyo and two neighbouring districts in 2024 surveyed 565 healthcare workers (HCWs) about attitudes to EBOV vaccines. Of these, 37.2% (n = 210) reported previous Ebola vaccination with rVSV-ZEBOV. Seventy-nine percent (n = 446) could not name a virus that caused Ebola disease, and 55.9% (n = 316) were unaware that rVSV-EBOV might only protect against EBOV. Most responders indicated that they would want to receive a booster or initial vaccine dose. The most common reason for Ebola vaccine hesitancy was safety concerns.37 Another study set in the Bundibugyo district in 2022 including 384 participants (55.7%, 214/384) with bat exposure reported that 54.2% (208/384) had poor knowledge of the risks associated with bat exposure. Increased exposure to bats was associated with being male (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 1.0–2.4, p-value = 0.038), staying in urban areas (OR = 1.9; p-value = 0.010), hunting (OR = 10.9; p-value = 0.024), and a positive perception to bat guano being safe as fertiliser (OR = 2.5; p-value = 0.045).31

Discussion

This RRNA identifies the sparse evidence-base for Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD), an emerging viral haemorrhagic fever. Consistent with the historically low number of detected BDBV outbreaks, most studies identified were observational, with only one non-randomized interventional study identified. The ongoing outbreak in the DRC and Uganda together with limited and inconsistent evidence for cross-reactivity following existing EBOV vaccination or natural infection, underscores the urgency of addressing critical research gaps across clinical, public health, and social science domains. As a high-consequence pathogen causing sporadic outbreaks, BDBV demands rapid research planning and capacity development. This need is underscored by findings from Paasi et al.,41 whose meta-regression including data from past Ebola outbreaks in Uganda (2000–2022), including BDVB demonstrated that each additional day from first case report to specimen collection was associated with a 3.8% point increase in CFR (β = 0.142; p = 0.025; R2 = 62%).41 Strengthening laboratory networks and accelerating outbreak protocols must be central to preparedness and response planning.

Although several studies have described the clinical presentation of BVD in adult populations, uncertainties remain regarding symptomatology and risk of complications in populations with immunosuppression due to age, pre-existing comorbidities or medications. Much of the evidence is derived from retrospective observational studies with relatively small sample sizes, limiting the ability to draw robust conclusions. Furthermore, variation in reported CFRs and haemorrhagic manifestations may be influenced by differences in case definitions, disease severity, access to healthcare, supportive care and diagnostics across settings and outbreaks. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, existing studies provide valuable baseline information that can inform preparedness and response planning. Future harmonised observational studies using pre-positioned protocols should characterise disease across diverse populations, including children and pregnant women, identify biomarkers and define risk of transmission, shedding, acute and long-term complications.42

This evidence is essential both for prioritising and protecting high-risk groups and for allocating limited healthcare resources effectively to improve patient outcomes, which is especially important in the affected resource-limited settings. Of particular urgency is the development of effective therapeutics and vaccines. Existing filovirus countermeasures target EBOV, and although a small number of studies indicated a level of cross-reactive immune responses following EBOV vaccination, the evidence is inconsistent and insufficient.26 Interventional trials targeting BDBV using existing EBOV vaccines and new BDBV specific vaccines and including children and pregnant women to evaluate safety, efficacy, and optimal dosing is required to ensure countermeasures are both effective and equitably applied. Such trials should include diverse population groups, and ideally include follow up of survivors to characterise risk of, and risk factors for post-infectious sequelae, durability of immune response and correlates of protection. WHO has developed the PARTNER adaptive filovirus trial protocol,10 and are coordinating efforts to reduce risk of proliferation of competing and underpowered trials, as was experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic.43 The PARTNER trial was activated and opened to inclusion in June 2026.

Evidence relating to transmission was limited but highlighted risk of potential super spreading events. One study identified that most secondary transmissions were linked to a single individual.7 This pose both a threat and opportunity for control and reinforces the importance of early detection of cases and coordinated surveillance to identify and break key transmission chains. This will require investments in resources for effective case identification, screening and rapid diagnostics, optimal care and effective engagement with communities, community leaders, and media.

We identified few social and behavioural studies but no implementation studies. Experience from previous Ebola outbreaks demonstrate that stigma and mistrust can be a barrier for healthcare seeking behaviour. A 2019 survey of 800 respondents in North Kivu and Ituri, DRC, identified widespread denial of Ebola's existence among community members and political leaders, with rumours portraying EVD as an invention or curse undermining trust in treatment centres, vaccines, and safe burials.44,45 Attitudes, uptake, and implementation of prevention and control strategies are highly contextual, shaped by social, cultural, religious, political, and socioeconomic factors. Reports of stigma in past BDBV survivors8 and limited understanding of Ebola vaccines among healthcare workers further emphasise the importance of meaningful community engagement.40 to identify and address these issues in a sensitive, ethical, compassionate and culturally appropriate ways to build trust, dispel misconceptions and fears and prevent wider psychosocial impact.46

Vaccine hesitancy has posed a challenge in previous emerging infectious disease outbreaks. Fear was identified as a key driver for EBOV vaccine hesitancy among healthcare workers in one study, yet most participants indicated that they would accept a vaccine if offered. A Guinea-based study concluded that Ebola vaccination campaigns must be multifaceted and locally tailored rather than uniform.40 A qualitative study set in Liberia and Uganda including Ebola survivors from past outbreaks identified the benefit of engaging survivors as experts at the decision table, recognising their unique role and the important role of communities in outbreak control.46 Engagement of survivors, diverse community representatives, health workers and local stakeholders in governance, co-design and co-implementation should be considered an essential component of outbreak detection, prevention, research, response and mitigation.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. Only 29 studies were identified. The heterogeneity in study designs and data reporting limits capacity for meta-analyses. Most studies were limited in size and demographic reporting: while the majority included adults, many did not specify age, ethnicity, or pre-existing risk factors, restricting the generalisability of findings across populations. Most studies were conducted more than a decade ago. Therefore, findings may be impacted by historical health system, cultural and geopolitical context at the time. Social and behavioural evidence was sparse, and given its contextual nature, may not be generalisable across sites and countries. Further, reliance on uncontrolled survey designs introduces risk of bias in inclusion, reporting and recall bias. This is not a systematic review, but a rapid appraisal of existing evidence, as such we did not undertake a formal quality appraisal, risk of bias analysis, case duplication or meta-analysis. Instead we present the study design, setting, case numbers and key objectives, to support identification of evidence gaps. We did not include grey literature since the focus was on identifying existing research evidence. The updated search focused on PubMed and MedRxiv, databases that rapidly publish new studies, in order to capture and share emerging evidence during outbreaks. We did not perform deduplication of cases between studies, therefore the total number of BVD cases reported may differ from the total number of cases detected. These methodological decisions reflect the purpose of the RRNA, which is to rapidly identify existing evidence to highlight evidence gaps. Research prioritisation is out of scope for the RRNA protocol, since this also require input from local, regional, international and experiential experts to ensure strategies address identified evidence gaps as well as local need. Notwithstanding these limitations, this appraisal provides the most comprehensive overview to date of the existing published research evidence across key domains relevant for prevention, management and response. The gaps should inform BDBV research prioritisations, supported by the WHO filovirus roadmaps,47 expert including community experiences and with an emphasis on addressing local need, to facilitate effective and equitable responses to protect affected and at-risk populations. The findings identify clear priorities for future research, including harmonised clinical characterisation studies to identify biomarkers of disease severity and long-term sequelae that can improve case detection, triage, resource allocation and patients long-term outcomes. Further, genomics and pathophysiology studies to accelerate identification of therapeutic targets and research to define viral shedding, infectivity of body fluids, including semen and breast milk, and the durability of immune responses following natural infection and vaccination to inform infection prevention control and public health guidelines. Medical countermeasures are key for prevention and control, investments into development of rapid diagnostic tests and coordination of adaptive therapeutic, supportive care, vaccine and post-exposure trials, integrated with social and behavioural research and meaningful community partnership, will be key game changers to priorities. Addressing these through coordinated international collaboration, standardised protocols and meaningful community partnership will strengthen the ongoing outbreak response efforts and preparedness for future outbreaks.

In conclusion, as a rare re-emerging pathogen, Bundibugyo virus disease is underresearched. Our appraisal identified a sparse evidence-base with profound gaps to address through implementation of standardised studies and adaptive trial protocols to facilitate analysis across settings as the epidemic evolves. The ongoing outbreak makes these gaps not a future priority but an immediate crisis. Rapid, coordinated investment in observational and interventional research with long-term follow-up, inclusion of the whole population, and integrated community and stakeholder engagement is needed. Coordination between funders, researchers, governments, international and local stakeholders including communities is essential, with priorities focused on addressing local need to generate the evidence needed is key to protect affected populations, improve outcomes and strengthen preparedness for future outbreaks.
