Association Between Pericoronary Fat Attenuation Index and High-Risk Plaque Features Assessed by Photon-Counting CT
Authors: Lingxin Kong, Bowen Li, Yicheng Han, Xueqiao Yang, Ruyue Ding, Sen Zhang, Xinwu Ma, Hui Gu, Ximing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the correlation between pericoronary fat attenuation index (pFAI) and the presence, characteristics, and number of high-risk plaque (HRP) features identified using photon-counting computed tomography coronary computed tomography angiography (PCCT-CCTA) in patients with suspected coronary artery disease (CAD).Patients and MethodsThis was a single-center prospective cross-sectional study. We prospectively enrolled 119 consecutive patients with suspected CAD who underwent PCCT-CCTA at a local hospital between August and September, 2025. pFAI was measured from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) images, and its correlation with individual HRP features and their cumulative number was investigated.ResultsA total of 119 patients with suspected CAD were prospectively enrolled (mean age: 58.9±11.3 years; 62.1% male). pFAI was significantly higher in the HRP group than in the non-HRP group (−90.1±7.5 HU vs. −98.4±6.7 HU, P<0.001). Patients with ≥2 high-risk features exhibited significantly higher pFAI scores than those without HRP features. The pFAI was not significantly increased in patients with only one HRP feature (P=0.408). Furthermore, pFAI was significantly higher in patients with spotty calcification (SC), positive remodeling (PR), or napkin-ring sign (NRS) (all P<0.05), but no significant increase in pFAI was found in patients with low-attenuation plaque (LAP). Multivariable analysis identified non-calcified plaque (NCP) volume, SC, and PR as independent factors associated with pFAI (all P < 0.05).ConclusionThe pFAI was significantly elevated in patients with HRP. Coronary arterial inflammation plays a pivotal role in HRP formation. The PCCT is a powerful tool for detecting HRP and assessing coronary arterial inflammation.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a primary cause of death across the globe1 and stands as a major public health issue. Perivascular inflammation plays a critical role in atherosclerosis progression and unstable plaque formation.2 This inflammatory activity promotes healing following vascular injury, subsequently creating a favorable environment for plaque initiation and progression. Furthermore, perivascular inflammation compromises plaque stability and significantly increases the susceptibility to rupture, fissuring, or erosion.3 Atherosclerosis itself represents a systemic, low-grade inflammatory state associated with immune system activation.4,5

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is the first-line imaging modality for detecting CAD. Pericoronary adipose tissue (PCAT) attenuation measured by CCTA reflects the degree of coronary inflammation.6 Mechanistically, inflammatory mediators released from the vascular wall inhibit the differentiation of adjacent preadipocytes. On CCTA scans, this phenotypic shift manifests as increased PCAT attenuation.7 Recent clinical evidence has demonstrated that elevated PCAT attenuation in both proximal and distal coronary segments correlates significantly with the presence of culprit plaques.8 The fat attenuation index (FAI) provides high sensitivity and specificity for mapping tissue inflammation, correlating strongly with 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography, adipocyte lipid content, and cell size.7 In addition to FAI, CCTA enables the simultaneous detection of high-risk plaque (HRP) features, including the napkin-ring sign (NRS), positive remodeling (PR), low-attenuation plaque (LAP), and spotty calcification (SC).

However, the relationship between perivascular inflammation assessed by PCAT and HRP features remains incompletely understood,9 particularly regarding individual HRP features, such as SC and PR. Photon-counting computed tomography (PCCT) has recently gained increasing clinical applications. Compared to conventional energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT), PCCT offers substantial improvements in image quality, such as increased spatial resolution, reduced artifacts, and lower electronic noise.10,11 PCCT enables a more accurate quantification of individual plaque components in patients with CAD than EID-CT12,13 and provides richer fat-attenuation information via spectral imaging.14,15 Furthermore, PCCT-based visualization and quantification of HRP features have been histologically validated.16 However, current evidence regarding PCCT-derived pericoronary fat attenuation index (pFAI) remains limited, and the reproducibility, clinical implications, and standardized assessment protocols of PCCT-based pFAI have not yet been fully established. Given the different detector technology of PCCT compared with conventional energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT), pFAI measurements obtained from PCCT may not be directly comparable with those derived from conventional CT systems.15 Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that PCCT-based pFAI quantification can be influenced by acquisition and reconstruction parameters, including virtual monoenergetic imaging levels and reconstruction algorithms.17 Therefore, Therefore, further studies are needed to validate PCCT-derived pFAI and clarify its clinical implications in coronary inflammation and HRP assessment.

Therefore, this prospective cross-sectional study utilized advanced PCCT imaging to systematically evaluate individual HRP features, their cumulative number, and their exact relationship with the pericoronary fat attenuation index (pFAI) to clarify the role of localized vascular inflammation in high-risk plaque development.

Material and Methods

Patient Population

This research was conducted strictly in accordance with the requirements of the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guideline. This was a prospective cross-sectional study. Clinical and imaging data were prospectively collected for 151 consecutive patients with suspected CAD who underwent photon-counting computed tomography coronary computed tomography angiography (PCCT-CCTA) at a Local Hospital between August 2025 and September 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age > 18 years and (2) suspected CAD with clinically indicated CCTA as the primary diagnostic test. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior myocardial infarction or known CAD (n = 8), (2) previous coronary revascularization (n = 23), and (3) poor image quality (n = 1). Ultimately, 119 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University and strictly followed the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. PCCT-CCTA Data Acquisition

All patients were examined using a PCCT scanner (NAEOTOM Alpha; Siemens Healthineers, Germany). All patients received sublingual nitroglycerin (0.1 mg) for coronary vasodilation before scan acquisition if no contraindications were found. CCTA was performed using restrospective electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated acquisition in QuantumPlusResearch mode, with an acquisition window of 30–80%. CARE Dose4D and CRRE keV were used in manual kV mode, with a tube voltage of 120 kV, CARE keV IQ level 75, rotation time of 250 ms, collimation of 96×0.2 mm, heart rate-adaptive pitch, and craniocaudal scan direction. The contrast agent was injected at a rate of 26 mgI/kg/s. Images were reconstructed using standard reconstruction, with a slice thickness of 0.4 mm, slice increment of 0.3 mm, convolution kernel Bv48, SPP reconstruction mode, quantum iterative reconstruction (QIR) level 3, and a reconstruction matrix of 512×512.

PCCT-CCTA Data Processing

All PCCT-CCTA data were analyzed using CoronaryDoc software (Shukun Technology), selecting the phase with optimal image quality. The coronary tree centerline was automatically extracted after importing the data into the post-processing software. Manual adjustment of the centerline was performed by the operator when necessary. Coronary arteries with a diameter of at least 1.5 mm were evaluated.18 Multiplanar reconstruction images were generated for each coronary artery and the lumen and vessel walls were automatically identified. The analysis included lesions in the left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, and right coronary arteries. The left main artery was considered part of the left anterior descending artery. When the obtuse marginal branch was more dominant than the left circumflex artery, it was prioritized for analysis. The plaque volume and volume of different plaque components were determined using predefined Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds: low-attenuation component (< 30 HU), noncalcified component (< 350 HU), and calcified component (≥ 350 HU).12

HRP was defined as a coronary lesion with at least two of the following high-risk plaque features: LAP, PR, SC, and NRS.19,20 SC was defined as a focal calcification within the non-calcified plaque with computed tomography (CT) values > 130 HU, and the maximum diameter of this calcification in any cross-section was less than 3 mm. PR was defined as a lesion in which the outer vessel diameter at the lesion segment divided by the average outer diameter of the proximal and distal reference vessels was greater than 1.1. LAP was defined as a plaque component with a CT attenuation value of < 30 HU. The pFAI analysis was also performed using CoronaryDoc software to measure the FAI around coronary plaques. The pFAI was defined as all voxels within the range of − 190 HU to − 30 HU, located within a radial distance from the outer vessel wall equal to the average diameter of the target vessel.15,21 The proximal segments of all three major epicardial coronary vessels (RCA, LAD and LCX) were analyzed. For the LAD and LCX, the analysis covered the range of 0–40 mm from their respective openings. For the RCA, the range of 10–50 mm from its opening was selected.7,22 All image analyses were performed by two experienced radiologists (with over five years of CCTA diagnostic experience) who were blinded to the clinical information.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collated and analyzed using SPSS 25.0 and MedCalc 19.6.4. Patients were categorized into HRP and non-HRP groups based on the presence of high-risk features on the CCTA images. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as medians (Q1, Q3) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentage), and the χ2 test was used. If necessary, the continuity-corrected χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess the between-group differences. Univariate linear regression analysis was performed to assess the relationship between pFAI and clinical characteristics as well as CCTA parameters in patients with suspected CAD. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in a multiple regression analysis to identify independent factors associated with pFAI. Multicollinearity among variables included in the multiple linear regression models was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Spearman correlation matrix was used to evaluate the correlation between perivascular inflammation levels and plaque characteristics, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. For comparisons involving different numbers of HRP features, Bonferroni correction was applied to reduce the risk of type I error caused by multiple comparisons. Laboratory parameters were collected only for baseline characterization and were not included in analyses evaluating the associations between pFAI and HRP features or in regression models. Missing laboratory data were not imputed.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

This prospective cross-sectional study included 119 patients with suspected CAD (age: 58.9 ± 11.3 years; 62.1% male). Among them, 29 (24.3%) were diagnosed with HRP based solely on the PCCT-CCTA images (Table 1). Some laboratory parameters were available only in a subset of patients. These parameters were used only for baseline description and were not included in subsequent analyses evaluating the associations between pFAI and HRP features. Compared with patients in the non-HRP group, those in the HRP group had more severe luminal stenosis (51% vs 21%, P = 0.015), greater plaque length and plaque volume (12.4 mm vs 6.0 mm; 39.0 mm3 vs 12.7 mm3, both P < 0.001), and larger volumes of LAP and NCP components (6.93 mm3 vs 0.76 mm3; 21.57 mm3 vs 7.76 mm3, both P < 0.001). Furthermore, patients with HRP had significantly higher pFAI in all three major coronary arteries (RCA: −90.1 ± 7.5 HU vs −98.4 ± 6.7 HU; LAD: −91.2 ± 8.1 HU vs −97.7 ± 6.2 HU; LCX: −87.2 ±7.5 HU vs −94.6 ± 6.8 HU, all P < 0.001; Table 2). Univariate and Multivariable Linear Regression Analyses of pFAI

Univariate analysis revealed that male sex (β= 3.935, P = 0.007), plaque volume (PV) (β= 0.017, P = 0.024), LAP volume (β= 0.187, P = 0.042), NCP volume (β= 0.063, P = 0.004), SC (β= 6.700, P < 0.001), PR (β= 7.340, P < 0.001), and NRS score (β = 7.591, P = 0.003) were significantly associated with RCA pFAI. In Model 1, which included male sex, PV, LAP volumes, NCP volumes, and HRP features, male sex (β= 2.785, P = 0.035) and the presence of HRP features (β= 7.229, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with RCA pFAI. In Model 2, which included male sex, PV, LAP volumes, NCP volumes, SC, PR, NRS, SC (β= 5.286, P = 0.006), and PR (β= 4.995, P = 0.016) were independently associated with RCA pFAI (Table 3). Discussion

This prospective cross-sectional study evaluated the clinical correlations between PCCT-CCTA-derived plaque composition, HRP features, and pFAI. The main findings were as follows: 1) the presence of HRP features was associated with higher levels of ppericorronary inflammatory changes, as reflected by increased RCA pFAI values in lesions exhibiting spotty calcification (SC), positive remodeling (PR), or the napkin-ring sign (NRS); 2) both SC and PR were independently associated with increased RCA pFAI values, which may reflect local perivascular inflammatory changes; and 3) Compared with CAD patients with no or only 1 HRP feature, those with ≥2 HRP features showed significantly higher pFAI values. However, no statistically significant difference in pFAI was observed between patients with two HRP features and those with ≥3 HRP features.

Although retrospective ECG-gated CCTA offers the advantage of flexible reconstruction of cardiac phases and consistently high image quality, radiation exposure remains a key consideration in clinical practice. Previous investigations have shown that advances in acquisition techniques, including prospective high-pitch turbo-flash protocols, can markedly decrease radiation dose compared with conventional retrospective ECG-gated dual-source CT without compromising diagnostic performance.23 Building upon these advances, photon-counting CT represents a promising technological development, with enhanced detector efficiency and advanced reconstruction capabilities potentially enabling further optimization of radiation dose while maintaining high-quality coronary imaging.

Owing to its ultra-high temporal and spatial resolution, PCCT enables more stable acquisition of high-quality CCTA houts, extending the scan time or increasing the radiation dose.24 In calcified plaques, blooming artifacts on conventional EID-CT frequently corrupt plaque composition measurements and artificially exaggerate luminal stenosis. Compared with conventional imaging modalities, the UHR imaging capabilities of PCCT can display more detailed plaque information and reduce the impact of calcification artifacts on the assessment of calcified volumes, thereby allowing more accurate quantification of different plaque components.25 In addition, PCCT-CCTA can reveal HRP that is difficult to detect by EID-CT, including SC with diameters < 1 mm and an NRS with a low-attenuation core surrounded by a high-signal ring. Its detection sensitivity and specificity have significantly improved, and are highly consistent with the histological standards of optical coherence tomography, providing non-invasive and repeatable imaging evidence for the early identification of vulnerable plaques.26 In this study, 24.4% of patients had HRP features, a proportion notably higher than that reported in previous EID-CT studies.27 This discrepancy may reflect the enhanced sensitivity of PCCT in detecting high-risk plaque features that may be difficult to identify using conventional EID-CT, providing additional imaging information for plaque characterization.

Mechanistically, the current literature highlights bidirectional paracrine crosstalk between coronary plaques and perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT). Vascular-derived inflammatory mediators diffuse outward from inflamed plaque segments into the perivascular space, subsequently suppressing local adipogenesis and driving attenuation gradients detectable on CCTA.6 Crucially, the superior spatial resolution and refined soft-tissue quantification of PCCT over conventional EID-CT enable the capture of these subtle attenuation shifts with unprecedented precision,10,11 providing a potential imaging marker of localized vascular inflammatory changes. Concurrently, dysfunctional PCAT actively fuels atherogenesis via an “outside-to-inside” signaling axis, where the loss of vasoprotective adipokines exacerbates adventitial inflammation and induces adverse vascular remodeling during early stage pathogenesis, frequently before overt structural vessel wall damage manifests.6,28,29

In the present study, RCA pFAI was used as the representative measure of coronary inflammation. This decision was supported by both our preliminary analysis and previous evidence. In our cohort, pFAI values differed significantly among the three major coronary arteries, indicating that inherent differences between coronary branches may introduce variability into pFAI assessment. Therefore, a single coronary branch was selected to minimize inter-vessel heterogeneity. We considered it important to explicitly report this methodological choice, since the present study also evaluated the RCA as a key target.30 Previous studies have demonstrated that pFAI measured around the proximal RCA provides a reliable assessment of coronary inflammatory status and has been widely validated as a representative marker of global coronary inflammation.22 The proximal RCA is also the most commonly used site for pFAI assessment, as it is surrounded by relatively abundant and continuous pericoronary adipose tissue and is less affected by adjacent myocardial tissue, resulting in more reproducible measurements.31 Accordingly, RCA pFAI was adopted as the primary indicator of pericoronary inflammation in the current study.

Our data are consistent with previous findings showing an association between elevated PCAT attenuation and non-calcified plaque (NCP) burden. Specifically, we found that patients presenting with HRP features had a significantly larger NCP volume, and larger NCP volume was associated with increased RCA pFAI values.6,32 This aligns with prior reports detailing a robust correlation between heightened PCAT attenuation and patient-level HRP prevalence.33,34 However, while historical cohorts have consistently captured this broad, patient-level association, granular, lesion-specific insights into discrete high-risk features within non-obstructive CAD populations have long been hindered by the inherent spatial resolution constraints and severe blooming artifacts characteristic of conventional EID-CT. Capitalizing on the technical leaps in PCCT, the present study is the first dedicated evaluation of the intricate interplay between individual HRP phenotypes and lesion-specific coronary perivascular inflammation in suspected CAD. These findings provide further evidence supporting the association between individual HRP features and local perivascular inflammatory changes in suspected CAD patients. The clinical relevance of CT-derived adipose tissue biomarkers may extend beyond coronary atherosclerosis. Recent studies have explored their application in other inflammatory conditions, with CT-assessed epicardial adipose tissue volume showing an association with disease severity in patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia.35 These findings suggest that adipose tissue characteristics obtained from CT imaging may capture broader inflammatory changes, rather than reflecting only localized coronary vascular inflammation.

Furthermore, our study found that pFAI was not significantly elevated in patients with only one HRP feature compared with those without any HRP features, whereas pFAI was significantly increased in patients with ≥2 HRP features. This suggests that the presence of LAP alone may not be sufficient to identify plaques associated with increased inflammatory changes; rather, the co-existence of other HRP features, particularly SC or PR, appears may be associated with higher PCAT attenuation. These HRP features have been reported to be associated with more advanced atherosclerosis, intraplaque hemorrhage, and the formation of large necrotic cores with microcalcifications.36,37 Studies have indicated a weak association between PCAT attenuation and advanced atherosclerotic plaques.38,39 Consistently, in this study, no statistically significant difference in pFAI was found between patients with 2 HRP features and those with ≥3 HRP features, indicating that having ≥2 HRP features does not reflect a higher degree of vascular inflammation. However, owing to the sample size limitation and the small number of patients with ≥3 HRP features, this conclusion requires further validation in future large-scale studies conducted in multiple centers involving a large number of participants.

This study has several limitations: (1) Our single-center cross-sectional study was conducted, which inherently introduces potential selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate the potential prognostic implications of PCCT-derived plaque characteristics and pFAI. (2) Given the limited sample size, particularly the small number of patients with HRP features, the statistical power may have been limited, especially for analyses involving individual HRP features and different numbers of HRP features. This should be further investigated in future larger studies. In addition, although Bonferroni correction was applied to reduce the risk of type I error caused by multiple comparisons involving different HRP features and HRP feature numbers, these findings require further validation in larger cohorts. (3) The pFAI values measured using PCCT differed from those reported in previous EID-CT-based studies. Previous studies have demonstrated that reconstruction algorithms and scanning parameters may influence pFAI quantification, which may partly explain the discrepancy observed in the present study. However, evidence regarding PCCT-specific pFAI assessment remains limited, and standardized reference values have not yet been established. Future large-scale multicenter studies with standardized acquisition and reconstruction protocols are warranted to further validate PCCT-derived pFAI measurements and facilitate harmonization across different CT systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with HRP features exhibited higher RCA pFAI values, particularly in those with two or more HRP features. Non-calcified plaque (NCP) volume, spotty calcification (SC), and positive remodeling (PR) were independently associated with increased RCA pFAI. These findings suggest that pFAI is associated with HRP features and may serve as a potential imaging marker of coronary inflammation and plaque vulnerability.
