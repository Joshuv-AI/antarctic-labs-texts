Differential magnetometry with partially flipped Dicke states Preprint: Preprint/1
Iagoba Apellaniz, Manuel Gessner, Géza Tóth

Abstract
We study magnetometry of gradients and homogeneous background fields along all three spatial axes using two spatially separated spin ensembles. We derive trade-off relations for the achievable estimation precision of these parameters. Dicke states, optimal for homogeneous field estimation, can be locally rotated into states sensitive to magnetic gradients by rotating the spins in one subensemble. We determine bounds for the precision for gradient metrology in the three orthogonal directions as a function of the sensitivities of the homogenous field in those directions. The resulting partially flipped Dicke state saturates the bounds above, showing similar sensitivity in two directions but significantly reduced sensitivity in the third. Exploiting entanglement between the two ensembles, this state achieves roughly twice the precision attainable by the best bipartite separable state, which is a product of local Dicke states. For small ensembles, we explicitly identify measurement operators saturating the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, while for larger ensembles, we propose simpler but suboptimal schemes. In both cases, the gradient is estimated from second moments and correlations of angular momentum operators. Our results demonstrate how the metrological properties of Dicke states can be exploited for quantum-enhanced multiparameter estimation.

II
Theoretical background

We briefly explain the separability concept of quantum states, and we describe the Hamiltonian acting on the system.
We also present the main tool used to compute the achievable precision, the multiparametric Cramér-Rao bound and its properties.

II.1
Separability of quantum states

A quantum state of
N
particles is fully separable if it can be written as a statistical mixture of product states
Werner 1989
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Friis et al. 2019

\varrho=\sum_{k}p_{k}\varrho_{k,1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\varrho_{k,N}.

(1)

Otherwise, the quantum state is entangled.

Let us now divide the particles into two groups, ‘a’ and ‘b’. A state is bipartite separable if it can be written as

\varrho=\sum_{k}p_{k}\varrho_{k,{\rm{a}}}\otimes\varrho_{k,{\rm{b}}},

(2)

where
\varrho_{k,{\rm{a}}}
and
\varrho_{k,{\rm{b}}}
are quantum states of the two different parties.

Whereas all fully separable states are bipartite separable, the opposite statement is not always true.
Bipartite separable states might be entangled within the parties, thus they are not necessarily fully separable.

II.2
Cramér-Rao bound for multiparameter quantum metrology

First, let us summarize briefly basic facts about single-parameter metrology.
In the most fundamental task in quantum metrology, we have to consider a density matrix undergoing the dynamics

\varrho_{\theta}=U_{\theta}\varrho U_{\theta}^{\dagger},

(3)

where the unitary is given as

U_{\theta}=e^{-iA\theta}.

(4)

Based on measurements on
\varrho_{\theta},
we would like to estimate the parameter
\theta
.
The precision of estimating
\theta
with an unbiased estimator is bounded from below by the Cramér-Rao bound as
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 1982
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Petz 2008
;
Braunstein et al. 1996
;
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzanski et al. 2015
;
Pezze and Smerzi 2014
;
Tóth and Apellaniz 2014
;
Pezzè et al. 2018
;
Paris 2009

(\Delta\theta)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\nu F[\varrho,A]}.

(5)

Here, the quantum Fisher information (QFI) is computed as

Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 1982
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Petz 2008
;
Braunstein et al. 1996

{{F}}[\varrho,A]=2\sum_{\lambda\neq\mu}\frac{(p_{\lambda}-p_{\mu})^{2}}{p_{\lambda}+p_{\mu}}|\langle\lambda|A|\mu\rangle|^{2},

(6)

where the density matrix is given by its eigendecomposition as

\varrho=\sum_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\!\langle\lambda|.

(7)

Moreover, in Eq. (
5
)
\nu
is the number of independent repetitions. The Cramér-Rao bound in Eq. (
5
) can be saturated, under certain reasonable assumptions, in the large
\nu
limit by an optimal measurement and a suitable estimator.
In the rest of the paper, we will consider
\nu=1
for simplifying our formulas, however, the results for
\nu>1
can straightforwardly obtained.

For two operators, the QFI is defined as

{{F}}[\varrho,A,B]=2\sum_{\lambda\neq\mu}\frac{(p_{\lambda}-p_{\mu})^{2}}{p_{\lambda}+p_{\mu}}\langle\lambda|A|\mu\rangle\langle\mu|B|\lambda\rangle,

(8)

which is linear in the second and third arguments, it is also invariant under the permutation of
A
and
B.
We can recover the usual QFI by imposing
A=B
. For pure states,
{\hat{\Psi}}:=|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|
,
{{F}}[\Psi,A,B]=4(\langle\{A,B\}/2\rangle_{\hat{\Psi}}-\langle A\rangle_{\hat{\Psi}}\langle B\rangle_{\hat{\Psi}})
, and hence
{{F}}[\Psi,A]=4(\Delta{A})^{2}_{\hat{\Psi}},
that is, the QFI equals the variance times four.

In the multiparameter case, the metrological performance of the quantum state is given by the Cramér-Rao matrix inequality
Paris 2009
;
Braunstein and Caves 1994
;
Holevo 1982
;
Helstrom 1976
;
Petz 2002
;
Petz 2008

\bm{C}\geqslant{\bm{F}}^{-1},

(9)

where the elements of the QFI matrix are given as

{\bm{F}}_{ij}={{F}}[\varrho,A_{i},A_{j}],

(10)

and the covariance matrix is defined as

\bm{C}_{ij}={\rm Cov}[\theta_{i},\theta_{j}]=\langle\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\rangle-\langle\theta_{i}\rangle\langle\theta_{j}\rangle.

(11)

Here
\theta_{i}
are the parameters and
A_{i}
are the corresponding generating Hamiltonians.
Note that the diagonal elements of
\bm{C}
are the variances
(\Delta{\theta_{i}})^{2}
of the unknown parameters.

II.3
Hamiltonian

For differential magnetometry, one has to take into account the overall effects of an unknown homogeneous field.
For simplicity, we restrict the magnetic field to point towards a single
l
-direction, where
l=x,y,z.

Hence, for small distances in a certain
x
-direction, the magnetic field can be described as a linear function of
x
,

B_{l}=B_{0}+B_{1}x.

(12)

The simplest one-particle Hamiltonian for the field-particle interaction looks like

h=\gamma(B_{0}\mathds{1}\otimes j_{l}+B_{1}{\hat{x}}\otimes j_{l}),

(13)

where
j_{l}
and
{\hat{x}}
are the spin
l
-component operator and the position operator respectively, and
\gamma=g\mu_{\rm B}
is the gyromagnetic ratio which encodes the
g
-factor and the Bohr magneton.
The homogeneous field rotates the system globally, whereas the gradient field acts differently depending on the position of the particle.

The Hamiltonian of the whole system is simply the sum of each of the one-particle Hamiltonians

H=\gamma\left(B_{0}\mathds{1}\otimes J_{l}+B_{1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}\right),

(14)

where
J_{l}
is the
l
-component of the total angular momentum, with
l=x,y,z,
and
N
is the total particle number.

The system is split in two ensembles along the
x
-direction, and we place the origin such that the first ensemble is at
x=-d
and second at
x=+d.

We can reformulate the Hamiltonian, acting on the spin-subspace only, as

H=\gamma\left[B_{0}J_{l}+B_{1}d(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}-J_{l,{\rm{b}}})\right],

(15)

where ‘a’ stands for particles on the first well and ‘b’ for particles on the second well.
In general, the splitting might not be even and we use
N_{\rm{a}}
to refer to the number of particles in the well ‘a’ and
N_{\rm{b}}
for the well ‘b’, where
N=N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}
.
Note also that
J_{l}=J_{l,{\rm{a}}}+J_{l,{\rm{b}}}
.

The state evolves under the unitary operator
U=\exp(-iHt)
, where
t
is the evolution time.
The homogeneous phase-shift and the gradient phase-shift imprinted on the state are

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma B_{0}t,

(16a)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma(B_{1}d)t,

(16b)

respectively.
Note that the unknown parameter
b_{1}
encodes the distance
d
.
Finally, the unitary time-evolution operator takes the form

U=\exp\!\left\{-i\left[b_{0}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})+b_{1}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})\right]\right\}\!,

(17)

where we identify
J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}
as the homogeneous phase-shift generator and
J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}
as the gradient phase-shift generator.

The generators for the homogeneous and the gradient phase-shifts,,
J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}
and
J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}
, are related to each other by a “partial flipping” operation

R_{\perp,{\rm{b}}}(\pi):=\mathds{1}_{\rm{a}}\otimes\exp(-i\pi J_{\perp,{\rm{b}}}),

(18)

which rotates by
\pi
the spin of the particles in ‘b’ around the
\perp
-axis perpendicular to
l
.
Thus,

R_{\perp,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)^{\dagger}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})R_{\perp,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)=J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}.

(19)

For instance, we can have
l=z
and
\perp=x.

II.4
Gradient metrology in a two-well system

Let us now apply the formalism of quantum metrology presented in Sec.
II.2
to the problem considered in our article.
For states insensitive to the gradient of the field, we have that
[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]=0
.
Thus, the only parameter that can be estimated is
b_{0}
.
Its achievable precision is calculated utilizing the Cramér-Rao bound as

(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}\leqslant{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}],

(20)

where the
{{F}}
is the quantum Fisher Information (QFI).

On the other hand, for states insensitive to the homogeneous field,
[\varrho,J_{l}]=0
holds. Thus, the achievable precision for
b_{1}
is

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}\leqslant{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}].

(21)

From now on, we will use
{F}_{l,+}:={{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]
and
{F}_{l,-}:={{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]
for simplicity.

For states sensitive to both
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
, instead of Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
), we have a
2\times 2
Cramér-Rao matrix inequality

\begin{pmatrix}(\Delta{b_{0}})^{2}&\Cov[b_{0},b_{1}]\\
\Cov[b_{0},b_{1}]&(\Delta{b_{1}})^{2}\end{pmatrix}\geqslant{\bm{F}}_{l}^{-1},

(22)

where
\Cov[b_{0},b_{1}]
is the covariance between
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
.
The QFI matrix elements are given by

\displaystyle({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,0}

\displaystyle={F}_{l,+}={F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}+2{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}+{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}},

(23a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}&=({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,0}\\
&={F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}+{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}+{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}-{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}\\
&={F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}-{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}},\end{split}

(23b)

\displaystyle({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,1}

\displaystyle={F}_{l,-}={F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}-2{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}+{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}},

(23c)

where we use
{F}_{l,rr^{\prime}}
for
{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,r},J_{l,r^{\prime}}]
,
{F}_{l,r}
for
{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,r}]

for readability, and the properties of QFI (
8
) to rewrite and simplify the matrix elements.

Hence, using the algebraic formula for the inverse of a
2\times 2
matrix and from the diagonal terms in Eq. (
22
), the corresponding precision bounds for
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
are

\displaystyle(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,0}-\frac{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}^{2}}{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,1}}=4\frac{{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}-{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}^{2}}{{F}_{l,-}},

(24a)

\displaystyle(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,1}-\frac{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}^{2}}{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,0}}=4\frac{{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}-{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}^{2}}{{F}_{l,+}}.

(24b)

Since the two generators commute, it is possible to saturate both bounds at the same time
Ragy et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2019
.
Note that for homogeneous and gradient precision, we have in the denominator the QFI that corresponds to the gradient and the homogeneous parameter, respectively.
It suggests that the better the state for estimating the homogeneous field, the worse for estimating the gradient of the field, and
vice-versa
.

Moreover, if
{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}]={{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]
, then the off-diagonal elements of the QFI matrix,
({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}
and
({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,0}
, vanish as can be seen in Eq. (
23b
).
Hence, the QFI matrix is diagonal, and we recover the precision bounds for
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
as

\displaystyle(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l}],

(25a)

\displaystyle(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}].

(25b)

The bounds are the same as if the state was insensitive to the corresponding second parameter, Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
), i.e., a single-parameter estimation problem.

It is instructive to consider the extreme cases when either
N_{\rm{a}}
or
N_{\rm{b}}
is zero.
In this case, all the particles are in one of the wells and therefore, cannot be used for gradient magnetometry.
If
N_{\rm{a}}=0
then the Hamiltonian is simplified from Eq. (
17
) to

H=(b_{0}-b_{1})J_{l}
, and one can only estimate the value of the field at wells ‘b’, and estimate
b_{0}-b_{1}
.
On the other hand, if
N_{\rm{b}}=0
then the Hamiltonian simplifies from Eq. (
17
) to

H=(b_{0}+b_{1})J_{l}
, and one can only estimate the value of the field at well ‘a’, and estimate
b_{0}+b_{1}
, see Ref.
Fadel et al. 2023
.

We now analyze the joint performance for estimating both parameters.
Using the properties of the QFI given in Eq. (
8
), we obtain the sum of the bounds in Eqs. (
24
) as

\begin{split}(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}+(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}\leqslant\left({F}_{l,+}+{F}_{l,-}\right)\left[1-\frac{\big({F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}-{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}\big)^{2}}{{F}_{l,+}{F}_{l,-}}\right].\end{split}

(26)

Note that the term in the square bracket on the right-hand side of the inequality is equal to or smaller than one.
Hence, we can bound the sum of the achievable precisions by

\begin{split}(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}+(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}&\leqslant{F}_{l,+}+{F}_{l,-}=2\left({F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}+{F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}\right).\end{split}

(27)

We obtain the same inequality, if we add the inequalities for single parameter estimation tasks given in Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
).

For fully separable states, the right-hand side of Eq. (
27
) is tightly bounded from above by
2(N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}})=2N
.
Which means that there are separable states for which

\begin{split}{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,\rm{a}}{+}J_{l,\rm{b}}]&=N,\\
{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,\rm{a}}{-}J_{l,\rm{b}}]&=N,\end{split}

(28)

hold if
{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}}={F}_{l,{\rm{b}}}
.

On the other hand, for entangled states, we have that

{F}_{l,+}+{F}_{l,-}\leqslant 2(N_{\rm{a}}^{2}+N_{\rm{b}}^{2})\leqslant N^{2},

(29)

where the second inequality is saturated for an evenly split state, for which
N_{\rm{a}}=N_{\rm{b}}.

The inequality in Eq. (
29
) tells us that the Heisenberg limit,
(\Delta{\theta})^{-2}\leqslant N^{2}
, cannot be saturated for estimating the homogeneous field and the gradient field at the same time.
Note also that the bound in Eq. (
29
) can be saturated if the states is of the form
|\Psi_{\rm{a}}\rangle\otimes|\Psi_{\rm{b}}\rangle,
which are the tensor product of two single-subsystem states.
Nevertheless, with such states one can only reach half of the precision limit
{F}_{l,\pm}=N^{2}/2
for each of the parameters, since
{F}_{l,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}=0.

III
Impossibility of efficient gradient metrology with BECs

We briefly review the findings of Ref.
Apellaniz et al. 2018
concerning gradiometry with a single BEC. We show that the precision of gradient metrology cannot surpass the shot-noise limit.

In a BEC all the particles share the same spatial state
|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|
.
Hence, the whole state can be written as
|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|^{\otimes N}\otimes\varrho
, where
\varrho
is the spin state.
The Hamiltonian is of the form Eq. (
14
), and

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|{\hat{x}}_{n}|\Psi\rangle,

(30a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|({\hat{x}}_{n}-\mu)^{2}|\Psi\rangle=\langle\Psi|{\hat{x}}_{n}^{2}|\Psi\rangle-\mu^{2}

(30b)

are the mean position and the variance of the position of the BEC, respectively.
The corresponding multiparameter phase-shift generators are
J_{l}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}j_{l,n}
and
\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}
. Note that in this section the generators are different from the case considered in Eq. (
23
), where the generators were simply
J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{\pm}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}.

We compare the two systems at the end of the section.

The QFI matrix elements for pure states are

\displaystyle({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,0}

\displaystyle=4(\Delta{J_{l}})^{2},

(31a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}&=({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,0}\\
&=4\left(\langle J_{l}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}\rangle-\langle J_{l}\rangle\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}\rangle\right)\\
&=4\mu(\Delta{J_{l}})^{2}=0,\end{split}

(31b)

\displaystyle({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,1}

\displaystyle=4\left(\langle\biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}\biggr)^{2}\rangle-\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\hat{x}}_{n}\otimes j_{l,n}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=4\left(\sigma^{2}\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}j_{l,n}^{2}\rangle+\mu^{2}\langle\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}j_{l,n}j_{l,m}\rangle-\mu^{2}\langle J_{l}\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=4\left(\sigma^{2}\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}j_{l,n}^{2}\rangle+\mu^{2}(\Delta{J_{l}})^{2}\right)

(31c)

\displaystyle=4\sigma^{2}\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}j_{l,n}^{2}\rangle,

where we set
\mu=0
without loss of generality.
Hence, the achievable precision is bounded from above as

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}\leqslant({\bm{F}}_{l})_{1,1}-\frac{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,1}^{2}}{({\bm{F}}_{l})_{0,0}}\leqslant 4\sigma^{2}\langle\sum_{n=1}^{N}j_{l,n}^{2}\rangle,

(32)

which at the same time is bounded from above as
(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}\leqslant\sigma^{2}N
for spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particles.

Note that the upper-bound has a
\sigma^{2}
prefactor.
In order to compare it with the results of this work, one should compute
\sigma^{2}
for the double well.
For systems described with Eqs. (
15
), the size of the system
\sigma^{2}
is computed by

\sigma^{2}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}d^{2}}{N}-\left(\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm{a}}}(-d)+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm{b}}}d}{N}\right)^{2}=d^{2}\frac{4N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}}{N^{2}},

(33)

where one can neglect
d^{2}
for being embeded into
b_{1}
, Eq. (
16
).
Finally in order to compare upper-bounds between BECs and double-well systems of the same size, one can consider that the achievable precision for the BECs can be written as

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm BEC}\leqslant\frac{4N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}}{N}

(34)

where
N_{\rm{a}}
and
N_{\rm{b}}
belong to the double-well system the BEC is compared against.
Finally for comparing between a BEC and an evenly-split double-well
system, we have that
(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm BEC}\leqslant N.

IV
Flipping operator and partially flipped Dicke state for gradiometry

Efficient gradiometry is still possible if we carry out simple local operations on the quantum state before the gradient measurement.
In particular, based on Eq. (
18
) and Eqs. (
25a
) and (
25b
), one can apply the unitary flipping operator
R_{\perp,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)
over the states that have high sensitivity on the homogeneous field to obtain states that have high sensitivity on estimating the gradient of the field.
The opposite is also true.

To illustrate this, one can take for example the Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state with half of the particles at each well to estimate magnetic fields pointing towards the
z
-direction.
The GHZ state

|{\rm GHZ}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle^{\otimes N}+|1\rangle^{\otimes N})

(35)

commutes with
J_{z,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}
.
Hence, the corresponding bound for
b_{0}
is

(\Delta{b_{0}})^{-2}\leqslant 4(\Delta{J_{z}})^{2}_{\rm GHZ}=N^{2}.

(36)

If we apply the flipping operator

R_{x,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)=\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{x}^{\otimes N/2}

(37)

to the GHZ state along the
x
-direction, we obtain

|\widetilde{\rm GHZ}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle_{{\rm{a}}\phantom{{\rm{b}}}}^{\otimes N/2}|1\rangle_{{\rm{b}}}^{\otimes N/2}+|1\rangle_{{\rm{a}}\phantom{{\rm{b}}}}^{\otimes N/2}|0\rangle_{{\rm{b}}}^{\otimes N/2}),

(38)

which commutes with
J_{z}
.

The corresponding bound for the estimation of
b_{1}
is optimal,

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}\leqslant 4[\Delta(J_{z,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{z,{\rm{b}}})]^{2}_{\widetilde{\rm GHZ}}=N^{2}.

(39)

In the following we take this approach to construct states that are sensitive to gradient fields based on states that are sensitive to homogeneous fields
Fadel et al. 2023
.

IV.1
Partially flipped Dicke states

The Dicke state can be used to measure the homogeneous magnetic field.
In this section, we will show, how to obtain a quantum state from the Dicke state via local unitaries, that is useful for gradient metrology.

In general, Dicke states are simultaneous eigenstates of
\bm{J}^{2}
and
J_{z}

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{2}|j,m_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=j(j+1)|j,m_{z}\rangle,

\displaystyle J_{z}|j,m_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=m_{z}|j,m_{z}\rangle.

We are mostly interested in the Dicke state for which
j=N/2
and
m_{z}=0
,

|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle\equiv|N/2,0\rangle.

(40)

As a state of an
N
-qubit system,
|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle
is a symmetric
state with
\langle J_{z}\rangle=0
and
(\Delta{J_{z}})^{2}=0,
and large variances
(\Delta{J_{x}})^{2}
and
(\Delta{J_{y}})^{2}
.
Since it is a pure state, we have

{{F}}[\varrho,J_{z}]=4(\Delta{J_{z}})^{2}=0.

(41)

Hence, the Dicke state is insensitive to rotations around the
z
-axis.
On the other hand, for this state,

{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l}]=4(\Delta{J_{l}})^{2}=\frac{N(N+2)}{2},

(42)

for
l=x,y
, is large, thus the state can be used for phase estimation over rotations around the
x
- and
y
- axes, or any axis in the
xy
-plane.

For spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particles, the Dicke state
|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle
can be written as

|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle=\binom{N}{N/2}^{-1/2}\sum_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}(|0\rangle^{\otimes N/2}|1\rangle^{\otimes N/2}),

(43)

where the summation is over all distinct permutations of the spins.

A Schmidt decomposition of the Dicke state given in Eq. (
43
) corresponding a bipartitioning to
N_{\rm{a}}
and
N_{\rm{b}}
spins can be written as
Stockton et al. 2003

|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle=\sum_{m=-N_{\rm{a}}/2}^{N_{\rm{a}}/2}c_{m}|m\rangle_{\rm{a}}|{-}m\rangle_{\rm{b}},

(44)

where we define the expression

c_{m}={\binom{N}{N/2}^{-1/2}}\sqrt{{\binom{N_{\rm{a}}}{N_{\rm{a}}/2+m}}{\binom{N_{\rm{b}}}{N_{\rm{b}}/2+m}}},

(45)

and the coefficients appearing in Eq. (
45
) are usual Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.
We consider
N_{\rm{a}}\leqslant N_{\rm{b}}
without loss of generality, and we used the notation
|m\rangle_{\rm{a}}=|N_{{\rm{a}}}/2,m\rangle_{\rm{a}}
and
|m\rangle_{\rm{b}}=|N_{\rm{b}}/2,m\rangle_{\rm{b}}.

Since the Dicke state is very sensitive to rotations around the
x
- and
y
-axis, we will apply the flipping operator
R_{z,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)
along the
z
-axis,

R_{z,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)=\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{z}^{\otimes N/2}.

(46)

Hence, the partially flipped Dicke state,
|\widetilde{\rm D}_{N}\rangle:=R_{z,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)|{\rm D}_{N}\rangle
, can be used for gradient magnetometry along any direction perpendicular to
z
-axis.

R_{z,{\rm{b}}}(\pi)
acts only on
|m\rangle_{\rm{b}}
, and based on Eqs. (
18
) and (
44
), we have

|\widetilde{\rm D}_{N}\rangle=\sum_{m=-N_{\rm{a}}/2}^{N_{\rm{a}}/2}c_{m}(-1)^{m}|m\rangle_{\rm{a}}|{-}m\rangle_{\rm{b}}.

(47)

IV.2
Gradient magnetometry with partially flipped Dicke states

For the task of estimating the three components of a magnetic field a quantum state cannot surpass the following precision bound
Tóth 2012

Hyllus et al. 2012

(48)

where
\lambda_{\max}(X)
is the maximal eigenvalue of the matrix
X

The GHZ and Dicke states among others saturate this bound.

Observation 1
.

For differential magnetometry a similar figure of merit would be

\sum_{l=x,y,z}{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]\leqslant N(N+2)+4\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}}),

\sum_{l=x,y,z}{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]=N(N+2)+\frac{4N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}}{N-1}.

(50)

The flipped Dicke is closer to saturate the Eq. (

IV.3
Precision bound for partially flipped Dicke state

We study the precision bound for the gradient parameter
b_{1}
, Eq. (
24b
), for a magnetic field pointing towards the
y
-direction when the initial state is a flipped Dicke state given in Eq. (
47
).
First, we compute the quantities
{F}_{y,{\rm{a}}}
,
{F}_{y,{\rm{b}}}
and
{F}_{y,{\rm{a}},{\rm{b}}}
appearing in Eq. (
24b
)

\displaystyle{F}_{y,{\rm{a}}}

\displaystyle=4(\Delta{J_{y,{\rm{a}}}})^{2}=4\langle J_{y,{\rm{a}}}^{2}\rangle=\frac{N_{\rm{a}}[(N_{\rm{a}}+1)N-2]}{2(N-1)},

(53a)

\displaystyle{F}_{y,{\rm{b}}}

\displaystyle=4(\Delta{J_{y,{\rm{b}}}})^{2}=4\langle J_{y,{\rm{b}}}^{2}\rangle=\frac{N_{\rm{b}}[(N_{\rm{b}}+1)N-2]}{2(N-1)},

(53b)

\displaystyle{F}_{y,{\rm{a}}{\rm{b}}}

\displaystyle=4\Cov[J_{y,{\rm{a}}},J_{y,{\rm{b}}}]=4\langle J_{y,{\rm{a}}}J_{y,{\rm{b}}}\rangle=-\frac{N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}N}{2(N-1)},

(53c)

where
\Cov[A,B]=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{A,B\}\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle
.
Then, the optimal precision bound for
b_{1}
is obtained as

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\max}=\frac{2N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}(N^{2}-4)}{N[N-2+(N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}})^{2}]}.

(54)

For an evenly split state, we have

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\max}=\frac{N(N+2)}{2}

(55)

as expected from Eq. (
25b
).

Observation 2
.

For a fixed number of particles
N
, the best metrological performance is attained if the ensemble is split evenly.
Let us consider states with not evenly split subsystems, keeping in mind that
N
must be even for the
|\widetilde{{\rm D}}_{N}\rangle
state.
We find that adding particles by pairs to one of the wells decreases the achievable precision (
54
).

Proof.
The first statement can be proved, since substituting
N_{\rm{b}}
by
N-N_{\rm{a}}
in Eq. (
54
), we find its maximum at
N_{\rm{a}}=N/2
.
For the second statement, we need to
substitute
N_{\rm{a}}
by
N/2
,
N_{\rm{b}}
by
N/2+n
, and
N
by
N+n.
Hence, we obtain

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\max}=\frac{N(N+2n)[(N+n)^{2}-4]}{2(N+n)(N-2+n+n^{2})},

(56)

where
N
is the original particle number and
n
are the particles added to one of the wells.
For
n=2
this function is smaller than Eq. (
54
) and it monotonically decreases with an increasing

For
n\gg 1
, we have that
(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\max}=N
even though the total particle number is now
N+n
.
∎

Finally, we find that for the product of two Dicke states, the achievable precision is
(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\max}=N/2(N/2+2),
which is just twice the achievable precision obtained in one of the wells for sensing the homogeneous field
Apellaniz et al. 2018

55

We have concluded the study of the partially flipped Dicke state split into two parts.

V
Measurement operators for partially flipped Dicke state

In this section, we will look for an operator
\mathcal{M}
to estimate the gradient parameter
b_{1}
that is not affected by the unitary dynamics

\exp[-ib_{0}(J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{y,{\rm{b}}})]

(57)

generated by the homogeneous field parameter
b_{0}
, c. f. Eq. (
17
).

Thus, we impose the condition

[J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},\mathcal{M}]=0.

(58)

Based on the error-propagation formula, the achievable precision is

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}=\frac{|\partial_{b_{1}}\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{\varrho}|^{2}}{(\Delta{\mathcal{M}})^{2}_{\varrho}}=\frac{|\langle[J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},\mathcal{M}]\rangle_{\varrho}|^{2}}{(\Delta{\mathcal{M}})^{2}_{\varrho}}.

(59)

Note that it would sufficient to require

\langle[J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},\mathcal{M}]\rangle=0,

(60)

for partially flipped Dicke states. However, even with the more stringent condition in Eq. (
58
) we will obtain an optimal operator,
the calculations will be simpler, and our results will be valid for any quantum state.

V.1
Optimal measurement operator

The procedure to find an optimal operator is as follows.
Let us define for a given
\varrho
(in this case the flipped Dicke state) a set of
K
operators

\{M_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}

(61)

that fulfill the conditions

\displaystyle[J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},M_{k}]

\displaystyle=0,

(62a)

\displaystyle(\Delta{M_{k}})^{2}_{\varrho}

\displaystyle\neq 0.

(62b)

We require that the gradient phase-shift generator can be written as
J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{y,{\rm{b}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}M_{k}
,
and the optimal measurement operator as
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}M_{k}
, where
{\bf n}
and
{\bf m}
are
K
-element real vectors.

Then, based on Ref.
Gessner et al. 2019
, the optimal precision is obtained as

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm opt}=\bm{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{C}\bm{n},

(63)

and the optimal measurement is characterized by

\bm{m}=\alpha\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{C}\bm{n},

(64)

where
\bm{C}_{k,l}=-i\langle[M_{k},M_{l}]\rangle_{\varrho}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{k,l}=\Cov_{\varrho}(H_{k},H_{l})
.

We start by defining the set of
M_{k}
operators. Let us consider only the symmetric subspace in the two subsystems and the operators acting in this subspace, as such a basis will be sufficient
to find the optimal operators. They live in the space determined by the basis states

|{\rm D}_{N_{{\rm{a}}}}^{(m_{\rm{a}})}\rangle_{y}\otimes|{\rm D}_{N_{{\rm{b}}}}^{(m_{\rm{b}})}\rangle_{y},

(65)

where
m_{{\rm{a}}}=0,1,...,N_{{\rm{a}}},

m_{{\rm{b}}}=0,1,...,N_{{\rm{b}}},
and the Dicke states of
N
particles with
m
excitations is defined as

|{\rm D}_{N}^{(m)}\rangle=\binom{N}{N/2}^{-1/2}\sum_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}(|0\rangle^{\otimes(N-m)}|1\rangle^{\otimes m}),

(66)

and the
y
subscript indicates that the Dicke states are in the
y
-basis, rather than in the
z
-basis. Equivalently, we can map the problem to determine the operators acting on two particles, as discussed in Appendix
A
. The operators must be given in the

|j_{y,{\rm{a}}}\rangle_{y}\otimes|j_{y,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{y}

(67)

basis, where
|j_{y,{\rm{a}}}\rangle_{y}
are eigenstates of the
J_{y,{\rm{a}}}
operator with eigenvalue
j_{y,{\rm{a}}},
and they are given in the
y
-basis. The states
|j_{y,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{y}
are defined analgously.

Since all operators must commute with
J_{y}\equiv J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{y,{\rm{b}}}
as expressed by Eq. (
62a
), they have a block diagonal structure in the basis we use.
The dimension of the state we have to consider grows rapidly with the size of the system.
This implies that the number of operators one has to consider scales with
\mathcal{O}(N_{\min}^{2}N_{\max}N)
, see Appendix
D
.
On the other hand, we can consider the generator of the gradient phase-shift,
M_{0}=J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},
as an additional operator for the set Eq. (
61
).
With this choice
\bm{n}=\delta_{0,k}\equiv(1,0,0,...,0)^{T},
in which case, the calculation of Eq. (
63
) simplifies and only a single column of the matrix
\bm{C}_{k,l}
needs to be computed as described in Ref.
Gessner et al. 2019
.
However, the method still works for relatively small systems only.

Our method makes it possible to obtain explicitly the optimal operator saturating the Cramér-Rao bound (
54
) for not too large system sizes, see Figure
2
.
These results suggest that the Cramér-Rao bound can be saturated with such operators, even for large systems.

V.2
Measurement operator based on moments of local angular momentum operators

We analyze measurement operators as function of the local angular momentum operators
J_{x,{\rm{a}}}
,
J_{x,{\rm{b}}}
,
J_{z,{\rm{a}}}
and
J_{z,{\rm{b}}}
in order to make the estimation task more accessible for experiments.
Among all measurements that are insensitive to the homogeneous field, we choose those that maximize the error-propagation formula (

) for

.

We identify one of such operators as

\mathcal{M}=J_{z,{\rm{a}}}J_{x,{\rm{b}}}-J_{x,{\rm{a}}}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}.

(68)

One can compute the derivative of the operator
\mathcal{M}
with respect to
b_{1}
at

\begin{split}|\partial_{b_{1}}\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{\varrho}|^{2}&=|\langle[J_{y,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{y,{\rm{b}}},\mathcal{M}]\rangle_{\varrho}|^{2}\\

(69)

for the partially flipped Dicke state, see Appendix
B
for more details.

(\Delta{\mathcal{M}})^{2}
, one can calculate first the expectation value of the first moment,

\langle\mathcal{M}\rangle_{\varrho}=\langle J_{z,{\rm{a}}}J_{x,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{\varrho}-\langle J_{x,{\rm{a}}}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{\varrho}=0

(70)

which is zero, due to that for partially flipped Dicke states given in Eq. (
47
) the eigenvalues of the constituents

add up to zero.

|m\rangle_{\rm{a}}|{-}m\rangle_{\rm{b}}
are eigenstates of
J_{z,{\rm{a}}}
and
J_{z,{\rm{b}}}
, and when acting on them with
J_{x,{\rm{a}}}
or
J_{x,{\rm{b}}},
the result is a linear combination of
|m{\pm}1\rangle_{\rm{a}}|{-}m\rangle_{\rm{b}}
or
|m\rangle_{\rm{a}}|{-}m{\mp}1\rangle_{\rm{b}},
.
Hence, the state is orthogonal to the initial state.
Therefore, the result of Equation (
70
) must be zero.
Moreover, all expectation values with an odd number of
J_{l,r}
operators are zero for
l=x,y.

Finally, the variance is equal to the second moment of the
\mathcal{M}
operator,

(\Delta{\mathcal{M}})^{2}_{\varrho}=\langle\mathcal{M}^{2}\rangle_{\varrho}=\langle J_{z,{\rm{a}}}^{2}J_{x,{\rm{b}}}^{2}\rangle_{\varrho}-\hbox{}\\
-\langle J_{z,{\rm{a}}}J_{x,{\rm{a}}}J_{x,{\rm{b}}}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{\varrho}-\langle J_{x,{\rm{a}}}J_{z,{\rm{a}}}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}J_{x,{\rm{b}}}\rangle_{\varrho}+\langle J_{x,{\rm{a}}}^{2}J_{z,{\rm{b}}}^{2}\rangle_{\varrho}=\\
=\frac{N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}N(3N-10+(N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}})^{2})}{32(N-3)(N-1)}.

(71)

See Appendix
B
for more details on the computation of the expectation values.

Thus, the error-propagation formula ("epf") for
\mathcal{M}
and over the flipped Dicke states is

(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}=\frac{2N_{\rm{a}}N_{\rm{b}}(N-3)(N+2)^{2}}{N(N-1)(3N-10+(N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}})^{2})}.

(72)

The relative precision compared with the achievable bound (
54
) is

\frac{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}}{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm max}}=\frac{(N+2)(N-3)(N-2+(N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}})^{2})}{(N-1)(N-2)(3N-10+(N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}})^{2})},

(73)

which for equal splitting becomes

\frac{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}}{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm max}}=\frac{(N+2)(N-3)}{(N-1)(3N-10)}.

(74)

In the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit we obtain

\frac{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}}{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm max}}\approx\frac{1}{3}.

(75)

Splitting the system into two different wells may introduce an imbalance that depends on the partitioning procedure.
To model the partition noise we consider that
N_{\rm{a}}=N/2+\delta
and
N_{\rm{a}}=N/2-\delta
.
The variance for
\delta
, the particle imbalance, is commonly modeled by
(\Delta\delta)^{2}=N/4

Vitagliano et al. 2023
, hence, we consider
(N_{\rm{a}}-N_{\rm{b}})\propto\sqrt{N}
.
In Eq. (
73
), the term
(N_{\rm{a}}-N_{\rm{b}})^{2}
can be substituted by
\alpha N
, where the parameter
\alpha
quantifies the partition noise.
We obtain

\frac{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}}{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm max}}=\frac{(N+2)(N-3)(N-2+\alpha N)}{(N-1)(N-2)(3N-10+\alpha N)},

(76)

which in the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit becomes

\frac{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm epf}}{(\Delta{b_{1}})^{-2}_{\rm max}}\approx\frac{1+\alpha}{3+\alpha}.

(77)

For an increasing
\alpha
, the right-hand side of Eq. (
77
) increases. E.g., for
\alpha=1,
the right-hand side of Eq. (
77
) is
1/2
. As
\alpha
increases the relative difference is reduced between the error obtained in Eq. (
72
) and the Cramér-Rao bound (
54
). Thus, gradient estimation by measuring Eq. (
68
) is robust against particle number imbalance, which indicates that our method can be used in experiments.

VI
Conclusions

We studied gradient metrology with quantum states in a two-sell system. It is known that we have a Bose-Einstein condensate of two-states atoms, then the precision of gradient metrology has a shot-noise scaling with the particle number. We show that if one applies a simple single-particle operations in one of the wells, then gradient metrology is possible a maximal precision corresponding to a Heisenberg scaling. We consider measuring the gradient and the homogeneous magnetic field along
x
,
y,
or
z
directions, and study the inequalities describing the trade-off between these metrological tasks.

Appendix A
Proof of Observation
1

Proof.
Our system is split into two wells. Let us first assume that the states are symmetric in each of the wells.
In this case, we can map the quantum state of the particles in the two wells into the quantum state of two spins with
j_{\rm{a}}=N_{\rm{a}}/2
and
j_{\rm{b}}=N_{\rm{b}}/2
.
Hence, from angular momentum theory, the total angular momentum spin number can have the following values

j=|j_{\rm{a}}{-}j_{\rm{b}}|,\dots,j_{\rm{a}}{+}j_{\rm{b}}.

(78)

The minimum and maximum eigenvalues of the total angular momentum operator are

\begin{split}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{J}^{2})&=\frac{|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|(|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|+2)}{4},\\
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{J}^{2})&=\frac{N(N+2)}{4},\end{split}

(79)

where
\lambda_{\min}(X)
and
\lambda_{\max}(X)
denote the smallest and largest eigenvalue of
X,
respectively.
If we expand the square of the total angular momentum with the angular momentum components of the two ensembles ‘a’ and ‘b’, we obtain

\begin{split}\bm{J}^{2}&=\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{+}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}=\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}^{2}+J_{l,{\rm{b}}}^{2}+2J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})\\
&=\frac{N_{\rm{a}}(N_{\rm{a}}+2)}{4}+\frac{N_{\rm{b}}(N_{\rm{b}}+2)}{4}+2\sum_{l=x,y,z}J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}.\end{split}

(80)

Thus, finding the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\bm{J}^{2}
means maximizing and minimizing, respectively, the eigenvalues of the sum of the correlations
\langle J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}\rangle
.
States maximizing
\langle\bm{J}^{2}\rangle
are living in the
j=(N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}})/2
subspace, while states minimizing
\langle\bm{J}^{2}\rangle
are living in the
j=|N_{\rm{a}}-N_{\rm{b}}|/2
subspace.

Hence, we can find that the largest eigenvalue for

\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\\
=\frac{N_{\rm{a}}(N_{\rm{a}}+2)}{4}+\frac{N_{\rm{b}}(N_{\rm{b}}+2)}{4}-2\sum_{l=x,y,z}J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}

(81)

finding the smallest eigenvalue for the sum of the correlations
{J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}}
.
We conclude that the states with the smallest eigenvalue for
\bm{J}^{2}
maximize the eigenvalue of the operator defined in Eq. (
81
).
Thus, from Eq. (
80
) and for such states,

\Big\langle 2\sum_{l=x,y,z}J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}\Big\rangle\\
=\frac{|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|(|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|+2)}{4}-\frac{N_{\rm{a}}(N_{\rm{a}}+2)}{4}-\frac{N_{\rm{b}}(N_{\rm{b}}+2)}{4}.

(82)

Hence,

\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\Big)\\
=\frac{N_{\rm{a}}(N_{\rm{a}}+2)}{2}+\frac{N_{\rm{b}}(N_{\rm{b}}+2)}{2}-\frac{|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|(|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|+2)}{4}.

(83)

Using that
|N_{\rm{a}}{-}N_{\rm{b}}|=N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}-2\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})
and
N=N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}
, we arrive at

\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\Big)=\frac{N(N+2)}{4}+\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}}).

(84)

Thus, maximizing and minimizing
\langle\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\rangle
means minimizing and maximizing, respectively, the sum of the correlations
\langle J_{l,{\rm{a}}}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}\rangle
.
Clearly, states maximizing
\langle\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\rangle
are living in the
j=|N_{\rm{a}}-N_{\rm{b}}|/2
subspace, while states minimizing it are living in the
j=(N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}})/2
subspace.

Let us now consider the case that the state of the particles in the two wells are not in the symmetric subspace. Let us consider the case when the state is in the eigenspace of

J_{x,l}^{2}+J_{y,l}^{2}+J_{z,l}^{2}

(85)

with an eigenvalue
j_{l}(j_{l}+1)
for
l=a,b
and
j_{l}
can be smaller than
N_{l}.
Then, analogously to Eq. (
84
), we obtain that

\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\Big)\\
=(j_{\rm{a}}+j_{\rm{b}})(j_{\rm{a}}+j_{\rm{b}}+2)+2\min(j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}),

(86)

which is smaller than or equal to the right-hand side of Eq. (
84
) giving the upper bound for
j_{\rm{a}}=N_{\rm{a}}/2
and
j_{\rm{b}}=N_{\rm{b}}/2.
Thus, the largest eigenvalue is maximal if
j_{\rm{a}}=N_{\rm{a}}/2
and
j_{\rm{b}}=N_{\rm{b}}/2.

Let us now consider a general quantum state
\varrho
of the two wells.
Let us define

\varrho_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}}=P_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}}\varrho P_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}},

(87)

where
P_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}}
projects to the eigenspace of Eq. (
85
) with an eigenvalue
j_{l}(j_{l}+1)
for
l={\rm{a}},{\rm{b}}.

Then, it is easy to see that with our angular momentum measurements our state is not distinguishable from the state

\varrho=\sum_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}}p_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}}\varrho_{j_{\rm{a}},j_{\rm{b}}},

(88)

thus it is sufficient to consider quantum states of the form given in Eq. (
88
).
Clearly, we obtain the bound

\Big\langle\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\Big\rangle\leq\frac{N(N+2)}{4}+\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}}),

(89)

where the bound can be saturated by some state. Thus, in order to obtain the bound, it was sufficient to consider the case when the state of each well is symmetric.

Finally, we have that

\sum_{l=x,y,z}{{F}}[\varrho,J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}}]\leqslant 4\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{l=x,y,z}(J_{l,{\rm{a}}}{-}J_{l,{\rm{b}}})^{2}\Big).

(90)

Hence, Eq. (
49
) follows.

K^{*}_{N_{\min},N_{\max}}=1+N_{\min}+\frac{N_{\min}(1-N_{\min}^{2})}{3}+(1+N_{\min})^{2}N_{\max}

(114)

where
N_{\min}\equiv\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})
and
N_{\max}\equiv\max(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})
.
If we impose that
(\Delta{M_{k}})^{2}_{\varrho}=0
for Dicke states given in Eq. (
43
), the number is smaller

K_{N_{\min},N_{\max}}=\frac{K^{*}_{N_{\min},N_{\max}}+N_{\min}+1}{2},

(115)

Proof.
We will consider only the symmetric states in the two subspaces a and b in order to reduce the number of operators.
As in Eq. (
78
), the whole Hilbert space can be considered as adding
j_{\rm{a}}=N_{\rm{a}}/2
and
j_{\rm{b}}=N_{\rm{b}}/2
angular momentum subspaces. Thus, the dimension of the space is
(N_{\rm{a}}+1)(N_{\rm{b}}+1).
Equivalently, from quantum angular momentum theory, the dimension is

\sum_{j=j_{\min}}^{j_{\max}}2j+1
, where there are

j_{\max}-j_{\min}+1=\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})+1
independent total angular momentum subspaces and
j_{\max}=j_{\rm{a}}+j_{\rm{b}}
and
j_{\min}=|j_{\rm{a}}-j_{\rm{b}}|
.

It is easy to see that the degeneracy of the eigenvalues of
J_{y}
follow

d(m_{y})=\begin{cases}\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})+1&\textnormal{if $|m_{y}|\leqslant j_{\min}$,}\\
\frac{N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}}{2}+1-|m_{y}|&\textnormal{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(116)

Hence, in a degenerate subspace there are
d(m_{y})^{2}
independent Hermitian operators which commute with the original operator,
d(m_{y})
diagonal terms and
d(m_{y})[d(m_{y})-1]
real and complex off-diagonal terms.

Thus, the total number of independent Hermitian operators is

K^{*}=\sum_{m_{y}=-j_{\min}}^{j_{\min}}[\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})+1]^{2}+\\
+2\sum_{m_{y}=j_{\min}+1}^{j_{\max}}\left(\tfrac{N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}}{2}+1-m_{z}\right)^{2},

(117)

where the factor of two appears in front of the the second sum taking into account a summation for
m_{y}=-j_{\max},\dots,-(j_{\min}+1)
. Finally, using that

2|j_{\rm{a}}-j_{\rm{b}}|=\max(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})-\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}}),

(118)

we arrive at

K^{*}=\frac{1+\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})}{3}\bigl\{3+\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})-\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})^{2}+\\
+3\max(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})[1+\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})]\bigr\},

(119)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
114
).

Our system consisting of a partially flipped Dicke state given in Eq.(
47
), has non-zero overlap with eigenstates of eigenvalues
m_{y}=j_{\max},j_{\max}-2,\dots,-j_{\max}+2,-j_{\max},
which is the case also for the Dicke state.
In that case, only Hermitian operators that act on such subspace may have
(\Delta{M_{k}})^{2}_{\varrho}\,{\neq}\,0
.
Therefore, the sum in Eq. (
117
) reduces to

K^{*}=\sum_{\mu_{y}=-j_{\min}/2}^{j_{\min}/2}(\min(N_{\rm{a}},N_{\rm{b}})+1)^{2}+\\
+2\sum_{\mu_{y}=j_{\min}/2+1}^{j_{\max}/2}(\tfrac{N_{\rm{a}}+N_{\rm{b}}}{2}+1-2\mu_{z})^{2},

(120)

where we introduced a new summation variable
\mu_{y}=2m_{y}
and changed the limits accordingly.
Equation (
115
) follows.

N_{\min}

Hence, the amount of computations required for not so-large systems increases rapidly.
