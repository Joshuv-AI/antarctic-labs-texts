Trait-Like and State-Like Oxytocin: A Biomarker of Alliance in Patients with Depression and Borderline Personality Disorder
Authors: Galit Peysachov, Amit Tchizick, Jaime Delgadillo, Simone Shamay-Tsoory, Sigal Zilcha-Mano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and borderline personality disorder (BPD) show a poorer prognosis compared to patients without BPD. A potential contributor is the complexity of establishing a therapeutic alliance with patients with BPD. A potential underlying biological mechanism is oxytocin (OT), a hormone associated with social bonding and trust. The aim of this study was to assess whether the interaction between OT pre-session (session-level trait-like) and change in OT throughout the session (state-like) in both patients and therapists predicts the therapeutic alliance in patients with MDD and varying severity levels of BPD. We hypothesized that patients with higher BPD severity would show a compensatory pattern, where an increase in state-like OT would compensate for low session-level trait-like OT, resulting in a stronger alliance. We also explored this interplay in the therapists’ OT.MethodEighty-four patients from a randomized controlled trial were assessed for BPD severity prior to beginning 16 sessions of short-term psychotherapy. Both patients and therapists provided saliva samples pre- and post- sessions 4, 8, 12, and 16 for OT analysis. Alliance was measured post-session. A series of linear mixed-effects models was used to test the hypothesis.ResultsOur hypothesis was partially confirmed. In patients with higher BPD severity, when therapists had low session-level trait-like OT, the more they increased in state-like OT, patients rated their alliance as stronger. However, this compensatory pattern did not emerge in patients' OT.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate the potential importance of OT as a biomarker of therapeutic processes in patients with MDD and BPD.

Introduction

Psychotherapy for major depressive disorder (MDD) is only effective for about half of patients [1, 2], and the response to treatment is further hindered in the presence of comorbid borderline personality disorder (BPD) [3]. BPD is characterized by severe interpersonal difficulties [4] that pose unique challenges to the formation of a good enough therapeutic alliance [5–7] – which is consistently identified as the most robust predictor of treatment outcomes across psychotherapy modalities [8].

Oxytocin (OT), a neuropeptide implicated in social bonding and trust [9], is increasingly recognized as a biological marker of both interpersonal functioning [10] and alliance quality in psychotherapy [11]. Individuals with BPD typically exhibit lower OT levels compared to healthy controls and an atypical or a blunted OT response to interpersonal stimuli [12–19]. These findings suggest that OT dysregulation may have the potential to underlie the interpersonal difficulties observed in BPD and to serve as a biological marker of alliance formation and change in psychotherapy [20].

Recent conceptual advances emphasize the need to differentiate between trait-like and state-like components of any proposed mechanism of therapeutic change [21]. Trait-like OT can refer to one of the following: a general trait-like of the individual, such as pre-treatment baseline levels, or a more local trait-like OT, which refers to the trait-like OT at a more localized point in time, such as pre-session (session-level trait-like OT). It is especially important to track a session-level trait-like OT during psychotherapy treatment, which captures the individual’s characteristic capacities and capabilities to adapt to a specific stage of the treatment. In contrast, state-like OT refers to fluctuations that occur within-session relative to the local trait-like OT, such as changes from pre- to post-session [22, 23]. Such components play different roles in psychotherapy across a variety of mechanisms [21, 23–25], including the alliance [26, 27]. It has been further demonstrated that the interaction between these two components has the potential to shed light on individual-specific mechanisms of change [28]. For example, it has been shown that in the case of rumination, patients with higher levels of trait-like rumination benefited most from greater reductions in state-like rumination throughout treatment, as was seen in a larger reduction of symptoms [28]. Exploring the interaction between trait-like and state-like OT could identify for whom and when (based on the individual’s characteristic OT level) OT increases may support therapeutic change [29].

Given the centrality of the therapeutic alliance in psychological treatment outcomes [8], especially among patients with BPD, and the role of OT in social connection, this study investigates how both session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT levels – in patients and therapists – relate to alliance quality. Building on prior work, we propose that OT could serve as a biological marker of alliance formation, particularly among patients with MDD and higher severity of BPD. To date, few studies have directly examined OT in relation to the therapeutic alliance in patients with BPD, and none have systematically explored the interaction between trait-like and state-like OT. One recent study found that patients with BPD exhibited low trait-like OT compared to healthy controls and demonstrated state-like increases during treatment, suggesting a potential normalization effect [30]. However, the role of the interaction between these components remains untested. Moreover, OT levels in therapists may also play a critical role. For example, a study found that therapists’ OT levels increased in response to patients’ distress, suggesting that state-like OT in therapists may reflect emotional attunement [31].

The present study seeks to address the gap in the literature by examining how the interaction between session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT in both patients and therapists predicts therapeutic alliance in patients with MDD and BPD symptoms, in MDD treatment. In formulating our hypotheses, we draw on theoretical models of personalized mechanisms of change – capitalization versus compensation [28]. The compensatory model predicts that individuals with low trait-like OT will benefit more from state-like increases, compensating for a potential interpersonal vulnerability, reflected by poorer trait-like levels of OT characterizing the individual before the beginning of the session. In contrast, the capitalization model posits that individuals with high trait-like OT will gain more from further increases, capitalizing on existing strengths. Based on studies showing that patients with BPD characteristically show low trait-like OT and a blunted response of state-like OT [12–19], we hypothesized a compensatory pattern would emerge. Meaning, in patients with higher severity of BPD symptoms, when they arrive at treatment with low session-level trait-like OT, the more the patients experience an increase in state-like OT, the higher the patients will evaluate the therapeutic alliance and benefit from it. The corresponding hypothesis for the therapists’ OT was exploratory, as empirical findings in this field of research remain scarce. This study tests these patterns in the context of therapeutic alliance formation, offering new insights into OT’s role as a potential mechanism of change in treatment for MDD patients with BPD symptoms. The data for the current study are drawn from a completed randomized controlled trial (RCT) providing short-term psychotherapy treatment to patients with MDD, including patients with varying severity levels of BPD symptoms [32–34].

Methods

Study Design

A total of 118 patients were included in the pilot and active phases of a completed RCT providing psychotherapy treatment for MDD [33, 34] As part of this RCT, both patients and therapists provided saliva samples to measure OT prior to and post each treatment session (for further details in the measures section below). The protocol for collecting OT through saliva samples from the patients was implemented after the beginning of the RCT, therefore 21 patients were excluded from the sample for the present analysis. An additional 13 patients were excluded due to their treatment being held remotely during the COVID-19 pandemic from the beginning of treatment to the end, preventing saliva samples from being collected. This resulted in a sample of N = 84 eligible cases for inclusion in the current study.

The patients were recruited through advertisements offering 16 (50-min) sessions of free treatment for depression. The RCT compared supportive (ST) and supportive-expressive psychotherapy treatment (SET) [35, 36]. All participants signed informed consent forms to participate in the RCT.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) meeting MDD diagnostic criteria based on structured clinical interviews for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [4], scores above 14 on the 17-item Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HRSD) [37] at two evaluations, 1 week apart, and current MDD based on the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview [38]; (b) if the patients were on medication, their dosage had to be stable for at least 3 months before entering the study, and they had to be willing to maintain a stable dosage for the duration of the treatment; (c) age between 18 and 60 years; (d) fluency in Hebrew; and (e) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) current risk of suicide or self-harm (HRSD suicide item score >2); (b) current substance abuse disorder; (c) current or past schizophrenia or psychosis, bipolar disorder, or severe eating disorder requiring medical monitoring; (d) history of organic mental disease; or (e) currently in psychotherapy. During enrollment to the RCT, patients with various comorbidities were included, such as personality disorders, to represent the typical population of individuals with MDD. Psychotherapy was found to be effective for patients with MDD only, and with MDD and comorbid disorders [39].

Participants

Patients

Eighty-four patients were included in the current study sample. Their mean age was 31.04 years (SD = 8.70), and 61.9% were female. Their mean level of education was 14.49 years (SD = 2.10); 50% reported that their monthly family income was below the average monthly income per family (about USD 4,212), 22.6% reported it was average, 23.8% reported it was above average, and 3.6% did not report income; 65.5% were employed, 32.1% were unemployed, and 2.4% did not report employment. Of the participants, 83.3% were single, 13.1% married, 2.4% were divorced or separated, and 1.2% were widowed. 81% were Jewish, 1.2% were Christian, 8.3% were Muslim, 6% were “other,” 1.2% were Druze, and 2.4% did not report their religion. In addition to MDD, 75% (N = 63) of the patients were diagnosed with one or more personality disorders, with comorbidity rates as follows: avoidant (N = 34, 40.5%), obsessive-compulsive (N = 32, 38.1%), dependent (N = 12, 14.3%), paranoid (N = 9, 10.7%), narcissistic (N = 11, 13.1%), borderline (N = 8, 9.5%), schizoid (N = 7, 8.3%), histrionic (N = 4, 4.8%), and schizotypal (N = 1, 1.2%).

Therapists

Eight therapists with formal training in psychodynamic treatment and at least 5 years of experience in its practice were part of the pilot and active phases of the RCT. The therapists attended a 20-h training workshop in supportive and expressive techniques before seeing patients. All therapists completed treatment of 2 pilot patients, one of each treatment condition, and had to demonstrate sufficient adherence before moving to the active phase. During the pilot and active phases of the RCT, the therapists received weekly personal and group supervision provided by two experienced licensed clinical psychologists, who were supervised by an international expert in SET. Two of the therapists did not continue after the training phase, one due to accepting a full-time position elsewhere and another due to demonstrating low levels of adherence. This resulted in six therapists who were included in the study. To account for a potential therapist effect, therapists provided treatment in both conditions to act as their own controls and avoid nesting of therapists within treatment conditions, which may result in unwanted confounding.

The mean age of the six therapists was 42.33 years (SD = 4.41), and five of them were female. All six therapists were married or cohabitating. Their mean years of experience was 14.41 (SD = 5.42). All therapists had psychodynamic training, two also had CBT training, and one also had biofeedback training. In the current study, the mean number of patients each therapist treated was 14 (SD = 6.20), range = 7–23.

Treatment

Patients received 16, weekly 50-min sessions of SET, a time-limited psychodynamic treatment adapted for depression, either in a expressive-focused condition (SET) or in a supportive-focused condition (ST). Patients were randomized into one of the two treatment conditions and assigned to one therapist. Assignment to the treatment condition was conducted by an external institution, not involved in the study. Comprehensive treatment protocols were used for SET: the Luborsky manualized treatment [35, 36]. The ST condition included all supportive techniques detailed in the manual, excluding the expressive techniques [40]. Supportive techniques refer to techniques used to promote a healthy therapeutic relationship, and expressive techniques refer to techniques used to improve the patients’ self-understanding through interpretations of interpersonal themes [35]. The adherence of therapists to the treatment manuals was assessed using the Penn Adherence-Competence Scale (PACS) [41]. The results of the PACS coding were as expected [33].

Measures

Diagnosis of BPD

BPD was assessed using the Structured Interview for the Diagnosis of Personality Disorders [42]. This instrument is a comprehensive, semi-structured clinical interview for the assessment of DSM-IV Axis II diagnoses. The SIDP-IV includes neutral questions organized into topical sections to produce a natural flow in the interview. The number of criteria for each personality disorder in the DSM-IV varies from 7 to 9, while in BPD specifically, there are 9 criteria. Criteria were rated as follows: 0 = absent, 1 = sub-threshold, 2 = present, 3 = strongly present. A score of 2 or more on at least 3–5 criteria (depending on the personality disorder in question) is required for a diagnosis of the identified personality disorder using a dichotomous measure. The required criteria for the diagnosis of BPD using the dichotomous measure are to meet at least 5 criteria out of the 9 total possible criteria. The instructions for the SIDP-IV specify a scoring rule according to which behavior typical of the past 5 years represents the basis for the ratings. SIDP-IV interviewers were master’s-level or doctoral-level clinical psychologists who received extensive training and supervision in the administration of the SIDP-IV. Personality disorders were examined both as a dichotomous and a dimensional variable [43]. Consistent with compelling arguments made for the assessment and study of BPD as a continuous dimension [44–48], in the current study, we operationalized BPD as a continuous measure for the main analyses. It has been shown in previous studies that, for the measurement of BPD in particular, a continuous measure is most informative for patients who do not reach the dichotomous criteria [49] and is more reliable than a dichotomous measurement [50]. For sensitivity analysis, we repeated the main analyses with BPD as a dichotomous measure. In the current study, baseline measurements of BPD were included in the analysis. The inter-rater reliability of the BPD scale for the whole RCT was assessed using intraclass correlation, yielding a value of 0.88.

Oxytocin

Saliva samples were collected prior to and post sessions 4, 8, 12, and 16, from both patients and therapists. Patients and therapists were asked to refrain from eating, drinking (other than water), smoking, and having intimate contact with others (for example, hugging) for at least 30 min before the saliva sample collection procedure. Samples were collected at a fixed time of the day from each dyad. Patients and therapists were asked to place cotton swabs in their mouths, provided in Sarstedt Salivette® (Nümbrecht, Germany) containers, for 2 min. They were then asked to report on factors possibly affecting OT levels in an online questionnaire (e.g., medication use, alcohol or drug consumption, menstrual cycle phase, and contraceptive use). Saliva samples were stored at −20°C until analysis. Samples were delivered in dry ice, under temperature-controlled maintenance, to the daacro GmbH & Co. KG Lab, University of Trier, Trier, Germany, where they were thawed, vortexed, and analyzed in duplicates to assure the reliability of results. Sample intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variance (CV) were calculated. Inter-assay CVs of <15% are generally acceptable [51]. Our sample yielded mean inter-assay CVs of 3.4%. While there are several different approaches to measuring OT, such as plasma, for the current study, saliva samples were chosen due to being a less intrusive method of OT measurement [11].

Alliance

The therapeutic alliance was assessed using the self-report Working Alliance Inventory (WAI) [52, 53]. The WAI consists of 12 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). The WAI was completed by patients following each session, immediately after patients provided post-session saliva samples, and a mean score was calculated for each session throughout treatment. For the current study, only alliance ratings, which were collected following sessions that included OT measurements (sessions 4, 8, 12, and 16), were included in the analysis. The internal reliability for WAI throughout the treatment in the whole RCT was 0.93.

Statistical Analysis

Due to the nested structure of the data (sessions nested within patients, nested within therapists), we conducted a series of linear mixed-effects models using the lme4 package in R. The dependent variable in these models was the patient-rated post-session therapeutic alliance, measured as a time-series. To identify the best-fitting model for capturing the effects of time, we compared five models [54]: a null model (no time effect), a model with time as a fixed effect, a model with both fixed and random slopes for time, and two models using a log-transformed time variable (with and without a random slope). The rationale for using a log-transformed time function fits with wider evidence that a log-linear function is commonly the best-fitting trend for time-series data in psychotherapy studies [55]. As expected, based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the model including log-transformed time as a fixed effect provided the best fit to the data and was used in all subsequent analyses (for BIC scores see online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549729). Alternative covariance structures were fit (e.g., autoregressive) and the best-fitting structure (with log-transformed time as a fixed effect without assuming an autoregressive covariance structure) was retained in hypothesis-testing models [56]. The model retained showed that patient-rated alliance increased throughout treatment (for further details see online suppl. Table S2). We ran two models to test the study hypotheses examining the interaction between patients’ BPD severity at baseline, session-level trait-like OT, and state-like OT as a predictor of the alliance following the session, with separate models for therapists’ and patients’ OT measures. Session-level trait-like OT was defined as the OT level prior to session and state-like OT was defined as the change from pre- to post-session (for descriptive statistics see online suppl. Tables S3 and S4), no additional data transformations were implemented [27]. Correlation analysis demonstrated that session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT in the current study do not reflect the same construct. In patients’ OT, the correlation was negligible (r = 0.01) and in therapists’ OT, the correlation was small (r = −0.24).

Regarding missing data, in WAI, only 0.61% of data were missing. For patients, 7.64% of session-level trait-like OT and 8.26% of state-like OT were missing; 7.34% due to treatments being conducted remotely during COVID-19, and the remaining 0.3% and 0.92%, respectively, were missing due to technical issues. For therapists, 33.03% of session-level trait-like OT and 33.33% of state-like OT were missing; 25.38% were missing because collection of saliva samples from therapists was introduced into the protocol at a later point. 7.34% due to treatments being conducted remotely during COVID-19, and the remaining 0.31% and 0.61%, respectively, were missing due to technical issues. Because most of the missing data were due to external reasons and are less likely to be related to the patients and therapists included in the study, it can be assumed to be missing completely at random [57, 58].

Therapist Session-Level Trait-Like and State-Like Components of OT Predicting the Alliance, with BPD Severity as Moderator

The three-way interaction between therapist session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT and BPD severity was significant (b = −0.35, p = 0.044), and yielded an effect size of ηp2 = 0.03, which is considered to be between small and moderate [59]. In the following description of the three-way interaction, “low” and “high” levels refer to 1 SD below and above the mean, respectively. In patients with low BPD severity, when the therapist arrived at the session with a low session-level trait-like OT, the more the therapist increased in their state-like OT from pre- to post-session, the lower the patient rated their alliance following the session. When the therapist arrived at the session with a high session-level trait-like OT, the more the therapist increased in their state-like OT from pre- to post-session, the higher the patient rated their alliance following the session. In patients with high BPD severity, the opposite effect was found. When the therapist arrived at the session with a low session-level trait-like OT, the more the therapist increased in their state-like OT from pre- to post-session, the higher the patient rated their alliance following the session. When the therapist arrived at the session with a high session-level trait-like OT, the more the therapist increased in their state-like OT from pre- to post-session, the lower the patient rated their alliance following the session (for further details see Table 2; for a visualization of the predicted model see Fig. 1).

Findings were replicated when controlling for the patients’ age, marital status, gender, and treatment condition (for further details, see online suppl. Tables S7−S10). In addition, patient-rated alliance did not differ between treatment conditions (for further details, see online suppl. Table S11). Discussion

Although in the past decade many studies have emphasized the importance of investigating trait-like and state-like OT as the biological marker of the interpersonal difficulties and change of patients with and without BPD symptoms [20], little is known about the interaction of these components. The overarching goal of the current study was to investigate the interaction between characteristic session-level trait-like OT taken prior to the treatment session, and state-like OT as the change from pre- to post-session, as a biological marker of the therapeutic alliance in patients with MDD and BPD symptoms. The present study contrasted two fundamental treatment personalization frameworks in psychotherapy research – compensation and capitalization models [28]. The findings partially support the hypothesized compensatory pattern; however, only in the therapists’ session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT. No findings supporting either pattern emerged in regards to the patients’ session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT.

In patients with higher severity of BPD symptoms, therapists showed a compensatory pattern. Meaning, when therapists arrived at the session with low session-level trait-like OT, the more they increased in their OT throughout the session – the higher their patients rated their alliance immediately following the session. These findings are consistent with literature suggesting the conceptual interplay between trait-like and state-like components of compensation, when the state-like component can compensate for session-level trait-like deficits [28]. A possible post hoc explanation of the compensation pattern that emerged in the therapists can be the characteristic emotions and interpersonal challenges that emerge when treating patients with higher BPD severity. Clinical and theoretical literature suggests that therapists tend to feel more negative emotions [60–63] and face more interpersonal struggles, including a struggle to establish a strong therapeutic alliance, when treating patients with higher BPD severity [7, 64]. The low session-level trait-like OT may be a result of the therapists’ experience of these emotions and their anticipation of facing these difficulties when arriving at the sessions [10, 65–70], or as a result of an insecure attachment formed with these patients [71, 72]. The increase in state-like OT can be an indication of a positive process occurring in the relationship that later manifests in a better alliance as rated by the patient [11, 73]. This finding is one of the few studies that addressed session-level trait-like and state-like components of OT in the partner interacting with individuals with BPD, and their effects on these individuals. This is consistent with other studies showing the importance of taking into account the trait-like and state-like components of OT when trying to understand the experience of the patient [31]. The sensitivity analyses suggest that this finding is robust, as it was replicated when using the original model and a model accounting for an AR1 structure.

The interaction between session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT in the patients was not found in this study to be a biological marker of the therapeutic alliance, depending on the severity of BPD symptoms of the patients. This is the first study to investigate the interaction between session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT in patients with MDD and BPD symptoms. The findings are not consistent with previous research suggesting the importance of OT in psychotherapy for depression [74] and alliance quality [11]. However, the findings are consistent with previous research suggesting within-group differences that may play a role in the interplay of the trait-like and state-like OT in patients with BPD symptoms [18, 75]. For example, a study showed that while there was no difference in trait-like OT between patients with BPD and healthy controls, patients with BPD and disorganized attachment showed lower levels of trait-like OT compared to patients with BPD and an organized attachment, and to healthy controls [18]. Due to a small sample size, we were not able to test complex models of moderation. It would be important to assess directly the moderation effect of such baseline characteristics of the patients in future studies.

These findings may hold important clinical implications if replicated in future studies. These findings indicate that increasing state-like OT is important for therapists with low session-level trait-like OT, when treating patients with higher severity of BPD symptoms. For example, the repair of ruptures in the alliance could potentially exert this influence over state-like OT [11], in order to foster the establishment of a strong alliance. Therapists may benefit from psychoeducation targeted at raising their awareness of internal processes occurring within them during the session, which could provide them with more nuanced information about the current state of the interaction with the patient [76].

This study has several limitations, with the main limitation being a small sample size. This did not allow a model taking into account session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT both of the patient and the therapist together, the assessment of complex moderation models and the exploration of systematic moderators, such as marital status. Therefore, it would be very important to assess the replicability of the reported findings in larger samples. Another limitation that is common in the field of OT trait-like and state-like research, is that in order to track the repeatedly changing characteristic session-level trait-like OT throughout psychotherapy, we measured the OT level once before the beginning of the session [11, 77, 78]. This single local measurement of session-level trait-like OT limits our ability to discuss the broader implications of trait-like OT. To allow a more comprehensive disentanglement of session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT, future studies should include more than two time points per-session and other approaches to trait-like and state-like disentanglement [27]. It would also be important to replicate the findings using other approaches for OT measurement, aside from salivary samples [79]. Another limitation is that the current study is observational, as we focused only on measurement of OT, and not administration of OT. We acknowledge the importance of investigating the benefits of administering nasal OT to patients with higher BPD severity and to their therapists [20]. Administration of nasal OT could potentially facilitate a stronger alliance between therapists and patients with higher BPD severity, compensating for their characteristic interpersonal struggle. This in turn could lead to a better treatment outcome in patients with higher BPD severity when receiving psychotherapy treatment for MDD [74]. Therefore, we encourage future studies to incorporate administration of nasal OT using an experimental design to explore the effect of increasing state-like OT in the case of low trait-like OT, both of the patients and of their therapists, on their alliance throughout psychotherapy. Notwithstanding these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study seeking to investigate the interplay between session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT of the therapists, in patients with MDD and BPD symptoms and its effect on the alliance.

Conclusion

The overarching goal of this study was to investigate how the interaction between session-level trait-like OT (pre-session) and state-like OT (pre- to post-session change), either of the patients’ OT or of the therapists’ OT, predicts the therapeutic alliance evaluated by patients with MDD and varying severity of BPD symptoms. For patients’ OT, no significant interaction was found. For therapists’ OT, the findings suggest that a compensation pattern emerges when treating patients with higher BPD severity. Meaning, the patients benefited most in the alliance when their therapist arrived at the session with low session-level trait-like OT and compensated for it throughout the session with increases in state-like OT. When treating patients with low BPD severity, a capitalization pattern emerges, where the patients benefited most in the alliance when their therapist arrived at the session with high session-level trait-like OT and capitalized on it throughout the session with increases in state-like OT. These findings point to the importance of therapists being mindful of their session-level trait-like OT while approaching the session, as well as their change in state-like OT throughout the session, as these can be important indicators of the alliance formed with patients with MDD and BPD symptoms. Future studies should be conducted on larger samples to be able to explore potential moderators that may deepen our understanding for whom, when, and why the interaction between session-level trait-like OT and state-like OT can serve as an alliance marker.
