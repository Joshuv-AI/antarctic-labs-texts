Self-Image Multiplicity in a Concave Cylindrical Mirror
Thach A. Nguyen, Kaitlyn S. Yasumura, Duy V. Tran, Trung V. Phan

Abstract
Concave mirrors are fundamental optical elements, yet some easily observed behaviors are rarely addressed in standard textbooks, such as the formation of multiple reflected images. Here we investigate self-imaging – where the observer is also the observed object – using a concave cylindrical mirror. We predict the number of self-images visible from different observation points and classify space into regions by image count. We then test these predictions with an inexpensive stainless-steel concave cylindrical mirror commonly found in teaching labs. This activity links geometrical optics principles to direct observation and provides a ready-to-use classroom demonstration and student exercise.

II
Two-Dimensional Theoretical Analysis

II.1
On the Formation of Self-Image

Let us recall that a point-like observer sees a given self-image only if
both
of the following criteria are satisfied for that image:

•

Comeback condition:
there exists a ray that leaves the observer, reflects on the mirror (possibly multiple times), and returns to the same point (see Fig.
2
A1).

•

Visibility condition:
for that same ray, the infinitesimal
outgoing
bundle that accompanies it after reflection converges to a point located in front of the observer; this convergence point corresponds to the apparent position of the self-image (see Fig.
2
A2).

In other words, each self-image corresponds to a
comeback
ray that also satisfies the
visibility condition
; if either condition fails, that self-image does not appear. Let us define the
order

n
of a self-image as the number of reflections the corresponding light-ray undergoes before returning to the observer, then
n=1,2,3,...
(see Fig.
2
B1). Higher-order self-images (
n>1
) appear in pairs because of path reversibility (see Fig.
2
B2).

of the corresponding self-image.

The
comeback condition
can be analyzed in a
two-dimensional
concave mirror model, which makes the image-counting geometry transparent (as illustrated in Fig.
2
). The
visibility condition
, however, must be treated in
three dimensions
because a cylindrical mirror is
astigmatic
: horizontal (
transverse
) and vertical (
sagittal
) rays focus at different locations, so the small outgoing bundle may not form a single sharp focus in front of the eye
Hecht 2012
. To keep the explanation simple, we defer visibility, astigmatism, and their consequences for image sharpness to Appendix
B
. There we show that, because the
sagittal
focusing point is always visible, the
comeback condition
alone suffices for image counting.

II.2
Comeback Condition and Spatial Partition with Self-Image Count

In this Section, for each observer position, we identify every comeback ray, since each distinct comeback ray corresponds to one self-image. Using these counts, we partition space into regions distinguished by the number of visible images.

II.2.1
On-Axis Observer

If there exists a
n>1
-order light-ray path that starts from the observer S then returns to S after
n
reflections, we seek to determine an expression for the distance
r(n,\alpha)
between O and S, where
\alpha
is the arc half-angle between the first and last reflection points, i.e. R
1
and R
n
, respectively. Here, R
1
, R
2
, …, R
n
represent the reflection points of the light-ray path on the mirror (see Fig.
3
B1). The arc angle
\gamma
between successive reflection points, i.e. R
j
and R
j+1
, can be calculated with
2\alpha/(n-1)
, since the total arc between R
1
and R
n
is partitioned into
n-1
equal intervals. The incidence angle
\beta
is the same at every reflection point along the path, which can be found by e.g. looking at the sum of all angles in the triangle R
1
OR
2
:

\pi=\widehat{\text{R${}_{2}$R${}_{1}$O}}+\widehat{\text{R${}_{1}$OR${}_{2}$}}+\widehat{\text{ OR${}_{2}$R${}_{1}$}}=\beta+\gamma+\beta\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \beta=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{n-1}\ .

(1)

We can then determine the angle
\delta
between the in-coming light-ray and the axis
\Delta
by e.g. looking at the sum of all angles in the triangle R
1
OS:

\pi=\widehat{\text{SR${}_{1}$O}}+\widehat{\text{R${}_{1}$OS}}+\widehat{\text{ OSR${}_{1}$}}=\beta+(\pi-\alpha)+\delta\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \delta=-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{n\alpha}{n-1}\ .

(2)

We have found how to obtain all relevant angles in terms of
\alpha
. Now, let us look at the distance
h
between the point R
1
and the axis
\Delta
, which can be calculated in two different ways:

•

From the center O with the viewing angle
\alpha
:

h=\mathcal{R}\tan\alpha\ .

(3)

•

From the observer S with the viewing angle
\delta
:

h=\left[r(n,\alpha)\mathcal{R}\cos\alpha\right]\tan\delta\ .

(4)

We can equate Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
4
) then apply the expressions of angles found in Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
2
) to arrive at the following formula:

r(n,\alpha)=-\mathcal{R}\left[\sin\alpha\tan\left(\frac{n\alpha}{n-1}\right)+\cos\alpha\right]\ .

(5)

Thus, increasing
\alpha
leads to a decrease in
r(n,\alpha)
. Because the half-opening angle
\theta
bounds
\alpha
from above (i.e.
\alpha\leq\theta
), there is a minimal distance (from O and along
\Delta
) where
the
n>1
-order self-images first appear, which is given by
r(n,\theta)
. For any
\theta\leq\pi/2
, the required distance to start seeing more that one self-imaging is
r(2,\theta)\geq 0
.

If the maximum visible self-image order at a given point is
n>1
, then – since no geometry constraint forbids the visibility of lower order self-images – all lower-order self-images are also visible. Given that all higher-order self-images come in pair (as mentioned before and illustrated in Fig.
3
B2), the total number of visible self-images is
N=2n-1
.

From Eq. (
5
), we notice that the distance
r(n,\theta)
reaches infinity when

\frac{n\theta}{n-1}\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{2}\ ,

therefore the maximum order
n_{\max}
of self-images can be seen – as a function of the half-opening angle
\theta
– is given by:

n_{\max}(\theta)=\left\lfloor\frac{\pi}{\pi-2\theta}\right\rfloor\ ,

(6)

where
\lfloor...\rfloor
is the floor-function. In other words, for a concave mirror with half-opening angle
\theta
, the axis
\Delta
can be divided into intervals on which the visible self-image count takes odd values 1, 3, …,
N_{\max}=2n_{\max}(\theta)-1
, i.e.

r\Big|_{\varphi=0}=0\xleftrightarrow{\ N=1\ }r(2,\theta)\xleftrightarrow{\ N=3\ }r(3,\theta)\xleftrightarrow{\ N=5\ }...r(n,\theta)\xleftrightarrow{\ N=2n-1\ }...\xleftrightarrow{\ N=N_{\max}\ }r\left(n_{\max}(\theta),\theta\right)\ .

(7)

The set
\{r(n,\theta)\}^{n_{\max}(\theta)}_{n=2}
is the collection of points that partition the axis
\Delta
into segments of different visible self-image counts
N
. The position
r(n>1,\theta)
is the boundary point between
N=2(n-1)-1
segment and
N=2n-1
segment.

II.2.2
Off-Axis Observer

Not only do all higher-order self-images appear in pairs, owing to the reversibility of light-ray paths, but for reflections on a circular concave mirror, every light-ray path that satisfies the comeback condition is also symmetric about the line connecting the mirror’s center O and the observer S. Consequently, if the observer S is off-axis – along a line with polar angle
\varphi\neq 0
– the number of self-images remains the same as in the on-axis case, except that the effective opening angle is modified to
\theta^{\prime}=\theta-\varphi
(see Fig.
3
B2). Therefore, we can generalize the axis-partition in Eq. (
7
) to the two-dimensional space partition, in which the boundaries between regions of different visible self-image counts
N
– say, region
N=2(n-1)-1
and region
N=2n-1
, with
n>1
– are described by the curves:

r(\varphi)=r(n,\theta^{\prime})=-\mathcal{R}\left\{\sin(\theta-\varphi)\tan\left[\frac{n(\theta-\varphi)}{n-1}\right]+\cos(\theta-\varphi)\right\}\ ,

(8)

where we have used Eq. (
5
).

Let us demonstrate the application of Eq. (
8
) with a concrete example. Consider a concave semicircular mirror, in which the half-opening angle is
\theta=\pi/2
. For convenience, we normalize the radius of the concave mirror to one unit, i.e.
\mathcal{R}=1
. Using Eq. (
8
), we can determine all the spatial boundaries that separate regions that correspond to different numbers of self-images. As shown in Fig.
4
, the two-dimensional space surrounding the semicircular concave mirror is thus divided into color-coded regions, each labeled by the number of self-images observable from that location. Furthermore, according to Eq. (
6
), the space is partitioned into infinitely many such regions, since
n_{\max}\rightarrow\infty
.

IV
What have We learned?

Using only elementary ray-optics and symmetry arguments, we have quantified how a concave half-cylindrical mirror produces self-image multiplicity. We then have verified our theoretical findings with simple experiments, which are right at the level of introductory lab. The work shows that seemingly routine laboratory setups can conceal rich, quantitative insights and highlights the value of curiosity-driven investigation. Because sound waves obey similar reflection laws, our results may also inform geometric acoustic simulation
Savioja and Svensson 2015
;
De Sena et al. 2015
;
Vorländer 2013
.
