Publication trend analysis and synthesis via large language model: A case study of engineering in PNAS
Mason Smetana, Lev Khazanovich

Abstract
Scientific literature is increasingly siloed by complex language, static disciplinary structures, and potentially sparse keyword systems, making it cumbersome to capture the dynamic nature of modern science. This study addresses these challenges by introducing an adaptable large language model (LLM)-driven framework to quantify thematic trends and map the evolving landscape of scientific knowledge. The approach is demonstrated over a 20-year collection of more than 1,500 engineering articles published by the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), marked for their breadth and depth of research focus. A two-stage classification pipeline first establishes a primary thematic category for each article based on its abstract. The subsequent phase performs a full-text analysis to assign secondary classifications, revealing latent, cross-topic connections across the corpus. Traditional natural language processing (NLP) methods, such as Bag-of-Words (BoW) and Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF), confirm the resulting topical structure and also suggest that standalone word-frequency analyses may be insufficient for mapping fields with high diversity. Finally, a disjoint graph representation between the primary and secondary classifications reveals implicit connections between themes that may be less apparent when analyzing abstracts or keywords alone. The findings show that the approach independently recovers much of the journal’s editorially embedded structure without prior knowledge of its existing dual-classification schema (e.g., biological studies also classified as engineering). This framework offers a powerful tool for detecting potential thematic trends and providing a high-level overview of scientific progress.

Introduction

Today’s research is often dense, highly specialized, and interdisciplinary, making it more difficult for the public to understand. Many people feel poorly informed about significant scientific discoveries, partly because scientists often are not trained to communicate with non-experts
Field and Powell 2001
,
Luzón 2013
. Such a misunderstanding could be partially attributed to the “curse of knowledge” – a cognitive bias where experts struggle to communicate with non-experts due to their deep understanding of a subject, which can lead to the use of overly complex language and jargon
Sharon and Baram-Tsabari 2014
. This can confuse those without specialized knowledge or even those conducting cross-disciplinary research
Guo et~al. 2024
. For example, engineers might refer to a “100-year flood” without explaining it as a flood with a 1% annual chance, which can cause unintended misinterpretation among readers
Bruine~de Bruin and Bostrom 2013
.

With the recent advancement of large language models (LLMs), there is growing potential to reframe how scientific knowledge is synthesized and disseminated. Current methods for organizing scientific literature, from static journal categories to sparse keyword tagging, have become challenging to integrate and navigate the interdisciplinary nature of modern research. They create a bottleneck for in-depth analysis, requiring experts from diverse disciplines to procure meaningful insights. This study introduces a hybrid framework that combines the contextual power of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) with traditional machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) techniques to autonomously map the thematic landscape of science. The proposed approach analyzes a 20-year collection of 1,519 articles classified under the
Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
(PNAS) Engineering subcategory, which is among the lowest level of subcategories within the physical sciences, representing a dense and wide-ranging archive of embedded scientific knowledge. A two-stage classification pipeline was designed not just to classify, but to differentiate: it first uses abstracts to establish an article’s primary thematic identity and then analyzes the full text content to uncover its secondary themes, which could reveal implicit methodological applications or cross-disciplinary impacts.

To demonstrate how this content-driven approach overcomes the limitations of conventional bibliometric and scientometric techniques, which often rely on static, journal-assigned categories or keyword co-occurrence, the paper demonstrates how LLMs can be used to automatically generate thematic subtopics that evolve within scientific literature. A disjoint graph representation is then introduced to map the interplay between an article’s primary (abstract) and secondary (full-text) classifications. This analysis surfaces a network of disciplinary bridges and content flows in PNAS, such as the intersections between engineering and adjacent domains like biology or medicine, that are not readily apparent from abstract or keyword analysis alone. Ultimately, the goal is to establish that the analytical capabilities of LLMs can be leveraged to provide a dynamic and interpretable map of a domain’s structure and evolution.

Related Works

Several studies have demonstrated that audiences engage with and respond more favorably to information presented in a straightforward, comprehensible manner
Markowitz 2024
. Therefore, translating complex scientific texts into plain language is a valuable practice. This process, often called lay summarization, involves the creation of simplified, non-technical summaries of scientific research, which strengthens the media ecosystem by enabling scientists to communicate more effectively with journalists, policymakers, and the public
Kuehne and Olden 2015
. Accurate, simplified summaries can thus enhance the visibility and impact of research, fostering a greater appreciation for scientific findings within society
Schmitz 2023
,
Guo et~al. 2020
.

Beyond effective communication, it may also be necessary to understand how science is produced, framed, and shared, especially in high-impact journals like PNAS
Schekman 2008
,
Verma 2015
. Retrospective trend analyses and bibliometric reviews serve as compelling tools for this purpose, allowing for the assessment of research significance, the tracking of shifting priorities, and the improvement of communication strategies
Shiffrin and Börner 2004
,
Bubela et~al. 2009
. Mapping such trends further supports meta-research, or research on research, which seeks to improve how we perform, verify, and communicate scientific work
Zawacki-Richter and Naidu 2016
,
Ioannidis 2018
. These analyses ultimately promote transparency, reproducibility, and greater public confidence in science
Wallach et~al. 2018
,
Lupia et~al. 2024
.

Previous reviews of the PNAS journal have provided valuable insights into its evolution and topical structure. In 2004, Mane and Börner
Mane and Börner 2004
applied burst detection and co-word association maps to identify emerging trends from 1982 to 2001, highlighting the journal’s growth and increasing diversity. Around the same time, Boyak
Boyack 2004
used a range of data mining and analytical techniques to further characterize the journal’s evolving content. Since then, subsequent studies have transitioned toward exploring the journal’s disciplinary structure. For example, in 2010, Airoldi et al.
Airoldi et~al. 2010
revisited the prescribed classification system, using statistical ML models to analyze the words in abstracts and the co-occurrence of references. They argued that the existing categories do not fully capture the extent of interdisciplinary research, suggesting that the number of articles classified under multiple categories is only a small fraction of what it should be.

Regarding disciplinary focus, several researchers have noted a prevelance of high-impact journal publications in life science research areas (e.g. molecular biology, genetics, immunology, and nueroscience). Ding et al.
Ding et~al. 2018
compared the disciplinary structure of PNAS to other high-impact journals, namely
Nature
and
Science
, by analyzing publications from 2004-2006 and 2014-2016. Their analysis revealed that PNAS publications were highly concentrated in biology and medicine, making it less discipline-diverse than the other journals. Xie et al.
Xie et~al. 2018
explored collaboration patterns among the biological, physical, and social sciences based on over 50,000 PNAS articles published from 1999 to 2013. They found that many authors, particularly in the physical and social sciences, were engaged in interdisciplinary work and that such research was not limited to highly prolific authors.

More recently, Milojević
Milojević 2020
, also investigating similar high-impact journals, confirmed this non-uniform disciplinary coverage by automatically classifying articles from 2005 to 2015 into fourteen broad research areas. The study showed that disciplines like bioscience and medicine tend to be well-represented; however, other important fields, such as engineering, mathematics, and computers, each make up less than 1% of contributions. These findings suggest that the journals’ structure may not reflect a truly multidisciplinary scope, leaving necessary fields underrepresented and their trends poorly understood.

Addressing the gap in representing and understanding fields like engineering could benefit from novel analytical approaches. Recent advancements in GenAI, especially LLMs, offer capable new tools for accessing and synthesizing scientific knowledge
Markowitz 2024
,
Zhang et~al. 2025
. These models can process and analyze vast amounts of text, making them valuable for assisting literature reviews, extracting findings, and generating hypotheses across disciplines
Thapa and Adhikari 2023
. Derivative transformer models, such as those in the Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT) family, excel in understanding and generating human language, with wide-ranging applications in summarization, translation, and beyond
petro\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hbox{}\crcr}}
. The potential for these tools to revolutionize science communication and accessibility is immense, offering a promising future for the field.

In practice, GenAI’s application to scientific literature has primarily concentrated on a specific challenge: improving public accessibility through lay summarization
Shyr et~al. 2024
. Initiatives like Paper Plain and numerous studies have demonstrated that LLMs can effectively simplify complex texts to enhance user comprehension and engagement
Markowitz 2024
,
Guo et~al. 2020
,
August et~al. 2023
. This work is valuable and has established a strong precedent for using AI to bridge the gap between scientists and the public. However, this prevailing focus on summarization has left the deeper analytical capabilities of these models for meta-research largely underexplored, presenting an intriguing area for additional investigation.

While making science accessible is one goal, using AI to understand the very structure and evolution of science is another equally critical challenge. The same contextual understanding that allows an LLM to simplify text also allows it to identify thematic connections, map disciplinary overlaps, and detect emergent trends. While LLMs demonstrate increasing proficiency and technical reasoning in domain-specific tasks
Huang and Chang 2023
, their capabilities can be further enhanced by integrating them with established ML and NLP methods to analyze large-scale textual data. A conjunction of such techniques makes it possible to systematically to extract, classify, and synthesize complex scientific knowledge
Peng et~al. 2023
,
Dagdelen et~al. 2024
, representing a significant but still underexploited opportunity to advance research understanding.

Journal Structure and Engineering in PNAS

Over the past two decades, PNAS has published nearly 70,000 original research articles, averaging more than 3,000 articles per year
National Academy of Sciences 2025
. The journal’s scope includes the physical, social, and biological sciences, with authors being able to dually select a major and minor subcategory upon submission
Airoldi et~al. 2010
. As shown in the
Appendix, Table
2
, the biological sciences account for the majority of publications, while the social sciences contribute the fewest. Neuroscience is the most published subcategory, whereas Demography is the least, with publications only appearing since 2022.

Between 2005 and 2024, over 1,500 articles fell into the Engineering category, a subset of the physical sciences, representing just 2% of all PNAS publications. In 2024 alone, the journal published over 3,200 original research articles, of which 140 (4.4%) were classified under Engineering. While modest, this proportion reflects the journal’s historical focus on the biological sciences. However, publication rates within the physical sciences, including Engineering, have increased over time, while output in the biological sciences has decreased relatively. This shift suggests a gradual broadening of the journal’s disciplinary reach.

The existing major and minor dual-classification schema further reveals implicit interdisciplinary connections and diversity in PNAS research. Within the engineering collection nearly 44% of articles were also categorized under a biological science subfield, with Applied Biological Sciences (11.5%), Medical Sciences (10.8%), and Biophysics and Computational Biology (6.3%) being the most common. In contrast, co-classification with social science subfields was rare, occurring in fewer than 1% of articles.

A preliminary keyword analysis of this engineering subcategory further reinforces its inherent diversity and highlights limitations of traditional analytical approaches. Each author selects at least three unique keywords per submission; a majority of which were used in less than one percent of the articles (15 or fewer occurrences). Dominant terms like “microfluidics,” which appear in 4.8% of publications (72 occurrences), and “drug delivery” (2.9%) show consistent representation. In contrast, emerging terms such as “machine learning” (1.5%) have only gained prominence in the last four years. This extreme sparsity makes it challenging to identify significant research themes using keywords alone. Without additional context or domain expertise, the relevance of many terms (e.g., “fatigue,” “aptamer,” “crispr”) remains ambiguous. These challenges suggest that traditional keyword analysis or supervised classification would require substantial, often impractical, expert oversight. As such, the PNAS engineering corpus of over 1,500 diverse articles offers a unique opportunity to apply an automated, context-aware analysis.

3
Thematic Mapping of Engineering Abstracts

3.1
Primary Abstract Classification

The analysis began by constructing a high-level thematic map of PNAS engineering articles to provide a basis for further corpus exploration and an interpretable overview of major research areas. The subset of engineering-focused literature from 2005 to 2024 was analyzed using the previously described LLM-assisted method. The first of this two-phase approach involved the classification of 1,519 abstracts ($x_i ∈X$) through an iterative pipeline of unsupervised K-means clustering, LLM-based topic generation, and reclassification (
Appendix, Section A
). This framework produced a stable set of research topics ($S^*$), along with temporal trends and disciplinary structures that were not readily apparent from the journal’s existing classification scheme.

The iterative procedure intially grouped abstracts into $k=7$ clusters through K-means via high-dimensional embeddings from OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model
OpenAI 2024a
. The clusters were then labeled and summarized by OpenAI’s GPT-4o mini LLM
OpenAI 2024b
. These generated titles ($T_j^t$) and descriptions ($D_j^t$) were finally used to reclassify the abstracts into relevant groups, with the LLM as a classifier. Abstracts belonging to clusters that failed to meet a reclassification agreement score of 60% ($A_j^t≥
τ
=0.60$) were reprocessed in subsequent iterations. The entire procedure converged after $t=9$ iterations, at which point fewer than 10% ($
δ
=0.10$) of abstracts remained in the residual $k+1$ “Other” category. Only 16 of 63 (25%) total cluster-topic pairs achieved sufficient agreement scores (
Appendix, Fig.
9
).

To ensure LLM reliability, each abstract was intermittently reclassified five times against the generated ($T_j^t$, $D_j^t$) topic set. A “3-out-of-5” confidence criterion was met by 96.2% of abstracts, with over half receiving consistent labels across all five runs, indicating strong internal consistency in the topic assignment (
Appendix, Fig.
10
). The final, stable set of sixteen primary topics ($S^*$) captured 90.1% of all abstracts – a result driven by the convergence criterion $
δ
=0.10$, which effectively reduced the overuse of the “Other” category and promoted more meaningful topic consolidation.

3.2
Theme Validation with Traditional NLP Approaches

Conventional NLP techniques, including Bag-of-Words (BoW) and Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) analyses, were employed to validate the thematic structure identified through LLM-driven classification. These frequency-based models help confirm the shared vocabulary within thematic groups. This validation was conducted on the abstract collection containing over 63,000 words, including 48,500 unique unigrams (single words) and bigrams (word pairs). BoW was applied with lemmatization, the process of reducing a word to its base or root form, consolidating the vocabulary to a more manageable size of 6,451 unique unigrams (See
Appendix, Section
B.1
). A similar notable sparsity, as observed through author-selected keywords, is illustrated through this process.
Fig.
3
plots word frequency $f$ against rank $r$, demonstrating a clear inverse relationship ($f∝
\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}}
$) consistent with Zipf’s law – a characteristic feature of linguistic data where the most common word occurs about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on
Zipf 1935
,
Piantadosi 2014
.

4
Thematic Mapping of Full-Text

4.1
Secondary Full-Text Classification

While abstract-level classification provided a useful summary of each article’s primary focus, it may overlook secondary or embedded themes in the full text. To address this, the classification process was extended to the complete article content, allowing for multi-label assignments across the previously established sixteen primary topics ($S^*$). 1,490 articles were successfully parsed from PDF to plain text, segmented, and classified, resulting in an additional dataset of 18,636 discrete texts (See
Appendix, Sections
D
-
E
). Unlike the primary classification, in which the LLM assigned a single topic per abstract ($y_i ∈S^* | x_i$), this approach allowed segments ($z_ik ∈Z_i$) to be independently classified under one or more topics ($u_ik ⊆S^* | z_ik$). The intentional allowance for multi-label classification identified thematically interconnected content, enabling a deeper exploration of how engineering research spans multiple domains.

Three distinct cases arise from this analysis (See
Appendix, Section
E.1
): Case 1 – Single, Specific Topic Assignment, Case 2 – Indefinite “Other” Assignment, and Case 3 – Multiple Topic Assignments. Of the segments analyzed, 25% were classified into either a single dominant topic or exclusively as “Other”: 3,735 segments (Case 1, 20%) showed clear topical focus, while 957 (Case 2, 5%) could not be confidently assigned to any topic. The remaining 75% were assigned multiple topics (Case 3), demonstrating the extensive breadth of the research and expanding the number of effective classifications to 46,639. On average, these multi-topic segments were linked to 2.6 ± 1.5 topics. Segments with more than four topic assignments were atypical and marked by ambiguity or overly general language, potentially prompting the model to “guess” its classification.

Conceptually, the graph connects the topic of each abstract ($y_i ∈S^* |x_i$) to the topics of its corresponding full-text segments ($u_ik ⊆S^* |z_ik$). Each edge signifies a connection, allowing for the identification of cross-topic relationships not evident at the abstract level.

Conversely, a column-wise summation of $M$ reveals that the Material Science category accumulated 3,561 segment labels from articles originally classified under other topics. This brought the Material Science topic to 5,378 labels or 10.8% of the entire corpus.

5
Discussion

5.1
Stability of Classified Research Topics

The proposed abstract classification method, meticulously refined over several ($t=9$) iterations, was essential for achieving stable and coherent research topics. Topic descriptions ($T_j^t,D_j^t$) were revised until a convergence threshold ($
δ
=0.10$) was met, at which point 10% of the remaining articles were assigned to the $k+1$ “Other” category. This careful iterative refinement often resulted in more specific and accurate topic descriptions.

The evolution of water-related topics illustrates the dynamic nature of this process. An early iteration ($t=2$) identified a broad “Water Sustainability” topic from 132 abstracts. However, the LLM-based reclassification shows that only 16.7% of these abstracts aligned with the generated description (which referred to governance strategies for water purification and sustainability). By the final iteration, this cluster evolved into a more focused topic: “Water Purification.” GPT-4o mini eventually revised the description, emphasizing membrane technologies and wastewater treatment, which increased the agreement score ($A_j^t$) to 66.7% (Topic 14,
Appendix, Table
3
). This demonstrates that the method can be adapted to different research areas, ensuring higher topic agreement and stability with a more focused definition when applied to a smaller, more relevant subset of abstracts.

These findings also highlight the inherent limitations of standard clustering algorithms like K-means for this textual data type. Finding an optimal number of clusters ($k$) is a well-known challenge. Common heuristics such as the elbow method and Silhouette score were unreliable for this dataset and showed no clear optimum (
Appendix, Fig.
8
), likely due to data sparsity and outliers
Shi et~al. 2021
,
Patel et~al. 2022
. While other algorithms like DBSCAN are designed to identify outliers, their performance can be hindered in high-dimensional embedding spaces
Duan et~al. 2021
. In contrast, the iterative framework presented in this study effectively managed outliers by assigning them to the dedicated “Other” category at each step.

A further limitation of standard clustering algorithms is the lack of direct convergence criteria. The proposed approach addresses this by incorporating user-defined thresholds ($
τ
$ and $
δ
$) to govern the topic stabilization process. Without such controls, the number of topics may not converge, creating overly specialized categories with insufficient representation. For instance, in a late iteration ($t=9$), a potential “Imaging Techniques” topic included only ten abstracts. Allowing such small topics to form would be analogous to arbitrarily increasing the cluster count in a standard algorithm, undermining the goal of identifying significant research areas.

The LLM-based reclassification and agreement criteria provided a practical and robust framework for identifying context-driven research topics. A primary advantage is its direct interpretability; generating descriptive labels and descriptions for each cluster immediately clarifies the substance of topics. Furthermore, this iterative method offers an important technical benefit. By processing a smaller, controlled number of clusters ($k$) in each iteration, the framework mitigates the “lost in the middle” problem, where LLM performance degrades when processing large contexts
Liu et~al. 2024
. This approach reduces the input complexity for the LLM at each step, ensuring more predictable and accurate classification.

5.2
NLP Insights on LLM-Generated Topical Structure

The thematic structure identified by the primary abstract classification was validated using established NLP methods. Analyses with BoW and c-TF-IDF confirmed that the sixteen generated topics were not arbitrary. Instead, each topic reflected a distinct vocabulary and research focus. For example, the clear separation in term distributions between Tissue Engineering and Material Science supports the model’s capacity to distinguish between disciplinary boundaries.

The word frequency distribution of the abstract collection aligned with Zipf’s law
Zipf 1935
. Although the fitness was not perfect (i.e., not precisely following the theoretical Zipf curve in
Fig.
3
b
), the data exhibited a structure where a few terms occurred with a high frequency (e.g., “study,” “method”). In contrast, a “long-tail” of terms rarely appeared, meaning that a large number of terms occurred infrequently in the corpus (“e.g., “mesenchymal,” “photovoltaic”). This linguistic pattern highlights the key advantages of LLMs, which use attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of common and rare terms based on context
Vaswani et~al. 2017
. This capability is evidenced by how specific terms like “dislocation” were confined to the Material Science topic in PNAS – likely attributed to the discussion about dislocation of a material at an atomic level, rather than joint-related dislocations in the body (e.g. shoulder, knee).

GPT-4o mini’s ability to prioritize contextual significance over mere frequency was also observed. For instance, “microfluidics” was a common author-provided keyword (
Fig.
4
) but ranked low in overall word frequency ($f=17$, $r=587$). Despite this low frequency, the LLM correctly identified its importance by creating a dedicated Microfluidics Innovation group (topic 13), which was expanded from 1.6% of abstracts to 4.4% of the full-text corpus. Furthermore, the model demonstrated an ability to remove the ambiguity of terms based on context. In many cases, terms only become distinct when interacting with other words. For example, the bigram “mechanical properties” was almost exclusively associated with Material Science, whereas “mechanical” in Tissue Engineering likely referred to physical stimuli applied to cells. The LLM’s capacity to process intermittent multi-word phrases was critical for accurate topic separation.

The absence of a dedicated topic for “machine learning” or “deep learning,” despite their prevalence as author keywords from 2020 to 2024 (
Fig.
4
), was also an informative finding. This absence was not attributed to the model’s failure but rather to the Computer Science subcategory’s low representation in the original PNAS classification system – which only accounts for 0.38% of all published articles in the journal (
Appendix, Table
2
). More importantly, none of the Engineering articles were dually classified as Computer Science in the journal’s original schema. As ML and AI have become integral to modern research, these topics may seek more representation in the journal.

While frequency-based models like BoW and c-TF-IDF provide functional model validation and confirmation, they are inherently context-agnostic. These models treat each word in isolation, unable to infer meaning beyond occurrence counts. As a result, they are limited in their ability to capture semantic relationships or detect subtle shifts in meaning across disciplinary boundaries. In contrast, the LLM-based approach successfully captured latent semantic structures. This was demonstrated by the fact that high-frequency terms from the BoW analysis (e.g., “cell,” tissue,” “matrix”) were also central to the most significant topic derived by the model (Tissue Engineering). This consistent alignment between the LLM’s topical structure and observed statistical patterns provides strong evidence for the classification’s validity. It suggests the model uncovered a meaningful, linguistically grounded structure, highlighting its strength for exploratory analysis where no formal ground truth is available.

5.3
Cross-Topic Connections in Engineering Research

Contrary to criticisms that PNAS underrepresents engineering
Milojević 2020
, this analysis shows its importance in adjacent domains through secondary cross-topic connections. The relationships between abstract-level topics and their corresponding full-text segments were captured in a bipartite graph, represented by an asymmetric adjacency matrix (
Appendix, Table
5
). This structure revealed a complex network of connections that mirrored and expanded upon the insights from the journal’s original dual-classification system.

Based on abstracts alone, the PNAS system identifies Tissue Engineering as a dually published topic (
Table
1
) – obtaining a high precision (73.8%) and recall (28.6%). The LLM-based analysis independently affirmed this, positioning Tissue Engineering (and stem cell behavior) as a central hub in PNAS Engineering that connects multiple domains. Such qualitative alignment is supported by the quantitative, corpus-level analysis. The journal’s baseline dual-classification rate for engineering was 44.3%; remarkably, the secondary full-text classification assigned a similar proportion of multi-labeled segments (42.5%, 21,074 segments) to topics associated with high duality (“Very High” and “High” lift). The remaining 57.5% of text was more engineering-centered and less likely to be dually classified under the original schema, all autonomously revealed by the proposed method.

The normalized adjacency matrix (
Fig.
6
) provided further insights into the journal’s topical structure. Its strong diagonal elements, where most segments align with their parent abstract’s topic, confirmed the thematic coherence of the classifications. However, notable deviations from this pattern were observed for topics like Nanoparticle Technology (for targeted cancer therapy) and Soft Robotics. These fields often function as secondary themes embedded within broader biomedical narratives in PNAS. For instance, segments from Nanoparticle Technology abstracts were frequently classified under Diagnostic Technologies, reflecting applications such as the T-cell isolation nano-vials developed by Koo et al.
Koo et~al. 2024
. The matrix also highlighted emerging fields potentially overlooked by the journal’s formal taxonomy, such as Shape-Morphing Materials, which showed significant segment activity despite having no dual classifications in the original PNAS system. This suggests it is a growing, embedded research area whose importance was made visible through this full-text approach.

Finally, the analysis highlighted the role of dominant engineering topics such as Material Science and Fluid Dynamics. These fields exhibited strong thematic self-correlation and significant inter-topic dependence, indicating they are core disciplines that also serve as the technical foundation for many bio-oriented innovations. Their prevalence reinforces the convergence, rather than mere coexistence, of biological and engineering domains. These findings affirm that the journal reflects deep interdisciplinary integration and that the LLM-driven framework not only corroborates the original classification system but expands upon it, offering a dynamic and adaptable method for mapping the evolving contours of modern science.

5.4
Challenges with LLMs

Despite impressive advances, LLMs have limitations, particularly the risk of hallucination where a model generates plausible-sounding but incorrect or fabricated information
Huang and Chang 2023
,
Farquhar et~al. 2024
. These risks were intentionally engaged as this study employed an exploratory, unsupervised classification system. Nevertheless, the results demonstrated the robustness and potential of LLMs for scientific text mining, instilling optimism about the future of their application.

Several structured safeguards must be implemented to mitigate the risks of bias and hallucination. To combat this, the order of abstracts was shuffled before LLM classification to reduce sequence-dependent bias, which can influence predictions in autoregressive models when inputs are batched (e.g., more than one text is labeled simultaneously). Additionally, a “3-out-of-5” consensus rule was enforced, meaning an abstract had to be consistently assigned to the same topic in at least three out of five runs to be retained for subsequent analysis. The high consistency of the model was evidenced by the fact that this criterion filtered out only 5% of abstracts (
Appendix, Fig.
10
), reinforcing the reliability of the classification process.

In future studies, orchestrating multiple LLMs across different architectures or model providers could only enhance classification. Additional improvements may also come from more advanced text parsing and extraction techniques, especially for handling domain-specific artifacts such as complex chemical names or specialized terminology in scientific publications. While these models are not without flaws, their ability to infer complex structures from highly technical jargon, especially without additional supervised training, demonstrates their promising role in exploratory research and trend detection across disciplines.

Overall, classical NLP techniques remained indispensable for validating GPT-4o mini’s outputs. While LLMs can operate as “black-box” systems, interpretable models such as BoW and c-TF-IDF allow for the validation and contextualization of patterns they detect. In this study, these traditional tools were not merely complementary but essential for confirming the semantic coherence of the LLM-assigned topics. This hybrid approach verified that the LLM-derived groupings aligned with known linguistic structures and underscored the value of blending modern generative AI capabilities with established, interpretable NLP techniques.

5.5
Future Analyses of the Engineering Coverage in PNAS

While this study presents a stable snapshot of engineering research in PNAS, the true value of the proposed methodology lies in its dynamic application for future analysis. The sixteen topics identified here provide a robust baseline of the current landscape. However, as scientific frontiers evolve, this classification framework is designed to adapt. New research trends will inevitably emerge, similar to how topics like Catalysis and Energy have gained prominence in recent years (
Fig.
2
). Such a topic may not have surfaced as a distinct cluster from the abstracts prior to 2015, demonstrating how this approach can track the rise and fall of research paradigms over time.

The current classifications can be preserved as a benchmark for the immediate future. This provides a valuable resource for prospective authors, allowing them to situate their work within the context of related engineering research previously published in PNAS. The stability of these themes, as confirmed by the NLP validation, offers confidence that they represent significant and persistent areas of inquiry. Based on the analysis, a reasonable prediction is that the strong influence of biologically oriented research on these topics will continue, ensuring that many of the current themes remain relevant in the journal.

The long-term utility of this framework is its capacity for simple re-evaluation. As new research develops, for instance, at the intersection of Engineering and Computer Science, the “Other” category will likely be ever-changing and fluid. A significant increase in the size of this category can serve as a trigger to re-run the classification algorithm. This forward analysis would allow for identifying new, emerging subtopics and could reveal shifts in the relevance of existing ones. For example, specific topics may expand, while others, which were once considered “cutting edge,” may shrink or merge as the field’s focus evolves. This dynamic reclassification is a key feature of the methodology, enabling a continuous and updated understanding of the topical landscape – unless the “Other” category remains stable.

6
Conclusion

As a well-known and impactful scientific journal, the
Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
(PNAS) plays a central role in shaping modern scientific discourse. Understanding the composition of its published work is therefore essential for researchers and the public alike. This study provides a high-level exploratory analysis of engineering research within PNAS, introducing an LLM-based framework for mapping scientific themes and identifying cross-disciplinary trends. In direct contrast to conventional bibliometric and scientometric techniques that rely on static journal-assigned categories or keyword co-occurrence, the proposed method’s content-driven approach enabled the automatic generation of thematic subtopics that evolve within bodies of scientific texts.

By developing an unsupervised classification pipeline, the devised apporach moved beyond validating existing categories to actively uncover the latent thematic architecture of two decades of PNAS engineering research. In conjunction with GPT-4o mini, the article abstracts were first clustered into sixteen major theme groups. The internal consistency of these clusters was confirmed through a hybrid validation process combining LLM-based classification with traditional NLP techniques (e.g., BoW, c-TF-IDF), which also highlighted the limitations of frequency-based methods when applied to diverse scientific texts. Conventional approaches may struggle with keyword sparsity and require expert oversight, whereas the LLM framework autonomously surfaces domain-specific themes – a capability that is expected to improve with future model advancements.

While abstract-level classification provided a useful summary of each article’s primary focus, it may overlook secondary or embedded themes in the full text. The extension of the classification process to the complete article content revealed a network of disciplinary bridges and content flows, highlighting frequent intersections between engineering and adjacent domains like biology or medicine. The utility of this approach is fully realized through a disjoint graph representation that maps the interplay between an article’s primary and secondary classifications and reveals the interdisciplinary nature of research, which may not be apparent through abstract or keyword-based analyses. The resulting thematic structure aligned with the existing PNAS dual-classification system and expanded upon it by detecting latent cross-topic connections. For instance, a strong convergence of biomedical engineering, diagnostic technologies, and material science was revealed through the full-text analysis, highlighting connections less apparent at the abstract level.

Identifying these trends is critical for researchers tracking their fields’ trajectory and the wider community, as scientific literature is often dense and difficult to comprehend. This work highlights the potential of LLMs to address this challenge. The selected model demonstrated an ability to generate concise, plain-language descriptions for highly technical research areas, translating dense jargon into accessible topics (e.g., Material Science, Tissue Engineering). This capability has significant implications for education and interdisciplinary collaboration, helping to bridge the gap between experts and broader audiences. By extending beyond lay summarization, this study exploited the analytical abilities of LLMs to show a dynamic and interpretable mapping of a domain’s structure and evolution. Looking ahead, further advancements in LLMs, such as improved language understanding and generation capabilities, could significantly enhance their utility in scientific communication and understanding, making them even more indispensable for interdisciplinary research.

Ultimately, this analysis revealed a breadth of engineering-related research featured in PNAS. Contrary to some critiques, the findings show a robustly interdisciplinary landscape where key topics like Tissue Engineering and Diagnostic Technologies are deeply interconnected with biological and physical science domains. While the overall number of engineering articles is modest, their growing volume over time reflects an increasing emphasis on the integrative nature of the discipline – as exemplified by life science portions of articles being co-classified with more engineering-focused topics like Material Science or Fluid Dynamics. The proposed framework provides a snapshot of the present and a dynamic tool for the future; it can be reapplied over time to track the evolution of research topics within the journal as new fields emerge. Its adaptability makes it a scalable template for conducting similar large-scale trend analyses across other journals and bodies of scientific research.

Appendix A
Primary Abstract Classification

Let $X={x_1,x_2,…,x_n}$ be a set of abstracts and $X^t$ be a subset of $X$ in iteration $t$ ($X^t⊆X$). Initially, at iteration $t=1$, the subset will equal the complete set of abstracts ($X^1=X$). To construct the data set, the original research articles were programmatically retrieved under the
Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
(PNAS) category “Engineering” by querying the Crossref repository, which yielded 1,493 articles (99.1% coverage). To broaden the analytical scope, 26 additional commentary and perspective articles were included, resulting in a total of 1,519 documents.

A.1
Clustering

The recursive process begins by clustering the current set of abstracts, $X^t$, into $k$ clusters using the K-means algorithm – similar to the procedure performed in
Smetana et~al. 2024
. This results in a partition of $X^t$ into cluster groups $C^t={c_1^t,c_2^t,…, c_k^t}$. Each abstract $x_i ∈X^t$ is first encoded into a dense vector representation in $
R
^d$ using an embedding model $
E
(⋅)$. In this implementation, OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model is used
OpenAI 2024a
, with a hidden dimension of $d=1,536$. In order to conserve computational resources, the original embedding calculations were reused throughout the analysis.

The K-means algorithm operates on these embeddings to compute an optimal partitioning by minimizing intra-cluster distances
Patel et~al. 2022
. Specifically, it leverages cosine similarity (computed via the dot product between normalized vectors) as a similarity metric, commonly used for clustering in high-dimensional embedding spaces
Neelakantan et~al. 2022
. This clustering approach is error-agnostic in the sense that, by fixing the number of clusters $k$ in each iteration, the method maintains consistency and avoids the need for external optimization criteria (e.g., inertia, silhouette scores)
Shi et~al. 2021
. However, this also assumes that an appropriate $k$ can be predefined or selected heuristically for the data set at each recursion step. Future implementations may explore adaptive methods to dynamically select $k$, although such approaches would require additional convergence checks and complexity monitoring to ensure stability across recursive iterations. The clustering operation at iteration $t$ can be formally represented as:

C^t=\text{KMeans}\Big[\mathbb{E}\left(X^t\right),k\Big]

(1)

A.2
LLM-Generated Thematic Categories

The large language model (LLM) used in this study is OpenAI’s GPT-4o-mini
OpenAI 2024b
, which assists in generating thematic categories based on the results of the K-means clustering step. For simplicity, the model is denoted as $f(⋅)$, where input texts are autoregressively processed to generate outputs through probabilistic predictions across multiple, connected transformer blocks
Vaswani et~al. 2017
. This generation process can be expressed as:

f(\cdot)=g\Big[h(\cdot,W\_h),W\_g\Big]

(2)

In this representation, $h(⋅, W_h)$ refers to the transformer blocks, constituting the LLM architecture's core. These blocks process the input through multiple layers of multi-head self-attention and feed-forward neural networks, enabling the model to capture complex dependencies between different parts of the input text
Vaswani et~al. 2017
. The parameters $W_h$ represent the learned weights of these transformer blocks, which are optimized during pretraining on large, diverse text corpora using self-supervised objectives, typically next-token prediction tasks. The output layer is denoted as $g(…, W_g)$, where $W_g$ represents the learned weights of this final layer. The output layer typically includes a SoftMax function, which produces a probability distribution over a learned vocabulary, allowing the model to sequentially generate the most likely tokens based on the input
Brown et~al. 2020
.

For each $c_j^t ∈C^t$, a subset of the most representative abstracts is selected to provide input for thematic generation. Representativeness is determined by calculating the cosine similarity between each abstract’s embedding $
E
(x_i ∈c_j^t)$ and the corresponding cluster centroid, derived from K-means. This defines the subset of the top $k$ abstracts for cluster $c_j^t$, to be concatenated and used as the input prompt for the LLM $f(⋅)$ to generate a theme label $T_j^t$ and description $D_j^t$:

Summarize Topics Prompt

The following is a list of paraphrased quotes from multiple papers:

Also, generate 5 keywords (1 or 2 words each) and a single all-inclusive title (a few words) that may represent these papers. Provide your output as JSON as exemplified in the following format:

Example Output:

{
"summary": "distilled summary",
"title": "comprehensive title",
"keywords": ["keyword1", "keyword2", "keyword3", ...]
}

These two prompts are intentionally separated to consolidate information and reduce the length of injected context during LLM inference. If the model is provided too much context during inference, it may struggle to accurately summarize the information and get “lost in the middle”
Liu et~al. 2024
. In this prompt, only one output example is shown for conceptual simplification, but this can be expanded to multiple based on user preferences.

A.3
LLM-Based Reclassification

After thematic category generation, the LLM is prompted to reclassify each abstract $x_i ∈X^t$ by assigning it to one of the $k$ thematic categories derived from the clustering step. Each thematic category $c_j^t$ is now characterized by a theme label $T_j^t$ and theme description $D_j^t$, both generated by the model in the previous step. These thematic representations serve as contextual guidance for the reclassification task. The LLM $f(⋅)$ is used to perform this reclassification by evaluating the semantic alignment between each abstract $x_i$ and the set of thematic categories ($T_j^t$, $D_j^t$). Specifically, the model is prompted with each abstract $x_i$, along with the complete list of thematic labels and descriptions, and outputs the predicted category assignment $y_i^t$:

y\_i^t=f\Big[x\_i\mid(T\_j^t,D\_j^t)\_{j=1}^k\Big]

(3)

This formulation represents a conditional classification task, where the LLM selects the most appropriate category $j$ for each abstract based on the similarity between the content of $x_i$ and the semantic meaning conveyed by each ($T_j^t$, $D_j^t$) pair. By leveraging its self-attention mechanisms and pretrained knowledge
Vaswani et~al. 2017
, the LLM can assess complex relationships between the abstract text and the thematic descriptions, thereby refining the classification beyond the initial unsupervised clustering. This step ensures that each abstract is explicitly aligns with a thematic category, as interpreted and articulated by the model. Similar to the prompts utilized to generate thematic categories ($T_j^t$, $D_j^t$), the prompt used to reclassify abstracts and later used for full-text classification is as follows:

{classes}

Provided Text:
{text}

Report your output as JSON as exemplified below. If no categories seem appropriate for the text select the "Other" classification. Report the associated id in the "{id}" key and the predicted category in the "{class}" key FOR EACH UNIQUE ID in the provided text.

A.4
Reclassification Protocol

A structured reclassification protocol was implemented to enhance robustness and reduce susceptibility to hallucinations. In each iteration $t$, the model was prompted to reclassify every abstract five times using the previously generated topic labels $T_j^t$ and descriptions $D_j^t$. A classification was considered valid only if at least three of the five predictions agreed on the same topic label. This “3-out-of-5” consensus criterion increased confidence and reproducibility by minimizing the likelihood of predictions driven by chance, given the inherent stochasticity of LLM outputs
Farquhar et~al. 2024
.

Additionally, to mitigate sequence-related biases due to the autoregressive nature of Transformer-based models
Radford et~al. 2019
, abstracts were processed in randomized order across runs. Since these models rely on prior tokens to generate subsequent output, simultaneous classification of multiple inputs could theoretically result in in-context interference
Ferrando et~al. 2023
. A safeguard against such sequence-dependent prediction artifacts was introduced by randomizing input batches and repeating sub-iterations. Together, these precautions ensured that the reclassification process emphasized stability, reducing the influence of transient context effects or hallucinated outputs.

A.5
Agreement Score

Following reclassification, an agreement score is computed to evaluate the consistency between the initial K-means clustering assignments and the subsequent LLM-based classifications, signifying stability. For each cluster $c_j^t$, the agreement score $A_j^t$ is defined as the proportion of abstracts $x_i ∈c_j^t$ for which the LLM-assigned category $y_i^t$ matches the original cluster label $j$. An indicator variable $
λ
_i$ is introduced, equal to 1 if $y_i^t=j$ and 0 otherwise:

A\_j^t=\frac{1}{|c\_j^t|}\sum\_{x\_i\in c\_j^t}\lambda\_i

(4)

This agreement quantifies the alignment between the unsupervised clustering and the LLM-guided classification. A high agreement suggests that the LLM’s understanding of the thematic category is consistent with the original embedding space partition.

A.6
Cluster Acceptance Criteria

Clusters with an agreement score $A_j^t ≥
τ
$, where $
τ
$ is a predefined threshold (empirically set at $
τ
≈0.60$, based on benchmark evaluations of embedding-based clustering accuracy
Muennighoff et~al. 2023
), are considered stable. These clusters are accepted into the final stable classification set $S^t$:

S^t\subseteq\{c\_j^t\in C^t|A\_j^t\geq\tau\}

(5)

A cluster was considered stable if the agreement score $A_j^t ≥
τ
= 0.60$, meaning that the LLM reclassification aligned with the original K-means cluster in at least 60% of the cases. Abstracts belonging to unstable clusters (i.e., those below the threshold) were carried forward to the next iteration for re-clustering and reclassification.

A.7
Recursive Refinement of Unstable Clusters

Clusters where $A_j^t <
τ
$ are deemed unstable and excluded from $S^t$. The abstracts belonging to these unstable clusters are aggregated to form a new subset $X^t+1$, which is recursively reprocessed in the next iteration:

X^{t+1}=X^{t}\subseteq\{c\_j^t\in C^t|A\_j^t<\tau\}

(6)

The recursive classification process continues until the number of remaining abstracts to be reclassified in the next iteration is less than a termination threshold $
δ
$, defined as a fraction of the size of the initial dataset $X^1$. In this study, $
δ
$ is set to 10%. Recursion terminates when:

|X^{t+1}|<\delta\cdot|X^1|

(7)

The recursive process will result in $T$ iterations, where $T$ represents the final iteration before the termination condition is met. Any abstracts that remain unclassified after $T$ iterations – comprising less than $
δ
$ of the original dataset $X^1$ – may indicate that the model has exhausted the primary thematic categories identifiable within the data. These remaining abstracts can be grouped into a general or “Other” category, acknowledging that they either lack a coherent thematic alignment with the stable clusters or represent outliers relative to the derived thematic structure. This final step ensures that all abstracts are accounted for, while maintaining the integrity of the primary classification schema.

A.8
Final Output

The union of all stable clusters identified across the $T$ recursive iterations yields the final set of classified thematic clusters. Each cluster in this set is associated with an LLM-generated theme label $T_j^t$ and description $D_j^t$ produced during its respective iteration. The final output is denoted as $S^*$, which consists of all stable clusters collected over $T$ iterations:

S^*=\bigcup\_{t=1}^{T}S^t=\{C\_1^*,C\_2^*,\dots,C\_{n^*}^*\}

(8)

Since stable clusters may have been formed during different iterations, each stable cluster $c_j^t$ initially indexed by $j$ must be remapped to a new global index $J$ in the final set $S^*$. This remapping creates a consistent, non-overlapping index system for all final themes $C_J^* ∈S^*$ where $J ∈{1,2, …, n^*}$ ($n^*$ represents the total number of stable themes). Each final cluster $C_J^*$ retains its corresponding $T_J$ and $D_J$, which are derived from the iteration and cluster in which it was initially generated.

Following the remapping of clusters, each abstract $x_i ∈X$ previously assigned to cluster $c_j^t$, with a corresponding LLM-based classification $y_i^t=j$, is reassigned to its new global theme index $J$ in $S^*$. This ensures that all predictions $y_i^t$ from each iteration align with the unified final thematic index:

y\_i^*=J\text{ such that }x\_i\in C\_J^*

(9)

Appendix B
NLP Word Frequency Analysis for Abstracts

To validate and interpret the results of the primary LLM-based classification, traditional natural language processing (NLP) techniques were applied as a baseline feature analysis. Given the absence of a formal ground truth labeling system for engineering research in PNAS, word frequency statistics, including Bag-of-Words (BoW) and class-based term frequency–inverse document frequency (c-TF-IDF), served as independent checks to assess the internal consistency and distinctiveness of the topic groupings.

B.1
Bag-of-Words

The BoW model is a foundational technique in NLP used to quantify text by representing documents as collections of individual words, irrespective of grammar or word order
Qader et~al. 2019
. It operates on the premise that word frequency alone can provide a coarse but informative content summary across a collection of documents. Formally, the BoW representation of a document $d_i$ is a vectorized form:

BoW(d\_i)=[f\_1,f\_2,\dots,f\_n]

(10)

Where,

$ f_k$ is the raw frequency of the $k^th$ word in the document

$ n_V$ is the size of vocabulary $V$ defined across all documents

This vectorized representation allows for straightforward comparison of word usage across different topic categories derived from the primary classification step. To reduce vocabulary redundancy and improve interpretability, lemmatization was applied before BoW construction. Lemmatization is a word normalization technique that maps inflected words to their dictionary form (or “lemma”) – for example, converting “engineering,” “engineered,” and “engineer” all to “engineer.” This approach is generally more precise than stemming, which may produce invalid root forms. Unlike stemming, which often truncates words to base fragments, lemmatization ensures that the resulting tokens remain linguistically valid. In this analysis:

•

All terms were lemmatized to collapse semantically equivalent variations and focus on root forms.

•

Numerical tokens were removed, as they do not significantly contribute to categorical differentiation in this context.

•

Stop words, including common English terms such as “the,” “and,” “is,” and “are”, were excluded to prevent them from dominating the analysis.

•

Bigrams were decomposed into individual unigrams prior to lemmatization to facilitate consistent comparison across topics and to identify shared linguistic roots.

These preprocessing steps reduced vocabulary noise and allowed for a more accurate interpretation of dominant features associated with each topic. Subsequently, the lemmatized BoW vectors were used to compare term prominence across classes, supporting broader insights into topic semantics and thematic cohesion.

B.2
Modified Class-Based TF-IDF

From an NLP standpoint, TF-IDF is a widely used technique for understanding textual importance and thematic structure across documents
Saha et~al. 2023
, as provided by the following equation:

\text{TF-IDF}(x,d)=tf(x,d)\cdot\log{\left(\frac{N}{df(x)}\right)}

(11)

Where,

$ tf(x,d)$ is the term frequency of word $x$ in document $d$

$ N$ is the total number of documents

$ df(x)$ is the number of documents containing term $x$

However, standard TF-IDF fails to reflect inter-class distinctiveness when applied to grouped or clustered textual data, as in the case of the topic classes generated through LLM-based classification. To address this, a modified class-based TF-IDF (c-TF-IDF) approach was implemented, which reorients TF-IDF to operate at the group level, quantifying how characteristic a term is within a specific topic cluster relative to all others. This method, introduced by Grootendorst
Grootendorst 2022
, is particularly useful when categories are generated through unsupervised methods, as it amplifies terms that are disproportionately present in one group over others:

\text{cTF-IDF}(x,c)=tf(x,c)\cdot\log{\left(1+\frac{A}{df(x)}\right)}

(12)

Where,

$ tf(x,c)$ is the term frequency of word $x$ in class $c$

$ A$ is the average number of words per class

To reduce the dominance of extremely frequent terms, the term frequency is further scaled using a square root transformation:

tf\_{\text{scaled}}(x,c)=\sqrt{tf(x,c)}

(13)

In contrast to Grootendorst’s original implementation, which combines c-TF-IDF with dimensionality reduction (UMAP) and clustering (HDBSCAN), this approach uses the classification outputs from LLM-driven topic modeling as predefined groups. Each LLM-derived topic was treated as a single class c, and the corresponding abstracts were analyzed to extract term patterns specific to that group.

The original PNAS keyword structure
National Academy of Sciences 2025
was mirrored by preserving both unigrams and bigrams during tokenization. Bigrams often capture domain-specific phrasing (e.g., “gene expression” vs. “gene” and “expression”) and were essential in retaining semantic specificity. Tokenization was performed using standard whitespace splitting, and a predefined stop word list was used to remove generic, non-informative terms. Words appearing in fewer than five documents or more than 80% of documents were excluded. All terms were lowercased, and no stemming or lemmatization was applied to preserve scientific terminology.

Appendix C
Comparison with PNAS Classification System

To evaluate the interdisciplinary strength of the new LLM-based classification framework, its results were compared against PNAS’s original dual-classification system
National Academy of Sciences 2025
. Specifically, this comparison assessed the degree to which the new topic groupings aligned with articles originally labeled under two distinct scientific categories by the journal.

Of the original 1,519 engineering-related articles retrieved, 26 commentary and perspective pieces were excluded from this analysis. Additionally, 13 research articles were omitted due to parsing errors, resulting in a working set of 1,493 articles. Among these, 661 articles were initially classified into two PNAS categories, yielding a baseline dual-classification rate of 44.3%. This value serves as a reference point for interpreting the interdisciplinary character of each LLM-derived class.

Three standard information retrieval metrics were used to contextualize the relationship between the new classifications and the original PNAS dual labels: Precision, Recall, and Lift
Hahsler and Karpienko 2017
,
Ibrihich et~al. 2022
. Together, these metrics provide a quantitative basis for understanding how the LLM-based classification reproduces and enhances the detection of interdisciplinary patterns in the PNAS Engineering literature. Precision measures the proportion of articles within a given LLM-derived class originally dually classified by PNAS:

\text{Precision}=\frac{\text{Number of Dually Classified Articles in Class}}{\text{Total Count of Articles in Class}}

(14)

Then, Recall quantifies the share of all dually classified articles (from the whole database) captured by a specific LLM topic. Higher Recall suggests broader coverage of interdisciplinary material by that topic class.

\text{Recall}=\frac{\text{Number of Dually Classified Articles in Class}}{\text{Total Number of Dually Classified Articles in Database }(661)}

(15)

Finally, Lift provides a normalized measure of how much more (or less) concentrated a topic is in dually classified articles compared to the baseline of 44.3%. It reflects the relative enrichment of interdisciplinary work in a class.

\text{Lift}=\frac{\text{Precision of Class}}{\text{Baseline Rate}}

(16)

•

$
Lift
> 1.0$ indicates the class has a higher-than-expected concentration of dually classified articles.

•

$
Lift
= 1.0$ suggests the class aligns with random expectation.

•

$
Lift
< 1.0$ implies the class has less dually classified articles than average

Appendix D
Article Parsing and Segmentation

Before proceeding to the secondary classification of full-text, the article PDF files were first preprocessed and converted into plain text with a Python script. The script extracts the raw text from PDF format, enabling the subsequent analysis and processing of full-text data. Once converted to plain text, a separate Python script parses the text into smaller, manageable segments. This step ensures that the document is broken down into smaller sections (e.g., paragraphs, sentences, or thematic blocks), which allows for more granular classification and analysis. 1,490 (98.1%) of 1,519 articles were successfully parsed, converted from PDF to plain text format, and algorithmically segmented. The remaining 27 articles could not be parsed with the described process, which may be attributed to artifacts in the document or file sizes that were too large to process.

Appendix E
Secondary Full-Text Classification

After the text is converted and parsed, each abstract $x_i ∈X$ that has already been classified into one of the final thematic clusters $C_J^*$ during the primary classification (where $y_i^*=J | x_i$) is now linked to a set of full-text segments $Z_i={z_i1, z_i2, …, z_in}$. The segmentation process varies depending on the length and structure of the document, but it is crucial for chunking the full text into digestible pieces that can be analyzed individually. Each segment $z_ik$ is then classified using the same set of themes $S^*$ derived from the primary classification step. The LLM $f( ⋅)$ is then employed to classify the segments, which maps each segment $z_ik$ to one of the thematic categories in the final set $S^*$. The output $u_ik$ is assigned to $z_ik$ (similar to the mapping of $x_i$ to $y_i^*=J$ in the previous step):

z\_{ik}\xrightarrow{f(\cdot)}u\_{ik}\subseteq S^*

(17)

One key benefit of segmenting and classifying full-text data is the ability to identify thematic interconnections across different clusters. By leveraging the concept of bipartite graphs, the relationship between abstracts and their corresponding full-text segments can be visualized and analyzed to uncover interdisciplinary potential.

E.1
Multi-Label Tolerance

In this full-text classification phase, each text segment underwent a multi-label classification process, allowing for assignment to multiple topics. Unlike the primary classification, which assigned a single topic to each abstract, this method exposed each segment $z_ik$ not only to the stable topics associated with its parent abstract $x_i$, but also to all other stable topics derived across iterations. As a result, a segment could be associated with multiple topics, even if those differed from the parent abstract’s classification.

For the stable topics identified across the iterative process, segments were incrementally evaluated against each topic, with an “Other” category available as a fallback across iterations. This yielded three possible outcomes for any given segment:

•

Case 1. Single, Specific Topic Assignment:
The segment is confidently assigned to one topic across all iterations, with all other classification attempts reverting to “Other.” This indicates a clear, singular thematic focus.

•

Case 2. Indefinite “Other” Assignment:
All attempts return “Other,” suggesting the segment does not strongly align with any predefined topic.

•

Case 3. Multiple Topic Assignments:
The segment is assigned to more than one topic, revealing its interdisciplinary character or thematic ambiguity.

E.2
Bipartite Graph Representation

In a bipartite graph, there are two sets of nodes: the left set representing the abstracts $x_i$, each linked to its associated theme clusters $C_J^*$ during primary classification, and the right set representing the full-text segments $z_ik$, each classified into one ore more of the themes $S^*$ during secondary classification. The edges between the nodes represent the relationship between the abstract and its segments, with the connection indicating that a segment $z_ik$ belongs to a particular $u_ik$ that is also associated with abstract $x_i$.

This graph structure provides a powerful way to analyze interdisciplinary research by identifying patterns in how themes from different disciplines are interconnected across full-text segments. For example, segments classified into one theme might also be associated with segments from other themes, indicating areas of overlap or potential collaboration between engineering subdomains. This visualization can explore how knowledge from one domain (e.g., biomedical) may connect with other areas (e.g., material science) within the context of a research paper, potentially highlighting interdisciplinary trends that could inform new avenues of research.

E.3
Graph Structure

A bipartite graph is a type of graph $G=(U, V, E)$ where $U$ and $V$ are two disjoint sets of nodes (vertices) and $E$ is a set of edges (connections) that link nodes from one set to nodes in the other set, with no edges connecting nodes within the same set
Pezzoti et~al. 2018
,
Kötter et~al. 2015b
. In the context of this classification framework, the bipartite graph represents the relationship between abstracts and the full-text.

Specifically, the set $U$ (left-hand nodes) corresponds to the abstracts $x_i$ in the dataset, each of which has been classified into one of the final thematic categories from the primary classification step ($C_J^*$), where $y_i^*=J$. The set $V$ corresponds to the text segments $z_ik$ associated with each abstract $x_i$, where each segment is classified into thematic categories from $S^*$ in the secondary classification step. The graph is represented by the following:

\displaystyle U=&\{y\_i^*|i=1,2,\dots,n^*\}&\text{ (abstract-level classification)}

(18)

\displaystyle V=&\bigcup\_{i=1}^{n^*}\{u\_{ik}|k=1,2,\dots,n\_i\}&\text{ (segment-level classification)}

(19)

\displaystyle E=&\{(y\_i,u\_{ik})|\forall k,z\_{ik}\in Z\_i,f(z\_{ik})=u\_{ik}\subseteq S^*\}&\text{ (edges)}

(20)

One node $y_i^*=J$ can have multiple edges to different segment classifications $u_ik$. The segment agrees with the abstract's primary classification if $u_ik=y_i^*$. Otherwise, if $u_ik ≠y_i^*$, it suggests cross-theme classification, indicating potential interdisciplinary connections.

E.4
Adjacency Matrix

If more than one theme, for example $C_1^*$ and $C_2^*$, are linked to the same abstract $x_i$ via segment classifications $u_ik ⊆S^*$, then these themes are considered connected in the graph. These edges represent co-classification within the same document and indicate potential interdisciplinary links. The co-occurrence between themes can be captured using an adjacency matrix $M ∈
R
^n^* ×n^*$, where each element captures the number of times more than one theme in $S^*$ appears together in the same abstract $x_i$ based on the derived text segments $Z_i$. The matrix can be defined as:

M\_{ik}=\sum\_{i=1}^{|X|}\sum\_{k=1}^{n\_i}{u\_{ik}|y\_i^*}

(21)

The adjacency matrix ($M$) shows the result of this structure: left-hand abstract nodes ($U$, row in $M$) connected to the right-hand full-text nodes ($V$, column in $M$) through a series of graph edges ($E$). The weights presented in M compile these edges from each abstract’s primary classification ($y_i^* ∈S^* | x_i$) to their corresponding full-text segments ($u_ik ⊆S^* ∀z_ik ∈x_i$) asymmetrically. Since each segment was classified independently from its parent abstract, they could have been classified into a different topic under the same classification schema ($T_j^t ∈S^*$). For example, GPT-4o mini may have classified an abstract ($x_i$) as topic 1 ($y_i=T_1^1$); however, the individual segments ($z_ik ∈Z_i$) could have been classified into any of the other topics for $u_ik ⊆S^*$ where $u_ik ≠T_1^1$.
