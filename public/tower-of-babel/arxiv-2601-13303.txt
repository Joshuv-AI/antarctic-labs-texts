Verifying Local Robustness of Pruned Safety-Critical Networks
Minh Le, Phuong Cao

Abstract
Formal verification of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) is essential for safety-critical applications, ranging from surgical robotics to NASA JPL autonomous systems. However, the computational cost of verifying large-scale models remains a significant barrier to adoption. This paper investigates the impact of pruning on formal local robustness certificates with different ratios. Using the state-of-the-art α , β \alpha,\beta -CROWN verifier, we evaluate ResNet4 models across varying pruning ratios on MNIST and, more importantly, on the NASA JPL Mars Frost Identification datasets. Our findings demonstrate a non-linear relationship: light pruning (40%) in MNIST and heavy pruning (70%–90%) in JPL improve verifiability, allowing models to outperform unpruned baselines in proven L ∞ L_{\infty} robustness properties. This suggests that reduced connectivity simplifies the search space for formal solvers and that the optimal pruning ratio varies significantly between datasets. This research highlights the complex nature of model compression, offering critical insights into selecting the optimal pruning ratio for deploying efficient, yet formally verified, DNNs in high-stakes environments where reliability is non-negotiable.

Verification of deep neural networks is critical for adoption in safety-critical domains such as high-performance autonomous spaceflight

This paper is among the first to verify the local robustness properties of pruned neural networks across: 1) different pruning ratios and 2) two safety-critical domains of image recognition in Mars Frost Identification

The key steps are:

•

Training:
10 ResNet4 models (different seeds) trained on MNIST to 99% test accuracy. A similar step is repeated on Mars Frost Identification with different parameters.

•

Pruning:
Magnitude-based pruning of convolutional layers (smallest absolute weights).

•

Finetuning:
Retraining pruned models to recover 99% test accuracy.

•

Verification:
Defining properties to ensure no adversarial examples exist within an
L_{\infty}
distance of
\epsilon
for 100 inputs per model.

Major results.
First, lightly pruned neural networks outperform their unpruned baselines in local robustness verification. In particular, MNIST models with 40% pruned connections have most of their local robustness properties verified across all
\epsilon
tested. On the other hand, heavily pruned neural networks (with 70%-90% connections pruned) exhibit optimal provable local robustness in JPL but significantly lower provable local robustness in MNIST than the baseline unpruned models.

Our results reiterate the value of pruned neural networks across a wide range of applications, demonstrated in safety-critical tasks such as Mars Frost Identification, while also calling for further work to explain the substantially different optimal pruning ratios across datasets.

Our robustness proofs for pruned neural networks demonstrate the trustworthiness of their inference results for safety-critical tasks where human lives or a billion-dollar mission are at stake.

Putting our method in perspective.

A state-of-the-art efficient machine learning approach is model pruning, which sets certain weights to zero, effectively removing connections. Model pruning has been shown to reduce the number of connections by up to 90% with negligible impact on both accuracy and adversarial robustness
[
3
]
, and is therefore a highly promising solution to the increasing size of models and datasets. However, the local robustness verification of pruned neural networks, which is crucial for safety-critical applications, has not been widely investigated. On this topic, Zhangheng et al.
[
10
]
and Guidotti et al.
[
2
]
show that pruning can improve verifiable local robustness of networks. However, these papers do not investigate how the pruning ratio affects local robustness verification.

To advance the state of the art in both efficiency and formal correctness, this paper proves that for a given input, there does not exist any adversarial input with a sufficiently small
L_{p}
distance from the original input that can change the model’s output
[
13
]
. In the rest of the section, we provide an overview of Local Robustness Verification, a key method for bringing
\alpha,\beta
-CROWN into the safety-critical domain.

1.1
Neural Network Pruning

Neural network pruning refers to setting a percentage of model weights to zero, effectively removing those connections. In this paper, we adopt a naive pruning approach that removes weights with the smallest absolute values in convolutional layers, because these weights are likely less important and pruning them would have minimal impact on the model
[
3
]
. In addition, we only prune connections from the convolutional layers, because residual and fully connected layers are more sensitive to pruning
[
9
]
.

Neural Network Verifiers and

To demonstrate the local robustness properties of neural networks, we use neural network verifiers. Currently, the state-of-the-art verifier is
\alpha,\beta
-CROWN
[
16
]
, which has won the International Verification of Neural Networks Competition for the last five years, from 2021 to 2025

5

. We choose to use this verifier given its clear superiority over the past few years.

2
Methodology

2.1
Choices of Benchmark Dataset

We use two datasets as our benchmark for training neural networks: the MNIST dataset
[
8
]
and a Mars Frost Identification dataset published by the NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)
[
15
]
. We include the MNIST dataset because it is a commonly used benchmark in neural network verification research (e.g.
[
6
]
,
[
16
]
), given that neural network verification is a difficult task and simpler datasets are preferred. We also include a dataset by JPL on identifying whether an image of the surface of Mars contains frost to demonstrate the application of neural network pruning in a dataset closer to the real-world, safety-critical setting.

2.2
Training, Pruning, and Verification of NNs

We choose a ResNet4 neural network architecture because it is sufficiently complex that a heavily pruned neural network can maintain accuracy, while remaining sufficiently small for easier local robustness verification.

Training.
For each dataset, we train 10 baseline neural networks using seeds from 10 to 100 in increments of 10. We use cross-entropy loss and the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of
10^{-4}
. We also use a learning rate scheduler with an initial learning rate of
10^{-4}
that is multiplied by 0.3 every 30 epochs.

Importantly, we stopped training once the test set accuracy reached a threshold selected through experimentation so that all pruned models achieved this minimum test set accuracy. This threshold is 99.4% for the MNIST dataset and 84% for the JPL dataset, enabling fairer comparisons of their certified local robustness.

Pruning.
After training the baseline unpruned neural networks, we prune them using pruning ratios ranging from 10% to 80% in increments of 10%. We use global unstructured L1 pruning only in convolutional layers, pruning a certain percentage of the weights with the smallest magnitudes. After pruning, we retrain the models with the same hyperparameters until they reach the test set accuracy threshold. For each pruning ratio, we prune all 10 baseline models with 10 different seeds, yielding 10 pruned models per pruning ratio.

In total, we trained 90 neural networks for each dataset: 10 in 9 categories, including the unpruned baseline and 8 pruned networks at different pruning ratios. We note that the pruning percentage mentioned in this paper refers to the percentage of weights in convolutional layers only. Nevertheless, because the vast majority of weights are in convolutional layers (98.4% in MNIST models and 99.1% in JPL models), this percentage can also approximate the overall pruning ratio.

Local Robustness Verification.
For each neural network, we verify its local robustness properties against the first 100 inputs from the corresponding dataset using the
\alpha,\beta
-CROWN verifier. We use the AMD EPYC 9454 (Zen 4) CPU and the NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPU for verification (as well as for neural network training and pruning). The verification of each network-input pair is limited to 5 minutes and can use 1 CPU core, 61 GB of RAM, and 1/4 of the GPU’s compute power with 20 GB of GPU memory.

3
Results

We aim to answer two main research questions:

•

RQ1:
What is the optimal pruning ratio for local robustness verification in each dataset?

•

RQ2:
Are the optimal pruning ratios across datasets similar?

We find that the optimal pruning ratio is 40-50% for MNIST and 70-80% for JPL. This is a surprising result because the optimal pruning ratios vastly differ across datasets.

We find the result surprising because the optimal pruning ratios across the datasets differ significantly. Lightly (40 - 50%) pruned networks are optimal for MNIST, while heavily (70 - 80%) pruned networks are optimal for JPL despite performing poorly in MNIST. We believe this warrants an investigation into why different datasets yield different optimal pruning ratios for local robustness verification. Broadly speaking, this result also suggests that other pruning settings, such as pruning criterion and granularity, might have different impacts across tasks and, if true, calls for explanations of these findings.

4
Future Work and Conclusions

This paper investigates the effect of pruning ratios on local robustness verification of neural networks and finds that optimal pruning ratios differ substantially across tasks. In the future, we will explain the effects of different pruning settings (e.g., pruning ratio, criterion, and granularity) on certified local robustness. We will investigate this in areas such as high-frequency trading, FDA-approved AI medical devices, supercomputer job scheduling, and other safety-critical tasks.
