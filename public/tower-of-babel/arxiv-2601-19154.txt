Abstract
. Shuffling is a powerful way to amplify privacy of a local randomizer in private distributed data analysis, but existing analyses mostly treat the local differential privacy (DP) parameter ε 0 \varepsilon_{0} as the only knob and give generic upper bounds that can be loose and do not even characterize how shuffling amplifies privacy for basic mechanisms such as the Gaussian mechanism. We revisit the privacy blanket bound of Balle et al. (the blanket divergence ) and develop an asymptotic analysis that applies to a broad class of local randomizers under mild regularity assumptions, without requiring pure local DP. Our key finding is that the leading term of the blanket divergence depends on the local mechanism only through a single scalar parameter χ \chi , which we call the shuffle index . By applying this asymptotic analysis to both upper and lower bounds, we obtain a tight band for δ n \delta_{n} in the shuffled mechanism’s ( ε n , δ n ) (\varepsilon_{n},\delta_{n}) -DP guarantee. Moreover, we derive a simple structural necessary and sufficient condition on the local randomizer under which the blanket-divergence-based upper and lower bounds coincide asymptotically. k k -RR families with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 satisfy this condition, while for generalized Gaussian mechanisms the condition may not hold but the resulting band remains tight. Finally, we complement the asymptotic theory with an FFT-based algorithm for computing the blanket divergence at finite n n , which offers rigorously controlled relative error and near-linear running time in n n , providing a practical numerical analysis for shuffle DP.



1.
Introduction

Shuffling has emerged as one of the most powerful tools for improving the
utility–privacy trade-off of differentially private (DP) distributed data analysis
(
14
;
16
)
.
In the single-message model, a population of
users first applies a local randomizer to their data and then submits the
resulting messages to an anonymizing shuffler.
A growing body of work has demonstrated that, even when the local
randomizer satisfies only relatively weak privacy guarantees, shuffling
can significantly amplify privacy at the level of the aggregated
mechanism, leading to substantially improved accuracy compared with
pure local DP
(
10
;
3
;
9
)
.

From both a theoretical and a practical perspective, two complementary
ingredients are crucial.
On the one hand, we need general analytic results that explain
how

and
to what extent
shuffling amplifies privacy for a given local
mechanism.
On the other hand, practitioners ultimately require numerically accurate
finite-
n
estimates of the privacy parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)

for concrete protocols.
In the central-DP setting, the latter need has led to a shift from
coarse composition theorems to tight numerical accountants (for example,
for DP-SGD), which in turn has enabled substantially stronger
utility–privacy trade-offs in practice
(
12
)
.
In contrast, shuffle DP currently lacks, both from a theoretical and a
practical perspective, tools that offer a comparable level of accuracy
and flexibility.

Limitations of existing shuffle analyses.

Most existing theoretical and numerical analyses of shuffling focus on
local randomizers that satisfy
pure
local DP (i.e.,
(\varepsilon_{0},0)
-local DP) and treat
\varepsilon_{0}
as the main knob
controlling privacy amplification
(see, e.g.,
(
3
;
9
)
).
This
\varepsilon_{0}
-centric viewpoint has two important limitations.

First, the parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
summarizes the local privacy
guarantee, but it is a rather crude descriptor from the viewpoint of
shuffle DP: it largely ignores the structural properties of the local
mechanism that actually govern privacy amplification.
In particular, existing general bounds typically do not distinguish
between, say, a Laplace mechanism with a given noise scale, a

k
-randomized response (
k
-RR) mechanism with a given
k
, or other
natural families of local mechanisms.
As a consequence, generic upper bounds expressed solely in terms of

\varepsilon_{0}
can be quite loose.
Indeed, several works have shown that by exploiting the specific
structure of a given mechanism one can obtain significantly tighter
bounds on the effect of shuffling
(e.g., for
k
-RR one can show that privacy amplification becomes
stronger as
k
increases, even when
\varepsilon_{0}
is kept
fixed
(
10
;
5
)
).
Yet, we currently lack a general understanding of what intrinsic
properties of a local mechanism govern its “shuffle efficiency.”

Second, the restriction to pure local DP mechanisms excludes a
wide range of natural and practically relevant local randomizers.
Pure local DP is a convenient sufficient condition for privacy
amplification by shuffling, but it is far from necessary.
In practice, one often encounters approximate-DP local mechanisms or
mechanisms that do not satisfy any DP condition.
Surprisingly, even for the Gaussian mechanism, arguably one of the most prominent mechanisms in central DP, it is not known how to precisely
characterize privacy amplification by shuffling.
Balle et al.
(
3
)
already pointed out
that the Gaussian case is technically challenging, and subsequent work
by Koskela et al.
(
13
)
observed that
even numerical accounting for Gaussian shuffling is nontrivial.
To date, existing results for shuffling of Gaussian mechanisms are essentially limited to lower bounds
(
15
;
7
)
.
While there are attempts to generalize shuffling guarantees to approximate local DP (i.e.,
(\varepsilon_{0},\delta_{0}>0)
-local DP) in a mechanism-agnostic fashion
(
10
;
6
)
, such
general bounds tend to be overly pessimistic; they often show little visible amplification for moderate values of
n
, because they are driven by worst-case mechanisms that do not reflect the structure of the specific mechanism.

From a practical standpoint, this is problematic: insisting on pure local DP may force system designers to forgo local mechanisms with superior utility properties simply because their analytic and numerical tools cannot analyze them under shuffling.

The privacy blanket and blanket divergence.

A central tool in the analysis of shuffle DP is the
privacy
blanket
introduced by Balle et al.
(
3
)
.
Given a local randomizer
\mathcal{R}
, the privacy blanket is a
data-independent distribution
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}
defined using
\mathcal{R}
.
Balle et al. showed that
\delta
of the shuffled mechanism can be upper-bounded by a divergence computed under the blanket distribution; we refer to this quantity as the

blanket divergence
.
This blanket-divergence bound is currently the best-known general upper
bound on privacy amplification by shuffling
(
20
)
.

However, the blanket divergence itself is
still poorly understood, largely due to its analytical and numerical intractability.
In particular, Balle et al.
(
3
)
mainly
derive generic upper bounds via Hoeffding- and Bennett-type concentration
inequalities based on the local DP parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
.
Consequently, these bounds can be quite loose and fail to capture how the blanket divergence depends on the structure of the underlying local randomizer, as well as how tight the blanket-based upper bounds are compared with known lower bounds.

This is partly because Balle et al. explicitly aim to obtain finite-
n
upper bounds.
More generally, carrying out a precise finite-
n
analysis of shuffling through the blanket
divergence is intrinsically challenging: DP requires a worst-case guarantee over all neighboring datasets (i.e., taking the supremum of the blanket divergence over all neighboring input pairs
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
).
Identifying the pairs that maximize the blanket divergence at finite
n
is highly nontrivial in general.
In contrast, in the
\varepsilon_{0}
-local DP setting, the single parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
already summarizes the worst case over all neighboring input pairs, so concentration-based bounds can be expressed directly in terms of
\varepsilon_{0}
without explicitly resolving the supremum over
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
.

Our goal and approach.

In light of the above limitations, our goal is to circumvent the apparent
finite-
n
bottlenecks of generic concentration-based analyses by taking a
different route, namely an asymptotic analysis of the blanket divergence.
While this sacrifices exactness at any fixed
n
, it allows us to address the
conceptual and practical limitations discussed above.

On the theoretical side, we replace the
\varepsilon_{0}
-centric view by a
single scalar parameter, the
shuffle index

\chi
, which encodes the
structure of the local randomizer.
As shown in Lemma
3.2
, by applying the central limit theorem (i.e., normal approximation), for a sequence

\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
in the regime
1
1

1

This corresponds to the moderate deviation regime required to apply the asymptotic expansion of the central limit theorem.

\varepsilon_{n}=\omega(n^{-1/2})
and

\varepsilon_{n}=O(\sqrt{\log n/n})
, the blanket divergence for a fixed
distinct pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
admits the asymptotic expansion

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}=\varphi\bigl(\chi\,\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{n}\bigr)\,\left(\frac{1}{\chi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}\right)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

where
\varphi
denotes the PDF of the standard normal distribution and the shuffle index
\chi
takes the simple form
\sqrt{\gamma}/\sigma
associated with the pair

(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
.
That is,
\chi
is the
only
mechanism-dependent constant
appearing in the leading term.
Thus
\chi
fully governs how well a given local randomizer interacts with
shuffling, in a way that is both quantitatively precise and conceptually transparent.

Moreover, by analyzing the worst-case value of the shuffle index over
neighboring input pairs (i.e., the infimum of
\chi(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
over

x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}
), we obtain an asymptotic characterization of the worst-case
blanket divergence and hence of the shuffled mechanism’s

(\varepsilon_{n},\frac{\alpha}{n}(1+o(1)))
asymptotic profile (Theorem
3.3
) of DP (i.e., upper bound).
Because we can express not only upper bounds but also lower bounds in terms of the blanket divergence, we can quantitatively assess the tightness of the upper bound.
In particular, we derive a simple structural necessary and sufficient
condition on the local randomizer under which the blanket
divergence yields an asymptotically optimal characterization (i.e., matches the upper bound and the lower bound) of shuffling
(Theorem
3.4
).
This condition is satisfied, for example, by the
k
-RR family with
k\geq 3
.
Moreover, we show that the Laplace and Gaussian
mechanisms do not satisfy the condition but the upper and lower bounds differ from
the true shuffled privacy
\varepsilon
by at most about
30\%
.
This shows that the blanket divergence, when analyzed through the shuffle
index, provides a remarkably tight description of shuffling.

On the practical side, asymptotic expansions alone are not sufficient for
numerical accounting at finite
n
.
We therefore complement our asymptotic analysis with a finite-
n
algorithm
for computing the blanket divergence at the asymptotically worst-case pair

(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
.
Our algorithm is based on an FFT approximation of the distribution (Section
4.1
), combined with explicit control of the
truncation, discretization, and aliasing errors
(Theorem
5
).
Leveraging the asymptotic expansion from
Lemma
3.2
, we show that the parameters can be tuned by a parameter
\eta
so that the relative error is
O(\eta)
, while the
running time scales as
\widetilde{O}(n/\eta)

(Theorem
4.3
).
In this way, the asymptotic theory not only clarifies the intrinsic
“shuffle efficiency” of a local randomizer via the shuffle index, but also
guides the design and analysis of practical numerical accountants for shuffle DP.

Related Work and Our Contributions

Another line of work seeks to go beyond the
\varepsilon_{0}
-centric
view by exploiting additional structure of the local randomizer, typically in
the form of a small number of scalar parameters.
Feldman et al.
(
11
)
introduced a
stronger clone paradigm and showed that, for mechanisms such as
k
-RR, one can
parameterize the effect of shuffling using
\varepsilon_{0}
together with two additional scalar quantities, yielding substantially tighter bounds than
\varepsilon_{0}
-only analyses, although even in the
k
-RR case exact
optimality is not established.
Building on this, Wang et al.
(
21
;
22
)
refine the stronger clone-based analysis, using two scalar parameters, and provide sufficient conditions on the local randomizer under which their bounds become optimal.
A subtle point is that the proof of Lemma 4.5 in
(
21
)
implicitly assumes the existence of a nontrivial post-processing map (see the errata of
(
11
)
).
Outside specific mechanism classes such as
k
-RR where no such map is needed (see Theorem 7.1 of
(
11
)
), the results should therefore be interpreted with some care.

Several works have proposed numerical methods for evaluating shuffle DP guarantees.
Koskela et al.
(
13
)
use the clone paradigm together with FFT to approximate the privacy loss of shuffled mechanisms.
Their approach, however, is restricted to pure local DP mechanisms and inherits the looseness of the clone paradigm, as shown by Su et al.
(
20
)
.
Su et al.
(
20
)
propose a numerical analysis of the blanket divergence based on FFT, similar in spirit to ours.
However, they still assume pure local DP and do not provide rigorous control of the relative error; in addition, the running time scales as
O(n^{2})
in the worst case when targeting a constant additive error.
Biswas et al.
(
5
)
give an exact combinatorial method for computing the privacy guarantees of shuffled
k
-RR, but their analysis focuses on fixed neighboring database pairs rather than general
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees.

Our contributions.

Compared with the above works, our contributions are as follows:

•

We are the first to give a unified shuffle DP analysis that does not assume pure local DP and applies to arbitrary local randomizers under mild regularity assumptions.

•

We theoretically analyze the blanket divergence including the exact leading constant factor and summarize its leading behavior by a single shuffle index
\chi
, yielding mechanism-aware bounds and a necessary-and-sufficient optimality condition.

•

We develop an FFT-based blanket-divergence accountant with rigorous relative-error
O(\eta)
guarantees and near-linear
\widetilde{O}(n/\eta)
running time.

•

We empirically validate our asymptotic and FFT-based analysis, and in a distribution-estimation task we show that generalized Gaussian mechanisms achieve favorable privacy-accuracy trade-offs compared with pure local DP mechanisms.

2.1.
Differential Privacy (DP)

We first recall the notion of DP and express it in a form based on the hockey-stick divergence.

Definition 2.1 (Hockey-stick divergence).

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be an output space equipped with a reference measure
(counting measure in the discrete case, Lebesgue measure in the continuous
case).
Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on
\mathcal{Y}
that admit
densities
p
and
q
with respect to this reference measure, and fix a
parameter
\alpha\geq 1
.
The
hockey-stick divergence
of
P
from
Q
of order
\alpha
is

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}(P\|Q):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bigl[p(y)-\alpha\,q(y)\bigr]_{+}\,dy,

where
[u]_{+}:=\max\{u,0\}
.
In the discrete case, the integral should be read as a sum over

y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Definition 2.2 (Differential privacy via hockey-stick divergence
(
8
;
4
)
).

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an input domain and
\mathcal{Z}
an output space.
A randomized mechanism is a map

\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}

that takes a dataset
x_{1:n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
as input and outputs a
random element of
\mathcal{Z}
.
For each dataset
x_{1:n}
we write
\mathcal{M}(x_{1:n})
for the
corresponding output distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
, and we denote its
density by the same symbol when convenient.
Two datasets
x_{1:n},x^{\prime}_{1:n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
are said to be neighboring (
x_{1:n}\simeq x^{\prime}_{1:n}
) if they differ in exactly one element. For a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\mathcal{M}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if and only if

\sup_{x_{1:n}\simeq x^{\prime}_{1:n}}\mathcal{D}_{e^{\varepsilon}}\bigl(\mathcal{M}(x_{1:n})\,\big\|\,\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime}_{1:n})\bigr)\leq\delta.

Sometimes, we say that
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for
x_{1:n}\simeq x^{\prime}_{1:n}
if
\mathcal{D}_{e^{\varepsilon}}\bigl(\mathcal{M}(x_{1:n})\,\big\|\,\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime}_{1:n})\bigr)\leq\delta

(
19
)
.

In the shuffle model, the mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is typically constructed by applying a local randomizer independently to each user’s input.

Definition 2.3 (Local randomizer and blanket distribution).

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an input space and
\mathcal{Y}
an output space.
A
local randomizer
is a randomized mechanism

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}.

For each input
x\in\mathcal{X}
we write
\mathcal{R}_{x}
for the
distribution of the random output
\mathcal{R}(x)
.
We assume that there is a reference measure on
\mathcal{Y}
(counting
measure in the discrete case, Lebesgue measure in the continuous case)
such that each
\mathcal{R}_{x}
admits a density, and we write

\mathcal{R}_{x}(y)

for this probability mass function or density, depending on the context. The
blanket distribution

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}
associated
with
\mathcal{R}
is defined for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
by,

\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y):=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{R}_{x}(y),\qquad\gamma:=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y)\,dy\in(0,1],\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(y):=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y).

where, in the purely discrete case, the integral should be read as a sum
over
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
The scalar
\gamma
is called the
blanket mass
of
\mathcal{R}
.

Following Balle et al.
(
3
)
, we work in the
single-message
randomize-then-shuffle
model.That is, we assume black-box access to an ideal functionality
shuffler
2
2

2

In contrast to the multi-message shuffling model, which typically
requires access to a stronger ideal functionality
\mathcal{S}
capable of
splitting or decorrelating multiple messages from the same user (for example
by hiding timing and grouping information), the single-message shuffling model
only relies on a comparatively simple “anonymization” ideal functionality
that permutes user identities. This makes the single-message model
significantly easier to implement in practice, and therefore in this work we
restrict attention to the single-message shuffling setting.
.

Definition 2.4 (Single-message shuffling and shuffled mechanism).

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a local randomizer. Given a dataset
x_{1:n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, each user

Y_{i}:=\mathcal{R}(x_{i})\in\mathcal{Y}.

The

is a randomized map

\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{Y}^{n}\to\mathcal{Y}^{n}

which samples a permutation
\pi

\mathcal{S}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}):=(y_{\pi(1)},\dots,y_{\pi(n)}).

A shuffled mechanism is
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{R}^{n}

Shuffled mechanisms are the main object of study in this paper and the following upper bound is known.

(

Fix

.
Then,

\displaystyle\delta(x_{1:n},x_{1:n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}},\gamma}^{\mathrm{blanket}}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}):=\frac{1}{n\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1:M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}\\

Definition 2.7 (Privacy amplification random variable).

Fix the pair

(

.

•

If

2.2.
Local Randomizers Beyond Pure Local DP

Most existing analyses of shuffling focus on
local randomizers that satisfy
pure
local DP
(i.e.,
(\varepsilon_{0},0)
-DP at the user level).
One of the main goals of this paper is to remove this restriction and
develop a more general analysis that applies to arbitrary local
randomizers satisfying the following mild regularity assumptions, without requiring
pure local DP at the local level.

Assumption 2.8 (Regularity conditions for the local randomizer).

Consider the local randomizer
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
.
We assume that there exists
\rho_{0}>0
such that, for every pair

x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and every reference distribution

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}\bigr\}\,\cup\,\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\bigr\},

the following conditions hold uniformly over all
|\varepsilon|\leq\rho_{0}
.

(1)

Uniform moment bounds.

For every integer
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl[\,|l_{\varepsilon}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})|^{k}\,\bigr]<\infty.

(2)

Non-degenerate variance.

The variance

\sigma^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})\bigr)

is strictly positive, and the following conditions hold:

\int\frac{\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\,dy<\infty
and

\int\frac{\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\,dy<\infty.

(3)

Structural condition.

Either of the following holds.

(Cont)

l_{\varepsilon}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})
has a nontrivial absolutely continuous component with respect to Lebesgue
measure whose support contains a fixed nondegenerate bounded interval

I\subset\mathbb{R}
independent of

\varepsilon
.

(Bound)

There exists a constant
C<\infty
such that

|l_{\varepsilon}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})|\leq C\quad\text{almost surely}.

Assumption
2.8
is mild: any nontrivial local randomizer that satisfies pure local DP automatically satisfies these conditions, and even when pure local DP does not hold, if the privacy amplification random variable
l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})
is non-degenerate (i.e., not almost surely constant), then one can enforce the bounded case (Bound) in (3) by truncating the output space to a large but bounded interval (i.e., by slightly modifying the local randomizer).

Accordingly, we formulate our results in a mechanism-agnostic way and do
not commit to a specific local randomizer.
Nevertheless, it is useful to keep in mind a concrete running example satisfying Assumption

without pure local DP and truncation.
In particular, we will often refer to the

mechanism
, which forms a flexible family of location mechanisms (parameterized by the shape

) and simultaneously encompasses the Laplace mechanism
(
\beta=1
) and the Gaussian mechanism (
\beta=2
).
The latter case is especially noteworthy, as the Gaussian mechanism has
been explicitly highlighted in prior work
(e.g., Balle et al.
(

)
and

(

)
) as technically challenging to analyze in the shuffle

Definition 2.9 (Generalized Gaussian local randomizer).

Let
\mathcal{X}:=[0,1]
be the input domain and fix
parameters

and
\beta\in[1,2]
. The
generalized Gaussian
local randomizer

\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is defined by

\mathcal{Y}:=\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}(x)=Y,\ \text{where }Y\sim\phi_{x}^{\beta}

and

In other words, for each input

, the output

is obtained by adding generalized Gaussian noise
(with parameters

3.
Analysis of Shuffling via the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Lemma
2.5
and
Theorem
2.6
show that, for the
shuffling mechanism, the DP parameter
\delta
is bounded above
and below by expressions in terms of the blanket
divergence.
Consequently, by analyzing the blanket divergence, we can assess the tightness of the privacy guarantees obtained via the blanket
divergence.

3.1.
Analysis of Blanket Divergence

Our goal here is to analyze the blanket divergence by deriving an asymptotic expansion and identifying the leading term, including the explicit constant factor.
The key observation is that the blanket divergence can be expressed in terms of sums of i.i.d. random variables (even though the common distribution depends on
n
), which allows
us to apply CLT-type asymptotic expansions
(see Theorem
A.1
and Theorem
A.3
for details).
To prepare for this, we first
rewrite
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}
and normalize it into forms that are more amenable to the application of these theorems.

Lemma 3.1 (Transformations for the blanket divergence).

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a local randomizer and a fixed pair

x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Let the reference distribution be

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}\bigr\}\,\cup\,\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\bigr\}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, define the
following random variables:

Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
,

X_{i}:=l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})

for
i=1,\dots,n
,

B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\gamma)
, independent of

(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
and

Z_{i}:=B_{i}X_{i}
and
S_{m}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}
for
m\geq 1
.
Let

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}]
and

\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1})\in(0,\infty).

For each
m\geq 1
define the normalized sum

T_{m}:=\frac{S_{m}-m\mu_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{m}}

and its CDF

F_{m}(x):=\Pr\left[T_{m}\leq x\right]

together with the thresholds

t_{m}:=-\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{m}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}},

and

\eta_{m}(z):=\frac{z}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{m}}.

Then the blanket divergence admits the following two equivalent representations:

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{e^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[Z_{1}\,\Pr\bigl[S_{n-1}>-Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{1}\,\bigl\{1-F_{n-1}\bigl(t_{n-1}-\eta_{n-1}(Z_{1})\bigr)\bigr\}\right].

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{e^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr[S_{n}>u]\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{n}\gamma}\,\int_{t_{n}}^{\infty}\bigl(1-F_{n}(x)\bigr)\,dx.

The full proof is deferred to Appendix
A.1
.
We retain both transformations because the conditions required to identify the leading term differ between the continuous (Cont) and bounded settings (Bound).
In the continuous case of Assumption
2.8
,
we apply Theorem
A.1
to the representation
(
1
).
In the bounded case (Bound), we instead apply Theorem
A.3
to
the representation (
2
). The resulting
leading terms coincide, and we obtain the following result.

Lemma 3.2 (Asymptotic expansion of the blanket divergence).

Assume that the local randomizer
\mathcal{R}
satisfies Assumption
2.8
.
Fix a pair
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and let

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}\bigr\}\,\cup\,\bigl\{\mathcal{R}_{x}:x\in\mathcal{X}\bigr\}.

Set

\sigma^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl(l_{\varepsilon=0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})\bigr)

and define

\chi:=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{\sigma},

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is the blanket mass of
\mathcal{R}
when
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}
and
\gamma=1
when

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathcal{R}_{x}
for some
x
.

Assume that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and that

\varepsilon_{n}=\omega(n^{-1/2})

and

\varepsilon_{n}=O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}\right).

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

(3)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{e^{\varepsilon_{n}},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}\;=\;\varphi\bigl(\chi\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{n}}-1\bigr)\sqrt{n}\bigr)\,\left(\frac{1}{\chi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{n}}-1\bigr)^{2}n^{3/2}}\right)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\;=\;\varphi\bigl(\chi\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{n}\bigr)\,\left(\frac{1}{\chi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}\right)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

The
o(1)
term depends only on the constants appearing in
Assumption
2.8
.

See Appendix
A.2
for the full proof. The leading term in (
3
) is obtained
by replacing

with the standard normal CDF

in

\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{1}\,\bigl\{1-\Phi\bigl(t_{n-1}-\eta_{n-1}(Z_{1})\bigr)\bigr\}\right]

via Mills’ ratio and a Taylor expansion.
In other words, Lemma
3.2

shows that the normal approximation not only captures the correct rate, but is asymptotically exact at the level of the leading constant.
The bulk of the lengthy proof is devoted to verifying that all
other error contributions are asymptotically negligible compared to this main term.
A crucial structural fact is that, for any local randomizer

\mathcal{R}
, one has the identity for
\mu_{\varepsilon}
in Lemma
3.1
,

\mu_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}\right]=\gamma\bigl(1-{\rm e}^{\varepsilon}\bigr),

so that the dependence on
\varepsilon_{n}
in the leading term of

form shown above.

Our key conceptual finding is that the leading constant depends on the
local randomizer only through the single parameter

\chi=\frac{\sqrt{\gamma}}{\sigma}.

In particular, the relationship between the local randomizer

\mathcal{R}
and the blanket divergence is fully encoded, at the level
of the leading asymptotics, by the one-dimensional index
\chi
.
Owing to its central role, we refer to
\chi
as the
shuffle index
.

3.2.
Main Result

Up to this point we have analyzed the blanket divergence for a
fixed

pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
of inputs.
In contrast, (
\varepsilon_{n},\delta_{n}
)-DP requires a guarantee that holds
uniformly
over all neighboring datasets

x_{1:n}\simeq x^{\prime}_{1:n}
(i.e.,
\delta_{n}=\sup_{x_{1:n}\simeq x^{\prime}_{1:n}}\mathcal{D}_{e^{\varepsilon_{n}}}(\mathcal{M}(x_{1:n})\|\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime}_{1:n}))
).
Our next goal is therefore to extend the
fixed-pair asymptotics of Lemma
3.2
to an
asymptotically
worst-case guarantee over all neighboring datasets.

Recall that Lemma
3.2
identifies, for each
fixed pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
and each choice of reference distribution

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
, a
shuffle index

\chi(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime};\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})

such that the corresponding blanket divergence has the asymptotic form
(
3
).
In particular, the dependence on the
local randomizer enters the leading term only through the single scalar

\chi
.
Consequently, the problem of upper bounding
\delta_{n}
reduces to understanding the worst value of
\chi
over all pairs
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
under
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}

Establishing the tightest lower bound on

corresponds to finding the pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
and the reference distribution

that minimize the shuffle index.

3.3.
Shuffle Index

In this subsection we investigate shuffle indices
\chi
.
Since DP is inherently concerned with worst-case

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}})

.
To quantify how tight this upper bound is, we compare it with the lower

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}})

As

\frac{\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}})}{\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}})}\;=\;\frac{\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}}{\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}}\,(1+o(1)),

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}/\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}

We term
\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}

and

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}
the

.

Our next result characterizes when
\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}

4.
Analysis of Blanket Divergence via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

The results in the previous section provide an asymptotic characterization of the blanket divergence.
However, in applications one is ultimately interested in the
finite-
n
behavior of the shuffled mechanism, and asymptotic expansions alone do not
directly yield numerically accurate values of the privacy parameter
\delta
for concrete choices of
n
.

In this section, we therefore develop a complementary, non-asymptotic approach for computing the blanket divergence at finite
n
.
Our goal is to design an algorithm that, for a given local randomizer

\mathcal{R}
and parameters
(n,\varepsilon)
, produces rigorous upper and
lower bounds on

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})
with explicitly controlled error and
provable running time guarantees.
The main idea is to exploit the same
structure that underlies our CLT-based analysis: the blanket divergence can be
expressed in terms of sums of i.i.d. privacy amplification random variables.
This observation allows us to approximate the distribution of the sum by means
of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).
To obtain rigorous bounds rather than
heuristic approximations, we carefully track the errors introduced by
truncation of the summands, discretization on a grid, and FFT wrap-around.
By combining these error bounds with the asymptotic expansion from
Lemma
3.2
, we can both tune the numerical
parameters (window size, grid resolution, truncation level) and analyze the
overall complexity of the method.

The starting point is another representation of the blanket divergence.

6.
Limitations and Conclusion

Our analysis has a number of limitations that we highlight as directions for future work.

First, when studying the generalized Gaussian mechanism we restricted attention to the one-dimensional case.
This restriction is not conceptually essential.
Our asymptotic and FFT-based arguments extend in principle to higher-dimensional local randomizers; however, in high dimensions the blanket mass
\gamma
of the Gaussian mechanism decays exponentially in the dimension.
As a consequence, the lower shuffle index
\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}
shrinks rapidly with the dimension, and the blanket-divergence upper bound becomes essentially vacuous for practically relevant values of
n
.
This is particularly problematic when analyzing locally private federated learning or shuffled DP-SGD through our framework.
At the same time, the upper shuffle index
\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}
exhibits essentially the same behavior as in the one-dimensional case, so that the gap between upper and lower shuffle indices widens with the dimension.
Closing this gap in high dimensions appears to require techniques that go beyond the blanket divergence, which remains an open problem.

Second, our moderate-deviation guarantees are formulated in terms of an
asymptotically
worst-case neighboring pair, that is, a pair
(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{1}^{\prime\ast})
that attains the worst-case shuffle index in the limit
n\to\infty
.
While this is natural from the point of view of our CLT-based analysis, it does not rule out the possibility that, for finite
n
, special pairs might induce slightly worse
(\varepsilon,\delta)
trade-offs than those induced by the asymptotic worst-case pair.
Establishing conditions under which the asymptotically worst-case pair is also worst-case (or near worst-case) at finite
n
is a practically relevant problem that we leave for future work.

Despite these limitations, our work is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to go beyond the pure local DP viewpoint and organize privacy amplification by shuffling via the shuffle index.
Our framework provides a unified way to reason about shuffle amplification and to obtain tight
(\varepsilon,\delta)
guarantees.
We believe that these conceptual and technical advantages are valuable both for the theoretical understanding of shuffle DP and for its practical deployment in real systems.

We analyze the blanket divergence by deriving an asymptotic expansion based on central limit theorems (CLT). The general CLT-based results used in our analysis are stated below in a form tailored to our setting, with the level of abstraction
kept to the minimum necessary. In particular, we treat the continuous case

Theorem A.1 (Triangular-array Edgeworth expansion after Angst–Poly
(
1
)
).

Let
(X_{i,n})_{1\leq i\leq n,\,n\geq 1}
be a triangular array of
real-valued random variables such that, for each fixed
n\geq 1
,
the variables
X_{1,n},\dots,X_{n,n}
are independent and identically
distributed (row-wise i.i.d.), while the common distribution may depend
on
n
. For each
n
set

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i,n},

B_{n}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(S_{n}),
and

F_{n}(x):=\Pr\left[\frac{S_{n}}{B_{n}}\leq x\right].

Fix an integer
s\geq 3
. Assume:
(i)
\forall r\in\mathbb{N},\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}[|X_{1,n}|^{r}]<\infty
.
(ii) For all

\mathbb{E}[X_{1,n}]=0
and
\inf_{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{1,n})\geq C>0

satisfies Cramér’s condition. That is,

\sup_{n}\limsup_{|t|\to\infty}\bigl|\mathbb{E}\big[e^{it\cdot X_{1,n}}\big]\bigr|<1.

For

\kappa_{r,n}:=\frac{1}{B_{n}^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{r}(X_{i,n})

the normalized cumulants of
S_{n}/B_{n}
, and let

be polynomials in

, whose coefficients are universal polynomials in

\kappa_{3,n},\dots,\kappa_{s,n}
.

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|F_{n}(x)-\Phi(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{s-2}P_{j,n}(x)\,\varphi(x)\biggr|=o\bigl(n^{-(s-2)/2}\bigr),\qquad n\to\infty.

Remark A.2.

Theorem
A.1
is obtained by specializing the
general triangular-array Edgeworth expansion of Angst and
Poly
(
1
, Thm. 4.3)
to the
one-dimensional row-wise i.i.d. case. Their theorem covers much more
general triangular arrays (including non-identically distributed and
vector-valued arrays); in this paper we only need the resulting
triangular-array extension of the classical Cramér-type Edgeworth
expansion of Petrov
(
17
, Ch. VI, Thms. 3–4)

Compared with the hypotheses of
(

we adopt stronger assumptions in the spirit of Petrov: we require the
existence of moments of all integer orders and replace the weak mean
Cramér condition by the classical Cramér condition for the
common distribution of

. When the distribution of
X_{1,n}
does not depend on

reduces to Petrov’s
one-dimensional Edgeworth expansion for sums of i.i.d. random

Remark A.4.

Proof.

We first explain the structure of the proof.
The argument proceeds in three steps.

First, in order to work with bounded summands that simplify the analysis, we introduce a
mean-preserving truncation of the privacy amplification variable in the (Cont) case and define the corresponding truncated blanket divergence.

Second, for the truncated variables we analyze the expectation appearing in

Write

A direct computation shows that for each fixed

Step 2: Solving the leading equation via the Lambert
W

Fix

\chi>0

g_{n}(\varepsilon;\chi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\cdot\frac{1}{\chi u^{2}\sqrt{n}}.

The equation

is thus equivalent to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\frac{1}{\chi u^{2}\sqrt{n}}=\frac{\alpha}{n},

which can be rearranged as

\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{u^{2}}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\alpha\sqrt{2\pi}\,\chi u^{2}}.

Multiplying both sides by

Setting

we obtain

hence

where
W(\cdot)
is the principal branch of the Lambert
W

follows that

\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}-1=\frac{u}{\chi\sqrt{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\chi^{2}n}\,W\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\alpha\chi\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)},

and hence

is the unique solution of

with

\varepsilon>0

From the standard asymptotics
W(z)=\log z-\log\log z+O(1)
as

see that

and therefore

which implies
\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\chi)\to 0

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\chi)=\omega(n^{-1/2})
and

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\chi)=O(\sqrt{\log n/n})

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\chi)

lies in the moderate-deviation regime where

3.2
is applicable. Moreover, from
the explicit expression it is clear that

Step 3: An upper bound via the blanket divergence.

We first derive an asymptotic upper bound on

By Lemma

Taking the supremum over

yields

\delta_{n}(\varepsilon)\leq\sup_{x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}}\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{e^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}},\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\,\|\,\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}).

For each pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
define

\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})\bigr)}},

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}:=\inf_{x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}).

Assumption
2.8

Applying the uniform asymptotic

26

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}

\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}})

in
\chi

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{e^{\varepsilon_{n}},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}},\gamma}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\,\|\,\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=g_{n}\bigl(\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}});\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr)\,\bigl(1+\theta_{n}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})\bigr)

uniformly over all
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}

\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha,\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}})

Therefore the mechanism

In the purely discrete case, all integrals over

Recall the notation from Section

For

\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y):=\inf_{z\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{R}_{z}(y),\qquad\gamma:=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y)\,dy,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(y):=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y).

For

We first prove the following lemma.

Lemma A.9 (Pairwise ordering and equality condition).

Let
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
be fixed and consider the pairwise
shuffle indices

\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})\bigr)}},\qquad\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}):=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x})\bigr)}}.

\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})\;\geq\;\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})\qquad\text{for all }x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if there exists an input

\mathcal{R}_{x^{\ast}}(y)=\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y)\qquad\text{for almost every }y\in A(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}).

Proof.

Fix
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}

\Delta(y):=\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(y)-\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y),\qquad y\in\mathcal{Y}.

Note that
\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\Delta(y)\,dy=0
, since both

\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}
are probability distributions
with respect to the same reference measure.

Step 1: Variance under
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}

For
Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}
we have

l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})=\frac{\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(Y)-\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(Y)}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(Y)}=\frac{\Delta(Y)}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(Y)}.

Since

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}}[l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})]=0
, its variance is

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{BG}}^{2}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\Delta(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(y)}\,dy.

On the complement of
A(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
we have
\Delta(y)=0
, so

\sigma_{\mathrm{BG}}^{2}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})=\int_{A(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})}\frac{\Delta(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(y)}\,dy.

Step 2: Variance under
\mathcal{R}_{x}
.

Similarly, for any

and

and again the mean is zero. Hence

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{2}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Var}_{Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\Delta(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{x}(y)}\,dy=\int_{A(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})}\frac{\Delta(y)^{2}}{\mathcal{R}_{x}(y)}\,dy.

Step 3: Comparison of variances.

By definition of the blanket, for all

and

Since

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}}(y)=\underline{\mathcal{R}}(y)/\gamma

Therefore

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}=\inf_{x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})\;\geq\;\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}},

while the existence of
(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{1}^{\prime\,\ast})
with

\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{1}^{\prime\,\ast})=\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}

shows that

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}\leq\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1}^{\ast},x_{1}^{\prime\,\ast})=\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}.

Hence
\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}=\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}
, which
is condition (i). This completes the proof.

∎

A.5.
The proof of Lemma
4.1

See
4.1

Proof.

Recall the definition of the blanket divergence (generalized to an
arbitrary reference distribution
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
):
for
M_{0}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\gamma)
and

Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
,

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\text{blanket}}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})\;=\;\frac{1}{n\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{M_{0}}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})\Bigr)_{+}\right],

where
a_{+}:=\max\{a,0\}
and

l_{\varepsilon}(y)=l_{\varepsilon}(y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}):=\frac{\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(y)-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y)}{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}.

We will transform this expression into the claimed probability
representation in two steps.

Step 1: Size-biasing the binomial and isolating one summand.

Let
X_{i}:=l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})
and

S_{m}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i},\qquad m\geq 1.

Then, for each fixed
m\geq 1
,

(S_{m})_{+}=S_{m}\mathbf{1}\{S_{m}>0\}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{m}>0\}.

Taking expectation and using the symmetry of the
X_{i}
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(S_{m})_{+}\bigr]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{i}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{m}>0\}\bigr]=m\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{1}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{m}>0\}\bigr].

Hence, conditioning on
M_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\text{blanket}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(S_{M_{0}})_{+}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(S_{M_{0}})_{+}\bigm|M_{0}\bigr]\,\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,M_{0}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{1}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{M_{0}}>0\}\bigm|M_{0}\bigr]\bigr].

Now recall that the size-biased version of a nonnegative integer-valued
random variable
M_{0}
is defined by

\mathbb{P}(M=m):=\frac{m\,\mathbb{P}(M_{0}=m)}{\mathbb{E}[M_{0}]}\qquad(m\geq 1).

When
M_{0}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,\gamma)
, its size-biased version is

M\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma),

and
\mathbb{E}[M_{0}]=n\gamma
.
Therefore, for any function
g
,

\frac{1}{n\gamma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[M_{0}g(M_{0})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[g(M)\bigr].

Applying this with

g(m)=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{m}>0\}\mid M_{0}=m]
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\text{blanket}}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[X_{1}\,\mathbf{1}\{S_{M}>0\}\bigr],

where now
M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)
and

S_{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}X_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})
.

In other words,

(27)

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\text{blanket}}=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)\\
Y_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\end{subarray}}\Bigl[l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1})\,\mathbf{1}\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigr\}\Bigr].

Step 2: Change of measure on
Y_{1}
.

By definition of
l_{\varepsilon}
,

l_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(y)-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y).

Hence, for any bounded measurable function
h
(possibly depending on
other random variables),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl[l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1})\,h(Y_{1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\int h(y)\,l_{\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int h(y)\,\bigl(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}(y)-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y)\bigr)\,dy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}}[h(Y_{1})]-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}}[h(Y_{1})].

In (
27
), take

h(Y_{1}):=\mathbf{1}\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigr\},

and treat
M
and
Y_{2:n}
as part of the outer expectation.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\text{blanket}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{M,Y_{2:n}}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigl[l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1})\,\mathbf{1}\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\}\bigm|M,Y_{2:n}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{M,Y_{2:n}}\Bigl[\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigm|M,Y_{2:n}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigm|M,Y_{2:n}\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\\
Y_{2:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\\
M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}\\
Y_{2:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\\
M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i})>0\Bigr].

This is exactly the desired expression.
∎

A.6.
The proof of Theorem
5

See
4.2

Proof.

This proof is an adaptation of the FFT error analysis of Gopi et al.
(
12
)
to our blanket-divergence setting.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
,
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, and distinct inputs

x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Let
M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)
and

Y_{2:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},

all independent of
M
.
Define

(28)

p(x):=\Pr_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\Bigl[l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{1};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})>0\Bigr].

We write
l(Y_{i})
as a shorthand for

l_{\varepsilon}(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})
in the following.
From the transformation lemma (i.e., Lemma
4.1
), the blanket divergence can be written as

D_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\mathrm{blanket}}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})\;=\;p(x_{1})-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}p(x_{1}^{\prime}).

Our goal is to compare each
p(x)
with the corresponding main-term approximation

P(c,F_{x})
output by the algorithms, and then combine the two inequalities.

Step 1: Truncation error.

Fix constants
s,w^{\mathrm{in}}
and,
for
i\geq 2
, let

Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}\sim l(Y_{i})\ \text{conditioned on}\ l(Y_{i})\in\bigl[s,s+w^{\mathrm{in}}\bigr].

Then for each
x\in\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
we have

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{M}l(Y_{i})>0\Bigr]

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>0\Bigr]+\Pr\bigl[\perp\bigr],

(30)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>0\Bigr]-\Pr\bigl[\perp\bigr].

We abbreviate the truncation error by

E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x):=\Pr\bigl[\perp\bigr].

Step 2: Discretization error.

Fix a bin width
h>0
, and for
i\geq 2
let
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
be a discretized version of

Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
on the grid
\mathcal{G}=\{x_{j}=jh:j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
such that

|Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}|\leq h/2
almost surely.
Let

\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}:=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\Bigr].

Then using the union bound for any
c\geq 0
we can write

\displaystyle\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>0\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>-l(Y_{1})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}>-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}>c\Bigr]+\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}>-c-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\Bigr].

We therefore define the discretization error

E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}}):=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}>c\Bigr]

and obtain the upper bound

(31)

\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>0\Bigr]\;\leq\;E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})+\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}>-c-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\Bigr].

A symmetric argument, using a window shifted by
-c
, yields a corresponding lower bound

(32)

\Pr\Bigl[l(Y_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}>0\Bigr]\;\geq\;-E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})+\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}>+c-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\Bigr].

Step 3: Aliasing error and FFT main term.

Fix an outer window size
w^{\mathrm{out}}>0
and define

[-L^{\mathrm{out}},L^{\mathrm{out}}]:=[-w^{\mathrm{out}}/2,w^{\mathrm{out}}/2]
.
We extend the PMF of the discretized variable
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
by zero outside

[-L^{\mathrm{out}},L^{\mathrm{out}}]
and use the FFT on a grid of size
N
such that
the period
P=2L^{\mathrm{out}}
satisfies
P/m=h
for some integer
m
.
This exactly corresponds to computing the distribution of the random sum

S:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{di}},

where
\widetilde{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
is the zero-padded periodic extension of
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
.
The difference between
S
and the true discretized sum

\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
is the aliasing error; we define

E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}}):=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\neq S\Bigr].

Then using the union bound for the term appearing in (
31
) we have

\displaystyle\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}>-c-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\neq S\Bigr]+\Pr\bigl[S>-c-l(Y_{1})-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\bigr]

(33)

\displaystyle=E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})+\sum_{z}\Pr[S=z]\,\Pr\bigl[l(Y_{1})>-c-z-\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}]\bigr],

where in the last equality we have simply re-centered the threshold by the mean of

\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
, which only affects
\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}
.

Let
F_{x}
be the CDF of
l(Y)
under
Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}
.
Then the inner probability in (
33
) is

\Pr\bigl[l(Y_{1})>-c-z-\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}]\bigr]=1-F_{x}\bigl(-c-z-\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}]\bigr).

By construction, the algorithm
CalculatePMF
computes the PMF

\mathbf{p}_{S-\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}}
of the centered sum
S-\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}
, where

\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}:=(n-1)\gamma\,\mu_{Z^{\mathrm{di}}},

and
CalculateMainTerm
computes

\widehat{p}_{c}(x):=\textsc{CalculateMainTerm}(\mathbf{p}_{S-\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}},F_{x},c)=\sum_{j}\mathbf{p}_{S-\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}}[j]\,\Bigl(1-F_{x}\bigl(-c-z_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}]\bigr)\Bigr),

which coincides with the right-hand probability in (
33
).
Thus, combining (
31
) and (
33
) with the truncation step
(
29
), we obtain

(34)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{p}_{c}(x)+E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})+E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x).

A completely symmetric argument, using
-c
instead of
+c
in the union bounds,
gives a second quantity

\widehat{p}_{-c}(x)=\textsc{CalculateMainTerm}(\mathbf{p}_{S-\mu_{S}},F_{x},-c)
such that

(35)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\geq\widehat{p}_{-c}(x)-E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})-E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})-E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x).

Step 4: Incorporating the factor
(1-\Pr[\perp])
.

In the theorem statement, the main term is defined as

P(c,F_{x}):=(1-\Pr[\perp])\,\widehat{p}_{c}(x),\qquad P(-c,F_{x}):=(1-\Pr[\perp])\,\widehat{p}_{-c}(x),

where
\Pr[\perp]=E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x)
is the truncation probability (note that
\perp

depends only on
(Y_{i})_{i\geq 2}
, hence is the same for
x_{1}
and
x_{1}^{\prime}
).

Since
0\leq\widehat{p}_{\pm c}(x)\leq 1
, we have

\widehat{p}_{c}(x)-E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x)\;\leq\;P(c,F_{x})\;\leq\;\widehat{p}_{c}(x),

and similarly for
-c
.
Therefore, from (
34
) we obtain

(36)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\leq P(c,F_{x})+E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x),

and from (
35
)

(37)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\geq P(-c,F_{x})-E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x;h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})-E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})-2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x).

Step 5: Combining the two hypotheses.

Apply (
37
) with
x=x_{1}
and (
36
) with
x=x_{1}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle p(x_{1})

\displaystyle\geq P(-c,F_{x_{1}})-E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})-E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})-2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}),

\displaystyle p(x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq P(c,F_{x_{1}^{\prime}})+E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime}),

where we have suppressed the explicit dependence on
(c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})

in the notation of the error terms.

Hence

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\mathrm{blanket}}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=p(x_{1})-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}p(x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq P(-c,F_{x_{1}})-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}P(c,F_{x_{1}^{\prime}})-\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}(c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}}),

where

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}:=E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr).

Similarly, apply (
36
) with
x=x_{1}
and (
37
) with
x=x_{1}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle p(x_{1})

\displaystyle\leq P(c,F_{x_{1}})+E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}),

\displaystyle p(x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq P(-c,F_{x_{1}^{\prime}})-E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime})-E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})-2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime}),

so that

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}^{\mathrm{blanket}}(\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}\|\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=p(x_{1})-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}p(x_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq P(c,F_{x_{1}})-\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}P(-c,F_{x_{1}^{\prime}})+\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}(c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}}),

where

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}:=E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr).

By construction, both
\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}
and

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}
are nonnegative and depend only on the numerical
parameters
(c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})
and on the distributions

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}},\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}
through the truncation,
discretization, and aliasing probabilities.

See Section
C
for a discussion of how to rigorously control these error terms in practice.

This proves the desired two-sided bounds.
∎

A.7.
The proof of Theorem
4.3

See
4.3

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}\in[L_{n},U_{n}]
from Theorem
5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl|\widehat{D}_{n}-\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{U_{n}-L_{n}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(P(c;x_{1})-P(-c;x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\bigl\{P(c;x_{1}^{\prime})-P(-c;x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr\}\Bigr)+\frac{\delta^{\mathrm{up}}_{\mathrm{err}}+\delta^{\mathrm{low}}_{\mathrm{err}}}{2}

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl|P(c;x_{1})-P(-c;x_{1})\bigr|+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\bigl|P(c;x_{1}^{\prime})-P(-c;x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr|+\frac{\delta^{\mathrm{up}}_{\mathrm{err}}+\delta^{\mathrm{low}}_{\mathrm{err}}}{2}.

In particular, the total approximation error decomposes into a main
term, given by the differences
P(c;\cdot)-P(-c;\cdot)
, and the
numerical error terms
\delta^{\mathrm{up}}_{\mathrm{err}}
and
\delta^{\mathrm{low}}_{\mathrm{err}}

(truncation, discretization, and aliasing).
We bound each term in (
38
) relative to
the true blanket divergence
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}
.

We first consider the main term.
Recall that
M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)
and

Y_{2:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
, independent of
M
.
Let

S_{n-1}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}\bigl(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr),

and let
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
denote the truncated, discretized, and
periodized version of
S_{n-1}
that is actually used in the FFT
computation.
Moreover, for
x\in\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
we write

Z_{1}:=l_{\varepsilon}\bigl(Y_{1};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr),\qquad Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x},

and
\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}
is defined as in the proof of
Theorem
5
(i.e.,
\mu_{S^{\mathrm{tr}}}-\mu_{S^{\mathrm{di}}}
).
With this notation, the main term appearing in (
38
)
can be expressed as

(39)

\displaystyle P(c;x)-P(-c;x)

\displaystyle=(1-\Pr[\perp])\,\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[-c<\widetilde{S}_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\leq c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr],

where
\perp
is the truncation event defined in
Theorem
5
.
In other words, the quantity
P(c;x)-P(-c;x)
is precisely the probability
that the random sum
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}
falls
inside the band
(-c,c)
, up to the multiplicative factor
(1-\Pr[\perp])
.

The approximating sum
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
is somewhat inconvenient to
analyze directly, so we next replace it by the true sum
S_{n-1}
at the
price of an additional error term.
Let

\widetilde{S}_{n-1}=S_{n-1}+E_{n-1},\qquad E_{n-1}:=\widetilde{S}_{n-1}-S_{n-1},

where
E_{n-1}
collects the truncation, discretization, and aliasing
errors.
Fix
c>0
and define the good event

G:=\bigl\{|E_{n-1}|\leq c/2\bigr\},\qquad G^{c}:=\bigl\{|E_{n-1}|>c/2\bigr\}.

Then, for any realization of
Z_{1}
and on the event
G
, the inclusion

\bigl\{-c<\widetilde{S}_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\leq c\bigr\}\subset\bigl\{|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<c+|E_{n-1}|\bigr\}\subset\bigl\{|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c\bigr\}

holds.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl[-c<\widetilde{S}_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\leq c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]

(40)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]+\Pr\bigl[|E_{n-1}|>c/2\bigr].

Inserting this bound into (
39
) and taking the
expectation over
Z_{1}
yields

(41)

\displaystyle|P(c;x)-P(-c;x)|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]+\Pr\bigl[|E_{n-1}|>c/2\bigr].

Here, we used
1-\Pr[\perp]\leq 1
.

Next we further decompose the numerical error term

\Pr[|E_{n-1}|>c/2]
into truncation, discretization, and wrap-around
(aliasing) contributions.

Recall that

S_{n-1}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}l_{\varepsilon}\bigl(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)

is the true sum, and that
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
denotes the truncated,
discretized, and periodized version of
S_{n-1}
used in the FFT
computation. As before we write

\widetilde{S}_{n-1}=S_{n-1}+E_{n-1},\qquad E_{n-1}:=\widetilde{S}_{n-1}-S_{n-1}.

We view the construction of
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
as a sequence of three
approximations:

•

Truncation:

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}},

where
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
is coupled with
l(Y_{i})
such that
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}=l(Y_{i})
whenever
l(Y_{i})\in[s,s+w^{\mathrm{in}}]
.

•

Discretization:

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}},

where
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
is the discretized version of

Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
on the grid
\mathcal{G}=\{x_{j}=jh:j\in\mathbb{Z}\}

with
|Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}|\leq h/2
almost surely.

•

Wrap-around (aliasing):

\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
is obtained from
S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}
by
periodic extension outside the outer window

[-w^{\mathrm{out}}/2,w^{\mathrm{out}}/2]
.

Accordingly, we decompose the total error into three components:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}

\displaystyle:=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}-S_{n-1},

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}

\displaystyle:=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}-S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}},

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{Malias}}

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{S}_{n-1}-S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}},

so that

E_{n-1}=E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}+E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}+E_{\mathrm{Malias}}.

By the triangle inequality we have

|E_{n-1}|\leq|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|+|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|+|E_{\mathrm{Malias}}|.

Hence, for any
c>0
,

\Bigl\{|E_{n-1}|>\tfrac{c}{2}\Bigr\}\subset\Bigl\{|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>\tfrac{c}{6}\Bigr\}\,\cup\,\Bigl\{|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>\tfrac{c}{6}\Bigr\}\,\cup\,\Bigl\{|E_{\mathrm{Malias}}|>\tfrac{c}{6}\Bigr\},

and by the union bound this yields

(42)

\Pr\bigl[|E_{n-1}|>c/2\bigr]\leq\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c/6\bigr]+\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c/6\bigr]+\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Malias}}|>c/6\bigr].

This decomposes the tail probability of the total numerical error into
three contributions corresponding to truncation, discretization, and
wrap-around, respectively.
In particular, since the error terms

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}
and

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}
in
Theorem
5
are also built from these three errors, we evaluate each of them separately in the sequel.

Recall that the error terms in Theorem
5
are
given by

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}=\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}:=E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})+2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime})\bigr),

where, for brevity, we suppress the dependence of

E_{\mathrm{alias}},E_{\mathrm{disc}},E_{\mathrm{trunc}}
on

(c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},w^{\mathrm{out}})
.
Thus
\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}=\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}
, and

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}

\displaystyle:=2E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1})+2\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(x_{1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}

\displaystyle:=E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{alias}}

\displaystyle:=E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1})+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}E_{\mathrm{alias}}(x_{1}^{\prime}).

Combining this with (
41
) and
(
38
), and using that the bound on

\Pr[|E_{n-1}|>c/2]
does not depend on
x
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\bigl|\widehat{D}_{n}-\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}^{\prime}}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\,\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c/6\bigr]+\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\,\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c/6\bigr]+\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}

(43)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon}}{2}\,\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Malias}}|>c/6\bigr]+\delta_{\mathrm{alias}}.

In other words, the total approximation error splits into a
main
band
term (the first two lines of (
43
)) plus
three groups of numerical error:

•

truncation error:

\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}
and

\Pr[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c/6]
,

•

discretization error:

\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}
and

\Pr[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c/6]
,

•

aliasing (wrap-around) error:

\delta_{\mathrm{alias}}
and

\Pr[|E_{\mathrm{Malias}}|>c/6]
.

Thus each of the three numerical effects (truncation, discretization,
and wrap-around) contributes via a
\delta
-part (coming from
Theorem
5
) and an
E_{\mathrm{M}}
-part
coming from the replacement of
\widetilde{S}_{n-1}
by the true sum

S_{n-1}
in the main term.

Step 1: Main-term error and choice of
c_{n}
.

We first evaluate the main term using the following lemma.

Lemma A.10 (Band probability).

Assume the setting of Lemma
3.2
, and suppose that the local
randomizer
\mathcal{R}
satisfies Assumption
2.8
.

Let
(c_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence with
c_{n}>0
such that

\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n-1}}\longrightarrow 0,

and suppose that

\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}=O\left(\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right).

Write

t_{n}:=-\frac{\mu_{n}\sqrt{n-1}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}}.

Then there exists a constant
C<\infty
, depending only on the
constants in Assumption
2.8
, such that for all
sufficiently large
n
and for every
x\in\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
,

(44)

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c_{n}\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]\;\leq\;C\,\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n-1}}\,\varphi(t_{n}).

We prove this lemma in Section
A.7.1
.
We now evaluate the main-term error.
Recall from Lemma
A.10
that for any sequence

c_{n}>0
satisfying
c_{n}/(\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n-1})\to 0
we have
for each
x\in\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
,

(45)

\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}}\Bigl[\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}+Z_{1}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|<\tfrac{3}{2}c_{n}\,\big|\,Z_{1}\bigr]\Bigr]\;\leq\;C\,\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n-1}}\,\varphi(t_{n}),

for some constant
C<\infty
independent of
n
.
Inserting this bound into the midpoint error decomposition
(
43
) yields

(46)

\bigl|\text{(main term in \eqref{eq:full-error-by-type})}\bigr|\;=\;O\!\left(\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n}}\,\varphi(t_{n})\right).

On the other hand, by the asymptotic expansion of the blanket divergence,
we have

(47)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}\;=\;\varphi\bigl(t_{n}\bigr)\,\left(\frac{1}{\chi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}\right)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

where
\chi>0
is a constant.

Dividing (
46
) by (
47
), and
using that
\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\to\sigma>0
as
n\to\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\bigl|\text{(main term)}\bigr|}{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{\dfrac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n}}\varphi(t_{n})}{\varphi(t_{n})\,\dfrac{1}{\chi^{3}}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}}\right)(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=O\!\left(c_{n}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n\,\frac{\chi^{3}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}}\right)(1+o(1))

(48)

\displaystyle=O\!\left(c_{n}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n\right),

where the last step uses that
\chi^{3}/\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}
is bounded
uniformly in
n
.

We would like the contribution of the main term to the overall relative
error to be at most
\eta_{\mathrm{main}}
, that is,

\frac{\bigl|\text{(main term)}\bigr|}{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{\mathrm{main}}.

From (
48
), a sufficient condition is

(49)

c_{n}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n\;\lesssim\;\eta_{\mathrm{main}}.

Equivalently, we may choose
c_{n}
of order

(50)

c_{n}\;=\;\Theta\!\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{main}}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n}\right).

With such a choice, the main-term contribution to the relative error is

O(\eta_{\mathrm{main}})
.

Finally, we check that the technical assumption of
Lemma
A.10
,
namely
c_{n}/(\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n})\to 0
, is satisfied.
Using (
50
), we have

\frac{c_{n}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon_{n}}\sqrt{n}}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{main}}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}\right).

Under our standing assumption
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n\to\infty

(e.g.
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=\Theta(\log n/n)
), the right-hand side tends
to
0
, so the condition of
Lemma
A.10
indeed holds.

In particular, in the canonical regime

\varepsilon_{n}^{2}=\Theta(\log n/n)
, we obtain the more explicit scaling

c_{n}\;=\;\Theta\!\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{main}}}{\log n}\right).

Step 2: Truncation error and choice of
(s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})
.

Recall the truncation event

\perp:=\Bigl\{\exists i\in\{2,\dots,n\}:B_{i}=1,\ l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})\notin[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]\Bigr\},

and write

E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(n;s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}):=\Pr[\perp].

Moreover, let

S_{n-1}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i};x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}),\qquad S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}},

where
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
are i.i.d. copies of the truncated random
variable used in the FFT construction, and define

E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}:=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}-S_{n-1}.

We control
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}
and
\Pr[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6]

in terms of the blanket divergence.

Control of
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}
.

Denote by

\tau_{n}:=\max\{|s_{n}|,\ |s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}|\}

the truncation radius.
Assumption
2.8
guarantees that
l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y)

has finite moments of all integer orders.
In particular, for every
p\geq 2
there exists a constant

C_{p}<\infty
such that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y)|^{p}\bigr]\leq C_{p}\qquad\text{for all }n.

By Markov’s inequality, this implies a polynomial tail bound

(51)

\Pr\bigl[|l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y)|>T\bigr]\;\leq\;C_{p}\,T^{-p},\qquad T>0.

Using the binomial structure of
M-1
and a union bound, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(n;s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigl[\perp\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\Bigl[\bigcup_{i=2}^{n}\bigl\{B_{i}=1,\ l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i})\notin[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]\bigr\}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq K_{\gamma}\,n\,\Pr\bigl[|l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y)|>\tau_{n}\bigr]

(52)

\displaystyle\leq K_{\gamma}C_{p}\,n\,\tau_{n}^{-p},

for some constant
K_{\gamma}<\infty
depending only on
\gamma
.

Dividing (
52
) by the blanket divergence, we get

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(n;s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})}{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}

\displaystyle\leq K^{\prime}_{\gamma,p}\,n\,\tau_{n}^{-p}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}}{\varphi(t_{n})}(1+o(1))

(53)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}_{\gamma,p}\,\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{5/2}\,\frac{\tau_{n}^{-p}}{\varphi(t_{n})}(1+o(1)).

In the regime of interest (in particular when
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n
and

t_{n}
grow at most logarithmically in
n
), the prefactor

\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}/\varphi(t_{n})
grows at most polynomially in
n
.
Hence we can choose
p
large enough and a polynomially growing sequence

\tau_{n}
such that the right-hand side of
(
53
) is arbitrarily small.
For concreteness, fix
p\geq 4
and set

(54)

\tau_{n}:=n^{\beta}(\log n)^{1/p}

with some
\beta>0
sufficiently large (depending on
p
and on the
growth rate of
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n^{3/2}/\varphi(t_{n})
).
Then there exists
n_{0}
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

(55)

\frac{E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(n;s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})}{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}\leq\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}}{4},

where
\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}>0
is the prescribed accuracy knob.

Control of
\Pr\left[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6\right]
.

Recall that
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}
is defined as
l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i})
conditioned on the truncation interval.
Under the natural coupling where
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}=l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i})
whenever
l_{\varepsilon_{n}}(Y_{i})\in[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]
, we have

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}=S_{n-1}\quad\text{whenever the event }\perp^{c}\text{ occurs}.

Consequently, on the event
\perp^{c}
, the error
E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}-S_{n-1}
is identically zero.
This implies the inclusion

\bigl\{|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr\}\;\subset\;\bigl\{E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}\neq 0\bigr\}\;\subset\;\perp.

Therefore, regardless of the value of
c_{n}
, we have the simple bound

(56)

\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\;\leq\;\Pr[\perp]\;=\;E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(n;s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}).

Using the bound (
55
) derived in the previous step, we immediately obtain

(57)

\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\;\leq\;\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}}{4}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}.

Combining (
55
) and (
57
), and recalling that
\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}
in (
43
) is a linear combination of
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}
terms, we conclude that the total truncation contribution is bounded by

\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}+\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mtrunc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{trunc}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}

for a suitable constant
C_{\mathrm{trunc}}
.
Thus, the truncation contribution to the overall relative error is
O(\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}})
.

Step 3: Discretization error and choice of
h_{n}
.

On the truncated interval
[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]
we discretize
the privacy amplification random variable

Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}

into a grid with mesh size
h_{n}>0
, obtaining

Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
such that

|Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}|\leq\frac{h_{n}}{2}\quad\text{almost surely.}

We denote

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}},\qquad S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}},

and write the discretization error of the sum as

E^{\mathrm{disc}}:=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}-S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}=\sum_{i=2}^{M}\bigl(Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}\bigr).

Following the notation in the proof of
Theorem
5
, we set

\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}:=\mathbb{E}[E^{\mathrm{disc}}],\qquad U_{n}:=E^{\mathrm{disc}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}.

By the hypothesis of Theorem
4.3
we assume
that there exists a constant
K_{\mathrm{ES}}<\infty
such that

(58)

|\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|\leq K_{\mathrm{ES}}\,c_{n}\qquad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.

Reducing the constant in the definition of
c_{n}
if necessary, we may
and do assume that for
n
large enough

(59)

|\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|\leq\frac{c_{n}}{12}.

Control of
E_{\mathrm{disc}}
.

Recall that in Step 2 of the proof of
Theorem
5
the discretization error for a
fixed hypothesis
x\in\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
was defined as

E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c_{n},h_{n},s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}):=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}>c_{n}\Bigr].

Equivalently, if we set

R_{n}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}\bigl(Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}\bigr),

then, we have
E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c_{n},h_{n},s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})=\Pr[R_{n}>c_{n}]
.
The summands of
R_{n}
are conditionally independent, mean zero, and
bounded in absolute value by a constant multiple of
h_{n}
, while

M-1
has mean
(n-1)\gamma
and sub-Gaussian tails.
Hence
R_{n}
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy of order
nh_{n}^{2}
.
By Hoeffding’s inequality there exists a constant
c>0
such that

(60)

\Pr\left[R_{n}>c_{n}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(-c\,\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{nh_{n}^{2}}\right).

Fix a discretization accuracy knob
\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}>0
and define

\Lambda_{h}:=\log\frac{4}{\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}.

We choose

(61)

h_{n}:=\frac{c_{n}}{\sqrt{c\,n\,\Lambda_{h}}}.

Then (
60
) yields

E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c_{n},h_{n},s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})\leq\exp(-\Lambda_{h})=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}}{4}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}.

In particular, since
\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}
in
Theorem
5
is a linear combination of

E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1};\cdot)
and
E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x_{1}^{\prime};\cdot)

with nonnegative coefficients depending only on
\varepsilon_{n}
,
we obtain

\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{disc},1}\,\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}

for some constant
C_{\mathrm{disc},1}>0
.

Under our standing regime
\varepsilon_{n}^{2}n=\Theta(\log n)
we
have
\log(1/\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}})=\Theta(\log n)
.
Consequently

\Lambda_{h}=\log\frac{4}{\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}}=\Theta(\log n),

and in particular
h_{n}
in (
61
) satisfies

h_{n}=\Theta\left(\frac{c_{n}}{\sqrt{n\log n}}\right)

up to multiplicative constants.

Control of
E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}
.

In the decomposition (
42
) we introduced the
random variable

E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}:=S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}-S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}=E^{\mathrm{disc}},

which measures the effect of discretization at the level of the sum

S_{n-1}
when passing from
S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{tr}}
to

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}
.
We control the tail probability

\Pr\left[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c_{n}/6\right]
using the decomposition

E^{\mathrm{disc}}=U_{n}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}
.
Here,
U_{n}:=E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}-\mathbb{E}[E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}]=E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}
.

By (
59
), for all sufficiently large
n
we have

|\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|\leq c_{n}/12
, and therefore

\bigl\{|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr\}=\bigl\{|U_{n}-\Delta_{\mathrm{ES}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr\}\subset\bigl\{|U_{n}|>c_{n}/12\bigr\}.

Consequently,

(62)

\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\leq\Pr\bigl[|U_{n}|>c_{n}/12\bigr].

The random variable
U_{n}
is a sum of conditionally independent,
mean-zero increments, each bounded by
h_{n}
in absolute value.
Applying Hoeffding’s inequality once more, we obtain

(63)

\Pr\bigl[|U_{n}|>c_{n}/12\bigr]\leq 2\exp\!\Bigl(-\kappa\,\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{nh_{n}^{2}}\Bigr),

for some constant
\kappa>0
.
With
h_{n}
chosen as in (
61
), the right-hand side of
(
63
) is bounded by

2\exp(-\kappa\Lambda_{h})\leq\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}}{4}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}

for all sufficiently large
n
, after possibly adjusting the numerical
constant in the definition of
\Lambda_{h}
.
Combining this with (
62
), we conclude that

(64)

\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\leq\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}}{4}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}.

Putting together the bounds for
E_{\mathrm{disc}}
and

E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}
, and recalling that the discretization contribution
in (
43
) is a linear combination of these
quantities with constants depending only on
\varepsilon_{n}
and

\gamma
, we obtain that there exists a constant
C_{\mathrm{disc}}>0

such that

\delta_{\mathrm{disc}}+\Pr\bigl[|E_{\mathrm{Mdisc}}|>c_{n}/6\bigr]\leq C_{\mathrm{disc}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}\,\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}.

In particular, the discretization contribution to the overall relative
error is
O(\eta_{\mathrm{disc}})
.

Step 4: Aliasing error and choice of
w_{n}^{\mathrm{out}}
.

We now control the aliasing (wrap-around) error arising from the finite
FFT window. Recall that after truncation and discretization we consider
the sum

S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}},

where the
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
are i.i.d. copies of the discretized
summand on
[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]
.
By construction, each
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
is supported in an interval of
radius at most
\tau_{n}
, hence

|Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}|\leq\tau_{n}
almost surely.
Assumption
2.8
further implies that

\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}})
is bounded away from zero and infinity
uniformly in
n
, so that

v_{n}:=\mathrm{Var}\bigl(S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}\bigr)=\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\Bigr)=\Theta(n).

Let
\mu_{n}^{\mathrm{di}}:=\mathbb{E}[S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}]
.
By Bernstein’s inequality, for any
u>0
,

(65)

\Pr\bigl[|S_{n-1}^{\mathrm{di}}-\mu_{n}^{\mathrm{di}}|>u\bigr]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2\bigl(v_{n}+\tau_{n}u/3\bigr)}\right).

From the choice of
p
in Step 2 we have

(66)

\tau_{n}=o(\sqrt{n}),

so that for
u
of order
\sqrt{v_{n}\Lambda_{w}}
the second term in the
denominator of (
65
) is negligible compared to

v_{n}
.
More precisely, define

\Lambda_{w}:=\log\frac{4}{\eta_{\mathrm{alias}}\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}},

where
0<\eta_{\mathrm{alias}}<1
is a prescribed accuracy parameter
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{blanket}}
is the blanket divergence at level

\varepsilon_{n}
.
Set

u_{n}:=\sqrt{C_{w}\,v_{n}\,\Lambda_{w}}

for a sufficiently small constant
C_{w}>0
.
Then, using

\sigma_{\mathrm{BG}}^{2}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}):=\mathrm{Var}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BG}})\bigr)=2k(p-q)^{2}.

By definition,

\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{\mathrm{BG}}^{2}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})}}=\sqrt{\frac{q}{2(p-q)^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{0}}+k-1}{2\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{0}}-1\bigr)^{2}}}.

In particular,
\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
does not depend on the
particular choice of the distinct pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
, and hence

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}=\inf_{x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}}\chi_{\mathrm{lo}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})=\sqrt{\frac{q}{2(p-q)^{2}}}.

Upper shuffle index.

For the upper shuffle index we take the reference distribution to be one of
the local randomizers
\mathcal{R}_{x}
. Fix
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}
, and consider

l_{0}(y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x})=\frac{\phi_{x_{1}}(y)-\phi_{x_{1}^{\prime}}(y)}{\phi_{x}(y)},\qquad Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{x}.

By symmetry, the variance only depends on how
x
is positioned relative to

\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
.

•

If
x=x_{1}
(or symmetrically
x=x_{1}^{\prime}
), then

l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}})=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p-q}{p},&Y=x_{1},\\[4.0pt]
-\dfrac{p-q}{q},&Y=x_{1}^{\prime},\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

so that

\mathrm{Var}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x_{1}})\bigr)=(p-q)^{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\Bigr).

•

If
k\geq 3
and
x\notin\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
, then

\mathrm{Var}\bigl(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x})\bigr)=2\,\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{q}.

Since for
p>q
we have

2\,\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{q}\;>\;(p-q)^{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\Bigr),

the variance is maximized (and hence
\chi
is minimized) by choosing

x\notin\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}\}
whenever
k\geq 3
. Because
\gamma=1
for

\mathcal{R}_{x}
, we obtain, for
k\geq 3
,

\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})=\inf_{x\in[k]}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}(l_{0}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{x}))}}=\sqrt{\frac{q}{2(p-q)^{2}}}

Thus,
\chi_{\mathrm{up}}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
also does not depend on the particular
choice of the distinct pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
, and hence, for
k
-randomized response with
k\geq 3
, the lower and upper
shuffle indices coincide:

\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{up}}=\underline{\chi}_{\mathrm{lo}}=\sqrt{\frac{q}{2(p-q)^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{0}}+k-1}{2\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon_{0}}-1\bigr)^{2}}}.

Appendix C
Implementations of FFT algorithms

C.1.
Parameters

The error terms in Theorem
5
admit explicit and
rigorous upper bounds.
Let

M\sim 1+\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,\gamma)\quad\text{as}\quad M=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i},

where
B_{2},\dots,B_{n}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\gamma)
variables,
independent of the blanket samples. In particular,

\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i}Z_{i},

so every error event can be expressed as a tail event of a sum of independent variables of the form
B_{i}Z_{i}
.

(i) Truncation error
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}
.

Let
q:=\Pr[l(Y)\notin[s,s+w^{\mathrm{in}}]]=F_{\mathrm{ref}}(s)+1-F_{\mathrm{ref}}(s+w^{\mathrm{in}})
, where

F_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the blanket CDF. Then the truncation error is

E_{\mathrm{trunc}}:=\Pr\bigl[\perp\bigr]=1-\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1-q)^{M-1}\bigr]=1-(1-\gamma q)^{n-1},

which is computable exactly from
F_{\mathrm{ref}}
and the numerical
parameters
(n,\gamma,s,w^{\mathrm{in}})
. In particular,

E_{\mathrm{trunc}}\leq(n-1)\gamma q
by the union bound.

(ii) Discretization error
E_{\mathrm{disc}}
.

Let
D_{i}:=Z_{i}^{\mathrm{tr}}-Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
and observe that,
by construction,
|D_{i}|\leq h/2
almost surely. Using the selector
representation we may write

\sum_{i=2}^{M}D_{i}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i}D_{i},

where
B_{2},\dots,B_{n}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\gamma)

variables independent of
(D_{i})_{i\geq 2}
. Define

X_{i}:=B_{i}D_{i}-\mathbb{E}[B_{i}D_{i}],\qquad S_{\mathrm{disc}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{n}X_{i}.

Then the variables
X_{i}
are independent and bounded as

|X_{i}|\;\leq\;K_{\mathrm{disc}}:=\frac{h}{2}\quad\text{a.s.}

Moreover, since
|D_{i}|\leq h/2
we have

\mathrm{Var}(X_{i})=\mathrm{Var}(B_{i}D_{i})\leq\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[D_{i}^{2}]\leq\gamma\,\frac{h^{2}}{4}=:v_{\mathrm{disc}}^{2}.

Hence Bernstein’s inequality yields, for any
a>0
,

\Pr\bigl[|S_{\mathrm{disc}}|>a\bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{2(n-1)v_{\mathrm{disc}}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}K_{\mathrm{disc}}a}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{2(n-1)\gamma h^{2}/4+\frac{2}{3}(h/2)a}\right).

Since

E_{\mathrm{disc}}(x;c,h,s,w^{\mathrm{in}}):=\Pr\Bigl[S_{\mathrm{disc}}>c\Bigr]\leq\Pr\bigl[|S_{\mathrm{disc}}|>c\bigr],

we obtain the explicit upper bound.

(iii) Aliasing error
E_{\mathrm{alias}}
.

Similarly, the aliasing error corresponds to the event that the sum of
the discretized blanket variables leaves the outer FFT window. Using
the selector representation,

\sum_{i=2}^{M}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}},

where the truncated discretized variables
Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}
lie in the
bounded interval
[s,s+w^{\mathrm{in}}]
. Setting

Y_{i}:=B_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}-\mathbb{E}[B_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}]
we
obtain independent, bounded variables with
|Y_{i}|\leq K_{\mathrm{alias}}

and variance at most
v_{\mathrm{alias}}^{2}
, both computable from the
truncated blanket distribution and
\gamma
. Bernstein’s inequality
applied to
S_{\mathrm{alias}}:=\sum_{i=2}^{n}Y_{i}
then yields an
explicit exponentially small upper bound of the form

E_{\mathrm{alias}}:=\Pr\Bigl[\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i}Z_{i}^{\mathrm{di}}\notin[\,-w^{\mathrm{out}}/2,\,w^{\mathrm{out}}/2\,]\Bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\frac{(w^{\mathrm{out}}/2)^{2}}{2(n-1)v_{\mathrm{alias}}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}K_{\mathrm{alias}}w^{\mathrm{out}}/2}\right).

That is, the aliasing error can also be explicitly controlled.

In particular, all three error contributions

E_{\mathrm{trunc}},E_{\mathrm{disc}},E_{\mathrm{alias}}
admit explicit,
computable upper bounds in terms of the CDFs

F_{\mathrm{ref}}
and the numerical parameters

(n,\gamma,s,w^{\mathrm{in}},h,w^{\mathrm{out}})
. Hence the total
error terms
\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}
and

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}
in
Theorem
5
can be made as small as desired by
a suitable choice of these knobs.

Here, we outline a systematic procedure for selecting the FFT
parameters given
\alpha,\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}},\eta_{\mathrm{disc}},\eta_{\mathrm{alias}},\eta_{\mathrm{main}}
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we may write the blanket
divergence in the form
12
12

12

We use (
13
) to be more precise.

D^{\mathrm{blanket}}_{\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon},n,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}},\gamma}=D_{n}(\varepsilon)\,\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Set

\varepsilon_{n}=\frac{\alpha}{\chi}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}.

Moreover, Lemma
A.10
yields

E_{\mathrm{main}}(c_{n})=\eta_{\mathrm{main}}\,D_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})

for the choice

c_{n}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4\alpha^{2}}\,\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{main}}}{\log n},

which is obtained by solving for
c_{n}
from the leading term in
(

Note that
E_{\mathrm{main}}
is not a numerical approximation error term
(in particular, it is not included in

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{up}}
or

\delta_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathrm{low}}

E_{\mathrm{main}}

D_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})
.

We then choose the remaining FFT parameters in the following order.

(1)

Truncation window.

First we fix truncation parameters
s_{n}
and
w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}
and let

q_{n}:=\Pr\bigl[l_{\varepsilon}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})\notin[s_{n},s_{n}+w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}}]\bigr].

(s_{n},w_{n}^{\mathrm{in}})

E_{\mathrm{trunc}}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}\,D_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})

\eta_{\mathrm{trunc}}>0

(2)

Discretization step size.

Next we fix a grid width

E_{\mathrm{disc}}\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{\frac{\gamma}{2}\,nh_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\,c_{n}h_{n}}\right),

We then choose
h_{n}
so that this upper bound satisfies

E_{\mathrm{disc}}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}\,D_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})

for a prescribed
\eta_{\mathrm{disc}}>0
.

(3)

Outer FFT window.

Finally, for the aliasing (wrap-around) error we have the bound

E_{\mathrm{alias}}\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{(w_{n}^{\mathrm{out}/2})^{2}}{2(n-1)v_{\mathrm{alias}}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}K_{\mathrm{alias}}w_{n}^{\mathrm{out}}/2}\right),

are fixed as above,

and

can be computed explicitly from the truncated

w_{n}^{\mathrm{out}}
so that

E_{\mathrm{alias}}\leq\eta_{\mathrm{alias}}\,D_{n}(\varepsilon_{n})

for a prescribed
\eta_{\mathrm{alias}}>0
.

C.2.
Computation of CDFs

The algorithms
1
–
2

require as input the CDFs of the privacy amplification random variable.
In the discrete case (e.g.,
k
-randomized response), these CDFs can be
computed by direct enumeration.

In the generalized Gaussian setting, the map
l_{\varepsilon}(y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})
is strictly decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
whenever
13
13

13

This restriction on the parameter range is not essential.
When
y\mapsto l_{\varepsilon}(y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})
is not monotone,
one can still represent the CDF of
l_{\varepsilon}(Y)
as a finite sum
of terms
F_{\mathrm{ref}}(b_{j}(u))-F_{\mathrm{ref}}(a_{j}(u))
over the
finitely many intervals
[a_{j}(u),b_{j}(u)]
on which

l_{\varepsilon}(y)\leq u
, where each endpoint
a_{j}(u),b_{j}(u)
can be
computed by one-dimensional root finding.
However, in the moderate-deviation regime
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0

considered in this paper, the monotonicity condition is satisfied for
all sufficiently large
n
, so we impose it in the main text for
simplicity.

\varepsilon\leq\ln 2-\left(\frac{x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}}{2\alpha}\right)^{\beta},

Under this condition the privacy amplification map is invertible, and
for any reference distribution
Y\sim\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}}
with CDF

F_{\mathrm{ref}}
, the CDF of

X:=l_{\varepsilon}(Y;x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{ref}})

is given by the simple composition formula

F_{X}(x)=\Pr[X\leq x]=\Pr\bigl[Y\geq l_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x)\bigr]=1-F_{\mathrm{ref}}\!\bigl(l_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x)\bigr).

Thus the only remaining technical task is to evaluate the inverse map

l_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
.

For generalized Gaussian mechanisms with shape parameters

\beta=1
(Laplace) and
\beta=2
(Gaussian), the inverse

l_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
can be expressed in closed form, so that

F_{X}(x)
is available analytically (in terms of
\Phi
in the Gaussian
case). For general
\beta\in(1,2)
, the inverse is no longer available
in closed form, but it can be computed numerically by solving a
one-dimensional root-finding problem for each
x
, which yields
arbitrarily accurate values of
F_{X}(x)
at a negligible cost compared
to the FFT step.
