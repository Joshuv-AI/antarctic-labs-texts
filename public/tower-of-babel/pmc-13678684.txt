The challenges of direct Claisen–Schmidt condensation in the synthesis of polyhydroxylated bischalcones: experimental results supported by DFT calculations
Authors: Rui Pereira, Mariela M Nolasco, Vera L M Silva, Eduarda Fernandes, Marine Desage-El Murr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe synthesis of chalcones commonly relies on the Claisen–Schmidt condensation, an attractive reaction owing to its operational simplicity and favorable atom economy. However, its application to acetophenones bearing multiple hydroxy substituents can be problematic, often requiring hydroxy protection and subsequent deprotection, thereby diminishing the sustainability advantages of the approach. In this work, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed at the CAM-B3LYP/6-311++G(d,p) level to provide a qualitative electronic rationale for the factors that may account for this limitation. The nucleophilic behavior of the starting species was examined under basic, acidic, and metal-assisted conditions, using molecular models both with and without the hydroxy groups to evaluate the hypothesis that competing nucleophilic sites could interfere with the desired condensation and hinder the reaction. The results indicated that strong basic conditions favor extensive phenol deprotonation, generating several competing nucleophiles. In this situation, the resulting phenolate species were more nucleophilic than the corresponding enolates, favoring undesired reaction pathways. Coordination of lithium or calcium to the two phenolate groups decreased their nucleophilicity but failed to suppress the unwanted reactivity of the remaining phenolate. In contrast, under acidic conditions, the enol species exhibited greater nucleophilic character than the phenolic groups, providing no support for the proposed competition between phenolates and enolates as the sole explanation for the unsuccessful reaction. Nevertheless, the acidic environment may facilitate isomerization of the bischalcone products, which could subsequently promote degradation and/or undesired cyclization processes. Taken together, experimental and computational results indicate that the failure of the reaction arises from multiple competing factors rather than from a single mechanistic limitation. Among the conditions investigated, the acid-mediated pathway appears to be the most promising starting point for further optimization of the synthesis of polyhydroxylated bischalcones.Keywords: bisacetophenones, bischalcones, Claisen–Schmidt condensation, density functional calculations, Fukui functions, green chemistry

Introduction

The Claisen–Schmidt condensation has been the backbone of chalcones’ (1,3-diarylprop-2-en-1-ones) synthesis for over 100 years [1], following the first report by Claisen in 1881 [2]. This method is a specific case of cross-aldol condensation, in which two different carbonyl-based molecules are mixed, one capable of enolization to act as a nucleophile and the other incapable of the same feat, to act as an acceptor [3–5]. It remains one of the primary methods in organic synthesis for carbon–carbon bond formation, owing to its broad substrate scope and mild reaction conditions, despite its below-average reaction rate and susceptibility to side reactions [6]. Cross-aldol condensation is also a very efficient reaction regarding atom economy and lack of hazardous and toxic reagents, making it an optimal approach in line with green chemistry principles [6–8].

This reaction is based on the chemistry of the carbonyl group, specifically ketones and aldehydes. Keto–enol tautomerism allows these molecules to act as nucleophiles or electrophiles, depending on the environment and catalysts involved [9]. With the right combination of two molecules and the right catalyst, new carbon–carbon bonds form, creating extended molecules. However, the efficiency of this reaction is greatly reduced when using molecules with multiple nucleophilic groups, such as polyhydroxylated compounds. It becomes difficult to control which nucleophile reacts first and where it will react, and many side reactions can occur. In these cases, both the yield and the ease of purification of the reaction products drop considerably.

Due to this obstacle, most reports in the literature avoid the reactivity of these additional nucleophilic groups by using protecting groups to render them temporarily inert and then carrying out the intended cross-aldol condensation [10–13]. Only the added protecting groups are then removed. This strategy has been successful for many decades, allowing chemists to prepare many derivatives that would otherwise be impossible to synthesize. Yet, this adaptation of the synthetic pathway contradicts the initial advantages described for the cross-aldol condensation. The addition of protecting- or deprotecting-group steps severely reduces atom economy and introduces many hazardous and toxic reagents into the synthesis, necessary to introduce or remove these protecting groups [14–15]. Therefore, some authors have devoted their research to finding ways to perform cross-aldol condensation with multiple nucleophilic groups, without the need for protecting groups [16–18].

Chalcones (or 1,3-diarylprop-2-en-1-ones) belong to the flavonoid family and are a major class of natural products commonly found in plants, fruits, vegetables, tea, and other foodstuffs. They are also well recognized for their pharmacological activities [19–20]. The importance of the present research becomes particularly clear for novel chalcone derivatives, especially bischalcones. Bischalcones are a subclass of chalcones with growing interest from a photophysical and medicinal point of view, and thus, efforts to improve their synthesis have been made [21–23]. This is necessary because the efficiency of the cross-aldol condensation with these molecules is even more hindered by the presence of other nucleophiles, and the protection and deprotection reactions contribute to the production of increased amounts of waste and a decrease in yield [21].

Some authors have been successful in carrying out cross-aldol condensation under classic basic or acidic conditions of substrates with a reduced number of hydroxy groups, either one or two, usually hydroxy groups adjacent to carbonyl groups [24]. Other authors use metal-based basic salts to coordinate with the substrates to accelerate the reaction [16–17].

In this context, the initial objective of this work was to establish a successful method for the cross-aldol condensation of a polyhydroxylated bisacetophenone with 4-methoxybenzaldehyde. However, none of the methods and reaction conditions investigated afforded the expected product. Therefore, the subsequent objective was to investigate this reaction and evaluate the hypotheses proposed to explain the experimental observations using DFT calculations. In this study, DFT calculations were employed as qualitative tools to interpret chemical reactivity, providing an electronic-structure rationale for the experimentally observed behaviour and identifying the molecular sites most susceptible to nucleophilic and electrophilic attack. This approach was intended to provide insight into the factors underlying the failure of the tested reaction conditions to yield the desired product.

Computational details

To study the reactivity of the initial molecules under different reaction conditions, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used to assess molecular stability and reactivity descriptors for the molecule in question [27–29]. CAM-B3LYP with 6-311++G(d,p) basis set was the functional used. B3LYP is a generalized hybrid-gradient functional that incorporates a 20% mixture of exact exchange with additional semiempirical adjustments to both semi-local exchange and correlation components [30]. CAM-B3LYP (Coulomb-attenuating method) is an optimization of the B3LYP functional for better predictions of long-range interactions and intramolecular charge transfer [30]. All structures were initially optimized using the CAM-B3LYP functional with 6-311++G(d,p) basis set in the gas phase. A re-optimization was conducted at the same functional and basis set level employing the implicit SMD solvation model with methanol as the chosen solvent [31]. All optimized structures were free of any imaginary frequencies, proving the minimum energy state for the system. In specific cases, a quadratically convergent SCF procedure (SCF=QC) was needed to reach a minimum of energy. Additional information concerning the electronic structures of the optimized geometries was obtained by a natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis [32]. All calculations were performed with the Gaussian 09 program (G09), version A.02 [33], and visualized with GaussView, version 6 [34].

Results and Discussion

In this work, the direct route for the synthesis of bischalcone 3 was the cross-aldol condensation of 1,1'-(2,4,6-trihydroxy-1,3-phenylene)bis(ethan-1-one) (1) with 4-methoxybenzaldhyde (2), represented in Scheme 2. The different reaction conditions tested are described in Table 1.

Attempted syntheses of bischalcone 3.

All methodologies were tested at least twice but failed to yield the desired bischalcone. Base-promoted condensation (KOH, CaCl2/KOH, LiHMDS, and LiOH) (Table 1, reactions i–iv) resulted in the complete consumption of the starting reactant 1, yet no product was detected, only signs of degradation. Using CaO (Table 1, reaction v) led to full recovery of both reactants. The application of Ca- and Li-based bases aimed to limit the reactivity of the initial molecule via metal complexation. While some literature reports describe successful direct cross-aldol condensation between hydroxy ketones and aldehydes using this strategy, all attempts in this case were unsuccessful.

Acid-promoted condensation with BF3·Et2O (Table 1, reactions vi and vii) induced a color change in the reaction mixture, but both starting reactants remained mostly intact, with a greater difference observed at higher temperatures. Reactions in the presence of a solid-supported acid such as Amberlite 15, assisted by ultrasound (Table 1, reaction viii), failed to produce any product, and both reactants were recovered. Reaction with a catalytic amount of p-toluenesulfonic acid (p-TSA) (Table 1, reaction ix) resulted in the complete depletion of compound 1 and the formation of an orange spot at the bottom of the TLC plate, regardless of the eluent system used. Comparison with reference compound 3 showed no presence of it in the reaction mixture. No identifiable product was obtained from the crude reaction mixture, and isolation attempts failed.

Chemical reactivity exploration

Upon verifying that the reaction failed in all the reaction conditions tested, a hypothesis was proposed. The reaction fails because the nucleophilicity of the required atoms for the Claisen–Schmidt condensation is inferior to the nucleophilicity of the remaining hydroxy groups. To test this hypothesis, the chemical reactivity of the optimized initial nucleophile was investigated using Fukui functions calculated with natural bond orbital (NBO) charges, achieved with the Gaussian 09 program (G09), version A.02 [33,43]. These were calculated for each atom through DFT calculations at the CAM-B3LYP/6-311++G(d,p) basis set level of theory within an implicit SMD solvent model for methanol (see Supporting Information File 1). Methanol was the only solvent used for uniformization purposes and to allow comparability between the different nucleophilicities within the same environment.

This study is structured around three main approaches: base-promoted, acid-promoted, and metal-assisted base-promoted condensation. Each approach is first examined using a model reaction with a substrate lacking hydroxy groups, followed by analysis of molecule 1 (Scheme 2) using the same methodology. This hypothesis will be tested in all these reaction conditions by comparing the results with and without additional hydroxy groups.

Base-promoted condensation

Model reaction: This study began with the analysis of the cross-aldol condensation between a model bisketone and an unsubstituted aromatic aldehyde in methanol. The reaction was first conducted under basic conditions, following the method described by L. Claisen and Claparède [2]. The key difference from the classic condensation is the presence of two ketone moieties within the same structure, allowing the reaction to occur twice. The model bisketone was dissolved in methanol, a protic solvent, and potassium hydroxide was added to achieve a concentration of approximately 1.8 M KOH. Methanol was chosen as the solvent because the reagents have low solubility in the aqueous mixture. In solution, hydroxide ions will produce potassium methanolate in an equilibrium reaction (Equation 10), as shown below, which will be the dominant base in solution [44]. The conversion rate of this reaction depends on the ratio of methanol to KOH, but it is always close to full conversion in dilute solutions, so the concentration of KOH added will be similar to the final concentration of potassium methanolate generated [44–45].

The methanolate (MeO−), will remove the acidic protons of bisketone 4, leading to the formation of a double enolate 5 (Scheme 3) [45].

The next step is the addition of benzaldehyde 6, a strong electrophile, to the solution. Benzaldehyde (used as a simplified model) does not contain the methoxy substituent (present in 4-methoxybenzaldehyde used in the performed experiments), which has a clear electronic effect, as it is an electron-donating group that increases the electron density of the aromatic ring and can influence the electrophilicity of the aldehyde carbonyl through resonance and inductive effects. However, it is not expected that this effect fundamentally changes the overall mechanistic picture of the Claisen–Schmidt condensation. The key elementary steps considered in our analysis – enolate formation, nucleophilic addition to the aldehyde, proton-transfer steps, and subsequent dehydration – remain chemically analogous. Thus, one enolate will attack the carbonyl group of the aldehyde, yielding an aldol (Scheme 4) [46].

The main problem with this reaction is the presence of multiple hydroxy groups, all with nucleophilic potential.

As mentioned above, the first step is the formation of enolates. However, in this case, several phenolates can be formed in addition to the enolates. Using DFT calculations with phenol as a reference acid, the predicted pKa of the phenol protons is markedly different from that of the acidic α-protons. The first phenol deprotonation has a pKa of 4.6, and the first α-protons have a pKa of 19.1 (see the methodology explained in the Computational details section). Therefore, the base in solution will produce phenolic protons much sooner than the other protons (Scheme 7). The only way to limit this issue would be to avoid the formation of the phenolates while forming the enolates. Also, an excess of base is required to favor the dehydration step [46], making this control over the amount of base non-viable.

The α-carbons C10 and C13 of the enol are the most nucleophilic sites in the molecule, even compared to the hydroxy groups (Table 5). This shows that the expected addition reaction is possible from a reactivity point of view, meaning that the failure to yield the intended bischalcone may not be related to the initial reactivity of the molecule. Therefore, the hypothesis initially proposed may not be valid for these acid reaction conditions. The problem could lie ahead, particularly with the stability of the bischalcone in these conditions. The formation of the intended bischalcone may be followed by a subsequent isomerization reaction, very common in 2’-hydroxychalcones. Successful examples of acid catalysis in polyhydroxylated chalcone and bischalcone synthesis usually lack any hydroxy groups in the 2-position of the A ring, to avoid this isomerization issue [1,21,50].

The isomerization/cyclization reaction previously mentioned can culminate in the formation of several flavonoid derivatives such as flavanones and deoxyanthocyanidins [51]. However, no such derivatives were isolated from the reaction crude because purification was unsuccessful due to extensive degradation. Since isomerization is catalyzed in an acidic medium, isolating the bischalcone before cyclization was not possible (Scheme 14). Therefore, the problem was not the Claisen–Schmidt condensation but keeping the bischalcone in solution and avoiding isomerization that may be promoted in acidic conditions.

Cyclization of bischalcone 3 in an acid medium.

Although these bischalcone isomerization/cyclization side reactions were experimentally inferred, without explicit calculations of the cyclization pathway and its activation barriers, the computational results do not independently establish that this pathway is responsible for the observed failure.

Metal-assisted base-promoted condensation

The last main strategy employed to synthesize bischalcone 3 directly was the use of metal complexes. As initially stated, there are examples in the literature of successful direct cross-aldol condensation reactions between hydroxy ketones and aldehydes (base-sensitive compounds) catalyzed or assisted by alkali metal salts [16,52–53]. Calcium-based salts, such as CaO and Ca(OH)2, have been reported to be efficient catalysts for this reaction, as have lithium bases [17]. Both are used in equivalent quantities.

Model reaction: The first step for this mechanism is the full deprotonation of all acidic groups. Both metal catalysts share the same mechanism; the basic anion will capture the acidic proton from the α-carbon, while the divalent cation coordinates with the resulting enolate 17 like a Lewis acid (Scheme 15).

Enolate 17 and the metalation step in a basic medium.

The order of these two steps depends on the strength of the bonds formed. Some elements, such as boron, have a short bond length, about 1.4–1.6 Å, and therefore only bind to one oxygen of the molecule. However, calcium and lithium cations can complex with two or even four oxygen atoms [54]. The resulting aldol is further stabilized by coordination of the new hydroxy group with these divalent metals (Scheme 16).

Addition step of the Claisen–Schmidt condensation mechanism in a basic medium with further enol stabilization by the metal cation.

Experimental reaction: For the molecule under study, the reaction was initially attempted with two calcium-based bases, Ca(OH)2 and CaO (Scheme 17)

Attempted synthesis of bischalcone 3 in basic medium in the presence of calcium salts.

In the presence of hydroxy groups in the o-positions relative to the carbonyl group, the calcium cation coordinates with this oxygen atom, shifting and stabilizing the charge in this group, making it less reactive. The predicted complex is the one with two calcium cations coordinated to two carbonyl groups, as this is a known divalent metal (Scheme 18) [55]. During the structure optimization process, a third calcium atom was also positioned near oxygen 18 (Figure 8) for additional coordination. However, since this cation is divalent, it lacked an additional intramolecular position suitable for bonding at the right distance, leading to failure to predict a stable complex structure with three calcium cations.

As we can see in Table 6, the outstanding result is O18, the only oxygen that is not coordinated to a calcium atom. This oxygen has an exceptionally high nucleophilic potential, eclipsing the nucleophilicity of the α-carbons C12 and C15. Calcium acts to reduce the nucleophilicity of the other two phenolates, but the remaining one is much more active as a result, making it by far the most reactive site in the molecule.

Lithium bases (LiOH and LiHMDS) have also been associated with the ability to catalyze cross-aldol condensation, exhibiting similar behavior to Ca2+ [56–57]. It has been known for many decades that lithium bases have a strong affinity for carbonyl and hydroxy groups [58]. The O–Li bond is strong, so the first step in enolate formation involves Li–O bond formation before the proton is removed from the α-position by the basic nitrogen atom of the amide [58–59]. Lithium cations have four coordination sites – two are possible within the same molecule, and those we do not show are occupied by either solvent molecules or another molecule of 11. However, different combinations would be possible between lithium cations and 11, since lithium can bind to phenolates, enolates or both (Scheme 19) [60].

Formation of lithium cation enolates 20 and 21 in basic medium.

Lithium enolates can exist as aggregates (dimers, tetramers, higher aggregates), and their aggregation state and structure are strongly dependent on the solvent and on the presence of coordinating species. In this study, the calculations were performed using the implicit SMD methanol model, which was selected to account for the overall solvent environment while keeping the computational treatment tractable. The main objective of the calculations was primarily to provide a qualitative mechanistic rationale and compare the relative feasibility of the proposed pathways rather than to quantitatively reproduce the aggregation equilibria of lithium enolates in solution.

Two structures, 20 (binding energy = −399.327 kJ·mol−1) and 21 (binding energy = −559.956 kJ·mol−1) were selected as the most stable ones (Figure 9, Table 7 and Table 8), and we assumed two variations of 20 to account for the two possible orientations of the lithium atom that is single-bonded to the phenolate. We also assumed only one monomer with a variable number of coordinated lithium atoms, and we did not consider the possibilities of dimers and other conjugated structures with multiple molecules of 11, as the focus is on the reactivity of a single molecule.

A key challenge in this reaction arises from the differences between our initial complexes and the regular mechanism of action of lithium enolates. Typically, lithium cations coordinate with the two oxygen atoms of the resulting enol after the initial addition step, shifting the equilibrium towards more product formation. However, in this case, the lithium cations are already coordinated with two oxygens before the electrophile addition, reducing their ability to stabilize the resulting enol.

Lithium complexes enhanced stabilization and charge distribution (as shown in Table 8), reducing the nucleophilicity of the phenolates. However, they remain sufficiently nucleophilic to compete with the enolates, as evidenced by the similar reactivity values of the atoms involved. In structure 21, the complex with the lowest binding energy, the α-carbons show higher reactivity than the stabilized lithium phenolates, but phenolates still compete substantially. This makes it difficult for the molecules to selectively follow the correct path, hindering the intended mechanism and leading to other side reactions.

Conclusion

This study focused on the reactivity of the starting trihydroxy bisacetophenone 1 in the Claisen–Schmidt condensation with 4-methoxybenzaldehyde (2). Various experimental conditions (basic, acid, and metal-assisted basic conditions) were tested to synthesize trihydroxy bischalcone 3 directly, but all attempts failed, mostly due to the competitive reactivity of the hydroxy groups. Strategies to mitigate this problem, such as metal chelates or different catalysts, did not solve this issue. Other activation methods, such as ultrasonic waves, were also ineffective, mainly because they do not interfere with the core reaction mechanism. A hypothesis was proposed to explain experimental observations, and DFT calculations were employed as qualitative tools to interpret chemical reactivity. Experimental observations remain the primary basis for the proposed reaction behavior, whereas computational results are used as complementary evidence to rationalize the observed reactivity. Fukui-function and related computational analyses provided a qualitative electronic rationale for the experimentally observed behavior, rather than a quantitative prediction of reaction selectivity or definitive proof of the reaction pathway. Studies involving transition-state calculations, activation barriers, reaction-energy profiles, and explicit solvent molecules will be further necessary to distinguish competing pathways kinetically and elucidate the reaction mechanism.

Still, the results of this work suggest that, under strong basic conditions, full deprotonation of phenol groups led to competition between nucleophiles, with phenolates outperforming enolates, causing side reactions. Lithium and calcium complexation reduced the nucleophilicity of the two phenolates but failed to suppress the unwanted reactivity of the remaining phenolate. In acidic conditions, the enols exhibited superior nucleophilicity to the phenols, not validating our initial hypothesis. However, the acidic conditions may promote bischalcone isomerization, potentially leading to degradation or undesired cyclization and other byproduct formation.

Alternative approaches may yield different results. Exploring different solvents could reduce the nucleophilicity of the hydroxy groups or enhance catalyst solubility. Another less explored hypothesis was the role of heterogeneous catalysis, where surface interactions could alter reactivity differently from homogeneous systems. With acid catalysis, the main obstacle for this reaction might be isomerization/degradation of the bischalcone, and not the success of the Claisen–Schmidt condensation. Therefore, a future successful strategy must avoid the isomerization reaction that naturally occurs in an acid medium or assisted by light. To do so, different solvents could be tested, as well as lower temperatures, or weaker acids could be used to mitigate this effect. By avoiding the classical methods tested in this work and exploring different options, one can strive for a more favorable outcome for this reaction.
