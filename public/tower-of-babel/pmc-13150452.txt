Bridging the Gap Between Vaccination Awareness and Practice in Heart Failure
Authors: Sunki Lee
Published: 2026

Respiratory infection is one of the most important and clinically relevant triggers of heart failure (HF) decompensation.1) In patients with HF, even relatively limited infectious insults may provoke systemic inflammation, worsen hemodynamics, and accelerate clinical deterioration. For this reason, vaccination has increasingly moved beyond the domain of general public health and has become part of contemporary cardiovascular prevention.2) Influenza and pneumococcal vaccination have long been emphasized in HF care, and attention is now expanding toward newer vaccines, including herpes zoster, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).3,4) Yet a familiar question remains: how consistently are these preventive strategies incorporated into routine HF practice?

In this issue of the International Journal of Heart Failure, Yoon et al.5) report a nationwide survey of Korean healthcare professionals regarding awareness, attitudes, and self-reported practice patterns related to vaccination in patients with HF. More than 95% of respondents considered vaccination important or very important, and 86.6% of physicians reported prior experience prescribing or recommending vaccination. Influenza and pneumococcal vaccines were the most consistently recognized and recommended, whereas awareness and reported recommendation of newer vaccines were substantially lower. The survey also identified several practical barriers, particularly limited time during outpatient visits, insufficient patient awareness, concerns about adverse reactions, and financial burden. These findings are clinically meaningful because they highlight not only what clinicians think about vaccination, but also where preventive intent appears to weaken in daily care.

The most important message of this report may be the difference between broad endorsement and more modest routine implementation. Vaccination was often recommended to only a small number of patients per clinic session, and systematic review of vaccination status was inconsistent.6,7) This gap is not unique to vaccination. In HF care, many interventions fail not because the evidence is absent, but because clinical ownership is diffuse and workflow integration is incomplete.8) In that regard, one notable finding is that most respondents regarded the HF specialist as the most appropriate clinician to recommend vaccination.5) This suggests that vaccination is increasingly recognized as part of HF care itself rather than as a task to be fully delegated to other specialties or to primary care alone.

At the same time, the lower uptake of newer vaccines should be interpreted cautiously. Reduced recommendation of herpes zoster, COVID-19, and RSV vaccines may not simply reflect a knowledge deficit in HF care. Some of these vaccines lie at the boundary between HF specific prevention and broader age or comorbidity-based preventive medicine.2,4) Indeed, the survey instrument asks both which vaccines are “recommended for patients with HF” and which are personally recommended in clinical practice, while also acknowledging age-based indications and cost-related considerations. Accordingly, the lower use of newer vaccines may reflect not only incomplete awareness, but also uncertainty regarding clinical ownership, patient selection, timing, and reimbursement.

Several limitations of the present study also deserve emphasis. First, the response rate was low, with 171 responses among 2,515 invited members. In addition, respondents were predominantly cardiologists and HF specialists. Thus, the survey likely reflects the views of a particularly engaged professional subset rather than the full spectrum of real-world practice. Second, the study evaluates self-reported recommendation patterns and perceived patient coverage, not chart-documented behavior or actual patient-level vaccine uptake. Therefore, the findings are best interpreted as a snapshot of awareness, attitudes, and reported practice rather than a direct measure of implementation performance. These limitations do not diminish the value of the report, but they do define the appropriate scope of its conclusions.

Another important contribution of this study is its emphasis on economic friction. Financial burden emerged as a relevant barrier from both patient and provider perspectives, and improvement in government and insurance policy was identified as a potential strategy for increasing uptake. This point is especially important for newer vaccines, which may be more costly and less established in routine cardiovascular care. In such settings, lower uptake may reflect not only educational gaps but also structural barriers related to affordability and reimbursement.3,9) If vaccination is to become a routine component of HF care, these policy-level constraints cannot be ignored.

Ultimately, this study reminds us that preventive strategies in HF matter only when they are translated into routine action. Vaccination already has a strong biologic rationale and growing clinical support. The next challenge is implementation: clarifying clinical ownership, embedding vaccination review into HF clinic workflow, improving patient education, and reducing cost barriers that may disproportionately affect newer vaccines. The field may therefore need to move beyond guideline endorsement alone and toward a more deliberate model of preventive integration in HF care.
