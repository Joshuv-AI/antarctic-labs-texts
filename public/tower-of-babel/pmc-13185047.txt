Bailout valve-in-valve therapy during transcatheter aortic valve replacement in bicuspid and tricuspid aortic stenosis: an observational single-centre study in China
Authors: Yi Zhang, Yanjiani Xu, Xi Wang, Tianyuan Xiong, Yijian Li, Yuan Feng, Mao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBailout valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is a critical rescue strategy for procedural failure, yet evidence regarding its outcomes in bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) anatomy remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective, single-centre study analysed 1597 patients (48.3% BAV) undergoing TAVR. Patients were stratified by the requirement for bailout ViV, which was conducted for significant residual aortic regurgitation (AR) or valve embolisation. Predictors were identified via multivariate logistic regression. Early- and mid-term survival outcomes were compared using Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) via entropy balancing.ResultsBailout ViV was required in 6.20% of patients (BAV: 6.87%; tricuspid aortic valve (TAV): 5.57%). Larger annulus perimeter and significant residual AR after initial deployment were identified as consistent independent predictors of bailout ViV across all cohorts. Additionally, lower annulus calcification volume, non-repositionable self-expanding valves and the learning phase were predictors in the overall cohort. Significant mitral regurgitation and lower calcification volume in BAV and male sex in TAV cohorts were independent risk factors. IPTW-adjusted analysis revealed significantly higher 30-day all-cause (HR 3.09, p=0.019) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 3.49, p=0.021) in the bailout ViV group. However, no significant differences were observed in mid-term all-cause or cardiovascular mortality between groups.ConclusionsBailout ViV was associated with elevated early mortality but offered satisfactory mid-term survival. Key predictors include anatomical challenges (large annulus and insufficient calcification) and procedural factors (non-repositionable self-expanding valve, learning phase TAVR and significant residual AR after the first prosthesis).

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is currently established as the guideline-recommended first-line therapy for symptomatic severe aortic stenosis (AS).1 2 However, the landmark randomised controlled trials that form the basis of these guidelines excluded patients with bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) anatomy—a cohort often presenting greater technical challenges during intervention. BAV represents one of the most prevalent congenital heart diseases, affecting approximately 1%–2% of the general population.3 Reflecting surgical experience, BAV morphology constitutes up to 50% of younger patients requiring aortic valve intervention.4 Consequently, the prevalence of BAV within TAVR cohorts is inevitably rising, as the procedure extends to progressively younger patient demographics.5

The complex valvular anatomy typical of BAV, often accompanied by a dilated ascending aorta, contributes to higher rates of adverse procedural events, including device failure, conversion to surgery and significant paravalvular leakage.68 Among these complications, transcatheter aortic valve migration and embolisation (TVME) represents a significant contributor. Although TVME occurs at a rate of approximately 1%—only marginally higher than other critical complications like annular rupture or aortic dissection—it has historically received less attention.9 The asymmetric calcification and irregular sinus morphology commonly seen in BAV patients predispose them to a higher incidence of TVME compared with those with tricuspid aortic valves (TAVs).9 TVME is a primary indication for emergent bailout valve-in-valve (ViV) implantation during TAVR, although significant residual regurgitation due to leaflet dysfunction also contributes in a minority of cases.10

A key clinical dilemma arises when post-TAVR aortic regurgitation (AR) is not severe enough to clearly mandate intervention, yet still raises concern. Compounding this uncertainty, evidence on bailout ViV outcomes specifically in BAV patients remains limited. Further investigation is therefore essential to inform clinical decision-making in this high-risk group. Previous studies, conducted mainly in TAV patients, have consistently linked bailout ViV procedures with considerable early and long-term mortality and morbidity.1012 To address the current evidence gap regarding bailout ViV therapy, particularly in high-risk BAV patients, we conducted a retrospective cohort study. This research analysed the predictors and compared the mid-term clinical and echocardiographic outcomes of bailout ViV TAVR against single-prosthesis TAVR in patients with severe AS, including both BAV and TAV anatomies.

Methods

Study population and design

This retrospective, observational study was conducted at a single high-volume tertiary TAVR centre. Consecutive patients undergoing procedures between April 2014 and April 2024 were enrolled. The inclusion criterion was the successful deployment of either a single transcatheter aortic valve prosthesis or multiple prostheses during the index procedure. Key exclusion criteria included 1) ViV implantation performed in a separate procedure unrelated to the index TAVR, (2) a primary diagnosis of pure AR, (3) aortic valve anatomy that precluded clear identification as bicuspid or tricuspid morphology, (4) bailout ViV therapy administered due to ectopic valve deployment and (5) patients with a degenerated aortic valve prosthesis. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University.

Patients were systematically categorised into four distinct cohorts for comparative analysis: (1) BAV AS patients who underwent bailout ViV implantation, (2) BAV AS patients who received a single prosthesis, (3) TAV AS patients who underwent bailout ViV implantation and (4) TAV AS patients who received a single prosthesis. TAVR procedure

Patient eligibility for TAVR and the corresponding procedural strategy were determined through a comprehensive evaluation by a local multidisciplinary heart team. The standard TAVR protocol used at the centre has been described in a previous publication.13 The transfemoral approach served as the primary vascular access route in most cases, and procedures were typically performed under monitored anaesthesia care rather than general anaesthesia. The selection of valve type and size was at the operator’s discretion, guided by periprocedural multimodality imaging evaluation of the patient’s vascular and valvular anatomy. Coronary protection strategy (guidewire, balloon or coronary stent implantation at the at-risk coronary ostium at the operator’s discretion) was performed following preoperative CT-based coronary risk assessment.

Bailout ViV implantation was employed under the following circumstances: (1) significant residual paravalvular or central AR (≥moderate) after initial valve deployment that was deemed clinically unacceptable or (2) valve embolisation—either aortic or ventricular—resulting in a non-functional or unstable initially implanted valve. An example of preprocedural cardiac CT angiography in a BAV patient and postprocedural imaging following bailout ViV with a self-expanding prosthesis is shown in the online supplemental figure 1. After valve deployment, procedural outcomes were systematically evaluated using intraprocedural echocardiography to assess residual AR and valve haemodynamics, supplemented by fluoroscopy to confirm optimal device position, stability and absence of coronary obstruction.

Data collection and follow-up

A comprehensive dataset encompassing baseline characteristics, clinical parameters, procedural details and outcomes was prospectively collected for all participants. Data fields comprised demographic information (sex, body mass index (BMI), age); risk stratification scores and comorbidities (Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality, New York Heart Association functional class, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, anaemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and active or historical cancer); laboratory parameters (serum albumin levels and estimated glomerular filtration rate); echocardiographic assessments (peak aortic valve velocity, mean aortic valve gradient, left ventricular ejection fraction and presence of ≥moderate mitral or tricuspid regurgitation); vascular CT assessments (annulus perimeter, annulus calcification volume, bicuspid valve morphology classification and BAV subtypes); procedural details (emergency status, procedure date, predilation or postdilation and prosthesis type (categorised as balloon-expandable valves and self-expanding valves, with the latter further divided into repositionable and non-repositionable self-expanding valves based on their retrievability)) and systematic recording of postprocedural echocardiographic and clinical outcomes (new permanent pacemaker implantation, stroke, life-threatening or major bleeding incidents, stage 3 acute kidney injury (AKI), aortic dissection, conversion to surgery, transfusion requirements and postoperative hospitalisation duration).

Early postoperative complications were adjudicated according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria.14 Stroke was defined as new neurological deficits with corroborative neuroimaging evidence. Life-threatening or major bleeding was identified by a haemoglobin drop of >3 g/dL with transfusion of ≥2 units of red blood cells. Stage 3 AKI was defined as serum creatinine elevation >300% above baseline or reaching ≥354 µmol/L with an acute increase ≥44 µmol/L.

Standardised echocardiographic evaluation was performed at the 1-year follow-up. Survival status was actively monitored by a dedicated clinical follow-up team at 30 days, 6 months and annually thereafter through outpatient visits or structured telephone follow-up. The survival outcome included all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Based on descriptions in previously published literature, survival outcomes during follow-up of 1–5 years postprocedure were described as mid-term outcomes.1517

Statistics

Missing data (<5%) were excluded, while 5%–30% missingness was handled via multiple imputation by chained equations. Continuous variables were summarised and compared based on their distribution. Data with a normal distribution are presented as mean±SD and were compared using the student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median with IQR and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentages and compared using the Pearson χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Given that procedural learning is a gradual and continuous process, segmented logistic regression was applied to analyse the learning curve for avoiding bailout ViV, based on data from the two operators with the highest TAVR volumes.

The multivariate logistic regression model for bailout ViV incorporated clinically relevant variables and those with a univariate p<0.01. 30-day and mid-term all-cause and cardiovascular survival were evaluated using weighted regression models based on inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) via entropy balancing. Results were presented as cumulative mortality curves. All analyses were conducted in R (V.4.3.3). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

This retrospective, observational study was conducted at a single high-volume tertiary TAVR centre, and patients or the public were not involved in the design of the study.

Results

Patients with BAV and TAV were stratified by the status of bailout ViV for comparative analysis (table 1 and online supplemental table 1). Single prosthesis implantation was performed in 93.80% (1498/1597), while bailout ViV was required in 6.20% (99/1597) of cases (online supplemental table 2).

In the BAV cohort, bailout ViV was performed in 6.87% (53/771) of patients. Compared with the single prosthesis group, patients requiring bailout ViV were more frequently male (75.47% vs 56.27%, p=0.006) and had larger median annulus perimeters (29.90 mm vs 27.00 mm, p<0.001). Baseline echocardiography also revealed a higher prevalence of significant mitral regurgitation (MR) in the bailout ViV group (28.30% vs 12.41%, p=0.001). Procedurally, significant residual AR following the initial valve deployment (22.64% vs 3.21%, p<0.001) and the use of postdilation (69.81% vs 53.06%, p=0.018) were significantly more frequent in the bailout ViV group. Postprocedurally, life-threatening or major bleeding events were more common in the bailout ViV group (32.08% vs 15.88%, p=0.002). Over a median follow-up of 1078 days, all-cause mortality was significantly higher in the bailout ViV group (20.75% vs 10.45%, p=0.021), with a trend towards increased cardiovascular mortality (11.32% vs 4.87%, p=0.089).

In the TAV cohort, bailout ViV was required in 5.57% (46/826) of patients. Compared with the single prosthesis group, the bailout ViV group comprised a higher proportion of males (86.96% vs 53.85%, p<0.001) and exhibited larger median annulus perimeters (30.25 mm vs 26.96 mm, p<0.001) and calcification volumes (489.88 mm³ vs 361.18 mm³, p=0.004). Procedurally, the omission of predilation was more frequent in the bailout ViV group (22.73% vs 13.12%, p=0.031), and no balloon-expandable or Lotus valves were used. Significant residual AR following initial deployment (26.09% vs 1.03%, p<0.001) and the use of postdilation (56.52% vs 35.26%, p=0.004) were also significantly higher in this group. Over a median follow-up of 999 days, cardiovascular mortality was significantly higher in the bailout ViV group (17.39% vs 6.67%, p=0.015), with a numerical trend towards increased all-cause mortality (26.09% vs 15.51%, p=0.058).

Predictors of bailout ViV

Segmented logistic regression analysis performed on a consecutive cohort of TAVR patients operated by the same operators identified a learning curve threshold of 201 cases (95% CI 30 to 372 cases) for bailout ViV procedures (online supplemental figure 2).

Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed incorporating significant univariate predictors (online supplemental table 3) and clinically relevant factors, including valve morphology (BAV or TAV), annulus calcification volume, prosthesis type and the TAVR learning phase (table 2). Larger annulus perimeter and significant residual AR after initial deployment were consistently identified as independent predictors of bailout ViV across the overall cohort (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.29, p<0.001; OR 10.43, 95% CI 5.42 to 20.07, p<0.001), the BAV cohort (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.33, p<0.001; OR 8.51, 95% CI 3.28 to 22.07, p<0.001) and the TAV cohort (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.34, p<0.001; OR 17.59, 95% CI 6.14 to 50.40, p<0.001).

Survival analysis

Covariate balance assessment for the IPTW-adjusted analysis is presented in online supplemental figure 3. In the overall cohort, the bailout ViV group showed significantly elevated risks of 30-day all-cause mortality (HR: 3.09, 95% CI 1.21 to 7.92, p=0.019) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 3.49, 95% CI 1.20 to 10.10, p=0.021). However, no significant differences were observed in mid-term all-cause (HR 1.29, 95% CI 0.65 to 2.55, p=0.465) or cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.01, 95% CI 0.81 to 4.98, p=0.132) (figure 2).

In the subgroup analyses, neither the BAV nor the TAV cohorts demonstrated statistically significant differences between the bailout ViV and single prosthesis groups. Specifically, in the BAV cohort, the risks were comparable for both mid-term and 30-day all-cause mortality (HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.36 to 6.40, p=0.578 and HR 1.63, 95% CI 0.42 to 6.30, p=0.482, respectively) as well as cardiovascular mortality (online supplemental figure 4A). Similarly, in the TAV cohort, no significant differences were found in mid-term or 30-day all-cause mortality (HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.45 to 3.19, p=0.725 and HR 2.26, 95% CI 0.54 to 9.48, p=0.264, respectively) or cardiovascular mortality outcomes (online supplemental figure 4B).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first dedicated analysis of bailout ViV therapy in BAV patients. We report three key findings: (1) bailout ViV was required in 6.20% of cases (BAV: 6.87%; TAV: 5.57%), a relatively high rate likely attributable to the use of first-generation self-expanding valves and the learning curve. (2) Larger annulus perimeter and significant residual AR after initial deployment were universal predictors of bailout ViV. Additional predictors included lower calcification volume, non-repositionable self-expanding valves and the learning phase in the overall cohort; significant MR and lower calcification in the BAV cohort and male sex in the TAV cohort. (3) After IPTW adjustment, the bailout ViV group showed higher 30-day all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the overall cohort, but mid-term mortality did not differ significantly. These trends were not observed in the subgroup analyses of BAV and TAV patients.

Rates and mechanism of bailout ViV in TAVR

TVME, the primary indication for bailout ViV, has been previously reported in approximately 1%–2% of TAVR cases.11 In our study, the incidence of bailout ViV was higher in both BAV and TAV populations, which may be explained by the prevalent use of first-generation self-expanding valve systems and the early learning phase of TAVR in our institution. These devices have been independently associated with an increased risk of TVME requiring bailout intervention.9 11 12 However, retrievable device technology alone may not fully prevent TVME,9 as retrievability—a characteristic mainly of self-expanding valves—paradoxically correlates with a higher intrinsic risk of embolisation, since recapture is typically feasible only before 90% valve expansion. This mechanistic limitation is reflected in our cohort by the absence of bailout ViV cases involving the fully retrievable Lotus valve, although this observation should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size.

We evaluated the learning curve for bailout ViV TAVR as a potential predictor. Although the estimated learning threshold was 201 cases, the precision of this estimate was limited by a wide 95% CI (30 to 372 cases). Nonetheless, logistic regression identified the learning phase as a significant predictor in the overall population. While established risk factors include advanced age, low BMI, challenging anatomy, BAV morphology and aggressive postdilation,9 11 12 our multivariate analysis identified larger annulus perimeter and significant residual AR after initial deployment as the only consistent independent predictors across all cohorts.

Intriguingly, our analysis revealed a distinct contrast regarding the impact of calcium load. While the univariate analysis suggested higher calcification volumes in the bailout ViV group, the multivariate model identified lower annulus calcification volume as an independent predictor of procedural failure after adjusting for confounders. This phenomenon highlights the critical mechanical role of calcification in device fixation. After controlling for the influence of confounders such as annulus size or prosthesis type, the ‘anchoring’ function of calcium becomes a potential factor. Sufficient calcification provides the necessary friction coefficient at the landing zone; conversely, a minimally calcified annulus lacks adequate grip, thereby predisposing the device to migration or instability—a mechanism that mandates immediate bailout ViV implantation. In the BAV cohort, significant MR was an independent predictor, likely due to its contribution to haemodynamic instability during deployment. Furthermore, male sex was associated with a higher risk of bailout ViV in the TAV population, warranting specific consideration during clinical management.

Prognosis of bailout ViV

Bailout ViV therapy in broad TAVR populations has been consistently associated with elevated in-hospital mortality, emergency surgery rates, major adverse events and reduced long-term survival.1012 In our study, early adverse event rates were numerically higher in the bailout ViV groups across both cohorts. Specifically, the BAV bailout group exhibited a significantly elevated incidence of life-threatening or major bleeding, underscoring the haemorrhagic risks inherent to complex manoeuvres in BAV anatomy. Despite these early risks, bailout ViV demonstrated a satisfactory outcome. In both BAV and TAV populations, we observed no valve reinterventions or significant late AR progression, with haemodynamic performance comparable to that of single prosthesis recipients.

Our time-to-event analysis demonstrates a distinct biphasic pattern in prognosis. While bailout ViV patients faced significantly elevated 30-day all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, no significant differences were observed in mid-term survival compared with the single-prosthesis group. This indicates that the disparity of prognostic risk may be largely confined to the early periprocedural phase, likely driven by acute complications. For patients who survive the initial high-risk period, bailout ViV serves as an effective rescue strategy, offering mid-term survival and haemodynamic performance comparable to uncomplicated TAVR. The majority of deaths (60.87%, 14/23) in the bailout ViV group were cardiovascular in origin, including cardiogenic shock, aortic dissection, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, ventricular fibrillation and cerebral infarction. Non-cardiovascular causes included pulmonary infection, COVID-19, pancreatitis, malignancy and multiple organ failure. Thanks to rigorous preoperative CT-based assessment combined with appropriate coronary protection strategies, the incidence of intraprocedural coronary obstruction in this cohort was low. However, given the higher prevalence of anomalous coronary anatomy in the BAV population and the increased coronary risk in this subgroup undergoing bailout ViV with two valves, thorough preoperative coronary risk evaluation and appropriate coronary protection strategies are of paramount importance.18

Clinical significance

Our findings confirm that bailout ViV implantation is associated with higher short-term mortality compared with conventional single-prosthesis TAVR, necessitating careful clinical decision-making. Special vigilance is warranted for phenotypes identified as high-risk, specifically BAV patients with significant MR and male TAV patients. Furthermore, minimal annulus calcification, particularly in the BAV cohort, requires meticulous prosthesis selection to mitigate the risk of device migration. In such high-risk scenarios, as well as in patients with large annular dimensions, first-generation self-expanding valves should generally be avoided.

To minimise the need for bailout procedures, preventive strategies targeting significant residual AR are paramount. These include comprehensive preprocedural imaging, dedicated predilatation combined with simultaneous aortography to optimise the landing zone, and precise control of implantation depth while balancing the need for postdilatation.19 When bailout ViV becomes unavoidable, meticulous periprocedural management and intensive early monitoring are critical. Collectively, these risk mitigation strategies, refined patient selection, precise sizing and proactive AR prevention can enhance the safety profile of TAVR and improve clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the single-centre, observational and retrospective design may limit the generalisability of our findings and reduce the statistical power for detecting rare clinical endpoints. Additionally, the reliance on a single heart team’s expertise for procedural decisions could have introduced inherent bias. Second, although it represents one of the largest contemporary cohorts of BAV patients receiving bailout ViV therapy, the sample size remains moderate given the complexity of the clinical question. Third, despite the statistical uncertainty regarding the learning curve threshold of 201 cases for minimising bailout ViV rates with the wide 95% CI (30 to 372 cases), the learning phase variable remained a robust predictor in our multivariate model. This confirms that the accumulation of institutional experience is undeniably a critical determinant in reducing the incidence of bailout ViV procedures.

Conclusions

Independent predictors of bailout ViV included larger annular perimeter, significant residual AR, lower calcium volume, non-repositionable self-expanding valves and the learning phase, with significant MR (in BAV) and male sex (in TAV) identified as cohort-specific risks. Although bailout ViV was associated with higher early mortality, it did not compromise mid-term survival compared with single-prosthesis TAVR.
