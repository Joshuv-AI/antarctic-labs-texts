Sensitivity of gargle samples compared to swabs for SARS-CoV-2 detection with nucleic acid amplification testing: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Iryna Kayda, Kwana Lechiile, Mai-Lei Woo Kinshella, Jeffrey N Bone, Pascal M Lavoie, David M Goldfarb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGargle sampling emerged as a novel method for diagnostic testing during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, yet uncertainty remained about its performance when compared with conventional sampling methods.ObjectiveTo evaluate the performance of self-collected gargle samples compared to traditional healthcare worker (HCW)-collected upper respiratory tract swabs for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) detection with nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT).MethodsWe conducted a systematic review (PROSPERO registration: CRD42022312628) to (1) estimate sensitivity of gargle sampling, (2) estimate the difference in sensitivity between gargle and swab methods, and (3) understand how various testing contexts may impact gargle sensitivity. MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, Global Index Medicus, and preprint servers were searched. Studies reporting primary data and investigating COVID-19 diagnostic performance of self-collected gargle samples compared to HCW-collected swabs tested using NAAT were included. Quality assessment was performed, and random effects meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled gargle sensitivity and mean difference in sensitivity between gargle and swab methods.ResultsSearches identified 1453 results with 32 studies included. Meta-analysis pooled 34 gargle-swab comparisons. Gargle sensitivity was estimated to be 92.2% (95% confidence interval: 89.6% to 94.2%), and 3.3% (0.4% to 6.3%) less sensitive than swab collection. Gargle sensitivity was greater than 87.0% across diverse patient characteristics, settings, type or volume of gargle liquid, length of gargling time, wait time prior to gargling, and reference swab type used. Greatest sensitivities were observed when gargle sampling for 30 s or greater using 5–9 mL of saline. Gargle sensitivity of 93.0% (87.8% to 96.1%) was observed when there was no required wait time, and 90.1% (87.0% to 92.5%) when compared to combined nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal swabs.ConclusionGargle sampling may be a sensitive, alternative method for SARS-CoV-2 detection across various testing contexts, and its implementation has potential to reduce barriers associated with HCW-collected swabs, that may be challenging to collect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03206-1.Keywords: Gargle samples, Swab, COVID-19, Molecular testing, SARS-CoV-2, Diagnosis

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the virus responsible for the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, has led to over 7 million deaths and over 775 million confirmed cases globally since its emergence in 2019 [1]. Rapid spread of SARS-CoV-2 increased demand for diagnostic testing and high volumes of sample collection, which challenged clinical laboratories and healthcare systems around the world. Molecular diagnostic testing for respiratory viral infection often requires the collection of a nasopharyngeal (NP) swab by a trained healthcare worker (HCW), although other upper respiratory tract swab types have also been validated for clinical use, including combined nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal (NPOP) swabs [2–4]. Collected samples undergo laboratory processing by nucleic acid amplification methods, such as reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), for detection of virus specific sequences. Accessible, rapid, and sensitive diagnostic testing during the pandemic has been vital for identifying new infections, managing outbreaks, the clinical care of COVID-19 patients, and minimizing future spread [5, 6]. Testing challenges related to traditional swab collection were reported during the pandemic including resource shortages of swabs, HCWs, and personal protective equipment (PPE). Aversion and testing fatigue have also been reported due to the discomfort of swab collection and their low acceptability from patients, particularly children and those undergoing frequent testing [7–9]. In the post pandemic era, the volume of testing for respiratory viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 has increased significantly and still remains at much higher levels than in the pre-pandemic period and therefore optimizing pre-analytical methods for testing remains relevant [10, 11].

Gargle sampling has emerged as a less invasive, self-collect, swab-independent method for COVID-19 testing. This method involves gargling liquid, such as saline or water, to facilitate collection of a sample from an individual’s mouth and throat for subsequent RT-PCR testing. Several studies globally have developed variations of this gargle collection method and independently investigated its performance when compared to swabs [12–15]. Gargle sampling received high acceptability from patients and been demonstrated to be stable at room temperature for up to seven days [16]. Combined with its potential for unobserved self-collection, gargle sampling would allow for scale-up of testing and address resource constraints, particularly in settings where trained HCWs or specialized swabs are minimally available [17, 18]. This sample type can also be useful to facilitate and encourage participation in surveillance and/or research programs. There has been variation in the reported performance of gargle samples globally, ranging from a sensitivity of 60.7% to as high as 100.0% [14, 19]. These individual studies sampled varying populations with various methods and were often single-center studies evaluating relatively small numbers of SARS-CoV-2 positive samples [20–42]. A previous systematic review estimated a sensitivity of 91% for SARS-CoV-2 detection when compared to NP or NPOP swab collection [43]. However, this work did not explore the difference in sensitivity between saline and water sampling methods, compare with other swab sampling methods, and included only studies published up to September 2021. Several additional studies on this topic have since been published but not yet included in the prior meta-analysis. Incorporating these new studies would allow for updated results and further analyses investigating various, unexplored factors on gargle sample performance. Thus, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to further explore the sensitivity of gargle sample collection when compared to HCW-collected upper respiratory tract swabs for SARS-CoV-2 detection with nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT).

Methods

This work was conducted in accordance with the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020” guidelines (S1 Table) [44]. A protocol was registered on the “International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO)” (CRD42022312628) prior to beginning the review and conducting searches [45].

Search strategy, selection criteria, and screening

Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid EMBASE, Web of Science, Global Index Medicus, medRxiv, bioRxiv, and Research Square were searched from 14 March 2022 to 14 June 2024 using advanced search terms, subject headings, and combinations of relevant keywords (S2 Table) [46]. No limits were placed on language, dates, or study design. When a publication and its earlier preprint were both found in searches, the publication was selected for inclusion. A Google Scholar alert, consultation with experts in the field, and looking through reference lists of relevant articles were also used to locate potentially suitable studies. All references were managed using EndNote software [47] and subsequently uploaded to Covidence software [48] for de-duplication and screening.

After de-duplication, title/abstract and full text screening were carried out in two stages by two reviewers (IK and KL) working independently, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (MWK), using Covidence software [48]. Studies reporting primary data and investigating COVID-19 diagnostic performance of self-collected gargle samples compared to a HCW-collected reference swab sample were included. Studies required at least five matched samples (gargle and swab) tested with a NAAT platform within 10 days of diagnosis. and must have involved a form of “gargling”, “throat lavage”, or “throat wash” during the gargle sample collection. Studies with individuals that had matched samples collected for COVID-19 testing were included, with no restrictions on age, demographics, or symptoms. Randomized clinical trials, case series, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and/or cross-sectional studies, both peer reviewed publications and non-peer reviewed preprints, were included. International publications written in languages read by the review team (English, French, or German) were eligible. Studies that conducted multiple gargle-swab comparisons with discrete differences in methodologies (i.e., different populations sampled, different liquids, tested on different assays etc.) and reported these results separately were considered as separate studies/comparison groups in analyses.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Included studies underwent data extraction in duplicate by two reviewers (IK and KL) working independently using a predetermined data collection form. After initial duplicate data entry with three articles, consensus checks were done via discussion between the reviewers to ensure quality and consensus in the manner of data collection and answer any reviewer questions. Subsequently, duplicate data extraction was continued for all articles to complete the data collection. Data extraction captured country, study dates, study design, study participant characteristics, as well as details pertaining to reference swab collection, gargle sample collection, lab testing protocol, the number of samples used for both index and reference testing, and the number of samples reported positive for SARS-CoV-2 via gargle, swab, both, or either sample type. When information of interest was not provided in the study report, we contacted study corresponding authors directly by email (three attempts). Individual studies were excluded from being pooled in the meta-analysis when the unavailability of data prevented calculation of a study estimate.

Quality assessment was conducted using the “Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2)” tool (S3 Table) [49] by two independent reviewers (IK and KL), and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (MWK). Risk of bias plots were developed using robvis software and reported to visualize the quality of included studies [50]. Where evidence was unclear, studies were given the domain-specific assessment of “no information” for the purposes of visualization with the traffic light and summary bar plots.

Data synthesis

R statistical software (version 4.4.1) and meta, metafor, dplyr, and MKinfer packages were used to estimate effect measures, heterogeneity, and to synthesize results from all included studies. The primary analysis estimated the overall mean sensitivity of gargle sampling for SARS-CoV-2 detection with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). We also estimated the pooled mean difference in sensitivity (with 95% CI) between gargle and swab sampling methods for SARS-CoV-2 detection. Given the absence of a gold standard test in terms of sensitivity, a composite reference standard was used to define a positive result with either sample (gargle or HCW-collected respiratory swab) testing positive being considered a positive reference standard. Using this as a method for the reference standard did not allow for an estimation of specificity [51].

Individual studies collected paired samples (gargle and swab) from each of their study participants, and both were tested for SARS-CoV-2. Data from each individual study was extracted regarding the number of reported SARS-CoV-2 positive gargle samples, number of reported SARS-CoV-2 positive swab samples, and these were each divided by the total number of SARS-CoV-2 positive results on any sample type (gargle only, swab only, or both), known as the composite reference standard, to yield estimates of sensitivity for both gargle and swab sample type, as well as yield estimates of standard error, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval for each individual study.

We estimated the mean difference in sensitivity between sampling methods, standard error, and 95% CI for each study, using the Wilson method for paired sample collections [52, 53]. The sensitivity estimate for gargle was subtracted from the sensitivity estimate for swab to yield the difference in sensitivity between the two for each individual study, and these study estimates were pooled in meta-analysis to yield the mean sensitivity difference between gargle and swab sample types across all included studies. We computed the confidence interval for the paired difference in proportion using the Wilson method, which is appropriate for situations were proportions approach zero or 1, using the binomDiffCl function in R and the method reported in Newcombe et al. [53]. Individual study estimates were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis with inverse variance weighting. The Sidik-Jonkman estimator was used to estimate tau-squared (2) and a Hartung-Knapp adjustment was applied. These account for the relatively small number of studies, expected high heterogeneity across studies, and to minimize the possibility of falsely positive estimates of effect size [54–56]. To estimate the overall mean sensitivity of gargle sampling, random-effects meta-analysis with generalized linear mixed models was conducted to pool the individual study estimates for gargle sensitivity calculated above [57]. Estimates of 2, I-squared (I2) values, and forest plots were reported to visualize heterogeneity of included studies [58].

Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate sources of heterogeneity when three or more comparable studies/comparison groups were available for pooling. A priori defined subgroups included the type of reference swab(s), patient characteristics (i.e. age group, presence of symptoms, HCWs), sample collection method (i.e. type of gargling liquid, length of gargling time, wait time), type of analytical method used (i.e. testing method, gene targets), and study quality (excluding studies/comparison groups with “high” or “unclear” risk of bias in two or more of the four domains). Post hoc subgroup analyses were also performed to explore if performance varied depending on whether laboratory developed tests (LDTs) or commercial assays were used for study testing.

Results

Searches identified a total of 1453 studies (MEDLINE – 537; EMBASE − 705; Web of Science – 197; Global Index Medicus – 0; medRxiv/bioRxiv – 9; Research Square – 3; Google Scholar alert/expert recommendation – 2) (Fig. 1). After de-duplication and initial screening, 62 full texts were reviewed. Of these, 30 were excluded due to ineligibility, and five due to missing information required to calculate study estimates [13, 14, 32, 39, 42] (S4 Table). Our review included 27 studies, with 34 gargle-swab comparison groups pooled in meta-analysis.

Characteristics of included studies and quality assessment

Characteristics of all included studies (n = 27) and comparison groups (n = 34) are summarized in S5 Table. There were gargle-swab comparison groups from Europe (n = 11), North America (n = 13), South America (n = 1), Africa (n = 1), and Asia and the Middle East (n = 8), with sample collection dates ranging March 2020 to September 2022, covering the original SARS-CoV-2 strain and subsequent variants. Study populations included adults and children (< 18 years old), symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, outpatients and inpatients, healthcare workers (HCWs), individuals with a suspected infection and newly presenting for COVID-19 testing, as well as those with a previous lab-confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis resampled for study purposes. Swab methods used for comparison included combined nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal swabs (n = 12), combined nasal/oropharyngeal swabs (n = 4), oropharyngeal (or throat) swabs (n = 3), and nasopharyngeal swabs (n = 15). Gargle collection methods varied with use of saline, water, or premade gargling solution, volumes of liquid ranging from 2.5 mL to 20 mL, and collection time instructions ranging from 5 to 10 s to 30 s. RT-PCR was commonly used as the NAAT analytical method (n = 31); three studies used modified, extraction-free, “direct” RT-PCR methods (n = 3). Laboratory-developed (n = 9) and commercial (n = 24) assays were utilized for COVID-19 testing in combination with several testing devices.

Quality assessment found that all included studies were at “low” concern regarding applicability for this review. Several studies were deemed to be at risk of bias due to “high” or “unclear” assessments in the QUADAS-2 domains of “patient selection”, “index testing”, and “reference testing” (S1 Fig) due to inclusion of individuals with prior lab-confirmed COVID-19, rather than solely individuals with suspected infection. These studies did not report blinding of index and reference test results during study sample testing, although this may be less of a concern as testing was performed with an objective molecular test (S2 Fig).

Primary analysis and subgroup analyses

After pooling across all 27 studies/34 comparison groups (2330 total SARS-CoV-2 positive samples), primary analysis found that overall gargle sample sensitivity was 92.2% (95% CI: 89.6% to 94.2%) (Fig. 2). Gargle sampling was estimated to be 3.3% less (0.4% less to 6.3% less) sensitive than standard swab collection by HCWs (Fig. 3).

All estimates of overall gargle sensitivity, mean difference between gargle and swab sensitivities, and the associated heterogeneity values from numerous subgroup analyses are summarized in Table 1. Gargle sensitivity was estimated to be greater than 87.0% across all subgroups stratified by patient population sampled, gargle sampling method, reference swab method, and NAAT analytical method used. Although point estimates of sensitivity varied slightly, confidence intervals were largely overlapping and indicated non-significant differences in gargle sensitivity across subgroups. In addition, gargle sample sensitivity was found to be no more than a few percentage points lower than swab sensitivity across all subgroups.

A total of six studies/seven comparison groups were included in sensitivity analysis based on their having overall “low” risk of bias assessments and/or less than two domains with a “high” or “unclear (no information)” risk of bias assessment [15, 17, 33–35, 38]. After repeating the primary analysis with only these high-quality studies/comparison groups (681 SARS-CoV-2 positive samples total), overall gargle sample sensitivity was estimated to be 92.1% (86.7% to 95.5%) overall, and 2.6% less (7.7% less to 2.5% more) sensitive than swab collection.

Discussion

While a few percentage points lower than swab collection, we found a relatively high overall sensitivity for gargle sampling (92.2%) when compared to standard swab collection for SARS-CoV-2 detection with NAAT. This is comparable and slightly higher than the 91% overall gargle sensitivity previously estimated in the only other published meta-analysis on this topic [43]. At 92.2% gargle sensitivity overall found in our review, and greater than 87.0% sensitivity across all subgroups explored, its performance considerably exceeds the sensitivity seen with rapid antigen tests, which have been accepted for use in COVID-19 testing [59]. After stratifying our results by only the highest quality studies, we found that overall gargle sensitivity and the mean difference in sensitivity did not change significantly, which further supports the robustness of our estimates.

In alignment with a previous meta-analysis [43], gargle performance was greater than 90.0% across swab comparators used, even when compared to high-quality yet uncomfortable NPOP swabs, which require the services of a trained HCW with little to no potential for self-collection [60]. Additionally, gargle sampling appears to have comparable performance to saliva, which has been deemed to be acceptable for COVID-19 testing [52]. A recent meta-analysis reported 86.9% (82.3% to 90.4%) sensitivity for saliva [52], which is lower but comparable to the overall gargle sensitivity of 92.7% (87.2% to 95.9%) when compared to NP swabs in our review. In comparison to saliva, gargle sampling maintains several additional benefits such as being a more homogenous, less viscous sample that may be easily introduced into the lab testing workflow and avoid the additional pre-analytical steps that have been needed for molecular testing of saliva samples (e.g. the introduction and emulsification of Proteinase K) [61].

Notably, gargle self-collection was only a few percentage points less sensitive than HCW-collected swabs, which has not been previously estimated. Though slightly less, this may not preclude implementation of gargle sampling for COVID-19 testing in certain real-world contexts as clinically used tests commonly vary in their diagnostic performance to some degree. For example, combined NPOP swabs yield a slightly greater detection of respiratory viruses compared to NP swabs alone [62] though both are deemed to be comparable in performance [4, 15, 16, 23].

Our review suggests that gargle sampling for COVID-19 testing may be optimized by gargling 30 s or greater, and with 5–9 mL liquid. A longer gargle sampling time may result in capturing a greater amount of potential viral material from the mouth/throat. To the best of our knowledge, our review has been the first to stratify gargle liquid volumes to three groups, which observed the highest sensitivity was observed when gargling with an intermediate amount of liquid (5–9 mL), but further research would be needed to better understand optimal saline volume to use. Our review was also the first to explore the impact of precautionary wait times. Though there was a trend toward greater sensitivity in studies employing a 60-minute wait, gargle sensitivity estimates were comparable across different wait times including when there was no required wait time. Lengthy wait times may potentially be shortened or removed without sacrificing test sensitivity, which supports implementation in point-of-care settings, where ill patients may be encouraged to take oral fluids regularly and a lengthy required wait time for gargle testing may be a significant barrier.

Our work also has limitations. We used a composite reference standard given that swab collection is clearly an imperfect collection, and we were not able to estimate specificity of gargle sampling because we considered any positive result to be a true positive. Although there are limitations of this approach (e.g. incorporation bias), the question of clinical sensitivity of different sample types has been the more relevant issue and would explain why this approach has generally been used across meta-analyses comparing sample types for SARS-CoV-2 diagnostics [52, 63]. We included studies with known heterogeneity, although this reflects the real-world differences in testing approaches employed across regions and individual labs. Several studies included individuals with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections, which may introduce selection bias. However, we were able to stratify and explore gargle performance in studies with and without lab confirmed COVID-19 patients in our subgroup analyses and found comparable results. Studies were conducted during emergence of certain SARS-CoV-2 variants and largely prior to widespread COVID-19 vaccination, which may alter the biology of SARS-CoV-2 infection and disease. Thus, our findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be fully applicable for detection of newly emerging variants or diagnosis in vaccinated individuals.

Conclusion

Given its performance when processed with the many varying approaches that are commonly used for COVID-19 testing of swabs in several regions, our findings demonstrate that gargle samples are a versatile, highly amenable, alternative sample type for SARS-CoV-2 molecular detection. It is a swab-independent, self-collect method that performs comparably, with only slightly reduced sensitivity, to swabs when tested with already existing laboratory methods, allowing this sample type to be readily implemented in the clinical laboratory. Given that it is more acceptable to patients, it may be useful for situations where frequent, repeated testing is warranted, such as travel or workplace COVID-19 screening, or for testing those that have difficulty with swab collection. Widespread implementation of gargle sampling would support ongoing testing efforts and genomic surveillance for SARS-CoV-2. Future research is also encouraged to continue optimization of this method and to evaluate gargle sampling for other commonly circulating respiratory viruses. Continued innovation with gargle sampling and other alternative sampling methods, particularly as it relates to self-collection, has potential to strengthen future respiratory viral diagnostics.
