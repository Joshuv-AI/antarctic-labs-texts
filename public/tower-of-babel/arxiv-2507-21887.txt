Convergence of complex martingales in supercritical multi-type general branching processes in L q L^{q} for 1 < q ≤ 2 1<q\leq 2
Konrad Kolesko and Matthias Meiners and Ivana Tomic

Abstract
. Nerman’s martingale plays a central role in the law of large numbers for both, single- and multi-type, supercritical general branching processes. There are further, complex-valued Nerman-type martingales in the single-type process that figure in the finer fluctuations of these processes. We construct the analogous martingales for the process with finitely many types and give sufficient conditions for these martingales to converge in L q L^{q} for q ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] q\in(1,2] . Keywords: Crump-Mode-Jagers processes, martingales, general branching processes, multi-type branching, random characteristic Subclass: MSC: 60J80



1.
Introduction

Consider a Crump-Mode-Jagers branching process in which each individual is assigned one of finitely many types,
labeled
1,\ldots,p
. Denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{j}
the number of type-
j
individuals born up to and including time
t\geq 0
.
The dynamics of the process are governed by a matrix of offspring point processes
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,p}

where
\xi^{i,j}
is the point process of birth times of type-
j
offspring produced by an ancestor of type
i
.
Suppose that the process is supercritical and irreducible
and that there exists a Malthusian parameter
\alpha>0
such that the dominant (Perron-Frobenius)
eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
of Laplace transforms of the intensity measures
\mu^{i,j}\coloneqq\mathbf{E}[\xi^{i,j}]

evaluated at
\alpha
equals
1
.
Under this condition, one can construct a nonnegative martingale
(W_{t}(\alpha))_{t\geq 0}
,
known as Nerman’s martingale, where

W_{t}(\alpha)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\frac{v_{\tau(u)}}{v_{i}}e^{-\alpha S(u)},

with
\mathcal{C}_{t}
denoting the set of individuals who are the first in their line of descent to be born strictly after time
t
,

\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p})^{\mathsf{T}}
is the unique normalized nonnegative right Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)

and
S(u)
and
\tau(u)
representing the birth time and type, respectively, of individual
u
. The following law of large numbers holds:

e^{-\alpha t}\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{j}\to c_{i,j}W(\alpha)

as
t\to\infty
where
c_{i,j}>0
is a constant depending on the types
i,j

and
W(\alpha)=\lim_{t\to\infty}W_{t}(\alpha)
is the limit of Nerman’s martingale.
Depending on the specific additional assumptions imposed, this convergence may hold
in
\mathbf{P}^{i}
-probability, in
L^{1}(\mathbf{P}^{i})
, or
\mathbf{P}^{i}
-almost surely,
where
\mathbf{P}^{i}
denotes the law of the process with a type-
i
ancestor.
These results also extend to more general processes—specifically, general branching processes counted with a random characteristic.
For further details, see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.

A refined result, also valid for general branching processes counted with a random characteristic,
has been obtained recently for the single-type case
[
4
]
, in which we write
\xi
for
\xi^{1,1}
.
In this paper, the finiteness of the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}\mu
of
\mathbf{E}[\xi]
at
\frac{\alpha}{2}
is assumed.
In this case, the roots
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of the equation
\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=1
come into play.
For any such
\lambda
with
\frac{\alpha}{2}<\lambda\leq\alpha
, there is a Nerman-type martingale
(W_{t}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}

where

W_{t}(\lambda)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\lambda S(u)},\quad t\geq 0.

Under suitable
L^{2}
-assumptions, these martingales converge in
L^{2}
.
We write
W(\lambda)
for the limit of
(W_{t}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{2}
.
Assuming, for simplicity of presentation, that there is no root
\lambda
satisfying
\Real(\lambda)=\frac{\alpha}{2}

and that all roots
\lambda
with
\frac{\alpha}{2}<\Real(\lambda)\leq\alpha
are simple,
one has

e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\bigg(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}-c_{\alpha}e^{\alpha t}W(\alpha)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{\alpha}{2}<\Real\lambda<\alpha:\\
\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=1\end{subarray}}c_{\lambda}e^{\lambda t}W(\lambda)\bigg)\mathrel{\vbox{\halign{\hfil#\hfil\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{d}$\cr\kern-0.21529pt\cr$\to$\cr}}}c\sqrt{W(\alpha)}N

where
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{\varphi}
denotes a general branching process counted with random characteristic
\varphi
,

c,c_{\alpha},c_{\lambda}
are constants (depending on
\varphi
,
\lambda
and
\mu
),
and
N
is a standard normal random variable independent of the branching process, see
[
4
, Theorem 2.9]
.

A multi-type extension of this result would be highly desirable, as it is expected to have many applications.
First, we need the multi-type analogues of the martingales
(W_{t}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
.
The multi-type counterpart of the characteristic equation
\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=1
is given by

(1.1)

\det(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda)-\mathbf{I}_{p})=0,

where
\mathbf{I}_{p}
denotes the
p\times p
identity matrix.
To establish a corresponding result in the multi-type setting,
it is necessary to identify all martingales associated with each solution
\lambda
to Equation (
1.1
),
and to prove their convergence in
L^{2}
.
This is the main objective of the present paper.

2.
Assumptions and main result

We continue by rigorously formulating the model, followed by a precise statement of our main results.

2.1.
Model description

In this section, we formally introduce the multi-type general (Crump-Mode-Jagers) branching process.
The process begins with a single individual—the
ancestor
—born at time
0
.
It is assigned the label
\varnothing
, the empty tuple, and, like every individual in the process,
is of one of finitely many types:

\displaystyle\tau(\varnothing)\in[\,p\,]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,p\},

where
p\in\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
denotes the number of distinct types.
If
\tau(\varnothing)=i
, meaning the ancestor is of type
i
, then its children of type
j
are born at the points of the random point process
\xi^{i,j}
, for
j=1,\ldots,p
.
Throughout, we assume that each
\xi^{i,j}
is supported on
[0,\infty)
, and hence its total mass is given by

N^{i,j}=\xi^{i,j}([0,\infty))
.
The random variables
N^{i,j}
take values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
, where
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
In particular, we allow for the possibility that
\mathbf{P}(N^{i,j}=\infty)>0
,
which means that the ancestor may give rise to an infinite number of children of a given type.
We denote by

\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,p}

the
p\times p
matrix of point processes that determines the reproduction behavior of the ancestor.
The individual processes
\xi^{i,j}
are referred to as
offspring processes
,
and the matrix
\boldsymbol{\xi}
as the
offspring process matrix
.

Throughout this text, we denote by
\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{e}_{p}
the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
,
considered as row vectors.
Using this notation, we express the vector of offspring point processes for an individual of type
i
as

(2.1)

\displaystyle\xi^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi^{i,j}\,\mathsf{e}_{j}=(\xi^{i,1},\ldots,\xi^{i,p}),

which collects, component-wise, the reproduction processes for each offspring type.
Similarly, we define the offspring count vector for an individual of type
i
as

N^{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{p}N^{i,j}\,\mathsf{e}_{j}.

We further define

\displaystyle\xi(\cdot)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi^{\tau(\varnothing),j}(\cdot)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{k}}(\cdot),

where
N=\sum_{j=1}^{p}N^{\tau(\varnothing),j}=\xi([0,\infty))

is the total number of offspring of the ancestor
and
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure with a point at
x
.
The point process
\xi
thus records the birth times of all children of the ancestor, irrespective of their types.
Without loss of generality, we assume

0\leq X_{1}\leq X_{2}\leq\ldots,

in which case each birth time
X_{k}
can be recovered as

X_{k}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\xi([0,t])\geq k\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}

where
X_{k}=\infty
indicates that the ancestor has strictly fewer than
k
children.

The multi-type general branching process is then constructed by allowing each child of the ancestor to produce offspring
according to an independent copy of the point process matrix
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
These offspring, in turn, reproduce independently according to further independent copies of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and so on.
To formalize this, we introduce the standard Ulam-Harris notation.
Let
\mathbb{N}^{0}\coloneqq\{\varnothing\}
denote the set containing only the empty tuple.
The infinite Ulam-Harris tree is defined as

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathbb{N}^{n},

i.e., the set of all finite sequences (tuples) of natural numbers.
We abbreviate elements
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathcal{I}
by writing
u_{1}\ldots u_{n}
.
Given another element
v=v_{1}\ldots v_{m}\in\mathcal{I}
, their concatenation is denoted
uv=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}v_{1}\ldots v_{m}
.
The Ulam-Harris tree
\mathcal{I}
serves as the set of labels for all potential individuals.
Each
u\in\mathcal{I}
identifies a unique individual with ancestral line

\displaystyle\varnothing\rightarrow u_{1}\rightarrow u_{1}u_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow u_{1}\ldots u_{n}=u,

where
u_{1}
is the
u_{1}
-th child of the ancestor,
u_{1}u_{2}
the
u_{2}
-th child of
u_{1}
, and so on.
We write
|u|=n
to indicate that
u
belongs to generation
n
.
For
u\in\mathcal{I}
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the label
ui
corresponds to the
i
-th child of
u
, and we refer to
u
as the parent of
ui
.
More generally, we write
u\preceq v
if
v=uw
for some
w\in\mathcal{I}
,
and say that
u
is an ancestor of
v
, and
v
is a descendant of
u
.
If, in addition,
u\neq v
, we write
u\prec v
.
For
u=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote by
u|_{k}
the ancestor of
u
in generation
k

if
k\leq|u|
and
u|_{k}=u
, otherwise.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}=(\xi_{u}^{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,p}
,
u\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\{\varnothing\}

be a family of copies of
\boldsymbol{\xi}\eqqcolon\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\varnothing}
.
We write

\displaystyle\xi_{u}^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi_{u}^{i,j}\mathsf{e}_{j}.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P})
be a probability space on which all
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}
,
u\in\mathcal{I}
are defined and i. i. d. and
independent of
\tau(\varnothing)
, the random variable that gives the ancestor’s type
and write
\mathbf{P}^{i}
if the ancestor’s type is
i\in[\,p\,]
.
We denote the associated expected value operators by
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{E}^{i}
, respectively.

Generation
0
of the process is given by
\mathcal{G}_{0}\coloneqq\{\varnothing\}
.
The first generation is represented by the random set

\mathcal{G}_{1}\coloneqq\{k\in\mathbb{N}:X_{k}<\infty\}\subseteq\mathbb{N},

where each
k\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
is assigned a type
\tau(k)\in[\,p\,]
.
Now suppose that generation
\mathcal{G}_{n}
has been constructed and let
u\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
be an individual of type
\tau(u)\in[\,p\,]
.
Define the point process

\xi_{u}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\xi_{u}^{\tau(u),j}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{u}}\delta_{X_{u,k}},

where
N_{u}=\xi_{u}([0,\infty))
is the total number of offspring of
u
and

X_{u,k}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\xi_{u}([0,t])\geq k\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

where the infimum of the empty set is set to be
\infty
.
Then the
(n+1)
-th generation is given by

\mathcal{G}_{n+1}\coloneqq\{uk\in\mathcal{I}:u\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\text{ and }X_{u,k}<\infty\}.

The full population is described by the family tree

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{G}_{n}.

Each
u\in\mathcal{T}
has a type
\tau(u)\in[\,p\,]
and a well-defined birth time
S(u)\in[0,\infty)
, given by

S(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{u|_{k-1},u_{k}}\quad\text{for }u=u_{1}\ldots u_{n}.

For
t\geq 0
and
j\in[\,p\,]
, define the counting process

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{j}\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{j\}}(\tau(u))\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t-S(u)),

which counts the number of individuals of type
j
born up to and including time
t
.
The corresponding vector process is

\mathcal{Z}_{t}\coloneqq(\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{p}),

and
(\mathcal{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the multi-type general branching process.

2.2.
Assumptions

We define
\mu^{i,j}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{E}[\xi^{i,j}]
as the intensity measure of
\xi^{i,j}
for
i,j\in[\,p\,]
,
and let
\boldsymbol{\mu}\coloneqq(\mu^{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,p}=\mathbf{E}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]
denote the corresponding matrix.
We write
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}
for the Laplace transform of
\mu^{i,j}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty],\quad\theta\mapsto\int e^{-\theta x}\,\mu^{i,j}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x}),\quad i,j=1,\ldots,p

and
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}\vcentcolon=(\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j})_{i,j\in[\,p\,]}
for the corresponding matrix.
If
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(\theta)<\infty
for some
\theta\geq 0
, then, by monotonicity,
\mathcal{L}\mu
is finite on
[\theta,\infty)
.
We canonically extend
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}
to
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j})=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(\Real(z))<\infty\}
.
The domain of the matrix-valued Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}
is then defined as the intersection of the domains of its components,
that is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})=\bigcap_{i,j\in[\,p\,]}\!\!\!\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j})=\bigg\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\int e^{-\Real(z)x}\,\mu^{i,j}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{x})<\infty\text{ for all }i,j\in[\,p\,]\bigg\}.

Notice that
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)
is a nonnegative matrix for every
\theta\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\mathbb{R}

and hence possesses a maximal nonnegative eigenvalue
that equals the spectral radius
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)}
of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)
.
Notice further, that
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)}
is decreasing and continuous in
\theta\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})\cap\mathbb{R}
,
see e.g.
[
8
, Lemma A.2]
for a reference.

The following assumptions hold throughout the paper.

(A1)

The spectral radius
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)}
of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)=(\mu^{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[\,p\,]}

satisfies
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)}<1
.

(A2)

There exists a
Malthusian parameter
, i.e., there exists an
\alpha>0

such that the spectral radius
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)}
of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
equals
1
.

We suppose that
\alpha>0
is maximal with the property
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)}=1
.
Notice that
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)\to\rho_{\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)}
as
\theta\to\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem,
hence the spectral radius
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)}
of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\theta)
is strictly smaller than
1
eventually,
proving that if there exists some
\alpha>0
with
\rho_{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)}
, then there is also a maximal one.

Further, notice that (A
1
) and (A
2
)
combined imply that
(\mathcal{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
does not explode in finite time,
see e.g.
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
for a recent discussion.

Notice that (A
2
) implies the supercriticality of the general branching process
in the irreducible (primitive) case and hence that the survival set

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{\#\mathcal{G}_{n}\geq 1\}

satisfies
\mathbf{P}^{i}(\mathcal{S})>0
for all
i\in[\,p\,]
.
To see this, first notice that the mean offspring matrix satisfies
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)\geq\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
component-wise.
Further, (A
2
) implies that
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(0)>\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(\alpha)
for at least one pair
(i,j)\in[\,p\,]^{2}
.
In fact, since
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
has dominant eigenvalue
1
, it has at least one positive entry,
that is, there exists a pair
(i,j)\in[\,p\,]^{2}
with
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(\alpha)>0
.
This means that
\mu^{i,j}(0,\infty)>0
and hence, by monotonicity,
\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(0)>\mathcal{L}\mu^{i,j}(\alpha)
as claimed.
From the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[
13
, Theorem 1.1(e)]
(after truncation of infinite entries if necessary),
we conclude that
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(0)
has principle eigenvalue
\rho>1
. This implies
\mathbf{P}^{i}(\mathcal{S})>0

by classical theory for multi-type Galton-Watson process, see e.g.
[
2
, Theorem II.7.1]
.

However, we do not make the assumption that the branching process is irreducible (equivalently, that
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
is a primitive,
i.e., has a positive matrix power).

2.3.
Construction of complex Nerman-type martingales

The multi-type counterpart of the single-type equation
\mathcal{L}\mu(\lambda)=1

is the following characteristic equation, see also
[
8
]
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda))=0,

where
\mathbf{I}_{p}
denotes the
p\times p
identity matrix and
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})
.
Henceforth, let
\lambda\in{\kern 0.0pt\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\mathrm{o}}
, the interior of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})
,
be a solution of (
2.3
).
Notice that (A
1
) and (A
2
)
imply that
\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))\not=0
for all
z
in an open neighborhood of
\lambda
.
Indeed,
\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))
is a holomorphic function of
z\in{\kern 0.0pt\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\mathrm{o}}

and it is not constant as it vanishes at
\alpha
and is non-zero eventually by the discussion following (A
2
).
Thus, the set of zeros has no accumulation point in
{\kern 0.0pt\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\mathrm{o}}
.

Whenever
z\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})
is such that
\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))\not=0
,
we can invert
\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)
using the formula

(2.4)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))}\;\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)),

where
\mathrm{adj}(A)
denotes the adjoint of the matrix
A
.
Letting
z\to\lambda
, we see that each entry of
(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}
has a pole with multiplicity at most
the multiplicity of the zero of
\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))
at
z=\lambda
.
We write
\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{N}
for the maximal order of the poles
of the entries of
(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}
at the point
\lambda
.

Further, we denote by
a^{i,j}_{\lambda,k}
the coefficient of
(z-\lambda)^{-k}

in the Laurent expansion of the
(i,j)
-th entry of
(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}

around
\lambda
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Notice that
a^{i,j}_{\lambda,k}=0
for
k>\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}
.
We define
A_{\lambda,k}\vcentcolon=(a^{i,j}_{\lambda,k})_{i,j\in[\,p\,]}

as the matrix collecting all coefficients corresponding to the term
(z-\lambda)^{-k}
.
Consequently, the resolvent
(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}
admits the representation

(2.5)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}A_{\lambda,n}(z-\lambda)^{-n}+h_{1}(z),

where
h_{1}(z)
is a vector-valued function with holomorphic components.
Similarly, expanding
\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)
around
\lambda
, we obtain the Taylor series

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1}\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda))^{(m)}}{m!}(z-\lambda)^{m}+h_{2}(z)(z-\lambda)^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}

with
h_{2}
denoting another vector-valued holomorphic function.
Multiplying (
2.5
) on the left by (
2.6
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{p}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}

(2.7)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\sum_{m=0}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1}\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda))^{(m)}}{m!}(z-\lambda)^{m}+h_{2}(z)(z-\lambda)^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\biggl)\biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}A_{\lambda,n}(z-\lambda)^{-n}+h_{1}(z)\biggl).

By expanding the product and comparing the coefficients on the left- and right-hand sides, we conclude

(2.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-j}\frac{(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda))^{(m)}}{m!}A_{\lambda,m+j}=0\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}.

Hence,

(2.9)

\displaystyle A_{\lambda,j}=\sum_{m=0}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-j}\frac{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(m)}(\lambda)}{m!}A_{\lambda,m+j}\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}.

These identities can also be captured in matrix form, by writing

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A_{\lambda,1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{\lambda,\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda)&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{{}^{\prime}}(\lambda)&\frac{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\lambda)}{2!}&\cdots&\frac{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1)}(\lambda)}{(\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1)!}\\
0&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda)&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{{}^{\prime}}(\lambda)&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda)&\ddots&\frac{\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\lambda)}{2!}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{{}^{\prime}}(\lambda)\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{\lambda,1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{\lambda,\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\end{pmatrix}.

We rewrite the block matrix in (
2.10

\displaystyle\int\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\lambda s}&-se^{-\lambda s}&\frac{s^{2}}{2!}e^{-\lambda s}&\cdots&\frac{(-s)^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1}}{(\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1)!}e^{-\lambda s}\\

and the

Writing

Now define the i.i.d., integrable random

.
Additionally,

(2.15)

\displaystyle Z^{\tau(u)}_{u}(\lambda)=(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Z_{u}(\lambda)=\int(\exp(\lambda,-s)\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}^{\tau(u)}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s}))\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}

Define the random (

As usual, we let

.
Here, a basic observation is that

We now define the Nerman-type complex martingales in the multi-type setting.
To this end, we first define the

Notice that

is integrable with respect to
\mathbf{P}
for every

.
Now define

(2.17)

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda)\vcentcolon=\exp(\lambda,-t)\mathcal{Z}^{\phi_{\lambda}}_{t}\vcentcolon=\exp(\lambda,-t)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})\phi_{\lambda,u}(t-S(u)),\quad t\geq 0.

We define

for

,
with

,

.
It is straightforward to check that

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that (A

) and (A
2
) hold,
and let

Then,

is a martingale with respect to the filtration

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{\tau(\varnothing)}[W_{t}(\lambda)]=W_{0}(\lambda)=(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(\varnothing)})\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}.

Further,

can be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}(\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}

where
\mathcal{C}_{t}\vcentcolon=\{uj\in\mathcal{T}:S(u)\leq t<S(uj)\}
denotes the coming generation at time

that is, the set of all individuals born strictly after time

t

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\alpha
be such that the matrix

\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
is primitive and has spectral radius
1
. As we described in the Introduction, the classical Nerman’s martingale in such a case is a multiple of

V_{t}\vcentcolon=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}v_{\tau(u)}e^{-\alpha S(u)}.

A natural question is how
V_{t}
is related to the matrix‑valued martingale

W_{t}(\alpha)
introduced in our Theorem
2.1
. Because every coordinate of
W_{t}(\alpha)
is itself a martingale,
one might wonder whether still other real valued martingales can be obtained, for instance by inserting terms such as
S(u)^{j}e^{-\alpha S(u)}
.

We claim that in this setting the order of singularity

\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}
equals
1
and that

A_{\alpha,1}=a\,\pi_{\mathbf{v}},

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and

\pi_{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}
is the orthogonal projection onto the
one‑dimensional eigenspace spanned by
\mathbf{v}
; here
\mathbf{w}
is the unique
left (row) eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
with

\mathbf{w}\mathbf{v}=1
. Consequently,

\displaystyle W_{t}(\alpha)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\,\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)}A_{\alpha,1}=a\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\,\mathbf{v}_{\tau(u)}\,\mathbf{w}=a\,V_{t}\,\mathbf{w},

so every coordinate of
W_{t}(\alpha)
is merely a multiple of the martingale
V_{t}
.

It remains to justify the claim above. For

s\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})
define

\rho(s)=\rho(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s))
, the spectral radius of

\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)
. Perron–Frobenius implies that
\rho(s)
is the
dominant eigenvalue, all others having strictly smaller modulus. Because the eigenvalues of a matrix depend continuously on its coefficients (see, e.g.,

[
12
, Theorem 3.1.2]
), we may write

\displaystyle\det(I-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s))\;=\;(1-\rho(s))\,h(s),

where
h
is continuous and bounded away from
0
in a neighbourhood of
\alpha
.
It is known that the function
\rho
is strictly decreasing and convex (see
[
8
, Proposition A.1. and Lemma A.2.]
). This implies that
\alpha
is a simple root
\rho(s)=1
. Therefore, the function
\det\bigl(I-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\bigr)
has a simple zero at
s=\alpha
, which in turn means that the matrix-valued function
(I-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(s))^{-1}
has a simple pole at
\alpha
. Consequently, the multiplicity
\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}
equals
1
.

Finally, from (
2.10
) we have

A_{\alpha,1}=\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)A_{\alpha,1}
. Similar argument yields
A_{\alpha,1}=A_{\alpha,1}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)
.
Iterating these identities and letting the number of iterations tend to infinity gives

A_{\alpha,1}=\pi_{\mathbf{v}}A_{\alpha,1},\qquad A_{\alpha,1}=A_{\alpha,1}\pi_{\mathbf{v}},

so
\operatorname{im}(A_{\alpha,1})=\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v}
and

\ker(A_{\alpha,1})=\{\mathbf{v}\}^{\perp}
. Thus
A_{\alpha,1}
is indeed a scalar
multiple of the projection
\pi_{\mathbf{v}}
.

2.4.
L^{q}
-convergence for
1<q\leq 2

Consider the situation of Theorem
2.1
.
In this section, we provide sufficient conditions for the convergence of

in
L^{q}
for
1<q\leq 2
.
While our primary interest—motivated by a central limit theorem for the multi-type Crump-Mode-Jagers process—lies in the case
q=2
,
we also include the cases
1<q<2
, as they require little additional effort.
We consider the martingale in the form

\displaystyle W_{t}(\lambda)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(\varnothing)})\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}.

Naturally, for
(W_{t}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
to be an
L^{q}
-martingale,
we require that
\mathbf{E}[\|Y_{u}\|^{q}]<\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator matrix norm.
Equivalently, this condition holds with the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, i.e.,
\mathbf{E}[\|Y_{u}\|_{\mathsf{HS}}^{q}]<\infty
,
where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{HS}}
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm.
As we shall see, the following assumption is sufficient to guarantee this:

(A3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl\lVert\int\Big(1+s^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1}\Big)e^{-\theta s}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})\biggl\rVert^{q}\biggl]<\infty

where
\theta=\Real(\lambda)
.

Our main result regarding convergence in
L^{q}
is the following:

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that (A
1
) and (A
2
) hold,
and let
\lambda\in{\kern 0.0pt\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu})}^{\mathrm{o}}
satisfy
\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\lambda))=0
.
Further suppose that, for some
1<q\leq 2
, (A
3
) holds.
If
\Real(\lambda)>\frac{\alpha}{q}
, then
(W_{t}(\lambda))_{t\geq 0}
is an
L^{q}
-bounded martingale.

Remark 2.4
.

The condition
\Real(\lambda)>\frac{\alpha}{q}
is sharp only in the primitive case, i.e., when
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\alpha)

has a matrix power with positive entries only. In the reducible case, the condition may be relaxed in some cases.
We refrain from providing more details.

3.
Examples

Before turning to the proofs of our main results, we first present a few examples.
The first example illustrates what can happen in the non-primitive case,
namely, if the process is started with a type that is subcritical, the corresponding martingale may be trivial.

Example 3.1
.

Consider a multitype general branching process with two types, labeled
1
and
2
.
Individuals of type
1
produce both type-
1
and type-
2
offspring at constant rate
1
,
whereas individuals of type
2
do not produce type-
1
offspring.
Instead, each type-
2
individual either has a single child at a random (exponential) time with probability
1/2
,
or no offspring at all, also with probability
1/2
.
All reproduction events are assumed to be independent.

More formally, we assume that
\xi^{1,1}
,
\xi^{1,2}
,
B
, and
\zeta
are independent.
The processes
\xi^{1,1}
and
\xi^{1,2}
are homogeneous Poisson point processes on
[0,\infty)
with rate
1
.
Type
2
-individuals do not produce type
1
offspring, i.e.,
\xi^{2,1}=0
, and produce at most one type-
2
offspring:
specifically,
\xi^{2,2}=B\delta_{\zeta}
,
where
B\in\{0,1\}
is a Bernoulli random variable with
\mathbf{P}(B=1)=\mathbf{P}(B=0)=\frac{1}{2}
,
and
\zeta
is an exponential random variable with unit mean.

Note that if the population starts with an individual of the second type,
the entire population eventually becomes extinct, as the corresponding Galton–Watson process is subcritical.
The associated Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)
takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 3.0pt\tfrac{1}{z}&\tfrac{1}{z}\\
0&\tfrac{1}{2(1+z)}\end{pmatrix}.

One can easily verify that
\alpha=1
and

\displaystyle(z-1)(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(z))^{-1}\to\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{4}{3}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

as
z\to 1
. The martingale
W_{t}(1)
corresponding to the parameter
\alpha=1
is given by

\displaystyle W_{t}(1)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-S(u)}\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)}\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{4}{3}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}:\\
\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)}=1\end{subarray}}e^{-S(u)}\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{4}{3}\end{pmatrix},

and
W_{0}(1)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(\varnothing)=1\}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac{4}{3}\end{pmatrix}
.
In particular, when the branching process starts with an individual of type 2,
the martingale
W_{t}(1)=(0\,0)
for all
t\geq 0
.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the process introduced in Example 4.5 of
[
8
]
. It is a two-type process in which particles of type 1 give birth to type-1 particles according to a Poisson point process with intensity
\alpha>0
, and particles of type 2 independently produce type 2 offspring, also according to a Poisson point process with intensity
\alpha
. Additionally, each type 1 particle immediately produces exactly one type 2 particle at its time of birth.

In this case, the multiplicity is
\mathtt{k}_{\alpha}=2
, and the corresponding matrices associated with the singularities are given by

\displaystyle A_{\alpha,2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha^{2}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{\alpha,1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&2\alpha\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

This allows us to explicitly express the martingale
W_{t}(\alpha)
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{t}(\alpha)=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&-S(u)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)}\right)(\mathsf{f}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes A_{\alpha,1}+\mathsf{f}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes A_{\alpha,2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\bigg(\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\Big(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&2\alpha\end{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}+\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=2\}}\Big)

\displaystyle\phantom{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\bigg(}+\begin{pmatrix}-S(u)\\
1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha^{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}&\big(2\alpha-\alpha^{2}S(u)\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}+\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=2\}}\\
0&\alpha^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, up to multiplicative constants, there are exactly two distinct martingales:

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}e^{-\alpha S(u)}\big[\big(2-\alpha S(u)\big)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=1\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau(u)=2\}}\big].

Moreover, under the measure
\mathbf{P}^{1}
, both the martingales are non-degenerate.

Proof of Theorem

.

First, we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\lVert Y\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl\lVert\biggl(\int\exp(\lambda,-s)\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})-(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\!\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p})\biggl)\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}\biggl\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl\lVert\biggl(\int\exp(\lambda,-s)\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})-(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\!\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p})\biggl)\biggl\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl].

Since for any
p\times p
matrix
A
, it holds that
\lVert A-\mathbf{I}_{p}\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\leq(\lVert A\rVert_{\mathsf{HS}}+\lVert\mathbf{I}_{p}\rVert_{\mathsf{HS}})^{q}\leq c_{q}(\lVert A\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}+1)
for some finite constant
c_{q}>0
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\lVert Y\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}]

\displaystyle\leq c_{q}\lVert\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl\lVert\biggl(\int\exp(\lambda,-s)\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})-(\mathbf{I}_{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}}\!\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p})\biggl)\biggl\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl]

\displaystyle\leq c_{q}\lVert\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl(1+\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl\lVert\biggl(\int\exp(\lambda,-s)\otimes\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})\biggl)\biggl\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl]\biggl)

\displaystyle\leq Cc_{q}\lVert\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{\lambda}\rVert^{q}_{\mathsf{HS}}\biggl(1+\sum_{l=0}^{\mathtt{k}_{\lambda}-1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{p}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl(\int e^{-\Real(\lambda)s}s^{l}\,\xi^{i,j}(\mathrm{d}\mathit{s})\biggl)^{q}\biggl]\biggl).

for some constant
C>0
, where in the final step we used the fact that the function
x\mapsto x^{q/2}
is subadditive on
[0,\infty)
.
The finiteness of the last expression follows from assumption (A
3
).

We now turn to the
L^{q}
-boundedness, i.e.,
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbf{E}[\|W_{t}(\lambda)\|^{q}]<\infty
,
which, in view of (
4.2
), is equivalent to

(4.3)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbf{E}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}\bigg\|^{q}\bigg]<\infty.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times k}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
be fixed vectors. Then,
(x\otimes 1)
and
(1\otimes y)
are vectors in the space
\mathbb{R}^{1\times k}\otimes\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{p\times 1}
, respectively.
We assume that
|x|,|y|\leq 1
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm, here and throughout.
For (
4.3
) to hold, it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle(x\otimes 1)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)

is an
L^{q}
-bounded martingale. Define

\displaystyle M_{n}(\lambda)\vcentcolon=\sum_{|u|\leq n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)

and notice that its
n
th increment, namely,

\displaystyle M_{n}(\lambda)-M_{n-1}(\lambda)=\sum_{|u|=n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)

is, conditionally on
\sigma(\tau(\varnothing),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}:|u|<n)
, a weighted sum of independent, centered random variables, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Therefore, we may apply the von Bahr–Esseen inequality (see
[
14
, Theorem 2]
for the original version
and
[
3
, Lemma A.1]
for a version for complex-valued random variables) twice: once directly,
and once conditionally on
\sigma(\tau(\varnothing),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}:|u|<n)
, to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\biggl[

\displaystyle\biggl|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)\biggl|^{q}\biggl]

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl|\sum_{|u|=n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)\biggl|^{q}\biggl]

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}\bigg[\mathbf{E}\biggl[\biggl|\sum_{|u|=n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)\biggl|^{q}\,\bigg|\,\tau(\varnothing),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{u}:|u|<n\biggl]\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 16\,\mathbf{E}\biggl[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}|(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)|^{q}\biggl].

Then, using
|x|,|y|\leq 1
and the independence of
\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})
and
Y_{u}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{E}[|\mathbbm{1}_{\{S(u)\leq t\}}(x\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\otimes\mathsf{e}_{\tau(u)})Y_{u}(1\otimes y)|^{q}]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|u|=n}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\lVert\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\rVert^{q}\biggl]\mathbf{E}[\lVert Y_{u}\rVert^{q}].

Using
\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{q}]\leq\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|_{\mathsf{HS}}^{q}]<\infty
and the condition
\theta>\alpha/q
,
we find
\delta>0
so small that
q(\theta-\delta)>\alpha
and some finite constant
C_{\delta}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|u|=n}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\lVert\exp(\lambda,-S(u))\rVert^{q}\biggl]\mathbf{E}[\lVert Y_{u}\rVert^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{q}]\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}\biggl[\sum_{|u|=n}e^{-q(\theta-\delta)S(u)}\biggl]

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{q}]\mathbf{E}[\mathsf{e}_{\tau(\varnothing)}]\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(q(\theta-\delta))^{n}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\delta}\mathbf{E}[\|Y\|^{q}]\mathbf{E}[\mathsf{e}_{\tau(\varnothing)}](\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(q(\theta-\delta))^{-1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}<\infty

where
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times p}
denotes the row vector with all entries equal to one.
Further, notice that we have used that
q(\theta-\delta)>\alpha
,
hence the spectral radius of
\mathcal{L}\boldsymbol{\mu}(q(\theta-\delta))
is strictly smaller than
1
and the infinite series of matrices converges.
The proof is complete.
∎

Appendix A
The Kronecker product

Consider a
m\times n
matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i=1,\ldots,m,j=1,\ldots,n}
and a
k\times l
matrix
B
.
Following
[
1
, Chapter 2.2]
, the Kronecker product of the matrices
A
and
B
, denoted by
A\otimes B
,
is defined as the
mk\times nl
matrix

\displaystyle A\otimes B\vcentcolon=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}B&\cdots&a_{1n}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}B&\cdots&a_{mn}B\end{pmatrix}.

In other words, the Kronecker product increases the dimensions of both matrices
by replacing each entry of
A
with a scaled copy of the entire matrix
B
, resulting in a block matrix.
