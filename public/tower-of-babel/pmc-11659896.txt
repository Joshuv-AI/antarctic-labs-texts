Design, Synthesis, and Evaluation of Trihalomethyl Ketone Derivatives of Neocarzilin A as Improved Antimetastatic Agents
Authors: Noah M Moriarty, Annaleigh M Benton, Lauren E Gartenhaus, Andrew R Nelson, Haley A Harper, Carli J McMahan, Bennett D Elzey, Jason A Hanna, Elizabeth I Parkinson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVesicle Amine Transport-1 (VAT1) is a protein that is overexpressed in many cancers, including breast cancer, glioblastoma, and angiosarcoma. High VAT1 expression correlates with poor overall survival, and genetic knockout models of VAT1 indicate potent antimigratory activity, suggesting that VAT1 is a promising antimetastasis target. Recently, the natural product neocarzilin A (NCA) from Streptomyces carzinostaticus was reported to be the first validated small-molecule inhibitor of VAT1, having strong activity in metastasis models of angiosarcoma and breast cancer. While knockdown of VAT1 has no effect on cell viability, NCA has significant cytotoxicity, suggesting that NCA is not selective for VAT1. Additionally, NCA has poor aqueous solubility, making in vivo administration of NCA challenging and thus limiting its therapeutic potential. Here, we report the design, synthesis, bioactivity, and pharmacokinetics of novel NCA derivatives with improved drug-like properties. Specifically, we have developed derivatives with altered warheads, replacing chlorines on the trichloroketone with fluorines. Using a modified synthetic route, we accessed NCA derivatives with greater than 25-fold improvements in solubility and 30-fold improvements in the antimigratory to antiproliferative bioactivity ratio. The two best derivatives, along with the parent, were analyzed for oral bioavailability, with the two more soluble derivatives showing greatly improved bioavailability. Overall, these studies have resulted in the development of VAT1 inhibitors with improved properties, which will enable further study of the pharmacological inhibition of VAT1 as an antimetastatic strategy. Additionally, these studies provide insights into novel trihalomethyl ketone warheads and identify chlorodifluoroketone as a potent and selective new warhead.Keywords: vesicle amine transport-1, neocarzilin A, trihalomethyl ketone, covalent inhibitor, antimetastasis, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Recent advancements in targeted cancer therapeutics have dramatically improved survival rates and patient outcomes, especially for patients with early-stage diseases.1,2 Unfortunately, advanced cancers have not seen the same improvements, with 90% of all cancer deaths attributed to metastatic disease.3,4 In 2018, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved metastasis-free survival as an end point in clinical trials, resulting in the emergence of migrastatics as anticancer therapeutics.5 Migrastatic compounds target fundamental cell motility pathways to inhibit migration and metastasis. This contrasts with traditional anticancer cytotoxic compounds, which target cancer cell proliferation/survival.6 One advantage of migrastatics not targeting proliferative pathways is that they generally have much lower toxicity and thus fewer adverse side effects compared to traditional chemotherapeutics.7 In addition, resistance is less common for migrastatics since they do not have a selective pressure to produce resistant subpopulations.8 While some migrastatic therapeutics are currently being developed with promising activity, such as the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitor defactinib,9 no small molecules have been FDA-approved with a primary indication as an antimetastatic agent.3,10,11 More targets and lead molecules need to be identified to fully examine the potential of migrastatics.

Vesicle Amine Transport-1 (VAT1) is an understudied protein with links to metastatic progression of breast cancer, angiosarcoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, glioblastoma, and others.12−15 It was found to be highly overexpressed in these cancers, with higher expression correlating with worse overall survival.12,13 VAT1 has many proposed functions, including transportation of phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine between the ER and mitochondria, regulation of mitochondrial fusion, and NADPH-dependent oxidoreductase activity (Figure 1A).16−19 Unfortunately, the link between these activities and the role of VAT1 in cancer progression has not been described. VAT1 has also recently been reported to interact with a variety of proteins critical for focal adhesion complex formation, such as vinculin and talin-1, thus providing a potential link between VAT1 and cancer cell migration.14 Genetic knockdown of VAT1 in breast cancer cells results in significantly lowered cell migration and invasion, corroborating early reports that VAT1 regulates cell migration in glioblastomas.14,20 We have recently identified VAT1 as a target gene of the microRNA miR-497 in angiosarcoma, a rare and aggressive soft tissue sarcoma with the highest mortality rate among sarcomas.15 VAT1 is overexpressed in angiosarcomas, and we found that CRISPR-mediated knockdown or microRNA suppression of VAT1 significantly lowered cell migration in angiosarcomas, further supporting the role of VAT1 in regulating cancer cell migration. In an angiosarcoma tumor allograft model, mice treated with mir-497 had complete abrogation of the tumor, highlighting the therapeutic potential for inhibiting one of its top target genes VAT1.15

Neocarzilin A (NCA) is a natural product from Streptomyces carzinostaticus that was recently reported as the first validated small-molecule inhibitor of VAT1 (Figure 1B).14,21 NCA is composed of a long alkyl tail and an electrophilic trichloromethyl ketone warhead, which is hypothesized to bind VAT1 through a reversible covalent interaction group. Unfortunately, due to the transient nature of the reversible covalent warhead, the site of NCA modification was not previously identified.14 Treatment of angiosarcoma or breast cancer cells with NCA resulted in potent antimigratory activity, consistent with VAT1 genetic knockdown models.14,15 However, NCA also exhibited potent (<1 μM) antiproliferative activity, which is unlikely to be due to VAT1 inhibition, as VAT1 depletion has no effect on cell proliferation or viability.14,15 It has recently been postulated that the antiproliferative activity of NCA is due to an off-target interaction with Bone Marrow Stromal Antigen 2 (BST-2), causing its lysosomal degradation and downstream inactivation of the epithelial growth factor receptor (EGFR).22 The differentiation of these activities represents a unique opportunity to leverage the development of a more selective inhibitor of VAT1 as a migrastatic lead. Previously, in addition to NCA, Gleisner et al. tested the analogues NCB, NCC, and NCA′, which reduce the number of chlorines on the warhead (NCC), remove the stereocenter (NCB), or flip the stereocenter (NCA′).14 While NCB and NCC are both natural products from S. carzinostaticus, each of the tested analogues lost both antimigratory and antiproliferative activity. In addition to these natural derivatives, they also examined a derivative bearing an alkyne handle (NC-1) that had significantly reduced cytotoxicity yet maintained antimigratory activity in the low micromolar range. This highlighted the potential for optimizing antimigratory activity while minimizing off-target toxicity. Despite NCA having potent antimigratory activity in vitro, its poor solubility has made in vivo evaluation of VAT1 inhibition challenging. Previous attempts at formulation proved difficult, requiring both dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and Solutol HS 15 as solubilizing agents.14 Thus, we set out to develop a series of NCA derivatives to optimize the solubility and selectivity for the antimigratory activity.

When developing covalent or reversible covalent inhibitors, the reactivity of the warhead is a critical component. Commonly, the reactivity of the warhead is reduced to increase selectivity and limit toxicity, as has been observed with the FDA-approved antidiabetic saxagliptin and anti-SARS-CoV-2 nirmatrelvir, as well as inhibitors of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase.23−25 However, the opposite strategy (i.e., adding a more reactive warhead) was also successfully utilized. Adding an α-cyano group to acrylamide warheads increases their electrophilicity, which shifts the binding mode from irreversible to reversible covalent inhibition due to the acidity of the α-hydrogen in the covalent adduct.26 This strategy led to the successful development of rilzabrutinib, a promising therapeutic in chronic immune thrombocytopenia.27 Reversible covalent inhibitors are advantageous because they often have the potency and long target engagements of irreversible inhibitors without the associated toxicities.28 Reversible covalent warheads are also often more stable and selective than their irreversible counterparts. Common reversible covalent warheads include nitriles, α-cyanoacrylamides, boronic acids (e.g., bortezomib), and α-ketoamides (e.g., bocepravir, Figure 1B).28,29 The trifluoromethyl ketone is another reversible covalent warhead capable of reacting with cysteine or serine/threonine nucleophiles (Figure S1).30 Trifluoroketones have largely been studied in the context of cysteine and serine protease inhibition, including the elastase inhibitor ZD-0892, which showed promising selectivity and toxicity profiles in clinical trials.31 This warhead has also been explored in inhibitors of histone deacetylases and the anticancer targets FGFR4 and JAK3 kinase, highlighting the potential of this underdeveloped warhead.32,33 Intriguingly, it has recently been discovered that the trifluoromethyl ketone is approximately 100-times more selective for serine/threonine nucleophiles compared to cysteine because serine/threonines are hard nucleophiles and trifluoroketones are hard electrophiles.34 This is in contrast to the pairing of a soft acrylamide electrophile more favorably reacting with the softer cysteine nucleophiles.

Trihalomethyl ketones are known for their ability to form hydrates in aqueous systems, with most examples exclusively focused on trifluoromethyl ketone warheads.35−38 While the ketone form is the primary reactive species for covalent adduct formation, the hydrate is the more soluble form.39 Therefore, we hypothesized that substituting chlorines of the trichloromethyl ketone warhead of NCA with fluorines would allow for tuning of the solubility and reactivity of the warhead. With this strategy, we designed a series of derivatives of the NCA scaffold with different halogenation patterns around the warhead and different lengths of the polyene core to explore warhead reactivity and improve solubility. We utilized Schrödinger Glide to identify in silico hits and then synthesized a small library of NCA derivatives based on these predictions. We identified the chlorodifluoroketone and trifluoroketone derivatives as more selective and soluble antimetastatic leads. The chlorodifluoroketone is particularly interesting since, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of this warhead in the literature. These molecules were then analyzed alongside NCA for their oral bioavailability, with the more soluble derivatives demonstrating greatly improved bioavailability.

Molecular Modeling and Derivative Design

NCA and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) are the only small molecules identified to bind to VAT1.14,40 4-HNE is a generally toxic aldehyde produced through oxidative stress and thus is unlikely to be a promising drug lead. NCA, on the other hand, shows potent inhibition of migration and proliferation in breast cancer and angiosarcoma cell lines and thus was of interest as a potential migrastatic. Its poor solubility and lack of selectivity provided motivation to identify improved derivatives. Initially, we evaluated potential derivatives in silico.14 NCA is hypothesized to bind VAT1 via a covalent interaction in the NADPH/phospholipid-binding pocket.14 However, the reactive residue on VAT1 was not identified. Previously, a site-directed mutagenesis study revealed that glutamic acid (E113), a residue found close to the known NADPH binding site, was necessary for NCA binding (Figure 2). However, due to the low nucleophilicity of the residue and the distance from the active site, we expect that this residue is involved in proper protein folding, not direct binding to NCA.14 To evaluate the potential NCA target amino acids in VAT1, NCA was docked into the binding pocket of the VAT1 crystal structure (PDB: 6LHR) using the Schrödinger Glide docking suite.17,19 The putative substrate binding pocket of VAT1 is a long, thin hydrophobic channel with little accommodation for large or sterically bulky substrates. To reduce bias and identify potentially favorable binding poses, NCA was first docked noncovalently in the VAT1 ligand binding site. NCA exhibited a conserved low energy binding conformation, with the warhead occupying the substrate binding site adjacent to where the nicotinamide ring of NADPH had previously been identified to bind. The alkene region extends across the hydrophobic channel, reaching across the NADPH/phospholipid-binding site with the sec-butyl tail stopping short of where the adenine of NADPH binds, which is near the opening of the pocket and is largely solvent-exposed. Interestingly, in this binding model, the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the NCA warhead is positioned less than 3 Å away from threonine 175 (Figure S2). In Schrodinger, computational mutagenesis was performed to exchange the proposed interacting threonine for an alanine. Upon energy minimization, the protein folding and active site structure were unchanged. However, loss of Thr175 abolished the low energy binding poses of NCA, suggesting that it is important for binding of NCA to the pocket and that it may act as the nucleophilic residue involved in adduct formation with NCA. No other nucleophilic residues were within appropriate bonding distance to the docked NCA. For this reason, we propose that Thr175 is the residue that is covalently modified by NCA.

Initial derivative design focused on identifying compounds with improved predicted solubility and conserved or improved docking scores with VAT1 (Tables 1 and S1). With Thr175 identified as the most likely nucleophilic target of VAT1, we performed covalent docking studies, with potential derivatives forming covalent adducts with Thr175. We first examined whether changing the length of the alkene region of the molecule would affect the predicted affinity via docking scores. Decreasing the length of the alkene region has a positive impact on the predicted solubility, as predicted by SwissADME calculations (ESOL model, SwissDrugDesign), but the top docking poses were less conserved and had lower docking scores. Increasing the length of the alkene chain had the opposite effect, improving the docking score and pose by stretching further across the hydrophobic channel of VAT1 but reducing the predicted solubility. Further elongation or shortening of the polyene region beyond 2 carbons resulted in a large reduction in either solubility or docking score, respectively. For this reason, we only considered derivatives with a 2-carbon elongation or truncation.

We next altered the unusual trichloromethyl ketone covalent warhead, which is generally understudied in the medicinal chemistry literature. In contrast, the trifluoromethyl ketone covalent warhead has been more thoroughly explored, especially in the protease inhibition literature.30,31,41

We were thus interested in determining whether fluorinating the warhead could have a positive impact on binding. Replacing chlorine with fluorine improves the predicted aqueous solubility of the resulting NCA derivatives (Table S1), as would be expected given the higher hydrophobicity of chlorine. The reduction in the steric bulk that results from replacing chlorine with fluorine also impacts the docking affinity for VAT1. We modeled a series of covalent warheads replacing one, two, or all three of the chlorines with fluorines and found that the resulting derivatives bound the pocket in a similar manner as NCA with similar or improved docking scores (Figures 2 and S2, Tables 1 and S1). The substrate binding pocket is confined by the presence of Phe326 and Leu328, with the warhead sterically directed away from these residues for NCA. However, the dichlorofluoroketone warhead (NC-2) is positioned such that the smaller fluorine atom is directed at the edge of the binding pocket, allowing the bulkier chlorine residues to face the interior of the pocket. For the chlorodifluoroketone (NC-3) and trifluoroketone (NC-4) warheads, their smaller bulk further improves predicted binding affinity and even introduces a halogen bonding interaction with one of the fluorine atoms and the backbone amide N–H of Leu328, improving their respective docking scores. We also explored the possibility of switching the warhead to others with a high affinity for threonine residues. The boronic acid warhead was of considerable interest due to its use in the inhibition of an active site, threonine, in the case of bortezomib.29 However, noncovalently or covalently docked boronic acid and hydroxy boronic acid derivatives resulted in a loss in the conservation of low energy binding conformations similar to NCA and had much poorer docking scores (Table S1). In addition, acrylamide and α-cyanoacrylamide derivatives similarly had poor docking scores and a loss of favorable binding conformations. This is potentially due to moving the electrophilic center of the warhead away from the carbonyl carbon to the β-carbon of the acrylamide, resulting in a lower binding affinity. Due to their interesting in silico binding and predicted solubilities, the alternative length derivatives and halomethyl ketone warheads with differing patterns of chlorines and fluorines were chosen for further evaluation.

Synthesis of NCA Derivatives

Synthesis of NCA and derivatives was carried out as previously described with slight modifications (Scheme 1).14,42 Starting with methyl 4-bromocrotonate and triethyl phosphite being reacted neat in an Arbuzov reaction to afford phosphonate 1 in quantitative yield on a 10-g scale. S-(−)-2-Methylbutanol (2) was oxidized to the aldehyde using Dess–Martin Periodinane (DMP). In previous publications, manganese dioxide (MnO2) was used for this oxidation. However, in our hands, yields were more consistent with DMP, likely due to inconsistent activation of MnO2 and the volatility of the aldehyde.14,421 was subjected to lithium bistrimethylsilyl amide (LiHMDS) to afford the phosphonate ylide intermediate, which was subjected to Horner Wadsworth Emmons (HWE) olefination with the aldehyde to afford the diene methyl ester 3 in 50–70% yield over two steps on a gram scale.43 Quantitative reduction of 3 with diisobutylaluminum hydride (DIBAl-H) afforded alcohol 4. 4 was oxidized to the aldehyde using DMP and then directly reacted with the ylide triphenylphosphoranylidene 2-propanone in a Wittig olefination to afford the common intermediate ketone 5 in 50–60% yield over two steps on a 500 mg scale.44 Direct reduction to the aldehyde with DIBAl-H was also successfully attempted, as has been previously demonstrated, but yields were more consistent when using multiple equivalents to reduce to the alcohol followed by oxidation to the aldehyde with DMP. Ketone 5 was reacted with LiHMDS to afford the enolate, which was acylated directly with (CX3CO)2O or CX3COCl (X = H, Cl, F) to afford NCA, NCC, and NC-3 to NC-5 in 40–75% yield on a 100 mg scale.45 For NC-2, dichlorofluoroacetic acid was reacted with oxalyl chloride in the presence of catalytic DMF to afford acid chloride 6, which was directly subjected to the enolate of ketone 5 to afford NC-2.

Divergent Syntheses of NCA and NCA Derivatives

The truncated alkene derivatives (NC-6 to NC-9) were accessed in a similar manner. Briefly, ethyl bromoacetate was reacted with triethyl phosphite neat in an Arbuzov reaction to afford phosphonate 7 in quantitative yield on a 10-g scale. S-(−)-2-Methylbutanol was oxidized to the aldehyde with DMP and reacted with the phosphonate ylide derived from 7 reacting with LiHMDS. This afforded the α,β-unsaturated ester 8 in 50–70% yields over two steps on a gram scale. This ester was reduced with DIBAl-H to afford alcohol 9 in a quantitative yield. 9 was oxidized to the aldehyde and reacted with the ylide triphenylphosphoranylidene 2-propanone to afford the common intermediate ketone 10 in 40–60% yield over two steps on a 650 mg scale. Ketone 10 was reacted with LiHMDS to afford the enolate that was directly acylated with (CX3CO)2O (X = Cl, F) to afford the truncated derivatives NC-6 to NC-8 in 15–55% yield on a 35–100 mg scale.

The synthesis of the elongated derivative NC-9 started from the diene ester 3, which was reduced to the intermediate diene alcohol 4. 4 was oxidized to the aldehyde using DMP and reacted with the phosphonate ylide resulting from reacting 7 with LiHMDS in an HWE olefination reaction. This afforded triene ester 11 in 48% yield over two steps on a 400 mg scale. Ester 11 was reduced to alcohol 12 by using DIBAl-H in quantitative yield. 12 was oxidized to the aldehyde and subjected to a Wittig reaction with the commercially available ylide triphenylphosphoranylidene 2-propanone to afford the elongated ketone 13 in 37% yield over two steps on a 230 mg scale. 13 was reacted with LiHMDS to afford the enolate and subsequently acylated with trichloroacetic anhydride to afford NC-9 in 77% yield on a 100 mg scale.

Aqueous Solubility

With these derivatives in hand, we first set out to identify derivatives with improved aqueous solubility using laser nephelometry (Table 1 and Figures S3–S12).46−48 NCA was very poorly soluble (12 μM). As predicted, the shorter alkene NC-6 has approximately 4-fold greater aqueous solubility than NCA, while the longer alkene region results in lower aqueous solubility (<10 μM). Due to the very poor solubility of the longer alkene derivative, further elongated derivatives were not explored. As hypothesized, the fluorinated warheads dramatically improved solubility, leading to up to 30-fold greater solubility for the trifluoro derivative NC-4 compared to the parent NCA, with a stepwise increase in the solubility being observed for each additional fluorine on the warhead. During the solubility studies, the increases in observed solubility were far greater than those predicted by ESOL.49,50 Others have previously found that trifluoromethyl ketones exist in equilibrium with their hydrated forms (Figure 3A).31,37 These hydrated forms are predicted to be more water-soluble compared to their ketone counterparts (Figure S13). If these derivatives exist in equilibrium with the hydrated form in aqueous media, this may help explain the large enhancement in solubility.51

The HDAC inhibitor 8b and the clinically evaluated human leukocyte elastase inhibitor ZD-0892 have previously been shown to equilibrate to the hydrate species in water at different ratios depending on the substituents surrounding the warhead. (B) Equilibrium of ketone to hydrate in aqueous media for NCA and the derivatives NC-7 to NC-9. n = 3; error is standard deviation. To better understand the predominant form of the derivatives in an aqueous buffer, we set out to quantify the amount of hydrate that exists in equilibrium with the ketone using liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LCMS/MS). NCA, NC-2, NC-3, or NC-4 was pre-equilibrated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the ratio of ketone to hydrate was quantified (Figures 3B and S13–S18). As expected, the trichloromethyl ketone existed primarily as the ketone with little hydrate (∼2%) being observed. Chlorines are not as electron-withdrawing as fluorines, leaving the electrophilic carbonyl carbon less polarized than derivatives with fluorines. As such, α-chlorinated ketones have not previously been determined to be in equilibrium with their respective hydrates. Interestingly, all of the fluorinated warheads were capable of forming a substantial amount of hydrate, with each additional fluorine resulting in statistically significant higher amounts of hydrate. For NC-4, there were nearly equal amounts of ketone (55%) and hydrate (45%) in the equilibrium. When taking into account the amount of hydrate, the predicted aqueous solubility for the fluorinated warheads is much more accurately predictable using ESOL (Figure S13).

Bioactivity

We next analyzed our derivatives for their antiproliferative and antimigratory activity. We first screened for antimigratory activity via a scratch/wound closure assay (Table 1 and Figure S19).14,52 The triple-negative breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231 was used for initial testing because the VAT1 expression profile is well understood and NCA has previously shown both antiproliferative and antimigratory activity in this cell line.14 Cells were treated with 1 μM NCA or derivatives, and the ability of the cell monolayer to fill in the scratch over a period of 24 h was analyzed. Interestingly, the trifluoromethyl ketone derivative NC-4 showed little activity in the scratch/wound closure assay despite having great solubility and molecular modeling scores. This may be due to the high amount of hydrate present for the trifluoromethyl ketone derivative. It is known that the ketone is the reactive form,39 and the more compound present as the hydrate, the less active the compound is expected to be. In contrast, the chlorodifluoromethyl ketone derivative NC-3 exhibited the best antimigratory activity, suggesting the importance of finding the optimized balance in terms of reducing steric bulk in the active site and limiting the equilibrium of the hydrate.39 Unsurprisingly, NC-5, which has a relatively unreactive methyl group in place of the trihalomethyl group, had no activity.

The antiproliferative activities of the NCA derivatives were then measured using the CellTiter-Glo viability assay (Table 1 and Figures S20 and S21).53 The parent NCA exhibits rather potent antiproliferative activity (IC50 < 1 μM) in a variety of healthy and cancerous cell lines despite genetic knockdown models for VAT1 showing no effect on cell viability. This suggests that NCA exhibits polypharmacology, and thus, we speculated that the derivatives with attenuated warhead reactivity could have reduced affinity for the targets causing the antiproliferative activity. In previous studies, small changes to the scaffold, such as the alkyne derivative used for chemoproteomics (NC-1), resulted in a 10-fold reduction of the antiproliferative activity but retention of the antimigratory activity against VAT1.14 We screened our derivatives against the MDA-MB-231 cells and found that the removal of one of the chlorines of NCA to make the natural product NCC resulted in a reduction in the antiproliferative and antimigratory activity, corroborating the results of previous studies.14 The truncated alkene derivative NC-5 exhibited a modest reduction in the antiproliferative activity (IC50 = 3.8 μM), although the elongated derivative NC-9 exhibited even greater potency than the parent NCA (700 nM), suggesting that the length and hydrophobic character were important for the antiproliferative activity. The greatest effect on antiproliferative activity was observed for the replacement of the chlorines with fluorines. The addition of fluorines to the warhead resulted in a stepwise decrease in cytotoxicity for both the normal length NC-2 to NC-4 derivatives and the truncated NC-6 and NC-7 derivatives (Table 1 and Figures S20 and S21). Interestingly, despite a potent reduction in the antiproliferative activity, compounds NC-2 to NC-4 retained their antimigratory activity in the scratch/wound closure assay at 1 μM. This encouraging result suggests that the warhead plays a significant role in the antiproliferative to antimigratory bioactivity ratio, leading us to compare them using a selectivity index (i.e., the ratio of antimigratory activity at 1 μM to the antiproliferative IC50).54,55 Compounds that exhibited a selectivity index greater than ten were selected for the confirmation of the antimigratory activity using a transwell migration assay (Figure 4).56 Although the truncated alkene derivatives NC-7 to NC-8 exhibited the greatest improvements to solubility and the highest selectivity index, they failed to exhibit a potent reduction of migration in the transwell migration assay, leaving compounds NC-3 and NC-4 as the most potent derivatives in the assays tested. Interestingly, NC-4 exhibited a lower reduction in migration than NC-3, suggesting that the high level of hydration of the trifluoromethyl ketone warhead can impede inhibition of VAT1, resulting in lower reductions in migration than the chlorodifluoromethyl ketone counterpart of NC-3. To further evaluate the effect of hydration on binding VAT1, the hydrated versions of NCA, NC-3, and NC-4 were modeled noncovalently in the putative substrate binding pocket of VAT1 (PDB: 6LHR, Figure S2). Since the covalent chemistry would not be possible with hydrate, covalent docking was omitted. From the docking study, it was found in all cases that the hydrated compounds had considerably worse docking scores and output poses than their ketone counterparts. This further suggests that high levels of hydration may negatively affect the binding of the inhibitor to VAT1.

Pharmacokinetics Analysis of NCA, NC-3, and NC-4

NCA has previously been tested in vivo for its ability to inhibit the migration of breast cancer cells to the lungs in a murine tail-vein model of metastasis.14 Despite the results being insignificant, NCA showed some potential for the strategy of VAT1 inhibition in vivo. After determining that NCA has very poor aqueous solubility, we hypothesized that the initial poor results for in vivo studies of NCA were likely due to poor pharmacokinetics resulting from poor solubility. Previously, it has been shown that aqueous solubility significantly correlates with bioavailability and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics. Indeed, models for predicting bioavailability and PK parameters, developed by Kasugi et al. and Parrott et al., both found that aqueous solubility was an important parameter for predicting oral bioavailability.57,58 Additionally, in the development of novobiocin derivatives, it was found that optimization of solubility was one of the most important determinants to improve the pharmacokinetics of their lead molecule.59 Improvements in solubility over solithromycin and gepotidacin also led to improved bioavailability and clearance pharmacokinetic parameters.59 We hypothesized that our more soluble derivatives NC-3 and NC-4 would exhibit a more favorable pharmacokinetic profile than NCA and thus would make them better tools for studying VAT1 inhibition in vivo.

To evaluate the pharmacokinetics, NCA or derivatives were then administered to mice at 25 mg/kg via oral gavage (per os, p.o.) administration. The formulation of NCA was a cloudy homogeneous suspension at 25 mg/kg (85% PBS pH = 7.4, 10% Solutol, 5% DMSO), but NC-3 and NC-4 were completely clear solutions, highlighting their increased solubility. Following compound administration, the plasma concentration was monitored over a period of 4 h. Due to the poor solubility and oxidative liability of the polyene core of NCA, it was expected that its clearance would be quite rapid, hence the reason for the analysis being terminated after 4 h. Additionally, in predicting the metabolism of NCA using SwissADME calculations of drug-like properties,4949 it was identified as a substrate for both CYP2C19 and CYP2C9. As expected, NCA exhibited a very short half-life and was largely cleared from systemic circulation after the first 1 h (Figure 5). The fluorinated derivatives maintained a much longer residence time in circulation, with relatively high concentrations of compound remaining at the 4 h time point when the experiment was terminated. The derivatives NC-3 and NC-4 both exhibited substantially increased AUC(0-infinity) in comparison to NCA. This highlights the increased concentration of drug that is entering circulation and is available for distribution compared to NCA and is reflected by a longer tmax and a higher Cmax for NC-3 and NC-4. However, while NCA is clearly in the elimination phase by t = 4 h, NC-3 and NC-4 are still likely in the distribution and postabsorption phases and are not exhibiting a linear elimination range from which to determine an accurate elimination rate constant. This conflates the data, leading to lower half-lives and mean retention times, confounding data comparison with NCA. However, given the significantly improved AUC for these compounds, it is clear that they are being absorbed and entering systemic circulation at a much higher rate than the parent NCA. This allows us to maintain therapeutically relevant concentrations (>1 μM) even at the terminal 4-h time point for both NC-3 and NC-4, while NCA dropped below this value after the first 15 min. This highlights the utility in improving the aqueous solubility to improve the pharmacokinetics of this class of compounds. It also corroborates previous results, highlighting that more soluble novobiocin derivatives exhibited slower clearance and improved bioavailability than the more poorly soluble initial hits.59 We similarly identified this in a covalent inhibitor system.

Conclusions

We have designed and synthesized a series of derivatives of the natural product NCA based on in silico analysis with its target VAT1 and SwissADME solubility predictions. The derivatives replace the warhead of NCA with a series of fluorinated counterparts that dramatically improve their solubility and maintain antimigratory activity. We performed a pharmacokinetic analysis of NCA and our top derivatives and found that the improvements in solubility of the derivatives resulted in a much-improved pharmacokinetic profile over NCA. These derivatives can serve as chemical tools to evaluate the potential antimetastatic strategy of VAT1 inhibition in vivo and guide the future development of migrastatic compounds targeting VAT1. Additionally, this study discovered that chlorodifluoromethyl ketones are a novel warhead for targeting threonine or serine residues.

Materials and Methods

Unless otherwise noted, ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) analyses were performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC in line with an ACQUITY RDa Detector and an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 1.7 μm diameter, 2.1 × 50 mm2 column. Waters_connect (Unifi) software v.3.0.0.15 was used in the analyses of the spectra.

Molecular Modeling Using Schrödinger’s Maestro and Glide Docking Software

Schrödinger’s Maestro software (Schrödinger Suite 2022-4, Schrödinger LLC) was used to fetch the VAT1 crystal structures (PDB codes: 6LHR, 6K9Y, 6LII).1,2 The protein waters were deleted, and in the scenario in which a ligand was present in the binding site of a monomer used for docking (e.g., NADPH or nitrite ions), the ligand was deleted. The protein structure was prepared for docking using the Protein Preparation Workflow Wizard in Maestro, performing hydrogen bonding assignment with PROPKA. Docking substrates (neocarzilin A, derivatives, NADPH control, phosphatidylserine control) were prepared as 2D structures utilizing PerkinElmer’s ChemDraw software. The structures were transferred into the Maestro workspace, and 3D energy minimization was performed using the LigPrep tool with an OPLS4 force field model. For noncovalent studies, the Receptor Grid Generation tool was used to prepare a grid box (20 Å) encompassing the putative binding pocket of VAT1, a channel that stretches across the interior of the protein and includes the NADPH and phosphatidylserine binding pockets. The Ligand Docking program within Glide was then used to noncovalently dock the prepared ligands into the VAT1 binding pocket with the extra precision setting. For covalent docking, the Covalent Docking tool in Glide was used with the nucleophilic addition into a double bond set as the covalent chemistry. Based on extensive profiling of the reactive residues within the putative binding pocket, threonine 175 was used as the covalently modified residue. This also was suggested in the noncovalent docking where the highest scoring poses for neocarzilin A were positioned within 3 Å of the oxygen of Thr175. This modeled the hypothesized hemiacetal formation upon covalent addition into the carbonyl of the trichloromethyl ketone. The extra precision setting was used for covalent docking, and MM-GBSA scoring was calculated for all output poses.

SwissADME Solubility Predictions

Structures of molecules (smiles strings) were uploaded on the SwissADME section of the SwissDrugDesign Web site.3 The predicted water solubility (ESOL model) was then recorded.

UPLC/MS Quantification of the Ratio of Hydration in Trihalomethyl Ketone Covalent Warheads

Stocks of test compounds were prepared in DMSO at 10 mM. One microliter of the prepared 10 mM stock was diluted with 99 μL of PBS (pH = 7.4) in Fisher 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes (graduated natural) in triplicate to afford a final concentration of 100 μM (1% DMSO in PBS). Compounds were incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow for equilibrium to be reached and analyzed using a Waters UPLC/MS in line with an ACQUITY RDa Detector with an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 1.7 μm diameter, 2.1 mm × 50 mm column. The mobile phases A and B were H2O + 0.1% formic acid (FA) and acetonitrile +0.1% FA, respectively, and samples were run on a gradient of 5–95% mobile phase B over 6 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL·min–1, followed by a gradient of 5–95% mobile phase B from 7 to 7.10 min and holding at 5% B until the run ended at 9 min. Waters_connect (Unifi) software version 3.0.0.15 was used in the identification of trihalomethyl ketone and hydration components and for peak area analysis.

Determination of IC50 Values by CellTiter-Glo

MDA-MB-231 cells (RRID:CVCL_0062) were obtained from M. Wendt at Purdue University. Cells were maintained in DMEM (SH30243, HyClone) with 10% FBS (SH30910.03, HyClone), 1× antibiotic-antimycotic penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B (PSA) (A5955, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 as described previously.9 Cell viability was determined using the CellTiter-Glo Assay (G7570, Promega, Madison, WI) measuring luminescence with a BioTek Synergy 2 (BioTek/Agilent, Winooski, VT, RRID:SCR_020536) following treatment with NCA or derivatives for 72 h.

Scratch/Wound Closure Antimigratory Assay

Scratch assays were performed by seeding cells at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/mL. Cells were pretreated with 1.0 μM NCA or derivatives and incubated for 24 h. A scratch wound was produced using a sterile pipet tip, and the resulting scratch was imaged at t = 0. Media was replaced with 1% FBS DMEM with 1.0 μM of NCA or derivatives, and scratch wounds were imaged again at t = 24 h. Sixteen independent fields per sample were used and analyzed in ImageJ.10 The area of each scratch was calculated using ImageJ software and compared to the area of the scratch at t = 0 h. Percent migration was normalized to that of the DMSO control.

Transwell Migration Assay

Transwell migration assays were performed with transwell permeable supports (3422, Corning) with 2.5 × 104 cells suspended in serum-free DMEM. DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was used as a chemoattractant in the bottom chamber. After 18 h, nonmigrated cells were removed from the inner chamber with a cotton swab, and migrated cells were stained with crystal violet. The resulting stained transwell insert was imaged and quantified by manually counting the number of migrated cells in six independent fields per sample. Each biological replicate was normalized to the corresponding DMSO control, which was set to 100% migration.
