Management of Malunited Paeditric Surgical Neck Humerus Fracture proves the phrase “Less is More”
Authors: Ravi Kumar, Anita Saran, Akshat Gupta, Abhishek Kumar Mishra, Prateek Sihag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA malunited proximal humerus fracture treated unconventionally, not in line with the literature recommendations, achieved excellent functional results at 6 months of follow-up.Case presentationA 14 year-old boy presented with an inability to raise his right arm above shoulder level and a deformity in the right shoulder. Following the X-ray and further investigations, the fracture shows angulation and displacement beyond the acceptable criteria. After consideration of the patient's demands, analysis of the difficulties faced by the patient, and consideration of the surgical challenges, it was decided not to treat with Open Reduction Internal Fixation.ConclusionDespite the treatment not aligning with literature recommendations, the patient was able to achieve an excellent functional result. This malunited proximal humerus case highlights the importance of individualizing treatment for each patient and emphasizes the concept of ‘less is more’ in medicine.Keywords: Malunited surgical neck humerus fracture, Paediatric, Unconventional treatment, Less is more

Introduction

We live in a world where we continually seek data-driven decision-making. This is especially relevant when it comes to patient treatment. Often, we find ourselves so preoccupied with clinical complexities that we overlook straightforward solutions and easily get caught up in intricate issues. Not all complex problems have complex solutions. This idea was evident in a recent case encountered in our clinical practice. A 14-year-old boy presented to the outpatient department with a malunited fracture of the right surgical neck of the humerus, along with restricted motion in his shoulder. Initially, the injury and its associated complications appeared complex. However, after carefully analyzing the situation and its intricacies, we discovered that the solution was quite straightforward and needed to be shared with others to emphasize the concept of ‘less is more,’ which is sometimes relevant in medicine [1,2].

Case presentation

A 14-year-old right-hand dominant boy presented to the orthopedic outpatient department of the institution with restricted movement in his right shoulder following a polytrauma around six weeks ago. He sustained chest, abdominal, right shoulder, and head injuries in a road traffic accident, following which he was admitted to a state-run hospital. He had a chest tube placed for hemopneumothorax and had a poor GCS, for which he was put on a ventilator. The patient sustained a displaced surgical neck humerus fracture during the same event, which doctors at that hospital decided to treat non-operatively.

On examination, six weeks following the injury, his GCS was 15/15. He had multiple healed abrasions over the chest and abdomen, with a healed scar of chest tube insertion in the mid-axillary line. A bony prominence over the right shoulder from the fractured distal fragment was observed, tenting the anterior deltoid and skin over the shoulder. The range of motion observed in forward flexion (FF) was 80 degrees, abduction (Abd) was 45 degrees, external rotation (ER) was 10 degrees, and full internal rotation (IR). On close evaluation, it was noted that the prominent displaced proximal part of the distal fractured fragment impinged and produced a mechanical block to shoulder movement, especially external rotation and abduction (Fig. 1). Rotator cuff strength couldn't be assessed formally at this stage. The Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) was 15, indicating poor shoulder function.

Sketch showing mechanical block in abduction.

Diagnostics

An anteroposterior (AP) view X-ray of the affected shoulder showed a displaced malunited fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus (Fig. 2). On the CT scan, it was clear that there was 100 % displacement between the fractured fragments, and the angulation was 60 degrees (Fig. 3). The CT scan also revealed that the posterior cortex of the proximal fragment was uniting with the shaft.

Antero-posterior view of affected shoulder at the time of presentation.

2D and 3D CT scan images showing displacement at the fracture.

Treatment

The patient was placed in a beach chair position with an interscalene block and general anaesthesia. A Delto-Pectoral approach was utilized. The deltoid over the prominent bone was thinned out, and the prominent bony part was delivered out of the deltoid through the surgical wound. The prominent part was then shaved off, protecting the biceps tendon (Fig. 4). During the surgery, under anaesthesia assessment, it was found that the patient could achieve full function without a mechanical block. It was decided not to perform an osteotomy and re-fixation with plates and screws.

Sketch showing site of osteotomy during surgery.

Outcome

Post-operatively, the patient was mobilized the very next day and began scapular setting and rotator cuff strengthening exercises at 2 and 4 weeks, respectively. At the 6-month follow-up, he had achieved FF – 170 degrees, ER – 90 degrees, IR – T10, and abduction – 170 degrees. X-rays show good radiographic union at the fracture site (Fig. 5). Rotator cuff strength was 5/5 as per MRC grading in all four muscles. Functionally, he can perform all his activities of daily living without any pain. Radiographs at six months show good consolidation at the fracture site. The Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) was 48 at the 6-month follow-up, indicating satisfactory shoulder function.

Radiograps at six months follow up.

Discussion

The Treatment of paediatric proximal humerus fractures is based on age, fracture displacement, and the remodeling potential of the patient [3]. No literature suggests conservative treatment for a 14-year-old boy with 100 % displacement and 60° of angulation [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. According to literature recommendations, the fracture should be osteotomized, brought to length, and the fragments should be aligned until healing occurs using one of the different fixation methods [11].

However, the challenges included mobilizing the uniting fracture, addressing tissue contractures, extensive dissection to overcome tissue contractures, increased chances of infection due to prolonged surgical time, and the difficulty of choosing a fixation method that would keep the fragments in a well-aligned position while minimizing the risk of compromising the remaining growth of the proximal humerus.

After reviewing the literature, discussing the case within the department with my colleagues, and considering the patient's remaining growth and the morbidities associated with extensive surgical procedures, I decided to keep the options open. Plan A was to shave off the prominent part (Fig. 4) and assess the mechanical block during surgery. Plan B was to osteotomize, bring the fragments into alignment, and fix them with a plate and screws. Fortunately, on-table assessment confirmed that Plan A was sufficient, and I gained confidence that the patient would be able to achieve almost normal shoulder function.

The limitations of this case report are that it is a single case, and the results cannot be generalized. Secondly, the follow-up period is only six months.

Conclusion

This case is unique due to its challenges, and although the treatment provided was not recommended by the literature, we achieved excellent functional results. This forces us to rethink the established criteria for operative recommendations and emphasizes the importance of individualizing treatment. This case also reinforces the concept of ‘Less is More’ in medicine [1,2]. We live in an era of information overload, which has the potential to blind us and prevent us from seeing the forest for the trees.
