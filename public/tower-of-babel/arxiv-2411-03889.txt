On functional equations for Chow polylogarithms
Vasily Bolbachan

Abstract
Chow polylogarithms are some special functions arising in explicit description of the Beilinson regulator map. The most interesting functional equation for this function reflects its vanishing on the boundary in the Bloch’s cycle complex. We show that this functional equation formally follows from more simple ones, namely skew-symmetry, functoriality and multiplicativity. To prove this, we study some analogue of Bloch’s cycle complex and establish for this complex an analogue Beilinson-Soule vanishing conjecture. A. Goncharov defined a group of functional equations for classical polylogarithms. We show that any such functional equation formally follows from functional equations for Chow polylogarithms stated above.

Introduction

Let
X
be a smooth proper variety over
\mathbb{C}
. A. Goncharov
[
Gon05
]
constructed an explicit map from the motivic cohomology of
X
to its Deligne cohomology (see also
[
BGFT11
]
). In the simplest case
X=\spec\mathbb{C}
this construction is reduced to so-called Chow polylogarithms. Let
Y
be
(m-1)
-dimensional variety. We write
\mathbb{C}(Y)^{\times}
for the multiplicative group of non-zero rational functions on
Y
. Define a map
\theta_{Y}\colon\Lambda^{2m-1}\mathbb{C}(Y)^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}
by the following formula

\theta_{Y}(f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{2m-1})=\int_{Y(\mathbb{C})}\log|f_{1}|d\log|f_{2}|\wedge\dots\wedge d\log|f_{2m-1}|.

The Chow polylogarithm

\mathcal{P}_{m}
is defined by the formula

\mathcal{P}_{m}(Y,a)=\theta_{Y}(a)
. Up to some simple constant
[
GZ01
]
, this definition coincides with the definition given in
[
Gon05
]
.

Our immediate goal is to formulate functional equations for this function and investigate relationships between them.

An alteration
is a proper morphism which is generically finite. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2}
be two proper varieties and
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration. The following formula follows directly from the definition:

\mathcal{P}_{m}(Y_{2},a)=\dfrac{1}{\deg\varphi}\mathcal{P}_{m}(Y_{1},\varphi^{*}(a)).

(*)

Let
(F,\nu)
be a discrete valuation field. We denote by
\overline{F}_{\nu}
the residue field. The following proposition was proved in
[
Gon95
]
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
. There is a unique map
\ts_{\nu}\colon\Lambda^{k}F^{\times}\to\Lambda^{k-1}\overline{F}_{\nu}^{\times}
such that the following property holds. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in F^{\times}
and assume that
\nu(a_{2})=\dots=\nu(a_{k})
. Then
\ts_{D}(a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{k})=\nu(a_{1})\cdot(\overline{a_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge\overline{a_{k}})
.

Let
Y
be a smooth variety and
D
be an irreducible divisor. Denote by
\nu_{D}
the map
\ts_{\nu_{D}}
where
\nu_{D}
is a discrete valuation corresponding to
D
. Let us formulate another functional equation. Let
Y
be a proper
m
-dimensional variety and
f_{1},\dots,f_{2m}
are non-zero rational functions on
Y
. Assume that the union of zeros and poles of the functions
f_{i}
is a snc divisor on
Y
. Set
a=f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{2m}
. Then

\sum\limits_{D\subset Y}\mathcal{P}_{m}(a,\ts_{D}(a))=0.

(**)

To investigate the relationship between
(*)
and
(**)
we need the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

Denote by
\widehat{C}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
, freely generated by isomorphism classes of the pairs
[Y,a]
, where
Y
is smooth projective variety of dimension
m-1
and

. Denote by
\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
the quotient of
\widehat{C}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
by the following relations:

1.

Let
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration and
a\in\Lambda^{2m-1}(\mathbb{K}(Y_{2})^{\times})
. Then

[Y_{2},a]=\dfrac{1}{\deg\varphi}[Y_{1},\varphi^{*}(a)].

2.

For any

, we have

[Y,a+b]=[Y,a]+[Y,b].

The Chow polylogarithm
\mathcal{P}_{m}
provides the canonical map
\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}
given by the formula
[Y,a]\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{m}(Y,a)
. This implies that the group
\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
is non-zero.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
Y
be a smooth proper
m
-dimensional variety. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{2m}
be non-zero rational functions on
Y
and assume that the union of zeros and poles of the functions
f_{i}
is a snc divisor on

. Set

. Then the following formula holds:

\sum\limits_{D\subset X}[D,\ts_{D}(a)]=0\in\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K}).

In other words,

formally implies

. The case

gives classical Weil reciprocity law. This statement is a natural generalization of Weil reciprocity law to a variety of arbitrary dimension. We believe that the group
\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
has the same relationship to Chow polylogarithms as the classical Bloch group
[
Gon95
]

B_{m}(\mathbb{K})
has to classical polylogarithms.

Let
P\in\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1}]
. Define the following element of the group
\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
:

\gamma_{P}=[({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1},(1-x_{1})\wedge x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge(1-x_{m-1})\wedge x_{m-1}\wedge P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})].

The following statement follows from the proof of Theorem

The group

1.1
The complex
\Lambda
and Beilinson-Soule vanishing conjecture

The definition of cubical higher Chow groups can be found in
[
Lev94
]
. Let
\square^{n}=({\mathbb{P}^{1}}\backslash\{0\})^{n}
. Subvarieties given by the equations of the form
z_{i}=0,\infty
are called
faces
. The group
z^{m}(n)
is some subquotient of codimension
m
algebraic cycles on
\square^{n}
which are in good position with respect to faces. The graded abelian group
z^{m}(2m-*)
forms a chain complex where the differential is given by the sign sum of restrictions to codimension
1
faces. It is known that the cohomology of this complex is isomorphic to the motivic cohomology of
\mathbb{K}
.

To represent an algebraic cycle in
\square^{n}
we can take an algebraic variety
Y
of dimension
n-m
together with
n
non-zero rational functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
. The cycle is
\psi_{*}([Y])\cap\square^{n}
, where the map
\psi\colon X\to({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{n}
is given by the formula
\psi(z)=(f_{1}(z),\dots,f_{n}(z))
. The idea of
[
Bol23
,
Bol24
]
was to replace the data
(Y,f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
by the data
(Y,a)
where
a\in\Lambda^{n}(\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times})
. The differential is given by the sum of the residue maps.

Let
X
be an algebraic variety and
m,j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Set
p=\dim X+m-j,n=2m-j
. Denote by
\Lambda(X,m)_{j}
a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
, freely generated by the triples
[Y,a,\psi]
, where:
Y
is a variety of dimension
p
,
a\in\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}
and
\psi\colon Y\to X
is a proper morphism. The relations has the following form:

1.

Let
[Y_{2},a,\psi]\in\Lambda(X,m)_{j}
and
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration. Then

[Y_{2},a,\psi]=\dfrac{1}{\deg\varphi}[Y_{1},\varphi^{*}(a),\psi\circ\varphi].

2.

Let
[Y,a,\psi],[Y,b,\psi]\in\Lambda(X,m)_{j}
. Then

[Y,a+b,\psi]=[Y,a,\psi]+[Y,b,\psi].

In the case
X=\spec\mathbb{K}
we write
[Y,a]
instead of
[Y,a,\psi]
and denote this complex by
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
.
The differential in the complex
\Lambda(X,m)
is defined as follows. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}
and
a=f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
. Taking resolution of singularities, if necessary, we can assume that the union of zeros and poles of
f_{i}
is a snc divisor. By definition

d([Y,a,\psi])=\sum\limits_{D\subset Y}[D,\ts_{D}(a),\psi\circ i_{D}].

In this formula the sum is taken over all irreducible divisors and
i_{D}
is the canonical embedding
D
into
Y
. It was checked in
[
Bol24
]
that
d
is well defined and
d^{2}=0
.

The complex
\Lambda(X,m)
is in many respects similar to the complex calculating higher Chow groups. We conjectured that these two complexes have the same cohomology. While it is not known in full generality, we proved this statement in the case when
X
is a spectrum of a field and the degree is greater than or equal to the motivic weight minus one. It is natural to ask whether the complex
\Lambda(X,m)
satisfies an analog of the Beilinson-Soule vanishing conjecture. Standard reductions show that we can assume that
X=\spec\mathbb{K}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

We have
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
is zero for
j\leq 0
and
m\geq 1
.

So, surprisingly, the vanishing holds degree-wise. This statement gives some evidence for the original Beilinson-Soule vanishing conjecture. The Theorem
1.3
is a reformulation of the statement that the differential map
d\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{0}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{1}
is zero.

1.2
Application to classical polylogarithms

The classical
m
-logarithm is defined by the formula

Li_{m}(z)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{z^{k}}{k^{m}}.

We need a certain single-valued version of this function
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}_{m}}
defined in
[
Lev01
]
.
Denote by
\mathbb{Q}[{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K})]_{m}
a vector space freely generated by the points of
{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K})
. We denote the generators as
\{a\}_{m},a\in{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K})
. A. Goncharov
[
Gon95
]
defined a subspace
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathbb{K})\subset\mathbb{Q}[{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K})]_{m}
. If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and
\sum n_{\alpha}\{z_{\alpha}\}_{m}\in\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
then

\sum\limits_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{m}(z_{\alpha})=0.

Conjecturally, the group
\mathcal{R}_{m}
describes all functional equations for the function
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
.
The
m
-th Bloch group is defined by the formula
B_{m}(F)=\mathbb{Q}[{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(F)]_{m}/\mathcal{R}_{m}(F)
.
Let
Y
be an algebraic variety and
f\in{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K}(Y))
. Define an element in
\Lambda^{2m-1}\mathbb{K}(Y\times({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1})^{\times}
. If
f=0,\infty
, then this element is equal to
0
. Otherwise,

\omega_{m}(Y,f)=(1-x_{1})\wedge x_{1}\wedge(x_{1}-x_{2})\wedge x_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge(x_{m-2}-x_{m-1})\wedge x_{m-1}\wedge(x_{m-1}-f).

This form is closely related to the algebraic cycles representing classical polylogarithm in motivic cohomology
[
Lev94
]
.
In the case
Y=\spec\mathbb{K}
we will denote this element simply by
\omega_{m}(f)
. We have
[({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1},\omega_{m}(a)]=\gamma_{Q_{a}}
, where
Q_{a}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})=x_{1}\dots x_{m-1}-a
. We have the following result
[
Lev01
,
Gon05
]
:

Proposition 1.6
.

There is a constant
q_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that for any
a\in{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

{\mathcal{P}}_{m}(({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1},\omega_{m}(a))=q_{m}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}_{m}}(a).

(***)

Define a map
\mathcal{T}_{m}\colon\mathbb{Q}[{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{K})]_{m}\to\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
sending

\{a\}_{m}
to
[({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1},\omega_{m}(a)].

Theorem 1.7
.

The map
\mathcal{T}_{m}
sends
\mathcal{R}_{m}
to zero. In particular, we have the well-defined map
\mathcal{T}_{m}\colon B_{m}(\mathbb{K})\to\widehat{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
.

Informally speaking, this theorem says that any functional equation from
\mathcal{R}_{m}
for the function
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
is a formal corollary of
(*)
and
(***)
.

A definition of a period can be found in
[
KZ01
]
. A famous conjecture stated in loc. cit. says that any relations between periods formally follows from the projection formula, Stokes formula and additivity. We have the following corollary:

Corollary 1.8
.

Any relation from
\mathcal{R}_{m}
for the function
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
satisfies the statement of the Kontsevich-Zagier conjecture.

is generated by the element of the following form:

[Y,f_{1}\wedge(1-f_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge f_{m-1}\wedge(1-f_{m-1})\wedge g].

Denote this element by
\xi
. Consider the map
\widetilde{\psi}_{1}\colon Y\to({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1}
given by the formula
y\mapsto(f_{1}(y),\dots,f_{m-1}(y))
. We can assume that
\widetilde{\psi}_{1}
is regular. Denote the image of
\widetilde{\psi}_{1}
by
Z
. Let
\psi_{1}
be the natural map
Y\to Z
. Consider several cases:

1.

Let us assume that
\dim Z=\dim Y
. In this case the map
\psi_{1}
is an alteration. We can assume that the element
g
lies in
\mathbb{K}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m-1})
. We get:

\xi=\dfrac{1}{\deg\psi_{1}}[({\mathbb{P}^{1}})^{m-1},x_{1}\wedge(1-x_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m-1}\wedge(1-x_{m-1})\wedge Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})].

2.

Let us assume that
\dim Z<\dim Y
. Consider the map
\psi_{2}\colon Y\to{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
given by the formula
y\mapsto g(y)
. We can assume that
\psi_{2}
is regular. Denote by
\psi\colon Y\to Z\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
a map given by the formula
y\mapsto(\psi_{1}(y),\psi_{2}(y))
. If the map
\psi
were not dominant than the transcendence degree of the extension
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{K}(f_{1},\dots,f_{m-1},g)
would be strictly smaller than
m-1
. In this case Lemma
2.1
would imply that
\xi=0
. So we can assume that the map
\psi
is an alteration. We get

\xi=\dfrac{1}{\deg\psi}[Z\times{\mathbb{P}^{1}},\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge(1-\widetilde{f}_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{m-1}\wedge(1-\widetilde{f}_{m-1})\wedge t].

In this formula
\widetilde{f}_{i}
are some functions on
Z
and
t
is the canonical coordinate on
{\mathbb{P}^{1}}
. By Lemma
2.5
this element lies in
\mathcal{F}_{m}
and so is equal to zero.

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

If one of the functions
f_{i}
is constant, then

[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{2m-1}]=0.

Proof.

Define a map
mult\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m-1)_{0}\times\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{1}
given by the formula

[Y,f_{1},\dots,f_{2m-2}]\otimes\lambda\mapsto[Y,f_{1},\dots,f_{2m-2},\lambda].

By Theorem
1.5
, the group
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m-1)_{0}
is zero and so the map
mult
is zero. This implies the statement of the corollary.
∎

Denote by

\widetilde{\delta}_{2,F}(\{a\}_{2})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a=0,1,\infty\\
a\wedge(1-a)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let

\widetilde{\delta}_{m,F}(\{a\}_{m})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a=0,\infty\\
\{a\}_{m-1}\otimes a&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The group
\mathcal{R}_{m}(F)

.

To prove Theorem

Lemma 3.1

The restriction of

to

induces an isomorphism
\Lambda(X,m)^{\prime}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K}(X),m)

Proof.

Denote the map stated in the lemma by
s^{\prime}
. It is easy to see that
s^{\prime}
is surjective.
Let
U
be some non-empty open subset of
X
. Denote by
s_{U}
the natural map
\Lambda(X,m)\to\Lambda(U,m)
and by
s_{U}^{\prime}
the restriction of this map to

. To show that the map
s^{\prime}
is injective in is enough to show that for any non-empty

s_{U}^{\prime}
is injective.

Let
Z=X\backslash U
. Let
\Lambda(X,m)_{Z}
be the subgroup of
\Lambda(X,m)
generated by
[Y,a,\psi]
such that the image of
\psi
is contained in

. Otherwise

\Lambda(X,m)_{Z}\to\Lambda(X,m)

Let

. On the other hand as

.

It follows from

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Y
be a smooth variety,
\psi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
an alteration and

a divisor contracted under
\varphi
. Let

are non-zero rational functions on
Y
. Set
H=\psi(D)
and
\alpha=f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
. Assume that
[D,\psi^{*}(\alpha)]\neq 0
. Then there are
i\neq j
such that
H
lies on both divisors
\supp((f_{i}))
and
\supp((f_{j}))
.

Proof.

If for any
i
,
\ord_{D}(f_{i})=0
, then
[D,\varphi^{*}(\alpha)]=0
. So for some
i
, we have
\ord_{D}(f_{i})\neq 0
. We can assume that
i=1
. Assume by contradiction that for any
j\neq 1
, we have
\ord_{D}(f_{j})=0
. Then
\ts_{D}(\varphi^{*}(\alpha))=\nu_{D}(\varphi^{*}(f_{1}))\wedge\left.{\varphi^{*}(f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n})}\right|_{D}

is contracted under

, the transcendence degree of the field

is strictly smaller than

. So by Lemma
2.1
, we have
[D,\varphi^{*}(\alpha)]=0
. A contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Y
be a smooth

\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y

\widetilde{Y}
. Let
