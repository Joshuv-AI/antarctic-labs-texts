Nash-convergence of Game Dynamics and Complexity
Oliver Biggar, Christos Papadimitriou, Georgios Piliouras

Abstract
Does the failure of learning dynamics to converge globally to Nash equilibria stem from the geometry of the game or the complexity of computation? Previous impossibility results relied on game degeneracy, leaving open the case for generic, nondegenerate games. We resolve this by proving that while Nash-convergent dynamics theoretically exist for all nondegenerate games, computing them is likely intractable. We formulate the Impossibility Conjecture : if a locally efficient Nash-convergent dynamic exists for nondegenerate games, then P = P ​ P ​ A ​ D P=PPAD . We validate this for three specific families of dynamics, showing their tractability would imply collapses such as N ​ P = R ​ P NP=RP or C ​ L ​ S = P ​ P ​ A ​ D CLS=PPAD . En route, we settle the complexity of finding Nash equilibria of a given game that lie on a given affine subspace. Finally, we explain why the general conjecture remains open: we introduce a Proving Game to demonstrate that black-box reductions cannot distinguish between convergent and non-convergent dynamics in polynomial time. Our results suggest the barrier to Nash learning is not the non-existence of a vector field, but the intractability of computing it.

Introduction

The Nash equilibrium, proposed by John F. Nash in 1950
[
16
]
, is arguably the most influential idea in the history of economics. It launched Game Theory, inspired Arrow and Debreu to prove the fundamental theorems of welfare economics, while its universality — the fact that every game is guaranteed to have one — enabled economists everywhere, while designing new markets, institutions, or mechanisms, to contemplate the equilibrium with the certainty, afforded by Nash’s theorem, that such an equilibrium exists.

When game theory came under scrutiny by computer scientists in the late 1990s, certain serious computational shortcomings of the Nash equilibrium became clear. The Nash equilibrium was eventually proved intractable to compute in normal-form games even with two players
[
9
,
6
,
10
]
, while the multiplicity of Nash equilibria — the other computationally unwieldy aspect of the Nash equilibrium concept — appears to have no principled, computationally feasible remedy
[
12
]
. A common defense of the Nash equilibrium by economists in the face of such computational criticism is that
“despite all, the players will eventually get there.”
Notice that this argument shifts the debate from complexity theory to the realm of
learning dynamics in games,
see Section 2 for a brief introduction; by
learning dynamics
we mean dynamics that are meant to model the reaction of agents to the experience of repeated play. Indeed, dozens of different learning dynamics have been proposed by game theorists, economists, and computer scientists over the decades (some even preceding Nash’s theorem
[
5
]
), in many cases explicitly motivated by this quest for Nash-convergent dynamics. In fact, Nash’s own 1950 proof of existence
[
16
]
starts by defining a particular dynamic, now often called
the Brown-non Neumann-Nash dynamic,

[
18
]
which, however, is known to cycle. Still, to date there is no known dynamic
\phi
that is both
locally tractable and Nash converging.
A dynamic is locally tractable if, for any given game, the displacement
\phi(x)
— or the gradient
\dot{\phi}(x)
for a continuous-time dynamic — can be computed at any mixed strategy profile
x
in time that is polynomial in the description of the game; and it is Nash converging if it has the property that it is guaranteed to converge to a Nash equilibrium in all games and from all starting points.

In view of the failure of the research community over seven decades to come up with locally tractable Nash convergent dynamics, it was conjectured in 2019
[
17
]
that this absence reflects an even more serious deficiency of the Nash equilibrium: perhaps there can be
no
dynamic that is guaranteed to converge to the Nash equilibrium in all games — surely not a locally tractable one. Such failure would make the Nash equilibrium even less palatable as a solution concept. This line of work culminated more recently in the proof of an
impossibility theorem

[
14
]
proving the conjecture of
[
17
]
: There is a game in which there is no dynamic whatsoever — efficiently computable or not — that converges globally to precisely the game’s Nash equilibria. The decades long quest by economists for a Nash-convergent dynamic has been unproductive for concrete mathematical reasons!

The counterexample game used in the impossibility proof of
[
14
]
, however, is highly
degenerate
. A game is degenerate if there are mixed profiles that have more pure best responses than the dimension of the game implies, due to numerical coincidences. As this definition suggests, almost all games are non-degenerate — and in view of this observation the impossibility theorem of
[
14
]
appears to lose its bite a little.

In fact, degeneracy is
required
for such an impossibility result to hold, because the impossibility theorem
fails
to hold for nondegenerate games! It turns out that, in a nondegenerate game one can always define a dynamic whose fixed points are the game’s Nash equilibria, and in fact we show here that, for two such contructions of dynamics, there is a Lyapunov function “showing the way.” In Section 2 we describe rigorously — we believe for the first time — several such constructions. However, all these constructions of Nash convergent dynamics require exponential time to compute at any given point, in the worst case. In view of this, there seems to be only one research direction that promises to restore the spirit and intent of the impossibility theorem in the face of degeneracy:
Change the topic back to complexity theory,
and argue that there are
complexity obstacles
in the way to defining, for any nondegenerate game, a locally tractable Nash convergent dynamic.

This is the goal of the present paper. We describe four different approaches, of increasing generality, to the construction of Nash-convergent dynamics for any given non-degenerate game. This defines four different families of Nash-convergent game dynamics, which we dub Type 1 to Type 4. Type 1 is the simplest one and an incomplete version of it was already known to the authors of the impossibility theorem
[
14
]
. Type 2 is a more sophisticated geometric construction exploiting the well-known topological fact that non-degenerate games have an
odd
number of Nash equilibria
[
19
]
.

For each of these two types, it is easy to see that the dynamic has a
Lyapunov function,
a function mapping each point to the positive reals that is strictly decreasing with the dynamic, and zero at the fixed point Nash equilibria — and hence it is globally Nash convergent. Type 3 is a generalization of the first two types, in which the dynamic
comes
with a circuit promised to compute a Lyapunov function — in addition to the arithmetic circuit promised to compute the Nash convergent dynamic itself.

Finally, Type 4 is the most general class of dynamics — and our main interest in this line of work: we are simply given a circuit computing a dynamic that is promised to be Nash-convergent.

We show that each of Type 1, 2, and 3 cannot be locally tractable unless complexity classes collapse. For the sake of contradiction, we assume that we are given oracle access to an arithmetic circuit — or two circuits in the case of Type 3 — for computing the dynamic and possibly its Lyapunov function, and prove that, if indeed the circuits work as promised, a collapse of complexity classes must happen: P = PPAD for Type 1, NP = RP for Type 2, and CLS = PPAD for Type 3. The implication of these
black-box proofs
is that each of these three types of Nash-convergent dynamics needs in the worst case exponential-time computation. By
black-box proof
we mean a proof that assumes that a locally tractable Nash-convergent dynamic exists, and concludes that a complexity class collapse must hold.

To prove the complexity class collapses for Types 1 and 2 mentioned above, we need to solve in polynomial time the following interesting computational problem related to the Nash equilibrium, which we believe has never been considered before:
Given a game and a line (or half line) in the strategy space, is there a Nash equilibrium on this line?
We prove that this problem can be solved in polynomial time. In fact, the generalization in which, instead of a line, we are given an affine subspace of dimension
d
— and we seek the Nash equilibria on this space — can also be solved in polynomial time, as long as the dimension is
O(\frac{\log|G|}{\log\log|G|})
where by
|G|
we denote the length of the description of the game. If the dimension is polynomial in the description, the problem becomes NP-hard.

Coming back to the impossibility or Type 4 — the most general type — we have been unable to find a similar proof of complexity class collapse. We conjecture that, if such a circuit exists for every game, then P = PPAD. We believe that this is a central conjecture in game dynamics, whose proof will be an important step towards understanding the way in which games, learning dynamics, and computation interact.

We also believe that proving this
Impossibility Conjecture
will require the development of new types of complexity-theoretic arguments, for the following reason. The existence of a polynomial-time computable Nash convergent dynamic does not immediately contradict the established difficulty of computing Nash equilibria, simply because the dynamic may very well take exponentially many steps to converge. A new kind of hybrid topological/complexity-theoretic argument seems to be needed, treating learning algorithms as topological objects in order to establish that local polynomial-time computation creates some kind of complexity tension at remote points of a compact domain.
In fact, we prove a result that we believe captures the mathematical and conceptual difficulty described above in complexity-theoretic terms. We define a proof protocol that we call
the proving game,
or
PG
, which we believe captures the essence of a large class of black-box proofs of the Impossibility Conjecture. We prove that the adversary in
PG
has a winning strategy, a result suggesting that no black-box proof of the impossibility conjecture for nondegenerate games is likely. Black-box proofs are a very common way to prove complexity class collapse implications, and the only kind used so far in this line of work.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: After preliminary definitions, in Section 3 we define the four types of Nash-convergent dynamics sketched in the introduction, and in Section 4 we prove that, for each of the dynamics of the first three types, if that dynamic were computable in polynomial time in the description of the underlying game, then a complexity class collapse would ensue. In Section 5 we focus on the most general dynamics of Type 4, and we prove a result implying that a collapse theorem like those in Section 3 cannot be proved for Type 4 in the black-box proof style. Finally, in the last section we discuss the implications of these results and contemplate the way forward.

Games

A game

A

The Nash equilibrium is of paramount importance in Game Theory, and has been considered for 75 years as the default “meaning of the game,” the place where the action is destined to end up. However, as recounted in the introduction, intractability and non-uniqueness have shaken the field’s faith in the Nash equilibrium. A common counterargument of its adherents is that pairs or groups of players, or institutions and societies, will eventually and spontaneously end up at a Nash equilibrium — despite the adverse evidence. This brings in the important subject of

.

Dynamical Systems

A

\phi(\phi(x,t),t^{\prime}))=\phi(x,t+t^{\prime})

\phi(x,t):=f^{t}(x)=f(f(\dots(f(x))\dots)

While these two definitions differ in important ways, the two theories of continuous- and discrete-time dynamical systems are surprisingly parallel — for example, the impossibility result mentioned in the introduction, as well as the Fundamental Theorem of Dynamical System to be explained soon, hold for both.
In this paper, we shall work mostly with discrete-time dynamics;
however we occasionally use continuous time dynamics, and an exposition with the continuous-time variety would also be possible.

The purpose of the mathematical field of dynamical systems is to predict — and, implicitly, compute — the limit behavior of the system as time goes to infinity, when the system is started at some initial point in
X
. The simplest possible limit behavior would be for the dynamical system to converge to a fixed point, or, a bit more generally, a

In three or more dimensions, however, far more complex limit behaviors prevail, including the phenomenon of chaos. Researchers strove for over a century to restore the simplicity of Poincaré-Bendixson Theorem to higher dimensions, and these efforts culminated in the 1980s with the Fundamental Theorem of Dynamical Systems due to Charles Conley
[
8
]
. It states that all dynamical systems will eventually cycle, provided one redefines cycling: Call two points

if, for every
\epsilon

if

to one of the equivalence classes, called

This means that it will not only converge, but will do so guided by a

Dynamical systems are important in game theory. Starting even before Nash

5

Unfortunately, these systems fail to converge to Nash equilibria in general games, with convergence results typically limited to classes with special structure such as potential games
[

]
, two-player zero-sum games or two-player

[

Four Types of Nash-convergent Dynamics

It was already observed in

The central hypothesis of this paper is that this is true for all Nash convergent dynamics in nondegenerate games.

Type 1.

Since the game
G
is assumed nondegenerate, it follows that its Nash equilibria comprise a set of distinct points, and we call this set

\phi(x)=x+\frac{z-x}{|z-x|}\cdot\frac{|z^{\prime}-x|}{k}

\phi

\phi

Type 3.

In any Nash-convergent dynamic

Indeed, our Type 1 dynamic does have a Lyapunov function

Our third type of Nash-convergent dynamic generalizes Types 1 and 2: We are given two arithmetic circuits, one circuit that computes the dynamic

Type 4.

Finally, the fourth type of Nash-convergent dynamics is even more general — in fact, as general as possible: We are given a game
G
and an arithmetic circuit that is promised to compute the gradient of a Nash-convergent dynamic of
G

Is there a dynamic
\phi_{G}
for every nondegenerate game
G

4
Complexity Results and Conjectures

In this section we argue that no locally tractable Nash-convergent dynamics of each of the first three of the four types of the previous section can exist. All three are proofs by contradiction. First, we assume that such a dynamic exists. Then we use this hypothesized dynamic as a
black box
to solve a difficult problem, a problem whose efficient solution implies that a complexity class currently thought not to be P is actually P. We conclude that there is a complexity obstacle to the existence of such a dynamic. This style of complexity-theoretic argument is known as a
black-box proof
because it proceeds with no consideration to the precise details of how the dynamic is implemented. Note that each of these types of dynamic assume the game is non-degenerate — in each black-box proof, either we show a difficult problem can be solved or point out that the game is in fact degenerate.

For Type 1 dynamics, the argument requires the solution of a basic computational problem (
“is there a Nash equilibrium on a given line?”
) addressed in the next subsection. For Type 2, the argument is somewhat nontrivial and relies on the same algorithm. For the third type, the proof is a full-blown reduction, albeit a straightforward one, in which we show that any dynamic of the required kind would establish the collapse of two complexity classes that are not believed to coincide, CLS and PPAD.

Finally, Type 4 seems even harder to prove intractable:
we have been unable
to find a black-box proof for this, most general, case. In the next section we show that there is a mathematical reason why a black-box proof of this impossibility result is unlikely to exist, and thus a novel kind of complexity argument may be needed to settle the most general impossibility conjecture for degenerate games.

4.1
Nash Equilibria in an Affine Subspace

The proofs of this section require the efficient solution of the following problem: Given a line
x=A+B\cdot\lambda
for vectors
A
and
B
in the mixed strategy space
X

Nash on an affine space
to be the following computational problem: Given a game
G
and an affine space
\mathcal{A}
of dimension

x_{i}=A_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{ij}\cdot\lambda_{j},

one for each strategy (dimension)

Theorem 4.1
.

The

d=O(\frac{\log|G|}{\log\log|G|})
, and is NP-hard if
d=\Omega(|G|^{\delta}))
for any

is the size of the representation of game

Proof.

Consider the intersection of the space of mixed strategies
X
with the given
d

O(n)

1
]

n^{O(d)}

[
11
]

and, for each face — corresponding to a possible support of mixed strategies in the game — we test whether (a) the interior of the face contains a Nash equilibrium, achievable through Tarski elimination; and (b) whether this equilibrium — guaranteed to be unique — also belongs in the affine space
A
. The total time for this test is
n^{O(d)}
. Since
d=O(\frac{\log|G|}{\log\log|G|})
, this algorithm runs in polynomial time in

Now for the NP-hardness part of the Theorem: When

c>0

[
7
]

∎

Remark.

coincide with the symmetric Nash equilibria of the
symmetrization of
G
,
a game of the form
(A,A^{T})
. So, it suffices to consider the corresponding line in this space and find the symmetric Nash equilibria that it contains, if any. These equilibria are the solutions of the following system of degree-two inequalities:
(Ax)_{i}\leq x^{T}Ax,i=1,\ldots,n
.
Since the sought equilibria are on a line parametrized by
\lambda
, we can reduce this system to a system of quadratic inequalities involving the single variable
\lambda
. By sorting the roots of these quadratic polynomials in
\lambda
, we can solve the system of inequalities and identify all Nash equilibria on the line, if any. It turns out that this algorithm can be generalized to more than two players and higher dimensions, within bounds as prescribed in the theorem, through a rather involved argument employing Tarski’s elimination of quantifiers for the theory of reals.

4.2
Type 1 Dynamics

We now turn to applying the affine space result with
d=1
— that is, telling whether a Nash equilibrium lies on a line — in order to prove complexity lower bounds for Nash convergent dynamics in games of the first three types, starting with the easiest case of Type 1.

Theorem 4.2
.

If a locally tractable Nash convergent dynamic of Type 1 exists for all games, then
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a game, and let
\phi
be the assumed dynamic. It is easy to see that we can use the circuit computing
\phi
, together with the algorithm for finding a Nash equilibrium on a line, to find a Nash equilibrium of
G
. Since this PPAD-complete to do for arbitrary games, this completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Type 2 Dynamics

Theorem 4.3
.

If a locally tractable Nash convergent dynamic of Type 2 exists for all games, then
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{RP}
.

Proof.

We shall prove that, given oracle access to the hypothesized dynamic
\phi
, one can decide with arbitrarily high probability whether the game
G
has a unique Nash equilibrium, or more than one. Since the problem is NP-hard
[
7
]
, this would complete the proof. The test is simply the following: Choose a point in
X
at random, compute the direction of
\phi
on this points, and test if there is a Nash equilibrium on this line. If so, then the game almost certainly has a unique Nash equilibrium. If not, the game has multiple equilibria.

The key properties of the dynamic of Type 2 that ensure the correctness of the algorithm are that, (a) if the game has one Nash equilibrium,
\phi
heads towards it from all points in
X
, since there is only one polytope,
X
; and (b) if there are multiple equilibria, then in the interiors of all chambers
\phi
is perturbed slightly from pointing to the Nash equilibrium of the chamber, in order to be reconciled with the dynamic at the next even polytope. As a result, there are only two kinds of points in
X
that can possibly point to equilibria:

•

Those that happen to lie on the same hyperplane perpendicular to
\ell
at a Nash equilibrium, a set of measure zero; and

•

For each chamber, and each orbit in the chamber, a set of points in a polytope that happen to point to a Nash equilibrium
in another polytope.
It is easy to argue that this set of points is at most countable for each orbit in the chamber and each target Nash equilibrium, and thus also of measure zero.

This completes the proof.

∎

We believe that a careful analysis can strengthen the complexity implication of this result to
\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{P}
.

4.4
Type 3 Dynamics

We now turn to the next type of dynamics, in which we are also provided with a circuit promised to compute a Lyapunov function.

Recall that PPAD is the class of all search problems that are reducible to the problem
End of a Line,
in which we are given a circuit
C
defining an exponentially large directed graph
G_{C}
with indegrees and outdegrees that are certified to be either zero or one — that is, an implicit graph guaranteed to be the union of cycles and paths (including isolated nodes). In this graph, one node called
0
is certified to be a source, and the task of the
End of a Line
problem is to find either a node with indegree one and outdegree zero, or a node with outdegree one and indegree zero other than the
0
node.

CLS is a subclass of PPAD, defined through the addition of a second circuit
\Phi
computing a
potential function

\Phi
mapping the nodes of the graph to the nonnegative integers, with the property that for all edges
i,j
of
G_{C}
,
\Phi(i)>\Phi(j)
. Again, we seek either either a source or a sink of
G_{C}
.

Theorem 4.4
.

If a locally tractable Nash convergent dynamic of Type 3 exists for all nondegenerate games, then
\mathsf{CLS}=\mathsf{PPAD}
.

Proof.

The proof of the Theorem is clear from the above definitions of complexity classes: If a Nash convergent dynamic
\phi
of Type 3 existed for any nondegenerate game
G
, then the circuit computing
\phi
together with the circuit computing its Lyapunov function
L
would constitute a pair of circuits establishing that the Nash equilibrium problem for the arbitrary game
G
could be solved in the framework of the class CLS. Since this problem is complete for PPAD, the larger class PPAD must collapse into CLS, completing the proof.
∎

4.5
Type 4 Dynamics and the Impossibility Conjecture

We finally articulate the overarching open research problem proposed in this paper:

Conjecture 4.5

(The Impossibility Conjecture)
.

If a locally tractable Nash convergent dynamic exists for every nondegenerate game, then
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
.

Naturally,
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
is only the strongest collapse we can hope to prove in the conclusion of this statement. However, intermediate collapses, such as
\mathsf{CLS}=\mathsf{PPAD}
, are also very much worthy goals.

We believe that proving the Impossibility Conjecture would be a key step on the way of understanding game dynamics, as well as their connections to Complexity Theory. Further, this conjecture will require for its proof a new style of mathematical reasoning, a sophisticated methodology that draws from Complexity Theory, Game Theory, and the topological theory of dynamical systems. In other words, what is required is a novel theory in which algorithms are seen as topological objects. In the next section we define a formal framework which, we believe, goes a long way towards capturing this difficulty.

5
An Obstacle to Proving the Impossibility Conjecture

A black-box proof of the Impossibility Conjecture, if one existed, would proceed as follows. Let
\phi_{G}
be a hypothesized locally tractable Nash-convergent dynamic, defined for each game
G
. Suppose that, for any game
G
, we could query
\phi_{G}
at some sequence of points in polynomial time — where the next query can be computed in polynomial time from the answers to the previous ones — in such a way that, after a polynomial number of queries, we can compute a Nash equilibrium of
G
based on the answers. If such an algorithm were proved to exist, we would conclude that
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
, proving the conjecture.

In this section we will argue that no such proof strategy will work, for the following intuitive reason: there are Nash-convergent dynamics that provide
no information whatsoever
about the Nash equilibria within polynomially many black-box queries.

The Proving Game.

First recall
[
6
]
that there is a polynomial
r
such that the problem of finding a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium in a given two-player game
G
is PPAD-complete. We express this as follows:

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mathsf{PPAD}\neq\mathsf{P}
, then for every polynomial-time algorithm there are infinitely many two-player games
G
for which the algorithm will fail to compute a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium.

We next define a game denoted
PG,
played between a polynomially-bounded
Black-box prover
Bob and a computationally unbounded
Adversary
Alice. To start, Bob chooses a polynomial
p
. Then a two-player game
G
is chosen, and the following
run on game
G
of
PG
is played: Alice claims to possess a Nash-convergent discrete-time dynamic in
G
, and she challenges Bob to locate a Nash equilibrium of
G
by querying her at points of the mixed strategy space of
G
.

Bob and Alice play
p(|G|)
rounds, during each of which Bob chooses, after a
p(|G|)
-time computation, a point
x
in the domain
X
of
G
(presumably informed by Alice’s answers so far), and Alice responds by revealing the step
\phi(x)
of the claimed dynamic. If after the
p(|G|)
rounds Bob
cannot
reveal a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium of
G
, and Alice
can
describe a legitimate Nash-convergent dynamic that is consistent with all of Alice’s previous replies to the queries by Bob, then Alice
prevails
in the run of
PG
on
G
; otherwise Bob prevails.

Finally, Bob wins
PG
if he prevails
in all runs
, for all games
G
. Otherwise, Alice wins.

We believe that
PG
as defined above captures reasonably well the process of proving the Impossibility Conjecture by a black-box proof.
Hence, the following result can be seen as evidence that discovering a black-box proof of the Impossibility Conjecture is problematic and unlikely:

Theorem 5.2
.

If
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PPAD}
,
PG
is a forced win by Alice.

Remark:

Recall that
PG
is of interest only if
\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PPAD}
, because the Impossibility Conjecture would be false if
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
via Type 1 dynamics.

Proof.

We shall describe a winning strategy for Alice. In the run of the
PG
on a game
G
, in the first, interactive stage of the Proving Game, Alice responds to each of Bob’s queries
x
with
\phi(x)=x+2^{-|G|}\cdot{\dot{\beta}(x)}
, where
\dot{\beta}
is the direction of a standard game dynamic called the
Brown-von Neumann-Nash dynamic
, defined for each strategy
a
of player
i
as follows:

\dot{\beta}(x^{a})=\bigl[u_{i}(a,x_{-i})-\bar{u}_{i}(x)\bigr]_{+}-x_{i}^{a}\sum_{b}\bigl[u_{i}(b,x_{-i})-\bar{u}_{i}(x)\bigr]_{+}

Here,
u_{i}
is the utility function of player
i
and
\bar{u}_{i}
the average utility weighted over all strategies, while the
[.]_{+}
notation takes the max between zero and the argument. Intuitively, this is a best response dynamic in which each player moves probability mass to the strategies that have above average utility. It is important to note that this gives Bob no new information, because Bob could compute
\phi(x)
in polynomial time without input from Alice. In addition, the fixed points of this dynamic are exactly the Nash equilibria
[
18
]
, so Bob cannot compute a fixed point in polynomial time.

Now notice that the interaction between Alice and Bob so far can be seen as a candidate deterministic algorithm with the game
G
as input: the algorithm can output the polynomially many query points put forward by Bob, and check whether one of them is a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium.
Our proof of the theorem will focus on the run of the
PG
on the particular game
G
, guaranteed by Lemma 5.1, on which this algorithm fails,
and will establish that Alice will prevail in this game, completing the proof of the theorem. Among the infinitely many such games, we shall choose one that is large enough so that certain asymptotic inequalities hold.

Let
Q:=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}\}
to be the set of
query points
by Bob, with
|Q|\leq p(|G|)
. To define the required dynamic
\Phi
, we start by fixing
\Phi(x)=\phi(x)
for every
x\in Q
. We claim that
Q
has the following properties:

1.

Q
contains no
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium;

2.

Q
contains no
periodic points,
that is, no subset
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\}
such that
\phi(x_{i})=x+{i+1\bmod k}
;

3.

Q
contains no point
q
such that there is a Nash equilibrium within a distance of
2^{-2|G|}
from the line defined by
q
and
\phi(q)
.

Failure of each of the three claims contradicts that the Alice-Bob algorithm cannot find a
\frac{1}{r({|G|})}
-approximate Nash equilibrium of the present game
G
. For Claim (1) this is direct. For Claim (2), it follows from the following fact:

Lemma 5.3
.

If
q
is a periodic point of Alice’s dynamic, then it is an
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium

Proof.

The proof relies on the properties of
\phi
. Since
\phi
is a best response, and in addition it cycles, it follows that for every player and each strategy of the player the derivative of the utility of the player with respect to this strategy will change sign between
q
and
\phi(q)
for some point
q
of the cycle. Hence, by continuity, there is a point of indifference for this strategy of this player somewhere between
q
and
\phi(q)
. All these points of indifference are within a Euclidean distance of
p(|G|)2^{-|G|}
of each other, since each step of the cycle has length
2^{-|G|}
and the cycle has at most
p(|G|)
steps, and we can assume that this quantity is smaller than
\frac{1}{r(|G|)|G|^{2}}
. This, in addition to our assumption that all utilities are in
[0,1]
, implies that all these points are
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibria, completing the proof.
∎

Finally, if Claim (3) were false, we claim that Bob could find a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium of
G
, as follows: Suppose that the line defined by
q
and
\phi(q)
is
q+b\cdot\lambda
, and write the symmetrized equilibrium conditions
(Ax)_{i}\leq x^{T}Ax,i=1,\ldots,n
for
G
, where
n
is the number of strategies of
G
, in terms of the variable
\lambda
. The resulting system of quadratic inequalities in
\lambda
can be rewritten as simple ordering conditions on the sorted roots of the resulting
n
quadratic polynomials in
\lambda
. If Claim (3) is violated, these inequalities would have a near-solution, one that violates some of them by at most
2^{-|G|}
for some
\lambda
, and Bob can determine this near-solution by inspection of the inequalities. That would give Bob a
\frac{1}{r(|G|)}
-approximate Nash equilibrium of
G
.

To complete the proof we must show that, in the absence of these three conditions, computationally unbounded Alice can successfully carry out the second stage of
PG
by exhibiting a Nash-convergent dynamic
\Phi
compatible with her answers in the first stage.

We subdivide
Q
into
chains
, sequences
(q_{j})
in which
\phi(q_{i})=q_{i+1}
— this is possible because of Claim (3). Within each chain, we enclose each point
q
with a ball
B_{q}
with center
q
and radius
r=2^{-2|G|}
. Outside these balls,
\Phi(x)=x+\Delta(x)
is a discrete dynamic of Type 1, heading to one of the Nash equilibria of
G
, call in
T
, and slowing down when it is close to any Nash equilibrium. For example,
\Delta(x)=\frac{T-x}{2}|x-N|,
where
N
is the Nash equilibrium closest to
x
and
K>1
.

Notice that, in general, the
B_{q}
balls may overlap; however, we shall start by describing
\Phi
inside the union of the balls for the case the balls are disjoint. Let
x\neq q
be a point inside
B_{q}
, and let
x^{\prime}
be the point on the boundary of
B_{q}
that is closest to
x
. Then

\Delta(x)=\frac{|x-q|\Delta(x^{\prime})+(r-|x-q|)\delta(q)}{r}

In other words, as
x
moves inside
B_{q}
away from
q
,
\Phi(x)
is simply an interpolation between the center and the boundary of
B_{q}
.

To prove that this is a legitimate dynamic, we first note that it is the linear combination of two continuous dynamics, and so it is continuous. Hence we only need to make sure that no spurious fixpoints are created within the balls. In proof, first notice that the displacement
\Delta(x)
is the interpolation between two nonzero vectors
\delta(q)
and
\Delta(x^{\prime})
. For this interpolation to be zero, the two displacements would have to be collinear. However, since
\Delta(x^{\prime})
points to the Nash equilibrium
T
and
x^{\prime}
is within
2^{-2|G|}
from
q
, this can happen only if
T
is within distance
2^{-2|G|}
from the line joining
q
and
\phi(q)
, and Claim (3) is violated.

To handle overlapping balls, suppose that
x
lies at the intersection of
B_{q^{1}},\ldots,B_{q^{k}}
.
If
x
lies within the convex hull of the
q^{i}
’s, then
\Delta^{i}(x)
is the weighted average of the
\delta(q^{i})
s with weights inversely proportional to
x-q^{i}
(with the limit taken if
x=q_{i}
. Outside the convex hall and inside the union,
\Delta(x)
is the interpolation between the values of
\Delta
at the point
x^{\prime}
at the boundary of the union closest to
x
and the point at the boundary of the convex hull collinear with both
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Now notice that
\Delta(x)
everywhere in the union of the balls is the linear combination of the
\phi(q^{i})
’s with vector
\Delta(x^{\prime})
pointing to
T
. In addition, we can assume that
|\Delta(x)|\geq 2^{-|G|}
because
x
cannot be exponentially close to a Nash equilibrium by Claim (1). It follows that
\Delta(x)\neq 0
.

It remains to prove that
\Phi
does not cycle. For points outside
Q
this is self-evident, because the motion towards
T
overwhelms all others; and for points in
Q
we have Claim 2. This completes the proof.

∎

6
Discussion

The results of this work leave us at a quandary. Globally Nash convergent dynamics exist in all nondegenerate games, albeit they are all intractable to compute locally, and therefore unsuitable as models of player behavior. The impossibility conjecture is very plausible, chiefly because of the eight decades of the pursuit by a large community of economists, mathematicians, and computer scientists of an efficient and Nash converging dynamic in games. Proving the conjecture is an important research goal to pursue. However,
PG
and the results of the previous section cast doubts on the likelihood of a proof appearing soon. In view of this, it seems reasonable to also consider the following weaker version of the Impossibility Conjecture:

Conjecture 6.1

(The Weak Impossibility Conjecture)
.

If a locally tractable
globally
Nash convergent dynamic — that is, a Nash convergent dynamic equipped with a Lyapunov function — exists for every nondegenerate game, then
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PPAD}
.

Can
PG
be adapted by requiring Alice to return, in response to Bob’s challenge
x
and besides
q(x)
, the value
L(x)
of the dynamic’s Lyapunov function? We cannot see how to strengthen the argument so a proof of the weaker conjecture is ruled out.

Finally, in view the impossibility theorems and conjectures, we believe that the Nash equilibrium becomes even less attractive than before, and alternative solution concepts in Game Theory should be considered.
The solid motivation and rich theory of dynamical systems as models of the behavior of rational agents playing a game point to such an approach, first anticipated in
[
17
]
: Adopt the replicator dynamic
[
18
]
— the small step limit of the multiplicative weights update algorithm
[
2
]
— as the natural model of behavior of the players, and define the meaning of the game as the limit of this behavior:

The meaning of a game
G
is a function
\mu_{G}
that maps any prior distribution over
X(G)
to the limit distribution over the attractors of the replicator dynamic in
G
.

In other words, the meaning of
G
is the way in which repeated play of
G
transforms the correlated behavior of the players. This point of view motivates the following two important mathematical problems, which we believe are central to the development of a computational theory of learning dynamics:

Conjecture 6.2
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm which, given any game
G
, outputs a description of the attractors of the replicator dynamics in
G
.

Conjecture 6.3
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm which, given a game
G
and a mixed strategy
x
, computes the limit attractor of the replicator dynamic starting at
x
.

Resolving these important problems would provide a comprehensive, principled, and computationally accessible theory of the meaning of the game. See
[
4
]
for recent progress towards proving the first conjecture for two-player games, and
[
13
]
for the computational solution of an approximate version of the second.
