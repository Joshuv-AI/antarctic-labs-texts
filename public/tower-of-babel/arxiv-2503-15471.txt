Finite-momentum pairing and superlattice superconductivity in valley-imbalanced rhombohedral graphene
Maine Christos, Pietro M. Bonetti, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Inspired by the recent experimental discovery of superconductivity emerging from a time-reversal symmetry-breaking normal state in tetralayer rhombohedral graphene, we here investigate superconducting instabilities in this system. We classify the possible pairing instabilities, including states with commensurate and incommensurate center of mass momenta. As rotational symmetry is broken in the latter type of pairing states, their momentum-space structure is most naturally characterized by a “valley-independent Chern number”, measuring the relative chirality between the normal and superconducting state. We further demonstrate that superconductivity can condense at multiple incommensurate momenta simultaneously, leading to the spontaneous formation of a translational-symmetry-breaking superlattice superconductor. Studying multiple different pairing mechanisms and varying the degree of spin and valley polarization in the normal state, we compare the energetics of these superconductors. Our results demonstrate that valley-imbalanced rhombohedral tetralayer graphene can give rise to rich superconducting phenomenologies.

Superconductivity in the presence of valley imbalance is remarkable since time-reversal symmetry (TRS) is broken—a key symmetry for superconductors, as it guarantees the degeneracy of Kramers partners forming Cooper pairs. As such, Cooper pairs are no longer expected to always have vanishing center of mass (COM) momentum
[
22
]
and the superconductor is predicted to become non-reciprocal, i.e., display a superconducting diode effect
[
36
]
. Furthermore, as the normal state is already chiral itself, the superconductor is by construction chiral too. Nonetheless, it is still a meaningful and important question to clarify how this chirality affects the orbital character of the superconducting state and how superconducting instabilities can be classified in light of the reduced symmetries. In analogy to topological superconductivity induced by coupling
s
-wave superconductors to chiral systems
[
4
]
, one might wonder whether the normal state’s Berry curvature will always be inherited by the superconductor, making it topologically non-trivial, or whether it depends on microscopic energetic details.

In this work, we address these questions by a combination of symmetry-based classification of pairing and energetics. Using the continuum model
[
43
,
26
]
of rhombohedral tetralayer graphene and multiple different interactions mediated by fluctuations of bosonic modes, including phonons and spin fluctuations, we find that sufficiently large valley polarization not only leads to intravalley pairing (commensurate center-of-mass momentum
2\bm{K}
), as expected, but also to pairing at finite momentum
\bm{q}
within the active valley (incommensurate net Cooper-pair momenta
2\bm{K}+\bm{q}
). While, at finite
\bm{q}
, one cannot use rotational symmetry to classify the orbital structure of the order parameter, we introduce a “valley-independent Chern number”
\bar{C}
, where
|\bar{C}|\neq 0
corresponds to a topologically non-trivial superconducting state and the sign of
\bar{C}
encodes the relative chirality between the normal and superconducting phase. We find that, depending on the pairing mechanism and the normal state’s spin splitting, both topologically trivial and non-trivial phases are possible; for the latter, the chirality of the superconductor is found to be of the same orientation as that of the normal state (
\bar{C}>0
) for all interactions we consider.
Another important aspect is whether the order parameter condenses at a single wave vector
\bm{q}
(which we call a 1-
\bm{q}
state) or three
C_{3z}
-related wave vectors (3-
\bm{q}
state). We find that microscopic parameters in the underlying continuum model of the theory determine which scenario is energetically favored. While the 1-
\bm{q}
state preserves translational symmetry in observable quantities, the 3-
\bm{q}
state breaks it, leading to a superconducting superlattice. This can be detected in STM experiments
[
37
]
and is mathematically described in analogy to the continuum model of moiré graphene
[
16
,
8
]
, with interlayer terms corresponding to particle-hole couplings.

Model
—We model the normal state for superconductivity with the single-particle Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}_{c}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\eta,\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\eta,\sigma}\left(\epsilon_{\bm{k},\eta}-\mu+\eta\mu_{v}+\sigma\mu_{s}\right)c_{\bm{k},\eta,\sigma}

(1)

In the above,
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\eta}
is the dispersion of the active band of the continuum model of tetralayer rhombohedral graphene
[
43
,
26
,
19
]
at small, positive electron density and
\eta
and
\sigma
are summed over valley and spin indices, respectively. In all our numerics, we compute
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\eta}
with the same model parameters given in
[
19
]
. Further discussion of this continuum model description and the model parameters used can be found in App.
A
. We model spin and valley polarization in the normal state with
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{v}
, which phenomenologically capture at a mean-field level the iso-spin symmetry breaking induced by electronic interactions at energy scales larger than those of the superconductor. In general, both
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{v}
are allowed to have momentum dependence, though we choose them to be momentum indepent in our analysis. As these parameters are very difficult to predict theoretically or extract from experiment, we will study the superconducting phase diagram as a function of them. The chemical potential
\mu
is always adjusted such that the electron density is fixed to
n_{e}=0.6\times 10^{12}
cm
-2
, such that the density is near the value where valley-imbalanced superconductivity was observed in
[
21
]
. The band structure of Eq. (
1
) is plotted in Fig.
1
a for different values of
\mu_{v}
.

The model we consider has an SU(2)
s
spin rotation symmetry when
\mu_{s}=0
, (spinless) TRS relating the two valleys when
\mu_{v}=0
, and U(1) valley symmetry for arbitrary
\mu_{s,v}
. Additionally, Eq. (
1
) is invariant under
C_{3v}
point group symmetries, generated by in-plane reflection
\sigma_{v}
and threefold rotation
C_{3z}
about the
z
axis. As will become important for our discussion of intravalley pairing below, we will choose the
C_{3z}
rotation axis to go through the
A_{1}
sublattice and further take the phase of its representation in the microscopic 8-band model such that its
A_{1}
-
A_{1}
matrix element is
1
(see App.
A
).

We study interactions which originate from coupling the low-energy electrons to collective bosonic modes
\phi_{j}(\bm{q})=\phi^{\dagger}_{j}(-\bm{q})
with coupling of the form:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{fb}}=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{p}}\sum_{\eta,\eta^{\prime},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\phi_{j}(\bm{p})c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k},\eta,\sigma}F_{\bm{k},\bm{p},j}^{\eta\sigma,\eta^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{\bm{k}+\bm{p},\eta^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}

(2)

Such interactions can be thought of as originating from fluctuations of the various possible types of iso-spin polarization which may be proximate to superconductivity in the system, in analogy to the spin-fermion models initially studied in the context of high-temperature superconductivity
[
2
]
. We impose the aforementioned symmetries also on the entire interacting Hamiltonian such that the transformation properties of the bosons
\phi_{j}
under these symmetries are fully determined by the form factors (no summation convention)
F_{\bm{k},\bm{q},j}^{\eta\sigma,\eta^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}=\rho^{\eta\sigma,\eta^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}_{j}\langle u_{\eta\sigma}(\bm{k})|u_{\eta^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{k}+\bm{q})\rangle
where
\ket{u_{\eta\sigma}(\bm{k})}
are the Bloch wavefunctions of the active band of the continuum model
[
43
]
and
\rho
is a
4\times 4
matrix in spin and valley space. For simplicity, we assume the coupling matrices
\rho_{j}
before band projection are momentum independent. For instance, ferromagnetic order-parameter fluctuations correspond to
\rho_{j}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})\eta_{0}
. To also describe the attractive interactions coming from a phonon mode which transforms trivially under all symmetries, we will consider
\rho_{j}=\sigma_{0}\eta_{0}
.

Candidate pairing states
—Before proceeding with energetics, let us classify the possible pairing instabilities based on symmetries. In its most general form, the superconducting order parameter
\Delta_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}
is a matrix in spin and valley space and couples as

\mathcal{H}_{\text{MF}}=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\eta,\sigma}\left(\Delta_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}\right)_{\eta\sigma,\eta^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\eta^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}+\text{H.c.}

(3)

to the electrons of the active band in Eq. (
1
). Fermionic anti-symmetry requires
\Delta_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=-\left(\Delta_{-\bm{k},\bm{q}}\right)^{T}
and the presence of U(1)
v
symmetry implies that pairing is either entirely inter- or intravalley.

First focusing on
\bm{q}=0
in Eq. (
3
), the associated Cooper pairs have vanishing or lattice-commensurate COM momentum
2\bm{K}
for inter- or intravalley pairing, respectively. In both cases,
C_{3z}
is preserved and we can use its irreducible representations (IRs)
A
,
E
, and
E^{*}
to classify pairing. While the latter two are degenerate in the presence of time-reversal symmetry, this is not the case for
\mu_{v}\neq 0
. If
E
is favored in one valley (one sign of
\mu_{v}
), time-reversal implies that
E^{*}
is favored in the other (opposite sign of
\mu_{v}
). To make a meaningful statement, we compute the Chern number
C
of the superconducting state, which can be expressed as a low-energy quantity in the single “active” valley using that time-reversal symmetry is preserved before the spontaneous emergence of valley polarization in the normal state. We also compute the net Berry flux
\Phi_{N}
within the continuum model, to provide a measure for its chirality. We then define the “valley-independent Chern number” as
\bar{C}:=\text{sign}(\Phi_{N})C
, which, as opposed to
C
itself, is independent of the sign of
\mu_{v}
. We checked by acting with the microscopic representation of
C_{3z}
on the superconducting states in our numerics that the
A
state is achiral,
\bar{C}=C=0
, while
E
(
E^{*}
) has
\bar{C}=1
(
\bar{C}=-1
), see also Ref.
37
. A positive (negative)
\bar{C}
corresponds to a ferromagnetic (anti-ferromagnetic) coupling where the superconductor’s chirality is the same as (opposite to) that of the normal state it emerges from. To obtain some general understanding of the expected sign of that coupling for an attractive interaction as mediated by phonons with
\rho=\mathbbm{1}
, we take a normal-state dispersion with
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{-\bm{k}}
, parametrize the superconducting order parameter by its angle
\varphi
on the Fermi surface, to write the linearized gap equation as
\Delta_{\varphi}=\hat{K}\Delta_{\varphi}
. Expanding to second order in the momentum transfer of the interaction (App.
G
), we obtain
\hat{K}=\chi_{0}-i(\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi+\pi})k_{F}\chi_{2}\partial_{\varphi}+\chi_{2}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}/2
with
\chi_{0,2}>0
and
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
denoting the Berry connection along the Fermi surface. We can thus see that the sign of the latter determines whether positive or negative
m
are favored in
\Delta_{\varphi}\propto e^{im\varphi}
. Say
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}>0
(in a smooth gauge) such that
\Phi_{N}=\int\mathrm{d}\varphi\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}>0
; from the form of
\hat{K}
, we conclude that
m>0
will be favored, in turn leading to
\bar{C}=C>0
(ferromagnetic) or
\bar{C}<0
(antiferromagnetic) for an electron- (relevant to us here) and hole-like pocket, respectively.

As we will see below, where
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\neq\epsilon_{-\bm{k}}
, also
\bm{q}\neq 0
can be favored, corresponding to an incommensurate Cooper pair momentum. We will first start with a 1-
\bm{q}
state, i.e.,
\Delta_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=\delta_{\bm{q},\bm{q}_{0}}\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}_{0}}
with
\bm{q}_{0}\neq 0
, which preserves translational symmetry in gauge-invariant observables. Importantly, at fixed
\bm{q}_{0}\neq 0
, we cannot use
C_{3z}
and its IRs to classify pairing but we can still use
\bar{C}
. Finally note that, for
\mu_{s}=0
, all states further decay into spin-singlet and spin-triplet.

Leading 1-
\bm{q}
Instabilities
—For a given choice of fluctuations as specified by
\rho_{j}
, we study the superconducting instabilities by solving the linearized mean-field gap equation of the normal-state Hamiltonian (
1
) supplemented with the electron-electron interaction generated by integrating out the bosons in Eq. (
2
). We choose a Lorentzian static susceptibility
\chi(\bm{q})
for the bosons of the form
\chi(\bm{q})=g^{2}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{2}+|\bm{q}|^{2}}
, where
\alpha
can be thought of as a parameter quantifying how close the system is to criticality and
g^{2}
represents the overall strength of the interaction relative to the kinetic energy.

Once
\mu_{v}\neq 0
and time-reversal symmetry is broken, the states at
\bm{k}
and
-\bm{k}
are no longer degenerate and consequently it is no longer necessarily energetically favorable to pair electrons at opposite momenta, and non-zero
\bm{q}
can become favorable. We note the precise value of
\bm{q}
strongly depends on details of the Fermi surface; however, as illustrated in Fig.
1
b,
\bm{q}
is often approximately associated with the wave-vector for which there is the greatest approximate overlap between the shifted Fermi surfaces defined as
\epsilon_{\frac{\bm{q}}{2}\pm\bm{k},\eta,\sigma}=0
. To compute the value of
\bm{q}
and the associated order parameter
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}
, we solve the linearized gap equation for different values of
\bm{q}
and determine the leading instability from the value of
\bm{q}
with the largest leading eigenvalue
\lambda
, see Fig.
1
c. A finite critical value of
\mu_{v}
is necessary to obtain
\bm{q}\neq 0
, as a result of
C_{3z}
symmetry
[
36
]
, although as shown in Fig.
1
d, this critical value is small and beyond our numerical resolution for the underlying normal state we study.

While this behavior of
\bm{q}
is qualitatively the same for all pairing mechanisms we considered, the
\bm{k}
dependence of
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}
is sensitive to the type of bosons mediating the interaction. Starting with ferromagnetic fluctuations,
\rho_{j}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})\eta_{0}
, our results for varying degree of valley splitting
\mu_{v}
are summarized in Fig.
2
a-c. As expected for spin fluctuations, we find triplet pairing to be dominant. In the intervalley pairing regime at weak
\mu_{v}
, the anti-symmetry
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}=-\left(\Delta_{-\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}\right)^{T}
does not constrain
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}
to be even or odd in
\bm{k}
(see Fig.
2
b) due to the valley degree of freedom. This changes for larger
\mu_{v}
, where intravalley pairing is energetically favored and, hence, the triplet order parameter must be odd in
\bm{k}
, as one can see in Fig.
2
c. The state in Fig.
2
a clearly transforms under IR
A
but, as noted above, we cannot use the IRs of
C_{3z}
to classify the pairing states at finite
\bm{q}
as becomes apparent, e.g., in Fig.
2
b. Instead, we use the signed Chern number
\bar{C}
of the superconductor and find that the initial
\bar{C}=0
state transitions to a superconductor with
\bar{C}=1
, representing a ferromagnetic coupling between the normal state’s and superconducting chirality.

The behavior is different for phonons,
\rho_{j}=\sigma_{0}\eta_{0}
. In this case, the leading instability also condenses at finite
\bm{q}
above a critical value of
\mu_{v}
, but remains topologically trivial (
\overline{C}=0
) as valley polarization increases in Fig.
2
d-f, with vanishing Chern number,
\overline{C}=0
(see App.
E
for definition of
\bar{C}
for the case with Fermi surfaces in both valleys).

3-
\bm{q}
states
—So far, we just assumed that only one
\bm{q}
in Eq. (
3
) contributes. However, as can be seen in Fig.
1
,
C_{3z}
symmetry requires that at least three
\bm{q}
be degenerate in the
\bm{q}\neq 0
regime. In our numerical calculations, we find exactly three degenerate maxima in most cases (see Fig.
8
). To take this into account, we write

\Delta_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\phi_{n}\delta_{\bm{q},\bm{q}_{n}}\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}_{n}}
,
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}_{n}}
being the eigenvector of the linearized gap equation,

\bm{q}_{n}=C_{3z}^{n-1}\bm{q}_{1}
, and expand the free-energy as
[
1
]

\mathcal{F}\sim r\sum_{n=1}^{3}|\phi_{n}|^{2}+u\left(\sum_{n=1}^{3}|\phi_{n}|^{2}\right)^{2}+v\sum_{n,m>n}|\phi_{n}|^{2}|\phi_{m}|^{2}

(4)

where
u>\mathrm{min}\{0,-v\}
as required by stability and we assumed a lattice-incommensurate value of
\bm{q}_{n}
. In the superconducting state, we have
r<0
and the sign of
v
determines whether we will have a 1-
\bm{q}
(
v>0
) with only one of
\phi_{1,2,3}
non-zero and the properties discussed above, or a 3-
\bm{q}
(
v<0
) state, characterized by
|\phi_{1}|=|\phi_{2}|=|\phi_{3}|
. Note that the relative phase of
\phi_{n}
just determines the origin of the emergent superconducting superlattice which preserves
C_{3z}
. As shown in Fig.
3
(a), the 3-
\bm{q}
state exhibits an emergent vortex-anti-vortex lattice in real space, which leads to complex spatially varying tunneling signatures
[
37
]
. The phases of theory (
4
) can be characterized by three gauge invariant
composite
order parameters
\varrho_{mn}=\phi_{m}\phi_{n}^{*}
(
m\neq n
), describing charge density modulations with wave vector
\bm{q}_{m}-\bm{q}_{n}

[
3
,
46
]
. It is obvious to see that the single-
\bm{q}
phase has all
\varrho_{mn}=0
, while the 3-
\bm{q}
state has all three
\varrho_{mn}
nonzero and equal in absolute values.

\bm{q}

\bm{k}^{2}/(2m)
dispersion coupled to a

-wave superconducting order parameter which carries a finite COM momentum. Additionally, we deform the electron dispersion with a

term, whose strength we parametrize by

.
Integrating out the electrons and expanding to quartic order in
\phi_{n}
, we get expressions for the coefficients of Eq. (

) (App.

In Fig.

as a function of

|\bm{q}|
. We observe that at fixed

, a finite COM momentum is required to stabilize the 3-
\bm{q}

Spectra
—Finally, we will discuss the superconducting spectra in more detail. Starting with
\bm{q}=0
, any infinitesimal order parameter with
|\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}=0}|\neq 0
will immediately gap out the spectrum when
\mu_{v}=0
. However, when
\mu_{v}\neq 0
—similar to the case of inter-band pairing in twisted bilayer graphene
[
13
,
32
]
, where the states forming Cooper pairs are not degenerate either since they are in different bands—a finite amount of pairing strength is required for a full gap; below that, the system will generically have Bogoliubov Fermi surfaces
[
9
]
. At the same time, the thermal phase transition can become first order
[
13
,
32
]
and whether there is a nodal regime close to
T_{c}
or not depends on microscopic details.
We next turn to the scenario where
\bm{q}\neq 0
. In the case of the 1-
\bm{q}
state, as illustrated in Fig.
3
c, the gap, which emerges at finite pairing strength
|\Delta|
, decreases and ultimately vanishes with increasing
|\bm{q}|
, as the splitting between the paired energies increases.

To compute the spectrum of the 3-
\bm{q}
state, we have to take into account the broken translational symmetry and the associated superlattice reconstruction. This is done in analogy to the continuum model of twisted multilayer graphene
[
16
,
8
]
, where the superlattice now comes from the pairing terms. In practice, we use a continuum model with 17 shells of
\bm{Q}
vectors, which we find is sufficient to achieve convergence for our smallest
|\bm{q}|
value. The resulting gap shown in Fig.
3
b reveals that, similar to the case of the
1
-
\bm{q}
state, a finite amount of pairing strength is needed at small
\bm{q}
to generate a gap, with the threshold pairing strength increasing as
\bm{q}
increases.

Other normal states
—As at least one of the superconducting states in Ref.
21
is likely also spin polarized, we additionally comment on the effect of finite
\mu_{s}
in Eq. (
1
). In our numerics, we found no qualitative changes in the features of the pairing states for
\mu_{s}\neq 0
discussed so far, apart from sufficiently large
\mu_{s}
leading to triplet pairing also for phonons. The main quantitative modification is that additional spin polarization lowers the critical
\mu_{v}
for the formation of a topologically nontrivial superconductor (
\bar{C}=1
), see App.
B
and App.
H
for details. We also have studied the case of ferromagnetic fluctuations with a higher displacement field such that the fully spin and valley polarized normal state has an annular Fermi surface. In this case we find the dominant instability always has
C=0
, in agreement with the results of
[
17
,
40
,
41
,
28
]
. We also study fluctuations of a valley polarized order (see App.
C
) with
\rho=\sigma_{0}\eta_{z}
for varying
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{v}
. In this case we find the
A
state dominates when
\mu_{s}=0
with a finite
\mu_{s}
leading to a transition to the
E
state.

Conclusion
—We analyzed superconducting instabilities within a continuum model description of rhombohedral tetra-layer graphene as a function of spin and valley polarization. The analysis reveals rich superconducting physics, including the possibility of topological superconductivity and the spontaneous emergence of a superconducting superlattice.
As discussed in
[
37
]
, future scanning tunneling spectroscopy experiments will allow to probe key features of the identified candidate pairing states. We also note that a valley-imbalanced normal state will generically give rise to non-reciprocal superconductivity
[
36
]
, with asymmetric current-voltage characteristics, providing a complementary way of accessing the reduced symmetries of the system. Finally, although our analysis explored only superconducting states, it would be interesting to consider other instabilities which may compete with superconductivity in the system.

Model and Linearized Gap Equation

Appendix E
Chern Number

In this appendix, we will describe how the Chern number of each superconducting state is computed within the continuum model description of App.
A
. For each pairing state, we construct the mean-field Hamiltonian:

H_{BdG}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Psi_{\bm{k}}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}&\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}\\
\left(\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}\right)^{\dagger}&-\tilde{\epsilon}_{-\bm{k},\bm{q}}\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{\bm{k}}

(14)

Where
\Psi_{\bm{k}}
is a vector defined for each
\bm{k}
on half the Brillouin zone as:

\Psi_{\bm{k}}=\left(c_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,+},c_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,+},c_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,-},c_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,-},c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,+},c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,+},c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,-},c^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,-}\right)^{T}

(15)

and
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}
is the diagonal matrix constructed from the dispersions of each spin and valley flavor:

\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k},\bm{q}}=\text{Diag}\left(\xi_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,+},\xi_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,+},\xi_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\uparrow,-},\xi_{\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\downarrow,-}\right)

(16)

and
\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}
is the matrix defined in Eq.
9
. For a given band of Eq.
14
with corresponding set of eigenvectors at each
\bm{k}
point labeled as
a_{\bm{k}}
, we compute the Chern number of the superconducting state as:

C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{BZ}d^{2}\bm{k}\quad\Omega^{SC}(\bm{k})

(17)

where
\Omega^{SC}(\bm{k})
is the Berry curvature of the superconducting state, and is defined as:

\begin{split}\Omega^{SC}_{\bm{k}}&=\text{Im}\left[\text{log}\left(U_{\bm{k},\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{x}}}U_{\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{x}},\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{x}}+\delta k\bm{\hat{y}}}U_{\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{x}}+\delta k\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{y}}}U_{\bm{k}+\delta k\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{k}}\right)\right]\\
&\text{where}\quad U_{\bm{k},\bm{k^{\prime}}}=a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}F(\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k^{\prime}}-\bm{k})&0\\
0&F^{*}(-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},-\bm{k^{\prime}}+\bm{k})\end{pmatrix}a_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}\end{split}

(18)

U_{\bm{k},\bm{k^{\prime}}}=a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}F(\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},\bm{k^{\prime}}-\bm{k})&0\\
0&F^{*}(-\bm{k}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2},-\bm{k^{\prime}}+\bm{k})\par\end{pmatrix}a_{\bm{k^{\prime}}}\qquad\Omega(\bm{k})=\text{Im}\left[\text{log}\left(U_{\bm{k}_{1},\bm{k}_{2}}U_{\bm{k}_{2},\bm{k}_{3}}U_{\bm{k}_{3},\bm{k}_{4}}U_{\bm{k}_{4},\bm{k}_{1}}\right)\right]

(19)

Our continuum model has a cutoff
|\bm{\Lambda}|>k_{F}
within each valley (in our computations we use a hexagonal momentum space grid with a
\bm{k}
dependent cutoff, though this does not affect the following argument) and we may break the berry curvature into two contributions, one for integrand with
|\bm{k}|<|\bm{\Lambda}|
and one for integrand with
|\bm{k}|>|\bm{\Lambda}|
, such that we may write:

C_{SC}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|<|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{SC}_{\bm{k}}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|>|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{SC}_{\bm{k}}

(20)

We note that for cases where the normal state is totally valley polarized, we are taking the cutoff
|\bm{\Lambda}|
to be within only the single, active valley. For cases where the pairing is valley polarized but the normal state still has active Fermi surfaces in both valleys, we compute the Chern number only for the valley in which the pairing is active (although our definition of
C
is still well-defined and quantized in this case, we note that quantized response would not be observed due to the presence of the active Fermi surface in the other valley). For cases with an intervalley pairing, we take a cutoff
|\bm{\Lambda}|
in both valleys and compute the Chern number.

If
|\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\bm{q}}|
is sufficiently small for
|\bm{k}|>|\bm{\Lambda}|
, the second contribution may be rewritten as:

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|>|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{SC}_{\bm{k}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|>|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{\phi}_{\bm{k}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|<|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{\phi}_{\bm{k}}

(21)

where
\Omega^{\phi}_{\bm{k}}
is the Berry curvature of the normal state Bloch wavefunction. In the above we have used that the Berry curvature integrated over the entire band (including both valleys of the continuum model) must be 0. In this case, we have an expression for the Berry curvature defined for integrands only within our momentum cutoff:

C_{SC}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|<|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{SC}_{\bm{k}}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\bm{k}|<|\bm{\Lambda}|}d^{2}{\bm{k}}\quad\Omega^{\phi}_{\bm{k}}

(22)

Appendix G
Sign of "valley-independent Chern number"
\overline{C}

In this appendix, we will discuss on more general grounds how the underlying Berry curvature of the normal-state Bloch states determines the sign of the Chern number
C
of the superconductor (and, thus,
\overline{C}
) for the interactions we study. Our starting point is the linearized gap equation. In order to consider the simplest tractable case, we will set the COM momentum
\bm{q}\rightarrow 0
.
We also assume a finite (but not necessarily large) degree of both spin and valley polarization and neglect the cases of pairing between opposite valleys and spins, assuming that this scenario is energetically disfavored in a (potentially partially) polarized normal state. Under these assumptions, the linearized gap equation takes the form

\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{\gamma}\sim\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{V}^{\gamma,\delta}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(T)F^{\gamma,\delta}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}F_{-\bm{k},-\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{\gamma\delta}\Delta^{\delta}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}},

(26)

where
\mathcal{V}^{\gamma,\delta}_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}(T)
comprises both the interaction potential as well as thermal and kinetic factors.
In the above
\gamma,\delta
, are indices in the four-dimensional space of spin and valley. In general, the form factors
F
in terms of the normal-state Bloch wavefunctions
\ket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}
are
F_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}^{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\bra{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}\rho^{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\ket{u_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{k^{\prime}})}
, where, for given
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
,
\rho^{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
is a potentially non-trivial matrix in the remaining flavor quantum numbers (e.g., sublattices and layers). For concreteness, we will here first focus on the special case
\rho^{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\rho_{\gamma}\delta_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}
,
\rho_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
, relevant to the scenarios discussed in the main text. The form factors then simply become
F_{\bm{k},\bm{k^{\prime}}}^{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=\delta_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\rho_{\gamma}\braket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})|u_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{k}^{\prime})}
.

We next further assume that
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
will only be non-zero in the immediate vicinity of the Fermi surface. Within this thin strip around the Fermi surface, it is then natural to approximate
\Delta_{\bm{k}}
and the Bloch states/interaction matrix elements to only depend on the position on the Fermi surface, which we parametrize with an angular-like variable
\varphi
.
Then Eq. (
26
) can be compactly written as

\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\sim\int d\alpha\,V^{\gamma}_{\alpha}(T)F^{\gamma}_{\varphi,\varphi+\alpha}F^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi,\varphi+\alpha+\pi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\alpha}.

(27)

Since different
\gamma
do not couple, in order to solve the linearized gap equation, it is sufficient to focus on the values of
\gamma
with the largest transition temperature. This is why we suppressed the superscript
\gamma
in our description of this analysis in the main text.

To gain intuitive insights, let us further assume that the effective interaction is sufficiently short-ranged in momentum space (long-ranged in real space) such that the integrand in Eq. (
27
) can be expanded in powers of
\alpha
.
We start by expanding the form factors to leading order in
\alpha
:

F^{\gamma}_{\varphi,\varphi+\alpha}=\rho_{\gamma}\left(1+\alpha\braket{u_{\gamma}(\varphi)|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\gamma}(\varphi)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})

(28)

In the above expression, we can identify the second term in this expansion as proportional to the normal-state Berry connection along the Fermi surface since

i\braket{u_{\gamma}(\varphi)|\partial_{\varphi}u_{\gamma}(\varphi)}=k_{F}(\varphi)\hat{\bm{e}}_{\varphi}\cdot i\braket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})|\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{k}}u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}=:k_{F}(\varphi)\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\gamma},

(29)

where
k_{F}(\varphi)
is the Fermi wavevector at point
\varphi
on the Fermi surface and
\hat{\bm{e}}_{\varphi}
the unit vector tangential to the Fermi surface.
To leading order in
\alpha
the linearized gap equation reads

\begin{split}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}&=\rho_{\gamma}^{2}\int d\alpha\,V_{\alpha}(T)\left[1-i\alpha k_{F}(\varphi)\left(\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\gamma}+\mathcal{A}_{\varphi+\pi}^{\gamma}\right)\right]\Delta_{\varphi+\alpha}^{\gamma}\end{split}

(30)

We then similarly expand
\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\alpha}=\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\alpha\partial_{\varphi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{3})
. Keeping all terms up to order
\alpha^{2}
in the integrand, defining
\chi^{\gamma}_{n}:=\rho_{\gamma}^{2}\int d\alpha\,V_{\alpha}(T)\alpha^{n}
, and making the common assumption that the effective interaction is even such that
\chi_{1}=0
, Eq. (
30
) becomes

\begin{split}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}&\sim\rho_{\gamma}^{2}\int d\alpha\,V_{\alpha}(T)\left[\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\partial_{\varphi}^{2}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\right]-i\rho_{\gamma}^{2}k_{F}(\varphi)\int d\alpha\left[\alpha(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi})\right]\alpha\partial_{\varphi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\\
&=\chi^{\gamma}_{0}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}-ik_{F}(\varphi)\chi^{\gamma}_{2}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)\partial_{\varphi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{2}\chi^{\gamma}_{2}\partial^{2}_{\varphi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}.\end{split}

(31)

So the linearized gap equation can be stated as
\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}=\hat{K}^{\gamma}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}
with differential operator

\hat{K}^{\gamma}=\chi_{0}-i\chi^{\gamma}_{2}k_{F}(\varphi)\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)\partial_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{2}\chi_{2}\partial^{2}_{\varphi},

(32)

as stated in the main text. First, consider the limit without the term proportional to the Berry connection in
\hat{K}^{\gamma}
. If
\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}
is a solution of the gap equation,
\Delta^{\gamma}_{-\varphi}
, which has the opposite chirality, will always be degenerate with it (i.e., we have a non-chiral state or pairing in a multi-dimensional irreducible representation). The second term in Eq. (
32
) splits the degeneracy between these solutions and, thereby, selects a chirality. To see this, let us assume that
\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}
solves the full gap equation. Then the difference
\Delta\lambda
in eigenvalue between this solution and
\Delta^{\gamma}_{-\varphi}
reads as

\Delta\lambda=2\chi^{\gamma}_{2}\int d\varphi\,k_{F}(\varphi)\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)\left[-i\left(\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\right)^{*}\partial_{\varphi}\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}\right]=2|\Delta_{0}|^{2}\chi^{\gamma}_{2}\int d\varphi\,k_{F}(\varphi)\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)\partial_{\varphi}\alpha

(33)

where we wrote
\Delta^{\gamma}_{\varphi}=|\Delta_{0}|e^{i\alpha(\varphi)}
in the second equality. Taking an attractive interaction (
\chi_{2}>0
), this shows that the leading superconducting solution will be the one that maximizes a “weighted” version,
w_{\mathcal{A}}:=\int d\varphi\,k_{F}(\varphi)\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)\partial_{\varphi}\alpha
, of the winding number
w=\int d\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}\alpha\in\mathbbm{Z}
of the superconducting order parameter. Assuming that (there is a gauge where)
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}
has a fixed sign (as is the case in rhombohedral graphene), we see that

\,\text{sign}(\partial_{\varphi}\alpha)=\,\text{sign}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right)=\text{const}.,

(34)

linking the normal-state chirality to the chirality of the resulting superconductor.

Finally, it is left to connect the left and right hand side of Eq. (
34
) to the sign of
C
and
\Phi_{N}
, respectively, which will determine the sign of the valley independent Chern number
\bar{C}
. To this end, we first choose a gauge where the Bloch states
\ket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}
are smooth in the momentum-space area
A
enclosed by the Fermi surface. It then holds

\Phi_{N}=\int_{A}d^{2}\bm{k}\,\Omega_{\bm{k}}=\int_{A}d^{2}\bm{k}\,\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{k}}\times i\braket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})|\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{k}}u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}=\oint_{\partial A}d\bm{k}\,i\braket{u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})|\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{k}}u_{\gamma}(\bm{k})}=\int d\varphi\,k_{F}(\varphi)\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{\gamma},

(35)

where
\Omega_{\bm{k}}=-2\text{Im}\braket{\partial_{k_{x}}u_{\gamma}|\partial_{k_{y}}u_{\gamma}}
is the normal-state Berry curvature. This immediately implies

\,\text{sign}(\Phi_{N})=\,\text{sign}\left(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi}+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\varphi+\pi}\right).

(36)

As shown in
[
37
]
, in this gauge, the Chern number of the superconductor can just be obtained by integrating the “naive” superconducting Berry curvature,

\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{SC}}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2|\bm{g}_{\bm{k}}|^{3}}\bm{g}_{\bm{k}}\cdot(\partial_{x}\bm{g}_{\bm{k}}\times\partial_{y}\bm{g}_{\bm{k}}),\quad\bm{g}_{\bm{k}}=(\text{Re}\Delta_{\bm{k}},-\text{Im}\Delta_{\bm{k}},\xi_{\bm{k}})^{T}

(37)

over the low-energy theory, where
\xi_{\bm{k}}
is the normal-state dispersion. It is straightforward to see that within the low-energy theory

\,\text{sign}(C)=\,\text{sign}\left(\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{SC}}(\bm{k})\right)=-\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0})\,\text{sign}(\partial_{\varphi}\alpha),

(38)

where
\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0})<0
and
\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0})>0
for an electron-like and hole-like pocket, respectively. Combining Eqs. (
34
), (
36
), and (
38
), we come to our final conclusion:

\,\text{sign}(C)=-\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0})\,\text{sign}(\Phi_{N})\quad\text{and thus}\quad\,\text{sign}(\bar{C})=-\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0}).

(39)

For the conduction band in rhombohedral graphene, we have
\,\text{sign}(\xi_{\bm{k}=0})<0
and thus
\,\text{sign}(\bar{C})=1
, in line with our numerics. We close by pointing out that one could just as well have defined
\Phi_{N}
with an extra minus sign for a hole-like pocket, yielding
\,\text{sign}(\bar{C})=1
in both cases.
