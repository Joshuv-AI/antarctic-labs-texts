On Spectral Learning for Odeco Tensors: Perturbation, Initialization, and Algorithms ∗
Arnab Auddy, Ming Yuan

Abstract
We study spectral learning for orthogonally decomposable (odeco) tensors, emphasizing the interplay between statistical limits, optimization geometry, and initialization. Unlike matrices, recovery for odeco tensors does not hinge on eigengaps, yielding improved robustness under noise. While iterative methods such as tensor power iterations can be statistically efficient, initialization emerges as the main computational bottleneck. We investigate perturbation bounds, non-convex optimization analysis, and initialization strategies, clarifying when efficient algorithms attain statistical limits and when fundamental barriers remain.

Introduction.

Tensors, as higher-order generalizations of matrices, have emerged as powerful tools for representing and analyzing multi-dimensional data. They naturally arise in diverse applications such as multi-relational networks, spatiotemporal measurements, neuroimaging, and latent variable models. Unlike matrices, which capture only pairwise relationships, tensors encode multi-way interactions, offering richer structural insights. Among the various tensor models,
orthogonally decomposable (odeco) tensors
play a special role. Their decomposition structure parallels the eigendecomposition of matrices, but with important advantages in both statistical robustness and computational tractability.

In particular, odeco tensors arise in the
method of moments
for latent variable models. This framework has been especially influential in statistical machine learning, where moment-based tensor decompositions have enabled consistent parameter recovery in models such as topic models, hidden Markov models, and Gaussian mixtures (see, e.g.,
Janzamin et al. 2019
). This approach avoids likelihood maximization, which is often computationally intractable, while still ensuring consistency. Tensor-based methods have since become widely adopted in unsupervised learning, underscoring their importance in bridging statistical modeling and algorithm design. In these problems, the parameter of interest is typically modeled through the spectral decomposition of an odeco tensor. At an abstract level, they can be formulated as recovering the spectral parameters, i.e., singular vectors, of an odeco tensor
\mathscr{T}
from an observed tensor
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{T}+\mathscr{E}
where
\mathscr{E}
represents an error or noise tensor. The main goal of this paper is to provide a unified treatment to this type of problems, highlighting the statistical and algorithmic forces that shape its landscape. We argue that three ingredients are central: tensor perturbation bounds, iterative methods for non-convex optimization, and initialization strategies.

Perturbation bounds.

These establish the statistical limits of recovery under noise, revealing a surprising phenomenon: unlike matrices, tensor recovery is not constrained by eigengaps, making tensors statistically more robust in certain regimes. Perturbation analysis has traditionally focused on matrices, with seminal results such as the Davis–Kahan theorem (
Davis and Kahan 1970
) and Wedin’s theorem (
Wedin 1972
) providing eigenvalue and eigenspace stability under noise. See
Stewart and Sun 1990
for a comprehensive review. Extending these results to tensors highlights tensors’ inherent statistical advantage over their matrix counterparts.

Non-convex optimization.

Iterative methods such as tensor power iteration and deflation succeed in recovering spectral components of odeco tensors, provided they start from a sufficiently accurate initialization. From an algorithmic perspective, tensor recovery often reduces to solving non-convex optimization problems. Algorithms such as tensor power iteration and deflation provide practical procedures for decomposition. While the non-convex landscape is complex, recent analyses (see, e.g.,
Ge et al. 2015
) show that saddle points dominate but can be escaped efficiently with appropriate initialization. Once a “warm start” is available, iterative algorithms converge to the global solution, often achieving the same recovery guarantees as those implied by perturbation bounds. This interplay between optimization geometry and initialization is a defining theme in tensor analysis.

Initialization strategies.

Obtaining a nontrivial initial estimate is the principal computational bottleneck. Randomized approaches such as random slicing and sketching offer partial solutions. But these randomized methods fall short of bridging the statistical–computational gap. The gap resembles hardness results in other high-dimensional estimation problems. These analogies suggest that the limitations are not merely algorithmic deficiencies but reflect fundamental computational barriers.

The emerging consensus is that statistical guarantees alone are insufficient to characterize spectral learning for tensors. While perturbation bounds describe what is possible, actual performance hinges on initialization strategies and the geometry of non-convex objectives. Our contribution is to integrate these strands into a unified framework that explains when efficient algorithms can match statistical limits and when intrinsic barriers remain. By isolating the role of initialization as the critical step, we shed light on both the opportunities and the obstacles in high-dimensional tensor analysis.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we first review some basic properties of odeco tensors and show how usual iterative methods such as fixed point iteration can be applied for odeco tensor decomposition. Section 3 considers the special case when the “noisy observation”
\mathscr{X}
is also odeco. In this case, it is natural to estimate the spectral parameters of
\mathscr{T}
by those of
\mathscr{X}
. We provide refined perturbation bounds for these estimates. Section 4 considers more general cases where a good estimating procedure for the spectral parameters of
\mathscr{T}
requires consideration from both statistical and computational perspective. We show that the usual iterative methods such as power iteration combined with deflation can yield estimates that are efficient both statistically and computationally, provided that we can find a nontrivial initialization. Section 5 is devoted to the study of initialization. We argue that a higher signal-to-noise ratio is necessary to ensure a good initialization, and this is even more so if we aim to do away with the eigengap conditions. We conclude with a few remarks in Section 6.

2
Odeco Decomposition.

We denote vectors and matrices by boldfaced lower and upper case letters respectively: e.g.,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
; and tensors by script-style letters, e.g.,
\mathscr{T}
. Entries of a matrix or tensor is represented by upper-case letters with a set of indices, e.g.,
T_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}
is the
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})
entry of an
p
th order tensor
\mathscr{T}
. We first list a set of basic notations and tensor operations that we shall use throughout the paper.

2.1
Preliminaries.

A
p
-th order tensor
\mathscr{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{p}}
has the mode-
k
matricization

{\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times\prod_{j\neq k,j\in[p]}d_{j}}

with elements

\left({\sf Mat}_{k}(\mathscr{T})\right)_{i_{k}j}=T_{i_{1}\dots i_{k-1}i_{k}i_{k+1}\dots i_{p}}

where

j=1+\sum_{l\in[p],l\neq k}(i_{l}-1)J_{l}

with

J_{l}=\prod_{m\in[l-1],m\neq k}d_{m}
.
A tensor
\mathscr{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\dots\times d_{p}}
can be multiplied along its
k
-th mode by a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}\times d_{k}}
to get a new tensor

\mathscr{T}^{\prime}:=\mathscr{T}\times_{k}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\dots d_{k-1}\times m_{k}\times d_{k+1}\dots\times d_{p}}

with elements

T^{\prime}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k-1}j_{k}i_{k+1}\dots i_{p}}=\sum_{i_{k}\in[d_{k}]}T_{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}M_{j_{k}i_{k}}

for
i_{l}\in[d_{l}]
for
l=1,\dots,p
,
l\neq k
, and
j_{k}\in[m_{k}]
.
The spectral norm or operator norm of
\mathscr{T}
is defined as

\|\mathscr{T}\|:=\sup_{\mathbf{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{k}-1}}\langle\mathscr{T},\mathbf{u}_{1}\circ\dots\circ\mathbf{u}_{p}\rangle,

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
denotes the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Singular value decomposition (SVD) plays a central role in analyzing data formatted in matrices. It turns out that there are multiple ways to generalize SVD to higher order tensors. One of most popular approaches to tensor decomposition is the so-called Canonical Polyadic (CP) decomposition
Hitchcock 1927
;
Carroll and Chang 1970
;
Harshman 1970
that expresses a tensor as the sum of rank-one tensors, e.g., tensors that can be expressed as outer product of vectors:

\mathscr{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\circ\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(p)}.

(1)

where
\lambda_{i}
s are scalars, and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(q)}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{q}-1}
,
q=1,\ldots,p
and
i=1,\ldots,r
are unit vectors. The smallest integer
r
for which such a decomposition holds is called the CP-rank of
\mathscr{T}
. It is convenient to use the notation
\mathscr{T}=[{\bm{\lambda}};\mathbf{U}^{(1)},\mathbf{U}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{U}^{(p)}]
for CP decomposition as described by Equation
1
where
{\bm{\lambda}}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{U}^{(q)}=[\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)},\mathbf{u}_{2}^{(q)},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{r}^{(q)}]
. In particular if
\mathbf{U}^{(q)}
are orthonormal, we say
\mathscr{T}
is orthogonally decomposable (odeco).

2.2
Odeco decomposition.

While computing the minimum rank CP decomposition of a tensor is computationally hard in general, it can be done very efficiently for odeco tensors. In particular, we may do so by iteratively solving for zeros of the Riemannian gradient (
Edelman et al. 1998
;
Kressner et al. 2014
). Note that an odeco tensor
\mathscr{T}=[{\bm{\lambda}};\mathbf{U}^{(1)},\mathbf{U}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{U}^{(p)}]
inherits a manifold structure from its component matrices:

\mathbf{U}^{(q)}\in St(d,r)=\{\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}:\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}=\mathbb{I}_{r}\},

i.e., the Stiefel manifold of all
d\times r
matrices with orthonormal columns.

It is not hard to see that the SVDs of
\mathscr{T}
can be identified with the solution to

\text{maximize }_{\mathbf{A}^{(q)}\in St(d_{q},r)}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathscr{T}(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}_{i},\dots,\mathbf{a}^{(p)}_{i})^{2}.

See, e.g.,
Chen and Saad 2009
. We will consider an alternating update scheme, where each of the mode matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(q)}
is updated one by one. Recall that the tangent space of
St(d,r)
at
\mathbf{U}
is given by

T_{\mathbf{U}}(St(d,r))=\{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{B}+(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top})\mathbf{C}:\quad\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\,\mathbf{B}=-\mathbf{B}^{\top},\,\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\}.

The Riemannian gradient of a function
F(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})
is the projection of its Euclidean gradient along the tangent space. In our case,

F_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathscr{T}(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}_{i},\dots,\mathbf{a}^{(p)}_{i})^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(\langle\mathbf{a}_{k}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(1)}\rangle)^{2}

where
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(1)}=\mathscr{T}\times_{q\neq 1}\mathbf{a}^{(q)}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are held fixed, due to the alternating update scheme. Thus

\mathbf{G}=\frac{\partial F_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{(1)}_{i,j}}=\mathbf{V}^{(1)}\mathbf{D}^{(1)}

where
\mathbf{D}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix with entries

d^{(1)}_{kk}=\langle\mathbf{a}_{k}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}_{k}\rangle\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\dots,r,

and
\mathbf{V}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
is the matrix with
\mathbf{v}^{(1)}_{k}
as its
k
th column. Then the Riemannian gradient at
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}
becomes:

\nabla F_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})=\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{A}^{(1)}\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{(1)}.

(2)

See, e.g., Lemma 2.1 of
Wen and Yin 2013
. A critical point of the above optimization scheme is arrived at by setting

\nabla F_{1}(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})=0\iff(\mathbf{A}^{(1)})^{\top}\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{(1)}

or equivalently:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a}_{j}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle\left(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}_{i}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}^{(1)}_{j}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{a}_{i}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle\left(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}_{j}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{v}}^{(1)}_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i,j\leq r.

Holding
\mathbf{V}^{(1)}
fixed, the above equation is satisfied by setting

\mathbf{a}_{k}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(1)}\|}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{(1)}\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\dots,r,

suggesting a fixed point iteration scheme.

Note however that the output after
T
fixed point iterations is not necessarily orthogonal. To take care of this, we use a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization at the last step. This procedure is repeated for each mode. The detailed optimization scheme is in Algorithm
1
.

The following result shows that Algorithm

\mathscr{T}
with a random starting point.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{{\mathbf{U}}^{(q)}_{[t]}:1\leq q\leq p\}
be the iterates at the

-th iteration of Algorithm

where

3
Perturbation Bounds.

We first consider the case when an observed tensor
\mathscr{X}
is also odeco. A natural approach is to estimate the singular vectors of
\mathscr{T}
by those of
\mathscr{X}
, which can be readily computed using Algorithm
1
from the previous section.
Denote by

\mathscr{T}=[\{\lambda_{k}:1\leq k\leq d_{\min}\};\mathbf{U}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{U}^{(p)}],

and

{\mathscr{X}}=[\{\widehat{\lambda}_{k}:1\leq k\leq d_{\min}\};\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{(p)}]

their respective SVDs. We are interested in understanding the differences between the two sets of singular values and vectors in terms of the “perturbation”
\mathscr{X}-\mathscr{T}
. For an odeco tensor
\mathscr{T}
, its SVD determines that of
{\sf Mat}_{q}(\mathscr{T})
. More specifically,

{\sf Mat}_{q}(\mathscr{T})=\mathbf{U}^{(q)}({\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d_{\min}}))(\mathbf{V}^{(q)})^{\top},

(3)

where

\mathbf{V}^{(q)}=\mathbf{U}^{(1)}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{U}^{(q-1)}\odot\mathbf{U}^{(q+1)}\odot\cdots\odot\mathbf{U}^{(p)}.

Here
\odot
stands for the Khatri-Rao product. Thanks to Weyl’s and Davis-Kahan’s perturbation theorem the matricization of odeco tensors in (
3
) immediately implies that

|\lambda_{k}-\widehat{\lambda}_{k}|\leq\min_{1\leq q\leq p}\|{\sf Mat}_{q}(\mathscr{E})\|,

and

\sin\angle(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(q)},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(q)})\leq{2\|{\sf Mat}_{q}(\mathscr{E})\|\over\min\{\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k},\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k+1}\}}.

We note that the dependence of the above perturbation bounds for matricized singular vectors on the singular value gap is intrinsic and cannot be removed. This fundamental feature of matrix based perturbation bounds can be rigorously demonstrated through simple examples. See, e.g.,
Bhatia 2013
.

The above bounds are not optimal and can be substantially tightened in several ways that reveal key distinctions between matrices and higher-order tensors, for a number of reasons. Firstly, the presence of eigengap is necessary for identifying singular vectors for matrices, while odeco tensors are always identifiable up to a sign and permutation of the indices, thanks to the Kruskal’s theorem. This suggests tensor perturbation bounds that should similarly be free from any dependence on eigengaps. Secondly, flattening a tensor does not preserve the manifold structure present among odeco tensors. Especially in high dimensional applications where the dimensions along the tensor modes is large this additional structure makes tensors more resilient to noise than matrices.

More specifically, as shown by
Auddy and Yuan 2023
, there exist a numerical constant
C\geq 1
and a permutation
\pi:[d_{\min}]\to[d_{\min}]
such that for all
k=1,\ldots,d_{\min}
,

|\lambda_{k}-\widehat{\lambda}_{\pi(k)}|\leq C\|\mathscr{E}\|,

(4)

and

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle(\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(q)},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)})\leq{C\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda_{k}},

(5)

with the convention that
1/0=+\infty
.

Note that the above result gives perturbation bounds between
\mathbf{u}_{k}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}
for some unknown permutation
\pi:[d]\to[d]
. This is because, unlike matrices, where eigengap is necessary for singular vector perturbation analysis, we do not restrict that the singular values
\lambda_{k}
s are distinct and sufficiently apart from each other. As a result, we may not necessarily match the
k
th singular value/vector
(\lambda_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(p)})
of
\mathscr{T}
with that of
\mathscr{X}
.

As noted earlier, the tensor perturbation bounds (
5
) for singular vectors are fundamentally different from Wedin-Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
theorems due to the absence of the gap between singular values. This means that for higher-order odeco tensors, the perturbation affects the singular vectors separately. The perturbation bound (
5
) depends only on the amount of perturbation relative to their corresponding singular value.

A careful inspection of the proofs of
Auddy and Yuan 2023
shows that when the perturbation is sufficiently small, or for large enough singular values, we can derive the precise first order remainder term in the asymptotic expansion of
\mathscr{X}
around
\mathscr{T}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a permutation
\pi:[d_{\min}]\to[d_{\min}]
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)},\,\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(q)}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}(\mathscr{E})\times_{s\neq q}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(s)}\right)\leq\left(2+{\|\mathscr{E}\|\over\lambda_{k}}\right)\left(\dfrac{(1+\varepsilon)\|\mathscr{E}\|}{\lambda_{k}}\right)^{p-1}

(6)

provided that
\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq c_{\varepsilon}\lambda_{k}
for some constant
c_{\varepsilon}>0
depending on
\varepsilon
only.

When considering infinitesimal perturbation in that
\|\mathscr{E}\|=o(\lambda_{k})
, we can express the bound (
6
) for singular vectors as

{\rm sign}(\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{(q)},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)}\rangle)\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(q)}\|}\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(q)}+O\left({\|\mathscr{E}\|^{p-1}\over\lambda_{k}^{p-1}}\right),

(7)

where

\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(q)}=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(q)}+\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\cdot\mathscr{E}\times_{s\neq q}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(s)}.

The approximation
\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(q)}
in (
7
) for
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)}
, the singular vectors of
\mathscr{X}
, is accurate to the order of
O\left((\|\mathscr{X}-\mathscr{T}\|/\lambda_{k})^{p-1}\right)
in the direction of any vector perpendicular to
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(q)}
.

As an example demonstrating the above bound, consider the two
d\times d\times d
odeco tensors

\mathscr{T}=\lambda\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\mathbf{e}_{k}\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathscr{X}=\lambda\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}({\mathbf{e}}_{i}+\mathbf{v})\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}

(8)

where
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}:1\leq i\leq d\}
are the canonical basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\mathbf{e}_{d}-\dfrac{1}{d-1}\left(\mathbf{e}_{1}+\dots+\mathbf{e}_{d-1}\right)
. By the Kruskal theorem, the SVDs for both
\mathscr{T}
and
\mathscr{X}
are uniquely identified upto a sign and permutation. In particular, there exists a permutation
\pi:[d]\to[d]
such that

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(1)}=\mathbf{e}_{k}+\mathbf{v}
and

\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)}=\mathbf{e}_{k}
.
Meanwhile, note that
\mathscr{E}=\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{v}\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}\circ\mathbf{e}_{k}
, and thus

\|\mathscr{E}\|=\lambda\|\mathbf{v}\|=\lambda\sqrt{\frac{2}{d-1}}.

Now, since

\mathscr{E}\times_{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{e}_{k}=\lambda\mathbf{v}\,\,\text{for }k=1,\dots,d-1

we can write:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(1)}=\mathbf{e}_{k}+\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\mathscr{E}\times_{2}\mathbf{e}_{k}\times_{3}\mathbf{e}_{k}\,\,\text{for }k=1,\dots,d-1,

meaning that the approximation in (
7
) is exact for the first mode. Similarly, with

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(2)}=\mathbf{u}_{k}=\mathbf{e}_{k}

for
k=1,\dots,d-1
it can be checked that

\mathbf{u}_{k*}^{(2)}=\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(2)}+(\mathscr{X}-\mathscr{T})\times_{1}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(1)}\times_{3}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{(3)}=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{d-1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{k}

meaning

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(k)}^{(2)}=\mathbf{e}_{k}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(2)}\|}\mathbf{u}_{k,*}^{(2)}\,\,\text{for }k=1,\dots,d-1.

For this particular example, all higher order terms in (
7
)
vanish.

4
Odeco Approximation.

We now consider the more general situations where the observed tensor
\mathscr{X}
is not necessarily odeco. For simplicity, we shall assume that
d_{1}=d_{2}=\ldots=d_{p}=d
throughout the section. In this case, we need to first define our estimate for
\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{k}
s. One immediate approach is to first seek the best odeco approximation to
\mathscr{X}
as an estimate of
\mathscr{T}
:

\widehat{\mathscr{T}}=\argmin_{\mathscr{A}{\rm\ is\ odeco}}\|\mathscr{X}-\mathscr{A}\|,

and then estimate the singular vectors of
\mathscr{T}
by those of
\widehat{\mathscr{T}}
. By triangular inequality,

\|\mathscr{T}-\widehat{\mathscr{T}}\|\leq 2\|\mathscr{E}\|,

and thus we can apply the perturbation bounds from the previous section to derive error bounds for the estimated singular vectors. However, computing the best odeco approximation
\widehat{\mathscr{T}}
is difficult and this estimating procedure may not be practically feasible. Instead, we shall show here that a simply procedure based on power iteration and deflation can yield estimates with similar performance guarantees and perhaps even better statistical properties in some cases.

4.1
Power Iteration.

We first show that given initial estimators
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{[0]}^{(q)}
which are suitably close to the leading singular vector
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}
for
1\leq q\leq p
, we can improve the estimation accuracy of
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}
by an iterative scheme. More precisely, suppose we have initial estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{[0]}^{(q)}
such that

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{[0]}^{(q)},\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}.

(9)

The power iteration updates
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{[t]}^{(q)}
by

\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{[t+1]}^{(q)}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\mathbf{v}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{v}=\mathscr{X}\times_{p\neq q}\mathbf{u}_{[t]}^{(q)}

(10)

The following quantities based on the error tensor
\mathscr{E}
will determine the estimation accuracy of our iterative estimator:

\varepsilon_{1}:=\max\limits_{j,k}\|\mathscr{E}\times_{q\neq k}\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{j}\|\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{2}:=\max\limits_{j,k_{1},k_{2}}\|\mathscr{E}\times_{q\neq k_{1},k_{2}}\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{j}\|.

(11)

In contrast to
\|\mathscr{E}\|
which measures the largest linear combination of
\mathscr{E}
with all possible unit vectors along the
p
modes, the definitions of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
respectively restrict all but 1 and 2 modes’ linear combinations to be among the signal directions
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{(q)}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a numerical constant
C>0
such that if
C\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq\lambda_{1}
, after
T\geq C\log\varepsilon_{1}
steps of the power iteration (
10
) initialized at estimators satisfying (
9
), the estimators
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)}:=\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{1,[T]}
satisfy

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)},\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)})\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{1}}

and

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle\left({\rm sign}(\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)},\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}\rangle)\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{1},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq q}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(k)}\right)\leq\dfrac{C(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2})}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}.

(12)

Similar to the perturbation bounds in Section
3
, the error in estimating
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}
with
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)}
, depends only on
\lambda_{1}
and is free of any dependence on the gap between singular values. Moreover, the estimation rates depend on
\mathscr{E}
only through
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
, which by definition satisfy the inequalities:

\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{2}\leq\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq\lambda_{\min}/C

for some constant
C>0
. Thus, while Proposition
4.1
requires
\lambda_{1}\gtrsim\|\mathscr{E}\|
, the rate of estimation is affected by the smaller error norms
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
. While for iid Gaussian errors,
\varepsilon_{1}\asymp\varepsilon_{2}\asymp\|\mathscr{E}\|\asymp\sqrt{d}
, these three norms can differ in magnitude for other types of error tensors arising in practice. Thus the rates given in Proposition
4.1
is an improvement over existing results in the literature in important ways. An example is for random errors with heavy tails, where say for an error tensor with only finite
4^{\text{t}h}
moments,
\varepsilon_{1}\asymp\varepsilon_{2}\asymp\sqrt{d}
, while
\|\mathscr{E}\|\asymp d^{p/4}
. See
Auddy and Yuan 2022
for details. This difference can also arise when estimating higher order population moments through their sample counterparts, as is the case for multiview mixture models and ICA (see, e.g.,
Auddy and Yuan 2025
).

Finally, equation (
12
) is analogous to the perturbation bound in Theorem
3.1
, and shows the precise leading order term in the approximation of
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}
by
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)}
. This can be readily used for establishing distributional properties of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(q)}
under suitable conditions.

4.2
Deflation.

In the previous subsection, we showed that the power iteration successively improves estimation of the top singular vectors
\{\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(q)}:1\leq q\leq p\}
. We now move on to the remaining singular vectors
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{(q)}
for

. To ensure that the previously estimated leading singular vector
\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{1}
does not obfuscate the signal in the direction of, say,
\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{2}

j

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}_{j}=:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq j}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{i}\circ\dots\circ\mathbf{u}^{(p)}_{i}+\mathscr{E}+\sum_{i<j}\left(\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{i}\circ\dots\circ\mathbf{u}^{(p)}_{i}-\langle\mathscr{X},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(1)}_{i}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(p)}_{i}\rangle\,\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(1)}_{i}\circ\dots\circ\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(p)}_{i}\right)

5
Initialization.

The non-convex nature of the odeco approximation dictates that the power iteration described in the previous section depends crucially on the initialization of the singular vectors of
\mathscr{T}
.

Two obvious but sub-optimal choices initialization are through i) random starts, and ii) matricization of
\mathscr{T}
. Firstly, random start from the unit sphere requires a strong signal requirement of
\lambda_{\min}\geq Cd^{(p-2)/2}\|\mathscr{E}\|
for nontrivial initializations that obey (
9
) and (
14
). See, e.g.,
Arous et al. 2019
;
Richard and Montanari 2014
for the special case of rank one tensor SVD where
\mathscr{E}
is a noise tensor of iid Gaussian errors. As we will show below, this signal requirement is sub-optimal since the random start does not leverage sufficient information about the odeco structure, and hence can be weakened. Secondly, the naive matricization of
\mathscr{T}
once again faces the eigengap problem. Following our discussion in Section
3
, since the matrix SVD is uniquely identified only in the presence of eigengap, an odeco tensor
\mathscr{T}
with repeated singular values, even without noise, cannot be recovered from its matricization.

In practice, especially in spectral learning problems where the odeco estimation is most relevant, some auxiliary information about
\mathscr{E}
is often available, and the signal strength requirement might be much lower. We refer the interested reader to
Anandkumar et al. 2014
;
Anandkumar et al. 2015
;
Auddy and Yuan 2023
;
Belkin et al. 2018
for a more detailed discussion.

5.1
General Strategies.

A general strategy for initialization proceeds in two steps. In the first step, one applies HOSVD to the observed tensor
\mathscr{X}
to identify the linear span of the singular vectors along one or more modes of the odeco tensor
\mathscr{T}
. For example, the projection matrices, say

\mathbf{P}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)})^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{P}^{(1,2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)})(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)})^{\top}

can be estimated by

\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1)}(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1)})^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1,2)})(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1,2)})^{\top}

where
\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1)}:1\leq i\leq r\}
and
\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{(1,2)}:1\leq i\leq r\}
are the left singular vectors of
{\sf Mat}_{1}(\mathscr{X})
and
{\sf Mat}_{(1,2)}(\mathscr{X})
respectively. Note that
\mathbf{P}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{P}^{(1,2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}\times d^{2}}
, but both have rank
r
, and since
\mathscr{T}
is odeco, we must have
r\leq d
. Thus estimating the projection matrices will allow us to reduce the dimensionality of the problem from
d
or
d^{2}
to
r
.

The second step slices one or more modes of
\mathscr{X}
with random vectors. For example, one may slice
\mathscr{X}
along the first mode with
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1)}\bm{\theta}
, or along the two modes with
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
yielding matrices

\mathbf{X}_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathscr{X}\times_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1)}\bm{\theta})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}=\mathscr{X}\times_{1,2}(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}^{\prime})

respectively where
\bm{\theta}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{I}_{d})
and
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{I}_{d^{2}})
are independent of
\mathscr{X}
. The randomness in
\bm{\theta}
forces the singular values of
\mathbf{X}_{\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}
to be distinct. Moreover, repeating this initialization procedure for
L
vectors
\bm{\theta}_{1},\dots,\bm{\theta}_{L}
ensures that the singular value gap is large enough, so that we can recover
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)}
from the left and right singular vectors of
\mathbf{X}_{\theta_{i_{*}}}
for some
1\leq i_{*}\leq L
. The basic initialization procedure is summarized in Algorithm
3
.

We now show how random slicing enables nontrivial estimation of each singular vector of
\mathscr{T}
. Without loss of generality, let us focus on fourth order tensors and singular vectors along the first mode, i.e.,
\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{i}
. Suppose that
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}
is the projection estimator computed by HOSVD on an independent copy of
\mathscr{X}
. For
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
we obtain

{\sf Mat}_{1}(\mathscr{X}\times_{1,2}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\langle\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{u}^{(2)}_{i},\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}\rangle\mathbf{u}^{(3)}_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{(4)})^{\top}+{\sf Mat}_{1}(\mathscr{E}\times_{1,2}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}).

(19)

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}
is assumed to be independent of
\mathscr{X}
and hence
\mathscr{E}
, the entries of the matrix
{\sf Mat}_{3}(\mathscr{E}\times_{1,2}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta})
are independent copies of a random variable with mean zero, and variance
\|\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}\|^{2}
.

On the other hand, by sampling random Gaussian vectors
\bm{\theta}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{I}_{d^{2}})
, it is possible to create a large enough gap between the top two singular values. Since
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}
is close to
\mathbf{P}^{(1,2)}
,the singular values of the above matrix are approximately

\lambda_{i}\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)},\mathbf{P}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}\rangle=\lambda_{i}\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)},\bm{\theta}\rangle.

Since
\bm{\theta}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbb{I}_{d}^{2})
,
\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)},\bm{\theta}\rangle\sim N(0,1)
. Now suppose this random slicing is repeated
L
times. As
\bm{\theta}_{l}
varies for
1\leq l\leq L
,
\{\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(1)}\otimes\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(2)},\bm{\theta}_{l}\rangle:1\leq i\leq r,1\leq l\leq L\}
gives a collection of
Lr
independent Gaussian variables.
This allows us to ensure that the gap between the top two singular values of
{\sf Mat}_{3}(\mathscr{X}\times_{1,2}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}_{l_{*}})
is sufficiently large for some
1\leq l_{*}\leq L
as long as
L>2r^{2}\log d
. Consequently matrix perturbation bounds such as the Davis-Kahan theorem guarantees that
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{(3)}
can be accurately estimated by the top singular vectors of
{\sf Mat}_{3}(\mathscr{X}\times_{1,2}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}\bm{\theta}_{l_{*}})
.

The steps outlined above depend on the existence of
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}
computed from an independent copy of
\mathscr{X}
. Of course, this independent copy is not available in practice. However, we can randomly split
\mathscr{X}
into two halves
\mathscr{X}_{[1]}
and
\mathscr{X}_{[2]}
, which would be independent since
\mathscr{E}
is an iid ensemble. Next, we compute
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}^{(1,2)}
from
\mathscr{X}_{[1]}
, while using the projection matrix for random slicing on
\mathscr{X}_{[2]}
. To ensure that the signal strength is evenly spread across
\mathscr{X}_{[1]}
and
\mathscr{X}_{[2]}
, we require the singular vector matrix
\mathbf{U}^{(q)}
from at least one mode of
\mathscr{T}
to satisfy an incoherence condition. Without loss of generality, we assume this is satisfied for the
p
-th mode:

\max_{1\leq i\leq d,1\leq j\leq r}|u^{(p)}_{ij}|\leq\frac{1}{C\log(d)}

for a constant
C>0
.

With the above construction, the following theorem states the accuracy of the initialization method in Algorithm
4
, proceeding sequentially through the initialization-power iteration-deflation routine outlined in Section
4
. For
1\leq j\leq r
, Algorithm
4
is run with the deflated tensor
\mathscr{X}_{j}
defined in (
4.2
). We denote the estimators at the
j
-th step by
\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(q)}:1\leq q\leq p\}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

If
\mathscr{E}
is an iid ensemble whose entries satisfy
\mathbb{E}(E^{8})<C_{0}
,
L>C_{0}r^{2}\log d
,
\max_{1\leq i\leq d,1\leq j\leq r}|u^{(p)}_{ij}|\leq(C_{0}\log(d))^{-1}
and
\lambda_{j}\geq C_{0}d^{p/4}\log(d)
for a constant
C_{0}>0
, there exists another numerical constant
C>0
, a permutation
\pi:[r]\to[r]
such that

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(q)},\mathbf{u}_{\pi(j)}^{(q)}\right)\leq\dfrac{Cd^{(p-2)/2}r(\log d)+C\lambda_{j}\sqrt{dr}(\log d)}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}+\frac{\mathbbm{1}(p=4)}{\sqrt{\log d}}

for
1\leq j\leq r
, with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
.

Since

, we have a nontrivial initialization, in that (

) and (

) are satisfied, whenever

\lambda_{j}\geq Cd^{p/4}(\log d)

(20)

for a constant
C>0
. Note that the above signal strength requirement matches the computational lower bound for tensor SVD from

up to the multiplied logarithmic term.

With this initialization we can run the power iteration and deflation procedure in Algorithm

to find estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(q)}
that satisfy:

\max_{1\leq q\leq p}\sin\angle\left(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(q)},{\mathbf{u}}_{\pi(j)}^{(q)}\right)\leq\frac{C\sqrt{d\log(d)}}{\lambda_{j}}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j\leq r

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
, where we use Theorem
4.2
and the bound the error related quantities from Lemma

in the last step. In fact the fine-grained error analysis from Theorem

enables us to infer linear combinations of
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{(q)}
accurately, as the following theorem shows.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose
\mathscr{E}
is an iid ensemble whose entries satisfy
\mathbb{E}(E^{8})<C_{0}
,

with probability at least

. Moreover, under the same assumptions

Here

and

are the iid random variables defined as

for

.

On the other hand, for any

with probability at least
1-d^{-1}
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we provided a general framework for spectral learning with orthogonally decomposable tensors. Our results highlight three central themes: (i) perturbation bounds for odeco tensors demonstrate robustness to noise without requiring eigengaps, (ii) iterative algorithms such as power iteration and deflation achieve statistically optimal recovery once equipped with suitable initialization, and (iii) initialization itself remains the principal computational bottleneck, revealing an intrinsic statistical–computational gap.

Taken together, these findings clarify both the advantages of tensor methods over matrix analogues and the limitations imposed by non-convex landscapes. Beyond their theoretical significance, our results inform practical algorithm design for latent variable models, independent component analysis, and related applications.

Several directions remain open. Tightening lower bounds for initialization under weaker signal conditions would sharpen the boundary between statistical and computational feasibility. Extending our analysis to non-odeco settings or structured noise models also presents important challenges. We hope that this work helps to bridge the gap between statistical theory and algorithmic practice in high-dimensional tensor analysis.

|\langle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(q)},\check{\mathbf{u}}^{(q)}\rangle|\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{j}}|\mathscr{E}\times_{k\neq q}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{(k)}\times_{q}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(q)}|+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{0}}{\lambda_{j}}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}

(74)

since
\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{(q)},\check{\mathbf{u}}^{(q)}\right)\leq L_{T}\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{j}}
. Thus

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\|\leq

\displaystyle\sin\angle\left(\mathbf{u}^{(2)}_{j},\check{\mathbf{u}}^{(2)}\right)\max_{j+1\leq i\leq r}\lambda_{i}\prod_{q>2}\langle\mathbf{u}^{(q)}_{i},\,\check{\mathbf{u}}^{(q)}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{j}}\cdot\lambda_{j}\cdot\left(\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{0}+C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}/\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}\right)^{p-2}\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{j}}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{3}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}.

(75)

Next, we have using (
51
), (and similarly checking the bound for
\bm{\Delta}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}
), that with
L_{t}=L_{T}=C\varepsilon_{1}/\lambda_{j}
,

\|\bm{\Delta}_{j}\|\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{j}}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{3}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}.

(76)

Finally, following the steps of (
7.2
), we have

\|\mathbf{E}\|=\|\mathscr{E}\times_{q>1}\check{\mathbf{u}}^{(q)}\|\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\dfrac{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}{\lambda_{j}}+\left({C\varepsilon_{1}\over\lambda_{j}}\right)^{2}\|\mathscr{E}\|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}

(77)

since
\lambda_{1}\geq C\|\mathscr{E}\|
, and using the bounds on
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
. Plugging these bounds into (
72
), one has

\|\mathbf{Y}\|\leq\|\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\|+\|\bm{\Delta}_{j}\|+\|\mathbf{E}\|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}

(78)

which means, using (
7.4
) and (
73
), that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\|\mathbf{G}\|}{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{2}}\leq\dfrac{\|\mathbf{Y}\|^{2}+\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\|\mathbf{Y}\|}{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{2}}\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}^{4}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}+C\lambda_{j}\varepsilon_{1}}{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{2}}\leq\dfrac{C\varepsilon_{1}}{\lambda_{j}}.

(79)

Consider
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}
first. Using resolvent based series expansion of projection matrices, we have the following expression. See Theorem 1 from

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{\widehat{\lambda}^{4}_{j}}(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}-\dfrac{2}{\widehat{\lambda}^{6}_{j}}(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}

\displaystyle+\dfrac{2}{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{6}}(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}+{\cal R}_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{j}+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}\right)\right)(\tilde{\bm{\Lambda}}+\mathbf{U}^{\top}_{\perp}\bm{\Delta}_{j}+\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{E})

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\mathbf{E}+O(\varepsilon_{1}^{2})

(81)

where we repeatedly use the bounds on

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{j}=

\displaystyle\leq

(82)

On the other hand, by (

Referring back to (

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be iid random matrices with independent Rademacher entries
\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s),ij}
. Define

\mathscr{G}_{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times d\times d}
as

\mathscr{G}_{(s)ijkl}=\mathbf{E}_{(s),ij}^{2}\delta_{(ij)(kl)}.

Also define
\bar{\mathscr{G}}:=\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{G}_{(s)}
. We use symmetrization to bound the expectation first. Note that for independent copies
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s)}
of
\mathbf{E}_{(s)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{(s)}\circ\mathbf{E}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G}\right\rVert=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{E}_{(s)}\circ\mathbf{E}_{(s)}-\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s)}\circ\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s)}\right)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left((\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}\ast\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}\ast\mathbf{E}_{(s)})-(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}\ast\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}\ast\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s)})\right)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\big[(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\big]_{\rm offdiag}\right\rVert+\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(\mathscr{G}_{(s)}-\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{(s)})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})-\mathscr{G}_{(s)}\right\}\right\rVert+3\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(\mathscr{G}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G})\right\rVert.

The first inequality separates the tensor with entries
(i,j)\neq(k,l)
and those with
(i,j)=(k,l)
. The first kind is denoted by “
{\rm offdiag}
”, since it is similar to a matrix with the diagonal entries replaced by zeroes. For the “diagonal” part, we define
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{(s)}
just as
\mathscr{G}_{(s)}
, with the
\mathbf{E}_{(s),ij}
replaced by
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_{(s),ij}
. The second inequality uses triangle inequality on the “diagonal” tensor.

Note that
\mathbb{E}\left((\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\big|\mathbf{E}_{(s)}\right)=\mathscr{G}_{(s)}
. We now bound the first term conditional on
\mathbf{E}_{(s)}
. To this end, we shall use an
\varepsilon
-net argument as done in
Auddy and Yuan 2022
,
Nguyen et al. 2015
and
Latała 2005
.

For any integer
L,
write
S_{L}=\{0,1,\dots,2^{-L}\}.
It follows from Lemma 10 of
Nguyen et al. 2015
that the set
N_{L}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\leq 1,x_{i}^{2}\in S_{L}\}
forms a
(1/2)
-net for
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
by taking
L=\log d+c_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}
. Now define the projections

\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x})_{i}=x_{i}\mathbbm{1}(x_{i}^{2}=2^{-l})\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x})_{i}=x_{i}\mathbbm{1}(x_{i}^{2}\geq 2^{-l}).

Let
N_{l}=\Pi_{l}(N_{L})
and
N_{<l}=\Pi_{<l}(N_{L})
for
1\leq l\leq L
. Note that for any
\mathbf{x}\in N_{L},

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x})\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\sum_{m<l}\Pi_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}).

Let us define

\mathscr{H}=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})-\mathscr{G}_{(s)}\right\}

Expanding the sum for vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}
we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{2}\mathbf{x}_{2}\times_{3}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{4}\mathbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2}\underset{\mathrm{argmax}\,l_{i}=k}{\sum_{l_{k}=1}^{L}}\sum_{\underset{i\neq k}{l_{i}\leq l_{k}}}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\left(\sum_{l_{2}\leq l_{1}}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\right)\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\left(\sum_{l_{2}\leq l_{1}}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\left(\sum_{l_{1}\leq l_{2}}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\right)\times_{2}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\left(\sum_{l_{1}\leq l_{2}}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\right)\times_{4}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{<l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{<l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

Note that

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{H}\|=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{2}\mathbf{x}_{2}\times_{3}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{4}\mathbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in N_{L}}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

\displaystyle+\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in N_{L}}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{L}\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{<l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{<l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{l_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})

We will bound the first term as the other one follows by symmetry.

Fix
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in N_{L}
. Note that, conditional on
\mathbf{E}_{(s)}
,

\displaystyle Y_{s}=

\displaystyle\left\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\circ(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})-\mathscr{G}_{(s)}\right\}\times_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{2}\mathbf{x}_{2}\times_{3}\mathbf{x}_{1}\times_{4}\mathbf{x}_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}(\mbox{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{(s)}*\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{E}_{(s),ij}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{1i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{2j}^{2}\right)

satisfy
\mathbb{E}Y_{s}=0
, and have sub-exponential norms

\displaystyle\|Y_{s}\|_{\psi_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})\leq 2\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{E}_{(s),ij}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{1i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{2j}^{2}.

Thus by Bernstein inequality, for
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in N_{L}

\displaystyle|\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})|

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left(\dfrac{t}{n}\sum_{s}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|_{\psi_{1}}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\dfrac{t}{n}\cdot\max_{s}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|\right\}

with probability at most
\exp(-t)
. By Lemma 4 of
Latała 2005
,

|N_{l}|<|N_{<l}|<C2^{l}(1+L-l).

Thus by union bound,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in N_{L}}|\mathscr{H}\times_{1}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{2}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\times_{3}\Pi_{l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\times_{4}\Pi_{<l_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\left(\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n^{2}}\sum_{s}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|_{\psi_{1}}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n}\cdot\max_{s}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|\right\}

with probability at most
\exp(-t)
. Summing over
l
, the first term becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|_{\psi_{1}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sqrt{\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n}}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\sum_{i,j}\left(E^{2}_{(s),ij}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))_{i}^{2}(\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))_{j}^{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\dfrac{t\cdot 2^{-l}+(1+L-l)}{n}\right)^{1/2}\left\{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\dfrac{1}{n}\cdot 2^{l}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\max_{j}\left(\sum_{i}E_{(s),ij}^{4}2^{-l}\mathbbm{1}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1})_{i}\neq 0)\right)\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\dfrac{t+(\log d+c_{0})^{2}}{n}\right)^{1/2}\left\{\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\max_{j}\sum_{i}E^{4}_{(s),ij}\right\}^{1/2}.

Similarly the second term becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n}\cdot\max_{s}\|Y_{s}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{l=1}^{L}\dfrac{t+2^{l}(1+L-l)}{n}\cdot\max_{s}\sum_{i,j}E^{2}_{(s),ij}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))_{i}^{2}(\Pi_{<l}(\mathbf{x}_{2}))_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{2}{n}\max_{s,j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(t\cdot 2^{-l}+1+L_{l})E^{2}_{(s),ij}\mathbbm{1}(\Pi_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{1})_{i}\neq 0)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\log d}{n}\max_{s,j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}E^{2}_{(s),ij}.

Taking expectation conditional over
\mathbf{E}_{(s)}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\|\mathscr{H}\|\big|\mathbf{E}_{(s)})\leq\dfrac{C\log d}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\max_{j}\sum_{i}E^{4}_{(s),ij}\right\}^{1/2}+\dfrac{C\log d}{n}\max_{s,j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}E^{2}_{(s),ij}

and thus unconditionally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathscr{H}\|\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{C\log d}{\sqrt{n}}\left\{\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}(E^{8}_{(s),ij})\right\}^{1/4}+\dfrac{C\log d}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\max_{s,j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}E^{2}_{(s),ij}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\log d)\sqrt{\dfrac{d}{n}}+\dfrac{C(\log d)\cdot(d+(nd)^{1/4}d^{1/2})}{n}

by Rosenthal and Khintchine inequalities as used in the proof of Theorem 2.1 of
Auddy and Yuan 2022
. Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(\mathscr{G}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G})\right\rVert=\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(E^{2}_{(s),ij}-1)(\mathbf{x}_{1})_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{2})_{j}^{2}\leq\max_{i,j}\left\lvert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}E^{2}_{(s),ij}-1\right\rvert

By Chebyshev inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,j}\left\lvert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}E^{2}_{(s),ij}-1\right\rvert>t\right)\leq\dfrac{Cd^{2}n^{2}\mathbb{E}|E^{8}_{(s),ij}|}{n^{4}t^{4}}

and thus, integrating over
t

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}(\mathscr{G}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G})\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{\dfrac{d(\log d)^{2}}{n}},

provided
n\geq Cd
. Combining all the results above, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{(s)}\circ\mathbf{E}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G}\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{\dfrac{d(\log d)^{2}}{n}}

Finally using Talagrand’s concentration inequality for bounded functions (see Theorem 1.1 of
Klein and Rio 2005
), we get

\left\lVert\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\mathbf{E}_{(s)}\circ\mathbf{E}_{(s)}-\mathscr{G}\right\rVert\leq C\sqrt{\dfrac{d(\log d)^{2}}{n}}

with probability at least
1-d^{-2}
.
∎
