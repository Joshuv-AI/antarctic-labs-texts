Impact of lymph node yield on survival of patients with metastatic esophageal cancer: a population-based retrospective study
Authors: Bo Chen, Yuze Zhao, Haitao Li, Yu Li, Yun Su, Zefeng Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer is the seventh most common cancer worldwide, and its related mortality remains high. The association between lymph node yield and survival outcomes in patients with distant lymph node metastatic esophageal cancer has not been well studied. This study aimed to investigate the impact of LNY on the survival of patients with metastatic esophageal cancer and to identify prognostic factors affecting survival.MethodsA total of 825 patients with metastatic esophageal cancer who underwent primary tumor resection were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database between 2000 and 2021. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Propensity score matching was used to balance differences between groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to analyze prognostic factors.ResultsAmong the 825 patients included in this study, at least 6 LNYs were found to be beneficial for survival outcomes in the majority of mEC patients. Before propensity matching, the OS and CSS of the LNY > 6 group were significantly better than those of the LNY ≤ 6 group (median OS was 22.32 months and 13.3 months, respectively, P < 0.05). After propensity matching, the survival outcomes of the LNY > 6 group were still better than those of the LNY ≤ 6 group. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that age, race, pathological grade, and specific histological types were independent risk factors for OS. Subgroup analysis showed that patients with LNY > 6 benefited in most subgroups.ConclusionsThis study shows that the number of LNY is significantly associated with survival in patients with distant metastatic esophageal cancer, and adequate lymph node dissection may have association with improved survival. This finding provides an important basis for clinical decision-making and the design of future clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04087-6.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, SEER, Lymph node yield, Survival analysis, Prognosis

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is the seventh most common cancer worldwide and a significant cause [1]. In 2020, approximately 604,000 patients were newly diagnosed and 544,000 patients died [2]. Approximately 35–50% of EC patients have distant lymph nodes or organ metastasis [3]. Studies have reported that the 5-year relative survival rate of metastatic esophageal cancer (mEC) is less than 5%, and only 11.2% of patients can survive for more [4]. Although the progress of neoadjuvant therapy has improved the survival outcomes of EC patients with distant metastasis to some extent, surgical treatment is still the key treatment to prolong the life or at least improve the quality of life of patients.

Adequate lymph node dissection, the representative of quality surgery, remains the basic surgical principle of EC resection and a major determinant [5, 6]. LNY, as a quality indicator of lymph node dissection extension, is an objective and quantifiable marker. The surgical resection of LNY and pathological evaluation not only determine the accuracy of tumor staging but also determine the accuracy [7]. We believe that lymphadenectomy should be performed adequately, even in the case of distant lymph node metastasis. However, no studies have explored the association between LNY and survival outcomes in EC patients with distant metastatic disease, especially distant metastasis of lymph nodes. To better address this issue, we explored the impact of LNY on the survival of EC based on the US Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER), and further explored the important prognostic factors affecting this patient population, thereby providing more evidence for decision-making in clinical practice and more reference data for the design of future clinical trials.

Materials and methods

Data sources and patient selection

We used population-based cancer-related data from 17 cancer registries between 2000 and 2021 from SEER*Stat, version 8.4.4. We recruited 825 patients from the SEER database who had metastatic esophageal cancer (distant lymph node metastasis) and who had undergone lymphadenectomy. Figure 1 describes the selection process for the study population.

The description of the inclusion and exclusion process for the population

It is important to note that the SEER database strictly maintains patient confidentiality and does not disclose personally identifiable information. Therefore, there was no additional ethical review of this study. All procedures involving human subjects in this study adhered to the guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki issued in 1964 and its subsequent amendments or equivalent ethical standards.

Outcome of interest and variable

The primary outcomes of the study were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). OS was defined as the duration from the point of diagnosis to death from any cause or until the last available follow-up. CSS was defined as the period from diagnosis to death attributable to esophageal cancer and was identified [8]. In addition, we also collected about the number of patients (age, gender), LNY, tumor [primary tumor site, tumor stage, histological type, the American joint committee on cancer (AJCC) TNM staging, and outcome variables (follow-up time and condition), and other information. It should be noted that since SEER does not record specific surgical procedures, techniques, or procedure codes, detailed descriptions of the surgery are not available.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described as median [interquartile range] and categorical variables as frequency (%). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. The cutoff value for the number of LNY was calculated using X-tile software (version 3.6.1), which is an invaluable tool [9]. It was validated using the Maximally Selected Rank Statistics (MSRS) method, a results-oriented technique that provides cutoff points corresponding to the most significant associations with survival outcomes [10]. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. To mitigate differences in baseline characteristics, we performed 1:1 propensity matching (PSM) between groups with the use of the nearest neighbor method with a caliper of 0.10 to minimize selection bias [11, 12]. The balance of covariates was assessed with the use of the standardized mean difference (SMD) method. If the SMD is greater than 10%, this is generally considered a meaningful imbalance of factors between the two groups. Is analyzed, then using the Cox proportional hazards regression model to calculate the risk ratio (HR) and 95% CI, the single variable in the Cox regression analysis (P < 0.05 all factors input multivariate Cox regression analysis to determine the independent prognostic factors. No violations were identified when the proportional-hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Then make a subgroup analysis to measure the group within the association, and explore the potential interaction between LNY and other variables. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with the use of R software (version 4.0.4, http://www.r-project.org).

Results

Baseline characteristics and propensity score matching

A total of 825 EC patients with distant lymph node metastatic disease who met the inclusion criteria were extracted from the SEER database. Among the cohort of 825 patients with esophageal cancer, 443 (53.7%) were older than 60 years. Queue to men (83%), III/IV (48.5%), lower esophagus carcinoma (71.2%), adenocarcinoma (68.73%), dominant. The occurrence of distant organ metastasis is presented in Table S1 (Supplementary material). In addition, the vast majority of patients were white, accounting for 88.5% of the total. X-tile and MRSR methods were used to determine the optimal threshold for LNY, which was finally determined to be 6 (Fig. 2). According to the optimal LNY threshold as a benchmark, the patients were divided into ≤ 6 groups and > 6 groups, as shown in Table 1. There were significant differences in age, sex, Grade, grade classification, tumor location, and pathological type between the two groups. To mitigate the effects of confounding variables, a 1:1 propensity-score matching (PSM) strategy was used to create a final matched analysis cohort, and the matching process eliminated some significant differences between the two subgroups, as shown in Table 1. After PSM adjustment, SMDS for all characteristics were less than 0.1, indicating that the populations in the LNY ≤ 6 group were subsequently comparable to those in the LNY > 6 group (Supplementary Material).

Survival outcomes before and after propensity score matching

As shown in Table 2, before PSM, the survival outcome of the “LNY > 6” group was significantly better than that of the “LNY ≤ 6” group, with a median OS of 22.32 months (95%CI 19.56–25.09) and 13.3 months (95%CI 11.38–15.22), respectively. The median CSS time was 22.76 months (95% CI 19.37–26.15) and 14.55 months (95% CI 12.08–17.01), respectively. It is important to note that after the PSM “LNY > 6” group total survival outcome is still better than “LNY 6” or less group, consistent with the results of PSM before. The Kaplan-Meier curves confirmed these findings, with patients in the LNY > 6 group having significantly improved OS and CSS compared with those in the LNY ≤ 6 group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A, B). Again, after PSM, we observed a consistent trend (Fig. 3C, D).

Cox regression analysis of survival

We performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in the post-PSM population. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, the single-factor Cox regression results showed that age, race, classification, histology, and LNY a distant metastatic potential prognostic factors for patients with esophageal cancer OS and CSS (all P < 0.05). Next, variables that tended to be significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. After controlling for other prognostic factors, LNY > 6 patients were associated with better OS (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.49 0.69; P < 0.001) and better CSS (HR = 0.60, 95%CI: 0.49–0.69; P < 0.001). In addition, age ≥ 60, black ethnicity, III/IV, and histological type were independent risk factors for OS.

Discussions

To our knowledge, this is the first SEER-based large cohort study to explore the impact of LNY on survival in patients with distant lymph node metastatic esophageal cancer. In this study, we investigated the correlation between LNY and patients with distant metastatic esophageal cancer and further validated its role as the optimal surgical threshold index for lymph node dissection. In our study, patients with more than 6 LNY had significantly improved overall survival and cancer-specific survival, indicating that LNY is considered to be a strong predictor of survival and a valid indicator of surgical quality in clinical practice.

The number of LNY is an important prognostic factor for patients with esophageal cancer. However, there is no study on LNY in patients with distant lymph node metastasis. Accurate determination of lymph node status is essential for adequate staging judgment, and inadequate LNY assessment may lead to stage migration, with consequences for prognosis and possible treatment. It has been suggested that when the number of lymph nodes removed is insufficient, potentially metastatic lymph nodes may not be detected, resulting in tumor recurrence [13]. Some also believe that excessive lymph node dissection in late-stage cases does not necessarily lead to a good prognosis [14–16], which may be related to the physical condition of patients in the late stages of the disease and the postoperative recovery process. At the same time, in metastatic lymph node, lymph node plays an important role in the anti-tumor immune response [17], and excessive resection may further affect the patient’s subsequent systemic treatment.

Surprisingly, we found that most patients with metastatic esophageal cancer had to harvest at least 6 LNY before they had a survival benefit. Existing studies are consistent with our findings, and Mikko Uimonen et al. demonstrated that a [18]. The same meta-analysis showed significantly improved survival in patients with esophageal cancer with high lymph node yield (HR = 0.72; 95% CI = 0.62–0.84; P < 0.01) [7]. This also reflects the importance of this index as a key principle for lymph node resection in patients with metastatic esophageal cancer.

However, the major pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the association between higher LNY and good survival remain unknown. The existing view is that the number of lymph nodes present in some cancer patients partly reflects intrinsic tumor-host interactions. Tumor factors may stimulate reactive lymph node growth, making it more detectable by surgeons and pathologists, while tumor antigens may stimulate germinal centers, leading to an increased number of lymph nodes. Other ideas suggest that tumor-draining lymph nodes play a dual role in cancer immunity, involving both the triggering of anti-tumor immune responses and the tolerance to cancer. Moreover, with the development of cancer, the balance of the cancer immune system is gradually biased toward tolerance [19]. Biologically, the broad immune response is diminished in node-positive cancers. As the tumor progresses, the immune response may delay growth and lymph node spread. Tumors that subsequently develop lymph node involvement may lose some local immune control [20]. Therefore, LNY may be a marker of tumor-host immune interaction, and local lymph node excision may also help to readjust this “cancer-friendly” immune balance, thereby leading to a more positive prognosis for patients. In addition, further studies exploring the underlying mechanisms of the relationship between LNY and cancer immunity may identify novel therapeutic targets and should be continued.

The cases included in this study span over 20 years, from 2000 to 2021. During this period, there have been significant changes in the treatment patterns for metastatic esophageal cancer, particularly with the recent addition of immunotherapy (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors), which has had a major impact on treatment outcomes [1, 3] . While clinical intuition suggests that patient prognosis may be correlated with the year of treatment, our univariate analysis of the year revealed no significant impact (Supplementary material). We speculate that despite advancements in treatment over the past two decades, overall survival improvements for “patients with surgically treated metastatic esophageal cancer” may be limited, potentially depending more on other patient characteristics (such as LNY), rendering the year no longer the primary factor driving survival differences. Additionally, cases in the SEER database may vary in follow-up duration and data completeness across different years, and insufficient follow-up duration in some cases may also influence statistical results.

Our study still has some limitations. First, because of the retrospective cohort design, this study has the potential for selection bias, which could affect generalizability. Second, the current cohort does not provide more detailed information on individual surgeons, pathologists, and hospital-related factors, nor does it provide further details on the patient’s physical condition, general health or the pathological specimens acquired(e.g., information on resection status), which may also influence the analysis of surgical outcomes and survival outcomes. Third, survival outcomes for esophageal cancer patients depend not only on lymph node yield (LNY) but also critically on postoperative adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, as well as treatments for disease recurrence. These factors play essential roles in determining long-term prognosis and survival outcomes [1, 4]. More importantly, the discussion of the possible effect of neoadjuvant therapy on survival is complicated and uncertain by the lack of information on the use of immunosuppressive agents. Although the alternative statistical strategy that we used in our study (propensity-score matching) was able to mitigate confounding to some extent, it has limitations in the interpretation of these findings. Therefore, large-scale and multi-center cohort studies are needed for further exploration. Few quality measures are “perfect,” and the aim of our study remains to better elucidate the benchmark that has been set, especially when it is used as the standard for surgical quality assessment in patients with distant lymph node metastatic esophageal cancer.
