A two-gene prognostic model for lung adenocarcinoma using summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression
Authors: Jing Xu, Xinyu Liu, Zhouxiao Lu, XiaoYu Wu, Xuzhou Yu, Sheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study aimed to develop accurate prognostic assessment tools for patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) using Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization Analysis and statistical learning-based prognostic modeling methodsMethodsWe conducted SMR analysis to explore genetic associations between genes and LUAD, and analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The shared genes were subjected to the univariate Cox regression analysis and survival analysis to determine prognosis-related genes to construct a prognostic model with the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Individual gene contributions to the model were clarified through SHAP analysis, and predictive performance was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curve, the C-index, and calibration plots.Results59 shared genes were identified between the SMR and DEGs analyses. The univariate Cox regression analysis and survival analysis identified two prognosis-related genes (PKP2 and SLC7A11), which were used to develop a prognostic model. The Kaplan–Meier plot and univariate and multivariate Cox regression models illustrated that patients in the high-risk group had a poorer clinical outcome. The low-risk group showed enrichment of several immune-related pathways and showed higher infiltration levels of 15 immune cell types.ConclusionA two-gene prognostic model was developed to predict outcomes in patients with LUAD, which was also highly associated with immune status.Keywords: Summary-data-based mendelian randomization, shapley additive explanations, prognostic model, PKP2, SLC7A11

Introduction

Extensive population-based studies have repeatedly demonstrated that lung cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally and represents the primary contributor to cancer-associated deaths [1]. Recent data from 2020 revealed that diagnostic centers identified upwards of 220,000 individuals with this condition, while mortality statistics indicated over 180,000 fatalities attributed to pulmonary malignancies [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), constituting the majority subtype, represents roughly 85% of all lung cancer cases [3]. Within this category, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) accounts for approximately 65% of these diagnoses [3]. Current therapeutic approaches for this condition include molecularly targeted agents and immune-based therapies, reflecting a comprehensive, multimodal treatment paradigm [4]. Despite substantial improvements in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic modalities that have progressively increased patient survival, the disease continues to exhibit poor clinical outcomes [5]. In addition, due to its molecular diversity and frequent late-stage diagnosis, it poses significant clinical challenges. Therefore, it is critical to identify additional biomarkers to facilitate early detection and prognostic evaluation in LUAD. Such advancements could enhance risk classification and improve patient survival. Computational approaches applied to omics datasets have shown notable promise in identifying disease biomarkers. However, traditional analyses of gene expression-disease relationships are often confounded by external influences, such as environmental factors and lifestyle, as well as issues of reverse causation [6]. Pathological changes may alter gene expression profiles rather than result from them, complicating efforts to establish genuine genetic associations. Furthermore, observational studies face challenges in disentangling the complex interactions among genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and disease progression [7]. In addition, previous studies have developed radiomics-based [8] and bioinformatics-based [9] prognostic models for lung cancer, providing complementary perspectives to the present work.

Genetic instrumental variable analysis, commonly known as Mendelian randomization (MR), represents a sophisticated epidemiological approach that leverages naturally occurring genetic variations to evaluate genetic associations between risk factors and health outcomes [10]. This methodology capitalizes on the random distribution of genetic variants during conception, thereby simulating the conditions of a clinical trial while minimizing the influence of confounding variables that often distort traditional observational research [11]. The emergence of comprehensive genomic investigations, including genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analyses, has enabled researchers to conduct extensive MR analyses that elucidate potential genetic associations between gene expression and pathological conditions [12]. Innovative techniques such as summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) offer additional perspectives for examining the interplay between gene expression patterns and disease development [13]. The SMR approach employs MR techniques utilizing aggregated data to examine potential genetic associations between gene expression patterns and complex disorders. This methodology evaluates combined results from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and eQTL investigations to determine these relationships.

In this study, we first applied SMR-HEIDI analysis targeting LUAD gene expression to provide robust evidence for LUAD susceptibility. Next, we detected differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and LUAD tissue. We then used Cox proportional hazards regression to analyze genes associated with LUAD prognosis, and subsequently developed a prognostic model. Finally, the model’s performance was evaluated using various methods. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies that integrates SMR-based genetic evidence with differential expression analysis to construct a prognostic model for LUAD, providing a genetic perspective complementary to conventional expression-based signatures.

Materials and methods

Data sources

LUAD tissue eQTL data were obtained from the FinnGen database (Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, 515 cases, https://www.finngen.fi/en), which contains 27,046 genes on autosomal chromosomes. We gathered four gene expression profiles from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://www.cancer.gov) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) databases: TCGA-LUAD, GSE68465 (GEO cohort 1), GSE31210 (GEO cohort 2), and GSE41271 (GEO cohort 3). Relevant clinical variables were collected for all patients. Patients missing information on overall survival (OS) or survival status were excluded, and the remaining missing clinical variables were handled by complete-case analysis. SMR analysis and colocalization analysis

To determine the pleiotropic relationship between gene eQTLs and LUAD GWAS signals, we conducted SMR analysis. The cutoff value was set at an adjusted p-value < 0.05. The HEIDI (heterogeneity in dependent instruments) test further evaluated the contribution of other SNPs to the association between gene expression and LUAD. HEIDI test p-values > 0.05 suggested that this association was more likely to reflect a shared causal variant than to be driven by linkage.

To further assess whether the eQTL and LUAD GWAS signals shared a common causal variant, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis for the two candidate genes (PKP2 and SLC7A11) using the “coloc” R package. For each gene, genetic variants within ±1 Mb of the gene region were extracted from the eQTL and LUAD GWAS summary statistics, and the posterior probabilities of five hypotheses (H0–H4) were estimated under default priors, where H4 indicates that both traits are associated with the same causal variant. A posterior probability of H4 (PP.H4) > 0.75 was considered evidence of colocalization.

DEG analysis

The gene expression matrix for the TCGA cohort was obtained from level 3 RNA sequencing, which refers to processed data that has undergone standardized analysis and quality control. The data for GSE68465, GSE41271, and GSE31210 consisted of raw microarray data that were normalized using the MAS 5.0 algorithm with the R package “affy” (version 3.17). Batch effects between the RNA-seq and microarray datasets were corrected using the ComBat algorithm (“sva” package). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R package “limma,” with the criteria of an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FoldChange(FC)|) >1 and an adjusted p value (adj p value) <0.05.

Construction and evaluation of a prognostic model

Shared genes from SMR and DEG analyses were subjected to univariate Cox regression and survival analyses to identify OS-related genes with P values < 0.05. Genes common to both analyses were then used to construct a prognostic model and risk score formula using multivariate Cox regression. Finally, we used the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method, implemented with the “shapviz” package, to quantify the contribution of each gene to the risk score and to provide patient-level explanations of how each gene shifts the predicted risk, which complements the interpretation provided by the Cox regression coefficients [14].

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was reported, the C-index was calculated, and a calibration plot based on a bootstrap sample of 1,000 resamples was generated to assess the model’s predictive power. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap optimism correction (1,000 resamples) and repeated 5-fold cross-validation to confirm the robustness of the model. Survival analysis and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to demonstrate the significance of the risk score as a prognostic predictor. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using the cox.zph function.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To detect changes in basic biological mechanisms between low- and high-risk groups, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the R package “clusterProfiler”. We considered pathways significant if the adjusted P value, which accounts for multiple testing, was less than 0.05.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB)

The “maftools” package was used to analyze somatic mutation data and calculate TMB.

The evaluation of the association between the prognostic model and the immune state

The GSVA package was used to perform single-sample gene set enrichment analysis. This analysis revealed the activity of immune-related functions and the level of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs). Furthermore, differences in gene expression of immunomodulators between low- and high-risk groups were explored.

scRNA-seq data processing

The following filtering thresholds were applied: 1) genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were removed; 2) cells with fewer than 50 detected genes were removed; and 3) cells with more than 10% mitochondrial gene expression were removed. We used the “Seurat” package to conduct quality control analysis of the transcriptomic count matrix and employed the log-normal algorithm and a linear regression model for data normalization. Based on the top 2000 highly expressed and highly variable genes, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) to explore significant and influential dimensions. The t-Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (tSNE) algorithm was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the top 20 principal components and cluster the main cell clusters. The “SingleR” package was used for cell annotation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with R 4.1.3 (https://www.r-project.org). Normally distributed measurement data were presented as mean ± SD and compared using two-tailed paired t-tests, whereas non-normally distributed data were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data were presented as numbers (N) and compared using the chi-square test.

Construction and evaluation of a prognostic model

As shown in the flow diagram (Figure 1), we first performed SMR analysis to determine the association between gene eQTLs and LUAD. Manhattan plots showed that 690 genes strongly associated with LUAD (adjusted p < 0.05, HEIDI-P > 0.05) were located on chromosomes 1–22 (Figure 2A). A total of 825 DEGs were initially identified between normal and LUAD tissue (Figure 2B). Then, 59 common genes were filtered and further analyzed using univariate Cox regression and survival analysis in the TCGA cohort (Figure 2C). Two overlapping genes (SLC7A11 and PKP2) were identified and further used to establish a prognostic model (Figure 2D). Figure 2E-F showed the survival differences between the low- and high-expression groups of SLC7A11 and PKP2. Figure 2G presents the results of the univariate Cox regression analysis for SLC7A11 and PKP2. The co-expression analysis showed there was no correlation between the expression of SLC7A11 and PKP2 (Figure S1A). A single-gene scatter map demonstrated a positive correlation between SLC7A11 and PKP2 eQTLs and LUAD risk (slope > 0; Figure 1H-I). The SMR regional association plot revealed SLC7A11 and PKP2 were located on Chromosomes 4 and 12, respectively (Figure 2J-K), although the posterior probability of H4 (PP.H4) for SLC7A11 (0.15) and PKP2 (0.16) was <0.75 (Figure S1B). To improve model interpretability, a SHAP analysis was conducted to interpret the contribution of candidate genes. The SHAP bar plot and scatter plot indicated that PKP2 contributed to model classification more than SLC7A11 (Figure 2L-M). The SHAP Waterfall plot showed that the prognosis was predicted to be worse in patients with SLC7A11 = 0 and PKP2 = 1.91 (Figure 2N).

The Kaplan–Meier plot in the TCGA cohort showed that the high-risk group had a poorer clinical outcome (p < 0.001; Figure 3A). It was confirmed in GEO cohort 1 (p < 0.001), GEO cohort 2 (p < 0.001), and GEO cohort 3 (p = 0.002; Figure S2A). Furthermore, stratification analyses demonstrated that the low-risk patients had a better prognosis in each subgroup (Figure S3). To compare the prognostic model with clinical parameters (age, gender, smoking status, and stage), we fitted univariate and multivariate Cox regression models. In the TCGA cohort, the univariate Cox regression model indicated that the risk score was an important influencing factor for the OS of LUAD patients (HR = 2.450, 95% CI (1.448, 4.146), p < 0.001, Figure 3B), which was consistent with the results in three validation cohorts (GEO cohort 1: HR = 1.772, 95% CI (1.332, 2.357), p < 0.001; GEO cohort 2: HR = 2.251, 95% CI (1.371, 3.694), p < 0.001; GEO cohort 3: HR = 5.789, 95% CI (2.240, 14.959), p < 0.001, Figure S2B). The multivariate Cox regression model revealed that the prognostic model was an independent prognostic factor for the OS (HR = 2.514, 95% CI (1.495, 4.230), p < 0.001, Figure 3C). Similar results were observed in three validation cohorts (GEO cohort 1: HR = 1.715, 95% CI (1.276, 2.306), p < 0.001; GEO cohort 2: HR = 2.225, 95% CI (1.366, 3.722), p < 0.001; GEO cohort 3 (HR = 4.887, 95% CI (1.869, 12.780), P < 0.001, Figure S2C).

To assess the model’s predictive accuracy, we plotted the ROC and calibration curves and calculated the C-index. The AUCs of the model in the TCGA set at 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.801 (95%CI: 0.721–0.881), 0.796 (95%CI: 0.717–0.875), and 0.793 (95%CI: 0.713–0.873), respectively (Figure 3D); in GEO cohort 1, the AUCs were 0.792 (95%CI: 0.721–0.863), 0.784 (95%CI: 0.716–0.852), and 0.773 (95%CI: 0.701–0.845), respectively (Figure S4A); in GEO cohort 2, the AUCs were 0.820 (95%CI: 0.743–0.883), 0.812 (95%CI: 0.740–0.882), and 0.803 (95%CI: 0.733–0.873), respectively (Figure S4A); in GEO cohort 3, the AUCs were 0.810 (95%CI: 0.738–0.882), 0.813 (95%CI: 0.740–0.886), and 0.847 (95%CI: 0.777–0.917), respectively (Figure S4A). The C-index at 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in the four cohorts was above 0.7 (Figure 3E). The calibration plots of the TCGA cohort, GEO cohorts 1, 2, and 3 were presented in Figure 3F and Figure S4B. The Decision curve analysis (DCA) plot was presented in Figure S4C.

Association between the prognostic model and clinical parameters

There were no differences in the distribution of clinical parameters between the high- and low-risk groups (Figure S5A). In addition, the risk scores of patients across different subgroups did not differ (Figure S5B-H).

The prognostic model is highly associated with immune status

To investigate potential altered signaling pathways between the low- and high-risk groups, we performed GSEA. The results revealed that 18 pathways were enriched in the GSEA analysis, including 5 pathways in the high-risk group and 13 pathways in the low-risk group (Figure S6A). Interestingly, in the low-risk group, several immune-related pathways were activated, including the T cell receptor signaling pathway, the B cell receptor signaling pathway, the Chemokine signaling pathway, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production. Then, we assessed the association between the prognostic model and immune status. First, we explored the levels of 15 TIICs and the activity of 13 immune-related functions. The results revealed that immune function, as measured by CCR, Checkpoint, HLA, and T cell co-stimulation, was enhanced in the low-risk group (Figure S6B). Moreover, the enrichment score for 9 immune cell types (aDCs, DCs, iDCs, Mast cells, Neutrophils, T helper cells, Th2 cells, TIL, pDCs) in the low-risk group was increased (Figure S6C). Expression of 15 immune checkpoints was elevated (Figure S6D).

TMB

The most frequent mutation in the high-risk group was TTN (51%), followed by TP53 (49%) and MUC16 (44%) (Figure S6E). In the low-risk group, the top 3 mutated genes were TP53 (47%), TTN (42%), and CSMD3 (39%) (Figure S6F). Nevertheless, there was no difference in TMB between the high- and low-risk groups (Figure S6G).

Construction of a nomogram

Using four clinical characteristics (age, gender, smoking status, and stage) and the risk score, we developed a prognostic nomogram in the TCGA cohort to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in LUAD patients (Figure 5A). The AUCs of the nomogram for 1-, 3-, and 5-years OS were 0.827, 0.817, and 0.837 (Figure 5B), respectively, similar to the AUCs in GEO cohort 1, GEO cohort 2, and GEO cohort 3 (Figure S7A). Discussion

SMR is a method that enables us to distinguish genetic associations driven by shared causal variants from false associations arising from linkage disequilibrium and to clarify whether the observed association can be explained by variation in gene expression rather than linkage effects [13]. In this study, we performed an SMR analysis to examine potential genetic associations between target gene expression and LUAD. Then, we performed DEG analysis and identified 59 shared genes. Next, we performed univariate Cox regression and survival analyses to identify two OS-related genes and established a prognostic model using multivariate Cox regression. By adopting Cox proportional hazards regression, a widely used and interpretable statistical method, we developed a robust prognostic model. Our results showed that the model performed well across the four datasets from different sources. We calculated the risk score and divided all patients into low- and high-risk groups. The Kaplan–Meier plot and the univariate and multivariate Cox regression models indicated that the risk score was an independent prognostic factor in LUAD patients. Then, we assessed the model’s predictive power for LUAD prognosis using ROC, C-index, and calibration plots. All AUCs for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS across the four cohorts were >0.70 and significantly superior to those based on clinical parameters (age, gender, stage). The calibration plot demonstrated the consistency between the model-predicted and observed survival rates. Previously, Wu et al. established a prognostic model with five palmitoylation-related genes. The AUCs at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.669, 0.640, and 0.614, respectively [15]. Another study reported a mitochondrial lipid metabolism-related prognostic model based on four genes (ACSL4, ACSS3, CHPT1, and ACSM5), showing AUCs of 0.677, 0.657, and 0.645 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. Both of these AUCs were inferior to those of the present model [16]. From a clinical perspective, this simple two-gene risk score can stratify LUAD patients into low- and high-risk groups, which may help guide follow-up intensity and identify patients who could benefit from closer monitoring or more aggressive therapy. The accompanying nomogram further facilitates individualized estimation of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival in clinical practice.

The SHAP algorithm is a game-theoretic approach that quantifies the contribution of each feature to the predicted outcome [17]. For example, Wang et al. applied SHAP to interpret a multi-parameter model predicting post-VATS pain risk in NSCLC patients [18]. Here, SHAP was used to provide visual evidence complementing the multivariate Cox regression analysis, revealing that PKP2 significantly increased the prognostic risk in LUAD patients.

PKP2, a member of the plakophilin family, modulates focal adhesion turnover and cell adhesion by transcriptionally regulating β-integrin and activating β-catenin signaling [19]. PKP2 has been reported to promote lung cancer progression and radioresistance [20], as well as gastric and colon cancer development, correlating with tumor stage and poor prognosis [21,22]. SLC7A11 encodes a component of the sodium-independent anionic amino acid transport system that maintains cystine/glutamate antiporter activity [23], transporting cystine into cells for glutathione synthesis [24]. Consistent with our findings, high SLC7A11 expression has been reported in LUAD [25] and facilitates tumor progression and metastasis, predicting a poorer outcome [26–27]. These two genes are biologically connected through the common themes of redox homeostasis and EMT-related signaling: PKP2 regulates cell adhesion, beta-catenin signaling, and radioresistance, whereas SLC7A11 functions as a cystine/glutamate antiporter and a key regulator of ferroptosis. Their combined dysregulation may therefore cooperatively promote LUAD progression.

GSEA identified several immune-related pathways enriched in the low-risk group, suggesting a potential association between PKP2/SLC7A11 expression and the immune response; however, these immune-related findings are exploratory and correlative. Consistently, immune functions and immune cell infiltration were enhanced in the low-risk group. Previous studies in ovarian, hepatocellular, breast, renal, and adrenocortical cancers have likewise associated high SLC7A11 expression with increased immune cell infiltration [28–31]. ScRNA-seq analysis further revealed up-regulated PKP2 expression in myocytes, which are involved in inflammatory/immune responses and cytokine production [32].

In this study, we developed a prognostic model to predict OS in LUAD patients. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. (1) All data were obtained from public databases, which may introduce potential selection bias. (2) This was a retrospective study, and prospective validation is still required. (3) Residual platform heterogeneity may persist between the RNA-seq and microarray datasets despite batch correction. (4) The biological functions of PKP2 and SLC7A11 in LUAD have not been experimentally validated. (5) The generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations is limited. (6) The immune-related findings are correlative in nature and require further mechanistic investigation.

In summary, we constructed a prognostic model to predict outcomes in LUAD patients. The model was stable and reliable for predicting LUAD prognosis.
