Routine health examination indicators for fatty liver disease risk stratification in adults with thyroid dysfunction: a cross-sectional study of 46,311 individuals
Authors: Chaochao Wang, Huiting Rao, Xuefei Zhang, Guibiao Zhang, Guodong Xia, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFatty liver disease is frequently identified during routine health examinations, but practical strategies for risk stratification in adults with thyroid dysfunction remain limited. This study evaluated metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) prevalence across thyroid function categories and developed health examination–based risk stratification models.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 46,311 adults who underwent routine health examinations from November 2019 to February 2025. Participants were classified as euthyroid, hypothyroid, or hyperthyroid. MAFLD was defined as ultrasound-confirmed hepatic steatosis plus overweight/obesity, diabetes, or metabolic dysregulation. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analyses assessed thyroid-related associations. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression and multivariable logistic regression were used to develop models for adults with hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism. Discrimination, calibration, and utility for post-examination risk stratification were evaluated.ResultsAmong 46,311 participants, 42,978 were euthyroid, 2,645 had hypothyroidism, and 688 had hyperthyroidism. MAFLD prevalence was 33.0%, 35.5%, and 19.9%, respectively. FT3 was positively associated with MAFLD, whereas FT4 was inversely associated; TSH was not independently associated after full adjustment. The hypothyroid model retained age, BMI, ALT, TG, and HDL-C, with AUCs of 0.906 and 0.901 in the training and validation sets. The hyperthyroid model retained age, BMI, FT4, ALT, and HDL-C, with AUCs of 0.918 and 0.932. Both models showed good calibration and potential utility for post-examination risk stratification.ConclusionsRoutine health examination indicators can stratify fatty liver risk among adults with thyroid dysfunction and may help identify individuals requiring targeted liver-metabolic follow-up. External validation and prospective evaluation are needed.Keywords: Routine health examination, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, thyroid dysfunction, risk stratification, clinical prediction model

Introduction

Fatty liver disease has become one of the most common chronic liver conditions worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a major public health challenge. Recent global estimates suggest that non-alcoholic fatty liver disease affects approximately one-third of the adult population, with a rising burden driven by obesity, type 2 diabetes, and other cardiometabolic risk factors [1,2]. Because early-stage fatty liver disease is often asymptomatic, many individuals are first identified during routine health examinations rather than through liver-related clinical symptoms. Health examination programs therefore provide an important opportunity for early detection, risk stratification, and timely lifestyle or metabolic intervention. Current clinical guidance also emphasizes case-finding among individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors, abnormal liver enzymes, or radiological evidence of hepatic steatosis [3,4].

The terminology and diagnostic framework for fatty liver disease have evolved in recent years. Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), proposed in 2020, emphasizes the coexistence of hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction and can be diagnosed in the presence of hepatic steatosis plus overweight/obesity, type 2 diabetes, or evidence of metabolic dysregulation [5]. More recently, a multisociety Delphi consensus introduced steatotic liver disease (SLD) as an overarching term and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) as the replacement term for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, requiring hepatic steatosis plus at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [6]. This transition reinforces the metabolic and public health relevance of fatty liver disease. However, many retrospective health examination databases were established before full implementation of the MASLD framework and may not contain complete alcohol-intake information required for full steatotic liver disease subclassification. Therefore, in the present real-world screening study, MAFLD was retained as the operational outcome according to the 2020 international consensus criteria, while the findings are interpreted within the broader transition toward MASLD-oriented metabolic risk assessment.

Routine health examinations in China commonly include abdominal ultrasonography, thyroid function testing, liver enzymes, lipid profiles, fasting plasma glucose, blood pressure, and anthropometric measurements. These data are inexpensive, widely available, and repeatedly measurable, making them suitable for population-based risk assessment in health management centers and primary care settings. Compared with specialized imaging or histological evaluation, ultrasound-based fatty liver assessment combined with routine metabolic indicators is more feasible for large-scale screening. Nevertheless, how to use routinely collected health examination indicators to identify individuals at higher risk of fatty liver disease remains insufficiently explored, particularly among adults with thyroid dysfunction.

Thyroid dysfunction is frequently detected during health examinations and is closely related to systemic metabolic regulation. Thyroid hormones influence body weight, lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, energy expenditure, and hepatic metabolic activity [7,8]. Previous studies have reported associations between thyroid dysfunction, thyroid hormone levels, and fatty liver disease, but findings have not been fully consistent. The Rotterdam Study showed that lower thyroid function was associated with increased risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, with higher free thyroxine levels associated with lower risk [9]. A large Korean cross-sectional study reported that non-alcoholic fatty liver disease was associated with hypothyroidism across a broad spectrum of thyroid function [10], whereas a 4-year retrospective cohort study found that subclinical and overt hypothyroidism were not independently associated with incident non-alcoholic fatty liver disease after adjustment for metabolic factors [11]. More recently, a nationwide study using the MASLD framework suggested that hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism were associated with different patterns of MASLD risk [12]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study populations, fatty liver definitions, thyroid dysfunction subtypes, and adjustment for obesity, lipids, glucose metabolism, and comorbidities.

From a public health and health management perspective, adults with thyroid dysfunction represent a readily identifiable subgroup for targeted fatty liver risk assessment. These individuals often undergo repeated biochemical monitoring, creating opportunities to integrate liver-metabolic risk stratification into routine follow-up. Because thyroid dysfunction is linked to metabolic alterations that overlap with fatty liver risk, risk stratification within this subgroup may be more clinically actionable than applying a general fatty liver screening approach alone. However, previous studies have mainly focused on associations between thyroid dysfunction and fatty liver disease, while fewer have translated routine health examination data into practical screening-oriented risk stratification tools. Therefore, this large retrospective cross-sectional study of 46,311 adults undergoing routine health examinations had three aims: first, to evaluate the distribution of MAFLD across thyroid function categories; second, to examine the associations of routinely measured thyroid indices, including thyroid-stimulating hormone, free triiodothyronine, and free thyroxine, with MAFLD; and third, to develop and internally validate pragmatic risk stratification models for adults with hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism using routinely available clinical indicators. By reframing thyroid dysfunction as a screening-relevant subgroup within health examination practice, this study seeks to provide evidence for targeted fatty liver risk assessment and post-examination follow-up in real-world health management settings.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Health Management Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, China. Adults who underwent routine health examinations between November 2019 and February 2025 were screened. Routine examinations included thyroid function testing, biochemical measurements, anthropometric assessment, waist circumference measurement, blood pressure measurement, and abdominal ultrasonography.

Individuals were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older and had complete demographic and anthropometric information, thyroid function tests, liver biochemical tests, lipid profiles, fasting plasma glucose, and abdominal ultrasonography required for the study analyses. Participants with one or more of these required data elements missing were excluded, and the final analytic cohort was based on complete cases. Additional study-specific exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation; a history of thyroid cancer, thyroidectomy, or radioactive iodine therapy; current or prior use of thyroid hormones or antithyroid drugs; and major liver diseases, including viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, Wilson’s disease, liver malignancy, or previous liver surgery. The liver-related exclusions were applied to reduce etiologic heterogeneity. For individuals with repeated health examination records, only the most recent complete record was retained.

A total of 76,239 adults with thyroid function testing and abdominal ultrasonography were initially screened. Overall, 29,928 records or individuals were removed or excluded because of missing key data (n = 13,785), duplicate records (n = 1,601), multiple examinations beyond the most recent retained record (n = 12,856), pregnancy (n = 208), history of liver surgery (n = 52), history of thyroid surgery (n = 1,058), history of iodine-131 therapy (n = 42), history of viral hepatitis (n = 81), or current or prior use of antithyroid or thyroid hormone medications (n = 245). Finally, 46,311 individuals were included, comprising 42,978 euthyroid individuals, 2,645 individuals with hypothyroidism, and 688 individuals with hyperthyroidism. The hypothyroid group included 563 individuals with overt hypothyroidism and 2,082 with subclinical hypothyroidism; the hyperthyroid group included 350 individuals with overt hyperthyroidism and 338 with subclinical hyperthyroidism. Data collection and definitions

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were extracted from the electronic health examination database. Variables included age, sex, height, weight, waist circumference, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, medical history, medication history, liver enzymes and liver function indicators, lipid profile, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), and free thyroxine (FT4). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of 8–12 h. All laboratory tests were performed using standardized automated biochemical and immunoassay systems under institutional quality-control procedures.

Abdominal ultrasonography was performed by trained sonographers using standard clinical protocols. Hepatic steatosis was assessed according to conventional ultrasonographic features, including increased hepatic echogenicity, ultrasound beam attenuation, and impaired visualization of intrahepatic vessels or the diaphragm. Ultrasound findings were reviewed by experienced radiologists as part of routine quality control.

Thyroid function status was defined using institutional reference ranges: TSH, 0.38–5.57 mIU/L; FT3, 1.80–3.80 pg/mL; and FT4, 0.78–1.86 ng/dL. Euthyroidism was defined as TSH, FT3, and FT4 within the reference ranges. Overt hypothyroidism was defined as elevated TSH with decreased FT4, and subclinical hypothyroidism as elevated TSH with normal FT4. Overt hyperthyroidism was defined as suppressed TSH with elevated FT3 and/or FT4, and subclinical hyperthyroidism as suppressed TSH with normal FT3 and FT4. Diabetes was defined according to documented medical history, glucose-lowering medication use, or an existing diagnosis recorded in the health examination system. Hypertension was defined according to documented medical history, antihypertensive medication use, or an existing diagnosis recorded in the health examination system.

The primary outcome was metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), defined according to the 2020 international consensus criteria as hepatic steatosis plus overweight/obesity, diabetes, or metabolic dysregulation [5]. Hepatic steatosis was determined by abdominal ultrasonography. Overweight and obesity were classified according to Chinese criteria, with overweight defined as BMI ≥24 kg/m2 and obesity as BMI ≥28 kg/m2. Metabolic dysregulation was defined as the presence of at least two metabolic risk abnormalities among consensus-defined criteria, operationalized using available health examination variables including waist circumference, blood pressure-related abnormalities or antihypertensive treatment, TG, HDL-C, and FPG. Detailed operational criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1. MAFLD was retained as the operational outcome because complete alcohol-intake data required for full SLD/MASLD subclassification were unavailable.

Statistical analysis and model development

MAFLD prevalence was compared across thyroid function categories. Logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between thyroid dysfunction categories, thyroid-related indicators, and MAFLD. Progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 2 additionally adjusted for thyroid-related indicators where applicable; Model 3 further adjusted for ALT, AST, GGT, ALP, ALB, and TB; Model 4 further adjusted for TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and FPG; and Model 5 additionally adjusted for diabetes and hypertension. Model 5 therefore represented the fully adjusted model. Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to assess the shape of associations between FT3, FT4, and MAFLD.

Separate risk stratification models were developed for adults with hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism. Within each thyroid dysfunction group, participants were randomly divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 7:3. Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical relevance, routine availability, and potential association with fatty liver disease. In the training set, candidate predictors were evaluated using logistic regression and LASSO regression for variable selection [13], implemented with the glmnet package in R [14]. Selected variables were entered into multivariable logistic regression, and final predictors were determined according to statistical significance, clinical interpretability, and model parsimony.

Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoff values were calculated using the Youden index. Calibration was evaluated using calibration plots with bootstrap resampling. Decision curve analysis was used to assess potential clinical utility across threshold probabilities [15]. Because several candidate predictors overlapped with the metabolic criteria used to define MAFLD, the models were interpreted as screening-oriented risk stratification tools rather than independent diagnostic replacements. Nomograms and calibration plots were generated using the rms package, and decision curve analysis was performed using the rmda or ggDCA package according to the actual analytic workflow.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Between-group comparisons used the independent-samples t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or chi-square test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.2.0. Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics and reporting guidelines

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (No. KY2025074). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because anonymized retrospective health examination data were used. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guideline for observational studies [16], and prediction-model components were reported with reference to TRIPOD+AI recommendations [17].

Distribution of screening-defined MAFLD across thyroid function categories

Screening-defined MAFLD was common in this health examination cohort. MAFLD was present in 14,197 of 42,978 euthyroid individuals (33.0%), 939 of 2,645 individuals with hypothyroidism (35.5%), and 137 of 688 individuals with hyperthyroidism (19.9%) (Table 1). Compared with euthyroid participants, MAFLD prevalence was modestly higher in the hypothyroid group and substantially lower in the hyperthyroid group.

In univariate logistic regression analysis, overt hypothyroidism was associated with higher odds of MAFLD (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.33–1.86, p < 0.001), whereas subclinical hypothyroidism was not significantly associated with MAFLD (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.92–1.11, p = 0.811). Both overt hyperthyroidism (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.34–0.58, p < 0.001) and subclinical hyperthyroidism (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.44–0.74, p < 0.001) were associated with lower odds of MAFLD (Table 2). Because subclinical hypothyroidism showed no significant association with MAFLD in univariate analysis, subsequent subtype-specific multivariable and restricted cubic spline analyses focused on overt hypothyroidism, overt hyperthyroidism, and subclinical hyperthyroidism. In supplementary analyses among adults with hypothyroidism, thyroid nodules and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis were not significantly associated with MAFLD (Supplementary Table 2).

Associations of routinely measured thyroid indices with MAFLD

Progressively adjusted logistic regression models were used to further evaluate the associations of thyroid-related indicators with MAFLD in thyroid dysfunction subtypes that showed significant univariate associations (Table 3). In Model 5 (the fully adjusted model), FT3 was positively associated with MAFLD in overt hypothyroidism, overt hyperthyroidism, and subclinical hyperthyroidism, whereas FT4 was inversely associated with MAFLD in these groups. In contrast, TSH did not show an independent association with MAFLD after full adjustment. These findings suggest that, among routinely measured thyroid indices, FT3 and FT4 were more consistently associated with screening-defined MAFLD than TSH after adjustment for demographic, hepatic, metabolic, and comorbidity-related factors.

Screening-oriented risk stratification model in adults with hypothyroidism

Among 2,645 individuals with hypothyroidism, 1,706 did not have MAFLD and 939 had MAFLD. In the training set, LASSO regression selected eight nonzero-coefficient variables: age, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, ALT, GGT, TG, and HDL-C (Figure 3A,B). After multivariable logistic regression, age, BMI, ALT, TG, and HDL-C were retained as final predictors for the screening-oriented risk stratification model (all p < 0.001). A nomogram was constructed based on these five routinely available indicators (Figure 3C).

The hypothyroid model showed good discrimination in both the training and validation sets. In the training set (n = 1,851), the AUC was 0.906, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.274, sensitivity of 0.902, and specificity of 0.786 (Figure 3D). In the validation set (n = 794), the AUC was 0.901, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.314, sensitivity of 0.860, and specificity of 0.798 (Figure 3G). Calibration plots showed good agreement between predicted and observed MAFLD risk, with mean absolute errors of 0.021 in the training set and 0.016 in the validation set (Figure 3E, H). Decision curve analysis suggested potential net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities (Figure 3F, I).

Screening-oriented risk stratification model in adults with hyperthyroidism

Among 688 individuals with hyperthyroidism, 551 did not have MAFLD and 137 had MAFLD. In the training set, LASSO regression identified seven candidate predictors: age, hypertension, BMI, FT4, ALT, TG, and HDL-C (Figure 4A,B). Multivariable logistic regression retained age, BMI, FT4, ALT, and HDL-C as final predictors (all p < 0.05). A corresponding nomogram was developed using these routinely available indicators (Figure 4C).

The hyperthyroid model also showed good discrimination. In the training set (n = 481), the AUC was 0.918, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.119, sensitivity of 0.904, and specificity of 0.793 (Figure 4D). In the validation set (n = 207), the AUC was 0.932, with an optimal cutoff value of 0.254, sensitivity of 0.884, and specificity of 0.902 (Figure 4G). Calibration plots showed good consistency between predicted and observed MAFLD risk, with mean absolute errors of 0.023 in the training set and 0.025 in the validation set (Figure 4E,H). Decision curve analysis suggested potential clinical utility across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Figure 4F,I).

Discussion

This large health examination–based study showed that thyroid dysfunction was associated with distinct patterns of screening-defined MAFLD. Among 46,311 adults undergoing routine health examinations, MAFLD was slightly more prevalent in individuals with hypothyroidism and less prevalent in those with hyperthyroidism than in euthyroid participants. Subtype-specific analyses showed associations for overt hypothyroidism, overt hyperthyroidism, and subclinical hyperthyroidism, whereas subclinical hypothyroidism showed no significant association in univariate analysis. Among routinely measured thyroid indices, FT3 was positively associated with MAFLD and FT4 was inversely associated with MAFLD after multivariable adjustment, while TSH showed no independent association in fully adjusted models. Based on routine health examination indicators, we developed internally validated risk stratification models for adults with hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, both showing good discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility.

These findings support the value of integrating fatty liver risk assessment into routine health management practice. Fatty liver disease is often first detected during routine health examinations rather than through liver-related symptoms. The AGA clinical care pathway emphasizes risk stratification for clinically significant fatty liver disease in routine care settings, and the AACE guideline highlights the role of primary care and endocrinology settings in identifying fatty liver disease among individuals with metabolic risk profiles [18–20]. Fatty liver disease is also increasingly regarded as a multisystem condition requiring integrated metabolic and cardiovascular management rather than isolated liver-focused care [21]. In health examination settings, thyroid function testing, abdominal ultrasonography, anthropometric assessment, liver enzymes, lipid profiles, glucose measurements, and blood pressure are often obtained during the same visit. This creates a practical opportunity to incorporate liver-metabolic risk assessment into post-examination follow-up, particularly in adults with thyroid dysfunction, a readily identifiable subgroup.

The thyroid-related findings are broadly consistent with previous evidence but also highlight the importance of thyroid dysfunction subtype and metabolic confounding. In a large German database study, hypothyroidism was associated with higher incident NAFLD risk, whereas hyperthyroidism was associated with lower risk [22]. A dose-response meta-analysis suggested that FT3 and FT4 may have opposite associations with NAFLD risk, while a previous systematic review reported an association between hypothyroidism and NAFLD with substantial between-study heterogeneity [23,24]. These observations are compatible with our finding that FT3 was positively associated with MAFLD, whereas FT4 was inversely associated. However, the lack of association for subclinical hypothyroidism suggests that mild thyroid dysfunction may have a weaker or more confounded relationship with fatty liver disease than overt thyroid dysfunction. Therefore, thyroid-related associations should be interpreted within a broader cardiometabolic context, including adiposity, dyslipidemia, glucose metabolism, and comorbid disease.

The opposite associations of FT3 and FT4 with MAFLD should be interpreted as clinical risk signals rather than evidence of causality. Thyroid and liver metabolism are closely interconnected, and thyroid hormone signaling has direct effects on hepatic lipid handling and metabolic homeostasis [25,26]. Experimental evidence has suggested that TSH may promote hepatic triglyceride accumulation through SREBP-1c-related pathways, although TSH was not independently associated with MAFLD after full adjustment in our study [27]. Circulating FT3 may partly reflect adaptive metabolic changes related to adiposity, insulin resistance, or altered peripheral deiodination rather than a direct causal pathway. Therefore, thyroid indices may be more useful as components of routine metabolic risk assessment than as isolated causal markers.

The risk stratification models developed in this study may provide practical tools for post-examination follow-up in adults with thyroid dysfunction. A key strength of this approach is that it uses indicators routinely available in health examination settings, without requiring additional specialized testing. Existing non-invasive tools and models for fatty liver disease, including ultrasound-derived fat fraction, recent health examination–based nomogram models, the ZJU index, and the Fatty Liver Index, also rely on accessible imaging, anthropometric, or biochemical indicators to estimate fatty liver risk in general or Chinese populations [28–31]. Our models differ by focusing specifically on adults with hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism and by using routinely available indicators from health examination practice. The high AUC values suggest good internal performance, but these models should not be interpreted as replacements for formal MAFLD assessment because several predictors overlap with the metabolic criteria used to define MAFLD. Rather, they may help prioritize individuals who require lifestyle counseling, repeat liver-metabolic assessment, or further clinical evaluation.

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional and single-center design limits causal inference and external generalizability. Second, several potentially relevant covariates were not systematically available, including lifestyle-related factors such as dietary intake, physical activity, and smoking, as well as thyroid autoantibodies and information on the duration or longitudinal course of thyroid dysfunction. Residual confounding and incomplete characterization of thyroid disease therefore cannot be excluded. Third, the complete-case approach resulted in the exclusion of 13,785 individuals with one or more missing required data elements, and variable-specific missingness patterns could not be reliably reconstructed; selection bias related to missing-data exclusion is therefore possible. Fourth, MAFLD was defined using ultrasonography and routine metabolic indicators, which are practical for health examination settings but less precise than MRI-based, elastography-based, or histological assessment [32,33]. Complete alcohol-intake data were also unavailable, precluding full classification under the current SLD/MASLD framework [34]. In addition, several predictors overlapped with the metabolic criteria used to define MAFLD, which may have contributed to the high apparent model performance. Accordingly, the models should be interpreted as screening-oriented risk stratification tools rather than diagnostic replacements. Future studies should validate these models in independent multicenter cohorts and evaluate reduced models excluding overlapping diagnostic components.

Conclusion

Routine health examination indicators enabled effective fatty liver risk stratification among adults with thyroid dysfunction. These findings support targeted liver-metabolic follow-up in this readily identifiable screening population, although prospective multicenter validation is needed before broader implementation.
