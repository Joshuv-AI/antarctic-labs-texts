Intrapancreatic accessory spleen mimicking pancreatic malignancy: Diagnostic role of Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell scintigraphy
Authors: Farise Yilmaz, Lutfu Perktas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolid lesions of the pancreatic tail pose a significant diagnostic challenge due to their potential overlap with malignant neoplasms on conventional imaging. Intrapancreatic accessory spleen is a rare benign entity that may closely mimic pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and other malignancies, leading to unnecessary invasive procedures. We present a 40-year-old male with an incidentally detected 2 cm pancreatic tail mass that demonstrated imaging characteristics similar to those of the spleen on contrast-enhanced computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Following a multidisciplinary tumor board decision, Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell scintigraphy with single-photon emission computed tomography was performed, revealing focal radiotracer uptake in the pancreatic tail lesion with intensity and distribution identical to those of the native spleen, confirming the diagnosis of intrapancreatic accessory spleen. This case illustrates the decisive diagnostic value of Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell scintigraphy in differentiating intrapancreatic accessory spleen from pancreatic malignancies, thereby preventing unnecessary surgical intervention.Keywords: Intrapancreatic accessory spleen (IPAS), Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell scintigraphy (HDRBC), Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (pNET), SPECT, Pancreatic tail mass, Nuclear medicine

Case report

Imaging findings

A 40-year-old male patient was incidentally found to have a solid mass measuring approximately 2 cm in the tail of the pancreas on noncontrast abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1A and B). The lesion appeared well-circumscribed with signal intensity characteristics isointense to the splenic parenchyma on all pulse sequences. Given that malignancy could not be excluded on the basis of MRI alone, the patient underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT), which demonstrated attenuation and enhancement characteristics in all contrast phases virtually identical to those of the native spleen (Fig. 1C). Tumor markers including CA 19-9, CEA, and Chromogranin A were within normal limits, and gastrointestinal endoscopy was unremarkable.

Following a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, the imaging constellation raised the possibility of IPAS, and the patient was referred to our nuclear medicine department for functional confirmation. Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell (HDRBC) scintigraphy with single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) acquisition was performed. The study demonstrated focal radiotracer accumulation in the pancreatic tail lesion, with uptake intensity and spatial distribution identical to those of the native spleen on transaxial, sagittal, and coronal projections (Fig. 1D-F). No additional ectopic splenic foci were identified.

Management

Given the definitive functional confirmation of intrapancreatic accessory spleen (IPAS) by Tc-99m HDRBC SPECT scintigraphy, surgical intervention was deemed unnecessary. The patient was managed conservatively with clinical and imaging follow-up. The planned extensive surgical resection—which carried significant perioperative morbidity—was avoided entirely through this non-invasive diagnostic approach.

Follow-up

The patient remained asymptomatic throughout the follow-up period. No interval change in the size or characteristics of the pancreatic tail lesion was observed on subsequent imaging, which was consistent with the benign and static nature of IPAS. No surgical or invasive intervention was required.

Discussion

IPAS represents a distinct subset of accessory spleens characterized by the ectopic embedding of functional splenic tissue within the pancreatic parenchyma. Embryologically, accessory spleens arise from incomplete fusion of mesenchymal cells originating in the dorsal mesogastrium [1]. While the splenic hilum is the most commonly reported location, the pancreatic tail is the second most frequent site owing to the close embryological proximity of these structures [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Vikse et al. [2] encompassing 22,487 patients reported an overall pooled accessory spleen prevalence of approximately 14.5% [2]. The reported incidence of IPAS specifically is substantially lower, estimated at approximately 0.13% [3].

Epidemiologically, accessory spleens, including IPAS, have been reported to show a female predominance in some series, with a predilection for patients in their second to fourth decades of life [4]. The present patient—a 40-year-old male—falls within the reported age range but represents the less common gender distribution, underscoring the importance of maintaining IPAS in the differential diagnosis regardless of sex.

On CT imaging, IPAS characteristically appears as a well-defined, round or oval lesion smaller than 2 cm situated in the pancreatic tail, displaying attenuation and contrast enhancement kinetics that precisely mirror those of the spleen across all phases [5]. Mortelé et al. [5] comprehensively described these CT features and emphasized that recognition of splenic enhancement kinetics—including the early heterogeneous and delayed homogeneous enhancement pattern—is essential for accurate diagnosis [5]. In the present case, the lesion demonstrated attenuation and enhancement characteristics nearly identical to those of the native spleen, which constituted the critical imaging clue leading to the suspicion of IPAS.

Despite these characteristic features, IPAS has been repeatedly documented as a source of diagnostic error, most frequently being misidentified as a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. This misdiagnosis carries serious clinical consequences, as pNETs prompt aggressive surgical management including distal pancreatectomy. Arkadopoulos et al. [6] reviewed multiple IPAS cases and underscored the recurrent pattern of diagnostic misinterpretation, advocating for routine inclusion of IPAS in the differential diagnosis of pancreatic tail masses prior to any invasive decision [6]. Ding et al. [7] further demonstrated, through a CT and MRI evaluation of IPAS cases, that although cross-sectional imaging provides important diagnostic clues, functional confirmation is frequently indispensable for achieving a definitive diagnosis [7]. Distal pancreatectomy itself carries substantial risks of pancreatic fistula, postoperative hemorrhage, splenic infarction, new-onset diabetes mellitus, and prolonged hospitalization—morbidity that is entirely avoidable when IPAS is correctly identified preoperatively [8]. Several imaging features assist in distinguishing IPAS from pNET when scintigraphy is unavailable: unlike pNETs, which typically demonstrate intense early arterial enhancement with subsequent washout, restricted diffusion, and occasional cystic degeneration or calcification, IPAS shows enhancement and diffusion characteristics that closely parallel those of the native spleen throughout all phases. Kang et al. [9] recently showed that quantitative MRI R2* mapping, expressed as a mass-to-spleen ratio, can further improve this distinction when conventional sequences are equivocal [9].

MRI contributes additional diagnostic information through its multiparametric characterization of tissue. Kim et al. [10] reported that IPAS demonstrates homogeneous signal intensity isointense to the spleen on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, with an identical contrast enhancement pattern, and established a strong correlation between these MRI characteristics and Tc-99m HDRBC scintigraphic findings [10]. The present case was concordant with these described MRI features, further reinforcing the clinical suspicion for IPAS prior to nuclear medicine referral.

Tc-99m HDRBC scintigraphy exploits the physiological mechanism by which thermally denatured erythrocytes are selectively sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system of the spleen. Approximately 90% of injected labeled cells accumulate within splenic tissue, conferring exceptional specificity for the identification of functional splenic parenchyma irrespective of its anatomical location [10]. SPECT acquisition substantially enhances spatial resolution and anatomical correlation compared with planar scintigraphy, enabling precise localization of ectopic splenic foci to specific pancreatic segments. In the present case, SPECT demonstrated focal uptake in the pancreatic tail lesion that was indistinguishable in intensity and distribution from the native spleen, providing definitive noninvasive confirmation of IPAS and obviating the need for surgical exploration. In summary, the key imaging finding that secured the diagnosis was focal Tc-99m HDRBC uptake within the pancreatic tail lesion that was visually indistinguishable from that of the native spleen, a characteristic pattern of functioning splenic tissue that is not expected in pancreatic neoplasms.

Teaching point

IPAS should be routinely considered in the differential diagnosis of incidentally detected solid pancreatic tail lesions that demonstrate splenic enhancement kinetics on cross-sectional imaging; Tc-99m HDRBC SPECT scintigraphy is a highly specific and noninvasive modality that can provide definitive confirmation of IPAS and prevent unnecessary surgical intervention.
