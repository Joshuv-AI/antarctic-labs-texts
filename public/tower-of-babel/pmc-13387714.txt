Introduction

The human brain undergoes complex structural changes with aging, including atrophy and ventricular enlargement.1,2 Because biological aging varies across individuals, brain aging may deviate from chronological age. The brain age gap (BAG) quantifies this deviation3 (Figure S1) and provides a clinically meaningful summary of cumulative neuroprotective and neurotoxic processes.4 Accurate BAG estimation potentially enables early risk stratification for neurodegenerative diseases such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia,5–7 prognostication and monitoring of disease progression through longitudinal changes in brain aging, quantitative evaluation of treatment effects by detecting slowed or reversed brain aging, and enrichment and outcome assessment in clinical trials by identifying individuals with accelerated brain aging.4,8

BAG estimators have commonly utilized convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to extract spatial features from brain MRIs.5,9,10 While effective at capturing global structural patterns, CNNs may miss subtle, localized changes relevant to brain aging. Transformer-based models have therefore been introduced to better capture relationships across brain regions.3,6,7 However, limited generalizability across demographics and imaging protocols remains a challenge. In traditional transformers, computational cost increases with the square of the input dimensionality.11 For high-resolution 3D MRI volumes, this results in substantial memory and processing demands, which limit its scalability.

To mitigate these challenges, we introduce OpenMAP-BrainAge. Leveraging the improved generalization of pretrained foundation models,12,13 we adopt a transformer-based foundation model,13 pretrained on 52 robotic proprioception and vision datasets spanning diverse environments and hardware configurations, which helps mitigate bias arising from variability in MRI acquisition. To balance local detail and global context while controlling model complexity, we employ pseudo-3D inputs sampled from sagittal, coronal, and axial views.3,7 We further incorporate volumetric features from 280 brain structures,14 whose changes are associated with aging.7 These multiview and regional features are integrated through a modified self-attention mechanism inspired by the stem-and-trunk design.13 The model was trained and tested on the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative 2 & 3 (ADNI 2&3)15 and Open Access Series of Imaging Studies 3 (OASIS 3)16 datasets, and externally tested for cross-domain robustness using the Australian Imaging Biomarkers and Lifestyle Study of Ageing (AIBL) dataset.17

Materials and methods

OpenMAP-BrainAge

OpenMAP-BrainAge is a transformer-based framework for estimating brain age from MRI-derived inputs (code is available at https://github.com/OishiLab/OpenMAP-BrainAge). The model takes as input (1) pseudo 3D T1-weighted brain MRI representations constructed from 30 central sagittal, coronal, and axial slices, and (2) quantitative 280 volumetric features extracted from whole-brain parcellation using OpenMAP-T1.14 It outputs a single continuous brain age prediction per subject (Figure 1A). The architecture integrates multiview image and volumetric information using attention mechanisms (Appendix A) and consists of encoders, stems, a trunk, and task-specific heads. MRI inputs are processed with a 3D ResNet1818 pretrained on the Kinetics-700,19 while volumetric features are embedded using a 2-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP). The stem module13 (Figure 1B) compresses tokenized image features into fixed-size representations using learnable queries, reducing computational cost (Appendix B). A transformer trunk pretrained on 52 robotic datasets13 integrates multiview and regional features via self-attention,11 and a regression MLP head predicts biological age. This design allows flexible adaptation to multiple data types while maintaining efficiency through shared modules and pretrained initialization.

Architecture design of OpenMAP-BrainAge. (A) OpenMAP-BrainAge. The model takes pseudo 3D T1-weighted brain MRI representations constructed from central 30 slices in each of 3 anatomical views (image data) and quantitative 280 volumetric features extracted from whole-brain parcellation using OpenMAP-T114 (numerical data). A shared image encoder (*) extracts spatial features from each view, which are compressed by view-specific stem modules. A transformer trunk integrates embeddings across views and volumetric information, and the head predicts biological age. FC denotes a fully connected layer; MLP denotes multilayer perceptron. (B) Stem architecture. Stem module introduces a fixed number m of learnable queries (much fewer than the number n of tokens from the image input), as following Wang et al.13 Then, we capture the relationship between these learnable queries and the keys from the input through cross-attention to generate embeddings for the input. This design reduces the image input to a fixed dimensional representation and lowers computational complexity from quadratic to linear with respect to the size of input.

Dataset

We trained and validated our model using cognitively normal (CN) participants from the ADNI 2& 315 and the OASIS 3,16 aged 42 to 95. The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). CN participants were assumed to have aligned biological and chronological ages and were randomly divided into 8: 1: 1 for train, validation, and test purposes (4631 scans from 1173 subjects, 584 scans from 145 subjects, and 543 scans from 146 subjects in each), with scans from a subject confined to one split. Scans acquired from a subject at different time points were treated as independent samples. Subjects who exhibited changes in cognitive status across time were excluded. For clinical correlation analyses, we additionally included 1831 scans from 512 subjects for dementia and 3934 scans from 761 subjects for MCI, defined by Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR; CN = CDR 0, MCI = CDR 0.5, and dementia CDR 1 and above. Overt and severe dementia (CDR 2∼3) was also included.15,16) All data were collected with institutional review board approval and informed consent; the de-identified dataset obtained from ADNI and OASIS were considered exempt from further review. Dataset details are in Table S1.

3D-acquired T1-weighted MRI scans were processed using OpenMAP-T1,14 including skull-stripping, rigid alignment to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and downsampling to 2 × 2 × 2 mm resolution. Pseudo-3D inputs were generated by cropping 128 × 128 × 30 voxel volumes from each anatomical view, followed by min-max intensity normalization. Data augmentation during training included random rotations (±20°) and random transformations (±20 pixels), each applied with 50% probability.

Model robustness and generalizability were evaluated using the CN group from the AIBL dataset,17 an independent Australian cohort containing 648 scans from 275 subjects (60 to 92 years old) with similar scanning protocols and preprocessing steps as the ADNI dataset. Data was collected by the AIBL study group. AIBL study methodology has been reported previously.17

Model training and evaluation

Brain age prediction was formulated as a regression task. The model was trained to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted brain age and chronological age. Additional training details are provided in Appendix C. For clinical analyses, the brain age gap (BAG) was defined as the difference between predicted brain age and chronological age. Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using mean absolute error (MAE).

To compare our model with previous published work, we retrained the following state-of-the-art neural networks under our setup: (i) MLP: a 2-layer fully connected network with ReLU activation to run the regression task. (ii) 3D ResNet1818,20: pretrained on the Kinetic-700 video dataset19 for classification. We changed the final fully connected layer to fit the brain age prediction task and fine-tuned all layers with our training data. (iii) Vision Transformer (ViT)21: we selected the ViT-Base model (12 layers, 768 hidden size, and 12 attention heads) pretrained through BERT self-supervised image pretraining (BEiT)12 on the ImageNet-1K dataset.22 The 2D convolutional patch embedding layer was replaced with a 3D version to handle MRI volumes. We fine-tuned all parameters over our training setup. (iv) Simple Fully Convolutional Network (SFCN)10: contained 7 convolutional layers, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling. We re-initialized the model with Kaiming uniform initialization23 and trained it over our training dataset. (v) Global–Local Transformer3: contained VGG8 as the image feature backbone to extract information from both the global and local pathways and an attention layer to merge the information. The Global–Local Transformer took 2D axial slices as input and averaged across these axial slices for the 3D scenario.

To interpret model predictions, we computed gradient-based attribution maps for each test sample (Appendix D). The gradients were averaged across the test set to obtain a population-level importance map. To enable regional comparison, gradient values were normalized by the volume of each brain region. Regions were then ranked based on their normalized contributions, and the top contributing regions were identified for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (SciPy and statsmodels). Unless otherwise specified, tests were 2‑sided with a significance threshold of P < 0.05, and continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD.

Brain age prediction accuracy was assessed in CN participants by quantifying the association between predicted age and chronological age using Pearson’s correlation. Robustness was evaluated by comparing the performance between internal and external datasets under an unpaired 2-sample t-test. Computational cost is reported in GFlops, as a standardized measure of the number of floating‑point operations required by each model when evaluated on the same input, independent of hardware or implementation-specific optimizations.

Model design choices were evaluated via ablation studies that compared single‑view versus 3‑view inputs and the inclusion of volumetric inputs, evaluating their impact on accuracy and robustness across internal and external sets.

Differences in BAG across brain disorder groups (CN, MCI, and dementia) were summarized using group‑wise descriptive statistics (distribution center and 95% confidence intervals) for the top‑performing models. To quantify separability, we performed pairwise receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses and reported the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals. We also reported standardized effect sizes for BAG between groups to complement ROC metrics with Bonferroni adjustment.

Associations between BAG and cognitive performance were evaluated using Pearson correlations with Mini‑Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores. We additionally computed partial correlations controlling for chronological age, sex, and years of education. For cognitive analyses, we used the assessment closest in time to the MRI within a prespecified window of 2 months.

Performance on external cohort

In contrast, other models showed larger MAE increases (by 0.14 to 0.61 years) and correlation drops, demonstrating the superior robustness of our approach to domain shift.

Ablation analysis

Ablation results (Table S2) showed that multiview inputs reduced MAE from 3.72 to 3.51 years. Adding volumetric information resulted in a slight increase in MAE on the ADNI 2&3 and OASIS 3 test set (from 3.51 to 3.65 years). However, volumetric features notably improved robustness under domain shift. On the external dataset (AIBL), the “three-view” model exhibited a performance drop from an MAE of 3.51 years to 3.70 years, whereas the “three-view + volumetrics” model maintained consistent performance (MAE = 3.54 years externally vs. 3.65 years in-house).

Compared with 3D ResNet18 and 3D ViT (Figure 5), our model demonstrated greater separation between CN and MCI groups, with mean BAG values increasing from 0.15 to 2.55 years, compared with smaller shifts observed for the 3D ResNet18 (−0.89 to 1.46 years) and the 3D ViT (−0.08 to 2.06 years). For the comparison between CN and dementia, our model showed a mean BAG increase from 0.15 to 6.12 years. This magnitude of separation was comparable to that observed with the 3D ResNet18 (−0.89 to 5.83 years) and the 3D ViT (−0.08 to 5.94 years). The ROC and Cohen’s d standardized measure of effective size is reported in Table S3 and the associated Bonferroni-adjusted DeLong P-value is reported in Table S4. In ROC analyses, our model achieved the highest performance for CN vs MCI (AUC = 0.642, Cohen’s d = 0.510), slightly exceeding both 3D ResNet18 (AUC = 0.639, d = 0.441) and 3D ViT (AUC = 0.628, d = 0.405). However, for CN vs dementia and MCI vs dementia, performance was comparable to 3D ViT but slightly lower than 3D ResNet18. Statistically significant differences were observed only between our model and 3D ResNet18 in the dementia-related comparisons, while differences with 3D ViT were not significant.

Gradient-based feature attribution

Gradient-based analysis (Appendix D) identified white matter and deep gray matter structures as dominant contributors to age prediction, consistent with prior studies.1,2 The anterior and posterior limbs of the internal capsule (ALIC and PLIC) were among the most influential regions (Figure S4) and showed significant age-related volume decline (Figure S5) with ALIC exhibiting r = −0.21 (P < 0.001) and PLIC with r = −0.27 (P < 0.001). Discussion

Accuracy and generalizability

Our stem-and-trunk transformer architecture effectively integrates multiview MRI and volumetric inputs, enabling more efficient cross-view information synthesis than traditional CNN-based feature concatenation. Unlike CNNs, which restrict cross-view interactions through shared kernels, self-attention allows explicit modeling of relationships across anatomical views. Although some 3D transformer models achieve slightly lower MAE under specific conditions, they are less reliable in older age groups and substantially more computationally expensive. In contrast, heterogeneous pre-training on robotic proprioception and vision datasets improved robustness to acquisition variability, resulting in stable performance across cohorts, including the external AIBL dataset.

Ablation analyses indicate that multiview inputs are sufficient for strong in-house accuracy, whereas adding volumetric features improves cross-cohort generalizability. This balance between accuracy and stability is particularly advantageous for real-world clinical deployment, where demographic and protocol heterogeneity are unavoidable.

Categorical interpretability

We observed a monotonic increase in BAG with diagnostic severity, progressing from cognitively normal participants to MCI and dementia. This pattern is consistent with prior studies3,5,7 and supports the clinical relevance of BAG as a marker of neurodegenerative burden. Importantly, previous work has emphasized that accuracy in cognitively normal populations alone is insufficient, as models must also differentiate disease-related deviations in brain aging.5 Our model demonstrated competitive separation between CN, MCI, and dementia groups, comparable to or exceeding prior reports,7,9 indicating sensitivity to pathological aging beyond normative variation. However, despite this, the model showed slightly lower AUC than 3D ResNet18 in dementia-related classifications. While BAG captures population-level shifts, AUC evaluates individual rankings. As such, our model appears more sensitive to continuous disease-related trends rather than optimized for binary classification.

Functional interpretability

BAG also showed significant negative correlations with MMSE and MoCA scores, reflecting worsening cognitive performance with accelerated brain aging. These associations have been infrequently examined in prior work,26 and the stronger correlations observed with our model suggest improved sensitivity to clinically meaningful cognitive differences. This improved sensitivity may result from the model’s ability to integrate multiview structural information with regional volumetric features, enabling it to capture aging-related changes closely linked to cognitive function.

Biological interpretability

The gradient map from our model predominantly highlighted the white matter, caudate, and thalami, consistent with prior cross-sectional and longitudinal studies.2,24,25,27 In particular, the ALIC and PLIC were listed among the most critical regions for age prediction, congruent with a prior study,1 which reported a strong linear decline in ALIC and PLIC volumes with age. The ALIC contains the frontopontine fibers involved in motor planning and coordination,28 the thalamocortical fibers that contribute to cognitive and executive functions,29,30 and corticostriatal and caudatoputaminal fibers involved in the reward system and motor modulation.31 The PLIC contains the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts essential for motor functions,28 and the superior thalamic, optic, and auditory radiations responsible for transmitting sensory information.32 Research shows a significant linear negative correlation between age and the volume of these fiber tracts.1,33 The decline in the volume and integrity of the ALIC and PLIC is primarily linked to degeneration in myelinated fibers due to aging.34 Our model captured these biological changes, offering potential utility for early pathological assessments.

Limitations and future work

Multiple scans per subject were treated as independent samples during evaluation, which may slightly overestimate model performance. Although some prior studies3,7 report lower MSE when trained on large multi-cohort datasets, differences in cohort composition, age distribution, and evaluation protocols limit direct comparison. Within specific age ranges, performance across studies is often comparable, suggesting overall metrics may obscure age-dependent biases.

Our current work is a cross-sectional study. Future longitudinal studies tracking individual brain age trajectories will establish the mechanistic relationship between BAG and dementia. We observed a decelerated trend in BAG among older individuals in the dementia group, potentially due to a survival effect or to the under-representation of subjects aged 85+ in the training dataset. Since the model learns age-related patterns only within the training age range (≤ 95 years), extrapolation beyond this range is limited. Consequently, BAG values may be artificially constrained at higher ages, reducing sensitivity to age-related deviations in older cohorts.

Our focus on older adults and Alzheimer’s disease-related phenotypes enhances relevance to age-related neurodegeneration but limits generalizability to other neurological or psychiatric conditions. While BAG correlates with cognitive performance, it may not optimally discriminate between adjacent stages such as CN and early MCI. Future studies should evaluate broader populations and age ranges to assess external validity and age-by-disease interactions.

Although we demonstrated generalization across independent cohorts, ADNI and AIBL use relatively harmonized MRI protocols. Robustness to more heterogeneous scanners, acquisition parameters, and real-world clinical workflows remains to be established. Additional validation across diverse imaging environments, potentially incorporating harmonization and domain adaptation strategies, will be important for clinical applications.

BAG may also be influenced by lifestyle, vascular, and socioeconomic factors.4,26 While analyses adjusted for age, sex, and education, broader covariates were not comprehensively modeled, which may limit causal interpretation. Future studies incorporating further covariate measurements, including vascular risk, physical activity, genetic factors, and longitudinal outcomes, may clarify the biological pathways reflected by BAG.

Importantly, BAG is not intended as a diagnostic biomarker for Alzheimer’s disease or other specific neurodegenerative etiologies. Established approaches, including volumetric MRI analysis, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, and amyloid or tau PET imaging,35 remain superior for molecular diagnosis. Instead, BAG should be interpreted as a quantitative marker of accelerated brain aging and neurobiological burden. Its clinical value lies in characterizing the downstream structural impact of dementia and potentially serving as a non-invasive adjunct for risk stratification, disease monitoring, and evaluation of physiological aging changes caused by Alzheimer’s disease.

Conclusion

We introduced the interpretable OpenMAP-BrainAge, a model that effectively combines different views and volumetric data using a stem-and-trunk architecture. Our model achieved comparable state-of-the-art accuracy and improved generalizability. It also reduced computational burden for better scalability with 3D MRI data. We found a positive association between BAG and cognitive decline including MCI and dementia. The BAG was also related to cognitive performance measures. Future work with longitudinal data and larger cohorts may enhance insights into brain aging and aid in quantification of physiological changes associated with Alzheimer’s dementia.
