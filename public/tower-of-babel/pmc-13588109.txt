Machine Learning-Based Model to Predict Survival in Resectable Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Guoteng Qiu, Shizheng Mi, Jiali Chen, Haichuan Wang, Le Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMany studies attempted to precisely predict survival or recurrence of patients with resectable intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma using perioperative indicators. However, machine learning has been infrequently applied to predict postoperative survival in patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). We therefore compared the performance of three prognostic models: the Cox proportional hazards model, random survival forest, and DeepSurv.MethodsLeast absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was used for variable selection, and 13 variables were finally selected for model training. Model discrimination was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC). Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the integrated Brier score (IBS).ResultsA total of 394 consecutive patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma who underwent curative resection between January 2010 and December 2016 were retrospectively enrolled. In the validation set, the Harrell’s C-index was 0.680 for CoxPH, 0.700 for random survival forest, and 0.680 for DeepSurv. The random survival forest achieved the highest mean time-dependent AUC (0.738) and the lowest IBS (0.147), followed by DeepSurv (AUC: 0.723; IBS: 0.235) and CoxPH (AUC: 0.713; IBS: 0.148). The final model was visualized on a web-based platform (https://shizhengmi.shinyapps.io/exam/).ConclusionThe random survival forest demonstrated favorable discrimination and calibration, highlighting the potential value of ensemble learning in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The top five predictors in the final model were CA19-9, albumin, tumor differentiation, CEA, and tumor number.

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), the second most prevalent subtype of primary liver cancer, has a poor prognosis and imposes a significant burden on the health system. Asia has a higher incidence of liver cancer than other regions, necessitating greater attention.1 To date, hepatic resection remains the first-line therapy for ICC, but it does not yield a satisfactory prognosis. One factor that hinders the effectiveness of radical surgery is postoperative relapse, with a recurrence rate as high as 50% within 12–24 months after surgery.2 Once patients experience early recurrence (12–24 months), the five-year overall survival drops drastically to approximately 10%.3,4 Cholangiocarcinoma is often characterized by substantial genetic, epigenetic, and tumor microenvironment heterogeneity, all of which contribute to tumor development, progression, recurrence, and therapeutic response.5 Consequently, the prognosis of patients with cholangiocarcinoma is highly heterogeneous, indicating the need for accurate prognostic prediction and risk stratification.

Recent studies have identified numerous molecular biomarkers for prognosis in hepatobiliary cancers, including microRNAs, epigenetic alterations, and protein biomarkers.6,7 While preoperative lymph node status and Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade are among the most important prognostic factors for ICC.8 In addition, the risk of postoperative recurrence is increased by macro/microvascular invasion, R1 resection, and multiple lesions.2 These prognostic risk factors have facilitated the development of several prognostic models. In 2018, Liang et al developed a “radiomics score” using logistic regression.9 A nomogram integrating the radiomics score with TNM stage was developed for early postoperative recurrence and demonstrated robust generalizability. The model achieved an AUC of 0.86 in the external validation cohort. Nevertheless, it did not incorporate serological or pathological information, and the use of binary labels rather than time-to-event data may have compromised the robustness of the analysis. Subsequently, a stepwise Cox model with additional indicators was proposed, and its metrics were superior to those of the AJCC staging system in the external validation.10 However, the model demonstrated modest predictive performance, with a C-index of 0.69 and 0.67 for the internal and external validation cohorts, respectively. In this scenario, the machine-learning algorithm may improve predictive performance while retaining as many features as possible.

Over the past five years, the application of machine learning in prognostic modeling for cholangiocarcinoma has increased substantially.8,11–13 This does not imply, however, that machine learning algorithms are inherently better than Cox regression, since other studies have also fitted outstanding models using Cox regression.14,15 There are two prerequisites for Cox regression: First, the hazard increases proportionally over time; Second, there must be an approximately linear relationship between the predictors and the log hazard. Once nonlinear correlations exist or too many features are included, Cox regression may not yield optimal metrics, which is why alternative algorithms are needed. Random survival forest (RSF) is an ensemble tree algorithm that grows trees by selecting candidate variables at each split to maximize the survival difference between daughter nodes by the Log rank test.16 This algorithm exhibits variable screening and bootstrap resampling, which promotes the model’s accuracy without limiting the number of input features. Moreover, researchers attempted to integrate deep learning with survival analysis and proposed the DeepSurv algorithm, a model that combines Deep Neural Network (DNN) with Cox regression.17 Theoretically, DeepSurv can capture complex nonlinear relationships through a deep neural network architecture. Thus, we postulated that a more precise prognostic model for ICC could be developed using the aforementioned machine-learning approach.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Follow-Up

Patients with pathologically confirmed ICC who underwent curative resection in the hepato-biliary and pancreatic department of West China Hospital, SCU from January 2010 to December 2016 were consecutively enrolled. Variables include demographics (sex, age, ascites, liver cirrhosis), preoperative blood tests (platelet count (PLT), total bilirubin (TB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transferase(GGT), albumin (ALB), prothrombin time (PT)), tumor markers including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), α-fetoprotein (AFP), carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9), and pathology (tumor size, tumor number, macrovascular invasion (MaVI), microvascular invasion (MVI), tumor differentiation, capsule invasion, margin status and T stage). Because most patients did not undergo lymphadenectomy, we are unable to provide detailed information on lymph node status.

All patients received standard follow-up at one and three months after hospital discharge, including routine blood examinations, liver and kidney function, tumor biomarkers, and abdominal CT or MRI. These evaluations were conducted every three months during the first two years and every six months thereafter until the last follow-up in January 2019.

The primary outcome of this study is Overall Survival (OS), a right-censored, time-to-event data including both survival time and survival status. Death during the follow-up was considered an event. Patients who were lost to follow-up or remained alive at the end of follow-up were censored.

Ethics Approval and Inclusion Criteria

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [Grant No. 2024(74)]. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective cohort study design. All patient data were de-identified before analysis to protect patient confidentiality.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Patients undergoing intention-to-treat surgery; 2. The diagnosis of ICC was confirmed by histological examination and complied with the 2019 WHO classification;18 3. There were no missing clinical or follow-up data. 4. Curative resections need to be ensured (R0 or R1 resection).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Patients younger than 18 years, 2. Patients without histopathological confirmation of ICC, 3. Patients undergoing non-curative resection (R2) or palliative surgery, 4. Patients with incomplete clinical or follow-up data.

Data Preparation

The raw data contains 10 categorical variables: sex (male/female), ascites (yes/no), liver cirrhosis (yes/no), tumor differentiation (poor/moderate to well), tumor number (solitary/multiple), macrovascular invasion (presence/absence), microvascular invasion (presence/absence), capsule invasion (yes/no), T stage (T1-T2/T3/T4), and margin status (R0/R1) and 13 continuous variables. Given that the upper reporting limit for serum CA19-9 in our center was 1000 U/mL, CA19-9 was dichotomized using a cutoff of 200 U/mL, consistent with a previous study.19

In data processing, continuous covariates were standardized using Z-score normalization before training. The scaling strategies (mean and standard deviation) were estimated from the training set and then applied to the validation set to avoid data leakage. Subsequently, model performance was assessed using the standardized validation cohort.

Technical Flowchart and Hyperparameter Tuning

The technical flowchart of our study is presented below (Figure S1): 1. Preprocessing of the raw data; 2. Randomly splitting the entire cohort into a training set and a validation set, with a ratio of 7:3; 3. Feature selection by LASSO regression; 4. Model construction and hyperparameter tuning; 5. Internal validation and model performance evaluation; 6. Web application of the final model.

The “hyperTuning()” function in the “dnn” package is used to optimize the hyperparameters of the DeepSurv model. A random search strategy was adopted to optimize the hyperparameters, including alpha (search range: 0.5–0.97), lambda (search range: 1.0–10), learning rate (search range: 0.0001–0.001), and the number of hidden nodes. Fifteen randomly sampled hyperparameter combinations (R=15) were evaluated using five-fold cross-validation (K=5) concordance index, and the optimal hyperparameters were selected accordingly.

For the RSF model, the number of trees (ntree) was determined according to the out-of-bag error (OOB error), and the number of variables tried at each split (mtry) was optimized using the learning curve. The remaining parameters (nodesize, nodedepth) were kept at their default settings unless obvious overfitting or underfitting was detected.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts, while continuous data were reported as mean with standard deviation (SD). Continuous data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, according to the data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The RSF and DeepSurv models were developed in R (version 4.3.3) with “randomForestSRC” and “dnn” packages.

Six time points (6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months) were selected to calculate the mean AUC, which, together with the concordance index (C-index) and integrated brier score (IBS, calculated using the “pec” package), was used to identify the optimal model. Differences in C-index were assessed using the “compareC” package, whereas differences in time-dependent AUCs were evaluated using the “Compare()” function provided by the “timeROC” package. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05.

As lambda increased, coefficients were gradually shrunk toward zero, with irrelevant variables excluded from the model (Figure S2a). Figure S2b showed the relationship between log(lambda) and cross-validation partial likelihood deviance. At λ=0.037, the model achieved the minimum cross-validation loss and retained thirteen features. Machine-Learning Models and Hyperparameter Optimization

RSF and DeepSurv models were subsequently developed and assessed. As shown in Figure S3, the OOB error could be considered an independent validation and confirmed that the optimal number of trees was 60 (ntree=60). With the optimal mtry=4 nodedepth=5, nodesize = 20, the RSF model consistently outperformed the Cox model, with higher C-indices in both training and validation cohorts as well as a higher mean AUC (Table 3). Furthermore, we investigated whether the DeepSurv model could achieve better performance than Cox and RSF models. Using four hidden layers (nodes: 20, 19, 20, and 16 nodes), the DeepSurv model (epochs=5000, batch_size=128, learning_rate=7e-03, λ=2.0, α=0.64) showed better predictive performance than the Cox regression, although the improvement was modest (Table 3).

Random Survival Forest Serves as the Final Model

As seen in Figure 1, RSF consistently achieved the highest AUC at each time-point among three models up to 54 months, after which their performance became comparable. The RSF model achieved a higher C-index than the Cox model in the validation cohort, but not reaching a statistical significance (P=0.115). In addition, the RSF model yielded a higher mean time-dependent AUC across the six time points, with a statistical advantage at 6 months (P=0.034, Table S1). The top five most important variables were CA19-9, ALB, Tumor differentiation, CEA and Tumor number (Figure S4). Moreover, the time-dependent AUCs at 1, 3, and 5 years were 0.734, 0.736, and 0.739, respectively (Figure 2). In order to evaluate the consistency between the actual and estimated survival probability, the calibration curves were constructed and shown in Figure 3. The agreement between predicted and observed risks was demonstrated by Apparent line. The robustness of the model remained convincing after bootstrap-resampling 1000 times. (“Bias-corrected” line). The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities. The mean absolute error (MAE) in the training cohort was 0.019, 0.018, and 0.008 at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively (Figure 3a–c). The MAE in the validation cohort was 0.058, 0.055, and 0.054 at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively (Figure 3d–f). Discussion

In this proof-of-hypothesis study, our findings indicate that machine learning algorithms (RSF and DeepSurv) can improve the performance of prognostic models, leading to more precise personalized treatment. When retaining a substantial number of features, the machine-learning models achieve outstanding discrimination, which is not attainable with Cox regression. The RSF model consistently achieved higher time-dependent AUCs than the Cox model throughout the first 54 months of follow-up, with statistically significant differences observed during the first 6 months (P=0.034). Although the overall multivariable Cox model satisfied the proportional hazards assumption, ascites showed evidence of a time-varying effect (P=0.04). These findings suggest that the RSF model provides more accurate and robust performance than the Cox model in the presence of potential non-proportional hazards or complex relationships among predictors. Ultimately, the RSF model was visualized via a user-friendly and readily accessible web-based application.

Currently, there is an excessive emphasis on early recurrence of ICC,4,9,19,21,22 while studies on the overall survival of ICC are scarce. By defining relapse within two years as early recurrence (ER), the time-to-event data is converted into binary data, facilitating the application of machine learning algorithms and improving the clinical interpretability of the prediction model. The application of decision trees was thereafter extended to time-to-event data, enabling the development of ensemble tree algorithms for survival analysis.8 A study employed a gradient boosting machine (GBM) model to forecast postoperative survival in patients with ICC who underwent liver resection.12 This model significantly outperformed AJCC stage and MEGNA score,23 highlighting the potential of ensemble tree-based methods for survival prediction. More recently, DeepSurv, a deep learning-based survival model, has been applied to predict survival in patients with distal cholangiocarcinoma, achieving remarkable performance.13 In our internal validation cohort, the RSF model achieved a higher C-index than the Cox regression; however, the difference was not significant (P=0.115). Likewise, time-dependent AUCs were generally higher for the RSF model, with a statistically significant advantage observed only at the 6-month time point. Nevertheless, it is crucial to recognize that advanced algorithms do not necessarily outperform conventional methods. A previous study developed a LASSO-Cox regression model for predicting postoperative recurrence in distal cholangiocarcinoma, which achieved excellent discrimination with an AUC exceeding 90%.14 Accordingly, the advantage of the RSF model observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, tumor differentiation and GGT levels differed significantly between the training and validation cohorts, these differences were likely caused by random splitting rather than systematic baseline imbalance. Nevertheless, these differences may have introduced a certain degree of uncertainty into model validation, and external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before broad clinical implementation.

The variable significance plot, shown in Figure S4, provides insight into the relative contributions of individual predictors. Preoperative CA19-9 represents the most crucial determinant of survival in patients with cholangiocarcinoma, as reported by several studies.21,24–27 Therefore, patients with elevated preoperative CA19-9 levels may warrant close surveillance and careful consideration of postoperative adjuvant treatment. Interestingly, liver cirrhosis is considered a significant predictor in the Cox model, whereas its contribution appeared to be smaller in the RSF model. Compared with hepatocellular carcinoma, ICC is less frequently associated with chronic viral hepatitis and liver cirrhosis,27 which may explain the limited contribution of liver cirrhosis to survival in the RSF model. Furthermore, our RSF model identified preoperative CEA levels and tumor differentiation as crucial predictors of prognosis in ICC, consistent with the previous report.28

Several prognostic models for tumor recurrence or overall survival have previously been developed using conventional or advanced algorithms. The MEGNA score is one of the most widely used survival models for ICC.23 It assigns one point for each of the five risk factors, including multifocality, direct invasion of adjacent extrahepatic organs, high tumor grade, lymph node positivity, and age > 60 years. In the external validation, the MEGNA score showed superior discrimination to the AJCC staging system. Unlike the MEGNA score, our RSF model incorporates a broader range of routinely available clinicopathological variables that are more representative of contemporary clinical practice. Age was excluded during the LASSO-based feature selection, while lymph node status was not retained in the final model. The other two multicenter studies based on classification and regression tree (CART) also stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups, with lymph node status included as one of the stratification variables.8,11 Our previous study demonstrated that the rate of lymph node dissection was approximately 50%,29 which is consistent with data from an international multicenter study.8 Lymph node status is not routinely available for many patients, which may limit the clinical utility that rely on this variable. Notably, tumor size and number demonstrated a nonlinear association with prognosis.11 In Cox regression, the above variables require appropriate transformation or flexible modeling, whereas the RSF model can capture complex nonlinear relationships without transformation of predictors. In addition, the Eastern hepatobiliary surgery hospital-intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (EHBH-ICC) grading system has been proposed for Asian patients with ICC.10 However, the model demonstrated modest discriminative ability in both internal and external validation, and formula-based risk calculation may limit clinical applicability. In comparison, our RSF model has been implemented as a user-friendly web-based calculator.

To our knowledge, this work represents the first application of the DeepSurv algorithm in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The DeepSurv model appears to be less suitable for datasets with a small sample size, but it is also superior to Cox regression. The internal mechanism of DeepSurv is more intricate than that of RSF and involves more hyperparameters, making hyperparameter tuning more challenging. Several limitations of this study need to be noted: First, given the retrospective design of this study and lack of external validation, the generalizability of the final model requires further confirmation in future studies. Therefore, the potential advantage of the RSF model in external validation should be interpreted with caution. Second, radiomic features were not included in this study, while their potential prognostic value cannot be ignored. Finally, although a cutoff value of 200 U/mL was adopted based on previous studies, the optimal CA19-9 threshold remains to be established.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated the performance of three prognostic models in predicting long-term survival in patients with ICC. Finally, the RSF model demonstrated the best performance among them and was implemented as a web-based calculator (https://shizhengmi.shinyapps.io/exam/). The five most important predictors were CA19-9, ALB, Tumor differentiation, CEA, and Tumor number. The model has the potential to support personalized risk assessment and guide individualized treatment for patients with resectable ICC.
