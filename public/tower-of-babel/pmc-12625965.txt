Trends and characteristics of hospitalisation and mortality among centenarians in old-age homes and communities: a territory-wide study in Hong Kong, 2012–2021
Authors: Hong-jie Yu, Eric Tsz-Chun Lai, Ruby Yu, Jean Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the trends and characteristics of hospitalisation and mortality among centenarians residing in old-age homes (OAHs) and their community-dwelling counterparts in Hong Kong over the past decade.MethodsThis retrospective analysis used electronic admission records of all centenarians (aged ≥100 years) admitted to public hospitals in Hong Kong between 2012 and 2021. Centenarians were categorised based on living arrangements at initial admission: OAHs or community dwellings. Trends in hospitalisation rates, mortality and length of stay (LOS) were assessed using the Mann-Kendall test. All-cause readmission and mortality risks at 7, 30, 90, 180 and 365-day postdischarge were compared using logistic and Cox regressions with a random hospital-specific effect.ResultsThe study analysed 41 221 episodes involving 8360 centenarians, with 4739 (56.7%) from OAHs. The crude hospitalisation rate decreased from 626 in 2012 to 169 per 1000 population in 2021, while total hospitalisations rose by 69.7% (from 3039 to 5156 episodes). Crude mortality rates fell from 239 to 80 per 1000 population, but total deaths increased by 123% (from 418 to 932 persons). Total LOS increased by 85% (from 22 548 to 41 760 days), although average LOS per episode remained stable. In-hospital mortality rates increased significantly for OAH residents (from 42% to 50%) and community-dwelling centenarians (from 29% to 58%). Centenarians in OAHs exhibited longer stays, more frequent admissions and higher mortality from respiratory diseases, alongside increased long-term readmission and mortality risks at 180 and 365-day postdischarge.ConclusionsThe past decade observed a substantial increase in hospitalisations and mortality among Hong Kong centenarians. Those residing in OAHs were associated with a more complex clinical profile, including longer LOS, more frequent admissions and higher long-term risks of readmission and mortality. These findings underscore the need for specialised care models tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of this growing demographic, particularly in institutional settings.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, Community Health

Introduction

The global ageing population is experiencing a steady increase,1 largely attributed to rising longevity.2 3 According to data from the WHO, life expectancy at birth increased from 66.8 years in 1990 to 73.1 years in 2019 globally.4 Of which, Hong Kong has the highest life expectancy, with figures rising from 79.4 years for men and 85.5 years for women in 2006 to 82.4 years and 88.1 years, respectively, in 2019.5 However, this increase in life expectancy has not correspondingly translated into a proportional enhancement in the length of time of living healthy.6

Our previous study revealed that, in Hong Kong, the growth in disability-free life expectancy at age 65 years from 2007 to 2020 was significantly slower than the increase in total life expectancy.7 Additionally, we reported a trend of decline in activities of daily living8 and a rise in frailty9 among older Chinese adults in Hong Kong throughout the 2000s, with later cohorts of older adults being frailer than earlier cohorts. As a result, there is growing concern about whether medical and long-term care services can cope with future demand following these demographic trends.

Following population ageing, the number of centenarians worldwide, particularly in developed settings, is projected to sustain an increasing trend.10 Nonetheless, there is a notable lack of studies assessing hospitalisation trends in this population. Data from the American National Hospital Discharge databases indicated that in 2010, oldest-old adults (aged ≥85 years) constituted 2% of the total population, but accounted for 10% of the total days of care.11 Similarly, data from Spain revealed that oldest-old adults represented 3.2% of the population, accounting for 10.4% of total hospital discharges between 2012 and 2015.12 These figures underscore the heightened medical needs of this demographic, which intensify with advancing age.12 For example, the number of hospitalisations of American centenarians (aged ≥100 years) increased by 18%, from 24 750 in 2004 to 29 099 in 2008.13 This upward trend is projected to continue, with projections suggesting a 735% increase in the centenarian population by 2050.13 Hong Kong has the most rapidly increasing rate of centenarians, with the proportion of the population projected to reach the age of 100, rising from approximately 3.5% in 2010 to 7.5% in 2019.14 Unfortunately, little is known about the characteristics of hospitalisation among centenarians in Hong Kong.

Another defining feature of centenarian populations is their significant reliance on institutional long-term care, which critically shapes their healthcare access and utilisation.15 16 It was estimated that 26.7%–51.5% of centenarians reside in care facilities worldwide.17,19 In Hong Kong, the long-term care infrastructure is characterised by a severe shortage of capacity, with approximately 75 000 care home beds serving a population of 1.68 million older adults.20 21 Within this system, 43.3% of beds are government-subsidised, located in public care homes or in private homes participating in government purchase schemes.21 Access to these subsidised beds is low-cost and rationed through a standardised dependency assessment administered by the Social Welfare Department.21 Medical support for these residents is fragmented, involving a combination of public hospital community geriatric outreach teams and visiting medical officers from the private sector.22 For older adults living in the community, the public healthcare system provides low-cost services through general outpatient clinics, specialist outpatient clinics in hospitals and Accident and Emergency Departments, which also attend to non-emergency cases. Those receiving Comprehensive Social Security Assistance after means-testing are exempt from fees, while others pay nominal charges.22 This complex and resource-constrained ecosystem positions older adults’ place of residence, whether in an old-age home (OAH) or the community, as a critical socio-medical determinant of their later-life health outcomes and mortality trajectory.23

Trends in mortality among centenarians living in care homes have shown heterogeneity. For example, the number of centenarians in Japan experienced an average annual increase of 6.8% in the proportion of deaths in care homes from 2006 to 2016,18 whereas that in England showed an annual decrease of 0.36% from 2001 to 2020.17 It is important to note that in Hong Kong, home deaths are exceptionally rare among this demographic, largely due to cramped living conditions and limited access to visiting healthcare professionals.24 Consequently, the vast majority (over 90%) of individuals nearing the end of life, including OAH residents, are admitted to hospitals for terminal care, which profoundly influences recorded mortality patterns.24 A prior study using data from the Hong Kong Births and Death Registry indicated a continuous increase in the number of deaths among centenarians from 2001 to 201010; however, changes in the subsequent decade and differences between centenarians residing in OAHs versus those in community settings remain unexplored.

The present study aimed to characterise trends in hospitalisation and mortality among centenarians in Hong Kong from 2012 to 2021 and to assess potential differences between centenarians living in OAHs and those living in communities. Such data would provide evidence to address the question of how well the current healthcare system contributes to the health lifespan of centenarians.

Methods

Study design and data source

Data were extracted from the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), overseen by the Hospital Authority (HA) of Hong Kong. The HA functions as a statutory organisation responsible for the management of 43 public hospitals, 49 specialist clinics and 73 general outpatient clinics across Hong Kong.25 The CDARS is an electronic database that aggregates comprehensive patient information, including demographic data, mortality records, diagnoses, procedures, medication prescriptions and laboratory results, from all public healthcare institutions in the area. This system accounts for approximately 80% of the hospitalisations in Hong Kong. The integrity of the data captured by CDARS has undergone thorough validation and has been employed in numerous epidemiological research endeavours.25

Study population

All centenarians who were admitted to public hospitals administered by HA in Hong Kong from 2012 to 2021 were identified, resulting in 43 528 episodes involving 8595 patients. Among these, day patients with a length of stay (LOS) of less than 1 day and those with an unspecified date of hospital death were excluded, leading to the removal of 2307 episodes involving 235 patients from the analysis. Finally, 41 221 episodes involving 8360 centenarians were analysed.

Measurement

Length of stay

LOS refers to the number of days that a patient remains in the hospital and is commonly used as a metric to assess the efficiency of hospital care units and patient flow.26 In this study, we calculated the average LOSs per episode/patient, as well as the total LOSs across all episodes, from the initial admission since the age of 100 years until the death of each patient, or the end of the follow-up period on 31 December 2021, whichever is earlier. An episode is defined as an admission record associated with the same consultant within the same hospital.27

Readmission and mortality

Index admission was defined as the initial hospitalisation occurring after the age of 100 years. The readmission rate was calculated for any hospitalisation return, regardless of the diagnosis, to any public hospital administered by the HA within 7, 30, 90, 180 or 365 days after their initial discharge. Additionally, all-cause mortality rates within the same intervals were calculated. Furthermore, the survival time from the index admission and the last discharge to the date of death was also computed.

Principal diagnosis and death main cause

Each episode had a principal diagnosis, and each death was linked to a main cause, both of which were coded according to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). The main cause of death was determined based on the list of causes and the corresponding ICD-10 codes (https://platform.who.int/mortality/about/list-of-causes-and-corresponding-icd-10-codes) by the WHO. Principal diagnoses and main causes of death that accounted for over 2% of the total were presented, while the remaining categories were aggregated into an ‘others’ category.

Statistical analysis

Annual numbers and crude rates of hospitalisation and death, along with in-hospital mortality and LOS specific to living arrangements, were calculated. The crude hospitalisation/mortality rates (per 1000 population) were derived by dividing the total number of hospitalisations or deaths by the total centenarian population for each year, providing insights into population health performance.28 The annual population of centenarians was estimated based on the 2011, 2016, and 2021 census reports,29 employing second-degree polynomial curve fitting (see online supplemental table 1). In-hospital mortality rate (expressed as the percentage of death of total admitted patients for each year) was also calculated to indicate hospital performance and patient outcomes. The Mann-Kendall test was used to assess monotonic trends.30

Characteristics of hospitalisation episodes were presented according to living arrangements, including sex (female and male), age groups (100, 101, 102, 103, 104 and 105+ years), admission season (March to May for Spring, June to August for summer, September to November for Autumn and December to February for Winter), public assistance (yes or no), discharge to death (yes or no), single episode status (yes or no, indicating whether the centenarian had only one admission record), LOSs per episode and principal diagnoses. Patient-level characteristics were presented based on living arrangements at the time of initial admission, including age, sex, season of admission, total LOSs, admission counts, survival time since the initial admission and last discharge, and main cause of death. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and proportions, while continuous variables were reported as medians with the first and third quartiles owing to their skewed distributions, including age, LOS, admission frequencies and survival time. Differences in living arrangements were assessed using the χ² test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, the assumption of normality was assessed and found to be violated; consequently, non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon test) were employed as reported.

Additionally, logistic and Cox regression analyses, incorporating a random hospital-specific effect, were conducted to evaluate differences in readmission and mortality risk at postdischarge from the initial admission between centenarians residing in OAHs and communities. To account for potential variations in readmission and mortality risks over time,31 32 we assessed these risks at 7, 30, 90, 180 and 365-day postdischarge following the initial admission referring to prior studies.32 Model 1 represented an unadjusted analysis and model 2 adjusted for age, sex, seasonality (considering excess winter mortality33), publicly assisted status, LOS and primary diagnosis of the initial admission. To enhance the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding patients with only a single admission record and those whose living arrangements had changed during the observation period. Considering the study period covered the outbreak and transmission of the COVID-19 pandemic, we further compare the episodes characteristics between pre-COVID (2012–2019) and COVID (2020–2021) to identify the potential impact of COVID-19 on hospital utilisation among centenarians.

All analyses were conducted using R software (V.4.4.1), and a two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences in living arrangements.

Patient and public involvement

This retrospective analysis is based on the Clinical Management System database. No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct or dissemination of this research.

Results

From 2012 to 2021, the final analysis included 41 221 hospitalisation episodes, with 67.6% originating from OAHs. Among 8360 unique patients, 56.7% (4,739 centenarians) resided in OAHs at their initial admission.

Trends in hospitalisation, mortality and LOS

Trends analysis showed a significant increase in the number of hospitalisation episodes, patients and deaths among centenarians. The total annual number of hospitalisation episodes rose by 69.7%, increasing from 3039 in 2012 to 5156 in 2021 (see figure 1). The number of patients also increased substantially, from 1094 to 1959, representing an approximately 80% increase. The number of deaths increased by 123%, from 417 to 932. Contrarily, the crude rates of hospitalisation and mortality decreased markedly, from 626 to 169 and from 239 to 81 per 1000 population, respectively (see figure 1). Additionally, the total LOSs increased significantly, from 22 548 to 41 760 days; however, the average LOSs per episode (p=0.074) and per patient remained constant (p=0.283, see figure 1). Notably, the overall trend in in-hospital mortality was not significant (p=0.074), whereas stratified analysis showed that the in-hospital mortality increased significantly (online supplemental table 2) for both centenarians residing in OAHs (from 42% to 50%, p=0.049) and communities (from 29% to 58%, p=0.004). Furthermore, a significant decline was observed in the proportion of patients admitted two or more times, decreasing from 53.1% to 46.4% (p=0.007) among community-dwelling centenarians, while this change was not statistically significant for those in OAHs (p=0.283).

Episode-level and patient-level characteristics

As shown in table 1, episodes from OAHs predominantly involved females (86.4% vs 72.8% in the community) and individuals receiving public assistance (84.2% vs 52.9%). Both groups had a median age of 101 years; however, the proportion of patients aged ≥105 years was higher in the OAH group (11.1% vs 7.7%). The three most common principal diagnoses for both groups were Diseases of the Respiratory System (ICD-10 codes J00–J99), Symptoms, Signs and Abnormal Clinical and Laboratory Findings, Not Elsewhere Classified (ICD-10 codes R00-R99) and Diseases of the Circulatory System (ICD-10 codes I00–I99). Respiratory diseases were more prevalent among OAH residents (33.8% vs 22.8%), whereas circulatory diseases were more common among community residents (13.9% vs 9.1%). During the COVID-19 period (online supplemental table 3), centenarian admissions for specific respiratory diseases fell significantly to 25.7% of all admissions, down from 31.6% pre-COVID-19. In contrast, admissions for non-specific diagnoses (R00–R99) rose to 20.5% from 17.7%. Concurrently, the profile of admitted patients shifted, with a higher proportion coming from the community (35.0% vs 31.6%).

Patient-level characteristics were displayed based on the living arrangement at the initial admission (table 2). Centenarians residing in OAHs exhibited longer total LOSs (26 vs 25 days), and higher admission frequencies (four times vs three times), but a shorter survival time from initial admission to death, with a median of 304 days, compared with 346 days for those in the community. Likewise, the survival time from the last discharge to death was shorter in the OAH group, with 65.7% of centenarians in the OAH group being discharged due to death. Respiratory infections, primarily pneumonia, were identified as the leading cause of death in both populations; however, the proportion of deaths attributed to cardiovascular diseases was significantly lower among centenarians in OAHs (9.6%) than among their community-dwelling counterparts (14.9%).

Readmission and mortality risk

Table 3 presents the differences in all-cause readmission and mortality risks between centenarians residing in the community and those residing in OAHs following their initial admission. After adjusting for age, sex, payment source, seasonality, LOS and primary diagnosis at the initial admission, logistic regression analysis revealed no significant difference in readmission risk between the groups in the short term (7-day and 30-day postdischarge). However, the OAH group was associated with a 1.25-fold increase in the risk of readmission within 90 days (95% CI 1.13 to 1.38), a 1.37-fold increase within 180 days (95% CI 1.24 to 1.52) and a 1.55-fold increase within 365 days (95% CI 1.38 to 1.74). Likewise, Cox regression indicated the OAH group was associated with a 13% (95% CI 4% to 23%) higher mortality risk within 180 days and a 17% (95% CI 8% to 27%) higher risk within 365 days compared with the community group, while the mortality risk at 7, 30 and 90-day postdischarge was similar between groups.

Sensitivity analyses excluding single admissions (882 community, 827 OAH) showed higher readmission rates in both groups, confirming long-term (180-day and 365-day) differences in readmission and mortality risks between the groups. The analysis excluding patients who changed living arrangements (911 community, 649 OAH) showed lower readmission rates but maintained significant long-term differences (data not shown).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate trends and characteristics in hospitalisation and mortality among centenarians residing in OAHs and communities, using territory-wide electronic admission records from Hong Kong. Although hospitalisation and mortality rates have significantly declined from 2012 to 2021, there has been a marked increase in the absolute numbers of hospitalisations, readmissions and deaths, driven by the exponential growth of the centenarian population. Our findings also reveal a gradual rise in in-hospital mortality among centenarians in both OAHs and community settings. Moreover, notable disparities were observed in hospitalisation and mortality characteristics based on living arrangements. Specifically, centenarians in OAHs exhibited longer LOSs and more frequent admissions but shorter survival times than their community-dwelling counterparts. Furthermore, the difference in all-cause readmission and mortality risks varied over time. Centenarians residing in OAHs demonstrated higher long-term readmission and mortality risks within long-term (180-day and 365-day) postdischarge; however, the short-term (7-day and 30-day) risk was similar between the groups. Additionally, the substantial proportion of centenarians residing in OAHs also indicates that despite Hong Kong having one of the highest life expectancies at birth, individuals who reach the age of 100 or older are often reliant on others for care. The excess LOSs, frequent readmissions and higher mortality rate of approximately 50% within 1 year following the initial discharge reflect a challenging end-of-life care scenario for this population.

The observed increase in mortality among centenarians is consistent with a prior study in Hong Kong,10 which reported a 136% increase in the number of deaths from 2001 to 2010. Moreover, we similarly identified that pneumonia was the leading cause of death.10 Our study further documented a 70% increase in the annual number of hospitalisations among centenarians from 2012 to 2021, aligned with evidence from Western settings.13 34 These increases might be attributed to several interrelated factors.34 35 As life expectancy continues to rise, an increasing number of individuals are reaching the age of 100 years. According to the 2021 census report, the centenarian population in Hong Kong has increased five times, from 1890 in 2011 to 11 575 in 2021.29 Furthermore, innovations in diagnostics and treatments enable the effective management of complex health issues, shifting admissions to older ages that were previously avoided.36 Additionally, comprehensive preventive healthcare services, including health assessments, counselling and education, provided by Elderly Health Centres, along with highly subsidised medical services in public hospitals,37 have enhanced access to specialised care in hospitals for older adults. Despite a dramatic increase in the numbers of hospitalisation and death, crude rates of them have significantly declined. This can be primarily attributed to the exponential growth of centenarians, suggesting an increased number of older adults surviving longer with frailty.38 Moreover, the gradual rise in in-hospital mortality also imposed considerable pressures on palliative care services.

This study further highlighted the disparities in the characteristics of hospitalisation and mortality between centenarians residing in OAHs and those living in communities. Consistent with previous studies,16 39 40 we found that centenarians residing in OAHs had a shorter survival time from both their index admission and the last discharge to death, as well as longer LOSs and higher admission frequencies than their community-dwelling peers. Moreover, our findings revealed that OAH residents had higher risks of long-term readmission and mortality. This was responsible for the severe multimorbidity and frailty status of OAH residents at admission.16 39 40 Additionally, the care provided in OAHs tends to be more reactive, with any adverse health alter often resulting in hospitalisations, potentially destabilising their overall health status.41 The reasons underlying the similar difference in short-term (7-day and 30-day) readmission and mortality risks remain unclear. Generally, short-term risks are indicative of the quality of care provided during hospitalisation42; however, improvements in in-hospital care may have limited influence on readmission rates for older adults, particularly when contrasted with the critical role of postdischarge care and planning.43 Additionally, frequent OAH-hospital transitions are of less suitability, potentially leading to inefficient allocation of healthcare resources and burdening the healthcare system. Evidence from the American National Inpatient Study, which included 52 618 centenarians, indicated that the mean total hospital charges per centenarian nearly doubled between 2000 and 2009; however, 29.4% of the total cost could potentially be preventable.44 Moreover, frequent readmissions negatively impact the mental and spiritual well-being of older adults, affecting their daily living conditions.45 Each transfer also poses a potential threat to the continuity and safety of care.46 In Hong Kong, the life expectancy for centenarians is approximately 3 years,5 and the median survival time from the initial admission to death in our study is less than 1 year. This raises the question of how palliative care can be improved to extend health lifespans within this brief period.

It is uncertain whether any advance care plans are in place for centenarians, including admission approaches and discharge planning. Engaging residents and their families in discussions about such plans can guide end-of-life care decisions, allowing preferences for palliative care over life-prolonging interventions to be expressed. The promotion of advanced care and discharge plans and palliative care facilities15 in OAHs may improve the quality of dying while reducing costs and minimising unnecessary transitions to acute hospitals. Further studies are necessary to evaluate the extent to which improved health outcomes and reduced costs are associated with preventable transitions to hospitals among centenarians.

We also identified that respiratory diseases were the leading health concerns in both principal diagnosis and main cause of death, particularly in OAHs. This reinforces prior research highlighting the increased risk of pneumonia and respiratory infections in institutional settings due to close living quarters and greater exposure to pathogens.47 The high prevalence of aspiration pneumonia among frail OAH residents may also contribute to its commonality. Conversely, the lower proportion of circulatory diseases among community residents (9.1% vs 13.9%) may reflect better management of cardiovascular health through lifestyle choices and access to preventive care among OAH residents. Additionally, the frequent use of ill-defined diagnosis codes (R00–99) suggests centenarians often present with geriatric syndromes like frailty and multimorbidity rather than clearly defined diseases. Meanwhile, community-dwelling centenarians had more admissions for injuries and external causes (S00-T98), likely due to greater mobility and fall risks, compared with OAH centenarians who typically receive closer supervision.48 Aligned with previous studies in Hong Kong,49 hospitalisations peaked in winter, particularly for OAH residents, potentially due to seasonal influenza and indoor crowding, underscoring the need for better environmental controls in residential facilities. Finally, during the COVID-19 pandemic, we observed a shift in hospitalisation patterns: fewer OAH centenarians were admitted, with fewer respiratory diagnoses and more ill-defined conditions, likely due to altered healthcare-seeking behaviour rather than true declines in illness.50 A sharp decline in admissions in 2020, especially for respiratory conditions (25.7% pre-COVID-19 vs 31.6% during COVID), followed by a rebound in 2021, supports this observation. This can be attributed to fears of infection and public health advisories, such as lockdown and quarantine measures, which deterred hospital visits for mild-to-moderate symptoms.

This study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the paramount limitation is the potential for residual confounding. The decision to reside in an OAH is strongly influenced by a greater burden of underlying frailty, cognitive impairment and multimorbidity16 51 not fully captured in our administrative data. Consequently, the observed associations between OAH residence and poorer outcomes (eg, longer stays, higher mortality) likely reflect this fundamental baseline vulnerability rather than a causal effect of the living arrangement itself. Correspondingly, we could not fully account for confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, individual comorbidities and the influence of social support networks, which may further skew the results. Second, the analysis was based on living arrangements at the time of initial admission and did not account for transitions between communities and OAHs, although sensitivity analyses suggested consistent findings. Third, reliance on electronic admission records introduces potential biases related to data accuracy and completeness, as inconsistent documentation practices across facilities may compromise the reliability of diagnoses.52 Finally, the study’s quantitative design overlooks critical qualitative aspects of care, such as quality of life and patient satisfaction in different settings, which are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of centenarians’ well-being.45 46 53 These limitations preclude causal inferences and highlight the need for more nuanced research in this population.

Conclusions

This retrospective cohort study identified significant associations between living arrangements and hospitalisation outcomes among centenarians in Hong Kong. Those residing in OAHs were associated with longer hospital stays, shorter survival times and higher long-term risks of readmission and mortality. These findings highlight the profound vulnerability of the OAH centenarian population and raise critical considerations for end-of-life care. The rising trends in hospitalisations and mortality underscore the need for healthcare systems to adapt to the distinct needs of this demographic. Future research should further delineate their healthcare requirements and the sustainability of elderly care resources to ultimately improve care for this vulnerable group.
