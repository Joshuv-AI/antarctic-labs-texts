Dual activation of cuproptosis and excessive autophagy by copper-bismuth metal-organic frameworks-loaded detachable microneedles in oral leukoplakia
Authors: Keyi Zhou, Meihua Gao, Yuqi Wan, Beibei Cong, Tingting Zhu, Zhou Ye, Tao Zhang, Wanchun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral leukoplakia (OLK), a prevalent precancerous lesion with considerable malignant transformation potential, remains a major clinical challenge due to the lack of effective and minimally invasive therapies. Here, we developed novel copper-bismuth metal-organic frameworks (CBM) and incorporated them into a bilayer detachable microneedle system (CBMMN) to achieve localized and sustained delivery. CBM markedly downregulated pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 3 (PDK3), thereby orchestrating the cooperative activation of cuproptosis and excessive autophagy, which synergistically suppressed the viability of dysplastic oral keratinocytes. In vivo studies further demonstrated that CBMMNs effectively eliminated OLK with minimal systemic toxicity, underscoring their therapeutic efficacy and biosafety. By combining cuproptosis and excessive autophagy, CBMMNs establish a mechanistic foundation distinct from conventional approaches and offer a novel therapeutic approach for OLK management.

Introduction

Oral leukoplakia (OLK) is the most common precancerous lesion of the oral mucosa, with malignant transformation rates ranging from 0.13% to 34.0% [1]. Current clinical management faces a fundamental dilemma: achieving complete eradication of dysplastic lesions while preserving the structural and functional integrity of the oral mucosa. Although minimally invasive approaches such as photodynamic therapy and laser ablation have been increasingly adopted, their efficacy remains limited by the depth of lesion infiltration and the severity of epithelial dysplasia. Moreover, the instability of its long-term outcomes has been documented [2,3]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies capable of selectively eliminating cells with malignant potential while maintaining mucosal homeostasis.

Cuproptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death, is triggered by copper-dependent accumulation of lipoylated proteins, leading to collapse of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. This mechanism has shown considerable therapeutic promise in cancer treatment [4]. Recent evidence of copper homeostasis imbalance in OLK lesions [5] suggests that elevated local copper levels may provide a unique metabolic window for cuproptosis-based interventions. Nevertheless, the role of cuproptosis in precancerous lesions remains largely unexplored. Thus, targeting cuproptosis holds significant therapeutic promise. However, single-modality induction carries the risk of adaptive resistance in dysplastic cells [6]. Notably, OLK lesions exhibit autophagic activation, which is generally thought to support abnormal keratinocyte proliferation [7]. Given the bidirectional nature of autophagy-maintaining cellular homeostasis under physiological conditions but inducing autophagy-dependent cell death (ADCD) when excessively activated [8], we hypothesize that coupling cuproptosis with excessive autophagy could provide a more effective strategy for eradicating heterogeneous lesion cells in OLK.

Current approaches to induce cuproptosis rely on copper-based biomaterials or copper ions combined with small-molecule inducers [9]. Yet, single copper ion delivery may provoke resistance through enhanced metal efflux or antioxidant defenses [10]. To overcome this limitation, we introduce a copper–bismuth (Cu-Bi) bimetallic synergistic system. Bi3+ reacts with glutathione (GSH) to form irreversible Bi(GS)3 precipitates, thereby depleting GSH and alleviating its negative impact on therapeutic efficacy in precancerous lesions [11], while simultaneously promoting autophagic activation [12]. Moreover, bismuth itself is a pharmaceutically relevant metal with excellent biocompatibility, further supporting its suitability for therapeutic applications [13]. On this basis, we establish a novel Cu-Bi dual-track synergistic therapeutic strategy for OLK. Metal-organic frameworks (MOF) constructed from tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (TCPP) offer an attractive delivery platform due to their excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity [14]. Here, we propose TCPP-based MOFs loaded with copper and bismuth ions (CBM) as a safe and efficient carrier system to support controlled therapeutic interventions in OLK.

To further optimize local administration, we employ microneedle (MN) technology. Conventional hyaluronic acid (HA)-based dissolvable MNs often cause rapid burst release due to their strong hydrophilicity, resulting in drugs being unable to exert sustained effects [15]. To address this, we design detachable MNs loaded with CBM (CBMMNs). The upper layer dissolves rapidly upon contact with the oral mucosa, ensuring painless penetration and minimizing foreign body sensation, while the underlying matrix incorporates CBM with sustained-release agents to achieve continuous and controlled drug delivery.

In summary, this study develops a mechanism-driven therapeutic system that integrates CBM with detachable MNs for OLK treatment. The CBMMNs system not only provides a mucosa-friendly delivery platform, but also initiates a dual-mode therapeutic strategy by simultaneously inducing cuproptosis and excessive autophagy. This dual-action mechanism enables the precise elimination of potentially malignant cells while preserving mucosal homeostasis. To further elucidate the molecular basis of CBMMNs-induced cell death, transcriptomic profiling was performed to identify candidate regulators relevant to mucosal pathology and biomaterial design. Collectively, these advances provide multidimensional innovation in material design, mechanistic insight, and delivery strategy, offering a promising path toward precision therapy and clinical translation for OLK.

Synthesis and characterization of CBM and Cu MOF

CBM was synthesized via a solvothermal method. Briefly, a stock solution of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (100 mg) and Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (20 mg) was added to DMF containing TCPP (20 mg) and PVP (0.5 g), followed by adjustment of the final volume to 20 mL with DMF. The reaction mixture was transferred into a stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 80 °C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting product was collected by centrifugation and washed three times each with DMF and deionized water to remove residual reactants. As a control, Cu MOF was synthesized using the same procedure but without the addition of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O [16].

The morphology and microstructure of the samples were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL, Japan). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging was performed using a Quanta™ 250 FEG system (FEI, USA) to further resolve lattice structures and nanosheet interfaces. Elemental distribution was analyzed by elemental mapping via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Oxford Instruments, Germany). XPS (Axis Supra; Kratos Analytical Ltd., UK) was employed to determine the surface elemental composition and chemical states. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a UV-8000 spectrophotometer (Shanghai Metash Instruments Co., Ltd., China) to assess the optical properties of the samples.

GSH-responsive behavior and metal ion release of CBM

To assess the concentration-dependent GSH depletion effect of CBM, mixtures containing 400 μL of GSH solution (10 mM) and 100 μL of CBM dispersion (100 μg/mL) were incubated at designated time points. After incubation, 200 μL of DTNB (20 μM) was added to each sample to quantify the residual GSH. The absorbance of the resulting mixture was measured at 420 nm (Ax). Relative GSH depletion rate was calculated using the following equation: Relative GSH depletion (%)= (Ax-ACBM)/(AGSH-ACBM) × 100%. Ax is the absorbance of the sample at 420 nm at a given time point, ACBM is the background absorbance of CBM without GSH, and AGSH is the absorbance of the GSH-DTNB reaction.

To evaluate the formation of Bi(GS)3 complexes, the reaction system was incubated for 1 h in the absence of DTNB and subsequently analyzed by UV-Vis spectrophotometry. The absorbance at 350 nm was recorded to monitor the characteristic spectral changes associated with Bi3+ coordination to GSH, serving as an indicator of Bi(GS)3 complex formation.

To investigate the GSH-responsive release behavior of copper and bismuth ions from CBM, 2 mg of CBM were dispersed in 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM GSH or PBS alone (control). The suspensions were incubated at 37 °C under gentle shaking for 24 h to mimic physiological conditions. The concentrations of Cu2+ and Bi3+ ions in the supernatant were quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES; Agilent, USA).

Cell culture

The Wuhan University School of Medicine provided dysplastic oral keratinocytes (DOK) and human oral keratinocytes (HOK). Cal-27 cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China). DOK cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. HOK cells and Cal-27 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM with identical concentrations of serum and antibiotics. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using a humidified incubator (WIGGENS Co., Ltd., Germany).

Cellular uptake

DOK cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing FITC-labeled CBM (30 μg/mL), followed by incubation for 1, 3, 6, and 9 h, respectively. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with tissue fixative, and imaged using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Leica Microsystems, USA). In parallel, cells receiving identical treatments were harvested and subjected to flow cytometric analysis using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Changes in FITC fluorescence intensity were quantified to assess cellular responses, following standard acquisition and gating protocols.

Cell viability, migration, and cell cycle detection

HOK, DOK, and Cal-27 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, fresh DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium containing various concentrations of Cu MOF or CBM (0, 15, 30, 45, 60, and 75 μg/mL) was added. After 24 h of incubation, cells were washed three times with neutral PBS, and cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to estimate cell viability.

DOK and Cal-27 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h to allow cell attachment. A straight scratch was made across the cell monolayer using the tip of a 1 mL pipette along a pre-marked horizontal line on the back of the plate. Detached cells were removed by washing three times with PBS. Fresh medium containing Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL) was added to each group, followed by incubation for 24 and 48 h. Cell migration was monitored by capturing images at each time point using an inverted microscope.

DOK cells were seeded into culture plates and incubated with Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, cells were harvested by trypsinization and centrifugation to collect cell pellets. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Assessment of intracellular redox imbalance, copper accumulation, and mitochondrial dysfunction

DOK cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL) for an additional 24 h. Intracellular GSH levels were quantified using a Reduced GSH Content Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To evaluate intracellular ROS levels, DOK cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL), followed by an additional 24 h of incubation. After treatment, cells were incubated with the cell-permeable fluorescent probe 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) using a ROS Assay Kit at 37 °C for 30 min, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, cells were washed thoroughly with serum-free medium to remove excess dye, and fluorescence signals were visualized using CLSM.

DOK cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL) for an additional 24 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS and collected by centrifugation. To normalize copper content to total protein, intracellular protein concentration was first determined using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, intracellular copper (Cu2+) levels were quantified using a Copper (Cu2+) Colorimetric Assay Kit (Complexing Method).

To assess mitochondrial integrity, DOK cells were treated with Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution using the Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (with JC-1) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence shift from red to green was analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate changes in ΔΨm.

To evaluate intracellular ATP levels, DOK cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing Cu MOF or CBM (30 μg/mL), followed by an additional 24 h of incubation. After treatment, cells were collected and lysed. Intracellular protein concentration was first determined using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit to normalize ATP content. Subsequently, intracellular ATP levels were quantified using the CheKine™ Micro ATP Content Assay Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Ultrastructural analysis of cell morphology by Bio-TEM

To investigate whether CBM induced morphological alterations in DOK cells, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing CBM (30 μg/mL), followed by an additional 24 h of incubation. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS, harvested by trypsinization, and collected by centrifugation. The obtained precipitation was fixed, dehydrated, embedded, sliced, and measured by Bio-TEM.

Protein expression analysis by immunofluorescence and western blotting

After treatment, cells were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Following three additional washes with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min. After blocking with 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 h, membranes were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, cells were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (rabbit anti-mouse IgG conjugated with FITC) for 90 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI solution (C1006; Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) for 10 min in the dark. Fluorescence signals were visualized using CLSM with appropriate filter settings.

After treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and harvested using a cell scraper. The collected cells were centrifuged to obtain pellets, which were lysed in RIPA buffer on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected as total protein extracts. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE using precast gels (80 V for 30 min and 120 V for 70 min), followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1.5 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing three times with TBST for 10 min each time, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system.

Gene expression analysis by RT-qPCR

After treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS, and total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a UV spectrophotometer by measuring the A260/A280 ratio. Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR II. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix following the manufacturer's protocol. Amplification curves and cycle threshold (Ct) values were recorded in real time. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing and analysis

DOK cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with CBM for another 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using NanoPhotometer® (Implen, Germany) and Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). Library preparation was performed using oligo(dT) magnetic bead enrichment, followed by cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Library quality was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). Qualified libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech, China) with a paired-end 150 bp strategy. Raw reads were filtered using Trimmomatic to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR (v2.7.10a), and gene-level quantification was performed with featureCounts. DEGs were identified using DESeq2, with padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 as thresholds. All sequencing and bioinformatic analyses were performed by Fynn Biotechnologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Cells were transfected with 2.5 ng of plasmid DNA using GP-transfect-Mate reagent (G04008; Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 24 h of incubation, cells were collected for subsequent experiments. Sequences used for siRNA transfection are listed in Table S2.

3D spheroid culture and invasion assay

To generate 3D spheroids, DOK cells were seeded at a density of 4000 cells/well in 96-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning Inc., USA). From day 2 to day 6, spheroids were exposed daily to 0.72 μM 4NQO for 90 min to induce precancerous transformation [17]. After medium replacement, spheroids were incubated with 30 μg/mL CBM for 5 days. Morphological changes in spheroid size were monitored using an optical microscope.

DOK spheroids pretreated with 4NQO were subjected to 3D invasion assays using growth factor-rich Matrigel [17]. Briefly, 50 μL of Matrigel was added to each well of a 96-well ultra-low attachment plate, ensuring that spheroids were positioned centrally. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min to allow Matrigel polymerization. After gel solidification, complete culture medium was added, and spheroid morphology was monitored by optical microscopy after 5 days of incubation.

Fabrication, characterization, and delivery performance of CBMMN patches

MN patches were fabricated using a layer-by-layer casting method [18]. Briefly, 50 μL of the CBM/PVA/GA mixture, containing final concentrations of 85 mg/mL PVA, 85 mg/mL GA, and 6 mg/mL CBM, was pipetted into the microcavities of a PDMS mold. The filled mold was subjected to ultrasonic degassing for 20 min to eliminate air bubbles and ensure uniform filling. Excess solution was recycled, and the mold was air-dried at room temperature for 24 h to allow the CBM to concentrate at the needle tips. Subsequently, 150 μL of HA solution (300 mg/mL) was added to form the needle body and backing layer. The mold was vacuum-degassed for 10 min and then dried under ambient conditions for another 24 h. The fully formed MN patches were carefully demolded using tweezers and stored in a desiccator until further use. Blank MN patches were prepared using the same procedure without the addition of CBM.

The morphology of the fabricated MN patches was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; TESCAN, Czech Republic). Before imaging, samples were sputter-coated with gold to enhance surface conductivity. For fluorescence-based material distribution analysis, Cy5.5-labeled CBM/PVA/GA and FITC-labeled HA mixtures were used to fabricate MN patches following similar procedures. The spatial distribution of fluorescent components within the MN matrix was visualized using CLSM. Mechanical strength of the CBMMN patch was assessed using a displacement force test station (Shenzhen Sun Technology Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, each patch was vertically fixed on the platform with MNs facing upward. The sensor probe was lowered at a constant speed of 1 mm/s until contact with the MN tips, and the force-displacement curve was recorded. To evaluate skin penetration capability, in vitro insertion experiments were performed using excised rat lingual dorsal mucosa. After insertion, the tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by H&E staining. Tissue sections were subsequently observed under an optical microscope to assess MN penetration depth and tissue integrity.

PBS was selected as the release medium to evaluate the drug-releasing performance of CBMMN patches. Each patch was placed on the surface of the medium, ensuring that the CBMMNs were fully immersed in 5 mL of PBS. The samples were incubated in a shaker at 37 °C and 70 rpm. At predetermined time points (0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 1 d, 3 d, 5 d, 7 d, 9 d, 12 d, and 14 d), 100 μL of the release medium was collected and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS to maintain a constant volume. To quantify the released CBM, a standard calibration curve was established using characteristic absorbance at 420 nm, measured by UV-Vis spectrophotometry. The cumulative model drug release percentage was analyzed by dividing the cumulative amount released by the total drug dosage in the CBMMN patch.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy against OLK lesions and biosafety study

Every experiment involving animals has been carried out following a protocol (approval number: QDU-AEC-2022232) approved by an ethical commission of Ethics Committee of Medical College of Qingdao University. Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (male, 6-week-old) were purchased from Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (China). All rats were maintained in a standardized laboratory animal facility under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The environment was controlled with a 12-h light/dark cycle, a constant temperature of 23 °C, and relative humidity maintained at 50%. Following a one-week acclimation, SD rats received 0.002% 4NQO in drinking water for 12 weeks to establish OLK models [19]. During the induction period, animals had free access to food and water.

Given the lack of standardized therapeutic agents for OLK, no positive control was included in this study. SD rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 4 per group): normal control, OLK model, OLK model treated with blank MNs (OLK + MNs), and OLK model treated with CBMMNs (OLK + CBMMNs). MNs were applied intralesionally to the dorsal lingual mucosa on days 1 and 15 under anesthesia. On day 30, morphological changes in the tongue leukoplakia lesions were recorded. All animals were subsequently euthanized, and tongue tissues were harvested for histological analysis using H&E staining. Immunohistochemical staining for PDK3, DLAT, and ATG5 was performed according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Body weights of rats were measured before and after treatment to monitor systemic health. Upon sacrifice, major organs including the hearts, kidneys, livers, lungs, and spleens were collected for histopathological evaluation. Arterial blood samples were obtained for complete blood routine analysis and serum biochemical analysis of hepatic and renal function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro 2024 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Flow cytometry data were processed using FlowJo V10, while mean fluorescence intensities, lesion area changes, and grayscale values were quantified with ImageJ. All samples and animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups and processed accordingly.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CBM

Our study successfully developed CBM via a commonly used one-step synthetic approach, as shown in Fig. 1A. For comparison, a reference monometallic nanostructure, Cu-TCPP (Cu MOF), was synthesized under identical conditions [16]. TEM imaging confirmed the distinct morphological differences between CBM and Cu MOF. CBM exhibited a uniform ultrathin rectangular nanosheet morphology, with a length of approximately 80-130 nm, a width of 60-100 nm, and a notably thin thickness (Fig. 1B). Notably, the introduction of bismuth leads to nanosheets with a more discernible rectangular outline, in contrast to the irregular nanosheets formed by Cu MOF (Fig. S1). This morphological evolution is likely associated with the influence of Bi3+, whose stereochemically active 6s2 lone pair has been suggested to promote a more organized self-assembly between the metal centers and TCPP ligands [20]. The rectangular morphology of CBM, with its sharper edges, may further facilitate an initial localized piercing of the cell membrane, thereby supporting more efficient internalization [21]. In addition, this nanosheet-shaped morphology can afford CBM a high surface area, which facilitates the exposure of active sites and thereby enhances its biological performance [22].Fig. 1Synthesis and Characterizations of CBM. EDS revealed a homogeneous distribution of Cu, Bi, C, N, and O throughout the CBM (Fig. 1C). Further EDS elemental quantification of CBM is shown in Fig. S2. This morphology was subsequently confirmed by HRTEM (Fig. S3). The clear lattice fringes of Cu and Bi corresponded to interplanar spacings consistent with those of the individual Cu MOF and Bi MOF, respectively [23,24]. We also compared the UV-Vis spectrum of Cu MOF with CBM (Fig. S4), which indicated the expansion of the porphyrin π-system upon Bi coordination. Additionally, X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed that CBM exhibited additional and distinct characteristic peaks compared with the Cu MOF (Fig. S5). The elemental composition was further examined by XPS survey. (Fig. S6). The peaks of Cu 2p in Cu MOF show that copper mainly exists in the form of Cu2+ (Cu 2p3/2 at 934.8 eV and Cu 2p1/2 at 954.7 eV) and a small amount of Cu+ (Cu 2p3/2 at 932.95 eV and Cu 2p1/2 at 952.3 eV) (Fig. 1D). The peaks arising from 4f7/2 (159.4 eV) and 4f5/2 (164.7 eV) were clearly observed (Fig. 1E). The distance between such two peaks was measured to be 5.3 eV. It indicates that Bi is successfully doped into CBM in the form of Bi3+. Collectively, these results unequivocally demonstrate the successful construction of a phase-pure bimetallic CBM nanostructure.

In a high GSH concentration environment, TCPP-based MOF frequently competes with GSH in binding to metal nodes, thereby facilitating metal decomposition [25]. Consequently, we hypothesized that CBM also exhibited GSH-responsive degradation characteristics. Through ICP-OES analysis, we observed that the presence of GSH efficiently enhanced the release of Cu2+ and Bi3+ in CBM (Fig. S7). Then, we found that more GSH could be depleted by CBM at longer incubation times (Fig. 1F). After 180 min of incubation, almost 80% of GSH was consumed by CBM, suggesting the desirable GSH depletion effect (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, after incubation with GSH, the coordination characteristic peak at 350 nm increased significantly (Fig. 1H), indicating the formation of Bi(GS)3. This complex arises from Bi3+ released from CBM coordinating with the sulfhydryl (-SH) groups of GSH via stable metal-sulfur interactions. These results reveal that CBM exhausts GSH through multiple mechanisms beyond the well-established redox-mediated depletion employed by copper ions (Cu2+ + 2GSH → Cu+ + GSSG + 2H+). Thus, the engineered CBM exhibits complementary GSH consumption pathways and intrinsic GSH responsiveness, which is expected to enhance its therapeutic performance in the GSH-enriched microenvironment characteristic of OLK lesions.

In vitro effect of CBM on the cytotoxicity toward HOK and DOK cells

We first assessed the effect of CBM on the viability of normal HOK cells. CBM exhibited insignificant cytotoxicity toward HOK cells across different concentrations (Fig. S8), underscoring its excellent biocompatibility. This can be attributed to the intrinsic stability of the CBM under physiological conditions, which minimizes nonspecific ion release and preserves redox homeostasis. This behavior supports selective targeting of diseased cells while maintaining safety in normal tissues. Such biocompatibility is essential for potential biomedical applications.

We next evaluated the effects of CBM on cell behaviors in DOK cells. To evaluate whether the CBM exerted superior inhibitory effects on DOK cells, Cu MOF was included as a comparative control in the CCK-8 assay. Both materials suppressed DOK cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2A). Notably, CBM exhibited significantly better inhibitory effects on DOK cells than Cu MOF at equivalent concentrations. Based on these results, 30 μg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments, as it was close to the IC50 of CBM. Next, we conducted scratch wound assays and observed wound healing at 24 h and 48 h. The results revealed that CBM exhibited stronger migration inhibition ability than Cu MOF at both time points (Fig. 2B and C). Additionally, the effects of CBM and Cu MOF on the cell cycle progression of DOK cells were investigated. Both treatments induced cell cycle arrest during the S phase (DNA synthesis phase), resulting in a significant reduction in the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase (late DNA synthesis/division phase) (Fig. 2D). But the impact of CBM was more significant than Cu MOF (Fig. S9). Given the pronounced biological effects of CBM, we examined whether its cellular uptake efficiency could explain the observed bioactivity. Flow cytometry analysis yielded consistent results (Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that DOK cells internalize CBM in a time-dependent manner, reaching saturation after ∼3 h. Such early saturation suggests efficient uptake of CBM, which may underlie its downstream biological effects. Consistent with prior research [26,27], CBM is likely internalized mainly through receptor-mediated endocytosis and clathrin-dependent pathways.

Furthermore, based on a previous study [17], a 3D DOK cell model was established to deeply evaluate the inhibitory effect of CBM in vitro. Notably, direct treatment with CBM resulted in a marked decrease in the area of 4NQO-DOK spheroids. (Fig. S10). We further performed an invasiveness assay using Matrigel and obtained similar results. This physiologically relevant model further supports the therapeutic potential of CBM for OLK.

As OLK carries a defined risk of progression to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [28], we further included Cal-27 carcinoma cells to assess whether CBM exerts comparable effects after malignant transformation. CCK-8 assays confirmed that CBM markedly reduced Cal-27 cell viability (Fig. S12), while scratch wound healing assays demonstrated a significant inhibition of cell migration (Fig. S13). These results indicate that CBM not only suppresses the progression of OLK cells but also exerts therapeutic effects against fully transformed carcinoma cells, underscoring its potential as a broad-spectrum anti-oral cancer agent.

In vitro efficacy of CBM in inducing cuproptosis and autophagy of DOK cells

Having established the cytotoxic effects of CBM on OLK, we next investigated the efficacy of CBM in inducing cuproptosis and autophagy of DOK cells. We initially assessed whether CBM could effectively reduce intracellular GSH levels. It showed that GSH levels in DOK cells were effectively reduced after exposure to either Cu MOF or CBM for 24 h, but the latter exhibited a significantly greater extent of reduction (Fig. 3A). Next, intracellular copper levels were examined. The outcome confirmed that the group of CBM exhibited markedly higher intracellular Cu levels (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that, once internalized, CBM establishes the conditions required for cuproptosis induction. The superior GSH-depleting capacity of CBM can be attributed not only to the redox interactions between GSH and Cu ions, but also to the coordination of GSH with Bi ions, resulting in the irreversible formation of Bi(GS)3. Then, the aggregation of the lipoylated protein dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), a hallmark event during cuproptosis, was primarily assessed by IF detection. The result showed that, compared to Cu MOF induction, CBM stimulation caused a significant increase in DLAT aggregation signals in DOK cells (Fig. 3C). DLAT is a core component of the mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC). Its abnormal oligomerization is one of the signature events of cuproptosis [30]. Specifically, when Cu ions are overloaded, Cu+ can directly bind to the lipoic acid group of DLAT, inducing a conformational change and leading to its abnormal aggregation.

Next, we further detect the expression of Fe-S cluster proteins and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70). Compared with the Cu MOF group, the CBM group exhibited a marked decrease in the expression of ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) and lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS), accompanied by a pronounced increase in HSP70 expression (Fig. 3C, Fig. S14). FDX1 is a key member of the Fe-S cluster protein family, possessing both copper reductase activity and providing the essential electrons for LIAS to synthesize lipoic acid [31,32]. The current view holds that Cu ion overload disrupts Fe-S cluster stability and synthesis [33]. The findings of FDX1 and LIAS downregulation obtained in this study are consistent with this view. HSP70, as a classic molecular chaperone protein, is upregulated in response to protein toxicity stress [34]. Abnormal changes in the aforementioned proteins can prompt the recruitment of HSP70 to try to restore protein homeostasis. The combined alterations in these markers collectively suggest that the copper-bismuth bimetallic synergistic application strategy is more conducive to cuproptosis.

Excessive Cu ions accumulation in cells induces pathological alterations in relevant proteins, ultimately causing complete mitochondrial dysfunction and triggering cell death. In this context, we further evaluated the impact of CBM on mitochondrial function. Specifically, the JC-1 fluorescent probe was utilized to quantify alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Our results revealed that the Cu-Bi bimetallic coupling strategy led to a significant decrease in the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio of JC-1, which is indicative of mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization (Fig. 3D, Fig. S15). Subsequently, we determined the level of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the terminal product of mitochondrial energy metabolism. Compared with the effect induced by Cu MOF, CBM exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect on ATP production in DOK cells (Fig. S16). As key indicators for evaluating mitochondrial function [35], the changes in JC-1and ATP clearly confirm the role of CBM in inducing mitochondrial dysfunction.

Additionally, it is noteworthy that ATPase copper transporting beta (ATP7B), a key copper transporter protein, can eliminate excess free copper ions within cells to prevent intracellular copper accumulation [36]. Thus, we additionally assessed ATP7B expression levels. Results revealed that CBM treatment significantly downregulated ATP7B expression in DOK cells (Fig. S17). Suppression of ATP7B expression is crucial for avoiding cuproptosis treatment resistance [37]. Concurrently, the functional activity of ATP7B requires energy from ATP [38]. Evidently, the simultaneous impact of CBM on the expression and function of ATP7B enhances intracellular copper accumulation, thereby increasing therapeutic sensitivity to cuproptosis.

During cuproptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction is often accompanied by massive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Thus, we employed the fluorescent probe dichlorofluorescein to detect intracellular ROS levels. It showed that the CBM group induced significantly greater ROS production compared to the Cu MOF group (Fig. 3E and F). This result is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies.

A certain degree of autophagy upregulation is inherent in OLK tissue itself [7]. This is considered survival-promoting autophagy, facilitating the continuous proliferation of malignantly transformed cells. Autophagy can exert both survival-promoting protective effects and death-promoting effects within the same cell [8]. Under certain conditions, further activation of autophagy can lead to cell death, a phenomenon termed ADCD. ROS serve as the pivotal switch driving the transition from autophagic protection to ADCD [39]. Additionally, Bi and Cu have been demonstrated to effectively enhance autophagy, respectively [12,40]. Thus, we hypothesized that CBM may possess the potential to synchronously induce excessive autophagy.

To validate this hypothesis, we further examined autophagy-related proteins associated with excessive autophagy activation, including autophagy-related protein 5 (ATG5) and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta-II (LC3B-II). The western blot (WB) results supported our hypothesis. Compared to Cu MOF, the application of CBM significantly enhanced the protein expression of ATG5 and LC3B-II (Fig. 3G–I). ATG5 is a key protein in autophagy, participating in the early stages of autophagosome formation and promoting the elongation and maturation of autophagic vesicles [41]. Due to its ability to directly initiate the process of ADCD, ATG5 is considered essential for inducing ADCD [42]. In addition, LC3B-I is converted into LC3B-II after lipidation, which participates in autophagosome formation. In the context of mitochondrial damage, the occurrence of fatal autophagy is closely associated with the sustained elevation of LC3B-II [42].

Concurrently, we employed Bio-TEM to observe whether CBM could effectively coordinate cuproptosis and ADCD. In most cells, mitochondria exhibited significant swelling with the almost complete disappearance of internal cristae (Fig. 3J, Fig. S18), which is consistent with the characteristic ultrastructural features of cuproptosis [43]. These cells were also accompanied by a small number of autophagosomes and autolysosomes. Notably, in another subset of cells that did not display obvious cuproptosis-related mitochondrial alterations, a substantial accumulation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes was observed, suggesting robust autophagy activation, possibly accompanied by autophagic dysfunction. Such ultrastructural changes are regarded as characteristic morphological alterations of ADCD following excessive activation of autophagy [44]. In contrast, no decisive morphological features of other major cell death modes were directly observed. For example, characteristic features of apoptosis, such as membrane blebbing and overt apoptotic body formation [45], and the ferroptosis-associated feature of condensed mitochondrial membrane densities [46], were not detected. Taken together, these results indicate that CBM simultaneously induces representative morphological changes associated with cuproptosis and ADCD, supporting the involvement of both cell death modes in its in vitro anti-OLK effects (Fig. 3K). Evidently, the coordinated activation of cuproptosis and excessive autophagy can serve as a novel therapeutic approach for precancerous lesions.

The role of PDK3 in cooperative activation of cuproptosis and autophagy by CBM

Identification and validation of PDK3 downregulation following CBM treatment

To explore the key molecules mediating this synergistic effect of cuproptosis and autophagy in DOK cells, we isolated total RNA from untreated DOK cells and DOK cells treated with CBM for transcriptomic sequencing analysis. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the standard of padj ≤ 0.05 as well as |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and plotted as a volcano map. The results showed that compared with the untreated group, there were 1642 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) significantly downregulated and 1134 DEGs significantly upregulated in the CBM-treated group (Fig. S19). To dissect the functional associations of the DEGs, we further performed Gene Ontology enrichment analysis and presented the top 15 significantly enriched terms in the categories of biological processes and molecular functions. Among these, heat shock protein binding (GO: 0031072), response to xenobiotic stimulus (GO: 0009410), protein folding chaperone (GO: 0051082), and ATP-dependent protein folding chaperone (GO: 0140662) all indicated that significant proteotoxic stress occurred in DOK cells following CBM treatment (Fig. S20). Changes in the expression of genes related to intracellular calcium-gated chloride channel activity (GO: 0005229) and transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine kinase activity (GO: 0030297) were closely associated with the regulation of mitochondrial function. Furthermore, the term phosphatidylserine binding (GO: 0001786) was significantly enriched, suggesting abnormal changes in cellular autophagic activity. These functional changes at the transcriptomic level are highly consistent with the previously validated evidence stating that CBM synergistically induces cuproptosis and activates autophagy in DOK cells.

To gain deeper insights, we carried out additional protein interaction network analysis targeting the PDK3 molecule. The result indicated that PDK3 could interact with cuproptosis-associated molecules, including DLAT, as well as with FOXO4 [50], which was involved in autophagy regulation (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, we conducted additional verification via RT-qPCR and WB experiments. The results demonstrated that PDK3 mRNA and protein expression levels were significantly decreased following CBM treatment (Fig. 4C, Fig. S21). This is highly consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results, providing reliable experimental evidence for the differential expression characteristics of PDK3.

PDK3 overexpression and knockdown validate the role of PDK3 in CBM-induced cuproptosis and autophagy

To clarify the biological function of PDK3, we further used small interfering RNA (siRNA) and plasmid transient transfection techniques to construct PDK3 knockdown and overexpression cell models, respectively (Fig. 4D). Verification via RT-qPCR and WB experiments confirmed that both genetically modified cell models were successfully established (Fig. S22). Next, to investigate the effect of PDK3 regulation on cuproptosis, we employed IF to measure the expression level of FDX1 and the aggregation of DLAT. Intervention with si-PDK3-1 and si-PDK3-2 significantly reduced the expression of FDX1 while significantly increasing the aggregation of DLAT (Fig. 4E); however, PDK3 overexpression treatment led to opposite changes in the above indicators (Fig. 4F, Fig. S23). Notably, CBM treatment exerted a less significant effect on FDX1 expression and DLAT aggregation in PDK3-overexpressing cells than in cells with normal PDK3 expression. This clearly reveals that PDK3 is a key regulatory molecule in the process of cuproptosis induced by CBM, providing a core target basis for the mechanism.

Next, we further detect the expression levels of autophagy key markers ATG5 and LC3B-II. Treatment with si-PDK3-1 and si-PDK3-2 significantly increased the expression of ATG5 and LC3B-II (Fig. 4G). In contrast, PDK3 overexpression inhibited the expression of these two proteins. Moreover, when cells with PDK3 overexpression were treated with CBM, the expression of ATG5 and LC3B-II failed to increase significantly (Fig. 4H). The above results preliminarily indicate that PDK3 is a key molecule in the synergistic promotion of cuproptosis and autophagy by CBM.

To further validate the regulatory role of PDK3 in CBM-mediated OLK therapy, we employed the CCK-8 assay to examine changes in proliferative activity of DOK cells following PDK3 knockdown or overexpression. Results showed that the proliferation was significantly inhibited after treatment with si-PDK3-1 and si-PDK3-2 (Fig. 4I), whereas the OE-PDK3 group exhibited a significantly stronger proliferative promotion effect on DOK cells than the control group (Fig. 4J). Furthermore, when CBM treatment was applied to PDK3-overexpressing cells, the inhibitory effect on DOK cells proliferation was notably attenuated. Existing studies have suggested that PDK3 can be regulated by both the janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling pathway and the histone lysine demethylase 4A/E2F transcription factor 1 (KDM4A/E2F1) complex [51,52], providing a possible basis for understanding how CBM suppresses PDK3 expression. Since activation of JAK/STAT is known to be sensitive to intracellular metal ion levels [53,54], it is plausible that the copper and bismuth ions released from CBM disturb metal homeostasis in DOK cells and thereby contribute to the downregulation of PDK3 through modulation of this pathway. In parallel, bismuth ions preferentially interact with protein-associated metal-binding sites and have been reported to competitively bind alkB homolog 7 (ALKBH7) [55], suggesting that they may also interfere with the activity of other metal-dependent proteins. Given that KDM4A is an iron-dependent histone demethylase [56], CBM-induced alteration of the intracellular metal environment may impair KDM4A activity, weaken E2F1-mediated transcriptional regulation, and consequently reduce the transcriptional support for PDK3 expression. Taken together, these observations suggest that the inhibitory effect of CBM on PDK3 may be associated, at least in part, with coordinated regulation of the JAK/STAT3 axis and the KDM4A/E2F1 complex.

In addition, previous studies have shown that PDK3 can promote colorectal cancer proliferation by activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway [57]. Given that the PI3K-AKT pathway is also significantly activated in OLK and its related cancerous tissues [58], it is reasonable to speculate that PDK3 may be involved in the development and malignant transformation of OLK by regulating it through a similar mechanism. Although the present study only provides a preliminary understanding of the role of PDK3 in OLK, it offers new insight into the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying the disease. Further studies are still needed to clarify the functional significance of PDK3 in OLK and to determine its specific regulatory patterns across a broader range of signaling pathways.

Preparation and characterization of CBMMNs

MNs-based drug delivery has emerged as a promising approach and offers a novel route for intraoral administration [59]. To improve the applicability and efficiency of MNs for intraoral drug delivery, we designed a detachable MN system well suited for clinical oral applications (Fig. 6A). The lower layer was a drug-loaded layer made of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and gelatin (GA), which enabled sustained drug release; the upper layer was a fast-dissolving layer composed of HA. SEM images of the fabricated MNs showed that there were 25 needles in total, arranged in a 5×5 array (Fig. 6B). The diameter of the needle base was approximately 400 μm, and the total height of the needle tip was about 650 μm (including 350 μm for the column part and 300 μm for the tip part), with a distance of 300 μm between adjacent needles. Then, we evaluated the dissolution performance of the upper layer of the MNs. The results showed that after applying the MNs to the in vivo tongue tissue, the backing layer of the MNs was observed to dissolve gradually in the oral cavity over time (Fig. 6C). Approximately 5 min later, the lower needle body could be completely separated from the upper backing layer, and the residues of the backing layer could also be easily peeled off. Subsequently, we formally loaded CBM into the lower needle body (Fig. 6D). To further clarify the distribution characteristics of the drug in the MNs, we first labeled CBM with Cy5.5 fluorescence and then mixed it into the lower-layer raw materials; meanwhile, we labeled the upper-layer raw materials with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). The laser confocal microscopy imaging results showed that CBM-Cy5.5 was uniformly distributed in the lower layer of CBMMNs (Fig. 6E). Next, we determined the drug release performance of CBMMNs. The results indicated that the drug dissolution rate of the MNs was relatively fast within the first day, followed by a progressive slowdown, and the release profile became essentially stable after approximately 7 days. (Fig. 6F). Therefore, CMBMNs provide a sustained drug release effect with an appropriate duration for treatment, meeting the clinical demand for therapy.

We further evaluated the mechanical strength of CBMMNs, and the result remained satisfactory, with every 25 needles capable of withstanding a pressure of approximately 20 N (Fig. 6G). In addition, hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining results further confirmed that the CBMMNs could effectively penetrate the full thickness of the tongue epidermis (Fig. 6H). Building on this capacity for tissue penetration, the CBMMNs can utilize the moist intraoral environment to facilitate rapid dissolution of their upper layer, ultimately enabling the retention of the sustained-release layer containing CBM within the tongue tissue. This design differs from previous reported intraoral MNs that use fully and rapidly dissolving materials [60]. The two-layer structure of CBMMNs is particularly suitable for OLK, a condition that usually requires prolonged intervention [61]. Given the continuous turnover of the keratinized oral epithelium [62], burst release may fail to maintain effective drug exposure to newly generated dysplastic cells near the basal layer. In contrast, sustained release may provide more durable local suppression and thus potentially reduce the development of adaptive tolerance during CBM treatment of OLK. In addition, one previous study suggested that metal ion-based therapy could enhance local immune responses in OLK and thereby assist in eliminating dysplastic oral keratinocytes [63]. However, rapid drug release at high local concentrations may also trigger excessive early immune stimulation, which could subsequently promote adaptive immune tolerance or excessive inflammation and thus be unfavorable for local mucosal homeostasis [64]. Overall, compared with burst release, sustained drug release may offer greater therapeutic benefit for OLK treatment. Moreover, the rapid dissolution of the upper layer can reduce foreign-body sensation and improve comfort during use. In conclusion, this design of CBMMNs has promising clinical application prospects. Together, these features suggest that CBMMNs hold strong potential for future clinical application in OLK.

Next, we performed immunohistochemical staining to analyze the therapeutic mechanism of CBM in vivo. We first observed that the cuproptosis-associated protein DLAT was significantly reduced in OLK tissue compared with the control group (Fig. 7C and D). Following treatment with CBMMNs, DLAT expression was restored to levels approaching those in normal tissues. Reduced DLAT expression in OLK tissue suggests impaired pyruvate-to–acetyl-CoA conversion and altered aerobic respiration, consistent with the Warburg effect described in OLK and OSCC tissues [65].

At the same time, we observed that the expression level of the autophagy-related protein ATG5 in tissues showed an increasing trend among the normal group, OLK group, and OLK + CBMMNs group (Fig. 7C and D). Consistent with previous reports showing elevated LC3B in OLK [66], our data confirm that OLK tissue exhibits autophagic activation. Notably, CBMMNs further amplify this response, driving a markedly heightened autophagic state. Collectively, these findings indicate that cuproptosis and excessive autophagy synergistically mediate the therapeutic effects of CBMMNs against OLK in vivo.

Moreover, in vitro studies have revealed PDK3 is a key molecule mediating the inhibitory effect of CBM on DOK cells. So, we also examined PDK3 expression levels in tongue tissue (Fig. 7C and D). PDK3 expression was significantly increased in OLK tissue compared with normal tissue, and was significantly reduced after CBMMNs treatment. The high expression of PDK3 in OLK tissue correlates with alterations in PDK3 expression observed in other cancerous tissues [67,68]. This suggests that PDK3 potentially holds pivotal regulatory functions in the development and progression of precancerous lesions. Combined with in vitro findings, the results reinforce the statement that CBM released from CBMMNs can treat OLK by inhibiting PDK3 expression in abnormally proliferating cells via cuproptosis and excessive autophagy activation.

We also collected the major organs of rats in each group for H&E staining. The results showed that no abnormal changes were observed in the organs of rats in all groups (Fig. 8C). Meanwhile, we collected arterial blood from rats in each group to conduct blood routine and serum biochemical analyses (Fig. 8D and E). There were no significant differences in the levels of red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), platelets (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), urea (UREA), creatinine (CREA), alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST) among all groups. The above results indicate that the complete therapeutic system CBMMNs developed in this study possesses favorable in vivo biocompatibility. Nevertheless, prolonged local persistence of bismuth-containing precipitates may result in adverse effects such as tissue discoloration and sustained activation of cellular metal-response pathways [70]. However, the oral mucosa has continuous self-renewal and repair capacity. Therefore, locally deposited bismuth-containing precipitates may gradually decrease through mucosal turnover and local clearance. In addition, any locally retained bismuth-containing residues that are released into saliva may be cleared by salivary flow or swallowed and subsequently excreted through the gastrointestinal tract, predominantly in an unabsorbed form [71]. These possibilities remain to be verified by future long-term biodistribution, elemental quantification, and clearance studies.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully synthesized CBM with a rectangular nanosheet morphology via a one-step approach. Compared with the monometallic Cu MOF, CBM markedly enhanced therapeutic efficacy on OLK both in vitro and in vivo. In OLK, the accumulation of copper ions contributes to triggering cuproptosis, but copper ion enrichment alone may lead to treatment resistance through enhanced metal efflux or activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms. The incorporation of bismuth can not only effectively potentiate the cuproptosis-inducing effect of copper ions in OLK but also promote excessive autophagy through PDK3 downregulation. Additionally, Bi3+ ions can react with the excessive GSH present in OLK, forming an irreversible Bi(GS)3 precipitate, resulting in GSH depletion. Hence, CBM containing both copper and bismuth exerts a dual therapeutic mechanism involving cuproptosis and autophagy, demonstrating superior potential for the treatment of precancerous lesions. Simultaneously, the detachable CBMMNs developed in our work enable CBM to treat OLK using a delivery manner better suited to the oral environment. Given these advantages, we believe that this strategy holds considerable promise as a therapeutic approach for OLK with a low risk of adverse reactions.
