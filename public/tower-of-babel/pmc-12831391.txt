A certain set of signs that could be compatible with Kabuki syndrome: a case report of an Iranian girl and review of literature
Authors: Fatemeh Owlia, Maryam Irannezhad, Zahra Bahrololoomi, Shima Mosallaeipour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKabuki syndrome is a rare congenital disorder with multisystem involvement and distinctive craniofacial features. While mutations in KMT2D and KDM6A are commonly associated, approximately 20–25% of patient cases lack molecular confirmation and require phenotype-based diagnosis.Case presentationsWe report a case of a 6-year-old Iranian girl presenting with multiple hallmark features of Kabuki syndrome type II, including characteristic facial morphology, skeletal anomalies, and prominent oral findings. Dental manifestations included hypodontia, malocclusion, delayed eruption, and ectopic molars. Notably, the patient also exhibited pectus excavatum, a skeletal anomaly not commonly reported in Kabuki syndrome, which expands the phenotypic spectrum. Radiographic evaluation supported the clinical diagnosis and revealed additional craniofacial anomalies.ConclusionThis case underscores the diagnostic value of oral and skeletal features in Kabuki syndrome and highlights the importance of multidisciplinary assessment. It reinforces the role of phenotype-driven diagnosis in rare congenital disorders, particularly in patient cases lacking genetic confirmation, and documents a rare thoracic anomaly, pectus excavatum, within the clinical spectrum of Kabuki syndrome.

Introduction

Kabuki syndrome [1] is a disorder that can affect various parts of the body. The overall prevalence is 1 in 32,000, with no preference for sex, ethnicity, or age [2–4]. It is an autosomal dominant syndrome [5–7]. While certain mutations in KMT2D and KDM6A have been identified, the genetic basis of the syndrome remains unknown in approximately 20–25% of patients [7, 8]. In such cases, referred to as Kabuki syndrome type II, diagnosis relies on clinical evaluation and recognition of characteristic features rather than molecular confirmation [4].

The five hallmark dysmorphic traits that serve as key diagnostic indicators are distinct facial characteristics (long palpebral fissures with eversion of the lower eyelid, arched and broad eyebrows with lateral thinning, short columella with a depressed nasal tip, and large or cupped ears), postnatal growth retardation, intellectual disabilities, skeletal abnormalities, and unusual dermatoglyphic patterns [9, 10].

Almost every patient with Kabuki syndrome (KS) presents with a typical combination of facial features. Maxillary recession and midfacial hypoplasia are commonly observed in patients with KS and are crucial in the clinical diagnosis of KS [11].

Oral findings are reported in over 60% of patient cases of Kabuki syndrome [1]. These findings include missing teeth, malocclusion, a high-arched palate, dental anomalies, widely spaced teeth, hypodontia, screwdriver-shaped incisors, delayed tooth eruption, and ectopic upper molars, as demonstrated in patients with KS [11–13].

This paper presents a case study of a 6-year-old Iranian girl fulfilling multiple clinical criteria for Kabuki syndrome type II, highlighting the importance of phenotype-driven diagnosis. The objective of this report is to demonstrate the principal oral manifestations of Kabuki syndrome, emphasizing the significance of accurate diagnosis and multidisciplinary care. By integrating radiographic evaluation with clinical findings, this report aims to enhance awareness among pediatricians, oral medicine specialists, and radiologists regarding the variable manifestations of this syndrome.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old Iranian girl with a chief complaint of delayed tooth eruption visited a dentist over a year. Finally, she was referred to the oral and maxillofacial medicine department in Yazd Dental School.

The patient had no history of developmental delays, systemic illness, or immunodeficiency. Her parents are nonconsanguineous, and there is no family history of syndromic conditions. Psychosocially, she lives in a stable home environment, attends mainstream school, and shows normal social and academic development. She did not exhibit delays in speech or walking, intellectual disabilities, or a history of susceptibility to infections. There was no history of delayed tooth eruption. Through the physical examination, some interesting features, such as short stature, persistent fingertip pads, and clinodactyly of the fifth digit, were observed. Strangest of all, the pectus excavatum was observed during the chest examination. Although pectus excavatum is not commonly reported among patients with Kabuki syndrome, its presence in this case adds to the spectrum of skeletal anomalies and underscores the importance of thorough systemic evaluation in affected individuals.

The craniofacial examination revealed several distinct facial features, including arched eyebrows; long eyelashes; epicanthus; ptosis; strabismus; long palpebral fissures in both eyes; a nose with a short columella, wide base, and depressed tip; large, cupped ears; micrognathia; and retrognathia of the maxilla (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Extraoral images: (a) frontal; (b) left side of face; (c) pectus excavatum; (d) clinodactyly and fingertip pads

The intraoral examination revealed an anterior crossbite, a high-arched palate, a small “V-shaped” mandibular dental arch, a high frenum of the upper lip, and rotated mandibular primary canines. Besides, attrition of the maxillary central incisors was observed, indicating the occlusal interference with the mandibular canines. Only a carious lesion on tooth number 64 was present. Diagnostic assessment

The panoramic and periapical radiographs revealed ankylosis of the mandibular primary second molars, as well as missing mandibular permanent second premolars and maxillary lateral incisors (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Intraoral and radiography images: (a) maxillary and mandibular teeth; (b) high-arched palate; (c) high frenum; (d) panoramic view; (e) anterior occlusal radiography; (f) anterior mandibular preapical radiography

Intraoral and radiography images: (a) maxillary and mandibular teeth; (b) high-arched palate; (c) high frenum; (d) panoramic view; (e) anterior occlusal radiography; (f) anterior mandibular preapical radiography

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) was performed owing to the complexity of dental anomalies, including suspected hypodontia, delayed eruption, and abnormal root morphology. The decision was made after multidisciplinary discussion, balancing diagnostic necessity with radiation safety under the as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA) principle. The scan was limited in field and optimized for pediatric exposure. CBCT showed that several deciduous teeth (nos. 53, 52, 62, 75, 72, 71, 81, 82, and 85) had not erupted yet (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Some sections of cone-beam computed tomography views: (a) axial section of maxillary teeth; (b) axial section of mandibular teeth; (c) coronal section of the maxillary, frontal, and ethmoid sinus; (d) sagittal section of the sphenoid sinus

Multiplanar CBCT images clearly show that there is little to no pneumatization in the majority of the paranasal sinuses. Figure 3 shows that both the frontal and sphenoid sinuses are aplastic, whereas the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses are hypoplastic. These results can be interpreted as within physiological limits.

Therapeutic intervention

The patient had not undergone any multidisciplinary assessments before this visit or received any prior diagnosis or syndrome-specific treatment before presenting to our clinic. Our team initiated a comprehensive evaluation on the basis of her craniofacial and dental features, which led to the clinical suspicion of Kabuki syndrome. A team, including specialists in orthodontics, maxillofacial surgery, periodontics, and pediatric dentistry, should investigate patient problems. On the basis of the unfavorable position of the unerupted deciduous teeth in the maxilla and the potential risk of damaging the buds of permanent teeth during manipulation of the area, as well as considering the patient’s young age and lack of proper anchorage for expansion, a less invasive treatment plan was proposed. This plan involved extracting teeth 51 and 61, as well as surgically removing teeth 72, 71, 81, and 82. By eliminating mechanical barriers, space will be created for the anterior permanent teeth to grow. The treatment plan will be adjusted on the basis of the amount of eruption and the position of the teeth during subsequent follow-up sessions, following a careful watch-and-wait approach (Table 2).

Discussion

KS was first described in the early 1980s in Japan and named after the characteristic facial features that resemble the makeup of actors in the Kabuki theatre in Tokyo [14, 15]. In 2019, an international consensus established diagnostic criteria for KS [4, 16].

The diagnosis of KS is based on clinical criteria, which include five major features: distinct facial characteristics, postnatal growth retardation, intellectual disabilities, skeletal abnormalities, and unusual dermatoglyphic patterns [9, 10]. This patient case was classified as Kabuki syndrome type II on the basis of fulfillment of multiple clinical criteria in the absence of molecular confirmation. Approximately 20% of individuals with Kabuki syndrome do not exhibit detectable variants in KMT2D or KDM6A, and a clinical diagnosis may be established through characteristic findings and exclusion of other syndromes [17, 18]. On the basis of clinical findings, systemic condition, and familial history, Hardikar syndrome (HS) [19], CHARGE syndrome, and KS can be considered as a possible diagnosis. HS is a disorder of multiple anomalies, predominantly characterized by cleft lip/palate, liver and biliary tract disease, intestinal malrotation, obstructive uropathy, and retinopathy. In our case, liver, intestinal, and visual disorders were not present, which are mandatory signs for the diagnosis of HS, and they can be ruled out from the diagnosis. Moreover, CHARGE syndrome is a genetic condition that affects many parts of a child’s body, including their heart, nerves, genitals, eyes, and ears [18]. Features, such as a square face, short stature, wide ears, prominent columella, and broad nasal tip, which are essential signs for the diagnosis of CHARGE syndrome, were not present in our case. Therefore, CHARGE syndrome could be discarded as a diagnosis as well. In this patient, the presence of features such as growth restriction and fingertip pads lead us to consider KS as the main diagnosis for this patient. Moreover, dental findings supported the clinical diagnosis of KS [20]. The most common missing teeth in KS syndrome are maxillary lateral incisors and mandibular central incisors [12]. In this case, in addition to the missing maxillary lateral incisors, agenesis of mandibular second premolars was observed as well. Cardiac anomalies affect approximately 42% of patients with KS [19, 21]. It should be noted that in this case, the patient presented with pectus excavatum, which is an unusual anomaly in KS without any signs of cardiac problems. To the best of our knowledge, no patient with simultaneous engagement of KS and pectus excavatum has been reported in Iran yet. The patient in this case exhibited all the typical characteristics of Kabuki syndrome, except intellectual disabilities, which is a common finding in 46% of patients with KS [20]. Paranasal sinus hypoplasia is another unusual finding associated with this patient. It has not been reported in KS until now, which may either reflect age-related developmental variation or represent an atypical finding. Several syndromes are associated with sinonasal anomalies resulting from defects in midfacial skeletal growth. Some of the most well-known defects in midfacial skeletal growth include Treacher–Collins syndrome, Binder’s syndrome/maxillomandibular dysplasia, Williams syndrome, elfin facies syndrome, Apert’s syndrome, and Crouzon’s syndrome. All of these syndromes are linked to bilateral paranasal sinus effects [22–25]. While paranasal sinus hypoplasia is a common feature in some syndromic diseases, this finding in our patient case resembles that seen in the abovementioned syndromes.

Although in this individual genetic testing was not performed owing to the refusal of her parents, the patient exhibited multiple hallmark features of Kabuki syndrome. According to the 2019 consensus criteria, such cases may be compatible with type II Kubaki syndrome. However, patients with type I typically have an affected parent owing to the autosomal dominant (AD) genetic pattern. The possibility of incomplete penetration of the related gene or epigenetically silent variant cannot be excluded, and further molecular analysis may be considered if resources allow [18]. Treatment for KS is unique to the child and focuses on alleviating their symptoms. Treatment options may include orthodontic procedures to address functional occlusion, improve aesthetics, and correct problems related to arch length, extraction, or implantation to address tooth development issues and enhance the patient’s quality of life [26–28].

Conclusion

This case report highlights the characteristic dysmorphic features of Kabuki syndrome [1]. Maxillofacial manifestations of KS may serve as alarm signs for early diagnosis to prevent further problems. It should be noted that dentists play a crucial role in identifying and referring individuals presenting with Kabuki syndrome accurately. Comprehensive facial and intraoral examinations are essential for making a proper diagnosis. Given the diverse range of oral and craniofacial features, a multidisciplinary team is necessary for dental treatment.
