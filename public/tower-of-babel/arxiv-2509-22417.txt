Uniform Hyperbolicity, Bandgaps and Edge Modes in Aperiodic Systems of Subwavelength Resonators
Habib Ammari, Clemens Thalhammer, Alexander Uhlmann

Abstract
. We aim to characterise the spectral distributions of bi-infinite, semi-infinite, and finite aperiodic one-dimensional arrays of subwavelength resonators, constructed by sampling from a finite library of building blocks. By adopting the modern formalism of uniform hyperbolicity, we are able to strengthen and rigorously prove a Saxon-Hutner-type result, fully characterising the spectral gaps of the composite bi-infinite aperiodic system in terms of its constituent blocks. Crucial to this approach is a change of basis from transfer matrices to propagation matrices, allowing for a block-level characterisation. This approach also enables an explicit characterisation of edge-induced eigenmodes in the semi-infinite setting. Finally, we leverage finite section methods for Jacobi operators to extend our results to finite systems – providing strict bounds for their spectra in terms of their constituent blocks.



Keywords.
Saxon-Hutner theorem, propagation matrices, pseudo-ergodicity, dominated splitting, uniform hyperbolicity, invariant cone criterion, Jacobi operators, block disordered systems, Johnson’s theorem, Coburn’s lemma, semi-infinite systems.

AMS Subject classifications.

35J05, 35P20, 37D20, 37A30, 47B36.

1.
Introduction and motivation

In this work, we study the spectral properties of aperiodically arranged one-dimensional arrays of subwavelength resonators. A subwavelength resonator is a bounded inclusion with a large contrast in material parameters compared to the background material. The high contrast enables the resonators to strongly interact with waves whose wavelength far exceeds the typical resonator size – giving rise to subwavelength resonant eigenmodes,
i.e.
, eigenmodes whose corresponding wavelength is much larger than the resonator size. The quintessential example of such resonant behaviour was first identified by Minnaert
[
36
]
, who studied the exotic acoustic properties of air bubbles in water; see also
[
11
,
30
]
. Subwavelength metamaterials have since emerged as a powerful paradigm for wave manipulation in both acoustic and electromagnetic settings
[
19
,
20
,
33
,
29
,
28
,
13
,
12
,
14
]
.

For our purposes, the central tool enabling the study of such subwavelength resonances is the
capacitance matrix
. Namely, from
[
23
,
9
]
, we know that for an array of
N
subwavelength resonators there exist exactly
N
subwavelength resonant frequencies, characterised in leading order by the eigenproblem for
VC
. Here, the
N\times N
matrix
C
is called the
capacitance matrix
. It encodes the geometry of the array and is tridiagonal and symmetric in the one-dimensional setting. The matrix
V
encodes the material parameters and is always positive-definite and diagonal.

We study aperiodic arrays of such subwavelength resonators, constructed by sampling from a library of finitely many distinct blocks (each potentially containing multiple individual resonators) and arranging them linearly. The resulting systems are strongly disordered in the sense that they are more than just a compact perturbation away from a periodic system, preventing the use of tools such as the truncated Floquet-Bloch transform
[
8
]
suitable for slightly aperiodic settings. As described in
[
7
,
5
]
, such arrays display a rich variety of localisation behaviours and spectral distributions which may nonetheless be characterised by studying the individual resonator block properties. The approach of sampling from a finitely valued library of building blocks was also explored, respectively, in
[
22
]
and
[
15
]
for the Schrödinger operator and in the generic cocycle setting.

As the number
M
of blocks sampled tends to infinity, the capacitance formulation strongly converges to a Jacobi operator (
i.e.
, tridiagonal and self-adjoint) on the lattice
\ell^{2}(\Z)
. Sampling the blocks independently and identically yields an ergodic sequence of resonators and a
metrically transitive
random Jacobi operator
[
37
]
, which can be seen as a generalisation of the widely studied one-dimensional Schrödinger operators with random potentials
[
1
,
21
]
. However, the main results of this paper do not rely on the specific sampling of the block sequence but rather on a type of genericity condition called
pseudo-ergodicity
, ensuring that the sequence contains every possible finite subsequence.

The central objective of this paper is to rigorously characterise the spectral gaps (
i.e.
, the regions devoid of eigenvalues) of the composite bi-infinite, semi-infinite, and finite systems in terms of their constituent blocks – known in the field of condensed matter physics as a
Saxon-Hutner-type
result.
Theorem

3.20
formulates such a result in the modern language of
uniformly hyperbolic
cocycles and provides the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of spectral gaps.

Uniform hyperbolicity is a powerful concept that originated from taking a dynamical system point of view to study the spectra of one-dimensional Schrödinger operators. Essentially, a random family of determinant one matrices
P(j)\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
is
uniformly hyperbolic
if they possess uniformly decaying or growing shared invariant directions. For a one-dimensional lattice Schrödinger Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}:\ell^{2}(\Z)\to\ell^{2}(\Z)
with the associated spectral equation
(\mathcal{H}-\lambda)\bm{v}=0
, the
i
th
transfer matrix
T^{\lambda}(i)\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
transfers forward the tuple
({\bm{v}^{(i)}},{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}})^{\top}
by one entry,
({\bm{v}^{(i+1)}},{\bm{v}^{(i)}})^{\top}=T^{\lambda}(i)({\bm{v}^{(i)}},{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}})^{\top}
, where
{\bm{v}^{(i)}}
denotes the
i
th
entry of
\bm{v}
and the superscript
\top
denotes the transpose.
In a result dating back to
[
26
]
, the uniform hyperbolicity of the family of transfer matrices
T^{\lambda}
at some frequency
\lambda\in\R
was proven to be equivalent to the fact that the corresponding Schrödinger operator exhibiting a gap,
\lambda\notin\sigma(\mathcal{H})
, at that frequency, linking the transfer matrix dynamics to the spectral characteristics.
This approach has led to significant advances in the study of random and quasiperiodic one-dimensional operators
[
25
,
16
,
15
,
38
]
.

For Jacobi operators, the appropriate generalisation for the uniform hyperbolicity of the transfer matrices turns out to be the existence of a
dominated splitting

[
34
,
35
,
2
]
. However, instead of working directly with the transfer matrices, it will prove very fruitful to first perform a change-of-basis to the cohomologous family of
propagation matrices
. Physically, for a given resonator the propagation matrix
P^{\lambda}(i)
propagates the subwavelength resonant solution
u
, in terms of its value and derivative at the left exterior edge of the resonator
(u(x_{i}^{\iL}),u^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\iL}))^{\top}
, across the
i
th
resonator yielding
the identity

(u(x_{i+1}^{\iL}),u^{\prime}(x_{i+1}^{\iL}))^{\top}=P^{\lambda}(i)(u(x_{i}^{\iL}),u^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\iL}))^{\top}.

The key advantage of this approach is that the
i
th
propagation matrix only depends on the properties of the
i
th
resonator, making many analytical approaches significantly more tractable and natural. In particular, this will allow for the collection of individual resonator propagation matrices into block propagation matrices, enabling us to state
Theorem

3.20
in terms of block properties. Furthermore, for the semi-infinite Jacobi operator setting, this approach will allow us to explicitly characterise the additional eigenmodes due to edge effects, as stated in
Theorem

4.4
.

Finally, we will adapt some results on the finite section method of random Jacobi operators to our setting
[
31
,
18
]
. In particular, we will prove a physical version of Coburn’s lemma. Consequently, we can show that the spectra of finite resonator block arrangements are fully determined by the spectra of semi-infinite arrangements. This allows us to, up to the possibility of edge modes, extend the spectral characterisation of block disordered systems in terms of their block propagation matrices to the finite case.

Our main results in
Theorems

3.20
,
4.4
and
4.7
are illustrated in
Figure

1
, showing that, up to some explicitly characterisable edge modes, the spectral distribution of the composite finite-block disordered systems is completely determined by the constituent blocks.

Based on the symmetrisation approach introduced in
[
6
]
,
Theorems

3.20
,
4.4
and
4.7
can be extended to provide a rigorous derivation of the limiting spectral distribution as the number of blocks tends to infinity of one-dimensional block disordered subwavelength resonator systems that are subject to non-reciprocal damping, induced by an imaginary gauge potential. This completely elucidates the mechanisms of localisation in disordered non-reciprocal systems first studied in
[
4
,
3
]
.

Furthermore, in the language of block propagation matrix cocycles, a number of seminal results from the study of random Schrödinger operators may naturally be extended to our limiting bi-infinite Jacobi operator. In particular, the existence and positivity of the Lyapunov exponent
[
24
]
, as well as the corresponding absence of an absolutely continuous spectrum
[
27
]
.

The paper is organised as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce one-dimensional chains of subwavelength resonators, recall the main results on the capacitance matrix approximation, and describe the block disordered construction. In
Section

3
, we introduce the limiting bi-infinite Jacobi operators, as well as their transfer matrix cocycles. We then introduce the dominated splitting version of Johnson’s theorem for Jacobi operators and then proceed to link the existence of a dominated splitting for the transfer matrix cocycle to the uniform hyperbolicity of the block propagation matrix cocycle via a series of lemmas. This ultimately allows us to formulate
Theorem

3.20
. The flow of reductions in
Section

3
is summarised in
Equation

3.15
. In
Section

4
, our aim is to characterise the spectra of semi-infinite and finite systems. In the semi-infinite case, we find the additional modes due to edge effects by studying the block propagation matrix cocycles, culminating in
Theorem

4.4
. Finally, for finite systems, we leverage the theory of finite sections of Jacobi operators to bound the spectra of finite block disordered arrays by the appropriate semi-infinite limits, yielding
Theorem

4.7
.

2.
Setting and tools

We begin by introducing the notions of
block disordered
systems of
subwavelength resonators
and the main tools used to study them, including the capacitance matrix approximation and the propagation matrix formalism.
To that end, this section will contain some results from
[
7
]
, and we refer to this work for further details. For the remainder of this work, for any vector
\bm{v}\in\C^{n}
or
\bm{v}\in\ell^{2}(\Z)
,
{\bm{v}^{(i)}}
shall denote its
i
th
component and
\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert=\big(\sum_{i}\left\lvert{\bm{v}^{(i)}}\right\rvert^{2}\big)^{1/2}
the
\ell^{2}
-norm.

2.1.
Subwavelength resonators

The central systems of interest in this work are one-dimensional chains of subwavelength resonators (see
[
9
,
23
,
10
,
7
]
). That is, we consider an array
\mathcal{D}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}^{\iL},x_{i}^{\iR})
consisting of
N
resonators
D_{i}=(x_{i}^{\iL},x_{i}^{\iR})
. We will denote
\ell_{i}=x_{i}^{\iR}-x_{i}^{\iL}
for
1\leq i\leq N
the
length
of the
i
th
resonator and
s_{i}=x_{i+1}^{\iL}-x_{i}^{\iR}
for
1\leq i\leq N-1
the
spacing
between the
i
th
and
(i+1)
th
resonators.

The resonators are distinct from the background medium due to differing wave speeds and densities that are given by

(2.1)

v(x)=\begin{cases}v_{i}&\text{if }x\in D_{i},\\
v&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{D},\end{cases}\quad\rho(x)=\begin{cases}\rho_{b}&\text{if }x\in D_{i},\\
\rho&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{D}.\end{cases}

After further imposing an outward radiation condition, we obtain the following coupled system of Helmholtz equations for the resonant modes (see
[
23
, (1.6)]
). The resonance problem is to find
\omega
such that there is a nontrivial solution
u
to

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}u+\frac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}u=0,&\text{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{D},\\
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}u+\frac{\omega^{2}}{v_{i}^{2}}u=0,&\text{in }D_{i},i=1,\ldots,N,\\
u|_{\iR}(x^{\iLR}_{i})-u|_{\iL}(x^{\iLR}_{i})=0,&\text{for }i=1,\ldots,N,\\
\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\iR}(x^{\iL}_{i})-\frac{\rho_{b}}{\rho}\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\iL}(x^{\iL}_{i})=0,&\text{for }i=1,\ldots,N,\\
\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\iL}(x^{\iR}_{i})-\frac{\rho_{b}}{\rho}\left.\frac{\dd u}{\dd x}\right|_{\iR}(x^{\iR}_{i})=0,&\text{for }i=1,\ldots,N,\\
\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}|x|}-\mathbf{i}\frac{\omega}{v}\bigg)u(x)=0,&\text{for }|x|\text{ large enough,}\end{cases}

where for a function
w
we denote by

\displaystyle w|_{\iL}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{s\downarrow 0}w(x-s)\quad\mbox{and}\quad w|_{\iR}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{s\downarrow 0}w(x+s)

if the limits exist.

We are interested in the
subwavelength high-contrast regime
. Namely, we denote by
\delta=\rho_{b}/\rho
the
contrast parameter
and consider the resonant frequencies
\omega(\delta)
with non-negative real part that satisfy

\omega(\delta)\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad\delta\to 0.

As shown in
[
23
]
, in this regime there exist exactly
N
subwavelength resonant frequencies, characterised in leading order by a
material matrix

V
that is diagonal and a tridiagonal
capacitance matrix

C
, respectively defined as

(2.3)

\displaystyle V=\diag\left(\frac{v_{1}^{2}}{\ell_{1}},\dots,\frac{v_{N}^{2}}{\ell_{N}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

and

\displaystyle C=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\frac{1}{s_{1}}&-\frac{1}{s_{1}}\\
-\frac{1}{s_{1}}&\frac{1}{s_{1}}+\frac{1}{s_{2}}&-\frac{1}{s_{2}}\\
&-\frac{1}{s_{2}}&\frac{1}{s_{2}}+\frac{1}{s_{3}}&-\frac{1}{s_{3}}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&-\frac{1}{s_{N-2}}&\frac{1}{s_{N-2}}+\frac{1}{s_{N-1}}&-\frac{1}{s_{N-1}}\\
&&&&-\frac{1}{s_{N-1}}&\frac{1}{s_{N-1}}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

The following results are from
[
23
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider a system consisting of
N
subwavelength resonators in
\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exist exactly
N
subwavelength resonant frequencies
\omega(\delta)
that satisfy
\omega(\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
. Furthermore, the
N
resonant frequencies are given by

\omega_{i}(\delta)=\sqrt{\delta\lambda_{i}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

where
(\lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
are the eigenvalues of the eigenvalue problem

VC\bm{u}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\bm{u}_{i}.

Furthermore, the corresponding resonant modes
u_{i}(x)
are approximately given by

u_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}^{(j)}V_{j}(x)+\mathcal{O}(\delta),

where
\bm{u}_{i}^{(j)}
is the
j
th
entry of the vector
\bm{u}_{i}
and
V_{j}(x)
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, are defined as the solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\frac{\dd{{}^{2}}}{\dd x^{2}}V_{j}(x)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{D},\\
V_{j}(x)=\delta_{ij},&x\in D_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,N,\\
V_{j}(x)=\BO(1),&\mbox{as }\left\lvert x\right\rvert\to\infty.\end{cases}

We can thus fully understand the subwavelength resonant modes by studying the eigenvalue problem for the
generalised capacitance matrix

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq VC
and will often use
\lambda_{i}
and
\omega_{i}
interchangeably, where it is clear from context. Note that the eigenvalue problem for
\mathcal{C}
plays an analogous role as the tight-binding model for Schrödinger operators. Therefore, we refer to it as the discrete model for the Helmholtz operator in (
2.2
).

2.2.
Block disordered systems

Since we are interested in the band structure of disordered systems, where local translation invariance is broken, we must develop ways to construct and describe such systems. As discussed in
[
7
]
, one such way is to consider a finite number of distinct
“building blocks”
consisting of (possibly multiple) subwavelength resonators. Later, constructing disordered resonator arrays from simple building blocks will enable us to characterise the “unusual” spectral properties of the array by looking at the building blocks.

A subwavelength block disordered system is a chain of subwavelength resonators consisting of
M
blocks of resonators
B_{\chi^{(j)}}
sampled accordingly to a sequence

\chi\in\{1,\dots,D\}^{M}\eqqcolon D^{M}

from a selection
B_{1},\dots,B_{D}
of
D
distinct resonator blocks, arranged on a line. Each resonator block
B_{j}
is characterised by a sequence of tuples
(v_{1},\ell_{1},s_{1}),\dots,(v_{\len(B_{j})},\ell_{\len(B_{j})},s_{\len(B_{j})})
that denote the wave speed, length, and spacing of each constituent resonator. Here,
\len(B_{j})
denotes the total number of resonators contained within the block
B_{j}
.

We will often abuse notation and write
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}B_{\chi^{(j)}}
to denote the resonator array constructed by sampling the blocks
B_{1},\dots B_{D}
according to
\chi\in D^{M}
and then arranging them in a line. Having thus constructed an array of subwavelength resonators, we can alternatively write
\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}
in line with
Section

2.1
.
Note that as
M
denotes the total number of sampled blocks and
N
the total number of resonators, we always have
M\leq N
.

2.3.
Block sequences

Apart from the makeup of the individual blocks, the crucial factor determining the behaviour of block disordered systems is how these blocks are arranged. To enable later analysis, we will introduce the case of block sequences sampled independently and identically in detail. We consider a block distribution with sampling probabilities
p_{1},\dots,p_{D}
, such that
\sum_{d=1}^{D}p_{d}=1
and
p_{d}>0
for all
d=1,\dots,D
.

We can make this sampling formal in a probability-theoretic sense.

Definition 2.3

(Independently and identically sampled discrete sequences)
.

We slightly overload notation to denote the discrete sequence space
D^{\Z}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,D\}^{\Z}
and construct the probability space of
independently and identically sampled discrete sequences

(D^{\Z},\mathcal{F},\prob)
as follows. Let

C(i_{1},\dots,i_{n},X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{n})\coloneqq\{\chi\in D^{\Z}\mid(\chi^{(i_{1})},\dots,\chi^{(i_{n})})\in X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{n}\}

be the
cylinder set
for
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\subset\{1,\dots,D\}
and
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in\Z
. On these cylinder sets, we define the probability measure

\prob(C(i_{1},\dots,i_{n},X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{n}))\coloneqq F(X_{1})\cdot\dots\cdot F(X_{n}),

where
F(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}p_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{j}(X)
is the block probability distribution.
Extending
\prob
to the minimal
\sigma
-algebra containing all cylinder sets
\mathcal{F}
, we obtain the probability space
(D^{\Z},\mathcal{F},\prob)
.

Finally, we endow
D^{\Z}=\{1,\dots D\}^{\Z}
with the topology obtained by taking the countable product of the discrete topologies on
\{1,\dots D\}
. In this topology,
D^{\Z}
is a compact metric space.

We can extend this definition to finite sequences on
D^{M}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,D\}^{M}
by employing the
truncation projection

\mathcal{P}_{M}:D^{\Z}\to D^{M}
to push forward the probability space
(D^{\Z},\mathcal{F},\prob)
to
(D^{M},\mathcal{P}_{M}^{*}\mathcal{F},\mathcal{P}_{M}^{*}\prob)
.

A key property of the family of
i.i.d.
sequences is its metric transitivity under the group of shifts.

Definition 2.4

(Metrically transitive group)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\prob)
be a probability space and
\mathcal{S}
a topological group of automorphisms on
\Omega
. Then,
\mathcal{S}
is called
metrically transitive
if

(i)

\mathcal{S}
is
stochastically continuous
,
i.e.
,
\lim_{S_{1}\to S}\prob(S_{1}X\triangle SX)=0
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
X\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference;

(ii)

\mathcal{S}
is measure-preserving,
i.e.
,
\prob(SX)=\prob(X)
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
X\in\mathcal{F}
;

(iii)

any
\mathcal{S}
-invariant set
X\in\mathcal{F}
has either probability
1
or
0
.

Here,
\mathcal{S}
-invariance of a set
X\in\mathcal{F}
is understood to mean
SX=X
for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
.

The following follows immediately from the cylinder set construction.

Lemma 2.5
.

The group of shifts
\mathcal{S}=\{S^{j}\mid j\in\Z\}
where
(S^{j}\chi)(i)=\chi(i+j)
is a metrically transitive group of automorphisms on
(D^{\Z},\mathcal{F}_{D},\prob_{D})
.

Another crucial notion is the idea of
pseudo-ergodicity
of an element
\chi\in D^{\Z}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathbb{I}\in\{\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{N}\}
. We say that a sequence
\chi\in D^{\mathbb{I}}
is
pseudo-ergodic
if any finite sequence
\chi^{\prime}\in D^{M}
, where
M\in\mathbb{N}
, is contained in
\chi
.

More precisely, for any
\chi^{\prime}\in D^{M}
, there must exist
j\in\Z
such that
\mathcal{P}_{M}S^{j}\chi=\chi^{\prime}
. We will denote this by
\chi^{\prime}\prec\chi
.

Pseudo-ergodicity of
\chi
is equivalent to the orbit
\operatorname{Orb}(\chi):=\{S^{j}\chi\mid j\in\Z\}
being dense in
D^{\Z}
and is a kind of genericity condition in the sense that it occurs with probability one for
i.i.d.
sequences.

Lemma 2.7
.

Any sequence
\chi\in D^{\Z}
is pseudo-ergodic with probability one.

Proof.

We define the sets

\Psi_{M}\coloneqq\{\chi\in D^{\Z}\mid\chi^{\prime}\prec\chi\text{ for all }\chi^{\prime}\in D^{M}\},

which are
\mathcal{S}
-invariant and have
\prob(\Psi_{M})>0
for all
M\in\N
. The latter part follows after realising that finding all sequences
\chi^{\prime}\in D^{M}
lined up sequentially starting at
0
occurs with nonzero probability if
p_{d}>0
for all
d=1,\dots,D
. By metric transitivity, we must thus have
\prob(\Psi_{M})=1
for all
M\in\N
.

On the other hand, we denote
\Psi:=\{\chi\in D^{\Z}\mid\chi\text{ pseudo-ergodic}\}
and find

\Psi=\bigcap_{M=1}^{\infty}\Psi_{M}.

But due to the fact that
\prob(\Psi_{M})=1
for all
M
, we also must have
\prob(\Psi)=1
, as desired.
∎

2.4.
Resonator sequences

At this point, we would also like to connect the sampling of blocks to the corresponding sequence of resonators. Consider a block disordered system with blocks
B_{1},\dots B_{D}
sampled with probability
p_{1},\dots,p_{D}
yielding an
i.i.d.
block sequence
\chi\in D^{\Z}
. This uniquely determines the sequence of resonators, which we shall encode using tuples from the set

R\coloneqq\left\{(d,r)\mid d\in\{1,\dots D\},r\in\{1,\dots,\len(B_{d})\}\right\}\subset\N^{2}.

The tuple
(d,r)
denotes the
r
th
resonator of the
d
th
block. We thus get a bijective map

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Phi:D^{\Z}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)\coloneqq\Phi(D^{\Z})\subset R^{\Z}

\displaystyle\chi

\displaystyle\mapsto\alpha\coloneqq(\dots,(\chi^{(0)},1),\dots,(\chi^{(0)},\len(B_{\chi^{(0)}})),(\chi^{(1)},1),\dots),

which we can use to push forward the
i.i.d.
probability space
(D^{\Z},\mathcal{F},\prob)
onto the isomorphic
(\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z),\mathcal{F}_{R},\prob_{R})
, where
\mathcal{F}_{R}\coloneqq\Phi^{*}\mathcal{F}
and
\prob_{R}\coloneqq\Phi^{*}\prob
. Thus,
\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
is the space of all valid resonator sequences obtained from a block sequence via
\Phi
.

From this construction, it is easy to see that any resonator sequence
\mu=\Phi(\chi)\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
is a bi-infinite Markov chain with finite state space (also known as a two-sided subshift of finite type). In particular, if a resonator tuple
(d,r)
is not at the end of the block (so
r<\len(B_{d})
), then it is deterministically followed by
(d,r+1)
. Conversely, if
(d,r)
is at the end of a block (so
r=\len(B_{d})
), then it is followed by one of
\{(1,1),\dots,(D,1)\}
with probability
p_{1},\dots,p_{D}
, respectively. From this observation, it follows that this Markov chain is homogeneous and irreducible, as the transition probabilities are independent of location and any state is reachable from any other.

Example 2.8
.

Consider a block disordered system with blocks that are either a single resonator
B_{1}=B_{single}
or a dimer block
B_{2}=B_{dimer}
, as in
Example

2.2
. We sample the blocks
i.i.d.
with
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
occurring with probability
p_{1}
and
p_{2}=1-p_{1}
, respectively, yielding a sequence
\chi\in 2^{\Z}
.
The corresponding resonator sequence
\alpha=\Phi(\chi)\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
consists of the tuples
R=\{(1,1),(2,1),(2,2)\}
encoding the single resonator and the two dimer resonators, respectively. The finite state space
R
then allows us to collect the transition probabilities in the
transition matrix

P=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}&p_{2}&0\\
0&0&1\\
p_{1}&p_{2}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
P_{ij}
is the transition probability from the
i
th
state to the
j
th
state and we encode the block tuples
(1,1),(2,1),(2,2)
as states
1,2,3
, respectively.

Lemma 2.9
.

The group of shifts
\mathcal{S}=\{S^{j}\mid j\in\Z\}
where
(S^{j}\alpha)(i)=\alpha(i+j)
is a metrically transitive group of automorphisms on
(\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z),\mathcal{F}_{R},\prob_{R})
.

Proof.

This result follows from
[
37
, Example 1.15b)]
where the essential ingredients are the fact that we consider homogeneous Markov chains and that the finite state space together with irreducibility ensures the uniqueness of the stationary state.
∎

Finally, in this subsection, we note that
\Phi
also allows us to push forward the topology of
D^{\Z}
as well as the definition of pseudo-ergodicity onto the space of resonator sequences
\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
. This equips
\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
with the structure of a compact metric space, equivalent to taking the subspace topology
\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)\subset R^{\Z}
, where
R^{\Z}
is again equipped with the topology of the countable product of discrete spaces.
Furthermore, the pushforward of the pseudo-ergodicity definition matches
Definition

2.6
in the sense that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
we have

\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\text{ pseudo-ergodic}\iff\alpha^{\prime}\in\alpha\quad\forall\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(M)\iff\overline{\operatorname{Orb}(\alpha)}=\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z),

where
\mathbb{L}_{R}(M)
is the space of finite truncations of sequences in
\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
.
Note that this does not imply that a pseudo-ergodic
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
must contain
every

\alpha^{\prime}\in R^{M}
but merely the finite truncations of sequences obtained from block sequences and thus in the image of
\Phi
.

3.
Jacobi operators and cocycles

In the infinite block sequence limit, the subwavelength resonance problem is no longer described by a capacitance matrix
\mathcal{C}=VC
but a real Jacobi operator on
\ell^{2}(\Z)
.

Definition 3.1

(Jacobi Operator)
.

A (real)
Jacobi operator
is a symmetric tridiagonal operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\Z)
. It can be described by two real sequences
{\bm{a}^{(i)}},{\bm{b}^{(i)}}\in\ell^{\infty}(\Z)
and is given by

\displaystyle J:\ell^{2}(\Z)

\displaystyle\to\ell^{2}(\Z)

\displaystyle{\bm{v}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle\mapsto(J\bm{v})^{(i)}={\bm{a}^{(i-1)}}{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}}+{\bm{a}^{(i)}}{\bm{v}^{(i+1)}}+{\bm{b}^{(i)}}{\bm{v}^{(i)}}.

Because
{\bm{a}^{(i)}}
and
{\bm{b}^{(i)}}
are bounded and
J
is tridiagonal and symmetric,
J:\ell^{2}(\Z)\to\ell^{2}(\Z)
is a well-defined, linear, bounded, and self-adjoint operator.

We note that the symmetry requirement on
J
does not pose any issues since the capacitance matrix eigenvalue problem for
VC
demonstrated in
Theorem

2.1
is equivalent to the symmetric eigenvalue problem for
V^{\frac{1}{2}}CV^{\frac{1}{2}}\sim VC
.

Remark 3.2
.

As we aim to understand the spectrum of large block disordered systems, we investigate the symmetrised capacitance matrix
V^{\frac{1}{2}}CV^{\frac{1}{2}}
in the large resonator array asymptotic
N\to\infty
. Here,
N\to\infty
is understood as starting with an infinite array of subwavelength resonators (not necessarily arranged in blocks) described by sequences of wave speeds
(v_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
, resonator lengths
(\ell_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
and spacings
(s_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
and constructing a sequence of increasingly large capacitance matrices
\mathcal{J}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2N+1)\times(2N+1)}
from the truncated sequences
(v_{i})_{i=-N}^{N},(\ell_{i})_{i=-N}^{N},(s_{i})_{i=-N}^{N}
.

If we consider
(J_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
as a sequence of operators on the sequence space
\ell^{2}(\Z)
, we find that this sequence converges strongly to a Jacobi operator
\mathcal{J}:\ell^{2}(\Z)\to\ell^{2}(\Z)
with the following bands:

(3.1)

{\bm{a}^{(i)}}=-\frac{v_{i}v_{i+1}}{s_{i}\sqrt{\ell_{i}\ell_{i+1}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{b}^{(i)}}=\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{\ell_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{s_{i-1}}+\frac{1}{s_{i}}\right).

Given the blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{D},
any sequence
\chi\in D^{\Z}
uniquely determines a sequence of resonators
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
and thus also unique sequences of wave speeds
(v_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
, resonator lengths
(\ell_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
and spacings
(s_{i})_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}
. Condition
Equation

3.1
thus allows us to define a Jacobi operator
\mathcal{J}(\alpha)
for any resonator sequence
\alpha
, making
\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)\to\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\Z))
a
random operator
on the probability space of the resonator sequences
(\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z),\mathcal{F}_{R},\prob_{R})
. We will often overload the notation to mean
\mathcal{J}(\chi)\coloneqq\mathcal{J}(\Phi(\chi))
.

We have the following result from
[
2
, Proposition 2]
ensuring the almost sure invariance of the spectrum

\sigma(\mathcal{J}(\alpha))
.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
is pseudo-ergodic, then we have

\sigma(\mathcal{J}(\alpha^{\prime}))\subset\sigma(J(\alpha))

for
any

\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
.

3.1.
Transfer matrix cocycles

To study the spectral properties of such tridiagonal Jacobi operators
\mathcal{J}
, it will prove very fruitful to investigate the solutions of the
spectral equation

(3.2)

(\mathcal{J}-\lambda)\bm{v}=0,

for some formal solution
\bm{v}\in\C^{\Z}
, not necessarily
\ell^{2}
-summable or even bounded.

We can exploit the tridiagonal structure to obtain the following iterative characterisation of
\bm{v}
.

Definition 3.4
.

For any resonator sequence
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}_{R}(\Z)
and frequency
\lambda\geq 0
, we define the
transfer matrices
(also known as the
Jacobi cocycle map
) as

T^{\lambda}:\Z\to\operatorname{GL}(2,\R)
given by

(3.3)

T^{\lambda}(i)\coloneqq\frac{1}{{\bm{a}^{(i)}}}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-{\bm{b}^{(i)}}&-{\bm{a}^{(i-1)}}\\
{\bm{a}^{(i)}}&0\end{pmatrix}

for any
i\in\Z
, where
{\bm{a}^{(i)}}
and
{\bm{b}^{(i)}}
are defined by (
3.1
).

Notably, for any
\bm{v}\in\C^{\Z}
solving
Equation

3.2
, we have

(3.4)

\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{v}^{(i+1)}}\\
{\bm{v}^{(i)}}\end{pmatrix}=T^{\lambda}(i)\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{v}^{(i)}}\\
{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}}\end{pmatrix}

for all
i\in\Z
.

We further define the
cocycle iteration
as

(3.5)

T^{\lambda}_{n}(i)\coloneqq\begin{cases}T^{\lambda}(i+n-1)\cdot\cdot\cdot T^{\lambda}(i),&n\geq 1,\\
I_{2},&n=0,\\
(T^{\lambda}(i+n))^{-1}\cdot\cdot\cdot(T^{\lambda}(i-1))^{-1},&n\leq-1,\end{cases}

and find

(3.6)

\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{v}^{(i+n)}}\\
{\bm{v}^{(i+n-1)}}\end{pmatrix}=T_{n}^{\lambda}(i)\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{v}^{(i)}}\\
{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}}\end{pmatrix}

for all
n\in\Z
.

We note that, by
Equation

3.1
, we have
{\bm{a}^{(i)}}\neq 0
for all
i\in\Z
and therefore,
Equation

3.3
is well defined.

It will often prove useful to think of the transfer matrices acting on the
real projective space
.

Definition 3.5

(Real projective space)
.

Consider the equivalence relation
\sim
on
\R^{2}
defined by
\bm{u}\sim\bm{v}
if and only if
\bm{u}=\lambda\bm{v}
for
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\R^{2}
and
\lambda\in\R
. The
real projective space
(of dimension one)
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
is then defined as the quotient
\R^{2}/\sim
. For any
\bm{u}\in\R^{2}
, we will denote by
\overline{\bm{u}}
the equivalence class of
\bm{u}
under
\sim
.

It is clear that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the one-dimensional subspaces of
\R^{2}
and the elements of
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
.
Furthermore, the group
\operatorname{GL}(2,\R)
acts on
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
via the matrix-vector product of representatives,
i.e.
,
A\overline{u}\coloneqq\overline{Au}
.

We can define a metric on
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
by

(3.7)

\displaystyle d:\mathbb{RP}^{1}\times\mathbb{RP}^{1}

\displaystyle\to\R

\displaystyle(\overline{\bm{u}},\overline{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sqrt{1-{\left(\frac{\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\right\rangle}{\left\lVert\bm{u}\right\rVert\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert}\right)}^{2}}=\sin\measuredangle(\overline{\bm{u}},\overline{\bm{v}}),

where
\measuredangle(\overline{\bm{u}},\overline{\bm{v}})
denotes the angle between
\overline{\bm{u}}
and
\overline{\bm{v}}
.

A crucial concept relating the properties of the transfer matrix cocycle to the spectrum of
\mathcal{J}
is the notion of
dominated splitting
, adapted from
[
2
]
.

Definition 3.6

(Dominated splitting)
.

We say that

T\in\ell^{\infty}(\Z,\operatorname{GL}(2,\R))

admits a
dominated splitting
(DS) if there exist two maps
s,u:\Z\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
such that

(DS1)

T
-invariance:

For every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

T(i)u(i)=u(i+1)\quad\text{and}\quad T(i)s(i)=s(i+1);

(DS2)

Domination:

There exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta>1
such that

\bigl\lVert T_{N}(i)\,\vec{u}(i)\bigr\rVert\;>\;\eta\,\bigl\lVert T_{N}(i)\,\vec{s}(i)\bigr\rVert

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and all unit vectors

\vec{u}(i)\in u(i)
and

\vec{s}(i)\in s(i)
, where

T_{N}(i)=T(i+N-1)\cdot\cdot\cdot T(i);

(DS3)

Angle separation:

There exists
\delta>0
such that

d\bigl(u(i),s(i)\bigr)>\delta\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathbb{Z},

where
d
is defined by (
3.7
);

(DS4)

Non-degeneracy:

For the
N\in\mathbb{N}
chosen in (DS2), we have

\inf_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\left\lVert T_{N}(i)\right\rVert>0.

In this case, we write
T\in\mathcal{DS}
.

The reason why the existence of a dominated splitting is so crucial is that it completely determines the spectral gaps of
\mathcal{J}
; see
[
2
, Theorem 4]
.

Theorem 3.7

(Dominated splitting version of Johnson’s theorem)
.

\lambda\notin\sigma(\mathcal{J})\iff T^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{DS}.

This, together with
Proposition

3.3
, gives an almost-sure characterisation of the spectrum for any resonator sequence.

Intuitively, this theorem holds because if
T^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{DS}
, the stable and unstable directions allow the construction of a Green function, ensuring that
\mathcal{J}-\lambda
is invertible. Conversely, if
\lambda
lies in the resolvent of
\mathcal{J}
, one may use the fact that, by a Combes-Thomas-type estimate, the Green function decays exponentially to construct a dominated splitting.

The remainder of this section will consist of a series of reductions enabling us to find simple necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a dominated splitting in terms of block properties. An overview of these reductions is given in
Equation

3.15
.

3.2.
Propagation matrix cocycle

The transfer matrix cocycle has two key weaknesses. First, we have in general

\det T^{\lambda}(i)={\bm{a}^{(i-1)}}/{\bm{a}^{(i)}}\neq 1,

which prevents the use of the theory of
\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
-cocycles from being used. And secondly, the
i
th
transfer matrix
T^{\lambda}(i)
depends not only on the properties of the
i
th
resonator but also on the surrounding resonators. This complicates the association of the resonator properties with the transfer matrix properties.

To remedy this, we perform a change of basis at the transfer matrix level and switch to propagation matrices.

Definition 3.8

(Propagation matrix)
.

We define the
conjugacy sequence

Q:\Z\to\operatorname{GL}(2,\R)
as

(3.8)

Q(i)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{v_{i}}{\sqrt{\ell_{i}}}&0\\
\displaystyle\frac{v_{i}}{\ell_{i}s_{i-1}}&\displaystyle-\frac{v_{i-1}}{\ell_{i-1}s_{i-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

The
propagation matrix cocycle
is then defined as
P^{\lambda}:\Z\to\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)

(3.9)

P^{\lambda}(i)\coloneqq Q(i+1)T^{\lambda}(i)(Q(i))^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle 1-s_{i}\frac{\ell_{i}}{v_{i}^{2}}\lambda&s_{i}\\
\displaystyle-\frac{\ell_{i}}{v_{i}^{2}}\lambda&1\end{pmatrix},

and the cocycle iteration
P^{\lambda}_{n}(i)
is defined analogously to
T^{\lambda}_{n}(i)
.

This relation between
T^{\lambda}
and
P^{\lambda}
is a cocycle cohomology.

Definition 3.9

(Cocycle cohomology)
.

We say that two cocycles
A,B:\Z\to\operatorname{GL}(2,\R)
are
cohomologous
if there exists a conjugacy sequence
Q:\Z\to\operatorname{GL}(2,\R)
such that

(3.10)

A(i)=Q(i+1)B(i)(Q(i))^{-1}

for all
i\in\Z
.

We note that cocycle cohomology interacts nicely with the cocycle iteration, and we have

(3.11)

P^{\lambda}_{n}(i)=Q(i+n)T_{n}^{\lambda}(i)(Q(i))^{-1}

for all
n\in\Z
. From
Equation

3.9
, it is immediately clear that the propagation matrix remedies the two issues of the transfer matrix outlined above. That is, it always has
\det P^{\lambda}(i)=1
and depends only on the properties
\ell_{i},s_{i},v_{i}
of the
i
th
resonator.

Remark 3.10
.

In the literature (see, for instance,
[
32
, Chapter 11]
),

\det T^{\lambda}(i)={\bm{a}^{(i-1)}}/{\bm{a}^{(i)}}\neq 1

is often overcome by choosing the conjugacy sequence

B(i)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&{\bm{a}^{(i)}}\end{pmatrix}

corresponding to a change of basis
({\bm{v}^{(i+1)}},{\bm{v}^{(i)}})^{\top}\to({\bm{v}^{(i+1)}},{\bm{a}^{(i)}}{\bm{v}^{(i)}})^{\top}
.

The conjugacy sequence
Q
is based on a similar idea with clear physical intuition:
We can split
Q(i)=D(i)V(i)
with

D(i)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{i-1}}&\displaystyle-\frac{1}{s_{i-1}}\end{pmatrix},\quad V(i)=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{v_{i}}{\sqrt{\ell_{i}}}&0\\
0&\displaystyle\frac{v_{i-1}}{\sqrt{\ell_{i-1}}}\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
V(i)
then corresponds to the change of basis
({\bm{v}^{(i)}},{\bm{v}^{(i-1)}})^{\top}\to({\bm{u}^{(i)}},{\bm{u}^{(i-1)}})^{\top}
undoing the similarity transform
V^{\frac{1}{2}}CV^{\frac{1}{2}}\sim VC
that was performed to obtain the Jacobi operator
\mathcal{J}
from the capacitance problem
VC
. Following
Theorem

2.1
,
{\bm{u}^{(i)}}
can thus physically be understood to be the first-order approximant of the solution
u(x_{i}^{\iL})
of
Equation

2.2
on the resonator
D_{i}
.

Now, to remedy the non-locality of
T^{\lambda}
,
D(i)
performs a change of basis from

({\bm{u}^{(i)}},{\bm{u}^{(i-1)}})^{\top}=(u(x_{i}^{\iL}),u(x_{i-1}^{\iL}))^{\top}\to(u(x_{i}^{\iL}),u^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\iL}))^{\top},

i.e.
, to the tuple
(u(x_{i}^{\iL}),u^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\iL}))^{\top}
encoding
u(x_{i}^{\iL})
together with the exterior derivative
u^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\iL})=\lim_{x\uparrow x_{i}^{\iL}}\frac{d}{dx}u(x)
. Here, the form of
D(i)
again follows from the first-order approximation of
u(x)
obtained in
Theorem

2.1
.

For a propagation matrix cocycle in
\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
, we can now define the concept of uniform hyperbolicity
[
38
]
, which is a strengthened version of the dominated splitting for
\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
-matrices.

Definition 3.11

(Uniform hyperbolicity)
.

We say that a cocycle

P\in\ell^{\infty}(\Z,\operatorname{SL}(2,\R))

is
uniformly hyperbolic
(UH) if
there exist two maps
s,u:\Z\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
such that

(UH1)

P
-invariance:

For every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

P(i)u(i)=u(i+1)\quad\text{and}\quad P(i)s(i)=s(i+1);

(UH2)

Uniform growth:

There exist
C>0
and
\eta>1
such that

\left\lVert P_{-n}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert,\left\lVert P_{n}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert\leq C\eta^{-n}

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\in\N
and all unit vectors

\vec{u}(i)\in u(i)
and

\vec{s}(i)\in s(i)
.

In this case, we write
P\in\mathcal{UH}
.

Indeed, for
\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
-matrices, uniform hyperbolicity and dominated splitting are equivalent.

Lemma 3.12
.

Consider a cocycle
P:\Z\to\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
. Then

P\in\mathcal{UH}\iff P\in\mathcal{DS}.

Proof.

“
\implies
”
: Let
P\in\ell^{\infty}(\Z,\operatorname{SL}(2,\R))
uniformly hyperbolic. Then conditions (DS1) and (DS2) follow immediately, (DS4) follows from the fact that
P(i)\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
, and (DS3) is
[
38
, Lemma 2]
.

“
\impliedby
”
: We now consider
P\in\ell^{\infty}(\Z,\operatorname{SL}(2,\R))
admitting a dominated splitting
u,s:\Z\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
. We want to prove that
P
is also uniformly hyperbolic (no necessarily with the same
u,s
) and use
[
38
, Theorem 1]
to equivalently prove that there exists
c>0,\xi>1
such that

\left\lVert P_{n}(i)\right\rVert\geq c\xi^{n}

for all
n\in\N
and
i\in\Z
.

We can repeatedly apply (DS2) to find

Claim:

\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)u(i)\right\rVert>\eta^{K}\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)s(i)\right\rVert\quad\text{for all }i\in\Z,K\in\N.

Proof.

We proceed by induction and let
i\in\Z
. The case
K=1
follows by (DS2).
For the induction step we again use (DS2) with
i^{\prime}=i+(K-1)N
to have

\left\lVert P_{N}(i+(K-1)N)\vec{u}(i+(K-1)N)\right\rVert>\eta\left\lVert P_{N}(i+(K-1)N)\vec{s}(i+(K-1)N)\right\rVert.

Using (DS1) repeatedly, we find

\vec{u}(i+(K-1)N)=\frac{P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{u}(i)}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert},\quad\vec{s}(i+(K-1)N)=\frac{P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert},

and plugging this into the above inequality yields

\frac{\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert}>\eta\frac{\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert}.

Now, by the induction hypothesis, we have

\frac{\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert}{\eta^{K-1}\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert}>\frac{\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{u}(i)\right\rVert}>\eta\frac{\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert}{\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert},

and the claim follows after multiplying both sides of the inequality by
\eta^{K-1}\left\lVert P_{(K-1)N}(i)\vec{s}(i)\right\rVert
.
∎

For any
K\in\N
and
i\in\Z
, we denote
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq 0
the singular values of
P_{KN}(i)
and have

\sigma_{1}\geq\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)u(i)\right\rVert>\eta^{K}\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)s(i)\right\rVert\geq\eta^{K}\sigma_{2}.

Crucially, because
\det P(i)=1
for all
i
, we also have
\det P_{KN}(i)=1
and thus

\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}=\left\lvert\det P_{KN}(i)\right\rvert=1.

Plugging this into the inequality above yields

\sigma_{1}=\left\lVert P_{KN}(i)\right\rVert>\sqrt{\eta}^{K},

ensuring that the norm of
P_{KN}(i)
grows exponentially in
K

\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{J}(\chi_{+}))\setminus\sigma(\mathcal{J}(\chi^{\prime}))
for some

4.3.
Spectral bounds for finite arrays

Having characterised the spectra of semi-infinite Jacobi operators, we are now in a position to do so for the finite case. Intuitively, this is due to the fact that for large system sizes

Theorem 4.7

Define

and let

Let

\chi_{M}

\mathcal{J}_{N}(\chi_{M})-\lambda
is singular, but
\lambda\notin\Sigma
. Let

where the sequence of real numbers

Appendix A
Dynamically defined cocycles

In this paper, we have considered
\operatorname{SL}(2,\R)
-cocycles as maps

However, there is an alternative but closely related formalism of

\displaystyle(S,A):\Omega\times\R^{2}

\displaystyle\to\Omega\times\R^{2}

\displaystyle(\chi,\bm{v})

\displaystyle\mapsto(S(\chi),A(\chi)\bm{v}).

In this formalism, uniform hyperbolicity is defined as follows.

We say that a dynamically defined cocycle

there exist two maps

A

For every
\chi\in\Omega

There exist

such that

for all
\chi\in\Omega
,

and all unit vectors

and

Here, analogously to the sequential case,
