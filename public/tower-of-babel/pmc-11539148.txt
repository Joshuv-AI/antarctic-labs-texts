Protocol for cell proliferation and cell death analysis of primary muscle stem cell culture using flow cytometry
Authors: Pauline Garcia, Orane Mercier, Jade Ravent, Fabien Le Grand
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySkeletal muscle is critically dependent on the function of muscle stem cells (MuSCs) for effective muscle repair following injury. Here, we detail a protocol for the isolation of primary muscle cells and subsequent analysis of proliferation capacity in vitro using EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) on fixed cells. We also describe a cell death analysis on living cells with the identification of early- and late-apoptotic cells, as well as necrotic cells, through the incorporation of propidium iodide and YO-PRO-1 staining.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Garcia et al.1Subject areas: cell biology, cell culture, cell isolation, flow cytometry, microscopy, stem cells

Before you begin

The muscle microenvironment, composed of various interacting cell types, plays a crucial role during muscle regeneration.2 Disruptions in these interactions can affect the entire regeneration process.3 Muscle Stem Cells (MuSCs) are central to this process, which, upon injury, exit from quiescence and activate, proliferate, differentiate into myocytes, and fuse to form new myofibers. This myogenic process is tightly regulated both spatially and temporally by transcriptional and epigenetic factors. However, the specific role of epigenetic regulation in MuSC function remains poorly understood. We characterized the role of the histone methyltransferase SETDB1, known for its involvement in chromatin compaction and gene repression, in protecting MuSCs against the de-repression of transposable elements.1 Such de-repression is perceived as a pathogen by MuSCs, leading to proliferation arrest and cell death.

While protocols have been established to assess cell proliferation and apoptosis in MuSCs through immunostaining, these methods present certain limitations. Notably, MuSCs exhibit high sensitivity and are prone to detachment, introducing a bias in cell death measurements when performing the TUNEL assay.4,5

In this protocol, we describe the steps to perform simultaneous cell death and cell proliferation analysis via flow cytometry of myoblasts extracted from the same hindlimb muscles using EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine), Propidium Iodide (PI) and YO-PRO-1.

EdU is a thymidine analog that integrates into DNA during the S-phase of the cell cycle, similar to BrdU (Bromodeoxyuridine), but with the advantage of being non-reactive in biological systems. The detection of EdU utilizes a copper (I)-catalyzed click chemistry reaction, where an azide-modified fluorescent dye covalently binds to the incorporated EdU, forming a stable triazole ring. This method is advantageous because it offers a straightforward protocol that can be completed in under 30 min, producing more reproducible results and a higher signal-to-noise ratio compared to other techniques.

PI is a fluorochrome that binds to double-stranded DNA and RNA, primarily by intercalating between base pairs. Widely used in flow cytometry, PI is a membrane-impermeant dye, meaning it is typically excluded from viable cells. When used for staining, PI provides valuable information about the cell cycle and DNA content within a cell population.

YO-PRO-1 is a green fluorescent DNA marker that binds to the DNA of cells with compromised plasma membranes, making it a useful early marker for cell death. As a nuclear marker, YO-PRO-1 selectively binds to the DNA of dying cells, providing insight into cell viability and the progression of cell death.

Muscle hindlimb dissection and digestion

Timing: 20 min

This step explains how to dissect hindlimb muscles from the mice followed by a mechanical digestion.

1.Euthanize the mouse according to ethical procedures.

Euthanize the mouse according to ethical procedures.

2.To prevent fur contamination, begin by spraying the entire mouse body with 70% ethanol, remove skin, and extract hindlimb. Dissection of mice hindlimbs skeletal muscles is done as follows;a.Position the mouse on its back, and secure one hindlimb to a raised clamp to hold the leg in an extended, vertical position. Carefully pinch the outer skin of the hindlimb, and gently cut and remove the skin and fascia to reveal the underlying muscles of the leg.b.Locate the gastrocnemius muscle, which runs along the back of the lower leg. Firmly grip the Achilles tendon (distal end) of the gastrocnemius and gently pull the muscle toward its origin at the femur. Cut the muscle as close to the femur as possible, and place it in a dish for further processing.c.Next, locate the tibialis anterior muscle, which originates from the anterolateral surface of the tibia and inserts at the medial cuneiform via its distal tendon. Gently separate the muscle belly from the underlying connective tissue. Cut the distal tendon near its attachment. Then, cut the muscle at its origin as close as possible to the tibia to fully detach it.d.Then, harvest the quadriceps femoris of the mouse by cutting all along the bone.e.Finally, do the same with the gluteal muscles by cutting all along the bone.

Expected outcomes

The protocol allows for analysis of cell proliferation and cell death coming from the same muscle hindlimb extracted primary muscle cells and the same passage, giving robust results. The cell proliferation assay provides cell proportion in S phase, but also in G1 and M phase of the cell cycle. Moreover, the cell death assay allows to obtain the percentage of early apoptotic cells, late apoptotic cells, and necrotic cells.

Limitations

Muscle primary cells display different growth capacity, therefore biological samples might not be treated at the same time if they do not display the same number of cells after 4 days of growth. Consistent results need to be done on the same number of cells between experimental replicates. Inversely, make sure that there are enough cells in each sample to record enough events. If not, the results obtained will not be relevant.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The number of cells detected by the flow cytometer is too low.

Potential solution

Make sure that the parameters were correctly set up and check the event trigger set up. Please ensure that there are enough cells in the controls (unstained, PI-only, EdU only and YO-PRO-1 only) in order to appropriately perform the compensation. Some cells can also be lost with washes. To overcome this loss, seed more cells at the beginning of the experiment.

Problem 2

The number of PAX7-positive cells is under 80%.

Potential solution

Please make sure that the columns are correctly attached to the MACS strainer. If so, you can change the LS column with LD columns, more stringent for the cell selection. You can also add an α7-integrin positive selection if there are too many fibroblasts.

Problem 3

There is a lot of background in my PAX7 immunostaining.

Potential solution

Perform more washes with PBS 1X. You can also use another PAX7 antibody (Santa Cruz) if the hybridoma gives a weak signal.

Problem 4

Myoblasts easily detached from the dishes.

Potential solution

Several coating solutions do exist and can replace the collagen: gelatin, for example, or Geltrex.

Problem 5

During the cell proliferation assay, the signal of Propidium Iodide is too strong.

Potential solution

On fixed cells, the signal of Propidium Iodide can be very strong. Do not hesitate to decrease the final concentration of Propidium Iodide or to increase the number of washes.

Problem 6

There is a purity of less than 80%.

Potential solution

A pre-plate can be done on a dish not coated with col I in order to let the FAPs attached to the plastic and therefore harvest the floating MuSCs after 3 h.
