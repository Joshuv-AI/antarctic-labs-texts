Impaired P4HA1-Driven Hydroxyproline Formation Mediates Scleral Collagen Disruption in Form-Deprivation Myopia
Authors: Jing Li, Jiaojiao Feng, Yibo Han, Xiaoqi Gong, Shuhan Li, Guodong Tang, Yuxi Liu, Junru Wang, Xiaoyun Dong, Yixue Yin, Yunxiao Xie, Hongsheng Bi, Jike Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeScleral extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling is a hallmark of myopia progression, yet its metabolic drivers remain poorly defined. This study investigated whether disruption of arginine-proline metabolism—specifically impaired P4HA1-mediated proline hydroxylation—contributes to hydroxyproline deficiency, collagen disorganization, and axial elongation in form-deprivation myopia (FDM).MethodsMonocular FDM was induced in guinea pigs for two weeks. Refractive errors, axial length, scleral morphology, and collagen ultrastructure were assessed. Untargeted and targeted metabolomics were performed to characterize metabolic alterations. AAV8-P4HA1 or control vector was delivered via sub-Tenon's capsule injection to the form-deprived eyes of two animal subgroups to test whether restoration of P4HA1 expression could rescue metabolic and structural abnormalities. Pharmacological inhibition of P4HA (1,4-DPCA) in cultured primary fibroblasts evaluated the cellular consequences of impaired proline hydroxylation.ResultsRelative to untreated eyes, FDM resulted in a myopic refractive shift, axial elongation, and scleral thinning, reduced collagen fibril diameter, and ECM disorganization. Metabolomic analyses revealed disruption of arginine–proline metabolism, with elevated arginine and proline but reduced hydroxyproline, accompanied by increased ARG1, OAT, and PYCR1 expression and decreased P4HA1 and P4HA3 expression. P4HA1 overexpression restored hydroxyproline levels, corrected the metabolic imbalance, increased COL1A1, reduced MMP2, reinstated TIMP2, and improved collagen density and alignment, thereby mitigating axial elongation. Pharmacological inhibition of P4HA reduced hydroxyproline and collagen production.ConclusionsReduced scleral P4HA1 expression in FDM eyes is associated with reduced scleral hydroxyproline content and collagen structural disorganization. That restoration of scleral P4HA1 levels inhibits FD-related excessive axial elongation points to the P4HA1-hydroxyproline axis as a potential target for myopia control.

Myopia (nearsightedness) is a common refractive error characterized by blurred distance vision resulting from excessive axial elongation of the eyeball.1,2 It poses a major and growing global public health challenge, with projections estimating that nearly half of the world's population—approximately 4.76 billion individuals—will be affected by 2050, compared with 1.41 billion in 2000.3 The burden is particularly severe in East Asia, where 80%–90% of young adults are myopic and 10%–20% develop high myopia,4 a condition that markedly increases the risk of irreversible vision loss from complications such as retinal detachment and glaucoma.5 Despite its high prevalence, the molecular mechanisms underlying myopia progression remain incompletely understood.

The sclera is the primary target organ mediating axial elongation in myopia.6 As the load-bearing outer shell of the eye, its biomechanical integrity depends on a collagen-rich ECM, composed predominantly of type I collagen produced by scleral fibroblasts.2,7,8 During myopia development, the sclera undergoes pronounced ECM remodeling, characterized by reduced collagen fibril diameter, disorganized fiber architecture, and decreased tensile strength, thereby permitting progressive ocular elongation.9–11 At the cellular level, these structural changes are accompanied by increased fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation, which further disrupts ECM synthesis and organization.12,13 While scleral remodeling has traditionally been attributed to an imbalance between collagen synthesis and degradation, accumulating evidence suggests that upstream factors—such as local hypoxia signaling,12,14 altered fibroblast metabolic states,15 and dysregulated nutrient and amino acid availability16—also play critical roles. These upstream factors are closely tied to metabolic reprogramming. Scleral hypoxia has been shown to promote fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transdifferentiation by enhancing glycolysis and lactate production.12 In parallel, collagen synthesis and post-translational modification depend heavily on the availability of specific amino acids, particularly proline, lysine, and glycine.17 Together, these findings point to metabolic dysregulation as a central contributor to impaired ECM homeostasis and scleral weakening during myopia progression.

Metabolomics provides a powerful approach for characterizing such metabolic alterations in disease states18 and has been widely applied in ophthalmic research, including studies of myopia,19 dry eye,20 macular degeneration,21 diabetic retinopathy,22 uveitis.23 Recent metabolomic analyses of the myopic sclera have revealed significant disruptions in amino acid metabolic pathways. Independent studies by Liang et al.19 and Lin et al.16 identified altered scleral amino acid profiles in animal models of myopia, with Lin et al.16 further demonstrating that glycine supplementation inhibited scleral remodeling. These findings suggest a functional link between amino acid metabolism and scleral structure. However, a direct mechanistic connection between specific metabolic enzymes, metabolites, and myopia progression remains to be established.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the metabolic basis of scleral remodeling in FDM. By integrating metabolomic profiling with molecular and morphological analyses, we sought to identify key metabolic pathways linking amino acid metabolism to ECM homeostasis. We further investigated how disruption of these pathways contributes to collagen structural disorganization and axial elongation. Collectively, our findings provide mechanistic insight into the metabolic regulation of scleral remodeling and identify the P4HA1-hydroxyproline axis as a potential therapeutic target for myopia intervention.

Methods

Animal Experiments

Two-week-old male guinea pigs (110–120g) were randomly assigned to FDM or normal control (NC) groups. FDM was induced by placing a light-diffusing hood (60% light transmittance) on the right eye for 2 weeks, while the left eye remained uncovered. Hoods were checked daily and replaced as needed.24 All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

After cycloplegia with 1% tropicamide, refraction was measured by streak retinoscopy. Axial length was assessed by A-scan ultrasonography using tissue-specific sound velocities (anterior chamber: 1557 m/s; lens: 1723 m/s; vitreous: 1540 m/s).25 All measurements were performed by an examiner masked to group assignment.

At the end of the form deprivation treatment period, slit-lamp biomicroscopy was performed to assess the anterior segment and rule out inflammation or other ocular abnormalities. Only eyes without detectable abnormalities were included for tissue collection, and all samples were obtained from the right eye. Guinea pigs were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 4% pentobarbital sodium. Whole globes were then aseptically enucleated under a laminar flow hood and placed in sterile saline solution. A circumferential incision was made at the equator to remove the anterior segment and obtain an eyecup, and the lens and vitreous body were discarded. The retina and choroid were carefully dissected off, and the post-equatorial sclera was collected. Post-equatorial scleral tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent molecular analyses. The tissue collection procedure is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

AAV8-P4HA1 Sub-Tenon's Injection

Recombinant adeno-associated viral vectors encoding P4HA1 (AAV-CMV-MCS-3Flag-EF1-ZsGreen-P4HA1) and the corresponding empty vector control (AAV-CMV-MCS-3Flag-EF1-ZsGreen) were constructed. Viral genome titers were quantified by RT–PCR and normalized to approximately 1.95 × 10¹³ vg/mL.

Guinea pigs were randomly assigned to four groups: NC, FDM, FDM treated with AAV8 empty vector (FD-AAV8-Vector), and FDM treated with AAV8-P4HA1 (FD-AAV8-P4HA1). In the AAV-treated groups, 10 µL of viral vector (5 µL per site, injected 180° apart) was injected into the sub-Tenon's capsule of the right eye only using a 33-gauge needle.26 Under a stereomicroscope, the conjunctiva was gently grasped with forceps and the globe was rotated to expose the operative field. The needle was then inserted 2 mm posterior to the limbus in the superior temporal and inferior nasal quadrants and advanced 1–2 mm along the curvature of the globe into the sub-Tenon's space to deliver the viral vector. No leakage or reflux was observed during injection. After needle withdrawal, gentle external pressure was applied with a sterile cotton swab to disperse the drug. Postoperative care with 0.2% polyacrylic acid was applied daily for four days. The injection sites are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

To evaluate scleral transduction efficiency, animals were euthanized 21 days after injection. Eyes were enucleated, and the sclera was dissected, quartered, flat-mounted, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. ZsGreen fluorescence was imaged using a digital pathology scanner (KF-FL-020; KFBIO, Yuyao, China) (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Primary Scleral Fibroblast Culture and Treatment

Scleral tissues were minced into 1 × 2 mm fragments and cultured in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, GlutaMAX, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged on reaching ∼80% confluence, and passages 2–3 were used for experiments.27 Logarithmically growing scleral fibroblasts were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to the P4HA inhibitor 1,4-dihydrophenanthrolin-4-one-3-carboxylic acid (1,4-DPCA; 0.625–80 µM) for 24 hours. Cell viability was then assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Goonie Biotech, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions to determine the non-cytotoxic concentration range for subsequent experiments, with absorbance measured at 450 nm.

Histology and Transmission Electron Microscopy

The intact eyeballs were placed into 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation and incubated for 24 hours. Tissues were then dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and deparaffinized using standard procedures. For general morphological assessment, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Collagen organization was further evaluated using Masson's trichrome staining following overnight potassium dichromate incubation. Stained sections were scanned using a Digital Pathology Scanner. To ensure consistency across samples, images were acquired for analysis at a standard location 1200 µm from the optic disc using KFSlideOS (version 1.0.5) software. The measurement locations are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S3.

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, tissue sampling was performed under a dissection microscope. Using a scalpel, a full-thickness ocular tissue block with an approximate surface dimension of 1 × 1 mm, including the retina, choroid, and sclera, was excised at a site approximately 1 mm directly temporal to the optic nerve head along the temporal meridian.28 A schematic illustration of the TEM sampling site is shown in Supplementary Figure S4. The tissue block was immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde electron microscopy fixative (G1102-1.5ML; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for primary fixation, followed by postfixation in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydration through a graded ethanol series, and embedding in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (50–60 nm) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Cross-sectional images of collagen fibrils were acquired using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at magnification ×30,000. To ensure relative consistency in sampling location, images were randomly acquired from the middle scleral layer for evaluation of collagen fibril ultrastructure.28,29 For each sample, one representative TEM image was analyzed, and three biological samples were included in each experimental group. All images were acquired and analyzed in a blinded manner by the same investigator using an identical workflow across all groups. Quantitative analysis of collagen fibrils was performed using ImageJ software. All clearly identifiable transversely sectioned collagen fibrils within each image were measured to determine fibril counts and diameters. To minimize potential bias caused by differences in fibril numbers among images, percentage frequency histograms of fibril diameters were generated using OriginPro 2025.28,29 The median value was used to indicate the center of the distribution, and the mean fibril diameter was calculated for each image. The average fibril diameter per image was subsequently used for group comparisons.

Immunofluorescence and Collagen Damage Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on scleral tissue sections to evaluate the expression and colocalization of vimentin (Vim) and P4HA1 proteins. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, the samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Vim and P4HA1. Appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, the samples were mounted with antifade medium for imaging. For cultured fibroblasts, immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess P4HA1 protein expression. The procedure was similar to that used for tissue sections, with fibroblasts seeded onto coverslips and treated as described above. Unfolded collagen chains in cultured fibroblasts were detected using a Cy3-conjugated collagen hybridizing peptide (R-CHP; 3Helix Inc., Salt Lake City, UT, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the probe was heat-denatured, rapidly cooled, and then incubated with cells at 4°C overnight. Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser-scanning confocal microscope. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software to quantify fluorescence intensity.

Metabolomic Profiling

To ensure sufficient metabolite levels, six samples per group were analyzed for non-targeted metabolomics, with each sample prepared by pooling post-equatorial scleral tissues from five guinea pigs. For targeted amino acid metabolomics, five samples per group were analyzed, with each sample prepared by pooling post-equatorial scleral tissues from four guinea pigs.

Non-Targeted Metabolomics

Frozen scleral tissues were sectioned on dry ice, homogenized in water, and extracted with methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). After centrifugation, supernatants were dried under vacuum, reconstituted in acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v), and subjected to LC–MS analysis. Non-targeted metabolomic profiling was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolites were separated on a HILIC column using water containing ammonium acetate/ammonium hydroxide and acetonitrile as mobile phases. Samples were analyzed in randomized order with quality control (QC) samples interspersed to monitor system stability. Mass spectrometry was conducted using electrospray ionization in positive and negative modes. Full MS and data-dependent MS/MS spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 70–1200 at a resolution of 60,000, with dynamic exclusion applied.

Targeted Amino Acid Metabolomics

Targeted amino acid analysis was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Winooski, VT, USA) coupled to an AB Sciex 6500+ QTRAP mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a HILIC column using water/acetonitrile-based mobile phases containing ammonium formate and formic acid. Samples were analyzed in randomized order with QC samples included throughout the run. Mass spectrometry was performed in positive and negative electrospray ionization modes using multiple reaction monitoring for targeted detection.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Raw data were converted to .mzXML using ProteoWizard and processed with XCMS for peak detection, alignment, retention time correction, and area extraction. Metabolites were annotated using the LUG database. Features with >50% missing values or RSD >50% in QC samples were removed, and remaining missing values were imputed by KNN. Multivariate analyses (PCA, PLS-DA, OPLS-DA) were performed in R. Differential metabolites were identified using volcano plots (log₂ fold change and −log₁₀ P-value) with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using KEGG.

Gene Expression Analysis (qPCR)

Scleral tissues were retrieved from the −80 °C freezer. Each sample was prepared by pooling two post-equatorial scleral tissue samples, with three samples analyzed per group. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cDNA was synthesized with HiScript II QRT SuperMix (Cellagen Technology, San Diego, CA, USA). QPCR was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Expression levels of target genes were normalized to β-Actin as an internal reference, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.30

Protein Analysis by Capillary Western Blot

The relative protein levels of ARG1, OAT, PYCR1, P4HA1, P4HA2, P4HA3, MMP2, TIMP2, COL1A1, and β-actin were measured using a ProteinSimple Abby capillary Western blotting analyzer (ProteinSimple, San Jose, CA, USA).31 For protein extraction, each sample was prepared by pooling two post-equatorial scleral tissue samples, with three samples analyzed per group. Tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, thoroughly homogenized at 4°C, and spun in a centrifuge at 8000 rpm for five minutes. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Bio, Shanghai, China), then adjusted to 1 µg/µL. Western blotting was performed using the Abby system, following the manufacturer's protocol. All the antibodies used in immunoblot analysis are listed in the Supplementary Table S2. Quantitative analysis of the protein bands was carried out using Compass software (version 7.0.0).

Amino Acid Quantification

Levels of arginine (BC5635), proline (BC0295), and hydroxyproline (BC0255) in the post-equatorial scleral samples were measured using commercial kits (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing, China). Tissue and cell supernatants, obtained by homogenization and centrifugation, were reacted with the kit reagents according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 525 nm (arginine), 520 nm (proline), and 560 nm (hydroxyproline) using a UV spectrophotometer, and concentrations were determined from standard curves.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. The distribution of datasets was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. For comparisons between independent groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test were applied depending on the normality of the data. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was used for normally distributed data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

FDM Induces Axial Elongation and Scleral Structural Remodeling

At baseline (0 weeks), no significant differences in refractive error or axial length were detected between FDM and NC eyes (P > 0.05). Following two weeks of form-deprivation induction (Fig. 1A), the refraction in the NC group was 1.83 ± 0.79 D. In contrast, form-deprivation induced significant myopia in the FDM groups (−1.13 ± 0.75 D at two weeks) (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, compared with the NC group, the axial lengths of guinea pigs in the FDM group were significantly increased (at two weeks, NC vs. FDM: 8.00 ± 0.07 mm vs. 8.10 ± 0.11 mm, P < 0.01) (Fig. 1C).

Histological analysis confirmed that form deprivation successfully induced key myopic structural changes. H&E staining revealed a significant reduction in scleral thickness in FDM eyes compared with NC controls (FDM: 52.66 ± 3.87 µm vs. NC: 65.81 ± 3.06 µm; P < 0.05) (Figs. 1D, 1E). This thinning was accompanied by a disruption of scleral structure, as evidenced by Masson's trichrome staining, which showed a looser and more disorganized tissue architecture in FDM sclera, in contrast to the dense and well-organized appearance observed in NC tissues (Fig. 1E). These alterations were further corroborated by TEM, which showed that in FDM eyes, scleral collagen fibers were smaller in diameter and more sparsely arranged compared to the NC group (Figs. 1F, 1G). At the molecular level, post-equatorial scleral samples from FDM eyes exhibited decreased expression of the COL1A1 gene and protein, while showing significantly increased expression of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2) protein and significantly decreased expression of tissue metalloproteinase inhibitor-2 (TIMP2) protein (Figs. 1H–J). This indicates increased collagen degradation in post-equatorial scleral samples from FDM eyes.

Untargeted Metabolomics Reveals Broad Metabolic Dysregulation in FDM Post-Equatorial Sclera

To obtain an overview of form deprivation-induced metabolic changes in the post-equatorial sclera, we performed untargeted metabolomics. By principal component analysis, the sclera of the FDM group was clustered separately from that of the NC group (Figs. 2A, 2B). Volcano plots illustrate the differential expression of post-equatorial scleral metabolites between the FDM and NC groups. Analysis of data acquired in positive ion mode identified 129 differentially expressed metabolites, including 78 significantly upregulated and 51 significantly downregulated metabolites (VIP>1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). The results of analysis of the assay data in negative ion mode identified 195 differentially expressed metabolites, 116 of which were significantly upregulated and 79 were significantly downregulated (VIP>1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). The metabolites with significant changes were mainly enriched in the aminobenzoate degradation, bile secretion, purine metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cAMP signaling pathway, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, caffeine metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism and arginine biosynthesis, among others (Fig. 2E). Among these, multiple pathways associated with amino acid metabolism showed the most significant alterations.

Targeted Metabolomics Identifies Specific Impairment in the Arginine–Proline–Hydroxyproline Axis

To precisely quantify amino acid alterations in the post-equatorial sclera of FDM eyes, we performed targeted metabolomics. This analysis identified 14 significantly dysregulated amino acids, with nine upregulated and five downregulated in FDM eyes compared to NC (P < 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant perturbations in several metabolic pathways, including arginine and proline metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism and lysine degradation metabolic pathways (Fig. 3A). Notably, within the arginine and proline metabolism pathway, we found a marked accumulation of arginine and proline alongside a significant decrease in hydroxyproline in the FDM group (Figs. 3B, 3C). Concurrently, the lysine degradation pathway was also disturbed, as evidenced by increased levels of lysine and its derivative, 5-hydroxylysine, in the FDM group (Figs. 3B, 3C). Given that proline, lysine, and their hydroxylated derivatives are essential for collagen synthesis and stability, these metabolic alterations indicate impaired collagen biosynthesis and maturation in the myopic sclera.

Dysregulation of Key Enzymes in the Arginine–Proline–Hydroxyproline Axis

To explore the molecular basis of dysregulation in the arginine–proline–hydroxyproline axis, we assessed the expression of key pathway enzymes. Western blot analysis showed that proline-synthesizing enzymes (ARG1, OAT, and PYCR1) were significantly upregulated in the post-equatorial sclera of FDM eyes compared with NC controls, whereas the proline hydroxylase subunits P4HA1 and P4HA3 were markedly downregulated (Figs. 4A, 4B). These changes indicate a metabolic shift toward proline accumulation at the expense of hydroxylation, likely altering collagen structure in the myopic sclera.

P4HA1 Overexpression Rescues Metabolic, Structural, and Functional Defects in FDM

Given that P4HA1 is the rate-limiting subunit for type I collagen hydroxylation and that the role of P4HA3 remains largely unexplored, we focused on P4HA1 for functional rescue experiments. To investigate its causal role in myopic scleral remodeling, AAV8-P4HA1 or control vector was delivered via sub-Tenon's capsule injection to the form-deprived eyes of two animal subgroups (Fig. 5A). Guinea pigs injected with AAV8-ZsGreen exhibited visible expression in the sclera, whereas no such expression was observed in the eye without AAV8 injection (Supplementary Fig. S5). These results confirm successful transfection.

Compared with the NC group, both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups exhibited significant myopic shifts in refractive error and marked axial elongation. In contrast, P4HA1 overexpression significantly attenuated these changes (Figs. 5B, 5C). Histological analyses further demonstrated that, relative to the NC group, both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups exhibited marked scleral thinning on H&E staining and structural disorganization on Masson's trichrome staining. By comparison, the FD-AAV8-P4HA1 group exhibited increased scleral thickness and markedly improved scleral structural organization compared with both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups (Figs. 5D, 5E). Consistent with the histological findings, TEM analysis revealed that collagen fibrils in the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups were significantly thinner and more loosely arranged than those in the NC group, whereas the AAV8-P4HA1 group showed increased fibril diameter and more compact fibril packing (Figs. 5F, 5G). Notably, no obvious ultrastructural improvement was observed in the FD-AAV8-Vector group compared with the FDM group, suggesting that the empty vector itself did not exert a significant structural rescue effect.

To confirm successful overexpression in the post-equatorial sclera, we next examined P4HA1 expression at both the transcriptional and protein levels. Compared with the NC group, P4HA1 mRNA and protein expression were significantly reduced in both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups, whereas both were markedly restored in the P4HA1 overexpression group (Figs. 5K–M). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that P4HA1 expression in scleral fibroblasts was markedly decreased in both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups, whereas AAV8-P4HA1 treatment effectively restored its expression in the post-equatorial sclera (Fig. 5H).

At the metabolic level, arginine, proline, and hydroxyproline metabolism in the post-equatorial sclera was markedly disrupted in both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups compared with the NC group, whereas these abnormalities were substantially alleviated following P4HA1 overexpression (Fig. 5I). This metabolic improvement was accompanied by a significant upregulation of both COL1A1 mRNA and protein expression in the FD-AAV8-P4HA1 group, indicating enhanced collagen biosynthesis (Figs. 5J, 5L, 5M).

Meanwhile, the relative mRNA expression levels of OAT and PYCR1 were significantly increased in both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups compared with the NC group, but were markedly reduced after AAV8-P4HA1 treatment. Consistently, the relative protein expression levels of ARG1, OAT, and PYCR1 were significantly elevated in the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups, whereas these increases were attenuated after P4HA1 overexpression. In addition, compared with the NC group, MMP2 expression was abnormally increased and TIMP2 expression was reduced in both the FDM and FD-AAV8-Vector groups, whereas P4HA1 overexpression significantly attenuated these changes (Figs. 5L, 5M). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that P4HA1 overexpression mitigates myopic scleral remodeling through correction of metabolic imbalance and enhancement of collagen biosynthesis.

Pharmacological Inhibition of P4HA Activity Impairs Collagen Synthesis in Vitro

Primary scleral fibroblasts were treated at approximately 70% confluence with 1,4-DPCA. 1,4-DPCA is a pan-P4HA inhibitor that does not selectively target P4HA1. Non-cytotoxic concentrations were determined using a CCK-8 assay. Concentrations up to 10 µM did not affect viability, whereas concentrations exceeding 10 µM caused marked cytotoxicity (Fig. 67A). Accordingly, 10 µM 1,4-DPCA was selected for subsequent 24-hour interventions.

Subsequently, Western blotting and hydroxyproline quantification were conducted to evaluate the impact of pharmacological P4HA inhibition on collagen metabolism. After 24 hours’ treatment with 1,4-DPCA, protein expression levels of P4HA1 remained unchanged, whereas COL1A1 expression was significantly reduced (Figs. 6B, 6E). Consistent with impaired proline hydroxylation, intracellular hydroxyproline content was markedly decreased in 1,4-DPCA–treated scleral fibroblasts (Fig. 6H). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that 1,4-DPCA did not alter P4HA1 protein abundance (Figs. 6C, 6F); however, staining with collagen-hybridizing peptide (R-CHP) revealed a pronounced accumulation of structurally abnormal collagen in treated cells (Figs. 6D, 6G). Although 1,4-DPCA is a pan-P4HA inhibitor rather than a P4HA1-specific agent, these results demonstrate that suppression of proline hydroxylation reduces hydroxyproline production and thereby impairs collagen structure in scleral fibroblasts.

Discussion

Although progressive scleral remodeling and collagen disorganization are hallmarks of FDM, the metabolic mechanisms underlying these structural changes remain poorly defined. In this study, we identify the P4HA1–hydroxyproline axis as a key mediator linking metabolic reprogramming to scleral matrix integrity. Downregulation of P4HA1 in form-deprived eyes was associated with reduced hydroxyproline production and impaired collagen organization, suggesting that defective proline hydroxylation may contribute to the structural abnormalities observed in the scleras of myopic eyes (Fig. 7).

The sclera serves as the primary structural determinant in myopia, with its biomechanical integrity largely reliant on type I collagen.10 The unique triple-helical configuration of collagen, built on repetitive -X-Y-Gly sequences, derives its thermal and mechanical stability primarily from hydroxyproline residues at the Y-position. Hydroxyproline is produced by the hydroxylation of proline, catalyzed by collagen prolyl 4-hydroxylases (C-P4Hs). It stabilizes the triple helix through stereoelectronic effects and water-mediated hydrogen bonds, helping to maintain fibrillar integrity under physiological stress.17,32 In our study, metabolomic profiling of the FDM post-equatorial sclera revealed a pronounced accumulation of proline alongside a marked decrease in hydroxyproline. This suggests that the hydroxylation step becomes a metabolic bottleneck during visual deprivation. The resulting imbalance points to altered scleral collagen organization as a key mechanism driving the biomechanical weakening of the scleral and so accelerated axial elongation. Notably, previous metabolomic studies have also reported amino acid dysregulation as a biochemical hallmark of myopic remodeling. Liang et al.19 and Lin et al.16 demonstrated widespread perturbations in scleral amino-acid metabolism; however, whereas Lin et al.16 observed decreased glycine, proline, and lysine in a lens-induced myopia model, our FDM model exhibited proline and lysine accumulation with stable glycine levels. These discrepancies may arise from differences in modeling strategies, intervention duration, or the regions of sclera sampled. Collectively, these studies underscore that severe disruption of amino acid metabolism is a key driver of scleral remodeling in myopia.

Given the critical role of hydroxyproline in maintaining the correct folding and stability of collagen triple helices, we examined the expression of C-P4Hs and found significant downregulation of P4HA1 and P4HA3, but not P4HA2, in the FDM post-equatorial sclera. Among these isoforms, P4HA1 is recognized as the most ubiquitously expressed and functionally non-redundant isoform, such that its loss of expression leads to underhydroxylation of collagen.33 This pivotal role is underscored by the embryonic lethality of P4ha1−/− mice34 and by human genetic studies showing that biallelic P4HA1 mutations cause a congenital connective tissue disorder featuring high myopia,35 directly linking its deficiency to ocular structural instability. Our functional experiments align with these findings: AAV8-mediated P4HA1 overexpression in FDM post-equatorial sclera restored hydroxyproline content, improved scleral structure and attenuated myopic progression. Together, these data support a causal role for P4HA1 loss in scleral remodeling.

Beyond its canonical catalytic role, P4HA1 may have pleiotropic effects on ECM homeostasis. It can activate YAP signaling to promote COL1A1 transcription36 and participates in intracellular collagen trafficking, where its inhibition provokes endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and impairs secretion.37 Multiple studies have demonstrated that ER stress is closely associated with scleral remodeling.38,39 Ikeda et al.26 discovered that scleral ER stress, through activation of the PERK and ATF6 axes and scleral collagen remodeling, serves as a key regulator of axial elongation in myopia progression. Furthermore, our group's research has also identified pathological changes indicative of ER stress, such as increased ATF6 expression, in the sclera of form-deprived myopic guinea pigs.31 Although these mechanisms were not directly tested here, they suggest that P4HA1 downregulation in myopia could disrupt collagen homeostasis through multiple routes-reducing hydroxylation efficiency, altering biosynthetic signaling, and impairing secretion. Moreover, targeted metabolomic analysis in the present study revealed the metabolism of both lysine and 5-hydroxylysine to be perturbed in the FDM post-equatorial sclera. Previous studies have demonstrated that P4HA1 deficiency can disrupt multiple post-translational modifications of collagens—including prolyl hydroxylation, lysyl hydroxylation, and O-glycosylation—and alter the site-specific occupancy of 3-hydroxyproline and 5-hydroxylysine.40 In this context, 5-hydroxylysine serves to play a critical role in the post-translational modification of collagen, contributing to collagen cross-linking and triple-helix stability, and its dysregulation may compromise collagen fiber structural integrity and biomechanics stability.41 Together, these observations suggest that dysregulation of lysine and 5-hydroxylysine metabolism in the FDM post-equatorial sclera may be linked to P4HA1 deficiency. Therefore we propose that P4HA1 represents a key early step in a chain of events that disrupts scleral collagen maturation and ultimately scleral mechanical stability, leading to the excessive, rapid axial elongation of eyes undergoing myopia progression, as seen with form deprivation.

Amino acid metabolism-linked remodeling also extends into immune regulation. Arginine metabolism, in particular, contributes to myopic scleral remodeling through its dual control of nitric oxide and polyamine pathways that govern macrophage polarization.42 Classically activated M1 macrophages metabolize arginine via inducible nitric-oxide synthase (iNOS) to produce nitric oxide, promoting a pro-inflammatory milieu, whereas M2 macrophages—associated with tissue repair and fibrosis—primarily use arginase-1 to convert arginine into ornithine and polyamines.43 This metabolic dichotomy is highly relevant to myopia, as Zheng et al.44 reported that form deprivation promotes M2 macrophage polarization in the sclera. ARG1 serves as a hallmark marker of M2 macrophages.45,46 This is consistent with our observation of increased ARG1 expression in the FDM sclera. However, ARG1 is not exclusive to M2 macrophages47; it is also expressed in non-immune cells. Our findings reveal abnormal arginine metabolism in FDM sclera, suggesting potential cross-talk between arginine/proline metabolism and immune metabolic signaling. This intercellular interaction warrants further investigation. Arginine metabolism also influences ocular growth through effects on the choroid and nitric-oxide-mediated vascular regulation. Li et al.48 demonstrated that intravitreal inhibition of nitric-oxide synthase in a lens-induced myopia model suppressed axial elongation, increased choroidal thickness, and mitigated fibrosis, supporting the importance of arginine-derived nitric oxide in ocular remodeling. In this context, the disrupted arginine-proline-hydroxyproline network identified here provides a unifying metabolic axis connecting scleral structural failure, immune imbalance, and choroidal signaling.

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. First, our metabolic and molecular analyses were conducted at a single time point (two weeks) in guinea pigs. This may not fully capture the dynamic nature of scleral remodeling or potential species-specific differences. Second, although we observed a strong correlation between P4HA1 loss and collagen disruption, the upstream regulatory mechanisms of P4HA1 remain unclear, and the concurrent downregulation of P4HA3 suggests possible compensatory or collaborative functions that require further investigation. Third, AAV8-mediated gene delivery was not specific to scleral fibroblasts, and sub-Tenon injection may not achieve uniform transduction across the entire sclera. Because transduction efficiency in different scleral quadrants was not assessed, we cannot exclude potential off-target effects or uneven transgene expression. In addition, TEM-based ultrastructural analysis of collagen fibers was limited to the middle scleral layer, and potential interlayer differences were not assessed. Finally, although P4HA1 overexpression reduced MMP2 protein expression, the regulatory relationship between these two factors remains unclear.

To address these limitations, future studies should incorporate multiple time points to track the temporal evolution of metabolic and enzymatic changes during myopia progression. Conditional knockout or transgenic models could elucidate upstream signaling pathways controlling P4HA1, while combined manipulation of P4HA3 would help clarify functional interactions. Fibroblast-specific conditional models or cell-type–restricted viral vectors, along with quantitative assessment of AAV transduction across scleral quadrants, could provide insight into cell-type specificity and spatial effects. Future ultrastructural studies should include larger sample sizes and multiple non-overlapping imaging fields per sample to improve the robustness and representativeness of collagen fibril analysis.

Future research should also prioritize longitudinal tracking of scleral metabolic and structural changes, investigation into whether P4HA1 deficiency induces ER stress and its potential synergy with MMP inhibition, and exploration of crosstalk between scleral metabolism and immune regulation. Such studies may reveal novel mechanisms and therapeutic targets for myopia control.
