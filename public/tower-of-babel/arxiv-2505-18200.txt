CrossRF: A Domain-Invariant Deep Learning Approach for RF Fingerprinting
Fahrettin Emin Tiras, Hayriye Serra Altinoluk

Abstract
Radio Frequency (RF) fingerprinting offers a promising approach for drone identification and security, although it suffers from significant performance degradation when operating on different transmission channels. This paper presents CrossRF, a domain-invariant deep learning approach that addresses the problem of cross-channel RF fingerprinting for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) identification. Our approach aims to minimize the domain gap between different RF channels by using adversarial learning to train a more robust model that maintains consistent identification performance despite channel variations. We validate our approach using the UAVSig [ 1 ] dataset, comprising real-world over-the-air RF signals from identical drone models operating across several frequency channels, ensuring that the findings correspond to real-world scenarios. The experimental results show CrossRF’s efficiency, achieving up to 99.03% accuracy when adapting from Channel 3 to Channel 4, compared to only 26.39% using conventional methods. The model maintains robust performance in more difficult multi-channel scenarios (87.57% accuracy adapting from Channels 1,3 to 2,4) and achieves 89.45% accuracy with 0.9 precision for controller classification. These results confirm CrossRF’s ability to significantly reduce performance degradation due to cross-channel variations while maintaining high identification accuracy with minimal training data requirements, making it particularly suitable for practical drone security applications.

Introduction

The spread of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in commercial, recreational, and military domains has created significant security issues, especially with regard to unauthorized drone operations in restricted airspaces. In 2024, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) reported a significant number of drone sightings at or near airports, emphasizing the urgent need for reliable drone detection and identification systems
[
2
]
. Radio Frequency (RF) fingerprinting has emerged as a promising approach for drone security, leveraging the unique hardware-level imperfections in transmitters to identify specific devices non-invasively at the physical layer, without relying on protocol-level identifiers that can be easily spoofed.

Nevertheless, traditional RF fingerprinting methods face a critical problem: they perform poorly when operating on different transmission channels. Fingerprinting models trained on one channel often experience a significant accuracy degradation when applied to signals transmitted on different frequency channels, limiting their practical use in real-world security applications. This performance degradation is due to the sensitivity of RF fingerprints to channel-specific characteristics, creating a domain gap between different transmission environments. For instance, when testing is performed in different channel conditions and environments, accuracy drops considerably, making these systems ineffective for practical deployment where channel changes are inevitable
[
3
]
.

Domain adaptation techniques have demonstrated promise in addressing similar challenges across various fields. Tzeng et al. proposed Adversarial Discriminative Domain Adaptation (ADDA)
[
4
]
, demonstrating its efficiency in aligning feature distributions between source and target domains. Nevertheless, the application of such techniques to RF fingerprinting, especially in the context of cross-channel drone identification, has received limited attention despite its potential to address the problem of channel variation.

This paper addresses the critical gap in cross-channel RF fingerprinting for drone identification systems. We demonstrate how domain adaptation techniques can minimize the performance degradation caused by channel variations, enabling more robust and practical drone security solutions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a comprehensive literature review covering RF fingerprinting, domain adaptation techniques, and UAV identification systems. Section 3 describes our CrossRF methodology based on ADDA. Section 4 presents experimental results. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

Literature Review

This section provides a comprehensive review of the relevant literature on RF fingerprinting, its uses in drone security, and the use of domain adaptation techniques to enhance its robustness across multiple transmission channels. We assess the evolution of RF fingerprinting from conventional methods to deep learning approaches, investigate the critical challenges of channel variations and environmental factors that limit its practical deployment, and explore how adversarial domain adaptation techniques can be used to overcome such limitations.

Radio Frequency (RF) fingerprinting has emerged as a promising method for non-invasive device identification by exploiting hardware-level imperfections in transmitters. These imperfections produce unique signal characteristics that can be used to discriminate even devices of the same make and model. The technique has improved markedly from early approaches using traditional machine learning algorithms to more sophisticated deep learning methods which obtain higher accuracy and robustness.

Hall et al. demonstrated the applicability of RF fingerprinting for detection of rogue devices in wireless networks

, while Ureten and Serinken showed how transient signal characteristics can also be used for high-accuracy device identification

[

. These fundamental works have established RF fingerprinting as a feasible approach to enhance security in wireless communications.

The application of RF fingerprinting to drone identification has been given significant attention due to growing security concerns related to unauthorized drone operations. Ezuma et al. developed techniques for micro-UAV detection and classification using RF fingerprints in the context of wireless interference, demonstrating the potential of the current method for drone security applications. Similarly, Al-Sa’d et al. suggested sophisticated algorithms using deep neural networks for drone detection and identification using RF fingerprints

2.1
Channel Variation Challenges

Despite its promise, conventional RF fingerprinting techniques face several critical challenges that limit their practical deployment. The most significant challenge is the poor performance when operating across different transmission channels. Fingerprinting models trained on one channel often experience substantial accuracy degradation when applied to signals transmitted on different frequency channels due to domain shifts among these channels
[
9
]
.

Environmental factors and signal interference make RF fingerprinting even more complex in real-world scenarios. As demonstrated by Gutierrez del Arroyo et al., models trained only on a specific frequency channel can suffer dramatic performance drop when applied to data from other channels—dropping from MCC values exceeding 0.9 (showing near-perfect accuracy) to values below 0.05 (equivalent to random guessing)—highlighting the extreme sensitivity of these systems to changes in receiver characteristics and environmental conditions
[
9
]
. Furthermore, real-world RF environments often comprise significant signal interference from other devices operating in the same frequency bands, like Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, making it particularly difficult to isolate specific drone signals. Previous studies have shown that such interference can seriously affect the accuracy and reliability of RF fingerprinting systems in practical deployments
[
10
]
.

The computational complexity of RF fingerprinting algorithms, especially those based on deep learning, also presents problems for deployment on resource-limited systems. Real-time processing requirements and energy limitations in practical security applications require efficient algorithms that can maintain high accuracy while minimizing computational overhead, as discussed by Mo et al. in their work on compressively sensed RF signals for UAV detection
[
11
]
.

2.2
Domain Adaptation for RF Fingerprinting

Domain adaptation has emerged as a promising approach to address the challenges of cross-domain generalization in various fields. Tzeng et al. introduced Adversarial Discriminative Domain Adaptation (ADDA), an important innovation that enables domain-invariant feature learning via adversarial training
[
4
]
. This approach has performed remarkable success on computer vision tasks and presents significant potential to address domain shifts in RF fingerprinting.

Based on adversarial domain adaptation, Chen et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of domain adaptation techniques, especially for cross-device RF fingerprinting, showing accuracy improvements of 7-15% compared to conventional methods
[
12
]
. By aligning the distribution of the data from different receiver devices, their approach eliminates the distribution shift that would otherwise violate the assumption of independent and uniformly distributed data, which is essential for supervised learning.

Specifically for RF fingerprinting, Wang et al. proposed the use of prototypical networks for cross-domain adaptation with a small number of labeled data from the target domain
[
13
]
. Their approach demonstrates how few-shot learning techniques can reduce the domain shift caused by changing RF environments providing a practical solution for scenarios where collecting large amounts of labeled data from new environments is not possible.

2.4
Research Gap and Motivation

While significant progress has been made in both RF fingerprinting and domain adaptation independently, their integration, especially for cross-channel drone identification remains largely unexplored. Existing approaches either achieve high accuracy on a single channel or require extensive retraining when operating frequencies change, limiting their practical utility in dynamic security environments.

The few studies that have attempted to address cross-channel adaptation for RF fingerprinting have focused primarily on cross-device scenarios rather than cross-channel scenarios within the same device

3
Methodology

This section outlines the experimental setup and methodology used to validate our proposed domain-invariant RF fingerprinting approach based on Adversarial Discriminative Domain Adaptation (ADDA)
[
4
]
for drone identification. Our primary goal is to assess whether this approach can maintain device identification accuracy across different RF channels with minimal data requirements.

For our research, we utilized the UAVSig dataset
[
1
]
, a comprehensive collection of real-world over-the-air RF signal captures specifically designed for drone identification research. This dataset includes RF signals from 4 identical DJI M100 UAVs and 4 C1 DJI remote controllers transmitting across 4 different frequency channels in the 2.4 GHz ISM band.

The dataset comprises 96 one-drone files, 432 two-drone files, and 192 controller files. The signals were originally captured using a USRP B205mini software-defined radio with a 20 dBi panel antenna, recording 50 MHz of bandwidth centered at 2.4435 GHz, encompassing all four 10 MHz drone channels. The original sampling rate of 50 MSa/s ensured precise capture of RF signal characteristics essential for fingerprinting.

Having acquired this comprehensive dataset, the next step involved preparing the data for our ADDA-based approach. To improve processing efficiency, we transformed the original dataset from MATLAB format to Parquet format, which reduced storage requirements while preserving signal characteristics and improving loading times during experimentation. Our implementation utilized PyTorch for neural network architecture, Python libraries for data processing, and Optuna for hyperparameter optimization.

Our preprocessing pipeline maintained the integrity of RF fingerprinting features while preparing the data for ADDA. For each drone and controller, we extracted significant segments from each capture to preserve sufficient fingerprinting information. Then a sliding window technique was applied with appropriate overlap to segment continuous signals into manageable windows with consistent dimensions. The raw In-phase (I) and Quadrature (Q) components were preserved in their original form to maintain all signal characteristics, with each signal represented as a two-channel input.

With the preprocessed data ready, we now turn to the design of our ADDA-based architecture. Our domain-invariant RF fingerprinting approach extends ADDA with specific adaptations for processing radio frequency signals from UAVs, as illustrated in Figure
1
. The architecture consists of three primary components working together to achieve domain adaptation across different RF channels.

The Source Encoder extracts discriminative features from source domain data through a sequence of five 1D convolutional layers. Each convolutional layer is followed by batch normalization (BN), LeakyReLU activation, and dropout regularization to prevent overfitting. The encoder concludes with an AdaptiveAvgPool1D layer that accommodates variable input lengths, followed by a fully connected (FC) layer with LeakyReLU activation and dropout.

The Target Encoder mirrors the Source Encoder’s architecture precisely, enabling effective weight transfer during initialization. However, while structurally identical, the Target Encoder undergoes adversarial optimization to learn domain-invariant features that generalize across different RF channels.

The Discriminator serves as the adversarial component that distinguishes between source and target domain features. It begins with a Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL)
[
17
]
that inverts gradients during backpropagation which is a crucial element for adversarial training. Following the GRL are three fully connected layers with batch normalization, LeakyReLU activation, and dropout, culminating in a final FC layer with LogSoftmax activation that produces domain classification probabilities.

The Classifier processes the encoded features to identify specific drone devices. It consists of a fully connected layer with LeakyReLU activation and dropout, followed by a final FC layer that outputs class probabilities through a softmax function.

This architecture enables effective learning of domain-invariant representations that maintain discriminative power for UAV identification while minimizing the impact of channel variations, addressing a fundamental challenge in RF fingerprinting systems.

To effectively train this model and achieve robust domain adaptation, we implemented a two-stage training process. Our training process consists of two sequential stages designed to balance source domain performance with target domain adaptation. First, we train the source encoder and classifier on labeled source domain data. The second stage focuses on domain adaptation through adversarial training. To prevent catastrophic forgetting during this adaptation process, we incorporate a distillation loss in our training. As described by Kirkpatrick et al.
[
18
]
, catastrophic forgetting occurs when neural networks trained sequentially on different tasks lose performance on previously learned tasks.

To address this challenge, we employ knowledge distillation following the approach of Hinton et al.
[
19
]
. This method preserves the knowledge acquired in the source domain while allowing adaptation to the target domain. The distillation loss is calculated as the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the output distributions of the source and target encoders:

L_{\text{distill}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{source}}\|p_{\text{target}})

(1)

where
p_{\mathrm{source}}
and
p_{\mathrm{target}}
are the softmax outputs from the source and target models respectively. Specifically, for the distillation loss:

L_{\text{distill}}=T^{2}\cdot\sum_{i}p_{\text{source},i}\cdot\log\left(\frac{p_{\text{source},i}}{p_{\text{target},i}}\right)

(2)

where T is the temperature parameter used in the softmax calculation. The complete objective function for target encoder training becomes:

L_{target}=-\lambda_{adv}\cdot L_{discriminator}+\lambda_{distill}\cdot L_{distill}

(3)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}
are weighting parameters that balance the adaptation and knowledge preservation processes. These parameters, along with learning rates and network architecture configurations, were optimized using Optuna, a Bayesian hyperparameter optimization framework. This systematic approach allowed us to efficiently identify optimal model configurations for each adaptation scenario.

For deeper analysis of the complex relationships between various performance factors across our experimental scenarios, we employed PyReason
[
20
]
, a temporal logic reasoning framework. This enabled us to construct a factor relationship network that visualizes the interconnections between adaptation success factors, accuracy metrics, and scenario-specific parameters, providing additional insights into our approach’s effectiveness.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed CrossRF, a domain-invariant RF fingerprinting approach based on Adversarial Discriminative Domain Adaptation for accurate drone identification across different frequency channels. Our approach addresses a critical challenge in RF fingerprinting: maintaining high identification accuracy despite channel variations without requiring extensive retraining.

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach across multiple adaptation scenarios. In single-channel adaptation, our method achieved up to 99.03% accuracy when adapting from Channel 3 to Channel 4, and 81.94% when adapting from Channel 1 to Channel 2. For the more challenging multi-channel adaptation scenario, adapting from Channels 1,3 to Channels 2,4, our approach achieved 87.57% accuracy. These results represent significant improvements over the baseline performance without adaptation (26.39-26.94%).
