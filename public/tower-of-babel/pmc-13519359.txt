Automated tooth numbering on panoramic radiographs versus cone-beam computed tomographs: a diagnostic accuracy study of a commercial artificial intelligence system
Authors: Nora Sultani, Natalia Kazimierczak, Zbigniew Serafin, Wojciech Kazimierczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the diagnostic accuracy of a commercial artificial intelligence system for automated tooth numbering on panoramic radiographs and cone-beam CT (CBCT) and to quantify case-level reliability.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center diagnostic accuracy study, consecutive patients who underwent both panoramic radiography and CBCT in 2024 were included. The index test was automated tooth numbering generated by Diagnocat using the Fédération Dentaire Internationale numbering scheme. The reference standard was modality-specific consensus of 2 experienced, blinded readers. Tooth-position performance metrics with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using patient-level cluster bootstrap. Case-level reliability was defined as the proportion of examinations with completely error-free numbering across all evaluated tooth positions.ResultsThe study analyzed 171 paired panoramic radiographs and CBCT examinations, evaluating 4900 tooth positions per modality (9800 total). Tooth-position performance was identical and high across modalities (per-tooth accuracy 99.29% for both; overall position-based accuracy 98.73% [95% CI 97.97–99.38]). At the case level, 148/171 (86.5%) panoramic radiographs and 155/171 (90.6%) cone-beam computed tomography examinations were error-free.ConclusionsDespite high tooth-position metrics, approximately 1 in 10 to 1 in 7 examinations required at least one manual correction, demonstrating a gap between granular accuracy and case-level reliability.Advances in knowledgeReporting case-level, error-free outputs alongside tooth-position metrics provides a more clinically meaningful estimate of reliability for automated tooth numbering and supports safer workflow implementation.Keywords: tooth numbering, panoramic radiography, cone-beam CT, artificial intelligence, diagnostic accuracy, dentomaxillofacial radiology

Introduction

Accurate tooth numbering is foundational to diagnostic reporting, treatment planning, and interdisciplinary communication in dentistry. Errors in tooth numbering propagate through clinical workflows, leading to misinterpretation, inappropriate treatment, and medicolegal risk; in forensic odontology, reliable tooth numbering is critical to identification.1,2 Multiple numbering systems are used worldwide—Palmer/Zsigmondy, Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI), and the Universal system—each with practical trade-offs for digital records and quadrant reference.3,4

Traditionally, tooth numbering is performed manually on panoramic radiographs (PAN) or cone-beam CT CBCT scans. While PANs are widely used for initial assessments given their accessibility and broad anatomical coverage, CBCT imaging provide 3D visualization and greater diagnostic precision, especially in cases involving impacted, missing or supernumerary teeth.5–7 Nevertheless, manual tooth numbering can be time consuming and is prone to human error, particularly in complex anatomical or clinical scenarios.1,8–10

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning algorithms, into dental radiology offers a promising solution to these challenges by automating diagnostic tasks with high speed and consistency.9–11 Platforms such as Diagnocat can now automatically detect and number teeth according to the FDI system across multiple imaging modalities, including PAN images and CBCT scans.6,7

Previous studies have demonstrated high tooth-level accuracy for AI-based tooth numbering on PANs.12,13 However, 2 critical gaps in understanding hinder clinical translation. First, the comparative performance on 2D versus 3D imaging acquired from the same patients remains unquantified. A paired, within-subject design is essential to isolate the effect of imaging modality from case-mix and spectrum bias, ensuring that any observed differences reflect the informational gain of CBCT over PAN rather than inter-patient variability in anatomy, disease burden, restorations, or artifacts. This rationale is clinically consequential: PAN and CBCT are both used in routine practice for whole-dentition assessment, yet CBCT entails higher cost and radiation exposure, and its use must be justified by demonstrable incremental diagnostic reliability at the patient level. Second, and more critically, it is unknown how high tooth-level statistical metrics translate into the pragmatic, patient-level reliability required in clinical workflows, where a single tooth numbering error can compromise an entire case, trigger wrong-site interventions, or necessitate time-consuming manual correction. Tooth-level accuracy can mask clinically relevant failure rates when errors compound across a full dentition (eg, even low per-tooth error rates can yield an unacceptably high probability of at least one error per patient), whereas patient-level, error-free tooth numbering better reflects medico-legal standards and operational needs.

Prior work on AI-based tooth numbering has focused mainly on panoramic radiographs, reporting high per-tooth accuracy but rarely evaluating patient-level, error-free outputs or paired comparisons within the same individuals across modalities.10 A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis synthesized accuracy ranges for deep-learning models on dental radiographs but did not address patient-level reliability or paired PAN-CBCT analyses, which are critical for workflow adoption.14 Our study fills this gap by using a within-subject, paired design (each patient contributes both PAN and CBCT), and reporting both tooth-level metrics and patient-level “perfect-case” endpoints for a widely used commercial system.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of a commercially available AI platform for automated tooth numbering on PAN and CBCT examinations, using expert consensus as the reference standard. The study hypothesized that CBCT’s 3D information confers a higher patient-level tooth numbering reliability than PAN despite similar tooth-level metrics.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study aimed to assess the accuracy of tooth numbering performed by Diagnocat, an AI-based dental diagnostic software. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Bioethics Committee of <anonymized for per review> (date of approval April 23, 2025). To ensure patient confidentiality, all imaging data utilized in the study were anonymized before analysis. The study adheres to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) 2015 guidelines.15

Dataset

The study initially comprised 200 consecutive patients referred for PAN imaging and CBCT at a private dental center between January 2024 and December 2024. Referrals were made primarily by orthodontists and general dental practitioners. Indications for CBCT, using a large field of view (FOV) of 13 × 10 cm, included suspected periapical lesions identified on PAN or the presence of bilaterally impacted teeth. All PAN and CBCT examinations were performed on a single Hyperion X9 PRO unit (MyRay). Cone-beam CT scans were acquired using the device’s Regular preset (90 kV, 36 mAs, CTDIvol 4.09 mGy) with a 13 cm × 10 cm FOV and reconstructed with a 0.3-mm slice thickness. Following eligibility criteria were utilized.

Inclusion criteria were the availability of both modalities for the same patient, permanent or mixed dentition with identifiable FDI positions, and complete maxilla and mandible coverage.

Exclusion criteria were fully edentulous arches, cleft lip/palate precluding consistent FDI mapping, severe artefacts, FOV not encompassing whole dentition, unreadable images, and prior segmental resection that removes FDI positions.

Handling of variants: congenitally missing, impacted, and supernumerary teeth were retained. For analysis, the 32 permanent FDI positions (#18–#48) were treated as fixed tooth positions. When a primary tooth occupied a position corresponding to a permanent successor, the permanent tooth was considered absent for that position in the reference standard. If the AI labeled that primary tooth as the permanent successor (eg, labeling a primary canine as “23”), this was considered a numbering error for the intended permanent FDI charting output.

After application of eligibility criteria, 175 patients were eligible for analysis. All images were retrieved from the center’s digital archive. Patient identifiers were removed prior to analysis, and each dataset was assigned a unique study code to enable blinded evaluation.

Following upload and AI processing, 4 CBCT datasets were excluded from the CBCT analysis arm due to motion artifacts that rendered tooth identification unreliable, reducing the CBCT evaluation set to 171 examinations. These 4 datasets were identified during quality review of AI outputs; the AI system did not generate a motion-artifact flag or detection alert for any of them, and their exclusion was based on reader assessment of image quality against the pre-specified exclusion criterion of severe artefacts. For transparency, we note that the AI system did process these 4 datasets and generated tooth-numbering outputs; however, the reference standard could not be established reliably due to degraded image quality, precluding a valid accuracy assessment. The paired analysis was therefore performed on n = 171 matched PAN-CBCT pairs with in total 9800 (2 × 4900 for each dataset) tooth positions.

To characterize the anatomical complexity of the included cases, we retrospectively classified each patient using the consensus reference data. Cases were designated as complex if they exhibited at least one of the following:

impacted teeth (any tooth not fully erupted into the arch),

impacted teeth (any tooth not fully erupted into the arch),

supernumerary teeth,

supernumerary teeth,

congenitally missing teeth (hypodontia),

congenitally missing teeth (hypodontia),

mixed dentition with primary teeth occupying permanent positions,

mixed dentition with primary teeth occupying permanent positions,

presence of intraoral metallic dental materials (restorations, prosthetic components, orthodontic appliances, and endodontic materials).

By this classification, 168 of 171 patients (98.2%) were considered complex.

AI image analysis

Diagnoses were generated using Diagnocat (version 1.0, DGNCT LLC). All DICOM studies were uploaded and processed in April 2025 using the vendor’s then‑current web application and model pack. Because the platform is proprietary, algorithmic and training‑data details are not publicly available. However, the producer claims that the system utilizes convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on over 35 000 dental radiographs.

The software automatically processed the images to detect and enumerate the teeth based on the FDI numbering system.9 Outputs were harmonized into patient rows with 32 tooth positions (permanent dentition range) to enable one-to-one comparison against the reference. No manual edits were permitted.

Reference standard and data comparison

The reference standard was established by consensus between 2 board-certified readers (a radiologist and an orthodontist, each with over 10 years of clinical experience). To ensure independence, the readers were blinded to the AI-generated results and to each other’s initial interpretations. During their assessment, readers had access only to the patient’s age and sex and were blinded to all other clinical history and referral information. The reading process was performed in 2 separate, sequential sessions to minimize recall bias.

Session 1 (PAN analysis): both readers independently annotated all PAN images, recording the presence or absence and the FDI number for each of the 32 permanent tooth positions on a standardized data collection form. They subsequently held a consensus meeting to review and resolve any discrepancies through discussion and joint review of the images, creating a final PAN consensus dataset that served as the reference standard for that modality.

Session 2 (CBCT analysis): following a washout period of at least 4 weeks to mitigate recall, the same procedure was repeated for all CBCT images. The readers independently annotated the scans and then met to create the final CBCT consensus dataset.

For each imaging modality, the AI output was compared directly against the corresponding modality-specific consensus dataset (ie, AI-PAN output vs Reader-PAN consensus; AI-CBCT output vs Reader-CBCT consensus).

Each permanent tooth position (#18–#48) was independently assessed by both readers for presence/absence and FDI number. Disagreements were resolved in a consensus session to form the reference standard per modality. Supernumerary teeth were analyzed descriptively; gemination/fusion were treated as a single unit for tooth numbering.

Performance was assessed using a position-based framework across the 32 permanent FDI tooth positions (11-18, 21-28, 31-38, 41-48). For each examination, the reference standard defined whether a permanent tooth was present at each FDI position and, if present, the correct FDI label for that position. The AI output was considered correct at a given position only if it assigned the same FDI label to the tooth occupying that position (exact-match). This approach enables both tooth-position accuracy reporting and clinically interpretable case-level assessment of fully error-free examinations.

Sample size

This retrospective study included a consecutive series without an a priori sample size calculation. Consistent with STARD guidance for diagnostic accuracy reporting, we emphasize the precision of our estimates (95% confidence intervals [CIs]) rather than retrospective power. Therefore, we have removed post-hoc power calculations, which can be misleading when based on observed effects. The width of the reported CIs reflects the achieved precision for tooth- and patient-level endpoints in this cohort.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and range for age, as well as frequencies and percentages for sex distribution. The diagnostic performance of the AI platform was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), overall accuracy, and the F1-score. 95% CIs for tooth-level metrics used patient-level cluster bootstrap (resampling patients with replacement; 2000 replicates) to respect teeth-within-patient clustering.

Metrics were computed per permanent FDI position. A true positive (TP) was recorded when a permanent tooth was present at a given FDI position in the reference standard and the AI correctly assigned that same FDI label to the tooth occupying that position (exact match). A false negative (FN) was recorded when a permanent tooth was present at that FDI position in the reference standard but the AI did not provide the correct label for that position (including mislabeling to another position or omission). A false positive (FP) was recorded when the AI assigned an FDI label to a position that was absent in the reference standard or assigned an incorrect tooth to that FDI position. A true negative (TN) was recorded when a permanent tooth was absent at that FDI position in the reference standard and the AI did not assign a tooth to that position. Under this mapping, a single misnumbered tooth contributes both an FN at its true position and an FP at the predicted position.

To allow comparison of error frequency across tooth groups of different size (incisors, canines, premolars, molars), the absolute number of AI numbering errors within each group was normalized to a rate per 1000 evaluated tooth positions (errors/total positions in the group × 1000).

Paired case-level comparison was calculated for patients with both modalities available after exclusions (paired subset), and case-level performance was evaluated using a binary endpoint (“error-free examination”), defined as no tooth-numbering errors recorded for the examination. The paired difference in the proportion of error-free examinations between CBCT and panoramic radiographs was assessed using McNemar’s exact test for paired binary outcomes. The absolute paired difference was reported together with a 95% CI, estimated by patient-level paired bootstrap resampling.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to assess whether selected clinical complexity factors influenced AI numbering accuracy. The relationship between the number of missing teeth per patient (count variable) and the number of AI errors per patient was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), chosen because neither variable was normally distributed. For binary clinical factors (presence vs absence of impacted teeth, metallic dental materials, orthodontic appliances, mixed dentition, and supernumerary teeth), between-group differences in per-patient error counts were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; when the outcome was dichotomized (any error vs no error) and expected cell frequencies were small, Fisher’s exact test was used instead.

To assess inter-reader agreement for CBCT and PAN analyses, Cohen’s kappa (κ) was calculated and interpreted according to the scale proposed by Landis and Koch (< 0 poor; 0.00-0.20 slight; 0.21-0.40 fair; 0.41-0.60 moderate; 0.61-0.80 substantial; 0.81-1.00 almost perfect).16 The diagnostic accuracy metrics were calculated according to the method presented by Hicks et al.17 All the analyses were conducted in R software (ver. 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient characteristics

After applying eligibility criteria, the final study cohort comprised 171 patients with both imaging datasets. The 171 patients had a mean age of 31.4 ± 14.5 years, and 119 (69.6%) were female. The paired McNemar test was performed on n = 171 PAN and CBCT datasets. Both datasets contained a total of 4900 tooth positions for analysis. The mean number of teeth per patient was 28.7 ± 3.7 ( ± SD).

The study sample displayed a high prevalence of clinical complexity factors: intraoral metallic dental materials (restorations, prosthetic components, orthodontic appliances, and endodontic materials) were the most common finding, affecting 78.9% of cases, followed by missing teeth in 71.3% and impacted teeth in 28.1%. Orthodontic appliances were present in 20.5% of participants, while mixed dentition and supernumerary teeth were relatively rare, observed in 5.8% and 1.2% of cases, respectively. Overall, nearly all individuals (98.2%) exhibited at least one clinical complexity criterion.

Patient-level tooth numbering accuracy

While the tooth-level metrics suggest high performance, the patient-level analysis provides a more clinically pragmatic assessment of the AI’s reliability in a real-world workflow. For the panoramic dataset, an analysis of the error logs showed that 23 out of 171 scans (13.5%) contained at least one tooth numbering error. For the CBCT dataset, 16 out of 171 scans (9.4%) exhibited one or more errors. Consequently, the AI platform achieved a patient-level accuracy of 86.5% for PAN (148 of 171 radiographs were perfectly enumerated) and 90.6% for CBCT scans (155 of 171 scans were perfectly enumerated) (Table 4).

In the paired cohort (n = 171), 139 examinations were error-free on both modalities, 9 were error-free only on CBCT, 16 were error-free only on PAN, and 7 were not error-free on either modality. McNemar’s exact test did not demonstrate a statistically reliable difference between modalities (P = .230). Subgroup analyses

Clinical complexity factors were recorded for all 171 paired patients and merged with per-tooth reports. The prevalence of examined factors was: missing teeth 122/171 (71.3%), metallic restorations 135/171 (78.9%), impacted teeth 48/171 (28.1%), orthodontic appliances 35/171 (20.5%), mixed dentition 10/171 (5.8%), and supernumerary teeth 2/171 (1.2%). Of 171 patients, 168 (98.2%) met at least one complexity criterion, confirming a high-complexity cohort.

Per-tooth accuracy remained high across all subgroups and both modalities (Table 5; range 98.3%-99.9%). For missing teeth, a weak negative Spearman correlation between missing-tooth count and error count was observed on PAN (ρ = −0.098, P = .203) and was near-zero on CBCT (ρ = 0.001, P = .995), indicating no meaningful relationship. For impacted teeth, patients with impacted teeth showed marginally higher per-tooth accuracy (CBCT 99.8%, PAN 99.9%) compared with those without (CBCT 99.1%, PAN 99.0%); a Mann-Whitney test was significant on PAN (U = 2564, P = .025) but not CBCT (P = .141), likely reflecting the greater anatomical distinctiveness of unerupted teeth in 2D projection. Metallic dental materials were present in 135/171 patients (78.9%); accuracy was similar between those with and without metallic objects on both modalities (Fisher’s exact P = .198 for CBCT; P = .583 for PAN). Discussion

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the Diagnocat AI platform for automated tooth numbering across 2 common imaging modalities: panoramic radiographs and CBCT. Our results demonstrate a high degree of tooth-level accuracy, with the AI platform correctly enumerating 99.29%. of teeth on both PAN and CBCT datasets. However, while on patient-level performance varied between imaging modalities (86.5% for PAN and 90.6% for CBCT). These findings confirm that small, rare tooth-level errors can accumulate to clinically meaningful per-case failure rates, underscoring the importance of reporting patient-centric endpoints when evaluating AI intended for routine clinical use.

The identical sensitivity for both modalities (99.29%) indicates that the AI was equally capable of detecting present teeth on PAN and CBCT scans, with exactly the same number of errors in this paired cohort. Notably, the modalities showed different error distributions by tooth group: CBCT errors clustered more heavily in the molar region (10.7/1000 positions), while PAN errors were more evenly distributed across premolars and molars (8.0 and 6.3/1000 respectively), with higher anterior error rates on PAN than CBCT. While CBCT showed a modestly higher patient‑level error‑free rate than PAN, we avoid causal attribution. Three‑dimensional information and higher spatial resolution are plausible contributors, particularly for impacted third molars, crowding, or overlaps, but artifacts and reader/AI priors can also influence outcomes. Most errors in numbering involved scenarios such as impacted molars, severe crowding or supernumerary teeth, mirroring patterns reported in previous studies where both AI and human examiners struggle in atypical cases (Figure 8).18–22 The slight advantage seen for CBCT likely stems from its 3D nature and higher spatial resolution, which enables more accurate differentiation of adjacent teeth and complex anatomical features.23–26 Accordingly, we frame the CBCT advantage as hypothesis‑generating and encourage multi‑center, device‑diverse replication with pre‑specified subgroups.

Challenging adolescent cone-beam computed tomographic image shows ambiguous posterior findings that could be interpreted as supernumerary teeth, complicating adjacent FDI assignments (eg, #15/#25).

Despite great level of accuracy on tooth level, our further analysis revealed that although individual errors were rare, they were distributed across a notable portion of the cases, meaning a clinician would need to intervene and manually correct the output in a non-trivial number of scans. Furthermore, the patient-level analysis quantifies the clinical advantage of CBCT over panoramic imaging in a more meaningful way. While the tooth-level accuracy was identical across modalities, the patient-level analysis shows a 4.1 percentage point advantage in error-free rate for CBCT (90.6% vs 86.5%). However, this difference was not statistically reliable on paired testing (McNemar P = .230) and the 95% CI included zero (−1.8 to +9.9 pp). Accordingly, the data support the interpretation that both modalities enable high tooth-position performance, while any case-level advantage of 3-dimensional imaging—although plausible and directionally favorable—appears small in this cohort and should be confirmed in larger, external datasets. The clinical implication is practical rather than binary: automated numbering can meaningfully reduce manual workload and standardize charting, but it should be deployed with a verification step (or confidence-flagged review) because residual errors concentrate at the case level even when tooth-level metrics are high.

The paired design, implemented within a single patient cohort undergoing both PAN and CBCT for the same clinical indication, enabled a true within-subject, head-to-head comparison of the AI system’s diagnostic accuracy across these 2 standard modalities for whole-dentition assessment, while minimizing confounding and supporting modality-specific clinical inference. This significant disparity between the near-100% tooth-level accuracy and the 86%-90% patient-level accuracy highlights a critical consideration for the clinical implementation of AI tools: per-instance performance does not always translate directly to per-case reliability.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that AI-based tooth detection models can maintain high sensitivity at granular level, across imaging types when trained on sufficiently diverse datasets.18,22,23,27–32 The present study extends this by directly comparing AI performance across both modalities in the same patient cohort, allowing control for patient-related variability. Importantly, the choice between PAN and CBCT must be guided by clinical indication and radiation protection principles, not by downstream AI performance. Our data show that once either modality has been acquired on clinical grounds, the AI system delivers comparable tooth-numbering accuracy on both, although the error distribution across tooth groups differs between modalities.

While the AI system can serve as a useful first-pass charting tool that reduces the number of teeth requiring manual annotation, our data demonstrate that it does not yet eliminate the need for clinician oversight: at the patient level, approximately 1 in 7 PAN and 1 in 10 CBCT examinations still contained at least one numbering error requiring manual correction. The system therefore functions as a labor-reducing aid rather than a fully autonomous replacement for manual charting, and implementation should include a verification step for every case.

From a reporting perspective, our data illustrate why tooth-level accuracy alone is an insufficient proxy for clinical reliability. Patient-level composite outcomes (eg, “perfect tooth numbering”) map more directly to decision points (accept AI output vs perform manual correction) and align with broader guidance to match evaluation metrics to intended clinical utility.17 Future benchmarks for dental AI should therefore include both granular (tooth-level) and holistic (patient-level) measures, ideally with paired-modality analyses wherever the same individual contributes PAN and CBCT.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as a single-center evaluation using imaging data from a single device, our findings may not be generalizable to other patient populations, imaging hardware, or clinical settings. Second, the analysis was retrospective, and the sample size, while substantial, was based on a consecutive series rather than an a priori power calculation, which may limit the precision of our estimates. Third, we evaluated a single, proprietary AI platform which significantly limits direct reproducibility and future comparative work. Finally, while we performed exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by clinical complexity factors (missing teeth, impacted teeth, metallic restorations, orthodontic appliances, mixed dentition, and supernumerary teeth—present in 2/171 cases), the study was not powered for these comparisons. Per-tooth accuracy remained high across all subgroups (range 98.3%-99.9%). The only statistically significant finding was a higher PAN error rate in patients without impacted teeth compared to those with impacted teeth (Mann-Whitney P = .025), likely reflecting the greater anatomical distinctiveness of unerupted teeth. All other subgroup differences were non-significant. These analyses are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, multi-center, prospective investigations.

An AI platform for automated tooth numbering delivered high and identical tooth-level accuracy (99.29% for both modalities) on both PAN and CBCT, yet 1 in 7-10 studies still required at least one manual correction at the patient level, with CBCT showing a modest however not statistically conclusive in this cohort. Implementation should therefore pair AI with rapid human verification of case-level outputs and adopt evaluation frameworks that emphasize patient-centric reliability alongside traditional tooth-level metrics.
