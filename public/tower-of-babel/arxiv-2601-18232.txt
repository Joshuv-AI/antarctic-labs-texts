Complex-Valued-Matrix Permanents: SPA-based Approximations and Double-Cover Analysis
Junda Zhou and Pascal O. Vontobel

Abstract
Approximating the permanent of a complex-valued matrix is a fundamental problem with applications in Boson sampling and probabilistic inference. In this paper, we extend factor-graph-based methods for approximating the permanent of non-negative-real-valued matrices that are based on running the sum-product algorithm (SPA) on standard normal factor graphs, to factor-graph-based methods for approximating the permanent of complex-valued matrices that are based on running the SPA on double-edge normal factor graphs. On the algorithmic side, we investigate the behavior of the SPA, in particular how the SPA fixed points change when transitioning from real-valued to complex-valued matrix ensembles. On the analytical side, we use graph covers to analyze the Bethe approximation of the permanent, i.e., the approximation of the permanent that is obtained with the help of the SPA. This combined algorithmic and analytical perspective provides new insight into the structure of Bethe approximations in complex-valued problems and clarifies when such approximations remain meaningful beyond the non-negative-real-valued settings.



I
Introduction

I-A

Permanents

For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, let
[n]\triangleq\set{1,\dots,n}
and let

\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the symmetric group of
[n]
, i.e., the set of all
permutations of
[n]
. For a complex-valued matrix

\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{i,j})_{i,j}
of size
n\times n
, the permanent of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

is defined to be

\operatorname{perm}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\triangleq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\prod_{i\in[n]}\theta_{i,\sigma(i)}.

In this work, we study approximations of
\operatorname{perm}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
when the entries
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are i.i.d. complex-valued random variables.

Note that already the calculation of the permanent of non-negative-real-valued
matrices is computationally intractable for large
n
, and also its efficient
approximation is challenging. (For more details, see, e.g., the discussion in

[
1
]
.) The approximate computation of
the permanent of complex-valued matrices is even more challenging, as the real
and imaginary parts of
\prod_{i\in[n]}\theta_{i,\sigma(i)}
can be positive,
zero, or negative, and with that leading to “constructive and destructive
interferences” when approximately computing the real and imaginary parts of

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\prod_{i\in[n]}\theta_{i,\sigma(i)}
.

The permanent of a complex-valued matrix arises, for example in Boson sampling,
a candidate proposed
[
2
]
for
demonstrating quantum supremacy. In Boson sampling, the measurement outcome of
a linear optics system follows a distribution with an exponential-size support,
and each non-zero probability depends on the permanent of a complex matrix.
Based on conjectures on the complexity and distribution of permanents of random
complex matrices, it is proven
[
2
]

that no classical algorithm can efficiently sample (exactly or approximately)
from the outcome distribution, unless certain complexity classes collapse.

Towards resolving the complexity conjectures, progress

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]

has been made to identify the sources of computational difficulties and push
the limit of classical methods. In this light, we apply the techniques
developed for normal factor graphs
(NFGs)
[
8
,
9
]

for non-negative-real-valued matrices to complex-valued matrices.

I-B

Factor graphs and the sum-product algorithm

A factor graph

[
10
,
9
]

represents the factorization structure of a multivariate function. More
precisely, for a so-called global function that can be written as the product
of so-called local functions, a factor graph shows the local functions and
their variable dependencies by drawing a function node for every local
function, a variable node for every variable, and an edge if a variable appears
as an argument of a local function. The partition sum (or partition function)
of a factor graph is the sum of the global function over all configurations,
i.e., all possible assignments of values to the variables.

For a factor graph whose partition sum is computationally intractable, various
techniques

[
11
,
12
]

were proposed for efficient approximations of the partition sum. In particular,
the so-called Bethe approximation is obtained by running the sum-product
algorithm (SPA), also known as loopy belief propagation, until convergence, and
doing certain calculations based on the SPA fixed-point messages. In the
context of a permanent for a non-negative-real-valued matrix
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, a
normal factor graph
\mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be formulated such that its partition
sum equals
\operatorname{perm}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
(see, e.g.,

[
1
]
). The resulting Bethe
approximation is known as the Bethe permanent
\operatorname{perm}_{\mathrm{B}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and has been
analyzed extensively through the lenses of variational analysis, the SPA, and
graph
covers
[
1
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.

Toward better understanding the Bethe approximation of a partition sum, it is
of particular interest to study the ratio
Z(\mathsf{N})/Z_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathsf{N})
, where

Z(\mathsf{N})
represents the partition sum of a normal factor graph
\mathsf{N}
and
where
Z_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathsf{N})
represents the Bethe approximation of
Z(\mathsf{N})
. For various
classes of factor graphs it has been observed
that
[
16
]

\displaystyle\frac{Z(\mathsf{N})}{Z_{\mathrm{B}}(\mathsf{N})}

\displaystyle\approx\biggl(\frac{Z(\mathsf{N})}{Z_{\mathrm{B},2}(\mathsf{N})}\biggr)^{\!\!2},

(1)

where

is the so-called degree-

Bethe approximation of

[

. The expression

III
Sum-Product Algorithm on DE-NFG

Finding the log partition sum for a general NFG (with function nodes taking on
non-negative real values) is equivalent to minimizing the Gibbs free energy
function

[
12
,
11
]
.
A computationally tractable relaxation is to replace the Gibbs free energy
function with the Bethe free energy function and enlarge the feasible region.
One efficient algorithm to minimize the Bethe free energy function is the
sum-product algorithm (SPA)
[
10
]
. This
line of method has been analyzed in detail for the specific NFG
\mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

in
[
1
]
and shown to work very well.

Most of the relevant concepts involved in the SPA extend naturally from NFGs to
DE-NFGs (see Appendices
B
&

C
for
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and

[
19
]
for general DE-NFGs).
However, generalizing the (primal) Bethe free energy function from NFGs to
DE-NFGs is challenging, as the latter involves complex-valued functions. In
Appendix
B
, we
propose a generalization of the Bethe free energy function for

\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Although its stationarity conditions can be obtained with
the help of Wirtinger calculus (see e.g.,

[
20
]
), it does not have the desired
convexity properties. This is similar to the well-known stationary-action
principle from physics (sometimes imprecisely called the minimum-action
principle).

Various details of running the SPA on
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and

\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\epsilon})

summarized in Appendix
C

obtaining convergence of the SPA, a mid-point damping rule is applied, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{outgoing}}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{message\ update}}(\set{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{incoming}}_{t-1}})+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{outgoing}}_{t-1}\bigr).

Definition 12

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\ \middle|\begin{array}[]{c}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i})=1,\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}\succeq 0,\quad\forall i\in[n];\\

\triangle

Remark 13
.

We make some remarks about the above definition.

•

The belief
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}
is associated with the set of valid configurations
of
\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}
. In particular,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}(\ell,k)
is associated with the valid
configuration
(x^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j})_{j}=\mathbf{u}_{\ell},(x^{\mathrm{L}^{\prime}}_{i,j})_{j}=\mathbf{u}_{k}
,
where
\mathbf{u}_{\ell}
is the
\ell

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

The first three constraints in
\mathcal{B}
ensure that each matrix of
a pseudo-belief is a valid quantum mass function

[
23

, which is a
quantum generalization of the probability mass function (used for defining
the belief polytope of
\mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

[
1
, Def. 9]
).

•

The last two constraints in
\mathcal{B}
are referred as the “edge
consistency” constraints. They essentially require that the marginal of

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}
with respect to
\widetilde{x}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}
is the same as
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}
, and
similarly for
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}
. An alternative formalism is provided in the lemma
below.

•

Strictly speaking, we shall also introduce belief matrices for
variables
\widetilde{x}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}
and
\widetilde{x}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}
(see Fig.
2

\widetilde{f}_{i,j}

Lemma 14

C-C

SPA fixed-point belief

The SPA-based belief at function nodes
\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}
and
\widetilde{f}_{i,j}
at
time
t
are given by, respectively,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,t}(\ell,k)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j}\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j,t}\big([j=\ell],[j=k]\big),(\ell,k)\in[n]^{2}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,t}(\ell,k)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i}\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j,t}\big([i=\ell],[i=k]\big),(\ell,k)\in[n]^{2}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j,t}

\displaystyle\propto\mathbf{W}_{i,j}\odot\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j,t}\odot\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j,t},

and they are normalized according to the sum constraints, i.e.,

\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,t})=1,\quad\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,t})=1,\quad\text{and }\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{1}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j,t})=1.

Proposition 18

(See
[
17
]
)
.

At the fixed point of the SPA, the fixed-point belief vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathrm{F}}
satisfies the edge consistency
constraints. Furthermore, if the SPA is initialized with positive semidefinite messages, then
all fixed-point beliefs are positive semidefinite.

As a direct consequence of the above proposition, a fixed point of the SPA corresponds to
a pseudo-belief in the belief polytope. It turns out that fixed points of the SPA have
a deeper connection with stationary points of the Bethe free energy function.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}
be a complex matrix that contains no
0
, and
\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be the
associated DE-NFG. The interior stationary points of the Bethe free energy function
F_{\mathrm{B}}

correspond to non-diagonal fixed points of the SPA and vice versa.

Proof.

The idea of the proof of Theorem
19
is the same as

[
12
, Thm. 2]
, and we use the complex derivatives
defined in the Wirtinger calculus (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
).

We associate Lagrangian multipliers with the equality constraints defining

\mathcal{B}
and form the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{B}}+\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{i,j}\Bigl[\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{1}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}\big)-1\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i}\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}\Bigl[\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}\big)-1\Bigr]+\sum_{j}\gamma^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}\Bigl[\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}\big)-1\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\Bigl\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})-\bigl[\mathbf{V}_{j}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}\mathbf{V}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr]_{x,x^{\prime}}\Bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\mathrm{Re}\Bigl\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})-\bigl[\mathbf{V}_{i}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}\mathbf{V}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr]_{x,x^{\prime}}\Bigr)\Bigr\},

where
\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{i},\gamma^{\mathrm{R}}_{j},\gamma_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
.
Since only interior stationary points are considered, all beliefs are positive definite.

By setting the complex derivatives
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}(\ell,k)},\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}(\ell,k)}
and

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})}
to be zero, we obtain
the following stationarity conditions:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})=&\,\big(\mathbf{W}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})\big)^{-1}\exp\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})+\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(x^{\prime},x)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})+\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}(x^{\prime},x)}\big)-1+\gamma_{i,j}\Big\}\\
\ln\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i}(\ell,k)=&\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sum_{j\neq\ell}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(0,0)+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,\ell}(1,1)-1-\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{i},&\ell=k\\
&\sum_{j\neq\ell,k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(0,0)+\frac{1}{2}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,\ell}(1,0)+\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,\ell}(0,1)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,k}(0,1)+\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,k}(1,0)}\big)-1-\gamma^{\mathrm{L}}_{i},&\ell\neq k\end{aligned}\right.\end{split}

and similarly for
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{R}}_{j}
.

Finally, by identifying the Lagrangian multipliers with fixed-point messages
of the SPA, i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})&=\ln\overleftarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{L}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})\\
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})&=\ln\overrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,j}(x,x^{\prime})\end{aligned}\right.,\quad\forall\,(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}^{2},(i,j)\in[n]^{2},

we obtain the fixed-point beliefs from the stationarity conditions. (Note that
we require that the off-diagonal entry of each SPA message be non-zero, so
that the above quantities are well-defined.) The above shows that an interior
stationary point of the Bethe free energy function is a fixed point of SPA.
The reverse direction is the same as in the proof of

[
12
, Thm. 2]
.
∎

Lemma 21
.

For
(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})\times\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2})
, if there exists
(i,j)\in[n]^{2}

\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(1)}=(1,0)

\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(2)}\neq(1,0)

Proof.

Fix such an index
(i,j)
. Partition
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
into two subsets according to
the
(i,j)
-th edge weight:

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}=\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{p}}\,\sqcup\,\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{c}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{p}}
consists of those

\widehat{\mathsf{N}}
whose
(i,j)
-th edge has weight matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{c}}
consists
of those
\widehat{\mathsf{N}}
whose
(i,j)
-th edge has weight matrix

\widehat{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{c}}_{i,j}

Define a pairing map

\kappa(\widehat{\mathsf{N}})=\widehat{\mathsf{N}}^{\prime}
be the factor graph

\widehat{\mathsf{N}}
by changing only the
(i,j)
-th edge weight

\widehat{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{i,j}
to
\widehat{\mathbf{E}}^{\mathrm{c}}_{i,j}

\kappa

is a bijection, so
\bigl|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{c}}\bigr|=\bigl|\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{i,j,\mathrm{p}}\bigr|

\widehat{\mathcal{N}}_{2}
is partitioned into disjoint pairs

(i,j)
-th edge

On the other hand, since

and

have the same
(1,0)

\mathcal{S}_{n}

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}

[

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)}

Lemma 23
.

For the quantities defined above, it holds that
\frac{Z_{\mathrm{B},2}^{2}}{(n!)^{4}}=b_{n}(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-4}))

Proof.

We prove

Part 1:

i=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}(i)
,
\prod_{j}g_{i,j,\mathrm{avg}}(\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(1)},\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(2)})=|\theta_{i,\sigma_{1}(i)}|^{4}

independent of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)}
.

•

For a nontrivial cycle
C
of
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
, we have

\prod_{ij:i\in C}g_{i,j,\mathrm{avg}}(\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(1)},\widehat{x}_{i,j}^{(2)})=\prod_{i:i\in C}|\theta_{i,\sigma_{1}(i)}\theta_{i,\sigma_{2}(i)}|^{2}
if and only
if
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)}
have the same assignment for

\widehat{x}_{i,j}
for all
i\in C
. In other words, if a nontrivial cycle
C
is
assigned as a
(0,1)
-cycle in
\widehat{\mathsf{N}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and as a

(1,0)
-cycle in
\widehat{\mathsf{N}}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
(or vice versa), then

g_{i,j,\mathrm{avg}}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)=0
for
i\in C
.

Hence,
\mathbb{E}[g_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(2)}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)})]\neq 0
if and
only if
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(2)}
have the same assignment on every
nontrivial cycle
C
in
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
. When all the constraints are
met, we have

\mathbb{E}[g_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(1)}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})]=\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}(\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell}/2)^{c_{\ell}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}.

Hence, we have
\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}_{\mathrm{B},2}]=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}{(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}=\Psi_{n}(\mu_{2,2},\mu_{1,1}^{2},1)
.

H-C

Case
\mu_{2,0}\neq 0

In the case
\mu_{2,0}\neq 0
, the main difference arises from the fixed points of
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
.
Recall that

g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i,\sigma_{1}(i)}\theta_{i,\sigma_{2}(i)}.

By independence of the matrix entries, the mixed expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\,\overline{g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\right]

factorizes over
i\in[n]
. As in the previous case, cancellation occurs on all
nontrivial cycles of
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
. However, when
\mu_{2,0}\neq 0
,
mismatches are allowed at fixed points of
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
.

More precisely, the expectation is nonzero if and only if

h(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=h(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\quad\text{on all nontrivial cycles of }\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1},

while on the fixed-point set

\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})\triangleq\{i\in[n]:\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}(i)=i\}

the values
(\tau_{1}(i),\tau_{2}(i))
may differ from

(\sigma_{1}(i),\sigma_{2}(i))
. If, for some

i\in\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})
,

\sigma_{1}(i)=\sigma_{2}(i)=j,\qquad\tau_{1}(i)=\tau_{2}(i)=j^{\prime}\neq j,

then the corresponding factor contributes
\mathbb{E}\Big[\theta_{i,j}^{2}\overline{\theta_{i,j^{\prime}}^{2}}\Big]=|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}\neq 0.

Fix
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and we analyze when the mixed
expectation does not vanish. For the nontrivial cycles of

\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
, the contribution is the same as in the previous case
and equals
\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}.
On the fixed-point set

\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})
, admissible pairs
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
are
obtained by permuting the fixed points and independently choosing
orientations, yielding

\big(c_{1}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})\big)!\cdot 2^{c(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}

such pairs. Summing over all these
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\,\overline{g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=2^{c(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{c_{1}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}}\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}(\rho)}\prod_{\ell=2}^{c_{1}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}|\mu_{2,0}|^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\rho)}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})},

where
\rho
records the cycle structure induced on the fixed points.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\overline{g_{\mathrm{avg}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}2^{c(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}\Big(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{c_{1}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}}\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}(\rho)}\prod_{\ell=2}^{c_{1}(\rho)}|\mu_{2,0}|^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\rho)}\Big)\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1})}

\displaystyle=n!\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\Big(\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{c_{1}(\sigma)}}\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}(\rho)}\prod_{\ell=2}^{c_{1}(\sigma)}|\mu_{2,0}|^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\rho)}\Big)\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell c_{\ell}(\sigma)}2^{c_{\ell}(\sigma)}.

It remains to simplify this expression. View
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{c_{1}(\sigma)}
as acting on the fixed points of
\sigma
, thereby
producing a permutation
\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Under this composition, each
nontrivial cycle of
\tau
arises in exactly one of two ways:

•

from a nontrivial cycle of
\sigma
, contributing a factor

2(\mu_{1,1}^{2})^{\ell}
;

•

from a nontrivial cycle of
\rho
acting on fixed points, contributing
a factor
|\mu_{2,0}|^{2\ell}
.

Moreover, for each resulting
\tau
there are exactly
2^{c(\tau)}
pairs

(\sigma,\rho)
producing it.

Consequently, the sum reorganizes into a single cycle-index expression:

\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]=n!\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\mu_{2,2}^{c_{1}({\tau})}\prod_{\ell=2}^{n}(2\mu_{1,1}^{2\ell}+|\mu_{2,0}|^{2\ell})^{c_{\ell}(\tau)}=\psi_{n}(\mu_{2,2},\mu_{1,1}^{2},2,|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}).

A similar argument holds for
\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{\mathrm{B},2}^{2}\big]
, so we have
\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{\mathrm{B},2}^{2}\big]=\psi_{n}(\mu_{2,2},\mu_{1,1}^{2},1,|\mu_{2,0}|^{2})
.

Appendix I
Proof of Corollary
9

The constant
C
in Corollary
9
is given by

C=-\frac{\mu_{2,2}-2\mu_{1,1}^{2}-|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}}{\mu_{1,1}^{2}}-\frac{|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}}{\mu_{1,1}^{2}-|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}}.

Note that
\mu_{1,1}^{2}-|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}>0
always hold by Assumption

7
, whereas
\mu_{2,2}-2\mu_{1,1}^{2}-|\mu_{2,0}|^{2}
does
not have a definite sign. The asymptotics of

\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[Z^{2}(\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[Z_{\mathrm{B},2}^{2}(\mathsf{N}_{\mathrm{DE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big]}}=\sqrt{\frac{\psi_{n}(\mu_{2,2},\mu_{1,1}^{2},2,|\mu_{2,0}|^{2})}{\psi_{n}(\mu_{2,2},\mu_{1,1}^{2},1,|\mu_{2,0}|^{2})}}

is the consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 24
.

For
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},b>c\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(a,b,2,c)

\displaystyle\sim(n!)^{2}\Big(b^{n}\frac{1}{1-c/b}e^{(a-2b-c)/b}\Big[(n+1)-\frac{a-2b-c}{b}-\frac{c}{c-b}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(c^{n})\Big),

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(a,b,1,c)

\displaystyle\sim(n!)^{2}\Big(b^{n}\frac{1}{1-c/b}e^{(a-b-c)/b}+\mathcal{O}(c^{n})\Big).

Proof.

Setting
z_{1}=a,z_{k}=mb^{k}+c^{k}
for
k\geq 2,m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, the generating function of
Z_{n}
is given by

C(x)=\exp\Biggl(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{z_{k}x^{k}}{k}\Biggr)=\exp\bigl((a-mb-c)x-m\ln(1-bx)-\ln(1-cx)\bigr)=\frac{e^{(a-mb-c)x}}{(1-bx)^{m}(1-cx)}.

When
b>c
, the singularity of
C(x)
that determines the asymptotics of

[x^{n}]C(x)
is
x=1/b

[
22
]
.

Define
f_{m}(x)\triangleq e^{(a-mb-c)x}(1-cx)^{-1}
, which is analytic around

x=1/b
and
f(1/b)\neq 0
. To find an asymptotics with the desired accuracy,
we consider the Taylor series expansion of
f_{m}(x)
around
x=1/b
,

f_{m}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}f_{m,k}(1-bx)^{k},

where
f_{m,k}=\frac{(-1/b)^{k}}{k!}f_{m}^{(k)}(1/b)
.

Then we have
C(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}f_{m,k}(1-bx)^{k-m}
. We compute the
asymptotics of
[x^{n}]C(x)
from
[x^{n}](1-bx)^{k-m}
:

•

For
0\leq k<m
, Thm. VI.1 in

[
22
]
gives more correction terms for the
asymptotics of
[x^{n}](1-bx)^{k-m}
. For
m=1
and
m=2
, the situation is
much simpler.

•

For
k\geq m
,
(1-bx)^{k-m}
is analytic at
x=1/b
, then

[x^{n}](f_{m,k}(1-bx)^{k-m})=\mathcal{O}(c^{n})
. (See

[
22
, Thm. IV.9&10]
)

For
m=1
and 2, we have

\frac{1}{1-bx}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(bx)^{n},\frac{1}{(1-bx)^{2}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)(bx)^{n}\text{ when }|bx|<1.

Hence, we have
[x^{n}]\frac{1}{1-bx}=b^{n}
and

[x^{n}]\frac{1}{(1-bx)^{2}}=b^{n}(n+1)
. Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(a,b,1,c)

\displaystyle=(n!)^{2}[x^{n}]\frac{e^{(a-b-c)x}}{(1-bx)(1-cx)}

\displaystyle\sim(n!)^{2}(f_{1,0}b^{n}+\mathcal{O}(c^{n}))

\displaystyle=(n!)^{2}\Big(b^{n}\frac{1}{1-c/b}e^{(a-b-c)/b}+\mathcal{O}(c^{n})\Big),

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(a,b,2,c)

\displaystyle=(n!)^{2}[x^{n}]\frac{e^{(a-2b-c)x}}{(1-bx)^{2}(1-cx)}

\displaystyle\sim(n!)^{2}(f_{2,0}b^{n}(n+1)+f_{2,1}b^{n}+\mathcal{O}(c^{n}))

\displaystyle=(n!)^{2}\Big(b^{n}\frac{1}{1-c/b}e^{(a-2b-c)/b}\Big[(n+1)-\frac{a-2b-c}{b}-\frac{c}{c-b}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(c^{n})\Big).\qed
