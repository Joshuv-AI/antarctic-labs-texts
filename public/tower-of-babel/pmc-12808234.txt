Tetracycline removal from an aqueous solution using bimetallic-impregnated activated carbon synthesized via one-step dry-pyrolysis of hawthorn kernels
Authors: Hakimeh Sharififard, Behniya Hafezi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A novel bimetallic Fe-Mn-impregnated activated carbon (Fe: Mn: AC) composite was synthesized via the one-step dry pyrolysis method and is applied to tetracycline (TC) removal from aqueous solution. Characterization results revealed a mesoporous structure with various functional groups on the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent. The impacts of solution pH, adsorption time (kinetics), and initial TC concentration on equilibrium in batch adsorption were studied. The highest TC removal efficiency using Fe: Mn: AC occurred at a pH of 6. The kinetic analysis using various models revealed that the data best fit the Elovich model, highlighting the significance of external diffusion (film diffusion) during the adsorption process. The equilibrium data aligned well with the Langmuir isotherm, which predicted a maximum adsorption capacity of 463.040 mg/g for TC adsorption at 25 °C. The primary mechanisms of adsorption included Hydrogen bonding, surface complexation with metal functional groups (M-π), Lewis acid-base interactions, π-π interactions, and pore filling. Continuous mode experiments in five cycles confirm the high adsorption capacity of the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent for effectively removing pharmaceutical contaminants from aqueous media (27.5% reduction in adsorption ability).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31638-2.

Introduction

Pharmaceutical compounds are widely used in the treatment of diseases. Among these substances, antibiotics are widely used to prevent and treat microbial infections in humans and animals1,2. Tetracycline is one of the most widely used antibiotics by humans and animals (due to better antibacterial activity, lower cost, and lower side effects). Tetracycline, consumed by humans and animals, is not fully absorbed and metabolized, and it is transported to the environment through their feces and urine1,2. The presence of antibiotics in the environment (water and soil resources), even in very small amounts, is very harmful to both ecosystems and human health3. The presence of antibiotics, such as tetracycline, in the environment leads to antibiotic resistance in water bacteria, which causes drug resistance in humans and animals, posing a serious threat to public health1. Therefore, it is crucial to remove the antibiotics from water. Several separation methods including biodegradation4, electrochemical method5, photocatalytic processes6, membrane technique7, and adsorption8, have been suggested for treating wastewater containing tetracycline.

Among the new methods that have attracted the attention of many researchers in recent years is the adsorption method, which is simple, low-cost (with low energy consumption due to being carried out at ambient temperature and pressure), flexible, and highly efficient9–17. Considering the role of the solid adsorbent in the adsorption process, various synthetic and natural adsorbents have been developed and utilized to adsorb a range of contaminants. Since adsorption is carried out on the surface of the adsorbent particles, adsorbents with a high specific surface area lead to high adsorption capacity and also high adsorption rate. Among the most popular adsorbents is activated carbon. Activated carbon is a special adsorbent that can adsorb various adsorbates from liquids and gases because of its porous surface, high chemical stability, and high mechanical strength13,15,17. Today, activated carbon receives considerable attention due to the availability and affordability (cheapness) of raw materials for synthesis, which are often prepared from agricultural or industrial waste. The process of producing activated carbon from these wastes involves two carbonization and activation stages. In the carbonization stage, by heating the raw material (at temperatures between 400 and 800 and sometimes 1000 degrees Celsius) and in the presence of an inert gas, non-carbon materials such as oxygen and hydrogen are destroyed. This is done to achieve a constant mass of carbon and also to create a primary and porous structure. To increase the porosity of the carbon material obtained from the carbonization stage, the activation stage is performed. Activation is performed by two methods: physical and chemical activation processes, or a combination of the two processes.

One effective method to enhance the adsorption capacity of activated carbon adsorbents is to deposit metal nanoparticles on their surfaces. This deposition not only increases adsorption capacity but also imparts magnetic properties to the adsorbent, enabling its separation from the solution using an external magnetic force18. Most research on depositing metallic nanostructures onto activated carbon surfaces employs the wet-impregnation method following the synthesis of activated carbon18–22. In contrast, one-step dry-pyrolysis is a less energy-intensive method for producing carbon adsorbents that are impregnated with metal nanoparticles23. The dry-mixed pyrolysis method can anchor metallic nanostructures onto the activated surface. In this technique, the metal salts (acting as the activating agent) are mixed with the raw material in a dry state, and the pyrolysis process is carried out on this dry mixture. This approach is more feasible for industrial-scale applications compared to the traditional wet-impregnation method, which mixes the raw material with a solution of activating agent (metal salts) before pyrolysis24,25. Despite the advantages of the dry mixed pyrolysis method—such as being achievable in one step, which saves time and energy—limited studies have been conducted in this area23. There are many Hawthorn (Crataegus) trees in the Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad Province. Therefore, a huge amount of hawthorn kernels is available and can be used as agricultural by-products to produce activated carbon.

In this research, first, Fe3O4/Mn2O4/activated carbon (Fe: Mn: AC) was synthesized from hawthorn kernel, a biodegradable and inexpensive adsorbent, using a one-step dry-mixed pyrolysis method. This material was then applied to remove tetracycline (TC) from aqueous solutions via both batch and continuous adsorption processes.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of fe: mn: AC via dry-mixed pyrolysis

After washing with distilled water, the hawthorn kernel were dried at room temperature for 72 h, then ground and placed in a sieve shaker to prepare a 35- to 40-mesh particle-size powder. 5 g of biomass powder, 2 g NaOH (≥ 99%, Merck), 3 g FeSO4.7H2O (≥ 99.5%, Merck), and 1 g KMnO4 (≥ 99%, Merck) were mixed homogeneously (using a household mixer). The mixture was put into a stainless steel container. The N2 gas was purged into the container for 1 h to remove the air (gas flow rate: 500 mL/min). The pyrolysis process was performed in two steps. In the first step, the container was heated at 350 °C using a muffle furnace (M11L-1100 model) for 60 min (heating rate: 10 °C/min). This step is performed to ensure that the reaction of biomass with NaOH occurs in the molten state23. In the second step, the mixture was heated at 600 °C for 150 min (heating rate: 10 °C/min). After cooling to room temperature, the product was washed with distilled water several times until reaching a constant pH of the washing water. Additionally, the concentrations of iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn) in the washing water were detected, and washing continued until neither iron nor manganese was detected in the washing water (analysis performed via AAS). This product was named Fe: Mn: AC. Fe: AC (Fe-impregnated activated carbon) adsorbent was synthesized in the same process (without KMnO4) to study the impact of two metallic elements on the properties and the adsorption capacity. The hawthorn kernel was pyrolyzed with NaOH in the same process, and the product was named AC (activated carbon). The production yields (%) for synthesizing AC, Fe: AC, and Fe: Mn: AC carbonaceous adsorbents were 39.78%, 44.67%, and 45.31%, respectively24.

Characterization of prepared adsorbent

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) technique using TESCAN, TESCAN-Vega3 equipment equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) and element mapping was applied to analyze the purity and morphology of prepared adsorbents. The crystal structures of the metallic elements (Fe and Mn) coated onto the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent surface were analyzed using XRD pattern (X-ray powder diffraction) with Rigaku Ultima IV (Japan) equipment. Zetasizer equipment (UK, Malvern) was applied to measure the zeta potential of prepared adsorbents. The specific surface area and porosity properties of the prepared adsorbent were determined using BELSORP mini II (Japan) equipment. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was applied to detect the functional groups of prepared adsorbents using Perkin-Elmer Spectrum GX equipment. A VSM (Vibrating Sample Magnetometer) analyzer (MDK, VSM, Iran) was applied to detect the magnetic properties of prepared Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent.

Continuous adsorption process

One key aspect of the adsorption process is the potential to use the synthesized adsorbent in a continuous (industrial) system. To assess this capability, adsorption experiments are performed continuously in the laboratory. In this study, the adsorption capacity of the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent in a continuous setup was tested using a glass column that is 50 cm long and 0.9 cm in diameter. A peristaltic pump was utilized to deliver the feed solution to the top of the column. More details about this experiment are available in the Supporting Information file. The continuous process of TC adsorption was conducted in five adsorption/desorption cycles. After adsorption, adsorbed TC molecules were removed from the saturated Fe: Mn: AC surface by contacting it with a 0.1 M HCl (37%, Merck) solution for 12 h at a flow rate of 5 mL/min1. Then, distilled water was passed through the column for 24 h before starting the next adsorption cycle. More details are presented in the Supporting Information file. The operational conditions for the adsorption process during regeneration experiments: Feed flow rate: 5 mL/min, adsorbent mass: 2.5 g, pH = 6, C0 = 200 mg L− 1.

Results and discussion

Characterization of adsorbent

In the FTIR spectrum of the synthesized AC (presented in Fig. 1a), peaks at 3426 cm− 1 and 2981 cm− 1 indicate O-H bond vibrations, and the stretching of the C-H bond. The presence of carbonyl groups (C = O) in the structure of AC is evident with peaks in the range of 1600–1750 cm− 1. The carbonyl group is a characteristic functional group of carboxylic acids, ketones, and aldehydes. The peaks appearing in the range of 1000–1380 cm− 1 correspond to the vibration of the C-O band, which is present in phenol and alkoxy functional groups. In the FTIR spectrum of Fe: AC adsorbent (Fig. 1a), peaks corresponding to the hydroxyl, carboxylic acid, phenolic, aldehyde, and ketone functional groups in the activated carbon structure are present. However, the intensity of these peaks has changed, and they have also shifted slightly, indicating the interactions between the surface functional groups of activated carbon with iron. Additionally, new peaks have been observed at 705 cm− 1 and 579 cm− 1, indicating the F-O bond vibrations21,23. In the FTIR spectrum of Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent (Fig. 1a), new peaks appear at 723 cm− 1, 573, and 450, indicating vibrations of Fe-O and Mn-O bonds21,23. The appearance of these new peaks, along with the hydroxyl, carboxylic acid, aldehyde, phenol, and ketone functional groups on the surface of the activated carbon, indicates the formation of new and additional functional groups on the modified adsorbent surface.

The XRD pattern of the Fe: Mn: AC nanocomposite, shown in Fig. 1b, displays characteristic peaks at 2θ values of 35.8°, 44.8°, and 57.2°. These peaks indicate the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure of Fe3O4. Also, a broad peak at 2θ of 20°−30° corresponds to the amorphous phase of activated carbon. The peak at 2θ = 35.9° and 62.6° is also attributed to the tetragonal crystal structure of manganes oxide (Mn2O4). Figure 1c presents the VSM loop of Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent. This loop demonstrates the magnetic properties of this adsorbent, due to the presence of Fe3O4 and Mn2O4 metallic structures on its surface, as confirmed by XRD and EDX analyses. The saturation magnetization of Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent is about 27 emu/g. Therefore, the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent can be separated from the solution using an external magnetic force.

The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms for the carbon-based adsorbents, along with the pore size distribution diagram, are presented in Fig. 2. The porosity parameters are detailed in Table 1. The BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) method was applied to measure the total pore volume (Vtotal pore) and the total specific surface area (SBET). Additionally, the t-plot method was used to determine the micropore volume (Vmicro(cm3/g STP))26,27. The isotherm diagrams (Fig. 2a) are of the IV type, indicating the meso-microporous structure of the synthesized adsorbents. Comparing the specific surface area values shows a decrease in surface area with the presence of nanoparticles, which is caused by the closure of metal nanoparticles in the pores of the activated carbon surface and a reduction in its surface area23,28. The data present in Table 1 indicate a decrease in the total pore volume of activated carbon by embedding nanoparticles in its structure. However, the volume percent of the adsorbents’ mesopores has increased by embedding nanoparticles on their surface. By depositing nanoparticles on the surface of activated carbon, the volume percentage of mesopores increased from 34% for AC to 53% for Fe: AC and 98% for Fe: Mn: AC (Fig. 2b). Since adsorbate molecules/ions move more easily in mesopores than micropores, this enhances their diffusion rate in the adsorption onto Fe: AC and Fe: Mn: AC adsorbents9,29,30. In addition to increasing the volume percentage of mesopores, the average pore diameter increased from 6.2817 nm to 10.601 nm for Fe: AC and 16.049 nm for Fe: Mn: AC by placing metal nanoparticles on the surface of the activated carbon.

TC removal

Batch process

The pH parameter is a highly influential factor in adsorption from an aqueous medium, and its impact on TC removal through the adsorption process is very significant. Besides impacting the charge of the adsorbent surface, the pH of the solution also affects the ionic state of the organic substances such as TC in the wastewater. The pHZPC (zero point charge) is a key property of the adsorbent that defines its surface charge type (positive or negative) at various pH levels. According to the zeta potential analysis (presented in Fig. 4a), the pHZPC value for AC, Fe: AC, and Fe: Mn: AC adsorbents is 8.4, 7, and 6.6, respectively. The adsorbent surface is positive at pH below pHZPC (due to protonation of the functional groups) and has a negative charge at pH above pHZPC due to deprotonation of the functional groups).

According to the pKa values of tetracycline in an aqueous environment, tetracycline exists as a cation H4TC+ at pH < 3.4, as an uncharged molecule H3TC0 at pH between 3.4 and 7.7, as an anion H2TC− 1 at pH between 7.7 and 9.7, and as an anion H1TC− 2 at pH above 9.7. In an acidic environment with a pH of less than 3.4, the adsorption efficiency is low due to the repulsion between the protonated functional groups (-OH2+, -COOH2+, M-OH2+) on the positively charged adsorbent surface and the H4TC+ 1 cation. As the pH increases (pH < 3.4 or pH > 7.7), the protonation of the functional groups decreases, reducing the repulsion. This allows tetracycline neutral molecules (H3TC0) to interact more effectively with the functional groups of the adsorbent surface, resulting in an increased adsorption efficiency and TC removal25,31,32. The maximum removal of tetracycline using AC, Fe: AC, and Fe: Mn: AC adsorbents occurs at pH values of 7, 7, and 6, respectively (Fig. 4b). As the pH increases (above the pHzpc of the adsorbents), the adsorption amount decreases due to negative charge (because of the deprotonation) of the adsorbent surface and the presence of tetracycline in its anionic form.

Time is the critical parameter impacting the economics of the process. When the process is completed in less time, it becomes more economically viable. Figure 5a shows the amount of TC adsorbed per gram of AC, Fe: AC, Fe: Mn: AC adsorbents (q (mg/g)) over different time intervals. The Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent can remove over 90% of TC within 50 min of contact. The data show that the adsorption process reaches equilibrium in approximately 420 min, 360 min, and 100 min for TC adsorption using AC, Fe: AC, AND Fe: Mn: AC, respectively. At this equilibrium time, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of AC, Fe: AC, and Fe: Mn: AC adsorbents are 161.0 mg/g, 177.60 mg/g, and 196.28 mg/g, respectively. The data indicate a significant reduction in the time required to reach equilibrium in the TC adsorption process by depositing metal nanoparticles on the surface of the activated carbon adsorbent. This improvement can be due to the change in the porous structure of the activated carbon adsorbent and the change in its surface functional groups following the deposition of metal nanoparticles. By depositing iron and manganese metal nanoparticles on the AC surface, the proportion of mesopores increases significantly- by 53% for the adsorbent modified with iron nanoparticles (Fe: AC) and by 98% for the adsorbent modified with two metals (Fe: Mn: AC). The presence of mesopores facilitates the movement of TC molecules and reduces the time to reach equilibrium. The mesopores reduce internal mass-transfer resistance, which facilitates faster adsorption kinetics and desorption processes. This enhances the overall rate of adsorption on mesoporous adsorbents compared to purely microporous adsorbents. The diffusion of adsorbate molecules into the mesopores occurs through surface and Knudsen diffusion mechanisms, which these diffusion mechanisms depend on the adsorbate-surface interactions and pore size. These diffusions in mesopores complement adsorption on the pore walls, contributing to efficient mass transport33–36. Furthermore, the creation of new functional groups, such as F-O, Mn-O, creates more adsorption sites on the surface of activated carbon. This enhances the adsorption of TC molecules through complex formation and Lewis acid-base interactions37.

Experimental kinetic data were analyzed with pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), and Elovich models to determine the dominant type of interaction. Additionally, to assess the significance of the intra-particle diffusion mass transfer step relative to film diffusion, the experimental data were fitted to the intra-particle diffusion model. Suppose the experimental data fit the intra-particle diffusion model (a regression coefficient near 1 and a plot that passes through the origin). In that case, this suggests that intra-particle diffusion (internal diffusion) plays a more dominant and controlling role compared to film diffusion (external diffusion). Adsorption mechanism

Figure 8a displays the FTIR spectra of Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent before and after TC adsorption. The changes are obvious. The peak intensity and position have shifted considerably, and new peak has appeared. These changes in the peak strength and location of surface functional groups (including -COOH, -OH, Fe-O, Mn-O) reveal the chemical interactions between the surface functional groups of Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent and the molecules of tetracycline. The functional groups on the surface of activated carbon (-OH and -COOH) attract TC molecules by forming hydrogen bonds with the -OH and -NH2 groups in the TC structure47. Variations in the position and intensity of peaks associated with -OH, C-O, and C = O functional groups in the FTIR spectrum of Fe: Mn: AC following TC adsorption can indicate this chemical interaction (confirmed by the Elovich model). The aromatic (graphite-like) rings in the activated carbon structure attract TC molecules by creating a π-π interaction with the benzene ring in the TC structure30,47. Metal functional groups (Fe-O, Mn-O) can also form surface complexes with organic compounds (surface complexation) such as TC molecules1,2. Also, these metal centers (Fe and Mn) on the adsorbent surface act as Lewis acids and adsorb TC molecules by creating Lewis acid-base interactions with the oxygen of the -C = O, -OH groups (as Lewis bases) in the structure of tetracycline molecules37. The shift and change in the intensity of the peaks of these metal functional groups in the Fe: Mn: AC FTIR spectrum after adsorption confirm these interactions (confirmed by the Elovich model). Table 7 displays the changes in the characteristic peaks. Another mechanism of TC adsorption with the mesoporous Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent is pore filling (physical adsorption). To investigate the importance of pore diffusion (pore filling), a BET analysis was performed on the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent after TC adsorption. Reports indicate that the characteristic peak of TC is found at a wavelength of 1452 cm− 1, which is also evident in the FTIR spectrum of Fe: Mn: AC after TC adsorption1.

Figure 8b presents the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm diagram following the adsorption of TC. When compared to the adsorption-desorption diagrams of the adsorbent prior to TC adsorption, there is a noticeable decrease in surface porosity and specific surface area, with values dropping from 391 m²/g to 125 m²/g. Additionally, the total pore volume decreased from 0.65 cm³/g to 0.3415 cm³/g. This decrease in specific surface area and total pore volume indicates the diffusion of TC molecules into the adsorbent mesoporous structure and occupation of the pores. Conclusion

In this study, hawthorn kernels were transformed into a porous mesoporous adsorbent with multiple functional groups through a single-step dry pyrolysis process. This process utilized iron and manganese metal salts along with sodium hydroxide. The resulting bimetallic Fe-Mn-impregnated activated carbon (Fe: Mn: AC) was tested for its ability to remove tetracycline (TC) from aqueous solutions. The kinetic data fitting using the Elovich model indicated that chemical adsorption played a significant role. Furthermore, the equilibrium data aligned well with the Langmuir model. Various chemical interactions, including surface complexation, hydrogen bonding, Lewis acid-base interactions, π-π interactions, and physical adsorption, contributed to the adsorption of TC molecules by the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent. Results from continuous mode experiments over five cycles demonstrated the high adsorption capacity of the Fe: Mn: AC adsorbent for effectively removing pharmaceutical contaminants from water, with only a 27.5% reduction in adsorption capability observed.
