Time-weighted mean arterial pressure and pump flow during cardiopulmonary bypass in adults and its effects on neurological outcome parameters: a retrospective study protocol
Authors: Christoph Klivinyi, Michael Schoerghuber, Nikolaus Schreiber, Stephanie Fida, Manfred Fritz, Jakob Pannold, Martin Holler, Simon Orlob, Helmar Bornemann-Cimenti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionCardiopulmonary bypass has been used to perform complex cardiac surgery for over 70 years. Advances in bypass techniques and perioperative medicine have increased the safety of cardiac procedures, leading to reduced morbidity and mortality. Nevertheless, cardiopulmonary bypass still carries risks, including systemic inflammation and dysfunction of various organs. To date, optimal blood pressure management during cardiopulmonary bypass remains a subject of ongoing debate. Conflicting evidence exists regarding negative outcomes associated with both low and high mean arterial pressures. Current clinical guidelines recommend a broad target range for mean arterial pressure during cardiopulmonary bypass, which underscores the existing gap in knowledge. In non-cardiac surgery, the time-weighted average of mean arterial pressure has been used to determine minimum safe thresholds, with greater deviation from 65 mm Hg associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. However, the definition and reporting of low blood pressure during cardiopulmonary bypass varies between studies, and the use of time-weighted averages below the threshold is still uncommon. Details on pump flow during extracorporeal circulation are seldom reported.Methods and analysisWe plan to conduct a retrospective, single-centre data analysis to investigate the effects of both arterial blood pressure and extracorporeal pump flow, including their time-weighted averages and areas under defined thresholds, during cardiopulmonary bypass on neurological outcomes in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery between 2014 and 2023. The study will include both elective and emergency procedures, with separate analyses conducted based on the urgency and complexity of the operations. Digitally recorded anaesthesia and perfusion records will be imported and validated to extract information on haemodynamic parameters, neurological monitoring and extracorporeal circulation. Ischaemic and haemorrhagic strokes will be identified by screening postoperative brain imaging records for keywords indicating neurological events. Diagnostic data and additional patient and procedural information will be extracted from the local cardiac surgery database and hospital information system. Information about incidence and course of postoperative delirium will be extracted from the patient data management system used in intensive care. Introduction

The first successful open heart surgery on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was performed in 1953,1 with high mortality rates in the beginning. Advances in bypass techniques, surgical techniques and anaesthesiology led to rapid refinement and increased safety and efficacy of open heart surgery using CPB and enabled physicians to perform increasingly complex operations.2 Although most patients tolerate extracorporeal circulation for cardiac surgery well, its possible harmful side effects are often encountered (eg, systemic inflammation, stroke and neurological dysfunction, other end-organ dysfunction including heart, lung and kidneys).3 Conflicting evidence regarding minimum thresholds for perfusion pressure during CPB has been around for decades, ranging from lack of deleterious effects due to mean arterial pressures (MAP) lower than 50 mm Hg to increased risk of stroke and declining kidney function.4 5 One argument that fuels discussion to date is the ever-increasing age and complex medical history of patients undergoing cardiac surgery and whether results from studies conducted in the early 2000s still hold true. Gold et al compared target MAP during CPB ranging from 50–60 mm Hg to a high MAP group of 80–100 mm Hg, resulting in an incidence of major cardiac and neurological outcomes of 12.9% vs 4.8%, respectively.6 In a follow-up study by Charlson et al, a high MAP group of 80 mm Hg (the retained standard of care for their institution following their first study) was compared with an individual MAP target group.7 They failed to show any benefit of an individual tailored (higher) MAP target. With normothermic temperature management during CPB and thus absence of decrease in circulatory demand, both pump flow and perfusion pressure needs are to be met.8 Ono et al evaluated the area under the curve of blood pressure below the limits of cerebral autoregulation and its duration and found an increased risk of morbidity or mortality.9 Azau et al have shown that a MAP target of 50–60 mm Hg compared with 75–80 mm Hg did not result in a different rate of acute kidney injury nor clinically assessed neurological complications.10 It is noteworthy, though, that the need for vasopressor to achieve the higher MAP was increased fourfold. Likewise, Vedel et al conducted imaging studies of patients in either high (70–80 mm Hg) or low (40–50 mm Hg) groups and found no difference in volume or number of new cerebral infarcts.11 A meta-analysis by Kotani et al,12 including three of the aforementioned studies,6 10 11 came to the conclusion that the evidence of the effect of MAP less than or greater than 65 mm Hg is uncertain regarding acute kidney injury or overall mortality.

Sun et al conducted a retrospective cohort study in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery requiring CPB and found that a MAP of less than 65 mm Hg for each cumulative duration of 10 min was associated with an increased risk of stroke, further increasing with lower MAP of 55 mm Hg and below.13 They, however, solely focused on pressure and provided no information about pump flow.

Currently, it is recommended to maintain a MAP of 50–80 mm Hg during CPB.14 The rather generous target range still suggests a persisting knowledge gap. Interestingly, most studies exclude intraoperative low-flow (and thus probably low-pressure) phases during CPB, to underpin their expectation of relevance of MAP during full pump flow. Schreiber et al have shown that the time-weighted average (TWA) MAP and its deviation from 65 mm Hg during CPB increase the risk of acute kidney injury,15 a parameter that has also been used to study the effect of MAP during non-cardiac surgery.

We suspect that during CPB, both the TWA MAP and its deviation from 65 mm Hg (area under threshold, AUT), as well as TWA pump flow (thus reflecting systemic vascular resistance), are risk factors for adverse events. TWA AUT might be a better method to assess the dose of hypotension compared with addition of durations of hypotension. Furthermore, means to achieve a target MAP might matter, as we suspect that high doses of vasopressors may lead to unphysiological regional perfusion, that is, less blood flow for cerebral perfusion. Guinot et al have shown that the use of norepinephrine infusion increased the risk of major postoperative events.16 As it has been shown in a large-scale retrospective analysis in non-cardiac surgery patients,17 that any amount of time in hypotension is relevant for adverse outcomes, we will include any duration of hypotension in our analysis, including phases of low flow.

Methods and analysis

Scope and population

We plan to conduct a retrospective, single-centre data analysis, investigating the effects of arterial pressure and perfusion during CPB on neurological outcome parameters in adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery from 2014 to 2023. We will include both elective and emergency surgeries, but perform separate analysis per acuity and magnitude of operation. We expect 500–700 cases per year to be included in our analysis. We will exclude all patients not meeting inclusion criteria (see table 1) or failure to import or match different sources of data (eg, non-readable data in anaesthesia records, failure to match unique patient or case identifiers for anaesthesia or perfusion records or patient metadata).

Outcome parameters

We will assess neurological outcome parameters using search terms for common keywords in imaging studies (either CT or MRI), signalling ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke up to 1 year after surgery. Likewise, we will assess recorded diagnoses in the hospital information system for up to 1 year after surgery. We aim to assess incidence and duration of postoperative delirium from exports of the patient data management systems used in intensive care (either Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist, Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit or free text documentation by physicians during rounds, whichever is more reliably available).

Data sources

Lots of different data sources from different medical subsystems have to be aligned in order to shed light on our chosen outcome parameters. We will use a locally hosted JupyterLab (Project Jupyter, https://jupyter.org/) instance at server infrastructure at the Medical University of Graz. Python (Python Software Foundation, https://www.python.org/) scripts are used to extract data from various file formats and combine them into Pandas dataframes (pandas, https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/index.html). Vitabel (vitabel, https://github.com/UniGrazMath/vitabel) will be used to screen data for validity and synchronise time series. Statistical analysis will be carried out either in Python or R (The R Project for Statistical Computing, https://www.r-project.org/), depending on the methods used.

Archived surgical scheduling will be used to identify patients suitable for inclusion and data extraction. To obtain haemodynamic and metadata, we will process raw exports of archived anaesthesia protocols (eg, heart rate, different pressure readings from different sources, medication timing and dosing, etc). If multiple sites are used for obtaining arterial pressure readings, we will use the proposed method by Sun et al.13 However, we do not have access to the raw data recorded by the monitoring tools, which may provide high-resolution measurements several times per second but are not routinely stored. Instead, we use an abstraction layer from the patient data management system that generates archival anaesthesia protocols with lower resolution. Due to previous data analysis from non-cardiac surgery conducted at our institution, we expect the timing of haemodynamic data points to vary depending on the parameter (eg, heart rate vs invasive arterial pressure), ranging from 20 s to 60 s intervals. As of the registration of the study protocol and preparation of this manuscript, data have not yet been accessed, and further details of data quality cannot be provided at this point.

After algorithmic smoothing, the validity of data will be assessed by trained investigators of our study group, as described above, using plots of vital recordings. Outliers or clearly out-of-place data points may be omitted (eg, blood sampling artefacts). Once we have access to the haemodynamic parameters and on validating our data, we will try to develop an appropriate algorithm to detect and handle outliers to limit the amount of manual interventions. Anaesthesia records also contain information about near-infrared spectroscopy readings.

Data related to CPB will be extracted from data dumps of perfusion records, containing information about pump flow and pressure of the extracorporeal circuit with an expected resolution of three data points per minute, each 20 s apart. The local cardiac surgical registry will be used to obtain metadata about cardiac procedures and outcome parameters (type of operation, European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) II, etc). Data from the hospital information system will be used to aid in identifying and matching different records from subsystems, as different types of identifiers are used (unique patient identification numbers vs non-unique case numbers vs continuous/sequential numbering in perfusion records). Diagnoses prior to the operation until 1 year after surgery will be used for categorisation and to assess morbidity relevant to outcome parameters. If technically feasible, records from the patient data management system used in intensive care will be used to assess duration of ventilation, length of stay, use of vasoactive substances, transfusion of blood products and assessment of postoperative delirium.

Written records of imaging studies (ie, CT, MRI) are using free text description of possible pathologies but do not document the suspected diagnosis in a standardised manner or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding. Thus, we have to rely on search lists to identify possible pathological imaging studies and perform a manual review if there is a new pathology corresponding to stroke that has been identified. We will also use automated processing of ICD codes corresponding to relevant new diagnoses in the postoperative period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics will summarise patient demographics, intraoperative variables and outcome parameters. Categorical variables will be reported as counts and percentages, while continuous variables will be presented as mean±SD or median with IQR, based on data distribution.

Primary analysis

Our primary objective is to investigate the association between TWA MAP (figure 1) and its deviation from a predetermined threshold of 65 mm Hg (TWA AUT) and pump flow during CPB and the incidence of postoperative stroke and delirium.

Univariable analysis will be performed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression models will be built to estimate the independent association of TWA MAP, TWA AUT and TWA pump flow with binary outcomes (ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, delirium). Covariates will include age, sex, EuroSCORE II, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status (PS) Classification System, Simplified Acute Physiology Score, type and urgency of surgery and vasopressor requirement during CPB (see table 2). For continuous outcomes such as duration of delirium, we will use generalised linear models (GLMs) (eg, Poisson or negative binomial regression, depending on dispersion).

Given the potential non-linearity of effects, we also plan to use generalised additive models with spline smoothing terms for continuous predictors such as TWA MAP, TWA AUT and TWA pump flow to more flexibly model their association with outcomes.

Time-to-event analysis

We will perform Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for 30-day, 6-month and 12-month mortality, and compare groups using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression will be used to evaluate the effect of intraoperative MAP and pump flow on mortality, adjusting for the same covariates as above. Proportional hazards assumptions will be checked using Schoenfeld residuals.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

We will conduct stratified analyses based on: surgery type (valve vs Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) vs combined procedures), surgical urgency (elective vs emergency), high versus low vasopressor requirement (amount of concomitant administered vasopressors >1, norepinephrine >0.2 μg/kg/min) as well as inotropy requirement pre-CPB and post-CPB.

Additionally, we will repeat models excluding patients with significant missing data or unmatched records, and assess robustness using multiple imputation for missing values.

Model validation and diagnostics

Model fit and calibration will be assessed using Akaike information criterion for model comparison, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for discrimination, Hosmer-Lemeshow test for calibration (logistic models) and residual analysis for GLMs.

All tests will be two tailed with a significance level set at p<0.05. Effect estimates will be reported with 95% CIs.

The local ethics committee approved our study (Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Graz, IRB00002556, 36-296 ex 23/24). The study protocol was registered at the Center for Open Science (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/FAMV3). We aim to publish the results of our study preferably in an open access format.

Limitations and bias

As this is a single-centre study, there is evidently operator-induced bias in all involved practitioners of the multidisciplinary team that is necessary to perform cardiac surgery (ie, surgery, anaesthesiology, perfusion specialists), which we cannot correct for. There are standards of care established by standard operating procedures, especially in our anaesthetic practice, but individual approaches may vary due to changing surgical approaches. Depending on the team composition, there may be different changes in approaches during cardiac surgery as patient-centred signs of malperfusion occur (eg, increase of pump flow if near-infrared spectroscopy readings are low, early substitution of low haematocrit, etc). As we are conducting a prolonged evaluation spanning 10 years, there is an expected change in personnel and team composition. Unfortunately, as there is no registry for certain endpoints (eg, stroke in the perioperative period and up to 1 year after surgery, postoperative delirium), we are dependent on completeness and validity of documentation in the health records. Lastly, limitations from the retrospective design are well established.
