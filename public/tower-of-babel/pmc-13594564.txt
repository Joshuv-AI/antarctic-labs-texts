Cancer Stem Cell Quiescence: Molecular and Microenvironmental Evidence for an Actively Regulated Adaptive Survival Strategy
Authors: Yasin Ahmadi, Trefa M Abdullah, Sakhavat Abolhasani, Amin Jalizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer stem cells (CSCs) are a therapy‐refractory subpopulation that drives tumor persistence, recurrence, and metastasis. A defining characteristic of CSCs is their ability to reversibly exit the cell cycle and enter a quiescent state, thereby escaping cytotoxic therapies that primarily target proliferating cells. Although numerous reviews have examined CSC dormancy and quiescence, less attention has been devoted to recently identified regulators and to the molecular events associated with the transition into quiescence. This narrative review consolidates current knowledge on CSC‐specific entry regulators, including the pRb/RBL2–E2F1/4–GCN5 axis, Fbxw7–c‐Myc pathway, PML–PPARδ–FAO axis, ID1/ID3–p21 network, JARID1B‐mediated G0‐like states, CDH1–SIRT5–Cyclin F axis, and AP‐1/ETS network, alongside emerging evidence on epigenetic regulation, circadian influences, and immune‐mediated signals. We critically evaluate the exhaustion hypothesis, delineate a therapeutic framework that distinguishes between prevention of quiescence entry, elimination of dormant CSCs, and forced reactivation, and identify key knowledge gaps. By integrating recent advances within a structured perspective, this review provides a framework for understanding how CSCs exploit quiescence for survival and therapy resistance and informs strategies to target this resilient population.Keywords: cancer stem cell, cellular exhaustion, dormancy, quiescence, therapy resistance, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Contemporary oncology recognizes tumors not as homogeneous masses but as complex, spatially organized ecosystems. Single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses consistently reveal a heterogeneous tapestry of malignant cells coexisting with cancer‐associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells, pericytes, diverse immune infiltrates, and a remodeled extracellular matrix [1]. This tumor microenvironment (TME) is a functionally active determinant of tumor progression, metastasis, and critically, therapeutic response [2].

Within the malignant compartment itself, a hierarchical organization often exists. The bulk of a tumor is comprised of non‐cancer stem cells (non‐CSCs), the more differentiated progeny that drive rapid tumor expansion but possess limited self‐renewal capacity [3, 4]. In contrast, cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a minor subpopulation, approximately 0.1%–2% in many solid and hematologic cancers, that sustains long‐term tumor growth, mediates metastasis, and exhibits profound resistance to conventional therapies [5, 6]. CSCs do not represent a fixed cellular subset. Instead, accumulating evidence indicates that non‐CSCs can reversibly acquire stem‐like properties through phenotypic plasticity, particularly under selective pressures imposed by chemotherapy, hypoxic conditions, or tumor microenvironmental cues. This dynamic interconversion between CSC and non‐CSC states sustains intratumoral heterogeneity, enhances tumor adaptability, and represents a major obstacle to durable therapeutic responses [7, 8].

Beyond their capacity for self‐renewal and phenotypic plasticity, CSCs share another defining characteristic with normal tissue stem cells: the ability to enter a reversible state of cell‐cycle arrest known as quiescence. A cardinal feature of many normal adult stem cells, such as those in the hematopoietic system, muscle, and hair follicle, is their ability to enter a prolonged state of quiescence, or G0 phase. This dormancy preserves genomic integrity, prevents replicative exhaustion, and ensures long‐term regenerative capacity. For instance, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) may cycle only once every 30–50 days under homeostasis, activated precisely by stress or injury signals before returning to quiescence via tightly regulated pathways like TGF‐β and p53 [7, 8].

Increasing evidence suggests that CSCs exploit and dysregulate this evolutionarily conserved quiescence program, adapting it to promote tumor persistence, therapeutic resistance, and disease recurrence [9, 10, 11, 12]. Functional assays, including label retention and cell‐cycle analysis, consistently demonstrate that subsets of CSCs reside in a quiescent or slow‐cycling state. In chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), leukemic stem cells can remain dormant for years, explaining disease relapse long after apparently successful tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy [13, 14, 15, 16]. Similarly, therapy‐resistant, tumor‐propagating cells in glioblastoma and breast cancer models display a pronounced G0/G1 arrest compared to their proliferative counterparts [17]. Exit from quiescence is often triggered by regenerative pressures, such as therapy‐induced depletion of bulk tumor cells, and is mediated by inflammatory cytokines and niche‐derived signals [18]. Following repopulation, CSCs can re‐enter quiescence, potentially regulated by pathways like Notch, Wnt, and hypoxia‐inducible factors (HIFs), thereby re‐establishing a therapy‐resistant reservoir [19].

Over the past decade, a growing body of literature has firmly established that cellular dormancy, and specifically the quiescence of CSCs, represents not a passive decline but rather an actively regulated, reversible, and adaptive survival program. Vera‐Ramirez and Hunter [20] were among the first to formally articulate dormancy as an adaptive cell stress response mechanism, highlighting the critical balance between ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK activity as a key molecular determinant of the proliferative versus quiescent state. Sistigu et al. [21] further expanded this framework by linking dormancy mechanisms to the CSC phenotype, emphasizing that pathways governing dormancy entry and exit, particularly TGF‐β and Wnt signaling, strongly resemble those regulating cancer cell stemness. Kangwan et al. [22] characterized quiescence as an actively maintained state involving low metabolic activity, reduced translation, and autophagy induction, whereas Ayob and Ramasamy [23] identified quiescence as a core property of CSCs alongside survival, stemness, and adaptive capacity. Massagué and Ganesh [24] focused on metastasis‐initiating cells, describing intrinsic programs that allow these cells to survive stress and enter slow‐cycling states. More recently, Barnieh et al. [25] defined dormancy as an adaptive survival state driven by tumor hypoxia, extracellular matrix remodeling, and therapy‐induced stress, whereas Wang et al. [26] provided extensive mechanistic detail on the metabolic reprogramming, including shifts to oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, autophagy activation, and epigenetic regulation, that underpin dormancy as an adaptive strategy.

Although previous reviews have highlighted the importance of CSC dormancy, the molecular pathways that specifically orchestrate entry into quiescence, as opposed to its maintenance or subsequent reactivation, have received relatively limited attention as a distinct conceptual framework. Moreover, the possibility that quiescence may, under certain contexts, arise from proliferative exhaustion or stress‐induced functional decline has received relatively limited critical evaluation. Our review contributes to the existing literature by synthesizing recently identified regulators of CSC quiescence entry, critically evaluating the exhaustion hypothesis, integrating emerging evidence linking ferroptosis vulnerability to quiescent CSC states, and proposing a therapeutic framework that distinguishes prevention of quiescence entry, elimination of dormant CSCs, and forced reactivation.

In this narrative review, we synthesize current knowledge regarding the molecular and microenvironmental mechanisms that regulate CSC entry into quiescence. We discuss transcriptional, epigenetic, metabolic, immune‐mediated, and niche‐derived pathways implicated in the establishment of dormant CSC states and examine how these mechanisms interact to promote therapeutic resistance and disease persistence. We also evaluate evidence supporting adaptive and exhaustion‐associated models of quiescence, explore emerging vulnerabilities of dormant CSCs, including ferroptosis sensitivity, and outline therapeutic strategies aimed at preventing quiescence entry, eliminating dormant CSCs, or inducing their reactivation. Finally, we highlight key unanswered questions and future directions for the field.

Molecular Regulators of Quiescence in Cancer Stem Cells

Intrinsic Mechanisms Directly Governing CSC Quiescence

Entry into and maintenance of quiescence in CSCs are regulated by a coordinated network of intrinsic molecular mechanisms that operate within the context of oncogenic dysregulation [27, 28, 29]. CSCs residing in the G0 phase display low metabolic activity, reduced DNA synthesis, and heightened resistance to differentiation cues [30, 31]. This dormant state functions as a reservoir that preserves CSC plasticity and enables adaptive responses to environmental stressors such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and therapy‐induced pressure [29, 32]. Recent primary studies have elucidated several molecular pathways that directly govern the entry into or maintenance of this dormant state specifically in CSCs.

Among the most rigorously characterized mechanisms is the cell‐autonomous pRb/RBL2–E2F1/4–GCN5 transcriptional axis, identified through quantitative proteomic and functional genomic approaches in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and breast cancer CSCs [33]. This study demonstrated that E2F1 and E2F4 actively induce the expression of WNT ligands including WNT7A, WNT7B, WNT10A, and WNT4, whereas the retinoblastoma family members pRb and RBL2 repress this WNT signaling, thereby regulating CSC self‐renewal, chemoresistance, and invasiveness. Critically, the epigenetic enzyme GCN5 was identified as a downstream regulator that deposits H3K9ac marks onto WNT promoters and enhancers, functionally linking the cell‐autonomous transcriptional machinery to paracrine signaling networks that orchestrate CSC plasticity and G0 phase dynamics [33]. This axis represents a direct, multi‐omic validated pathway controlling CSC quiescence entry and exit with therapeutic implications for eliminating this refractory population.

The Fbxw7 ubiquitin ligase pathway constitutes a second highly established mechanism, with direct evidence from breast cancer models demonstrating its pivotal role in maintaining slow‐cycling, chemotherapy‐resistant tumor cells [34]. Fbxw7 was found to be highly expressed in quiescent breast cancer cells and CSCs, and its genetic ablation forced dormant tumor cells to exit the quiescent state and re‐enter the cell cycle, rendering them susceptible to paclitaxel in both immunodeficient xenograft and immunocompetent allograft models [34]. Mechanistically, FBXW7 maintains quiescence by targeting the proto‐oncoprotein c‐Myc for degradation, and its deletion leads to c‐Myc accumulation and subsequent cell cycle re‐entry. Breast cancer patients with low FBXW7 expression exhibited significantly longer overall survival, particularly among long‐term survivors, underscoring the clinical relevance of this pathway [34]. This evidence establishes Fbxw7 as a bona fide intrinsic regulator of CSC quiescence whose inhibition represents a viable strategy to overcome chemoresistance.

The promyelocytic leukemia (PML) protein pathway has been directly implicated in maintaining CSC quiescence, particularly in leukemia‐initiating cells [35]. Ito and colleagues demonstrated that PML maintains leukemia‐initiating cell quiescence by regulating apoptosis, proliferation, and mTOR signaling, with Pml−/− cells showing increased cell‐cycle entry and initial hyperproliferation but undergoing stem cell exhaustion with impaired regenerative potential [35]. These findings underscore PML's essential role in preserving stem cell function and long‐term competence in the malignant context. Therapeutic targeting of PML has been shown to eradicate quiescent leukemia‐initiating cells [35].

The inhibitors of DNA‐binding proteins, particularly ID1 and ID3, have been identified as important regulators of CSC quiescence through direct primary evidence. A landmark study established that ID1 and ID3 function together to govern colon cancer‐initiating cell self‐renewal through cell‐cycle restriction driven by the cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor p21, with silencing of ID1 and ID3 reducing the cancer‐initiating cell fraction and impairing long‐term self‐renewal [18, 19]. In a distinct mechanism involving alternative splicing, the Id1b isoform was shown to specifically maintain cells in a quiescent state while promoting self‐renewal and CSC‐like properties, with overexpression causing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, reduced proliferation, and increased expression of CSC markers including ALDH1A1, Notch‐1, Sox‐2, and Oct‐4 [36]. Supporting evidence in triple‐negative breast cancer demonstrated that depletion of ID proteins leads to reversible cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase, indicating that ID proteins actively prevent CSCs from entering quiescence [37]. Research in epithelial ovarian cancer identified an active RUNX1‐ID1/ID3 axis governing the undifferentiated state of CSC‐like cells, with RUNX1 maintaining CSCs through upregulation of ID1 and ID3 and its inhibition inducing CSC differentiation [38]. Collectively, ID proteins regulate CSC quiescence through p21‐mediated control, alternative splicing, and transcription factor networks that prevent differentiation and cell cycle exit.

The protein acetyltransferase KAT5 was recently shown to regulate transcriptional and proliferative heterogeneity in glioblastoma, with KAT5 activity suppressing the emergence of quiescent G0‐like subpopulations while promoting glioma stem‐like cell self‐renewal. KAT5 inactivation significantly decreased tumor progression and increased survival after standard‐of‐care treatment, suggesting a dynamic role in G0 ingress and egress [39]. A functional genomic screen in primary human acute myeloid leukemia identified an AP‐1/ETS transcription factor network as a critical regulator of quiescent label‐retaining cells, with enforced expression of JUN necessary and sufficient for label‐retaining cell quiescence, whereas ETS1 and FLI1 suppressed label‐retaining cell retention. This quiescent program was associated with chemotherapy persistence in diverse acute myeloid leukemia patients [40].

The H3K4me3 demethylase JARID1B enables transit between distinct CSC states in oral cancers, with a G0‐like cell‐cycle fraction characterized by low reactive oxygen species and suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling, distinguishing it from a JARID1Bhigh stem‐like compartment [41]. The cell cycle regulator CDH1 promotes entry into quiescence in colorectal cancer cells by dampening SIRT5‐mediated ubiquitination, thereby decreasing Cyclin F expression, inducing G0/G1 phase blockage, and enhancing stemness [42].

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway contributes to CSC maintenance and, in certain contexts, supports a dormant, treatment‐resistant phenotype through activation of GLI1/2 transcription factors, which induce stemness‐associated (SOX2, NANOG) and anti‐apoptotic (BCL‐2) genes. However, its direct role in CSC quiescence appears less well established than that of Notch or TGF‐β signaling [43, 44]. In breast cancer models, Hh signaling has been shown to strengthen CSC generation through Twist‐dependent mechanisms [43]. Importantly, Hh signaling has been demonstrated to influence a dormant, treatment‐resistant state through regulation of self‐renewal and survival pathways, although its direct role in quiescence entry is less well defined than that of Notch or TGF‐β [44].

Core developmental stemness pathways are intricately linked to cell‐cycle control. Although often associated with proliferation, Wnt/β‐catenin signaling can contribute to a slow‐cycling state in certain contexts, potentially by activating CDKN1C or other transcriptional networks that balance self‐renewal with cell‐cycle exit. TGF‐β acts as a potent inducer of cytostasis, driving the expression of p15INK4B and p21CIP1 and leading to reversible cell‐cycle arrest. In squamous cell carcinoma, TGF‐β‐induced quiescence is a major mechanism of chemoresistance in tumor‐propagating cells [45]. The Notch signaling pathway, frequently hyperactivated in CSCs, contributes both to stemness maintenance and quiescence through downstream effectors including HES and HEY transcriptional repressors that suppress genes required for cell‐cycle progression and differentiation [44, 46].

The p38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase pathway is a stress‐responsive signaling cascade that contributes to the maintenance of quiescence in CSCs [47]. Stress stimuli such as inflammatory cytokines or oxidative stress activate upstream kinases MKK3 and MKK6, which phosphorylate p38 MAPK, leading to transcriptional and post‐translational programs that promote cell‐cycle arrest and stress adaptation [48, 49]. In CSCs, p38 signaling suppresses proliferative signaling partly by promoting the internalization and degradation of EGFR [50] and by facilitating the degradation of stemness‐associated transcription factors such as SOX2, OCT4, and c‐Myc [51]. Through these mechanisms, sustained p38 MAPK activity helps enforce a reversible quiescent state that allows CSCs to tolerate cellular stress and contributes to therapy resistance and tumor recurrence [52].

Activation of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐γ (PPAR‐γ) by agonists such as pioglitazone has been shown to induce cell‐cycle entry in human leukemia stem cells from patients with chronic myeloid leukemia, mediated by downregulation of STAT5, HIF‐2α, and CITED2 [53, 54, 55]. In three patients with chronic myeloid leukemia with residual disease on imatinib, temporary administration of pioglitazone achieved a sustained complete molecular response for up to 4.7 years after withdrawal, providing compelling clinical evidence that targeting PPAR‐γ can erode the quiescent CSC pool [53]. The complex interplay of these intrinsic regulators and signaling cascades establishes a coordinated molecular network that governs the entry into, maintenance of, and exit from CSC quiescence. Epigenetic Regulation of CSC Quiescence

Epigenetic regulators play a fundamental role in stabilizing the quiescent, stem‐like state in CSCs. Through coordinated histone and DNA methylation, these modifiers establish a chromatin environment that supports CSC quiescence, survival, and therapy resistance.

Polycomb group proteins represent critical epigenetic regulators of CSC quiescence. BMI1, a core component of the Polycomb repressive complex 1, represses differentiation and senescence genes including the INK4a/ARF locus, thereby supporting self‐renewal and chemoresistance [56, 57, 58]. Similarly, EZH2, the catalytic subunit of Polycomb repressive complex 2, trimethylates H3K27 to silence target genes, promoting a less differentiated, therapy‐resistant, and often dormant CSC phenotype [59, 60].

Histone methylation beyond Polycomb has been directly implicated in CSC quiescence maintenance. In breast cancer models, H3K4 methyltransferases KMT2B and KMT2D sustain CSC quiescence, reduce proliferation, and impart chemoresistance. Inhibition of these methyltransferases leads to loss of the quiescent CSC phenotype in dormancy models in vivo, demonstrating a causal epigenetic role in quiescence maintenance [61]. As noted above, the H3K4me3 demethylase JARID1B enables transit between distinct CSC states, with a G0‐like cell‐cycle fraction characterized by suppressed PI3K/AKT signaling [41].

The AP‐1/ETS transcription factor network has recently emerged as a critical epigenetic regulator of quiescence in leukemia stem cells through functional genomic profiling in primary human acute myeloid leukemia [40]. This quiescent program is associated with chemotherapy persistence across diverse acute myeloid leukemia patients [40].

KAT5 regulates transcriptional and proliferative heterogeneity in glioblastoma through its acetyltransferase activity, suppressing the emergence of quiescent G0‐like subpopulations while promoting glioma stem‐like cell self‐renewal [39].

DNA methylation provides additional regulatory layers for CSC quiescence. Direct evidence from oral squamous cell carcinoma demonstrates that Sox2 promoter demethylation leads to Sox2 expression, thereby transforming CSCs from the G0 phase to the division stage, establishing a functional link between DNA methylation and G0 exit [62]. DNA methyltransferases also correlate with CSC‐like circulating cells and dormancy characteristics, suggesting that DNA methylation cooperates with histone modifications to regulate quiescent CSC states [63]. In disseminated dormant cancer cells, DNMT activity suppresses cell cycle progression and contributes to quiescence, whereas histone methylation at residues such as H3K4 and H3K27 is associated with diminished expression of growth‐promoting genes, reinforcing a dormant chromatin landscape [64].

Together, these findings indicate that epigenetic modifiers including BMI1, EZH2, KMT2B/D, JARID1B, the AP‐1/ETS network, KAT5, and DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) coordinate histone and DNA methylation to establish a chromatin environment that actively maintains CSC quiescence, survival, and therapy resistance.

Regulatory Mechanisms in Normal Stem Cells

The entry into and maintenance of quiescence in CSCs are regulated by mechanisms that largely mirror those controlling dormancy in normal stem cells, although they operate within a context of oncogenic dysregulation [1, 2, 3]. Among the most extensively studied normal stem cell systems is the hematopoietic stem cell compartment, which has provided fundamental insights into quiescence regulation that inform the understanding of CSC dormancy [65].

Among the molecular regulators of quiescence, cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors are among the most direct mediators of cell‐cycle arrest. Members of the CIP/KIP family, including p21CIP1/WAF1, p27KIP1, and p57KIP2, impose G0/G1 arrest primarily through inhibition of cyclin E‐CDK2 and cyclin D‐CDK4/6 complexes. In normal stem cell systems, particularly HSCs, CKIs play central roles in preserving the quiescent stem cell pool, and similar regulatory functions appear to operate in malignant stem cells [65, 66]. Among these inhibitors, p57 appears to play a particularly dominant role in sustaining stemness and quiescence. In quiescent HSCs, cyclin D1 forms a cytoplasmic complex with p57, positioning cyclin D1 for rapid activation upon mitogenic stimulation [66, 67]. Exposure to mitogenic signals increases cytoplasmic cyclin D1 levels and initiates the transition from G0 to G1/S [68, 69, 70], whereas high cytoplasmic p57 maintains cells in a dormant state [66, 67]. p57Kip2 and p27Kip1 cooperate to maintain HSC quiescence through interactions with Hsc70 [66]. In p57−/− Lin−Sca‐1+c‐Kit+ cells, compensatory upregulation of p27 and p18 has been observed, suggesting partial functional redundancy in maintaining HSC quiescence [71]. Following thrombopoietin stimulation, p27, but not p18, is markedly increased and exhibits cytoplasmic localization resembling that of p57 in wild‐type HSCs, indicating that p27 may compensate for p57 loss in preserving quiescence. Simultaneous deficiency of p57 and p27 fails to sustain long‐term HSC maintenance, demonstrating their cooperative role in regulating quiescence and self‐renewal [66]. Comparative studies further indicate that p57 exerts a stronger influence on stemness preservation than p21 or p27; p21 deficiency primarily impairs self‐renewal under stress conditions such as DNA damage, whereas p27 deletion alone has a limited impact on HSC number or self‐renewal capacity [67, 72, 73].

The FOXO family of transcription factors, including FOXO1, FOXO3, and FOXO4, links environmental stress signals to cell‐cycle arrest by transcriptionally activating CKIs such as p27 and genes encoding antioxidant enzymes in HSCs. Through these mechanisms, FOXO factors support cell survival under metabolic and oxidative stress conditions that commonly arise within the tumor microenvironment [74, 75, 76]. Foxo3a is essential for the maintenance of the HSC pool, and FoxOs are critical mediators of HSC resistance to physiologic oxidative stress [72, 74].

In normal HSCs, Fbxw7 functions as the substrate‐recognition component of an SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that targets several key cell‐cycle regulators and oncogenic proteins for degradation. Prominent substrates include cyclin E, c‐Myc, Notch1 [77, 78], and components of the mTOR pathway [79], all of which influence the balance between proliferation and quiescence [80]. Through ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of c‐Myc, Fbxw7 preserves quiescence and self‐renewal capacity [81]. Genetic deletion of Fbxw7 disrupts HSC dormancy, reduces bone marrow transplantation efficiency, and promotes the development of T‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Conversely, forced expression of Fbxw7α in LSK cells induces cell‐cycle dormancy by suppressing substrates such as c‐Myc, Notch1, and phosphorylated S6 within the mTOR signaling pathway [71]. Fbw7−/− HSCs exhibit impaired quiescence, diminished self‐renewal capacity, and loss of competitive repopulating ability due in part to stabilization of c‐Myc [82]. Ablation of Fbxw7 eliminates leukemia‐initiating cells by preventing quiescence, demonstrating that this pathway operates similarly in both normal and malignant stem cells [81].

PML maintains HSC quiescence by regulating apoptosis, proliferation, and mTOR signaling, with Pml−/− HSCs showing increased cell‐cycle entry and initial hyperproliferation but undergoing stem cell exhaustion with impaired regenerative potential [35]. The PML‐PPARδ‐FAO pathway regulates HSC division mode, with activation promoting asymmetric division that preserves the quiescent HSC pool, whereas genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition leads to symmetric commitment and stem cell exhaustion [83].

The circadian clock also influences stem cell maintenance in normal tissues, with core circadian regulators including BMAL1 and CLOCK influencing cell‐cycle progression and stem cell maintenance [84, 85, 86]. In epidermal stem cells, the circadian molecular clock creates cellular heterogeneity [85], suggesting conserved regulatory mechanisms between normal and malignant stem cell compartments.

Extrinsic Microenvironmental Regulation of CSC Quiescence

The CSC state is dynamically instructed by a specialized microenvironment or niche that not only protects CSCs but also actively signals to induce and maintain their quiescence.

Regions of low oxygen are a hallmark of solid tumors and a potent driver of CSC quiescence. Hypoxia stabilizes hypoxia‐inducible factors HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α, which orchestrate a transcriptional program promoting cell‐cycle arrest via p21 and p27, a shift to glycolytic metabolism, and enhanced stemness [87]. The hypoxic niche thus creates a microenvironment that promotes CSC dormancy and confers resistance to therapeutic stress.

Beyond hypoxia, the niche imposes metabolic challenges including fluctuating nutrient availability of glucose and glutamine, low pH, and oxidative stress [88, 89]. CSCs exhibit metabolic plasticity, often shifting between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to survive [90, 91]. This adaptability is not a sign of exhaustion but an active reprogramming. Under nutrient deprivation, the enforced low‐energy state can trigger a state of metabolic quiescence, conserving resources and avoiding proliferation‐linked death [91, 92].

Stromal cells, particularly cancer‐associated fibroblasts, are active architects of the quiescent niche. As a major secretory product of cancer‐associated fibroblasts, stromal‐derived TGF‐β is a primary extrinsic signal driving CSC quiescence and chemoresistance, as demonstrated in squamous cell carcinoma models [45, 93]. The chemokine CXCL12, secreted by perivascular and bone marrow stromal cells, engages its receptor CXCR4 on CSCs. This CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is critical for homing to and maintaining CSCs in protective niches that support dormancy, a key mechanism in metastatic latency [94, 95]. In some contexts, such as prostate cancer bone metastases, bone morphogenetic proteins from the bone stroma can induce dormancy in disseminated tumor cells, a population thought to be enriched for CSCs [96].

The immune compartment exerts complex and dual pressures on CSC quiescence. Cytotoxic lymphocytes and natural killer cells represent critical effectors of immune surveillance capable of directly targeting tumor cells, including cancer stem‐like populations, through cytotoxic mechanisms and cytokine secretion [96, 97, 98, 99]. Interferon‐gamma, produced by Th1 cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells, is a central mediator of tumor immunoediting and has been shown to induce tumor cell cycle arrest and quiescence, thereby contributing to an immunologically mediated dormant state [100, 101]. In contrast, proinflammatory conditions associated with chronic immune activation and sustained cytokine signaling by IFN‐γ and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha can paradoxically promote tumor cell survival, growth arrest, and immune evasion [102, 103]. Combined IFN‐γ and TNF‐α signaling has been demonstrated to induce stable growth arrest and senescence‐like programs in cancer cells [104, 105], a phenotype that may favor the persistence of dormant, therapy‐resistant cancer stem‐like cells in certain contexts [106].

Emerging evidence indicates that circadian clock genes also contribute to CSC quiescence regulation [107, 108]. Core circadian regulators, including BMAL1 and CLOCK, influence cell‐cycle progression and stem cell maintenance in both normal and malignant tissues [84, 85, 86]. In cancer models, disruption of circadian gene expression correlates with altered CSC quiescence dynamics and chemotherapy sensitivity [109, 110], suggesting that chronotherapeutic approaches might target quiescent CSCs more effectively. Although this area requires further investigation, the intersection of circadian biology and CSC dormancy represents an important emerging frontier not fully addressed in the existing quiescence literature [107, 108, 111].

Quiescence as an Actively Regulated, Adaptive Survival Strategy

CSCs Survival Context

CSCs establish a survival‐centric context by orchestrating key signaling pathways, transcription factors, and epigenetic programs that maintain stemness and enable adaptation under diverse microenvironmental and therapeutic stresses. Canonical developmental pathways initiate core stemness programs. The Wnt/β‐catenin pathway drives nuclear transcription of c‐MYC, LGR5, and Cyclin D1, promoting self‐renewal, proliferation, and metabolic adaptation [44, 112, 113]. Notch signaling, via the NICD‐RBPJκ‐MAML1 complex and downstream effectors HES1, HEY1, and c‐MYC, preserves stemness while suppressing differentiation [114, 115]. Hedgehog signaling activates GLI1/2 transcription factors, reinforcing stemness and survival through anti‐apoptotic genes, including BCL‐2 and pluripotency factors SOX2 and NANOG [114, 115, 116].

The PI3K pathway integrates survival with metabolic adaptation, quiescence, and redox defense via AKT, mTOR [117, 118, 119], and FOXO [120, 121]. Inflammatory and immune‐mediated signals, particularly IL‐6/IL‐8 activation of STAT3 and NF‐κB, amplify survival, EMT (epithelial‐mesenchymal transition), chemoresistance, and immune evasion while driving autocrine and paracrine niche support through VEGF, IL‐6/IL‐8, Survivin (BIRC5), MCL‐1, and PD‐L1 [122, 123].

Pluripotency transcription factors OCT4 [124], SOX2 [125, 126, 127], NANOG [128, 129], and c‐MYC [130] coordinate stemness, anti‐apoptotic signaling, and therapy resistance. Hypoxia‐stabilized HIF‐1α/2α drives glycolysis, angiogenesis, and stemness gene expression [112, 131, 132]. Chromatin remodelers BRG1/SMARCA4 maintain accessibility [133, 134, 135], whereas BMI1/PRC1 repress differentiation [58, 136, 137], collectively sustaining a stress‐adaptive transcriptional landscape.

DNA repair effectors (RAD51, CCNE1, PCNA) [138, 139], quiescence regulators (p21, p27, FOXO) [45, 140], drug efflux transporters (ABCG2) [138, 141], and antioxidant defenses (NRF2, NQO1) provide genomic stability and therapy resistance [138, 141, 142]. Notably, the antioxidant programs regulated by NRF2 and NQO1 are intimately linked to ferroptosis susceptibility, as they control glutathione metabolism and lipid peroxide clearance, pathways that have recently emerged as critical vulnerabilities in drug‐tolerant persister cells and quiescent CSC‐like states [143, 144]. NRF2 is a master transcription factor that orchestrates antioxidant gene expression, including glutathione synthesis enzymes and NQO1, a flavoprotein that detoxifies electrophilic substrates and prevents redox cycling [145]. Under oxidative stress, NRF2 translocates to the nucleus and activates cytoprotective programs that maintain redox homeostasis [146]. Through glutathione biosynthesis and NQO1‐mediated detoxification, this pathway protects quiescent CSCs from oxidative damage. However, because NRF2 and NQO1 regulate the balance of lipid peroxides that determines ferroptosis sensitivity, their pharmacological inhibition may deplete glutathione reserves, increase lipid peroxide accumulation, and sensitize quiescent CSCs to ferroptosis cell death [146, 147]. Beyond redox regulation, autophagy and metabolic flexibility (glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, glutamine utilization) further support survival under nutrient and genotoxic stress [128, 148, 149]. Emerging evidence indicates exosome‐mediated signaling enhances niche recruitment and reinforces survival programs [150, 151].

Overall, CSCs are transcriptionally, epigenetically, and metabolically reprogrammed to prioritize survival above all else. Integration of these pathways establishes a robust context in which CSCs maintain stemness, resist therapy, and propagate tumors under diverse microenvironmental pressures (Figure 2).

Extracellular cues derived from the tumor microenvironment, including neurotrophins (NG/BDNF/NT‐3), inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐8, and growth factors), and hypoxia, converge on intracellular signaling cascades such as PI3K–AKT–mTOR, JAK/STAT3, and NF‐κB. Metabolic and environmental stress responses further reinforce this survival framework. Hypoxia‐inducible factors (HIF‐1α/HIF‐2α) drive metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis, whereas oxidative stress activates NRF2‐dependent antioxidant programs that maintain redox homeostasis. Concurrently, DNA damage responses mediated by ATM/ATR and associated repair machinery (e.g., RAD51 and PCNA) preserve genomic stability under stress. Collectively, the coordinated integration of developmental signaling, microenvironmental cues, stress responses, and genome maintenance mechanisms sustain CSC persistence, adaptive plasticity, and resistance to anticancer therapies.

Maintaining Survival and Stemness Requirements in the Quiescent State

Quiescent CSCs are not metabolically or functionally inert; they actively maintain sophisticated defense machinery [31]. Direct transcriptomic analyses of long‐term label‐retaining quiescent CSCs from colorectal cancer organoids and xenografts reveal a coordinated enrichment of p53 pathway components and downstream cell cycle inhibitors, including CDKN1A (p21), reflecting active maintenance of dormancy and cell cycle arrest [152]. These findings confirm that quiescent CSCs actively engage regulatory programs that preserve genomic integrity during periods of low proliferative activity.

Comparative transcriptomic profiling across multiple tumor types, including non‐small cell lung cancer and colorectal cancer, identified a shared quiescence signature in slow‐cycling cancer cells enriched for CSC features. This signature encompasses genes associated with stemness, self‐renewal, and EMT, with ZEB2 consistently upregulated, suggesting that quiescence is coupled to phenotypic plasticity and survival mechanisms [153]. Additional functional characterization of quiescent colorectal CSCs demonstrated elevated expression of pCRAF and pASK1, proteins that integrate anti‐apoptotic and stress response signaling, contributing to chemoresistance and mesenchymal traits [154].

Side Population Cells as a Surrogate Model: Limitations and Cautions

Side population (SP) cells are defined by their ability to efflux Hoechst 33,342 dye via ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters, particularly ABCG2 and ABCB1, and have been widely used as an enriched source of stem‐like cells in cancer research [155, 156]. The SP assay has become a standard method for isolating cells with stem cell characteristics, especially when specific surface markers are unknown [157, 158]. However, important limitations must be noted. SP cells are not equivalent to quiescent CSCs; ABCG2 expression identifies both fast‐cycling and primitive stem‐like subsets within SP fractions, and SP cells constitute a heterogeneous population that does not directly correspond to experimentally defined quiescent CSCs [159]. Moreover, key features commonly attributed to CSCs, including ABC transporter expression, DNA damage response proteins, and metabolic adaptability, have not been directly characterized in purified quiescent CSC populations. Thus, while SP cell studies provide functional evidence of ABC transporter‐mediated drug efflux and chemoresistance, they should be interpreted as a surrogate stem‐like compartment with significant limitations [160]. This knowledge gap, the absence of direct characterization of ABC transporter function in bona fide quiescent CSCs, represents an important direction for future investigation and cautions against overinterpreting SP cell data as definitive evidence of quiescent CSC biology.

Quiescence Versus Exhaustion in CSCs

To provide a balanced assessment, we note that no CSC‐specific studies have explicitly argued for an “exhaustion” model of quiescence. The exhaustion hypothesis is derived primarily from the T‐cell immunology and normal stem cell aging literatures, and its direct applicability to CSCs remains unproven.

An alternative hypothesis suggests that CSC quiescence may arise from “exhaustion,” wherein proliferative fatigue and cellular stress, including prolonged chemotherapy, redirect resources toward drug efflux and DNA repair rather than proliferation, potentially leading to temporary cell‐cycle arrest [157, 158]. However, this framework has limited empirical support in the context of CSC biology. In immunology, exhausted T cells progressively lose effector functions, sustain inhibitory receptor expression (e.g., PD‐1, TIM‐3), and exhibit epigenetic and transcriptional programs that impair reinvigoration [157]. Similarly, stem cell exhaustion in normal tissues involves depletion of the regenerative pool due to repeated cycling or accumulated damage, resulting in diminished repopulation capacity [158].

In contrast, quiescent CSCs represent a slow‐cycling, protective subpopulation that preserves self‐renewal and the capacity to re‐enter proliferation, with evidence suggesting they maintain survival programs and stress‐response pathways; they are not dysfunctional; rather, they occupy a strategically dormant state [29, 31, 161]. Our assessment of the literature reveals no direct evidence that CSCs enter quiescence as a consequence of proliferative fatigue or loss of stem cell function. Instead, therapy often enriches for pre‐existing quiescent CSCs or actively induces quiescence via signaling pathways such as TGF‐β [45]. Descriptions of CSCs as “slow‐cycling” or “dormant” emphasize their protective, resilient nature rather than depletion [46].

It is also critical to distinguish quiescence from senescence, which involves irreversible cell‐cycle arrest and a distinct secretory phenotype, the Senescence‐Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP). CSCs avoid senescence, instead leveraging reversible quiescence to ensure survival and persistence [29]. Of note, recent literature has introduced the concept of “reversible senescence” or “senescence with rescue,” in which previously senescent cells can re‐enter the cell cycle under certain conditions. This phenomenon blurs the traditional boundary between quiescence and senescence and may have relevance to CSC dormancy, though it remains underexplored in the CSC field. Therefore, while the “exhaustion” hypothesis provides a conceptual contrast, it is not supported by functional or molecular data; quiescent CSCs exemplify adaptive resilience rather than terminal decline.

Model System Considerations and Limitations

A critical challenge in studying CSC quiescence is the absence of universal G0 markers [162]. Current approaches rely on label retention assays, Hoechst dye efflux, or pyronin gating, each with significant limitations. Label retention assays, for instance, depend on the dilution of fluorescent dyes over multiple cell divisions, but dye retention can be affected by factors such as dye efflux, degradation, and cell death, confounding the identification of truly quiescent cells [163, 164]. Hoechst dye efflux, which defines side population (SP) cells, identifies cells with active ABC transporters rather than quiescence per se, and has been shown to include both fast‐cycling and primitive stem‐like subsets [165, 166, 167].

Pyronin Y staining, which measures RNA content, can distinguish G0 from G1 cells but requires careful optimization and does not provide functional confirmation of quiescence [166, 168, 169]. The development of reliable surface markers or functional reporters for quiescent CSCs would greatly accelerate mechanistic studies and therapeutic development [162]. These limitations explain why much of the evidence for CSC quiescence is necessarily indirect and why findings from model systems must be interpreted with caution.

Beyond marker limitations, the conclusions drawn in this review are based on evidence from diverse experimental models, including murine xenografts, patient‐derived organoids, human cell lines, and ex vivo functional assays. Each model system carries inherent advantages and limitations. Murine models permit in vivo validation of quiescence regulators and therapeutic responses but may not fully recapitulate the human tumor immune microenvironment. Patient‐derived organoids retain greater physiological relevance and enable mechanistic dissection but lack native stromal and immune components. Human xenografts in immunocompromised mice facilitate study of CSC behavior but cannot capture immune‐mediated quiescence signals. The field would benefit from standardized approaches to isolate and characterize quiescent CSCs across model systems, as well as from increased use of humanized mouse models and co‐culture systems that better approximate the native tumor niche. Readers should interpret the evidence with these model‐specific constraints in mind.

Therapeutic Implications: Targeting the Adaptive, Quiescent State

Recognition of quiescence as an active, adaptive survival program necessitates therapeutic strategies that extend beyond conventional antiproliferative agents to directly target this resilient state.

One approach involves preventing CSC entry into quiescence, thereby sensitizing tumor‐propagating cells to therapy. Inhibition of TGF‐β signaling, for example, with the TGF‐β receptor inhibitor galunisertib, or blockade of downstream Smad signaling, can suppress therapy‐induced quiescence and enhance chemosensitivity in squamous cell carcinoma models [45]. Likewise, targeting the Notch pathway with γ‐secretase inhibitors (GSIs) or monoclonal antibodies disrupts signaling that maintains CSC quiescence and stemness, reducing the CSC pool and improving responses to chemotherapy and radiotherapy in preclinical studies [159].

An important consideration regarding prevention of quiescence entry is the potential unintended consequence of CDK inhibitors as therapeutic agents. CDK4/6 inhibitors, widely used in breast cancer and increasingly explored in other malignancies, function by enforcing cell‐cycle arrest [170, 171]. Preclinical evidence reveals that CDK4/6 inhibition can paradoxically enrich CSC populations in specific contexts. In ER‐positive breast cancer, palbociclib‐resistant cells exhibit elevated stemness, including increased mammosphere formation, upregulation of stem cell markers (Oct‐4, Sox2, Nanog), and elevated ALDH‐positive cell populations, with CXCR4 identified as a key driver of stemness maintenance and palbociclib resistance via activation of the WNT5A/β‐catenin pathway [170]. In lung adenocarcinoma, CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance has been linked to HES1 overexpression, which promotes cancer cell stemness through SOX9 upregulation via STAT3 phosphorylation; targeting this pathway with HES1 inhibitors (e.g., VR23) or pSTAT3 inhibitors (e.g., napabucasin) synergistically enhanced palbociclib efficacy in preclinical models [172]. However, the relationship between CDK4/6 inhibition and CSC biology is context‐dependent. In triple‐negative breast cancer, palbociclib was shown to reduce the CD44+/CD24− and ALDH+ CSC subpopulations and inhibit their self‐renewal capacity, suggesting that CDK4 inhibition can also eliminate CSCs in certain contexts [173]. Additionally, in patient‐derived glioma stem cell‐enriched cell lines, prolonged palbociclib exposure induced a senescent‐like quiescence rather than true senescence, and cells resumed proliferation upon drug removal, indicating that palbociclib can induce a reversible quiescent‐like state in CSCs [174]. Collectively, these findings indicate that CDK4/6 inhibitors do not uniformly drive CSC formation; rather, their effects on CSCs are context‐dependent, varying by tumor type, genetic background, and the specific mechanisms of acquired resistance. This complexity warrants further investigation to determine whether CDK4/6 inhibitor‐treated tumors show evidence of CSC enrichment and whether combination with CSC‐targeting agents could mitigate resistance.

A complementary strategy aims to eliminate CSCs within their dormant niche by exploiting vulnerabilities specific to the quiescent state. Inhibitors of BMI1, such as PTC596, disrupt BMI1‐mediated transcriptional repression and DNA damage responses, sensitizing CSCs to platinum‐based chemotherapy and suppressing tumor initiation [56, 57]. Quiescent CSCs also exhibit distinct metabolic dependencies, including reliance on autophagy, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [90, 175]. Pharmacological inhibition of these pathways, for instance, with chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine, may selectively target dormant CSC reservoirs. Additionally, interference with epigenetic regulators that maintain transcriptional plasticity, including BET bromodomain and EZH2 inhibitors, may disrupt the poised transcriptional state that enables rapid CSC reactivation [64, 87].

A particularly compelling vulnerability of quiescent and therapy‐tolerant states has recently emerged from studies of drug‐tolerant persister (DTP) cells, which share key features with quiescent CSCs, including slow‐cycling behavior, stemness‐associated gene expression, and reversible chromatin‐mediated adaptation [144]. DTP cells exhibit a marked dependence on the lipid‐peroxide‐detoxifying enzyme GPX4, and pharmacological inhibition of GPX4 selectively eliminates these cells through ferroptosis—an iron‐dependent, non‐apoptotic cell death driven by accumulation of lipid peroxides [143]. This GPX4 dependency has been independently confirmed in high‐mesenchymal therapy‐resistant states, which similarly exhibit mesenchymal gene expression and oxidative stress vulnerability [176]. The ferroptosis pathway is regulated by multiple nodes that may represent additional therapeutic targets in quiescent CSCs, including the cystine‐glutamate antiporter SLC7A11 (xCT), which provides cysteine for glutathione synthesis [177]; the ferroptosis suppressor protein FSP1 (AIFM2), which functions in a GPX4‐independent manner to reduce lipid peroxides [178]; and the balance of polyunsaturated phospholipids that determines membrane susceptibility to peroxidation [179]. Notably, the redox and antioxidant programs that quiescent CSCs maintain to survive stress, including NRF2 and NQO1, are intimately connected to ferroptosis sensitivity, as these pathways regulate glutathione metabolism and lipid peroxide clearance [146, 147]. Thus, targeting ferroptosis through GPX4 inhibition, SLC7A11 blockade, or modulation of lipid metabolism may offer a rational strategy to eliminate quiescent CSC reservoirs that resist conventional therapies. The reversible, chromatin‐mediated nature of the persister state directly reinforces the argument that quiescent CSCs represent an actively maintained, adaptable population rather than a terminally exhausted one [144].

A third, more speculative strategy involves forced reactivation of quiescent CSCs to render them susceptible to cytotoxic therapy. Although experimental approaches using cytokines or growth factors have been explored, this strategy carries significant risk because incomplete elimination of reactivated CSCs may accelerate tumor regrowth.

Finally, microRNAs (miRNAs) represent additional regulatory nodes governing CSC quiescence and stemness. Reduced expression of miR‐221/miR‐222 correlates with elevated p27/p57 levels and maintenance of quiescence in leukemia and glioblastoma models, suggesting that restoring these miRNAs could promote cell‐cycle re‐entry [180]. Similarly, the miR‐200 family, which regulates EMT and stemness, influences the balance between dormancy and proliferation [180]. Targeting these miRNA networks, therefore, represents a promising, though technically challenging, therapeutic avenue [181]. Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions

Several critical knowledge gaps warrant further investigation. First, the absence of universal G0 markers for quiescent CSCs remains a major technical hurdle. Current approaches rely on label retention, Hoechst dye efflux, or pyronin gating, each with significant limitations. The development of reliable surface markers or functional reporters for quiescent CSCs would greatly accelerate mechanistic studies and therapeutic development.

Second, the relationship between CSC quiescence and therapeutic CDK inhibition requires systematic investigation. Given that CDK4/6 inhibitors enforce cell‐cycle arrest through pathways that overlap with natural quiescence regulators, preclinical studies should examine whether these agents expand the quiescent CSC pool and whether combination with CSC‐targeting strategies could prevent this.

Third, the continuum between quiescence, senescence, and exhaustion remains poorly defined in the CSC context. The emerging concept of “reversible senescence” challenges the traditional binary distinction and may have relevance to CSC dormancy that has not been explored. Understanding the molecular determinants that distinguish reversible quiescence from terminal senescence or exhaustion would inform therapeutic strategies that avoid promoting irreversible states while eliminating therapy‐resistant CSCs.

Fourth, the translational gap between preclinical models and human disease remains substantial. Validation of quiescence mechanisms in patient samples, particularly from minimal residual disease settings, is urgently needed. Development of patient‐derived models that recapitulate the full tumor microenvironment, including immune components, would enable more accurate assessment of quiescence‐regulating pathways and therapeutic responses.

Fifth, the heterogeneity of quiescent CSC populations within tumors requires more detailed characterization. Single‐cell transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses of quiescent CSCs across different tumor types and treatment contexts may reveal common and context‐specific vulnerabilities that could be therapeutically exploited.

Conclusion

The question of whether CSCs quiescence represents an actively regulated survival strategy or a state of cellular exhaustion has been approached in this review by systematically examining the molecular, microenvironmental, and functional evidence. Although both conceptual frameworks offer useful perspectives, the accumulated data strongly support interpreting CSC quiescence as a dynamic, actively maintained adaptive program rather than a passive consequence of proliferative exhaustion. It should be noted that this interpretation, whereas strongly supported, is not entirely novel, as previous reviews have proposed similar frameworks strategy [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Nevertheless, our review provides a specific increment through a comprehensive synthesis of recently identified CSC‐specific entry regulators, critical evaluation of the exhaustion hypothesis with explicit acknowledgment that no CSC‐specific studies support this framework, integration of emerging ferroptosis vulnerability data, and delineation of a therapeutic framework distinguishing prevention of quiescence entry, elimination of dormant CSCs, and forced reactivation.

This interpretation is supported by several lines of evidence. Quiescent CSCs engage coordinated intrinsic regulatory networks, including cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors (p21, p27, p57), FOXO transcription factors, and ubiquitin‐mediated control via Fbxw7 that actively enforce G0 arrest while preserving self‐renewal capacity. Extrinsic niche signals, particularly hypoxia, TGF‐β, and stromal‐derived factors, actively instruct and sustain the quiescent state rather than merely accommodating it. Transcriptomic profiling further indicates that quiescent CSCs maintain expression of stemness‐associated genes, stress‐response pathways, and anti‐apoptotic programs, reflecting functional engagement rather than metabolic or transcriptional inertia. Importantly, and in contrast to exhausted or senescent cells, quiescent CSCs retain the capacity to re‐enter the cell cycle upon appropriate signals and sustain long‐term tumor‐propagating potential.

However, several nuances warrant acknowledgment. The distinction between active regulation and exhaustion is not always absolute in practice. Microenvironmental stressors, including chronic therapy exposure, may blur this boundary, and some features associated with quiescence, such as reduced metabolic activity and cell‐cycle arrest, could, under extreme or prolonged stress, converge with aspects of cellular exhaustion. Furthermore, the emerging concept of “reversible senescence” challenges the traditional binary distinction between quiescence (reversible) and senescence (irreversible), suggesting a more nuanced continuum of dormant states that CSCs may occupy. The available evidence does not entirely exclude the possibility that exhaustion‐like or senescence‐like states may emerge in certain contexts, particularly within harsh tumor microenvironments or following repeated therapeutic insult. We emphasize that the “exhaustion” hypothesis has not been directly tested in CSCs, and the absence of evidence does not constitute evidence of absence. However, the preponderance of functional and molecular data indicates that CSC quiescence is predominantly characterized by active, reversible regulation rather than terminal decline.

The evidence synthesized here derives primarily from murine models, human xenografts, and organoid systems, each with inherent limitations (see Section 4). Validation of these findings in human tumors, particularly in patients with minimal residual disease, remains an important priority for the field.

This conceptual framing carries meaningful therapeutic implications. If quiescence is indeed an adaptive survival strategy, then targeting the specific mechanisms that enforce and maintain this state, including dormancy‐promoting signaling pathways (TGF‐β, Notch), epigenetic regulators (BMI1, EZH2), and metabolic dependencies (autophagy, OXPHOS), may offer rational avenues for eliminating this therapy‐resistant reservoir. Emerging evidence that drug‐tolerant persister cells, which share key features with quiescent CSCs, are selectively vulnerable to ferroptosis through inhibition of the lipid‐peroxide‐detoxifying enzyme GPX4 suggests that targeting this pathway may represent a particularly promising strategy to eliminate dormant CSC populations. An important consideration for therapeutic development is the potential that CDK4/6 inhibitors, which enforce cell‐cycle arrest, could inadvertently promote CSC quiescence, warranting combination strategies that eliminate dormant cells rather than merely arresting them. Strategies aimed at preventing entry into quiescence or selectively eradicating quiescent CSCs, rather than merely targeting proliferating bulk tumor cells, will likely be required to address minimal residual disease and reduce tumor recurrence.

In summary, the evidence synthesized in this review suggests that CSC quiescence is best understood as an actively regulated, adaptive survival strategy, a state of reversible cell‐cycle arrest that preserves stemness, enables persistence through therapeutic stress, and ultimately contributes to treatment failure. Although the exhaustion hypothesis provides a useful conceptual counterpoint, it does not adequately account for the coordinated molecular programs and functional properties that characterize quiescent CSCs. We acknowledge that the absence of CSC‐specific evidence for an exhaustion model does not definitively disprove its existence, and exhaustion‐like features may emerge under extreme conditions. However, based on currently available evidence, CSC quiescence is predominantly a manifestation of active adaptation rather than passive decline. Further investigation into the contextual factors that may shift this balance will refine our understanding and inform more effective therapeutic targeting.
