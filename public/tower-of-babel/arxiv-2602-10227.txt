Wave scattering by a transversal defect in a discrete waveguide
Elena Medvedeva, Raphael Assier, Anastasia Kisil

Abstract
We study wave scattering by a finite transversal strip in a discrete square-lattice waveguide with Dirichlet boundary conditions imposed on the strip and the waveguide walls. The setting is motivated as a discrete analogue of the classical continuous waveguide problem with a screen. The corresponding Wiener–Hopf formulation leads to an equation with a 4 × 4 4\times 4 matrix kernel, which reduces to a 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix kernel under some symmetry assumptions. The factorisation prospects of this kernel are discussed, but this route is not followed. Instead, an exact analytical solution is obtained using the pole removal technique. This contrasts with the continuous case, where only approximate solutions are currently available. The reflection and transmission coefficients resulting from an incident duct mode are computed with an accuracy up to 10 − 13 10^{-13} , showing consistency with theoretical predictions from continuous waveguide theory. In particular, full reflection and zero transmission are recovered as the frequency approaches the cut-off value for the incident mode. Finally, the solution is validated against a numerical computation of the diffraction problem via the Boundary Algebraic Equations method with a tailored lattice Green’s function.



1
Introduction

Discrete lattice models have a long history in the analysis of wave propagation and scattering.
One of the most widely used frameworks for modelling discrete systems is the mass-spring model introduced by Slepyan for studying dynamic fracture in discrete structures
[
52
]
. This simple yet powerful approach has been applied to lattices of masses connected by springs to investigate not only dynamical fracture phenomena in lattice systems
[
31
,
34
,
30
,
10
]
, but also wave scattering problems, pioneered by Sharma
[
43
,
44
]
and further developed in subsequent works, for example
[
33
,
35
,
29
]
. Similar methods have also been used to study wave propagation in waveguides
[
53
,
54
]
and in elastic bodies
[
32
]
with embedded microstructures.

Building on Slepyan’s work, the theory of wave propagation in discrete waveguides has been studied extensively by Sharma, beginning with
[
49
,
48
]
, where dispersion relations and normal modes were derived for infinitely long square- and triangular-lattice waveguides under various boundary conditions on the walls. Some of these results were expressed implicitly through linear combinations of Chebyshev polynomials and were subsequently employed in
[
50
,
51
,
28
]
.

The classical problem of scattering by a transversal screen in a continuous waveguide has been studied extensively using the Wiener–Hopf method
[
16
,
57
]
among other approaches
[
7
]
; see
[
13
]
for a comprehensive review. This waveguide formulation also arises from the problem of diffraction by an infinite periodic grating by reducing it to a waveguide with quasi-periodic conditions on the walls.
In this work, we consider the discrete analogue of this problem: wave scattering by a finite strip within a discrete waveguide, as shown in Fig.
1
.

Similarly to the continuous case
[
13
,
7
]
, a finite strip in a discrete waveguide leads to a matrix Wiener–Hopf equation. In both discrete and continuous settings, when no specific symmetry is assumed, the kernel is a
4\times 4
matrix, for which no exact factorisation is known. Indeed, factorising matrix kernels is substantially more difficult than factorising scalar kernels
[
36
,
18
,
38
]
. However, there are important differences between the discrete and continuous settings, offering a useful framework for investigating how discretisation influences waveguide theory.

In particular, in the continuous formulation, the matrix Wiener–Hopf kernel can be factorised in the Khrapkov–Daniele form
[
13
,
14
]
under specific symmetry assumptions. However, as discussed in Section
4
, the matrix kernel arising in the discrete formulation, although seemingly simpler than its continuous counterpart and also belonging to the class of Khrapkov–Daniele matrices
[
17
]
under the same symmetry assumption, is nevertheless more difficult to factorise directly.

Moreover, in the general continuous case (no specific symmetry), only approximate solutions can be obtained via the pole removal method
[
13
,
7
]
. In contrast, as shown in the present paper, for the discrete problem, the pole removal method leads to a finite system of linear equations and therefore yields an exact solution.

Additionally, the discrete formulation admits an exact solution through the system of boundary algebraic equations
[
37
]
, a method that has been applied in related contexts in
[
45
,
46
,
29
]
. An analogy between the continuous and discrete Wiener–Hopf formulations of diffraction problems was also discussed in
[
19
]
, where waveguide problems were considered in particular.

In summary, analysing the discrete waveguide formulation enables an assessment of the applicability of mathematical techniques developed for the continuous case, as in
[
29
,
23
]
, and provides a way to examine whether the theoretical predictions for reflection and transmission coefficients obtained in the continuous setting
[
40
]
are preserved in the discrete analogue.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we formulate the scattering problem for a discrete waveguide with a transversal screen and discuss the structure of the wavefield inside the waveguide. In Section
3
we formulate the matrix Wiener–Hopf problem for the general and symmetric cases, followed by a discussion on the prospects of matrix kernel factorisation in Section
4
. In Section
5
we derive a solution to the Wiener–Hopf problem using the pole removal technique, and in Section
6
we derive an expression for a tailored lattice Green’s function for a discrete waveguide and formulate a solution in terms of a system of boundary algebraic equations. In Section
7
, we analyse the obtained reflection and transmission coefficients. Finally, conclusions are drawn in Section
8
.

2
Discrete waveguide with a transversal screen

2.1
Geometry of the problem

Figure 1
:
A discrete waveguide of a square lattice with a transversal Dirichlet screen and Dirichlet
waveguide walls,
u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=0
for
(m,n)\in\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime\prime}

.

We consider an incident duct mode plane wave within an infinite discrete square-lattice waveguide containing a transversal defect (Fig.
1
). The lattice supports the propagation of anti-plane waves.

The total displacement field
u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}
in the waveguide satisfies the discrete Helmholtz equation:

\Delta u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}+\Omega^{2}u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=0,\quad\{m,n\}\in(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{N})\backslash(\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\cup\Gamma^{\prime}),

(2.1)

where

\Delta u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{tot}}_{m+1,n}+u^{\text{tot}}_{m-1,n}+u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n+1}+u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n-1}-4u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}.

(2.2)

is the five-point discrete Laplacian,
\Omega
is the lattice frequency,
\mathbb{Z}_{N}\equiv\{\,n\in\mathbb{Z}:-N_{1}\leq n\leq N_{2}\,\}
, and
\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
are the sets of nodes defining the waveguide walls and the transversal screen:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}=\{m\in\mathbb{Z};\ n=-N_{1}\cup n=N_{2}\},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime\prime}=\{m=0;-n_{1}\leq n\leq n_{2}\}.

(2.4)

We set Dirichlet boundary conditions for the total field on the screen and the waveguide walls:

\displaystyle u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=0,\qquad\{m,n\}\in\Gamma^{\prime\prime};

(2.5)

\displaystyle u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=0,\qquad\{m,n\}\in\Gamma^{\prime}.

(2.6)

The total field is defined as a sum of the known incident field
u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}
and the unknown scattered field
u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}
:

u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}+u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}.

(2.7)

2.2
Dispersion relation

We will seek a solution to (
2.1
), (
2.5
)-(
2.7
) as a combination of waves of the form:

u_{m,n}=x^{m}\ \left(y^{n}-y^{-n}\right)=x^{m}\operatorname{s}(n),

(2.8)

where we defined the ‘sine’ and, for future convenience, the
‘cosine’ functions as:

\displaystyle\operatorname{s}(n):=y^{n}-y^{-n},\qquad\operatorname{c}(n):=y^{n}+y^{-n}.

(2.9)

Note that, for brevity, we do not explicitly indicate in the notation that
\operatorname{s}(n)
and
\operatorname{c}(n)
depend on
y
(and hence on
x
).

We assume that
|x|\leqslant 1,\ |y|\leqslant 1
in (
2.8
), and that
x,\ y

satisfy the lattice dispersion relation:

\Omega^{2}-4+x+x^{-1}+y+y^{-1}=0.

(2.10)

Note that only the waves satisfying the boundary conditions on the waveguide walls (
2.6
) may exist in the waveguide
[
49
,
48
]
. Each such wave can be written as a superposition of waveguide modes
u_{m,n}^{j}
defined by

u^{j}_{m,n}=x_{j}^{m}\operatorname{s}_{j}(n+N_{1}),\qquad\operatorname{s}_{j}(n):=\left[y_{j}^{n}-y_{j}^{-n}\right],

(2.11)

where
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
are given by

x_{j}=e^{iK_{j}},\quad y_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}},\quad\theta_{j}=\frac{j\pi}{N},

(2.12)

for some real Bloch wavenumber
K_{j}
, whose values are restricted to the Brillouin zone
[-\pi,\pi]
, and

N=N_{1}+N_{2}.

(2.13)

Therefore, each mode has a sine shape with a complex amplitude:

u^{j}_{m,n}=2i\ x^{m}_{j}\sin{\left[\theta_{j}(n+N_{1})\right]}.

containing an integer number of half-periods between the walls. The piston mode (plane waves) of the form
x_{j}^{m}
cannot propagate in the Dirichlet waveguide due to the boundary conditions on the walls. Because of periodicity, there are
N-1
physically meaningful values of
j
:
j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
. This contrasts with continuous waveguides, which may support infinitely many modes.

Substituting (
2.11
) into the discrete Helmholtz equation (
2.1
) yields the lattice dispersion relation (
2.10
) for the
j
-th waveguide mode:

\Omega^{2}-4+x_{j}+x_{j}^{-1}+y_{j}+y_{j}^{-1}=\Omega^{2}-4+2\cos K_{j}+2\cos\theta_{j}=0.

(2.14)

From (
2.12
),
\theta_{j}\in[0,\pi]
, and the dispersion relation (
2.14
) implies that
\Omega
lies within
\left[0,2\sqrt{2}\right]
for the lattice to support plane-wave propagation. As explained in
[
25
,
55
,
41
]
, the values
\Omega=0
,
\Omega=2
and
\Omega=2\sqrt{2}
are resonant degeneracies, which we omit here. We hence assume
\Omega\in\left(0,2\sqrt{2}\right)\setminus\{2\}
.

The critical (cut-off) lattice frequency, i.e. the minimal frequency at which the
j
-th mode
u^{j}_{m,n}
(
2.11
) becomes propagating, is obtained from (
2.14
) for
K_{j}=0
:

\Omega_{j}=\sqrt{2-2\cos{\theta_{j}}},\qquad j\in\{1,N-1\},

(2.15)

where
\theta_{j}
is defined in (
2.12
). For
\Omega<\Omega_{j}
, the
j
-th mode
u^{j}_{m,n}
is evanescent, decays rapidly and hence and does not propagate in the waveguide. In particular, waves with frequency below the first critical frequency
\Omega_{1}
cannot propagate in a duct with Dirichlet boundary conditions on its walls, resulting in a zero–frequency band gap.

Thus, we define the incident field
u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}
in (
2.7
) as a propagating waveguide mode of the form (
2.11
):

u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}=u^{p}_{m,n}=x^{m}_{p}\operatorname{s}_{p}(n+N_{1}),

(2.16)

for some
p\in\{1,N-1\}
such that
\Omega>\Omega_{p}
. The function
\operatorname{s}_{p}
and the associated
y_{p}
are defeined as in (
2.11
)-(
2.12
).

2.3
Boundary-value problem for the waveguide

From the boundary condition on the screen we may show (see Appendix
A
) that the problem, and therefore the unknown scattered field is symmetric in
m
:

u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{sc}}_{-m,n}.

(2.17)

Then from (
2.1
) and (
2.17
) it follows for
m=0,\ -N_{1}<n<n_{1}
and
m=0,\ n_{2}<n<N_{1}
:

\frac{1}{2}(u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n+1}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n-1})+u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n}+\left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2}-2\right)u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}=0.

(2.18)

Note also that the incident field (
2.16
) is defined such that:

u^{\text{in}}_{m,-N_{1}}=u^{\text{in}}_{m,N_{2}}\equiv 0.

(2.19)

Since the problem is symmetric with respect to
m=0
, we only need to consider the right half of the waveguide where
m\geqslant 0
. Then, from the Helmholtz equation (
2.1
), the boundary conditions on the walls (
2.6
) and (
2.19
), and the boundary conditions (
2.5
) and (
2.18
), we formulate the mixed boundary-value problem for the scattered field:

\displaystyle\Delta u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}+\Omega^{2}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=0,

\displaystyle m>0,\ -N_{1}<n<N_{2};

(2.20)

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{m,-N_{1}}=u^{\text{sc}}_{m,N_{2}}=0,

\displaystyle m\geqslant 0;

(2.21)

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}=-u^{\text{in}}_{0,n},

\displaystyle-n_{1}\leqslant n\leqslant n_{2};

(2.22)

\displaystyle 2u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n+1}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n-1}+(\Omega^{2}-4)u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}=0,

\displaystyle-N_{1}<n<-n_{1}\text{ or }n_{2}<n<N_{2}.

(2.23)

For scattering problems in infinite domains, for the problem
to be well posed, one must specify the
radiation condition
at infinity. We will do this through the
limiting
absorption principle
: if
\Omega
is assumed to have a small positive
imaginary part
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
, we set the condition by imposing that the
resulting scattered field
u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}
must be exponentially decaying at
infinity. The true physical solution is then understood as the limit of these
admissible solutions when
\varepsilon_{\Omega}\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

3
Wiener–Hopf formulation

3.1
Odd and even discrete Fourier transforms

In order to find the analytical solution, we will derive a Wiener–Hopf formulation for the discrete waveguide with a transversal screen in a similar manner to
[
13
]
, where the Wiener–Hopf equation is written for the continuous waveguide with periodic boundary conditions on the walls.

In the discrete waveguide problem, to pass to the spectral domain and derive a Wiener–Hopf equation, the geometry allows only a Fourier transform along the
m
axis (see Fig.
1
), which, unlike the usual formulations
[
44
,
45
,
29
]
, is perpendicular to the strip. For this reason, in the continuous analogue, the authors of
[
13
]
used sine or cosine transforms, motivated by the boundary conditions on the screen and the adjacent gap.

In the discrete case, we therefore introduce the discrete analogues of the sine and cosine transforms. We first define the discrete Fourier transform of
u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}
as

U(x,n)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}x^{-m}=U_{-}(x,n)+U_{+}(x,n),

(3.1)

where

\displaystyle U_{+}(x,n)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}x^{-m}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{-}(x,n)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{-1}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}x^{-m}

are the half-transforms.
As discussed above,
\Omega
has a small imaginary part
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
due to the limiting absorption principle, so it follows from the dispersion relation and symmetry that

|u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}|<e^{-\varepsilon|m|},

where
\varepsilon
is the minimal imaginary part of all
K_{j}
, arising from
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
in the dispersion relation.
Thus, the functions
U_{-}(x,n)
and
U_{+}(x,n)
are analytic for
|x|<e^{\varepsilon}
and
|x|>e^{-\varepsilon}
, respectively. We will further refer to these as the regions inside and outside the unit circle
|x|=1
. Hence, (
3.1
) is analytic in the annulus

\mathcal{A}:\{e^{-\varepsilon}<|x|<e^{\varepsilon}\},

which includes the unit circle.

The inverse transform to recover the physical field
u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}
is

u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}U(x,n)x^{m-1}dx,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z},

(3.2)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a closed counterclockwise contour, which lies within the annulus of analyticity
\mathcal{A}
of
U(x,n)
and encircles the origin.
Note that, due to the symmetry (
2.17
),

U_{-}(x,n)=U_{+}(x^{-1},n)-u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}.

(3.3)

Therefore, it is sufficient to find
U_{+}(x,n)
and consequently
u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}
to determine the full transform
U(x,n)
.

We will then define the discrete analogues of the sine and cosine transforms – the
odd

U_{(o)}(x,n)
and
even

U_{(e)}(x,n)
transforms:

\displaystyle U_{(e)}(x,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}(x^{m}+x^{-m})=\Phi(x,n)+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n},

(3.4)

\displaystyle U_{(o)}(x,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}(x^{m}-x^{-m})=\Psi(x,n)+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n},

(3.5)

where
the functions
\Phi(x,n)
and
\Psi(x,n)
are defined as:

\displaystyle\Phi(x,n)

\displaystyle=U_{-}(x,n)+U_{+}(x,n),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,n)

\displaystyle=U_{-}(x,n)-U_{+}(x,n).

(3.7)

Note also that by (
3.1
) and (
3.6
) it follows that
\Phi(x,n)\equiv U(x,n)
.

The transforms defined by (
3.4
)-(
3.5
) are analytic in the annulus of analyticity
\mathcal{A}.
Integrating (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) over
\mathcal{C}
, exchanging the sum and integral symbols, and using the fact that

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}x^{-m-1}dx=\delta_{m,0},

where
\delta
is the usual Kronecker delta, we obtain the inverse transforms of (
3.5
) and (
3.4
) as:

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}U_{(e)}(x,n)x^{-m-1}dx-u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}\delta_{m,0},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0};

(3.8)

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}U_{(o)}(x,n)x^{-m-1}dx+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}\delta_{m,0},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0},

(3.9)

where
\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}=\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:m\geqslant 0\}
,
\mathcal{C}
is a closed counter-clockwise contour, which lies within the annulus
\mathcal{A}
, and
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta, defined as

\delta_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1,\quad i=j,\\
0,\quad i\neq j.\end{cases}

(3.10)

Using the definitions (
3.4
)-(
3.5
), we may rewrite (
3.8
)-(
3.9
) as:

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\Phi(x,n)x^{m-1}dx,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0};

(3.11)

\displaystyle u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\Psi(x,n)x^{m-1}dx+2u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}\delta_{m,0},\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}.

(3.12)

Note that we are allowed to change the sign in front of
m
in
x^{-m-1}
in (
3.11
)-(
3.12
) due to the symmetry of the problem (
2.17
).

3.2
Wiener–Hopf problem formulation

Similarly to the continous analogue
[
13
]
, motivated by the boundary conditions (
2.22
)-(
2.23
) we apply
the even transform (
3.4
) to the Helmholtz equation (
2.20
) for
-N_{1}<n<-n_{1}
or
n_{2}<n<N_{2}

, we get:

\Lambda(x)\Phi(x,n)+\Phi(x,n+1)+\Phi(x,n-1)+2u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n}+[\Omega^{2}-4]u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n+1}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n-1}=0,

(3.13)

where
\Phi(x,n)
is defined in (
3.6
) and

\Lambda(x)=\Omega^{2}-4+x+x^{-1}.

(3.14)

Applying the odd transform (
3.5
) to the Helmholtz equation (
2.20
) for
-n_{1}\leqslant n\leqslant n_{2}
, we get:

\Lambda(x)\Psi(x,n)+\Psi(x,n+1)+\Psi(x,n-1)+[\Omega^{2}-4+2x]u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n+1}+u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n-1}=0,

(3.15)

where
\Psi(x,n)
is defined in (
3.7
).
Note that to obtain (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) we used the following relations:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m+1,n}x^{m}=x^{-1}U_{-}(x,n),

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m-1,n}x^{m}=xU_{-}(x,n)+xu^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}+u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n},

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m-1,n}x^{-m}=x^{-1}U_{+}(x,n)+u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n},

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}u^{\text{sc}}_{m+1,n}x^{-m}=xU_{+}(x,n)-xu^{\text{sc}}_{0,n}.

Then from (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) and the boundary conditions (
2.22
)-(
2.23
) it follows that

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)\Phi(x,n)+\Phi(x,n+1)+\Phi(x,n-1)=0,\quad

\displaystyle-N_{1}<n<-n_{1}\quad\text{or}\quad n_{2}<n<N_{2};

(3.16)

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)\Psi(x,n)+\Psi(x,n+1)+\Psi(x,n-1)=r(x,n),\quad

\displaystyle-n_{1}\leqslant n\leqslant n_{2},

(3.17)

where from (
2.22
) and (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) we define the function
r(x,n),
known for
-n_{1}<n<n_{2},
as:

r(x,n)=\left[\Lambda(x)+y_{p}+y_{p}^{-1}+\left(x-x^{-1}\right)\right]\operatorname{s}_{p}(n+N_{1}).

(3.18)

For
n=-n_{1}
and
n=n_{2}
,
r(x,n)
in (
3.17
) is known up to the two unknown constants
u^{\text{sc}}_{0,-n_{1}-1}
and
u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n_{2}+1}
:

\displaystyle r(x,-n_{1})

\displaystyle=\left[\Lambda(x)+\left(x-x^{-1}\right)\right]\operatorname{s}_{p}(n_{1}^{*})+\operatorname{s}_{p}(n_{1}^{*}+1)-u^{\text{sc}}_{0,-n_{1}-1},

(3.19)

\displaystyle r(x,n_{2})

\displaystyle=\left[\Lambda(x)+\left(x-x^{-1}\right)\right]\operatorname{s}_{p}(n_{2}^{*})+\operatorname{s}_{p}(n_{2}^{*}-1)-u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n_{2}+1},

(3.20)

where
n^{*}_{1}=-n_{1}+N_{1},\ n^{*}_{2}=n_{2}+N_{1}
.

Equations (
3.16
)-(
3.17
) are a homogeneous and an inhomogeneous difference equation, respectively. From the theory of difference equations
[
22
]
, we seek solutions to (
3.16
)-(
3.17
) using the ansatz

\displaystyle\Phi(x,n)=A_{1}(x)\ y^{n}+B_{1}(x)\ y^{-n},\quad

\displaystyle-N_{1}<n<-n_{1};

(3.21)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,n)=A_{2}(x)\ y^{n}+B_{2}(x)\ y^{-n},\quad

\displaystyle n_{2}<n<N_{2};

(3.22)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,n)=C(x)\ y^{n}+D(x)\ y^{-n}+R(x,n),\quad

\displaystyle-n_{1}\leqslant n\leqslant n_{2},

(3.23)

where
A_{1}(x),\ B_{1}(x),\ A_{2}(x),\ B_{2}(x),\ C(x),\ D(x)
are unknown functions, and the function
y(x)
in (
3.21
)-(
3.23
) obeys the dispersion relation (
2.10
) and
is defined as:

y(x)=-\frac{\Lambda(x)}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda(x)^{2}-4}}{2},\qquad(y(x))^{-1}=-\frac{\Lambda(x)}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda(x)^{2}-4}}{2},

(3.24)

where we choose the branch of the square root such that
\operatorname{Im}(\sqrt{\Lambda^{2}-4})>0
, and therefore
|y(x)|\leqslant 1
.

The function
R(x,n)
in (
3.23
) is a particular solution of the inhomogeneous equation (
3.17
), which is defined from (
3.18
) as:

R(x,n)=\frac{r(x,n)}{\Lambda(x)+y_{p}+y_{p}^{-1}}=\Upsilon(x)\operatorname{s}_{p}(n+N_{1}),

(3.25)

where

\Upsilon(x)=1+\Pi(x),\qquad\Pi(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{(x-x_{p})(x-x_{p}^{-1})},

(3.26)

and the function
\Pi(x)
is found from the dispersion relation for the incident field (
2.14
) and the definition of
\Lambda
(
3.14

4
On the matrix kernel factorisation for the symmetric case

The crucial step in the Wiener–Hopf method is the factorisation of the kernel (
3.75
), such that
\mathbf{K}_{2}
is the product of
[\mathbf{K}_{2}]_{-}
and
[\mathbf{K}_{2}]_{+},
analytic inside and outside the unit circle, respectively. However, whereas factorisation of a scalar kernel is relatively straightforward
[
36
]
, a rigorous factorisation of a matrix kernel is substantially more difficult
[
18
,
38
]
, with no known general procedure available. In this section, we will discuss several approaches that may potentially be applied for factorising matrix the kernel (
3.75
).

4.1
Rational matrix factorisation

The structure of the matrix (
3.75
) implies that certain known methods for factorising matrices with polynomial entries may be applied here. In particular, an explicit algorithm for the Wiener–Hopf factorisation has been developed by Adukov et al.
[
2
]
, based on the method introduced in
[
4
]
. This technique is designed for the exact factorisation of
2\times 2
matrix functions with Laurent polynomial entries
3
3

3

Laurent polynomials differ from ordinary polynomials in that they may contain terms of negative degree.
and rational coefficients, thereby avoiding the numerical instabilities possible in approximate approaches.
The authors implemented this approach in the
ExactMPF
package for
Maple

[
2
]
, which enables the exact decomposition of matrix polynomials through symbolic computation.
Conditions for the stability of the factorisation are discussed in
[
5
,
6
]
.

Within this work, the applicability of the
ExactMPF
package has been investigated for the matrix (
3.75
).
We explored two possible approaches to applying this algorithm.

Our first approach is to premultiply the matrix (
3.75
) by its common denominator to obtain a polynomial matrix, which can then be supplied directly to the
ExactMPF
package. This method is applicable only when the determinant is exactly factorisable, that is, expressible as a product of polynomials whose roots lie either strictly inside or strictly outside the unit circle. For the present matrix, this condition holds only when the imaginary part of
\Omega
is positive,
\varepsilon_{\Omega}>0
. Even then, the method breaks down at the determinant factorisation stage: the high-order polynomials that arise, even in the simplest admissible case
\ell_{0}=2
and
\ell=2
, exceed the limits of exact arithmetic, preventing the algorithm from reliably determining whether their roots lie inside or outside the unit circle.

For the second approach, we may notice that the determinant of the original matrix (
3.75
) is always equal to
1
, while its entries are rational functions. To apply the
ExactMPF
package, these entries must be approximated by Laurent polynomials on the unit circle for
\varepsilon_{\Omega}>0
. However, once such an approximation is introduced, the determinant is no longer equal to
1
.
Alternatively, we may transform the matrix (
3.75
) into the form described in
[
5
]
, which guarantees that the determinant of the resulting matrix remains constant and equal to
1
, even when the entries are approximated by Laurent polynomials. The difficulty with this approach is that the smaller the imaginary part of
\Omega
,
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
, the less smooth the matrix entries become on the unit circle. Consequently, for any physically meaningful
\varepsilon_{\Omega}
, obtaining a sufficiently accurate approximation requires Laurent polynomials of extremely high order, which are not compatible with the algorithm.

Finally, we may conclude that, although the algorithm, developed in
[
2
]
, is in principle applicable to the factorisation of the matrix (
3.75
), its practical implementation becomes complex and will not be pursued in this work.

\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{2}\mathbf{K}_{2}\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{T}_{\ell+\ell_{0}^{\prime}}(z)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}-\operatorname{T}_{\ell-\ell_{0}^{\prime}}(z)&-2\operatorname{T}_{\ell_{0}^{\prime}}(z)\operatorname{V}_{\ell-1}(z)\\
2(\gamma/2)^{2}\operatorname{T}_{\ell}(z)\operatorname{V}_{\ell_{0}^{\prime}-1}(z)&-\operatorname{T}_{\ell-\ell_{0}^{\prime}}(z),\\
\end{array}\right),

(4.4)

with

\gamma=\operatorname{s}(1)=\sqrt{\Lambda(x)^{2}-4},\qquad\ell_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{\ell_{0}-1}{2},

(4.5)

where
\ell_{0}^{\prime}
must be an integer parameter, equal to half the length of the screen for odd values of
\ell_{0}
.

It is straightforward to show that in the special case
\ell=\ell_{0}^{\prime}
the matrix function (
4.4
) takes the form

\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{2}\mathbf{K}_{2}\mathbf{S}=g_{2}(x)\mathbf{I}+g_{2}(x)\mathbf{J}(x),

(4.6)

where

g_{1}(x)=-\frac{1}{\operatorname{T}_{2\ell}\left(z(x)\right)},\quad g_{2}(x)=\frac{\operatorname{T}_{\ell}\left(z(x)\right)\operatorname{V}_{\ell-1}\left(z(x)\right)}{x\operatorname{T}_{2\ell}\left(z(x)\right)},\quad\mathbf{J}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&-2x\\
\tfrac{1}{2}x\gamma^{2}&0\\
\end{array}\right).

(4.7)

The matrix function
\mathbf{J}(x)
satisfies the condition (
4.2
) with

\Delta(x)=-x^{2}(\gamma(x))^{2}=-\left[x^{2}+(\Omega^{2}-6)x+1\right]\left[x^{2}+(\Omega^{2}-2)x+1\right],

(4.8)

which is a polynomial of degree
4
. Therefore, we have shown that, in the discrete case also, we have a matrix of the Khrapkov–Daniele class. However, when the degree of
\Delta(s)
exceeds
2
, the factors in (
4.6
) exhibit exponential growth at infinity
[
1
]
, which prevents the direct application of Liouville’s theorem. Several approaches to overcome this difficulty have been discussed in the literature
[
8
,
12
,
1
]
. These procedures are all quite complex and lie beyond the scope of this work.
Note that in the continuous case this problem does not arise, as the corresponding polynomial degree is less than 2
[
13
]
, and hence the factorisation is easier.

Importantly, even if we manage to factorise the matrix (
4.4
), it will not allow us to solve the Wiener–Hopf equation (
3.74
) straight away, as the matrix function
\mathbf{S}
has two reciprocal poles, as follows from its structure. Thus, an additional step, i.e., the application of the pole removal method
[
20
,
21
]
is required. Alternatively, we may follow the procedure of the direct continuous-discrete analogy, described in
[
19
]
(see remark below).

Remark 4.1
.

To derive the Wiener–Hopf equation with the kernel in the standard Khrapkov–Daniele form, we may transform the unknowns in (
3.74
) as follows:

[\mathbf{U}_{2}]_{\pm}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{\pm}\mp\mathbf{T},

(4.9)

where
\mathbf{S}
is defined by (
4.3
) and
\mathbf{T}
is a known column function. This substitution introduces new unknown functions

[

]
. Further details are omitted for brevity. Substitution of (

4.3
From kernel factorisation to additive splitting

Although, based on the above, we will not pursue the direct factorisation of the matrix (
3.75
), its rational structure implies that we can circumvent this step by applying the pole removal technique, a standard method frequently used in Wiener–Hopf problems (see
[
20
,
21
,
7
]
). The method yields an algorithmic procedure for additive splitting of a rational function, in which the pole contributions are subtracted via the residue theorem.

This method has also been employed in
[

,
7
]
; in our case, however, the application of the method is simpler and more straightforward, since the matrix (
3.75
) has only a finite number of poles. This enables an exact solution, in contrast to the case of the continuous waveguide. The procedure is discussed in the next section.

(5.56)

Remark 5.1
.

Analogously to the symmetric waveguide case, we may also obtain a solution for the system of equations (
3.38
)-(
3.39
), (
3.42
)-(
3.43
) in the general setting via the pole removal method. A sketch of this analysis is provided in Appendix
D
.

Note that the integrands in (
5.54
)-(
5.55
) are meromorphic functions, with each pole corresponding to a waveguide mode. By the residue theorem, the amplitude of each mode in (
5.55
) can be obtained from the residues at these poles:

u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{q}M_{q}\,u^{q}_{m,n},\qquad q=2j-1,\quad j=\{1,J\}

(5.57)

where
u^{q}_{m,n}
is defined in (
2.11
), and the amplitude
M_{q}
is given by

M_{q}=\operatorname{Res}\left[\frac{\Phi_{1}(x)}{x\operatorname{s}(\ell-1)},\ x=\nu_{j}^{-}\right],\quad j\in J.

(5.58)

In the indexing of (
5.57
), we account for the fact that the odd incident mode in the fully symmetric waveguide, as discussed earlier, can only be scattered into odd modes. The coefficients
M_{q}
can in principle be obtained analytically from (
5.46
) and (
5.53
), although the resulting expressions are lengthy and are therefore omitted here.

We have successfully implemented this residue-based method; however, in order to validate our results we require an additional independent numerical approach. For this reason, we now turn to the Boundary Algebraic Equations method.

6
Numerical solution via Boundary Algebraic Equations method

The exact solution to the problem formulated in (
2.20
)-(
2.23
), can be derived by the so-called Boundary Algebraic Equations (BAE) method
[
26
,
15
]
, which can be employed for discrete scattering problems involving a finite scatterer on a square lattice
[
37
]
using the lattice Green’s function. This method represents a fully discretised counterpart to the well-known boundary element method, which utilises the Green’s function for continuous space, as formulated in
[
58
]
. An exact solution for the scattering of a plane wave by a finite strip in free space was previously obtained by Sharma
[
46
]
, and implemented in our work
[
29
]
for comparison. Our objective is to derive a similar result for the waveguide. To do so, we must derive an expression for the tailored lattice Green’s function, which satisfies boundary conditions on the waveguide walls (
2.6
).

6.1
Tailored Green’s function for the waveguide

We introduce a tailored Green’s function
G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}
, where
(m_{0},n_{0})
is a location of a point source, such that
G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}
satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\Delta G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}+\Omega^{2}G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}=\delta_{m,m_{0}}\delta_{n,n_{0}},

(6.1)

\displaystyle G_{m,-N_{1}}^{m_{0},n_{0}}=G_{m,N_{2}}^{m_{0},n_{0}}=0,

(6.2)

where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta, defined by (
3.10
).

We introduce the discrete Fourier transform and its inverse as:

\widehat{G}_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}x^{-m},\qquad G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\widehat{G}_{n}(x)x^{m-1}dx,

(6.3)

where the integration contour
\mathcal{C}
lies within the region of analyticity of
\widehat{G}_{n}
, which can be taken as the unit circle for complex
\Omega
. Note that
\widehat{G}_{n}
also depends on
m_{0}
and
n_{0}
, but for brevity we do not display it explicitly.

Applying (
6.3
) to (
6.1
)-(
6.2
) leads to:

\displaystyle\Lambda\widehat{G}_{n}+\widehat{G}_{n-1}+\widehat{G}_{n+1}=x^{-m_{0}}\delta_{n,n_{0}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{-N_{1}}=\widehat{G}_{N_{2}}=0,

(6.5)

where
\Lambda(x)
is defined by (
3.14
). Then, we seek a solution of (
6.4
)-(
6.5
) in the forms:

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(1)}=A_{1}(x)\ y^{n}+B_{1}(x)\ y^{-n},\quad

\displaystyle-N_{1}<n<n_{0};

(6.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(2)}=A_{2}(x)\ y^{n}+B_{2}(x)\ y^{-n},\quad

\displaystyle n_{0}<n<N_{2};

(6.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}=A_{0}(x)\ y^{n_{0}}+B_{0}(x)\ y^{-n_{0}}+\frac{1}{\Lambda+2},\quad

\displaystyle n=n_{0};

(6.8)

where
y(x)
is defined by (
3.24
).

Writing (
6.4
) for
n=-N_{1}+1
and
n=N_{2}-1
and considering the boundary conditions (
6.5
):

\displaystyle\Lambda\widehat{G}_{-N_{1}+1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle+\widehat{G}_{-N_{1}+2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=0,

(6.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda\widehat{G}_{N_{2}-1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle+\widehat{G}_{N_{2}-2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=0.

(6.10)

Then, substituting (
6.6
)-(
6.7
) to (
6.9
)-(
6.10
) we can eliminate
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
in (
6.6
)-(
6.7
):

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(1)}=A_{1}^{*}\ \operatorname{s}(n+N_{1}),\qquad

\displaystyle n<n_{0};

(6.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(2)}=A_{2}^{*}\ \operatorname{s}(n-N_{2}),\qquad

\displaystyle n>n_{0};

(6.12)

where
A_{1}^{*}=A_{1}y^{-N_{1}},

A_{2}^{*}=A_{2}y^{N_{2}}
and
\operatorname{s}(n)
is defined by (
2.9
).

From the equation (
6.4
), written for
n=n_{0},\ n=n_{0}+1,\ n=n_{0}-1
we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{lclclll}\Lambda\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}&+&\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}+1)}^{(2)}&+&\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}-1)}^{(1)}&=&x^{-m_{0}},\\
\Lambda\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}-1)}^{(1)}&+&\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}&+&\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}-2)}^{(1)}&=&0,\\
\Lambda\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}+1)}^{(2)}&+&\widehat{G}_{(n_{0}+2)}^{(2)}&+&\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}&=&0.\end{array}\end{cases}

(6.13)

Substituting (
6.11
)-(
6.12
) to (
6.13
), we can solve the system for
A_{1}^{*}
,
A_{2}^{*}
,
\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}
and therefore find :

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(1)}=\frac{\operatorname{s}(n_{0}-N_{2})}{\operatorname{s}(N)\ \operatorname{s}(1)}\ \operatorname{s}(n+N_{1})\ x^{-m_{0}},\qquad

\displaystyle n<n_{0};

(6.14)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n}^{(2)}=\frac{\operatorname{s}(n_{0}+N_{1})}{\operatorname{s}(N)\ \operatorname{s}(1)}\ \operatorname{s}(n-N_{2})\ x^{-m_{0}},\qquad

\displaystyle n>n_{0};

(6.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{n_{0}}=\frac{\operatorname{s}(n_{0}-N_{2})\ \operatorname{s}(n_{0}+N_{1})}{\operatorname{s}(N)\ \operatorname{s}(1)}\ x^{-m_{0}}\qquad

\displaystyle n=n_{0};

(6.16)

Note that both (
6.14
) and (
6.15
) give (
6.16
) when written for
n=n_{0}.
Hence the tailored Green’s function for the waveguide can be recovered by the inverse transform as defined in (
6.3
) as:

G_{m,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\widehat{G}_{n}^{(1)}x^{m-1}dx,\qquad-N_{1}<n\leqslant n_{0};\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\widehat{G}_{n}^{(2)}x^{m-1}dx,\qquad n_{0}\leqslant n<N_{2}.\end{cases}

(6.17)

6.2
Boundary Algebraic Equations system

The solution in the form of the system of boundary algebraic equations for the scattering by a finite Dirichlet strip in a free space has been obtained in
[
46
]
. In the case of a waveguide, the solution is analogous, apart from the fact that instead of the discrete free-space Green’s function, we use the tailored lattice Green’s function (
6.17
).

The solution for the scattered field at some point
(m_{0},n_{0})
is written as:

u^{\text{sc}}_{m_{0},n_{0}}=\sum_{n=-n_{1}}^{n_{2}}G_{0,n}^{m_{0},n_{0}}[2u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n}+(u_{-1,n}^{\text{in}}+u_{1,n}^{\text{in}})+\delta_{n,n_{2}}(u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n+1}+u_{0,n+1}^{\text{in}})+\delta_{n,-n_{1}}(u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n-1}+u_{0,n-1}^{\text{in}})].

(6.18)

We refer to Appendix
E
for the detailed derivation of (
6.18

The coefficients
u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n}
in (
6.18

6.18
) for
m_{0}=0,\ n_{0}\in\{-n_{1},n_{2}\}
as:

\boldsymbol{u}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{N}}-\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{N}}\right)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{N}}\boldsymbol{u}^{\text{in}}+(u^{\text{sc}}_{0,n_{2}+1}+u_{0,n_{2}+1}^{\text{in}})\mathbf{p}+(u^{\text{sc}}_{0,-n_{1}-1}+u_{0,-n_{1}-1}^{\text{in}})\mathbf{q}\right],

(6.19)

where
\mathcal{N}=n_{1}+n_{2}+1
,
\boldsymbol{u}=[u^{\text{sc}}_{1,-n_{1}},...,u^{\text{sc}}_{1,n_{2}}]^{\top}
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{\text{in}}=\frac{1}{2}[(u_{-1,-n_{1}}^{\text{in}}+u_{1,-n_{1}}^{\text{in}}),...,(u_{-1,n_{2}}^{\text{in}}+u_{1,n_{2}}^{\text{in}})]^{\top}

\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{N}}
is the

\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}

\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{N}}=2\left(\begin{array}[]{llll}G^{1,-n_{1}}_{0,-n_{1}}&G^{1,-n_{1}}_{0,-n_{1}+1}&\ldots&G^{1,-n_{1}}_{0,n_{2}}\\

G^{1,-n_{1}+1}_{0,-n_{1}}\\

(6.20)

The two unknown constants

u_{0,n_{2}+1}^{\text{in}}\end{array}\right),

7
Reflection and transmission coefficients

To find the reflection and transmission coefficients, we represent the total field on the left and right sides of the strip as:

u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}+u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}=\begin{cases}u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}+u^{\text{ref}}_{m,n},&m<0;\\
u^{\text{tr}}_{m,n},&m>0;\end{cases}

(7.1)

where
u^{\text{ref}}_{m,n}
is the reflected field and
u^{\text{tr}}_{m,n}
is the transmitted field. From (
5.57
), (
7.1
) and the symmetry property of the scattered field (
2.17
) if follows that

\displaystyle u^{\text{ref}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{q}M_{q}\,u^{q}_{-m,n},

\displaystyle m<0,

(7.2)

\displaystyle u^{\text{tr}}_{m,n}=u^{\text{sc}}_{m,n}+u^{\text{in}}_{m,n}=\sum\limits_{q}M_{q}\,u^{q}_{m,n}+u^{p}_{m,n},

\displaystyle m>0,

(7.3)

where
q
and
u^{q}_{m,n}
are defined in (
2.11
).

From (
7.2
)–(
7.3
) it follows that the amplitudes of the scattered modes
M_{q}
, defined by (
5.58
), correspond to the reflection coefficients:

R_{q}=M_{q}.

(7.4)

The transmission coefficient for the incident mode is

T_{p}=1+M_{p},

(7.5)

where
p
denotes the index of the incident mode, while the transmission coefficients for the other supported modes, with
q\neq p
, are

T_{q}=M_{q},\quad q\neq p.

(7.6)

Numerically,
T_{q}
and
R_{q}
can also be obtained from the wavefield, computed using (
6.18
) or (
5.54
)-(
5.56
) by exploiting the orthogonality of the waveguide modes.

To illustrate what we have in mind by orthogonality, we define the discrete inner product by

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{n=-N_{1}}^{N_{2}}f(n)\,\overline{g(n)},

(7.7)

with the overbar denoting complex conjugation.
For all mode numbers
1\leqslant j,k\leqslant N-1
, (
2.11
) gives

\langle\operatorname{s}_{j},\operatorname{s}_{k}\rangle=2(N+1)\,\delta_{jk},\qquad\|\operatorname{s}_{j}\|^{2}=\langle\operatorname{s}_{j},\operatorname{s}_{j}\rangle=2(N+1).

Then, for some
m_{r}\gg 1
, which is a distance between some waveguide crossection and the strip, the transmission and reflection coefficients for mode number
q\in\{1,\dots,J\}
are given by

T_{q}=\frac{\langle u^{\text{tot}}_{m_{r},n},\,\operatorname{s}_{q}\rangle}{\|\operatorname{s}_{q}\|^{2}}\,x_{q}^{\,m_{r}},\qquad R_{q}=\frac{\langle u^{\text{sc}}_{-m_{r},n},\,\operatorname{s}_{q}\rangle}{\|\operatorname{s}_{q}\|^{2}}\,x_{q}^{\,m_{r}},

(7.8)

where the incident mode
p
is chosen to be of unit amplitude, meaning that

\frac{\langle u^{\text{in}}_{m,0},\,\operatorname{s}_{p}\rangle}{\|\operatorname{s}_{p}\|^{2}}=1.

Fig.
3
shows how the reflection and transmission coefficients of the incident mode number
p=1
,
T_{p}
and
R_{p}
depend on the lattice frequency
\Omega
. In particular, the reflection coefficient tends to
-1
as
\Omega
approaches the cut-off frequency of the incident mode
\Omega_{p}
, defined in (
2.15
). Physically, this corresponds to full reflection and zero transmission. This behaviour is consistent with the theoretical result obtained for the continuous waveguide with a transversal Dirichlet screen, as discussed in
[
40
]
.

Note that throughout this work we have studied the incident mode with index
p=1
as the most natural physical choice. However, the theoretical results are derived for the general case, where
p
can take any value in
\{1,N\}
.

\text{Re}[T_{p}]

The total
energy flux
through a transverse cross-section of the waveguide must remain constant to satisfy energy balance. The energy flux from the cross-section at
x=m
to
x=m+1
is given in
[
9
]
as the real part of the negative inner product (
7.7
) of the force applied to the cross-section
x=m
by the cross-section
x=m+1
, and the velocity of the cross-section
x=m
:

\mathcal{E}_{f}=-\frac{1}{2}\text{Re}\left[\left\langle(u^{\text{tot}}_{m+1,n}-u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}),\ \dot{u}^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}\right\rangle\right],

(7.9)

where
\dot{u}^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}
in the orthogonal product denotes time derivative of the displacement field, which is equivalent to
i\Omega u^{\text{tot}}_{m,n}
in the time-harmonic case, considered in this work.

Then, writing (
7.9
) for some
m<0
and
m>0
(on the left- and right-hand sides of the screen, respectively), using (
7.1
)–(
7.3
) together with the inner product definition (
7.9
) and the orthogonality properties of the waveguide modes, and equating the resulting expressions, after some straightforward algebraic manipulations we obtain the energy flux balance equation:

|v_{p}|-\sum_{q}|v_{q}|\,|R_{q}|^{2}=\sum_{q}|v_{q}|\,|T_{q}|^{2}.

(7.10)

Here,
v_{q}
denotes the
group velocity

[
9
,
48
]
of the mode with index
q
, which quantifies the speed at which the envelope of a wave packet, and hence the energy, propagates through the lattice waveguide. It is defined as

v_{q}=\frac{d\Omega}{dK_{q}}=\frac{2\sin K_{q}}{\Omega}.

(7.11)

Note that the
q
-th waveguide mode in (
2.11
)–(
2.12
) is defined such that for positive
K_{q}
the mode propagates in the positive direction of the
m
-axis. Therefore, using (
7.11
), the group velocity of the mode with index
q
propagating to the right is taken as
|v_{q}|
, while for the same mode propagating to the left it is
-|v_{q}|
.

Normalising (
7.10
) by
|v_{p}|
, we obtain the energy balance equation

\sum_{q}\left(\frac{|v_{q}|}{|v_{p}|}\,|T_{q}|^{2}+\frac{|v_{q}|}{|v_{p}|}\,|R_{q}|^{2}\right)=1\quad\text{or}\quad\sum_{q}\left(|\widetilde{T}_{q}|^{2}+|\widetilde{R}_{q}|^{2}\right)=1,

(7.12)

where we defined the weighted reflection and transmission coefficients by rescaling (
7.5
) as

|\widetilde{T}_{q}|=\sqrt{\frac{|v_{q}|}{|v_{p}|}}|T_{q}|,\qquad|\widetilde{R}_{q}|=\sqrt{\frac{|v_{q}|}{|v_{p}|}}|R_{q}|.

(7.13)

The dependence of the magnitudes of (
7.13
) on the lattice frequency for the first four propagating odd modes is shown in Fig.
4
. Note that since the group velocity vanishes near the band edges (
K_{q}\to 0
or
K_{q}\to\pi
) for all
q\neq p

it follows that
\widetilde{R}_{q}\to 0
and
\widetilde{T}_{q}\to 0
as
\Omega\to\Omega_{q}
.

The equation (
7.12
) provides an additional means to verify our results. It is satisfied by the exact analytical solution in (
5.58
) up to an accuracy of
10^{-13}
, which is a remarkable result.
As seen in Fig.
4
, the same equation for the coefficients, obtained via orthogonal products from the wavefield computed using the boundary algebraic equations method (
6.18
), is satisfied with a larger error near the cut-off frequencies than when applying the residue theorem (up to
10^{-3}
).
This arises because we chose
m_{r}=20
, which is too close to the scatterer in the considered geometry, so that some energy still leaks into the evanescent modes at the chosen cross-section. Such difficulties are not uncommon, as numerical methods often struggle near cut-off frequencies in the continuous case
[
24
]
. The error can be reduced by increasing
m_{r}
, although here it was intentionally kept small to highlight the differences.

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we have studied wave scattering by a finite transversal strip in a discrete square-lattice waveguide, viewed as a discrete analogue of the classical continuous waveguide problem with a screen. We first formulated the boundary-value problem and then derived its associated matrix Wiener-Hopf equation.

In the general setting, this leads to a
4\times 4
matrix kernel, while in the symmetric case it reduces to a
2\times 2
kernel, similarly to the continuous formulation. For the Wiener–Hopf method, we only analysed the symmetric case in detail. We discussed possible approaches to the factorisation of the kernel; although direct factorisation proved impractical, as the Khrapkov–Daniele form in the special case was shown to be more complicated to factorise than in the continuous case. However, the rational structure of the matrix allowed us to apply the pole removal technique. This provided an exact analytical solution, in contrast to the continuous case, where approximations are required due to the infinite number of poles in the matrix. Note that the same technique is applicable to the system
4\times 4
matrix kernel (see Appendix
D
).

The exact solution for the wavefield, obtained by the pole removal technique, was verified numerically by applying the Boundary Algebraic Equations method with a lattice Green’s function tailored to the waveguide. The two methods provide consistent solutions with good agreement.

From the analytical solution of the Wiener–Hopf system we derived expressions for the reflection and transmission coefficients. These were also computed numerically, via orthogonality relations applied to the wavefield obtained from the system of boundary algebraic equations, and shown to satisfy the energy balance condition. The analytical results agree with high accuracy, while the numerical results converge when the sampling is taken sufficiently far from the scatterer. Comparison with the continuous waveguide case confirms consistency of behaviour, especially near cut-off frequencies. In particular, the full reflection and zero transmission was recovered for the frequency approaching the cut-off value for the incident mode.

Overall, this work establishes a framework for treating discrete waveguide scattering problems with transversal defects, using numerical and analytical approaches and clarifying the relationship between the discrete and continuous formulations of the Wiener–Hopf method. Future work may include constructing a matrix factorisation of the matrix Wiener–Hopf kernel, as discussed in Section
4
, where we observed that the matrix of Khrapkov–Daniele type arising in the symmetric case is more complex than its continuous analogue, yet potentially factorisable by methods such as those in
[
8
]
. Further directions include a detailed analysis of the applicability of the method from
[
2
]
for the factorisation of matrix polynomials. While the direct use of the algorithmic software developed in
[
2
]
appears challenging, it may be possible to apply explicitly the algorithms for the direct factorisation of meromorphic matrix functions described in
[
3
]
.
It would also be worthwhile to compare the accuracy and performance of the pole-removal method for solving the diffraction problem, as discussed in Section
5
, with the more general approach of factorising the associated rational matrix. Additional developments might also consider generalising the techniques discussed here to other lattice geometries, such as those in
[
47
]
, motivated by physical applications, as well as exploring more complicated boundary conditions and obstacle geometries.
