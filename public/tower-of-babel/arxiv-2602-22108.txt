Tight Bounds for Online Scheduling in the One-Fast-Many-Slow Machines Setting
John Jeang, Vladimir Podolskii

Abstract
In the One-Fast-Many-Slow decision problem, introduced by Sheffield and Westover [ 17 ] , a scheduler, with access to one fast machine and infinitely many slow machines, receives a series of tasks and must decide how to allocate the work among its machines. The goal here is to achieve the minimal overhead of an online algorithm over the optimal offline algorithm that knows all tasks and their arrival times in advance. Three versions of this setting were considered: Instantly-committing schedulers that must assign a task to a machine immediately and irrevocably, Eventually-committing schedulers whose assignments are irrevocable but can occur anytime after a task arrives, and Never-committing schedulers that can interrupt and restart a task on a different machine. Sheffield and Westover constructed online algorithms in all three settings. In the Instantly-committing model the optimal competitive ratio is equal to 2. In the Eventually-committing model, they give an algorithm achieving a 1.678 competitive ratio, while a lower bound of 1.618 was established by Kuszmaul and Westover in [ 12 ] . In the Never-committing model, Sheffield and Westover show that the optimal competitive ratio lies in the interval [1.366, 1.5]. In the latter two settings, the exact values of the optimal competitive ratios were left as an open problem, moreover Kuszmaul and Westover [ 12 ] conjectured that the lower bound in the Eventually-committing model is tight. In this paper we resolve this problem by providing tight bounds for the competitive ratios in the Eventually-committing and Never-committing settings. For the Eventually-committing model we prove Kuszmaul and Westover’s conjecture by providing an algorithm that achieves a competitive ratio equal to the previous lower bound of 1 + 5 2 ≈ 1.618 \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.618 . Notably, our algorithm occasionally leaves the fast machine free even in the presence of unassigned tasks, as was shown to be necessary for any improvement by Sheffield and Westover. For the Never-committing model we provide an explicit Task Arrival Process (TAP) lower bounding the competitive ratio to the previous upper bound of 1.5. Unlike the TAP used to prove the previous lower bound, which uses only two tasks, we construct a series of TAPs with a growing number of tasks that approach the upper bound of 1.5.



1
Introduction

Online algorithms deal with streaming data in which decisions are made without complete knowledge of the entire problem instance. An online algorithm’s performance is measured relative to that of the optimal
offline
algorithm that knows the entire problem instance prior to all decisions. Optimizing this metric, known as the
competitive ratio
, for online algorithms has a wide range of applications to scheduling, networking, and learning theory
[
21
]
.

Scheduling algorithms aim to efficiently distribute incoming tasks to optimize for some goal (e.g. makespan, tardiness, fairness). Efficient scheduling algorithms have been well-studied in both the offline and online regime for their potential application to a variety of settings such as cloud computing, service, and industrial environments
[
10
,
16
,
18
]
. Sometimes in online scheduling, tasks can take advantage of specialized hardware to expedite processing. However, when resources are limited, it is unclear how to prioritize tasks. One of the approaches to study this problem was suggested by Sheffield and Westover
[
17
]
when they introduced the One-Fast-Many-Slow Decision Problem (OFMS). In OFMS, an algorithm, with access to a single instance of accelerated hardware and infinitely many weak processors, receives tasks, whose sizes and arrival times are unknown in advance, in an online stream and must allocate resources to minimize the completion of the set of tasks as a whole.

The OFMS problem is motivated by its relevance to the Serial to Parallel Decision Problem (SPDP)
[
12
]
, which addresses multiprocessor scheduling with parallelizable tasks. The SPDP question concerns itself with the scheduling of perfectly parallelizable tasks on
p
identical processors. Indeed, Sheffield and Westover reduce SPDP to OFMS in the limit where the number of processors greatly outnumbers the number of tasks. The reduction works by isolating a small number of processors to be used only for the serial implementation of tasks and treating the rest of the processors as a single high performance collection for tasks implemented in parallel; for more information we refer the reader to the paper
[
17
]
.

In both settings the tasks are unknown ahead of time and arrive in an online stream and the authors analyze bounds on the competitive ratios of online algorithms. In the case of OFMS the following bounds are known:

•

Instantly-committing schedulers:
Tasks must be assigned to a specific machine immediately upon arrival and cannot be moved. The matching lower
[
12
]
and upper
[
17
]
bounds of 2 on the competitive ratio are known.

•

Eventually-committing schedulers:
Tasks can be assigned any time after arrival, but cannot be moved. The best known lower bound is
\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.618

[
12
]
and the best known upper bound is
\approx 1.678

[
17
]
.

•

Never-committing schedulers:
Tasks can be moved and restarted on a different machine. The best known lower and upper bounds are
\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\approx 1.366
and
1.5
respectively
[
17
]
.

Thus, although in the Instantly-committing model the optimal competitive ratio is known, its value in the other two settings were left in
[
17
]
as an open problem. Moreover, it is conjectured in
[
12
]
that the lower bound in the Eventually-committing model is tight.

Results.

In this paper we resolve the open question pertaining to the optimal competitive ratio of an online algorithm in the OFMS problem in the Eventually-committing and Never-committing settings. Specifically we prove the conjecture made in
[
12
]
by providing an optimal Eventually-committing scheduler achieving a competitive ratio of
\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}=\varphi
meeting the previous lower bound from
[
12
]
. In the Never-committing model, we give an explicit task arrival process that lower bounds the competitive ratio of any online algorithm to 1.5, matching the previous upper bound from
[
17
]
.

Techniques.

Our main technical contribution is providing an optimal
\varphi
-competitive online algorithm, in the Eventually-committing setting. In
[
17
]
, the authors provide the optimal
non-procrastinating
algorithm for the OFMS problem. That is, their algorithm is optimal among algorithms that do not delay task assignments when the fast machine is free. Their analysis consist of bounding the amount of time spent by their algorithm’s fast machine on tasks assigned
differently
from the optimal algorithm. At a high level, their algorithm does excellent for many Task Arrival Processes (TAP for short), but struggles in the case where it misallocates a small task that should have been held onto in wait of more information from the TAP.

Our algorithm modifies the previous algorithm by occasionally holding onto tasks even when the fast machine is free. The main technical work is showing that this modification does not incur too much delay as to hinder the strengths of the previous algorithm, but provides significant enough delay such that tasks are assigned more accurately. The key insight is making this delay independent of a tasks arrival time and solely dependent on the size of the task. This allows us to bound the time at which tasks will receive additional delay thereby allowing it to perform similarly to the previous algorithm in
[
17
]
, late in the TAP, but significantly differently at the beginning of the TAP. From here we can use similar techniques to bound the amount of time spent, by our algorithm’s fast machine, on misallocated tasks.

In the Never-committing setting, we come up with an explicit sequence of TAPs lower bounding the competitive ratio of online algorithms in this model to the previous upper bound of 1.5. These TAPs, consist of a growing number of tasks, differing significantly from the two-task TAP giving the previous
\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}
lower bound.

deals with robust single-machine scheduling where jobs are known in advance but the release date of a job is given as an interval, rather than a point, in which the job arrives.

The online setting has typically been explored in the context of unknown jobs and arrival times, dealing with either a single processor
[
6
]
or multiple identical processors
[
1
]
. In
[
9
]
, they consider the problem of scheduling jobs that may require multiple machines to process. The case of stochastic scheduling concerns itself with tasks coming from a known

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The Problem

In this section we formally state the, previously introduced,
One-Fast-Many-Slow Decision Problem
(OFMS), where an online algorithm with access to infinitely many slow machines and a single fast machines must assign tasks to machines with the goal of minimizing the total completion time (makespan) of the set of tasks as a whole.

We first define a task
\tau_{i}=(f_{i},s_{i},t_{i})
, representing the runtime on the fast machine, runtime on a slow machine, and arrival time respectively. We enforce that
t_{i}\geq 0
and
s_{i}\geq f_{i}>0
. An instance of OFMS is a Task Arrival Process (TAP)
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}\}
, with
t_{j}\geq t_{i}
for
j>i
. An online scheduling algorithm
\mathcal{M}
incrementally receives portions of
\mathcal{T}
upon the arrival of tasks. That is,
\mathcal{M}
learns
\tau_{i}
at
t_{i}
, whereas and offline scheduling algorithm
\mathsf{N}
receives the entirety of
\mathcal{T}
at the start.

A scheduling algorithm’s goal is to assign tasks to be run on machines to minimize the completion time of
\mathcal{T}
. The completion time is defined to be the minimum time
t
at which all tasks in
\mathcal{T}
have finished running.

In the OFMS problem, each machine can only run a single task at a time. A machine is said to be free in the
closed
intervals in which it is not running any tasks (and thus there is a notion of “the last time a machine is free”). We are interested in two previously defined models for this problem:

1.

Eventually-committing schedulers
: A scheduler may hold on to a task without immediately assigning/starting it, but once the task begins running, the assignment cannot be changed.

2.

Never-committing schedulers
: A scheduler may hold on to a task without immediately assigning/starting it, and even after the task begins running, the task can be moved to start over on a different machine.

In
[
17
]
, Westover and Sheffield also define
Instantly-committing
schedulers in which a scheduler must commit and queue a task to a particular machine upon arrival. In this case the competitive ratio of the optimal online algorithm is known to be 2.

We note that there are several algorithms, with possibly different assignments, that solve the offline version of OFMS. We describe one such algorithm,
\mathsf{OPT}
, that we’ll use for the remainder of the paper.

Given an OFMS problem instance
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathsf{OPT}
first calculates the completion time of the assignment,
q_{0}
, that allocates all tasks in
\mathcal{T}
to the fast machine. Then for every task
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
,
\mathsf{OPT}
calculates the completion time of an assignment
q_{i}
defined as follows: for all
\tau_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
,

\text{ $\tau_{j}$'s assignment}=\begin{cases}\text{a slow machine if $s_{j}+t_{j}\leq s_{i}+t_{i}$}\\
\text{the fast machine otherwise,}\end{cases}

(1)

Let
K(q_{i})
denote the completion time of assignment
q_{i}
.
\mathsf{OPT}
then simply uses the assignments associated with the minimum completion time
c=\min_{i}(K(q_{i}))
. Intuitively,
\mathsf{OPT}
assigns a task to the slow machine whenever it can do so without increasing the completion time of
\mathcal{T}
. To see that this algorithm produces an optimal assignment, consider an optimal assignment
\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathcal{G}
assigns all tasks in
\mathcal{T}
to the fast machine then this is exactly the
q_{0}
assignment, otherwise consider
\mathcal{G}
’s last-ending slow machine, working on
\tau_{k}
, that ends at
s_{k}+t_{k}
. First note that all of the slow machines in the
q_{k}
assignment will finish before
s_{k}+t_{k}
. Second, notice that
\mathcal{G}
’s fast machine will contain every task that is on
q_{k}
’s fast machine because it contains every task
\tau_{i}
such that
s_{i}+t_{i}>s_{k}+t_{k}
. Thus
q_{k}
’s fast machine finishes at least as quickly as
\mathcal{G}
’s fast machine and so
q_{k}
must be an optimal assignment.

Importantly, in this algorithm we have that for all tasks
\tau_{j}
such that
s_{j}+t_{j}\leq c
,
\mathsf{OPT}
assigns
\tau_{j}
to a slow machine, and we make frequent use of this property in our proofs.

2.2
Notation

At every given point in time
t
, there is an optimal offline allocation of just the subset of tasks in
\mathcal{T}
that have arrival time less than or equal to
t
. We use
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
to denote the optimal offline assignment of this subset of tasks and we use
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
for the optimal offline assignment of the whole
\mathcal{T}
. We refer to the completion time of
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
as
\mathsf{C}^{t}
, and to the total completion of
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
as
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
. We sometimes drop the argument and use
\mathsf{OPT}
to denote the final assignment when it is clear from context. Furthermore, we use
\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t}
to refer to the runtime of
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
’s fast machine. Finally, when talking about the optimal offline algorithm without reference to a specific time we just use
\mathsf{OPT}
. For an online algorithm
\mathcal{M}
, we use
\mathcal{M}_{f}
to denote the runtime of
\mathcal{M}
’s fast machine after the entire TAP has arrived.

R(P)
will denote the earliest time at which an online algorithm
\mathcal{M}
, knows that the optimal offline algorithm must take at least
P
time (e.g. by calculating
\mathsf{C}^{t})
.

For ease of communication, we define a notion of
largeness
, where we say that a task
\tau_{x}
is larger than
\tau_{y}
if and only if
s_{x}+t_{x}>s_{y}+t_{y}
. Note that this notion does not care about the time it takes the tasks to run on the fast machine.

3
Eventually-committing scheduler

3.1
A
\varphi
-Competitive Eventually-committing Scheduler

We define
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, the golden ratio. Throughout the paper we use the fact that
\varphi
is the positive solution to the equation

\varphi=1+\frac{1}{\varphi}
.

In this section we provide a
\varphi
-competitive algorithm for the eventually-committing setting of OFMS. In
[
17
]
, Sheffield and Westover define
non-procrastinating
algorithms as those that only delay the machine assignment of tasks if the fast machine is occupied. They show that a competitive ratio of
\approx 1.678
is optimal for non-procrastinating algorithms, and thus to achieve a competitive ratio of
\varphi\approx 1.618
our algorithm needs to occasionally delay assigning a task to a machine even if the fast machine is free. To do so we first define the notion of an
eligible
task. A task
\tau_{i}
is eligible at time
t
if

t\geq\frac{f_{i}}{\varphi}.

(2)

Our algorithm will only assign eligible tasks to the fast machine and therefore may sometimes procrastinate.

Finally we note that an online algorithm
\mathcal{M}
can (efficiently) calculate
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
and
\mathsf{C}^{t}
at time
t
, by running the algorithm for
\mathsf{OPT}
on the tasks that have arrived at or before time
t
. This gives
\mathcal{M}
a lower bound for
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
at time
t
. We say that
\mathcal{M}

safely
assigns a task to a slow machine if the finishing time of that machine is guaranteed to be within the
\varphi
-competitive ratio with the completion time of the optimal offline algorithm.

We now give the formal description of our Eventually-committing scheduler
\mathcal{H}
.

Algorithm
\mathcal{H}
:

At
t_{0}=0
,
\mathcal{H}
initializes an empty standby set
\mathcal{S}
. Whenever a task
\tau_{i}
arrives, it is immediately added to
\mathcal{S}
. Additionally
\mathcal{H}
continuously evaluates all tasks in
\mathcal{S}
at all times
t
as follows:

1.

If
s_{i}+t\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{t}
, then start
\tau_{i}
on an unused slow machine and remove it from
\mathcal{S}
.

2.

If
all
of the following conditions hold:

(a)

the fast machine is free or just finished a task,

(b)

\tau_{i}
has the largest
s_{i}+t_{i}
value among all tasks in
\mathcal{S}
,

(c)

t\geq\frac{f_{i}}{\varphi}
(
\tau_{i}
is eligible),

then start
\tau_{i}
on the fast machine and remove it from
\mathcal{S}
.

We refer to
\mathcal{S}
as the
standby
set, and denote
\mathcal{S}_{t}
to be the set of task on standby at time
t
.

At a high level,
\mathcal{H}
first does a “slow-check” to see if a task can safely be assigned to a slow machine. If this check fails,
\mathcal{H}
then does a “fast-check” to see if the task can be assigned to the fast machine. We will frequently use these check conditions to characterize the performance of
\mathcal{H}
.

Although
\mathcal{H}
makes these checks continuously, we observe the checks need only to be made during specific events. Notice that the slow-checks only need to occur upon the arrival of a task, because
s_{i}+t
only increases in
t
while
\varphi\mathsf{C}^{t}
does not change in between the arrival of tasks. The fast-checks only need to occur upon the arrival of a task, when a task on the fast machine finishes, or when the largest task in
\mathcal{S}
becomes eligible. Therefore, the algorithm can actually be made discrete without changing its behavior because there are only finitely many events warranting checks. This observation allows us to pick
\delta
small enough such that for any event happening at time
t
, there are no events between
t-\delta
and
t
. From this we can use the notation

t^{-}=t-\delta

(3)

for what can be thought of as “the time just before
t
”.

The eligibility condition, (c) in the fast-check, is what causes
\mathcal{H}
to sometimes procrastinate. However notice that eligibility is concerned with the absolute time
t
but not the arrival time
t_{i}
of a particular task
\tau_{i}
. Intuitively, this is how
\mathcal{H}
manages to procrastinate mostly at the beginning of the TAP, but not too much near the end of the TAP.

Further notice that the eligibility condition is not present in the slow-check. This is because there are infinitely many slow machines, so we are not worried about keeping any of them free and thus
\mathcal{H}
simply runs tasks on slow machines whenever it can safely do so.

Finally, we emphasize that the conditions in the fast check must be met simultaneously. To run a task
\tau_{i}
on the fast machine at time
t
, it is not sufficient for
\tau_{i}
to merely be the largest among all eligible tasks in
\mathcal{S}_{t}
; it must be the largest among all tasks in
\mathcal{S}_{t}
. If there is a largeness tie, and at least one task participating in the tie is eligible, then
\mathcal{H}
assigns one of the eligible tasks to the fast machine, breaking ties arbitrarily among the eligible tasks participating in the tie.

At this point it is also useful to introduce the following sets of tasks that will help us with our analysis. For a given TAP
\mathcal{T}
we define the following:

•

\mathcal{A}
(“Actual”): The set of tasks that both
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
assign to the fast machine;

•

\mathcal{F}
(“Fake”): The set of tasks that
\mathcal{H}
assigns to the fast machine but
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
assigns to a slow machine;

•

\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\subset\mathcal{F}
: The set of tasks in
\mathcal{F}
such that
s_{i}+t_{i}>\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
;

•

\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}\subset\mathcal{F}
: The set of tasks in
\mathcal{F}
such that
s_{i}+t_{i}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
.

To avoid confusion, it is also important to note some subtleties. Firstly, we note that
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{F}
does not capture any of the tasks that
\mathcal{H}
assigns to its slow machine; most of the time we simply do not care about these tasks since they finish within a
\varphi
competitive ratio of
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
’s completion time, by the design of the algorithm. However, there is no explicit mechanism that prohibits the possibility of a task
\tau
from being assigned to
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
’s fast machine, while being assigned to the slow machine by
\mathcal{H}
. While these tasks are not relevant for most of our analysis, it is important to remember that they exist since they can show up in standby set
\mathcal{S}
and affect
\mathsf{OPT}
’s fast machine.

Finally, we note that since
\mathcal{H}
only safely assigns tasks to slow machines, then to prove that
\mathcal{H}
is
\varphi
-competitive, it suffices to consider just the performance of its fast machine.

3.2
Initial Observations and Properties

Before analyzing the performance of our algorithm, it is useful to familiarize ourselves with some of the basic properties of
\mathcal{H}
,
\mathsf{OPT}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
and
\mathcal{A}
that will greatly simplify our analysis. We begin by proving some properties of
\mathsf{OPT}
.

Note that for
t_{1}<t_{2}
it is possible for
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{1})
to assign a task
\tau_{i}
to the fast machine while
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{2})
assigns
\tau_{i}
to a slow machine (note that
\mathsf{OPT}
is an offline algorithm, and so this swapped assignment is retroactive and thus effective at
t_{i}
and not
t_{2}
). This is because
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{2})
may benefit from freeing up space on the fast machine for other tasks arriving at
t_{1}<t\leq t_{2}
. In this case we say that
\mathsf{OPT}
has
swapped

\tau_{i}
’s assignment. This next lemma tells us that tasks only swap from the fast machine to a slow machine.

Lemma 1
.

If
\tau_{i}
is assigned to a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{1})
, then it will be assigned to a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{2})
for all
t_{2}>t_{1}
.

Proof.

Consider a task
\tau_{i}
assigned to a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{1})
. Let
t_{2}
be any time greater than
t_{1}
. We have

s_{i}+t_{i}\leq\mathsf{C}^{t_{1}}\leq\mathsf{C}^{t_{2}},

(4)

where the first inequality comes from the fact that
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{1})
’s completion time must be at least the completion time of
\tau_{i}
. The second inequality comes from the fact that all task parameters are non-negative and thus
\mathsf{C}^{t}
is monotone in
t
.

Since
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
assigns a task
\tau_{i}
to a slow machine whenever
s_{i}+t_{i}\leq\mathsf{C}^{t}
, we conclude that
\tau_{i}
must be assigned to a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{2})
.
∎

Lemma

1
helps us further characterize some of
\mathsf{OPT}
’s behavior.

Lemma 2
.

Each task
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{F}
is assigned to the fast machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{i})
.

Proof.

First consider a task
\tau_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
. We know that
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
assigns
\tau_{a}
to the fast machine, and from
Lemma

1
we know that
\mathsf{OPT}
does not swap task assignments from slow to fast. Therefore
\tau_{a}
must be assigned to the fast machine the entire time since its arrival at
t_{a}
.

Now consider a task
\tau_{z}\in\mathcal{F}
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{z})
assigns
\tau_{z}
to the slow machine. Then during
\mathcal{H}
’s slow-check we have

\frac{s_{z}+t_{z}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{z}}}\leq\frac{s_{z}+t_{z}}{\underbrace{s_{z}+t_{z}}_{\text{$\mathsf{OPT}$'s completion of $\tau_{z}$}}}=1<\varphi.

(5)

This means that
\mathcal{H}_{f}
would also assign
\tau_{z}
to a slow machine at
t_{z}
. However this is a contradiction since
\mathcal{H}
eventually assigns all tasks in
\mathcal{F}
to the fast machine, and
\mathcal{H}
is an Eventually-committing scheduler that cannot change the assignment of tasks. Therefore
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{z})
assigns
\tau_{z}
to the fast machine.
∎

Finally, in our next lemma, we build a classification of the tasks assigned to
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine.

Lemma 3
.

For any
\tau_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\tau_{b}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
, and
\tau_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
we have the following:

1.

s_{m}+t_{m}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}<s_{b}+t_{b}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}<s_{a}+t_{a}
,

2.

f_{b}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
,

3.

f_{m}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi^{2}}
.

Proof.

The first two inequalities from item 1 come from the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
. The third inequality from item 1 comes from the fact that
\tau_{b}
is ultimately put on one of
\mathsf{OPT}
’s slow machines and the completion times of all of
\mathsf{OPT}
’s slow machines are bounded by
\mathsf{OPT}
’s total completion time. Finally the last inequality from item 1 comes from the fact that
\tau_{a}
ultimately goes on
\mathsf{OPT}(\infty)
’s fast machine, and therefore must be unable to go on
\mathsf{OPT}
’s slow machine without increasing the completion time since
\mathsf{OPT}
puts tasks on a slow machine whenever possible.

To see items 2 and 3, it suffices to show that for any
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
we have that
f_{i}<\frac{s_{i}}{\varphi}
, and then we can just apply item 1. Consider
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
. To show
f_{i}<\frac{s_{i}}{\varphi}
, we recall that by
Lemma

2
we know
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{i})
assigns
\tau_{i}
to the fast machine, but
\mathcal{H}
doesn’t assign
\tau_{i}
to a slow machine at this time so we have

\varphi<\frac{s_{i}+t_{i}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{i}}}\leq\frac{s_{i}+t_{i}}{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t_{i}}}\leq\frac{\overbrace{s_{i}+t_{i}}^{\tau_{i}\text{ on $\mathcal{H}$'s slow machine}}}{\underbrace{f_{i}+t_{i}}_{\text{minimum time on $\mathsf{OPT}$'s fast machine}}}\implies f_{i}\leq\frac{s_{i}}{\varphi}.

(6)

∎

With these initial observations in hand we can proceed to the main analysis.

3.3
Analysis

Our goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

\mathcal{H}
is a
\varphi
-competitive Eventually-committing scheduler.

Recall that we only have to concern ourselves with the performance of
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine. Our first strategy for analyzing
\mathcal{H}_{f}
will be to characterize the tasks from
\mathcal{F}
. These can be thought of as misassigned “extra work” that contribute to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
since the optimal offline algorithm assigns them to slow machines once the entire TAP has arrived. Our second strategy will be to bound the amount of time that
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine spends procrastinating.

We will need several key lemmas to combine these two ideas and bound
\mathcal{H}_{f}
. We start with proving that misassigned tasks that
\mathcal{H}
runs on the fast machine, cannot arrive too late.

Lemma 4
.

All tasks
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
arrive (strictly) before
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
.

Proof.

All tasks
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
are run on the fast machine by
\mathcal{H}
, but ultimately on a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}
once the entire TAP has arrived. This implies

\varphi\mathsf{C}^{t_{i}}<s_{i}+t_{i}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)}.

(7)

The first inequality comes from the fact that
\mathcal{H}
doesn’t immediately assign
\tau_{i}
to a slow machine. The second inequality comes from the fact that the runtime of
\tau_{i}
cannot exceed the total runtime of the entire TAP since
\mathsf{OPT}
ultimately runs
\tau_{i}
on a slow machine. The last inequality comes from the fact that
\mathsf{C}^{R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)}\geq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
by definition of
R
. Thus
t_{i}<R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi))
.
∎

For further analysis we need to define a few key sets of tasks. Let
\tau_{l}
be the last task to arrive strictly before
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
:

•

\alpha=\mathcal{S}_{t_{l}}\cap\mathcal{A}
,

•

\beta=\mathcal{S}_{t_{l}}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
,

Furthermore, for
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
we define

\beta^{t}=\mathcal{S}_{t}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}.

(8)

Importantly, we note that for
t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
,

\beta^{t_{2}}\subseteq\beta^{t_{1}}\subseteq\beta,

(9)

because as time progresses for
t>R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
, no additional tasks from
\mathcal{F}
arrive (by
Lemma

4
) but some may be removed from standby.

For any set
X
we define
\Sigma_{X}=\sum_{i\in X}f_{i}
, the total load
X
can add to the fast machine if all tasks in
X
are run successively without pause. Using this notation, we see that
\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{2}}}\leq\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{1}}}\leq\Sigma_{\beta}
.

In our next lemma, we show that
\Sigma_{\alpha}
and
\Sigma_{\beta}
cannot be too large.

Lemma 5
.

\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta}<\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
.

Proof.

Observe that any task
\tau_{i}\in\alpha\cup\beta
must be assigned to the fast machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{l})
or else
\mathsf{C}^{t_{l}}
would exceed
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
, by Lemma 3, which is not possible since
t_{l}<R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
. Thus we have

\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta}<\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t_{l}}\leq\mathsf{C}^{t_{l}}<\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}.

(10)

∎

From this we define

0<\varepsilon=\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}-(\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta}).

(11)

Our next lemma bounds how long tasks from
\mathcal{A}
can stay ineligible.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\tau_{a}
be a task in
\mathcal{A}
. If either
\tau_{a}\in\alpha
or both
t_{a}\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
and
\beta^{t_{a}}\neq\emptyset
, then
\tau_{a}
is eligible, for
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine, for all
t\geq\max(t_{a},\Sigma_{\alpha},\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}})
.

Proof.

Consider a task
\tau_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
arriving at
t_{a}\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
with
\beta^{t_{a}}\neq\emptyset
. If the task is eligible upon arrival then we’re done. Otherwise we have that
t_{a}<\frac{f_{a}}{\varphi}
.

From
Lemma

2
, we know that
\tau_{a}
is assigned to the fast machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{a})
. Furthermore we know that for any
\tau_{i}\in\beta^{t_{a}}
,
\mathcal{H}
does not start
\tau_{i}
on a slow machine at
t_{a}
because all tasks in
\beta^{t_{a}}
are ultimately run on
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine. Thus similarly to (
6
) we have

\varphi<\frac{\overbrace{s_{i}+t_{a}}^{\tau_{i}\text{ on $\mathcal{H}$'s slow machine at $t_{a}$}}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{a}}}\leq\frac{s_{i}+t_{a}}{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t_{a}}}\leq\frac{s_{i}+t_{a}}{f_{a}+t_{a}}\implies\frac{s_{i}}{\varphi}>f_{a}.

(12)

This tells us that
t_{a}<\frac{f_{a}}{\varphi}<\frac{s_{i}}{\varphi^{2}}\leq\frac{s_{i}+t_{i}}{\varphi^{2}}
. From this we can see that if any
\tau_{i}\in\beta^{t_{a}}
were assigned to a slow machine by
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{a})
then
\mathcal{H}
would assign
\tau_{i}
to a slow machine because then we would have the following slow-check for
\tau_{i}
:

\frac{s_{i}+t_{a}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{a}}}\leq\frac{(s_{i}+t_{i})+\frac{s_{i}+t_{i}}{\varphi^{2}}}{\underbrace{s_{i}+t_{i}}_{\tau_{i}\text{ on $\mathsf{OPT}(t_{a})$'s slow machine}}}=1+\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}}\leq\varphi.

(13)

This gives us

\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}}+f_{a}\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t_{a}}

(14)

because all of
\alpha\cup\beta^{t_{a}}\cup\{\tau_{a}\}
are on
\mathsf{OPT}(t_{a})
’s fast machine. Furthermore, recall that for any
\tau_{i}\in\beta^{t_{a}}
, we have
s_{i}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
from
Lemma

3
because
\beta^{t_{a}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
. Taken together we can get a stronger version of (
12
). That is, we now have that for any
\tau_{i}\in\beta^{t_{a}}

\varphi<\frac{s_{i}+t_{a}}{\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{t_{a}}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\frac{f_{a}}{\varphi}}{\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}}+f_{a}}=\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\frac{f_{a}}{\varphi}}{(\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}-\varepsilon)+(\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}}-\Sigma_{\beta})+f_{a}}.

(15)

Rearranging the inequality from the first and last expression gives us
\varphi(\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}})>(\varphi-\frac{1}{\varphi})f_{a}=f_{a}
and thus
\tau_{a}
will be eligible for
t\geq\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{a}}}
.

Now consider a task
\tau_{a}\in\alpha
. It is clear that it will be eligible for all
t\geq\frac{\Sigma_{\alpha}}{\varphi}
since
f_{a}\leq\Sigma_{\alpha}
. Therefore
\tau_{a}
will certainly be eligible for
t\geq\Sigma_{\alpha}
.

Combining these two results gives us the lemma.
∎

Next we have a useful lemma that simplifies our analysis. For this we remind the reader of the notion of
t^{-}
, defined in (
3
), as the timestep “just before
t
”.

Lemma 7
.

Assume there exist some time before
\mathcal{H}_{f}
when
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free, and let
t
be the last of such times. If all of the following conditions hold

1.

t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
,

2.

\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}\cap S_{t^{-}}=\emptyset
,

3.

\mathcal{A}\cap S_{t^{-}}\neq\emptyset
,

4.

All tasks in
\mathcal{A}\cap S_{t^{-}}
are eligible (at
t^{-}
),

then
\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the four conditions of the lemma hold.

Observe that conditions 1 and 2 imply that the remaining contribution to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
from
\mathcal{F}
-tasks after time
t
is bounded by
\Sigma_{\beta}
because we can assume by
Lemma

4
that no additional tasks from
\mathcal{F}
arrive after
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
.

Conditions 3 and 4 imply that there is at least one large task on standby preventing the tasks from
\mathcal{A}
from running on
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine at
t^{-}
. This is because
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free at
t^{-}
if
t
is the last time it is free. Thus there exists a non-empty subset of ineligible tasks
V\subset\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}
such that
V\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
and that for every task
\tau_{v}\in V
and
\tau_{a}\in\mathcal{A}\cap S_{t^{-}}
we have

t_{v}+s_{v}>t_{a}+s_{a}>\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(16)

where the last inequality comes from
Lemma

3
. This implies that all tasks in
V
are assigned to the fast machine by
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
, otherwise
\mathsf{OPT}
’s final completion time would be larger than
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
. Let
\tau_{w}\in V
be such that

f_{w}=\max_{\tau_{i}\in V}(f_{i}).

(17)

In other words,
\tau_{w}
is the task with the longest fast runtime in
V
. Since
\tau_{w}\notin\mathcal{A}
, it does not contribute to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
. We can now characterize the contribution to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
from
\mathcal{A}
as follows:

\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\geq\widetilde{\mathsf{C}}^{\infty}\geq f_{w}+\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}f_{i}\implies\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-f_{w}\geq\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}f_{i}.

(18)

Since
\tau_{w}
is ineligible, then
t<\frac{f_{w}}{\varphi}
. Since
t
is the last time
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free, we have

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+\overbrace{\Sigma_{\beta}}^{\text{work from $\mathcal{F}$}}+\underbrace{\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{A}}f_{i}}_{\text{work from $\mathcal{A}$}}<\frac{f_{w}}{\varphi}+\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-f_{w})\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)\mathsf{C}^{\infty}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(19)

where in the second inequality we use the fact that
\Sigma_{\beta}<\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
from
Lemma

5
.
∎

Next we prove a lemma that narrows down our analysis.

Lemma 8
.

If
\mathcal{H}
starts a task
\tau\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
at any time
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
then
\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

First observe that if any task
\tau_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
is waiting on standby at
t=\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
, then
\mathcal{H}
will be aware that
\mathsf{OPT}
takes at least
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
time and will run
\tau_{m}
on a slow machine because we have

\underbrace{s_{m}+t}_{\tau_{m}\text{ on $\mathcal{H}$'s slow machine}}=s_{m}+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}<s_{m}+t_{m}+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(20)

which is impossible since tasks from
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
are all run on
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine. Since no tasks can arrive after
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
, this implies that no task from
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
will start after
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Now let
t^{*}\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
be the last time that
\mathcal{H}
starts a task
\tau_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
prioritizes tasks with larger
s_{i}+t_{i}
, then by
Lemma

3
there must be no tasks
\tau\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{S}_{t^{*}}
. By
Lemma

4
, no tasks from
\mathcal{F}
will arrive from this point forward, and thus all remaining work contributing to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
is from
\mathcal{A}
. Further we note that from this point forward at most
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t^{*}
amount of work can arrive from
\mathcal{A}
because
\mathsf{OPT}
must complete these tasks on the fast machine; notice that because
t^{*}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
this amount of work is valid.

Since for
\tau_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
we have
f_{a}<\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
, we know that all tasks from
\mathcal{A}
will be eligible by
\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
. Thus if
\mathcal{H}
runs continuously from
\max(t^{*}+f_{m},\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi})
then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{f}

\displaystyle\leq\max(t^{*}+f_{m},\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi})+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t^{*})

(21)

\displaystyle=\max(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+f_{m},(1+\frac{1}{\varphi})\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t^{*})

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\max((1+\frac{1}{\varphi^{2}})\mathsf{C}^{\infty},(1+\frac{1}{\varphi})\mathsf{C}^{\infty})

(23)

\displaystyle=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(24)

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
f_{m}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi^{2}}
from
Lemma

3
.

Otherwise
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free at some point after
\max(t^{*}+f_{m},\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi})
. Let
t>\max(t^{*}+f_{m},\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi})
be the last time
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free.

Suppose we have
\mathcal{A}\cap S_{t^{-}}=\emptyset
. Since
\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{S}_{t}=\emptyset
, this implies that from
t
onward
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine only works on tasks from
\mathcal{A}
, with arrival time greater than or equal to
t
. Since
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine does this work continuously it must finish processing the set
\mathcal{A}
at least as quickly as
\mathsf{OPT}
’s fast machine. This gives us
\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Otherwise we have that
\mathcal{A}\cap S_{t^{-}}\neq\emptyset
. We note that since all tasks in
\mathcal{A}
are eligible at
\max(t^{*}+f_{m},\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi})<t
, then they are all also eligible at
t^{-}
. Thus we have all the necessary conditions to invoke
Lemma

7
.
∎

Lemma

8
basically tells us that we can assume that all tasks in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
start before
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
. Another way to think about it is that no tasks from
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
are in
\mathcal{S}_{t}
for any
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
.

The next lemma strengthens
Lemma

7
and tells us that
\mathcal{H}
performs well, unconditionally, if its fast machine is ever free at a late time.

Lemma 9
.

If
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free at any time
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)\leq t<\mathcal{H}_{f}
then
\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

If
t\geq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
then all tasks have arrived and all tasks are eligible. To see the latter observe that for any task
\tau_{i}
, we have

f_{i}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}<\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\leq\varphi t,

(25)

where the first inequality comes from the fact that
\mathsf{OPT}
finishes processing
\tau_{i}
on some machine prior to
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
. In this case if
\mathcal{H}
is free, then there must not be any tasks on standby and thus
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine has already completed and thus
t\geq H_{f}
and the lemma is vacuously true. So we only focus on the case where
t<\mathsf{C}^{\infty}
.

Consider the last time
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
such that
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is free.

Due to
Lemma

8
, we can assume that
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{t}=\emptyset
. Thus we have that

\Sigma_{(\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}})\cap S_{t}}=\Sigma_{\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cap S_{t}}=\Sigma_{\beta^{t}}.

(26)

In other words, the total contribution of
\mathcal{F}
tasks on standby, to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
, is bounded by
\Sigma_{\beta^{t}}
.

Case 1 (
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
):

In this case, from time
t
, the total contribution from
\mathcal{A}
-tasks to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
is at most
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t
and the total contribution from
\mathcal{F}
-tasks is
\Sigma_{\beta^{t}}\leq\Sigma_{\beta}
. Therefore we have

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t)+\Sigma_{\beta^{t}}\leq t+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t)+\Sigma_{\beta}\leq(1+\frac{1}{\varphi})\mathsf{C}^{\infty}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(27)

where the last inequality comes from
Lemma

5
.

Case 2 (
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
and all tasks from
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}
are eligible):

This case gives us all the conditions necessary to invoke
Lemma

7
.

Case 3 (
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}\neq\emptyset
but some tasks in
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}
are ineligible):

Let us define
\tau_{a}
to be the last arriving task in
\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}
such that
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)\leq t_{a}<t
. If
\tau_{a}
doesn’t exist then the contribution from
\mathcal{S}_{t}
to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
is bounded by
\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta}<\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
. Since the maximum load for all future tasks from
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t
we get

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-t)+(\Sigma_{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\beta})<\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}.

Thus for the remainder of this case we’ll assume that
\tau_{a}
exists. Now suppose that
\beta^{t_{a}}=\emptyset
. This means that from
t
onward,
\mathcal{H}_{f}
does not get any contribution from
\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, since some task in
\mathcal{S}_{t}\cap\mathcal{A}
is not eligible we know that
t<\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
. Thus we have

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+\underbrace{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}_{\text{All work comes from $\mathcal{A}$}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}.

(28)

Thus for the remainder of this case we’ll assume that
\beta^{t_{a}}\neq\emptyset
.
Now consider some ineligible task
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{t^{-}}\cap\mathcal{A}
. We have that
t_{i}\leq t_{a}
and that
\beta^{t_{a}}\subseteq\beta^{t_{i}}\neq\emptyset
. We can now invoke
Lemma

6
to bound
t
. Concretely we have
t\leq\max(t_{i},\Sigma_{\alpha},\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{i}}})
. By assumption the maximum cannot resolve to
t_{i}
since
t_{i}<t
.

If the maximum resolves to
\Sigma_{\alpha}
we have

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+\underbrace{(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\Sigma_{\beta^{t}})}_{\text{maximum work from $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{F}$}}\leq\Sigma_{\alpha}+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\Sigma_{\beta})<\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(29)

where the last inequality comes from
Lemma

5
.

Otherwise the maximum resolves to
\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{i}}}
and we have

\mathcal{H}_{f}\leq t+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\Sigma_{\beta^{t}})\leq(\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta}-\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{i}}})+(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}+\Sigma_{\beta^{t}})\leq(\varepsilon+\Sigma_{\beta})+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}\leq\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}+\mathsf{C}^{\infty}=\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty},

(30)

where in the penultimate inequality we used the fact that
\Sigma_{\beta^{t_{i}}}\geq\Sigma_{\beta^{t}}
because
t\geq t_{i}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main theorem, restated here, which requires some case analysis.

Theorem

(Restatement of
Theorem

1
)
.

\mathcal{H}
is a
\varphi
-competitive in the Eventually-committing task model.

Proof.

As previously mentioned, it suffices to just consider the performance of
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine. Furthermore by
Lemma

9
we can assume for the remainder of the proof that
\mathcal{H}
’s fast machine is not free for any
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
(and is thus operating continuously for
t\geq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
). Importantly this implies that some task is running at
t=R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
, we call this task the “stuck task”
\tau_{u}
. We clarify that
\tau_{u}
can be a task that starts at
t=R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
, but not a task that finishes at
t=R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
. There are two cases to consider.

Case 1 (
s_{u}+t_{u}>\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
):

Define
\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}=\{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{A}:t_{i}<R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{late}}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}
. In this case, by largeness,
\tau_{u}
must be a member of one of
\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\text{late}}
, or
\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}
. Our strategy will be to characterize the contribution of these sets to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
.

Let
t<R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
be a time when all tasks in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}
have already arrived; this must exist due to
Lemma

4
. Since
\mathsf{C}^{t}<\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
all of these tasks must be on
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
’s fast machine at this time or else
\mathsf{OPT}(t)
’s runtime would be greater than
\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}
. Therefore we have
\Sigma_{\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
.

Notice that
\mathcal{A}_{\text{late}}
can contribute at most
\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
since
\mathsf{OPT}
must complete the work on its fast machine. Furthermore, by
Lemma

8
, we know that no tasks from
\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
can contribute to
\mathcal{H}_{f}
after
R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{f}

\displaystyle\leq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)+\Sigma_{\mathcal{F}_{\text{big}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\text{early}}}+\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{\text{late}}}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)+\frac{\mathsf{C}^{\infty}}{\varphi}+\left(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}-R(\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi)\right)

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)\mathsf{C}^{\infty}

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi\mathsf{C}^{\infty}.

(34)

Case 2 (
s_{u}+t_{u}\leq\mathsf{C}^{\infty}/\varphi
):

In this case we have
\tau_{u}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{small}}
by
Lemma

3
. Let
t^{*}
be the time in which
\mathcal{H}
starts
\tau_{u}
on the fast machine. Since

4
Never-committing scheduler

Now we move onto the next considered model in which task assignment can be altered.

In this section we show that for any Never-committing scheduler
\mathcal{M}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a TAP
\mathcal{T}
for which
\mathcal{M}
cannot achieve a competitive of
1.5-\varepsilon
on
\mathcal{T}
. We note that this matches the upper bound of
[
17
]
.

As a reminder, in this setting a scheduler
\mathcal{M}
has the ability to restart a task on a different machine, so our proof will have to consider this possibility.

Theorem 2
.

No online algorithm
\mathcal{M}
can achieve a competitive ratio of
1.5-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, in the Never-committing task model.

Proof.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{M}
achieving a competitive ratio of
1.5-\varepsilon
for all TAPs
\mathcal{T}
. We give a strategy for an adversary by generating a TAP for
\mathcal{M}
.

First, let
k
be any even integer such that
k\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
.

Consider a TAP
\mathcal{T}
consisting of

•

\tau_{0}=(1,1.5,0)
,

•

\tau_{i}=(\frac{1}{k},\infty,\frac{1.5i}{k}),\forall i\in[1,\frac{k}{2}-1]
,

•

\tau_{L}=(0.75,\infty,0.75)
,

where the use of
s_{i},s_{L}=\infty
just forces
\mathcal{M}
to put
\tau_{i}
on the fast machine;
\infty
can be substituted by a large enough constant.

To clarify, this is a TAP that an adversary has in mind, but the adversary can choose to truncate the TAP (not send any remaining tasks from
\mathcal{T}
) whenever they want to.

From this TAP description, we can easily compute the optimal offline completion time at each arrival time. Clearly
\mathsf{C}^{0}=1
by assigning
\tau_{0}
to the fast machine. For any
i\in[1,\frac{k}{2}-1]
we have that

\mathsf{C}^{t_{i}}=\max(\underbrace{1+\frac{i}{k}}_{\text{work on fast machine}},\underbrace{\frac{1.5i}{k}+\frac{1}{k}}_{\text{start $\tau_{i}$ at $t_{i}$}})=1+\frac{i}{k}\text{ for $i<\frac{k}{2}$},

(46)

which is achieved by assigning all task that have arrived up to
t_{i}
to the fast machine. This is apparent because if any of the tasks are assigned to a slow machine, then the completion time would be at least 1.5 which is greater than
1+\frac{i}{k}
for
i<\frac{k}{2}
.

Finally, upon the arrival of
\tau_{L}
, we have

\mathsf{C}^{t_{l}}=\mathsf{C}^{0.75}=1.5,

(47)

which is achieved by assigning
\tau_{0}
to a slow machine and all other tasks to the fast machine. Now we use these completion times to complete the argument. Our basic strategy will be to show that
\mathcal{M}
can never put
\tau_{0}
on a slow machine.

Notice that upon the arrival of
\tau_{0}
,
\mathcal{M}
cannot put this task on the slow machine, or else the adversary will simply not send any additional tasks from
\mathcal{T}
and the competitive ratio will be 1.5.

Upon the arrival of any task
\tau_{i}
,
\mathcal{M}
will have to assign
\tau_{i}
to the fast machine because its slow runtime is infinite. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{M}
moves
\tau_{0}
to a slow machine at this time the competitive ratio would be given by at least

\frac{s_{0}+t_{i}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{i}}}=\frac{1.5+1.5\frac{i}{k}}{1+\frac{i}{k}}=1.5.

(48)

Thus if
\mathcal{M}
moves
\tau_{0}
to a slow machine, then the adversary can simply stop sending tasks and
\mathcal{M}
will have failed to meet a competitive ratio of
1.5-\varepsilon
.

Finally, consider the arrival of
\tau_{L}
. If
\mathcal{M}
moves
\tau_{0}
to the slow machine at this time, the competitive ratio will be at least

\frac{t_{l}+s_{0}}{\mathsf{C}^{t_{l}}}=\frac{0.75+1.5}{\mathsf{C}^{0.75}}=\frac{2.25}{1.5}=1.5,

(49)

which does not achieve the desired competitive ratio of
1.5-\varepsilon
.

This means that
\tau_{0}
must be on
\mathcal{M}
’s fast machine. Moreover, all other tasks must also be on
\mathcal{M}
’s fast machine because their slow runtimes are infinite. Thus all the tasks must be on the fast machine for
\mathcal{M}
, yielding a total completion time of

1+\left(\frac{k}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)+0.75=2.25-\left(\frac{1}{k}\right),

(50)

and thereby giving a competitive ratio of

\frac{2.25-\frac{1}{k}}{1.5}\geq 1.5-\frac{\varepsilon}{1.5}>1.5-\varepsilon.

(51)

This is a contradiction since
\mathcal{M}
is a
(1.5-\varepsilon)
-competitive online algorithm. Thus there does not exist any
(1.5-\varepsilon)
-competitive algorithms.
∎
