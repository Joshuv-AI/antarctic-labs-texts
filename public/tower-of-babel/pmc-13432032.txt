Implementing Trauma‐Informed Care in a General Setting: A Qualitative Study of Community Health Nurse Perceptions
Authors: Renee Fiolet, Victoria N Roberts, Alison M Hutchinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim(1) To explore community health nurses' experiences of implementing trauma‐informed care in a general health context to individuals impacted by sexual assault or domestic violence; and (2) to identify essential components of implementing trauma‐informed care in general health settings.Design and MethodA naturalistic inquiry approach was used, incorporating a focus group (n = 4) and a semi‐structured interview (n = 1) with community health nurses who implement trauma‐informed care in general health services in Victoria, Australia. Data were inductively analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsNurse descriptions of implementing trauma‐informed care are described across four main themes demonstrating competence in translating theory into trauma‐informed care. These nurses recognised what trauma can look like in patients. They were able to identify the essential components of trauma‐informed care, determine when trauma‐informed care is working, and describe the benefits to clinicians. Themes demonstrate that implementation of trauma‐informed care enables recognition of trauma, can help reduce the risk of re‐traumatisation (through anticipating and planning interventions to support the unique needs of trauma survivors), can strengthen patient‐centred care for trauma survivors, and enhance the experiences of clinicians and survivors alike.ConclusionsImplementation of trauma‐informed care into general health settings has the potential to enhance person‐centred care with professionals trained to anticipate, recognise, and respond to trauma. Recommendations from this study include future research on building capacity for all healthcare providers to understand the importance of taking a trauma‐informed approach to care, and large‐scale evaluation of the impact of trauma‐informed care approaches in mainstream health services on trauma responsiveness, patient satisfaction, and staff morale.Implications for PracticeThis study provides an understanding of the benefits associated with implementing trauma‐informed care in generalist settings with important implications for training, practice and research.Patient or Public ContributionThere was no involvement of patients in this study; however, one of the authors is a lived‐experience researcher in this topic area.Reporting GuidelinesEQUATOR guidelines were followed, using the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist.Keywords: health care sector, nurse perceptions, psychological trauma, sexual assault, trauma‐informed care

Introduction

Trauma experiences are prevalent, with 14% of Australians experiencing physical or sexual abuse before the age of 15 (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2021–2022 [ABS]). A further 3.8 million adults (20% of the Australian population) have experienced physical or sexual abuse by an intimate partner or family member since the age of 15 (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2021–2022), and estimates of exposure to all traumatic life events are much higher, with estimations that 75% of all Australians will experience a traumatic event within their lifetime (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2024). Traumatic life events are known to create significant emotional dysregulation, physical and mental harm, which often result in chronic physical or behavioural health disorders, and are linked to many leading causes of death (Felitti et al. 2019; McEwen 2017). Given the prevalence and impact of traumatic experiences within Australia, recent research has led to recommendations that healthcare providers and the broader health system play a more significant role in responding to trauma, despite the challenges and emotional burden associated with such work (Bulford et al. 2024; Hegarty et al. 2022). Consequently, healthcare providers should be adequately prepared to deliver trauma‐informed care so that such care can be delivered across healthcare systems regardless of the setting.

Healthcare experiences have the potential to cause re‐traumatisation for patients who have a history of traumatic life events such as exposure to actual or threatened death, sexual assault, family or domestic violence (Reeves 2015). Multiple barriers to accessing and engaging in health care exist for victim‐survivors of sexual assault and domestic or family violence (Fiolet et al. 2022; Sultana et al. 2023; Tarzia et al. 2020). Some barriers described include fear of stigma and disbelief, trust issues, power imbalance, lack of consent and control, provider inadequacy to recognise and manage trauma symptoms, environmental challenges, and systemic limitations impacting sensitive care (Pilkington et al. 2025; Tarzia et al. 2020). Similarly, Due et al. (2020) highlight the barriers to primary health access associated with the psychological impacts of trauma and the need to provide adequate health responses. Appropriate supports and interventions are required to minimise harm and support recovery from exposure to trauma; doing so is essential to finding safety and re‐establishing power and control in an individual's life (Varghese and Emerson 2022). To mitigate further distress and address barriers to engaging in healthcare, applying trauma‐informed care in practice is necessary.

Trauma‐informed care (TIC) is an evidence‐based approach that enables services to understand the impact of trauma, recognise the signs and symptoms, and respond in a way that promotes recovery and avoids re‐traumatisation (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2014 [SAMHSA]). SAMHSA describes six principles of TIC, including safety, trustworthiness and transparency, peer support, mutuality and collaboration, empowerment, voice and choice, cultural, and historical issues (2014). Implementation of TIC in health care settings is critical to promote safety, prevent re‐traumatisation and engage positively with people who have experienced adverse life events (Goldstein et al. 2024). Although the delivery of TIC has been positively associated with those who have a history of trauma, it makes sense that all individuals can benefit from the approach, particularly in health care settings, when they are often most vulnerable. TIC is consistent with patient‐centred care (PCC), which focuses on building trust, offering patients a sense of control, clinician respect for patient preferences and aversions, and providing care facilitated through humanistic communication (Byrne et al. 2020; Rossiter et al. 2020). TIC also demonstrates alignment with the priorities of PCC, which has been theorised and embraced since the early 1990's (de la Perrelle et al. 2022; Rossiter et al. 2020). Person‐centred care focuses on humanity and respect for the individual, and is described as individualised care determined by the needs of the person and not their diagnosis (Rossiter et al. 2020). Given the strong focus on promoting choice and empowerment, it is not surprising that both person‐centred care approaches and trauma‐informed care have been embraced by nurses who are passionate about providing tailored, effective and safe care for their clients (Kwame and Petrucka 2021; Dowdell and Speck 2022).

However, while PCC approaches enrich service delivery, TIC offers an enhancement of the principles of PCC through an understanding of trauma; it builds on PCC and offers an opportunity to develop the untapped service potential to be responsive, safe, and more accessible for people impacted by trauma (Delaney 2018). Kamsky (2019) suggests that nursing is a pivotal discipline for introducing a trauma‐informed care approach, given its central role in patient engagement and holistic health. Research regarding the efficacy and implementation of TIC has primarily focused on mental health settings and specialist areas, and is yet to be sufficiently explored within general health care settings (Brown 2020; Due et al. 2020; Hegarty et al. 2022; Oral et al. 2016; Varghese and Emerson 2022). General care settings can include general practice clinics, community health centres, multidisciplinary centres (MDCs—co‐located partner organisations) and more. This study targets critical gaps in the evidence about TIC practice and explores the implementation of trauma‐informed principles in a general healthcare setting. The research topic is relevant for the broader health sector personnel working with people impacted by trauma, including—but not limited to—people with disabilities and those from refugee, asylum seeker, and First Nations communities.

Research Questions and Aims

The question addressed in this research was: How do community health nurses working within general healthcare settings implement trauma‐informed care in their interactions with victims/survivors of sexual assault and/or family violence?

We aimed to explore: (1) how community health nurses implement components of trauma‐informed care into their practice, and (2) the benefits of trauma‐informed care from the perspectives of nurses. The research may inform how TIC is implemented in health care more generally and contribute to the growing evidence on improving health care responses for people with lived experience of trauma.

Methods

A naturalistic inquiry approach was chosen in the planning of this qualitative study as it is supportive of multiple realities, enables the researcher and the participant to interact, and encourages the researcher to embrace their values and recognise their positionality as part of the research process (Lincoln 1985). The qualitative design incorporating in‐depth semi‐structured focus group and an individual interview was used to explore nurses' perspectives and descriptions of the implementation of TIC. A qualitative study design was chosen to explore real‐world application, to obtain rich descriptions and develop an in‐depth understanding of the wealth of operational experience in a community health nurse program co‐located with other agencies in multidisciplinary centres (MDCs) to support victim/survivors of sexual assault and/or family violence (Creswell and Poth 2016; Ellis and Hart 2023). The in‐depth semi‐structured focus group method was chosen to elicit detailed information on the benefits associated with working as a community health nurse in this setting, as generic qualitative methods are flexible yet exploratory (Ellis and Hart 2023). Participants were invited to explore TIC implementation in a health care setting and the impact of trauma‐informed care on patient engagement, barriers to care, and the benefits of implementing a TIC approach. Creswell and Poth (2016) suggest that the advantages of focus group interviews include time efficiency, cost‐effectiveness, and the potential to support a higher level of comfort in sharing experiences. Data collection was conducted via a secure videoconferencing platform to support access and privacy. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (Monash Health, approval number 2021‐281029).

Researcher Positionality and Reflexivity

The female research team conducting the data analysis comprised trained registered nurses positioned within diverse nursing contexts. Two researchers held formal appointments as nurse researchers, whilst the principal researcher served as State multidiscpilinary centre (MDC) Nurse Coordinator, with responsibilities encompassing programme strategy, education, research, nursing support, and oversight—notably, without direct patient care provision. This positioning within the nursing profession, particularly at the intersection of clinical and strategic practice, has fundamentally shaped how the research team interprets and engages with the analytical process. The collective positionality of the research team was further informed by multifaceted dimensions. Specifically, one team member's lived experience of sexual assault, combined with their accumulated practice experience and subject matter expertise in sexual violence and domestic and family violence, has significantly influenced their interpretive lens and analytical approach. Accordingly, the researchers acknowledge that their interpretation of the data is inextricably linked to, and has been substantively shaped by, these embodied and professional standpoints.

Setting and Context

The research was conducted within the Community Health Nurse Program located in multidisciplinary centres (MDCs) across seven regional and metropolitan sites in the state of Victoria, Australia. MDCs co‐locate a range of partner agencies to provide a patient‐centred, integrated and holistic response to provide safety, support and access to a broad range of services (Victorian Police 2025). The population served within the MDC involved in this study are victims/survivors of domestic/family violence or sexual assault who live within the local area. Community health nurses working in MDCs practice TIC using a social model of health, providing health assessments, care planning, referrals, health service navigation, health literacy, and education (Department of Families 2019). The MDC community health nurse program employs up to the equivalent of 1.2 full‐time positions at each MDC site (totalling 12 nurses across all sites in Victoria). In contrast, health services outside the walls of the MDC typically do not implement TIC unless they are a specialist service. Specific training for trauma‐informed care is provided to community nurses in the MDC using a short online learning package, peer support, reflective practice, and ongoing professional development opportunities.

Recruitment

All nurses (12) from MDCs in Victoria were invited to participate in a focus group. Recruitment was conducted by providing nurse managers with an email invitation with a participant information and consent form attached. Managers were asked to provide confirmation when the email invitation was sent to the community health nurses. Nurses wanting to participate contacted the researcher to discuss involvement, ask questions about the study, provide consent, and schedule their focus group attendance. A follow‐up email was sent by the manager two weeks after the first invitation to those who had not replied. Those who did not express their interest in taking part before the focus group were unable to participate. Written or verbal informed consent was obtained prior to or at commencement of the focus group. While five of the 12 nurses agreed to participate, one could not attend the focus group, and therefore, an individual interview was arranged for them.

The researchers conducting the interview and focus group were nurses who had prior training in the identification, management, and prevention of vicarious trauma, had collaboratively designed the focus group discussion guide (Appendix S1), and a distress protocol was developed in case of participant distress but was not required (Appendix S2).

Data Analysis

An external professional transcription service with expertise in handling sensitive material transcribed the audio recordings verbatim before being uploaded into NVIVO software to support the coding process. Inductive data analysis was conducted using a reflexive thematic analysis approach (Braun and Clarke 2021, 2022). Using Braun and Clarke's approach, the principal researcher and co‐facilitator firstly read and re‐read the transcripts to familiarise themselves with the data, and then undertook independent initial coding of the data. The entire nursing research team reviewed these initial codes together and developed a codebook in Microsoft excel together. As a group, the team revised the coding framework until consensus on these codes was achieved. Codes were grouped into categories by two team members (RF and VR) before the research team collaboratively reflected upon the meanings within the data to develop and refine the themes (Braun and Clarke 2021, 2022, 2024). A brief summary of themes and sub‐themes was circulated to participants for feedback; however, none of the five indicated any need for change.

Results

Four community health nurses participated in a focus group and one in an individual interview. All five participants identified as female and were aged between their mid‐twenties and mid‐fifties. The voices of all five community health nurses participating in the study offer an insight into how TIC had been implemented in their work. Nurses articulated how they recognised trauma, verbalised how they understood TIC, and shared the strategies they used to minimise re‐traumatisation. The use of TIC in a general setting was described across four main themes: what trauma can look like in patients; components of trauma‐informed care; when TIC is working; and benefits to clinicians. Each main theme explored the distinct components of implementing TIC and successfully embedding it in everyday practice where nurses are familiar, confident, and have experience with the nuances of TIC in their context of practice.

What Trauma Can Look Like in Patients

Being able to recognise and describe what trauma can look like in patients is the first main theme emerging from the data demonstrating successful delivery of TIC. Nurses were able to identify psychological and physical signs of trauma as well as its toll on emotions.

Everyone responds [to trauma] in different ways. We have some clients who get agitated or angry or aggressive, or tearful. Some dissociate completely, so just withdraw, can't speak. We've had someone who had a code blue called on them because they thought they were having a cardiac arrest, but they were just so triggered. (Focus group [FG] participant)

According to participants, implementing TIC in health care settings is built on their knowledge and experience, which enables them to anticipate, assess, and respond to patient cues with targeted interventions. Foundational knowledge of common trauma reactions was reported as a key element of a nursing assessment. Using this information, nurses spoke of being able to identify signs of increasing distress, retriggering of trauma, disengagement with treatment, or health deterioration. Identification and interpretation of trauma symptoms were considered important for the implementation of TIC; these responses were further explained as psychological signs, physical signs, and emotions.

Nurses identified psychological signs that often indicate mental health deterioration.

You may find as well that clients who self‐harm may increase—say, if they cut themselves or something, you may notice that frequency increases, it becomes a little bit more severe (FG participant).

Another focus group participant reflected on repeated symptoms of declining mental health: ‘fast pace of talking, like racing with her talking, which brought me back to what she was like 12 months prior, when her mental health was declining’ (FG participant).

Similarly, for patients experiencing trauma‐related distress or being re‐traumatised, nurses noted ‘I've also seen increasing substance use amongst some of our clients at times when they're really triggered, or when their trauma is—yeah, they're in distress’ (FG participant).

Physical signs or hypo or hyper arousal were often described in relation to the stress response experienced by patients when accessing health services: ‘Even just like actual physical responses in appointments—flinching, crying out, those sorts of responses, or becoming really quiet and not being able to speak up, that freeze response’. Other physical manifestations described included agitation, tearfulness, aggression, withdrawal, and complete dissociation.

When it came to nurse recognition of the impact of trauma, participants described this through accounts about patient emotional states and captured the emotional vulnerability and rapidly evolving states experienced with distress or being re‐traumatised. Nurses described emotions associated with trauma distress, including highly anxious states, mistrust, anger, feeling overwhelmed and unsafe, and were able to link these emotions back to their experiences of trauma:

Clients of mine will have had generational trauma in working with social services and that kind of thing. They just really, really lack trust in healthcare services, etc., because they're concerned that somebody is going to report them. (FG participant).

Components of Trauma‐Informed Care

The second main theme that emerged from analysis captured the core components required for the use of TIC in practice as described by the nurses. These core components enable nurses to assess trauma, then develop, resource, and implement appropriate TIC strategies. From the outset, nurses incorporated evidence‐informed TIC approaches: ‘Prior to even starting the conversation I was just engaging with her and getting an understanding of how we work, what she was wanting, not going straight into asking things’ (FG participant). TIC components are further described across six subthemes: Demonstrating knowledge and understanding; Developing trust; Creating safe spaces; Shared decision making; Supportive care; and Taking the time.

Demonstrating Knowledge and Understanding

Nurses' knowledge and understanding of trauma were critical to the implementation of TIC when identifying intended outcomes, strategies, and informing considerations.

Listening in a non‐judgmental way … I think it's really important to have an understanding of the fact that for trauma‐informed care doesn't mean somebody having to talk about their trauma with you, because that re‐traumatises them to do that (FG Participant).

This trauma knowledge supported providing comprehensive care facilitated aimed at limiting the negative impacts of trauma while demonstrating an understanding of needs:

Having a knowledge and consideration of the experiences that have brought a person to where they are in their life and how experiences of trauma can affect their engagement with health services, and providing care that facilitates service with ease and understanding of those issues (FG participant).

Developing Trust

The importance of establishing trust was highlighted in the discussion and is a precursor to successful patient engagement: ‘developing over time a sense of trust between the worker and the client, considering that they've had an event in their life where their trust has most likely been broken’ (FG participant). Focus group participants suggested clear communication, transparency, setting expectations, ensuring confidentiality, demonstrating an understanding of trauma, and warm (in‐person) introductions as conducive to developing trust. ‘Because somebody they trust, who they're talking to their trauma about, can just do a quick little warm referral introduction, and that works’ (FG participant).

Creating Safe Spaces

Nurses described a comprehensive overview of the importance of, and how to create safe spaces for victim/survivors:

The safety element is so important…I think safety is something that a lot of our clients have never known. So, to be able to foster that and then help people engage in healthcare in a space that they feel is safe (FG participant).

Beyond physical safety, the discussion incorporated broader concepts of safety, including cultural safety and safety considerations relevant to the trauma experience. Creating safe spaces included curious enquiry that informed a risk assessment and strategy for anticipating, minimising, and managing distressed states. ‘We've discussed…when they're in a non‐heightened phase, what works for them in terms of de‐escalating those feelings’ (FG participant). Nurses described using strategies to manage emotional distress, which included controlling their voice (tone, volume, pace), acknowledging what was happening, facilitating grounding with rhythmic movement (walking), and changing environments when necessary. Other strategies included reducing stimuli of noise and bright lights, and suggesting other aids such as weighted blankets and textile mats.

Shared Decision Making

Participants described common threads of empowerment related to decision making across goal setting activities, care planning and successful outcomes: ‘I think empowerment is such a big—that's a central concept. I know, of trauma‐informed care, but it's such a big part of our role, and whether that's empowering people to make their own choices about what issues matter to them…. letting them choose’ (FG Participant). Shared decision‐making involved giving patients a voice. ‘It's not somebody else telling them what they have to do…….Sometimes, it's less obvious, and no one's listened to the client before about it’ (FG participant). Other empowerment strategies included breaking down goals into micro steps, progressing slowly and carefully to enable the development of confidence, while also knowing when to step back as required.

Supportive Care

Supportive care was consistently described by participants, with one focus group participant referring to acknowledgment of the individual and their experiences as an essential part of providing trauma‐informed supportive care; she insisted that some nurses will even state something similar to: ‘we see you, and this is what we're going to do to make this a good experience for you’ (FG participant). Supportive care was described and enacted through nurse advocacy and handover of TIC information. It was highly valued by the participants and was considered essential to reducing re‐traumatisation. During the focus group discussion, there was evidence of using TIC knowledge to plan for supportive care:

I think having that conversation before appointments, too, so then if the client is triggered in an appointment, maybe you know what strategies work best for them. So, whether it's' tell me five things you can see in the room', or … something so they know that they're supported, and then you as well have those strategies you can enact, if they're in distress (Interview participant).

Taking the Time

Nurses discussed the highly anxious state that many patients with trauma experience and considered the significant impact of taking time to relieve anxiety and prepare strategies. ‘Before an appointment, talking through, okay, what might be helpful to bring with you if A, B, and C happens’ was described by a (FG participant). Following up on numerous issues concurrently was associated with a risk of the patient becoming overwhelmed: ‘Working on when I do the health assessment and healthcare goals, working on one referral at a time, or one point at a time with the clients doing too much is too much’(FG participant). Consequently, nurses spoke about the need to work in stark contrast to their normal way of working and slow down; taking the time was a considered approach and considered essential. One focus group participant described how they reassured patients with ‘we can stop at any time’.

When Trauma‐Informed Care is Working

Participants suggested that TIC is not a static process; they described how they observed responses to actions and strategies they put in place before reflecting on their efficacy. All participants perceived this as a critical aspect of TIC. For nurses involved in this study, they defined evidence of efficacy to be founded in the response they received from patients:

So if they're feeling safe and they have trust in me, I'm providing choices, and they get to be empowered. I think if that's occurring, then I feel like that's trauma‐informed care in practice (FG participant).

Efficacy was also defined by another FG participant as the ability to advocate for and establish a connection between the victim/survivor and other essential services: ‘The next day, she was raving….and it completely changed the way she felt about the health service having had a response that acknowledged her trauma’ (FG participant). Nurse assessment of the impact of TIC allowed for an opportunity to reflect and consider additional strategies or to adapt TIC in real time. Nurses' measure of success in using a TIC approach is further described through demonstrations of patient empowerment, feedback and engagement.

Victim/survivor empowerment was considered by participants as a measure of TIC success; defined by one participant as when a patient states:

I think I can do that myself now. So, them, slowly being able to become more self‐sufficient again, and yeah, just giving them just an increased level of confidence to be able to tackle things on their own (FG participant).

Several practices that were reported to demonstrate patient empowerment responses included identifying their consent, giving patients a choice, and the power of how and when they would like to be contacted. Evaluation of empowerment‐focused interventions was considered successful where there was evidence of transition from supported health care to independent health care. While supported health care was described by participants as care that builds on patient strengths to provide the minimum level of support required for service navigation, advocacy to facilitate safety with external services, and supporting traumatised patients with executive functioning, memory gaps, and emotional dysregulation, participants indicate that independent health care is demonstrated when individuals no longer require additional support to independently engage with health care services. Nurses also measured empowerment through patient demonstrations of confidence, self‐sufficiency, and evidence that they have re‐established faith in themselves.

Patient feedback was considered a measure of success of TIC practice according to participants in our study; satisfaction was described when patients reported having a good experience or when patients were appreciative of the care they received. Specific examples of positive patient experiences included accounts of a grateful patient who reported their experience as fantastic: ‘Because she was listened to all the way through’ (FG participant). Participants described victim survivors' demonstration of relief as further evidence of successful TIC:

They're often like, oh, that wasn't as bad as I expected, or I got through that, and I didn't think I was going to (FG participant).

According to study participants, when patients felt seen and heard, they were more likely to stay and engage. Alternatively, ‘if they're not being seen or heard, they're quite easy to shut down and build a wall’, one focus group participant explained. Another stated that ‘their engagement will tell you whether or not they've received that—whether that trauma‐informed care has been effective or not’ (FG participant). Yet another focus group participant commented: ‘I think a way my clients respond when they're engaged in a trauma‐informed way, is they'll answer the phone when I call’ (FG participant), as this was never guaranteed when working with victim survivors of sexual assault and family violence, according to participants.

Benefits to Clinicians

Participants reflected on their experiences before and after implementing TIC, and identified that the positive results they observed were not only benefits to patients but also direct and indirect benefits for themselves:

There's so many benefits as a clinician, because I think we've probably all worked in healthcare for a long time, prior to coming to this work, or anyone who's working in any sort of healthcare community type role, and people are used to working with complex clients (FG participant).

Benefits to nurses were described by the nurses as recognition and validation of the complexity of their role, however, more so, a sense of efficacy was perceived as beneficial, and this was felt by participants when they observed patients achieving positive outcomes: ‘They can actually have their symptoms relieved’ (FG participant). While some of the benefits to providing a trauma‐informed approach revolved around patient accomplishment, participants also described more internal motivators and benefits to providing trauma‐informed care. A deeper understanding of these benefits is explored through the nurses ability to not only set boundaries in their work, but also their ability to demonstrate enhanced confidence, feel less overwhelm, and the pride they found in their work.

Participants credited implementing TIC to being supportive of nurses being able to set good therapeutic boundaries, which they consider essential in their position; this was especially true when it comes to explaining to patients what they could and could not do within their role:

It helps in setting boundaries, I think in terms of the care, like what we can provide within our scope and what is outside our scope. But also acknowledging we can link you into what is outside our scope. So yeah, trying to not take on more than we can manage, trying to limit the risk of vicarious trauma, which is pretty real in this job (FG participant).

As a recurring theme, boundaries were considered an important aspect of TIC and were considered to be protective for the patient and nurse. Establishing boundaries with TIC provided role clarity, which was perceived by the participants to have a positive impact on them.

Participants reported the development of both skills and confidence as a result of implementing TIC and practicing within clearly defined clinical boundaries:

I feel like I've got almost good boundaries to operate within or something. I can care for them safely, in a way where they're not unpacking a whole lot of stuff. I can help them navigate the health system in a much more skilled way (FG participant).

Confidence emerged with the knowledge that care delivered using TIC was the best therapeutic approach that could be offered: ‘It gives me more confidence to provide the best sort of care and the best sort of approach I can when I'm working with someone’ (FG participant).

According to focus group participants, TIC—and the confidence derived from it—was perceived to enhance the nurses' emotional state by reducing the sense of stress and overwhelm. Being trained in TIC led to some feeling they were better able to cope with their work:

I feel like probably when I was more like a novice, at the beginning of my nursing career, it might have felt more overwhelming working with somebody who I knew had experienced some traumatic event (FG participant).

Participants expressed that at the beginning of their career, knowledge of a patient's history of trauma was anticipated to present challenges in clinical practice. However, despite acknowledging challenges, they described the confidence they drew from being armed with TIC training: ‘[TIC training] gives me (the clinician) more confidence to provide the best sort of care and the best sort of approach I can when I'm working with someone’ (FG participant).

Discussion of TIC delivery led to numerous accounts from participants about the positive impact their care had on patients. Some reported that their patients had expressed gratitude for ‘such a good experience’ and they perceived the care they had provided to ‘totally changing the way people felt about the health service’. Participants described pride in being able to relieve symptoms, and provide care where patients are ‘taken seriously and not fobbed off’, and they felt fulfilment when they witnessed the happiness of patients who reported a fantastic experience. Participants described feelings of pride about the quality of their program's organisational performance: ‘I was really proud of my organisation that they did a good job’ (FG participant).

Discussion

This study explored community health nurses' experiences of implementing trauma‐informed care in a general health setting to individuals impacted by sexual assault or domestic violence and sought to identify essential components of implementing trauma‐informed care in this context. These findings serve as an early exemplar of how TIC can be implemented in general healthcare settings, offering a compelling case for its adoption and highlighting key factors that influence its successful integration.

Implementation of TIC, as described by participants in this study, involves respect for and responsiveness to individual patient needs, acknowledgement of experiences, and incorporating patient values and preferences in decision‐making, closely aligning with principles of PCC (Kwame and Petrucka 2021). However, beyond a PCC approach, our study shows that TIC distinctly acknowledges the specific impact of trauma on an individual's wellbeing and enables adaptation of care accordingly; therefore, offering support to persons who are generally reluctant to seek care (Dowdell and Speck 2022). While there is overlap in these two approaches to care, demonstrated in the focus of the patient, respecting their values and involving them in decision making, the application of a trauma lens within TIC enables the nurse to understand how a patient's past experiences can shape their response to and engagement in care.

Our findings show that nurses using TIC can recognise the physical, psychological, and emotional signs of trauma and demonstrate use of the principles of TIC such as trustworthiness, empowerment and choice (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2014) in practice. Successful delivery of TIC was measured by participants in our study through patient feedback and where they perceived themselves to be working collaboratively with patients to achieve engagement and empowerment. Importantly, our study supports previous evidence that using a TIC approach to patient care offers benefits to both patients and clinicians alike (Huo et al. 2023).

Our first main theme suggests that having TIC knowledge and recognising what trauma can look like in patients can help overcome incorrect assumptions and influence behaviours, bias, or judgements that create barriers to accessing health care (Fischer et al. 2019). According to participants, foundational knowledge of the sequelae of trauma can positively influence a clinician's approach and help in providing the kind of care the patient needs at the time. Having the skills and knowledge to recognise trauma can empower the clinician to look beyond the presenting patient's avoidant or hyper‐aroused state (Dowdell and Speck 2022) to understand what is causing it. Our study findings suggest that foundational knowledge of trauma responses supports appropriate and responsive TIC strategies that can protect against incorrect diagnosis, inadequate treatment, or re‐traumatisation in care (Dowdell and Speck 2022).

Participants provided tangible examples of trauma and its impact, expressed through emotional, physical, and psychological indicators; these insights could support clinicians who have not yet received training in TIC to recognise trauma‐related distress. What trauma can look like in patients highlights the use of theoretical knowledge informing TIC implementation, suggesting that TIC training has the potential to support application at a practice level. Nurses' descriptions of recognising the signs that trauma is present provide opportunities for intervening early and ensuring that care is provided in a trauma‐informed way. However, it is important to note that using a TIC approach to care is not just about being able to recognise trauma, but about understanding how to tailor care to minimise its impact, making the approach beneficial to most patients, particularly for those who are reluctant or fearful to engage with health care.

Demonstrating knowledge and understanding of TIC is critical to implementation of the approach in general health care settings (Huo et al. 2023). In this study, TIC knowledge is demonstrated to inform strategies through nurse descriptors of curious enquiry with patients to increase the accuracy of TIC assessment and allowing for care planning that is responsive and tailored. These findings are consistent with known enablers of TIC implementation such as staff knowledge, training in TIC, and adequate preparation before implementation (Fischer et al. 2019; Huo et al. 2023) and indicate that clinicians using TIC are equipped to provide patients with support and an understanding of their experience. Beattie et al. (2019) confirm these findings, suggesting that an understanding of trauma neurobiology enables health care staff to develop strategies and systems that accommodate an understanding of what causes aggressive responses. Our study shows that knowledge and understanding provide the impetus for planning, pre‐empting, and purposeful interventions that recognise trauma; this is evidenced through strategies implemented to optimise the health experience, support engagement, and prevent re‐traumatisation. These findings illustrate that TIC also has the potential to increase staff perceptions of physical and psychological safety (Beattie et al. 2019; Fischer et al. 2019), and therefore provide more responsive care.

Goddard et al. (2022) conceptualised nursing‐specific TIC by identifying its attributes, antecedents, and consequences, while also emphasising the need for it to be universal across health care settings; our study is an early exemplar that TIC can indeed be introduced into a general care setting by community health nurses. Concepts of universal TIC infer that screening of trauma is not required for the provision of TIC; true to theoretical assumptions, TIC implementation in this study suggests that reducing the need to retell their story was a strategy activated to reduce re‐traumatisation risk. Furthermore, participants acknowledged the strengths‐based focus of TIC, which led to empowerment and resilience, and reduced trauma triggers in the patient–nurse relationship on the path towards healing and health (Goddard et al. 2022). Enhancing PCC with an understanding of trauma through TIC approaches may ultimately improve patient participation, treatment adherence, and health outcomes, and so should be considered a universal ‘trauma precaution’ for all patients across all contexts (Dowdell and Speck 2022). Findings from participants' experiences of implementing TIC illustrate how health care providers can apply these in practice more broadly, demonstrating knowledge and understanding of how a TIC approach can shape PCC to be responsive to trauma‐specific needs (Goldstein et al. 2024; Raja et al. 2015). However, a challenge to introducing TIC more broadly is that trauma‐informed education is currently limited for healthcare providers, often only being offered in settings where specialist services (such as mental health care) are accessed. Consequently, although our study shows that TIC has applicability across health care settings, the fragmented training opportunities currently on offer, limit its potential to enhance experiences of care more broadly across the sector.

Findings from this study highlight the necessary components of trauma‐informed care by articulating clinician perceptions of the essential elements required to provide TIC in nursing, and provide practical examples from their role. Participants used communication strategies to identify information about the impacts of trauma critical to informing their TIC strategy when working with the patient to ensure positive outcomes, experiences and recovery. For example, validating an individual's experience and providing an empathetic response using TIC communication demonstrates an understanding of trauma and supports the development of trust consistent with establishing trust and rapport in trauma‐informed care reported elsewhere (Raja et al. 2015); this can assist in addressing issues of distrust of the medical system experienced by victim‐survivors. Trust is enhanced using communication strategies to provide clarity and explore expectations, explain confidentiality, provide ownership of information, show transparency, and provide anticipatory guidance (Dowdell and Speck 2022). Equipped with knowledge and understanding of trauma, strategies to develop trust can be adapted for the individual patient in the relevant clinical setting. This intentional establishment of trust and deliberate efforts to tailor care not only encourages access to care but also supports safety when receiving care.

Our study showed that creating safe spaces incorporates both universal and targeted TIC strategies relevant to the individual's experience. Universal strategies are defined as those that recognise the prevalence of traumatic experiences and seek to consistently operate from a TIC perspective embedded in all aspects of practice without knowledge of the individual's trauma exposure or experience. Targeted strategies are individualised strategies developed using knowledge of the individual's experience and trauma impacts to address their specific needs. Knowledge and understanding of physical or psychological signs of trauma support the early identification and response to a perceived threat or absence of safety. TIC involves understanding a patient's lived experience to offer person‐centred choices and a structure to support safety and anxiety regulation (Raja et al. 2015). Using communication strategies to monitor for signs of distress, anxiety, or pre‐emptively identify potential threats was discussed as a successful safety strategy, which is consistent with existing literature (Raja et al. 2015). Shared decision‐making empowers the patient to make decisions about their care that are informed by the clinical information and evidence provided by the health service. Dowdell and Speck (2022) suggest that credibility is built when patients are invited to be involved in their care and to choose whether to engage with a potentially traumatising intervention. Using curious enquiry, nurses in this study explained how they increased awareness and understanding of patient safety to facilitate choices for the patient; this may involve options for interruption or control over who or how the intervention is delivered (Johnsson et al. 2018). In this way, TIC makes it possible to engage the patient in their care experience by acknowledging their experience and respecting their values, preferences, and needs, and is consistent with PCC as described by Kwame and Petrucka (2021). However, TIC also goes beyond PCC in regard to shared decision‐making because of the encouragement of patients to control boundaries in care provision.

Supportive care within TIC implementation was exemplified through nurses' efforts to advocate for and protect patients' rights and safety. This was important when patient voices were unheard, or they were unable to speak for themselves and is reflected in studies on health advocacy in professional nursing practice literature more broadly (Heck et al. 2022). Being cognisant of trauma‐related distress, which may cause a dissociative state, is critical in implementing TIC and effectively addressing individual needs using empathy and assertive communication (Johnsson et al. 2018). It capitalises on established trust and rapport and demonstrates patient empowerment through the essential level of support required for engagement. Taking the time captures strategies to address the sense of overwhelm and threats to safety that arise when health care services control the pace of care and overlook the opportunity to engage the patient in aspects of care related to pace or number of interventions. While timing is always a factor to consider in delivering appropriate care, this study supports existing evidence that taking time is required to minimise the residual and ongoing impact of trauma (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2014).

When trauma‐informed care is working, it contributes to staff wellbeing and job satisfaction, both of which are important when working with individuals who may be experiencing complex cases of trauma and may protect against burnout. Clinicians successfully established patient engagement in the most complex circumstances, minimising re‐traumatisation, facilitating de‐escalation of potentially heightened states, and patient empowerment, suggesting the efficacy of their interventions, which they reported to be rewarding. Our study findings of nurse interactions with patients, which illustrated awareness of trauma‐informed care, sensitivity, and responsiveness are reflected in other recent studies (Fleishman et al. 2020). TIC evidenced in this study demonstrates that interventions are not a set‐and‐forget process, but rather dynamic, requiring ongoing assessment and monitoring of efficacy with the potential to adjust as required. Patient feedback provides valuable confirmation of the impact and value of the nurse's role, which our participants identified as gratifying; this suggests that evaluating the efficacy of TIC could positively contribute to workforce sustainability through staff satisfaction and may support efforts to prevent clinician burnout.

Staff perceptions of the benefits to clinicians of TIC are echoed by Hall et al. (2021) who suggest that education about trauma‐informed care principles supports reducing clinicians' own risk of burnout and supports their mental health. Fleishman et al. (2020) similarly suggest that TIC supports nurse understanding of maladaptive patient coping strategies, creates space for empathy, and provides direction to identify alternative strategies, and suggest that a trauma‐informed workplace can result in nurses feeling safer and better supported. Our findings are also consistent with those of Beattie et al. (2019) who suggest that TIC supports staff physical, emotional, and psychological safety by reducing inappropriate patient reactivity, supporting staff understanding, and empowering responses to threats, thereby reducing their potential to trigger reactivity in patients. It may therefore be possible to create more positive workplaces for nurses through the implementation of a TIC approach to care that is proving to be beneficial to both patient and provider. Additionally, as we grow our awareness of the high number of nurses and caring professions impacted by issues such as intimate partner violence and child abuse (McLindon et al. 2022), being trained in TIC could potentially have benefits for nurses positioned in dual roles of both carer and survivor. In summary, adopting a TIC approach to care provision could also serve as a protective factor for addressing workplace distress, demonstrating mutual benefit when adopted in health care settings.

Strengths and Limitations

There are several strengths and limitations of this study. A major strength of this study is highlighting the voices of nurses who have been trained to provide TIC, an approach that is most often used in specialist areas of care, such as mental health, rather than in general settings. All researchers conducting the research and data analysis recognise their positionality as nurses and the influence this has on their interpretation of the data. However, in the context of this study, an understanding of the nursing field and the challenges of the role as nurses strengthen the data analysis.

Limitations include the small sample size involving one focus group and one individual interview, which limits the transferability of study findings beyond this general healthcare setting in Victoria. While small sample sizes make it hard to demonstrate data saturation, we do feel that the quality and impact of the messaging provides information power. Small sample size can also increase the risk of sampling bias which may distort interpretations; however our study did manage to almost half of all potential participants who were only a small group of twelve to begin with. Using reflexive thematic analysis has strengths; however, there is a chance that the researchers' positioning results in some themes being missed. As a result, the findings may not be applicable to the broader population from which the sample was drawn, which is a major limitation if the goal is to inform broader decision‐making. In addition, representation is limited by the fact that all nurses were working in various general community settings but focused on providing support to one group of individuals: survivors of sexual assault or domestic violence. There is, however, strong relevance between the experiences of those who experience these forms of violence and those who survive other forms of trauma such as child abuse, neglect, emotional abuse, and exposure to traumatic events, making these findings relevant to hundreds of thousands of Australian peoples presenting to health services. Future research might include the effectiveness of implementing TIC amongst broader populations.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Findings from this study have led to recommendations relevant to policy, practice and research as follows:

TIC training should be a component of all undergraduate healthcare education and ongoing professional development and training to strengthen PCC and improve healthcare accessibility and engagement for traumatised individuals. Until this is feasible, TIC implementation can be supported through the resourcing of teams who have trauma expertise to provide advice, build staff capacity, and respond to trauma‐impacted individuals.

Conclusion

Although nurses currently take a person‐centred approach to providing care, the findings from this study show that they perceive that a trauma‐informed approach makes care more accessible and effective. This study provides an exemplar of how TIC can be implemented in general settings, and the findings suggest that nurses observe patients to find TIC responsive to their needs; these findings can be used to inform how TIC can be embedded into practice more generally in the future. TIC training empowers clinicians to operate with a comprehensive understanding of trauma and the ability to adapt their learning to their local practice context. A thorough knowledge and understanding of TIC allows health providers to identify trauma symptoms, avoid re‐traumatisation, and tailor responses appropriately, ultimately enhancing engagement and improving patient experiences while also providing a positive and rewarding workplace for nurses. For those reasons, it is hard to understand why TIC is not implemented in every health care setting.
