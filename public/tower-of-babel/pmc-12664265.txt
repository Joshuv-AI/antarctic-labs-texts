Change in workplace violence against female healthcare workers in a tertiary hospital in Henan Province, China: a four-wave cross-sectional study
Authors: Chuning He, Min Zhang, Qianqian Luo, Na Chen, Yujie Liu, Yiming Huang, Fuyuan Wang, Yuting Tang, Xinxin Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace violence (WPV) is a widespread issue that harms the dignity and well-being of female healthcare workers globally. This study assesses changes in WPV against female employees in a central Chinese hospital over four waves of research from 2018 to 2022.MethodsCross-sectional surveys were conducted annually from 2018 to 2019 and 2021 to 2022, excluding 2020 due to COVID-19. The questionnaire, developed with the ILO and WHO, gathered data on demographics, experiences of physical and psychological violence, incident handling, and awareness of hospital policies.ResultsA total of 3,466 responses were collected, showing a significant reduction in both physical and psychological WPV. Physical violence incidence dropped from 7.82% in 2018 to 1.2% in 2022 (R = -0.13, P < 0.001), and psychological violence decreased from 46.94% to 22.89% (R = -0.20, P < 0.001). Female healthcare workers with heightened concern about WPV were more likely to report both types of violence (e.g. for physical violence, OR in 2022, 13.87; 95% CI, 1.67–115.21). Frequent patient contacts consistently correlated with higher psychological WPV (e.g. OR in 2022, 4.27; 95% CI, 2.07–8.78). Factors like working in outpatient/emergency departments, unfamiliarity with WPV reporting, and lack of encouragement to report also contributed to violence, though not consistently significant. Despite declining violence rates, satisfaction with institutional handling of incidents remained low (below 25%), while satisfaction with handling of sexual harassment improved significantly from 0% to 41.67%(P = 0.002). Prevention measures, such as environmental improvements and patient screening, saw increased perception over the years.ConclusionThe study shows a systematic decline in WPV against female healthcare workers in this hospital, indicating potential benefits of the comprehensive interventions. However, low satisfaction with incident handling and persistent barriers to reporting highlight the need for strengthening organizational response mechanisms. These findings have practical implications for designing more effective and context-specific WPV prevention and support strategies in healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25379-0.

Introduction

A global agreement has been established to eradicate all kinds of violence against women, bolster their empowerment in professional settings, and foster their creative and leadership abilities [1], as underscored by the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 5 (Gender Equality) [2] and SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth) [3].

One of the barriers to achieving these goals is Workplace Violence (WPV) [4], which can manifest in forms of physical and psychological violence, with psychological violence further divided into verbal abuse, bullying, sexual harassment, and ethnic discrimination [5–7].International studies conducted over the past 15 years in countries such as Italy, India, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Japan have shown that the main forms of workplace violence women face are psychological and sexual violence [8, 9]. Many women experience both simultaneously, seriously undermining their dignity, value [10, 11] and health [12–14]. WPV directly undermines the specific targets of Sustainable Development Goals 5 and 8. It directly violates the objective of SDG 5 by perpetuating violence and discrimination against women, undermining their safety, empowerment, and path to leadership. Simultaneously, WPV contradicts the "decent work" principle of SDG 8 by creating hostile and unsafe environments, which reduces productivity, increases turnover, and hinders inclusive economic growth. Therefore, eradicating WPV is essential for making progress on both gender equality and sustainable economic development.

Various international initiatives have been undertaken to address this issue. In 2019, the International Labour Organization (ILO) released Convention No. 190 [15] and Recommendation No. 206 [16], forming the frist global action framework on violence and harassment in the world of work. Additionally, documents such as the United Nations Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) [17], the Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action [18], and various UN and ILO guidelines collectively constitute an international legal framework to protect women’s rights and eliminate workplace violence [19].

Healthcare settings are particularly prone to violent incidents [20, 21], with distinct gender-specific characteristics [22–25]. According to a study in the United States, female healthcare workers are more likely to experience weekly workplace violence, suffer more physical and psychological symptoms, and consider leaving the profession after such incidents [26]. A US prospective cohort study of emergency workers indicated that females experienced violence nearly three times more frequently per day than males (3.0 versus 0.9 times) [27]. Similarly, a survey in southern China (2018–2019) found that while physical violence affected 4.0% of female healthcare workers, psychological violence was pervasive, with verbal abuse affecting 39.9% and overall psychological violence reaching 41.2%. This disparity underscores the view that violence against female healthcare workers is the "tip of the iceberg" of gender power imbalance issues [28].

However, there are gaps in the current research, particularly in the continuous monitoring of various types of violence that female healthcare workers experience in the workplace. There is a lack of systematic measurement and documentation of how these acts of violence change over time. To address this research gap, this study conducted a consistent investigation into the violence experienced by female medical staff at a hospital in the Central China region. It compares the changes in the incidence of workplace violence in 2018, 2019, 2021 and 2022 at the pilot hospital, explores the influencing factors of workplace violence against women, and identifies effective measures to against workplace violence. It is important to note that due to the lockdown measures implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the collection of data for that year was compromised, leading to an absence of relevant statistical data. The goal is to continuously refine the hospital’s violence prevention and control measures and promote the integration of workplace violence prevention and control into the hospital’s occupational health protection system.

In our research, we categorized individuals based on biological sex as "female" or "male" for the purpose of analysis. While a binary classification was used, we acknowledge and respect the full diversity of gender experiences and identities.

Methods

Study settings and subjects

The subjects of this study were female healthcare workers at a general hospital in Henan Province, central China, including frontline clinical staff, administrative personnel, temporary employees, visiting doctors, medical students, and external staff involved in security and logistics services. The hospital is a large Class A tertiary comprehensive institution that integrates patient care, education, research, clinical training, rehabilitation, and preventive healthcare. It has an approved bed capacity of 2,100, with an average daily outpatient volume of 2,192 in 2023. The hospital provides 54 clinical and medical technical specialties and operates 82 departments. By 2025, the hospital will employ more than 1,900 staff members, with a male to female ratio of approximately 1:4, and the workforce has been expanding steadily in recent years. The inclusion criteria were obtaining informed consent and voluntary participation in the survey. The exclusion criteria were: ① individuals who had been absent from their positions for over a year due to vacation, training, etc.; and ②individuals who were unwilling to participate.

Assessment tools

The survey utilized the "National Case Study Tools on Workplace Violence in the Health Sector (Chinese version) [29] ", a questionnaire jointly developed by several international organizations including ILO and WHO. With formal authorization from the ILO, our team translated the original English questionnaire into Chinese. While the structure and content of the violence-related section remained consistent with the original English version, the questionnaire was locally adapted with a more comprehensive sociodemographic section tailored to the Chinese context. This included items such as job title (reflecting the hierarchical structure common in Chinese healthcare settings) and salary range. Pre-tests were conducted with 26 participants in Beijing and 155 participants in Shenzhen in May 2018. The final instrument was subsequently validated among 978 healthcare workers in Zhengzhou in 2018, demonstrating good psychometric properties. The re-test reliability showed a Cronbach’s α of 0.83 [30]. The questionnaire mainly consists of four parts: ① demographic and professional characteristics of the respondents; ② the occurrence and response of physical violence in the workplace; ③ the occurrence and response of four types of psychological violence: verbal abuse, bullying/mobbing, sexual harassment, and racial discrimination; and ④ an assessment of the hospital’s policies and interventions concerning violent incidents.

Data collection

In June 2018, our research team initiated a baseline cross-sectional survey at the hospital, targeting healthcare workers to assess the prevalence and influencing factors of workplace violence experienced within the past year. Subsequent cross-sectional surveys were executed in July 2019, September 2021, and June 2022 using the identical questionnaire to monitor the trends and factors associated with hospital violence over the subsequent years. The criteria for participant inclusion and exclusion remained consistent across all four survey rounds. Therefore, this study is a four-wave cross-sectional study. In this study, the electronic version of the questionnaire was disseminated through social media platforms, specifically WeChat, to targeted work groups. All employees of the hospital who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were invited to participate in the survey each year. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and the questionnaires were promoted for at least two weeks until an acceptable response rate was achieved. The process involved active participation from project collaborators, who helped with the distribution of the questionnaire to ensure a broad reach within the relevant female healthcare workers from different departments. However, no direct involvement of patients or the public was included in the design or conduct of the research.

As the survey was conducted anonymously to protect participants’ privacy and encourage honest reporting, individual respondents were not tracked across waves, and overlap in participation across years may have occurred. Given the study’s focus on institutional-level trends rather than individual follow-up, duplicate responses were considered methodologically acceptable and meaningful in this study.

Quality control

In 2018, we conducted comprehensive training sessions in the hospital during the preparatory phase, focusing on the questionnaire’s structure and content, as well as clarifying the definitions of workplace violence and harassment among healthcare workers to ensure consistency in understanding these concepts and avoid potential sources of bias. During the survey, informed consent was firstly obtained from all respondents. Post-survey, our team members meticulously organized the responses, implemented rigorous data checks, and conducted systematic coding and analysis. The electronic questionnaire required all items to be completed before submission, so no missing data occurred. Questionnaires with implausible or inconsistent responses, logical errors, or completion times under 200 s (deemed implausibly rapid based on pilot testing) were excluded.

Statistical analysis

The collected electronic questionnaires were first imported into Microsoft Excel to establish a comprehensive database. Descriptive statistics were calculated for Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents in the four-wave survey. A χ2 test was conducted for univariate analysis in each wave to explore the potential association between exposure to workplace violence and respondents’ characteristics. The significant factors were involved in the binary logistic regression analysis, employing the forward stepwise (likelihood ratio) method. Additionally, a Mantel–Haenszel χ2 test was applied to compare the trends in the rates of physical and psychological violence, and the changes in workplace violence victim satisfaction with the handling of these incidents and respondents’ recognition of anti-violence measures in the workplace among the four waves of survey. The analyses were based on a 95% CI, with a p-value of less than 0.05 indicating statistical significance. No sensitivity analysis was conducted, as the study design and analysis methods were straightforward, and the data did not involve complex assumptions or missing data that would necessitate such an analysis. IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) were used for all data analyzes.

Results

Across the four survey waves, the total numbers of both male and female eligible staff at the hospital were 1,109 in 2018, 1,195 in 2019, 1,332 in 2021, and 1,452 in 2022. The corresponding overall response rates were 89.72% (995/1,109), 83.93% (1,003/1,195), 71.02% (946/1,332), and 93.73% (1,361/1,452), respectively. Among the respondents, female participants accounted for 804 (80.8%), 851 (84.8%), 754 (79.7%), and 1,085 (79.7%) each year, consistently reflecting the gender distribution of the hospital's workforce. After rigorous data checks, there were 793, 846, 748, and 1,079 valid responses from female staff in each wave. As a result, a total of 3,466 survey responses were collected, which may include several multiple submissions from the same participant. The large sample number and stable proportion of female respondents ensure that the data is particularly representative of the female staff subgroup. The relatively lower response rate in 2021 was primarily due to challenges related to the COVID-19 pandemic, which made face-to-face survey administration difficult.

Socio-demographic characteristics

Supplementary materials 1 outlines the key socio-demographic characteristics of the surveyed population over the four-year period. Although all surveys were conducted within the same hospital, the samples were recruited on a voluntary basis and were subject to staff turnover. As a result, some of demographic and occupational characteristics (e.g., department, shift work, worry about WPV) varied significantly across waves. Still, some similar patterns can be observed. Age was divided into three groups based on the median of the outcome: under 31 years, 31–40 years, and over 40 years. A consistent majority, specifically over 80% in each survey wave, were under 40 years old. In terms of organizational work arrangements, a significant portion of the participants were involved in shift work (ranging from 80.97% to 87.83%), night shifts (ranging from 55.74% to 68.30%), patient contact during work (ranging from 85.54% to 91.05%), and physical contact with patients (ranging from 53.94% to 67.65%). The level of concern regarding workplace violence fluctuated, peaking at 42.32% expressing concern in 2019. Between 57.12% and 66.08% of participants were aware of the hospital’s reporting process for workplace violence, with approximately half of them claiming to be familiar with the process (ranging from 46.53% to 52.92%). Moreover, a half of the surveyed healthcare workers, between 53.34% and 59.50%, felt encouraged to report incidents of workplace violence.

Change of prevalence for workplace violence in the four years

Over the four waves of the survey, the overall incidence rates of physical violence, psychological violence, and the four subtypes of psychological violence (verbal abuse, bullying/mobbing, sexual harassment, and racial discrimination) in the workplace showed a downward trend, as shown in Fig. 1. The total incidence rate of physical violence decreased from 7.82% in 2018 to 1.2% in 2022, while the incidence rate of psychological violence decreased from 46.94% to 22.89%. Among them, there was a high degree of overlap between the incidence rate of psychological violence and the incidence rate of verbal abuse, indicating that verbal abuse was the main form of psychological violence in the workplace at this hospital, followed by bullying/mobbing, with relatively lower incidence rates of sexual harassment and racial discrimination. The utilization of the Mantel–Haenszel χ2 test for trend to the incidence rates of five distinct types of WPV across the years 2018 to 2022 yielded significant results. The evolving trends in violence incidence rates across different occupations and departments were also examined respectively. Figure 2 presents the significant trends in the rates of physical violence, categorized by occupational(a) and departmental(b) factors. In terms of departmental differences, female healthcare workers in the emergency and outpatient departments experienced a markedly higher prevalence of physical violence compared to the other four departments, yet also with the most pronounced reduction (from 21.84% to 1.91%), followed by internal medicine. Changes in satisfaction among victims regarding the handling of the WPV Incidents

The hospital employs various methods to address workplace violence incidents, including reporting to authorities, legal prosecution, termination of treatment, verbal warnings. Victim satisfaction with the handling of these incidents is assessed on a scale from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), where scores of 1 to 3 are classified as "Dissatisfied and Below", scores of 4 to 5 as "Satisfied and Above". As shown in Fig. 4, the satisfaction of victims with the handling of workplace violence incidents has remained relatively low over the four years. In the case of physical violence, verbal abuse, and bullying/mobbing incidents, the proportion of respondents who were "satisfied" or "very satisfied" with the handling was below 25% over the four years, and the changes over the four years were not statistically significant at the 0.05 level of significance. Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive examination of the patterns and dynamics of workplace violence against female healthcare workers in a central Chinese hospital from 2018 to 2022, with the exception of 2020. The findings highlight several critical aspects of WPV and the effectiveness of interventions over the study period. The overall incidence of workplace violence, mainly composed of psychological violence, showed a significant decline in the four waves, suggesting potential benefits of the multifaceted interventions and organizational changes implemented during the study period. The observed reduction in WPV can be attributed to both societal and organizational factors. At the societal level, strengthened legislation and heightened hospital awareness of violence prevention have improved protection for healthcare workers, particularly females. Secondly, following the COVID-19 outbreak in 2019, hospitals nationwide reinforced measures including hierarchical access control, digital monitoring, and health code–based screening [19], creating a broader environment conducive to reducing WPV. This downward trend has also been corroborated by findings from other regions in China. For example, a southern large hospital reported a decline in total WPV from 60.90% in 2018 to 34.44% in 2021, with psychological violence declining from 60.90% to 33.89% [31]. Such consistency across hospitals suggests that the decline reflects broader systemic interventions rather than isolated effects. At the organizational level, in the five years, the leadership of the hospital has greatly enhanced their awareness the hospital has increased security personnel, improved the working environment, and organized more frequent emergency drills and communication training. Importantly, after the first survey in 2018, hospital leadership explicitly committed to strengthening protection of both the physical and psychological health of staff as part of its occupational health agenda. Since then, annual WPV-focused workshops of about 50 participants have been convened, bringing together occupational health experts and frontline healthcare workers to develop action plans toward the “zero violence” vision. Collectively, these multifaceted interventions and organizational changes have played a critical role in reducing WPV incidence in the study hospital.

On a more specific dimension, the incidence rates of violence have shown some fluctuations. The physical violence rates against technical and administrative staff have been on an upward trend from 2018 to 2021, followed by a decline in 2022. The psychological violence experienced by administrative staff have significantly increased from 2019 to 2021, and then dropped to a low point in 2022. These changes could be related to the COVID-19 pandemic. On one hand, China implemented policies requiring regular nucleic acid testing for the public, with the collection and testing of nucleic acid samples primarily handled by technical and administrative personnel, which may have increased their contact with patients and the likelihood of workplace violence [32]; on the other hand, the healthcare system was impacted by the pandemic, with administrative staff potentially facing greater pressure and challenges in policy formulation and execution [33].

Violence against women is preventable [34]. To prevent violence, we need to identify and mitigate the risk factors and amplify the protective factors. In our study, several factors were found related to the occurrence of WPV, including age, occupation type, department, level of concern about violence, patient contact, physical contact with patients, salary, not aware or not familiar of the procedures for reporting WPV and lack of encouragement to report incidents. These results are consistent with previous research findings [2, 35, 36]. While these associations are not causal due to the cross-sectional study design, they still provide clues for future causal inference and partially reveal the hospital's current occupational safety climate regarding workplace violence. In this study, a greater number of factors influence psychological violence than physical violence, which may be attributed to a higher incidence rate of psychological violence. In our study, high levels of concern about violence were positively correlated with increased incidences, suggesting that the hospital’s cultural atmosphere regarding violence is crucial and can significantly impact the work patterns and mindsets of female healthcare workers. Specifically, individuals with higher levels of concern about violence tend to be more sensitive to violent behaviors and have lower thresholds for defining what constitutes a violent incident, leading to a higher reporting of violent events in this self-assessment questionnaire. This further underscores the importance of fostering a positive and safe work environment, mitigating violence threats, and enhancing healthcare workers’ awareness and coping abilities regarding violence issues. Also, lack of awareness or unfamiliarity with the WPV reporting process increased the risk for female healthcare workers in certain years, which indicate the existence of reporting barriers in the hospital. Notably, the data reveals that individuals who were more aware and familiar with the reporting procedures and encouraged to report, experienced a lower incidence of violence. This correlation corroborates the findings of past studies [37]. Consequently, it highlights the imperative need to develop a thorough, efficient, and exhaustive system for reporting incidents of violence, coupled with the cultivation of a supportive environment.

Despite the decline in violence rates, satisfaction with the institutional handling of WPV incidents remained critically low, with fewer than 25% of respondents expressing satisfaction. Although a slight uptick in satisfaction for sexual harassment in the third and fourth year, this persistent dissatisfaction still reflects underlying systemic and institutional barriers, such as including insufficient staffing, inconsistent policy implementation across departments, persistent underreporting, limited institutional responsiveness, and cultural stigma around disclosure. These results further highlight the hospital’s lack of a workplace-specific, occupational health management framework. While severe incidents of WPV in China are addressed under legal frameworks such as the Civil Code, which mandates thorough and prudent handling, it is often the less visible, non-injurious incidents that cumulatively undermine overall satisfaction [38]. Such incidents are difficult to categorize and manage within broad legal guidelines, as they frequently intersect with occupational demands and employer management practices. Therefore, protecting healthcare workers’ physical and mental health requires institutionally supported approaches grounded in occupational health and workplace perspectives [39]. To further improve victims’ satisfaction with WPV management, related approaches should be adopted, including the implementation of a standardized hospital-wide response protocols with strict resolution timeframes; the establishment of independent oversight committees for case review; ensuring adequate rest and recovery time for victims; providing professional psychological support and therapeutic services; implementing standardized training on gender-based violence dynamics and institutional accountability; and setting up anonymous feedback channels and evaluation mechanisms to monitor and improve response effectiveness [40].

The consistent high perception of security measures as being in place underscores the importance that healthcare workers place on physical safety. This suggests that the hospital’s efforts in this area, such as increased security personnel, Closed Circuit Television Camera surveillance, and access control, are being recognized and appreciated by its female workforce. The monotonic increase in the perception effectiveness of public access control and check-in procedures for staff may be related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite the positive trends, the relatively low perception of check-in procedures and human resource development measures highlights where the hospital needs to work. Encouraging open communication and feedback from healthcare workers can help identify additional concerns or suggestions for improvement.

In China, gender equality issues have been increasingly prioritized. The relative domestic efforts resonate with global commitments to SDGs, specifically SDG 5 and SDG 8. This alignment is demonstrated through a robust legal framework. China ratified the UN CEDAW in September 1980, becoming one of the early adopters of the convention [41], signaling an early commitment to principles now central to the SDGs. Laws such as the Labor Law [42], the Labor Contract Law [43], and the Law on the Protection of Women’s Rights and Interests [44] explicitly grant women equal rights with men, prohibit sexual harassment, and stipulate legal responsibilities for infringing on women’s rights, directly operationalizing SDG 5's targets. China has also established labor protection regulations for female workers, including provisions for menstruation, pregnancy, childbirth, and lactation [45], which support women's continued participation in the workforce, a key aspect of SDG 8. On the other hand, in the area of occupational health protection for medical personnel, China has also made progress, reflecting SDG 8's mandate for safe and secure working environments. In terms of policy, national standards now include healthcare worker protection, with policies addressing occupational hazards, improving remuneration, and enhancing safety measures [45]. The Healthy China strategy [46] and COVID-19 response policies [47] further emphasize the protection and care of healthcare workers. Legislative achievements in China have fortified the occupational protection of healthcare workers. The Mental Health Law underscores the importance of safeguarding the well-being of mental health professionals [48]. Meanwhile, the Civil Code plays a pivotal role in curbing workplace violence and harassment against medical staff [49]. The Basic Medical and Health Care and Promotion Law build upon these protections by offering detailed guidelines for the occupational health of healthcare workers [50]. Lastly, the Doctor Law solidifies the rights of doctors to a secure and respectful work environment [51]. Society-wide efforts, involving public security, credit systems, and academic research, are driving the development and enforcement of policies against workplace violence in medical institutions [52]. These national-level initiatives may systematically influence the awareness and management practices of local hospitals, leading to a decrease in workplace violence and enhanced protection of the rights of female workers.

While China has made strides in enhancing security measures and training to maintain medical order, there is a notable gap in the legislative approach to workplace violence in medical institutions [53]. The consistent high perception of security measures and the persistent dissatisfaction with institutional handling of WPV incidents indicate that the current measures primarily focus on increasing security forces, adopting a public security perspective rather than a workplace-specific one. This approach overlooks the need for a more comprehensive occupational health and safety framework, which includes approving relevant conventions and managing risks from occupational hazards. The ILO conventions and recommendations on violence and harassment offer a scientific evidence-based approach that aligns with international labor standards [54]. They present a unified concept of violence and harassment, clarify the responsibilities of all stakeholders, and advocate for inclusive, systematic, and gender-equal preventive measures. Compared to 45 countries and regions that have made legislative progress [55], China’s legislation lacks a consistent definition of workplace violence, specific laws, and supporting technical standards. The legal system has yet to fully integrate the occupational diseases caused by psychological violence into its scope and the China's National Classification and List of Occupational Diseases.

Building on these findings, future research should implement multi-center longitudinal designs to track violence trends across diverse healthcare settings, establishing causal links between interventions and outcomes while enhancing generalizability. Understanding long-term trends and the efficacy of interventions across diverse settings will be essential. Moreover, while general interventions are necessary, future studies should prioritize developing targeted measures addressing specific types of violence, such as sexual harassment and ethnic discrimination, leveraging our observed unique dynamics and their improvement in satisfaction levels [56]. Additionally, addressing psychological concerns is paramount, necessitating embedded mental health support systems and fostering a reporting culture free from stigma [30]. Further investigation of systemic documentation barriers through mixed-methods approaches will optimize incident tracking. These directions respond directly to the limitations of single-site data while addressing critical gaps in subtype-specific management and psychological recovery, ensuring continuous improvement of prevention strategies for female healthcare workers.

To our knowledge, this is the first multi-year cross-sectional study on workplace violence in hospitals in China, and one of the few studies that research workplace violence from a female perspective. As a comprehensive pilot institution for managing workplace violence, the hospital under study is highly representative of similar-tier hospitals in China, thereby allowing the observed outcomes to reflect, to some extent, the potential effectiveness of integrated WPV interventions in comparable settings. This study’s strengths lie in its pioneering approach, longitudinal design spanning four waves of data collection (2018–2022), use of a standardized instrument (the ILO/WHO questionnaire), and a large sample size. These features enable a comprehensive analysis of trends and contributing factors related to WPV, yielding policy-relevant insights and evidence for preventive strategies in healthcare environments.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the voluntary nature of participation and hospital staffing changes resulted in varying demographic profiles across waves. While regression adjustments accounted for key confounders, unmeasured factors may bias trend estimates: the decline in WPV might be overestimated as later samples included some more lower-risk groups especially participants with less concern about WPV. However, given the high response rates, the declining level of WPV concern itself also reflects organizational trends and WPV prevention improvement within the hospital. Notably, despite fluctuations in the composition of other demographic characteristics over the four years, WPV incidence consistently decreased. This suggests these patterns may be systematic. Future studies should track longitudinal cohorts to disentangle true temporal changes from compositional effects. Second, the use of self-reported questionnaires may have introduced recall and reporting bias. Particularly, perception bias—shaped by evolving institutional responses and individual tolerance to WPV—could have influenced both the recognition and reporting of incidents. Low satisfaction with institutional handling of WPV incidents could have discouraged reporting, persistently low satisfaction could depress reporting and bias the observed prevalence downward, potentially exaggerating the observed decline. However, the high response rates across all survey periods help to constrain the potential scale of this bias. Furthermore, the improvement in satisfaction in later years would be expected to have the opposite effect—encouraging more reporting, meaning the recent decline may be a conservative estimate of the true improvement. Thus, while present, the influence of this bias on the overall trend is likely limited. Third, while this was a pilot study conducted at a single hospital in Henan Province, its design limits the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings across China. Forth, the cross-sectional nature of each wave precludes causal inference about factors driving WPV trends. In addition, the absence of data from 2020, due to pandemic-related disruptions, limits the continuity of trend analysis. This gap may obscure short-term fluctuations in WPV incidence that could have been associated with extreme stress during the early COVID-19 period. Consequently, the observed decline should be interpreted as a smoothed trend across non-consecutive years rather than a continuous four-year trajectory.

Conclusion

The four-year longitudinal study at a central Chinese hospital reveals a significant decline in the prevalence of workplace violence against female healthcare workers, strongly associated with the implementation of comprehensive, gender-sensitive interventions. Despite this progress, persistent challenges—including low satisfaction with incident handling and the barriers to reporting—highlight the gap between policy implementation and staff perception. The findings emphasize the need for continuous monitoring, improvement of reporting mechanisms and refinement of incident management procedures. Importantly, the study underscores the urgent necessity of nationwide, multi-center research to validate these associations across diverse settings, capture broader epidemiological trends, and build a robust evidence base for effective policymaking. To sustain this progress, institutional reforms must prioritize gender-sensitive occupational health policies that address the specific needs of female healthcare workers, ultimately fostering a safe, equitable, and supportive professional environment. To truly safeguard female healthcare workers, nationwide multi-center studies and institutional reforms advancing gender-sensitive occupational health policies are urgently needed.
