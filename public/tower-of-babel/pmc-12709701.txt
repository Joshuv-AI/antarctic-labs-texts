Preliminary evaluation of oxyntomodulin as a biomarker for metabolic risk stratification in adults with obesity
Authors: Mateusz Zwierz, Angelika Buczyńska, Maria Kościuszko, Katarzyna Sobieska, Agnieszka Adamska, Katarzyna Siewko, Adam Jacek Krętowski, Anna Popławska-Kita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxyntomodulin (OXM), secreted by enteroendocrine L-cells, regulates energy and glucose homeostasis and is a dual agonist of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucagon receptors (GCGRs), with anorexigenic, insulinotropic, and energy expenditure–enhancing effects. While synthetic analogues of OXM are under investigation as potential anti-obesity therapies, the physiological role and clinical significance of endogenous OXM in human obesity remain incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to evaluate fasting OXM levels and assess their association with anthropometric and biochemical parameters in individuals with varying degrees of excess weight.MethodsThe cross-sectional study included overweight (O1, n = 88) and obese (O2, n = 134) individuals, as well as a healthy control group (CG, n = 39). Body composition was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Biochemical and hormonal analyses encompassed glucose-insulin homeostasis and lipid profile, with a focus on fasting OXM concentration. Insulin resistance (IR) and β-cell function were evaluated using validated indices.ResultsFasting OXM levels were significantly elevated in the O2 compared to both the O1 and CG (p < 0.0001). OXM positively correlated with several anthropometric parameters, including visceral adipose tissue (VAT) mass and volume in O2. Logistic regression showed that higher OXM was significantly associated with the risk of obesity. Additionally, an increase in the risk of IR and related metabolic complications was observed with an increase in OXM levels, mainly in advanced stages of weight gain.ConclusionsThese findings provide preliminary evidence that OXM constitutes a potential biomarker of visceral adiposity and early metabolic dysregulation, serving as a measurable indicator of IR in obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07479-y.Keywords: Oxyntomodulin, Obesity, Insulin resistance, Visceral adipose tissue, Incretin hormones, Metabolic biomarkers

Introduction

Overweight and obesity currently constitute one of the most significant epidemiological challenges worldwide. According to projections from the World Obesity Atlas 2025, by 2030, nearly half of the adult population will have a body mass index (BMI) above 25 kg/m², contributing to a growing burden of chronic, non-communicable diseases [1, 2]. Excess body weight, particularly the accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (VAT), is a key driver of metabolic complications such as insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), dyslipidemia, hypertension, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). These conditions collectively impair quality of life, increase mortality risk [3], and generate escalating healthcare expenditures [4, 5]. Given the heterogeneity of obesity phenotypes and their variable metabolic risk, there is an urgent need to identify sensitive and specific biomarkers that enable early stratification of individuals at high risk for obesity-related complications. Such biomarkers may improve clinical decision-making, guide the personalization of interventions, and contribute to more effective prevention strategies.

Oxyntomodulin (OXM), a gut-derived peptide secreted by intestinal L-cells, has emerged as a potential candidate in this context. Derived from the preproglucagon gene, OXM acts as a dual agonist of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucagon receptors (GCGRs), enabling simultaneous regulation of appetite, glucose metabolism, and energy expenditure [3, 4]. While synthetic OXM analogues are currently under investigation as promising pharmacological treatments for obesity and T2DM [5], the physiological role and regulatory patterns of endogenous OXM secretion in humans remain poorly understood.

Previous studies examining circulating OXM levels in individuals with obesity were limited in number and generally did not include detailed assessments of body composition or fat distribution, which are key determinants of metabolic risk. Moreover, despite the central role of IR in obesity-related complications, there is currently no established biochemical marker that reliably reflects its presence or severity. In this context, the present work represents a preliminary study designed to investigate the potential role of OXM as a biomarker of metabolic disturbances in individuals with excess body weight and to evaluate its utility in predicting the risk of obesity-related complications. Specifically, we examined the associations between fasting OXM concentrations, IR indices, and body composition parameters assessed dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). This study provides initial evidence supporting the potential of OXM as an early indicator for identifying individuals at increased metabolic risk.

Materials and methods

Study design

A total of 350 patients were screened for this study, and after excluding individuals with comorbidities, particularly diabetes, dyslipidemia, and autoimmune disorders, 261 participants were consecutively enrolled based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. A thorough medical history review and physical examination ensured eligibility. Exclusion criteria encompassed smoking, alcohol consumption, taking medications that could affect carbohydrate metabolism (both hyper- and hypoglycemic agents), immunosuppressive drugs, or other agents potentially interfering with oxidative stress parameters. Furthermore, individuals with comorbidities that could influence the assessed metabolic outcomes were excluded. These criteria were verified on the basis of detailed medical history, physical examination, and review of medical records. Recruitment was conducted among clinic patients. The participants in this study were of Caucasian ethnicity and resided in the Podlaskie Voivodeship region of northeastern Poland. The final cohort was stratified into three groups according to BMI: 88 overweight individuals (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²; O1 group; 66 females, 22 males), 134 obese individuals (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²; O2 group; 85 females, 49 males), and 39 normal-weight healthy controls (BMI < 25 kg/m²; CG; 37 females, 2 males). BMI was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m) and was used as a standardized metric for classifying participants into weight-related categories.

Venous blood samples (5.5 mL) were collected from each clinical trial subject in the fasting state, subjected to centrifugation, and subsequently aliquoted. Samples were stored at -80°C until enrollment was completed, and laboratory analysis began. To investigate glucose metabolism, all participants underwent a standardized 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Blood glucose and insulin levels were measured at five predefined time points: baseline (0’), 60, 120, 180, and 240 min following oral administration of a glucose solution. This protocol facilitated a dynamic assessment of glycemic and insulin responses, enabling a comprehensive comparison of metabolic profiles across the study groups. Key biochemical parameters, including metabolic and hormonal indicators, were determined from fasting samples only.

Body composition assessment

Although BMI was applied as the primary criterion for group stratification, our study design also incorporated the broader perspective of Clinical Obesity by integrating advanced body composition assessments [6]. In order to capture metabolic risk more comprehensively, all participants underwent a comprehensive body composition assessment using both DXA and BIA. Anthropometric assessments included measurements of body weight, fat mass, muscle mass, total body water (TBW), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), body fat percentage (% body fat), as well as android and gynoid fat mass. DXA was performed with a Lunar iDXA (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA), providing measurements of lean mass, fat mass, bone mass, and VAT. BIA was conducted with an InBody 220 analyzer (Biospace, Seoul, Korea), yielding data on body weight, total body water (TBW), fat mass, skeletal muscle mass, body mass index (BMI), and resting metabolic rate (RMR). Device calibration was carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. All assessments were conducted under standardized conditions: in the morning, in a fasting state, after bladder emptying, and with hydration status controlled. The operator was not blinded to participants’ group allocation, as blinding was not feasible due to stratification based on BMI. These parameters provide a more refined characterization of obesity phenotypes and metabolic risk, consistent with the framework of Clinical Obesity, and allow interpretation of OXM levels not only in relation to body mass but also to fat distribution and visceral adiposity.

Biochemical measurements

Biochemical and hormonal analyses encompassed a broad spectrum of metabolic parameters, categorized into glucose-insulin homeostasis, lipid profile, inflammatory markers, and hormonal regulation, with a particular focus on OXM concentration.

Glucose and insulin metabolism

Long-term glycemic control was assessed by measuring glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) using capillary electrophoresis high-performance liquid chromatography (CE-HPLC) on the Bio-Rad D-10 Dual HbA2/F/A1c platform, ensuring high precision and reliability. Fasting glucose levels were determined enzymatically via a colorimetric method on the Roche C111 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Insulin and C-peptide concentrations were quantified using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (ECLIA) on the Roche E411 platform (Roche Diagnostics, Sussex, UK), a method known for its high sensitivity and specificity in assessing pancreatic function and insulin secretion.

Lipid profile and inflammatory markers

Serum lipid parameters, including total cholesterol (CHOL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and triglycerides (TG), were analyzed using enzymatic colorimetric assays on the Roche C111 device. C-reactive protein (CRP), a key inflammatory marker, was measured using the same method to evaluate systemic inflammation and cardiovascular risk.

Hormonal and endocrine assessments

Endocrine parameters, including cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH vitamin D), were analyzed using ECLIA on the Roche E411 platform, ensuring precise hormonal profiling.

OXM analysis

Venous blood samples were collected into plain serum tubes without the presence of anticoagulants or enzyme inhibitors. Following collection, the blood samples were left to clot for approximately 45 min at room temperature and were then centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the serum was separated. Samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Each aliquot was thawed only once and used for a single biochemical determination to prevent degradation of temperature-sensitive peptides. All biochemical measurements were performed on material that had been freshly thawed within six months of storage, a period that does not result in significant degradation of most metabolic biomarkers.

OXM concentrations were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with a commercial kit (ELISA Kit for Oxyntomodulin), Product No. CEV381Hu, Cloud-Clone Corp., Wuhan, China, strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 122 pg/mL with a detection range of 312–20000 pg/mL. According to the manufacturer, no cross-reactivity was observed with glucagon, glicentin, or GLP-1. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was < 10%, and the inter-assay CV was < 12%, confirming the reliability of the measurements.

The integration of CE-HPLC, ECLIA, and enzymatic assays ensured the accuracy and reproducibility of biochemical measurements. All analyses were conducted in a blinded manner within a single analytical run to minimize bias and enhance data robustness.

Calculation of IR indices

To evaluate IR and β-cell function, several well-established indices were calculated. In addition to the classical HOMA-IR index, alternative markers such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, the quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI), and indices based on C-peptide concentrations were also applied. The rationale for employing a panel of indices lies in the fact that different measures capture distinct aspects of insulin resistance (e.g., hepatic versus peripheral) and exhibit variable predictive value in clinical studies. Thus, the combined use of multiple indices provides a more comprehensive and robust assessment of insulin resistance and β-cell function. The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was determined by multiplying fasting glucose concentration (expressed in mg/dL) by fasting insulin level (expressed in µU/mL) and dividing the result by 405. This index provides an estimate of hepatic insulin resistance. The Homeostatic Model Assessment of β-cell Function (HOMA-β) was calculated by multiplying fasting insulin by 360 and then dividing by the difference between fasting glucose and 63. This index reflects pancreatic β-cell function and insulin secretion capacity. The Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI) was derived by taking the reciprocal of the sum of the logarithms of fasting glucose and fasting insulin levels. The Matsuda Index, which evaluates whole-body insulin sensitivity, was calculated by dividing 10.000 by the square root of the product of fasting glucose, fasting insulin, the mean glucose level during the OGTT, and the mean insulin level during the OGTT. The Triglyceride-Glucose Index (TyG) was obtained by taking the natural logarithm of the product of fasting TG and fasting glucose, divided by two. Additionally, the TyG-BMI index was calculated by multiplying the TyG index by BMI, thereby integrating both metabolic and anthropometric parameters into the assessment of IR. The McAuley Index was calculated using an exponential function that incorporates fasting insulin and TG levels. The Fasting Insulin Resistance Index (FIRI) was determined by multiplying fasting glucose by fasting insulin and dividing the result by 25, providing an estimate of IR similar to HOMA-IR. Finally, the Fasting Glucose to Insulin Ratio (FGIR) was obtained by dividing fasting glucose concentration by fasting insulin level.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism for macOS (Version 10.5.0, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean (minimum-maximum) and were derived from 39 participants in the CG, 88 in the O1, and 134 in the O2.

The normality of each dataset was assessed using the Normality and Lognormality Test, with the Shapiro-Wilk test selected as the method to test for Gaussian distribution (significance level α = 0.05). Variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR; 25th − 75th percentile). To evaluate differences across all three study groups (CG, O1, and O2), ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for normally distributed variables. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied as a non-parametric alternative. For pairwise post-hoc comparisons between groups, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for variables meeting the assumption of normality. Post-hoc comparisons following Kruskal-Wallis were performed using Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Additionally, pairwise comparisons between specific groups (CG vs. O1, CG vs. O2, and O1 vs. O2) were conducted. For these comparisons, if both groups exhibited a normal distribution, the parametric t-test was applied. If at least one of the groups did not meet the normality assumption, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To explore the potential of OXM as a marker of IR, a separate logistic regression model was constructed using IR status as the Y. Individuals were classified as IR only if at least four out of the following six objective indices met established cut-off criteria for IR: HOMA-IR, QUICKI, Matsuda Index, McAuley Index, FIRI, and FGIR. This criterion was selected based on sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating that ≥ 3/6 markedly overdiagnoses IR in healthy individuals, whereas ≥ 5/6 results in under-recognition of IR in metabolically at-risk subjects. The ≥ 4/6 threshold exhibited the highest classification stability across groups and showed strong correlation with a continuous composite IR burden score (IRz), derived from standardized direction-aligned IR indices (r = 0.90; Supplementary Table 2). All threshold sensitivity comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Specifically, the following thresholds were applied: HOMA-IR >2.5, QUICKI < 0.35, Matsuda Index < 3.19, McAuley Index < 5.8, FIRI >3.85, and FGIR < 4.5. Participants not meeting these criteria were coded as IR– (0), and those meeting at least four criteria were coded as IR+ (1). The applied cut-off values for all indices were based on established thresholds reported in the literature [7–10].

To assess whether disturbances in OXM levels were associated with an increased likelihood of O1 or O2, a simple logistic regression analysis was performed. Several models were constructed by fitting a binary outcome variable (Y), coded as 0 or 1 depending on group comparison. The primary independent variable in all models was serum OXM concentration. To evaluate the discriminative capacity of OXM, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore potential associations between the examined parameters.

Results

Analysis of body composition measurements in the studied groups

As expected, all analyzed body composition parameters differed significantly across the studied groups, with the most pronounced changes observed in the O2 group. The most substantial alterations were observed in VAT parameters. In the O2 group, VAT mass and volume increased by 624.41% and 624.48%, respectively, relative to the CG (p < 0.0001). Analysis of biochemical parameters in the studied groups

Fasting and post-load concentrations of both glucose and insulin during OGTT were significantly elevated in the O2 group compared to the CG and O1 group at all measured time points (p < 0.0001), except for glucose at 240’. In the O1 group, significant increases were limited to fasting glucose and glucose at 180‘, as well as insulin levels at 0’, 60’, 180’, and 240’, compared to the CG (p < 0.05). Both C-peptide and HbA1c levels were significantly higher in the O2 group compared to both CG and O1 (p < 0.0001), while C-peptide was also elevated in the O1 group relative to CG (p < 0.0001). Analysis of OXM levels in the studied groups

The analysis of OXM levels revealed that a statistically significant increase was observed only in the O2 group, where concentrations were higher compared to both the CG and the O1 group (p < 0.0001; p < 0.0001; respectively). In contrast, no significant differences were noted between the O1 and CG (p = 0.2258) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 4).

Analysis of IR indices in the studied groups

In the O2 group, all analyzed IR indices were significantly altered compared to both CG and O1. Similar but less pronounced differences were also observed in the O1 group compared to CG (p < 0.05), except for the TyG index, which did not differ significantly between these groups. Discussion

Despite the growing interest in the pharmacological applications of OXM, the endogenous dynamics of OXM levels in human obesity remain poorly understood. In the present study, we not only analyzed fasting OXM levels in the O1 and O2 groups, but also conducted a comprehensive assessment of metabolic health, including body composition analysis, evaluation of key biochemical parameters, and estimation of IR indices. The statistically significant increase in OXM concentration observed in the O2 group appears to represent a compensatory response to the complex metabolic disturbances associated with excessive adipose tissue accumulation.

According to existing literature, OXM is expected to exert anorexigenic effects, leading to reduced appetite, weight loss, and increased energy expenditure [3]. However, in our study, individuals in the O2 group showed significantly elevated endogenous OXM levels alongside increased body weight, suggesting that the expected weight-reducing effect may be diminished. Previous reports have proposed receptor resistance [11] or limited OXM bioavailability due to rapid degradation by dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) [12–15] as possible explanations, but our data cannot directly address these mechanisms. Evidence suggests that DPP-IV activity is elevated in obesity, correlating with adipocyte size [16], VAT [17], inflammation, BMI, and metabolic syndrome (MetS) [18, 19], indicating a systemic rather than tissue-limited effect.

Nevertheless, a study by Naserallah et al. found no statistically significant differences in OXM levels between the control group (CG) and the O2 group [20]. Similarly, Wewer Albrechtsen et al. reported that OXM levels in individuals with obesity did not differ significantly from those observed in normal-weight individuals prior to bariatric surgery [21]. The discrepancies between the results of the present study and previous reports may be due to the lack of comprehensive body composition assessments in earlier studies, particularly with regard to VAT content and distribution. The absence of this critical analysis may have contributed to observed heterogeneity among study groups, potentially leading to less reliable findings.

Additionally, while some studies have analyzed patients in terms of body composition and fat distribution, these investigations focused on another incretin hormone – GLP-1 – rather than OXM, which is the central focus of this work. These studies have confirmed correlations between elevated GLP-1 levels and higher prevalence of MetS [22, 23].

Of note, in the current study, no statistically significant differences in fasting OXM concentrations were observed between the O1 and CG. A plausible explanation for this observation may be the disruption of circadian rhythms in incretin hormone secretion in the context of excess adipose tissue, as demonstrated by Muñoz et al. A key finding of that study was the identification of a distinct circadian pattern of GLP-1 secretion in lean individuals – with the highest levels occurring postprandially and the lowest in the early morning. As adiposity increased, this rhythm became increasingly blunted and disrupted. Patients with obesity exhibited minimal diurnal variation in GLP-1 levels, suggesting significant dysfunction in the mechanisms regulating its secretion [22].

In light of our findings, one may hypothesize that the circadian rhythm of OXM secretion – like that of GLP-1 – may be less impaired in the O1 group than in the O2 group. This could explain the lack of statistically significant changes in fasting OXM levels in the O1 group. Nevertheless, the absence of data on circadian fluctuations in OXM secretion represents a major limitation of the present study.

Given the observed alterations in fasting OXM concentrations across the O1 and O2 groups, presumed to reflect adaptations to progressive adiposity, an analysis of body composition was essential to corroborate the extent and nature of fat accumulation in these cohorts. The anticipated increase in body weight and fat mass associated with rising BMI was accompanied by alterations in fat distribution. Although gynoid fat mass remained predominant across all study groups, the relatively greater increase in android fat mass compared to gynoid fat mass appears to have stronger predictive value than the absolute fat mass itself. This finding aligns with the observations reported by Zhou et al., who demonstrated that BMI trajectories are superior predictors of future obesity risk compared to single BMI measurements [24]. This pattern of fat redistribution is particularly relevant, as Vasan et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between android fat mass and VAT with HOMA-IR, impaired fasting glucose, hypertriglyceridemia, and hypertension, highlighting their contribution to elevated metabolic and cardiovascular risk, particularly in the context of T2DM and cardiometabolic disease [25]. In the present study, the increase in android fat mass nearly doubled that of gynoid fat mass, as evidenced by higher WHR values and significant increases in both VAT mass and VAT volume, particularly in the O1 and O2 groups. These findings suggest a shift toward abdominal obesity, which, as demonstrated above, is associated with greater metabolic risk and is considered more detrimental than other fat distribution patterns [26]. Furthermore, the elevated RMR, particularly in the O2 group, indicates an augmented energy demand attributable to the increased VAT which, according to the literature, has a higher metabolic activity compared to subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) [27]. The reported increase in RMR may also be attributable to an increase in muscle mass. Sajjadi et al. showed that overweight and obese women with elevated RMR had significantly higher skeletal muscle mass and fat-free soft tissue mass compared to those with normal or decreased RMR [28]. Similarly, Gitsi et al. demonstrated that an increase in muscle mass leads to an elevated RMR, suggesting a potential role for muscle mass in the process of reducing VAT mass [29]. Furthermore, skeletal muscle is the most modifiable component influencing RMR, with fat-free mass accounting for 60–70% of RMR, of which skeletal muscle contributes approximately 20–30% [30].

Given the unfavorable changes in body composition, it is essential to consider the associated metabolic consequences, which are reflected in the biochemical profile, beginning with glucose-insulin homeostasis. Analysis of the results of the OGTT revealed a stepwise increase in glucose and insulin concentrations from the CG through the O1 group to the O2 group. Abnormal fasting blood glucose (IFG) was observed in both O1 and O2 groups, with the most pronounced abnormalities seen among O2. Notably, IFG is considered a better predictor of the development of overt diabetes than impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), as it reflects hepatic IR [31]. Notably, a significant increase in key IR indices was observed in both O1 and O2 groups, accompanied by elevated fasting insulin, C-peptide, and post-load glucose levels during OGTT, particularly at the 60th and 120th minute, indicating compensatory hyperinsulinemia and increased risk of progression to T2DM. C-peptide, due to its longer half-life and greater stability in circulation, provides a more reliable measure of pancreatic β-cell function than insulin, offering valuable insight into insulin secretion and utilization [32]. Particularly noteworthy in the context of the aforementioned metabolic alterations is the study by Batmunkh et al., which demonstrates a clear association between glycemic status and cardiovascular risk. The authors report that disturbances such as IFG and hyperinsulinemia, even at the pre-diabetic stage, are significantly linked to an elevated risk of cardiovascular complications [33]. In the present study, HbA1c levels in the O2 group, while remaining within the normal range, exhibited a tendency to approach thresholds indicative of prediabetes. This observation suggests a potential for progressive impaired glycemic control and underscores the potential value of HbA1c levels as a risk indicator for the development of T2DM [34].

To gain initial insight into the potential metabolic role of OXM, we performed correlation analyses between OXM concentrations and selected indicators of biochemical parameters and body composition in the studied groups. The results of the analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between OXM and insulin at all time points of the OGTT in both the O1 and O2 groups. However, a notable distinction was observed in the dynamics of these correlations between the study groups. In the O2 group, the highest value of the OXM correlation was recorded at fasting, after which its strength gradually decreased in the subsequent stages of the OGTT test. In contrast, in the O1 group, the initial correlation was lower, reached a maximum at the 120th minute of the OGTT, and then gradually decreased. In O2, characterized by more severe obesity and higher IR, the higher fasting correlation may reflect a chronically activated compensatory mechanism with limited flexibility during glucose challenge, as evidenced by a gradual decrease in correlation during OGTT. In contrast, in O1 the delayed peak in correlation may reflect a more flexible and stimulus-dependent pattern of OXM secretion. Above interpretation is further supported by the consistent correlations of OXM with many IR indices, particularly in the O2 group, suggesting that OXM levels are closely associated with the degree of metabolic impairment in obesity. Its high association with indices of IR, such as HOMA-IR, QUICKI and Matsuda, and smaller association with lipid indices (e.g., TyG, TyG-BMI), suggests a specific role for OXM in assessing the incretin-insulin axis. Importantly, the significant correlations also observed in CG, although lower, may indicate that OXM is sensitive to early, still subclinical metabolic alterations preceding the development of overt IR, which is also important from the point of view of its potential use as a marker. These results are further substantiated by the findings of a rudimentary logistic regression, which demonstrated a statistically significant capacity of OXM to differentiate between subjects with IR. These results suggest that higher OXM levels are associated with increased odds of IR in this population. The dynamics of OXM’s correlation with insulin in the O1 group resemble those described by Carr et al. for GLP-1 in obese patients, who in our classification would be classified as O2. In the Carr et al. study, a delayed response of GLP-1 was observed after glucose administration – its concentrations in the first 180 min of the OGTT were lower than in lean subjects, but there was a compensatory response at a later stage, leading to a leveling off of GLP-1’s total AUC after 300 min. The highest correlation of GLP-1 with insulin in this group occurred at the 180th minute of the OGTT. In our analysis, the correlation of OXM with insulin in the O1 group also increased during the test and reached a maximum at the 120th minute, which may indicate a similar adaptive mechanism in response to glucose load [35]. The underlying mechanism responsible for this discrepancy between the two incretins remains to be elucidated. However, it is noteworthy that GLP-1 and OXM are secreted by the same L cells [36], and are likely released in parallel as part of the integrated secretory activity of these cells, suggesting that the observed differences in results may be attributable to methodological discrepancies. In the present analysis, the O1 group comprised subjects with a BMI ranging from 25 to 29.9 kg/m², while those in the O2 group exhibited a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², with no upper limit. In contrast, the Carr et al. study excluded overweight patients, focusing exclusively on obese subjects with a BMI between 30 and 35 kg/m² (mean 33.8 ± 0.6 kg/m²) [35]. In addition, our sample size was significantly larger (O1: n = 88; O2: n = 134) compared to the 12 patients in the Carr et al. study, which may also have affected the observed dynamics of the hormonal response. A similar pattern of correlations to OXM and IR rates was observed between OXM and C-peptide, further confirming the association of OXM with pancreatic β-cell function and compensatory mechanisms under IR. Correlations were statistically significant in all groups, reaching the highest strength in the O2 group, which may reflect the enhanced secretion of proinsulin in response to progressive IR and the potential role of OXM in supporting this process. The present findings are consistent with those of other researchers, who demonstrated that higher GLP-1 concentrations in children and adolescents were correlated with higher C-peptide levels, regardless of BMI [37]. Analysis of OXM’s correlation with OGTT glucose showed significant discrepancies compared to OXM’s correlation with insulin. A statistically significant positive correlation between OXM and glucose was only observed in O1 at the 120th minute and in O2 at the 60th, 120th, and 180th minutes. These findings suggest that, under conditions of advanced obesity, compensatory hyperinsulinemia no longer provides effective regulation of glycemia due to reduced cellular sensitivity to metabolic hormones and reduced ability of insulin to efficiently transport glucose into cells [38], leading to persistence of hyperglycemia despite increased insulin secretion. This mechanism is reflected in the O2 group in the form of a positive correlation of OXM with HbA1c. The lack of statistically significant correlations between OXM and HbA1c in the CG and O1 group may be attributed to the lower degree of IR in these populations, as reflected by lower median HOMA-IR values, which allows for partial maintenance of glucose metabolism without the need for compensatory upregulation of the incretin axis and OXM secretion. A similar set of relationships was observed by Stinson et al. when they analyzed the effect of GLP-1 on glycemic control in children and adolescents with BMI >90th percentile. The authors of the study noted a positive correlation between higher fasting GLP-1 levels and an increase in HbA1c. Furthermore, patients from the obesity clinic exhibited elevated GLP-1 levels in comparison to the CG, which is consistent with our observations of augmented OXM levels in O2. A notable observation from the Stinson et al. study is the presence of a correlation between GLP-1 and HbA1c in both obese and normal-weight children [37]. These findings suggest that the association between GLP-1 and HbA1c in the child and adolescent population may be independent of BMI and due to other mechanisms regulating glucose metabolism. However, a notable observation from our study of adult patients is the absence of a significant correlation between OXM and HbA1c in CG and O1, suggesting potential variations attributable to the age of the study populations, the severity of metabolic disorders, or distinct regulatory mechanisms. According to the findings of other researchers, there is a possibility that GLP-1 secretion may be impaired in states of chronic hyperglycemia [39]. These findings underscore the intricate dynamics between incretins, insulin, and markers of long-term glycemic control, while underscoring the limited efficacy of compensatory hormonal mechanisms in the O2. In summary, the observed correlations between OXM and insulin, C-peptide, and IR indicators suggest that OXM may serve as an early biomarker of incretin-insulin axis dysfunction and help stratify metabolic risk among obese individuals.

To further explore the role of OXM beyond biochemical parameters, its relationship with body composition was also examined. A notable finding was the significant positive correlation between OXM concentrations and both VAT mass and VAT volume in the O2 group. This correlation may indicate that the increase in OXM concentration in the presence of greater VAT signifies an adaptive response of the body to the developing IR. Notably, the positive correlation of OXM with RMR suggests that the increased concentration of this hormone may serve as the body’s compensatory mechanism to counteract further fat accumulation by regulating energy homeostasis. However, in the context of obesity, this mechanism appears to be ineffective, which may contribute to the persistent progression of the disease. The correlation of OXM with VAT mass and VAT volume is not an isolated phenomenon but is part of a broader context of fat distribution, as reflected by the positive correlation of OXM with both android fat mass and gynoid fat mass, particularly in the O2 group. Although previous analyses have shown that the growth rate of android fat mass surpassed that of gynoid fat mass, the positive correlation of OXM with both obesity phenotypes at almost equivalent levels indicates that OXM is not exclusively associated with VAT. Rather, it may also be associated with the less metabolically detrimental SAT. Nevertheless, the most significant limitation of this analysis is the absence of direct SAT determinations, which precludes the capacity to accurately compare OXM’s correlation with VAT and SAT.

The findings presented herein constitute a significant contribution to the field, as the extant literature is devoid of reports on the correlation between incretins, such as GLP-1 or OXM, and body composition parameters. Previously, only low levels of the anti-inflammatory adiponectin, combined with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory leptin, mediators secreted by adipocytes, have been considered a predisposing factor for obesity-related complications [40]. The observed correlations indicate the potential value of OXM as a biomarker of increased VAT, which is a key determinant of metabolic risk. While its association with RMR suggests a possible compensatory role, its limited effectiveness in counteracting fat accumulation supports its utility as a diagnostic indicator rather than a therapeutic target.

Preliminary, univariable analyses using simple logistic regression with OXM as the sole predictor, followed by ROC curve assessment, suggested limited and non-significant discriminative performance of OXM between CG and O1. In contrast, discrimination between CG and O2 was higher (AUC = 0.8109), and OXM also significantly differentiated O1 from O2, consistent with progressively higher OXM levels with increasing excess weight and a more advanced metabolic disturbance profile. These findings arise from univariable models that did not adjust for key confounders; therefore, the independent predictive value of OXM cannot be established here. Definitive evaluation of OXM’s diagnostic utility for metabolic-risk stratification will require rigorously adjusted multivariable logistic regression models and internal validation, and sensitivity assessments.

In conclusion, elevated OXM levels in the O2 group and their positive correlation with VAT mass and volume provide preliminary evidence for its potential as a biomarker of visceral adiposity and related metabolic risk. ROC analysis further suggests a discriminative capacity, particularly in identifying individuals with advanced metabolic disturbances. OXM’s associations with insulin OGTT, C-peptide, and IR indicators suggest its utility as an early marker of incretin-insulin axis dysfunction. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that elevated OXM levels may help stratify populations at increased metabolic risk and could assist in the early identification of individuals who might benefit from preventive interventions against obesity-related complications. We recommend further studies, particularly of a longitudinal and multivariable nature, to determine whether fluctuations in OXM levels precede clinical deterioration or merely reflect current metabolic status. Elucidation of this temporal relationship is imperative to substantiate OXM’s value as a predictive biomarker and to guide its potential implementation in preventive strategies or early interventions.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that fasting OXM levels were significantly elevated in O2 compared to CG. However, despite this increase, circulating OXM concentrations remained markedly below the thresholds required for activation of GLP-1R and GCGR, suggesting limited functional efficacy. The absence of significant differences in OXM levels in the O1 group may reflect an earlier stage of metabolic dysregulation, possibly associated with less pronounced disruption of its circadian secretion rhythm, similarly described for GLP-1. As BMI increased, we observed a marked shift toward abdominal obesity, with significant increases in android fat, VAT, and WHR. These changes in body composition were paralleled by deteriorating glucose metabolism, particularly in the O2 group, which approached HbA1c levels indicative of prediabetes. OXM showed strong positive correlations with VAT mass and android and gynoid fat distribution, providing preliminary evidence for its potential role as a marker of visceral adiposity and metabolic risk. Preliminary univariable logistic regression analysis suggested a relatively high discriminative power of OXM in differentiating individuals from the O2 group from those in the CG (AUC = 0.81), as well as distinguishing between the O1 and O2 groups. More importantly, simple logistic regression indicated that OXM levels were a statistically significant predictor of IR status (p < 0.001), supporting the hypothesis of an increased risk of IR with rising OXM concentrations. This finding suggests that OXM is not merely a passive correlate but may reflect active metabolic dysregulation associated with IR. Nonetheless, its concurrent associations with visceral adiposity and glycemic indices further support the notion that OXM may function as an indicator of broader metabolic disturbances rather than a direct causal agent. In this context, OXM emerges as a potential biochemical marker associated with IR, a role for which no specific, routinely used marker currently exists. From a clinical standpoint, these findings suggest that therapeutic modulation of OXM signaling may have limited efficacy when applied in isolation, particularly in the context of coexisting IR. It remains to be elucidated whether concurrent improvement in insulin sensitivity, achieved through pharmacological agents such as metformin, could improve the physiological responsiveness to OXM or lead to changes in its circulating levels, thereby increasing its relevance as a potential clinical biomarker. Summarizing, our findings provide preliminary support for the potential utility of OXM not as a direct therapeutic target, but as a secondary biomarker reflecting visceral adiposity and early metabolic dysregulation in individuals at increased risk of obesity-related complications, filling an important gap as a measurable biochemical indicator linked to IR.

Study limitations

Several significant limitations were identified in the analyzed study, which may affect the interpretation and generalizability of the results. The cross-sectional design is a significant limitation, as it prevents assessment of causality and the analysis of changes in OXM levels over time in response to obesity progression or therapeutic interventions; prospective longitudinal studies are necessary to address this issue.

Additionally, the lack of data on the circadian secretion of OXM is a considerable limitation, as the daily rhythms of incretin secretion can influence OXM levels at different times of the day, potentially better explaining the observed differences between groups. Acquiring such data in future research projects could enable a deeper understanding of the relationship between OXM’s circadian rhythm and body mass.

Moreover, in the context of OXM measurement, it should be emphasized that cross-validation with mass spectrometry was not performed. Although ELISA provides adequate sensitivity and specificity for large-scale studies, mass spectrometry in future studies could strengthen the methodological robustness and reproducibility of results, and such an approach may represent a more reference-standard method for OXM quantification.

Furthermore, the absence of direct measurements of SAT represents an important methodological constraint, limiting the ability to fully analyze the relationship between OXM and different types of adipose tissue. This complicates distinguishing whether OXM correlations primarily relate to VAT or also involve subcutaneous fat.

In future studies, it is recommended to construct multivariable regression models with adjustment for potential confounders, as well as to incorporate alternative indices such as WHR into the analyses. Examining their independent and combined effects on OXM levels would not only strengthen the robustness of the findings but also be consistent with the framework of Clinical Obesity.

Regarding the assessment of insulin resistance, the study relied on surrogate indices derived from OGTT rather than the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp, which remains the gold standard for quantifying insulin sensitivity. In future studies, complementary approaches could be considered, including the use of dichotomized thresholds to improve clinical applicability, latent class analysis to capture heterogeneity of IR phenotypes, and, where feasible, the application of the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp to provide a reference-standard validation. Such strategies may harness more information and better reflect the physiological spectrum of IR.

Finally, despite a relatively large sample size, further research involving larger and more demographically balanced populations is necessary to confirm the observed associations and determine their generalizability. In the present study, the sex distribution among groups was unequal, with a preponderance of females in the control group and a more balanced composition in the overweight and obese groups. Although this finding aligns with the natural demographic patterns of patients presenting to diabetology and endocrinology clinics, it may introduce potential confounding and limit the generalizability of the findings.
