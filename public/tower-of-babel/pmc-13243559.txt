Decoding the therapeutic promise of Solanum trilobatum in oral submucous fibrosis: a network pharmacology, docking, and bioactivity study
Authors: A. Murali Balasubramaniam, Saranya Ramsridhar, Joe Rex Brilient, Rajkumari Sriraman, M. N. Anusha, Chandini Rajkumar, Vishnu Priya Veeraraghavan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic, progressive, and potentially malignant disorder with limited therapeutic options. This study explored the therapeutic potential of Solanum trilobatum (S.trilobatum) using a network pharmacology and molecular docking approach. 5 phytochemicals from S. trilobatum were identified with favorable drug-likeness, good gastrointestinal absorption, and low predicted toxicity. Target analysis for 5 phytochemicals revealed 24 potential targets associated with OSF. The 24 targets were enriched in various pathways, and PPI analysis of the targets using MCODE identified six hub genes, namely PLG, ABCB1, PTGS2, HMGCR, NOS2, and MAPK3. Druggability analysis further confirmed that the identified hub genes could serve as potential therapeutic targets. ProteomicsDB analysis revealed the expression of the hub genes in the oral cavity. Docking confirmed that Solasodine exhibited significant binding affinities with the six targets, comparable to or stronger than the control drug dexamethasone, thereby suggesting their multitargeting potential. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation (100 ns) was performed for Solasodine, as it exhibited the highest binding affinity across all six targets, and the simulation confirmed the formation of a stable ligand–protein complex. The bioactivity analysis further confirmed the therapeutic potential of the phytochemicals. This study provides preliminary evidence that S. trilobatum phytochemicals may function as multitarget modulators in OSF through the regulation of key genes. In vitro assays demonstrated that S. trilobatum leaf extract exhibited dose-dependent antioxidant (DPPH and H2O2) and anti-inflammatory activities, with the highest activity observed at 500 µg/mL. These findings highlight the potential of S. trilobatum as a promising candidate for OSF therapy, but warranting further experimental studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46122-8.

Introduction

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSF) is a chronic, progressive, collagen-related disorder of the oral mucosa strongly linked to areca nut use, in which juxta-epithelial inflammation, increased collagen synthesis and cross-linking, and reduced collagen degradation drive fibrotic remodeling and functional impairment, with a recognized potential for malignant transformation to oral squamous cell carcinoma1–4. OSF pathobiology reflects persistent chemical and mechanical irritation from betel quid constituents, notably the areca alkaloid arecoline and flavonoids, which stimulate fibroblasts, alter collagen metabolism, and, together with copper-mediated upregulation of lysyl oxidase, promote excessive and stabilized collagen deposition in the submucosa5,6. Despite symptomatic therapies, effective disease-modifying strategies that target the multi-factorial inflammatory–fibrotic–oncogenic network in OSF remain limited, underscoring the need for multi-target interventions grounded in mechanistic insight7.

Medicinal plants have long been a valuable source of bioactive compounds for managing complex diseases8,9. Among these, S. trilobatum a traditional medicinal herb widely used in Indian systems of medicine has attracted attention for its diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and antifibrotic activities10–12. An investigation demonstrated antimicrobial activity of S.trilobatum against bacterial and fungal pathogens, along with anticancer activity against the HT-29 human colon cancer cell line. These findings emphasize its potential as a valuable source of bioactive compounds with therapeutic applications11. However, its therapeutic potential against OSF remains largely unexplored.

Network pharmacology offers insights into how medicinal plants with multiple active compounds act on several targets and pathways simultaneously, shifting from the traditional ‘one drug–one target’ model to a ‘multi-target–multi-effect’ strategy13–15. Applying this approach to S. trilobatum in the context of OSF is poised to elucidate plausible anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-oncogenic mechanisms, thereby informing rational multi-target interventions and accelerating the translation of traditional knowledge into mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we applied a network pharmacology approach to investigate the multitargeting potential of S. trilobatum against OSF systematically. By integrating phytochemical–target interactions, disease-associated gene networks, and pathway enrichment analyses, we aimed to uncover the molecular mechanisms through which S. trilobatum may exert therapeutic effects. The findings are expected to shed light on its role as a potential anti-fibrotic agent and contribute to the development of plant-derived multitarget therapeutics for OSF management.

Materials and methods

Phytochemicals retrieval

The phytochemicals of S. trilobatum were retrieved from the IMPPAT database (https://cb.imsc.res.in/imppat/), and duplicate entries were removed. The 2D structures, 3D conformations, and SMILES notations of the compounds were obtained from the PubChem database, while the 3D structures of the compounds unavailable in PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) were excluded from the study.

Drug-likeness, oral bioavailability, and pharmacokinetics prediction

We evaluated the drug-likeness and ADME profiles of the phytochemicals using the SwissADME web server (http://www.swissadme.ch/)16. Drug-likeness was analysed by Lipinski’s Rule of Five: molecular weight ≤ 500 Da, logP ≤ 5, no more than 5 hydrogen-bond donors, and no more than 10 hydrogen-bond acceptors. Compounds meeting these criteria were considered likely to have good oral bioavailability. Oral bioavailability was assessed using the Bioavailability Radar plot, which visually represents the key physicochemical parameters for each compound. Compounds whose property values fell entirely within the plot’s pink region were considered to have a favorable oral bioavailability profile. Furthermore, lipophilicity (WLOGP) and topological polar surface area (TPSA) were analyzed using the BOILED-Egg graphical model, which provides insights into drug bioavailability by illustrating P-glycoprotein (P-gp) interactions. We also assessed key pharmacokinetic features such as gastrointestinal absorption, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate status, and inhibition of major cytochrome P450 enzymes—providing insights into the overall pharmacological potential of the screened phytochemicals.

Toxicity analysis

The toxicity of Phytochemicals was predicted using the pkCSM web server (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/)17. The evaluation included AMES mutagenicity, human maximum tolerated dose, hERG I and II inhibition, and hepatotoxicity, providing insight into each compound’s safety for potential therapeutic use.

Bioactive compound target prediction

Potential protein targets for the selected phytochemicals were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction web server (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/)18. The canonical SMILES of compounds retrieved from the PubChem database were submitted to SwissTargetPrediction for target identification. This database serves as a reliable resource for identifying probable protein targets that may interact with the bioactive compounds.

OSF target retrieval

Genes associated with OSF were retrieved from two comprehensive databases, GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/)19 and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/)20, using the keyword ‘Oral Submucous Fibrosis.

Bioactive compound-OSF target network construction

To identify potential targets shared between the phytochemicals and OSF, a compound–OSf –target analysis was performed. The predicted targets of the phytochemicals were compared with OSF-associated genes, and the intersection between the two sets was visualized using a Venn diagram (https://www.interactivenn.net/)21. This approach enabled the identification of common targets, representing potential therapeutic genes for the management of OSF.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) and hub gene identification

PPI analysis was performed using the STRING database(https://string-db.org/), with the species set to Homo sapiens and the minimum interaction score set at 0.4. The resulting PPI network was generated and visualized using Cytoscape Version 3.10.322. The MCODE (Molecular Complex Detection) plugin in Cytoscape was applied to identify densely connected gene clusters within the PPI network. The analysis was performed with the parameters set as follows: node score cutoff = 0.2, k-core = 2, and maximum depth = 100. This approach enabled the extraction of core modules, which likely represent functional gene groups within the network.

Enrichment analysis

g: Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/) webserver was used to perform enrichment analysis of the common targets identified from the Venn diagram23. Pathway and process enrichment analyses were carried out for the overlapping genes, which included Gene Ontology (GO) domains—biological process (BP), cellular component(CC), and molecular function (MF)—as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways24–26.

Insights from proteomic databases

The expression profiles of the selected target proteins were assessed across various tissues using the ProteomicsDB database(https://www.proteomicsdb.org/)27. This analysis helps in assessing the distribution and potential biological relevance of the targets in different physiological contexts.

Druggability analysis

The druggability of the selected hub genes was evaluated using the Drug–Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb)(https://dgidb.org/)28. This analysis offers a comprehensive approach to evaluate the druggable potential of the hub genes.

Molecular docking

The 3D structures of the phytochemicals were retrieved from the PubChem database, while the 3D structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein structures were prepared using Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298 by removing water molecules and co-crystallized ligands, followed by the addition of polar hydrogens. The XYZ coordinates of the binding site were defined based on the co-crystallized ligand for docking studies. Molecular docking was carried out using PyRx software, with the grid box dimensions set according to the existing co-crystallized binding site. For each ligand, ten docking conformations were generated, and the conformation with the best binding affinity was selected and further analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298.

MD simulations

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS29. Nowadays, MD simulations are among the most widely used techniques in bioinformatics to assess structural stability. The protein topology was generated using AMBER99SB-ILDN forcefield in GROMACS, and the ligand topology was generated through ACPYPE30,31. Both topologies were then complexed and used for MD simulations. The periodic boundary conditions were set as a dodecahedron with a box dimension of 1.0. The box was then solvated using the Transferable Intermolecular Potential 3-Point (TIP3P) water model. Then, as a neutralisation step, we added 0.15 M NaCl to the system. The system has been energy-minimised (EM) by using the steepest algorithm. Then the system was equilibrated for 1 nanosecond at 300 K in the NVT step and 1 bar in the NPT step. The EM, NVT, and NPT steps have been performed using the MD Parameter files from the protein-ligand MD simulation tutorial of Lekumal32. Finally, MD simulations were carried out for 100 ns on the solasodine with HMGCR, MAPK3, PLG, PTGS2, ABCB1, and NOS2 complexes.

Biological activity prediction for leads

The biological activities of the identified lead compounds were predicted using the PASS web server (https://www.way2drug.com/PASSOnline/)33. This tool estimates the probability of a compound exhibiting specific biological effects based on its chemical structure. For this analysis, the canonical SMILES of the lead compounds were submitted to the server, and the predicted activities were used to gain insights into their potential pharmacological properties.

In vitro assays

Extract preparation

Leaves of S. trilobatum were freshly collected, washed thoroughly with tap water, and rinsed with distilled water to remove impurities. The cleaned leaves were shade-dried, ground into a fine powder, and 5 g of the powder was extracted by mixing with 100 mL of sterilized water. The mixture was heated at 80 °C for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate obtained was subsequently lyophilized to yield a dry powdered extract.

DPPH radical scavenging antioxidant assay

The antioxidant potential of the S. trilobatum extract was evaluated using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay34,35. In brief, 1.5 mL of 0.1 mM DPPH solution was combined with varying concentrations of the extract (25, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µg/mL). The mixtures were vortexed and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, after which the reduction in absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) scavenging assay

The antioxidant activity of the extracts was assessed using the hydrogen peroxide scavenging method as described previously36,37. In brief, 0.1 mL of the extract at varying concentrations (25, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µg/mL) was mixed with 3.4 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and 0.6 mL of 40 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and absorbance was recorded at 230 nm. A control containing all reagents without the extract was used as a reference, while ascorbic acid served as the standard. Anti-inflammatory assay

The anti-inflammatory activity of the S. trilobatum extract was assessed using the protein denaturation assay, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) serving as the substrate, as described by Mba et al.38. BSA was chosen because of its sensitivity to heat-induced denaturation. In this assay, 1% BSA solution was mixed with varying concentrations of the extract (25, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µg/mL) and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The mixtures were then heated at 70 °C for 15 min and allowed to cool to room temperature. Absorbance was recorded at 660 nm, using diclofenac sodium as the reference standard. Results and discussion

Phytochemicals retrieval

A total of nine compounds from S. trilobatum were retrieved from the IMPPAT database. After removing duplicates, five compounds were selected for further investigation. The corresponding PubChem IDs and 2D structures of these compounds are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Drug-likeness, oral bioavailability, and pharmacokinetics

All bioactive compounds complied with Lipinski’s rule of five, with only one noted violation. Stearic acid, palmitic acid, oleic acid, and linoleic acid showed a logP value greater than 5, indicating excessive lipophilicity. Despite this, their bioavailability score of 0.85 suggests a high likelihood of oral bioavailability. For solasodine, the consensus logP was 4.69 (< 5), which falls within the acceptable range. However, it was considered to have a single violation due to its relatively high lipophilicity. Its bioavailability score of 0.55 indicates only moderate oral bioavailability. The oral bioavailability of the compounds was analyzed using a radar plot. The pink region of the radar plot represents the optimal range required for oral bioavailability. All the compounds were observed to fall within this region, confirming their predicted oral bioavailability. Flexibility (FLEX) and polarity (POLAR) are critical properties influencing the oral bioavailability of compounds. Flexibility is assessed by the number of rotatable bonds, where compounds with more than 10 rotatable bonds are generally predicted to exhibit low oral bioavailability. Polarity, estimated through the topological polar surface area (TPSA), indicates that compounds with TPSA values between 20 Å2 and 130 Å2 are more likely to possess high oral bioavailability39. Collectively, the analysis indicated that all five compounds are likely to be orally bioavailable (Fig. 1).

Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that all compounds exhibited high gastrointestinal (GI) absorption. Among them, stearic acid, oleic acid, and linoleic acid were predicted to be non-permeant across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Solasodine was also identified as a substrate of P-glycoprotein (P-gp), while the other compounds were not. Additionally, none of the tested compounds were predicted to inhibit major cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, including CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4, indicating a low likelihood of metabolic interference. Toxicity profiling showed that none of the compounds exhibited AMES mutagenicity or hERG I inhibition. Solasodine was predicted to act as an inhibitor of hERG II channels and was also identified as hepatotoxic. Bioactive compound targets

Targets with a probability score of 0.1 or higher were selected for analysis. The number of targets identified for each compound was as follows: Solasodine16, Stearic acid (68), Palmitic acid (82), Oleic acid (94), and Linoleic acid (102), resulting in a total of 362 targets. After removing 197 duplicate entries, 165 unique targets were retained for further analysis. Supplementary Table S2 represents the Number of targets for each compound. Collectively, these five compounds were found to target 165 unique genes, underscoring their extensive interactions and potential therapeutic relevance.

Ni et al. investigated the potential targets of bioactive compounds from Javanica oil emulsion injection (JOEI) and identified 87 targets associated with its five active compounds40. In a similar approach, our study predicted 165 unique targets for five bioactive compounds, indicating a broader spectrum of potential interactions and therapeutic implications.

OSF targets

A total of 956 targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database, while 145 targets were obtained from the CTD database. Upon combining the results from both databases, 1,101 targets were identified. After removing 51 duplicate entries, 1,050 unique targets associated with OSF were retained for further analysis.

Bioactive compound-OSF target network

A Venn diagram was generated to illustrate the overlap between the 165 genes associated with the five compounds and the 1,050 genes linked to OSF (Supplementary File). The analysis revealed 24 overlapping genes, representing the common targets shared between the five compounds and OSF (Fig. 3). These intersecting genes indicate potential molecular targets through which the compounds may exert therapeutic effects on OSF. These results indicate a possible link between the five compounds and OSF, highlighting the need for further studies to explore their therapeutic potential.

The in silico target prediction platforms, such as SwissTargetPrediction and GeneCards may introduce false positives. To minimize this risk and reduce potential bias, a multi-step filtering and cross-validation strategy was employed in the present study. Predicted targets obtained from SwissTargetPrediction were cross-verified with OSF-associated genes curated from GeneCards, and only overlapping targets with strong disease relevance were retained for subsequent analyses.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) and hub gene

The 24 overlapping genes identified from the Venn diagram were further analyzed using the STRING database. A medium confidence threshold of 0.400 was applied to select relevant (PPIs). The resulting network had a PPI enrichment p-value of < 1.0 × 10−16, indicating a highly significant interaction network. Visualization in Cytoscape 3.10.3 revealed 24 nodes connected by 80 edges (Fig. 4a and b). MCODE analysis identified three modules: Module 1 (Fig. 4c) with an MCODE score of 4 (6 nodes, 10 edges), Module 2 (Fig. 4d) with a score of 3.5 (5 nodes, 7 edges), and Module 3 (Fig. 4e) with a score of 3 (3 nodes, 3 edges). Six genes from Module 1, being the most significant, were considered hub genes for further analysis. Modules represent densely connected regions within the PPI network. The hub genes identified from Module 1 were PLG, ABCB1, PTGS2, HMGCR, NOS2, and MAPK3. Enrichment profile

To explore the enrichment and functional annotation of 24 OSF-associated targets, GO and KEGG analyses were carried out using the g: Profiler web server (Fig. 5a and d). The results demonstrated significant enrichment in biological processes such as cellular response to chemical stimuli, cell–cell signaling, and response to chemicals. OSF is primarily associated with chronic exposure to chemical irritants such as arecoline from areca nut, which triggers inflammatory responses and fibroblast activation in oral tissues1. The enrichment in these processes suggests that the predicted targets may participate in cellular signaling events involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrotic tissue remodeling observed in OSF.

Molecular function analysis revealed enrichment in estrogen response element binding, phosphotyrosine residue binding, and signaling receptor binding, which are associated with receptor-mediated signaling pathways that regulate cell proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, key mechanisms contributing to fibrosis in OSF. With respect to cellular components, the enrichment in the cell periphery, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and receptor complexes suggests the involvement of membrane-associated signaling proteins and intracellular protein processing mechanisms that regulate cellular stress responses. KEGG pathway enrichment highlighted pathways such as pathways in cancer, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint signaling in cancer, which are particularly relevant because OSF is considered a potentially malignant disorder with a high risk of transformation into oral squamous cell carcinoma. Collectively, these findings suggest that the identified targets may play a pivotal role in regulating signaling cascades and immune-related mechanisms, underscoring their therapeutic relevance in the pathogenesis and management of OSF.

Expression profile of hubgene

The expression analysis of the six hub genes (PLG, ABCB1, PTGS2, HMGCR, NOS2, and MAPK3) revealed that they are actively expressed in tissues relevant to the oral cavity, including the salivary gland, oral epithelium, and saliva. This suggests that these genes may play a direct role in the local pathophysiology of OSF. The results were visualized as a heatmap, which employs a color-coding scheme to represent the expression levels of these genes across different tissues (Fig. 6).

Druggability

Druggability analysis of the hub genes indicated that all six possess the potential to serve as therapeutic targets. This suggests that modulating these genes could represent a viable strategy for therapeutic intervention. Supplementary Table S3 summarizes the interactions between the hub genes and various drugs, showing that each gene interacts with a diverse range of compounds, further emphasizing their potential as targets for drug development.

PLG has emerged as a potential therapeutic target due to its role in tissue remodeling and extracellular matrix regulation. ABCB1 is increasingly recognized as a key target for improving drug delivery and overcoming chemoresistance. PTGS2 has long been identified as a critical target for controlling inflammation and fibrotic processes. HMGCR has been highlighted as a promising target for modulating lipid-mediated signaling pathways involved in fibrosis. NOS2 is considered an important therapeutic target for mitigating oxidative stress and inflammatory damage. MAPK3 has been acknowledged as a vital target for regulating cell proliferation and fibrotic signaling. Collectively, these hub genes represent promising candidates for therapeutic intervention in OSF.

MD simulations

To further validate the docking results and assess the dynamic stability of the ligand–protein interactions, a 100ns MD simulation was performed for the Solasodine–target protein complex, as Solasodine demonstrated the highest binding affinity across all six targets in the docking analysis. MD simulations were performed to evaluate the structural stability and dynamic behaviour of the protein–ligand complexes. The backbone RMSD profiles (Fig. 8) indicate that all complexes rapidly reached equilibrium and remained stable throughout the simulation period, with only minor fluctuations, confirming that ligand binding did not disrupt the overall protein fold. RMSF analysis (Fig. 9) further revealed that most residues exhibited low flexibility, while slightly higher fluctuations were confined to loop regions, suggesting that ligand binding does not induce unfavourable structural instability.

Biological activity of leads

The biological activityof the identified leads were predicted using the PASS Online server. PASS provides two probability values for each predicted activity Pa (probability “to be active”) and Pi (probability “to be inactive”). Pa represents the probability that a compound will exhibit a specific biological activity. Pi represents the probability that the compound will not exhibit that activity. Biological activity analysis of the five compounds revealed that all possess anti-inflammatory, antineoplastic, and antioxidant properties. In all predicted activities, the Pa values are higher than the corresponding Pi values, suggesting that these compounds are more likely to exhibit the predicted biological activities. Higher Pa values indicate a stronger confidence in the predicted activity. Among the phytochemicals, Solasodine showed the highest probability for anti-inflammatory (Pa = 0.921) and antineoplastic activity (Pa = 0.914), indicating a strong potential for these pharmacological effects. Linoleic acid also demonstrated a relatively high probability for anti-inflammatory activity (Pa = 0.73). Regarding antioxidant activity, several compounds showed moderate predicted activity, including linoleic acid (Pa = 0.314), oleic acid (Pa = 0.283), and stearic acid and palmitic acid (Pa = 0.222). Compounds such as Stearic acid, Palmitic acid, and Oleic acid showed moderate probabilities for anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antineoplastic antimetabolite activities.

In OSF, chronic inflammation, abnormal fibroblast activity, and oxidative stress play central roles in disease progression. The anti-inflammatory effects of compounds may help reduce chronic inflammation and the release of pro-fibrotic cytokines in the oral mucosa. The antineoplastic activity can inhibit abnormal fibroblast and epithelial cell proliferation, potentially lowering the risk of disease progression toward dysplasia or malignancy. Additionally, their antioxidant properties may neutralize reactive oxygen species, protecting oral tissues from oxidative stress and supporting normal tissue remodeling. Previous studies have highlighted the chemopreventive and antioxidant properties of S. trilobatum. In a benzo(a)pyrene-induced lung carcinogenesis model in Swiss albino mice, S. trilobatum leaf extract significantly restored antioxidant defenses, reduced lipid peroxidation, normalized carcinogen-induced alterations in marker enzymes, and protected against histopathological changes in lung tissue10. These findings highlight its antioxidant and protective properties, supporting our results and further suggesting its potential application in fibrotic and premalignant conditions such as OSF.

In vitro antioxidant activity

The extract demonstrated strong antioxidant potential in both assays, exhibiting concentration-dependent activity. In the DPPH radical scavenging assay, the extract achieved 84.9% ± 0.35% inhibition at 500 µg/mL, compared with 95.5% ± 0.40% for ascorbic acid. Even at the lowest concentration tested (25 µg/mL), it retained notable activity of 72.6% ± 0.20%, confirming its ability to effectively neutralize DPPH radicals (Fig. 13a). Similarly, in the hydrogen peroxide scavenging assay, the extract displayed dose-dependent activity, reaching 82.78% ± 0.15% scavenging at 500 µg/mL, compared with 94.95% ± 0.10% for ascorbic acid, while at 25 µg/mL it still showed substantial activity of 67.02% ± 0.45% (Fig. 13b).

Anti-inflammatory activity of the extract

The extract also demonstrated notable anti-inflammatory activity in the protein denaturation (BSA) assay. At the lowest tested concentration (25 µg/mL), it showed 65.5% ± 0.37% inhibition of protein denaturation, while at the highest concentration (500 µg/mL), the inhibition reached 86.3% ± 0.18%. This effect was comparable to the reference standard diclofenac, which exhibited 91.6% ± 0.18% inhibition at the same concentration (Fig. 13c).

Both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties play crucial roles in the management of OSF. Oxidative stress, driven by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of OSF by promoting DNA damage, fibroblast activation, collagen cross-linking, excessive extracellular matrix deposition, and chronic inflammation, ultimately contributing to malignant transformation. Antioxidant activity helps neutralize ROS, thereby protecting against cellular injury and limiting oxidative stress–induced fibrotic remodeling. In this context, the significant antioxidant activity observed in the present study suggests that the phytochemicals of S. trilobatum may help mitigate ROS-mediated cellular damage associated with OSF. By scavenging free radicals, the extract may reduce the activation of pro-fibrotic and inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby complementing the multitarget molecular interactions identified through the in silico analysis. Although the antioxidant assay provides indirect evidence, these findings support the potential role of S. trilobatum in modulating oxidative stress–driven mechanisms involved in OSF progression. Similarly, chronic inflammation in OSF—characterized by persistent inflammatory signaling and cytokine release—further promotes fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition. By suppressing inflammatory mediators, anti-inflammatory activity can reduce tissue damage, fibrosis progression, and carcinogenic risk2,5,7,51–53. Together, these mechanisms suggest that phytochemicals with strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential, such as those from S. trilobatum, may provide therapeutic benefit in OSF by mitigating oxidative damage and inflammatory responses.

Namratha et al. (2023) conducted a randomized interventional study on OSF patients to evaluate the efficacy of curcumin. Their findings demonstrated that curcumin significantly improved mouth opening and reduced burning sensation when compared to a placebo. The therapeutic effects were attributed to its combined anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antifibrotic properties, which directly address the multifactorial pathogenesis of OSF54. These results support the growing evidence that plant-derived compounds can provide symptomatic relief and potentially slow disease progression, aligning with our findings on the phytochemicals of S. trilobatum.

Perumal et al. (2024) investigated the therapeutic role of astaxanthin, a potent natural anti-inflammatory compound, in patients with oral submucous fibrosis. Their study demonstrated significant improvements in clinical symptoms, including reduction of burning sensation and improvement in mouth opening. The observed effects were attributed to astaxanthin’s strong anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties55. These findings further emphasize the importance of anti-inflammatory phytochemicals in OSF management and are consistent with our results, where S. trilobatum phytochemicals demonstrated both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, supporting their potential in mitigating OSF progression.

Similarly, Shahjahan et al. (2005) reported that the methanolic extract of S. trilobatum exhibited strong chemopreventive potential in a rat model of diethyl nitrosamine (DEN)-induced and phenobarbital (PB)-promoted hepatocarcinogenesis. The extract notably reduced tumor burden and restored antioxidant defense mechanisms—decreasing lipid peroxidation (TBARS), elevating glutathione (GSH), and normalizing activities of key antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione reductase, glutathione peroxidase, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase—in both liver tissue and hemolysate. These results suggest that S. trilobatum may exert protective effects by modulating oxidative stress during carcinogenesis4,12. These findings corroborate the present results and suggest that the antioxidant and chemopreventive properties of S. trilobatum may underlie its potential therapeutic role in OSF.

In summary, our study provides preliminary evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of S. trilobatum in the management of OSF. Through a network pharmacology and molecular docking approach, we demonstrated that its phytochemicals exhibit multitargeting activity against key proteins involved in OSF. These findings suggest that S. trilobatum may act through a synergistic, multi-pathway mechanism to attenuate disease progression and reduce the risk of malignant transformation in OSF. Additionally, Solasodine emerged as the most promising lead compound, demonstrating comparatively higher binding affinity and more stable interaction profiles with key proteins implicated in OSF pathogenesis when compared with the other screened compounds. The in vitro antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities of the S. trilobatum extract further support the in silico findings, reinforcing its multitargeting potential and therapeutic relevance in OSF. Furthermore, additional in vitro assays and in vivo validation are required to substantiate its potential for clinical application.

Conclusion

This study provides a systems-level perspective on the therapeutic potential of S. trilobatum against OSF. Through network pharmacology, enrichment analysis, and molecular docking, we demonstrated that five phytochemicals from S. trilobatum possess favorable drug-like properties, good oral bioavailability, high gastrointestinal absorption, and minimal predicted toxicity. The compound–OSF target network revealed that these phytochemicals could modulate six strongly connected OSF-associated targets, molecular docking and dynamic simulation confirmed their multitargeting activity with strong binding affinities. These interactions suggest that S. trilobatum may exert anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects, thereby offering a holistic therapeutic approach for OSF management. Furthermore, predicted biological activities supported their potential mechanisms of action. Collectively, these findings highlight S. trilobatum as a promising source of multitarget therapeutic agents for OSF. The comparative in silico analysis identified Solasodine as a computationally prioritized lead compound. Importantly, the in vitro assays further confirmed these observations, as the S. trilobatum extract demonstrated strong antioxidant activity and significant anti-inflammatory potential. Together, these findings highlight the therapeutic promise of S. trilobatum in OSF management. Further in vitro and in vivo validation is essential to confirm its efficacy and establish its clinical applicability.
