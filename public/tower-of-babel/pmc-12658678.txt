Gold-Based Nanoparticles as an Additive for Combating Drug-Resistant Escherichia coli in Water-Based Paint
Authors: Romchalee Poopakaphunpong, Peeranut Prakulpawong, Dakrong Pissuwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rise of drug-resistant Escherichia coli (E. coli) poses a major challenge in both healthcare and materials science, particularly with regard to surface hygiene. Wall surfaces, particularly those in high-contact areas, can act as breeding grounds for bacterial growth and serve as potential transmission points for harmful bacteria, including drug-resistant strains. Beyond health concerns, bacterial contamination can cause aesthetic damage through bacterial-induced discoloration, which is another significant problem. To address this issue, we proposed that incorporating gold-based nanoparticles (DP1-GNPs) as an antimicrobial additive in paints could enhance antibacterial properties, especially against drug-resistant bacterial strains. This study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial effectiveness of DP1-GNPs incorporated into paint against drug-resistant E. coli BAA-1161, which was used as a drug-resistant bacterial model strain. We systematically compared the bacterial viability on paint films with and without DP1-GNPs under nonscrubbed and scrubbed conditions. The results showed a reduction in bacterial viability upon immediate exposure on both scrubbed blank water-based paint without antimicrobial agents (BP) but containing DP1-GNPs (BP/DP1-GNPs; 69.55 ± 5.39% bacterial viability) and nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films (85.15 ± 4.11% bacterial viability). Notably, when the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films were further treated with DP1-GNPs shield, the viability of E. coli BAA-1161 was reduced to 47.04 ± 2.41%, indicating the enhanced antibacterial performance of the shielded surface. Importantly, cytotoxicity tests indicate that lixiviated products of paint films containing DP1-GNPs were nontoxic to human skin fibroblasts, suggesting their safety on surfaces with frequent human contact. These findings highlight the potential of DP1-GNPs as antimicrobial additives in paint formulations, offering a promising strategy for combating drug-resistant bacterial contamination on painted surfaces.

Introduction

Paint is a substance used in homes, furniture, and various utensils. In addition, paint has been used in other places, such as hospitals, schools, and childcare centers. Water-based paint is an environmentally friendly alternative that is attractive for use in many types of living spaces, especially for interior painting, because it contains low volatile organic compounds (VOCs). In addition, it also provides other benefits such as quick drying and low toxicity, and it is odorless and nonflammable. Color and functional properties of paint are achieved by adding pigments and additives. However, water-based paints generally contain acrylic and thickeners and/or binders made from cellulosic compounds and polymers. These compounds can be a good carbon source to support the growth of microorganisms. Contamination of paints with microorganisms, especially bacteria and fungi, can affect paint properties such as color change, loss of adhesion and chalking, and paint defects. Bacteria and other microbes contaminating wall paints are associated with paint degradation by breaking down the polymer matrix resulting in paint layer surface damage and paint spoilage. Furthermore, the presence of bacterial contamination in paints can pose health risks. Since paints have been used on many touched surfaces, such as walls, floors, and doors, bacteria embedded in paint films can potentially survive and persist for extended periods (e.g., days to months). Various types of bacteria including Escherichia coli (E. coli) have been detected in paints, including water-based paints. − Consequently, these bacteria could be potentially transferred to humans.

The emergence and global spread of drug-resistant bacteria have become a public health challenge, resulting in numerous deaths among those infected by these bacteria. Especially Gram-negative bacteria, as they are more resistant than Gram-positive bacteria. Mutschlechner et al. also reported that ∼30% Gram-negative bacteria were found in paint products. This raises concerns regarding the potential of infectious disease transmission via contaminated surfaces, which demands attention for effective antibacterial interventions. To address the rising threat of Gram-negative drug-resistant bacteria, a multifaceted antimicrobial strategy is essential. One strategy to inhibit the growth of bacteria in paints is to add biocides. It has been reported that isothiazolinone-based biocides are the most commonly used in paints including water-based paints. , However, it has been reported that various isothiazolinone-based biocides can cause allergic contact dermatitis in both workers and consumers. − Owing to the adverse health effects of isothiazolinone-based biocides, it is necessary to reduce the use of isothiazolinone-based biocides and develop new antimicrobial additives.

Metal-based nanomaterials have been used as antibacterial agents against various bacterial strains , because of their physical and chemical properties that can enhance their bactericidal ability such as bacterial membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and biofilm formation inhibition. Furthermore, they offer the advantages of a straightforward synthesis and large-scale production. Tornero et al. and Farsinia et al. demonstrated the use of metal-based nanomaterials, such as zinc oxide and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), as antimicrobial additives in water-based paints. However, recent studies have reported the emergence of bacterial resistance to AgNPs, which may limit their long-term effectiveness. , Therefore, in this work, we proposed the use of gold-based nanomaterials as antibacterial additives in water-based paint films. The antibacterial properties of gold-based nanomaterials and their ability to inhibit biofilm formation have been demonstrated by Kamble et al. Although gold-based nanomaterials show promising potential against bacteria, to the best of our knowledge, their application in water-based paints remains unexplored. To address this gap, this study investigated the use of designed gold-based nanoparticles (DP1-GNPs) as antibacterial additives in water-based paints. Specifically, we evaluated their antibacterial efficacy against the drug-resistant Gram-negative strain E. coli BAA-1161, comparing antibacterial effects on scrubbed versus nonscrubbed painted surfaces. In addition, we characterized the integration of DP1-GNPs within paint matrices and examined the cytotoxicity of their leachates. Furthermore, this study evaluated the antibacterial effectiveness of paint films made from blank water-based paint without antimicrobial agents (BP) but incorporated with DP1-GNPs (BP/DP1-GNPs) and those shielded with a DP1-GNP layer. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the potential of DP1-GNPs as next-generation antimicrobial additives in water-based paints. We hypothesized that DP1-GNPs would not only exhibit strong antibacterial activity against E. coli BAA-1161 but also retain their effectiveness after abrasion, increase efficiency after shielding, and exhibit minimal cytotoxicity, thereby supporting their safe and functional application in antimicrobial paints or surface coatings. For comparison, two types of commercial paints were also evaluated for their antibacterial activities.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of DP1-GNPs and Paints Containing DP1-GNPs

DP1-GNPs used in this study were obtained from the Nanobiotechnology and Nanomaterials Research Laboratory. They were spherical in shape with a core–shell structure and had an average size of ∼20.24 ± 0.22 nm (Figure ). The zeta potential of DP1-GNPs was 52.83 ± 1.55 mV and the maximum absorption of DP1-GNPs was at ∼529 nm. The paints were formulated by using a high-speed mixer, resulting in homogeneous viscous liquid paints. The color of all prepared paints was white because of the presence of titanium dioxide (TiO2) as a white pigment. Paints were prepared as BP and BP mixed with DP1-GNPs (BP/DP1-GNPs).

(a) TEM image and (b) absorption spectrum of DP1-GNPs.

To confirm the presence of DP1-GNPs in the films, BP/DP1-GNPs films without scrubbing were sectioned to have a thickness of ∼65 nm and observed under a transmission electron microscope (TEM). As shown in Figure , the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum indicated the presence of gold in the BP/DP1-GNPs film. The atomic percentage of gold of ∼1.09 was detected in the BP/DP1-GNPs film, while no gold element was detected in the BP films. These results confirm that DP1-GNPs were incorporated into the paint. Following this confirmation, further investigations on the antibacterial property of BP/DP1-GNPs films were conducted.

TEM images and EDS spectra of (a) nonscrubbed BP and (b) nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films.

Effect of BP/DP1-GNPs Films on Drug-Resistant E. coli BAA-1161 Inhibition

To investigate whether DP1-GNPs in the BP film can inhibit the growth of drug-resistant bacteria, the antibacterial activity of BP/DP1-GNPs films was compared with that of commercial paint (CP1; BP with antimicrobial agents) films and commercial paint formulation containing AgNPs (CP2-AgNPs; BP with AgNPs) films. A BP film was used as the control. We investigated nonscrubbed paint films in contact with bacteria, bypassing the contact time (immediate exposure), and found that the bacterial viability of CP1 films treated with E. coli BAA-1161 (at ∼2.0 × 105 CFU mL–1) was reduced to ∼97.37 ± 3.74% compared with that of BP films. The viability of E. coli BAA-1161 on CP2-AgNPs was ∼95.01 ± 5.74%. The lowest viability of E. coli BAA-1161 cultured on the paint films was found in the BP/DP1-GNPs films (∼85.15 ± 4.11%) (Figure ). Although the reduction was not statistically significant, all tested paint films exhibited antibacterial activity against E. coli BAA-1161. Nevertheless, these results indicate that DP1-GNPs mixed with paint (BP/DP1-GNPs) provided the highest inhibitory activity against E. coli BAA-1161. We also extended the contact time between E. coli BAA-1161 and the paint films to 24 h. Following this period, the viability of E. coli BAA-1161 in contact with BP for 24 h significantly decreased to ∼2.50 ± 0.35%. These results suggest that although the viability of E. coli BAA-1161 bacteria was reduced after exposure to BP for 24 h, some E. coli BAA-1161 bacteria remained viable. This indicates that the BP film alone did not completely inhibit bacterial growth. In contrast, colonies of E. coli BAA-1161 in contact with CP1, CP2-AgNPs, and BP/DP1-GNPs films for 24 h were not detected. Our results indicate that the contact time and antibacterial additives (the biocide in CP1, AgNPs in CP2-AgNPs, and DP1-GNPs in BP/DP1-GNPs films) affected the antibacterial efficacy against E. coli BAA-1161. Furthermore, the findings confirmed that DP1-GNPs demonstrated strong antibacterial activity against E. coli BAA-1161.

Effect of DP1-GNPs on Bacterial Inhibition and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Generation

According to antibacterial results in the previous section, our findings indicate that DP1-GNPs demonstrated potential antimicrobial efficacy. Our objective here was not to explore the antibacterial mechanism of DP1-GNPs. However, it is worth noting that the positive charge of DP1-GNPs (52.83 ± 1.55 mV) might disturb the cell wall of E. coli BAA-1161 leading to bacterial death. We investigated the antibacterial activity of DP1-GNPs against E. coli BAA-1161 using minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays. As shown in Figure , the MIC and MBC of DP1-GNPs against E. coli BAA-1161 (0.5 × 108 CFU mL–1) after bacteria were incubated with DP1-GNPs for 1 h were 0.01 and 0.02 ppm, respectively. Gold-based nanoparticles with a positive zeta potential demonstrated low MIC (0.00016–0.00031 ppm) and MBC (0.00031–0.00062) against E. coli, as reported by Piktel et al. Vihodceva et al. also reported that positively charged nanoparticles demonstrated greater effectiveness in interacting with E. coli bacterial cell walls and eliminating E. coli than negatively charged nanoparticles. Furthermore, it has been reported that the arrangement of lipopolysaccharide and peptidoglycans at the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria can stimulate the release of ions from nanoparticles into cells.

MIC and MBC of DP1-GNPs against E. coli BAA-1161.

Additional potential mechanisms through which DP1-GNPs can inhibit bacterial growth upon entering bacterial cells include the generation of ROS. It was reported that ROS can induce lipid peroxidation, inhibit ATP production, and impede DNA replication leading to bacterial death. Therefore, we investigated the effect of DP1-GNPs on the ROS generation. E. coli BAA-1161 bacterial cells were treated with DP1-GNPs, prepared at the same concentration in BP, for 30 min, 3 h, and 24 h. Following treatment, intracellular ROS generation was quantified within the bacterial cell pellet using 2,7-dichlorofluorescin-diacetate (DCFH-DA). As shown in Figure , the highest level of ROS was detected in E. coli BAA-1161 treated with DP1-GNPs for 30 min. At 3 and 24 h of exposure, significant intracellular ROS levels were still detected in E. coli BAA-1161 treated with DP1-GNPs, in comparison to both untreated E. coli BAA-1161 (control) and E. coli BAA-1161 treated with 0.5 mM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Figure ). These results indicate that DP1-GNPs enhanced ROS generation, resulting in cell death. However, upon extending the incubation period, a significant decrease in intracellular ROS levels was detected. This reduction may be due to the aggregation and/or partial degradation of DP1-GNPs over time, which decreases their available reactive surface area and weakens their contact with bacterial cells. Consequently, the ability of the DP1-GNPs to promote ROS generation diminished during prolonged incubation.

ROS levels were determined using a fluorescence-based assay with DCFH-DA. Cytotoxicity Effect of Lixiviated Products (Leachates) of Paint Films

Following the investigation of the antibacterial activity of DP1-GNPs in paint films, it is important to investigate whether DP1-GNPs added to paints cause toxicity in human skin fibroblast cells (HSF cells) because the skin is the most likely tissue to come into contact with painted surfaces. Therefore, lixiviated products of nonscrubbed paint films (BP and BP/DP1-GNPs) were used to treat HSF cells for 24 h. The viabilities of HSF cells treated with lixiviated products from BP and BP/DP1-GNPs significantly dropped to 94.07 ± 0.90% and 91.26 ± 1.50%, respectively, when compared to those of untreated cells (Figure ). However, it clearly showed that the viability of HSF cells treated with lixiviated products from BP/DP1-GNPs was higher than 80%, which indicates no cytotoxicity to the cells. The study by Smulders et al. also reported that nanoparticles incorporated into paint formulations did not exhibit harmful toxicological effects. We further used a trypan blue assay to investigate the cell membrane permeability of HSF cells after treatment with lixiviated products from nonscrubbed paint films (BP and BP/DP1-GNPs) and found that the cell membranes of HSF cells remained intact after treatment, as shown in the Supporting Information.

Effect of Scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs Films on Drug-Resistant E. coli BAA-1161 Inhibition

Based on the results of BP/DP1-GNPs films against E. coli BAA-1161, the incorporation of DP1-GNPs antibacterial agents into paint formulations can greatly influence the ability of paints to prevent bacterial growth. As shown previously, upon immediate exposure, the BP/DP1-GNPs films had the highest inhibitory effect on E. coli BAA-1161. However, we raised a concern regarding the durability of the paint, as repeated scrubbing or cleaning may affect the efficiency of the nanoparticles incorporated into the paint formulation. Scrubbing can affect the physical integrity of the paint film and the availability of nanoparticles on the surface. This could affect their efficacy over time. Therefore, we investigated whether the scrubs on the paint film surface had any effect on the antibacterial activity of DP1-GNPs incorporated into BP. Prior to antibacterial testing, all paint films were scrubbed by using a wet abrasion scrub tester. Four scrubbed paint films (BP, CP1, CP2-AgNPs, and BP/DP1-GNPs) were exposed to E. coli BAA-1161 for immediate exposure and 24 h of contact. After immediate exposure, the viability of E. coli BAA-1161 exposed to scrubbed CP1, CP2-AgNPs, and BP/DP1-GNPs films decreased to ∼63.24 ± 10.68%, 97.12 ± 7.97%, and 69.55 ± 5.39%, respectively (Figure ). The results demonstrated that a significant reduction in bacterial viability compared to the scrubbed BP film upon immediate exposure appeared only in the scrubbed CP1 and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films.

As seen in Table , the antibacterial property of nonscrubbed and scrubbed paint films was different. The viability of E. coli BAA-1161 exposed to scrubbed CP1 (63.24 ± 10.68%) and BP/DP1-GNPs (69.55 ± 5.39%) films was lower than that of nonscrubbed CP1 (97.37 ± 3.74%) and BP/DP1-GNPs (85.15 ± 4.11%) films. These results imply that scrubbing enhanced the antibacterial activity of the CP1 and BP/DP1-GNPs films against E. coli BAA-1161. Antibacterial additives in CP1 and DP1-GNPs in BP/DP1-GNPs films might have low aqueous solubility, which affects the release of antibacterial additives in CP1 and BP/DP1-GNPs to interact with bacteria. When the scrub process was applied to paint films, it may potentially stimulate the release of antibacterial additives in CP1 and BP/DP1-GNPs, resulting in enhanced antibacterial properties. The similar results were also reported by Querido et al. However, the viability of E. coli BAA-1161 on scrubbed CP2-AgNPs (∼97.12 ± 7.97%) was similar to that of nonscrubbed CP2-AgNPs (∼95.01 ± 5.74%) (Table ). This might be due to the difference in physical properties between DP1-GNPs and AgNPs, especially their surface properties, which result in varying dispersibility in paint films. In the case of a 24 h contact period, the bacterial viability was ∼0.92 ± 0.37% in scrubbed CP1 films and ∼2.58 ± 0.80% in BP films. No bacteria were detected in the scrubbed CP2-AgNPs and BP/DP1-GNPs films.

Characterization of Nonscrubbed and Scrubbed Paint Films Containing DP1-GNPs

Based on the results of the effect of nonscrubbed and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films on the viability of E. coli BAA-1161, a more detailed investigation of the properties of nonscrubbed and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films was conducted to confirm whether DP1-GNPs remained in the paint films. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was used to detect gold elements in the BP/DP1-GNPs films. Nonscrubbed and scrubbed paint films were prepared as described in the Experimental Section. As shown in Figure , the presence of DP1-GNPs in nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films was ∼0.08 ± 0.01 mg kg–1. The gold content detected in scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films (∼0.04 ± 0.01 mg kg–1) was lower than that of nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs. As expected, BP alone did not detect the gold content. Nevertheless, the detection of gold in both nonscrubbed and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films confirmed that DP1-GNPs were present in the paint films.

Measurement of Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) Spectra

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was used to assess functional groups and chemical changes in the prepared paint films. The spectral examination was conducted over a range of wavenumbers from 400 to 4000 cm–1. As shown in Figure , the OH stretching band at 3694 cm–1 was detected in the BP and BP/DP1-GNPs films, with or without scrubbing. These films also exhibited an intense band at ∼1727 cm–1, which was attributed to the C=O stretching of carbonyl groups, primarily originating from the acrylic binder in the paint. Furthermore, the −OH deformation peak at ∼1420 cm–1 appeared along with peaks at 1164 and 874 cm–1, corresponding to asymmetric and symmetric C–N–C stretches, respectively. It is important to note that there was a slight shift from ∼1420 cm–1 (in BP) to ∼1416 cm–1 in nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films. The symmetric and antisymmetric stretching peaks of CH2 were at 2875 and 2955 cm–1, respectively. A similar appearance of these peaks in paint layers was also demonstrated by Lungulescu et al. The peak at ∼2921–2922 cm–1 was detected in BP paint films both with and without scrubbing, as well as in nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs paint films. However, a shifted peak at ∼2934 cm–1 was detected in the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films. Additionally, a peak at ∼2944 cm–1 was detected in the DP1-GNPs. Therefore, a peak at ∼2934 cm–1 in the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films may indicate interactions of DP1-GNPs in the paint matrix because the presence of gold was also confirmed by EDS analysis of the cross-sectioned nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films (Figure ). The similar change when nanoparticles were added into polymers was also reported by Ghazi et al.

FTIR spectra of nonscrubbed/scrubbed BP and BP/DP1-GNPs paint films and DP1-GNPs.

The FTIR spectrum of DP1-GNPs presented peaks at 719, 911, 960, 1462, 1487, 2849, 2917, 2944, 3018, and 3032 cm–1. The strong absorptions at 2918 and 2850 cm–1 were caused by C–H stretching vibrations. The bands in the region of 1400–1500 cm–1 arise from the C–H bending vibration. These bands are similar to those reported by Su et al. Upon comparing the nonscrubbed/scrubbed BP and nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films, it was observed thatthe peak positions remained unchanged.

Effect of Shielding DP1-GNPs on Scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs Films against Drug-Resistant E. coli BAA-1161

Based on previous findings, after immediate exposure of bacteria to paint films, bacterial viability remained quite high at ∼85–97% in all nonscrubbed paint films and ∼63–97% in scrubbed paint films. The antibacterial activities of nonscrubbed and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films were 85.15 ± 4.11% and 69.55 ± 5.39%, respectively (Table ). This motivated us to explore whether shielding paint films with DP1-GNPs again can enhance more bacterial inhibition after immediate exposure of E. coli BAA-1161 bacteria. Because a painted surface is typically subjected to cleaning processes, it is likely to undergo an abrasive treatment. Therefore, we investigated the shielding effect of DP1-GNPs on scrubbed paint films. We only focused on the shielding of DP1-GNPs on the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs film because this paint film already contained DP1-GNPs and provided a good efficiency on E. coli BAA-1161 inhibition. Additionally, we investigated the antibacterial properties immediately upon exposure to the film to determine the effectiveness of shielding in inhibiting bacterial growth. In this study, the bacterial concentration used was ∼3.0 × 104 CFU mL–1. As shown in Figure , immediate contact of bacteria with the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs film and the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs film shielded with DP1-GNPs led to a significant decrease in E. coli BAA-1161 viability. Compared to the control film (BP film), the bacterial viability was significantly reduced to 73.58 ± 5.34% for the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs and to 47.04 ± 2.41% for the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs film shielded with DP1-GNPs. These results demonstrated a significant bacterial reduction between the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films with and without shielding with DP1-GNPs upon immediate bacterial exposure. Shielding the film with DP1-GNPs results in ∼26% reduction in bacterial viability. These results indicate that DP1-GNPs had a high potential to eliminate E. coli BAA-1161.

E. coli BAA-1161 viabilities after applying to different scrubbed paint films upon immediate exposure of bacteria to the paint films. * denotes significant differences from scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs and shielding scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs, and ** denotes significant differences between scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs and shielding scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films upon immediate exposure (p < 0.05; n ≥ 5).

Overall, our results demonstrated that the shielding effect of DP1-GNPs on the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs film significantly reduced the E. coli BAA-1161 viability upon immediate exposure of bacteria to the paint film. This reduction could be attributed to the shielding effect of DP1-GNPs, which enhanced the opportunity for direct interaction between E. coli BAA-1161 bacteria and DP1-GNPs resulting in bacterial destruction upon immediate exposure through the mechanism described in the previous section.

According to the information provided by the paint company for CP1, this paint contained biocides as antimicrobial additives. Our results showed that DP1-GNPs can also provide antimicrobial effectiveness. As shown in Figure , some bacterial cells still survived on the scrubbed CP1 film after 24 h of contact. In contrast, no bacterial cells were detected in the scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films. When comparing the effectiveness of the scrubbed and nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs and CP2-AgNPs films upon immediate bacterial exposure, the BP/DP1-GNPs film demonstrated a higher effectiveness against E. coli BAA-1161 than the CP2-AgNPs film (Figures and ). Taken together, the results from this study indicate that DP1-GNPs can be potential antibacterial additives in water-based paints.

Conclusions

DP1-GNPs exhibited potential against drug-resistant bacteria (E. coli BAA-1161). Incorporating DP1-GNPs into paint formulations as alternatives to traditional biocides or AgNPs offers advantages in terms of antimicrobial efficacy. Unlike various biocides, DP1-GNPs are odorless and do not contribute to VOC emissions, resulting in safer paints than biocides for both workers and consumers. Additionally, the ability of DP1-GNPs to form a protective shield on the paint surface effectively diminished the number of drug-resistant bacteria, following their immediate contact with the paint film. This indicated their ability to enhance the antibacterial properties of the coating. Moreover, there was no cytotoxic effect of DP1-GNPs embedded in paint films on skin fibroblast cells. Therefore, DP1-GNPs are promising antibacterial agents for use in paints. However, further research is required to evaluate their long-term stability in paint formulations and antimicrobial performance under real environmental conditions, including factors such as UV exposure, humidity, cleaning processes, and surface aging. Moreover, future studies should investigate their effectiveness against other clinically important drug-resistant microorganisms.

Experimental Section

Materials

Celluloid transparent plastic sheets (TPS) with a thickness of 150 μm were purchased from a local stationery shop and used as cover sheets. The BP, liquid soap, and black plastic–vinyl chloride/acetate copolymer smooth matte surface-plasticizer-free (a black plastic panel) at a size of 165 × 432 × 0.25 mm (width × length × height) were provided by Beger Co., Ltd., Thailand. Nutrient broth (NB) powder was purchased from HiMedia (Nashik, India). Agar 1000 gel was purchased from Chem-Supply (Gillman, South Australia, Australia). Sodium chloride (NaCl), casein-peptone lecithin polysorbate broth (base), OmniPur polyoxyethylene (20) sorbitan monolaurate, 37% hydrochloric acid (HCl), and 65% nitric acid (HNO3) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). The DP1-GNPs used in this study were synthesized in our laboratory using a proprietary process. Due to proprietary reasons, the detailed synthesis procedure cannot be disclosed. However, comprehensive physicochemical characterization data are provided to ensure transparency and reproducibility, as shown in the Results and Discussion section.

It is worth noting that two different units were used to express DP1-GNP concentrations in this study, depending on the experimental context. The concentrations of DPs-GNPs were reported in ppm for suspension-based tests and for applications onto the paint film surface as a shielding layer. When DP1-GNPs were incorporated into a paint matrix, their concentrations were expressed in mg kg–1.

Characterization of DP1-GNPs

The morphology of DP1-GNPs was confirmed using TEM (JEOL, JEM-1400, Tokyo, Japan). The DP1-GNPs solution was centrifuged at 9391 g for 10 min. The pellet was then dropped onto a parafilm surface. Subsequently, a copper grid coated with Formvar was placed on the pellet for 5 min and rinsed with Milli-Q water for 30 s. The excess water was removed using filter paper, and the sample grid was stored in a silica gel box. A UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-1280, Kyoto, Japan) was used to determine the light absorption spectra of DP1-GNPs. The zeta potential of DP1-GNPs was measured using a Zetasizer (Malvern, Malvern-Zen3600, Worcestershire, United Kingdom).

Paint Film Preparation

Four paint formulations were used in this study. Owing to the confidential nature of the industrial recipes, the exact formulations of CP1 (BP containing biocides and TiO2 nanoparticles) and CP2-AgNPs (BP containing AgNPs) employed in this research were not revealed. The BP/DP1-GNPs formulation was prepared by mixing water-based paint without antimicrobial agents (BP) with 0.36 mg kg–1 of DP1-GNPs. A high-speed mixer was used to mix all components together. Thereafter, each prepared paint was applied to a black plastic panel and a four-sided film applicator was used to obtain each paint film with a thickness of 200 μm. Following this, all the paint films were dried at room temperature for 7 days and stored in a clean box until use in other experiments.

Scrubbed paint films were prepared using a wet abrasion scrub tester following the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM D2486) international standard test method (Figure ). The ASTM D2486 scrub resistance test was selected as a standardized method to simulate mechanical wear that may occur during cleaning or handling. This test provides reproducible conditions for evaluating the surface durability and reflects the mechanical aspects of wear encountered in real-world applications. Briefly, all of the prepared paint films were cut in half and placed on a wet abrasion scrub tester. The paint films were scrubbed with a sponge and a 6% liquid soap solution. The abrasion cycle was set to 1000 cycles with a load of 232 g. After the whole scrubbing process was finished, the paint films were dried at room temperature for 7 days. Following drying, the samples were stored in a clean container for further experiments.

ICP-MS

To confirm the presence of DP1-GNPs in paints, the gold content in the paint film was determined by using ICP-MS (Agilent 7900 ICP-MS, California, United States of America). To prepare for the measurement, paint films (nonscrubbed BP, nonscrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs, and scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs) were scraped from a black plastic panel to obtain 50 mg of paint powder. Paint powder (50 mg) was digested in 1 mL of aqua regia (a combination of HCl and HNO3 in a 3:1 ratio) at 83 °C for 1 h using a heat block (FinePCR, ALB128, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Following the digestion process, each sample was passed through a 25 mm syringe filter with a 0.45 μm nylon membrane (Johnson Test Papers, Oldbury, United Kingdom). The filtered samples were transferred into 15 mL conical tubes and analyzed using ICP-MS. The gold standard solution (1000 mg L–1) was diluted with Milli-Q water to obtain concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 25, and 50 μg L–1. Subsequently, prepared gold standard at each concentration (100 μL) was heated with 400 μL of aqua regia at 83 °C for 1 h using a heat block. Thereafter, each concentration of gold standard was mixed with 4500 μL of 1% HNO3. The gold standard and sample solutions were analyzed using ICP-MS. Different concentrations of gold were used to perform a linear calibration for the calculation of the gold content in the samples.

ATR-FTIR Spectroscopy

The chemical compositions of the nonscrubbed and scrubbed BP and BP/DP1-GNPs paint films were examined using an FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Frontier, Waltham, Massachusetts, ) with ATR. The paint film was cut into 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm. The spectra were scanned 16 times at a resolution of 4 cm–1 and recorded over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm–1. The spectra of DP1-GNPs were also measured by dropping the DP1-GNPs onto TPS and allowing them to dry at room temperature before the measurement.

TEM and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) Analysis

Nonscrubbed BP and BP/DP1-GNPs films were cut to a size of 0.5 × 0.5 mm and then placed in a mold. The epoxy resin (Epon-812) was poured into the mold and cured in an oven at 70 °C for 24 h. Following this, the prepared films were cross-sectioned to obtain a thickness of ∼65 nm using an ultramicrotome (RMC Boeckeler, Arizona). Next, the sectioned samples were examined by using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The EDS spectra were acquired by using an EDS detector (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

MIC and MBC Determination

The antimicrobial efficacy of DP1-GNPs was evaluated by using a resazurin-based assay. First, 2-fold serial dilutions of DP1-GNPs were prepared in NB (in concentrations ranging from 0.63 to 0.01 ppm). Each well of 96-well plates contained 100 μL of DP1-GNPs solution and 100 μL of E. coli BAA-1161 suspension (1 × 108 CFU mL–1), resulting in a final volume of 200 μL. The control wells contained 100 μL of NB and 100 μL of bacterial suspension. After that, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, 30 μL of resazurin solution (0.5 mg mL–1) was added to each well as a redox indicator and the wells were incubated for an additional 3 h. Resazurin is blue in its oxidized form and turns pink or becomes colorless when reduced by metabolically active bacteria. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of DP1-GNPs at which the resazurin color remained blue or less pink. Subsequently, 100 μL was taken from the wells to determine the MBC by plating onto NA and incubating at 37 °C. MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colonies were observed on NA.

Detection of ROS

ROS levels in E. coli BAA-1161 were measured using DCFH-DA. The bacteria were cultivated in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. A bacterial suspension (2 × 105 CFU mL–1) was treated with a DP1-GNPs ratio of 1:1 to achieve a final concentration of 0.36 ppm of DP1-GNPs, as used in the paint, compared with the control (Milli-Q water) and 0.5 mM H2O2. All samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, 3 h, and 24 h. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 4602 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and the pellet was resuspended in PBS without Ca2+ and Mg2+. Next, 100 μL of DCFH-DA (40 μg mL–1) was added to the pellet suspension. Subsequently, they were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark before measuring ROS levels in the pellet using a microplate reader (TECAN, Maddeford, Switzerland) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 528 nm.

Shielding Paint Film Preparation

Scrubbed BP and BP/DP1-GNPs films were prepared by cutting each film to a size of 2 × 2 cm. Both films were cleaned by soaking them in ethanol to remove surface contaminants. After cleaning, the films were dried in a biosafety cabinet (BSC). Subsequently, scrubbed BP/DP1-GNPs films were shielded with DP1-GNPs by applying 200 μL of a 0.36 ppm of DP1-GNPs solution covering its surface and allowing it to dry in the BSC.

Bacterial Viability on Paint Films

Bacterial testing was conducted based on the JIS Z 2801/ISO 22196 method to evaluate the antibacterial activities of the paint films. The bacterial strain used for testing was drug-resistant bacteria (E. coli BAA-1161) purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, United States of America). E. coli BAA-1161 were cultured in NB (15 g of NB powder dissolved in 1 L reverse osmosis (RO) water). All media were sterilized through an autoclaving approach. NB was inoculated with a colony of E. coli BAA-1161 and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density of 0.5 at 600 nm (OD600 nm) and diluted to obtain the desired concentration of bacteria.

The paint films (BP, CP1, CP2-AgNPs, and BP/DP1-GNPs) were cut to a size of 4 × 4 cm and then cleaned by soaking in ethanol, followed by drying in BSC. Each of the paint films were placed in a sterile Petri dish, and 100 μL of the bacterial suspension (∼2.0 × 105 CFU mL–1) was applied to the surface of each paint film and covered with TPS. The treated paint films were incubated under two conditions: immediate exposure and 24 h of contact time. Following this, paint films exposed to bacteria were immersed in 10 mL of soya casein digest lecithin polysorbate (SCDLP) broth (25 g of casein-peptone lecithin polysorbate broth dissolved in 960 mL of RO water and then mixed with 40 mL of polysorbate 20). Then, the paint film in SCDLP broth was mixed using a mixer (VELP Scientifica, ZX4, Usmate Velate, Italy) for 1 min. After mixing, the paint sample in the SCDLP was diluted with 0.85% NaCl. The diluted solution (100 μL) was spread on nutrient agar (NA; 15 g of NB powder and 15 g of agar powder dissolved in 1 L RO water). The NA dishes were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The number of viable bacteria was quantified by plate counting on NA dishes. In the case of investigating bacterial viability on the DP1-GNPs shielding paint films, the size of paint film was reduced to 2 cm × 2 cm, and the bacterial concentration used for testing was ∼3.0 × 104 CFU mL–1. The testing process was the same as that for the nonshielding paint films.

Cytotoxicity Test

The cytotoxicity test was conducted to investigate whether DP1-GNPs embedded in BP/DP1-GNPs paint films induced toxicity to the HSF cells. The nonscrubbed BP and BP/DP1-GNPs films (2 cm × 2 cm) were sterilized by soaking in ethanol and dried in a BSC. Each paint film was placed in a 6-well plate, followed by the addition of 3 mL of high glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (DMEM complete medium). Thereafter, the paint films in the 6-well plate were shaken at 37 °C for 7 days using an orbital incubator shaker (Gyromax 727, Concord, California, United States of America) at a speed of ∼120 rpm. This procedure was modified from our previous publication. The liquid part containing leached substances from paint films was collected and used for cell viability testing. HSF cells (purchased from NIBIOHN JCRB cell bank, Japan) were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The old cell culture medium was removed, and 100 μL of DMEM complete medium was added. Following this, 100 μL of the leached substance was added to the cells. Control cells were prepared by adding 200 μL of the DMEM complete medium. Control and treated cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After incubation, a viability assay was conducted using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent assay (Promega, Wisconsin, United States of America) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, the plate was shaken gently for 10 min in the dark, and the luminescence signal was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Statistical Analysis

Numerical data are expressed as mean values ± standard errors. GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0) was used for statistical analyses. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey–Kramer tests were used to assess statistical significance, with P < 0.05 considered significant.
