Gaskets of O ⁡ ( 2 ) O(2) loop-decorated random planar maps
Emmanuel Kammerer

Abstract
We prove that for n = 2 n=2 the gaskets of critical rigid O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) loop-decorated random planar maps are 3 / 2 3/2 -stable maps. The case n = 2 n=2 thus corresponds to the critical case in random planar maps. The proof relies on the Wiener–Hopf factorisation for random walks. Our techniques also provide a characterisation of weight sequences of critical O ⁡ ( 2 ) O(2) loop-decorated maps.

Introduction

1.1
O(n)
loop-decorated planar maps and their gaskets

Random
O(n)
loop-decorated random planar maps are a classical model which couples a statistical mechanics model with quantum gravity. Although they have been studied for more than thirty years by physicists and then by mathematicians, their geometry remains elusive. At criticality, they are conjectured to satisfy scaling limits towards Liouville quantum gravity surfaces with parameter
\gamma

\gamma

by
\gamma=2\sqrt{1-\arccos(n/2)/\pi}

\gamma=2/\sqrt{1+\arccos(n/2)/\pi}

\kappa=4/(1\pm\arccos(n/2)/\pi)
. See e.g. Conjecture 2.1 in
[
18

[
16

17
]
and the references therein. The precise model we deal with is the rigid

loop model on bipartite planar maps introduced in

A planar map
\mathfrak{m}
is a finite connected planar graph which is embedded in the sphere and seen up to orientation-preserving homeomorphism. Our planar maps are equipped with a distinguished oriented edge, called the root edge. The face on the right of the root edge is called the root face and is denoted by
f_{r}
. For every face
f

\deg(f)
and we call degree of
f
the number of edges surrounding
f
with multiplicity. The degree of
f_{r}
is called the perimeter of the map. We restrict ourselves to bipartite maps, i.e. whose face degrees are even. Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of finite rooted bipartite planar maps and, for all
\ell\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)}
be the set of planar maps of perimeter
2\ell
. If
\mathfrak{m}
is a map, we define the dual map
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
as the map obtained by exchanging the roles of the vertices and the faces. Two vertices of
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}
are adjacent if the corresponding faces of
\mathfrak{m}
share an edge on their boundary. A loop-decorated map
(\mathfrak{m},{\bf L})
is a (finite rooted bipartite planar) map
\mathfrak{m}
equipped with a loop configuration

of disjoint unoriented simple closed paths on the dual map
\mathfrak{m}^{\dagger}

do not go through the root face of

and are rigid in the sense that they only visit quadrangles and they enter and exit quadrangles through opposite sides. See Figure

1.2
Main results

This work aims at establishing the asymptotic behaviour of the partition function, i.e. the analogue of (
1.3
), for critical
O(2)
loop-decorated planar maps. Recall that a function
L:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is slowly varying if for all
\lambda>0
, the ratio
L(\lambda x)/L(x)
converges to
1
as
x\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\bf q
be an admissible weight sequence satisfying (
1.2
) with
n=2
. Then there exists a slowly varying function
L_{\bf q}
such that
1=O(L_{\bf q}(\ell))
,
L_{\bf q}(\ell)=O(\log\ell)
and

W^{(\ell)}\mathop{\sim}\limits_{\ell\to\infty}c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}\frac{L_{\bf q}(\ell)}{2\ell^{2}}.

Note that, contrary to the equivalent (
1.3
) in the case
n\in(0,2)
, in the above equivalent, a slowly-varying function appears. See Section
4
for some examples where the slowly-varying function is constant or of order
\log\ell
. This result shows that a weight sequence
\bf q
satisfying (
1.2
) with
n=2
is critical non-generic of type
a=2
, not necessarily in the sense of
[
13
]
, but in the more general setting of
[
25
,
12
]
who allow slowly varying functions. See Subsection 2.1 of
[
12
]
for the exact definition. Equivalently the weight sequence of the gasket of a critical
O(2)
loop-decorated map is critical non-generic of type
a=2
, i.e. the gasket is a
3/2
-stable map in the sense of
[
25
,
12
]
.

Actually, we will prove an analogous result on a class of random walks which will imply the above theorem. Recall that a function
h:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
\nu
-harmonic on a subset

for a positive measure
\nu

\mathbb{Z}
if for all

, we have
h(p)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\nu(k)h(p+k)
. Let
h^{\downarrow}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the function defined by

\forall\ell\geq 0,\quad h^{\downarrow}(\ell)=2^{-2\ell}\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall\ell\leq-1,\quad h^{\downarrow}(\ell)=0.

(1.4)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\nu
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{Z}
such that
h^{\downarrow}
is
\nu
-harmonic on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and such that
\nu
is not the Dirac mass at zero

\forall k\geq 1,\qquad\qquad\nu(k-1)\geq\nu(-k-1).

(1.5)

Then,

•

Either
\sum_{k\geq 1}k(\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1))\in[0,1)
and there exists
c>0
such that

\nu(-k)\mathop{\sim}\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{c\log k}{k^{2}};

•

Or
\sum_{k\geq 1}k(\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1))=1
and there exists a slowly varying function
L
such that
L(k)=o(\log k)
,
1=O(L(k))
and

\nu(-k)\mathop{\sim}\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{L(k)}{k^{2}}.

2
Wiener–Hopf factorisation

We recall here the Wiener–Hopf factorisation of a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. See e.g. Section 2.3 of the lecture notes
[
9
]
for more details on this tool and some applications to random planar maps, or Theorem 9.15 in the book
[
19
]
.

Let
\nu
be a probability law on
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a random walk of step distribution
\nu
starting at zero. The weak ascending ladder epochs
(T_{i}^{\geq})_{i\geq 0}
are the times at which
S_{n}
reaches its running maximum. In other words,
T_{0}^{\geq}=0
and for all
i\geq 0
,

T_{i+1}^{\geq}=\inf\{n>T_{i}^{\geq},\ S_{n}=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}S_{k}\},

where by convention
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. The weak ascending ladder heights
(H_{i}^{\geq})_{i\geq 0}
are defined by
H_{i}^{\geq}=S_{T_{i}^{\geq}}
when
T_{i}^{\geq}<\infty
and
H_{i}^{\geq}=\partial
when
T_{i}^{\geq}=\infty
, where
\partial
is a cemetery point. Similarly, the strict descending ladder epochs
(T^{<}_{i})_{i\geq 0}
are defined by
T^{<}_{0}=0
and for all
i\geq 0
,

T_{i+1}^{<}=\inf\{n>T_{i}^{<},\ S_{n}<\min_{0\leq k\leq n-1}S_{k}\}.

The strict descending ladder heights
(H_{i}^{<})_{i\geq 0}
are defined by
H_{i}^{<}=S_{T_{i}^{<}}
when
T_{i}^{<}<\infty
and
H_{i}^{<}=\partial
when
T_{i}^{<}=\infty
.

We denote the generating functions of
H^{\geq}_{1}
and
H^{<}_{1}
by

G^{\geq}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(z^{H_{1}^{\geq}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{k}\mathbb{P}(H_{1}^{\geq}=k)\quad\text{and}\quad G^{<}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(z^{H_{1}^{<}}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{k}\mathbb{P}(H_{1}^{<}=k)

Note that when
S
oscillates,
\mathbb{P}(H_{1}^{\geq}=\partial)=\mathbb{P}(H_{1}^{<}=\partial)=0
so that
G^{\geq}(1)=G^{<}(1)=1
. We also denote by
\varphi
the characteristic function of
S_{1}
defined by
\varphi(\theta)=\mathbb{E}(e^{iS_{1}\theta})
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. The following proposition relates the functions
\varphi,G^{\geq}
and
G^{<}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

(Wiener–Hopf factorisation)
For all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

1-\varphi(\theta)=(1-G^{\geq}(e^{i\theta}))(1-G^{<}(e^{-i\theta})).

The Wiener–Hopf factorisation was also used for the study of the peeling exploration of
O(n)
loop-decorated planar maps by Timothy Budd in
[
10
]
.

\sum_{\ell\geq 0}h^{\downarrow}(\ell)z^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{p\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{p}\geq 0\\

Thus, in view of (

G^{<}(z)=1-\sqrt{1-z}.

(3.2)

Besides, let us define the power series with non-negative coefficients

In particular, for all

By taking (

(3.3)

Let us define the power series

f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k}\coloneqq\frac{z}{\sqrt{1-z}}\left(1-G^{\geq}(z)\right).

(3.4)

We first compute the

\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{1}([0,2\pi])

3.3
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}}\left(-g(e^{i\theta})+g(e^{-i\theta})+e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)=f(e^{i\theta})-f(e^{-i\theta}),

hence, for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}}\left(-g(e^{i\theta})+g(e^{-i\theta})+e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)e^{-ik\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{|1-e^{i\theta}|}\left(2i\sin\theta-\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}2i\sin(j\theta)\right)e^{-ik\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sin(\theta/2)}\left(\sin\theta-\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\sin(j\theta)\right)\sin(k\theta)d\theta.

(3.5)

Next, for all integers
k,j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin(k\theta)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\sin(j\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{2ki\theta}-1}{e^{i\theta}-1}e^{i(1/2-k)\theta}\sin(j\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sum_{m=0}^{2k-1}e^{i(m+1/2-k)\theta}\sin(j\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos((m+1/2)\theta)\sin(j\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}\left(\sin((m+j+1/2)\theta)-\sin((m-j+1/2)\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{1}{m+j+1/2}-\frac{1}{m-j+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}-2\sum_{m=-j}^{k-j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}.

Therefore, (
3.5
) and the fact that
\sum_{j\geq 1}|g_{j}|\leq 1<\infty
yield, by Fubini-Tonelli, for all
k\geq 1
,

f_{k}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{k-1/2}+\frac{1}{k+1/2}\right)-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}.

(3.6)

Then, let us compute
\nu(-k)
for
k\geq 1
in terms of the
f_{k}
’s. By Proposition
2.1
, (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), for all
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
,

1-\varphi(\theta)=\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}\left(1-G^{\geq}(e^{i\theta})\right)=\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}}{e^{i\theta}}f(e^{i\theta})=\frac{2|\sin(\theta/2)|}{e^{i\theta}}f(e^{i\theta}).

(3.7)

Consequently, assuming that
\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\nu(k)

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{k=0}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{2\sin(\theta/2)}{e^{i\theta}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}e^{i\ell\theta}e^{-ik\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{k=0}-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{\ell}\sin(\theta/2)\cos((\ell-k-1)\theta)d\theta

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{k=0}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f_{\ell}\left(\sin((\ell-k-1/2)\theta)-\sin((\ell+k-3/2)\theta)\right)d\theta

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{\ell+k-3/2}-\frac{1}{\ell-k-1/2}\right),

where in the second equality we use the assumption that
\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
in order to exchange the series and the integral. Thus, for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\nu(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}.

(3.8)

The above equality is also valid when we do not assume that
\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
by density of
\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}([0,2\pi])
, since
\nu(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}-(1/(2\pi))\int_{0}^{2\pi}(2|\sin(\theta/2)|)e^{-i\theta}f(e^{i\theta})e^{-ik\theta}d\theta
and
f_{\ell}=(1/(2\pi))\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{i\theta})e^{-i\ell\theta}d\theta
.
In view of (
3.6
), we have completed the first step.

Step 2.
Let us now prove that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\leq 1
. This will be useful in order to obtain the asymptotics of
f_{k}
as
k\to\infty
. Assume by contradiction that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\in(1,\infty]
. Then, by (
3.6
),

\forall\ell\geq 1,\qquad\qquad f_{\ell}\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}-\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\frac{1}{j+\ell-1/2}{\bf 1}_{j\leq\ell}\right),

so that there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
\ell_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
,
f_{\ell}\leq-\varepsilon/\ell
. So

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq\ell_{0}}f_{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell+k-1)^{2}-1}\leq-\varepsilon\sum_{\ell\geq\ell_{0}}\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell+k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\leq-\varepsilon\int_{\ell_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}\frac{4}{4(x+k-1)^{2}-1}dx\sim-\varepsilon\frac{\log k}{k^{2}}

as
k\to\infty
, where the equivalent is obtained after taking the change of variable
y=x/k
. But the sum of the first
\ell_{0}
terms is a
O(1/k^{2})
, so that by (
3.8
) for all
k
large enough,
\nu(-k)<0
, absurd. As a result,
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\leq 1
.

Step 3.
Let us show that

\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j\geq k/2}g_{j}\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}<\infty.

(3.9)

One can first see that for all
k\geq 1
,

\sum_{j\geq 2k}g_{j}\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}=\sum_{j\geq 2k}g_{j}\sum_{m=j-k}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\leq\sum_{j\geq 2k}g_{j}2k\frac{1}{k}\leq 2\sum_{j\geq k}g_{j}

and
\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j\geq k}g_{j}=\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}<\infty
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j=k}^{2k}g_{j}\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j=k}^{2k}g_{j}\sum_{m=j-k}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{j=k}^{2k}g_{j}\log\left(\frac{j+k+1/2}{j-k+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=C\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{j/2\leq k\leq j}g_{j}\log\left(\frac{j+k+1/2}{j-k+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\int_{j/2}^{j}\log\left(\frac{j+x+1/2}{j-x+1/2}\right)dx,

where
C,C^{\prime}
are positive constants. The sum in the last line is finite since for all
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{j/2}^{j}\log

\displaystyle\left(\frac{j+x+1/2}{j-x+1/2}\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2j+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\left(2j+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\left(2j+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{3}{2}j+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\left(\frac{3}{2}j+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{3}{2}j+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\left(\frac{j}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\log\left(\frac{j}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{j}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}j,

for some constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
and since
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}<\infty
.

Similarly, for some constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{k/2\leq j\leq k}g_{j}\sum_{m=k-j}^{k+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{k/2\leq j\leq k}g_{j}\log\left(\frac{j+k+1/2}{k-j+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=C\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{j\leq k\leq 2j}g_{j}\log\left(\frac{j+k+1/2}{k-j+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\int_{j}^{2j}\log\left(\frac{j+x+1/2}{x-j+1/2}\right)dx<\infty.

This ends the proof of (
3.9
).

Step 4.
Next, we show that

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left|f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right|<\infty.

(3.10)

Note that by (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\left|f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\left|\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}-\frac{2}{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle{}\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell/2}g_{j}\left|\frac{2j}{\ell}-\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\right|+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq\ell/2}g_{j}\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}.

The first term is clearly summable. The last one is summable by (
3.9
). Finally, for the second term, we upperbound

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell/2}g_{j}\left|\frac{2j}{\ell}-\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell/2}g_{j}\left|\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{m+1/2-\ell}{\ell(m+1/2)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime\prime}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell/2}g_{j}\frac{j^{2}}{\ell^{2}},

where
C^{\prime\prime}>0
is a constant, and

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell/2}g_{j}\frac{j^{2}}{\ell^{2}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}j^{2}\sum_{\ell\geq 2j}\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}j^{2}\frac{1}{j}<\infty.

This proves (
3.10
).

Step 5.
Let us now deal with the case
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\in[0,1)
so as to prove the first point of Theorem
1.2
. By (
3.8
), for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\nu(-k)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right)\frac{4}{4(\ell+k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell+k-1)^{2}-1}.

The first term is a
O(1/k^{2})
as
k\to\infty
by (
3.10
). For the second term, for all
\ell_{0}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\ell_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}\frac{4}{4(x+k-1)^{2}-1}dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq\ell_{0}}\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell+k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{4k^{2}-1}+\int_{\ell_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}\frac{4}{4(x+k-1)^{2}-1}dx.

Moreover, after the change of variable
y=x/k
, the integral can be bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int_{\ell_{0}/k}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\frac{4}{4(y+1)^{2}}dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int_{\ell_{0}/k}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\frac{4}{4(y+1-1/k)^{2}-1/k^{2}}dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(1-1/k)^{3}}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int_{\ell_{0}/k}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\frac{4}{4(y+1)^{2}}dy.

Hence, since
\ell_{0}
can be chosen arbitrarily large so that
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{0}/2}jg_{j}
is close to
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}
,

\nu(-k)\mathop{\sim}\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{2(1-\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j})}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\log k}{k^{2}}.

(3.11)

Step 6.
We finally deal with the case
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
to prove the second point of Theorem
1.2
. We first assume that

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}|f_{\ell}|=\infty,

which is equivalent by (
3.10
) to the fact that

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j}}{\ell}=\infty.

(3.12)

We define the function
L
by setting

\forall x\geq 0,\qquad L(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}\frac{4x^{2}}{4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1}.

Let us show that
L
is a slowly varying function in the sense that for all
\lambda>0
, the ratio
L(\lambda x)/L(x)
converges to
1
as
x\to\infty
. Let us also introduce the function
\widetilde{L}
defined for all
x\geq 0
by

\widetilde{L}(x)=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j}}{\ell}\frac{4x^{2}}{4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1}.

By (
3.10
) and since for all
x\geq 2,\ell\geq 1
, we have
4x^{2}/(4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1)\leq 2
,

L(x)=\widetilde{L}(x)+O(1)

(3.13)

as
x\to\infty
.

Let
\lambda>1
. One upperbounds for all
x
large enough,

\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{L}(\lambda x)-\widetilde{L}(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j}}{\ell}\left|\frac{4\lambda^{2}x^{2}(4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1)-4x^{2}(4(\ell+\lambda x-1)^{2}-1)}{(4(\ell+\lambda x-1)^{2}-1)(4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j}}{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left|\frac{(4\lambda x(\ell+x-1)-4x(\ell+\lambda x-1))(4\lambda x(\ell+x-1)+4x(\ell+\lambda x-1))-4(\lambda^{2}-1)x^{2}}{(4(\ell+\lambda x-1)^{2}-1)(4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j}}{\ell}\left|\frac{4(\lambda-1)(\ell-1)x(4\lambda x(\ell+x-1)+4x(\ell+\lambda x-1))-4(\lambda^{2}-1)x^{2}}{(4(\ell+\lambda x-1)^{2}-1)(4(\ell+x-1)^{2}-1)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(\lambda)\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{x^{2}\ell(\ell+x)}{(\ell+x)^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\lambda)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2}}{(x+y)^{3}}dy

\displaystyle=C(\lambda)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+z)^{3}}dz,

where
C(\lambda)
is a positive constant depending on
\lambda
.
In particular,

\widetilde{L}(\lambda x)-\widetilde{L}(x)=O(1)

as
x\to\infty
. But by (
3.12
), we know that
\widetilde{L}(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
. As a consequence,
\widetilde{L}
is slowly varying. By (
3.13
),
L
is therefore slowly varying and
L(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
. Besides, since
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
, one can see that
\widetilde{L}(x)=o(\log x)
as
x\to\infty
by a comparison between series and integrals, hence
L(x)=o(\log(x))
as
x\to\infty
by (
3.13
). This ends the proof of the second point in the case where
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}|f_{\ell}|=\infty
.

Step 7.
In order to conclude, it remains to deal with the case where
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
and

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}|f_{\ell}|<\infty

(3.14)

and to show that
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}\neq 0
. Indeed, assuming (
3.14
), by dominated convergence and (
3.8
), we get

k^{2}\nu(-k)\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}f_{\ell}.

By (
3.10
), (
3.14
) and the fact that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
, we have
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}(1/\ell)\sum_{j>\ell/2}jg_{j}<\infty
, hence by Fubini-Tonelli,

\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}j\log j<\infty.

(3.15)

Next, by (
3.6
) and since
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
, for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\frac{j}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{j}{\ell+1/2}-\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\right),

and for all
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{j}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{j}{\ell+1/2}-\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}-\frac{1}{\ell+m+1/2}-\frac{1}{\ell-m-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}\right)-\sum_{\ell=1+m}^{n+m}\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}-\sum_{\ell=1-m}^{n-m}\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}-\sum_{\ell=n+1}^{n+m}\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}+\sum_{\ell=n-m+1}^{n}\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}-\sum_{\ell=1-m}^{0}\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{n-m+\ell-1/2}-\frac{1}{n+\ell+1/2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}\right)+\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{m+1}{(n+\ell+1/2)(n-m+\ell-1/2)}.

Note that for all
n\geq 1
,
m\geq 0
and
\ell\in[\![1,m]\!]
,

\left|\frac{m+1}{(n+\ell+1/2)(n-m+\ell-1/2)}\right|\leq\left\{\begin{matrix}2/|{n-m+\ell-1/2}|&\text{ if }&n\geq(m+1)/2\\
4/(n+\ell+1/2)&\text{ if }&n\leq m/2\text{ and }\ell\leq(m+1)/4\\
4/|{n-m+\ell-1/2}|&\text{ if }&n\leq m/2\text{ and }\ell\geq(m+1)/4.\end{matrix}\right.

As a result, there exists
C>0
such that for all
j\geq 1
,

\left|\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{m+1}{(n+\ell+1/2)(n-m+\ell-1/2)}\right|\leq C\sum_{m=1}^{j}\log m\leq C(j+1)\log(j+1).

Thus, in view of (
3.15
), by dominated convergence,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f_{\ell}\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\sum_{m=0}^{j-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}\right),

and the limit is non-zero since
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}=1
so that there exists
j\geq 1
such that
g_{j}>0
and we cannot have
g_{1}=1
since
\nu\neq\delta_{0}
, hence there exists
j\geq 2
such that
g_{j}>0
. This ends the proof.
∎

4
Weight sequences

In this section, we show how the computations in the proof of Theorem
1.2
enable to characterise weight sequences of critical
O(2)
loop-decorated maps and we give two examples.

4.1
Characterisation of weight sequences of critical
O(2)
decorated maps

Note that in the proof of Theorem
1.2
we computed
\nu(k)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
in terms of the
g_{j}=\nu(j-1)-\nu(-j-1)
for
j\geq 1
. Indeed, combining (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), one gets for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\nu(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}-\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}\sum_{m=\ell-j}^{\ell+j-1}\frac{1}{m+1/2}\Big)\right)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}.

(4.1)

In order to obtain examples of weight sequences of critical
O(2)
-decorated planar maps, an approach can be to choose a sequence
(g_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of non-negative real numbers such that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\leq 1
and to see whether
\nu
defined by (
4.1
) is indeed a probability measure on
\mathbb{Z}
such that
h^{\downarrow}
is
\nu
-harmonic and
\nu\neq\delta_{0}
. The following proposition actually states that it suffices to check that
\nu(k)\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(g_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative real numbers such that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}\leq 1
. Let
\nu
be defined by (
4.1
). Assume that
\nu(0)<1
and that for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\nu(k)\geq 0
. Then
\nu
is a probability distribution and
h^{\downarrow}
is
\nu
-harmonic on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Moreover, for all
k\geq 1
,

\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1)=g_{k}\geq 0.

In particular, the sequence
{\bf q}=(q_{k})_{k\geq 1}
defined by setting for all
k\geq 1
,

q_{k}=\nu(k-1)(\nu(-1)/2)^{k-1}

is the weight sequence of the gasket of a critical
O(2)
loop-decorated map. The associated triple
(\widetilde{\bf q},1/c_{\bf q},2)
is given by
c_{\bf q}=2/\nu(-1)
and for all
k\geq 1
,

\tilde{q}_{k}=g_{k}(\nu(-1)/2)^{k-1}.

Proof.

As in the previous section, let
f_{\ell}
be the expression in the big parenthesis in (
4.1
). Note that the steps 3 and 4 of the proof of Theorem
1.2
only use the fact that
g_{j}\geq 0
for all
j\geq 1
and that
\sum_{j\geq 1}jg_{j}<\infty
so that (
3.10
) holds. As a result, after writing

\displaystyle\nu(k)=

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\ell}\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1},

one can apply Fubini-Tonelli theorem to obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\nu(k)=1+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}.

Moreover, after identifying a telescopic sum,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}=2\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{4}{4k^{2}-1}-4=2\times 2-4=0.

This proves that
\nu
is a probability measure.

Next, let us check that the function
h^{\downarrow}
defined in (
1.4
) is
\nu
-harmonic on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Let
p\geq 1
. Since
h^{\downarrow}
is non-negative and is bounded, we may apply again Fubini-Tonelli and obtain that

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\downarrow}(p+k)\nu(k)=

\displaystyle h^{\downarrow}(p)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(f_{\ell}-\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi\ell}\right)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\downarrow}(p+k)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{2(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell/2}jg_{j})}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\downarrow}(p+k)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}.

So, in order to prove that
h^{\downarrow}
is
\nu
-harmonic, it suffices to check that for all
p\geq 1
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\downarrow}(p+k)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}=0.

(4.2)

Let
\nu_{\mathrm{sym}}
be the probability distribution defined by

\forall k\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\nu_{\mathrm{sym}}(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{4k^{2}-1}.

One can check that it is indeed a probability distribution using a telescopic sum. This probability distribution appears in a particular weight sequence of
O(2)
loop-decorated maps introduced in Remark 1 of
[
10
]
. Its characteristic function is given for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
by

\varphi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\theta)=1-|\sin(\theta/2)|.

(4.3)

Indeed, one readily computes for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin(\theta/2)e^{-ik\theta}d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\sin((k+1/2)\theta)-\sin((k-1/2)\theta))d\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{2}{k+1/2}-\frac{2}{k-1/2}\right).

We deduce from (
4.3
) the Wiener–Hopf factorisation
1-\varphi_{\mathrm{sym}}(\theta)=(1/2)\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}
, with the generating function of the strict descending ladder height
G_{\mathrm{sym}}^{<}(z)=1-\sqrt{1-z}
(the uniqueness of the Wiener–Hopf factorisation was recently proven in Theorem 3 of
[
15
]
). Hence, by doing the same computations as in the beginning of the proof of Theorem
1.2
, the function
h^{\downarrow}
is
\nu_{\mathrm{sym}}
-harmonic on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. In other words, for all
p\geq 1
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\downarrow}(p+k)\frac{4}{4k^{2}-1}=0,

hence (
4.2
).

Then, let us prove that
\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1)=g_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. Let
g(z)\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}z^{k}
. By (
3.10
), we deduce that the power series
f(z)\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}f_{k}z^{k}
has radius one and that
\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
. By doing the computations leading to (
3.6
) and (
3.5
) in reverse order, one gets that for all
k\geq 1
,

f_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}}\left(-g(e^{i\theta})+g(e^{-i\theta})+e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)e^{-ik\theta}d\theta.

Moreover, for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

|g(e^{i\theta})-g(e^{-i\theta})|=\left|\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k}2i\sin(k\theta)\right|\leq 2|\theta|\sum_{k\geq 1}kg_{k},

so that the function
\theta\mapsto({1}/({\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}}))(-g(e^{i\theta})+g(e^{-i\theta})+e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})
is bounded.

As a consequence, we obtain the equality for Lebesgue almost all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-e^{i\theta}}\sqrt{1-e^{-i\theta}}}\left(-g(e^{i\theta})+g(e^{-i\theta})+e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}\right)=f(e^{i\theta})-f(e^{-i\theta}).

Besides, let
\varphi
be the characteristic function of
\nu
. Since
\theta\mapsto f(e^{i\theta})\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,2\pi])
, we can also do the computation leading to (
3.8
) in reverse order and get (
3.7
) for Lebesgue almost every
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. Combining (
3.7
) with the above equality yields

e^{i\theta}\varphi(\theta)-e^{-i\theta}\varphi(-\theta)=g(e^{i\theta})-g(e^{-i\theta})

for Lebesgue almost every
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. This proves that
\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1)=g_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.
The next point of the proposition comes from exactly the same ideas as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The last points come from the gasket decomposition.
∎

4.2
Examples

Let us give two examples of models of critical
O(2)
loop-decorated planar maps. We start with an example from Timothy Budd and then give a new example which only has faces of degree
4
which are traversed with loops.

4.2.1
The symmetric example of Budd

A key ingredient of the proof of Proposition
4.1
is the symmetric probability distribution
\nu_{\mathrm{sym}}
given by

\forall k\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\nu_{\mathrm{sym}}(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{4k^{2}-1}.

Here the slowly varying function of Theorem
1.2
is a constant. The associated weight sequence
\bf q
for the gasket is given by

\forall k\geq 1,\qquad q_{k}=\left({\bf 1}_{k=1}+\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{1}{4(k-1)^{2}-1}\right)(3\pi)^{-k+1}

and
c_{\bf q}=3\pi
, while the associated triple
(\widetilde{\bf q},1/c_{\bf q},2)
is given by Remark 1 of
[
10
]
: using (
1.1
) and (
3.1
), one computes for all
k\geq 1
,

\tilde{q}_{k}=c_{\bf q}^{-k+1}(\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1))=(3\pi)^{1-k}\frac{2k}{\pi(k-3/2)(k-1/2)(k+1/2)(k+3/2)}+{\bf 1}_{k=1}.

From the above expression one can see that there are faces of arbitrary (even) degrees which are not traversed by loops. The partition function is given for all
\ell\geq 1
by

W^{(\ell)}=\frac{c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}}{2}\nu(-\ell-1)=\frac{(3\pi)^{\ell+1}}{\pi}\frac{1}{4(\ell+1)^{2}-1}.

4.2.2
Fully packed critical
O(2)
-loop decorated quadrangulations

Figure 2:
Left: a fully packed loop-decorated quadrangulation. Right: its gasket.

A new simple example of weight sequence is obtained by taking
g_{j}=0
for all
j\geq 1
. By (
4.1
), for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\nu(k)={\bf 1}_{k=0}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}\right)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k-1)^{2}-1}.

It clearly satisfies
\nu(k)\geq 0
for all
k\leq-1
and one computes for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\nu(k-1)-\nu(-k-1)

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{k=1}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}\right)\left(\frac{4}{4(\ell-k)^{2}-1}-\frac{4}{4(\ell+k)^{2}-1}\right)=0.

In particular,
\nu(k)\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, one computes
\nu(-1)=4/\pi^{2}
. Thus, by Proposition
4.1
, the weight sequence
{\bf q}=(q_{k})_{k\geq 1}
defined by

\forall k\geq 1,\qquad q_{k}=\left({\bf 1}_{k=1}+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{\ell-1/2}+\frac{1}{\ell+1/2}\right)\frac{4}{4(\ell-k)^{2}-1}\right)(\pi^{2}/2)^{1-k}

is the weight sequence of the gasket of a critical
O(2)
-decorated planar map and
c_{\bf q}=2/\nu(-1)=\pi^{2}/2
, while the associated triple is
(\widetilde{\bf q},2/\pi^{2},2)
, where
\widetilde{\bf q}
is the constant sequence equal to zero. Here, the
O(2)
-decorated map only has quadrangles and every quadrangle is traversed by a loop. See Figure
2
. The partition function is given, for all
\ell\geq 1
, by

W^{(\ell)}=\frac{c_{\bf q}^{\ell+1}}{2}\nu(-\ell-1)=\frac{(\pi^{2}/2)^{\ell+1}}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{k\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{k-1/2}+\frac{1}{k+1/2}\right)\frac{4}{4(k+\ell)^{2}-1}.

In this example, the slowly varying function
L_{\bf q}(\ell)
is equivalent to
(2/\pi^{2})\log(\ell)
as
\ell\to\infty
.
