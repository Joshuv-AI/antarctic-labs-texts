Comparative Evaluation of a Light-Cure Adhesion Booster on Shear Bond Strength Using Four Enamel Conditioning Techniques: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Polisetty Siva Krishna, Sampath Krishna Veni, K Jashva Vamsi, B. Rama Mohan Reddy, Gowri Sankar Singaraju, Prasad Mandava
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Effective bonding of orthodontic brackets is a critical factor for treatment success. Although acid etching with phosphoric acid is the gold standard, it may result in enamel loss and soft tissue irritation. Alternative enamel conditioning methods—such as sandblasting and laser etching—when combined with adhesion boosters, may enhance bond performance while preserving enamel integrity.Objectives:To evaluate the effect of a light-cure adhesion booster on the shear bond strength (SBS) of orthodontic brackets following four enamel conditioning protocols.Materials and Methods:Eighty extracted premolars were divided into four groups (n = 20 each): Group 1—35% phosphoric acid etching; Group 2—sandblasting; Group 3—sandblasting followed by acid etching; Group 4—Er: YAG laser etching. All specimens received a light-cure adhesion booster before bracket bonding with a light-cure adhesive. The shear bond strength was measured using a universal testing machine, and adhesive remnant index (ARI) scores were evaluated under stereomicroscopy.Results:One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference among groups (P < 0.001). Group 4 (laser) recorded the highest SBS (27.54 MPa), followed by Group 3 (27.36 MPa), Group 1 (16.87 MPa), and Group 2 (12.88 MPa), with the sandblasting the lowest values. Tukey’s post hoc test indicated statistically significant differences between Groups 1 vs. 3 and 4, and Groups 2 vs. 3 and 4 (P < 0.0001). ARI scores showed no significant intergroup differences.Conclusion:The use of a light-cure adhesion booster significantly enhanced SBS across all conditioning methods. Sandblasting, followed by acid etching and laser etching, demonstrated superior performance.

INTRODUCTION

Success in orthodontic treatment largely depends on achieving adequate bond strength between the bracket and enamel surface, which is influenced by three main factors: enamel conditioning while preserving tooth integrity, the adhesive system, and the bracket base design.[1] Reynolds reported that a minimum bond strength of 6.7–7.8 MPa is necessary for effective orthodontic treatment.[2] Acid etching with phosphoric acid transforms enamel into a high-energy hydrophilic surface that supports micromechanical retention. However, drawbacks, such as enamel loss and gingival irritation, have been reported.[3]

Alternative conditioning techniques have been introduced. Nandini et al.[4] demonstrated that sandblasting (air abrasion) improves enamel roughness and is safer for soft tissues. Usümez et al.[5] demonstrated that Er: YAG laser etching produces enamel alterations comparable to those achieved through acid etching, resulting from localized thermal microexplosions. Laser and air abrasion techniques have shown potential in reducing the adverse effects of acid etching.

Despite improvements in surface conditioning, bond failures remain a clinical concern. Adhesion boosters, such as Enhance LC—comprising hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), ethanol, and dimethacrylate—have been developed to improve resin infiltration and enhance bonding, particularly on moist or hypocalcified enamel surfaces. While Chung et al.[6] reported modest improvements with Enhance LC in new bracket bonding, another study[7] found significantly higher bond strength values when Adhesion booster (Enhance LC) was used across different substrates. Few studies also confirmed that Enhance LC significantly increases bond strength, especially in scenarios involving fluorosed or rebonded enamel.[89]

Most existing studies assessed adhesion boosters solely in conjunction with acid etching. There is limited literature comparing the efficacy of a light-cure adhesion booster across varied enamel conditioning methods. This study addresses that gap by evaluating four conditioning techniques—sandblasting, acid etching, their combination, and laser etching—used with a light-cure. The null hypothesis proposes no significant differences in bond strength or mode of bond failure among the tested methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This in vitro study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from the Institutional Ethical Committee, Narayana Dental College, Nellore (Lr.No. 6122/A4/MDS/Diss/2014-15).

Sample Size determination: A total of 80 samples (n = 20 per group) were determined to be sufficient for detecting statistically significant differences in shear bond strength (SBS) across four groups using one-way ANOVA with a power of ≥80% and significance level of 0.05. Previous in vitro studies[36] evaluating SBS used comparable group sizes (15–25 specimens), demonstrating adequate power for intergroup comparisons. Therefore, a sample size of 20 per group was adopted in the present study to ensure methodological validity.

Sample Selection: Eighty freshly extracted human premolars, extracted for orthodontic purposes, were selected. Inclusion criteria included intact crowns with normal morphological appearance. Teeth with hypoplastic enamel, caries, cracks, or fractures were excluded.

Specimen Preparation: Teeth were cleaned of debris, stored in distilled water to prevent dehydration, and mounted vertically in acrylic blocks (1 × 1 × 3 cm) with the buccal surface exposed and aligned perpendicular to the base [Figure 1].

Adhesion Booster Application: A light-cure adhesion booster (Enhance LC, Reliance Orthodontic Products, Itasca, IL, USA) was applied to all samples following surface conditioning and cured for 10 seconds as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bonding Procedure: Stainless steel orthodontic brackets (Victory series, 3M Unitek, Monrovia, CA, USA) were bonded using light-cure adhesive (Transbond XT, 3M Unitek) and cured for 10 seconds at 460 nm wavelength.

Shear Bond Strength (SBS) Testing: Samples were stored in distilled water at 37°C for 48 hours before testing. SBS was measured using a universal testing machine (Instron, Blue Star Ltd, India) with a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. Force at debonding was recorded in Newtons and converted to MPa by dividing by the bracket base area (13.8 mm²) [Figure 2].

Universal Testing Machine (Blue Star Ltd., India) setup for shear bond strength testing with inset showing bracket–tooth load application

Assessment of Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI): Debonded tooth surfaces were examined under 60× magnification using a scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss EVO MA 15, Germany). Evaluation was performed by a single calibrated operator blinded to the study design. The adhesive remnant index (ARI) was used to quantify the amount of resin left on the enamel surface after bracket debonding, based on the classification by Artun and Bergland.[10]

Scoring was done by counting the number of replicated mesh patterns visible in the SEM images. The proportion of mesh units visible post-debonding in each sample was compared to the total reference mesh units to assign ARI scores as follows [Figure 3]:

To assess intra-examiner reliability, 10 randomly selected samples were re-evaluated after one week. The reproducibility was found to be 96%, confirming high measurement consistency.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed normal distribution of SBS data. One-way ANOVA assessed intergroup SBS differences, followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. ARI scores were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. Software used: IBM SPSS Statistics version 20 (Armonk, NY, USA).

DISCUSSION

The success of fixed orthodontic appliances depends significantly on the reliable bond strength between brackets and enamel. Newman[11] was among the first to bond orthodontic brackets to enamel using the acid-etch technique. To mitigate adverse effects associated with acid etching, alternative conditioning techniques, such as crystal growth mechanism,[10] air abrasion,[12] and laser etching,[13] have been evaluated. Laser etching, through thermal microexplosions,[14] creates a roughened enamel surface suitable for bonding. Despite such innovations, bracket failures due to poor bond strength remain a challenge, especially on compromised enamel. Adhesion boosters like Enhance LC, which contains HEMA, have demonstrated increased hydrophilicity and better resin infiltration.[6] In the present study, we evaluated the impact of four enamel conditioning methods—acid etching (AE), sandblasting (SB), sandblasting followed by acid etching (SB+AE), and laser etching (LE)—in combination with the light-cure adhesion booster Enhance LC. The SBS and ARI outcomes were compared with previous literature to assess the efficacy of these techniques.

Among the four groups, the highest SBS was recorded for Group 4 (LE: 27.53 ± 9.09 MPa) and Group 3 (SB+AE: 27.35 ± 6.45 MPa). Group 1 (AE: 16.87 ± 3.05 MPa) and Group 2 (SB: 12.88 ± 4.25 MPa) exhibited significantly lower SBS compared to Groups 3 and 4, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey tests (P < 0.001) [Tables 2 and 3]. All these values far exceed the clinically acceptable threshold of 6.7–7.8 MPa recommended by Reynolds,[2] indicating strong and reliable bonding. These results suggest that both laser etching and the combined SB+AE method are highly effective when used with an adhesion booster.

The use of Enhance LC is well supported in the literature. Vamsilatha et al.[14] reported enhanced SBS of 12.03 MPa with Enhance LC in fluorosed enamel, and Gaur et al.[15] showed a 27.1% improvement with Assure Universal in fluorosed enamel. Varunjeet et al.[16] found 14.7 MPa with adhesion booster vs. 11.32 MPa with conventional primer. Similarly, a few other studies[917181920] consistently demonstrated superior SBS outcomes with adhesion boosters. J Singh et al.[7] found that Enhance LC yielded the highest SBS (16.02 ± 6.36 MPa) compared to Assure and OrthoSolo.

Earlier studies reported substantially lower SBS values using AE or LE alone, without boosters. Ozer T[21] reported 7.65 MPa (acid etching) and 2.94–7.17 MPa (laser). Usümez et al.[5] also noted 8.23 ± 2.3 MPa for AE and 5.64 ± 3.19 MPa for LE. Olsen et al.[22] found sandblasting and AE produced only 3.6 ± 2.2 MPa and 10.4 ± 2.8 MPa, respectively. These findings affirm the enhancement provided by adhesion promoters.

Our SB+AE group outperformed previous studies using similar methods. Cal-Neto et al.[23] recorded a shear bond strength of 16.42 ± 5.41 MPa for sandblasting, followed by acid etching without the use of adhesion boosters. Vicente et al.[24] investigated the effect of three adhesion promoters—OrthoSolo, All-Bond 2, and Enhance LC—applied after conventional acid etching. Among these, Enhance LC used with light bond adhesive achieved the highest bond strength of 20.68 ± 5.40 MPa, while Transbond XT with OrthoSolo yielded 19.02 ± 5.61 MPa. These values, although significantly improved over control groups, remain lower than the 27.35 ± 6.45 MPa achieved in our SB+AE group, demonstrating the enhanced efficacy of combining dual surface conditioning with a light-cure adhesion booster under standardized conditions.

The adhesive remnant index (ARI) scores provided insights into the mode of bond failure. Group 2 (SB) had the highest percentage of ARI score 0 (75%), indicating failure at the enamel–adhesive interface, which is favorable for enamel conservation. In contrast, Groups 3 and 4 predominantly showed ARI scores of 2 and 3, indicating cohesive failures within the adhesive, resulting in more resin remaining on the enamel surface [Table 5]. Though ARI score differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), they suggest stronger bonding and the need for careful debonding in SB+AE and LE groups. These observations align with a previous study[4] that emphasized the enamel-safe nature of adhesive failures in air-abraded groups.

Unlike prior studies, which examined either acid etching or sandblasting alone, or with varied primers, this study directly compared four standard conditioning techniques with a single standardized adhesion booster (Enhance LC) under identical conditions. This eliminates cross-variable interference and adds significant internal validity to the comparative results.

This study reinforces the clinical value of adhesion boosters—particularly Enhance LC—in significantly improving shear bond strength (SBS) across various enamel conditioning techniques. The combinations of sandblasting, followed by acid etching (SB+AE) and laser etching (LE) with Enhance LC, demonstrated superior bond strength outcomes, suggesting their particular suitability in challenging clinical scenarios such as fluorosed, hypocalcified, or bleached enamel.

The findings advocate for the routine consideration of light-cure adhesion boosters in orthodontic bonding protocols to enhance bracket retention and minimize bond failures. By reducing the incidence of bracket debonding, these combinations may contribute to shortened treatment duration, decreased chairside time, and improved patient compliance. Clinicians are encouraged to incorporate SB+AE or LE protocols alongside adhesion boosters in cases demanding enhanced bonding reliability, particularly where enamel integrity is compromised.

The study employed a well-structured experimental design with equal sample sizes across all four groups (n = 20), reducing the risk of selection bias. ARI scoring was performed by a blinded evaluator using a scanning electron microscope, improving objectivity. Standard materials, like Transbond XT and Enhance LC, were used, which enhance clinical relevance. Statistical analysis was rigorous, employing ANOVA and post hoc Tukey tests to confirm intergroup differences.

As an in vitro study, this research does not simulate the dynamic oral environment with factors like saliva, occlusal forces, or thermal cycling, which may influence real-world performance. Manual bonding may have introduced variability in adhesive thickness. Also, only one adhesion booster formulation was tested, which limits generalizability across brands.

Future studies should include clinical trials assessing long-term bracket survival and failure rates. Comparing different adhesion boosters under identical conditions could help determine their relative efficacy. Use of aging protocols such as thermocycling, and advanced surface analysis techniques (e.g. SEM, AFM) may provide further insight into enamel–adhesive interactions and durability.

CONCLUSION

The use of an adhesion booster significantly enhanced shear bond strength across all enamel conditioning protocols. Among these, the combination of sandblasting, followed by acid etching and laser etching, produced the highest bond strengths, suggesting a potential clinical advantage. While ARI scores showed no statistically significant differences between groups, the mode of bond failure varied. Sandblasting alone predominantly exhibited failures at the enamel–adhesive interface, which may be less detrimental to enamel integrity.

These findings indicate that pairing a light-cure adhesion booster with either laser etching or dual sandblast–acid conditioning offers a reliable strategy for optimizing bracket retention, especially in cases involving compromised enamel. Such an approach may enhance bonding efficiency and reduce the incidence of bracket failures during orthodontic treatment.
