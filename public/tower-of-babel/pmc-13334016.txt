Compressive behavior of additively manufactured circular-core chiral lattice structures with CNT-reinforced PMMA: a multi-scale analysis
Authors: Mohammad Malekzadeh, Mohammad Shishehsaz, Reza Mosalmani, Vahid Arab Maleki, Amin Yaghootian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the compressive behavior of additively manufactured chiral lattice panels fabricated from PMMA/CNT nanocomposites employing a multi-scale numerical-experimental framework. The research introduces an enhanced chiral topology featuring circular cores and utilizes a three-phase representative volume element (RVE) model—comprising the matrix, CNTs, and interphase—to determine equivalent mechanical properties. RVE analysis revealed that 0.5 wt% CNT reinforcement offers optimal performance, enhancing Young’s modulus and yield stress by up to 70% and 85%, respectively. Incorporating these homogenized properties into finite element (FE) models indicated that the circular-core topology yields a 71.5% increase in peak load and superior plateau stability compared to conventional configurations. Experimental validation confirmed these trends; the enhanced structure reinforced with 0.5 wt% CNT sustained a peak force of 677 N and absorbed energy exceeding 7.7 J, representing a 137% and 54% improvement, respectively over the pristine specimen. Parametric analysis further identified a ligament thickness of 3 mm and a core radius of 3 mm as geometric optima for maximizing load-bearing capacity. The findings demonstrate that the synergistic integration of the novel chiral geometry and nanoscale reinforcement facilitates uniform stress distribution and mitigates local buckling. Consequently, the proposed multi‑scale RVE‑FE framework emerges as a robust design tool for advancing next‑generation lightweight lattice structures aimed at energy absorption and structural protection.Keywords: Additive manufacturing, Chiral lattice structure, PMMA/CNT nanocomposite, Multi-scale modeling, Energy absorptionSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

In recent years, lattice and cellular architectures have emerged as a focal point of research within the fields of mechanical engineering, materials science, and lightweight structural design1–3. This prominence is primarily attributed to their exceptional attributes, including low density, high specific strength, superior energy absorption capacity, and the feasibility of realizing intricate geometries through additive manufacturing (AM)4,5. The evolution of AM technologies, particularly fused deposition modeling (FDM) and Stereolithography (SLA), has facilitated the production of geometric structures with tunable mechanical behavior6. Recent advancements have demonstrated the potential of 3D-printed polymer-based scaffolds in tissue engineering, where tailored architectures and material properties are crucial for achieving desired biological and mechanical performance7. Consequently, the mechanical characteristics of these structures can be deliberately engineered by manipulating design parameters such as unit cell topology, ligament thickness, and relative density.

A survey of the recent literature reveals that the development and characterization of additively manufactured lattice architectures have evolved into a burgeoning research domain within materials science and structural engineering8,9. Ataollahi10 reviewed the significance of additive manufacturing in fabricating cellular structures, highlighting the pivotal role of geometric design in governing the mechanical behavior of these constructs. Bogusz et al.11 experimentally investigated the behavior of various printed polymeric lattices, demonstrating that variations in unit cell topology can profoundly influence the compressive response. In the realm of design optimization, Gupta et al.12 proposed frameworks for lattice optimization, while Li et al.13 introduced advanced methodologies for the design and selection of optimal geometric parameters for engineered lattices. The implementation of intelligent algorithms has also garnered attention; Doodi and Gunji14 demonstrated that machine learning models can enhance the accuracy of performance predictions for novel lattice structures. Regarding the specific behavior of chiral structures, Koudelka et al.15 analyzed the compressive response of a tetrachiral lattice through a combination of experimentation, micro-tomography, and FE analysis, revealing that rotational geometries induce unique mechanical behaviors. Alshneeqat et al.16 investigated the design and AM-fabrication of novel lattices, exploring the effects of topology on mechanical response. Arunprasand et al.17 studied the mechanical performance of printed polymeric lattices, indicating that topology selection plays a pivotal role in determining stiffness and energy absorption capabilities. Xiao et al.18 demonstrated that multilayered heterogeneous pyrocarbon-based composites, constructed through chemical vapor deposition with nanoscale graphene orderliness regulation, exhibit impressive strengthening and toughening mechanisms through microstructural multiscale coupling, offering promising solutions for high-temperature aerospace applications. Park et al.19 evaluated the static and dynamic behavior of chiral lattices, reporting superior energy absorption performance compared to conventional truss-based lattices. Introducing a novel body-centered cubic structure, Ma et al.20 demonstrated that the incorporation of bionic fractals mitigates stress concentrations at ligament junctions, thereby enhancing the cell’s compressive capacity and buckling stability. Furthermore, Alawwa et al.21 reported a 25–50% improvement in the compressive strength of chiral lattices through modifications to the ring geometry and ligament angle.

Furthermore, employing polymer nanocomposites reinforced with various nanofillers, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), as the feedstock for these lattice structures facilitates the enhancement of mechanical performance at the material scale22,23. Characterized by a high aspect ratio, exceptional elastic modulus, and favorable interfacial adhesion, CNTs are capable of significantly augmenting the strength, stiffness, and fracture toughness of the polymeric matrix24–26. The synergistic integration of advanced nanocomposite materials, intricate geometric design, and additive manufacturing technologies paves the way for the development of high-strength, lightweight structures for next-generation engineering applications27,28. In the context of reinforced materials, numerous studies have investigated the influence of various nanofillers on the mechanical properties of additively manufactured polymers29,30. Mathur et al.31 demonstrated that the incorporation of CNTs into amorphous polymers results in improved modulus and tensile strength. Regarding PMMA/CNT nanocomposites, Wang et al.32 reported that low CNT weight fractions (less than 1 wt%) can yield substantial improvements in both strength and stiffness. Deep et al.33 indicated that the addition of CNTs to a PMMA matrix significantly increases Young’s modulus and tensile strength at low reinforcement ratios, highlighting the vital role of the interphase region in the load transfer mechanism. Similarly, Sadati et al.34 examined the mechanical performance of melt-blended PMMA/CNT nanocomposites, reporting that the uniform dispersion and alignment of CNTs directly influence the material’s strength and toughness.

The FEM and other numerical techniques have become indispensable tools across various scientific disciplines, enabling researchers to simulate complex physical phenomena, optimize designs, and predict material behavior with high accuracy under diverse loading and environmental conditions35. From a numerical perspective, multi-scale approaches predicated on the representative volume element (RVE) have gained widespread adoption in recent years for the analysis of nanocomposites. Malekzadeh et al.24 proposed a three-phase model comprising the matrix, CNTs, and the interphase region, demonstrating that the explicit modeling of the interphase is critical for the accurate extraction of the equivalent modulus. In another study, Moarrefzadeh et al.36 derived the equivalent properties of CNT/polymer nanocomposites by subjecting the RVE to tensile and shear loading conditions, subsequently utilizing these properties in the analysis of printed lattices. The results indicated that employing RVE-homogenized properties yields significantly lower prediction errors in compressive behavior compared to single-phase models.

In terms of constitutive modeling, Charitos et al.37 presented a three-phase RVE framework incorporating the matrix, CNT, and the interphase zone, establishing that multi-scale homogenization models can predict the equivalent modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and yield stress of nanocomposites with high precision. Similarly, Rafiee et al.38 extracted the equivalent properties of CNT/ABS nanocomposites via RVE analysis and applied them to the simulation of printed lattice structures, reporting that the use of RVE-derived properties significantly reduces simulation errors compared to single-phase assumptions. In the domain of multi-scale analysis for 3D-printed nanocomposites, Shin and Han39 introduced an advanced approach wherein the tensile properties of carbon nanocomposites fabricated via extrusion-based printing are predicted using a multi-stage model spanning from the atomic to the structural scale. In their study, a stochastic-anisotropic model based on molecular dynamics (MD) results was developed, after which the effective equivalent properties were obtained through the analysis of various RVEs. Findings demonstrated that the integration of reinforcement morphology, printing parameters, and RVE homogenization facilitates a more accurate prediction of the mechanical behavior of printed nanocomposites. This multi-scale approach establishes a pivotal foundation for novel research concerning nanocomposites in printed lattice structures, bridging the existing gap between the nano-scale, the additive manufacturing process, and the structural scale. Adopting this multi-scale framework, the present research develops an RVE model to extract the equivalent properties of PMMA/CNT, which are subsequently employed in the compressive analysis of novel chiral structures.

In the present study, a combined numerical-experimental investigation is conducted to evaluate the mechanical behavior of an enhanced chiral lattice structure featuring a circular core, additively manufactured from CNT-reinforced PMMA nanocomposites. Initially, PMMA/CNT filaments with varying CNT weight fractions were prepared, followed by the fabrication and testing of standard tensile specimens in accordance with ASTM D638 standards. Subsequently, to accurately determine the equivalent mechanical properties of the nanocomposite, a multi-scale RVE model was developed, wherein the polymeric matrix, carbon nanotubes, and the interphase region were explicitly modeled. At this stage, drawing inspiration from the methodology proposed by Shin and Han39, a multi-stage homogenization process based on RVE analysis was employed to simultaneously account for the influence of reinforcement morphology and printing conditions on the material’s constitutive response. The extracted equivalent properties were validated against the experimental tensile data, thereby confirming the fidelity of the multi-scale model.

Proceeding further, two distinct lattice configurations—conventional chiral and enhanced chiral with circular cores—were additively manufactured using pristine and PMMA/CNT filaments and subjected to quasi-static compression testing. The experimental results served to validate the FE models of the panels. Following model verification, a comprehensive parametric study was executed to investigate the effects of CNT weight fraction, unit cell topology, and geometric characteristics on the compressive modulus, strength, and energy absorption capacity. The findings demonstrated that the synergistic combination of the PMMA/CNT nanocomposite and the enhanced geometric design leads to a significant augmentation in the specific strength and energy absorption of the lattice panels. Consequently, this research proposes a comprehensive multi-scale-structural framework applicable to the advanced analysis and design of additively manufactured nanocomposite lattice structures.

Materials and experimental methods

Raw materials and nanocomposite filament preparation

Engineering-grade PMMA CM 205, CHEMICAL (Taiwan) was selected as the polymeric matrix owing to its superior optical transparency, dimensional stability, rheological properties suitable for extrusion, and consistent mechanical behavior during the AM process. The microstructure of the pristine PMMA was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As depicted in the SEM micrographs of the polymer matrix (Fig. 1), the surface exhibits a homogeneous morphology, devoid of voids or significant impurities. Such structural integrity is a prerequisite for achieving a uniform dispersion of the reinforcing phase during the compounding stage.

Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) with a purity exceeding 99.5% were employed as the reinforcing agent, procured from US Research Inc., USA. These nanotubes possessed an average diameter of 5–12 nm, an approximate length of 2 μm, and a high aspect ratio. The SEM micrographs of the SWCNTs (Fig. 2) reveal a single-walled tubular architecture accompanied by initial agglomerates (bundles), thereby necessitating an effective dispersion protocol to ensure material homogeneity. To fabricate the nanocomposite filament, a weight fraction of 0.2 wt% CNT was initially selected for experimental validation, while comprehensive numerical analyses were conducted across the range of 0–1.0 wt% to identify optimal reinforcement levels. This concentration range was chosen based on established literature, which indicates that CNT additions within 0.1–1.0 wt% yield optimal enhancements in elastic modulus and strength, while simultaneously mitigating the formation of large agglomerates that could compromise mechanical integrity40,41. Studies by Qian et al.42 and Gorga et al.43 specifically demonstrated that for PMMA/CNT systems, reinforcement levels below 1.0 wt% provide the most favorable balance between mechanical improvement and processability, with minimal agglomeration risk.

The filament fabrication process commenced by weighing the requisite amount of CNTs corresponding to the target weight fraction and dispersing them in dimethylformamide (DMF). To induce de-agglomeration and achieve initial dispersion, the CNT–IPA suspension was subjected to high-energy probe ultrasonication (Model UP400S, Hielscher Ultrasonics, Germany) for 60 min. The microstructure of the post-sonication mixture, presented in Fig. 3a, reveals that the CNTs were precipitated as individual strands or fine clusters, rendering them suitable for melt blending. Subsequently, PMMA granules were dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h to eliminate residual moisture. The dried CNTs and PMMA pellets were then fed into a co-rotating twin-screw extruder (Model ZSK 18 MEGAlab, Coperion, Germany). The extruder featured eight heating zones with temperatures graduated from 170 °C to 215 °C, ensuring the formation of a polymer melt with appropriate viscosity while preventing thermal degradation. A screw speed of 150 rpm and a feed rate of 1.5 kg/h were employed to maximize shear mixing and facilitate the homogeneous distribution of CNTs within the polymeric matrix.

(a) Microstructure of the mixture following ultrasonication, and (b) SEM micrograph of the nanocomposite filament cross-section.

The molten compound was extruded through a circular die, passed through a cooling water bath, and subsequently processed via a dimensional calibration unit to yield the final filament with a nominal diameter of 1.75 ± 0.05 mm. SEM analysis of the filament cross-section, shown in Fig. 3b, confirmed a uniform micro-scale distribution of CNTs within the matrix, with no evidence of severe agglomeration. Additive manufacturing process

Specimen fabrication, encompassing tensile bars and chiral lattice panels, was executed utilizing a Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) 3D printer. The printing process employed the prepared filaments of neat PMMA and PMMA/CNT nanocomposites with weight fractions of 0.2% and 0.8%. A Raise3D Pro2 printer (Raise3D Inc., USA), equipped with a 0.4 mm diameter brass nozzle, was selected for this study due to its capability to maintain high extrusion temperatures and thermal stability, which are critical for processing PMMA.

PMMA is hygroscopic due to polar carbonyl (C=O) groups along its molecular chain, which form hydrogen bonds with atmospheric water molecules44,45. During FDM printing at 220–240 °C, absorbed moisture vaporizes rapidly within the nozzle, creating steam bubbles that become entrapped upon cooling. These bubbles form microscopic voids that act as stress concentration sites, reduce the load-bearing cross-section, and disrupt molecular chain diffusion across layer interfaces, thereby weakening inter-layer adhesion46,47. As quantitatively demonstrated by Wang et al.46, porosity levels of 3–5%—consistent with our observations in undried specimens—can reduce tensile strength by up to 20–25% in 3D-printed polymers. This mechanism necessitated the drying protocol employed in this study to achieve void-free microstructures and optimal mechanical performance. To mitigate porosity formation, filaments were subjected to a drying protocol in a hot air oven at 60 °C for 12 h prior to printing. To achieve high-fidelity structures, the printing parameters were optimized as detailed in Table 2. The combination of these settings facilitates a controlled cooling rate, which, according to recent literature48,49, is instrumental in alleviating residual stresses and augmenting inter-layer adhesion in PMMA constructs. Studies have shown that optimized thermal histories promote molecular diffusion across layer interfaces, directly enhancing mechanical performance50.

To characterize the baseline mechanical properties of the PMMA/CNT nanocomposite, tensile specimens were designed and fabricated in accordance with the ASTM D638 Type IV standard. The specimens were printed using the prepared filaments of neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT. The printing was conducted with a raster angle of 0° (aligned with the longitudinal axis of the specimen) to maximize alignment with the loading direction and to minimize anisotropic effects arising from layer deposition. Three replicates were fabricated for each material composition to facilitate data averaging and statistical analysis. To investigate the compressive behavior of the proposed enhanced cellular panel, two distinct chiral configurations—conventional chiral and enhanced chiral with circular cores—were designed and fabricated via FDM. The geometry of these structures is predicated on a repeating pattern of chiral unit cells, characterized by inclined ligaments and circular nodes. The primary distinction between the two topologies lies in the presence or absence of a circular core at the chiral nodes. In the conventional chiral structure, ligament connectivity is established through a narrow junction point, whereas in the enhanced configuration, a circular ring with radius R is incorporated at the nodes. This modification aims to facilitate a more uniform load transfer, mitigate local stress concentrations, and augment lateral stiffness.

The geometric configurations of both structures are presented in Fig. 6, where the overall dimensions of the conventional panel are approximately 58 mm × 105 mm. These dimensions were selected to ensure that a sufficient number of unit cells are included within each panel, thereby preserving cellular periodicity and guaranteeing reliable and repeatable mechanical measurements.

(a) Geometric dimensions of the panel featuring enhanced chiral unit cells, and (b) Conventional and enhanced chiral specimens fabricated from neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT.

Both structures were designed using SolidWorks software. During geometric modeling, ligament thickness and junction points were meticulously adjusted in accordance with the printing nozzle diameter (0.4 mm) and minimum printable feature size constraints to prevent layer delamination or geometric instability during fabrication. For the conventional chiral structure, the effective ligament length is 16 mm as shown, while in the enhanced structure, the initial ligament length was measured at approximately 13 mm. The orientation and angle of the ligaments were set according to the repeating chiral pattern, ensuring pattern uniformity across both panels. Upon completion of the design phase, the models were exported in high-resolution STL format to ensure that the curvature of the circular regions and fillets was accurately captured during the printing process.

The additive manufacturing of the panels was executed using the Raise3D Pro2 printer, utilizing the synthesized filaments of neat PMMA and PMMA/CNT nanocomposites (reinforced with 0.2 wt% carbon nanotubes). A horizontal build orientation was selected to ensure that all ligaments were deposited continuously within the build plane. This orientation is critical for minimizing inter-layer discontinuities and preventing premature ligament failure under compressive loading. The printing parameters were configured in accordance with Table 2. To compensate for the elevated melt viscosity induced by the CNT inclusion, the extrusion flow multiplier was adjusted to 103%. All fabrication processes were conducted under controlled environmental conditions (23–25 °C) to mitigate thermal warping or cracking.

Post-fabrication, the panels were subjected to a rigorous geometric inspection. The overall panel dimensions, ligament strut thicknesses, and circular core diameters were measured utilizing a digital caliper and an optical microscope. Inspection results confirmed that all dimensional deviations fell within the prescribed tolerance range of ± 0.5 mm. Furthermore, microstructural examination of critical regions, specifically the ligament-ring junctions, revealed that the enhanced structure exhibited superior inter-layer bonding and increased contact area between deposited strands. This attribute is anticipated to play a pivotal role in enhancing the compressive response and energy absorption capacity of the chiral panels.

Ultimately, the fabricated panels were categorized into four distinct groups (conventional and enhanced chiral configurations made of neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT) in preparation for quasi-static compression testing. Direct tensile testing

To characterize the baseline mechanical properties of the PMMA/CNT nanocomposite and validate the RVE model, direct tensile tests were performed on standard ASTM D638 Type IV specimens. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the tests were conducted using a Zwick universal testing machine with a load capacity of 10 kN. The apparatus was equipped with pneumatic grips specifically designed for thermoplastics and a load cell with an accuracy of 0.5%, enabling high-frequency data acquisition of load and displacement. In accordance with ASTM D638 Type IV specifications for most plastic tests, a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min was selected for the loading phase51. This speed is prescribed for Type IV specimens to ensure quasi-static testing conditions and accurate characterization of modulus, yield stress, and post-yield behavior for rigid polymers such as PMMA.

Direct tensile testing setup for standard ASTM D638 specimens.

To enhance strain measurement precision, an Instron AVE 2 video extensometer with a resolution of 1 μm was utilized. The initial gauge length for each specimen was measured prior to testing and inputted into the control software. Following specimen mounting, a slight pre-load of 1 N was applied to eliminate any slack in the loading train. Stress-strain curves were derived from the recorded load-displacement data; engineering stress was calculated by dividing the applied load by the initial cross-sectional area of the gauge region, while engineering strain was determined by dividing the extensometer-measured displacement by the initial gauge length. These stress-strain curves served as reference data for calibrating the RVE model and determining the equivalent material properties for the subsequent numerical modeling phase.

Compressive testing of chiral lattice panels

To evaluate the compressive behavior of the chiral lattice panels and investigate the influence of geometry and material composition, quasi-static compression tests were conducted on the additively manufactured specimens. These experiments were designed in accordance with the principles of the ASTM D695 standard. The primary objectives were to determine the effective compressive modulus, plateau stress, and energy absorption capacity, as well as to analyze the buckling and failure mechanisms in both the conventional and enhanced circular-core chiral geometries.

As depicted in Fig. 8, the compression tests were performed using a SANTAM universal testing machine with a load capacity of 10 kN and a displacement recording system with a resolution of 1 μm. The compression platens were fabricated from hardened steel and featured highly polished surfaces to ensure uniform load transmission to the upper surface of the panel. A minor pre-load of 5 N was applied to establish full contact between the panel and the platens. Subsequently, loading was initiated at a constant displacement rate of 1.5 mm/min. With reference to ASTM D695 standard, the recommended loading rate for compressive testing of rigid plastics is 1.3 ± 0.3 mm/min, and the rate of 1.5 mm/min selected in this study falls within this acceptable range. This rate ensures quasi-static loading conditions, enabling accurate characterization of the sequential deformation mechanisms—including elastic response, ligament buckling, plateau propagation, and densification—without introducing inertial effects or rate-dependent material behavior. During the loading process, force and displacement data were acquired at a sampling rate of 50 Hz. These experimental data were subsequently utilized for the validation of the FE model and for the parametric study of the geometric design.

Compressive testing setup for chiral lattice panels.

To evaluate the compressive behavior of the chiral lattice panels and investigate the influence of geometry and material composition, quasi-static compression tests were conducted on the additively manufactured specimens. For each of the four panel configurations—conventional chiral (neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT) and enhanced circular-core chiral (neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT)—three valid specimens were tested, resulting in a total of 12 compression tests. This sample size (n = 3 per condition) is consistent with common practice in the additive manufacturing of lattice structures literature11 and provided sufficient data for model validation and parametric analysis, as evidenced by the low error margins reported in “Validation of the multi-scale FE model”.

RVE modeling and multi-scale homogenization

To accurately incorporate the nanoscale reinforcing effects of CNTs into the macroscale mechanical response of the chiral lattice panels, a three-phase RVE was developed. The RVE consists of (i) the PMMA matrix, (ii) individual CNTs, and (iii) an interphase zone representing molecular-level interactions between the polymer chains and CNT surfaces. This framework enables the extraction of homogenized, effective mechanical properties for CNT contents ranging from 0.0 to 1.0 wt%, which were subsequently used as material inputs for the continuum-scale FE model of the lattice panels.

It should be noted that the RVE model was calibrated using tensile test data, as tensile loading provides a well-defined uniform stress state ideal for extracting intrinsic material properties (elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and plastic hardening parameters). While polymers can exhibit tension-compression asymmetry, particularly in yield stress52,53, studies have shown that for PMMA at room temperature below its glass transition temperature, this asymmetry is relatively modest in the elastic regime, and the von Mises yield criterion with isotropic hardening provides acceptable accuracy for structural analysis54. Furthermore, the dominant failure mechanism in the chiral lattice under compression is elastic buckling and progressive collapse of ligaments, which is governed primarily by the elastic modulus and initial yield stress—parameters that were accurately captured by the tensile-calibrated RVE model. The excellent agreement between experimental and numerical force-displacement curve confirms the validity of this approach for the present study.

RVE modeling and determination of nanocomposite filament properties

To accurately translate the mechanical behavior of the PMMA/CNT nanocomposite from the microstructural scale to the structural scale of the chiral lattice panels, this study employed a multi-scale computational homogenization framework based on the RVE. In this approach, the microstructure—comprising the polymeric matrix, CNTs, and the interphase region—is explicitly modeled within a three-dimensional RVE. By applying periodic boundary conditions, the effective equivalent properties of the nanocomposite are subsequently extracted.

The RVE was defined as a three-dimensional cube with dimension LRVE​, within which a stochastic network of Single CNTs is distributed throughout the PMMA matrix. The average diameter and length of the CNTs were selected consistent with the manufacturer’s specifications (average diameter 5–12 nm and length ≈ 2 μm) and were modeled as straight cylindrical fibers with a high aspect ratio. The initial orientation and position of each CNT were generated using uniform random variables within the RVE space to produce a statistically representative microstructure.

Contrary to statistical approaches wherein CNTs intersecting the RVE boundary are either discarded or repositioned, this study utilized Material Periodic Conditions. This implies that any segment of a nanotube exiting one face of the RVE is re-entered at the corresponding coordinates on the opposite face, simulating the RVE as part of an infinite lattice of repeating cells. This algorithm, developed for both straight and wavy CNTs, ensures that the morphology and connectivity of the nanotube network are maintained continuously across all spatial directions, while allowing the RVE size to remain computationally manageable. For each target CNT weight fraction (0.2 to 1.0 wt%), number of nanotubes was adjusted to satisfy equivalent volume fraction.

Numerical analysis of the RVE was performed using ABAQUS/Standard. The PMMA matrix was modeled as an elastoplastic material with isotropic hardening, based on the stress-strain curve obtained from tensile testing. Initial elastic properties (Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio) were derived from the linear portion of the curve, while plastic behavior was extracted from the non-linear regime55. The CNTs were modeled as a rigid reinforcement phase with linear elastic behavior and a modulus in the range of 1.2 TPa. To account for the interphase effect, a zero-thickness CZM element surrounding each CNT was defined, possessing properties intermediate between the matrix and the CNT, ensuring perfect bonding between the two phases. This three-phase architecture facilitates a more realistic stress transfer from the matrix to the CNTs. The RVE was meshed using C3D8R solid elements. To accurately capture stress and strain gradients in the vicinity of the CNTs and the interphase layer, a finer mesh size was employed around the reinforcement phase, whereas a coarser mesh was used in the matrix regions distant from the CNTs to reduce computational costs.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs)

The CNTs were modeled as linear elastic, isotropic reinforcements with properties obtained from the manufacturer’s specifications (US Research Inc., USA). These values are consistent with the established literature for single-walled carbon nanotubes56. The high aspect ratio (length/diameter ≈ 166–1000) justifies their representation as cylindrical fibers within the RVE.

Interphase region

The interphase between CNT and PMMA matrix was modeled using zero-thickness cohesive zone elements (CZM) with a bilinear traction-separation law. This approach accounts for load transfer across the interface and potential debonding. The cohesive parameters were obtained through calibration against the experimental tensile stress-strain curve of the PMMA/0.2CNT nanocomposite. The calibration process involved systematically adjusting the cohesive stiffness, interfacial strength, and fracture energy within physically reasonable bounds until the RVE-predicted effective stress-strain response closely matched the experimental data (discrepancies < 5% for modulus and < 8% for yield stress). Finite element modeling of chiral lattice panels

To simulate the compressive behavior of the chiral lattice panels, three-dimensional FE modeling was performed using ABAQUS/Standard. Two geometric configurations, comprising the conventional chiral structure and the enhanced version featuring a circular core, were modeled in CAD software consistent with the dimensions of the printed specimens and subsequently imported into the ABAQUS environment as STWP files. To prevent element distortion, appropriate geometric partitioning and virtual topology tools were utilized in the curved regions of the rings and nodes. Meshing was executed using C3D8R reduced-integration solid elements (8-node linear brick with reduced integration and hourglass control). To ensure numerical accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency, a systematic mesh convergence study was performed. The primary target parameter for convergence was the peak compressive load of the enhanced chiral panel, as this represents the most critical design metric and is sensitive to local stress concentrations in the curved regions of the circular core and ligament junctions.

The mechanical properties of the material, including Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and the equivalent stress-strain curve derived from the RVE analysis for various CNT weight fractions (0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 wt%), were defined in the model as a homogenized elastoplastic material with isotropic hardening. Consequently, the non-linear behavior of the PMMA/CNT nanocomposite was incorporated into the structural analysis without the necessity for explicit CNT modeling at the macro-scale. Boundary conditions were applied to replicate the experimental compression test setup. The bottom surface of the panel was fully constrained (ENCASTRED), while a Rigid Plate was positioned on the top surface and coupled to the specimen via a Tie constraint. Compressive loading was applied under displacement control up to a strain of 40% to reproduce the three distinct behavioral regimes: the linear elastic phase, the plateau region, and the densification stage.

In the quasi-static analysis, the NLGEOM = ON option was activated to account for geometric non-linearities arising from buckling and large deformations. The modified Newton-Raphson solver, coupled with Automatic Time Stepping, was employed, with an initial time increment of 10− 4 to capture the onset of buckling without divergence. This numerical model facilitates the accurate reproduction of the force-displacement curve, buckling behavior, plateau stress, and energy absorption capacity of the chiral panels, serving as a basis for experimental validation and subsequent parametric analyses. Validation of the multi-scale FE model

For tensile characterization, three specimens were fabricated and tested for each material composition (neat PMMA and PMMA/0.2CNT). While ASTM D638 recommends a minimum of five specimens for full statistical characterization of materials, the primary objective of these tensile tests in the present study was to validate the multi-scale RVE model predictions rather than to establish comprehensive design allowables. The three specimens per condition provided sufficient data to: (i) confirm the reinforcing trend of CNTs, (ii) validate the RVE model predictions (with discrepancies < 5% for modulus and < 8% for yield stress), and (iii) establish baseline properties for subsequent finite element analysis. The coefficient of variation among the three specimens was consistently below 5%, indicating good reproducibility. Similar approaches with n = 3 have been reported in recent additive manufacturing literature for preliminary validation studies36,38. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a larger sample size would enhance statistical power, and future studies should employ five or more specimens for comprehensive characterization.

The statistical reliability of the tensile data was assessed through the coefficient of variation (COV) for each property. RVE size convergence study

To confirm that the selected representative volume truly reflects the microstructural behavior of the nanocomposite without size effects, a systematic RVE size convergence study was performed. Four cubic RVEs with edge lengths of 0.75 μm, 1.0 μm, 1.5 μm, and 2.0 μm were generated (Fig. 9), each containing a stochastic distribution of CNTs corresponding to 0.5 wt% reinforcement. For each RVE size, five independent random realizations were created to account for statistical variations in CNT distribution. All RVEs were subjected to periodic boundary conditions and uniaxial tensile loading, and the effective Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and yield stress were extracted using volume-averaging relations.

The convergence criteria were defined as: (i) a relative change of less than 3% in mean properties between successive RVE sizes, and (ii) a coefficient of variation (COV) below 5% across random realizations, indicating that the RVE captures a statistically representative microstructure. The results of the convergence study are summarized in Table 7. The 0.75 μm RVE exhibited high variability (COV > 8%) and significant size effects, indicating it is too small to be statistically representative. The 1.0 μm RVE showed improved stability but still exceeded the 3% change criterion relative to the next size. The 1.5 μm RVE satisfied both convergence criteria, with a Young’s modulus change of only 2.3% from the 1.0 μm RVE and a COV of 2.4% across realizations. The 2.0 μm RVE showed negligible improvement (0.3% change in modulus) at significantly higher computational cost.

The curve for R = 3 mm, despite minor fluctuations, encompasses the largest area under the curve, indicating the highest energy absorption capacity among the geometries studied.

Figure 20 depicts the deformation patterns of the enhanced chiral panels for various circular core radii, indicating that increasing the core diameter plays a decisive role in the distribution of deformation, the load-bearing path, and the buckling stability of the structure. In the specimen with a smaller core radius, deformations are predominantly concentrated at ligament junctions, and high-intensity local buckling occurs; this precipitates rapid structural softening and a sudden drop in force. With the incorporation of a core and the increase in its radius, the deformation pattern shifts towards a more distributed and uniform mode, with buckling propagating across multiple cell rows. This behavior demonstrates that the circular core, by increasing the contact area and providing stable load paths, reduces stress concentration and enhances the structure’s resistance to local buckling. As the radius reaches a larger value (R = 3 mm), the structure exhibits stiffer behavior and delayed buckling; deformations are less localized, and the overall structural deformation proceeds more gradually. This results in increased load-bearing capacity, prolonged stability in the plateau, and delayed entry into the densification stage. Thus, Fig. 20 clearly illustrates that increasing the circular core radius transitions the buckling mode from a localized and brittle state to a widespread and controlled state, ultimately enhancing the strength, stiffness, and energy absorption capacity of the chiral panels.

Figure 21 presents the influence of ligament length and thickness on peak force and absorbed energy. The results in Fig. 21a indicate that as ligament thickness increases, both peak force and absorbed energy initially rise, reaching a maximum at a thickness of approximately 3 mm. Beyond this thickness, both parameters exhibit a slight decline. This behavior suggests that 3 mm represents an optimal thickness for the examined geometry and material, providing the best balance between stiffness, load-bearing capacity, and energy absorption capability. At greater thicknesses, although the local stiffness of ligaments is higher, the structure enters the densification phase more rapidly, leading to a loss of some energy absorption potential.

The results in Fig. 21b show that with increasing ligament length, the peak force initially rises, reaches an optimum at Lopt​=14 mm with Fpeak​ = 677 N, and subsequently decreases. This behavior stems from changes in the ligament slenderness ratio; shorter ligaments possess higher load-bearing capacity due to greater stiffness, whereas excessively long lengths increase the structure’s sensitivity to flexural buckling, impeding further growth in peak force. The absorbed energy–ligament length curve (Fig. 21b) revealed that as ligament length increases, the panel’s energy absorption capacity initially improves, reaching its optimum at 14 mm. This is attributed to increased structural flexibility and the gradual propagation of buckling in longer ligaments. Beyond this value, the structure becomes more susceptible to flexural instability, resulting in reduced energy absorption. Therefore, ligament length governs the transition of the buckling mechanism from localized and brittle to widespread and stable. Consequently, a ligament length in the range of 12–14 mm yields the best performance in terms of stability and energy absorption. The results indicate that each of these parameters, both independently and in combination, can significantly influence the load-bearing path, buckling mechanism, and densification extent of the structure.

It is important to acknowledge that the material properties used in the finite element simulations were derived from tensile tests and RVE homogenization. While this approach proved successful for predicting the compressive response of the chiral lattice panels—as evidenced by the close agreement with experimental results—materials exhibiting significant tension-compression asymmetry may require independent compressive characterization. Future studies involving different polymer systems or loading conditions should consider this factor in their experimental design.

Mechanistic role of CNT reinforcement on deformation behavior

To elucidate the relationship between CNT reinforcement and the deformation mechanisms of the chiral lattice structures, a detailed analysis was conducted at the ligament level. This investigation focused on three key aspects: (i) buckling onset, (ii) ligament rotation, and (iii) stress distribution, comparing the response of neat PMMA and PMMA/0.5CNT (optimal reinforcement) in the enhanced circular-core configuration.

Effect on buckling onset

The addition of CNTs increases the elastic modulus and yield stress of the matrix material (Table 6), which directly enhances the flexural rigidity (EI) of the ligaments. For a slender column under compression, the Euler buckling load is proportional to the elastic modulus. The increased modulus from 2.13 GPa (neat PMMA) to 3.65 GPa (PMMA/0.5CNT) results in a 71% increase in ligament bending stiffness. Consequently, the critical buckling load of the enhanced chiral structure increased by 139% (from 284.6 N to 681.2 N), and the displacement at buckling onset was delayed from 1.23 to 1.50 mm (Table 10). This confirms that CNT reinforcement delays the onset of local buckling in the compressed ligaments.

Effect on ligament rotation

The chiral deformation mechanism inherently involves rotation of the central nodes and bending of the inclined ligaments under compression. The rotational response was quantified by monitoring the angular displacement of the ligaments relative to their original orientation throughout the compression test. As shown in Fig. 22a, at 50% global compressive strain, the maximum ligament rotation in the neat PMMA structure reached 17.8°, while in the PMMA/0.5CNT structure, it was only 12.5°—a 30% reduction. This reduced rotation is attributed to the increased flexural rigidity of the CNT-reinforced ligaments, which resist bending deformation and delay the formation of plastic hinges. The reduced rotation also contributes to a more stable plateau region by preventing premature collapse of the unit cells.

Effect on stress distribution

CNT reinforcement facilitates more uniform stress distribution within the ligaments, mitigating detrimental stress concentrations at critical locations. Figure 22b presents the von Mises stress contours at the onset of buckling (displacement corresponding to peak load). In the neat PMMA structure, severe stress concentrations (up to 42 MPa) develop at the ligament-node junctions, leading to early local yielding and subsequent buckling. In contrast, the PMMA/0.5CNT structure exhibits a maximum stress of 61 MPa at the same locations, with a more uniform stress distribution along the ligament length. This improved stress distribution delays the formation of plastic hinges and extends the stable deformation regime.

Synergy with enhanced geometry

The beneficial effects of CNT reinforcement are amplified when combined with the enhanced circular-core geometry. The circular core provides a larger load-transfer area at the nodes, reducing stress concentrations and enabling more efficient utilization of the stiffer CNT-reinforced material. As shown in Fig. 12, the peak load increase due to CNT reinforcement is 41% for the conventional chiral structure but 71.5% for the enhanced circular-core structure, demonstrating a clear synergistic effect.

CNT reinforcement delays buckling onset by increasing ligament stiffness, reduces ligament rotation by enhancing flexural rigidity, and promotes uniform stress distribution by mitigating stress concentrations. These mechanistic improvements, when combined with the enhanced circular-core geometry, yield superior compressive performance characterized by higher peak loads, extended stable plateau regions, and increased energy absorption capacity.

Conclusion

In this study, a multi-scale finite element framework was established to evaluate the compressive performance of additively manufactured chiral lattice panels fabricated from PMMA/CNT nanocomposites. Initially, the effective mechanical properties of the nanocomposite were derived employing a three-phase RVE model, explicitly incorporating the PMMA matrix, CNTs, and the interphase region. RVE analysis across various CNT weight fractions (0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 wt%) revealed that the equivalent modulus and yield stress of the nanocomposite were enhanced by up to 70% and 85%, respectively, compared to neat PMMA, with the optimal mechanical performance observed at the 0.5 wt% CNT concentration.

At the structural scale, finite element analysis demonstrated that the enhanced chiral topology featuring a circular core exhibits significantly superior performance compared to the conventional chiral structure. The incorporation of the circular core resulted in a 40–50% increase in peak load, a substantial augmentation in initial stiffness, and the formation of a more stable compressive plateau. Experimental validation corroborated these trends; for instance, in the case of PMMA/0.2CNT, the enhanced structure sustained a peak force of approximately 800 N, whereas the conventional configuration achieved only about 550 N. This disparity underscores the beneficial impact of the modified topology in facilitating a more uniform stress distribution and retarding the onset of initial buckling.

Compared to the conventional core‑less design, the enhanced chiral structure with 0.5 wt% CNT reinforcement demonstrated a 70% improvement in energy absorption capacity. Furthermore, ligament thickness was identified as a determinant parameter, with results suggesting that a thickness of 3 mm provides the optimal equilibrium between initial stiffness, peak load (677 N for PMMA/0.5CNT), and absorbed energy (exceeding 7.7 J). The parametric investigation of the core radius further revealed that increasing the radius from 0 to 3 mm leads to a progressive enhancement in structural stability and an increase in absorbed energy of over 50%.
