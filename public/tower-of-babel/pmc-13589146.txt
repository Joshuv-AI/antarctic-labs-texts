Effects of air velocity applied from 11 to 21 days of age on growth performance, body size traits, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities of Pekin ducks at 42 days of age
Authors: Xingda Yang, Jielin Xie, Yuji Sun, Rihong Guo, Dongyue Sun, Yang Yao, Yuanming Guo, Fang Chen, Shijia Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to determine whether air velocity applied during the brooding period exerts carryover effects on subsequent growth performance, body size traits, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities of Pekin ducks at 42 d of age. A total of 90 healthy 7-d-old male ducks with similar initial body weight were randomly assigned to 3 air velocity treatments (0, 0.5, and 1.0 m/s), with 3 replicates per treatment and 10 ducks per replicate. Air velocity treatments were applied from 11 to 21 d of age, after which all ducks were reared under the same conventional conditions until 42 d. Feed intake and body weight were recorded throughout the experiment, whereas body size traits, slaughter traits, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities were determined at 42 d of age. As a result, air velocity significantly affected BW, ADG, ADFI, and FCR at 42 d of age (P=0.001 for each variable). Ducks exposed to 1.0 m/s during brooding had the highest BW, ADG, and ADFI and the lowest FCR among the three treatments. Oblique body length, keel length, chest depth, chest width, and hip width were also greater in the 1.0 m/s group, whereas several other body size traits were unaffected. Breast muscle yield (P=0.260) and leg muscle yield (P=0.088) were not affected by treatment, whereas abdominal fat weight (P=0.024) and abdominal fat yield (P= 0.020) were higher in the 1.0 m/s group. Among the serum biochemical variables, only UA differed among treatments (P=0.0013), whereas GLU, TP, LDH, AST, ALT, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, and CHOL were unaffected. Activities of lipase, trypsin, amylase, sucrase, maltase, and alkaline phosphatase were generally higher in ducks exposed to 1.0 m/s during brooding, with significant treatment effects observed in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, whereas no significant differences were detected in the cecum. These findings indicate that air velocity applied from 11 to 21 d of age can exert carryover effects on subsequent growth, body development, carcass fat deposition, and luminal digestive enzyme activity in Pekin ducks.

Introduction

As duck production becomes increasingly intensive and environmentally controlled, the role of house microclimate in maintaining bird health and productivity has received growing attention. Compared with the growing period, the brooding period is a particularly sensitive stage in Pekin ducks because thermoregulatory capacity is not yet fully developed. Consequently, environmental management during early life is critical for maintaining normal growth, physiological stability, and later production performance (Tzshentke and Nichelmann, 1999; Xie et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021).

Among environmental factors, temperature and relative humidity have been studied more extensively than air velocity in meat ducks. Previous studies have shown that elevated ambient temperature can impair feed intake, body weight gain, body development, and antioxidant status in Pekin ducks (Xu et al., 2024). Relative humidity is also an important component of thermal load. Sun et al. (2023) reported that production performance and meat quality of Pekin ducks were optimized at moderate relative humidity, whereas excessively high humidity reduced productive performance and affected slaughter and meat quality traits. Further work demonstrated that humidity conditions during the first 21 d posthatch could influence later body size development, blood lipid status, and selected biochemical indices, suggesting that the early-life environment may exert carryover effects on subsequent performance (Sun et al., 2024). Together, these findings indicate that environmental conditions during brooding may have lasting consequences beyond the treatment period.

In contrast, the effects of air velocity during brooding have received comparatively limited attention in ducks, despite its recognized importance in poultry heat exchange. Air velocity influences sensible heat loss, convective heat dissipation, and thermal comfort, particularly under hot conditions (Yahav et al., 2004; Yahav, 2009). Studies in broilers have shown that increasing air velocity can improve feed intake, growth performance, and feed efficiency during heat exposure (May et al., 2000; Simmons et al., 2003). Dynamic airflow has also been shown to alter body surface temperature and behavioral responses in broilers under summer conditions (Akter et al., 2022). In addition to thermal regulation, insufficient air exchange may impair the removal of moisture and harmful gases, thereby worsening house air quality and increasing environmental stress (Swelum et al., 2021). Although these observations support the biological importance of air velocity in poultry production, corresponding evidence in Pekin ducks, especially during the brooding period, remains limited.

Therefore, the present study was designed to determine whether different air velocity treatments applied during brooding could exert lasting effects on later growth performance, body size traits, slaughter characteristics, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities in Pekin ducks at 42 d of age. We hypothesized that appropriate air velocity during the brooding period would improve the early microenvironment, thereby promoting subsequent growth, body development, and intestinal digestive function.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

A completely randomized design with 1 experimental factor was used in this study. A total of 90 healthy 7-d-old male Pekin ducks with similar initial body weight (BW) were randomly allotted to 3 air velocity treatments: 0, 0.5, and 1.0 m/s. Only male ducks were used to minimize potential sex-related variation in growth performance, body conformation, carcass traits, and physiological responses, thereby reducing biological variability among experimental birds. Each treatment consisted of 3 replicates, with 10 ducks per replicate. Air velocity treatments were applied from 11 to 21 d of age. After the treatment period, all birds were transferred to the same conventional environment and reared until 42 d of age to evaluate the carryover effects of brooding-stage air velocity on later growth performance, physiological status, and digestive function. During the experiment, feed intake was recorded daily by replicate, and birds were weighed as a group every 7 d. At 42 d of age, birds were sampled for body size measurements, carcass evaluation, serum biochemical analysis, and intestinal digestive enzyme assays.

Housing and management

The experiment was conducted from July to August 2025 in a single duck house at Gaoyou Duck Group in Jiangsu Province, China. At 7 d of age, ducklings were transferred to specially designed cages (2.0 m × 0.5 m × 1.5 m), and air velocity treatment began at 11 d of age. The cages were sealed with PEP film and each cage was equipped with 6 controllable negative-pressure fans to provide uniform airflow. All cages were positioned at the same horizontal level within the house to minimize environmental variation among treatments. Cage walls and floors were made of stainless steel, and birds were raised on polyethylene prefabricated slatted floors positioned 80 cm above the ground and supported by galvanized steel frames.

Temperature and relative humidity were monitored continuously using sensors installed approximately 50 cm above the slatted floor, and data were recorded automatically every 15 min. Air velocity was regulated by the controllable negative-pressure fans and measured in the bird activity zone using a handheld anemometer 3 times daily, after which the daily mean was calculated. Growth performance and sampling

Feed intake was recorded daily for each replicate throughout the experiment. Ducks were weighed by replicate every 7 d, and growth performance was calculated accordingly. The variables included body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR).Growth performance was further calculated separately for the treatment period (11–21 d), the post-treatment period (22–42 d), and the overall observation period (11–42 d). ADG, ADFI, and FCR were calculated for each replicate during each period, and the replicate was used as the experimental unit for statistical analysis. At 42 d of age, 3 ducks were randomly selected from each replicate (9 ducks per treatment; 27 ducks in total) for blood collection and subsequent slaughter. For carcass traits, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities, measurements obtained from the 3 sampled ducks within each replicate were averaged, and replicate means were used for statistical analysis. Body weight, average daily gain, average daily feed intake, and feed conversion ratio were determined based on replicate-level measurements, following procedures commonly used in Pekin duck growth studies (Wen et al., 2017).

Body size traits and carcass measurements

Before slaughter, feed was withdrawn for 12 h, and body size traits were measured using a measuring tape or vernier caliper. The traits included body length, oblique body length, keel length, chest depth, chest width, shank length, shank circumference, and hip width. Body size and carcass measurements were performed with reference to previously described procedures for Pekin ducks (Sun et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2017).Blood samples were then collected from the wing vein before slaughter. Following body size measurement and blood collection, the ducks were slaughtered, and the breast muscle, leg muscle, abdominal fat, liver, and gizzard were separated and weighed. Breast and leg muscles were collected from one side of each carcass, and the corresponding muscle weights and yields therefore represent unilateral measurements. Liver and gizzard weights were recorded as absolute weights and expressed in grams (g); relative liver and gizzard weights were not calculated.Breast muscle yield, leg muscle yield, and abdominal fat yield were calculated as follows:Breastmuscleyield(%)=breastmuscleweight/evisceratedweight×100%Legmuscleyield(%)=legmuscleweight/evisceratedweight×100%Abdominalfatyield(%)=abdominalfatweight/(abdominalfatweight+evisceratedweight)×100%

Serum biochemical analysis

At 42 d of age, blood samples were collected from the wing vein of the selected ducks before slaughter. Blood was transferred into tubes without anticoagulant, kept at 4°C, and centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 15 min under refrigerated conditions. Serum was separated and stored at −25°C until analysis. Serum biochemical variables included glucose (GLU), uric acid (UA), total protein (TP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (CHOL). Serum biochemical analyses were performed by Nanjing KingMed Center for Clinical Laboratory Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) using routine clinical biochemical procedures. GLU was determined using the hexokinase method, UA using the uricase method, TP using the biuret method, LDH, AST, and ALT using kinetic rate methods, LDL-C and HDL-C using direct methods, TG using an enzymatic method, and CHOL using the cholesterol oxidase method.

Intestinal digestive enzyme assays

Immediately after slaughter at 42 d of age, the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum were isolated, and intestinal contents were collected and stored in tissue preservation solution. Activities of trypsin, amylase, alkaline phosphatase, maltase, sucrase, and lipase were determined by colorimetric methods using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The catalog numbers were as follows: total protein (Cat. No. A045-2-3), amylase (Cat. No. C016-1-2), lipase (Cat. No. A054-2-1), trypsin (Cat. No. A082-2), alkaline phosphatase (Cat. No. A059-2-1), sucrase (Cat. No. A082-2-1), and maltase (Cat. No. A082-3-1). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.The selection of intestinal segments and digestive enzyme-related measurements was based on previous studies of intestinal digestive function in Pekin ducks (King et al., 2000).

Statistical analysis

All data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data that were not normally distributed were log-transformed and re-evaluated; if normality was still not achieved, nonparametric methods were to be used. Data meeting the assumption of normality were analyzed by 1-way ANOVA using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to separate treatment means when the overall treatment effect was significant. Significance was declared at P < 0.05. Data are presented as means ± SD. The replicate was considered the experimental unit for growth performance, and replicate means were used as the experimental unit for carcass traits, serum biochemical variables, and intestinal digestive enzyme activities.

During the post-treatment period (22–42d), ducks previously exposed to 1.0 m/s maintained a higher ADG (P=0.0011) and ADFI (P=0.001) than the other groups. FCR also differed among treatments (P=0.0357), with the 1.0 m/s group showing a lower value than the 0 m/s group and the 0.5 m/s group showing an intermediate response. At 42 d, BW was highest in the 1.0 m/s group, intermediate in the 0.5 m/s group, and lowest in the 0 m/s group (P=0.001). Over the entire 11–42d period, ducks in the 1.0 m/s group had the highest ADG and ADFI and the lowest FCR (P=0.001).

These results indicate that the growth advantage associated with 1.0 m/s began during the air-velocity treatment period and persisted after the treatment had ceased, becoming more pronounced during subsequent growth.

Discussion

Air velocity should not be regarded only as a physical environmental parameter; it may also indirectly affect intestinal function by modifying the local microenvironment around the birds. Increased air velocity can enhance convective heat dissipation and reduce the birds’ perceived temperature and local humidity around the body surface, thereby improving thermal comfort during the brooding period. A suitable brooding environment is essential for maintaining normal physiological activity, feed intake, and nutrient utilization in meat ducks, ultimately influencing growth performance and health status. Therefore, appropriate ventilation conditions during early growth may promote body weight gain and improve feed efficiency in poultry.

The present study demonstrated that air velocity applied during the brooding period exerted measurable carryover effects on the later performance of Pekin ducks. Although all birds were reared under the same conventional conditions after 21 d of age, ducks exposed to 1.0 m/s during brooding showed greater BW, ADG, and ADFI and a lower FCR at 42 d of age than ducks exposed to 0 or 0.5 m/s. These findings indicate that the early-life air environment may influence subsequent growth responses beyond the treatment period. The stage-specific analysis further showed that the growth response to air velocity developed progressively. During the 11–21 d treatment period, both the 0.5 and 1.0 m/s groups had higher ADG than the 0 m/s group, indicating that the growth response had already emerged during air-velocity exposure. Importantly, during the post-treatment period from 22 to 42 d, when all ducks were maintained under the same environmental conditions, ducks previously exposed to 1.0 m/s continued to show higher ADG than those in the 0 and 0.5 m/s groups. ADFI showed a similar persistent response, while FCR remained lower in the 1.0 m/s group than in the 0 m/s group. These findings suggest that exposure to 1.0 m/s during 11–21 d produced not only an immediate growth response but also a measurable carryover effect on subsequent growth after the air-velocity treatment had ceased.Previous studies have demonstrated that environmental conditions during the brooding period can exert lasting effects on Pekin ducks. In particular, relative humidity treatment during the first 21 d post-hatch has been reported to influence subsequent body size development and selected biochemical indices, although it did not significantly alter growth performance at 42 d of age (Sun et al., 2024). In the present study, the average ambient temperature during the treatment period was approximately 29°C, accompanied by relatively high relative humidity, indicating that the birds were exposed to a warm and humid environment. Under such conditions, increased air velocity may enhanced local heat dissipation in the vicinity of the birds, thereby alleviating thermal load and facilitating feed intake and growth. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that heat stress depresses feed intake and growth in poultry and that appropriate air movement can enhance convective heat loss and improve thermal comfort (Lin et al., 2006; Yahav, 2009; Lara and Rostagno, 2013; Xu et al., 2024).

Body size results further support the view that brooding-stage air velocity influenced later growth and body development. Ducks in the 1.0 m/s group showed greater oblique body length, keel length, chest depth, chest width, and hip width than ducks in the lower air velocity groups, whereas shank length and shank circumference were not affected. Body size traits are generally associated with growth performance and, to some extent, reflect muscle deposition and carcass potential in poultry (Kleczek et al., 2006). Previous studies have also shown that environmental conditions during early life can influence later body development (Moraes et al., 2002; Sun et al., 2024). In the present study, the pattern observed for body size traits was generally consistent with that of BW, suggesting that brooding-stage air velocity may influence later body conformation through its effects on overall growth and development. Similar relationships between body size development and body weight have been reported previously (Wu et al., 2020). Not all body size traits responded in the same manner. Traits more closely related to trunk development and soft tissue deposition, such as chest width, chest depth, and keel length, appeared to be more responsive to treatment, which is in agreement with previous reports linking these traits to breast muscle yield, carcass composition, and body weight in poultry (Weimer et al., 2020). In contrast, shank-related traits may reflect bony linear growth to a greater extent and may therefore be less sensitive to environmental variation, being more strongly influenced by age-related development and genetic background (Kokoszyński et al., 2019).

Most slaughter traits were not significantly affected by brooding-stage air velocity, although numerical differences were observed for several variables. In particular, ducks in the 1.0 m/s group tended to have greater breast muscle weight and breast muscle yield than those in the lower air velocity groups, but these differences did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that, under the present experimental conditions, appropriate air velocity may have contributed to improved muscle deposition, but the magnitude of this effect was limited relative to the variation among replicates. In contrast, abdominal fat weight and abdominal fat yield were significantly higher in ducks exposed to 1.0 m/s. Although the 1.0 m/s treatment improved growth performance and FCR, the increased abdominal fat deposition indicates that the response was not uniformly beneficial. Greater abdominal fat suggests that a proportion of the additional retained energy may have been partitioned toward adipose tissue rather than exclusively toward lean tissue growth. From the perspective of carcass composition, excessive abdominal fat is generally undesirable because it represents non-lean tissue deposition and may reduce carcass value. Importantly, the improved FCR observed in the 1.0 m/s group does not necessarily indicate greater efficiency of lean tissue deposition, because feed conversion reflects total body-weight gain rather than the composition of that gain. Therefore, the present results suggest a potential trade-off between improved growth performance and increased carcass fat deposition under the 1.0 m/s treatment. Further studies incorporating detailed carcass composition, muscle-to-fat ratio, and body composition measurements are needed to determine whether the growth advantage associated with higher air velocity is accompanied by a less favorable carcass composition. In addition, the tendency for greater breast muscle deposition and lower leg muscle proportion in the 1.0 m/s group is broadly consistent with the report of Zhang et al. (2012), who showed that chronic heat stress altered muscle distribution in broilers. Although direct comparisons should be made cautiously because species and experimental conditions differed, the present findings suggest that air velocity during brooding may influence later carcass composition, mainly through changes in energy deposition rather than broad changes in slaughter yield.

Serum biochemical responses were limited, with UA being the only variable that differed among treatments. Ducks in the 0.5 m/s group had lower serum UA than ducks in the 0 and 1.0 m/s groups, whereas most serum variables remained unchanged, suggesting that brooding-stage air velocity did not induce broad long-term disruption of systemic biochemical homeostasis. The treatment difference observed for UA may indicate variation in nitrogen metabolism. Elevated UA is generally associated with increased nitrogenous end-product formation and may be related to more active amino acid catabolism or a greater metabolic burden of nitrogen-containing compounds (Machín et al., 2004; van Milgen, 2021). In this study, ducks in the 1.0 m/s group achieved better growth performance despite having higher UA, which may reflect greater overall metabolic activity under a more favorable early-life environment. Reports in broilers have also shown that heat stress may increase UA and impair performance, whereas chronic heat stress may elevate AST and reduce weight gain (Attia and Hassan, 2017; Miah et al., 2026). In the present study, although AST did not differ significantly among treatments, its numerical values tended to decrease as air velocity increased, while growth performance improved. Collectively, these findings suggest that elevated air velocity during brooding may contribute to alleviate thermal burden and support later growth. However, in the absence of direct measurements of heat stress or protein turnover indicators, the biological mechanisms underlying the UA response remains to be clarified in future studies.

The intestinal digestive enzyme data provide additional evidence that brooding-stage air velocity may influence later digestive physiology. In general, ducks exposed to 1.0 m/s showed higher activities of lipase, trypsin, amylase, sucrase, maltase, and alkaline phosphatase in the small intestine. Significant treatment effects on these six digestive enzymes were observed in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, whereas no significant treatment effects were detected in the cecum. This segment-specific pattern is biologically plausible because the small intestine is the principal site of enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption in poultry, and previous research has demonstrated segment-dependent development of intestinal digestive functions in Pekin ducks (King et al., 2000). In contrast, the cecum has a more prominent role in microbial fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production, which may partly explain the weaker response of digestive enzyme activities in this segment (Hao et al., 2022). The intestinal health of poultry is closely related to productive performance, and the intestinal environment is influenced not only by nutrition and microbiota but also by external environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity, airflow, and pollutant concentrations (Drury, 1966; Ahmadi Babadi et al., 2022). Previous studies on poultry air velocity have focused mainly on growth and physiological responses (Yahav et al., 2001; Hossain et al., 2025), whereas the present study extends these observations by showing that digestive physiology may also be involved. Digestive enzyme activities can provide information on digestive processes, and brush-border enzymes such as sucrase, maltase, and alkaline phosphatase are closely associated with intestinal maturation and epithelial function when measured directly in intestinal mucosal tissue (Uni et al., 1998; Iji et al., 2001; Lan et al., 2024). However, because the enzyme activities in the present study were measured in intestinal contents rather than intestinal mucosa, these responses should be interpreted primarily as changes in luminal digestive enzyme activity rather than as direct evidence of enhanced mucosal maturation or epithelial function. Therefore, the present results suggest that exposure to 1.0 m/s during brooding was associated with altered digestive enzyme activity in the intestinal lumen later in life, which may partly contribute to the observed carryover effects on subsequent performance.

Conclusion

Air velocity applied specifically from 11 to 21 d of age produced measurable carryover effects on Pekin ducks at 42 d. Compared with 0 and 0.5 m/s, exposure to 1.0 m/s during this period was associated with higher BW, ADG, and ADFI, lower FCR, and greater development of several body-size traits. The 1.0 m/s treatment was also associated with higher digestive enzyme activities in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, whereas no significant treatment effects were detected in the cecum. However, the same treatment increased abdominal fat weight and yield, indicating a potential trade-off between improved growth performance and increased fat deposition. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that short-term air-velocity exposure during 11 to 21 d of age can influence subsequent growth trajectory, body development, carcass fat deposition, and luminal digestive enzyme activity in Pekin ducks.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, gas concentrations were not monitored inside the cages, so it was not possible to determine whether the observed effects of air velocity were mediated only through thermal regulation or also through changes in air quality. Second, although the study revealed carryover effects on growth, body development, and intestinal enzyme activity, direct measurements of body temperature, behavioral responses, oxidative stress, and intestinal morphology were not included. These variables would help clarify the mechanisms linking brooding-stage air velocity to later performance. In addition, only male Pekin ducks were included in the present study; therefore, extrapolation of these findings to female ducks should be made with caution.The relatively small number of independent replicates per treatment (n=3) represents an important limitation of the present study and may reduce the precision and generalizability of the estimated treatment effects. Although replicate means were used as the experimental units to avoid pseudoreplication, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously and should be validated in future studies using a larger number of independent replicate pens. Finally, the present findings were obtained under summer conditions characterized by relatively high temperature and humidity, and thus extrapolation to other seasons or production systems should be made with caution.
