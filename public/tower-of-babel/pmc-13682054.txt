Urorectal septum malformation sequence presenting with complete penoscrotal transposition, urethral atresia, and imperforate anus: A case report
Authors: Eden Belay Tilahun, Kaleab Habtemichael Gebreselassie, Gudeta Didi Assegie, Freselam Brhanu Taye, Mulualem Engliz Gule, Doruk Ozgediz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrorectal septum malformation sequence (URSMS) is a rare congenital anomaly featuring severe genitourinary and gastrointestinal malformations. We report a neonate with complete URSMS, characterized by complete penoscrotal transposition, a hypoplastic phallus without urethral opening, and an imperforate anus. Laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated serum creatinine of 2.47 mg/dL.Intraoperatively, the rectum and urethra formed a common channel at the phallic base. Anoplasty and urethrostomy were performed. Postoperatively, the infant was anuric and ultrasound confirmed bilateral multicystic dysplastic kidney. Recognition of URSMS is important for accurate diagnosis, surgical planning, and counselling regarding prognosis.Keywords: Penoscrotal transposition, Urorectal malformation sequence, Urethral atresia, Imperforate anus, Multicystic dysplastic kidney, Case report

Introduction

Urorectal septum malformation sequence (URSMS) is a rare malformation consisting of imperforate anus, absence of urethral or vaginal openings, ambiguous genitalia and anomalies of the lower urinary tract, genitalia, and kidneys 1, 2, 3, 4. The etiology of this malformation sequence is unknown, but it has been suggested that mesodermal defects causing failure of the urorectal septum to properly divide the cloaca or to fuse with the cloacal membrane. This leads to persistence of the cloacal membrane and cavity and abnormal differentiation of both the external and internal genitalia1,2,5

Penoscrotal transposition (PST), is a congenital anomaly where the scrotum is positioned cephalad to the penis. The first case of PST was reported in 1923 by Appleby 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. According to the positional exchanges between the scrotum and penis, PST can be classified into incomplete and complete forms. The incomplete form, which is the most common form, in which the penis is located in the midline of the scrotum, on other hand, the complete form in which the penis is nearly completely covered by the scrotum and is frequently associated with major and often life-threatening malformations involving the genitourinary, cardiovascular, or skeletal systems.5,7,8

We report a neonate, from a discordant twin pregnancy, who presented with complete penoscrotal transposition (CPST) associated with urethral atresia, imperforate anus, multicystic dysplastic kidneys and other anomalies as part of complete URSMS. To our knowledge, this constellation of anomalies has rarely been reported. This case highlights the embryological basis of these malformations, describes a unique operative finding of a blind-ending urethroanal channel, and discusses the implications for diagnosis and prognosis. This case report is written according to CARE guideline.

Case presentation

A 36 hours old male preterm neonate, delivered at 36 weeks of gestation as a Twin B, was referred to our facility due to failure to pass urine and meconium, associated with abnormal appearance of the external genitalia and imperforate anus. He was born weighing 2200 g to a para III mother via caesarean section. The APGAR score was 6 and 7 at the 1st and 5th minutes respectively. The cotwin (Twin A) was an alive male neonate weighing 2400 g with APGAR scores of 8 and 9 at the 1st and 5th minutes respectively. He didn't have any gross dysmorphic features and was stable. The mother had no known chronic medical illness and reported no medication use during pregnancy. Antenatal follow-up was irregular, and she did not have prenatal obstetric ultrasound scan. There was no known family history of congenital anomalies or similar genital malformations. Previous pregnancies and deliveries were uneventful, and the two children from prior pregnancies are alive and healthy.

On examination, the neonate was alert and active, weighing 2.2 kg, and in mild respiratory distress. The vital signs at admission were: heart rate 148 beats/minute, respiratory rate 60 breaths/minute, oxygen saturation 94% on supplemental oxygen, and temperature 36.7°C. No gross facial dysmorphic features were observed. Chest examination revealed clear breath sounds bilaterally with good and symmetric air entry. There was a continuous murmur which was best heard at the left upper sternal border on cardiovascular examination. The abdomen was grossly distended but was soft and non-tender, with no palpable masses or organomegaly. The umbilicus was clean; the umbilical cord had three vessels; two arteries and one vein.

Examination of the perineum revealed absence of anal opening with a flat perineum. A midline perineal bulge was noted anteriorly at the base of the phallus. The phallus was 7 cm long, and had soft consistency, lacking palpable erectile tissue. The scrotum was positioned cephalad to the penis, resulting in a complete reversal of the normal penoscrotal relationship. There was no visible urethral opening on the penis or the perineum. Both testes were found in the scrotum (Fig. 1).

Complete penoscrotal transposition with a long, flaccid, hypoplastic penis (a) and an anorectal malformation (b).

On musculoskeletal system, the right forearm was short with radial deviation of the hand. The remaining extremities appeared grossly normal. Neurologic examination was remarkable for weak and unsustained suckling reflex. Muscle tone, posture, other primitive reflexes, and limb movements were appropriate for gestational age.

The initial serum creatinine was 2.47 mg/dL. All other blood tests were normal. An abdominal ultrasonography showed both kidneys were located in the lower abdomen with echogenic walls and severely dilated pelvi-calyceal system. Urinary bladder was not visualized. Bowel loops were markedly dilated. Otherwise, there was no free peritoneal fluid collection. Echocardiography showed large patent ductus arteriosus.

The patient was taken to the operating room for examination under anesthesia, urethral catheterization, and diverting colostomy. No external urethral meatus could be identified on examination under anesthesia.

An anterior sagittal perineal incision was made over the visible midline perineal bulge at the base of the phallus. The rectum was identified immediately beneath the perineal skin. However, a small orifice was identified on the anterior wall of the rectum 0.5 cm from the its distal end. A 4-Fr feeding tube was gently introduced through the orifice into a short tubular structure presumed to represent the distal urethra. The feeding tube could be advanced only 2 cm before encountering firm resistance. Instillation of normal saline through the tube also encountered resistance, and communication with the proximal urinary tract could not be demonstrated. The tubular structure was carefully mobilized and completely separated from the rectum (Fig. 2). Its distal end was matured to the skin to create a possible urinary drainage site, and the catheter was left in situ for subsequent contrast evaluation. As cystoscopy and intraoperative fluoroscopy were not available at our institution, the exact nature and proximal extent of the structure could not be confirmed intraoperatively. Primary anoplasty was then performed since the rectum was immediately beneath the perineal skin, with the neoanus positioned within the poorly developed sphincter muscle complex, which showed weak contraction.

Intraoperative photograph showing the rectum (long arrow) and urethra (short arrow) identified at the base of the phallus following dissection and separation.

Postoperatively, the neonate passed meconium; however, he remained anuric. Repeat bedside ultrasonography demonstrated bilaterally enlarged multicystic kidneys with no identifiable functional renal parenchyma and a non-visualized urinary bladder, consistent with bilateral multicystic dysplastic kidneys (Fig. 3). Serum creatinine continued to rise, and the infant's clinical condition progressively deteriorated over the following two days. After counselling the parents regarding the infant's severe renal dysfunction, progressive clinical deterioration, and poor prognosis, the parents decided to discontinue further hospital-based care and take him home.

Renal ultrasound demonstrating kidneys completely replaced by multiple cysts, with no identifiable normal renal parenchyma (a) Right kidney, (b) Left kidney.

Discussion

Complete penoscrotal transposition (CPST) is a rare urogenital malformation characterized by complete reversal of the normal relationship between the penis and scrotum. Most reported cases occur in association with other malformations involving the genitourinary, anorectal, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and central nervous systems 5,8, 9, 10, 11. A disproportionately long flaccid and hypoplastic penis, complete urethral atresia, a bifid scrotum, and absence of the median raphe have also been reported.5,7 In our patient, CPST occurred as part of a complete URSMS and was associated with complete urethral atresia, imperforate anus, bilateral multicystic dysplastic kidneys, large patent ductus arteriosus, and a radial ray anomaly. The presence of these associated anomalies indicates a disruption in caudal embryogenesis rather than an isolated defect in development of external genitalia.5,9,12

URSMS represents a spectrum of malformations resulting from failure of normal cloacal partitioning during early embryogenesis 1, 2, 3, 4. It was first described by Escobar et al..1 The complete form is characterized by the absence of urethral, vaginal and anal openings and is lethal because urinary tract obstruction results in severe oligohydramnios and pulmonary hypoplasia. Partial URSMS presents with a single perineal opening draining a common cloaca, ambiguous genitalia, renal anomalies and has a more favourable prognosis.3,13 Our patient presented with the complete form of the sequence, with absent abnormal genitalia, perineal opening, renal dysplasia, cardiac and musculoskeletal malformations.

One of the important anatomic features observed in this case was the identification of a blind-ending distal urethral remnant adjacent to the anorectal canal. Intraoperatively, a 4-Fr feeding tube was advanced only 2 cm before encountering firm resistance, and the saline instilled through the tube has refluxed back immediately, indicating lack of continuity with the proximal urinary tract. Although fluoroscopic evaluation was unavailable at our institution, these findings, along with persistent postoperative anuria and subsequent ultrasonographic demonstration of bilateral multicystic dysplastic kidneys without a visible urinary bladder, strongly support complete urethral atresia. While urethrorectal and urethroanal communications have been described within the spectrum of URSMS, this blind-ending configuration is rare and further illustrates the severity of the underlying developmental defect. We identified a urethra and anal canal were covered by a perineal skin and share a common wall distally.1

The findings in our patient likely resulted from an early embryologic insult in the caudal mesoderm during the fourth to seventh weeks of gestation, when genital tubercle development, cloacal partitioning, and rupture of cloacal membrane occur. An abnormal urorectal septum formation would account for the anorectal malformation and abnormal urethral development, while abnormal positioning or intrinsic defects in differentiation of the genital tubercle contributed to complete penoscrotal transposition, the flaccid hypoplastic phallus, and urethral atresia 1, 2, 3,7,10,11Disruption of Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling, a critical regulator of caudal and mesodermal development, has been linked to abnormal cloacal, anorectal and genitourinary development.8,13 The associated renal, radial and cardiac anomaly suggest a generalized disturbance of mesodermal development rather than isolated genital malformation.5,9,12

An additional notable feature of this case is its occurrence in only one twin. Discordance for URSMS has previously been reported in twin pregnancies2 .5,14 Discordance in dizygotic twins can be explained by their different genetic backgrounds. Although the zygosity of our twins was not determined, the normal phenotype of the co-twin argues against a purely genetic mechanism and supports the hypothesis that postzygotic developmental events or localized embryologic disturbances contribute to the pathogenesis of URSMS. Furthermore, survival after birth in this infant, despite complete URSMS, may have been facilitated by preservation of amniotic fluid volume and fetal lung development through the normally functioning urinary tract of the co-twin, thereby reducing the severity of pulmonary hypoplasia. This observation, although speculative, has been proposed in previous reports of discordant twin pregnancies.2

This case provides further evidence that CPST may occur in association with the severe caudal developmental abnormalities of URSMS.5 Recognition of this association is clinically important because identification of CPST should prompt careful evaluation for associated anorectal, urinary, renal, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal anomalies. Advances in prenatal ultrasonography and fetal magnetic resonance imaging may facilitate antenatal diagnosis, counselling, and appropriate perinatal planning.12,15

Conclusion

Recognition of urorectal septum malformation sequence anomalies is helpful for accurate diagnosis, surgical planning, and counselling regarding prognosis. Prenatal detection of URSMS is important for counselling regarding prognosis and to decide on the pregnancy.
