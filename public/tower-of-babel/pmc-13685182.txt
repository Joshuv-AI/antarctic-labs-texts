Effects of short-term salt stress on photosynthetic characteristics, physiological metabolism, and ion homeostasis in soybean seedlings
Authors: Xinyu Zhou, Jiaqi Zhen, Xiyue Wang, Zhipeng Qu, Yuanqi Ma, Wei Zhao, Xinhe Wei, Shoukun Dong, Xiaomei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalt stress is a major abiotic stress limiting soybean production, with the seedling stage being the most salt-sensitive period. Elucidating the growth, photosynthetic, and physiological response mechanisms of soybean seedlings to salt stress holds significant theoretical value for breeding salt-tolerant cultivars.MethodsIn this study, we systematically investigated the effects of varying NaCl stress intensities (0–160 mmol⋅kg⁻¹) on plant growth, photosynthetic gas exchange, chlorophyll fluorescence, antioxidant physiology, and ion homeostasis using a salt-tolerant cultivar (Zhonglongdou 136, ZLD136) and a salt-sensitive cultivar (Henong 85, HN85).ResultsThe results demonstrated that increasing salt concentrations significantly inhibited plant growth and photosynthetic carbon assimilation. However, ZLD136 exhibited superior phenotypic stability and photoprotective mechanisms, evidenced by a significant increase in non-photochemical quenching (NPQt). Salt stress triggered a substantial rise in malondialdehyde (MDA) content and relative electrical conductivity (REC); notably, ZLD136 effectively mitigated membrane lipid peroxidation by maintaining higher antioxidant enzyme (superoxide dismutase SOD, peroxidase POD, and catalase CAT) activities and accumulating more osmolytes, including proline (Pro) and soluble sugars (SS). Furthermore, the salt-tolerant cultivar more efficiently coordinated the mitochondrial respiratory electron transport chain (cytochrome c oxidase COX and alternative oxidase AOX pathways) to dissipate excess reducing power and maintain cellular energy homeostasis. ZLD136 also possessed a stronger capacity for ion regulation, significantly outperforming HN85 in maintaining higher leaf K⁺/Na⁺ and Ca²⁺/Na⁺ ratios.ConclusionsSalt tolerance in soybean seedlings involves a comprehensive synergistic network, and the salt tolerance advantage of ZLD136 primarily stems from its superior ability to maintain ion balance, highly efficient photoprotective mechanisms, and a robust antioxidant defense system. Comprehensive analysis revealed that with intensifying salt stress, the physiological responses of soybean gradually transitioned from relying on the antioxidant enzyme system to the synthesis of osmolytes. Moreover, maintaining ion homeostasis (particularly a high leaf K⁺/Na⁺ ratio) is the core prerequisite for alleviating physiological and metabolic disorders, safeguarding photosynthetic functions, and ensuring short-term plant growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09567-7.

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is one of the most economically important food and oilseed crops globally, providing abundant plant protein (35%–40%) and oil (18%–22%) for human consumption [1–3]. Owing to their sessile nature, plants are highly susceptible to various abiotic stresses during growth and development, including drought, salinity, heavy metals, and extreme temperatures [4, 5]. Among these, soil salinization is a pervasive global agricultural challenge, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. Based on estimates from the GSAS map and the ESA CCI land cover map, approximately 10% of irrigated farmland, 10% of rain-fed farmland, and 8% of total agricultural land globally are affected by salinization or alkalization [6, 7]. This degradation is driven by both natural factors, such as insufficient rainfall and the exacerbation of surface and groundwater salinity, and anthropogenic factors, including improper land management, defective drainage systems, and inadequate irrigation [8, 9]. Consequently, the continuous accumulation of soil salts severely impairs plant growth and ultimately leads to substantial yield penalties [10].

The detrimental effects of salt stress on plants generally manifest in two distinct phases: osmotic stress and ionic toxicity. High-salinity environments rapidly lower soil water potential, inducing physiological drought. Upon prolonged exposure, excessive intracellular accumulation of toxic ions, particularly Na⁺ and Cl⁻, disrupts ion homeostasis and cell membrane integrity, severely inhibiting plant metabolism and, in severe cases, leading to plant mortality [11]. Furthermore, salt stress damages chloroplast structure, inhibits Photosystem II (PSII) activity and electron transport, and induces the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby triggering photoinhibition and reducing photosynthetic efficiency [12]. To mitigate salt-induced oxidative damage, plants have evolved sophisticated defense systems to regulate cellular homeostasis. These mechanisms include scavenging excess ROS by activating antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) [13], and maintaining cellular water potential through the accumulation of osmolytes, including proline (Pro) and soluble sugars (SS) [14]. Concurrently, mitochondrial respiration plays a crucial role. The cytochrome c oxidase (COX) and alternative oxidase (AOX) pathways are the two primary routes of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. When the COX pathway is impaired by environmental stress, the AOX pathway can maintain cellular energy and redox homeostasis by directly accepting electrons from the ubiquinone pool [15]. Additionally, salt stress significantly alters the profile of essential mineral ions (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Zn²⁺), which are not only indispensable nutrients for plant growth but also critical regulators of salt tolerance [16].

Although extensive research has investigated soybean responses to salt stress regarding growth inhibition, photosynthetic impairment, ROS scavenging, and ion compartmentalization, most existing studies have focused on isolated physiological or biochemical processes, lacking a systemic integration of the comprehensive salt tolerance network. In particular, as salt stress intensifies, the overall evolutionary patterns and coordination among antioxidant enzymes, osmotic regulation, and mitochondrial respiration remain poorly understood. Furthermore, plant salt tolerance is a complex network interwoven with multidimensional factors—including photosynthesis, osmoregulation, ion balance, and antioxidant defense—and this tolerance is not a static equilibrium but a highly dynamic adaptive process. The sequence of impairment across various traits, as well as the dynamic shifts in plant defense strategies under varying stress durations and intensities, remain a critical gap in current knowledge. Given that the seedling stage is the most salt-sensitive period during soybean development, physiological changes during the early stages of stress often directly determine seedling survival and the establishment of subsequent tolerance. Therefore, targeting this critical developmental window, we selected two soybean cultivars exhibiting significantly contrasting salt tolerance—Zhonglongdou 136 (ZLD136, salt-tolerant) and Henong 85 (HN85, salt-sensitive)—based on our preliminary screening. Using these materials, we systematically analyzed the early dynamic responses of growth morphology, photosynthetic characteristics, physiological metabolism, and ion homeostasis under varying NaCl concentrations. This study not only explored the dynamic characteristics of physiological parameters and the differential responses of the COX and AOX pathways between the two cultivars under different stress intensities, but also conducted a comprehensive analysis to systematically evaluate the indicators across multiple dimensions. By revealing the dynamic response relationships among morphological traits, photosynthetic parameters, and biochemical defense, this research aims to elucidate the key limiting factors of soybean growth under salt stress, thereby providing a robust theoretical basis for screening salt-tolerant cultivars and optimizing cultivation management.

Materials and methods

Test materials and design

The experiment was conducted in a controlled artificial growth chamber at Northeast Agricultural University, Harbin, China (126.7262°E, 45.7416°N). Two soybean cultivars with significantly contrasting salt tolerance, Zhonglongdou 136 (ZLD136, salt-tolerant) and Henong 85 (HN85, salt-sensitive), were used as plant materials. These two cultivars were selected based on a comprehensive two-stage salt tolerance evaluation (germination and seedling stages) of 43 soybean cultivars previously conducted in our laboratory. Seeds were provided by the Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences. To ensure material uniformity, all seeds used were harvested in 2024, with a germination rate exceeding 95%.

A pot experiment was established using non-perforated plastic pots (11.4 cm in height, 12.7 cm in top diameter, and 9.7 cm in bottom diameter). Non-perforated pots were specifically chosen to prevent salt leaching, thereby ensuring the absolute quantification of the applied salt concentrations. To preclude secondary stresses such as soil hypoxia or waterlogging typically associated with undrained pots, the pots were weighed daily and precisely replenished with water to maintain the soil moisture content strictly at 65%–70% of the maximum water-holding capacity, ensuring adequate root aeration. Each pot was filled with 450 g of sieved, air-dried soil. The basic physicochemical properties of the experimental soil were as follows: pH 7.21, organic matter 31.53 g/kg, total nitrogen 0.79 g/kg, total phosphorus 1.01 g/kg, total potassium 18.31 g/kg, alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen 126.67 mg/kg, available phosphorus 39.59 mg/kg, available potassium 180.59 mg/kg, and a background electrical conductivity (EC) of 0.12 dS/m (see Method S1 in the Supplementary Materials for the detailed methodology of EC measurement).

Plump and uniform seeds were sown at a density of eight seeds per pot and evenly covered with 50 g (approximately 2 cm) of sieved soil. The pots were arranged in a completely randomized design in the growth chamber at 25 °C with a 16/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod and 70% relative humidity. Illumination was provided by full-spectrum LED plant grow lights (white: red: blue = 4:1:1 ratio, PAR ≈ 300 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, spectral range 400–700 nm), which ensured sufficient PAR while simulating a natural full-spectrum light environment. Upon the full expansion of the unifoliate leaves (V1 stage), the seedlings were thinned. At this stage, seedling growth primarily relies on cotyledon reserves, and the roots and canopy have not yet developed significant spatial or resource competition, making the impact of thinning negligible. During thinning, seedlings near the pot edges or those exhibiting overly vigorous or weak growth were removed. Only three healthy, highly uniform seedlings (in terms of height, stem diameter, and developmental stage) located in the center of the pot were retained. Furthermore, pot positions were randomly rotated daily throughout the experiment to eliminate potential edge effects within the chamber, further ensuring the uniformity and comparability of seedling growth.

When the seedlings reached the V2 stage (the second trifoliolate leaf fully expanded), salt treatments were initiated. Five treatments were established based on the molar concentration of NaCl relative to the soil dry weight: 0 (Control, CK), 40 (S1), 80 (S2), 120 (S3), and 160 (S4) mmol·kg⁻¹. The added NaCl concentration served as the primary treatment variable, while the actual soil EC was synchronously measured as a validation metric reflecting the average stress intensity experienced by the rhizosphere (detailed in Table 1). To avoid salt shock, the predetermined amount of NaCl for each treatment was applied incrementally over two consecutive days. Detailed procedures for the precise salt application are provided in Method S2 of the Supplementary Materials. The salt stress treatment was considered officially initiated once the two-day application was completed and the target soil salt concentration was reached.

Salt concentration and corresponding salt usage

Sampling was conducted at five time points: prior to stress initiation (denoted as Day 0, before any salt addition), and at 1, 3, 5, and 7 days after the treatments. To ensure strict temporal consistency across treatments, the Day 0 sampling was performed first, followed by the two-day incremental salt application for all treatment groups. The morning (8:00–9:00 a.m.) following the final salt application was designated as Day 1 of the stress treatment, at which point both the control and treatment groups were sampled simultaneously. Subsequent samplings at 3, 5, and 7 days strictly followed this timeline. To prevent the destructive sampling from interfering with subsequent plant growth, six independent pots were completely randomly selected from each treatment at every time point. These six pots were divided into two groups, each containing three independent biological replicates (n = 3), ensuring that each pot represented a completely independent experimental unit. One group (n = 3 pots) was used for physiological assays; the penultimate leaves (the second youngest fully expanded leaves) from the three seedlings within a single pot were pooled as one biological replicate. This pooling strategy ensured sufficient sample volume and represented the average response level of that independent unit, thereby minimizing individual plant variance. These samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. The remaining group (n = 3 pots) was used to determine the morphological parameters of the shoots and roots.

Measurement indicators and methods

Soybean growth, biomass, and root morphological indicators

Plant height

At each sampling point, the plants were cut at the soil surface to eliminate measurement errors caused by natural bending or leaning. The main stem was manually straightened, and the distance from the cotyledonary node to the apical meristem was measured using a digital caliper.

Fresh Weight (FW)

Harvested plants were immediately separated into leaves and roots. The FW of the leaves was recorded promptly. The roots were carefully washed free of soil, gently blotted dry with absorbent paper to remove surface moisture, and immediately weighed.

Root morphological parameters

The washed roots were placed in a transparent acrylic tray filled with an appropriate amount of deionized water. Forceps were used to gently tease the roots apart to maximize spreading and minimize overlapping. The roots were then scanned using a flatbed optical root scanner (Phantom 9900XL, Microtek, China) at a resolution of 400 dpi, and the images were saved in TIFF format. Subsequently, the acquired images were analyzed using WinRHIZO v2019 software (Regent Instruments Inc., Quebec, QC, Canada) to determine total root length, root surface area, root volume, and the number of root tips.

Leaf area

Leaf area was determined using the leaf disc method, adapted from Wang et al. [17] with minor modifications. At each sampling, all leaves from the three seedlings within a single pot were excised and pooled. Using a cork borer (inner diameter 1.0 cm), 20 leaf discs were randomly punched from the pooled leaves per pot. To ensure representativeness and minimize weight variations caused by veins, the discs were exclusively sampled from the flat, central regions of the leaves, explicitly avoiding the main veins. The leaf discs and the remaining leaf tissues were separately placed in an oven at 105 °C for 30 min for enzyme deactivation, followed by drying at 65 °C to a constant weight. The dry weights of the leaf discs (W1) and the remaining leaves (W2) were accurately measured. The area of a single leaf disc (S) was calculated based on the borer’s radius. Dry Weight (DW)

To prevent the alteration and loss of chemical constituents, the leaf and root samples were initially heat-treated at 105 °C for 30 min to rapidly halt enzymatic activity. Following this deactivation step, the oven temperature was immediately reduced and maintained at 65 °C until the samples reached a constant weight, thereby preventing charring caused by excessive heat [18, 19].

Measurement of photosynthetic gas exchange parameters

Leaf gas exchange parameters, including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), stomatal conductance (Gs), and transpiration rate (Tr), were measured using a Yaxin-1101 portable photosynthesis system equipped with a Yaxin-11 A light control module (Beijing Yaxin Science and Instrument Co., Ltd., China). All measurements were conducted between 8:00 and 11:00 a.m. on the fully expanded penultimate leaves (the second leaf from the top). During measurement, the leaf chamber (6.5 cm² area) was clamped onto a flat region in the middle of the leaf, strictly avoiding the midrib to ensure complete chamber sealing and the uniformity of the measured photosynthetic tissue.

To ensure data reproducibility, environmental conditions within the leaf chamber were strictly controlled: photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) provided by the light control module was set at 300 µmol·m⁻² ·s⁻¹, CO2 concentration was maintained at 400 µmol·mol− 1, leaf chamber temperature was set at 25 °C, and the air flow rate was kept at 0.6 L·min− 1. The detailed calculation formulas for these gas exchange parameters are provided in Method S3 of the Supplementary Materials. For each treatment, three biological replicates (pots) were used. Within each replicate, two plants were randomly selected for technical measurements, and their average was used as the final value for that biological replicate.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were evaluated using a handheld MultispeQ V2.0 device integrated with the PhotosynQ platform (PhotosynQ Inc., East Lansing, MI, USA). Measurements were performed on the left side of the penultimate leaves, specifically in the area between the leaf margin and the midrib. The sampling strategy (three biological and two technical replicates per treatment, totaling six individual plant measurements) was identical to that used for gas exchange measurements.

Measurement procedures followed the “Photosynthesis RIDES 2.0” protocol. Prior to measurement, leaves were exposed to steady-state light for over 2 h to ensure the photosynthetic induction had reached a steady state. Light-adapted parameters were then recorded, including the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII under light (Fv′/Fm′), actual photochemical quantum yield of PSII (Phi2), quantum yield of non-regulated energy dissipation (PhiNO), quantum yield of regulated energy dissipation (PhiNPQ), photochemical quenching coefficient (qL), and the non-photochemical quenching associated with antenna complexes (NPQt). The specific calculation formulas for these fluorescence parameters are detailed in Method S4 of the Supplementary Materials.

Subsequently, leaves were dark-adapted for 30 min using dark-adaptation clips, followed by a saturating light pulse (5000 µmol·m− 2·s−1for 1 s) to determine the maximum photochemical quantum yield of PSII (Fv/Fm). The measurement and calculation of these parameters followed the methodologies described by Kalaji et al. [20], Tietz et al. [21], and Kuhlgert et al. [22]. Additionally, the device simultaneously calculated and recorded the relative chlorophyll content by measuring transmittance at specific wavelengths (red and near-infrared). This parameter, which highly correlates with traditional SPAD values, was used as a SPAD-equivalent index to evaluate relative chlorophyll abundance.

Membrane lipid peroxidation and osmolytes

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method, and relative electrical conductivity (REC) was measured using a DDS-307 conductivity meter, following the protocols described by Li et al. [23]. Proline (Pro) content was quantified using the sulfosalicylic acid method [24]. Soluble sugar (SS) and soluble protein (SP) contents were determined using the anthrone method and the Coomassie brilliant blue method, respectively, according to Wang et al. [25].

Assay of antioxidant enzyme activities

Enzyme extraction

Accurately weigh 0.5 g of frozen soybean leaf tissue and grind it into a fine powder in a pre-chilled mortar using liquid nitrogen. The powder was then homogenized on ice in 5 mL of pre-chilled phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract for the determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) activities [23]. To minimize the loss of enzyme activity, all extraction steps were strictly performed on ice, and the crude extracts were temporarily stored in the dark at 4 °C. All subsequent assays were rigorously completed within 3 h of extraction. Enzyme activities were uniformly expressed on a fresh weight basis (U/g FW).

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD)

SOD activity was measured using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction method. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 1.5 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 10 µM EDTA-Na₂, 2 µM riboflavin, and 50 µL of the enzyme extract. The mixture was illuminated under 4000 lx for 15 min, and the absorbance was measured at 560 nm. One unit (U) of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit NBT photoreduction by 50%.

Peroxidase (POD): POD activity was determined using the guaiacol method. The 3.06 mL reaction system consisted of 2.9 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.05 mL of guaiacol (20 mM), 10 µL of H₂O₂ (40 mM), and 100 µL of the enzyme extract. The change in absorbance at 470 nm was monitored, and one unit (U) was defined as an absorbance change of 0.01 per minute.

Catalase (CAT)

CAT activity was assayed via the ultraviolet absorption method. The 3 mL reaction mixture comprised 2.9 mL of H₂O₂ solution (20 mM H₂O₂ dissolved in 50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) and 100 µL of the enzyme extract. The consumption of H₂O₂ was monitored at 240 nm, with one unit (U) defined as a decrease in absorbance of 0.01 per minute.

Ascorbate Peroxidase (APX)

For APX extraction, 0.2 g of frozen leaf tissue was homogenized on ice in 1.6 mL of pre-chilled extraction buffer (50 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.8, containing 5 mM EDTA-Na₂ and 2 mM AsA). After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected as the APX extract. APX activity was determined based on its ability to catalyze the oxidation of ascorbate to monodehydroascorbate in the presence of H₂O₂. The 2 mL reaction system contained 1.7 mL of phosphate buffer (0.05 M, pH 7.8, with 0.1 mM EDTA-Na₂), 0.1 mL of AsA (5 mM), 0.1 mL of H₂O₂ (20 mM), and 0.1 mL of the crude enzyme extract. The change in absorbance at 290 nm was recorded immediately at 20 °C over a 40 s period. One unit (U) of APX activity was defined as an absorbance change of 0.01 per minute [25].

Measurement of energy metabolism

Cytochrome c oxidase (COX)

COX activity was determined using a commercial assay kit (BC0945, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Crude mitochondrial fractions were isolated via differential centrifugation. Briefly, 0.1 g of fresh tissue was homogenized in 1.0 mL of extraction buffer on ice and centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 11,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet (mitochondria-enriched fraction) was resuspended in 400 µL of extraction buffer and disrupted by sonication (200 W, 15 cycles of 5 s ON and 10 s OFF). The COX activity was calculated by monitoring the catalytic degradation of reduced cytochrome c. One unit (U) of COX activity was defined as the oxidation of 1 nmol of reduced cytochrome c per minute per gram of fresh weight (U/g FW).

Alternative oxidase (AOX)

The AOX protein concentration was quantified using a Plant AOX Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kit (HS186X-Pt, Shanghai Hengyuan Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) based on the double-antibody sandwich method. Briefly, samples were added to microplate wells pre-coated with a purified AOX-specific antibody, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated AOX antibody to form an antibody-antigen-enzyme-antibody complex. After thorough washing, the TMB substrate was added, which turned blue under HRP catalysis and finally yellow upon acidification. The color intensity was positively correlated with the AOX concentration. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the AOX concentration (ng/L) was calculated using a standard curve. It should be noted that this method is specifically designed to evaluate the protein abundance of AOX and the potential expression capacity of the alternative respiratory pathway, rather than its actual in vivo catalytic activity.

Determination of ion contents

The ion contents in leaves were determined following a rigorous microwave digestion procedure. Briefly, 0.1 g of dried, finely ground, and sieved leaf tissue was accurately weighed into a microwave digestion vessel, and 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, Guaranteed Reagent, 65.0%–68.0%) was added. The mixture was allowed to stand for 1 h with the lid closed. Subsequently, the vessels were sealed and digested using a programmed temperature gradient: heating to 120 °C over 5 min (held for 5 min), then to 150 °C over 5 min (held for 10 min), and finally to 190 °C over 5 min (held for 20 min). After natural cooling to room temperature, the vessels were carefully opened to vent the gas. The inner lids were rinsed with a small amount of deionized water. The digestion vessels were then placed on a temperature-controlled hot plate at 100 °C for 30 min to remove excess acid. Finally, the digested solution was diluted to a constant volume of 50 mL with deionized water and mixed thoroughly. The concentrations of sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), magnesium (Mg²⁺), and zinc (Zn²⁺) in the prepared solutions were analyzed using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES, Agilent 5800, Agilent Technologies, USA). Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates. Data analysis

Data organization and preliminary tabulation were performed using Microsoft Excel 2010. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05) and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test (p > 0.05). Because the experimental design involved three main factors (cultivar, salt concentration, and stress duration), a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the main effects and their interactions on the measured parameters. Post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test at a significance level of α = 0.05. The partial eta-squared (η2) was calculated to estimate the effect sizes (Supplementary Table S1). Graphical visualizations, including radar charts, bubble plots, and frequency distribution histograms, were generated using Origin 9.0 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted and visualized using R software (version 4.1.2) with relevant packages.

Results

Effect of salt stress on soybean growth and biomass

Salt stress significantly inhibited plant growth and biomass accumulation in both soybean cultivars, and the severity of this inhibition was influenced by both salt concentration and stress duration (Fig. 1). Shoot-related parameters, including plant height, leaf area, and fresh weight (FW), decreased continuously with increasing stress levels (S1–S4). At 7 d under the S4 treatment, the plant height of ZLD136 decreased from 150.32 mm to 103.40 mm (a 31.21% reduction), whereas that of HN85 decreased from 222.65 mm to 133.89 mm (a 39.87% reduction). Similar declining trends were observed for leaf area and shoot FW (Fig. 1A–C). Compared to CK at 7 d, the leaf area of ZLD136 and HN85 under the S4 treatment dropped by 55.61% (from 544.03 to 241.47 cm²) and 54.26% (from 592.49 to 271.01 cm²), respectively. Concurrently, the shoot FW of ZLD136 and HN85 decreased by 78.55% (from 9.37 to 2.01 g) and 81.35% (from 9.06 to 1.69 g), respectively.

Root development and dry matter accumulation exhibited a stage-specific response. At the initial stages of stress (1 and 3 d), the root FW, leaf dry weight (DW), and root DW under the S1 treatment showed an increasing trend compared to CK, although the differences were not significant. However, when the stress was prolonged to 7 d, all these parameters decreased significantly across all treatments (S1–S4) (Fig. 1D–F). Under the S4 treatment at 7 d, the root FW, leaf DW, and root DW of HN85 decreased from 12.65, 1.77, and 0.94 g (CK) to 5.90, 0.99, and 0.48 g, respectively (reductions of 53.35%, 44.07%, and 48.93%). The magnitude of these reductions in HN85 was higher than that in ZLD136 (which decreased by 49.50%, 40.43%, and 45.97%, respectively), indicating that ZLD136 has a stronger capacity for biomass maintenance. Effect of salt stress on soybean root morphological parameters

As shown in Fig. 2, the effect of salt stress on soybean root morphology exhibited distinct stage-specific characteristics. During the initial stress phase (1 and 3 d), the S1 treatment exerted a transient stimulatory effect on the roots of both cultivars. However, as the stress duration extended to 5 d, this stimulatory effect gradually disappeared, and most root parameters began to decline under the S2 treatment. By 7 d of stress, with the exception of the number of root tips, all root parameters decreased significantly with increasing salt concentration (S1–S4), reaching their lowest values under the S4 treatment. Under the S4 treatment, the root systems of both cultivars were inhibited (with reductions exceeding 40%), but ZLD136 demonstrated a better capacity for morphological maintenance than HN85 (Fig. 2A–C). Specifically, the total root length, root surface area, and root volume of HN85 decreased from 1976.01 cm, 246.93 cm², and 4.12 cm³ (CK) to 1073.98 cm, 121.88 cm², and 1.87 cm³ under the S4 treatment, representing reductions of 45.70%, 50.64%, and 54.55%, respectively. In contrast, the reductions in ZLD136 were lower; its total root length, surface area, and volume decreased from 1870.15 cm, 222.40 cm², and 3.88 cm³ (CK) to 1097.93 cm, 119.89 cm², and 1.90 cm³ under the S4 treatment (reductions of 41.30%, 46.10%, and 50.75%, respectively). Additionally, the number of root tips in both cultivars was less affected by salt stress, with maximum reductions ranging between 22.39% and 25.48%. Analysis of variance for morphological and root parameters

The results of the three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Table S2-1) showed that the main effects of salt concentration and stress duration were highly significant for all morphological parameters (p < 0.001). Specifically, salt concentration exhibited the largest effect sizes for leaf fresh weight (FW) (ηp² = 0.96) and leaf area (ηp² = 0.93), whereas stress duration had the highest effect size for plant height and leaf dry weight (DW) (ηp² = 0.92). Additionally, except for leaf DW and total root length, the cultivar main effect was significant or highly significant for all other parameters, with the largest effect sizes observed for average plant height (ηp² = 0.92) and the number of root tips (ηp² = 0.47).

Regarding interaction effects, the interaction between salt concentration and time was highly significant for all morphological parameters (p < 0.001), with relatively high effect sizes (ηp² = 0.40–0.79). The cultivar × salt concentration interaction was significant or highly significant for six parameters, including leaf FW, plant height, and root FW (p < 0.05 or p < 0.001). Furthermore, the cultivar × time interaction significantly affected plant height, leaf DW, root FW, root volume, and the number of root tips (p < 0.05). Further analysis revealed that the three-way interaction (cultivar × salt concentration × time) had significant effects on leaf FW (p < 0.01), average plant height (p < 0.01), root FW (p < 0.05), root volume (p < 0.001), and the number of root tips (p < 0.05). Among these, the three-way interaction exerted the largest effect size on root volume (ηp² = 0.52). However, this three-way interaction did not significantly affect leaf DW, root DW, leaf area, total root length, or root surface area (p > 0.05).

Effect of salt stress on photosynthetic gas exchange parameters

Salt stress significantly inhibited photosynthesis in both soybean cultivars, and the degree of inhibition increased with prolonged stress duration and increasing salt concentration (Fig. 3). The net photosynthetic rate (Pn) and stomatal conductance (Gs) exhibited similar declining trends, although Pn showed specific differences between the cultivars. Under the S1 treatment from 1 to 3 d, the Pn of ZLD136 showed a transient increase, whereas the Pn of HN85 was significantly lower than that of CK across all treatments. Meanwhile, Gs continuously decreased with increasing salt concentration (S1–S4) and prolonged stress duration. At 7 d under the S4 treatment, the Gs of ZLD136 and HN85 decreased by 80.94% and 70.58%, respectively (Fig. 3A, B). The transpiration rate (Tr) in ZLD136 exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease from 1 to 5 d of stress. In contrast, HN85 only showed a similar pattern at 1 d, after which its Tr decreased continuously (Fig. 3C).

The changes in intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) reflected shifts in the photosynthetic limiting factors for the two cultivars (Fig. 3D). Compared to CK, the Ci of HN85 under the S3 and S4 treatments at 1 d decreased by 60.64% and 70.81%, respectively, indicating that stomatal limitation was dominant at this stage. However, as the stress duration extended (3–7 d), although Gs remained significantly lower than that of CK, the Ci of HN85 did not continue to decline; instead, it showed an increasing trend. At 7 d under the S4 treatment, the Ci of HN85 was only 13.38% lower than that of CK. This trend indicates that under prolonged S4 treatment, the photosynthetic inhibition in HN85 shifted from stomatal limitation to non-stomatal limitation. In contrast, the Ci of ZLD136 exhibited a continuous declining trend under the S4 treatment, decreasing by 28.59% compared to CK at 7 d. This indicates that under the S4 treatment, the photosynthetic inhibition in ZLD136 was maintained at the stomatal limitation stage.

Effect of salt stress on soybean chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Salt stress significantly inhibited the PSII function of both soybean cultivars (Fig. 4). Photochemical efficiency and activity parameters (Fv/Fm, Fv′/Fm′, Phi2, and PhiNO) decreased continuously with increasing stress levels (S1–S4), reaching their lowest values at 7 d under the S4 treatment. The reductions in HN85 (which decreased by 48.95%–64.41% for these parameters at 7 d under the S4 treatment) were significantly higher than those in ZLD136 (24.09%–43.60%), indicating that ZLD136 maintained better stability of its photosynthetic apparatus (Fig. 4A, B, D, E). In contrast, thermal dissipation parameters (NPQt and PhiNPQ) increased significantly with increasing salt concentrations (S1–S4). At 7 d under the S4 treatment, the NPQt of ZLD136 increased by 8.67-fold, which enhanced its photoprotective regulatory capacity (Fig. 4C, F). Furthermore, qL and SPAD-equivalent index values exhibited clear cultivar specificity. ZLD136 maintained an increase in qL and a steady state of SPAD-equivalent index during 3–5 d of stress, whereas HN85 showed a distinct decreasing trend. Specifically, the SPAD-equivalent index value of HN85 decreased significantly by 19.02% at 7 d under the S4 treatment (Fig. 4G, H).

Analysis of variance for gas exchange and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

The three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Table S2-2) showed that salt concentration had a highly significant effect on all gas exchange and fluorescence parameters (p < 0.001), generally exhibiting very large effect sizes. Specifically, salt concentration had the strongest effects on NPQt (ηp² = 0.97) and PhiNPQ (ηp² = 0.88). Stress duration also significantly affected all photosynthetic parameters except for PhiNO and qL. Furthermore, the cultivar main effect was not significant for gas exchange parameters such as Pn, Tr, Gs, and Ci (p > 0.05), but it had a significant or highly significant effect on chlorophyll fluorescence parameters and SPAD-equivalent index (p < 0.05 or p < 0.001).

Regarding interaction effects, the cultivar × salt concentration interaction significantly affected all parameters except Tr and SPAD-equivalent index, showing particularly strong interaction effects on Ci (ηp² = 0.68) and NPQt (ηp² = 0.62). This indicates significant differences between the two cultivars in intercellular CO₂ regulation and thermal dissipation mechanisms under varying salt concentrations. In addition, the salt concentration × time interaction highly significantly affected most photosynthetic and fluorescence parameters, with the highest effect sizes observed for NPQt (ηp² = 0.89) and Fv/Fm (ηp² = 0.70). The cultivar × time interaction reached a significant level for gas exchange parameters, as well as Fv/Fm, NPQt, and Phi2 (p < 0.05). Further analysis showed that the three-way interaction (cultivar × salt concentration × time) had a significant effect on Gs, Ci, Fv/Fm, NPQt, and Phi2 (p < 0.05). Effect of salt stress on membrane lipid peroxidation and osmoregulatory substances

As shown in Fig. 5, the changes in malondialdehyde (MDA) content and relative electrical conductivity (REC) were similar between the two cultivars across all treatments, both increasing continuously with higher NaCl concentrations and prolonged stress duration. The most significant increases were observed under the S4 treatment. Compared to CK, the MDA contents of ZLD136 and HN85 at 7 d of stress significantly increased by 145.13% and 153.06%, respectively (Fig. 5A). In comparison, the increases in REC were even more pronounced, rising by 565.31% and 654.06%, respectively (Fig. 5B). Additionally, at 5 d of stress, the REC of both cultivars under the S4 treatment reached approximately 90%, indicating a substantial loss of cell membrane integrity.

Salt stress significantly induced the accumulation of osmoregulatory substances in both soybean cultivars, although they exhibited different dynamic patterns (Fig. 5C-E). Proline (Pro) content continuously increased with increasing NaCl concentration and stress duration (Fig. 5C), reaching its maximum increase at 7 d of stress. Notably, the Pro content in ZLD136 increased by 92.2-fold compared to CK, which was significantly higher than the 56.8-fold increase observed in HN85. The trend of soluble sugar (SS) content was similar to that of Pro; however, differences between the cultivars emerged under the S4 treatment at 7 d. The SS content of ZLD136 continued to rise (increasing by 75.41% compared to CK), whereas that of HN85 began to decline under the S4 treatment (Fig. 5D). In contrast, soluble protein (SP) content exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease during 1–3 d of stress (Fig. 5E), and then showed a continuous declining trend as the stress duration extended. At 7 d of stress, the SP contents of both soybean cultivars dropped to their lowest levels under the S4 treatment, decreasing by 41.51% and 42.09%, respectively.

Effect of salt stress on antioxidant enzyme activities in soybean

Salt stress significantly induced differential responses in the antioxidant enzyme systems of the two soybean cultivars (Fig. 6). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) activities generally exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease. Specifically, from 1 to 3 d of stress, SOD activity increased initially and then decreased with increasing NaCl concentrations; however, it decreased significantly under the S4 treatment from 5 to 7 d. POD activity maintained this initial-increase-then-decrease trend throughout the entire stress period, and the magnitude of increase in ZLD136 (up to 152.43%) was generally higher than that in HN85 (Fig. 6A, B). Catalase (CAT) activity showed an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing salt concentrations from 3 to 5 d, peaking under the S2 treatment for both cultivars. At 7 d of stress, ZLD136 still maintained this trend, whereas the CAT activity of HN85 decreased significantly compared to CK (Fig. 6C).

In contrast, ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity generally exhibited a declining trend, with differences in response time between the two cultivars (Fig. 6D). At 1 d of stress, the APX activity of ZLD136 across all treatments showed no significant difference compared to CK, and even increased slightly under the S4 treatment. It was not until 3 d of stress that the S3 and S4 treatments began to significantly inhibit its APX activity. Subsequently, from 5 to 7 d, the APX activity of ZLD136 decreased with increasing salt concentrations, dropping significantly by 40.75% compared to CK under the S4 treatment at 7 d. Conversely, the APX activity of HN85 had already decreased significantly compared to CK across all treatments at 1 d of stress.

Effect of salt stress on energy metabolism in soybean

Salt stress significantly regulated the activities of terminal oxidases in the mitochondrial electron transport chain of soybean leaves (Fig. 7). As the key enzyme of the cytochrome pathway (CP), cytochrome c oxidase (COX) activity exhibited significant differences between the two cultivars (Fig. 7A). Overall, ZLD136 maintained higher COX activity. At 3 d of stress, the AOX activities of both cultivars increased significantly with increasing salt concentrations (S1–S4), reaching their maximum values under the S4 treatment (increasing by 14.21%–20.72% compared to CK). From 5 to 7 d of stress, the trend of AOX activity shifted to an initial increase followed by a decrease, with significant differences between the cultivars. These results indicate that ZLD136 could effectively activate the alternative pathway to maintain metabolic balance under moderate-to-high salt conditions (S3–S4).

Effect of salt stress on ion contents in soybean

Salt stress significantly altered the ion homeostasis of the two soybean cultivars, characterized by the accumulation of Na⁺ and changes in essential ions (Fig. 8). Under CK conditions, the Na⁺ contents of both soybean cultivars were maintained at very low levels (0.08–0.15 mg/g). However, the Na⁺ content increased significantly with increasing salt concentrations and prolonged stress duration. At 7 d of stress, the Na⁺ contents of ZLD136 and HN85 reached 33.54 and 36.42 mg/g under the S4 treatment, respectively. Throughout the entire stress period (1–7 d), the Na⁺ accumulation in HN85 was consistently and significantly higher than that in ZLD136 (Fig. 8A). The K⁺ content showed a transient increasing trend during the initial stage of stress (1–3 d), with ZLD136 exhibiting a maximum increase of 25.92% under the S4 treatment; however, it decreased significantly under high salt concentrations during the later stage. In contrast, HN85 only displayed a similar pattern at 1 d of stress, peaking under the S2 treatment (Fig. 8B). As a key signaling ion, the Ca²⁺ content increased continuously with increasing salt concentrations. The increase in ZLD136 (57.40%) was significantly higher than that in HN85 (21.60%) under the S4 treatment at 7 d (Fig. 8C). Both Mg²⁺ and Zn²⁺ contents increased slightly at low salt concentrations but decreased gradually with prolonged stress duration and increasing salt concentrations. Specifically, the Mg²⁺ content decreased significantly by 14.33%–15.28% under the S4 treatment at 7 d (Fig. 8D). The changes in Zn²⁺ exhibited genotypic differences: ZLD136 showed the largest increase (24.26%) under the S1 treatment, whereas HN85 peaked (5.52%) under the S2 treatment (Fig. 8E).

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis (Fig. 10) revealed a high degree of similarity in the response patterns of the two varieties. Membrane damage indicators (MDA and REC) were significantly and positively correlated with both ion accumulation (Na+ and Ca2+) and osmolyte content (Pro and SS). This reflects the concomitant occurrence of ion toxicity and oxidative stress induced by salinity. Conversely, MDA and REC showed significant negative correlations with antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and APX) and soluble protein (SP) content, underscoring the critical role of the antioxidant system in mitigating membrane lipid peroxidation. Furthermore, Na+ accumulation was significantly negatively correlated with the levels of mineral elements (Mg2+ and Zn2+). Notably, in HN85, Na+ exhibited significant negative correlations with POD and CAT activities, suggesting that the antioxidant enzymes in HN85 are more susceptible to ion toxicity than those in ZLD136. The cultivar-specific differences further revealed distinct adaptive strategies. In HN85, K+ was significantly positively correlated with SOD, and Mg2+ and Zn2+ were positively correlated with SP, suggesting that these mineral elements may facilitate soluble protein formation in the sensitive cultivar. However, the significant negative correlation between POD and Na + in HN85 reflects the high vulnerability of its POD activity to ionic stress. In contrast, for ZLD136, a significant positive correlation was observed between APX and Mg2+, further revealing the contribution of mineral elements to redox homeostasis in the salt-tolerant cultivar. Moreover, SP showed significant synergistic positive correlations with SOD and CAT and was also significantly positively correlated with COX. The positive correlations between COX and both Mg2+ and Zn2+ indicate that ZLD136 more effectively integrates mineral elements to ensure a stable respiratory energy supply.

Comprehensive analysis of morphological, photosynthetic, and physiological changes in different soybean cultivars under salt stress

To comprehensively analyze the systemic response characteristics of soybean to salt stress, the dynamic changes in morphological traits, photosynthetic capacity, and physiological parameters were integrated over a time course (1–7 d) (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Regarding morphological characteristics, salt stress exhibited a significant negative effect on soybean morphology and biomass accumulation (Fig. S1-1). With prolonged stress duration, the bubble sizes representing the relative values of each parameter gradually decreased. Although the total root length and root surface area of both cultivars showed a slight increase at 1 d of stress, all morphological parameters except root tip number were significantly inhibited by 7 d (the average relative values of morphological parameters in ZLD136 and HN85 decreased by 0.93 and 1.05, respectively). Furthermore, ZLD136 exhibited better overall morphological maintenance than HN85. The responses of the internal photosynthetic and fluorescence systems exhibited distinct differential and stage-specific characteristics (Fig. S1-2). Photosynthetic gas exchange parameters demonstrated high environmental sensitivity, decreasing significantly as early as 1 d of stress. By 7 d, the cumulative reductions in the relative values of Pn, Tr, and Gs reached 1.50–1.92 in ZLD136 and 1.61–2.54 in HN85. In contrast, the responses of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters exhibited a distinct lag, with most parameters showing significant fluctuations only at 7 d. Except for the heat dissipation-related parameters PhiNPQ and NPQt, which increased significantly in both cultivars (with increments of 4.25 and 5.65), the other fluorescence parameters decreased significantly (with reductions ranging from 0.14 to 0.57 and 0.06 to 0.94, respectively). Further investigation revealed that physiological parameters possessed the highest sensitivity to salt stress, responding notably faster than the morphological and photosynthetic parameters (Fig. S1-3). At 1 d of stress, both cultivars initiated stress responses, characterized by significant increases in MDA, REC, osmotic adjustment substances (Pro and SS), and the activities of certain antioxidant enzymes (POD and CAT). However, as the stress continued to 5 and 7 d, the increased parameters were predominantly concentrated in membrane damage indicators (REC and MDA) and osmotic adjustment substances (with relative value increments reaching 1.41–117.93). Concurrently, CAT and APX activities decreased significantly over time, indicating an impairment of the antioxidant defense system. Based on the response timing and average variation magnitude of all parameters, the order of parameter sensitivity to salt stress was determined as follows: physiological parameters > photosynthetic gas exchange parameters > morphological parameters > chlorophyll fluorescence parameters.

Frequency distribution analysis further quantified the dynamic evolution of damage characteristics and defense strategies in soybean under different salt concentrations (S1–S4) (Fig. 11). With increasing salt concentration, the peak distribution of the relative values of morphological parameters significantly shifted to the left, from 0.95 (S1, accounting for 37.50%) to 0.55 (S4, accounting for 28.75%). The proportion of inhibited parameters (relative value < 1.0) increased from 55.00% to 97.50%. Further analysis indicated that under the S1 and S2 treatments, promotional effects (relative value > 1.0) were relatively widely distributed among root system and biomass parameters. However, under the S3 and S4 treatments, parameters with relative values > 1.0 were highly concentrated in root tip number, accounting for 59.00% and 100%, respectively. This indicates that despite severe impairment of overall biomass, root tip number was preferentially maintained under high salt stress.

Regarding photosynthetic and fluorescence parameter, the proportion of inhibited parameters (< 1.0) increased from 52.08% (S1) to 80.21% (S4) with rising salt concentration. Under the S4 treatment, the frequency distribution was relatively dispersed. Although the largest interval midpoint was 0.85 (accounting for 13.54%), the proportions of the 0.25, 0.35, and 0.75 intervals all exceeded 10%, reflecting the differential impairment degrees between gas exchange (0.2–0.4) and fluorescence (0.7–0.9) parameters. In contrast, parameters with relative values > 1.0 (with the overall proportion dropping to 19.79%) were almost exclusively concentrated in NPQt, PhiNPQ, and qL. This distribution characteristic indicates a pronounced activation of photoprotective mechanisms under conditions of severely restricted photosynthetic carbon assimilation.

At the physiological level, the data further revealed a shift in the focus of soybean physiological responses under different salt concentrations. The proportion of parameters with relative values > 1.0 exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, peaking at 62.50% under the S2 treatment. Composition analysis of the > 1.0 parameters showed that as stress intensified, the cumulative proportion of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT) decreased from 30.19% (S1) to 8.33% (S4), whereas osmotic adjustment substances and membrane damage indicators increased from 22.64% to 26.42% at S1 to 45.83% and 33.33% at S4, respectively. Simultaneously, under the S3 and S4 treatments, parameters with relative values < 1.0 were highly concentrated in antioxidant enzymes (accounting for 53.85% and 56.25%, respectively). This trend indicates an adjustment in defense priorities with increasing salt stress severity. Under mild-to-moderate stress, the response predominantly involved the activation of antioxidant enzymes; however, under severe stress conditions, accompanied by the impairment of the antioxidant defense system, the physiological response transitioned primarily toward the substantial accumulation of osmotic adjustment substances.

Discussion

When plants are subjected to environmental stress, physiological changes ultimately manifest in their external morphology. Thus, morphological traits are the most direct indicators of stress tolerance and are frequently used to evaluate plant responses to adverse environments [26, 27]. In this study, the aboveground growth of both soybean cultivars was significantly inhibited with increasing salt concentration and prolonged stress duration, which is consistent with previous observations of reduced plant height and biomass in soybean under salt stress [28, 29]. However, the salt-tolerant cultivar ZLD136 exhibited a significantly lower degree of impairment than the salt-sensitive HN85, demonstrating superior morphological adaptability. This difference likely stems from more efficient ion compartmentalization or osmotic adjustment mechanisms in ZLD136, which mitigate high-salinity-induced cellular damage and thereby maintain relatively higher biomass. Furthermore, we observed a certain promotional effect on dry weight and root fresh weight during the early stages of mild salt stress. This suggests that short-term and moderate salt stimulation may, to some extent, prime the plant’s defense system and optimize nutrient uptake [30, 31].

As the organ in direct contact with the soil, the root system is the first to encounter environmental fluctuations and subsequently undergoes rapid physiological and morphological adjustments. This adaptability is crucial for maintaining plant water homeostasis. We found that under low-salinity conditions, the number of root tips and root surface area in both cultivars, as well as the total root length in ZLD136, increased during the initial stage of stress. This phenomenon diverges from the traditional view that salt stress uniformly inhibits root growth [32]. This morphological change likely reflects a stress-induced structural adjustment. Specifically, under mild stress conditions, the expansion of the root exploration area (manifested as increased root surface area and volume) contributes to alleviating the water uptake constraints imposed by osmotic pressure changes [33]. This improvement in macroscopic morphology may be closely related to the optimization of microstructures, such as the development of internal vascular tissues and increased cortical thickness. However, as stress intensity escalated, excessive ion accumulation and severe osmotic stress eventually overwhelmed the plant’s buffering capacity, ultimately resulting in severe inhibition of root growth [34, 35].

Photosynthesis is critical for the survival of all green plants, playing a vital role in growth by synthesizing the carbon skeletons that form the core of biomolecules. Consequently, measuring leaf photosynthetic parameters has become an essential approach for evaluating plant salt tolerance [36]. The overall decline in the photosynthetic gas exchange parameters (Pn, Gs, Tr) of soybean under salt stress further verifies the high sensitivity of the photosynthetic apparatus to environmental stress, which is consistent with findings in Coreopsis tinctoria [37]. Extensive research indicates that the decline in photosynthetic rate is primarily attributed to stomatal and non-stomatal limitations. The former, driven by restricted stomatal conductance, typically manifests as a concurrent decrease in Pn, Gs, and Ci. The latter, caused by factors such as reduced chloroplast and Rubisco activities or impaired RuBP regeneration, is characterized by a decrease in Pn and Gs accompanied by an increase in Ci [38, 39]. Other studies have shown that the decline in Gs exerts a stronger impact on Tr than on Pn, indicating that transpiration is highly dependent on stomatal movement, and partial stomatal closure facilitates an effective improvement in water use efficiency [40]. In the present study, during the initial stage of the S4 treatment, the Ci of HN85 decreased in parallel with Gs, indicating that stomatal limitation was dominant at this point, and the restricted CO2 supply caused by rapid stomatal closure was the primary cause of the photosynthetic decline. However, as the stress duration prolonged, despite the continuous decline in Gs, the Ci of HN85 did not decrease further but instead exhibited an upward trend. This phenomenon suggests that prolonged high-salinity toxicity likely caused severe damage to the photosynthetic apparatus of HN85, leading to a gradual loss of intracellular CO2 assimilation capacity in mesophyll cells. Consequently, the photosynthetic inhibition transitioned from early stomatal limitation to a predominantly non-stomatal limitation. In contrast, the Ci of ZLD136 consistently maintained a downward trend under high-salinity conditions. This indicates that although the CO2 supply was substantially reduced due to stomatal closure, ZLD136 retained relatively intact photosynthetic carbon assimilation function and continued to consume internal CO2, thereby maintaining the photosynthetic inhibition at the stomatal limitation stage. This sustained stomatal limitation, while sacrificing a portion of instantaneous carbon assimilation capacity, likely optimized water use efficiency, thereby protecting the core photosynthetic components from more severe non-stomatal structural damage [41, 42]. Therefore, the lack of effective stomatal control in HN85 may be a critical factor underlying its high sensitivity to NaCl.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters further revealed differences in light energy utilization capacity between the cultivars. High salt stress led to a significant decrease in Fv/Fm and Fv′/Fm′, with a greater magnitude of decline observed in HN85. This indicates that salt stress disrupts chloroplast photosynthetic structures, leading to photoinhibition and photodamage, thereby reducing the primary light energy conversion efficiency and potential activity of photosystem II (PSII) [43, 44]. Regarding the light energy partitioning mechanism within PSII, Phi2, PhiNO, and PhiNPQ exhibit a strictly complementary relationship, satisfying the equation Phi2 + PhiNO + PhiNPQ = 1 [45]. Under abiotic stress, Phi2 and PhiNO typically decrease significantly, accompanied by a concurrent increase in PhiNPQ. Under abiotic stress, Phi2 and PhiNO typically decline, while PhiNPQ increases [46–48]. In the present study, as salt stress intensified, Phi2 decreased significantly in both cultivars; however, ZLD136 exhibited more pronounced increases in NPQt and PhiNPQ, and maintained qL for a longer duration. This suggests that the salt-tolerant cultivar can more effectively induce thermal dissipation mechanisms to convert excess light energy into heat, thereby mitigating oxidative damage caused by photoinhibition. Furthermore, under the S3 and S4 treatments, SPAD-equivalent index began to decline rapidly, further compromising normal photosynthetic performance, which is consistent with previous findings in soybean [49] and rice [50].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are widely recognized as dual-role molecules. At low concentrations, they act as key signaling molecules regulating normal plant growth, development, and stress responses; however, under abiotic stress, excessive ROS accumulation induces oxidative stress, leading to membrane lipid peroxidation, which manifests as significant increases in MDA and REC [51]. In this study, ZLD136 consistently maintained a relatively lower level of membrane damage compared to HN85, which is likely attributable to its highly efficient antioxidant enzyme regulatory capacity. SOD, POD, and CAT operate synergistically to maintain ROS below toxic levels [52]. Guo et al. [53] reported that under salt stress, the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT increased in two asparagus cultivars, with a more pronounced elevation observed in the salt-tolerant cultivar. In the present study, during the later stages of stress, SOD activity gradually declined as stress intensity increased, whereas POD and CAT maintained high activity levels. This divergent trend likely occurs because SOD catalyzes the dismutation of O2⋅− into H2O2. On one hand, excessive H2O2 accumulation may inhibit SOD activity via thiol oxidative modification; on the other hand, H2O2 acts as both a signaling molecule and a substrate, inducing and enhancing POD and CAT activities, thereby facilitating its own efficient scavenging [54, 55]. Furthermore, contrasting with some studies that observed increased APX activity under salt stress [56, 57], the overall APX activity in both cultivars in our study exhibited a downward trend. This may be because APX is highly sensitive to oxidative environments. The abrupt accumulation of ROS under salt stress likely exceeded the enzyme’s scavenging capacity, potentially causing irreversible oxidative damage and inactivation of the APX protein itself. Despite this overall decline, the two cultivars exhibited critical differences in the timing of APX inhibition. The APX activity of HN85 decreased rapidly at the onset of stress, indicating early and severe impairment of its APX scavenging system. In contrast, ZLD136 maintained relatively stable APX activity during the initial stress phase, effectively delaying the onset of oxidative damage. Moreover, ZLD136 demonstrated a more flexible regulatory capacity within its antioxidant network; even when APX activity eventually declined, POD and CAT activities remained at relatively high levels, which further reflects its superior ability to maintain membrane integrity.

Under adverse environmental conditions, plants accumulate osmolytes—such as proline (Pro), soluble sugars (SS), and soluble proteins (SP)—to maintain cellular water homeostasis and mitigate osmotic damage [58]. In the present study, ZLD136 consistently accumulated higher levels of Pro and SS throughout the stress period, indicating a superior osmoprotective capacity in the salt-tolerant genotype. This finding aligns closely with previous studies on barley [59] and sorghum [58]. However, the dynamic changes in SP content exhibited significant cultivar-specific differences. During the initial stage of stress, the transient increase in SP observed in both cultivars likely reflects an early stress response and the accelerated synthesis of stress-related proteins. Subsequently, as stress intensity and duration increased, the SP content in both cultivars gradually declined, with a more pronounced reduction in HN85. This decline likely occurred because severe salt stress exceeded the plants’ tolerance thresholds, thereby impairing de novo protein synthesis. Concurrently, excessive ROS accumulation induces oxidative damage to proteins, triggering their subsequent degradation by hydrolytic enzymes, which ultimately manifests as a significant reduction in overall SP content [60].

The mitochondrial electron transport chain (mETC) is the core of plant energy metabolism, consisting of the cytochrome c oxidase (COX) pathway and the alternative oxidase (AOX) bypass. The COX pathway drives ATP synthesis to ensure energy supply, whereas the AOX bypass acts as a non-energy-conserving electron sink, playing a pivotal role in diverting excess electrons [61]. In this study, we found that under salt stress, COX activity in both soybean cultivars exhibited an initial increase followed by a subsequent decline; however, the salt-tolerant cultivar ZLD136 maintained relatively high COX activity for a more extended period compared to HN85. This indicates that ZLD136 can sustain a more stable respiratory electron flow under stress, thereby ensuring the energy supply required for stress defense mechanisms. Furthermore, during the later stages of severe salt stress, ZLD136 sustained relatively high AOX activity, whereas that in HN85 declined rapidly. This divergence suggests that the salt-tolerant genotype can more effectively utilize the AOX pathway to alleviate over-congestion within the mETC, thereby preventing the over-reduction of the ubiquinone pool [62]. This synergistic regulation between the primary COX pathway and the AOX bypass enabled ZLD136 to effectively avert the complete stagnation of respiratory electron flow under extreme saline conditions, thereby enhancing its overall metabolic adaptability.

Maintaining ion homeostasis is a prerequisite for plants to sustain metabolic stability under salt stress [63]. In the present study, as NaCl concentration increased, leaf Na+ content in both cultivars increased continuously. Conversely, K+ content exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease during the early stage of stress, and a gradual decline during the later stage. This phenomenon suggests that during the initial stress phase, soybeans may maintain cellular osmotic pressure by enhancing the selective uptake and transport of K+. However, as Na+ continued to accumulate, competitive inhibition between these ions likely disrupted the K+ transport system, ultimately leading to the breakdown of ionic balance. While a conventional view posits that Na+ displaces Ca2+ on the plasma membrane, thereby reducing Ca2+ content [64], other studies indicate that maintaining high tissue Ca2+ concentrations under salt stress can promote K+ uptake, exerting a positive effect on plant salt tolerance [65]. In our study, Ca2+ content increased as stress severity escalated, which is consistent with findings in rice [66] and pomegranate [67]. This Ca2+ accumulation likely occurs because Ca2+, acting as a crucial second messenger, rapidly activates Ca2+ signaling pathways under stress to mitigate cellular damage. Specifically, stress triggers the opening of calcium channels on the plasma membrane, facilitating the influx of extracellular Ca2+ into the cytoplasm and resulting in a significant elevation of cytosolic Ca2+ concentration [68]. Furthermore, during the early stage of stress, Mg2+ levels in both cultivars initially increased and then decreased, followed by a continuous decline in the later stage; however, the overall changes were not statistically significant except on day 7. Although Zn2+ content fluctuated, it generally exhibited varying degrees of increase under low-salinity treatments. This trend likely reflects a prioritized metabolic allocation strategy under stress, aimed at safeguarding the central components of chlorophyll synthesis and providing essential cofactors for antioxidant enzymes [69, 70].

Rather than merely sustaining low Na+, high K+, and high Ca2+ levels, maintaining higher K+/Na+ and Ca2+/Na+ ratios serves as a more direct indicator of plant salt tolerance [71]. In this study, salt stress significantly decreased the ratios of all measured mineral elements to Na+, with the magnitude of reduction expanding as salinity increased, which is consistent with previous findings [72]. Notably, under mild to moderate salt stress (S1, S2), the ion ratios in ZLD136 were significantly higher than those in HN85, indicating a superior capacity for selective ion uptake and homeostatic regulation. However, under severe salinity (S3, S4), although ZLD136 still outperformed HN85 overall, the inter-cultivar differences gradually narrowed. This suggests that the severe saline environment exceeded the regulatory threshold of the salt-tolerant genotype, demonstrating that even relatively tolerant cultivars struggle to maintain an optimal ion balance under extreme salt stress.

Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the current study primarily focused on the physiological and ionic responses in leaves, without addressing the mechanisms of Na+ retention in roots and its long-distance transport. Additionally, our findings reveal only short-term adaptive strategies, which are insufficient to fully capture the long-term adaptability and final yield performance of these plants in complex field environments. Therefore, future research should incorporate long-term salt stress trials spanning the entire growth period. Systematically exploring the dynamics of ion transport and compartmentalization within the root-shoot continuum will be crucial for comprehensively elucidating the salt adaptation mechanisms of soybean at both the whole-plant and developmental levels.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated the physiological responses and regulatory mechanisms of soybean cultivars with contrasting salt tolerances under salt stress. The results demonstrated that while salt stress significantly inhibited soybean growth at the seedling stage, the adaptive morphological adjustments of the root system observed under low-salinity conditions likely serve as an effective strategy to mitigate early-stage osmotic stress. The salt-tolerant cultivar ZLD136 exhibited a superior growth status, which was closely associated with its capacity to maintain optimal root morphology, trigger efficient photoprotective thermal dissipation mechanisms, and deploy robust osmolyte accumulation and regulatory strategies. Furthermore, the activation of the antioxidant enzyme system and the alternative oxidase (AOX) pathway within the respiratory chain played pivotal roles in alleviating oxidative stress and maintaining cellular homeostasis. In conclusion, salt tolerance in soybean is not an isolated trait, but rather a highly synergistic network jointly orchestrated by ion balance, osmotic regulation, photoprotection, and antioxidant defense mechanisms.
