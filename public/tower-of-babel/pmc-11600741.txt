Transmissibility and control of tuberculosis in school outbreaks: a modeling study based on four outbreaks in China
Authors: Qiao Liu, Meng Yang, Qiuping Chen, Chan Liu, Yue He, Laurent Gavotte, Zeyu Zhao, Yanhua Su, Roger Frutos, Kaiwei Luo, Tianmu Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe elevated incidence of tuberculosis (TB) outbreaks in schools poses a significant challenge to prevention and control efforts in China. The commonality among most outbreaks is the failure to isolate patients at an early stage. Early isolation of TB cases is crucial for reducing the spread of TB within schools. This study aims to quantify the impact of different isolation proportions and durations on the attack rate of TB in schools. It explored the intervention effects of isolation measures in preventing and controlling TB in school settings. The goal is to provide insights that can serve as a reference for reducing the occurrence of TB outbreaks in schools.MethodsWe collected data from 4 school TB outbreaks. Susceptible-Exposed-Infected-Recovered (SEIR) model was used to fit the collected data and calculate transmissibility. Susceptible-Exposed-Infected-Quarantined-Recovered (SEIQR) model was employed to evaluate the effect of isolation. Effective reproduction numbers and cumulative incidence were used to quantify the transmissibility of TB.ResultsIn the 4 outbreaks, the majority of student cases were distributed in high grades of high school and universities, with a widespread occurrence of significant intervention delays. The median ascending reproduction value for the 4 outbreaks was 18.44 [interquartile range: 15.40-20.11]. Isolating 100% of the patients at the first month could reduce the number of cases by 99.47%, 87.99%, 96.48%, and 99.16%, respectively.ConclusionsThis study suggests that high schools and universities may represent significant high-risk environments for TB outbreaks. Early detection and isolation of cases are important strategies that can help reduce the risk of TB outbreaks, with observed case reductions of up to 99.47% when implemented promptly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10221-6.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a respiratory disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily transmitted through airborne droplets [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [2], in 2021, TB emerged as the second lethal infectious disease after coronavirus disease 2019, with a higher death rate than acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. An estimated 10 million people were diagnosed with TB, resulting in 1.5 million deaths globally. In 2020, China has been the world second large country with the highest TB burden while eight countries, including China, accounted for 57.33% of all new TB cases [2]. TB is still an important public health problem to be solved urgently.

An important reason for the high incidence of TB in China is the frequent occurrence of outbreaks, especially within schools. Globally, the number of reported TB outbreaks in schools has been increasing. For example, in Mongolia, the incidence rate among children aged 0–14 years in 2019 was 428 cases per 100,000 population, accounting for 10% of all reported TB cases [3, 4]. School TB outbreaks in schools are characterized by large number of patients and long duration. A meta-analysis of TB among Chinese students [5] revealed that 31.8% of school outbreaks lasted for more than four months. However, a common feature of most outbreaks is the failure to separate patients as soon as feasible and to hold them isolated until clinical improvement or diagnosis. Not only are early detection and isolated treatment of TB cases essential for lowering disease and death rates, but they are also critical for stopping the spread of TB in schools.

In the past, for most TB outbreaks, traditional epidemiological description or statistical methods were often used to analyze and study single outbreak events [6, 7]. Additionally, Fang et al. [8] established an early warning model using moving averages to help identify school TB outbreaks, while Wang et al. [9] explored the impact of diagnostic delays on TB transmission in schools. However, there has been a lack of systematic research on the multiple outbreaks utilized in transmission dynamics models. And the effectiveness of isolation in the prevention and control of TB in schools remains unclear [10–12]. Therefore, this study aims to analyze four TB outbreak incidents in China, summarizing and examining the transmission characteristics. By using a transmission dynamics model, the study will simulate the effectiveness of isolation under different isolation proportions and different isolate-time, providing insights for the prevention and control of TB in schools.

Methods

Data collection

We searched the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) for literature on school outbreaks. And then we chose publications for analysis that reported case counts higher than 20 and have precise reporting dates (at least to the month). The study ultimately included four school TB outbreaks (A, B, C, and D) that happened in three high schools and one university [13–16]. The information we gathered include total school population, gender, grade, reporting date, and case numbers.

Case diagnosis

The cases of four TB outbreak events in this study were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Tuberculosis (WS288-2008) [17].

Simulation of isolation

In this study, the SEIRQ model was constructed to simulate the effect of isolation measure in two dimensions according to the transmission mode of 4 TB outbreaks. Dimension 1: When the isolation proportion is set to 10 ~ 100%. Dimension 2: Simulate isolation measures at different time points.

In this study, effective reproduction number (Reff) and cumulative incidence were used to quantify the transmissibility of diseases.

Reff refers to the number of people who can be infected by a patient during the average illness period in the process of infectious disease transmission with certain intervention measures. Usually, when Reff is greater than 1, infectious diseases will be prevalent among people; when Reff is less than 1, it is considered that infectious diseases will gradually disappear. Agent-based model analysis

Based on the natural history of TB, we created an agent-based model (ABM) in which students were represented as agents with specific classes and dormitories [19]. Agents may be in these four states: susceptibility, exposure, infection, and recovery. Initially, each school had an infected person, and transmission occurred through varying frequencies of contact with classmates, roommates, and others. We performed 1000 times simulations to obtain the mean [20], standard deviation, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the new infections. Details can be found in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

In this study, R 4.2.2 was used for mathematical modelling, and the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with tolerance set at 0.05 was used for curve fitting. The degree of model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2) and the p-value. The “ggplot2” package in R 4.2.2 was utilized to create the graphical plots for visual representation. Python 3.7.0 was utilized for the ABM analysis. The Python code used for model building and figure generation is available at the GitHub repository (https://github.com/heyueyueyue/TB-ABM).

Our study collected data from four TB outbreaks in schools. One occurred in a university, while three occurred in high school. Student cases were primarily concentrated among junior year of college students (86.2%) and senior 2 in high school students (69.5%). In the four outbreaks, there were 94 male cases (70.7%) and 39 female cases (29.3%), with no statistically significant difference found between genders (p = 0.08). Epidemiological characteristic of four events

Event A occurred at a university with a total enrolment of 9,932 students and 1,167 faculty. Between August 2003 and March 2005, 51 cases of pulmonary TB (PTB) were detected, consisting of 50 students and one cafeteria staff member. The age range of student cases was 22 to 24 years old. Event B took place in a county high school with 2,655 students and 241 teacher members. During the period, the weather was hot, and all students in the classrooms were in a closed air-conditioned environment. By March 2009, a total of 26 cases were identified. Event C transpired in a comprehensive school encompassing primary, junior high, and senior high school grades. However, the high school section where the outbreak occurred was in a separate building, isolated from the primary and junior high education building. There were a total of 27 classes in the high school section, with 1,329 students and approximately 90 teacher members. Since the first case was reported on February 10, 2009, the high school section of the school has reported 31 cases. Event D unfolded in a private high school, identifying a total of 25 PTB cases. All cases emanated from the senior 2 in high school, with 14 cases originating from the same class. Factors contributing to the escalation and eventual outbreak of this incident included delayed diagnosis of the index case, refusal of prophylactic medication by close contacts, and insufficient monitoring and follow-up of close contacts during the later stages of the outbreak.

Time trend and implementation of intervention measures

The implementation of intervention measures for the four outbreak events is depicted in Fig. 2. The median duration of the four events was 19 months. The time of case discovery was concentrated mainly in the months leading up to the end of the outbreaks, primarily associated with extensive screening efforts. Measures taken during the outbreaks primarily focused on screening close contacts and administering preventive medication. Notably, all four incidents experienced significant delays in diagnosis, ranging from 40 to 80 days. Model fitting and transmissibility

According to the R2, the transmission dynamics model provided a good fit for the cumulative incidence of several outbreaks, which proved that the model was suitable for this study (Fig. 3), with R2 values of 0.95, 0.90, 0.85, and 0.90 for the four events, respectively. Additionally, we further applied an ABM for data fitting, and the results showed that the 95% CI of the ABM fit encompassed both the data reported in this study and the data fitted using the transmission dynamics model, which supports the reliability of the model's results (Supplementary Material).Fig. 3Result of model fitting and prediction of four events

Result of model fitting and prediction of four events

Based on the results from the transmission dynamics model, the Rascent of the four events were 17.08, 19.80, 10.34, and 21.05, respectively and the median Rascent for the four events was 18.44 [IQR: 15.40–20.11].Using the fitted model, we projected future trends of the outbreaks. Our findings indicated that, if the current trend continued, the peak number of predicted cases for these four events would reach 1,030 (t = 27), 276 (t = 15), 113 (t = 22), and 135 (t = 23), accounting for 9.28%, 9.53%, 7.93%, and 9.67% of the corresponding total population, respectively (Fig. 3).

Effectiveness of isolation

This study simulated and evaluated the effectiveness of isolation under the two dimensions of different isolation proportion and different isolate-time. As shown in Fig. 4, with an increase in isolation proportion and earlier isolation time, the number of cases gradually decreased. In Event A, when the isolation proportion ranged from 0.8 to 1.0 and the isolation time was t = 0 or t = 1, the cumulative number of cases could be reduced to below 1. Similarly, in Event D, when δ = 0.8–1.0 and t = 0–2 or δ = 0.7 and t = 0–1, the cumulative number of cases could also fall below 1. The study found that when the isolation proportion was determined, the faster the implementation of isolation measures led in a lower cumulative incidence. Moreover, the optimal isolation measure, isolating 100% of patients, could reduce the number of cases by over 85%. According to the simulation results of the SEIQR model, under isolation proportion of 1.0 and with the earliest isolate-time, the case number of events A, B, C and D were 0, 3, 1, and 0, respectively. Compared with the simulation predictions of the SEIR model (without isolation measures), proportion of decreasing case number in events A, B, C, and D was 99.47%, 87.99%, 96.48%, and 99.16%, respectively.

However, with the postponement of isolate-time, the effect of isolation would gradually be decreased. Even if the isolation proportion was increased, there would be still no way to reduce the cumulative incidence. When the isolation time was delayed until the actual end time and the isolation proportion ranges between 0.1 and 1, the reduction rate in the number of cases for Events A, B, C, and D remained almost unchanged. However, when the isolation time is delayed to one day before the actual end time, the reduction rates for Events A, B, C, and D ranged from 7.08% to 12.00%.

Discussions

This study employed a transmission dynamics model to investigate four TB outbreak events in China. Through a quantitative analysis of the rapid rise of cases during TB outbreaks across various incidents, the study introduced the concept of the transmission capacity (Reff or Rascent). Additionally, the research simulated and evaluated the comprehensive intervention effects in two dimensions: altering isolation proportions and isolate-time. The findings robustly demonstrate that prompt identification of patients and the implementation of isolation measures constitute highly effective interventions for controlling the transmission of TB.

This study revealed a considerable capability for TB transmission during outbreaks, with a median Rascent of 18.44 [IQR: 15.40–20.11] across four outbreaks. In other words, in densely populated settings, one patient could on average transmit the disease to eighteen individuals. Previous researches employed mathematical modeling to study the transmission dynamics of TB showed that the Reff of TB ranged from 1.2 to 3.5 [21, 22]. However, these studies were primarily based on family and community studies, and the Reff of school outbreaks was generally significantly larger than this range. A research had assessed the Reff for a single campus outbreak as 8.4 [23], while the estimated range of transmissibility in our study of four events was slightly higher. This difference may be attributed to factors such as the number of infected individuals, total population size, and the severity of the outbreak [24, 25]. Therefore, when quantifying the transmissibility, we should also combine different geographical environment, campus management and other factors to make a more accurate assessment.

TB outbreaks are most likely to occur in densely populated locations, especially schools. They are very prone to TB outbreaks due to crowded personnel, closed teaching and workplace and poor ventilation [18]. In this study, all four outbreak incidents occurred within schools, where classrooms frequently had closed doors and windows, and had crowded living rooms. The likelihood of the transmission of M. tuberculosis depends on various factors, including the concentration of infectious airborne droplet nuclei, duration of exposure to these particles, close contact with infected individuals, host susceptibility, and the infectivity of the TB strain. In enclosed spaces with high population density, the risk of infection rises dramatically. As a result, schools ought to improve environmental hygiene, carry out routine maintenance, and promote disinfection and proper ventilation in public areas.

Furthermore, we discovered that the occurrence of TB outbreaks in schools was not solely correlated with students' ages, but also with their lifestyle across various grade levels. Most student cases were mostly distributed in high grade of senior school and university [26]. In China, High school pupils have a strict entrance examination system for progression. Consequently, many students in high grade spend a lot of time studying for exams, which may lead to lack of sleep time, staying up late, lack of exercise, low immunity, high stress, psychological anxiety and so on. On the other hand, college students have more free time to socialize, increasing the opportunity to infect TB with more parties and social contacts. All of factors above are the inducements of TB infection in student. Moreover, it was discovered that some students did not timely report to the doctor after the onset of the disease, which led to the aggravation and spread of the disease.

Timely diagnosis, rapid detection of sick patients, and enhanced isolation proportions prove to be effective interventions in controlling TB transmission. In this study, as the isolation proportion increased and isolation time advanced, the number of cases greatly decreased. This finding was consistent with Archana et al.'s study [27], which suggests a significant reduction in TB incidence even just with isolating half of the cases. And Laura and others also found that through control measures: early detection and isolation of patients infected with M. tuberculosis, the infection rate could be reduced by 50% [28]. Besides, we observed that with the same isolation time, a higher isolation proportion resulted in a lower incidence rate. However, the effectiveness of isolation gradually diminished with delayed implementation, and even with a higher isolation proportion, the impact on incidence rate reduction was not substantial. This implies that if the epidemic is not effectively controlled in the early stages, the effectiveness of isolation measures in the later stages will be limited. In real-life scenarios, schools typically implement measures such as suspending classes and encouraging treatment after confirming TB cases. The challenge lies in how to enhance the rapid responsiveness of schools and students to the disease, highlighting the necessity of routine education, morning checks, and implementation of measures for absences due to illness in the prevention and control of TB in schools.

However, this study still has some limitations. Firstly, the parameters of model construction mainly came from references, without sensitivity analysis, which may have some influence on results. Secondly, the study's data were derived from four articles, lacking direct epidemic data collected from schools and may not comprehensively reflect the characteristics of TB transmission. Additionally, while our model accounted for the school environment, it did not simulate variations between semester and vacation periods, which could affect transmission dynamics over longer time scales. Given the long diagnostic delays and low completion rates of preventive medication in the four cases examined in this study, our next step will be to quantify the effects of reducing diagnostic delays and increasing preventive medication completion rates on TB prevention and management in schools.

Conclusion

High schools and universities emerge as high-risk environments for TB within schools. The four outbreak incidents exhibited strong infectivity, with a median Rascent of 18.44 [IQR: 15.40–20.11]. Isolating all patients in the first month of the four outbreaks resulted in case reductions of 99.47%, 87.99%, 96.48%, and 99.16%, respectively. Early isolation and maximizing isolation rates as soon as possible are crucial for preventing and controlling TB outbreaks, proving to be highly effective in preventing transmission.
