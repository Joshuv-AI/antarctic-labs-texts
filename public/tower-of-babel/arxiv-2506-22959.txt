The dimension of random subsets of self-similar sets generated by branching random walk
Pieter Allaart, Lauritz Streck

Abstract
. Given a self-similar set Λ \Lambda that is the attractor of an iterated function system (IFS) { f 1 , … , f N } \{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\} , consider the following method for constructing a random subset of Λ \Lambda : Let 𝐩 = ( p 1 , … , p N ) \mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N}) be a probability vector, and label all edges of a full M M -ary tree independently at random with a number from { 1 , 2 , … , N } \{1,2,\dots,N\} according to 𝐩 \mathbf{p} , where M ≥ 2 M\geq 2 is an arbitrary integer. Then each infinite path in the tree starting from the root receives a random label sequence which is the coding of a point in Λ \Lambda . We let F ⊂ Λ F\subset\Lambda denote the set of all points obtained in this way. This construction was introduced by Allaart and Jones [ J. Fractal Geom. 12 (2025), 67–92], who considered the case of a homogeneous IFS on ℝ \mathbb{R} satisfying the Open Set Condition (OSC) and proved non-trivial upper and lower bounds for the Hausdorff dimension of F F . We demonstrate that under the OSC, the Hausdorff (and box-counting) dimension of F F is equal to the upper bound of Allaart and Jones, and extend the result to higher dimensions as well as to non-homogeneous self-similar sets.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we compute the Hausdorff dimension of certain random subsets of self-similar sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, constructed via a branching random walk, or equivalently, via random labelings of infinite trees. Our main theorem solves a problem introduced by Allaart and Jones
[
2
]
in a considerably more general setting.

A
self-similar set
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, corresponding to a finite collection of similar contractions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is the unique non-empty compact set
\Lambda
such that

\Lambda=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(\Lambda).

It is said to satisfy the
open set condition
(OSC) if there is a non-empty bounded open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f_{i}(U)\subset U
for all
i
and
f_{i}(U)\cap f_{j}(U)=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
. While computing the dimensions of self-similar sets is a difficult problem in general, it has long been known that under the open set condition, the Hausdorff and box-counting dimensions of
\Lambda
are equal to the unique solution
s
of the equation

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s}=1,

where
r_{i}
is the contraction ratio, or Lipschitz constant, of
f_{i}
; see
[
7
,
12
]
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that we are given a self-similar set
\Lambda
satisfying the OSC. (For technical reasons we also assume that the above
U
is the interior of its closure- this will be explained in Section
2
in more detail). The set
\Lambda
can then be written as

\Lambda=\left\{x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{i_{n}}(0):\mathbf{i}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

We now construct a random subset
F
of
\Lambda
as follows. Fix an
M\in\mathbb{N}
with
M\geq 2
and consider the infinite
M
-ary tree (the infinite tree in which the root vertex has degree
M
and all other vertices have degree
M+1
) with edge set
E
. Fix a probability vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N})\in(0,1)^{N}
and let
\omega
be the random vector on
\{1,\dots,N\}^{E}
such that each
\omega(e)
is distributed according to
\mathbf{p}
(so
\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=j)=p_{j}
for all
j
) and
\omega(e),e\in E
are independent. We let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of infinite paths emanating from the root and write
P(k)
for the
k
-th edge of a path
P\in\mathcal{P}
. In this paper, we calculate the dimension of the random subset
F
of
\Lambda
defined by

(1.1)

F=F(M,\mathbf{p}):=\left\{x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega(P(1))}\circ\dots\circ f_{\omega(P(n))}(0)\Big|P\in\mathcal{P}\right\}.

To help the reader visualize the model, we first consider the explicit example of the Cantor set in
\mathbb{R}
(corresponding to
f_{1}(x)=x/3
and
f_{2}(x)=(x+2)/3
) and the binary tree. In this setting, we are given the Cantor set

C=\left\{x=2\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{i}}{3^{i}}:x_{i}\in\{0,1\}\ \forall i\right\},

a number
p\in(0,1)
, and the infinite binary tree emanating from a root vertex in which each edge
e\in E
has an associated Bernoulli random variable
\omega(e)
with
\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=0)=p,\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=1)=1-p
. The random set
F
from (
1.1
) is then given by

F=\left\{x=2\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega(P(i))}{3}:P\in\mathcal{P}\right\}.

Or, to describe the construction of
F
more dynamically: Imagine a “branching random walk with exponentially decreasing step sizes”, as follows. At time
n=0
, an ancestor particle sits at
x=0
. Just before time
n=1
, this particle splits into two “children”, each of which instantaneously and independently either stays at
x=0
with probability
p
, or moves to the right by
2/3
with probability
1-p
. This process continues: At any time
n
, there are
2^{n}
particles, each of which splits into two particles just before time
n+1
. The
2^{n+1}
children each either stay at the same location as their parent particle, or move to the right by
2(1/3)^{n+1}
, independently of each other and of previous generations. Thus, each particle always moves to the left endpoint of a “basic interval” of the Cantor set. Let
F_{n}
denote the set of all points occupied by at least one particle at time
n
. Thus,
F_{0}=\{0\}
,

F_{1}=\begin{cases}\{0\}&\mbox{with probability $p^{2}$},\\
\{2/3\}&\mbox{with probability $(1-p)^{2}$},\\
\{0,2/3\}&\mbox{with probability $2p(1-p)$},\end{cases}

etc. The sequence of subsets
(F_{n})
then converges in the Hausdorff metric to the set
F
we are interested in.

Before stating the most general version of our results (Theorem
2.2
below), we first present two special cases, both with two maps
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
and a binary tree (so
N=M=2
). We assume the OSC is satisfied, with the open set
U
being the interior of its closure. As above, we are given a
p\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=0)=p,\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=1)=1-p
for all
e\in E
. The first theorem gives an explicit formula for the dimension of
F
in the homogeneous case, when
r_{1}=r_{2}
. The second theorem deals with the inhomogeneous case (
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
) and illustrates that there are in general three essentially different cases with regard to the probability vector
\mathbf{p}
. This sets our model apart from other popular random fractal constructions, such as Mandelbrot percolation, where (conditioned on non-extinction) the Hausdorff dimension is given by a single formula.

Theorem 1.1
.

For the homogeneous IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
with equal ratios
r_{1}=r_{2}=r
and the binary tree,

\dim_{H}F=\dim_{B}F=\frac{\xi\log\xi+(1-\xi)\log(1-\xi)}{\log r}

almost surely, where
\xi=\xi(p)
is defined by

\xi(p):=\frac{\log 2p}{\log p-\log(1-p)}

for
p\neq 1/2
, and
\xi(1/2):=1/2
.

The setting of Theorem
1.1
(and arbitrary homogeneous self-similar sets) was recently considered by Allaart and Jones in
[
2
]
, who established the upper bound in Corollary
2.4
below and gave a non-trivial lower bound (which is sharp in the case of
p=1/2
). In that paper it is also shown that, when
p=1/2
, the random subset
F
has the maximal Hausdorff dimension
s
but has
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure zero. This article improves and significantly extends the results of
[
2
]
, on the one hand by calculating the exact value for the dimension and on the other by proving the results in a much more general setting (of inhomogeneous self-similar sets with arbitrarily many maps and a full
M
-ary trees, for arbitrary
M\geq 2
).

Suppose next that we have two similarities
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
but with different contraction ratios
r_{1}<r_{2}
, which makes the dimension of
\Lambda
equal to the unique
s
such that

(1.2)

r_{1}^{s}+r_{2}^{s}=1.

Theorem 1.2
.

For the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
with ratios
r_{1}<r_{2}
and the binary tree, let
s
be given by (
1.2
), and let
\tilde{s}=\tilde{s}(p)
satisfy

(1.3)

pr_{1}^{\tilde{s}}+(1-p)r_{2}^{\tilde{s}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Furthermore, define

\hat{s}=\hat{s}(p):=\frac{\log c_{1}(p)\log(2(1-p))-\log c_{2}(p)\log(2p)}{\log r_{1}\log(2(1-p))-\log r_{2}\log(2p)},

where

c_{1}(p):=\frac{\log(2(1-p))}{\log(1-p)-\log p},\qquad c_{2}(p):=\frac{\log(2p)}{\log(p)-\log(1-p)}=1-c_{1}(p).

There are thresholds
p_{*}
and
p^{*}
satisfying
0<p_{*}<1/2<p^{*}<1
such that, with probability one,

\dim_{H}F=\dim_{B}F=\begin{cases}s&\mbox{if $p_{*}\leq p\leq 1/2$},\\
\tilde{s}(p)&\mbox{if $1/2\leq p\leq p^{*}$},\\
\hat{s}(p)&\mbox{if $p\leq p_{*}$ or $p\geq p^{*}$}.\end{cases}

Specifically,
p_{*}
is the unique solution in
(0,1/2)
of the equation

\frac{\log(2p)}{\log(2(1-p))}=-\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{s}.

Thus,
\dim_{H}F
, as a function of
p
, is strictly increasing on
[0,p_{*}]
, constant on
[p_{*},1/2]
, and strictly decreasing on
[1/2,1]
.

Remark 1.3
.

That the Hausdorff and box dimensions of
F
coincide follows since
F
is “statistically self-similar” in the sense of Graf
[
6
]
(see
[
2

), but it is also a consequence of our proof, since we will estimate the Hausdorff dimension from below, and the box dimension from above.

1.1.
Connections with the literature

There is a vast literature on random fractals, and we limit ourselves here to the works most closely related to our model.
Statistically self-similar sets were introduced independently in the 1980s by Falconer
[
5
]
, Graf
[
6
]
, and Mauldin and Williams
[
11
]
. These models belong to a class of
infinite-variable
random fractals, where at each stage of the construction one chooses for each cylinder set at that stage an IFS at random from a given collection according to a given probability distribution, independently of other cylinders at that stage and of previous stages. This class also includes the model of
fractal percolation
, where one divides for instance a square into
M^{2}
subsquares (for some fixed
M\geq 2
) and retains each subsquare with probability
p
, or deletes it with probability
1-p
, independently of other subsquares, and keeps repeating this procedure ad infinitum.

By contrast, in
one-variable
random fractal models one chooses at each stage of the construction a single IFS at random and applies it to all cylinders at that stage. Hence, one-variable models are closer in nature to deterministic self-similar sets. To fill the void between the one-variable and the infinite-variable case, Barnsley et al.
[
3
]
introduced the
V
-variable
random fractals, which were further generalized by Järvenpää et al.
[
8
]
. Generalizing in a different direction, Troscheit
[
13
]
computes various fractal dimensions of both one-variable and infinite-variable random graph-directed self-similar sets.

In several of the above models, there is a positive probability that the limit set is empty, and one has to compute the dimension conditioned on non-extinction. The model we consider here precludes this possibility.

All of the above-mentioned papers assume some type of separation condition when computing the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set. Much less is known in the case of random self-similar sets with overlaps, although Koivusalo
[
9
]
manages to compute the Hausdorff dimension in a certain “uniform” model of random self-similar fractals without assuming any separation condition. In the present article, although we assume the open set condition for our initial IFS, the random subset
F
can be viewed as a statistically self-similar set with “extreme overlaps” - see
[
2
]
for an explanation. (In essence, we choose randomly from a collection of IFSs which may have one or more maps repeated.) As such, we believe that this article is a useful step towards a better understanding of random fractals with overlaps.

Aside from
[
2
]
, the only other paper we have found that considers a setup similar to ours is the work by Benjamini et al.
[
4
]
. Motivated by the study of Bernoulli convolutions, they consider an overlapping IFS of two maps and a randomly labeled binary tree. Rather than the random attractor
F
, their interest is mainly in the resulting random self-similar measure
\mu
supported on
F
; that is, the weak limit as
n\to\infty
of the distribution of the particles at stage
n
of the branching random walk.

2.
Definitions and statements of main results

We repeat the setup from the Introduction in a more formal manner. Consider an iterated function system (IFS)
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}
of similar contractions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
), where
N\geq 2
. We assume that there is a non-empty bounded open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(O1)

f_{i}(U)\subset U
for
i=1,\dots,N
;

(O2)

f_{i}(U)\cap f_{j}(U)=\emptyset
for all
i\neq j
; and

(O3)

U
is the interior of its closure.

Conditions (O1) and (O2) are the usual open set condition. The extra condition (O3), which is satisfied by many well-known examples of self-similar sets, will be needed to prove the lower bound for the Hausdorff dimension of our random subset. At the moment we do not know how to eliminate this condition.

Let
\Lambda
be the attractor of the IFS; that is,
\Lambda
is the unique non-empty compact set such that

\Lambda=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(\Lambda).

Then the Hausdorff dimension of
\Lambda
is the unique real number
s_{0}
satisfying

(2.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s_{0}}=1,

where
r_{i}
is the contraction ratio of
f_{i}
.

We construct a random subset of
\Lambda
in the following way. Let
M\geq 2
, and consider a full infinite
M
-ary tree
\mathcal{T}_{M}
; that is, a tree in which one vertex, the
root
, has degree
M
and all other vertices have degree
M+1
. Let
E
be the set of edges of
\mathcal{T}_{M}
, and put
\Omega:=\{1,2,\dots,N\}^{E}
. We equip
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
with the discrete topology and
\Omega
with the corresponding product topology. This induces a Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
on
\Omega
. We now let
\mathbb{P}
be the Bernoulli measure on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B})
associated with the probability vector
\mathbf{p}
. That is, under
\mathbb{P}
, the random variables
\omega(e),e\in E
are independent with
\mathbb{P}(\omega(e)=i)=p_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
. We will let
\mathbb{E}
denote the corresponding expectation operator.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all infinite paths in
\mathcal{T}_{M}
that start at the root. For a path
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
P(k)
the
k
th edge of
P
.

For
\omega\in\Omega
, define the set

F(\omega):=\Bigg\{x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\omega(P(1))}\circ\dots\circ f_{\omega(P(n))}(0)\Big|P\in\mathcal{P}\Bigg\}.

Clearly,
F(\omega)\subset\Lambda
, giving the trivial upper bound
\dim_{H}F\leq s_{0}
. Our approach in this article is to express the almost-sure dimension of
F
as the solution of a constrained maximization problem, which we then solve using elementary calculus.

In this paper, “
\log
” denotes the natural logarithm, and we make the usual convention that
0\log 0=0
. We define the following functions from
[0,\infty)^{N}
to
\mathbb{R}
:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\left(\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}-\log x_{i}\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\log(Mp_{i}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle h(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\log r_{i}.

Write
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
for short.

Theorem 2.1
.

We have,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

(2.5)

\dim_{H}F=\dim_{B}F=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},\ h(\mathbf{x})=1\big\}.

Proving this result will be our main task in the remainder of the paper. In Section
3
, we sketch its proof and explain where the different terms in the optimization problem come from. The constrained maximum cannot exceed the dimension of
\Lambda
, because

\dim_{H}\Lambda=\dim_{B}\Lambda=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},\ h(\mathbf{x})=1\big\}.

This last equation may be verified readily using Lagrange multipliers and the Moran equation (
2.1
). An immediate consequence is that, if
Mp_{i}\geq 1
for each
i
, then
\dim_{H}F=\dim_{H}\Lambda
almost surely. We will see in the next theorem that this is the case already under weaker conditions.

Theorem
2.1
reduces the problem of calculating the dimension of
F
to solving an explicit optimization problem, which we do in Section
7
to get our main result, the explicit Theorem
2.2
. Note that the constrained maximum is well defined since the domain
[0,\infty]^{N}\cap\{\mathbf{x}:h(\mathbf{x})=1\}
is compact. Because of the presence of a minimum in the objective function, the solution of the optimization problem breaks into three cases: We have to consider the maximum of
\psi
, the maximum of
\psi+\phi
, and the constrained maximum of
\psi
subject to
\phi(\mathbf{x})=0
, all under the further constraint
h(\mathbf{x})=1
. (This explains the three cases in Theorem
1.2
.) Doing so leads to the following general result, in which
\tilde{s}
is the solution of

(2.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}=\frac{1}{M}.

Theorem 2.2
.

The Hausdorff dimension of the random subset
F
is determined as follows:

(i)

If

(2.7)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log(Mp_{i})\geq 0,

then
\dim_{H}F=s_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

(ii)

If

(2.8)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i})\leq 0,

then
\dim_{H}F=\tilde{s}\leq s_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, with equality if and only if
M=N
and
p_{i}=1/N
for
i=1,\dots,N
.

(iii)

If

(2.9)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log(Mp_{i})<0<\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i}),

then there is a unique pair
(\hat{s},\hat{t})
of real numbers, with
\hat{s}>0
, satisfying the equations

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}=1,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log(Mp_{i})=0,

and
\dim_{H}F=\hat{s}<s_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

Remark 2.3
.

The conditions (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) are not mutually exclusive, but if both hold then we must have that
M=N
and
p_{i}=1/N
for
i=1,\dots,N
, in which case
\hat{s}=s_{0}
.

The theorem simplifies considerably if the IFS is homogeneous, i.e. if all the maps have the same contraction ratio. The stated dimension in the next corollary corresponds to the upper bound of Allaart and Jones
[
2
]
.

Corollary 2.4

(The homogeneous case)
.

Assume
r_{i}=r\in(0,1)
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Define the two quantities

L(N,\mathbf{p}):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{-p_{i}},\qquad U(N,\mathbf{p}):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{-1/N},

and observe that
L(N,\mathbf{p})\leq N\leq U(N,\mathbf{p})
. We have,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

\dim_{H}F=\dim_{B}F=-\frac{\log\rho}{\log r},

where
\rho
is determined as follows:

(i)

If
M\leq L(N,\mathbf{p})
, then
\rho=M
.

(ii)

If
L(N,\mathbf{p})\leq M\leq U(N,\mathbf{p})
, then

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Mp_{i})^{\lambda},

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is the unique solution of

(2.12)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\lambda}\log(Mp_{i})=0.

(iii)

If
M\geq U(N,\mathbf{p})
, then
\rho=N
.

Remark 2.5
.

The hypothesis of case (ii) is always satisfied when
M=N
, since

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\log p_{i}\geq-\log N=\log\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\bigg)\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_{i},

by concavity of the logarithm. But the range of values of
M
satisfying
L(N,\mathbf{p})\leq M\leq U(N,\mathbf{p})
can be much larger. For instance, take
N=3
and
\mathbf{p}=(0.05,0.2,0.75)
. Then
\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{-p_{i}}\approx 1.9886
and
\big(\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}\big)^{-1/N}\approx 5.1087
, so
M
could be
2,3,4
or
5
.

Remark 2.6
.

Consider the homogeneous case:
r_{i}=r
for each
i
, and assume
M=N
and
p_{i}=1/N
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then Corollary
2.4
implies
\dim_{H}F=s_{0}=-\log N/\log r
. It was shown in
[
2
, Proposition 5.1]
that
\mathcal{H}^{s_{0}}(F)=0
almost surely in this case. We suspect that
\mathcal{H}^{s_{0}}(F)>0
when
M>N
, but have not been able to prove this. (On the other hand, something even stronger can be shown when
M\geq 2N
, namely with
\mathbb{P}
-probability 1,
F
contains a similar copy of the full attractor
\Lambda
of the IFS
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}
, and
F
is even equal to all of
\Lambda
with positive probability – see
[
2
, Remark 5.6]
.)

\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
. The estimate from below uses the main theorem of Mauldin and Williams
[

]
on the Hausdorff dimension in random recursive constructions. We derive an upper estimate for the cardinality of
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
in Section
5
, and a lower estimate in Section
6
. In Section
7
we solve the constrained optimization problem of Theorem
2.1
to deduce Theorem
2.2
. Finally, in Section
8
, we prove the special cases given in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
2.4
.

3.
Sketch of proof of Theorem
2.1

Here we sketch the main ideas of the proof and explain where the maps
\psi
,
\phi
and
h
come from. This section may be skipped without loss of continuity.

To start, we sketch why

(3.1)

\dim_{B}\Lambda=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},\ h(\mathbf{x})=1\big\},

where
\psi(\mathbf{x})
and
h(\mathbf{x})
are defined by (
2.2
) and (
2.4
), respectively. Although this identity can be deduced from the Moran equation using the method of Lagrange multipliers, we give a more direct explanation here because it will help us introduce the main concepts of the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
For ease of exposition, we assume that
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
is a self-similar set satisfying the open set condition with the open set
U
being an interval. Put
I:=\overline{U}
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we cover
\Lambda
by intervals of length
\approx e^{-n}
. More precisely, define the stopping set

(3.2)

\Gamma_{n}:=\{\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\dots i_{l}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{*}:r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{l}}\leq e^{-n}<r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{l}-1}\}.

The intervals
I_{\mathbf{i}}:=f_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{i_{l}}(I)
, for
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\dots i_{l}\in\Gamma_{n}
thus form an
e^{-n}
-cover of
\Lambda
, and these intervals do not overlap due to the open set condition. We need to estimate the cardinality of this cover. Suppose that
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
and that for each
j
, the letter
j
occurs
k_{j}
times in the word
\mathbf{i}
; that is,

(3.3)

\#\{t\leq|\mathbf{i}|:i_{t}=j\}=k_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

Then
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
implies

(3.4)

r_{\min}e^{-n}\leq\prod_{i}r_{i}^{k_{i}}\leq e^{-n},

where
r_{\min}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq N}r_{i}
. Let
\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
denote the set of all words
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
satisfying (
3.3
). Note these words all have the same length
l=k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}
. By Stirling’s approximation, we have

\#\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})=\binom{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\approx\prod_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{-k_{i}},

where
\alpha_{i}:=\frac{k_{i}}{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}
and the
\approx
is understood to be up to polynomial factors (see Lemma
5.2
for a more precise version). Setting
x_{i}:=k_{i}/n
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
, we thus obtain

\#\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\approx\prod_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{-k_{i}}=e^{n\psi(\mathbf{x})}.

Furthermore, there are at most polynomially many distinct tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
satisfying (
3.4
).
Hence, again ignoring polynomial factors,

\displaystyle\#\Gamma_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\max\{\#\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N}):(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\ \mbox{satisfies \eqref{eq:kisize}}\}

\displaystyle\approx\exp\big(n\max\{\psi(\mathbf{x}):h(\mathbf{x})=1\}\big),

for large
n
, since (
3.4
) translates into
h(\mathbf{x})=1+O(1/n)
. From these asymptotics, (
3.1
) follows.

How does this change when one instead considers the random subset
F
of
\Lambda
? To sketch the proof of the upper bound and explain how the function
\phi
comes in, we fix again a scale
n
and observe that for a word
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
, the interval
I_{\mathbf{i}}
is only needed in a covering of
F
if
I_{\mathbf{i}}\cap F\neq\emptyset
. Hence we consider the (random) subcollection

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}:

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}:I_{\mathbf{i}}\cap F\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}:\ \mbox{at least one path in $\mathcal{P}_{|\mathbf{i}|}$ is labeled $\mathbf{i}$}\}.

In parallel with the above, we also let
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
denote the set of all words
\mathbf{i}\in\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
satisfying (
3.3
).
Put

a_{\mathbf{i}}:=\mathbb{P}(I_{\mathbf{i}}\cap F\neq\emptyset).

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\mathbb{E}\big(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\mathbb{E}\big(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\big)

\displaystyle=\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})}a_{\mathbf{i}},

where
\lesssim
is again to be interpreted up to a polynomial factor, and the first maximum is over tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
satisfying (
3.4
).
Now for
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
with length
l=k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}
, we estimate

(3.5)

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{l}}\big\{\mbox{path $P$ gets label sequence $\mathbf{i}$}\big\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{l}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mbox{path $P$ gets label sequence $\mathbf{i}$}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,M^{l}\prod_{j=1}^{N}p_{j}^{k_{j}}\right\}=\min\left\{1,\prod_{j=1}^{N}(Mp_{j})^{k_{j}}\right\}.

Combining the last two estimates, we obtain

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim\max_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\binom{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\min\left\{1,\prod_{j=1}^{N}(Mp_{j})^{k_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\exp\big(n\max\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:h(\mathbf{x})=1\}\big),

which explains the presence of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
in Theorem
2.1
. The proof of the upper bound is completed by a standard application of Markov’s inequality and the Borel-Cantelli lemma; see Section
5
for the details.

We next sketch the proof of the lower bound. Adapting a technique of Liu and Wu
[
10
]
, we first construct a sequence
\{F_{n}\}
of (random) subsets of
F
such that for each
n
,
F_{n}
is the limit set of a Mauldin-Williams random recursive construction (see
[
11
]
) and satisfies

(3.7)

\dim_{H}F_{n}\sim\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}.

The details of this construction are given in Section
4
.
Letting
n\to\infty
then yields

\dim_{H}F\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}.

Since
\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}
, the task then turns to estimating the probabilities
a_{\mathbf{i}}
from below. This would be easy if the paths in the tree were completely independent, as
a_{\mathbf{i}}
would then be constant on
\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
, but the cross-dependence of the different paths due to shared edges can make some of the
a_{\mathbf{i}}
exponentially small. For example, if
p_{1}
is such that
Mp_{1}<1
and
\mathbf{i}
is such that
i_{1}=\dots=i_{k_{1}}=1
, then by reasoning analogous to what we used for the upper bound,

a_{\mathbf{i}}\leq(Mp_{1})^{k_{1}},

and since each
k_{i}
is of order
n
for the tuple
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
maximizing the expression in (
3.6
), this shows that
\min_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\to 0
exponentially fast. Thus, in order to estimate
\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}
from below, we need to exclude highly ordered words as in the above example. This motivates the definition of the special subset
D_{n}\subset\Gamma_{n}
in the proof (see Section
6
), which roughly speaking consists of the sufficiently “random-looking” sequences
\mathbf{i}
. Via standard estimates we can show that the number of these “random-looking” sequences is not much smaller than the cardinality of
\Gamma_{n}(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
, and for elements in
D_{n}
, we are able to show a sufficient lower bound on
a_{\mathbf{i}}
via an iteration.

4.
The random stopping set

For each finite word
\mathbf{i}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{*}
, let
J_{\mathbf{i}}:=f_{\mathbf{i}}(\bar{U})
, where we write
f_{\mathbf{i}}=f_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{i_{n}}
for
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
. We also let
\mathcal{P}_{l}
denote the set of all paths of length
l
in the
M
-ary tree starting from the root, for
l\in\mathbb{N}
. To simplify the presentation somewhat, we assume without loss of generality that
|U|=1
. Denote

r_{\min}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq N}r_{i}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad r_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}r_{i}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the stopping set

(4.1)

\Gamma_{n}:=\{\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\dots i_{l}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{*}:r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{l}}\leq e^{-n}<r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{l}-1}\}.

Observe that the collection
\{J_{\mathbf{i}}:\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}\}
is an
e^{-n}
-cover of
\Lambda
, hence of
F
. However, not all of these sets are needed to cover
F
, so we define also the smaller (random) set

(4.2)

\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}:=\{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}:\ \mbox{at least one path in $\mathcal{P}_{|\mathbf{i}|}$ is labeled $\mathbf{i}$}\}.

Then
\{J_{\mathbf{i}}:\mathbf{i}\in\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}\}
is a more efficient
e^{-n}
-cover of
F
. Since the sets in this cover are roughly the same size, we need to estimate their number. In fact, as the next proposition shows, it is sufficient to determine the exponential growth rate of the
expected
cardinality of
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
. Let
\mathbb{E}
denote the expectation operator associated with the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}\leq\dim_{H}F\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}F\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}.

Proof.

We first prove the upper bound. Let

\lambda:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Put
Z_{n}:=\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
. Then, for all large enough
n
,

\mathbb{P}\big(Z_{n}>e^{(\lambda+2\varepsilon)n}\big)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}(Z_{n})}{e^{(\lambda+2\varepsilon)n}}\leq\frac{e^{(\lambda+\varepsilon)n}}{e^{(\lambda+2\varepsilon)n}}=e^{-\varepsilon n}.

As the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(Z_{n}>e^{(\lambda+2\varepsilon)n}\big)
converges, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
Z_{n}\leq e^{(\lambda+2\varepsilon)n}
for all sufficiently large
n
with probability one. Therefore, since
f_{\mathbf{i}}(U)
has diameter approximately
e^{-n}
for any
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
,

\overline{\dim}_{B}F\leq\lambda+2\varepsilon\qquad\mbox{$\mathbb{P}$-almost surely},

and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
along a discrete sequence gives

\overline{\dim}_{B}F\leq\lambda
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

For the lower bound, we borrow a technique of Liu and Wu
[
10
]
. The idea is to construct a sequence
\{F_{n}\}
of (random) subsets of
F
such that for each
n
,
F_{n}
is the limit set of a Mauldin-Williams random recursive construction and satisfies

(4.3)

\dim_{H}F_{n}\sim\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(Z_{n})}{n}.

Letting
n\to\infty
then yields the desired lower bound.

For the remainder of this proof, let
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
be the set of all finite paths in
\mathcal{T}_{M}
that start at the root.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\omega\in\Omega
, let
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}={\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\omega)=\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}={\mathcal{B}}_{1}(\omega)
be the set of finite paths
P=(P(1),\dots,P(l))\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
which receive a label sequence from
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}(\omega)
and such that no path of the same length as
P
which is lexicographically smaller than (i.e. to the left of)
P
receives the same label sequence as
P
. Thus,
\#{\mathcal{B}}_{1}=\#{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
. Observe also that no path in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
extends any other, because no word in
{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
extends any other. (In terms of the branching random walk, if there are two or more particles occupying the same interval of length approximately
e^{-n}
, we remove all but one of them before continuing the process.)

Next, we proceed inductively. For
k=1,2,\dots
, assume
{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
have been constructed. Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}
be the set of paths
P=P^{\prime}P^{\prime\prime}
in
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
such that
P^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
and
P^{\prime\prime}
receives a label sequence in
\Gamma_{n}
, and furthermore, no path of the same length as
P
which is lexicographically smaller than
P
receives the same label sequence as
P
. (We continue to remove “duplicate” particles in the branching random walk.)

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}
be the set of all label sequences of paths in
{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}
. Thus (by induction),
\#{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}=\#{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}
. For each
\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
, let
Y_{\mathbf{i}}
be the number of words in
{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}
that begin with
\mathbf{i}
.

For each
k
, by construction, the sets
J_{\mathbf{i}}:=f_{\mathbf{i}}(\bar{U}):\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
, are non-overlapping since the IFS
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}
satisfies the open set condition. Furthermore, for
\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
,

\#\{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}:J_{\mathbf{i}^{\prime}}\subset J_{\mathbf{i}}\}=Y_{\mathbf{i}}.

Note that the random variables
Y_{\mathbf{i}}:\mathbf{i}\in\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
are i.i.d. with the same distribution as
Z_{n}
. Hence, the set

F_{n}:=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}J_{\mathbf{i}}

is the limit set of a Mauldin-Williams random recursive construction (see
[
11
]
). Clearly,
F_{n}\subset F
. Since
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\geq 1)=1
,
F_{n}
is nonempty with probability one. By the main theorem of
[
11
]
, the Hausdorff dimension of
F_{n}
is the unique number
s_{n}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}}(r_{i_{1}}\dots r_{i_{|\mathbf{i}|}})^{s_{n}}\right)=1.

(By condition (O3), the set
K:=\bar{U}
satisfies the assumption of Mauldin and Williams that
K
is the closure of its interior.)
By the definition of
\Gamma_{n}
, this implies

\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(Z_{n})}{n-\log r_{\min}}\leq\dim_{H}F_{n}=s_{n}\leq\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(Z_{n})}{n},

from which (
4.3
) follows.
∎

In view of Proposition
4.1
, to complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
, it remains to show that

(4.4)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}\leq s^{*}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}

(and hence, the limit of
n^{-1}\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})
actually exists),
where
s^{*}
is the right-hand side of (
2.5
), i.e.

s^{*}:=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},\ h(\mathbf{x})=1\big\},

where
\psi
,
\phi
and
h
were defined in (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), respectively.
Our proofs of these inequalities are mostly combinatorial and necessarily somewhat technical. We prove the first inequality in Section
5
, and the second inequality in Section
6
.

5.
Proof of the upper bound

In this section we prove the first inequality in (
4.4
):

Proposition 5.1
.

We have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}\leq s^{*}.

We use the following estimate for multinomial coefficients, which is an easy consequence of Stirling’s approximation.

Lemma 5.2

(
[
1
, Lemma 7.4]
)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
k_{1},\dots,k_{N}
be nonnegative integers with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}=m
. Put
\alpha_{i}:=k_{i}/N
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then

\binom{m}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\leq 2\sqrt{m}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}\right)^{m},

where we use the convention
0^{0}\equiv 1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

For
\mathbf{i}\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{*}
, let

k_{j}(\mathbf{i}):=\#\{\nu\leq|\mathbf{i}|:i_{\nu}=j\},\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

Then

(5.1)

\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{i}\in\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})\leq\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}\min\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(Mp_{j})^{k_{j}(\mathbf{i})},1\right\},

where the inequality was established in (
3.5
).
If
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
and
k_{j}(\mathbf{i})=k_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,N
, then

(5.2)

\prod_{j=1}^{N}r_{i}^{k_{i}}\leq e^{-n}<r_{\min}^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{N}r_{j}^{k_{j}}.

Let
B_{n}
denote the set of
N
-tuples
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})
for which these inequalities hold. By the rightmost inequality in (
5.2
),

\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\log r_{j}>\log r_{\min}-n,

and so (since
\log r_{j}<0
) certainly
k_{j}\log r_{j}>\log r_{\min}-n
for each
j
. Hence

(5.3)

k_{j}\leq\frac{\log r_{\min}-n}{\log r_{j}}\leq\frac{n+|\log r_{\min}|}{|\log r_{\max}|},\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

so there is a constant
C>0
such that
\#B_{n}\leq Cn^{N}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, from (
5.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\in B_{n}}\binom{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\min\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(Mp_{j})^{k_{j}},1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{N}\max_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\in B_{n}}\binom{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\min\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(Mp_{j})^{k_{j}},1\right\}.

Put
\beta_{i}:=k_{i}/n
; then
\beta_{i}\geq 0
and (
5.2
) is equivalent to

(5.4)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\log r_{i}\leq-1<\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\log r_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\log r_{\min}.

Let
B_{n}^{\prime}
denote the set of
N
-tuples
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N})\in[0,\infty)^{N}
satisfying these inequalities. Let

\alpha_{i}:=\frac{k_{i}}{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}=\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{N}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

By Lemma
5.2
, we have

\binom{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\leq 2(k_{1}+\dots+k_{N})^{1/2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}\right)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}.

Note that
k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}\leq C^{\prime}n
for a suitable constant
C^{\prime}
, by (
5.3
). On the other hand, we can write
\alpha_{i}(k_{1}+\dots+k_{N})=\alpha_{i}(\sum_{j}\beta_{j})n=\beta_{i}n
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}^{-\alpha_{i}}\right)^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{N}}

\displaystyle\cdot\min\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Mp_{i})^{k_{i}},1\right\}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\right)^{\sum_{i}\beta_{i}}\prod_{i}\beta_{i}^{-\beta_{i}}\cdot\min\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Mp_{i})^{\beta_{i}},1\right\}\right]^{n}

\displaystyle=\exp\big(n\{\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}\big).

It follows that, for a suitable constant
C^{\prime\prime}
,

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})\leq 2C^{\prime\prime}n^{N}\sqrt{n}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B_{n}^{\prime}}\exp\left(n\{\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}\right).

Since the extra term
(1/n)\log r_{\min}
in (
5.4
) goes to zero as
n\to\infty
, we can replace the condition of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
satisfying (
5.4
) by
h(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\sum\beta_{i}\log r_{i}=1
without changing the last estimate by much. Specifically, we show:

Claim:
For each
\varepsilon>0
there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that for each
n\geq n_{0}
and each
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B_{n}^{\prime}
, there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\in[0,\infty)^{N}
such that
h(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})=1
and

\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}\leq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\}+2\varepsilon.

Proof of Claim:
Let
\varepsilon>0
be given. For any
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B_{n}
, we have, as in (
5.3
),

0\leq\beta_{i}\leq\frac{n\log r_{\min}-1}{n\log r_{i}}\leq\frac{\log r_{\min}-1}{\log r_{i}}\leq\frac{1+|\log r_{\min}|}{|\log r_{\max}|}=:K\qquad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,N\},

by the second half of (
5.4
).
Recalling our convention that
0\log 0=0
,
\psi
is continuous on
[0,K]^{N}
, hence it is uniformly continuous on this set. So there exists
\delta>0
such that, whenever
|\beta_{i}-\beta_{i}^{\prime}|<\delta
for all
i
, we have
|\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})|<\varepsilon
. Let

b:=\frac{\log r_{\min}}{\log r_{\max}},\qquad\tilde{b}:=b\max_{i}|\log(Mp_{i})|,

and choose
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
so large that

(5.6)

\frac{b}{n_{0}}<\delta,\qquad\frac{\tilde{b}}{n_{0}}<\varepsilon\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad n_{0}\geq(N-1)|\log r_{\min}|.

Given
n\geq n_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in B_{n}^{\prime}
, we construct a suitable vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
as follows.
By the second half of (
5.4
), there is an index
j
such that

(5.7)

\sum_{i\neq j}\beta_{i}\log r_{i}>\frac{N-1}{N}\left(-1+\frac{\log r_{\min}}{n}\right)\geq-1,

where the last inequality follows from (
5.6
).
Put
\beta_{i}^{\prime}=\beta_{i}
for
i\neq j
, and

\beta_{j}^{\prime}:=-\frac{1+\sum_{i\neq j}\beta_{i}\log r_{i}}{\log r_{j}}.

It follows at once that
h(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})=1
, and
\beta_{j}^{\prime}\geq 0
by (
5.7
). Furthermore,

|\beta_{j}^{\prime}-\beta_{j}|=\left|\frac{-1-\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\log r_{i}}{\log r_{j}}\right|\leq\frac{\log r_{\min}}{n\log r_{\max}}=\frac{b}{n}<\delta,

where the first inequality follows from (
5.4
). Hence,
\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})<\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\varepsilon
. Finally,

\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+|\beta_{j}-\beta_{j}^{\prime}||\log(Mp_{j})|\leq\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\frac{\tilde{b}}{n}<\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\varepsilon,

and the Claim follows.
\diamond

Combining the Claim with (
5.5
) and noting that the factor
n^{N}\sqrt{n}
is subexponential, we see that for each
\varepsilon>0
we can find
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon n}\max\left\{\exp\left(n\big\{\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}\big\}\right):\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\boldsymbol{\beta})=1\right\}

\displaystyle=e^{(s^{*}+\varepsilon)n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Proof of the lower bound

In this section, we prove the second inequality in (
4.4
):

Proposition 6.1
.

We have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}\geq s^{*}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N})
be any vector in
[0,\infty)^{N}
such that
h(\boldsymbol{\beta})=1
. It suffices to show that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})}{n}\geq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\min\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\}.

By continuity, we may assume without loss of generality that
\beta_{i}>0
for each
i
.

We divide the proof in a series of steps. For
\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}
, define

a_{\mathbf{i}}:=\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{at least one path in ${\mathcal{P}}_{|\mathbf{i}|}$ is labeled $\mathbf{i}$}\right),\qquad\mathbf{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}^{*},

so that

\mathbb{E}(\#\tilde{\Gamma}_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}.

Unfortunately, the probabilities
a_{\mathbf{i}}
are difficult to compute or even estimate in general. The example at the end of Section
3
shows that they can, in general, be exponentially small. Our goal is to construct a subset
D_{n}
of
\Gamma_{n}
of words in which each digit
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
appears approximately
\beta_{i}n
times, and then derive a lower estimate for
a_{\mathbf{i}}
,
\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}
.

Step 1. (Construction of
D_{n}
)
Since
h(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\sum\beta_{i}\log r_{i}=1
, we have for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6.1)

\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{1+\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor}<e^{-n}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\beta_{i}}\right)^{n}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor}.

Using these inequalities, we first construct a specific word in
\Gamma_{n}
in which each digit
i
occurs approximately
\beta_{i}n
times. If equality holds on the far right of (
6.1
), then the word

\mathbf{v}:=1^{\lfloor\beta_{1}n\rfloor}2^{\lfloor\beta_{2}n\rfloor}\dots N^{\lfloor\beta_{N}n\rfloor}

lies (barely) in
\Gamma_{n}
, where
i^{k}
denotes the digit
i
repeated
k
times. Otherwise, this word is too short to belong to
\Gamma_{n}
, but by the left-most inequality in (
6.1
), we can extend
\mathbf{v}
to a word in
\Gamma_{n}
by appending the string
12\dots i_{0}
, for some
i_{0}\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. In either case, then, there is an integer
i_{0}\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
such that the word

\mathbf{w}:=1^{\lfloor\beta_{1}n\rfloor}2^{\lfloor\beta_{2}n\rfloor}\dots N^{\lfloor\beta_{N}n\rfloor}12\dots i_{0}.

lies in
\Gamma_{n}
.
Let
L:=|\mathbf{w}|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor+i_{0}
, and write
\mathbf{w}=w_{1}\dots w_{L}
. Then

(6.2)

-N+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}n\leq L\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}n+N.

Note furthermore, that all permutations of
\mathbf{w}
that fix the last digit
w_{L}
also belong to
\Gamma_{n}
. Denote the set of all such permutations by
\hat{D}_{n}
. It would be easier to estimate the probability
a_{\mathbf{i}}
, for
\mathbf{i}\in\hat{D}_{n}
, if the occurrences of each digit were somewhat evenly distributed across the word
\mathbf{i}
. Therefore, we form a subset
D_{n}
of
\hat{D}_{n}
by imposing an additional requirement.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, let
t_{i}
be the number of occurrences of the digit
i
in
w_{1}\dots w_{L-1}
. Then
t_{i}
is either
\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor
,
\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor-1
, or
\lfloor\beta_{i}n\rfloor+1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}=L-1
. Put
m:=\lfloor\sqrt{L-1}\rfloor
, and assume
n
(and hence
L
) is large enough so that
m\gg N
. Put

k_{i}:=\left\lfloor\frac{t_{i}}{m}\right\rfloor,\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

and

q:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}.

Note that
L-1-mN<mq\leq L-1
. Write
L-1=mq+r
, so
0\leq r<mN
. Now let
D_{n}
be the set of all words in
\hat{D}_{n}
for which each of the first
m
blocks of length
q
contains the digit
i
exactly
k_{i}
times, for
i=1,\dots,N
. Precisely,
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\dots i_{L}\in D_{n}
if and only if
\mathbf{i}\in\hat{D}_{n}
and for each
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\#\{\nu\in\{(l-1)q+1,\dots,lq\}:i_{\nu}=i\}=k_{i}.

Step 2. (Estimating the cardinality of
D_{n}
)
We claim that

(6.3)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\#D_{n}}{n}\geq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}).

To see this, note first that
\psi
satisfies the positive homogeneity property

(6.4)

\psi(\alpha\mathbf{x})=\alpha\psi(\mathbf{x})\qquad\mbox{for any $\alpha>0$ and $\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N}$}.

We use Stirling’s approximation to get the initial estimate

\displaystyle\#D_{n}\geq{\binom{q}{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi q}\cdot q^{q}}{\prod\sqrt{2\pi k_{i}}\cdot\prod k_{i}^{k_{i}}}\cdot(1+o(1))\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{q^{q}}{(2\pi)^{N/2}q^{N/2}\prod k_{i}^{k_{i}}}\cdot(1+o(1))\right)^{m}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\log\#D_{n}}{m}

\displaystyle\geq q\log q-\frac{N}{2}\log 2\pi-\frac{N}{2}\log q-\sum_{i}k_{i}\log k_{i}+o(1)

\displaystyle=q\log q-\sum_{i}k_{i}\log k_{i}+O(\log q).

Here we want to replace
k_{i}
with
k_{i}^{\prime}:=\beta_{i}n/m
(which is not necessarily an integer), and similarly, replace
q
with
q^{\prime}:=\sum_{i}k_{i}^{\prime}
.
The definitions of
t_{i}
and
k_{i}
imply

(6.5)

\frac{\beta_{i}n-2}{m}-1<\frac{t_{i}}{m}-1<k_{i}\leq\frac{t_{i}}{m}\leq\frac{\beta_{i}n+1}{m},

so certainly
|k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime}|\leq 3
. The mean value theorem then gives
k_{i}\log k_{i}-k_{i}^{\prime}\log k_{i}^{\prime}=O(\log k_{i})=O(\log q)
. Similarly,
|q-q^{\prime}|\leq 3N
and so
q\log q-q^{\prime}\log q^{\prime}=O(\log q)
.
Observe that

q\leq\frac{L}{m}\sim\sqrt{L}\sim\sqrt{\sum\beta_{i}n}=O(\sqrt{n})

by (
6.2
).
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\log\#D_{n}}{m}

\displaystyle\geq q^{\prime}\log q^{\prime}-\sum_{i}k_{i}^{\prime}\log k_{i}^{\prime}+O(\log q)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\frac{\beta_{1}n}{m},\dots,\frac{\beta_{N}n}{m}\right)+O(\log\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+O(\log n),

where in the last step we used (
6.4
). Multiplying by
m/n
, we obtain (
6.3
).

Step 3. (Estimating
a_{\mathbf{i}}
for
\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}
)
Recall from Step 1 that all words in
D_{n}
have length
L
. Note that
a_{\mathbf{i}}
satisfies the recursion

(6.6)

a_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{L}}=1-(1-p_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}})^{M}.

This can be seen by a branching argument: Let
G_{\mathbf{i}}
be the event that
no
path of length
n
starting at the root of the tree has label sequence
\mathbf{i}
. For
k=1,\dots,M
, let
G_{\mathbf{i},k}
be the event that no path starting with the
k
th edge emanating from the root has label sequence
\mathbf{i}
. By independence,
\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathbf{i}})=\prod_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathbf{i},k})
. Now
G_{\mathbf{i},k}^{c}
happens if and only if the
k
th edge below the root receives label
i_{1}
and the label sequence
a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}
occurs at least once in the tree starting from the
k
th child of the root. By the stationarity of the labeling process, the latter event has probability
a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}
. Thus,

a_{\mathbf{i}}=1-\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathbf{i}})=1-\prod_{k=1}^{M}\big(1-\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathbf{i},k}^{c})\big)=1-(1-p_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}})^{M}.

Let

p_{\min}:=\min_{i}p_{i},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad p_{\max}:=\max_{i}p_{i}.

We assume that
p_{\min}>0
. This is not a real restriction: If
p_{i}=0
for some
i
, we simply remove the map
f_{i}
from the IFS and reduce the value of
N
. Since the function
x\mapsto 1-(1-p_{\min}x)^{M}
is increasing and concave on
[0,1]
, it follows from (
6.6
) that

(6.7)

a_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{L}}\geq\gamma a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}\qquad\forall i_{1},\dots,i_{L},

where
\gamma:=1-(1-p_{\min})^{M}>0
is a universal constant. Furthermore, it follows from (
6.6
) and Bernoulli’s inequality that

(6.8)

a_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{L}}\leq Mp_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}

and

(6.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{L}}&\geq Mp_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}-\binom{M}{2}p_{i_{1}}^{2}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}^{2}\\
&\geq Mp_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}(1-Ma_{i_{2}\dots i_{L}}).\end{split}

Recall that
L=mq+r+1
, where
m=\lfloor\sqrt{L-1}\rfloor
,
q=\sum_{i}k_{i}
and
0\leq r<mN
. Our goal is to show that the probability
a_{i_{j}\dots i_{L}}
cannot oscillate too much as
j
moves backwards from
L
to
1
. In view of the definition of
D_{n}
, we consider decrementing
j
in steps of size
q
. Precisely, we always take
j
to be one more than a multiple of
q
. To shorten notation, put

b_{l}(\mathbf{i}):=a_{i_{lq+1}\dots i_{L}},\qquad\mathbf{i}\in D_{n},\quad l=0,1,\dots,m.

First, (
6.7
) implies

(6.10)

b_{m}(\mathbf{i})\geq\gamma^{L-mq}=\gamma^{r+1}\geq\gamma^{mN}.

Fix
\eta>0
. Take
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. We consider two cases.
If

b_{l}(\mathbf{i})>\eta(Mp_{\max})^{-q},

then by (
6.7
),

b_{l-1}(\mathbf{i})\geq\gamma^{q}b_{l}(\mathbf{i})>\eta\left(\frac{\gamma}{Mp_{\max}}\right)^{q}.

Suppose instead that

(6.11)

b_{l}(\mathbf{i})\leq\eta(Mp_{\max})^{-q}.

Then
a_{i_{j}\dots i_{L}}\leq\eta
for
(l-1)q<j\leq lq
by (
6.8
), and so (
6.9
) implies

(6.12)

b_{l-1}(\mathbf{i})\geq\prod_{i=1}^{N}\big(Mp_{i}(1-\eta M)\big)^{k_{i}}b_{l}(\mathbf{i})=\big(M(1-\eta M)\big)^{q}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{k_{i}}\right)b_{l}(\mathbf{i}).

At this point it is convenient to define (as in the proof of the upper bound)

\alpha_{i}:=\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{N}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

From the left half of (
6.5
) it follows that

\frac{\sum_{i}\beta_{i}n}{m}<q+N+\frac{N}{m}.

Hence, by the right half of (
6.5
),

k_{i}\leq\frac{\beta_{i}n+1}{m}=\frac{\alpha_{i}\sum_{j}\beta_{j}n+1}{m}<\alpha_{i}\left(q+N+\frac{N}{m}\right)+\frac{1}{m}<\alpha_{i}(q+2N)+1,

so that

\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{k_{i}}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}(q+2N)+1}\geq\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}q}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\min}^{2N\alpha_{i}+1}=p_{\min}^{3N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}q}=:C_{\mathbf{p}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}q}.

Putting this estimate back into (
6.12
) yields

b_{l-1}(\mathbf{i})\geq C_{\mathbf{p}}\big(M(1-\eta M)\big)^{q}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}q}\right)b_{l}(\mathbf{i})\geq C_{\mathbf{p}}(1-\eta M)^{q}K_{\vec{\alpha}}^{q}\,b_{l}(\mathbf{i}),

where

K_{\vec{\alpha}}:=\min\left\{1,M\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right\}=\min\left\{1,\prod_{i=1}^{N}(Mp_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}\right\}.

(We do the estimate this way, because it will later be important that
K_{\vec{\alpha}}\leq 1
.) Now let

u(\mathbf{i}):=\#\{l\in\{1,\dots,m\}:\ \mbox{\eqref{eq:small-intermediate} holds}\}.

Then for each
\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}
we obtain, using that
u(\mathbf{i})\leq m
and
u(\mathbf{i})q\leq mq\leq L
,

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\eta\left(\frac{\gamma}{Mp_{\max}}\right)^{q}(1-\eta M)^{u(\mathbf{i})q}C_{\mathbf{p}}^{u(\mathbf{i})}K_{\vec{\alpha}}^{u(\mathbf{i})q}\,b_{m}(\mathbf{i})

\displaystyle\geq\eta\left(\frac{\gamma}{Mp_{\max}}\right)^{q}(1-\eta M)^{mq}C_{\mathbf{p}}^{m}K_{\vec{\alpha}}^{mq}\gamma^{mN}

\displaystyle\geq\eta C_{\mathbf{p}}^{m}\gamma^{mN}\left(\frac{\gamma}{Mp_{\max}}\right)^{q}(1-\eta M)^{L}K_{\vec{\alpha}}^{L},

where we also used (
6.10
). Note that this bound does not depend on
\mathbf{i}
. Since
m\leq\sqrt{L}
and
q\leq L/m\sim\sqrt{L}
, the factors
C_{\mathbf{p}}^{m}\gamma^{mN}
and
\left(\frac{\gamma}{Mp_{\max}}\right)^{q}
go to zero sub-exponentially (if at all), hence for all large enough
n
, we have

\min_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\geq(1-\eta M)^{L}(K_{\vec{\alpha}}-\eta)^{L}\geq\big\{(1-\eta M)(K_{\vec{\alpha}}-\eta)\big\}^{\sum_{i}\beta_{i}n+N},

by (
6.2
). Since
\eta
was arbitrary and
N
is constant, it follows that

(6.13)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\min_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\right)\geq\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\log K_{\vec{\alpha}}.

Step 4. (Conclusion)
Clearly, we have

\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\geq\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\geq\#D_{n}\cdot\min_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}.

Thus, by (
6.3
) and (
6.13
),

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\Gamma_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\#D_{n}}{n}+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\min_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{n}}a_{\mathbf{i}}}{n}

\displaystyle\geq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\log K_{\vec{\alpha}}.

Recalling that
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}/(\beta_{1}+\dots+\beta_{N})
and noting that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\log K_{\vec{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}\cdot\min\left\{0,\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\log(Mp_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{0,\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\log(Mp_{i})\right\}=\min\big\{0,\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})\big\},

we complete the proof.
∎

7.
Solving the constrained optimization

We first establish the existence and uniqueness of the pair
(\hat{s},\hat{t})
in Theorem
2.2
(iii).

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume

(7.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log(Mp_{i})<0<\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i}).

Then there is a unique pair
(\hat{s},\hat{t})
of real numbers, with
\hat{s}>0
, satisfying the equations

(7.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}=1,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log(Mp_{i})=0.

Proof.

Define the functions

G_{1}(s,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s}(Mp_{i})^{t},\qquad G_{2}(s,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s}(Mp_{i})^{t}\log(Mp_{i}).

Note that for fixed
t
,
G_{1}(s,t)
is strictly decreasing in
s
, with
\lim_{s\to\infty}G_{1}(s,t)=0
and
\lim_{s\to-\infty}G_{1}(s,t)=\infty
. Thus, for fixed
t
, the equation
G_{1}(s,t)=1
has a unique solution
s=g(t)
, say.
Differentiating implicitly yields

(7.4)

g^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s}(Mp_{i})^{t}\log(Mp_{i})}{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s}(Mp_{i})^{t}\log r_{i}}.

This shows that
G_{2}(s,t)=0
precisely at those points
(s,t)
where
s=g(t)
and
g^{\prime}(t)=0
. In other words, the curves

C_{1}:G_{1}(s,t)=1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad C_{2}:G_{2}(s,t)=0

intersect each other at those and only those points where
C_{1}
has a horizontal tangent line.

Next, we rewrite (
7.4
) as

0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{g(t)}(Mp_{i})^{t}\{g^{\prime}(t)\log r_{i}+\log(Mp_{i})\}.

Differentiating implicitly once more, we find

0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{g(t)}(Mp_{i})^{t}\left[g^{\prime\prime}(t)\log r_{i}+\{g^{\prime}(t)\log r_{i}+\log(Mp_{i})\}^{2}\right].

(These equations are well known in the multifractal analysis of self-similar sets.) It follows that
g^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0
, and so
g
is convex. In fact, it is strictly convex unless
Mp_{i}=r_{i}^{\lambda}
for all
i
and some constant
\lambda
. But we can rule out the latter possibility because this would imply that
\log(Mp_{i})
has constant sign, contradicting (
2.9
). Hence, there is at most one point where
g^{\prime}(t)=0
, and so
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
intersect at most once.

In order to show that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
do intersect, we need to show that
g^{\prime}(t)=0
for some
t
. Observe that the equation
\sum_{i}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}=1/M
is equivalent to
G_{1}(\tilde{s},1)=1
, so
g(1)=\tilde{s}
, and hence

\sum_{i}r_{i}^{g(1)}(Mp_{i})^{1}\log(Mp_{i})=\sum_{i}Mp_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i})>0

by the second inequality in (
2.9
). This implies
g^{\prime}(1)>0
by (
7.4
). On the other hand, the equation
\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s_{0}}=1
is equivalent to
G_{1}(s_{0},0)=1
, so
g(0)=s_{0}
and hence,

\sum_{i}r_{i}^{g(0)}(Mp_{i})^{0}\log(Mp_{i})=\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log(Mp_{i})<0

by the first inequality in (
2.9
). This implies
g^{\prime}(0)<0
. Therefore, there exists
\hat{t}\in(0,1)
such that
g^{\prime}(\hat{t})=0
, as required.

It remains to verify that
g(\hat{t})>0
. By the second inequality in (
2.9
),
Mp_{i_{0}}>1
for at least one
i_{0}
, and since
\hat{t}>0
, we have
(Mp_{i_{0}})^{\hat{t}}>1
. By the definition of
g
, we thus have

\sum_{i}r_{i}^{g(\hat{t})}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}=1<\sum_{i}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}},

and this implies
g(\hat{t})>0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

(i) Assume

(7.5)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log(Mp_{i})\geq 0,

and recall that

(7.6)

\displaystyle\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\left(\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}-\log x_{i}\right),

(7.7)

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\log(Mp_{i}),

(7.8)

\displaystyle h(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\log r_{i}.

Put
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N})
, where

(7.9)

\beta_{i}:=-\frac{r_{i}^{s}}{\sum_{j}r_{j}^{s}\log r_{j}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

It is immediate that
h(\boldsymbol{\beta})=1
, while (
7.5
) implies
\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\log(Mp_{i})\geq 0
. Furthermore,

\psi(r_{1}^{s_{0}},\dots,r_{N}^{s_{0}})=-s_{0}\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s_{0}}\log r_{i},

since
\sum_{i}r_{i}^{s_{0}}=1
. Thus, since
\psi(\alpha\mathbf{x})=\alpha\psi(\mathbf{x})
for all
\alpha>0
and
\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N}
(cf. (
6.4
)),

\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\frac{1}{\sum_{j}r_{j}^{s_{0}}\log r_{j}}\psi(r_{1}^{s_{0}},\dots,r_{N}^{s_{0}})=s_{0}.

Applying Theorem
2.1
we conclude
\dim_{H}F\geq s_{0}
almost surely. Since
F\subset\Lambda
, the reverse inequality holds trivially. Thus,
\dim_{H}F=s_{0}
almost surely.

(ii) Next, assume

(7.10)

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i})\leq 0,

where
\tilde{s}
satisfies
\sum_{i}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}=1/M
. Put

(7.11)

\beta_{i}^{\prime}:=-\frac{p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}}{\sum_{j}p_{j}r_{j}^{\tilde{s}}\log r_{j}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}:=(\beta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\beta_{N}^{\prime})
. Clearly
h(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})=1
, and (
7.10
) implies
\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\leq 0
. Note that

\displaystyle\psi\big(Mp_{1}r_{1}^{\tilde{s}},\dots,Mp_{N}r_{N}^{\tilde{s}}\big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}Mp_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}Mp_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\big(\log(Mp_{i})+\tilde{s}\log r_{i}\big).

So by (
6.4
),

\dim_{H}F\geq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{i}Mp_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\big(\log(Mp_{i})+\tilde{s}\log r_{i}\big)}{\sum_{j}Mp_{j}r_{j}^{\tilde{s}}\log r_{j}}-\frac{\sum_{i}p_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}\log(Mp_{i})}{\sum_{j}p_{j}r_{j}^{\tilde{s}}\log r_{j}}=\tilde{s}.

The reverse inequality follows since the vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
defined by (
7.11
) maximizes
\psi(\mathbf{x})+\phi(\mathbf{x})
subject to the constraint
h(\mathbf{x})=1
, as is easily seen by a Lagrange multiplier computation.
Hence,
\dim_{H}F=\tilde{s}\leq s_{0}
almost surely.

Finally, we show that
\tilde{s}=s_{0}
if and only if
N=M
and
p_{i}=1/N
for all
i
. Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}
again be the vector defined by (
7.9
). If
\boldsymbol{\beta}\neq\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
, then

s_{0}=\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})>\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\geq\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})+\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})=\tilde{s},

since
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the unique maximizer of
\psi(\mathbf{x})
subject to
h(\mathbf{x})=1
. On the other hand, if
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
, then it follows from equating the right hand sides of (
7.9
) and (
7.11
) that
p_{i}
is constant in
i
, and so
p_{i}=1/N
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Hence by (
2.6
),
\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\tilde{s}}=N/M
. So if
\tilde{s}=s_{0}
, it must be the case that
N=M
and
p_{i}=1/N
for all
i
. The reverse implication is obvious.

(iii) Assume (
7.1
). The existence and uniqueness of
(\hat{s},\hat{t})
were established in Lemma
7.1
. Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
be the vectors from (
7.9
) and (
7.11
), respectively. Since
\psi
is unimodal on the hyperplane
h(\mathbf{x})=1
with its constrained maximum at
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})<0
by the first inequality in (
2.9
), it follows that

\displaystyle\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})=0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})=0\big\}.

Likewise, since
\psi+\phi
is unimodal on
\{\mathbf{x}:h(\mathbf{x})=1\}
with its constrained maximum at
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}
and
\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})>0
by the second inequality in (
2.9
), we have

\displaystyle\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\phi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\phi(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})=0\big\}

\displaystyle=\max\big\{\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}:\mathbf{x}\in[0,\infty)^{N},h(\mathbf{x})=1,\phi(\mathbf{x})=0\big\}.

Thus, the constrained maximum of
\psi(\mathbf{x})+\min\{0,\phi(\mathbf{x})\}
over
\{\mathbf{x}:h(\mathbf{x})=1\}
must be attained on the hyperplane
\phi(\mathbf{x})=0
. Solving the Lagrange multiplier problem

\mbox{maximize}\ \psi(\mathbf{x})\qquad\mbox{subject to}\qquad h(\mathbf{x})=1,\quad\phi(\mathbf{x})=0

yields that the maximum is attained (uniquely) at the point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\hat{\beta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\beta}_{N})
given by

\hat{\beta}_{i}=-\frac{r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}}{\sum_{j}r_{j}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{j})^{\hat{t}}\log r_{j}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

We then have

\displaystyle\dim_{H}F

\displaystyle=\psi\big(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\big)=-\frac{\psi\big(r_{1}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{1})^{\hat{t}},\dots,r_{N}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{N})^{\hat{t}}\big)}{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log\big(r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\big)}{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\big\{\hat{s}\log r_{i}+\hat{t}\log(Mp_{i})\big\}}{\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\hat{s}}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}\log r_{i}}=\hat{s},

where the first equality used (
6.4
) and (
7.2
), and the last inequality follows from (
7.3
). Finally, that
\hat{s}<s_{0}
follows since
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\neq\boldsymbol{\beta}
(because
\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=0\neq\phi(\boldsymbol{\beta})
), and so
\hat{s}=\psi(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}})<\psi(\boldsymbol{\beta})=s_{0}
.
∎

8.
Proofs of the special cases

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

Suppose
r_{i}=r
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then (
2.1
) and (
2.6
) give

s_{0}=-\frac{\log N}{\log r},\qquad\tilde{s}=-\frac{\log M}{\log r}.

Furthermore, the conditions (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) simplify to
M\geq U(N,\mathbf{p})
and
M\leq L(N,\mathbf{p})
, respectively. Finally, for
L(N,\mathbf{p})\leq M\leq U(N,\mathbf{p})
, (
2.10
) becomes

(8.1)

r^{\hat{s}}\sum_{i}(Mp_{i})^{\hat{t}}=1,

and (
2.11
) reduces to

\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\hat{t}}\log(Mp_{i})=0.

Putting
\lambda:=\hat{t}
, we see that
\lambda
satisfies (
2.12
), and then (
8.1
) gives the desired expression for
\dim_{H}F
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In principle, it is possible (but cumbersome!) to derive Theorem
1.1
directly from Corollary
2.4
. It is simpler, however, to deduce it from Theorem
2.1
. For
N=M=2
, we are always in the case
L(N,\mathbf{p})\leq M\leq U(N,\mathbf{p})
, so the constrained maximum in Theorem
2.1
is attained on the line

\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\log(2p)+x_{2}\log(2(1-p))=0.

Together with the equation
h(x_{1},x_{2})=-(x_{1}+x_{2})\log r=1
, this gives a unique point
(x_{1},x_{2})
which satisfies

\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}=-\frac{\log(2(1-p))}{\log p-\log(1-p)}=1-\xi,\qquad\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}=\frac{\log(2p)}{\log p-\log(1-p)}=\xi.

Thus,

\displaystyle\dim_{H}F

\displaystyle=\psi(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}+x_{2})\psi\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi(1-\xi,\xi)}{\log r}=\frac{\xi\log\xi+(1-\xi)\log(1-\xi)}{\log r},

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In the non-homogeneous case, any of the three cases in Theorem
2.2
can apply, even for
N=M=2
. Here inequality (
2.7
) becomes

g(p):=r_{1}^{s_{0}}\log(2p)+r_{2}^{s_{0}}\log(2(1-p))\geq 0.

Note that
g(p)\to-\infty
as
p\searrow 0
,
g(1/2)=0
, and

g^{\prime}(p)=\frac{r_{1}^{s_{0}}}{p}-\frac{r_{2}^{s_{0}}}{1-p}<0\qquad\mbox{for $p\geq 1/2$},

since
r_{1}<r_{2}
. Hence
g(p)
has an additional zero
p_{*}
in
(0,1/2)
, and
g(p)\geq 0
if and only if
p_{*}\leq p\leq 1/2
.

Similarly, (
2.8
) becomes

\tilde{g}(p):=pr_{1}^{\tilde{s}}\log(2p)+(1-p)r_{2}^{\tilde{s}}\log(2(1-p))\leq 0,

where
\tilde{s}
is given by (
1.3
). Differentiating twice gives

\tilde{g}^{\prime}(p)=r_{1}^{\tilde{s}}\big\{1+\log(2p)\big\}-r_{2}^{\tilde{s}}\big\{1+\log(2(1-p))\big\}

and

\tilde{g}^{\prime\prime}(p)=\frac{r_{1}^{\tilde{s}}}{p}+\frac{r_{2}^{\tilde{s}}}{1-p}\geq 0.

Thus,
\tilde{g}
is convex. Furthermore,
\tilde{g}(1/2)=0
and
\tilde{g}^{\prime}(1/2)=r_{1}^{\tilde{s}}-r_{2}^{\tilde{s}}<0
. Finally,
\tilde{g}(p)\to\infty
as
p\nearrow 1
. Hence,
\tilde{g}(p)
has an additional zero
p^{*}
in
(1/2,1)
, and
\tilde{g}(p)\leq 0
if and only if
1/2\leq p\leq p^{*}
.

In the third case (when
p\leq p_{*}
or
p\geq p^{*}
), the formula for
\hat{s}
follows by solving the linear system

\displaystyle h(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=-x_{1}\log r_{1}-x_{2}\log r_{2}=1,

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{1}\log(2p)+x_{2}\log(2(1-p))=0,

and substituting the result into
\psi
.
∎
