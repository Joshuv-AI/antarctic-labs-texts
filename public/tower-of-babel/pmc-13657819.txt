Radiomics-based machine learning models decipher heterogeneity of anoikis-related molecular subtypes for prognostic stratification in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Linyong Wu, Dayou Wei, Wubiao Chen, Songhua Li, Zhendong Lu, Youwen Fu, Wenci Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) exhibits high molecular heterogeneity and frequent metastasis, which is strongly linked to anoikis dysregulation. This study aimed to investigate whether radiomics could offer a novel perspective for assessing the heterogeneity and risk stratification of HNSC associated with anoikis-related genes (ANRGs).MethodsA total of 118 HNSC patients with paired gene expression and imaging data were included. Radiomics features were extracted from computed tomography (CT) images using PyRadiomics. By employing consensus clustering methods, the heterogeneity of ANRGs-related HNSC was identified into different subtypes, and multi-machine learning model combinations were constructed based on radiomics features to predict the molecular subtypes. An ANRGs-related radiomics prognostic model was constructed to assess the association between subtypes and clinical outcomes.ResultsThree subtypes were identified via consensus clustering of 31 prognostic ANRG expression profiles: C1, C2, and C3. Compared to other subtypes, C1 exhibited significantly worse overall survival and distinct immune infiltration levels. Of the multi-combination machine learning models, 71% demonstrated robust generalization in predicting C1 [areas under the curve (AUC) >0.75). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator-random forest combined model achieved optimal performance, with an AUC of 0.86. The ANRGs-associated radiomics prognostic model showed significant prognostic value and was closely linked to pathways involving enhanced tumor invasiveness and immunosuppressive microenvironment.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that radiomics enables stratified prediction of ANRGs-associated HNSC heterogeneity while providing molecular annotation and prognostic evaluation for ANRGs-related radiomics features, highlighting its translational value in precision oncology.

Introduction

Anoikis, a form of programmed cell death, is triggered by the loss of interaction between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) (1). Dysregulation of anoikis, such as resistance to detachment-induced apoptosis, enables tumor cells to survive in the absence of ECM adhesion. This process is driven by activation of signaling pathways, allowing circulating tumor cells to evade apoptosis and initiate metastasis (2). Anoikis resistance promotes aggressive phenotypes, including stemness and immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling, which further accelerate tumor progression (3). Specific molecules associated with anoikis have been demonstrated to play roles in the development and progression of various tumors. For instance, CTSZ activated the NF-κB signaling pathway in glioblastoma, inhibiting anoikis and enhancing malignant traits, leading to poorer prognosis (4). Similarly, MIAT upregulation, stabilized by METTL3-mediated m6A modification, sponged miR-181a-5p, derepressed MCL1, and consequently promoted anoikis resistance and peritoneal metastasis in colorectal cancer, which led to poorer prognosis (5). Furthermore, the development of prognostic machine learning models based on specific anoikis-related molecular profiles is an emerging research focus. For example, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method has been utilized to develop machine learning models based on anoikis-related genes (ANRGs) for predicting the prognosis and tumor microenvironment in renal cell carcinoma (6). Additionally, Cox regression-based prognostic models incorporating ANRGs have been constructed to stratify osteosarcoma patients, enabling more effective clinical management (7). Thus, ANRGs may reflect tumor heterogeneity to a certain extent; accordingly, assessing anoikis-related heterogeneity may represent an important approach for predicting tumor progression and improving therapeutic outcomes.

Cluster analysis has been widely applied to explore tumor heterogeneity, offering potential insights for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Common clustering methods include K-means clustering and non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) (8). For instance, K-means clustering based on ANRGs identified two molecular subtypes of pancreatic cancer, with further predictive analysis conducted using machine learning models (9). Similarly, K-means clustering has been employed to identify ANRG-related molecular subtypes in lung cancer, followed by machine learning models to analyze their association with prognosis (10). Although many clustering results demonstrate strong performance and a close relationship with prognosis, machine learning models developed at the molecular level face challenges in clinical translation. This is primarily due to the lag time and high costs associated with molecular data acquisition, highlighting the need for more accessible, reproducible, and cost-effective predictive methods.

While ANRG-based molecular prognostic models have been developed for cancers like renal carcinoma and osteosarcoma, their clinical utility is limited by the invasiveness of molecular profiling. In contrast, radiomics provides a non-invasive alternative to capture tumor heterogeneity, yet its linkage to ANRG-driven molecular subtypes remains unexplored in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC). Radiomics, akin to gene sequencing, enables high-dimensional quantification of tumor information from medical images, offering a broader perspective for tumor information exploration compared to traditional visual information (11). An increasing body of research indicates that tumors with matched medical images and molecular sequencing data in public databases can be investigated for the association between radiomics and molecular heterogeneity. For example, based on consensus clustering of angiogenesis-related gene expression in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, two distinct molecular subtypes were identified. Subsequently, six radiomics features were employed to predict the angiogenesis-associated signature, achieving areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.75 in the training cohort and 0.81 in the testing cohort (12). Radiomics, characterized by its non-invasiveness, rapidity, and low cost, stands in contrast to molecular omics in analyzing tumor heterogeneity. However, research exploring the relationship between radiomics and molecular subtype heterogeneity remains scarce.

Based on the aforementioned background, HNSC exhibits high molecular heterogeneity and frequent metastasis, which is strongly linked to anoikis dysregulation (13,14). However, the role of ANRGs in HNSC progression and their non-invasive evaluation via radiomics remain unexplored. Simultaneously, providing molecular annotations and prognostic evaluations for ANRG-related radiomics features highlights their translational value in precision oncology. This study aimed to investigate whether radiomics could offer a novel perspective for assessing the heterogeneity and risk stratification of HNSC associated with ANRGs. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1628/rc).

Methods

Gene expression and imaging data sources

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Gene expression profiles and clinical data of 520 HNSC cases and 46 non-tumor samples were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Additionally, computed tomography (CT) data from 211 HNSC patients acquired within 2 weeks preoperatively were obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) (15). Based on the principle of matching, 208 HNSC cases with both gene expression and CT data were identified from the TCGA and TCIA cohorts. Two independent radiologists with over 3 years of experience reviewed the imaging and clinical data to exclude cases meeting any of the following criteria: (I) non-contrast-enhanced images, (II) images lacking clear HNSC lesions or with ambiguous tumor boundaries, (III) postoperative scans, (IV) images compromised by artifacts (motion or metallic artifacts) affecting lesion localization or evaluation, or (V) incomplete clinical parameters (age, gender, alcohol consumption history, pathological type, tumor grade, clinical N stage, clinical T stage, or clinical stage). After rigorous screening, 118 HNSC patients and 10 matched non-tumor controls were included in the final cohort, with a mean age of 60 years (range: 24–87 years) (Table 1).

Gene expression profiling processing

Transcripts per million (TPM) data of gene expression were downloaded from TCGA and TPM values were log2-transformed as log2(TPM + 1) for normalization, with only mRNA expression data retained. Based on the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and Harmonizome (https://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome/) databases, ANRGs were searched, and a total of 480 ANRGs were ultimately retrieved. Differential expression analysis of ANRGs between 118 HNSC samples and 10 non-tumor samples was performed using the “limma” package. Univariate Cox regression was applied to evaluate associations between ANRGs and overall survival (OS). Overlapping genes from differentially expressed ANRGs (DEG_ANRGs) and OS-associated ANRGs (OS_ANRGs) were subjected to unsupervised K-means clustering to define molecular subtypes of ANRGs. K-means clustering was performed using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus with the following parameters: 1,000 resampling iterations, 80% of samples (pItem =0.8), and 80% of features (pFeature =0.8) per iteration (16). The optimal number of molecular subtypes was determined by comprehensively evaluating the clustering results of K-means clustering and its average silhouette widths. Heterogeneity among subtypes was analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA), t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (17). Pathway activity scores of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were quantified via the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) algorithm, and subtype-specific activated pathways were identified using the Wilcoxon test (18). Immune infiltration levels were estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm to explore associations between subtypes and immune profiles.

Radiomics analysis associated with ANRGs

Three-dimensional (3D) segmentation of HNSC regions of interest (ROIs) was performed by two radiologists with over 5 years of experience in head and neck radiology using the open-source software ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0) (19). All images were resampled to a uniform voxel size of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm and intensity discretized with a bin width of 25 HU. A total of 1,781 radiomics features were extracted using the PyRadiomics package, including 107 original features and 1,674 features derived from transformed images via wavelet, square, square root, gradient, exponential, logarithm, and Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filtering (20). LoG filtering was applied with σ values ranging from 1 to 5 mm to capture multi-scale texture patterns. Features showing significant differences between subtypes were identified using the Wilcoxon test, while univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to screen features associated with OS. Overlapping features from both differential features (DEG_Features) and prognostic features (OS_Features) were used to develop machine learning predictive models with 12 algorithms: LASSO, ridge regression, elastic net (Enet), stepwise generalized linear model (Stepglm), support vector machine (SVM), glmBoost, linear discriminant analysis (LDA), plsRglm, random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine (GBM), XGBoost, and naive Bayes (NB). A cross-validation framework was implemented, where one algorithm was used for feature selection, and the remaining 11 algorithms were applied to construct classification models, generating up to 113 algorithm combinations (21). Spearman correlation coefficients were employed to analyze the correlations between the optimal model features and the core ANRGs. Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) was used to analyze the correlations between the features of the optimal model and genes, and to annotate the biological significance of these features. Multivariate Cox regression based on overlapping features was utilized to build an ANRG-associated radiomics prognostic model. Finally, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) method was applied to interpret feature importance in the prognostic model (22). The optimal cutoff value for the prognostic model was determined using the “survminer” R package, and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups (23). Differential analysis of genes between the high- and low-risk groups defined by the Cox regression model was performed using the limma package, followed by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the clusterProfiler package.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using SPSS (version 17.0) and R software (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test or Wilcoxon test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (%) and analyzed with the chi-square test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P<0.05.

Results

Differential expression and prognostic value of ANRGs in HNSC

To investigate the heterogeneity of ANRGs in HNSC, the expression patterns of 480 ANRGs were systematically analyzed. Volcano plot analysis revealed that 260 ANRGs were significantly dysregulated in 118 HNSC samples compared with 10 matched non-tumor samples (Figure 1A). Concurrently, 57 of the 480 ANRGs exhibited significant associations with OS (Figure 1B). Venn diagram analysis identified 31 ANRGs that overlapped between the dysregulated genes and those correlated with prognosis (Figure 1C). These overlapping ANRGs were subsequently subjected to further investigation regarding their relationships with clinical characteristics (Figure 1D).

Overlapping ANRGs-driven stratification identifies three subtypes in HNSC

K-means clustering was applied to stratify the 31 overlapping ANRGs, with the optimal cluster number determined by silhouette coefficient analysis. The average silhouette width for k=2, 3, and 4 was 0.53, 0.74, and 0.65, respectively, indicating superior cluster stability at k=3 (Figure 2). Consequently, 118 HNSC patients were classified into three distinct subtypes: C1 (26/118, 22.0%), C2 (38/118, 32.2%), and C3 (54/118, 45.8%). The heterogeneity among the three subtypes was systematically evaluated using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP. Notably, C1 exhibited pronounced heterogeneity compared to C2 and C3 across all three dimensionality reduction methods (Figure 3A-3C). The distribution differences among the three subtypes and the 480 ANRGs were presented in the heatmap of Figure 3D. Subsequent Wilcoxon tests identified subtype-specific molecular signatures, with each cluster demonstrating unique pathway activation patterns (Figure 4A). The results revealed that C1 was significantly enriched in tumor proliferation-related pathways, C2 was predominantly associated with metabolic reprogramming and signal transduction cascades, while C3 showed exclusive enrichment in immune and inflammation-related pathways. Based on these distinct biological features, the subtypes were designated proliferation subtype (C1), metabolic subtype (C2), and immune-inflammation subtype (C3).

The investigation of subtype-specific immune infiltration patterns revealed that, compared to other subtypes, C1 exhibited distinctive associations with central memory CD4+ T cell, central memory CD8+ T cell, memory B cell, natural killer cell, natural killer T cell, and neutrophil. In contrast, most immune cell populations showed no significant infiltration differences between C2 and C3 subtypes (Figure 4B).

The associations between the three subtypes and clinical characteristics were further investigated. Chi-square tests revealed significant differences among subtypes in pathological type (P=0.001), tumor grade (P=0.049), and worse OS (P<0.001). Specifically, C1 demonstrated a higher prevalence of non-laryngeal carcinoma and tumor grade 2. Notably, C1 exhibited significantly poorer OS compared to both C2 and C3 (median OS: 13.68 vs. 22.72 vs. 39.72 months, Figure 4C).

To validate the robustness of the clustering, the remaining 401 patients with OS data from a cohort of 520 HNSC patients were subjected to clustering using the same method. Interestingly, the optimal clustering solution remained at k=3, with an average silhouette width of 0.89. Prognostic analysis revealed that C1 subtype still exhibited the worst OS (Figure 5A). All three methods, PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP, characterized the three subtypes as having unique heterogeneity, especially the PCA method (Figure 5B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that the activated pathways in both the 118-patient and 401-patient groups shared 61% similarity. Among these, typical immune-related pathways included TGF-β signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, MAPK signaling, and Wnt signaling pathways. Furthermore, compared to other subtypes, C1 retained significant uniqueness in central memory CD4+ T cell, central memory CD8+ T cell, memory B cell, natural killer cell, natural killer T cell, and neutrophil, which was similar to the findings observed in the analysis of the 118 HNSC patients (Figure 5C).

Multi-combination radiomics machine learning models for predicting subtypes of HNSC

To investigate the heterogeneity of radiomics features in the HNSC-C1 subtype, the differential patterns of 1,781 features were systematically analyzed. Volcano plot analysis revealed that 579 features were significantly different in C1 (Figure 6A). Concurrently, 186 of the 1,781 features exhibited significant associations with OS (Figure 6B). Venn diagram analysis identified 32 features that overlapped between the differential features and those correlated with prognosis (Figure 6C). These overlapping features were subsequently subjected to further investigation regarding their relationships with clinical characteristics (Figure 6D). A total of 118 HNSC patients were stratified into a training cohort (n=95) and a validation cohort (n=23), with no significant differences observed in clinical characteristics between cohorts (Figure 7A). Machine learning models for predicting C1 were developed using 32 overlapping radiomics features, yielding 107 models incorporating >3 features (Figure 7B). Among these, 92% demonstrated an average AUC >0.70. The validation cohort revealed that 71% of models maintained AUCs >0.75, indicating robust generalizability across overlapping features.

Development of HNSC prognostic model based on overlapping rad iomics features

From the initial 32 overlapping radiomics features, 8 features demonstrating significant intergroup differences (P<0.01) were further selected. Multivariate prognostic analysis revealed 4 features as independent prognostic predictors, which were subsequently incorporated into a Cox proportional hazards regression model. SHAP value analysis identified wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Uniformity (mean SHAP: 4.644) and wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Entropy (mean SHAP: 3.860) as predominant contributors to prognostic predictions (Figure 9A,9B). Patients were stratified into high-risk (n=72) and low-risk (n=46) groups using an optimal cutoff determined by maximally selected rank statistics (Figure 9C). The model demonstrated time-dependent discriminative performance with 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year AUC values of 0.76, 0.78, and 0.70, respectively (Figure 9D). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified that gender (P=0.03), alcohol consumption history (P=0.04), and high-risk group (P<0.001) were significantly associated with OS. A prognostic nomogram incorporating these factors demonstrated favorable calibration consistency and clinical translational potential (Figure 10A-10C). GSEA further revealed that the high-risk group was characterized by enhanced tumor invasiveness, immunosuppressive microenvironment, and dysregulation of key pathways (Figure 10D,10E).

Investigation of HNSC heterogeneity based on clustering and machine learning model analysis

A systematic search of the PubMed database was conducted using the keywords “head”, “neck”, “cluster”, “subtype”, and “machine learning” to explore heterogeneity in HNSC published before March 2025. Thirteen studies utilizing gene expression data and three studies based on radiomics features were identified (24-39). For gene-based analyses, the investigated biological themes included cell differentiation, immune cell fractions, G protein-coupled receptors, cancer-associated fibroblasts, macrophages, PANoptosis, mitophagy, CD8+ T cells, multi-omics data (mRNA, lncRNA, miRNA, DNA methylation, somatic mutations), Golgi apparatus, and the mononuclear phagocyte system. The primary R packages employed were ConsensusClusterPlus, NMF, MOVICS, and ClassDiscovery. Clustering outcomes primarily identified 2 or 3 molecular subtypes. Among these, 10 studies developed prognostic machine learning models, with average AUC values ranging from 0.61 to 0.78. For radiomics-based analyses, all 3 studies focused on analyzing heterogeneity between clusters derived from imaging features, with none incorporating prognostic model development (Table 3).

Discussion

Radiomics holds the potential to refine prognostic risk stratification in cancer patients and characterize molecular-level heterogeneity alterations. In this study, we identified distinct molecular subtypes of HNSC with divergent clinical outcomes and biological significance based on ANRGs. Machine learning models incorporating radiomics features have been developed to predict the subtype with the poorest prognosis, revealing that radiomics features with high prognostic value exhibited robustness across algorithms. By biologically annotating ANRG-associated radiomics features, we elucidated their clinical relevance, thereby providing a non-invasive tool for preoperative risk stratification and immunotherapy response prediction.

HNSC remains a malignancy characterized by high molecular and clinical heterogeneity, posing significant challenges for prognostic prediction and personalized therapeutic strategies. ANRGs play critical roles in mediating anoikis resistance, with demonstrated importance across multiple cancers, including renal, breast, colorectal, hepatic, bladder, and ovarian cancers (40,41). For instance, ANRG-based prognostic models have been utilized as molecular biomarkers for risk stratification and survival prediction in breast cancer patients, as well as stratification factors for individualized and precision therapies (42). Similarly, ANRG-associated nomogram models could predict ovarian cancer prognosis and characterize immune landscapes (43). However, only a limited number of studies had explored ANRG heterogeneity in HNSC. For example, a prognostic nomogram based on 7 ANRGs was constructed for HNSC, revealing distinct immune statuses and drug sensitivities, which may assist clinicians in selecting personalized treatments for HNSC patients (44). Additionally, a seven-lncRNA anoikis-related risk signature was established to predict survival outcomes, immune landscape, and drug sensitivities in HNSC patients, potentially facilitating the design of personalized immunotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic strategies (45). Nevertheless, these existing ANRG-based models relied solely on genomic data, which is constrained by inherent limitations such as delayed acquisition, high costs, and impracticality for routine clinical implementation. Recent advancements in radiomics have opened new avenues for non-invasive characterization of tumor heterogeneity. Radiomics, analogous to genomic sequencing, enables high-dimensional quantification of tumor information from medical imaging, providing a cost-effective and rapid alternative for heterogeneity analysis. For example, Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based subregion-aware multitemporal radiomics features have been shown to delineate key pathways related to drug metabolism, PI3K-Akt signaling, estrogen response, hypoxia, and immune evasion, enabling accurate prediction of neoadjuvant chemotherapy response and long-term prognosis in luminal breast cancer (46). Recent advances in clustering and machine learning technologies have significantly promoted the exploration of HNSC heterogeneity. As mentioned above, 13 studies based on gene expression data and 3 studies based on radiomics features have explored HNSC heterogeneity phenotypes to characterize their prognostic value, employing various clustering methods, including the ConsensusClusterPlus, NMF, ClassDiscovery, and MOVICS packages. Current gene-centric studies predominantly focus on molecular themes such as immune infiltration, PANoptosis, and multi-omics integration, identifying 2–3 subtypes with distinct clinical outcomes. However, the number of prognostic models derived from these clusters ranges from 1 to 303, with AUC values for predicting HNSC ranging from 0.61 to 0.78, which may be attributable to the high dimensionality of genomic data and insufficient correction for confounders such as tumor purity. In contrast, radiomics-based studies are still scarce and narrowly focused, primarily analyzing imaging-derived clusters without developing prognostic models. This gap highlights a critical limitation: while genomic clustering provides biological insights, its clinical translation is hindered by delayed data acquisition and costs. However, whether radiomics can effectively capture the tumor heterogeneity represented by ANRGs in HNSC remains underexplored.

This study established a pipeline for analyzing ANRG-related molecular heterogeneity in HNSC and exploring the potential of radiomics-based non-invasive characterization of this heterogeneity, leveraging matched genomic and imaging data from the TCGA/TCIA databases. First, differential and prognostic analyses of 480 ANRGs identified 31 overlapping genes that were dysregulated in tumors and prognostically significant. K-means clustering based on these overlapping genes delineated three distinct molecular subtypes of HNSC (C1, C2, C3), which were associated with pathways such as proliferation-apoptosis evasion, metabolic reprogramming, and inflammatory dysregulation, reflecting the diversity of HNSC heterogeneity. These subtypes exhibited divergent prognostic values and immune infiltration patterns. Notably, the C1 subtype demonstrated significantly poorer OS and distinct infiltration levels of immune cells, including central memory CD4+ T cells, central memory CD8+ T cells, memory B cells, natural killer cells, natural killer T cells, and neutrophils, compared to other subtypes. These findings suggest that the C1 subtype represents an immunosuppressive phenotype resistant to conventional therapies.

The integration of radiomics further validates the clinical utility of the HNSC-C1 subtype. However, the varying utilization of radiomics features by different machine learning algorithms raises critical questions regarding which algorithms maximize feature efficacy or whether features exhibit generalizability across multiple algorithms. For instance, in the context of radiomics features extracted from chest CT images, a multicenter study demonstrated superior performance of RF over logistic regression (LR) and SVM algorithms, whereas a single-center study reported SVM dominance (47,48). These discrepancies may stem from divergent feature selection strategies. Against this backdrop, multi-combination models are increasingly explored for their potential to generalize feature utilization patterns. In this study, 113 combination models derived from 12 algorithms were applied for the first time to analyze ANRG-related molecular heterogeneity in HNSC. Among the 107 models predicting the C1 subtype, 92% achieved an average AUC >0.70. The validation cohort revealed that 71% of models maintained AUCs >0.75, indicating robust generalizability across overlapping features. Outstanding predictive performance was achieved by the LASSO + RF combined model, built upon four selected radiomics features, with AUCs of 0.98 in the training cohort and 0.86 in the validation cohort, underscoring its strong discriminative ability and generalizability. Although the observed correlations were modest, they suggested that the four radiomics features were associated with ANRG expression, with wavelet-HHH_glcm_Id and wavelet-LLH_glszm_LargeAreaEmphasis showing positive correlations with multiple ANRGs, possibly reflecting pro-survival signals, and original_firstorder_InterquartileRange and wavelet-HHH_glszm_ZonePercentage showing negative correlations, possibly indicating suppression of ANRGs. These associations support the biological relevance of radiomics features in capturing ANRG-driven molecular heterogeneity. To elucidate the biological relevance of these features, WGCNA was conducted using DEGs between HNSC tumors and normal tissues, revealing six co-expression modules. It was shown by correlation analysis that original_firstorder_InterquartileRange was significantly positively correlated with the green module, whereas wavelet-HHH_glcm_Id and wavelet-LLH_glszm_LargeAreaEmphasis were positively associated with the turquoise module, and wavelet-HHH_glszm_ZonePercentage was negatively correlated with it. It was revealed by KEGG enrichment that the green module was enriched in arachidonic acid metabolism, IL-17 signaling, and Staphylococcus aureus infection, implicating inflammatory and immune regulatory processes. In contrast, the turquoise module was enriched in cell cycle, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and DNA replication, indicating active proliferation and genomic maintenance. It was suggested by these distinct pathway enrichments that the radiomics features captured complementary aspects of tumor biology. Given that the worst prognosis was exhibited by the C1 subtype and the C1 subtype was linked to proliferation-related pathways, the turquoise module might be particularly relevant to the aggressive C1 phenotype. Concurrently, a multivariate Cox prognostic model was constructed to evaluate the prognostic value of ANRG-related radiomics features. The model revealed significant associations with tumor invasiveness and immunosuppressive biotypes in HNSC. To further interpret the contribution of radiomics features to prognosis, SHAP value analysis identified wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Uniformity and wavelet-HHH_firstorder_Entropy as predominant contributors to prognostic predictions. The prognostic nomogram integrating clinical factors (gender, alcohol consumption history) and radiomics risk scores exhibited strong calibration consistency, providing a practical tool for risk stratification in clinical settings.

Despite the novelty of this study in introducing radiomics as a non-invasive approach to characterize ANRG-related molecular heterogeneity in HNSC, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the retrospective design relying on public databases (TCGA/TCIA) inherently limited the generalizability of findings. The relatively small cohort size may introduce selection bias and reduce statistical power, particularly in clustering analyses. Although silhouette coefficients supported cluster stability, external validation using larger, multi-institutional cohorts is essential to confirm the reproducibility of the three subtypes and their biological relevance. Second, the limited sample size constrained the clinical interpretation of the molecular subtypes, particularly in relation to critical clinical variables such as human papillomavirus status and treatment response. Third, the associations observed between molecular subtypes and radiomics features remained correlative rather than causal. While bioinformatics analyses suggested plausible biological links, mechanistic validation through functional experiments was imperative. Future studies should employ in vitro or in vivo models to perturb specific ANRGs and assess concomitant changes in radiomics signatures, thereby establishing causality.

Conclusions

In conclusion, comprehensive analysis of ANRG expression profiles identified three molecular subtypes of HNSC with distinct prognostic outcomes and biological significance. Radiomics-based machine learning models effectively predicted high-risk HNSC subtypes non-invasively, serving as a practical alternative to ANRG-centric approaches. This framework demonstrates the potential to advance precision oncology by bridging imaging heterogeneity with molecular mechanisms in HNSC management, offering clinically actionable tools for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making.
