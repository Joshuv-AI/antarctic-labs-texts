Academic burden and coping among Generation Z Korean nursing students: a focus group study
Authors: Hwa-Mi Yang, Young-Eun Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore nursing students’ experiences of academic burden and their coping strategies to inform improvements in nursing education. Academic burden in nursing students is associated with decreased emotional well-being, reduced motivation, and difficulties in developing professional identity. These challenges may reflect characteristics often discussed in relation to contemporary students, such as a preference for autonomy and supportive learning environments, rather than being attributed to a specific generational group.MethodsA qualitative study utilizing focus group interviews and inductive content analysis. Twelve undergraduate nursing students from two universities in Seoul participated between March and April 2024. All had clinical experience and reported academic burden. Two semi-structured FGIs were conducted, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsFour main themes were identified: loss of agency within rigid academic structures, reflected in heavy workloads, peer comparison, and evaluation pressure that diminished self-efficacy and fostered emotional isolation; instability in professional identity during clinical training, exacerbated by unclear expectations and theory–practice gaps; emotional vulnerability shaped by comparison and suppression, leading to internalized distress; and efforts to sustain oneself through coping and relational support, including self-regulation and support from peers and institutional resources.ConclusionAcademic burden should be recognized not only as an individual challenge but also as a structural issue in nursing education. Learning environments that support autonomy, relational connection, and emotional well-being may better address the needs identified in this study, which align with characteristics often associated with Generation Z, while remaining grounded in the actual experiences of participants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09254-z.Keywords: Academic burden, Nursing students, Coping strategies, Generation Z, Qualitative Research

Introduction

In contemporary society, students face diverse academic demands that often lead to emotional, physical, and cognitive stress [1]. Academic burden refers to the accumulation of stress arising from these academic demands [2]. While a moderate level of burden can enhance motivation and achievement, excessive burden may result in psychological problems such as stress, anxiety, and depression, as well as health-related problems including sleep deprivation and poor dietary habits [2, 3].

Academic burden tends to intensify as students progress through school, and university students often experience higher levels of burden due to the autonomy and complexity of higher education learning environments [4]. Because the university period is critical for career exploration and identity formation, excessive academic burden may negatively influence students’ overall development and mental health.

As members of Generation Z, current university students experience academic burden within digitally connected environments that shape their learning behaviors and stress experiences [5, 6]. In Korea, Generation Z college students have reported increased anxiety and academic burnout, particularly following disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Perfectionistic tendencies and sensitivity to academic failure may further intensify academic burden within competitive social environments [7].

Among university students, nursing students face particularly demanding academic conditions because they must balance theoretical coursework, clinical practice, and preparation for national licensure examinations [8]. In Asia, academic burnout among nursing students is significantly higher than that of the general university student population [8], which may be attributed to highly competitive educational environments across countries such as Korea, China, Japan, and Singapore [1]. Within this context, academic burden in Korea may be further intensified by practice-intensive curricula, highly competitive employment conditions, and strong sociocultural expectations, including pressure to achieve academic excellence, secure stable employment, and fulfill family expectations. In Korea, this burden is further intensified by practice-intensive curricula, competitive employment conditions, and strong expectations from family and society.

In the Korean cultural context, academic burden is reinforced by values of collectivism, educational achievement, and filial piety [9], which encourage students to pursue academic excellence to meet both personal and family expectations [10]. The nursing profession in Korea, while respected, is also associated with high expectations and emotionally demanding responsibilities [11]. Moreover, stigma surrounding mental health and cultural emphasis on endurance may discourage students from openly discussing or seeking help for academic burden [12]. For Generation Z nursing students, this reluctance to seek help often coexists with openness in digital communication, creating a paradox between public online expression and private emotional suppression [7]. These dynamics highlight the need to explore nursing students’ experiences through qualitative inquiry.

Meanwhile, studies such as Matthew [13] have explored Generation Z nursing students’ emotional experiences specifically within simulation-based clinical learning contexts, focusing on situational, simulation-specific experiences rather than emotional challenges arising from the broader academic burden across the nursing program. Most studies have examined stress in relation to health behaviors, burnout mechanisms, or inter-major differences and have relied predominantly on quantitative methodologies [14–16].

Although Pérez-Jorge et al. [17] identified general stressors affecting students’ mental health through mixed methods, their study did not specifically address the academic burden experienced by nursing students. Consequently, there remains limited understanding of how Generation Z nursing students perceive and cope with academic burden across coursework, clinical practice, and professional preparation [18].

Therefore, this study aims to explore how Generation Z nursing students perceive and experience academic burden and how they cope with these challenges within contemporary higher education environments. Academic burden should be understood not merely as an individual stress response but as a multidimensional psychosocial phenomenon shaped by academic demands, social expectations, and institutional contexts [1, 19]. Examining student perspectives is essential because academic burden is inherently subjective and shaped by how students interpret and experience academic demands. While previous research has predominantly focused on measurable outcomes such as stress levels and academic performance, it has provided limited insight into students’ lived experiences. Understanding these perspectives is critical for developing effective, student-centered educational strategies and support systems. Using focus group interviews (FGIs), this study examines nursing students’ perspectives on academic burden, the factors that intensify or alleviate it, and the coping strategies they employ. The findings are expected to provide insights for developing educational environments and support systems that better address academic burden among nursing students.

Purpose

The purpose of this study is to explore Korean nursing students’ experiences of academic burden and their coping strategies using focus group interviews (FGIs) and to generate evidence that may inform the development of educational interventions aimed at reducing academic burden and improving learning outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed focus group interviews (FGIs) and used an inductive qualitative content analysis approach as described by Graneheim and Lundman [20] to explore nursing students’ experiences of academic burden and coping strategies. FGIs are well-suited for exploring shared experiences and generating rich insights through interaction among participants.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit nursing students from two universities in the Seoul metropolitan area. Participants were currently enrolled in the Department of Nursing and had completed at least one semester of clinical practicum. Students who reported experiencing academic burden and expressed willingness to participate were intentionally selected.

The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.

Participants

Between March 19 and April 4, 2024, 12 undergraduate nursing students from two universities in the Seoul metropolitan area were recruited. Purposeful sampling was applied.

students who had not yet completed clinical training and

Participants were recruited through bulletin board announcements, and interested students voluntarily contacted the research team by phone or email. Participants were recruited through voluntary responses to recruitment announcements, and academic burden was identified based on students’ self-reported experiences. Participation was entirely voluntary, and students were explicitly informed that their decision to participate or withdraw would not affect their academic evaluation, grades, or relationship with faculty. The researchers had no prior personal or professional relationship with the participants before recruitment.

Two FGIs were conducted with 5–7 participants in each group. This group size was determined based on methodological guidance recommending 4–8 participants per group to facilitate interactive yet in-depth discussion [21]. Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Daejin University (IRB No. 1040656-202401-HR-01-12). All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before participation, all students received detailed explanations of the study purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To ensure confidentiality, only minimal personal information (name and mobile phone number) was temporarily collected for communication purposes. After mobile gift cards were provided as compensation, identifying information was immediately destroyed.

All collected data were anonymized using unique participant codes and stored as encrypted files on a password-protected computer accessible only to the research team. Hard-copy materials were stored in a locked cabinet.

Data collections

Development of interview questions

Semi-structured interview questions were developed to explore nursing students’ experiences of academic burden and their coping strategies. The initial interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and previous literature addressing nursing students’ academic stress, clinical practicum burden, emotional difficulties, and coping or recovery strategies [15, 17, 22–24].

The interview questions were designed as open-ended prompts to encourage participants to share diverse and personal experiences. The draft guide was reviewed by two nursing faculty members and one qualitative research expert to evaluate content relevance and clarity.

The final interview guide included questions exploring students’ experiences of academic burden, emotional responses, memorable stressful situations, and coping or recovery strategies (see Supplementary File 1).

Data collection procedures

FGIs were conducted using the semi-structured interview guide. Two focus group sessions were held, each involving 5–7 participants, and each session lasted approximately 60–70 min. Interviews were conducted in quiet and private locations such as a faculty office or meeting room to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality. The focus groups were facilitated by two female nursing faculty members with doctoral degrees and prior experience in qualitative interviewing. One researcher served as the moderator, while the other observed group dynamics and recorded field notes. Before each session, the facilitator reviewed the study purpose and procedures and confirmed written informed consent.

The interviews were conducted by two female nursing faculty members with doctoral degrees and qualitative research experience. The same interview guide was used across both groups to ensure consistency.

During the sessions, key observations were recorded as field notes, and all discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ permission. Recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriptionist, and the transcripts were checked against the audio recordings for accuracy by the researchers. Participants were invited to review their transcripts (member checking), and no corrections were requested. No repeat interviews were conducted.

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. Specifically, after the second focus group interview, no new codes or themes emerged during analysis, and similar patterns were repeatedly observed across participants’ responses, supporting the adequacy of the sample and confirming saturation. This approach aligns with methodological guidance suggesting that saturation can be determined when additional data no longer yield new insights or variations in the themes.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis [20]. Following methodological guidance for FGIs, the focus group discussion was treated as the unit of analysis [25]. Two researchers independently reviewed the transcripts, identified meaning units, and conducted open coding. Codes were inductively derived from the data without applying a predefined framework. Condensed meaning units were grouped into subthemes and themes through iterative discussion among the researchers. Disagreements in coding or interpretation were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and theme development was maintained throughout the analytic process to enhance transparency and dependability. All analyses were conducted manually without qualitative analysis software, and regular team meetings were held to discuss coding decisions and enhance analytic rigor.

Ensuring rigor

This study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [26]. Credibility was enhanced through purposeful sampling of participants with relevant experiences and member checking of interview summaries and analytic interpretations. Transferability (fittingness) was supported by collecting data until thematic saturation and providing detailed descriptions of participants and research procedures.

For auditability, an experienced qualitative nursing researcher independently reviewed the analytic process and findings. To enhance confirmability, the researchers engaged in reflexive discussions to identify and bracket potential biases arising from their roles as nursing educators, ensuring that interpretations were grounded in the data.

Researcher preparation and qualifications

The research team consisted of nursing faculty members with more than five years of clinical practice experience and prior qualitative research publications. At the time of the study, the researchers were nursing educators who had prior experience teaching nursing students but were not involved in the academic evaluation of the participants. The researchers regularly participate in qualitative research seminars and methodological training, which supported the rigorous conduct of this study.

Results

General characteristics of participants

The study included a total of 12 undergraduate nursing students in their third and fourth years who had clinical practicum experience. Participants ranged in age from 20 to 32 years; 10 were female and 2 were male. Seven were third-year students and five were fourth-year students. All participants reported feeling academic burden during their nursing course (Table 1).

Main findings

The findings reveal that nursing students experience academic stress within rigid educational structures that diminish their sense of agency and create tension in forming their professional identity. These experiences often lead to emotional vulnerability and restrained expression of stress. Nevertheless, students continue to navigate these challenges by drawing on personal coping strategies, as well as relational and external resources, thereby maintaining their engagement in nursing education (Table 2). Themes and Sub-themes of the Findings

Academic Burden and Coping Process among Nursing Students

Theme 1. losing agency in learning within rigid academic structures

Students described their academic experiences as shaped by a mismatch between their desire for autonomous and meaningful learning and the rigid structure of nursing education. Within highly structured curricula and evaluation-driven systems, learning was often experienced as externally imposed rather than self-directed, gradually weakening their sense of agency and self-efficacy. This loss of agency appears to form the foundation of academic burden, influencing how students perceive and engage with subsequent academic and clinical experiences. Rather than being an isolated experience, this diminished sense of control appears to influence subsequent academic and clinical experiences, forming the foundation for later instability in professional identity and emotional vulnerability.

Subtheme 1.1 overload of assignments and schedule conflicts

Students reported that overlapping assignments, exams, and team projects created a sense of constant pressure. Rather than supporting meaningful learning, the accumulation of tasks within fixed schedules often left students feeling that academic activities were obligations to complete rather than opportunities for growth. The accumulation of assignments, exams, and team projects functioned as a persistent source of pressure, reflecting structural constraints on students’ ability to regulate their own learning processes. This pattern suggests a shift from meaningful engagement to task-oriented completion, in which learning becomes fragmented and externally driven rather than self-directed. Such fragmentation may limit opportunities for reflective learning and reduce students’ sense of preparedness, thereby contributing to decreased confidence as they transition into clinical training contexts.

“Two weeks before exams, when unfinished assignments kept piling up, I felt a heavy burden watching others study.” (Participant 4).

“Concurrent team projects and exam schedules reduced available study time and depleted energy, prompting doubt regarding the educational value of repetitive assignments.” (Participant 6).

“Because of fixed timetables and classes without choices, I can’t study in my own way and feel dragged along without control.” (Participant 8).

Subtheme 1.2 limited autonomy in learning

Academic evaluation and peer comparison further intensified this loss of agency. Grades were often internalized as indicators of personal worth rather than learning outcomes, leading students to question their competence and feel persistently behind their peers. Academic evaluation and peer comparison functioned not only as performance measures but also as mechanisms that shaped students’ self-concept, reinforcing a perception of inadequacy and reducing perceived control over learning. This internalization indicates that students’ sense of competence becomes increasingly externally defined, further weakening their agency and motivation for self-directed learning. As a result, students may enter clinical environments with diminished confidence and heightened sensitivity to evaluation, which can contribute to uncertainty and instability in their developing professional identity.

“When my first grades came out, they felt like my value, and from then on constant comparison made study a burden.” (Participant 1).

“As exams approach, a wave of anxiety hits—like I’m the only one behind, the only one who doesn’t know—and I shrink.” (Participant 5).

Theme 2. wavering professional identity in clinical training

Beyond classroom learning, clinical practice introduced another layer of tension as students confronted discrepancies between the ideals of nursing taught in school and the realities of clinical settings. These experiences often destabilized their developing professional identity.

This instability in professional identity can be understood as a continuation of the loss of agency identified in academic settings, as students who already feel limited control over their learning encounter further uncertainty in their roles within clinical environments.

Subtheme 2.1 silence and tension in clinical practicum

Clinical environments were described as unfamiliar and emotionally demanding. Students often felt pressure to adapt quietly to hierarchical and fast-paced settings, where emotional expression was implicitly discouraged. These findings suggest that clinical practicum environments reinforce passive adaptation, further constraining students’ agency and limiting opportunities for active learning and professional engagement.

“In each new setting I have to keep adapting, but I’m afraid to ask questions for fear of being a burden, so I just read the room. It feels much harder than class.” (Participant 8).

“Stories about someone fainting or being scolded were treated as normal; it made me feel like I was the strange one for seeing it otherwise.” (Participant 1).

Subtheme 2.2 role confusion from theory–practice gaps

Students frequently described feeling caught between roles. Although they were expected to behave like nurses, their limited authority and responsibilities reinforced a sense of ambiguity between being learners and professionals. This role ambiguity reflects a structural inconsistency between educational expectations and clinical realities, contributing to confusion in professional identity formation.

Such discrepancies not only weaken students’ confidence but also disrupt their ability to internalize a coherent professional identity, thereby extending the impact of academic burden into clinical learning contexts.

“In practicum we’re told to act like nurses, yet there are more things we’re told not to do; I was so tense that some days I just stood there.” (Participant 11).

“We learned patient-centered care, but in practicum fast, routine-based processing took priority; the gap startled me.” (Participant 9).

“The more practicum I did, the more I doubted whether I could become a nurse; the goal felt distant and my confidence dropped.” (Participant 10).

Theme 3. emotional vulnerability within silence and comparison

As academic pressure and professional uncertainty accumulated, students described experiencing emotional vulnerability characterized by self-doubt, comparison, and suppressed emotions. Within a culture that normalized endurance, emotional struggles were often internalized rather than openly shared. This emotional vulnerability emerges as a cumulative outcome of both diminished agency in academic learning and instability in professional identity, reflecting a layered process of psychological strain.

Subtheme 3.1 suppressed emotional expression

Students frequently felt that expressing emotional difficulty might be interpreted as weakness. This suppression of emotional expression indicates an internalization of cultural and institutional norms that prioritize endurance over emotional disclosure, further intensifying students’ psychological burden.

“Worried I’d look weak, I kept my mouth shut and even swallowed my tears.” (Participant 6).

“When seniors said, ‘Fourth year is really hard,’ I felt I shouldn’t say I’m struggling. Since everyone endures, I pushed myself to endure more.” (Participant 11).

“Even when practicum was hard, I held back tears so I wouldn’t be labeled weak.” (Participant 9).

Subtheme 3.2 heightened vulnerability through comparison

Peer comparison further intensified emotional strain. Rather than functioning as motivation, comparison operated as a mechanism that amplified self-doubt and emotional distress, reinforcing students’ perception of inadequacy. This process suggests that competitive academic environments do not merely increase workload but actively shape students’ emotional experiences, contributing to sustained vulnerability and reduced psychological resilience.

“When overwhelmed by assignments and aware that peers have already begun exam preparation, I experience impatience and anxiety, and observing others’ advantages provokes concern about falling behind in professional opportunities.” (Participant 4).

“When I see friends with good grades, I feel deprivation more than motivation, and it sometimes lingers like a wound.” (Participant 3).

“When others seem to do everything smoothly, I feel like I’m the only one struggling, which shrinks me and magnifies the burden.” (Participant 5).

Theme 4. seeking recovery pathways to sustain oneself

Despite persistent stress and vulnerability, students actively sought ways to sustain themselves within demanding academic environments. Recovery processes emerged through self-regulation, relational support, and the use of institutional and environmental resources. These recovery pathways can be interpreted as adaptive responses that enable students to maintain engagement despite ongoing structural and emotional challenges. Importantly, this stage represents not a resolution of academic burden but a continuous process of managing and negotiating its effects.

Subtheme 4.1 self-regulation strategies

Students described deliberate strategies to maintain emotional balance and continue functioning. These strategies reflect efforts to regain a sense of control in contexts where external demands limit autonomy, indicating a compensatory mechanism for reduced agency.

“The heavier the load, the more I try to act right away. Otherwise anxiety grows.” (Participant 3).

“To reduce stress, I lowered my expectations and kept telling myself, ‘This is enough.’” (Participant 4).

“To keep from collapsing, I do mind control—reminding myself, ‘Grades aren’t everything,’ and focusing more on becoming a good nurse in practice.” (Participant 5).

Subtheme 4.2 emotional support from relationships

Relationships with peers and family played a critical role in emotional recovery. Relational support functioned as a protective factor that mitigated emotional vulnerability and helped restore psychological stability. Through shared experiences and empathy, students were able to reframe their struggles and sustain their engagement in learning.

“Every brief chat, laugh, and moment of empathy with peers helped a lot.” (Participant 6).

“When I tell my parents what was hard, they listen well and take joy in my efforts, which feels like clear support.” (Participant 7).

Subtheme 4.3 use of external resources

Students also described seeking support from institutional resources and physical or digital spaces that enabled emotional expression. These external resources provided alternative spaces for emotional expression and reflection, compensating for the lack of psychological safety within formal academic and clinical environments. This suggests that institutional support systems play a critical role in buffering the negative effects of academic burden and facilitating students’ adaptive coping processes.

“At the counseling center, being listened to calmly felt comforting. Realizing I wasn’t the only one made me feel lighter.” (Participant 6).

“Through the health center’s counseling, I realized what I’d been suppressing and, for the first time, felt there were spaces on campus for me.” (Participant 11).

“Sitting alone in a quiet space gave me room to breathe; thanks to that, I kept my mental balance on many days.” (Participant 5).

“Anonymous posts on the campus app expressing challenges in practicum provided reassurance that I was not alone, and anonymity promoted candid sharing.” (Participant 9).

Discussion

Losing agency in learning within rigid academic structures

Participants described experiencing academic burden within rigid educational structures characterized by excessive assignments and scheduling conflicts. While diverse curricular activities are important for developing professional knowledge and communication skills, they also require substantial time and energy, functioning as a considerable burden for students [1]. This experience may be particularly salient for Generation Z students, who tend to value autonomy and active participation in learning.

One-way teaching methods and limited feedback further weakened learning motivation, while comparison with peers and evaluation-driven environments diminished self-efficacy [17]. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting decreased self-efficacy and academic engagement under heavy workloads [27]. Academic burden extended beyond task difficulty, provoking psychological stress and emotional anxiety [3].

Sustained academic pressure was reported to shift students’ emotional states toward boredom, fear, and indifference, ultimately threatening learning sustainability [1, 16] Accumulated psychological distress may eventually lead to exhaustion and increased risk of attrition [28]. These findings suggest that academic burden should be addressed not only at the individual level but also through structural educational support. These findings may be interpreted in light of characteristics often associated with Generation Z, particularly their preference for autonomy and active engagement in learning. The loss of agency described by participants may reflect a potential mismatch between students’ expectations and the rigid, evaluation-driven structure of nursing education.

Wavering professional identity in clinical training

Students also experienced uncertainty in forming their professional identity during clinical training. Theory–practice gaps, hierarchical practicum culture, and limited opportunities for questions created tension and confusion regarding their professional roles. Previous research similarly highlights the importance of instructor guidance, feedback, and supportive learning environments in clinical education [29].

In many clinical settings, a culture of silence discouraged active engagement. Asking questions was often perceived as burdensome, resulting in passive and survival-oriented learning. Hierarchical relationships with clinical instructors or staff nurses further restricted participation, while unclear roles during practicum led to frustration and decreased motivation [22].

The discrepancy between theoretical learning and clinical realities also weakened students’ confidence in their professional development [24]. Experiences of feeling “small” in practice made it difficult for students to imagine themselves as future nurses. For students accustomed to more open communication environments, such hierarchical clinical cultures may intensify feelings of uncertainty and emotional strain.

Within the Korean context, these experiences are further shaped by cultural norms such as nunchi and the hidden curriculum, which encourage silent adaptation and emotional restraint [12]. These dynamics highlight the importance of supportive supervision and reflective opportunities in clinical education [30].

Emotional vulnerability within silence and comparison

Participants frequently described suppressing their emotions within a culture where “everyone is struggling.” Strict graduation requirements, exam pressure, and excessive assignments are well-known sources of academic stress among university students [31]. Persistent comparison and competitive environments further intensified emotional isolation and reduced confidence in learning. Previous studies have shown that competitive educational environments are associated with higher anxiety and emotional exhaustion [32]. Such environments may undermine emotional well-being and academic persistence [15, 17]. Consequently, academic burden was closely related to emotional isolation and helplessness reinforced through silence and comparison [33].

Seeking recovery pathways to sustain oneself

Despite these challenges, students attempted to manage their stress through various coping strategies. Self-regulation behaviors, such as organizing schedules or completing assignments quickly, reflected efforts to control anxiety. However, emotional suppression as a coping strategy may lead to long-term burnout and depression [33]. Relational support played an important role in recovery. Emotional exchanges with peers and family helped reduce exhaustion and restore self-efficacy. Consistent with previous studies, social support functions as a key protective factor for resilience and psychological well-being among nursing students [23, 34].

Students also used physical spaces, such as lounges or rest areas, as temporary “emotional sanctuaries.” Anonymous online communities and peer conversations provided additional opportunities for emotional expression and empathy, helping reduce feelings of isolation. For many students, these informal and digital spaces functioned as psychologically safer environments for sharing stress.

Ultimately, social connectedness emerged as an important resource enabling students to endure academic burden and sustain their learning [35, 36]. These findings suggest that reducing academic burden requires a multi-level approach that integrates individual coping strategies with relational and institutional support systems.

The findings of this study suggest that academic burden among nursing students can be understood as a dynamic process rather than a set of isolated experiences. Specifically, excessive academic demands and rigid educational structures contribute to a loss of agency in learning, which in turn destabilizes students’ developing professional identity. This process is accompanied by heightened emotional vulnerability, particularly in contexts characterized by comparison and suppressed emotional expression. In response, students engage in various coping and recovery strategies to sustain themselves within these demanding environments. This interconnected progression highlights the structural and relational nature of academic burden, extending beyond individual stress responses.

The themes identified in this study, including academic overload, peer comparison, emotional vulnerability, and social support, align with previous research on nursing students’ academic stress. However, this study extends existing knowledge by conceptualizing academic burden as a multidimensional phenomenon shaped not only by academic demands but also by clinical practicum experiences and sociocultural expectations. The findings reveal that these dimensions are interconnected, demonstrating that academic burden is embedded within structural, relational, and practical learning contexts rather than arising solely from individual psychological responses. By examining students’ experiences across both classroom and practicum settings, the study provides deeper insights into how academic, social, and experiential factors converge to influence stress and coping.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on a small sample of nursing students from two universities in South Korea, which may limit the transferability of the results. Second, data were collected through focus group interviews, and participants’ responses may have been influenced by group dynamics. Future research should include more diverse samples and explore variations across different educational contexts to better understand nursing students’ experiences of academic burden. Despite these limitations, this study also has notable strengths. It offers in-depth insights into the lived experiences of Generation Z nursing students by utilizing focus group interviews, which facilitated rich interaction and collective reflection among participants. Furthermore, this study contributes to the literature by conceptualizing academic burden as a multidimensional phenomenon encompassing academic, clinical, and sociocultural factors, rather than examining isolated aspects of stress or burnout. These findings provide meaningful implications for the development of more supportive and student-centered nursing education environments.

Conclusion and implications

This study explored nursing students’ experiences of academic burden and their coping strategies. Excessive academic demands and competitive environments weakened students’ sense of agency and emotional well-being, particularly as they navigated uncertainty in forming their professional identity. Nevertheless, students sought to sustain themselves through self-regulation and relational support, drawing on social resources and recovery opportunities to maintain engagement in nursing education. Although this study involved a limited number of nursing students from specific universities in South Korea, the findings provide meaningful insights into the growing issue of academic burden in nursing education. Academic burden should be recognized not only as an individual challenge but also as an educational and institutional concern. Strengthening emotional, relational, and structural support in both academic and clinical learning environments may help foster more sustainable and supportive nursing education.
