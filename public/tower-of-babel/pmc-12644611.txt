Experimental realization of para-particle oscillators
Authors: C. Huerta Alderete, Alaina M. Green, Nhung H. Nguyen, Yingyue Zhu, B. M. Rodríguez-Lara, Norbert M. Linke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Para-particles are fascinating because they are neither bosons nor fermions. While unlikely to be found in nature, they may still offer accurate descriptions of exotic quantum phenomena. We report the quantum simulation of para-particle oscillators by tailoring the native couplings of two orthogonal motional modes of a trapped ion. Our system accurately reproduces the dynamics of para-bosons and para-fermions of even order, demonstrating the first experimental analogy of para-particle dynamics in any physical system and demonstrate full control of para-particle oscillators.

Introduction

Investigating particles that are neither bosons nor fermions is of fundamental interest in physics. One example is the so-called para-particles, which were formally introduced in the early years of quantum mechanics as a generalization of bosons and fermions1–3. They fall into two categories, para-bosons, and para-fermions, and are further classified by a parameter of deformation, or order of para-quantization, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\ge 1$$\end{document}. Like ordinary particles, para-particles are characterized by their spin and dimensionality in Hilbert space; para-bosons have integer spin and an infinite-dimensional representation, while para-fermions have half-integer spin and a finite-dimensional representation. Interest in para-particle statistics has recently re-emerged, particularly in the context of their relevance to strongly correlated matter and exotic quantum phases 4. During the initial development of the theory of para-particles, there was great interest in finding out if there are matter/field candidates for them in nature. However, it was shown that it is not possible to distinguish a para-particle from a collection of standard particles by measurement5. As a result, no particle from the standard model is currently considered a para-particle candidate2,6. The field has become purely theoretical but still has produced a string of remarkable results. Para-bosons and para-fermions were considered as candidates for the particles of dark matter/dark energy7–9 and as a tool to describe excitations in solids10. In para-quark models, quarks were considered as a para-fermion of order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=3$$\end{document} before the color model was introduced11. Proposals for the quantum simulation of para-bosons12 and para-fermions13 have renewed the interest in exploring possible uses of para-particles Earlier studies14,15 examined their thermodynamical properties, while more recent works have haghlited their utility in characterizing non-classical light16–20 and optical implementations21. Despite this wave of new activity, to the best of our knowledge, para-particle dynamics have not been realized experimentally, nor have the many theoretical predictions associated with them. Many physical systems have been put forward to produce experimental evidence, such as laser-inscribed arrays of evanescently coupled waveguides21 or hybrid systems involving two transmission line resonators controlled by a superconducting qubit22–24.

Following the theoretical proposals in Refs. 12,13, para-particle operators can be constructed using a spin-1/2 system coupled to two bosonic modes via combinations of Jaynes–Cummings (JC) and anti-JC interactions25,26. These constructions yield effective para-Bose and para-Fermi algebras under a parity-deformed representation that satisfies the trilinear commutation relations. Our experiment implements this mapping explicitly and measures observables–such as phonon occupations and spin polarization–that correspond to para-particle number operators in this framework.

We choose a trapped ion system27–30 for this experimental realization for its track record of motional state control31–36, tailored spin-motion interactions37,38, and high fidelity operations including reliable state preparation and measurement. Trapped ions provide a highly controllable quantum environment, which grants access to phenomena in regimes that are not otherwise accessible in nature39,40.

In this work, we report the analog quantum simulation of both para-bosonic and para-fermionic oscillators using the pseudo-spin of a trapped atomic ion and two orthogonal motional modes, with laser-induced couplings tailored to reproduce the desired interactions. Our system accurately captures the dynamics of para-particles of even order, providing the first experimental realization of para-particle oscillators in any physical setting. These results demonstrate full control of para-particle dynamics in a trapped-ion system and pave the way for experimental exploration of longstanding theoretical proposals and future applications involving para-statistical models.

Results

A para-particle oscillator may be understood as a parity–deformed oscillator that generalizes the standard Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein statistics associated with fermions and bosons3,41,42. The dynamics of these number operators further reflect the underlying structure of the para-particle Hilbert spaces: para-fermions evolve within a finite-dimensional space and thus display bounded, oscillatory behavior, while para-bosons, supported by an unbounded Fock ladder, exhibit monotonic growth under coherent driving. Our experimental results confirm these characteristic behaviors, validating the theoretical construction and its physical realization.

Experimental setup

During the experiment, we drive a combination of those operations, Eqs. (7)–(8), on both modes, x and y, simultaneously, see Fig. 2b, to realize the dynamics of a para-particle oscillator12,13. While the interaction structure determines whether para-bosonic or para-fermionic dynamics are realized, the para-particle order p is set by the initial state preparation–specifically, by initializing the system in a vacuum state with a chosen spin and phonon configuration that satisfies the algebraic condition given in Eq. (4). A typical experimental sequence is shown in Fig. 2c. First, we perform cooling of the motion and optical pumping to prepare the system in the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vert \downarrow , 0, 0 \rangle$$\end{document}45, and then transfer the system to a specific initial state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vert s, n_{x}, n_{y} \rangle$$\end{document} which determines the order of the simulated para-particle. To initialize the motional modes in a particular Fock state, phonon excitations are introduced by resonantly driving a combination of blue-sideband, red-sideband, and carrier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}-pulse to prepare the desired state, see Appendix C for more details. Then, we off-resonantly drive the two sideband transitions responsible for the para-particle dynamics with precisely defined intensities and detunings. After the para-particle evolution, we read out either the spin or the motional state. While the spin measurement is made directly with the expectation value calculated by averaging over many repetitions, the motional readout requires the spin as an intermediary. First, we reset the spin to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vert \downarrow \rangle$$\end{document} by applying an optical pumping pulse with negligible effect on the motional state population since our system is in the Lamb-Dicke regime. Subsequently, a blue-sideband pulse with frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{x(y)}$$\end{document} is applied to probe the Fock state distribution. This is repeated with increasing pulse duration resulting in Rabi oscillations with frequency components whose amplitudes depend on the Fock state populations31. Para-fermions

We first simulate a para-fermion of order 2, hence we prepare the vacuum state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vert 2; 0 \rangle \equiv \vert \downarrow , 0,1 \rangle$$\end{document}. The simulation consists of driving the red sidebands of both motional modes with equal Rabi frequency, Eq. (2). The spin oscillates with amplitude 0.5, Fig. 3a, and facilitates excitation exchange between x (blue line) and y (yellow line) motional modes, Fig. 3b. After a full oscillation of the spin, the population of the y mode switches coherently to the x mode. This represents the evolution of the para-Fermi number operator, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle \hat{\mathcal {N}}_{pF} \rangle$$\end{document} in Eq. (5), in the para-Fermi frame, shown in Fig. 3c. The dynamics become more complex as we increase the order, and hence scale up the number of occupied Fock states in the trapped-ion system. We also realize a system of order 10, starting from the vacuum state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\vert 10; 0\rangle \equiv \vert \downarrow , 0,5 \rangle$$\end{document}. The spin evolution exhibits oscillations that collapse and then revive partially, Fig. 3d, a feature also seen in the excitation exchange between motional modes, Fig. 3e. This translates into the expected para-Fermi number excitation, Fig. 3f.

The data points in Fig. 3 correspond to experimental results. Every data point is acquired by averaging over 300 measurements of the experimental sequence, and the error bars are statistical for the spin population and a result of the fitting procedure for the motional modes. Continuous lines correspond to a numerical simulation with up to eight Fock states per motional mode. We investigate the effects of motional heating in the trap as a potential source of error by including a Lindblad term in our numerical simulation, see Appendix E. We find them to be small, especially for shorter times of the evolution, see Fig. 3. We attribute the additional deviations visible in some places to fluctuations in the experimental parameters, such as intensity, which is subject to beam-pointing drift, and the motional mode frequency, which is subject to instabilities in the ion trap’s RF drive. The oscillatory dynamics observed for para-fermions are a direct consequence of the finite-dimensional Hilbert space associated with a given order p. For small p, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=2$$\end{document}, the system effectively behaves as a three-level oscillator, leading to simple, single-frequency spin oscillations, Fig. 3a. At higher orders, like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=10$$\end{document}, the system explores a richer set of states, and interference among multiple excitation pathways gives rise to multi-frequency beating and partial revivals, Fig. 3d. This structure reflects the coherent evolution of a generalized binomial state in the ion frame 13. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first physical realization of binomial states since their theoretical introduction 46,47.

Discussion

Driving dynamics with trapped ions that involve the Hilbert spaces of multiple orthogonal vibrational modes simultaneously is a powerful experimental capability for simulating exotic quantum systems 37,48. In this work, we demonstrate for the first time highly controllable realizations of both para-Fermi and para-Bose oscillators using a single trapped ion. This achievement enables access to deformed quantum statistics in a laboratory setting and marks a significant step forward in the experimental study of para-particles.

Our approach builds upon earlier theoretical proposals 12,13, which introduced the parity-deformed representation and showed how composite spin–boson operators could reproduce the algebraic structure of para-statistics. While those works focused on the design and feasibility of implementing such models, the present study achieves full experimental realization, including state preparation, coherent driving, and measurement of para-particle observables. The agreement between our experimental data and numerical simulations confirms the validity of the theoretical construction and provides the first physical evidence of para-particle oscillator dynamics.

This platform opens the door to more advanced quantum simulations involving interacting para-particles, many-body extensions, and the exploration of novel physical phenomena such as topological phases 49 and nonlinear quantum optics effects like multi-frequency conversion 50.
