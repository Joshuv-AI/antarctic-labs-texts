Effect of non-intubated spontaneous breathing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (NI-VATS) versus intubated general anesthesia (I-VATS) on the risk of postoperative venous thromboembolism
Authors: Jia Ye, Dong Chen, Zhimiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBy preserving spontaneous breathing and avoiding mechanical ventilation, Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery may attenuate perioperative inflammatory responses and promote early mobilization, thereby influencing the risk of venous thromboembolism. However, whether the risk of postoperative venous thromboembolism differs between Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and conventional Intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery remains unclear due to a lack of definitive evidence. We hypothesized that patients undergoing Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery would have a lower risk of postoperative venous thromboembolism.MethodsA single-center retrospective study was conducted. Patients with lung cancer who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic lung surgery at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Jinhua Central Hospital between March 2025 and July 2025 were enrolled and, based on the anesthetic approach, divided into the Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group (n = 25) and the Intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group (n = 30). The primary outcome was the risk of venous thromboembolism, as assessed by thrombotic complications, postoperative coagulation parameters, time to early postoperative ambulation, time to first flatus, and time to first defecation.ResultsNo perioperative venous thromboembolic complications occurred in either group. Regarding coagulation parameters, the D-dimer level in the Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group was 0.30 (0.04, 0.70) µg/L, which was significantly lower than 0.77 (0.34, 1.46) µg/L in the I-VATS group (P = 0.006). There were no statistically significant differences in prothrombin time (PT) or activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) between the two groups (P > 0.05). The time to early postoperative ambulation was significantly shorter in the Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group [0 (0, 0) days] than in the Intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group [1 (1, 1) days] (P < 0.001). The time to first flatus and defecation was 1 (1, 1) day in the Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group, significantly shorter than 2 (2, 3) days in the Intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the Non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group exhibited a significantly attenuated elevation in the inflammatory marker (leukocyte count: 2.24 ± 0.39 × 10⁹/L) compared with the Intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery group (5.82 ± 0.60 × 10⁹/L).ConclusionsNon-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery may reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism through several mechanisms: maintaining negative intrathoracic pressure generated by spontaneous breathing, lowering the risk of elevated intra-abdominal pressure, and facilitating early mobilization — all of which enhance venous return; substituting general anesthesia with combined regional anesthesia; and avoiding muscle relaxants, thereby attenuating surgery-induced hypercoagulability and preserving endothelial function.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers both in China and globally, particularly among middle-aged and elderly populations [1]. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery under intubated general anesthesia (I-VATS) is currently the main surgical treatment modality for lung cancer and has been widely adopted due to its minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery [2]. However, I-VATS is also associated with a range of complications, including cough, airway injury, difficult airway, adverse effects of anesthetic agents, dental injury, and laryngeal edema, all of which may adversely affect postoperative recovery [3, 4]. Among these, venous thromboembolism (VTE), a serious complication following thoracic surgery, is of particular concern in patients with lung cancer.

In the context of cancer-associated VTE, lung cancer accounts for approximately 20% of events, a proportion significantly higher than that of other malignancies. The risk of postoperative VTE in lung cancer patients is approximately 2.0% (range: 0.2%–19%), and the risk is notably higher in lung adenocarcinoma than in other histological types [5]. Studies have shown that adenocarcinoma disrupts the balance between fibrin deposition and degradation within the vascular system and secretes various procoagulant substances. Moreover, vascular endothelium infiltrated by cancer cells loses its antithrombotic properties, thereby heightening the risk of thrombosis [6, 7]. In addition to these tumor-intrinsic factors, treatment-related factors, patient-specific factors, and biomarkers all constitute predisposing elements for VTE formation. Regarding treatment, surgically induced blood loss and tissue injury can further activate the coagulation system, while anesthesia-related limb hypoperfusion and reduced venous blood flow also exert an influence. Patient-related factors include sex, age, smoking history, and prior history of VTE. Biomarkers such as D-dimer are useful in monitoring the occurrence of VTE events [8–10].

Intubated general anesthesia may further elevate the risk of VTE through multiple mechanisms. First, mechanical ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, thereby reducing venous return via the inferior vena cava. Hemodynamic studies have confirmed that elevated intrathoracic pressure directly impairs venous return efficiency, consequently increasing VTE risk [11, 12]. Second, muscle relaxants used during general anesthesia can induce shedding of the endothelial glycocalyx, a structure that plays a critical role in maintaining normal endothelial function and preventing thrombosis; its disruption heightens the risk of thrombus formation [5]. Third, the systemic inflammatory response may suppress the fibrinolytic system and upregulate the expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), further elevating the risk of thrombosis. Animal experiments published in *Nature Cardiovascular Research* have also demonstrated that under conditions of systemic inflammation, PAI-1 expression increases significantly, thereby promoting thrombus formation [13].

In recent years, Non-intubated thoracic surgery (NI-VATS) has been adopted by numerous clinical centers both domestically and internationally. This technique preserves spontaneous breathing and offers the combined advantages of minimally invasive anesthesia and enhanced recovery after surgery, gaining widespread acceptance among patients and clinicians and being broadly applied across a spectrum of thoracoscopic procedures [14–16]. To date, researchs on NI-VATS has primarily focused on enhanced recovery aspects such as avoidance of muscle relaxants, reduction of opioid consumption, decreased incidence of cough, and attenuation of the inflammatory response; however, the risk of postoperative VTE under this anesthetic approach remains poorly characterized [17, 18]. Therefore, this article focuses on examining the impact of NI-VATS on the risk of postoperative VTE.

Materials and methods

1. Patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic (VATS) lung surgery at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Jinhua Central Hospital between March 2025 and July 2025 were retrospectively enrolled. After excluding those who did not meet the eligibility criteria, the included patients were divided, based on the anesthetic approach, into the NI-VATS group (n = 25) and the I-VATS group (n = 30). All procedures were performed by the same attending surgeon. Postoperative routine pathological examination confirmed pulmonary malignancy in all patients (Fig.1).

2. Inclusion criteria

(1) Age between 18 and 75 years; (2) Postoperative routine pathological diagnosis of pulmonary malignancy; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I–III; (4) No other systemic diseases identified on preoperative examination; (5) Normal preoperative pulmonary function tests; (6) Absence of a difficult airway; (7) The surgical procedure in VATS; (8) No history of VTE; (9) Absence of pleural adhesions.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Preoperative pulmonary function tests indicating severe pulmonary ventilation dysfunction; (2) Intraoperative conversion to open thoracotomy; (3) Preoperative examination revealing coexisting respiratory disease, coagulation disorder, arterial or venous thrombosis, or hepatic or renal insufficiency; (4) Difficult airway; (5) Extensive intraoperative pleural adhesions; (6) history of VTE.

3. Anesthesia technique

I-VTAS group: General anesthesia was induced by intravenous administration of etomidate (0.25 mg/kg), sufentanil (30–40 µg/kg), and a muscle relaxant (cisatracurium 0.15 mg/kg or vecuronium 0.1 mg/kg). Following successful induction, a bronchial blocker or double-lumen tube was inserted to establish one-lung ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol (200–300 mg/h) or remifentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min), with doses adjusted according to intraoperative blood pressure; additional muscle relaxants were administered as needed.

Enrollment of flow chart

NI-VTAS group: anesthesia induction was achieved using a target-controlled infusion (TCI) of propofol (2–4.0 µg/mL) and sufentanil (0.1–0.2 µg/kg), after which a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) was inserted without the use of muscle relaxants. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol 1.5–3.5 µg/mL and remifentanil 0.03–0.1 µg/kg/min, supplemented with dexmedetomidine 0.3–1 µg/kg/h to attenuate respiratory depression and ensure adequate anesthetic depth. Dexmedetomidine was discontinued after pleural closure, and propofol and remifentanil were discontinued at the start of skin closure. Doses were adjusted intraoperatively according to the depth of anesthesia.

A multimodal analgesia strategy was implemented prior to the start of surgery.

Visceral pleural surface anesthesia: After entering the thoracic cavity, 5 mL of 2% lidocaine was sprayed onto the visceral pleural surface.

Intercostal nerve block: The T2–T11 intercostal nerves were blocked with 1 mL of a 1:1 mixture of 0.75% ropivacaine and 2% lidocaine.

Ipsilateral vagus nerve block: The vagus nerve on the surgical side was blocked using 2.5 mL of a mixture of 2% lidocaine and 0.75% ropivacaine.

All patients were provided with a postoperative patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump containing 50 µg of sufentanil and 30 mg of oxycodone dissolved in 100 mL of normal saline, which provided analgesia for approximately 48 h.

4. Outcome measures

The following demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected and analyzed: Sex, age, body mass index (BMI), surgical procedure, baseline comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart disease), preoperative Caprini score, preoperative white blood cell (WBC) count, preoperative coagulation parameters, prior history of VTE, operative time, time to first postoperative ambulation, time to first postoperative flatus and defecation, drainage characteristics (including drainage volumes on the first three postoperative days and total drainage volume), time to chest tube removal, postoperative WBC count, postoperative coagulation parameters, postoperative Caprini score, mechanical thromboprophylaxis measures, hemorrhagic complications, and VTE complications.

5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Normally distributed continuous data were compared using Student’s t-test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the rank-sum test and expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 details the baseline characteristics of the two groups. Surgical procedures included wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy, with no statistically significant difference in the distribution of procedures between the two groups. According to the Expert Consensus on VTE Prevention and Management in Hospitals of Zhejiang Province, the Caprini risk assessment model should be applied to all hospitalized surgical patients during the perioperative period to evaluate their VTE risk. This model assigns scores based on multiple indicators—including operative time, age, history of VTE, history of malignancy, central venous catheterization, and pulmonary function status—and subsequently stratifies patients into low-, moderate-, and high-risk categories.Hospitalized surgical patients with a high-risk Caprini score should receive anticoagulant prophylaxis in the absence of contraindications. All patients in both groups had low- to moderate-risk Caprini scores preoperatively; therefore, with the exception of one patient in the NI-VATS group who had a history of heart valve surgery and was switched from warfarin to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) for perioperative anticoagulation, none of the patients received preoperative anticoagulant therapy. In the NI-VATS group, six patients had a history of other malignancies, compared with only one in the I-VATS group. Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in sex, age, BMI, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease), or preoperative complete blood count (CBC) and coagulation parameters, confirming that the baseline characteristics were well balanced and highly comparable between the two groups.

All patients were monitored in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for 30 min after surgery. If no abnormalities were detected, they were transferred back to the general ward for continued monitoring and care. All patients followed our department’s standardized early postoperative mobilization protocol: immediately upon returning to the ward, the head of the bed was elevated, and bilateral lower extremity intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) was applied once. At 2 h postoperatively, if there were no contraindications, patients were encouraged to sit at the bedside. Thereafter, assessments were conducted every 2 h. When patients simultaneously met all of the following criteria—clear consciousness, stable vital signs, a pain score ≤ 4, absence of dizziness or nausea, and limb muscle strength ≥ Grade 4—they were assisted by nurses and family members to stand or walk out of bed.

In accordance with the Expert Consensus on VTE Prevention and Management in Hospitals of Zhejiang Province, all enrolled patients had a high postoperative Caprini score, indicating a comparable VTE risk between the two groups and necessitating anticoagulant prophylaxis for both. Anticoagulation was administered as daily subcutaneous injection of LMWH 0.4 mL, starting on postoperative day 1 and continuing until discharge. Blood samples for CBC and coagulation parameters were collected on postoperative day 2. In the NI-VATS group, apart from one patient who required continued anticoagulation due to pre-existing valvular heart disease, the remaining 24 patients did not receive postoperative LMWH anticoagulant therapy. In the I-VATS group, six patients either did not receive or had LMWH discontinued due to high postoperative drainage volume or bloody drainage, while the remaining 24 patients received LMWH thromboprophylaxis starting on postoperative day 1. No VTE-related complications (including deep vein thrombosis(DVT), pulmonary embolism, or cerebral infarction and other embolic events) occurred in either group postoperatively. Compared with the I-VATS group, NI-VATS group showed no differences in operative time, time to chest tube removal, or postoperative Caprini score; however, the time to first postoperative ambulation and time to first flatus and defecation were significantly shorter in the NI-VATS group. One patient in the I-VATS group developed a postoperative complication of incomplete intestinal obstruction, which resolved after treatment. With regard to drainage characteristics, there were no statistically significant differences in drainage volumes on the first three postoperative days or total drainage volume between the two groups, although the NI-VATS group tended to have less drainage overall (Table 2).

Discussion

With the continuous advancement and innovation of minimally invasive thoracoscopic surgery and anesthetic techniques, NI-VATS has gained widespread acceptance in the field of thoracic surgery. Its scope of application has progressively expanded from initially simpler procedures such as bulla resection and pulmonary wedge resection to more refined operations including segmentectomy and lobectomy, and even to highly complex surgeries such as tracheal resection and sleeve lobectomy [19]. During surgery, the preservation of spontaneous breathing in the contralateral lung effectively prevents the potential risks associated with mechanical ventilation, including barotrauma and volutrauma, thereby significantly attenuating the postoperative pulmonary inflammatory response [3, 20]. In addition, this approach avoids the use of muscle relaxants, which not only facilitates the early recovery of the cough reflex and expectoration capacity, effectively reducing the risk of postoperative pulmonary infection, but also promotes early ambulation, thereby decreasing the potential for DVT and accelerating early postoperative recovery [15]. Multiple studies and published reports have further demonstrated that NI-VATS offers significant advantages in reducing the risk of postoperative ileus, decreasing postoperative drainage volume, and shortening the length of hospital stay [15, 21–23].These combined benefits enhance perioperative patient comfort and satisfaction while providing robust support for the refinement and minimally invasive development of thoracic surgery.

In Western countries, great emphasis has long been placed on the prevention and management of postoperative VTE following thoracic surgery, supported by well-established prevention systems and extensive clinical experience. In contrast, attention to postoperative VTE prevention in thoracic surgery started relatively late in China, and to date, there remains no comprehensive guideline specifically addressing VTE prevention after pulmonary resection [24]. In 2016, Chinese thoracic surgeons participated in an international survey jointly initiated by the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) and the American Association for Thoracic Surgery, which investigated the global landscape of VTE prevention practices among thoracic surgeons. The findings revealed that among the 1,150 Chinese thoracic surgeons who participated—representing secondary and tertiary hospitals nationwide—approximately half worked in institutions lacking standardized protocols for postoperative thromboprophylaxis. Moreover, over one-third of respondents believed that VTE prophylaxis was unnecessary after hospital discharge, highlighting deficiencies in postoperative thrombosis management in China at that time [25].

The core of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) philosophy is to reduce surgical stress and complications through a series of evidence-based perioperative optimization protocols and measures, thereby achieving the ultimate goal of safe and accelerated recovery. Since surgery inherently engages Virchow’s triad, surgical patients are at extremely high risk for VTE. Once VTE occurs, it can not only disrupt the recovery process and lead to fatal pulmonary embolism, but also directly contradicts the core objectives of ERAS, which aim to reduce complications and shorten the length of hospital stay. Therefore, proactive and effective VTE prophylaxis is a prerequisite for the successful implementation of ERAS and for ensuring patient safety. Concurrently, the comprehensive ERAS strategy substantially strengthens and optimizes the effectiveness of traditional VTE prophylaxis. Rather than relying on anticoagulants in isolation, it employs multimodal, integrated interventions to synergistically counteract the three components of Virchow’s triad. Within the ERAS philosophy, VTE prophylaxis has been elevated from a single medical order to a multidisciplinary, collaborative treatment process spanning the entire perioperative period. By integrating mechanical prophylaxis, pharmacologic prophylaxis, and various components of enhanced recovery, it not only reduces the risk of VTE but also creates more favorable conditions for VTE prevention through promoting early recovery, ultimately forging a safer and more efficient surgical care model.

In our study, we observed that patients in the NI-VATS group had significantly shorter times to first flatus and defecation and to early postoperative ambulation compared with those in the I-VATS group. Ileus (or postoperative ileus) is a common complication following I-VATS. General anesthetic agents, postoperative opioid analgesics, and the stress response triggered by surgical trauma itself can all suppress intestinal peristalsis, leading to the accumulation of large amounts of gas and fluid within the intestinal lumen, culminating in paralytic ileus or severe intestinal distension. This ileus per se can significantly increase the risk of VTE [26]. The underlying mechanism is as follows: when a large volume of contents accumulates in the intestine, intra-abdominal pressure rises sharply, directly compressing the inferior vena cava and iliac veins — the major abdominal veins responsible for returning blood from the lower extremities — thereby markedly increasing resistance to venous return and significantly slowing blood flow velocity in the lower limbs. This creates “venous stasis” within the venous system, which is one of the critical requirements for triggering DVT [27–29]. Furthermore, positive-pressure mechanical ventilation administered during I-VATS and diaphragmatic elevation caused by ileus both lead to a marked increase in intrathoracic pressure, impeding venous return via the vena cava and resulting in a substantial reduction in venous return, similarly contributing to venous stasis in the lower extremities. Under these pathophysiological conditions, lower extremity DVT is more likely to form. Therefore, actively promoting postoperative recovery of intestinal peristalsis, achieving early passage of flatus and stool, and early ambulation not only relieve abdominal distension and discomfort and facilitate recovery of intestinal function, but also by rapidly decreasing intrathoracic and intra-abdominal pressure, relieving compression on the veins, and enhancing blood circulation — constitute vital measures for effectively ameliorating venous stasis and reducing VTE risk. Therefor, within the ERAS philosophy, early postoperative recovery of gastrointestinal function and early ambulation are regarded as core strategies for the prevention of thrombosis and other complications.

Studies have demonstrated that I-VATS and NI-VATS differ significantly in the extent of perioperative endothelial injury, with general anesthesia exerting a more pronounced inhibitory effect on endothelial function [30]. A controlled study comparing anesthetic modalities showed that the postoperative decrease in flow-mediated endothelium-dependent dilation was substantially greater in patients receiving general anesthesia than in those receiving combined spinal-epidural anesthesia, suggesting that different anesthetic strategies may influence the trajectory of postoperative endothelial recovery.This discrepancy is rooted in the fundamentally distinct biological behaviors of the two anesthetic approaches with respect to mechanical stimulation, pharmacological effects, and stress modulation [31]. From a mechanistic perspective, general anesthesia inflicts multiple insults on the vascular endothelium. Endotracheal intubation itself constitutes an intense nociceptive stimulus that can provoke an aberrant co-release of plasma endothelin and nitric oxide; although this dysregulated vasoactive profile may superficially present as a transient elevation, it actually reflects endothelial cell activation and injury under severe stress [30]. Meanwhile, volatile anesthetics such as sevoflurane and desflurane have been shown to directly impair endothelium-dependent vasodilation by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species and diminishing the bioavailability of nitric oxide. Certain intravenous anesthetics may also interfere with normal endothelial signal transduction, further aggravating dysfunction [32]. These factors, acting in concert, amplify oxidative stress and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoting structural disruption of the endothelial barrier and increasing vascular permeability.In contrast, the application of regional anesthesia and local anesthetics exhibits a more definitive endothelial protective effect. Local anesthetics such as ropivacaine and lidocaine possess direct anti-inflammatory properties independent of sodium channel blockade. In vitro, they can efficiently block the endothelial inflammatory signaling pathway induced by tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), inhibiting aberrant activation of Src kinase downstream of the PI3K/Akt pathway, thereby reducing neutrophil adhesion and transendothelial migration and preserving the integrity of the endothelial barrier at the molecular level [33]. Furthermore, by effectively blocking the afferent transmission of surgical nociception to the central nervous system, regional anesthesia markedly suppresses sympathetic overactivity and the systemic stress response, substantially mitigating the endothelial injury secondary to the neuroendocrine storm and creating favorable conditions for maintaining vascular homeostasis [34].

Studies have shown that the pre-to-postoperative difference in D-dimer is an independent risk factor for postoperative VTE in lung cancer patients, with a greater change indicating a higher likelihood of VTE [35]. In our study, compared with the I-VATS group, patients in the NI-VATS group exhibited a milder postoperative inflammatory response and a more modest change in D-dimer. Although none of these differences reached statistical significance, the findings that APTT and PT remained more prolonged in the NI-VATS group than in the I-VATS group, despite the I-VATS group receiving anticoagulation, along with less postoperative pleural drainage in the NI-VATS group, collectively suggest that the NI-VATS group tended toward a hypocoagulable state. Furthermore, the NI-VATS group, without receiving anticoagulant therapy, did not show an increased risk of developing DVT. Therefore, for surgical patients at higher risk of VTE, such as those with a family history of VTE or advanced age, NI-VATS may be considered as a strategy that can both reduce surgical trauma and effectively lower the risk of postoperative VTE. For these high-risk patients, the Tubeless approach not only offers a safer treatment option but also helps shorten postoperative recovery time and reduce the risk of complications, thereby improving overall treatment outcomes and recovery quality.

These findings carry important clinical implications. For surgical patients at elevated VTE risk—such as those with a family history of thromboembolism or advanced age—non-intubated anesthetic techniques may offer a valuable therapeutic alternative. By minimizing surgical trauma while simultaneously mitigating postoperative thrombotic risk, the tubeless approach could provide a safer treatment option for this vulnerable population. Furthermore, this technique may facilitate faster recovery and reduce complication rates, ultimately enhancing both the efficacy of surgical treatment and the quality of postoperative recovery in high-risk patients.

Conclusions

Clinicians should conduct comprehensive assessments of patient-specific thrombotic risk factors—including age, type of surgical procedure, comorbidities, and prior history of thromboembolism—to formulate individualized prevention or surgery strategies. For high-risk oncology patients, such as those undergoing surgery for lung cancer, preoperative thrombotic risk assessment is recommended, with consideration of prophylactic anticoagulation when indicated. Close postoperative monitoring of coagulation parameters and clinical signs is essential to enable timely detection and management of potential thrombotic complications.With the continuous advancement of medical technology, the NI-VATS approach also provides more options for the prevention and management of VTE. Furthermore, enhancing patient education to improve awareness of VTE and promote self-management capabilities constitutes a vital component of comprehensive prevention efforts.Through multidisciplinary collaboration and integrated intervention approaches, we anticipate further reductions in VTE incidence and mortality, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

This study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a limited sample size. Moreover, because no VTE events occurred, the true difference in VTE risk between the two groups could not be evaluated. VTE monitoring relied on non-systematic screening, which may have led to missed asymptomatic events. Consequently, inferring clinical benefit from surrogate endpoints is subject to bias, and the conclusions drawn are inherently limited. Future multicenter, large-sample, prospective cohort studies are warranted to further validate these findings.
