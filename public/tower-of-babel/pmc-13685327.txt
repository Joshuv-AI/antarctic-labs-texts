Background

Propofol-based sedation and anesthesia are integral to contemporary diagnostic and therapeutic gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy. Current gastroenterology guidelines recommend propofol, administered by appropriately trained personnel, for complex or prolonged procedures to optimize procedural conditions and reduce cardiopulmonary risk [1, 2]. Systematic reviews and large observational studies further support the safety, effectiveness, rapid recovery profile, and high patient satisfaction associated with propofol-based endoscopic sedation, while also highlighting substantial heterogeneity in sedative regimens, staffing models, and monitoring strategies across institutions and healthcare systems [3–5]. In routine clinical practice, however, propofol titration during GI endoscopy remains guided mainly by hemodynamic variables and behavioral responses, and no brain-based monitoring strategy has been specifically validated for this high-throughput procedural setting [1–3].

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a direct and time-resolved measure of cortical population activity and is central to current understanding of anesthetic-induced transitions of consciousness [6–9]. Under propofol, loss and recovery of responsiveness are associated with reproducible EEG changes, including the reorganization of slow-delta and alpha oscillations, reflecting altered thalamocortical and large-scale network dynamics [6]. More broadly, systems-neuroscience work has established that anesthesia is not simply a reversible coma-like state, but a drug-induced perturbation of arousal circuits and distributed cortical-subcortical networks [7–9]. These insights have stimulated growing interest in EEG markers that are not only physiologically informative, but also clinically useful for monitoring anesthetic depth and consciousness transitions [8–10].

Beyond conventional spectral analysis, transient oscillatory events and connectivity metrics may provide complementary information about anesthetic state. In particular, sigma-band activity is of interest because it overlaps with the conventional spindle frequency range and may reflect thalamocortical oscillatory processes that are not fully captured by broader high-frequency or beta-band summaries. Sleep spindles are 11–16 Hz burst-like oscillations generated within thalamocortical circuits and are closely related to sensory gating, sleep stability, and state-dependent information processing [11]. Spindle-like activity also occurs under general anesthesia and may reflect altered thalamocortical communication during anesthetic-induced loss and recovery of consciousness [8, 9, 11]. Therefore, separating alpha, sigma, and beta activity may preserve physiologically distinct information when characterizing EEG changes across anesthetic state transitions.

At the network level, anesthesia is accompanied by marked reconfiguration of functional connectivity. Phase-based measures such as the weighted phase lag index (wPLI) and imaginary coherence (iCOH), together with amplitude-based measures such as amplitude envelope correlation (AEC), capture distinct but complementary aspects of frequency-specific interactions between brain regions [12]. Converging EEG and neuroimaging studies have shown that propofol-induced unconsciousness is associated with altered cortico-thalamic interactions, reduced connectivity diversity, and disruption of higher-order large-scale network integration [13–18]. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for describing anesthetic state in terms of both local oscillatory activity and large-scale network organization [7–9].

Despite these advances, most EEG studies of propofol anesthesia have been conducted either in healthy volunteers or in operating-room settings under relatively controlled dosing paradigms, which differ substantially from the brief, stimulus-rich, and clinically dynamic conditions of GI endoscopy [13, 18]. In endoscopy practice, patients typically undergo rapid transitions between wakefulness, deep sedation, and recovery, often under combined exposure to propofol, opioids, and procedural stimulation. Yet few studies have jointly characterized spectral, spindle-like event, and functional connectivity changes across clinically defined anesthetic stages in this setting. This gap limits the translation of neurophysiological insights into practical EEG-informed monitoring strategies for procedural anesthesia.

This single-center prospective observational study characterized EEG markers of propofol-based anesthesia during GI endoscopy across four clinically defined stages: baseline, induction, maintenance, and recovery. Specifically, stage-dependent changes in spectral power, spindle-like events, and functional connectivity were quantified using a unified analytic pipeline in a pragmatic endoscopy environment. Importantly, EEG acquisition was performed with portable polysomnography (PSG), and the analyses focused on six scalp EEG channels covering frontal, central, and occipital derivations. The study therefore aimed to characterize clinically interpretable EEG correlates of anesthetic-state transitions and changes in responsiveness, thereby providing a neurophysiological basis for future EEG-informed monitoring in endoscopy suites.

Methods

Aim, study design and setting

The aim of this study was to characterize stage-dependent EEG changes during propofol anesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy and to identify EEG features associated with clinically defined transitions in responsiveness, using a pragmatic portable PSG-derived six-channel EEG setup. This was a single-center prospective observational study conducted at the gastrointestinal endoscopy center of the First Hospital of Jilin University.

Participants

Adult patients aged 18 years or older who were scheduled to undergo GI endoscopy under propofol-based anesthesia were consecutively screened for eligibility. Participants were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) planned GI endoscopy with propofol-based anesthesia; and (3) provision of informed consent by the participant or a legally authorized representative for intra-procedural EEG monitoring.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) known allergy or intolerance to propofol; (2) history of significant neurological disease, including stroke, brain tumor, traumatic brain injury, or clinically diagnosed cognitive impairment; (3) history of major psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, or anxiety disorders; (4) pregnancy or breastfeeding; and (5) any other condition judged by the investigators to make the patient unsuitable for study participation.

A total of 31 participants were included in the final analysis, including 22 undergoing screening endoscopy and 9 undergoing therapeutic GI procedures (Fig. 1). All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee.

Anesthesia procedure and clinical monitoring

All procedures were performed under routine clinical practice in the endoscopy center. Propofol was used as the main anesthetic agent, with drug regimens differing according to procedure type.

For therapeutic procedures, anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg, atracurium 0.2 mg/kg, sufentanil 1 µg/kg, and dezocine 5 mg, followed by continuous infusions of propofol (450 µg/kg/min) and remifentanil (0.5 µg/kg/min). Infusions were discontinued approximately 15 min before the end of the procedure. For screening endoscopy, anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg and remifentanil 0.1 µg/kg, followed by maintenance with propofol 300 µg/kg/min and remifentanil 0.1 µg/kg/min, which were stopped approximately 5 min before the end of the procedure.

Standard non-invasive intra-procedural monitoring, including blood pressure, electrocardiography, and pulse oximetry, was applied throughout the procedure in accordance with current sedation recommendations [1–3].

After recovery, participants completed a structured verbal questionnaire addressing internally generated imagery or thoughts, auditory perception, bodily discomfort or procedural sensations, and subjective passage of time during the interval from sedative administration to awakening. Dreaming during anesthesia was coded as “Yes” when a participant reported internally generated visual imagery, scenes, or an experience described as dream-like.

EEG acquisition and stage definition

Continuous polysomnographic signals were recorded throughout the peri-procedural period using a portable polysomnography (PSG) system (PSM-A, Mukie; Hangzhou Neurotrace Sci-Tech Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The recording montage included electroencephalography (EEG; 10 leads), electrooculography (EOG; 2 leads), submental electromyography (EMG; 3 leads), electrocardiography (ECG; 2 leads), and lower-limb EMG activity (4 leads). The EEG montage included six scalp recording electrodes (F3, F4, C3, C4, O1, and O2), bilateral mastoid electrodes (M1 and M2), and reference/ground electrodes. The present EEG analyses focused on the six scalp EEG channels (F3, F4, C3, C4, O1, and O2), which provided frontal, central, and occipital scalp coverage. Electrode impedances were maintained within the acceptable range according to manufacturer recommendations. Recordings covered the pre-procedural awake period, the anesthesia period during endoscopy, and the early recovery period after completion of the procedure.

Baseline EEG was recorded in the procedure preparation room before propofol administration, with participants awake, supine, and resting with their eyes closed. Four clinically defined anesthetic stages were subsequently identified using synchronized clinical timestamps, behavioral observations, and OAA/S assessments: baseline, induction, maintenance, and recovery. Loss of responsiveness was operationally defined as an Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score ≤ 1. Induction was defined as the interval from propofol administration to the first documented OAA/S score ≤ 1. Maintenance referred to the stable intra-procedural period after loss of responsiveness. Recovery was defined as the interval from the first purposeful response or verbal output to the end of EEG recording. Stage boundaries were aligned with the EEG time axis using the synchronized clinical time sheet [6, 12, 19].

EEG preprocessing

EEG signals were originally acquired at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. EEG preprocessing was performed in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Data were down-sampled to 256 Hz, notch-filtered at 50 Hz to remove line noise, and band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz. Independent component analysis (ICA) was applied as an auxiliary artifact-reduction procedure to identify components showing patterns consistent with ocular or muscle contamination. Relevant components were removed, followed by visual inspection to reject residual high-amplitude or nonphysiological artifacts. After preprocessing, EEG signals were re-referenced to the average reference. Only EEG segments retained after preprocessing and visual quality control were included in the subsequent spectral, spindle-like event, and functional connectivity analyses.

The main analyses were performed using the six scalp EEG channels F3, F4, C3, C4, O1, and O2. All available stage-specific EEG data were included in the main analyses. For each included participant-stage observation, all six scalp EEG channels were available for analysis. This approach provided a clinically feasible six-channel EEG summary for subsequent analyses.

Spectral analysis

Spectral analysis was performed in Python 3.10 using custom scripts. For each participant and stage, EEG signals from the six scalp channels F3, F4, C3, C4, O1, and O2 were segmented according to stage boundaries, and power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using Welch’s method with Hann windows. Band-specific power was calculated for the delta (δ, 0.5–4 Hz), theta (θ, 4–8 Hz), alpha (α, 8–12 Hz), sigma (σ, 12–15 Hz), and beta (β, 15–30 Hz) frequency ranges. Relative power was obtained by normalizing the power within each frequency band to the total power within 0.5–30 Hz. Spectral edge frequency 95 (SEF95), defined as the frequency below which 95% of the total spectral power was contained, was also calculated. These metrics were selected based on prior work linking spectral features and SEF95 to anesthetic depth [6, 19].

For the primary inferential analyses, spectral metrics were averaged across the six scalp EEG channels to generate one summary value per participant and stage. This averaged analysis should be interpreted as a channel-averaged EEG summary rather than as evidence of spatially homogeneous EEG dynamics.

Spindle-like event detection and feature extraction

Spindle-like event analysis was performed in Python 3.10. EEG signals from each scalp channel were band-pass filtered at 11–16 Hz, and the amplitude envelope was derived from the analytic signal using the Hilbert transform. The detection approach was adapted from previously reported Hilbert-envelope-based methods for spindle detection in sleep and anesthesia studies [11, 20, 21].

Candidate events were defined as contiguous periods during which the envelope exceeded its mean by 1.5 standard deviations and were retained if their duration was 0.5–2.0 s. All automatically detected candidates were subsequently reviewed by two sleep medicine specialists to confirm spindle-like morphology and exclude obvious residual artifacts.

Event count, density, mean duration, and mean peak frequency were extracted. Peak frequency was defined as the frequency corresponding to the maximum event-level spectral power within 11–16 Hz [22], and event density as the number of confirmed events per minute of analyzable EEG. Metrics were averaged across the six scalp channels to yield one value per participant and stage. The term “spindle-like event” was used because these events occurred during anesthesia and early recovery rather than conventionally staged non-rapid eye movement sleep; therefore, their physiological equivalence to typical sleep spindles cannot be established from the present scalp EEG recordings.

Functional connectivity analysis

Functional connectivity analysis was conducted in MATLAB R2022b using the FieldTrip toolbox. Preprocessed EEG data from the six scalp channels F3, F4, C3, C4, O1, and O2 were converted to FieldTrip format and segmented into non-overlapping 2-second epochs for each participant and stage.

For the connectivity analysis, the six EEG channels were treated as sensor-level nodes, and the 15 pairwise channel combinations were treated as edges. The resulting 6 × 6 connectivity matrices therefore represent pairwise sensor-level coupling among the six PSG-derived EEG channels rather than source-resolved or whole-brain network representations. Phase-based functional connectivity was estimated using a multitaper Fourier transform (mtmfft), from which weighted phase lag index (wPLI) and imaginary coherence (iCOH) were calculated for each channel pair in the delta, theta, alpha, sigma, and beta bands. These metrics were selected because they quantify phase-lagged coupling while reducing the influence of volume conduction [12, 23].

Amplitude-based connectivity was quantified using amplitude envelope correlation (AEC). For each frequency band, signals were band-pass filtered and transformed with the Hilbert transform to derive amplitude envelopes, and envelope time series were then correlated between channel pairs across segments. AEC was included as a complementary amplitude-based measure sensitive to amplitude-coupling changes across anesthetic states [12, 24]. For each participant, stage, band, and channel pair, connectivity values were averaged across epochs to generate a single estimate for statistical testing.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between participants undergoing screening and therapeutic endoscopy using independent-samples t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Stage effects on EEG-derived outcomes, including spectral, spindle-like event, and functional connectivity measures, were assessed across the four anesthetic stages: Baseline, Induction, Maintenance, and Recovery. For spectral and spindle-like event variables, values were first averaged across the six scalp EEG channels for each participant and stage. Linear mixed-effects models were then fitted with stage as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept. These models were fitted using all available stage-specific observations and can accommodate an unbalanced repeated-measures structure. In secondary analyses, procedure type (screening vs. therapeutic) was added as an additional fixed effect. In an additional sensitivity analysis, age was added as a fixed-effect covariate to the secondary models that included procedure type. Estimated marginal means and pairwise stage comparisons were obtained, and P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method.

For functional connectivity measures (wPLI, iCOH, and AEC), stage comparisons were performed using permutation-based paired tests with 1,000 permutations for each metric, frequency band, and channel pair, followed by FDR correction. All tests were two-sided, and an FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spectral and spindle-like event analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 and Python 3.10, and connectivity statistics were computed in Python 3.10.

Because this was a prospective exploratory observational study conducted in a real-world endoscopy setting, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The final sample size reflected the number of consecutively enrolled eligible participants with analyzable EEG recordings during the study period.

Spindle density differed significantly across anesthetic stages (Supplementary Table S2). Mean spindle density was 0.70 ± 0.50 events/min at baseline, 0.69 ± 0.56 events/min during induction, 0.43 ± 0.35 events/min during maintenance, and 1.20 ± 0.74 events/min during recovery. FDR-corrected pairwise comparisons showed that spindle density was higher during recovery than during baseline, induction, and maintenance. Maintenance also showed lower spindle density than baseline and induction.

Raw spindle count also differed across stages, with the highest number of detected spindle-like events observed during recovery. However, because raw event count is influenced by the duration of analyzable EEG, spindle density was considered the more interpretable measure of event occurrence. In contrast, mean event duration and mean peak frequency did not differ significantly across stages (Supplementary Table S2). Mean peak frequency remained within the sigma/spindle frequency range across all stages, suggesting that the stage-related change was mainly reflected in event occurrence rather than a marked shift in peak frequency.

In sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for age and procedure type, the overall stage effects and the direction and statistical significance of all FDR-corrected pairwise stage comparisons were unchanged (Supplementary Table S4). Age was not significantly associated with any spectral or spindle-like event measure in these models (all P > 0.20).

Across the five stage contrasts, AEC showed the most widespread differences after FDR correction, whereas phase-based connectivity measures showed only limited edge-specific changes. The number of FDR-significant edges was consistently greater for AEC than for wPLI or iCOH. Comparisons between baseline and induction, maintenance, and recovery yielded 48, 56, and 54 FDR-significant AEC edges, respectively, whereas wPLI and iCOH showed only a small number of significant edges. Overall, these findings suggest that propofol anesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy was associated with stage-related modulation of sensor-level pairwise connectivity, particularly in amplitude-based coupling. Given the sensor-level nature of the PSG-derived EEG recordings, these results should be interpreted as pairwise connectivity features rather than spatially resolved whole-brain network changes.

Discussion

Spectral EEG markers of anesthetic state transitions during GI endoscopy

This prospective EEG study in participants undergoing GI endoscopy under propofol-based anesthesia showed systematic spectral changes across clinically defined anesthetic stages using EEG recorded from a portable PSG system. From baseline and induction to maintenance and recovery, relative delta power decreased, whereas theta, alpha, sigma, and beta relative power generally increased. SEF95 showed a similar stage-related increase during maintenance and recovery. These findings suggest a shift toward relatively faster EEG components during later anesthetic stages in this pragmatic endoscopy setting.

These observations partly align with prior descriptions of propofol anesthesia, in which altered slow–delta and alpha activity reflect changes in thalamocortical coupling [6, 19, 25]. However, GI endoscopy differs from controlled operating-room or volunteer paradigms because procedures are brief, stimulus-rich, and clinically dynamic. Under these conditions, relative delta power may decrease even when slow activity remains present, because faster oscillatory activity and residual electromyographic components may contribute proportionally more to total 0.5–30 Hz power. The increase in SEF95 is consistent with this shift toward faster spectral content.

The separate evaluation of alpha, sigma, and beta activity is relevant because sigma activity overlaps with the conventional spindle frequency range and may capture thalamocortical oscillatory processes not fully represented by broader beta-band summaries. Propofol-related alpha activity is known to show anterior redistribution, with frontal enhancement and posterior attenuation [6, 20, 25]. Sigma-range activity may also vary across recording locations; therefore, channel-averaged alpha and sigma values should not be interpreted as spatially homogeneous. Because the primary spectral analysis used channel-averaged values, the present study was not designed to quantify frontal–occipital anticorrelation or regional alpha–sigma relationships. Alpha and sigma activity may share thalamocortical mechanisms, but they should not be interpreted as interchangeable markers. Age-related variability in propofol-induced alpha and sigma activity should also be considered, as anesthetic EEG power, alpha dynamics, and spectral coherence may differ substantially across age groups [26, 27]. Therefore, these spectral findings should be interpreted as exploratory PSG-derived EEG markers that require validation in larger cohorts.

Recovery-related spindle-like events

A second key finding was the stage-dependent modulation of spindle-like events in the 11–16 Hz range. Spindle-like event density was highest during recovery, whereas mean duration and mean peak frequency did not differ significantly across stages. This pattern suggests that the main stage-related change was reflected in event occurrence rather than a marked shift in event morphology or peak frequency.

The higher density of spindle-like events during recovery may be associated with transient thalamocortical reorganization during emergence from propofol anesthesia. Prior studies have reported anesthetic spindle-like oscillations under general anesthetics and dexmedetomidine sedation, supporting the view that sigma-range transient events may reflect state-dependent thalamocortical dynamics [20, 21, 28]. At the same time, the recovery period is also vulnerable to arousal-related movement and residual muscle activity, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory spindle-like EEG features rather than definitive physiological sleep spindles.

Spindle-like activity may not be physiologically homogeneous across the 11–16 Hz range. Sleep studies have shown that slower and faster spindles can differ in frequency, topography, and inter-individual expression [29]. In the present study, events were analyzed within a unified 11–16 Hz range, and the six scalp EEG channels did not allow definitive characterization of slow versus fast spindle-like subtypes. Future studies with larger samples and higher-density EEG should examine whether anesthetic dose, opioid co-administration, and procedural stimulation differentially influence spindle-like activity during induction and recovery.

Sensor-level connectivity changes captured by portable PSG-derived EEG

Functional connectivity analyses showed metric-specific stage-related modulation of pairwise sensor-level coupling. Across 15 channel pairs, AEC showed the most widespread FDR-significant differences, whereas phase-based measures, including wPLI and iCOH, showed only limited edge-specific changes. This pattern suggests that amplitude-based coupling may be more sensitive than phase-based coupling to anesthetic stage transitions in this PSG-derived EEG dataset.

The difference between AEC and phase-based measures is methodologically relevant. wPLI and iCOH emphasize phase-lagged coupling and reduce the influence of volume conduction, but they may be more conservative and sensitive to phase consistency and data length. AEC captures covariation in oscillatory amplitude envelopes and may therefore be more sensitive to broad state-related modulation of rhythmic activity [12, 24]. The observed AEC changes are broadly consistent with prior evidence that anesthesia alters functional integration and cortical communication [23, 24, 30–32], including studies of propofol-induced transitions of consciousness [33, 34].

Importantly, these findings should be interpreted as sensor-level connectivity features rather than whole-brain network organization. The six scalp EEG channels can capture pairwise coupling patterns across frontal, central, and occipital derivations, but they cannot support source-resolved network inference. Future studies using high-density EEG, source localization, and multimodal imaging are needed to determine which circuits are most closely linked to behavioral responsiveness during procedural anesthesia.

Clinical relevance for EEG-informed monitoring in endoscopy suites

From a translational perspective, the present findings suggest that portable PSG-derived EEG may provide clinically interpretable markers of anesthetic state during propofol-based GI endoscopy. Spectral activity, SEF95, spindle-like event density, and sensor-level connectivity captured complementary aspects of state transitions in a setting where anesthetic titration is often guided primarily by vital signs and behavioral responsiveness.

Rather than replacing existing depth-of-anesthesia indices, this framework highlights multi-domain EEG features that may complement current monitoring approaches [35–37]. Higher relative power in faster frequency bands, higher SEF95, recovery-related spindle-like event density, and stage-related AEC modulation may help characterize periods of sedation, recovery, or atypical EEG responses that are not evident from hemodynamic measures alone. Because the signals were acquired with portable PSG and analyzed using six scalp EEG channels, this approach may be feasible for routine endoscopy workflows, although its utility for guiding propofol titration or predicting patient-centered outcomes requires prospective validation.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

This study prospectively recorded EEG data during routine GI endoscopy under propofol-based anesthesia using a portable PSG system and analyzed complementary EEG domains, including spectral activity, spindle-like events, and functional connectivity. Continuous peri-procedural recording was successfully implemented in the routine endoscopy setting, supporting the practical feasibility of portable PSG-derived six-channel EEG acquisition in this context. This configuration provided a pragmatic balance between clinical deployability and neurophysiological interpretability. However, formal implementation outcomes, including setup time, staff burden, patient acceptability, and cost, were not assessed.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was modest and derived from a single center, limiting generalizability. Second, the PSG-derived six-channel EEG setup provided limited spatial coverage; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as sensor-level EEG features rather than spatially resolved cortical or whole-brain network markers. Quantitative stage-specific proportions of EEG data removed during ICA and visual artifact rejection were not preserved in the original preprocessing logs and could not be reliably reconstructed retrospectively. Artifact correction relied on six-channel ICA supplemented by visual inspection; therefore, residual ocular or muscle-related contamination cannot be entirely excluded. Third, individual cumulative propofol doses were not available for the present analysis. Although anesthetic infusion rates were weight-based and protocolized according to procedure type, total exposure varied with procedure duration. Opioid regimens also differed between screening and therapeutic procedures. Procedure type was included in secondary analyses, but residual confounding by cumulative propofol exposure, opioid co-administration, and procedural stimulation cannot be excluded. Finally, although EOG, EMG, ECG, and lower-limb EMG were recorded, the present analyses focused on EEG signals.

Future studies should validate these findings in larger multicenter cohorts with standardized sedation assessments, multimodal PSG analysis, and higher-density EEG. Such studies should determine whether multi-domain EEG markers can predict clinically actionable outcomes, including inadequate sedation, intra-procedural movement, delayed emergence, or recovery quality.

Conclusions

In participants undergoing GI endoscopy under propofol-based anesthesia, portable PSG-derived six-channel EEG captured stage-related changes in spectral activity, spindle-like events, and sensor-level functional connectivity across clinically defined anesthetic stages. Maintenance and recovery were characterized by higher relative power in faster frequency bands and higher SEF95, whereas recovery showed increased spindle-like event density without a marked shift in peak frequency. Functional connectivity analyses indicated more widespread changes in amplitude-based coupling than in phase-based measures. These findings provide a clinically grounded neurophysiological description of anesthetic-state transitions and changes in responsiveness in a real-world endoscopy setting and support further evaluation of portable PSG-derived EEG for procedural anesthesia monitoring.
