Introduction

Following severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-COV-2) infection, viral replication in target cells results in direct cell death. This damage triggers both cell-mediated and humoral immune responses which will result in virus clearing and a relatively mild disease in most patients.1 However, severe SARS-COV-2 infection shows many similarities to sepsis induced acute respiratory distress syndrome, both in clinical presentation and cytokine profile.2,3 Severe SARS-COV-2 infection triggers overactivation of the immune response with further tissue damage independent of viral load.4 Thus, severe SARS-COV-2 infection pathophysiology can be divided into two main stages: The initial direct viral damage, and the later immune-mediated damage.

In addition to the classical risk factors for severe disease5 malignancies, especially hematological, and individuals receiving immunosuppressant therapy6 were also shown to be at increased risk for severe disease. Interestingly, the known risk factors are known to cause either immune suppression or a chronic inflammatory state. Thus, severe disease may develop either due to excessive viral damage resulting from an inability to mount a robust immune response or due to uncontrolled inflammatory damage in the later stages of SARS-COV-2 infection.

As most infected individuals recover from SARS-COV-2 after generating inhibitory antibodies, one of the first strategies tested to treat coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was the use of plasma from convalescent patients. However, multiple disappointing studies have shown no significant effect7 resulting in an initial recommendation against the routine use of convalescent plasma (CCP) for the treatment of COVID-19, regardless of severity.8 Another approach utilized during the initial stages of the COVID-19 pandemic was administering monoclonal preparations. However, as new variants appeared, these antibodies were no longer effective. To date, only VYD222/Pemivibart is authorized for use as pre-exposure prophylaxis for immunocompromised adults and adolescents, although still under the Emergency Use Authorization.

The two stages of COVID-19 drove the development of treatments that can be divided into two main groups: Antivirals which are most effective in preventing severe disease, and anti-inflammatory drugs used in later more severe stages. Anti-viral drugs (e.g., molnupiravir, nirmatrelvir, ritonavir, remdesivir) are recommended for use in the initial stages of infection in order to prevent severe disease. This is due to the reduced efficacy demonstrated when administered at later inflammatory stages.9, 10, 11, 12 For individuals progressing to prolonged severe disease anti-inflammatory drug treatment (e.g., dexamethason, tocolizumab, baracetinib, vilobelimab) results in reduced mortality.13 Finally, an effective vaccine was developed and globally deployed, resulting in an impressive reduction in mortality.

However, the emergence of new variants, specifically Omicron BA1/2 with its 23 mutations in the spike protein, resulted in reduced efficacy of the first monovalent vaccine and monoclonal antibodies. Efficient treatment is especially important for immunosuppressed individuals, as vaccines are known to be less effective in preventing COVID-19 in these patients.14 Furthermore, after infection, failure to mount a sufficient defense may prolong symptoms and result in a more severe disease.15, 16, 17, 18, 19 This is supported by the overrepresentation of immunosuppressed patients in hospital and ICU admissions even after vaccination.20 For this reason, current recommendations include prophylactic post-exposure treatments for such individuals. Surprisingly, most anti-viral clinical trials did not address immunosuppressed patients. Interestingly, several reports from the last few years suggested that anti-viral therapy21, 22, 23, 24, 25 at later stages of infection, and CCP16,26, 27, 28 may be efficient for immunocompromised patients.21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Indeed, current consensus statements of several scientific societies recommend the use of CCP in combination with antiviral drugs in immunosuppressed individuals.29, 30, 31 The Israeli Society of Infectious Diseases recommends that for persistent COVID-19 in immunocompromised patients, treatment with a combination of antibody-based therapy and two antiviral drugs be administered for 5–10 days, despite the low-quality evidence.32

In the current case series, we report the use of antiviral therapy and “Omicron convalescent plasma” for the treatment of immunocompromised patients demonstrating prolonged COVID-19 symptoms and viral shedding.

Materials and methods

Data colllection

A retrospective review of computerized patient data including the timing of individual treatment was collected from January 2022 to March 2022 at the Hadassah Medical Center Jerusalem. This study was conducted in compliance with all relevant laws and institutional guidelines and has been approved by the institutional ethical review board authorization including waiver of informed consent for chart review (Authorization: 0184-23-HMO, Date 14 June 2023). Privacy rights of human subjects have been observed during the conduction of this study. Inclusion criteria were any immunosuppressed patients admitted to the hospital with confirmed SARS-COV-2 infection by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) following a previous SARS-COV-2 PCR confirmed result in the last 2 months (Supplementary Table S1), who also showed persistent symptoms such as fever and hypoxia from the initial test. The average SARS-COV-2 World Health Organization (WHO) severity score of the patients studied was 5.125 (range 4–7). Except for the single mortality, all patients were alive at 28 days. Record search post-discharge identified the latest known follow-up in our institution, with a median of 45.8 months, (range 1.08–49.70, not including the single in-hospital mortality case). Laboratory data at admittance are the maximum value for the first 24 hours after official admittance and values at discharge are the last laboratory value available during hospitalization. SARS-COV-2 infection was confirmed at admittance by PCR analysis of oral and nasal swabs and performed in the Hadassah Medical Center Jerusalem.

COVID-19 immunoglobulin quantification

When available, patient Immunoglobulin G levels were determined by quantifying Anti-S antigen using Abbott ARCHITECT analyzer.

Convalescent plasma

Convalescent plasma was centrally collected and processed by Magen David Adom, Israel’s National Emergency Pre-Hospital Medical and Blood Services Organization using their previously published protocol.33 Infusions were shipped for specific patients only at the request of Hadassah Medical Center's blood bank and were administered as soon as they arrived.

Statistical Analysis

For paired binary changes (e.g., fever vs. no-fever, oxygen use, and others) The non-parametric McNemar’s test was used. In the evaluation of white blood cell (WBC) counts we used Chi-square goodness-of-fit test for categorical distributions with more than two outcome groups, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for their continuous evaluation per patient, that was also used for CRP levels that had a non-normal distribution. p < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed on EXCEL (Microsoft, Redmond, USA).

Results

We describe a series of 23 immunosuppressed patients admitted for non-resolving COVID-19 between October 2021 and February 2022 at a single tertiary center. Sixty-one percent were female, median age of 61 (range 44–81) and 91% (21 of 23) vaccination rate with 2.6 average doses (range 1–4 doses). Immunosuppression resulted from hematological conditions in 74% of patients, 22% from solid organ transplant and 4% from other conditions. All patients were confirmed for persistent COVID-19 by an additional positive PCR test, with median WHO score of 5 (range 4–7 days) (Supplementary Table S1). The mean time between the first positive PCR test and the second PCR test confirming consistent COVID-19 was 24 days (range 6–45 days). Despite the initial treatment, patients continued to express COVID-19 symptoms requiring re-hospitalization, with 83% presenting with fever and 69% requiring oxygen supplementation. Of those requiring oxygen support, 44% used nasal cannulas, 25% required face masks, 25% used high-flow nasal cannulas, and 6% needed invasive ventilation. During hospitalization, 74% received anti-inflammatory treatment, and 62% of patients received anti-viral medications (23% Remdesivir, 35% Paxlovid and 4% Mulnoperivir) with 46% of those who had already received anti-viral therapy at initial diagnosis before the studied hospitalization. All patients received COVID-19 CCP therapy with an average time from first diagnosis to receiving CCP of 22 days. In addition to COVID-19-specific treatment, 69% received antibiotic treatment for suspected superimposed bacterial infection. Following these regimens, the median hospitalization time was 7 days (range 3–37 days) with 1 in hospital death. Upon admission almost all patients had normal or below normal white blood cell count with an average of 5.2 × 103 cells/mL. For the analysis of changes in WBC counts two approaches can be used. First, changes were categorized as increased, decreased, or unchanged (≥ 20% threshold). Of 22 patients, 14 (63.6%) showed increased count, 7 (31.8%) remained stable, and 1 (4.5%) showed decreased count, a change found to be significant (χ² = 11.6, df = 2, p = 0.003). When WBC counts were analyzed as continuous paired data, we confirmed a significant increase in WBC counts from admission to discharge (W = 29.5, p = 0.001). Together, these results confirm a consistent and statistically significant increase in WBC count. When comparing CRP levels, of 19 patients with complete data, 16 showed reductions, 2 showed increases, and 1 minimal change (W = 30.0, p = 0.007).

Discussion

After incubation, COVID-19 infection exhibits a distinct bimodal course, characterized by an initial phase of viral-driven tissue damage followed by a later phase of inflammatory over-activation.34 For this reason, usual care for high-risk patients consists of anti-viral therapy in the first days after infection and anti-inflammatory treatment for patients continuing to deteriorate.9,35,36 This approach, seems insufficient for an important group of immunosuppressed patients who are unable to mount adequate immune responses. These patients are at higher risk of developing a severe disease due to a prolonged viral phase. Interestingly, most large intervention studies did not address this population, leaving a gap in our knowledge of how best to treat prolonged or persistent COVID-19. Here we describe a series of 23 immunosuppressed individuals presenting with confirmed prolonged COVID-19. As most patients presented with hypoxia, the majority (74%) were treated with dexamethasone as indicated. However, patients were also given antiviral therapy in spite of being out of the recommended treatment window. Anti-viral treatment consisted of small molecules (paxlovid, remdesivir, mulnoperivir) for 62% of patients and Omicron CCP for all patients. Following treatment, 22 patients (96%) were discharged, with only 1 in-hospital mortality (4%). Combination therapy led to a statistically significant resolution of fever and improvement in hypoxia even for patients requiring intense oxygen support (69.5% on admittance vs. 48% on discharge). Fever and CRP also showed statistically significant improvement in most patients. Interestingly, increasing WBC counts were identified as a good indicator for improvement, showing a significant increase from a relatively low initial level. Importantly, leukocyte levels did not increase above 12 × 103 cells/ml in most cases. This effect may be due to the low initial WBC counts in patients with hematological neoplasms or on immunosuppressant therapy.

Our series suggests that for immunosuppressed individuals, the viral stage of COVID-19 may be prolonged and that treatment with a combination of antiviral therapies is warranted even if outside the recommended therapeutic window. In addition, CCP is an important tool to resolve infections in these patients.

Interestingly, there is still debate concerning the effectiveness of CCP treatment. A meta-analysis did not find any advantage of administering CCP to immunocompetent ambulatory or hospitalized patients.37 Further, subgroup analysis did not find significant effectiveness with immunosuppression.37 These findings are reflected in the current WHO guidelines that recommend CCP only for experimental use.38 However, an in-depth analysis of early CCP studies conducted by Focosi et al.7 showed that in several studies, patients had insufficient antibody titers and randomization for treatment was performed relatively late. It should be noted that overall antibody titer quantity was shown to be inferior to viral neutralization titers and that injected amount and patient weight need to be considered. Furthermore, studies testing CCP for prophylaxis purposes enrolled patients with relatively mild disease resulting in apparent reduced efficacy. Indeed, although in the “Randomized, Embedded, Multifactorial Adaptive Platform Trial for Community-Acquired Pneumonia” (REMAP-CAP)39 trial, CCP was found to be a futile intervention in overall COVID-19 patients, a non-significant benefit was detected in the small cohort of immunosuppressed participants. Interestingly, meta-analysis incorporating additional randomized controlled trials did find an overall reduction in all-cause hospitalization when CCP was given to outpatients within 5 days of symptoms onset.40 Further support for CCP effectiveness came from a more recent meta-analysis on hospitalized patients, which also found a significant reduction in mortality among hospitalized patients receiving CCP within 7 days.41 As for immunosuppressed patients, several studies have shown the potential benefit of CCP treatment in both unvaccinated27,42,43 and vaccinated patients.22,44 More recent data showed improved hospitalization rates22 and even reduced mortality16,26,45 after CCP administration. Most recently, the “Early High-Titer Convalescent Plasma in Clinically Vulnerable Individuals with Mild COVID-19” trial45 a randomized, open-label, multicenter study enrolling vaccinated immunodeficient patients with early (up to 7 days) mild COVID-19, confirmed the efficacy of CCP in preventing hospitalization or death. Taken together, these results lead several scientific societies to recommend the use of CCP in combination with antiviral drugs in immunosuppressed individuals.28,30,31 Furthermore, recently, the Food and Drug Administration revoked the Emergency Use Authorization for CCP and approved a biologics license application for two commercial CCP products.40

Our study expands current knowledge by demonstrating the potential efficacy of combined antiviral therapy with CCP use even in later stages of COVID-19 infection in immunodeficient patients. Importantly, in our study all patients were confirmed to have the Omicron variant, which was also the most prevalent variant in the plasma given, another potential explanation for the positive response to treatment.

Our study has some limitations, in addition to being retrospective, as a case series we lack matched controls. However, it may be suggested that as 18 of the 23 cases had an initial round of unsuccessful treatment, they can serve as internal controls. Considering that the main difference between “treatment rounds” is the administration of CCP, further strengthens its importance as a treatment option. Another limitation of our study is the fact that patients received additional treatments (anti-inflammatory and antibiotic,74% and 70% respectively) making it more difficult to isolate specifically the anti-viral treatment effect. Finally, we could not obtain specific data on CCP antibody concentration and collection time. However, the fact that all plasma was centrally collected and processed at a single blood bank during the study period provides some confidence of homogeneity. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that the total number of units and volume of CCP had no significant effect on survival.16

Conclusion

The emergence of new variants requires significant time to reengineer vaccines and produce new monoclonal antibody treatments. For this reason, anti-viral medication and variant specific convalescent plasma may still be needed as a rapid first response treatment. This is especially true for immunosuppressed individuals with reduced responses to vaccination, and difficulties in viral clearance. In these individuals, damage resulting from direct viral replication may be the main pathological process. Our series supports the possible effectiveness of administering antiviral therapies, and specifically convalescent plasma in immunosuppressed patients with persistent COVID-19 even long after the recommended therapeutic window. Further studies are needed in order to confirm the effectiveness and safety of such strategies in immunosuppressed patients with prolonged or persistent infection.
