Specialized cortical ER architecture modulates Ca2+ signaling dynamics
Authors: Raphael J Courjaret, Lloyd Lee, Hana Mohamed, Fang Yu, Lama Assaf, Melanie Fisher, David I Yule, Mark Terasaki, James Sneyd, Khaled Machaca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCa2+ signaling is ubiquitous and supports a multitude of cellular events. Specificity in Ca2+ signaling is encoded in its spatial and temporal dynamics. Ca2+ tunneling regulates the dynamics of Ca2+ signals downstream of store operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE). Here, we describe a novel cortical ER (cER) architectural feature, that we call the cER-basket, which underpins Ca2+ tunneling. We discovered the cER-basket through a combination of 3D reconstructions of the cortical ER at the ultrastructural level coupled to mathematical modeling of Ca2+ tunneling. Using insights from the modeling to inform experimental approaches and vice versa, we show that the detailed structural features of the cER-basket support Ca2+ tunneling. Therefore, we report a specific cortical ER structure, the cER-basket, which modulates cellular Ca2+ dynamics.Keywords: ER structure, calcium signaling, store-operated calcium entry, calcium tunneling, mathematical modeling

Introduction

Ca2+ signaling is ubiquitous and mediates a large variety of cellular events downstream of receptor activation. Given its large capacity to encode information, it is ideally suited to translate diverse cellular responses in the same cell. Signaling requires a rise in cytoplasmic Ca2+ due to either Ca2+ release from intracellular Ca2+ stores (typically the endoplasmic reticulum [ER]), or to Ca2+ influx from the extracellular space. At rest cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentrations are at ∼100 nM whereas the concentration in the Ca2+ sources (ER or extracellular fluid) ranges in the hundreds of micromolar to low millimolar.1,2 Ca2+ release from ER stores is mediated by two classes of ER resident Ca2+ channels, ryanodine receptors, or inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) receptors (IP3R). Multiple classes of channels mediate Ca2+ influx at the plasma membrane (PM) with store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) being activated by depletion of ER Ca2+ stores.3,4 Therefore, SOCE functionally links Ca2+ release and influx and allows for refilling of ER stores after a round of signaling.

The various Ca2+ sources combined with cytosolic and organelle Ca2+ buffering result in localized Ca2+ dynamics within different regions of the cell that activate effectors specifically depending on the amplitude of the Ca2+ signal, its spatial distribution, frequency, and duration.2,5,6 Therefore, Ca2+ signals represent a code that specifically activates a subset of Ca2+-dependent effectors based on the cell’s need. It follows that the detailed dynamics of Ca2+ signals are critical for cell physiology and signaling.

SOCE as one of the primary Ca2+ influx pathways in cells is a critical contributor to Ca2+ signaling dynamics and is mediated by the STIM and Orai gene families.4 STIMs are resident transmembrane ER proteins with a luminal domain that senses ER Ca2+ content, and changes conformation (activates) following a drop in ER Ca2+. Orais are Ca2+-selective channels that are diffusely distributed on the PM and are gated by direct physical coupling to STIMs. The interaction between STIM1 and Orai1 occurs at ER-PM contact sites (ERPMCS), which are close appositions between the ER and PM membranes (10–20 nm apart), without fusion. The proximity of the ER and PM at ERPMCS allows the long STIM1 C-terminal cytoplasmic domain to bridge the gap between the two membranes to interact with Orai1. Activated STIM1 translocates to ERPMCS where it clusters and recruits Orai1 by diffusional trapping, thus allowing for Ca2+ influx.3,7

Hence ERPMCS are required for SOCE. Indeed, their loss during mitosis is coupled with loss of SOCE in this phase of the cell cycle.8 Interestingly however, ERPMCS occupy an estimated 1%–4% of the PM area with the diameter of a contact site ranging between 100 and 300 nm.9,10,11 Furthermore, functional studies argue for a small number of Orai1 channels in each ERPMCS (1–5 channels).11,12 Hence Ca2+ influx through SOCE is localized to few dispersed point source entry sites (ERPMCS); and given the high cytoplasmic Ca2+ buffering capacity, is unlikely to diffuse far from these SOCE microdomains.13 Yet functionally SOCE activates effectors, such as Ca2+-activate PM channels (Cl− and K+), that localize away from the SOCE microdomain.14,15,16,17 This SOCE-dependent signaling to distal effectors is mediated by Ca2+ tunneling, a mechanism that expands the spatial and functional scope of SOCE. During Ca2+ tunneling, Ca2+ that diffuses out of the SOCE microdomain during the influx phase is taken up into the ER lumen by SERCA pumps within the ER that surrounds ERPMCS.14 Ca2+ then diffuses within the ER lumen and is released by IP3Rs that localize on average 1 μm away from ERPMCS in HeLa cells.14 Therefore, Ca2+ tunneling spatially expands SOCE signaling by not limiting it to effectors that localize within ERPMCS.15,18,19 Ca2+ tunneling is mostly cortical and is not efficient at activating effectors that localize deep within the cell.16,17,20 Given its ability to modulate cortical Ca2+ dynamics, Ca2+ tunneling represents an important contributor to cellular Ca2+ dynamics and thus signaling specificity.

We have recently shown that Ca2+ tunneling is important for fluid secretion in cultured cells, in mouse salivary acinar cells, and in vivo in mice sweat glands.14 This raises the interesting possibility that tunneling is the preferred signaling mode downstream of SOCE for distal effectors particularly in secretory epithelia. To better understand tunneling we employ a combination of complementary mathematical modeling and experimental approaches. Because tunneling is primarily cortical, we used serial electron microscopy (EM) and FIB-SEM to define the 3D architecture of the cortical ER in relation to the PM and coupled the findings to mathematical models to explore potential structural tunneling determinants. These studies reveal the need for a novel specialized cortical ER domain that we dub the “cortical ER basket (cER-basket),” to mediate effective Ca2+ tunneling in the cell cortex. They further show that the architecture of the cortical ER is an important determinant of Ca2+ signaling dynamics.

Results

Ultrastructure of the cortical ER around ERPMCS

Our functional and structural studies over the years have proposed a functional model for Ca2+ tunneling that is summarized in Figure 1A. Ca2+ flooding out of the SOCE microdomain is taken up by the population of SERCA pumps that localize just below ERPMCS, as SERCA is excluded from the ERPMCS proper,14 and transported within the ER lumen to distal effectors where it is released through IP3Rs. To be able to temporally and functionally isolate and study Ca2+ tunneling we devised the protocol outlined in Figure 1B that separates the store depletion and tunneling phases. There are certain aspects of Ca2+ tunneling that are difficult to ascertain experimentally, including the dynamics of Ca2+ within the SOCE microdomain, within different domains of the ER (at the junction, cortex, and deep), and at IP3Rs release sites. To obtain insights into these dynamics, we sought to create a mathematical model of Ca2+ tunneling.

Our first attempt to model tunneling is depicted in Figures 1C–1E. Based on the functional studies from Courjaret et al.14 in HeLa cells, we started with the architecture shown in Figure 1C. We applied our typical Ca2+ tunneling protocol to the model (Figure 1B), where we depleted Ca2+ stores in Ca2+-free solution, followed by adding Ca2+, to induce SOCE, and histamine to open IP3Rs to allow for Ca2+ tunneling. Under these conditions, based on our tunneling model and previous functional studies,16 we predict a high Ca2+ signal at cortical cytosolic position 2 (C2) as compared to deep within the cell at C3 (Figure 1C). However, the mathematical model showed a similar increase in cytosolic Ca2+ at these two locations during the tunneling phase (Figure 1D), with the associated expected changes in ER Ca2+ (Figure 1E). Furthermore, despite multiple attempts at altering different parameters within the model, we did not observe significant Ca2+ tunneling, as the levels of Ca2+ following His addition at C2 and C3 were comparable (Figure 1D), and the deep ER (E3) had a higher resting Ca2+ level than the cortical ER (E2) during the tunneling phase (Figure 1E). As expected, after removal of His—to close IP3Rs and eliminate ER Ca2+ release—the ER refills globally (Figure 1E).

The lack of tunneling in the model prompted us to revisit the cortical ER structure more carefully as we had previously systematically mapped the localization of the different effectors involved in tunneling.14 We were, therefore, interested in obtaining a 3D map at the ultrastructural level of the cortical ER surrounding ERPMCS. For these initial studies we chose a simple cell line, Jurkats T cells, with a less complex cortical ER and because it is a well-studied SOCE model.11

We produced serial EM sections (65 nm thick) across the entirety of three interphase Jurkat cells and traced cortical ER and PM using Reconstruct software to produce 3D renditions around ERPMCS. An example of ER and PM tracings (Figure 2A) as well as several 3D reconstructions of the cortical ER around ERPMCS are shown (Figure 2B). We consistently observe a subdomain of the cortical ER that is linked to ERPMCS through a narrow neck and runs parallel to the PM (Figure 2B, arrows). Because often this domain is linked to the PM at both ends by ERPMCS, we refer to it as a “cER-basket” (Figure 2B). Morphometric analyses of the cER-basket show an average depth relative to the PM of 171 ± 18 nm, a neck length of 191 ± 18 nm, and average length that runs parallel to the PM of 545 ± 47 nm (Figure 2C).

We were then interested in testing whether this cER-basket is present in other cell types. As serial EM has the limitation of a thick section (50–65 nm) thus restraining resolution in the z dimension, we resorted to FIB-SEM stacks from HeLa (cervix), U2-OS (bone), and HuH7 (liver) cells obtained with a slice thickness of 8, 5, and 8 nm, respectively, thus greatly enhancing resolution in the z dimension (EMPIAR database). Reconstructions from the three cell types reveal a pronounced cER-basket with a short neck linked to the ERPMCS, and a long ER that runs close to and parallel to the PM (Figures 2D–2F). The cER-basket structure in the three cells from different tissues appear structurally similar with varying length and different branching patterns linking back to ERPMCS and deep ER (Figures 2D–2F). Importantly, the conduits to the deep ER were often not close to the neck-ERPMCS region, unlike our initial modeling attempt where the conduit to the deep ER was continuous with the junctional ER (Figure 1C).

We further performed FIB-SEM analyses on our HeLa cells (see Figure 3) and observed a similar cER-basket. Morphometric analyses of the cER-basket in our HeLa cells show an average depth relative to the PM of 136.7 ± 9.57 nm, a neck length of 253.3 ± 19.64 nm, and average length that runs parallel to the PM of 629 ± 49.49 nm. These values are in similar ranges to Jurkats as discussed previously but larger as may be expected for the larger HeLa cells. Collectively these analyses define the cER-Basket as an ER sheet that runs parallel to the PM at a depth <200 nm and is connected directly to an ERPMCS at either one end of both ends. This structure demarcate a cortical domain delimited by the PM on one side the cER-basket on the other with potentially specialized Ca2+ dynamics. Of note, the structure of the cER-basket may vary from one cell type to another, depending on the physiological state of the cell, stages of the cell cycle, or even due to sample preparation, so more comprehensive analyses of the cER-basket in different cell types and conditions are warranted in the future.

Modeling Ca2+ tunneling

With the insights gained from the 3D reconstructions, we altered the architecture of our model to link the junctional ER at ERPMCS through a short neck to an extended cER-basket, which then links to the deep ER at its end to better represent the architecture observed in cells (Figure 4A); particularly HeLa cells as our Ca2+ dynamics data comes from this cell type. The ER in the new architecture is divided into 3 regions: (1) a junctional ER (jER) region (E1) at ERPMCS, which encompasses the ER that is in close proximity to the PM at the contact site (within 15–20 nm); (2) the cER-basket (E2) that links to the jER through a neck region; and (3) the deep ER (E3). We also followed cytosolic Ca2+ in 3 regions: (1) The SOCE microdomain delimited by the ER and PM within the ERPMCS (region C1); (2) The cell cortex, which is the area between the PM and cER-basket (C2); and (3) the deep cytosol (C3) (Figure 4A).

With this architecture the model readily exhibits Ca2+ tunneling with significantly higher Ca2+ levels in the cell cortex (region C2) as compared to deep within the cell (C3) (Figures 4A, 4B, and 4D, and Video S1). Within the SOCE microdomain (C1), Ca2+ levels are intermediate as they are sustained by the balance between SOCE influx and SERCA uptake in the neck region as Ca2+ leaks out of the SOCE microdomain (Figures 4B and 4D). The model also offers insights into the dynamics within the jER, in which lumenal Ca2+ levels are significantly higher than other regions of the ER during the influx phase, as the jER is fueled by Ca2+ entry through SOCE (Figures 4C and 4D). ER Ca2+ in the cER-basket (E2) is low during the tunneling phase due to release from cortical IP3Rs (Figures 4B–4D). It is interesting that the model predicts a slightly higher Ca2+ concentration in the cell cortex (C2) as compared to the ERPMCS (C1), arguing that tunneling is efficient at enriching Ca2+ at the IP3R release sites to activate distal effectors. Screenshots from the time course from the model are shown in Figure 4D with the inset highlighting the two distinct and separate high Ca2+ domains during the tunneling phase at ERPMCS and at IP3R release sites in the cell cortex. Thus, tunneling supports spatially distinct Ca2+ signaling microdomains within the cell cortex to activate different classes of effectors.

However, these findings from the model depend on the Ca2+ fluxes through SOCE (α1), SERCA (VSERCA), and IP3R (kf). So, to test the robustness of the model we changed these different fluxes and the Ca2+ diffusion rate within the ER lumen (DER) and tested the effect on tunneling. A discussion of the findings is presented in supplemental data. Briefly, Increasing DER enhances tunneling as it allows for faster transfer of Ca2+ from the junctional ER to the IP3R release site (Figure S1). Increasing flux through SOCE also improves tunneling as expected but at very low α1 levels tunneling is abrogated (Figure S2), arguing that at SOCE fluxes diffusion of Ca2+ out of the ERPMCS prevents SERCA from tunneling Ca2+ into the ER lumen. Increasing VSERCA also improves tunneling at the expense of Ca2+ levels in the SOCE microdomain (Figure S3). Finally, flux through the IP3R tracked with tunneling levels (Figure S4). These changes support our model as a good approximation for physiological tunneling.

The findings from the models argue that the extended cER-Basket is a critical structural feature to support Ca2+ tunneling. To further evaluate this idea, we limited the extent of the cER by joining the cER-Basket to the deep ER closer to the jER (Figure 4E). This relatively moderate alteration of the cortical ER architecture greatly reduced Ca2+ tunneling, as illustrated by the comparable Ca2+ levels at the cortex (C2) and deep (C3) (Figure 4F). This confirms the key role for proper cortical ER architecture, in particular the cER-basket to support Ca2+ tunneling.

Modulating the actin cytoskeleton

To validate the findings from the modeling experimentally, we sought to alter the architecture of the cortical ER and assess the effect on tunneling, SOCE, and Ca2+ release. It is important to test the effects on SOCE and IP3-dependent Ca2+ release in parallel as they both fuel tunneling. We initially targeted the actin cytoskeleton as it has been involved in regulating cell architecture and ER distribution,21 hoping that modulating the actin network would alter cER architecture. We used three drugs that interfere with actin: cytochalasin D (CytD) and latrunculin A (LatA) bind to actin monomers and interfere with actin polymerization; and jasplakinolide (JAS) is a cyclic peptide that induces actin polymerization and stabilizes actin filaments.

CytD treatment of HeLa cells resulted in remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton with less filamentous actin fibers and the formation of actin clumps (Figure 5A). This remodeling of the actin network led to a small reduction in thapsigargin (Tg) mediated SOCE and Ca2+ tunneling and did not affect histamine-induced Ca2+ release (Figures 5B–5D). LatA treatment decreased central actin fibers but not so much cortical actin (Figure 5E). In contrast to CytD, LatA was not associated with the appearance of actin clumps (Figure 5E). LatA decreased histamine-induce Ca2+ release, and led to a small reduction in SOCE, but did not affect Ca2+ tunneling (Figures 5F–5H). JAS treatment increases actin fibers throughout the cell (Figure 5I) and leads to small increases in both Ca2+ release and tunneling but did not affect SOCE (Figures 5J–5L). We did not directly test the effects of these drugs on the cortical ER structure. Collectively, these results show that significant modulations of the actin cytoskeleton have small to no effects on Ca2+ tunneling.

Brefeldin A enhances the cortical ER and Ca2+ tunneling

In search of interventions that affect the cortical ER, we tested brefeldin A (BFA), a fungal metabolite that inhibits ER to Golgi transport. BFA has pleiotropic effects on multiple targets involved in trafficking as it affects multiple targets, including the Golgi-associated coat proteins (coatomer), the guanine-nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) that activate (ARF), and other Golgi.22 Given the diverse effects of BFA and ER and Golgi vesicular transport, we reasoned that it may also affect cER and cER-basket architecture.

Confocal imaging of the cortical ER shows an increase in ER that tracks linearly under the PM following BFA treatment (Figure 3A), arguing that BFA increased cortical ER. Line scan profiles of the cell cortex crossing the PM and cER abutting the PM from super-resolution airyscan images, show a shift of the cER toward the PM in response to BFA (Figure 3B). To further validate this finding, we assessed the proximity of the cER to the PM in cells expressing Orai1 to paint the PM (Figure 3C). Intensity profiles of z-y scans across the cell allow us to quantify the intensity of the ER at the peak of Orai1, which marks the PM plane (Figure 3C). This analysis shows an enrichment of the cER in the PM plane (Figure 3C), thus supporting the line scan analyses (Figure 3B). Collectively the confocal super-resolution imaging supports the conclusion that the cER is enriched close to the PM in response to BFA.

BFA is a drug that has been used for quite some time to study vesicular trafficking and organelle dynamics. So, to support the confocal imaging data, we searched in the literature for reports documenting the effects of BFA at the ultrastructural level. In multiple cell lines, including liver,23 lymphocytes,24 rod,25 and plant cells,22 BFA treatment is associated with an expansion of the cER both in terms of length but more prominently in the size of the cER lumen. In the HUH7 liver cells in particular, where the cER-Basket is visible with its associated ERPMCS, there is a dramatic expansion of the cER lumen and length is clear.23 These data support our confocal super-resolution imaging and show that the cER-basket expands in response to BFA treatment.

However, the reports in the literature may have been under different experimental conditions, so we wanted to test the effects of BFA on the cortical ER in our HeLa cells. We, therefore, performed FIB-SEM analysis before and after BFA treatment (Figure 3D). 3D reconstructions show that BFA dramatically increased the density of the cER-basket and the cortical ER tubules in general as well as the length of the cER-basket (Figure 3D).

We then tested the effects of BFA on Tg-mediated SOCE (Figure 6A) and the much smaller and more physiological SOCE following CPA washout to maintain SERCA active and allow for store refilling (Figure 6B). BFA did not affect the Tg-mediated SOCE levels (Figure 6A), and only slightly increased CPA-mediated SOCE (Figure 6B). These results argue against a dramatic effect on STIM/Orai in terms of mediating SOCE in response to store depletion in the presence of BFA. Furthermore, BFA treatment under the tunneling protocol slightly increases His-induced Ca2+ release (Figures 6C and 6D). This finding coupled to the lack of changes in the Tg-releasable Ca2+ pool from the ER (Figure 6A) argue that BFA is not significantly affecting Ca2+ store content. In contrast to the mild effects on release and SOCE, BFA treatment dramatically enhanced Ca2+ tunneling by 4.2 + 0.2-folds (Figures 6C and 6E). This increase in tunneling cannot be readily explained by the minor increase in SOCE and is most likely associated with the remodeling of cER, in particular the cER-Basket in response to BFA as shown in Figure 3D.

One concern from the BFA treatment is that in addition to altering the cER-basket architecture it could also affect the distances and relationships between SOCE, SERCA, and IP3R, the critical modulators of tunneling. To test whether this is the case we imaged the distribution of SERCA and IP3R1 relative to STIM1 following store depletion in HeLa cells (Figure S5). BFA treatment had no effect on the relative distance between STIM1 and IP3R1 following store depletion (Figures S5A and S5B). Interestingly though, BFA treatment enriched the linear tracks of cortical licensed IP3R1 (Figure S5A), which presumably localize to the cER-basket, thus supporting the expansion of the cER-basket observed in EM studies. Similarly, BFA did not lead to observable changes in the distribution of SERCA relative to STIM1 following store depletion (Figure S5C). These data further support the conclusion that the dramatic increase in Ca2+ tunneling in response to BFA is due to expansion of the cER-basket. However, given the lack of other interventions to directly modulate the structure of the cER-basket and the pleiotropic effects of BFA it is difficult to conclusively establish causation, despite the support from mathematical modeling.

Discussion

Ca2+ tunneling is emerging as an important modulator of Ca2+ dynamics under physiological conditions particularly in secretory epithelia where it modulates the activity of apical Ca2+-activated channels.14 Tunneling is important to expand the signaling scope of the SOCE microdomain by efficiently activating distal effectors. It also temporally extends the duration of Ca2+ signaling using extracellular Ca2+ as a source. In addition, its cortical nature is well suited to support vectorial fluid transport across epithelia.

Experimental dissection of Ca2+ tunneling presents unique challenges due to its reliance on both Ca2+ release from stores and SOCE. Furthermore, the different Ca2+ microdomains involved in Ca2+ tunneling, including the SOCE microdomain at ERPMCS, the jER, and the cortical ER, represent cellular areas in which it is difficult to image Ca2+ dynamics during tunneling due to their small size and rapid changes in Ca2+ levels. Because of these challenges, we were interested in developing mathematical models that replicate Ca2+ tunneling to provide insights into these dynamics and the structural requirements for tunneling. Herein, we develop such a model, which replicates the dynamics of Ca2+ tunneling.

Our initial mathematical model did not replicate the tunneling Ca2+ dynamics observed experimentally as it did not show enhanced Ca2+ signals in the cell cortex during tunneling (Figure 1). This prompted us to investigate the ER structure in the cell cortex at the ultrastructural level to determine whether it affects tunneling. We used both serial EM and FIB-SEM data from various cell lines to reconstruct the cortical ER region. This analysis invariably, in different cell types, revealed a novel cER structure that runs parallel and close to the PM and is linked to ERPMCS on the PM side and to the deep ER on the nuclear side of the cell (Figures 2 and 6F). We refer to this structure as the cER-basket as it physically delimits a cortical cytoplasmic area with the other side confined by the PM. This structure is ideally suited for Ca2+ tunneling as it creates a specialized cortical cytoplasmic domain fenced by the PM and cER-basket to support spatially and temporally distinct Ca2+ signaling dynamics (Figure 6F). The role of the cER-basket in Ca2+ tunneling is supported by both mathematical models and experimentally. In our mathematical models the specific structural features of the cER-basket outlined previously were essential for the model to support Ca2+ tunneling (Figures 1 and 4). In support of this conclusion, altering the cER architecture in the model by decreasing the length of the cER-basket abrogates tunneling (Figure 4).

To support the modeling studies experimentally, we evaluated different approaches to alter cER architecture. We started by modulating the actin cytoskeleton, but this had little effect on tunneling (Figure 5). In contrast, treating cells with BFA, a drug that blocks ER to Golgi transport, dramatically increases Ca2+ tunneling (Figure 3). This increase in tunneling is associated with a significant expansion of the cER-basket both in terms of its length and lumenal diameter (Figure 3). Although the correlation between the expansion of the cER-basket and increased tunneling following BFA treatment does not directly prove a causal relationship, it is consistent with the findings from the modeling studies in support of an important role for the cER-basket in Ca2+ tunneling.

The presence of the cER-basket in four cell types (liver, bone, cervix, and T cells)—two tested in this study (HeLa and Jurkats) and two from the literature—argue that this is a ubiquitous ER structural feature in mammalian cells. This suggests that Ca2+ tunneling not only relies on the precise localization of the different Ca2+ signaling modulators following store depletion,14 but also requires a specialized cortical ER architecture in the form of the cER-basket. The dependence of Ca2+ tunneling on the cER-basket argues for an important role for it at the physiological level where Ca2+ tunneling has been shown to be critical for fluid secretion in salivary gland cells, sweat gland cells, and in vivo for sweat production in mice.26 Hence the ER structure at the subcellular level within the cell cortex could have significant implications for fluid secretion at the organ level. In addition, Ca2+ tunneling has been shown to be functional in various primary and immortalized cell types, including frog oocytes, HeLa cells, sweat gland cells, and salivary gland cells.14,16,17

Collectively, our findings describe a novel cortical ER architecture, the cER-basket, and argue for it playing an important role in modulating Ca2+ tunneling and thus cellular Ca2+ signaling (Figure 6F).

Limitations of the study

One limitation of the study is the specificity of the brefeldin A (BFA) in disrupting the cER-basket as BFA has broad effects on ER and Golgi structural integrity. Another limitation is the two-dimensional mathematical model used and how well it represents the three-dimensional cellular environment.

Ultrastructure analyses

FIB-SEM datasets for HeLa, Huh7, and U2-OS cells were obtained from the EMPIAR database (RRID:SCR_019237).29 The voxel size for HeLa dataset (EMPIAR accession number 10311) is 5x5x8 nm, for the HuH7 dataset (EMPIAR 11849) 4x4x8 nm, and for the U2-OS dataset (EMPIAR 11746) 2x2x5 nm. We further performed FIB-SEM analyses on our HeLa cells. Cells were fixed in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, EMS), 2.5% glutaraldehyde (GA, EMS) in 0.1M Sodium Cacodylate (Sigma-Aldrich) buffer, pH7.4) at RT for 10 minutes at ½ dilution then for 1 hour at the final concentration. Fixative was removed by washing 5 x 3 minutes in 0.1M cacodylate buffer and subsequently samples were incubated in 1% osmium (OsO4, EMS), 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1M cacodylate buffer for 40 minutes at RT. After washing in ddH2O for 5 x 3 minutes, this was followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C in 1:3 UAR in H2O (Uranyl Acetate Replacement, EMS). The next day, UAR was removed by washing in ddH2O for 5 x 3 minutes. The samples were dehydrated using solutions of increasing EtOH concentration (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 2x 100%), for 3 minutes each. Subsequent infiltration with resin (Embed-812, EMS) was done by first incubating in 50% resin in ethanol for 2 hours, followed by at least 3 changes of fresh 100% resin (including 1 overnight incubation). Next, samples were embedded in fresh resin and cured in the oven at 65°C for 72 hours.

For FIB-SEM imaging, embedded cells were mounted on aluminum SEM stubs (diameter 12 mm) and samples were coated with ∼20nm of Platinum (Quorum Q150T ES). FIB-SEM imaging was performed using a Zeiss Crossbeam 540 system with Atlas5 software. The Focused Ion Beam (FIB) was set to remove 6nm sections by propelling Gallium ions at the surface. Imaging was done at 1.5kV using an ESB (back-scattered electron) detector. The voxel size is 6x6x6 nm. FIB-SEM sample preparation and imaging were performed at the VIB Bioimaging core (RRID:SCR_011760, Ghent, Belgium).

For serial EM sectioning the methods were as previously described.31,32 Briefly, 65 nm thick sections were collected on kapton tape using an ATUM (automated tape collecting microtome), then imaged by back scatter in a scanning electron microscope. Serial electron microscopy (EM) images were aligned and reconstructed using Reconstruct software. Consecutive sections were manually aligned using fiducial landmarks placed with the Stamp Tool. Briefly, adjacent sections were opened simultaneously, and corresponding anatomical landmarks were marked in identical sequential order on both sections to ensure consistent point numbering. A minimum of four corresponding points per section pair were defined using the Stamp Tool. The section designated for transformation was then aligned using the “Align Section” function with rigid or linear transformation parameters. Alignment accuracy was verified by toggling between adjacent sections, and additional landmarks were added iteratively when necessary to optimize registration. Once satisfactory alignment was achieved, the corresponding landmarks were removed. This procedure was repeated sequentially throughout the image stack.

For the 3D reconstructions from serial EM and FIB-SEM images the PM and ER were manually traced in subsections of 40 slices each and the 3D models generated in the Reconstruct Software30 using the Boissonnat Surface option for 3D representation. For segmentation, structures of interest were defined within the Series Options panel by assigning unique names and colors. Boundaries were manually delineated on each section using drawing tools either Draw Freehand or Draw Closed Point by Point. Segmentation was performed sequentially across aligned sections to enable three-dimensional reconstruction of the structures of interest.

Intracellular Ca2+ imaging

Ca2+ imaging was performed using a PTI Easy Ratio Pro system (software version 1.6.1.0.101; Horiba Scientific) composed of a DeltaRAMX monochromator and a CoolSnapHQ2 camera attached to an Olympus IX71 inverted microscope fitted with a 20x/0.75 lens. For Ca2+ imaging the cells were loaded for 30 min with 2 μM Fura2-AM in a Ca2+-containing media (composition below) at room temperature and washed three times in Ca2+-free media prior to the experiments. Cells were excited at 340 nm and 380 nm for 100 msec at a 0.1 Hz frame rate and the ratio of the fluorescence intensity at 340/380 measured.

For SOCE/Tg experiments the cells were not perfused. Tg (1 μM) was applied for 20 minutes (enough for the Ca2+ baseline to recover) in a Ca2+-free media. The extracellular Ca2+ concentration was then raised to 2 mM through direct injection of Ca2+ into the dish. For SOCE/CPA experiments and tunneling the cells were perfused for 10 minutes with 10 μM CPA in a Ca2+ free solution and then washed for 20 minutes to remove the CPA and restore SERCA function. To record SOCE/CPA Ca2+ (2 mM) alone was perfused into the dish. For tunneling experiments an initial first application of 100 μM histamine in Ca2+-free media was performed to evaluate the responsiveness of the cells followed by CPA application. Tunneling was induced by the simultaneous application of 2 mM Ca2+ and 100 μM histamine for 30 seconds before perfusion with 2 mM Ca2+ only. A second application (not shown) of histamine was performed to evaluate the correct refilling of the ER stores after tunneling.

Drug treatment

Control cells were treated with vehicle (DMSO or methanol) or drugs for 30 min at room temperature. For Ca2+ imaging Fura loading was done simultaneously. Cytochalasin D (10 μM, PHZ1063, Thermo Fisher), Latrunculin (1 μM, 12370, Thermo Fisher) and Jasplakinolide (0.5 μM, 420127, Sigma) were dissolved in DMSO, Brefeldin A (10 μM, B7450, Thermo Fisher) was dissolved in methanol.

Mathematical model

The model is solved using the finite element method (written in Matlab), and is based on the following two assumptions: (1) within both the cytosol and the ER, Ca2+ diffuses with linear, isotropic diffusion, and reacts with buffers, which are assumed to be fast, linear and present in excess; (2) there are four fluxes in and out of the cytosol, and these are described by boundary conditions at the appropriate places, as shown by the colored lines in Figures 1C, 4A, and 4F. The fluxes through the IP3 receptor, SERCA, SOCE and PM pump are, respectively, denoted by JIPR, JSERCA, JSOCE, and JPM. Expressions for JIPR, JSERCA, and JPM are standard expressions [Sneyd, Han et al., PNAS, 114(7):1456-1461, 2017].
