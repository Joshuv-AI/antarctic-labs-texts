Invasive ambrosia beetles have zinc-enriched mandibles: support for the metal-prioritization hypothesis and insights into the mechanisms of metal deposition
Authors: Kendall O. Myers, Jessi A. Raubenolt, Cynthia Perkovich, Christopher Ranger, Matthew S. Lehnert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hardness of the structural tools of insects can be increased through the integration of transition metals into the cuticle. However, questions remain regarding metal predominancies for insect cuticle (i.e., metal-prioritization hypothesis) and whether metals accumulate in the cuticle across life stages. To address these questions, we characterized structures in an insect-fungal-plant host system, including mandibles of invasive ambrosia beetles, their fungal diet, and the wood the fungus digests. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy revealed low concentrations of transition metals in the wood, fungus, and neonate mandibles. High concentrations of zinc, however, were found in the distal regions of mandibles of third-instar larvae and adults, confirming diet as the source of transition metals and a lack of maternal inheritance. Confocal microscopy of mandibles showed sclerotization patterns that coincided with zinc distributions and nanoindentation confirmed increased hardness and elastic modulus in zinc-enriched locations. Patterns of zinc concentrations on the mandible surface suggest that metals do not accumulate in the cuticle through life stages. Instead, zinc-enriched cuticle is lost at each molt and replaced by zinc acquired from the previous instar’s diet, thus revealing a physiological mechanism by which specific transition metals are retained and accumulate within each instar for future cuticle deposition.Supplementary Information The online version contains supplementary material available at10.1038/s41598-026-53349-y.

Introduction

The cuticle of insects is arguably nature’s most successful biocomposite materials. Capable of hosting an array of adaptations, its mechanical properties can be augmented through several different mechanisms, including the extent of hydration1,2, presence of waxes3,4, elastomeric proteins5–7, and degree of sclerotization8–10. Additionally, several insect lineages modify their cuticle through the deposition of transition metals, which are often localized to specific regions of structures that serve as “tools”11–13.

Insect cuticular tools, such as mandibles and ovipositors, might have significantly high concentrations of zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), and iron (Fe) deposited in regions prone to experiencing high friction forces and subsequent wear14–16. Transition metal-augmented cuticle creates sacrificial and regenerative metal-ligand coordinated bonds in the cuticle-protein matrix that increase hardness and influence elastic modulus17,18. The metal-prioritization hypothesis (MPH), which was developed through studies of the elemental composition of structures among mole crickets (Gryllotalpidae)19, states that Zn is possibly the dominant transition metal due to its chemical characteristics and bond-forming abilities with histidine complexes in the insect cuticle. Although metal-enhanced cuticle has been reported in an array of insect lineages (e.g., Hymenoptera, Hemiptera, Diptera, Coleoptera, and Orthoptera)14,16,20–23, many questions remain, particularly if other lineages display prioritized rankings for specific transition metals in metal-diverse diets, in accordance with the MPH, and how the elemental composition of structures might change through the different life stages, especially if the functional role of structures changes. The purpose of this study is to address these questions by examining metal concentrations in the mandibles of different life stages of invasive ambrosia beetles, their plant habitat, and their fungal diet. Our preliminary studies showed the presence of transition metals on the external cuticle of the mandibles of ambrosia beetles, thus validating our use of ambrosia beetles for this study.

Ambrosia beetles (approximately 3,500 species worldwide) are members of the weevil family Curculionidae, Coleoptera (220,000+ described species in Curculionidae)24,25. They are specialized woodborers and among the most successful invasive insects globally26,27. Adult beetles use their mandibles to tunnel into healthy, stressed, dying, or dead hosts24,28,29. Entrances into the circular tunnels, i.e., galleries, can be associated with the tree’s defensive sap production and with extruded compressed sawdust “noodles” (Fig. 1a–c)30. Galleries are created within their host tree to rear offspring and cultivate nutritional fungal mutualists that serve as the sole food source for larvae and adults24,31 (Fig. 1d–f). Infestations by ambrosia beetles and the establishment of their associated fungi can result in branch dieback and tree death, with some invasive species transmitting highly destructive fungal pathogens32,33.

In the Xyleborini tribe, two species of ambrosia beetles, Xylosandrus germanus and X. crassiusculus, are native to Southeast Asia and have become established in North America and several other countries28,30. After leaving their natal gallery, adult females disperse to find a new host plant, start wood scraping and tunneling, and introduce their fungal mutualist24. Oviposition by the foundress typically occurs after the fungal mutualist becomes established within the host gallery34,35. The initial expansion of the gallery is mainly due to the adult female foundress, but the larvae further enhance it while the foundress physically blocks the tunnel entrance35,36. Males, being flightless and offspring of the foundress, are not known to contribute extensively to gallery expansion35,36. Differences in the functional roles of the mandibles between life stages and sexes of X. crassiusculus and X. germanus might parallelly change in the material properties of the cuticle. Therefore, ambrosia beetles are an ideal model organism for studying transition metal concentrations within a single structure across different developmental stages.

Here, we performed a comprehensive study to characterize the cuticle surface of larval and adult mandibles of two invasive ambrosia beetle species in North America, their fungal diet, and the wood the fungus feeds on, thus exploring metal concentrations through different trophic layers, a novel perspective for this field. We then use these results to address:whether metal concentrations in the mandibles differ between sexes and among life stages. We hypothesize that females will have the highest concentrations of transition metals due to their role in gallery formation;whether maternal inheritance plays a role in metal acquisition. Evidence indicates that transition metals are acquired from diet37–39; therefore, we hypothesize that the neonate larvae lack metals in the cuticle of their mandibles; andwhether transition metals possibly accumulate in the mandibles through different life stages, and if Zn is the main transition metal. We hypothesize that metals do not accumulate in structures over time and therefore require new metal deposition after each molt, because metals in high concentrations are toxic to insects40–42. We also hypothesize that Zn will be prioritized over other transition metals due to their bonding affinities with histidine complexes in the cuticle matrix, as suggested by the MPH19.

Results

Patterns in mandible sclerotization

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) showed the mandible cuticle was primarily red, green, or brown (a mixing of colors) (Fig. 2a). Regions with red autofluorescence were interpreted as having increased levels of sclerotization, those with green autofluorescence as having moderate sclerotization, and regions with blue autofluorescence were interpreted as being flexible, possibly indicating the presence of the elastomeric protein resilin6,7. Third-instar larvae and adult males and females of both species showed similar patterns of autofluorescence, where the distal regions had average color values as variations of red (with corresponding high a* values of the L*a*b* color value system), and the mid-regions were recorded as brown (color values shown in electronic supplementary materials, Table S1). We did not find significant differences in the percent area of deep red (regions with a* values > 45) between the medial and lateral sides of the mandibles within species, sex, or life stage; however, the percent area differed significantly between sexes and among life stages. Third-instar larvae and adult females had higher proportions of the mandibles represented by red than adult males (significant for X. crassiusculus, F(2,16) = 11.85; p = 0.0010), and was overall highest (i.e., largest region of extensive sclerotization) in females of both species (Fig. 2b, c).

Mandible morphology

We found that lengths of female mandibles were significantly longer than males and the larval instars for both species (X. crassiusculus, F(2,16) = 365.6047; p < 0.0001, X. germanus, F(3,19) = 36.2012; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3a–c). There were interspecific differences too, where adult females of X. crassiusculus had longer mandibles than X. germanus (F(1,11) = 21.5143; p = 0.0009), but the latter had mandibles with a larger incisor region (F(1,11) = 10.2502; p = 0.009) (Fig. 3a–c). Males of X. germanus also had a significantly larger incisor region (F(1,9) = 37.9824; p = 0.0003). Third-instar larvae had fewer differences between species, with only the tooth proportion being significantly larger in X. germanus (F(1,19) = 12.3880; p = 0.0078), and there were no significant differences for neonates between species.

The overall shape and proportions of the mandibles allocated to a tooth and incisor region strongly differed between larvae and adults. Adult mandibles of both species were more robust and had larger incisor regions, with mandibles of X. crassiusculus adults averaging approximately 39% and adults of X. germanus averaging approximately 53%. The neonates and third-instar larvae of both species, however, have incisor regions that averaged only approximately 30% and were significantly shorter than the adults (F(3,21) = 18.5867; p < 0.0001) (Table S2, Fig. 3a–c). Neonates and third-instar larvae have a large portion of their mandibles dedicated to the tooth region, with X. crassiusculus showing approximately 32% and X. germanus having approximately 40%, respectively (Fig. 3a–c).

Zinc deposition and cuticle hardness

Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) revealed that the total elemental composition of the external cuticle layers of mandibles from neonates, third-instar larvae, and adult males and females of both species contain 16 elements, including the reactive nonmetals, carbon (C) oxygen (O), and nitrogen (N), the alkali metals, halogens, and non-metals, chlorine (Cl), potassium (K), sodium (Na), sulfur (S), and phosphorus (P), the alkaline earth metals, calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg), and the transition, post-transition metal and metalloids, aluminum (Al), silicone (Si), Fe, Cu, Mn, and Zn (electronic supplementary materials, Table S3).

Patterns revealed the overall elemental composition of the external cuticle layers differed among life stages and sex in multivariate space for both species. A PERMANOVA on Hellinger-transformed proportions confirmed that species and life stage together accounted for ~ 5.7% of the variance, with the remaining variation attributable to micro-regional heterogeneity across the mandible (Pseudo-F = 10.63, R² = 0.057, p = 0.001) (electronic supplementary materials, Table S4). Spatial patterns were the dominant source of variation among all elements. Regional heterogeneity across mandible locations, often modulated by side (lateral vs. medial) for both abundant elements (e.g., C, O) and several trace elements (e.g., Cl, Na, Zn), indicates micro-regional enrichment at specific positions (e.g., apical vs. molar regions), and in many cases, side-specific contrasts. Species-level differences were observed, but these differences were largely conditional and emerged through specific interaction terms such as species × location or species × side interactions for several elements, including C, O, Cl, Na, Si, and Zn. This suggests that the concentration of each element on the mandibular surface varies between X. crassiusculus and X. germanus, despite their similar overall elemental compositions (electronic supplementary materials, Table S4).

Among the transition metals, Zn showed the strongest and most consistent effects (electronic supplementary materials, Table S4). Linear mixed-effects models revealed differences by location (χ²(6) = 279.86, p < 0.0001) and species × location interaction, with Zn enrichment concentrated on the apical and distal regions, particularly on the medial side of X. crassiusculus (Fig. 4a). Cu and Fe were present in lower concentrations but exhibited notable spatial and taxonomic trends. For example, X. germanus had higher mean Cu concentrations than X. crassiusculus (p < 0.05), and Fe was locally enriched along the lateral distal edge in X. crassiusculus (χ²(6) = 20.59, p < 0.002). Mn displayed complex but less pronounced patterns with multiple significant interactions (electronic supplementary materials, Table S4, Fig. 4b–e).

Constrained analysis of principal coordinates (CAP) analyses highlighted these material gradients within the mandibles. A CAP model constrained by species × life stage and including side + location as additive terms explained 25.7% of the constrained variance (Adj. R² = 0.257; electronic supplementary materials, Table S5), with the first two axes (CAP1, CAP2) accounting for ~ 92% of that variation. After accounting for side + location, a smaller but significant species × life stage effect remained (Adj. R² = 0.054; electronic supplementary materials, Table S6), indicating that differences among taxa and developmental stages persist even after controlling for micro-regional structure.

Elemental maps revealed a sharp distal-to-proximal gradient of Zn, with intense enrichment at the apical tooth and distal edge and minimal Zn near the proximal and molar regions. Quantitative line scans confirmed these gradients (Fig. 3d–g). In both species, mandibles of neonates contained negligible Zn, confirming the absence of early-life deposition through maternal inheritance of Zn concentrations. Zn concentrations in the mandibles of third-instar larvae and adult males and females, however, increased steeply toward the distal tip. Adult females of X. crassiusculus exhibited the longest Zn-enriched portion of the mandible, significantly exceeding those of adult males and third-instar larvae (F(2, 16) = 62.65; p < 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed in X. germanus (F(2, 14) = 9.52; p = 0.0033), with adult females having more extensive Zn-enriched region than adult males, and third-instar larvae having the shortest Zn-enriched regions. No interspecific differences were detected in the overall proportion of Zn-enriched cuticle length.

Significant differences (p < 0.05) were found in H and EM values between locations, between sexes within a species, and between species (Fig. 5c, d). The H values at the distal location were significantly higher than proximal regions in adult females of X. crassiusculus (385.24 Mpa and 165.70 Mpa, respectively, F(1,19) = 37.97, p < 0.0001) and X. germanus (251.92 Mpa and 143.53 Mpa, respectively, F(1,18) = 30.2361, p < 0.0001). The EM values showed a similar pattern for X. crassiusculus (9.55 Gpa and 3.63 Gpa, respectively, F(1,19) = 63.51, p < 0.001) and X. germanus (4.23 Gpa and 3.28 Gpa, respectively, F(1,18) = 7.6130, p = 0.0134) (Fig. 5). H values did not significantly differ between locations for adult males of either species, where males of X. crassiusculus had an average H value of 194.52 and 181.47 at the tip and base of the mandibles, respectively. Males of X. germanus had similar hardness values of 145.36 at the tip and 172.03 at the mandible base. EM values, however, were significantly higher at the distal region than the proximal region for adult males of X. crassiusculus (3.05 Gpa and 1.49 Gpa, respectively, F(1,19) = 14.0816, p = 0.0015), but higher at the base for adult males of X. germanus (1.89 Gpa and 3.23 Gpa, respectively, F(1,23) = 8.7486, p = 0.0073).

Adult males and females of both species displayed differences in their mechanical properties. For X. germanus, the distal location of the female mandibles was significantly harder (F(1,22) = 22.91, p < 0.0001) with higher EM (F(1,22) = 56.63, p < 0.001) compared to the same locations on male mandibles; however, this pattern was not observed for the proximal location (Fig. 5b–d). A similar pattern was observed for X. crassiusculus, where females had harder mandibles (F(1,20) = 44.34, p < 0.0001) with higher EM (F(1,20) = 104.83, p < 0.0001). The base of female mandibles of X. crassiusculus also had significantly higher EM values (F(1,18) = 15.16, p = 0.0012) compared to males.

Interspecific comparisons revealed that the distal location on the mandibles was significantly harder for both sexes of X. crassiusculus than those of X. germanus (females, F(1,18) = 16.56, p = 0.0008; males, F(1,24) = 6.3826, p = 0.0189). In addition, males of X. crassiusculus had higher EM at the distal location than X. germanus males (F(1,24) = 11.66, p = 0.0024); however, males of X. germanus had higher EM in the proximal location (F(1,18) = 9.18, p = 0.0075).

Transition metal concentrations in an insect-fungus-plant host system

SEM-EDX revealed patterns in changes in transition metal concentrations as they move through this insect-fungus-host system (Fig. 6a–g). Zn, Mn, Cu, and Fe were present in low concentrations in the wood of A. saccharum (electronic supplementary materials, Table S7). Similarly low concentrations were recorded from the Ambrosiella spp. fungus, except for higher concentrations of Mn. Transition metals were nearly absent from neonate mandibles of both ambrosia beetle species and similar to third-instar larvae, except for Zn, which was significantly higher in third-instar larvae for both species. Adult males and females of both species had high concentrations of Zn, whereas the other transition metals were present at low concentrations or absent. Comparisons of transition metal concentrations among the apical tooth region of mandibles (where high Zn concentrations were recorded) within each species and sex, along with the elemental concentration of the wood and fungus, revealed patterns where Cu and Fe did not significantly fluctuate in concentrations as they moved through the system for either species. In contrast, there were significant differences in Mn concentrations with higher concentrations in the fungus than the wood and mandibles of any life stage (adult females, F(4,22) = 4.91, p = 0.0075; adult males, F(4,20) = 4.81, p = 0.0097). Zn concentrations showed the most significant patterns in changes in concentrations among the wood, fungus, and beetles (Fig. 6d-g). When examining only adult males of X. crassiusculus along with the fungus, wood, and larvae of both instars, the highest concentrations of Zn were the third-instar larvae, though these were not significantly higher than the adult stage (Fig. 6d). In contrast, comparisons of Zn concentrations indicated no significant differences among the fungus, wood, and beetle life stages for females of X. crassiusculus, despite exhibiting higher concentrations in the adult life stages (Fig. 6e). Zn concentrations were significantly higher for adult females and males of X. germanus (adult females, F(4,22) = 8.3753, p = 0.0005; adult males, F(4,22) = 5.5610, p = 0.0043), but males did not significantly differ from third-instar larvae (Fig. 6d–g). These patterns, though showing higher concentrations of Zn in the adult stages, do not reflect the general accumulation of Zn across the life stages and indicate Zn as the main transition metal for cuticle deposition.

Discussion

Comparisons of metal concentrations between insects and the environment have primarily been studied in the context of heavy metal pollution43–45, not in the employment of transitional metals for structural adaptations. Here, we found that the wood of A. saccharum contained low concentrations of Zn, Cu, Mn, and Fe, the main transition metals used to augment insect cuticle11,14,46. Transition metals are acquired by plants from the soil47 and are essential for plant physiology, including serving as cofactors for metalloenzymes, impacting photosynthesis efficiency, and controlling pathogens48–50. Fungi also require low concentrations of transition metals for homeostasis, enzyme cofactors, and pathogen defense51–53. The Ambrosiella fungi secrete digestive enzymes into the host substrate and absorb nutrients into their hyphae, thus providing an avenue for the transport of transition metals from plant to fungus. The Ambrosiella species studied here had similarly low concentrations of transition metals as the wood, except for higher concentrations of Mn, which plays an important role in fungal physiology by affecting virulence, reproduction, and cell wall formation53–56. The ingestion of transition metals by insects plays important physiological roles beyond cuticle deposition, including support for protection against pathogens, involvement in respiration and energy metabolic pathways, DNA synthesis, oxidation reactions, and serve as important cofactors for many enzymes57. However, it is important to note that metal availability is strongly dependent on habitat and location, which could result in extensive variation among different populations of the same species.

Here, we addressed an important and fundamental question in this field: does maternal inheritance play a role in transition metal acquisition for cuticle deposition in neonate larvae? We found that all studied transition metals were nearly absent from neonate mandibles. The low metal concentrations in neonate mandibles are understandable if we consider two assumptions: (1) diet is the sole source of transition metals and (2) that the available metals for deposition are only acquired from diet by the previous instar. Transition metals acquired through maternal inheritance seems unlikely, as indicated by our results that neonates do not possess metal-augmented mandibles. Neonates of ambrosia beetles probably do not require a transition-metal-enhanced cuticle, as the fungus is their only food source and is a soft material and they play no role in gallery expansion.

Based on the patterns observed here, we propose a mechanism for metal deposition by which transition metals are first acquired from the diet during neonate feeding and are retained elsewhere in the body (possibly the Malpighian tubules58,59) until post-ecdysis, when they are integrated into the mandible cuticle. Previous studies found that transition metals are absent in the cuticle immediately after molting and that the deposition of Zn into the cuticle only occurs approximately 90 h or more post-ecdysis when the new cuticle is still hardening22. The general lack of significant differences between third-instar larvae (metals acquired during the second instar) and adult males (metals acquired during third-instar larvae) indicates that metals do not accumulate in the cuticle across life stages, but instead are lost with the exuviae, and new cuticle uses metals acquired from the diet of the previous instar (Fig. 7). It is worth noting, however, that the larvae were not sexed in this study and were assumed not to have sex-specific mechanisms, which is worth exploring in future studies. We suggest the presence of a mechanism for increased transition metal acquisition and deposition abilities in female mandibles that differ from males and larvae. It is unknown at this time how the females acquire increased levels of transition metals, but it could relate to the presence and higher abundance of transport proteins, e.g., dZip1 and dZip257,60, and/or augmented transport mechanisms such as higher concentrations of Zn-nanochannels61,62.

Zn was clearly the main transition metal for cuticle deposition in the studied species, which supports the metal-prioritization hypothesis (MPH)19. The MPH suggests that transition metals differ in their ability to integrate into cuticle and can therefore be placed on a ranking scale, Zn>Cu>Mn>Fe, which is influenced by the availability of metals in the environment, the importance of the structure to fitness, the chemistry of the transition metal ions, and the chemistry of the cuticular matrix. It is worth noting that other structures in ambrosia beetles might contain transition metals other than Zn to augment the hardness of their structures, but this was not studied here, as we only investigated the mandibles and are unaware of other hardened structures on ambrosia beetles that are prone to increased wear and abrasion and might contain transition metals, such as their ovipositors, which do not play a role in gallery formation. However, if future observations of ambrosia beetles reveal other structures that might experience increased wear (possibly the tarsi), we predict a transition metal other than Zn will be prevalent, as suggested by the MPH.

The collective examination of mandible traits, including morphology, extent of sclerotization, elemental analysis, and hardness and elastic modulus measurements provide a comprehensive overview of how these patterns relate to the differing functionalities of the mandibles among life stages and between sexes. The neonates and third-instar larvae had similar mandible morphology with large proportions allocated to a tooth region, which likely aid in feeding by gripping the hyphae of Ambrosiella fungi. The correspondingly small portion allocated to an incisor region indicates its possible role in gallery formation, which is lacking in neonates. Although the third-instar larvae have a small incisor region, their mandibles are further augmented with increased levels of sclerotization (similar level as adult females) and Zn deposition, which would increase mandible hardness and facilitate gallery formation.

In comparison, the adult beetles have mandibles of similar morphology, even between species, but they differ from larval mandibles in being more robust and having a larger incisor region. We suspect the incisor regions contribute significantly to chewing wood and gallery formation. The females are the primary creators of galleries, and their increased levels of sclerotization coupled with high concentrations of Zn results in a mandible cuticle capable of cutting through hard wood. Although the adult mandible morphology was similar between sexes, the lower levels of sclerotization and Zn concentrations of males likely render their mandibles as less capable of cutting wood. Our interpretations of these patterns are further supported by studies with nanoindentation for hardness and elastic modulus measurements. Only adult mandibles were studied for this component, but it was found that the distal regions of female mandibles were significantly harder than the males, indicating the adaptive value of increased Zn concentrations and sclerotization for gallery formation. In addition, although only two regions of the mandibles were measured for hardness and elastic modulus, we suspect the greater area of Zn coverage on the mandible cuticle of females, determined with the line scan measurements, suggests a greater region of extensive hardness than what is found on males or larvae. Although males and third-instar larvae have similar levels of Zn, the greater extent of sclerotization of the larval mandibles likely provides increased hardness to their mandibles, making a better tool for cutting through wood. We are not aware of studies that have measured the hardness or elastic modulus of Ambrosiella fungi, but A. saccharum have reported values of a modulus of rupture (the point of breaking the wood) at approximately 110 Mpa, indicating that the adult females have mandibles much harder than the wood, facilitating gallery formation, which would be more difficult for the softer mandibles of males. These findings provide evidence that the mandible characters of larvae and adults match well with function.

Despite the patterns revealed here, insights into this system would greatly benefit if future studies consider looking at the elemental composition of each cuticle layer in the mandibles, as our method for elemental analysis (EDX) only characterizes the exposed surfaces of structures and is limited in penetration depth. Other transition metals could be in other cuticle layers, thus creating strong biocomposite materials, as observed in the stingers of some scorpions63. In addition, we suggest using caution in interpreting the results from CLSM color quantification because each mandible was independently optimized with varying imaging settings, i.e., they were not standardized. We suggest that future studies consider this limitation if a similar color analysis is performed for quantifying colors in confocal imaging.

Conclusion

This study is the first to provide insight into metal concentration changes throughout different trophic levels in an insect-fungal-plant host system. The physiological mechanisms and complex determining factors for metal deposition in insect cuticle, particularly how metals are selectively transported to localized regions of specific structures, is not well understood, and should be addressed by future studies. However, our results shed light on this mechanism by showing that transition metals do not accumulate in structures through different life stages, but are instead likely lost with exuviae, and then replaced with new metals acquired and retained from the previous instar’s diet; however, future studies should explore this further. The proposed mechanism sets up an interesting scenario where the elemental composition of the cuticle is dependent on the metal concentrations in the diet, which could fluctuate among instars depending on what the insect consumed. However, as observed here, even low concentrations of transition metals in the environment are not completely paralleled in the cuticle composition; therefore, a mechanism for the accumulation of specific metals within each instar is likely present, which would explain the higher concentrations of metals in the insects compared to the fungus and wood.

Materials and methods

Rearing protocol for the studied ambrosia beetle species, fungus, and wood

Two species of ambrosia beetles were selected for our study, Xylosandrus crassiusculus (Motschulsky) and X. germanus (Blandford). Ambrosiella roeperi Harr. & McNew64 and A. grosmanniae Mayers, McNew & Harr65 are the nutritional fungal mutualists of X. crassiusculus and X. germanus, respectively. Live adult females were collected in 2024 from a deciduous woodlot in Wayne Co., OH, USA (40° 45′ 44″ N; 81° 51′ 17″ W) using ethanol-baited traps following Ranger et al.66. The woodlot was dominated by Quercus rubra L., Q. velutina Lamark, and Prunus serotina Ehrhart. The field-collected specimens were returned to the laboratory and stored in Petri dishes containing moistened filter paper at 3 °C for 2 to 3 days before infesting bolts of sugar maple for rearing purposes.

The field-collected beetles were then used to infest stem sections (i.e., bolts) (7.5 cm × 2.5 cm; L × D) of sugar maple, Acer saccharum L., freshly cut from 8-year-old leafed-out trees growing in 30.3 L containers in a standard nursery mix of 18 cubic feet pine bark, 1.9 cubic feet sphagnum peat moss, and 1000 g lime. In April 2024, each container received 500 g of granular micronutrient fertilizer (Micromax; ICL Specialty Fertilizers, Dublin, OH) containing 6.00% Ca, 3.00% Mg, 12.00% S, 0.10% B, 1.00% Cu, 17.00% Fe, 2.50% Mn, 0.05% Mo, and 1.00% Zn derived from CaMg(CO3)2, FeSO4·1H2O, MnSO4, ZnSO4, CuSO4·5H2O, Na2B4O7, and Na2MoO4·2H2O. The bolts that were pre-soaked for 24 h in an aqueous solution of 5% ethanol + 5% acetic acid (v/v). After soaking, the bolts were allowed to air-dry in a chemical fume hood for 1 h, and then the cut ends were sealed with parafilm to minimize desiccation of the wood. An individual adult female ambrosia beetle was then confined to a soaked bolt using a no-choice chamber as described by Ranger et al.30. The bolts were stored in an incubator at 25 °C to permit the development of selected life stages: neonates, third-instar larvae, and adult males and females. Galleries within the bolts were dissected using hand pruners to obtain the selected life stages, the fungus hyphae, and wood, which were then stored in a −80 ℃ freezer.

CLSM and color quantification to assess sclerotization levels

Both mandibles of third-instar larvae (n = 5), adult males (n = 5), and adult females (n = 6) for each species were removed using dissecting scissors and insect pins under a stereoscope (Olympus SZ51, Breinigsville, USA) and placed on a glass slide in a droplet of molecular-grade glycerol (≥ 99.0%, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Oakwood, USA). Due to limited availability of neonates and the fragility of their mandibles, we prioritized using neonate mandibles for elemental analysis rather than confocal microscopy and color analysis. The mandibles were then positioned with insect pins so that one mandible had the lateral side exposed and the other, from the same individual, had the medial side exposed. A high-resolution coverslip (12 mm diameter, thickness no. 1.5 H, Neuvitro Corporation, Vancouver, USA) was placed on top of the droplet and imaged within 24 h of preparation.

The autofluorescence of each mandible was observed at 20× magnification with an Olympus FluoView 3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Olympus, Breinigsville, USA) using previously published protocols7 to reveal possible differences in the extent of sclerotization. Four stable solid-state lasers were used with excitation wavelengths of 405, 488, 561 and 640 nm to detect the emitted autofluorescence at wavelengths of 415–485 nm (blue), 500–600 nm (green), 560–660 nm (red), and 644–744 nm (red), respectively. CLSM imaging was optimized independently for each mandible by increasing the laser strength and voltage so that each laser was at its maximum intensity while remaining below the threshold for oversaturation (laser strength, 3–15%; voltage 450–550 V). The number of z-slices per image was chosen according to the software recommendations, and a maximum-intensity projection was applied. The images were later opened in ImageJ and saved as a PNG image.

The patterns and extent of mandible autofluorescence were quantified using LensEye® color analysis software (ECS, Gainesville, FL USA) and previously published protocols6,67,68. Color quantification consisted of opening the CLSM images in Adobe® Photoshop and erasing the background to white. A pdf file of a red color standard (X-Rite ColorChecker Classic array) with known L* (Lightness, scale 0 to 100), a* (green to red, − 120 to 120), and b* (blue to yellow, − 120 to 120) color values of 37.78, 57.61, and 28.82, respectively, had a section highlighted with the “circle” shape tool in Adobe Photoshop that was copied and pasted into the upper right-hand corner of the image of the mandible. The image size was set to a width of 700 pixels and saved as a BMP.

The BMP images were opened in the LensEye® software, calibrated using the values from the red color standard, and analyzed to determine the average color of specific regions and the percent area of the mandible represented by extensive sclerotization (red region). The average color of the apical mandible region and the mid-region was quantified by drawing a circular region using the “ROI” tool and analyzing it. The percent area occupied by red autofluorescence was determined by selecting the “show contour” option in the software and setting the parameters to highlight only regions of the mandibles with a deep red color (a* values > 45).

SEM-EDX for mandible morphology and elemental analysis

The mandibles of neonates (n = 5 for both species), third-instar larvae (n = 5 for both species), and adult males (n = 5 for both species) and females (n = 5 for X. germanus, n = 7 for X. crassiusculus) were prepared for scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) for morphology and to reveal the elemental composition of the external layers of cuticle (EDX is limited to a depth of 0.1 to 5 μm into the cuticle). Mandibles were initially removed from the head with scissors and placed through a series of ethanol washes (80%, 90%, and 100%, 24 h each) and air-dried in a fume hood for 24 h. The mandibles were then secured to an SEM aluminum stub with double-sided carbon graphite tape, sputter coated with 20 nm of platinum (Electron Microscopy Sciences 150 T sputter coater, Morgantown, USA), and imaged with a JEOL 6010LV SEM (Peabody, USA) with EDX (X-Max50, Oxford Instruments, Concord, USA).

Mandible morphology was examined at 20 Kv, a spot size of 50, and 50x magnification. Each mandible had its length measured from the distal tip of the apical tooth to the base and the width measured from the lateral to the medial edge through the mandible midregion (Fig. 3a). In addition, we determined the proportion of the mandible occupied by the tooth and incisor regions on the medial side of the mandible to test for differences in morphology between sexes, species, and life stages. The tooth region is defined as the distal tooth and the incisor region is represented as an elongated region of the medial side of the mandibles that shows a thinner edge, likely to facilitate cutting and chewing during gallery formation. All subsequent morphological measurements were performed in ImageJ software69.

All EDX measurements were acquired in Aztec v6.1 software (Oxford Instruments, Concord, USA) at 20 kV, a working distance of 15 mm, a dead time (count rate) of 18%, and a spot size between 60 and 70. The 20 nm of platinum coating was entered into the software to remove platinum from the element readings. The “point-and-ID” tool was used to select five locations on the lateral side and seven locations on the medial side of mandibles that spanned distal to proximal regions and the percentage weight of each element in each location was recorded. Mandibles of neonates had their elemental compositions analyzed at 1000× magnification, and third-instar larvae and adults at 500× magnification. EDX also was performed on A. saccharum wood inside the galleries and on the collected Ambrosiella species hyphae at 500× magnification, following the same preparation as the mandibles, to determine if some transition metals are prioritized via changes in their concentrations as the flow through this insect-fungal-plant host system. Mandibles, wood, and fungus had three measurements acquired at each studied location and the concentrations were averaged together for a single representative value at that location. In addition, our preliminary experiments of the mandibles indicated high concentrations of Zn compared to other transition metals; therefore, the “line scan” tool was used to draw a line along the mandible length and run for two min to better understand the specific demarcation location and general rate of Zn enrichment. All recorded values were converted to atomic % before further statistical analysis.

Hardness and elastic modulus measurements with nanoindentation

A single mandible was randomly selected and removed from three adult males and females (n = 3 for each species) of each species (previously stored in a −80 ℃ freezer) with dissecting scissors to quantify hardness (H) and elastic modulus (EM) values with nanoindentation. The mandibles were cleaned in dH20 with a paintbrush, then placed on a droplet of epoxy (Gorilla Epoxy) on a glass slide and allowed to cure for one week (also allowing the mandible to dry). A nanoindenter (Bruker Hysitron TI Premier, San Jose, USA) with a Ti-0045 cono-spherical probe (90° cone angle, 5 μm tip radius, diamond coated) was used along with Triboscan v9.6 software (Bruker, San Jose, USA) to assess H and EM. It was determined prior to experimentation that the actual shape of the indenter probe agreed with the default area-function of a spherical probe, −A = π·h2 + 2πR·h, where A is the cross-sectional area, h is the indentation depth, and R is the probe tip radius.

Three measurements were acquired from each distal and proximal location (Fig. 5a), then averaged together as representative values. Measurements were acquired with a load-controlled quasi-static indentation test using a standard trapezoidal loading function (5 s loading, then 2 s dwell, followed by 5 s unloading times) and a maximum load set to 1000 µN. All measurements were performed with the cuticle surface positioned perpendicular to the indenter. Force-displacement curves were analyzed by the TriboScan software, which is based on the Oliver-Pharr Method70 and allowed us to determine the reduced modulus and H values. The reduced modulus was then converted to EM using the assumption that the Poisson’s ratio of the mandibles is 0.371,72.

Statistics

An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to test for significant differences (α = 0.05) among color values, percentage area deep red, and H and EM between locations per mandible side (lateral and medial) and between sides for each location within species. ANOVA was used to compare these variables across life stages and between sexes, both within and between species, as well as to analyze the metal concentrations in the wood, fungus, and beetle mandibles. Significant differences were ranked with Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc tests in JMP v. 18.0.

Elemental composition data of mandibles for the life stages and sexes were further analyzed in R73. Because each specimen contributed multiple measurements across mandibular regions, data were structured in long format with the factors species, sex (male, female), life stage (adult, neonate, third instar), side (lateral, medial), and location (apical, distal edge, dorsal [medial side only], proximal edge, midregion, molar [medial side only] and proximal region). To account for proportional data (atomic %), we used both raw proportions and arcsine–square root–transformed values for univariate analyses.

For multivariate community-style analyses of elemental composition, replicate measurements were aggregated within each individual, side, and location by taking their mean values. The data were then reshaped to wide format (elements as columns) and zero-filled for missing values. A Hellinger transformation (square root of relative abundances) was applied to reduce the influence of dominant elements and to make the data suitable for Euclidean distances. A permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed using the adonis2 function in the vegan package74, with 999 permutations. Our primary model tested the effects of species, life stage, and their interaction on overall elemental composition.

For univariate analyses of individual elements, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fit using the lme475 and lmerTest packages76. Models included fixed effects of species, side, location, sex, and life stage, with individual entered as a random effect to account for repeated measurements. Type III ANOVAs were used to assess the significance of main effects and interactions, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Tukey’s HSD using the emmeans package77. Constrained analysis of principal coordinates (CAP) was performed to understand patterns and understand if separation occurs in multivariate space as well.
