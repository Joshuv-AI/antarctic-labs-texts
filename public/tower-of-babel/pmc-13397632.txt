Genomic basis of local adaptation in Elymus sibiricus revealed by genotype–environment associations and common garden experiments
Authors: Yi Xiong, Qingqing Yu, Yanli Xiong, Junming Zhao, Xiaofei Ji, Xiong Lei, Jianbo Zhang, Xiao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnraveling the genetic basis underlying local adaptation represents a pivotal area of research within ecological and evolutionary biology. In the present study, we explored the genomic mechanisms that underpin the local adaptation of Elymus sibiricus, an indigenous grass species predominantly distributed in the temperate alpine regions of the Eurasian continent.ResultsPopulation structure analyses categorize 316 accessions of E. sibiricus into three distinct clusters: XJ (Xinjiang), NC (North of China), and the QTP (Qinghai-Xizang Plateau). Furthermore, through selection sweep analysis, we have pinpointed specific genes that show evidence of selection across these three groups, with these genes implicated in pathways related to local climate adaptation. A total of six gene exchange events among the 13 subgroups were detected, and the dynamics of population history revealed marked fluctuations in the effective population size of each subgroup in the recent past. Notably, we observed significant genetic differentiation (as measured by Fst) among the three clusters. We performed genetic–environment association (GEA) analysis and successfully identified a substantial number of candidate genes that correlate with temperature and rainfall, predominantly involved in pathways such as Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. In addition, we conducted a three-year common garden experiment in Hongyuan county, Sichuan, assessing six morphological indicators, including adaptive traits such as biomass, followed by genome-wide association (GWAS) analysis.ConclusionThe genes selected across populations, as well as those associated with local climate and adaptive phenotypes through association analysis, provide insights into the mechanism underlying the broad climate adaptability of E. sibiricus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09100-w.Keywords: Adaptative evolution, GEA, GWAS, Common garden experiment, Elymus sibiricus, Local environment

Introduction

In recent years, evolutionary biology has increasingly focused on understanding genomic variations that arise during population differentiation and the evolutionary forces driving plant genome divergence [37, 40]. Among these forces, adaptive evolution plays a pivotal role in enabling organisms to survive and reproduce across diverse environments, ultimately shaping species evolution and enhancing biodiversity. Local adaptation occurs when essential adaptive traits exhibit genetic differentiation among populations, allowing populations to evolve differently in response to shared environmental stressors due to their unique genetic composition [24]. While the remarkable adaptability of many species has allowed them to persist through historical climatic fluctuations [20], growing concerns exist that the current pace of climate change may exceed the capacity of many species to track suitable habitats. Habitat fragmentation, climate change shifts, and increased UV-B radiation, pose substantial challenges for numerous organisms [20], and limited gene flow between populations experiencing distinct environmental pressures can further enhance genetic divergence and promote local adaptation [38]. Understanding the genomic basis of local adaptation is therefore essential for predicting species’ response to ongoing environmental change.

The potential for populations to respond and adapt to shifting selection pressures depends on their ability to generate well-suited traits (such as biomass) under new environmental conditions [44]. This capacity, hinges on the presence of standing genetic variation, which provides the raw material for natural selection [1]. High levels of genetic diversity expand the range of potential traits available for selection, increasing the probability of successful adaptation, whereas populations with limited genetic variation may struggle to produce the phenotypic modifications necessary for survival and reproduction under novel selection pressures [13]. Consequently, assessing the level and distribution of genetic variation within and among populations is crucial for determining their adaptive potential and long-term persistence in dynamic environments.

Species with broader ecological niches and distribution are generally regarded as less vulnerable to climate change compared to endemic species with limited ranges [6]. The genus Elymus, the largest in cool-season Poaceae grasses, is distributed extensively across temperate, high‑altitude, and high‑latitude regions [41], making it an ideal model for investigating plant adaptability to diverse environmental conditions. Within this genus, Elymus sibiricus exhibits a wide distribution across Eurasia and is a dominant species in alpine regions, notably the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau [47]. It flourishes in diverse habitats such as meadows, riverbanks, mountain slopes, and valleys, spanning altitudes from 1,000 to 4,000 m [31, 32]. Its remarkable ecological success in heterogeneous environments, combined its broad geographic range, makes E. sibiricus a prime candidate for addressing the questions of whether climate gradients drive adaptive genomic differentiation, whether population structure aligns with geographic distribution, and whether candidate loci association with phenotypic variation under common garden conditions. Furthermore, E. sibiricus is increasingly regarded as a promising energy crop for cultivation on marginal land due to its high biomass output, effective fermentation properties, and fiber content that exceeds that of common energy grasses such as Phalaris arundinacea and Avena fatua [9, 28]. Critically, E. sibiricus is an allotetraploid (StStHH, 2n = 4x = 28). The presence of two homoeologous subgenomes can complicate SNP discovery, read mapping, population inference, and GWAS if not properly accounted for. To address these challenges, we employed a reference-guided approach with stringent filtering of multi-mapping reads and retained only variants with clear subgenome assignments where possible. Additionally, we validated population genetic inferences using complementary methods (e.g., PCA, ADMIXTURE) to ensure robustness against potential biases introduced by polyploidy.

Here, we performed specific-locus amplified fragment sequencing (SLAF-seq) on 316 wild accessions of E. sibiricus collected across its main distribution range. We systematically assessed genetic diversity, population structure, population historical dynamics, and gene flow, and conducted selective sweep analysis (combining Fst and Pi) to identify candidate genes under selection. Genotype-environmental association analysis (GEA) was used to detect climatic-associated loci. In addition, a subset of 172 accessions was evaluated in a common garden experiments over three years, measuring six morphological traits, followed by genome-wide association analysis (GWAS). Specifically, this study aimed to test the following hypotheses: (1) whether climate gradients drive adaptive genomic differentiation among populations; (2) whether inferred population structure is consistent with geographic distribution; and (3) whether candidate loci identified by GEA and selective sweep analyses are also associated with phenotypic traits in the common garden.

Materials and methods

Sampling and genomic DNA isolation

Our sample set included 316 E. sibiricus accessions, which are collecting from Xinjiang (XJ, China), the Qinghai-Tibet plateau (QTP, including Qinghai (QH), Sichuan (SC), Gansu (GS), and Tibet (Xizang, XZ)), North China (NC), and Siberia (SI). To minimize the risk of sampling clonal individuals resulting from vegetative propagation via tillering, we collected samples at intervals of at least 50 m within each population. Additionally, we performed a clone-filtering step based on multi-locus genotypes: samples with identical SNP haplotypes (shared across all polymorphic loci) were considered potential clones, and only one representative from each clonal group was retained for downstream analyses. The specimens are deposited in the Sichuan Experimental Base of the Elymus Research Institute, Sichuan Agricultural University with the deposition numbers beginning with "ES" (Table S1). All specimens were identified by Prof. Xiao Ma and Prof. Xing Fan of Sichuan Agricultural University. The leaf material of each accession was used to DNA isolation using the CTAB method, and the quality and quantity of DNA were detected using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

SLAF sequencing and variation calling

The strategy of SLAF-seq was performed based on the Sun et al. [42], which included chromosomal DNA digestion, SLAF fragment tailing with A at the 3' end, ligation of Dual-index adapter, gel electrophoresis, and universal primer PCR amplification. Two enzymes (RsaⅠ and HaeⅢ, New England Biolabs, Ipswish, MA, USA) were selected to digest the genomic DNA. Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to sequence the libraries using 125-bp paired-end reads. The high-quality reads were mapped to the subgenome-resolved reference genome of E. sibiricus [29], in which the St and H subgenomes were assembled separately. To avoid mis-mapping between homeologous sequences, reads were aligned to each subgenome independently, and reads that mapped equally well to both subgenomes (multi-mapping reads) were discarded. Only uniquely mapped reads with mapping quality ≥ 20 were retained for SNP calling. The shared SNPs obtained by GATK and SAMtools software were considered as the final SNP dataset, in a vcf format. The vcf file was analyzed using vcfR packages [25]. The SNPs with missing data ≥ 10% were filtered, and SNPs with the minor allele frequency > 5%, and depth > 4 were collected for subsequent analysis. Phasing was performed using Beagle v5.5 [5].

Population genetic and population structure analysis

To infer the genetic relationships of all the 316 samples, phylogenetic inferences, principal components analysis (PCA), discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC), and STRUCTURE were used. Phylogenetic relationships of individuals of E. sibiricus were constructed using the neighbor-joining method in the program MEGA version 10.0 [27]. The PCA and DAPC were analyzed using Plink2 and R package ‘adegenet’ [23], respectively. The Bayesian-based ADMIXTURE v1.4.0 [2] was used to infer the genetic memberships of the test accessions, with the number of genetic clusters (K) was setting from 1 to 15 with 10 replicates for each value of K. The number of Markov Chain Monte Carlo replications and the length of the burn-in period were set as 100,000 and 500,000. The optimal K value was identified using average likelihood and ΔK statistics [14]. Finally, CLUMPAK [26] was used to estimate the membership coefficients at the individual level. The Pi (nucleotide diversity, π) values of each subgroup were calculated using vcftools [10].

Demographic history and gene flow

To reconstruct the demographic history of the three genetically defined clusters, we applied the stepwise coalescent Markovian coalescent (SMC++) method [43]. Phasing was performed on the diploid consensus sequences using Beagle v5.5 [5]. A nuclear mutation rate of 3.75 × 10⁻⁸ per site per year was assumed, and a generation time of 1 year was used for the analysis [49]. To gain an understanding of the overall population structure, the population genetic distance was calculated and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using vcfR package. The population differentiation was reflected by pairwise Fst and gene flow events among those populations. The Fst values between two subgroups were calculated based on vcfR [25], adegenet [22], and hierfstat [18] R packages. The migration patterns among the genetically defined clusters were inferred using TreeMix v 1.13 based on the vcf file. The possible migration events (m) were set as 0–10.

Bioclimatic data extraction and genome-environment association analysis

Bioclimatic variables for the 316 E. sibiricus accessions were extracted using DIVA‑GIS software [21] from the WorldClim version 2.1 database (https://worldclim.org/) at a spatial resolution of 30 arc‑seconds (~ 1 km2), based on the baseline climate period 1970–2000. Nineteen bioclimatic variables (BIO1–BIO19) were initially obtained. In addition, altitude was extracted from the same WorldClim elevation layer and treated as an independent environmental variable. Thus, a total of 20 environmental variables (19 bioclimatic + altitude) were considered. Prior to genotype‑environment association (GEA) analyses, we reduced multicollinearity by calculating pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients among all 20 variables using the PerformanceAnalytics R package [36]. Any pair of variables with |r|> 0.8 was considered highly correlated; from each correlated pair, the variable with less biological relevance or lower variability was removed. The remaining uncorrelated set of variables was used for subsequent GEA analyses, with altitude explicitly included as a covariate to account for elevation effects on local adaptation.

To identify SNP loci potentially underlying local adaptation to climatic factors, we performed genotype-environment association (GEA) analyses using the bioclimatic variables that are independent of each other. After filtering SNPs (MAF > 0.05, missing rate < 10%), we applied two complementary approaches: latent factor mixed models (LFMM, using the R package lfmm) and redundancy analysis (RDA, using the R package vegan). For LFMM, we ran 20 independent runs with K = 3 latent factors (based on population structure) and adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR, q < 0.05). For RDA, we identified candidate outliers as SNPs with loading values exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the mean. Only SNPs detected by both methods were retained as robust GEA outliers. These outlier SNPs were then annotated against the reference genome to obtain candidate genes potentially involved in climate adaptation.

Selective sweep

The pairwise Fst values and Pi of each cluster were both calculated using vcftools [10]. We then identified selected regions between cluster pairs by combining the population differentiation index (Fst) and nucleotide diversity (Pi) using a sliding-window approach with a window size of 10 Kb and a step size of 1 Kb. Specifically, genomic windows with high Fst (top 5%) and low Pi (bottom 5%) were considered as candidate regions under selection, and the overlapping windows were merged. Then the Fst and Pi were combined to identify the selected regions and genes using available fst_pi_select_sweep.r R script (https://github.com/JH-Dai/GWAS/blob/main/scripts/fst_pi_select_sweep.r).

Common garden experiment and genome-wide association study

According to the sampling composition and climate space coverage, 172 E. sibiricus accessions were selected from the 316 samples and cultivated in a common garden at Hongyuan, Sichuan (with the altitude of 3,620 m) in 2019. The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with six blocks. Within each block, each accession was planted as a single plot containing five individual plants, with a spacing of 30 cm between plants. The biomass (dry yield, BM), plant height (PH), stem thick (ST), flag leaf length (FLL), flag leaf width (FLW), internode number (IN) of individual were measured based on all five individuals per plot in boot stage during 2020–2022. The phenotypic value for each accession in each block was calculated as the mean of the five individuals; block means were then used to compute the overall accession mean across the six blocks for each year. Broad-sense heritability (H2) was estimated for each trait using a linear mixed model with accession as random effect and block as fixed effect prior to GWAS. The final phenotypic values used for GWAS were the accession means averaged across the three years. To identify genetic loci underlying these agronomic traits, we performed genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using the following approach. The optimal model was performed with Q + K using GEMMA pipeline [46]. The genetic relationships of the tested individuals were taken into consideration based on the kinship and Q matrix generated from Admixture [2]. The quantile–quantile (QQ) plot was generated using the R package qqman v0.1.9 [45] to assess the distribution of observed versus expected -log10(P values), with a 95% confidence interval calculated from the expected distribution. GEC software (http://pmglab.top/gec/#/) was used to estimate the suggested P value which indicated that the suggested P is 1e-5. The genes within 50 Kb of a significant SNP’s flanking region (25 Kb on each side) were considered as candidate genes.

Results

SLAF-seq data set and population structure of 316 E. sibiricus accessions

After the filtering process, each accession produced about 1.0 Gb of high-quality clean reads, totaling 6,481,696 reads, with a Q30 ratio of 95.09% and a GC content of 46.91% (Table S1). Sequence alignment with the reference genome of E. sibiricus identified approximately 7,535,251 SLAF tags distributed across all 14 chromosomes (Fig S1A). Of these SLAF tags, 3,359,774 (44.59%) were found to be polymorphic. The average depth of SLAF tags varied from 5.05 to 36.84-fold for each accession of E. sibiricus. A total of 260,281 high-quality SNPs were found to be evenly distributed across all E. sibiricus chromosomes (Fig S1B), confirmed by the high mapping quality of the vcf file, considering parameters such as read depth, mapping quality, and Phred-scaled quality (Fig S2).

Based on their geographical distribution, the collected 316 accessions were further divided into 3 sub-populations (Fig. 1A), namely QTP, XJ, and NC (including NC and SI). The results of the phylogenetic tree revealed the categorical division of test accessions into three distinct clusters: Cluster I, Cluster II, and Cluster III. Notably, Cluster I primarily comprised accessions from QTP, Cluster II consisted mainly of accessions from XJ, and Cluster III mainly included accessions from the NC and SI (Fig. 1B). Additionally, a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, demonstrating that PC1 accounted for 45.75% of the total variance, while PC2 explained 8.79% of the total variance (Fig. 1C). The PCA plot illustrated that PC1 could separate the test accessions into three groups, which was consistent with the phylogenetic tree. The population structure analysis also exhibited three distinct genetic memberships (Fig S3), with accessions from the QTP region displaying a notably different genetic background compared to those from NC/SI and XJ regions (Fig. 1D). The QTP accessions showed the highest genetic diversity and greatest divergence from other clusters (Fig S4A). TreeMix identified two gene flow events (Fig S4B): a strong early flow from QTP to NC, and a weaker flow from QTP to XJ.

Selective sweep analysis and gene-environment association study

To investigate the evolutionary and adaptation mechanisms of these three clusters (XJ, QTP, and NC), the selected regions and genes of each cluster were identified by combined the Fst and Pi values (Fig S5). A total of 215, 86, and 67 genes were identified as the selected genes between NC vs XJ, QTP vs NC, and XJ vs QTP, respectively. KEGG annotation results indicated that the genes selected between NC vs XJ were mostly annotated in carbon metabolism (5 genes), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (4 genes), and fatty acid biosynthesis (4 genes) (Table S2). The genes selected between QTP vs NC, and XJ vs QTP were significantly enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism and plant-pathogen interaction (both containing 2 genes), and beta-Alanine metabolism and plant-pathogen interaction (both containing 2 genes).

After pairwise correlation analysis of the 20 environmental variables (Fig S6), seven independent factors (bio1, bio2, bio5, bio6, bio12, bio14, and bio15) with relatively important biological significance were retained for subsequent analysis based on a threshold of |r|> 0.8. These variables were further categorized into two groups: the first consisting of bio1, bio2, bio5, and bio6, associated with temperature, and the second consisting of bio12, bio14, and bio15, related to precipitation. Using LFMM and RDA analysis, a total of 36, 550, 24, and 173 genes were identified as candidate genes associated with temperature factors (bio1, bio2, bio5, and bio6), among which 70 genes were successfully annotated in the KEGG database (Table S3, S4). The most enriched pathways consisted of ribosome (10 genes), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (9 genes), ubiquitin mediated proteolysis (8 genes), and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (8 genes) (Table S5). Especially, we found that two genes EsiH02g0025590 and EsiH02g0025600 were shared in the association result of bio2 and bio6 (Fig. 2A, B), annotated with Zinc finger and Myb/SANT-like DNA-binding domain in the Pfam database. Moreover, a total of 77, 95, and 74 genes were associated with precipitation factors (bio12, bio14, and bio15), among which 67 genes were annotated in the KEGG database (Table S4, S5). The most enriched pathways consisted of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (6 genes) and biosynthesis of amino acids (5 genes). We also found two genes EsiH02g0043840 and EsiH02g0043850 shared in the association result of bio 12 and bio15 (Fig. 2C, D). Overall, the candidate genes associated with these important climatic factors, particularly the shared genes, were crucial in the environmental adaptation process of E. sibiricus.

Common garden experiment and genome-wide association study (GWAS)

The 172 E. sibiricus accessions, including 54 from the Cluster Ⅲ (47.37% of 114), 90 from the Cluster Ⅰ (65.69% of 137), and 28 from the Cluster Ⅱ (43.08% of 65), were selected from the 316 samples. Their climate space coverage largely overlaps with that of the full set, indicating that this subset adequately represents the overall climatic diversity (Fig S7). In the common garden experiment and genome-wide association study (GWAS), the Pearson correlation analysis of biomass (BM), plant height (PH), stem thickness (ST), flag leaf length (FLL), flag leaf width (FLW), and internode number (IN) of 172 accessions is shown in Fig. 3A, indicating that all pairwise correlation coefficients are less than 0.8. The PCA analysis revealed distinct patterns and relationships among these phenotypic indicators (Fig. 3B). The first and second components accounted for 44.1% and 25.8% of the total variance, respectively. In particular, the phenotypic indicators can be categorized based on the first component. GWAS identified 198, 180, 213, 298, 291, and 196 candidate genes for flag leaf length (FLL), biomass (BM), flag leaf width (FLW), internode number (IN), plant height (PH), and stem thickness (ST), respectively. The Manhattan plot and QQ plot for BM are shown in Fig. 3C and D (results for the other five traits are provided in Table S6 and Fig S8). Among them, ten genes were detected by both selective sweep and GWAS (selection linked to biomass), and 29 genes by both GEA and GWAS (environment–phenotype associations). Notably, only one gene, EsiS04g0007450, was identified by all three approaches, representing a high‑confidence target for polygenic adaptation (Fig S9).

Discussion

High-throughput sequencing technology has emerged as an invaluable tool for unraveling population structure and demographic history. By harnessing this technology, we can gain a comprehensive understanding of the intricate genetic relationships among different populations and the evolutionary forces that have shaped their genetic diversity over time. Here, the whole-genome SLAF sequencing was used to explore population architecture, demographic history, and genetic differentiation of E. sibiricus in different populations and/or wild accessions. Furthermore, GEA analysis using climatic factors and GWAS analysis using morphological indicators identified abundant candidate genes in shaping the evolutionary patterns and controlling the phenotypic diversity of E. sibiricus.

Selective sweeps and polygenic adaptation drive genomic architecture of E. sibiricus

Our results suggest that local adaptation to climate in E. sibiricus may be influenced by both diversifying and stabilizing natural selection acting on multiple genes associated with local environmental variables, indicating a potentially complex adaptive architecture. The observation that many candidate genes have small individual effects and are distributed across the genome raises the possibility that polygenic adaptation contributes to climate responses, although direct evidence for selection on standing variation or cumulative effects remains to be validated functionally [3, 49].

We found that 316 E. sibiricus accessions could be significantly divided into three clusters (XJ cluster, NC cluster, and QTP cluster) (Fig. 1), which was consistent with the results based on their chloroplast sequences [48]. The population in the QTP underwent significant natural pressures to adapt to the harsh environmental conditions found in high-altitude regions [51, 52]. These pressures include low atmospheric pressure, intense radiation exposure, rapid temperature fluctuations, and the challenges of surviving winters. A total of 86 and 67 genes were under selection between NC vs QTP and XJ vs QTP (Table S2), mainly responding to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism, propanoate metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. These pathways have been proven to be important in resisting low temperature [35, 50–52], UV-B [30], and hypoxia stress [15], providing biological support for the adaptive significance of the selected genes. Importantly, our population grouping was consistent with independent evidence from chloroplast sequences [48], RAPD markers [31, 32], and karyotype analyses [11], suggesting that the observed selection signals are unlikely to be artefacts of subjective grouping.

The unique terrain conditions of Xinjiang, known as the "three mountains sandwiched between two basins", have allowed the native plant in this region to have their own unique evolution and differentiation models, leading to geographical isolation from other regions. In addition, mountain landforms play a role in species distribution and population isolation. Therefore, the XJ population can be clearly distinguished in phylogenetic tree and PCA analysis (Fig. 1). The largest number of selected genes (215 genes) were found in XJ vs NC group (Table S2; Fig S5), with several pathways including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and aatty acid biosynthesis playing an important role in plant resistance to drought stress [16, 19, 53]. This may be due to the low rainfall in Xinjiang, which has led to the evolution of genes related to drought resistance in the local E. sibiricus accessions.

Selection signals are scanned between XJ, NC and QTP, indicate that directional or diversified natural selection exists among different geographical groups of E. sibiricus (XJ, NC, and QTP), as reflected in the diverse genes related to many key metabolic processes. This suggests that the genome of E. sibiricus has the potential to actively respond to rapid climate change, which benefits its long-term survival. This adaptive response is most likely aided by standing genetic variation, commonly expected in long-generation species [7], with QTP groups possessing stronger adaptability, as seen in their generally higher Pi values. However, according to the population historical dynamics, certain regions of the QTP, particularly SC and QH, have undergone a significant decrease in their effective population size over time. Additionally, recent climate data indicates abnormal high temperatures during the summer and frequent occurrences of extreme weather events poses a formidable challenge to the survival of the indigenous E. sibiricus in these areas, diminishing its ability to adapt. In this case, gaining an understanding of the specific regions and genes selected in response to the local climate environment among different geo-populations can provide a foundation for expanding the adaptable regions of E. sibiricus.

Integrating selective sweep, GEA, and GWAS reveals convergent and divergent genomic signatures of local adaptation in E. sibiricus

Selective environmental factors play a crucial role in the local adaptation, genetic diversity, and population structure of wild accessions [8]. Employing climatic variables as indicators of selective pressure can capture fundamental mechanisms underlying abiotic stress tolerance [17]. Evidence of local adaptation spans all 14 E. sibiricus chromosomes, with each climate variable exhibiting distinct genomic associations. Temperature and rainfall are the main environmental factors limiting the distribution of Elymus species [48, 49]. Through GEA analysis, we identified abundant SNPs and candidate genes significantly associated with temperature and rainfall. Temperature-associated genes were enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum—pathways known to confer high/low temperature stress resistance [12, 34]. Rainfall-associated genes also enriched multiple important pathways, with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis being common to both, underscoring its central role in climate adaptation.

Phenotypic plasticity, the ability of a genotype to produce different phenotypes under varying environments, is crucial for plant adaptability [39]. Biomass, a comprehensive indicator of growth and adaptability [33] for most forage species, is a key breeding target. In a common garden experiment conducted in high‑altitude areas over three years, GWAS detected 180 genes associated with biomass (Table S6), primarily involved in oxidative phosphorylation, protein export, pyrimidine metabolism, and starch/sucrose metabolism.

To address the possibility of stratified selection (i.e., selection balanced across groups or environments) and to test the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses by varying key parameters in sweep detection (window size, outlier thresholds) and GEA (different climate variable combinations). The overlapping patterns remained largely consistent, confirming the stability of our results. We then implemented an explicit integration framework to combine the three lines of evidence (selective sweep, GEA, GWAS). A total of 47 candidate genes were supported by at least two methods. Among them, 7 genes were detected by both sweep and GEA (convergent signals of climate‑driven selection), 10 by both sweep and GWAS (selection linked to biomass), and 29 by both GEA and GWAS (environment–phenotype associations). Notably, only one gene (EsiS04g0007450) was identified by all three approaches, representing a high‑confidence target for polygenic adaptation (Fig S9). Enrichment analysis of the overlapping gene sets revealed significant overrepresentation of pathways related to phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and oxidative phosphorylation (adjusted P < 0.01), confirming their central role in multiple layers of adaptation. In contrast, genes unique to sweep analyses were enriched for population‑specific selective sweeps (e.g., fatty acid biosynthesis in the XJ population), while GEA‑only genes were more dispersed across temperature‑ and rainfall‑responsive pathways, reflecting the divergent nature of signals. This integration framework thus provides a clear narrative: convergent signals (supported by ≥ 2 methods) pinpoint core adaptive mechanisms, whereas divergent signals highlight context‑specific selection pressures.

The adaptability of plants to changing environments is a complex process achieved through genetic variation and evolution [4]. Understanding the genetic diversity of as many genotypes as possible provides a theoretical basis for adaptability research. Moreover, common garden experiments reveal whether plants’ adaptation to their natural environment can keep pace with climate change [3]. The combination of these approaches yields a comprehensive understanding of both existing and potential adaptability.
