Vitamin D binding protein and receptor prevalence in a large population with periodontitis: single nucleotide polymorphism and transcriptomic profiling
Authors: Ziyan Nie, Xiaopan Hu, Peinan Hu, Peiqiang Li, Haijing Zhou, Xiaodong Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is an ongoing controversy regarding the expression of vitamin D receptor (VDR) and binding protein (VDBP) genes, as well as their polymorphisms, in periodontitis. We examined eight single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and performed a transcriptome-level bioinformatics analysis to clarify their relationship with periodontitis.MethodsTo explore VDR and VDBP polymorphisms, 600 subjects were included, including 307 patients with chronic periodontitis (CP) and 293 healthy controls. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral venous blood collected from each subject. A MassARRAY system was used to detect SNPs, including rs1544410G/A (BsmI), rs2228570C/T (FokI), rs7975232G/T (ApaI), rs731236T/C (TaqI), rs739837G/T, rs9729G/T, and rs3847987C/A in the VDR gene, and rs7041A/C in the VDBP gene. Then, we analyzed transcriptome sequencing datas of gingival tissues from two single-cell transcriptome sequencing studies to identify differential expression profiles. The objective was to further explore the potential association between VDR gene and gingival tissues in individuals with CP.ResultsThe regression analysis model revealed a significant relationship between rs739837G/T (P = 0.04) and rs7041A/C (P = 0.03) polymorphisms and CP susceptibility. Subjects carrying the TT genotype of rs739837 showed a decreased risk of developing CP compared to those carrying the GG + GT genotype (OR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.29–0.99). Individuals carrying the AC + CC genotype of rs7041 showed a reduced risk of developing CP compared to those with the AA genotype (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.51–0.97). Furthermore, allele C of rs7041 was found to have a protective effect against periodontitis (P = 0.03, OR = 0.75, CI = 0.58–0.98). However, no association was found between CP susceptibility and six other 6 SNPs (rs1544410, rs2228570, rs7975232, rs9729, rs731236, and rs3847987). Differential levels of VDR transcription were observed in gingival tissues during CP.ConclusionsVDR genetic variability and transcriptional expression are significant factors affecting susceptibility to CP. These findings suggested that rs739837 TT in VDR and rs7041 A/C in VDBP may be protective against periodontitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05227-0.

Background

Periodontitis is a group of chronic inflammatory diseases, the most common of which is chronic periodontitis (CP), characterized by alveolar bone resorption and plaque microbial infection as initiating factors and having an estimated worldwide prevalence of 10–15% [1]. Periodontitis results from the joint action of host genetic factor and environmental factors such as age, sex, smoking, and oral hygiene status [2]. Other risk factors such as stress, systemic conditions, and socioeconomic status have also been associated with its onset and progression [3]. It can cause gum bleeding, periodontal pus, loose teeth, and bad breath and ultimately lead to tooth loss without treatment, making it the primary cause of tooth loss in humans. Furthermore, untreated periodontitis may result in jawbone damage, impaired chewing function, and esthetic concerns, significantly affecting the quality of life of individuals. Increasing evidence shows that periodontal health status is closely related to systemic diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases [4–6], and recent studies also suggest links with adverse pregnancy outcomes, rheumatoid arthritis, and even Alzheimer’s disease [7]. The growing understanding of these associations highlights the importance of managing periodontal disease not only for oral health but also for overall systemic health.

Epidemiological studies have shown great variation in susceptibility to periodontitis among different individuals in a population [8, 9]. Factors such as genetic diversity, environmental influences, and lifestyle choices can significantly contribute to this variability [10]. Furthermore, periodontal disease is commonly reported in twins, family members, and across generations, suggesting a potential genetic component of susceptibility to periodontitis [11, 12]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are DNA sequence variations caused by single nucleotide changes at the genomic level, influencing susceptibility to periodontitis by affecting immune response and inflammation. Previous studies have linked specific SNPs to an increased risk of periodontal disease, emphasizing their role in disease [13]. Among these, polymorphisms in vitamin D receptor (VDR) and vitamin D binding protein (VDBP) genes are the most important because of the role of these genes in regulating inflammation [14–16]. VDR and VDBP are involved in the vitamin D metabolic pathway, which not only maintains calcium and bone homeostasis but also modulates the immune system’s ability to respond to bacterial pathogens, such as those involved in periodontal infections. Variations in these genes can influence the body’s inflammatory response, potentially leading to greater tissue destruction in the gums and supporting bone structures [17].

VDR is an intranuclear macromolecule that mediates the biological effects of 1α,25(OH)2D3, which is a gene transcription regulatory protein that plays a crucial role in mediating calcium and phosphorus balance, bone metabolism, regulating immunity, endocrine function, and cell differentiation [18]. Recent research also highlights its involvement in modulating the innate and adaptive immune responses, particularly in diseases characterized by chronic inflammation such as periodontitis [19]. VDR signaling is crucial for controlling inflammatory cytokine production, as well as for promoting the clearance of pathogens through immune cell activation [20]. The dysregulation of VDR pathways may contribute to exaggerated immune responses, leading to tissue destruction in periodontitis. VDBP is the major transporter in the vitamin D pathway, and it is known as group-specific component (GC) or GC-globulin [21]. Moreover, VDBP is involved in various immune activities such as monocyte responses and macrophage activation [22]. Variants in VDBP may affect the bioavailability of vitamin D, thereby influencing both bone health and immune function [23]. Many researchers have studied the correlation between VDR or VDBP SNPs and CP, especially regarding the relationship between VDR polymorphisms (such as ApaI, BsmI, TaqI, and FokI) and CP and the relationship between VDBP polymorphism rs7041 and CP. However, the results are inconsistent, which may be due to differences in race, population, sample size, and environmental factors [24–26].

To date, the conclusion about VDR or VDBP SNPs and CP remains controversial, and a large-scale analysis was needed to investigate the exact correlation between SNPs and transcriptomic expression of VDR and VDBP in periodontitis. Therefore, our study aimed to assess the relationship between rs1544410G/A (BsmI), rs2228570C/T (FokI), rs7975232G/T (ApaI), rs731236T/C (TaqI), rs739837G/T, rs9729G/T, and rs3847987C/A in the VDR gene, and rs7041A/C in the VDBP gene, and CP susceptibility in a population in northwestern China. Transcriptomic bioinformatic analysis provided a possible explanation for the association identified.

Methods

Subjects

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Genetic Association Studies (STREGA) recommendations [27]. A detailed checklist was included in the supplementary material. All participants signed an informed consent form, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Northwest Minzu University (XBMZ-YX-20220666). The sample size was first calculated by using the Rate-based Sample Size Formula [28]:

According to the relevant loci with statistical differences recorded in the previous literature, the frequency observed in periodontal patients with rs1544410 A/G (BsmI) in VDR gene was 51%, while the frequency observed in healthy controls was 16% [29]. Following the Rate-based Formula below, alpha was set at 0.05 and beta was set at 0.1. Allowing for a 25% attrition rate, the final calculation determined a minimum sample size of 88 patients in total.

To reduce the bias, we increased the sample size more than 6 times on this basis. Therefore, this case-control study included 600 unrelated middle-aged and elderly individuals aged 36–70 years and living in Gansu Province, northwest China. The recruited participants were employees or retirees who underwent an annual oral examination. All subjects were administered a questionnaire on their general conditions, systemic diseases, symptoms of periodontal disease, oral hygiene habits, living habits, and drug use. Oral examinations were performed by two highly qualified periodontists, with assessments showing a kappa value of 0.8. Periodontal status was divided into CP and control groups, which included healthy periodontal individuals and those with gingivitis, respectively [30]. Periodontal examinations included measurements of plaque index (PLI), bleeding on probing (BOP), probing depth (PD), and clinical attachment loss (CAL). CAL and PD were determined at six areas on the buccolingual side of oral cavity.

Inclusion criteria were the following: (i) from a family with three generations of Han Chinese; (ii) non-smoker; (iii) having more than 20 teeth; and (iv) understanding the study content and participating voluntarily. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosis with a systemic disease such as hepatitis, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, respiratory diseases, or HIV infection; (ii) biologically related sibling included in the study; and (iii) having taken antibiotics for one consecutive week within the 3 months preceding the study (Fig. 1).

DNA isolation and genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from 2-mL samples of peripheral venous blood in an EDTA anticoagulant tube using a DNA extraction kit (Nanjing Zhongke Bell, China). Optical density (OD) values of DNA samples were determined using a NanoDrop2000 instrument, and quality assessment was performed through 1.25% agarose gel electrophoresis to ensure compliance.

All eight SNPs in VDR and VDBP were detected on a MassARRAY platform (Agena Bioscience, USA) using flight mass spectrometry. Primers were designed using Assay Designer 4.0 software (USA) and the primers’ s synthesis was completed by Biotechnology Company (Beijing, China). The primer sequences used for the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The main steps of MassArray SNP subtyping are (i) PCR: 1 cycle at 94℃ for 5 min; 45 cycles at 94℃ for 20 s, 56℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 1 min; 1 cycle at 72℃ for 3 min; ending in a 4℃ hold; (ii) shrimp alkali enzyme purification (SAP): 37℃ for 20 min, 85℃ for 5 min, and a 4℃ hold; (iii) single-base extension reaction; (iv) resin purification; (v) chip point sampling; and (vi) mass spectrometry detection and data output [31]. Each sample underwent triplicate processing for DNA genotyping.

Acquisition and preprocessing of transcriptome profiling data

mRNA expression data for patients with periodontitis were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Two single-cell atlas datasets, GSE152042 and GSE206621, were used for screening and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Expression profiles were generated through comparative statistical analysis, considering mapping ratio, read length, and fragment sequence bias. Differential expression of VDR genes was screened using Seurat v4.3.0 in R v4.2.2, and P values < 0.05 were considered significantly different for the periodontitis and healthy groups. The quality of the original data was assessed using FastQC software. Specific cluster markers were identified using the built-in Seurat function FindALLMarkers, with a minimum log fold change of 0.25 and P value < 0.05. Clusters were visualized in two-dimensional space by T-Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE).

Statistical analysis

Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) for the eight SNPs in VDR and VDBP was assessed by Chi-square testing in all samples, as well as between the groups (P > 0.001). Differences in allele and genotype frequencies between the two groups were tested using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A logistic regression model was used to analyze the association of alleles and genotypes with sample phenotypes after adjusting for potential risk factors. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using version 17.0 of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). To provide more detailed insights into the relationships between groups and help to interpret the overall test result more clearly, we conducted a post-hoc analysis by the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value less than 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The flowchart of Fig. 1 outlines the study design for this case-control study, starting with the selection of subjects and establishing exclusion criteria. The subjects were divided into healthy (control) and CP groups (Table 1). The control group included 293 participants (148 males and 145 females), and the CP group included 307 participants (156 males and 151 females). The prevalence of CP among participants in this study was 51.20%. In total, 304 subjects were male (50.70%) and 296 were female (49.30%), with an average age of approximately 50 years. The comparison of age and sex distribution between the two groups did not yield any statistically significant difference (P > 0.05).

Compared with individuals with the GG + GT genotype, those with the TT genotype of rs739837 were observed to have a lower risk of CP (OR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.29–0.99). Similarly, individuals carrying the AC + CC genotype of rs7041 were found to have a lower incidence of CP than those with the AA genotype (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.51–0.97). However, no association was found between susceptibility to CP and the other six SNPs (rs1544410, rs2228570, rs7975232, rs9729, rs731236, and rs3847987). These results indicate that individuals carrying the rs7041 C allele had a decreased risk of CP (P = 0.03, OR = 0.75, CI = 0.58–0.98). These results suggest that presence of the C allele protects against the development of CP.

VDR expression differences in patients with CP

VDR SNPs may affect VDR mRNA expression in fibroblasts. To investigate the relationship between VDR and gingival tissues in CP, the abundances of 12 different cell types in the database were analyzed. Basic statistical information for the two datasets is presented in Table 5. Figure 2 displays a heatmap of the top 80 genes with the largest differences in expression in the GSE152042 dataset, showing that upregulation of COL family genes was significantly higher in fibroblasts in the CP group than in the control group (P < 0.05). Both databases showed a significant positive correlation between VDR expression and CP in fibroblasts, type 2 conventional dendritic cells, natural regulatory T cells, and cell cycling cells (Fig. 3). These results suggest that VDR may be a periodontitis marker in specific cell types, with the potential to be used as a novel therapeutic marker.

Discussion

Vitamin D plays a crucial role in regulating calcium balance and promoting healthy bone formation within the endocrine system. However, the global population is facing a widespread issue of vitamin D deficiency [32]. Studies have shown that vitamin D deficiency may be an important factor contributing to periodontal disease, leading to an increased risk of periodontitis and aggravating the inflammatory response. Moderate vitamin D supplementation reduces periodontitis [33–35]. Vitamin D content and activity in the body are closely related to the functions of VDR and VDBP, which are important mediators of vitamin D that regulate bone metabolism and play a role in immunity [36].

The human VDR gene is located on chromosome 12 and is localized to 12q1. Its most prevalent single-nucleotide polymorphisms are BsmI, FokI, ApaI, and TaqI [24]. The VDBP SNPs are mostly found in rs7041, located on exon 11 [37]. In this study, the association between VDR (BsmI, FokI, ApaI, TaqI, rs739837, rs9729, and rs3847987) and VDBP (rs7041) SNPs in a Chinese population with or without CP was investigated. In the present study, a Han Chinese population in the northwest region of China was selected, and the influence of age and ethnic groups was controlled as far as possible for greater generalizability.

Studies on the association between VDR gene polymorphisms and CP mainly focused on BsmI, FokI, ApaI, and TaqI. Some studies have shown a significant association between polymorphisms and CP [15, 24, 38–40]. According to a meta-analysis conducted on the Chinese population [41], individuals with the AA + AG genotype of the VDR BsmI polymorphism were found to be more prone to periodontitis in southern China, whereas those with the ApaI TT + TG genotype had a lower risk of developing periodontitis in northern China. In the present study, no significant correlation was found between VDR gene polymorphisms BsmI, FokI, ApaI, and TaqI and the risk of CP based on allele frequencies or any genetic model in northern China, which is consistent with some other studies, including a meta-analysis [16, 42–44]. However, the conclusions of relevant studies are inconsistent, which may be the result of study population compositions, including differences in race, age, oral health status, or ethnic groups. Moreover, one reason for the differences in results could be the small sample size. Therefore, determining the precise relationship between these specific VDR alleles and CP requires an increased sample size.

The VDR alleles rs739837, rs9729, and rs3847987 have been identified as being linked to an increased risk of developing diabetes, lung cancer, and dental caries [45–48]. To our knowledge, this study represents the first report investigating the potential association between VDR alleles rs739837, rs9729, and rs3847987 and susceptibility to CP. Interestingly, we found that the rs739837 polymorphism was associated with CP in a recessive model, and subjects carrying the TT genotype of rs739837 had a lower risk of CP than those with the GG + GT genotype (OR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.29–0.99). Therefore, the rs739837 polymorphism may affect susceptibility to CP in our population; however, whether it can be used as a susceptibility marker requires validation studies in a larger study population.

There is currently a lack of research examining the relationship between the VDBP rs7041 gene polymorphism and risk of developing periodontitis [49, 50]. In 2016, Song et al. [51] examined VDBP gene polymorphisms (rs17467825, rs4588, rs7401) and risk of generalized aggressive periodontitis among a Chinese population. The study revealed a significant association between the rs17467825 GG genotype and the risk of the disease, whereas no significant association was observed for rs7401. In 2019, Nazemisalman et al. [26] discovered a novel association between VDBP gene polymorphisms (rs7401, rs4588) and CP in an Iranian population, marking the first instance of such a relationship being reported. Their study found a significant association between haplotypes of these two genes and susceptibility to severe CP. However, the small sample size due to stratification was a limitation of this study. Based on the currently available literature, our present study is the first to analyze the potential association between VDBP (rs7041) gene polymorphisms and CP in a Chinese population. We found that the rs7401 polymorphism was associated with CP under a recessive model, and subjects with the AC + CC genotype also had a lower risk of CP than those with the AA genotype (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.51–0.97). Furthermore, allele C was found to protect against periodontitis in rs7041 individuals (OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.58–0.98).

VDR is known to protect against inflammation by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promoting the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines. Numerous studies have shown that it inhibits the NF-κB pathway, which is induced by tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in response to inflammation [52, 53]. VDBP is a carrier protein responsible for transporting vitamin D. It affects inflammatory status by binding to certain inflammatory molecules such as LPS or bacterial DNAs, helping to neutralize their pro-inflammatory effects [54, 55]. The association between the VDR (FokI) genotype and susceptibility to CP may reflect enhanced kappa B ligand (RANKL) expression, as previously demonstrated by knockdown of VDR using siRNA in human periodontal ligament (hPDL) cells [56].

In our study, we observed that the TT genotype of VDR rs739837 was associated with a decreased risk of CP compared to the GG + GT genotype of the same allele, indicating that the frequency of the TT genotype in periodontitis-susceptible patients is lower. Further analysis of VDR and gingival tissues in patients with CP revealed a significant positive correlation between VDR expression in fibroblasts and CP. There appears to be a connection between these two results. Specifically, in a study of hPDL fibroblasts and VDR, Küchler et al. found that increased physiological concentrations of 25(OH)D3 regulated decreased VDR mRNA expression associated with the TT genotype of rs739837 [57]. Therefore, an increase in 25(OH)D3 levels in hPDL fibroblasts may lead to a decrease in the TT genotype of the VDR polymorphism rs739837, thereby increasing a person’s susceptibility to periodontitis. Modulating mRNA expression levels to influence SNP expression may, at least in part, explain susceptibility to periodontitis. Another study found that the SNPs BglI (rs739837), BsmI (rs1544410), ApaI (rs7975232), FokI (rs2228570), and TaqI (rs731236) had no effect on VDR gene expression in hPDL fibroblasts under simulated orthodontic compressive force [58]. Under varying conditions such as inflammation or mechanical stress, the impact of VDR gene SNPs on the physiological functions and disease processes in periodontal fibrocytes may differ.

Given the established link between vitamin D deficiency and the increased risk of CP, appropriate supplementation of vitamin D may be an effective strategy to reduce inflammation and tissue damage in at-risk populations. Moreover, therapies that regulate VDR gene expression or increase 1α,25(OH)2D3 levels may further reduce the risk of developing the condition [59]. Based on the genetic screening of VDR and VDBP polymorphisms, particularly rs739837 and rs7041, it may help identify individuals more prone to developing CP. Developing personalized prevention and treatment plans for specific populations may be crucial in addressing genetic susceptibility. Additionally, given that VDR and VDBP play pivotal roles in regulating immune responses, immune-modulating therapies present a highly promising avenue for mitigating the chronic inflammation commonly associated with periodontal disease, potentially enhancing both immune resilience and tissue repair.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our present study indicates that individuals with the VDR rs739837 TT genotype and VDBP rs7401 AC + CC genotype in a population in northwestern China may have reduced susceptibility to CP. VDBP and VDR genetic variability are significant factors affecting the incidence of periodontitis. This study suggests the need for further exploration of VDR and VDBP genes through in vitro and in vivo experiments for possible treatment of periodontitis.
