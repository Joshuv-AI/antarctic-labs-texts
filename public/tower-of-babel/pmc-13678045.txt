A mature tertiary lymphoid structure-associated spatial niche is inversely associated with ACC1-related lipid metabolism in neoadjuvant-treated hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ronghua Wang, Biao Shen, Yanan Wu, Shuai Zhao, Wenhao Xu, Wangrui Liu, Jian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is among the most common and lethal malignancies worldwide, and most patients present with advanced disease for which systemic therapy is the mainstay of treatment [1,2]. The combination of immune-checkpoint inhibition with anti-angiogenic agents has transformed the therapeutic landscape of advanced HCC, and neoadjuvant strategies that combine tyrosine-kinase inhibitors with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies can convert locally advanced, initially unresectable tumors into resectable disease [3,4]. Nevertheless, only a subset of patients achieves a meaningful pathological response, and the tissue-level determinants that distinguish responders from non-responders are incompletely understood. A deeper understanding of the spatial and metabolic organization of the responding tumour microenvironment is therefore needed to refine patient selection and to identify actionable targets [5,6].

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are ectopic lymphoid aggregates that form in chronically inflamed and tumour tissues, and their presence has been associated with favourable outcomes and with response to immune-checkpoint blockade across multiple cancer types, including melanoma, sarcoma and renal-cell carcinoma [[7], [8], [9]]. In HCC specifically, intratumoral TLS have been linked to a lower risk of early recurrence, although the prognostic direction of TLS can depend on their location and maturity [10,11]. Mature TLS contain organized B-cell follicles with germinal-center activity, surrounding T-cell zones, mature dendritic cells and high endothelial venules, and their assembly is orchestrated by a stereotyped set of homeostatic chemokines including CXCL13, CCL19 and CCL21 acting through CXCR5 and CCR7 [12,13]. Despite this conceptual framework, the spatial neighbourhoods that harbour mature TLS in HCC, the cell-cell communication networks associated with them, and the relationship between TLS organization and tumour metabolic states have not been systematically resolved in the context of neoadjuvant systemic therapy.

Spatial transcriptomics now makes it possible to map cell-type composition, functional programs and ligand-receptor signaling within intact tissue architecture [14,15]. When combined with single-cell reference atlases through computational deconvolution and with neighborhood-based niche analysis [16,17], these data can reveal recurrent multicellular communities and their association with clinical phenotypes [18]. In parallel, growing evidence indicates that lipid and fatty-acid metabolism shapes anti-tumor immunity: de novo fatty-acid synthesis influences the balance of effector and regulatory T-cell differentiation, constrains CD8+ T-cell function, and is frequently elevated in tumor cells where it can foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment [19,20]. Whether fatty-acid metabolism is spatially and quantitatively coupled to TLS in human HCC remains unknown.

In parallel, tumor-associated lipid metabolism can influence immune-cell differentiation, trafficking and effector function and can reshape the nutritional, lipid and signalling environment in which lymphoid organization occurs [21,22]. De novo fatty-acid synthesis contributes to the functional specialization of T-cell populations, whereas lipid availability and membrane composition may affect receptor organization, migration and intercellular communication [19]. Nevertheless, TLS organization and lipid-metabolic reprogramming have predominantly been studied as separate features of the tumor microenvironment [23]. Although recent spatial evidence has linked tryptophan metabolism to impaired TLS maturation, it remains unknown whether mature TLS-associated immune organization is spatially related to ACC1-associated fatty-acid metabolism in HCC.

We therefore hypothesized that lymphoid-organizing chemokine programs and ACC1-associated lipid-metabolic tumor programs may define spatially contrasting, partially uncoupled states within HCC. To address this question, we integrated spatial transcriptomics from HCC treated with neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab with a single-cell reference, three bulk-transcriptomic cohorts and an independent pathological validation cohort. We identify a B-cell-enriched, mature TLS-associated niche, characterize its spatial distribution, functional programs and communication networks, and define an inverse, associative relationship between fatty-acid metabolism, chemokine signaling and TLS organization. We further derive a niche-based prognostic signature that generalizes across independent cohorts and identify ACACA (ACC1) as a marker and candidate component of a TLS-poor lipid-metabolic tumor state rather than as a demonstrated regulator of TLS-associated immunity.

Methods

Spatial transcriptomic data and patient classification

Seven 10x Genomics Visium spatial transcriptomic sections from patients with locally advanced HCC treated with neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus accession GSE238264 [24]. Sections were classified, according to the original study, as pathological responders (HCC1R, HCC2R, HCC3R, HCC4R) or non-responders (HCC5NR, HCC6NR, HCC7NR). Raw count matrices and tissue images were processed without modification of source files (Table S1).

Spatial data processing

Spatial data were processed in Python using SCANPY within the OmicVerse environment (SCANPY 1.11.5, squidpy 1.6.5) [25]. Spots outside tissue were removed, and counts were normalized to a common depth and log-transformed for visualization and gene-level analyses. After quality control, 17,292 spots were retained across the seven sections. A curated TLS transcriptional programme and immune programs (antigen presentation, B-cell activation, germinal center, T-cell activation, IFN-γ response, dendritic-cell maturation, lymphocyte chemotaxis, cytotoxicity) were scored per spot with the gene-set scoring function, using literature-based gene sets. Metabolic programs were quantified with scMetabolism KEGG pathway scores, and hypoxia and oxidative-stress programs were scored from curated gene sets.17 The overall analytical workflow is summarized in Supplementary Figure S1. Briefly, spatial transcriptomic data were integrated with a single-cell reference to identify multicellular niches and their immune and metabolic programs. The spatial transcriptomic specimens were obtained at resection following neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab. Pretreatment spatially profiled specimens were not available; consequently, the study was not designed to distinguish baseline spatial features from changes induced by either component of treatment.

Single-cell reference and deconvolution

The single-cell reference was obtained from GSE229772 [26], a longitudinal NCI-CLARITY liver-cancer cohort comprising 31 samples from 11 patients, including nine patients with HCC and two with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. In the source cohort, one patient received pembrolizumab, one received tremelimumab, eight received tremelimumab plus durvalumab, and one underwent resection alone. Therefore, the source dataset was heterogeneous with respect to diagnosis, sampling time and treatment and did not include the cabozantinib-plus-nivolumab regimen used in the spatial cohort. Following preprocessing and cell selection, 16,493 cells were retained as the single-cell reference for annotation and Tangram-based mapping [27]. The reference was used to estimate cell-type composition and was not used to transfer treatment-response labels. Major mapped lineages were evaluated according to concordance with canonical marker expression in the spatial data (Supplementary Figure S2). Spatial spots were deconvolved into cell-type fractions using Tangram in clusters mode [28], producing a spot-by-cell-type abundance matrix and per-cell-type fraction columns for each section.

Spatial niche identification

For each spot, a neighborhood cell-type composition was computed by averaging Tangram-derived fractions over spatially adjacent spots within a radius equal to 2.2 times the median nearest-neighbor distance per section. Neighborhood compositions were clustered by k-means (fixed random seed). The number of niches was not fixed a priori: k was scanned from 4 to 20 and each solution evaluated by within-cluster inertia, mean silhouette width, the Calinski-Harabasz and Davies-Bouldin indices, and the adjusted Rand index between adjacent solutions. Mean silhouette width was low and broadly flat across the whole range (0.081 to 0.095), as expected for neighbourhood-composition data in which niches grade continuously into one another, so k was treated as a choice of resolution rather than as a discrete latent structure to be recovered. k = 11 was selected as the smallest k at which the B-cell-, plasma-cell- and T-cell-enriched neighbourhood separated as a single cluster rather than remaining merged within a broader lymphocyte-rich compartment (best-match Jaccard against the primary solution 0.17 to 0.23 for k = 4 to 10 versus 0.64 to 0.74 for k = 11 to 20). Stability was further assessed at smoothing radii of 1.5 to 3.0 times the median nearest-neighbour distance and by Leiden clustering of the same composition matrix. The TLS-enriched niche (Niche5) was defined as the cluster with the highest mean TLS-overlap/B-cell composition; this cluster corresponded to k-means index 1, and all niche-indexed analyses used a Boolean niche membership flag to avoid index ambiguity. A uniform-manifold-approximation-and-projection embedding of the neighborhood-composition feature space was used to visualize niche relationships.

Cell-cell communication

Spatial ligand-receptor signaling was inferred with COMMOT [29]. TLS-organizing axes (CCL21-CCR7, CXCL13-CXCR5, CCL19-CCR7) and additional immune axes were quantified per spot from the received-signal matrices. Between-group comparisons of mean received signal were performed across all sections at the per-slide level, with significance assessed by Mann-Whitney tests, and axes were displayed across sections on shared colour scales.

Bulk cohorts and signature derivation

Three bulk HCC cohorts were used: TCGA-LIHC, GSE14520 [30] and GSE76427 [31]. Microarray probes were mapped to gene symbols using platform annotations retrieved with corresponding platform. A TLS signature score was computed as the mean z-score of a mature-TLS twelve-chemokine signature. A prognostic signature was derived from Niche5-associated genes using a machine-learning framework that benchmarks multiple survival-model families and performs embedded feature selection (Mime) [32], training on TCGA-LIHC and validating in GSE14520 and GSE76427. The selected elastic-net model (α = 0.1) retained six genes, and a risk score was computed as the linear combination of standardized expression and model coefficients. Patients were dichotomized at the median risk score for Kaplan-Meier analysis; overall survival was compared with the log-rank test, and hazard ratios were estimated by Cox proportional-hazards regression. Time-dependent receiver-operating-characteristic analysis and concordance indices were computed per cohort.

ACACA prognostic and functional analyses

ACACA prognostic analysis was performed in each bulk cohort by dichotomizing samples at the median expression for Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank test) and by fitting Cox models on standardized ACACA expression to estimate hazard ratios per standard deviation (lifelines). Correlations between ACACA (and other fatty-acid-metabolism enzymes) and immune-function signatures, TLS chemokines and the TLS signature were computed as Spearman coefficients, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction within cohorts. Immune-function signatures used the same gene sets as the spatial programme scores, with a dedicated CD8+ T-cell signature.

Spatial hotspot co-localization

For the spatial co-localization analysis, ACACA expression, CD8+ T-cell fraction, the antigen-presentation program and the TLS program were smoothed over spatial neighbors (radius equal to twice the median nearest-neighbor distance). Within each section, hotspots were defined as the top decile of each smoothed signal. The analysis was repeated across hotspot definitions from the top 5% to the top 25%, and in a threshold-free form using continuous Spearman correlations between the smoothed signals. Co-occurrence of ACACA hotspots with each target hotspot was quantified by odds ratios and Fisher exact tests, both per section and pooled across all seven sections, and per-section Spearman correlations of the smoothed signals were computed.

Pathological validation of spatially organized mature TLS and ACC1-associated lipid metabolism

An independent pathological validation cohort of 69 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded HCC specimens was used to validate the spatial-transcriptomic findings at the protein level. TLS status was assessed on H&E-stained and immunostained sections. Candidate tumor-associated TLS were initially screened on whole-slide H&E-stained sections and subsequently confirmed on spatially matched immunostained sections; H&E morphology alone was not considered sufficient for final classification. Only organized lymphoid aggregates with a largest cross-sectional diameter meeting the largest cross-sectional diameter criterion of at least 150 μm were evaluated as candidate TLS; smaller aggregates were excluded. A confirmed TLS was defined as a compact, organized CD20-positive B-cell aggregate accompanied by an adjacent or partially compartmentalized CD8-positive T-cell-rich region. Diffuse lymphocytic infiltration, unstructured immune-cell aggregates, and H&E-suspected aggregates that were not confirmed by immunostaining were not classified as TLS. A tumor was considered TLS-positive when at least one immunohistochemically confirmed TLS was identified. TLS maturation was evaluated separately according to established E-TLS/PFL-TLS/SFL-TLS pathological frameworks. E-TLS lacked a discernible CD21-positive follicular dendritic-cell network; PFL-TLS contained a CD21-positive follicular dendritic-cell network without a distinct CD23-positive germinal-center-like compartment; and SFL-TLS contained organized B- and T-cell regions together with both a CD21-positive follicular dendritic-cell network and a CD23-positive germinal-center-like compartment. Only SFL-TLS were classified as displaying mature TLS-like architecture. TLS classification was independently performed by two observers who were blinded to the clinicopathological and molecular data. Inter-observer agreement for binary case-level TLS status was assessed using unweighted Cohen’s kappa based on the independent assessments recorded before consensus review. Discrepant cases were subsequently reviewed jointly to establish the final consensus classification.

Four-micrometre-thick serial sections were prepared from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks. Routine haematoxylin-and-eosin staining was performed using a Leica ST5010 Autostainer XL (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) according to a validated laboratory protocol. Serial sections were subjected to immunohistochemical staining for CCL21, CXCL13, CCR7, ACC1, FABP5 and PLIN2 on a BOND-III automated staining system (Leica Biosystems). Following automated deparaffinization and antibody-specific heat-induced epitope retrieval, staining was visualized using the BOND Polymer Refine Detection system, with polymer-based horseradish peroxidase and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with haematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence was performed on additional serial sections using the Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Sequential staining cycles consisted of primary-antibody incubation, horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated secondary-polymer detection, tyramide-signal-amplification fluorophore deposition and heat-mediated antibody stripping before application of the next marker. The multiplex panel comprised CD20, CD8, CD21, CD23, CCL21 and ACC1/ACACA, with DAPI used for nuclear counterstaining. Single-stained sections, unstained sections and appropriate negative controls were used to establish the spectral library, assess signal specificity and determine marker-specific detection thresholds.

Bright-field H&E and immunohistochemistry slides were digitized at 40 × magnification using a Pannoramic 250 Flash III whole-slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). Multiplexed immunofluorescence slides were scanned at 20 × magnification using a Vectra Polaris quantitative pathology imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), followed by multispectral unmixing and removal of tissue autofluorescence using inForm software. Subsequent image annotation and quantitative analysis were performed using HALO (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA) and QuPath. TLS regions and spatially matched non-TLS tumour regions were annotated on whole-slide images by pathologists blinded to the clinicopathological and molecular data. Necrotic, haemorrhagic, folded or poorly stained areas were excluded. For multiplexed immunofluorescence, DAPI-positive nuclei were segmented using a watershed-based algorithm, and marker-positive cells were classified using marker-specific intensity thresholds established from the negative and single-stained controls. Cell densities and marker-positive areas were normalized to the analysed tissue area.

For immunohistochemistry, DAB and haematoxylin signals were separated by colour deconvolution. Marker expression was quantified using an H-score calculated as follows: 1 × the percentage of weakly positive cells + 2 × the percentage of moderately positive cells + 3 × the percentage of strongly positive cells, yielding a score ranging from 0 to 300. Three to five non-overlapping viable tumour regions were evaluated per case, and the mean value was used as the case-level measurement for statistical analysis. The same marker-specific thresholds and scoring criteria were applied to all specimens within the validation cohort.

Statistical considerations and reproducibility

Because Visium spots are not independent within a section, all between-group comparisons in the spatial cohort were conducted at the patient/slide level (n = 4 responders versus 3 non-responders); spot-level correlations are reported as descriptive measures with accompanying slide-level estimates where applicable. Niche5 was defined on transcriptional and architectural grounds and confirmed as mature TLS at the protein level by multiplexed immunofluorescence. Random seeds, software versions and input/output paths were recorded for all analyses. The spatial and single-cell datasets were not merged into a common expression space: Tangram was applied in clusters mode, which learns a mapping between annotated reference clusters and spatial spots rather than projecting both datasets into a shared embedding, and the single-cell reference itself was integrated across patients with Harmony prior to annotation. Cross-study technical differences therefore do not propagate additively into the spatial data. Figures were prepared following a consistent vector-graphics specification with a fixed colour scheme that avoids red, pink and magenta hues to prevent confusion with haematoxylin-and-eosin staining.

Results

TLS-associated spatial patterns differ between pathological responders and non-responders

To characterize the spatial organization of TLS in neoadjuvant-treated HCC, we analysed seven 10x Visium sections derived from patients who received neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab,23 comprising four pathological responders (HCC1R-HCC4R) and three non-responders (HCC5NR-HCC7NR), and yielding 17,292 transcriptionally profiled tissue spots after quality control. Haematoxylin-and-eosin imaging revealed lymphoid aggregates predominantly within and at the margins of responder tumors (Fig. 1A). Quantification of a curated TLS transcriptional program demonstrated higher TLS abundance per section in responders than in non-responders (Fig. 1B, C), and spatial maps of the program localized TLS-high regions to discrete foci in responder tissue (Fig. 1C). Canonical TLS marker genes, including CXCL13, CCL21, CCL19, MS4A1, CD79A and CR2, were more highly expressed in responders (Fig. 1D), and immune programs reflecting B-cell activation, lymphocyte chemotaxis and T-cell activation were correspondingly elevated (Fig. 1E). Because Visium spots are not independent observations within a section, all between-group comparisons were evaluated at the patient/slide level. Together, these exploratory observations suggest that TLS-associated transcriptional activity may be more prominent in responding HCC specimens and provide a rationale for examining the underlying multicellular spatial niches. Given the limited number of sections, these patterns should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of an association with treatment response.

Single-cell reference-based deconvolution resolves the cellular composition of HCC tissue spots

To assign cellular identities to spatial spots, we assembled a single-cell RNA-sequencing reference from publicly available HCC immunotherapy specimens (GSE229772), retaining hepatocellular samples and excluding intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma cases. After integration and balanced down-sampling, the reference comprised 16,493 cells annotated into twelve major lineages, including B cells, plasma cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, natural-killer/innate-T cells, proliferating T/NK cells, myeloid/tumor-associated macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts and hepatocyte/malignant cells (Supplementary Figure S2A). Lineage assignments were supported by canonical marker expression and reference-cell composition (Supplementary Figure S2B-D). We then applied Tangram in clusters mode to deconvolve each Visium spot into cell-type fractions. The resulting spatial maps recovered the expected tissue architecture, with B cells and plasma cells concentrated in lymphoid aggregates, CD8+ T cells distributed through immune-rich stroma, and endothelial cells delineating vascular regions (Supplementary Figure S2E). Extended spatial maps of all twelve Tangram-derived lineages, together with the mean lineage composition in responders versus non-responders and the composition of each individual section, are provided in Supplementary Figure S3A-C. These per-spot compositions provided the quantitative substrate for downstream niche identification.

Spatial niche analysis identifies a mature TLS-associated Niche5

To identify recurrent multicellular communities, we computed neighbourhood cell-type compositions for each spot and clustered them into eleven spatial niches by k-means (Fig. 2A). Among these, Niche5 spatially overlapped with regions showing high TLS-program scores (Fig. 2B) and was characterized by enrichment of B cells together with plasma cells and T cells (Fig. 2C). Gene Ontology analysis of Niche5-associated genes identified processes related to lymphocyte activation, B-cell receptor signalling, B-cell activation and T-helper-cell lineage commitment (Fig. 2D-E and Table S2). Consistent with this lymphoid-organizing phenotype, representative spatial maps showed expression of the TLS-associated chemokines CCL19, CCL21 and CXCL13 and their corresponding receptors CCR7 and CXCR5 within immune-rich regions (Supplementary Figure S4A-B). These maps provide qualitative expression-level context and are not intended as a section-level statistical comparison.

On the basis of these cellular and transcriptional features, we annotated Niche5 as a mature TLS-associated spatial niche rather than as a direct histological representation of mature TLS. The identification of this niche was robust to alternative analytical settings. Across the evaluated range of cluster numbers, k = 11 represented the smallest resolution at which the B-cell-, plasma-cell- and T-cell-enriched neighbourhood was recovered as a distinct cluster. The TLS-associated niche was also recovered across alternative neighbourhood radii and when k-means was replaced by Leiden clustering, although its precise boundaries and agreement with the primary solution varied across parameter settings (Supplementary Figure S5A-D).

Niche5 abundance and neighbourhood enrichment captured distinct properties of the tissue. Abundance quantified the proportion of spots assigned to Niche5 within each section, whereas neighbourhood enrichment quantified the preferential adjacency of Niche5 spots and therefore their tendency to form spatially coherent regions. Overall Niche5 abundance was numerically higher in responders but differed only at the level of a trend (P = 0.114). By contrast, Niche5 neighbourhood enrichment was greater in responders in the primary section-level analysis (P = 0.029). Thus, the response-associated difference was more apparent in the spatial aggregation of the niche than in the total proportion of Niche5 spots. These findings suggest that similar quantities of TLS-associated spots may differ in their degree of spatial organization. However, given the small cohort and the sensitivity of statistical significance to section-level resampling, this distinction remains exploratory. The per-section frequency of the mature TLS-associated niche is additionally shown in Supplementary Figure S5E.

TLS-organizing chemokine signaling is stronger in responders

Because TLS assembly depends on homeostatic chemokine signaling, we quantified spatial cell-cell communication along defined ligand-receptor axes using COMMOT. The TLS-organizing axes CCL21-CCR7, CXCL13-CXCR5 and CCL19-CCR7 showed directionally higher received signals in responders than in non-responders when summarized at the section level (Fig. 3A). Spatial maps of the inferred communication signals across all seven sections are shown on shared colour scales in Fig. 3B. Representative spatial expression maps of CCL19, CCL21 and CXCL13 and their corresponding receptors CCR7 and CXCR5, together with additional immune chemokines and receptors, provide expression-level spatial context for the inferred communication signals (Supplementary Figure S4A-B). Because these supplementary panels show representative responder and non-responder sections, they are interpreted as qualitative spatial support rather than as an independent section-level statistical comparison. Collectively, these observations support a concordant but exploratory association between TLS-organizing chemokine signaling and pathological response.

Niche metabolic states reveal an inverse association between fatty-acid metabolism and TLS organization

To relate niche identity to function, we scored immune and metabolic programs across all niches. Niches differed systematically in both immune programs and metabolic states (Fig. 4A, B), and contrasts focused on Niche5 confirmed elevated antigen presentation and lymphocyte-chemotaxis programs in responder Niche5 relative to non-responder Niche5. Within Niche5, fatty-acid metabolism was the pathway most inversely correlated with the TLS program (Spearman r = −0.35) and was also negatively correlated with chemokine programs, whereas other metabolic axes showed weaker associations (Fig. 4C-D). Spatial maps on a representative responder section illustrated contrasting distributions of fatty-acid metabolism, chemokine and TLS programs (Fig. 4E). Pairwise correlations further showed coordinated variation in which lower fatty-acid-metabolism scores accompanied higher chemokine and TLS-program scores (Fig. 4F). These relationships are associative and do not establish a directional metabolism-chemokine-TLS pathway.

A six-gene score provides a transcriptomic readout of the Niche5-associated lymphoid program

To test whether the cellular program of the TLS-enriched niche carries prognostic information beyond the spatial cohort, we derived a transcriptional signature from Niche5-associated genes and performed feature selection within bulk HCC cohorts using a machine-learning framework that compares many model families. Training on TCGA-LIHC and validating in GSE14520 and GSE76427, an elastic-net model retained six genes-CCR7, SELL, GZMK, IL7R, CD79A and MS4A1-each contributing a protective coefficient consistent with a TLS/lymphoid identity (Supplementary Figure S6A-B). Benchmarking across the candidate survival-learning workflows showed that random survival forest achieved the highest mean concordance index (0.672), whereas the elastic-net models showed mean concordance indices of approximately 0.61 (Table S3). The resulting risk score separated patients into prognostically distinct groups in all three cohorts, with higher risk corresponding to shorter overall survival (TCGA-LIHC hazard ratio 1.32, P = 8.6 × 10–4; GSE14520 hazard ratio 1.35, P = 4.9 × 10–3; GSE76427 hazard ratio 2.77, P = 6.5 × 10–4) and time-dependent areas under the curve in the expected range (concordance indices 0.59–0.65; Supplementary Figure S6C-D). After adjustment for the clinicopathological covariates available in TCGA-LIHC, however, the risk score was no longer independently associated with overall survival (adjusted hazard ratio 1.19, 95% confidence interval 0.94 to 1.53, P = 0.15), and clinical covariates alone achieved a higher concordance index than the signature alone (0.637 versus 0.591), with no significant gain from adding the signature (likelihood-ratio P = 0.15). The signature is therefore best regarded as a compact readout of intratumoral lymphoid organization rather than as a candidate clinical test. These results indicate that the lymphoid program captured by Niche5 is prognostically informative across heterogeneous bulk cohorts, while underscoring that bulk validation tests the transferability of the signature rather than the spatial niche itself.

ACACA expression is inversely associated with TLS-related immune programs and clinical outcomes in bulk HCC cohorts

The inverse association between fatty-acid metabolism and TLS-related programs within Niche5 prompted us to examine whether key lipogenic enzymes were similarly associated with TLS-related immunity in independent data. Across TCGA-LIHC, GSE14520 and GSE76427, fatty-acid-synthesis enzymes-most consistently ACACA, FASN and ACLY-were negatively correlated with TLS-organizing chemokines and with a mature-TLS chemokine signature, with ACACA showing the strongest and most reproducible negative association (mean Spearman r with CCL21 −0.38 and with the TLS signature −0.28; Fig. 5A). To provide orthogonal spatial validation at the protein level, we performed multiplexed immunofluorescence co-staining for ACC1 together with CCL21 or CCR7. ACC1-rich regions showed limited spatial overlap with both CCL21 and CCR7, and CCL21-positive or CCR7-positive immune regions were preferentially observed in areas with lower ACC1 staining, indicating a reciprocal spatial distribution between ACC1 expression and TLS-organizing chemokine signals (Fig. 5B). These observations are consistent with the negative associations identified in the bulk transcriptomic analyses.

Higher ACACA expression was associated with worse overall survival, reaching significance in TCGA-LIHC (hazard ratio, 1.27; P = 9 × 10–3) and showing concordant trends in GSE14520 and GSE76427 (P = 0.085 and P = 0.076, respectively; Fig. 5C). ACACA expression was likewise negatively correlated with antigen-presentation and CD8-positive T-cell programmes in TCGA-LIHC (Fig. 5D), and with antigen presentation, CD8-positive T-cell, cytotoxicity, T-cell activation, B-cell activation, IFN-gamma response, dendritic-cell maturation and lymphocyte-chemotaxis programmes across all three cohorts (Fig. 5E). Returning to the spatial data, ACACA was sparsely detected at the spot level, but pooled neighborhood-smoothed hotspots across all seven sections showed reduced co-occurrence of ACACA hotspots with antigen-presentation, CD8-positive T-cell and TLS hotspots (odds ratio 0.66 for antigen presentation, P = 8 × 10–6; odds ratios 0.83 for CD8-positive T cells and TLS, both P < 0.05). Odds ratios remained below unity across hotspot definitions from the top 5% to the top 25%, and the threshold-free analysis gave concordant negative correlations (Supplementary Figure S7). Across spatial transcriptomic, bulk transcriptomic and pathological analyses, higher ACACA/ACC1 expression was consistently associated with lower TLS-related immune activity. These observational findings do not establish whether ACC1 participates causally in TLS organization or whether both features reflect another underlying tumor or microenvironmental state.

Multiplexed immunofluorescence and pathological validation confirm the spatial separation between mature TLS and ACC1-associated lipid metabolism

To validate the spatial-transcriptomic inference at the protein and tissue-architecture level, we performed multiplexed immunofluorescence and serial immunohistochemistry on HCC tumor sections, followed by quantitative analysis in an independent pathological validation cohort of 69 HCC specimens. Multiplexed immunofluorescence demonstrated organized lymphoid aggregates composed of dense CD20-positive B-cell follicles, adjacent CD8-positive T-cell infiltration, CD21-positive follicular dendritic-cell networks and CD23-positive activated/mature B-cell compartments, supporting the presence of mature TLS-like architecture (Fig. 6A). CCL21 signal was enriched within these lymphoid aggregates, whereas ACC1/ACACA showed a contrasting spatial pattern and limited staining within the CD20/CD21/CD23/CCL21-rich TLS core. Serial immunohistochemistry further confirmed that TLS regions were enriched for the canonical lymphoid-organizing molecules CCL21, CXCL13 and CCR7, whereas ACC1 and the lipid-metabolism-associated markers FABP5 and PLIN2 were preferentially distributed in non-TLS tumor compartments (Fig. 6B, C).

Before consensus review, the two observers agreed on the case-level TLS classification in 66 of 69 specimens in the pathological validation cohort, corresponding to an overall agreement of 95.7% and a Cohen’s κ of 0.91. The three discrepant cases were jointly reviewed, after which one was classified as TLS-positive and two as TLS-negative, resulting in the final consensus classification of 25 TLS-positive (36.2%) and 44 (63.8%) TLS-negative tumors. When samples were stratified according to ACC1 expression, TLS positivity was markedly less frequent in ACC1-high tumors than in ACC1-low tumors (17.1% versus 55.9%; Fig. 6D), supporting an inverse association between ACC1 expression and TLS positivity. Quantitative immunostaining analysis showed that TLS-positive tumors had significantly lower ACC1, FABP5 and PLIN2 expression than TLS-negative tumors. Conversely, TLS-positive tumors showed significantly higher expression of the TLS-organizing chemokine-axis markers CCL21, CXCL13 and CCR7 (Fig. 6E). These findings indicate that TLS-positive HCC is characterized by a chemokine-rich immune microenvironment and a comparatively lipid-metabolism-low phenotype.

To further evaluate whether lipid-metabolism-associated markers were independently associated with TLS status, we performed multivariable logistic regression incorporating clinicopathological covariates. High ACC1 expression remained significantly associated with a lower probability of TLS positivity (OR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.07–0.68; P = 0.008), and high FABP5 expression showed a similar negative association (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.12–0.96; P = 0.041). High PLIN2 expression also showed a concordant trend toward reduced TLS positivity, although this did not reach statistical significance (OR, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.14–1.08; P = 0.070). By contrast, tumor size, vascular invasion and poor differentiation were not significantly associated with TLS positivity in this model (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these protein-level and cohort-level validation data support the spatial-transcriptomic observation that mature TLS occupy chemokine-rich immune niches that are spatially and quantitatively uncoupled from ACC1-associated lipid-metabolic tumor compartments.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated spatial transcriptomics, single-cell reference-based deconvolution, neighborhood niche analysis, bulk-cohort validation and protein-level pathological validation to define spatial immune architecture following neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab in HCC. We identified a B-cell-, plasma-cell- and T-cell-enriched multicellular niche, designated Niche5, that exhibited transcriptional, spatial and histological features of mature TLS. This niche was more spatially aggregated in responders and was accompanied by stronger inferred TLS-organizing chemokine signaling, including the CCL21-CCR7, CXCL13-CXCR5 and CCL19-CCR7 axes. In parallel, fatty-acid metabolism, particularly ACACA/ACC1-associated de novo lipogenesis, was inversely associated with TLS programs, chemokine expression, antigen presentation and CD8-positive T-cell immunity. A six-gene Niche5-derived lymphoid signature stratified survival across independent HCC cohorts, supporting its use as a transcriptomic readout of this spatially defined immune program. Finally, multiplexed immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry in an independent pathological validation cohort confirmed that mature TLS occupy chemokine-rich immune niches that are spatially and quantitatively separated from ACC1-, FABP5- and PLIN2-positive lipid-metabolic tumor compartments. Together, these findings characterize response-associated HCC specimens by the spatial organization of mature TLS and their reduced spatial co-occurrence with lipid-metabolic tumor states. Because the response-associated comparisons were derived from only seven sections in a single publicly available dataset, these observations are exploratory and hypothesis-generating and do not establish that Niche5 or ACC1-related lipid metabolism independently predicts response to the combination regimen.

The identification of Niche5 extends the current understanding of TLS in HCC from a histopathological observation to a spatially resolved, multicellular immune niche. Previous studies have largely evaluated TLS according to their presence, density or intratumoral location, whereas our analysis resolved TLS as a recurrent neighborhood state with a defined cellular composition and functional program [33,34]. Niche5 was enriched for B cells, plasma cells and T cells, spatially co-localized with TLS-high transcriptional regions, and showed biological processes related to lymphocyte activation, B-cell receptor signaling, B-cell activation and T-helper-cell lineage commitment. These features indicate that Niche5 represents more than a passive lymphoid aggregate; rather, it reflects an organized immune microenvironment capable of supporting local antigen-driven immune interactions [35,36]. This interpretation was further strengthened by multiplexed immunofluorescence, which confirmed CD20-positive B-cell follicles, adjacent CD8-positive T-cell infiltration, CD21-positive follicular dendritic-cell networks and CD23-positive activated or mature B-cell compartments. Together, these data support the concept that mature TLS in HCC constitute a spatially organized immune-response niche, in which B-cell, T-cell and stromal lymphoid components are assembled into a coordinated architecture [37,38].

A key feature of the response-associated TLS niche was stronger inferred signaling along canonical lymphoid-organizing chemokine axes [[39], [40], [41]]. In responders, received signals for CCL21-CCR7, CXCL13-CXCR5 and CCL19-CCR7 were directionally stronger than in non-responders, consistent with greater local lymphoid organization [42]. These axes have established complementary roles in TLS biology: CCL19 and CCL21 acting through CCR7 support the recruitment and positioning of T cells and dendritic cells within T-cell zones, whereas CXCL13-CXCR5 signaling promotes B-cell recruitment, follicular organization and B/T-cell compartmentalization. The enrichment of CCL21, CXCL13 and CCR7 in TLS regions by immunohistochemistry provides protein-level support for the presence of these ligand-receptor components in the spatially defined TLS niche [18,39,40,43]. In this cohort, the association with pathological response therefore appeared more closely related to coordinated lymphoid spatial organization than to immune-cell abundance alone [44]. Such organization could plausibly support local antigen presentation, lymphocyte positioning and immune-cell retention, but the present observational data do not establish these processes as determinants of response to cabozantinib plus nivolumab [45,46].

ACC1-associated lipid metabolism and mature TLS-associated immune organization defined spatially contrasting states in HCC [47,48]. Within Niche5, fatty-acid metabolism was the metabolic program most strongly and inversely correlated with the TLS program and chemokine signaling. Across three independent bulk HCC cohorts, ACACA was reproducibly and negatively associated with TLS chemokines, antigen-presentation programs, CD8-positive T-cell signatures and cytotoxic immune activity, while the pathological validation cohort provided concordant protein-level evidence [21,49,50]. ACACA-rich regions showed reduced spatial co-occurrence with antigen-presentation, CD8-positive T-cell and TLS-program signals, and TLS-positive tumors showed lower protein expression of ACC1 and other lipid-associated markers. These findings support an inverse and partially spatially uncoupled relationship but do not demonstrate complete mutual exclusivity or direct metabolic suppression of TLS formation [[51], [52]]. ACC1 should therefore be regarded as a marker and candidate component of a TLS-poor lipid-metabolic tumor state rather than as a demonstrated negative regulator of TLS formation.

The causal direction of this relationship remains unresolved [53]. Increased ACC1-associated lipid metabolism may contribute to a tissue environment less compatible with lymphoid organization; conversely, an established lymphocyte-rich microenvironment may alter tumor-cell metabolic programs. Alternatively, both states may be regulated by common upstream processes, including tumor-cell differentiation, oncogenic signalling, hypoxia, stromal composition and epigenetic remodeling [[54], [55], [56]]. ACC1 catalyses the committed step of de novo fatty-acid synthesis and supports the generation of membrane lipids and signalling intermediates in proliferating cells [19]. Tumor-cell lipogenesis could therefore alter extracellular lipid availability or competition for metabolic substrates, while selected fatty acids and oxidized lipids can impair tumor-infiltrating CD8-positive T-cell function [57,58]. These possibilities provide biological context but remain mechanistic hypotheses in the absence of compartment-specific perturbation experiments.

B-cell activation and germinal-center reactions similarly require dynamic metabolic remodeling. Germinal-center B cells can use fatty-acid oxidation to meet energetic demands, whereas lipid synthesis and desaturation contribute to membrane biogenesis, proliferation and plasma-cell differentiation [14]. An altered lipid environment could therefore be related to the B-cell and plasma-cell compartments required for follicular organization, although the direction and cellular specificity remain unresolved. Membrane-lipid composition can also affect receptor nanoclustering and chemokine-directed migration, providing a possible context for CCR7- or CXCR5-dependent recruitment and compartmentalization during TLS development [59,60]. However, we did not measure membrane composition, chemokine-receptor dynamics or lymphocyte migration. These mechanisms should therefore be considered hypotheses rather than explanations established by the present data.

The prognostic performance of the Niche5-derived signature further supports the clinical relevance of this spatially defined TLS program [60]. The six retained genes, CCR7, SELL, GZMK, IL7R, CD79A and MS4A1, collectively represent lymphocyte trafficking, T-cell activation or memory-like states, cytotoxic immune activity and B-cell identity. Their protective coefficients are consistent with the interpretation that Niche5 captures an organized lymphoid immune state rather than a nonspecific inflammatory signal. The ability of this signature to stratify overall survival in TCGA-LIHC, GSE14520 and GSE76427 suggests that the immune program embedded in the mature TLS niche is transferable across independent patient populations and technical platforms. This finding is important because the spatial cohort itself was limited in size and treatment context, whereas the bulk cohorts provide broader evidence that TLS-related lymphoid organization is linked to clinically meaningful tumor biology in HCC [61]. Nevertheless, the bulk signature should not be regarded as a direct substitute for spatial TLS assessment. Bulk transcriptomic data cannot distinguish organized TLS from diffuse immune-cell infiltration, nor can they resolve the spatial relationship between TLS and lipid-metabolic tumor regions [14,60]. Therefore, the Niche5-derived signature is best interpreted as a clinically relevant lymphoid surrogate that complements, rather than replaces, spatial and histological evaluation of mature TLS.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the spatial transcriptomic cohort was limited to seven Visium sections, comprising four pathological responders and three non-responders, and was derived from a single publicly available neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab dataset. Although all between-group comparisons were performed at the patient/slide level to avoid inflation of statistical significance through spot-level pseudo-replication, the small number of independent specimens substantially limits statistical power and generalizability. The observed differences between responders and non-responders should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a validated predictive biomarker. The three independent bulk cohorts support the prognostic relevance and cross-cohort transferability of the Niche5-derived lymphoid program, but bulk transcriptomic data cannot validate its spatial organization or treatment-specific predictive value. Similarly, the independent pathological cohort supports the tissue-level inverse association between TLS organization and ACC1-related lipid metabolism but did not include matched immunotherapy-response data. Larger, multi-institutional spatial cohorts with standardized treatment exposure, pathological response assessment and longitudinal clinical outcomes will be required to validate reproducibility and potential predictive relevance. We also note the boundaries of the present sensitivity analyses: the niche pipeline was not repeated with an alternative deconvolution method, formal cross-section integration diagnostics were not computed, and the six-gene signature was not benchmarked against previously published immune signatures. Establishing causality will require genetic deletion or knockdown of ACACA and pharmacological ACC1 inhibition in immunocompetent HCC models, followed by assessment of TLS-organizing chemokines, antigen presentation, CD8-positive T-cell recruitment and the initiation and maturation of organized lymphoid structures. Rescue of ACC1 activity and analysis of tumor-cell-specific versus immune-cell-intrinsic effects will be necessary to determine specificity and cellular directionality.

Second, Visium captures mixed-cell transcriptomes within each spot and therefore cannot fully resolve cell-intrinsic expression or direct cell-cell interactions at single-cell spatial resolution [62]. We attempted to mitigate this limitation by integrating single-cell reference-based deconvolution, neighborhood-level niche analysis and multiplexed immunofluorescence; nevertheless, higher-resolution spatial platforms will be needed to refine the cellular sources of chemokine and metabolic signals [63]. Third, the relationship between ACC1-associated lipid metabolism and TLS remains correlative. Our data demonstrate consistent spatial, transcriptomic and protein-level inverse associations between ACC1/FABP5/PLIN2-positive lipid-metabolic compartments and mature TLS, but they do not prove that ACC1 directly suppresses TLS formation or maintenance. Fourth, although the independent pathological validation cohort confirmed the negative association between lipid-metabolic markers and TLS positivity, this cohort did not include matched treatment-response data and therefore cannot independently establish predictive value. Finally, the Niche5-derived bulk signature provides a lymphoid transcriptomic readout, but bulk data cannot distinguish organized TLS from diffuse immune infiltration or capture the spatial relationship between immune and metabolic compartments. Despite these limitations, the convergence of spatial transcriptomics, single-cell-informed deconvolution, bulk-cohort analysis and protein-level pathology supports a reproducible inverse association between mature TLS organization and ACC1-related lipid-metabolic tumor states in HCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides exploratory evidence of a mature TLS-enriched spatial niche associated with pathological response following neoadjuvant cabozantinib plus nivolumab in HCC. Our findings identify an inverse and partially spatially uncoupled relationship between a mature TLS-associated immune niche and ACC1-linked lipid metabolism. Functional studies are required to determine whether ACC1 participates causally in the regulation of TLS organization. Given the limited spatial cohort, the response-associated findings should be considered hypothesis-generating, and larger independent spatial cohorts with matched treatment-response data are required to assess their reproducibility, generalizability and potential predictive value.
