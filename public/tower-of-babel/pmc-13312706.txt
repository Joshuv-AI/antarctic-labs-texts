Saccade-synchronized alpha rhythms predict strength of memory trace of stimulus orientation
Authors: Chiara Terzo, David C. Burr, Xin-Yu Xie, Maria Concetta Morrone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSaccadic eye movements allow us to sample the world sequentially, but pose challenges to the visual system, which must integrate information across successive fixations. The mechanisms subserving the integration may share properties with “serial dependence,” the assimilative bias caused by previous sensory experience on current perception.ResultsWe investigated serial dependence during saccadic eye movements by measuring ERP responses from participants during visually driven saccadic eye movements while they viewed a brief Gabor patch, presented at various orientations (± 35°, ± 45°, or ± 55°), and then reproduced its orientation. Our findings demonstrate that both alpha and beta oscillations are synchronized by the saccades. Importantly, we found that the strength of alpha oscillations correlates (negatively) with the magnitude of serial dependence, particular in the right parietal cortex, while that of beta oscillations does not.ConclusionsThese results provide evidence that pre-saccadic alpha oscillations contain information to be carried across saccades, probably fundamental for integration of visual information across saccades, and trans-saccadic perceptual stability.

Background

Saccadic eye movements, which sample the world 2–3 times per second, present many challenges to the visual system [1–4]. One of the greatest is integrating pre-saccadic with post-saccadic information, to create a stable representation of the world.

There exists a good deal of evidence that integration does occur across saccades. Although integration does not involve simply superimposing the pre- with the post-saccadic images, as once suggested [5], much useful information is transferred across saccades [6]. For example, motion [7] and also orientation [8] signals are remembered across saccades, with clear spatial selectivity. Trans-saccadic integration between peripheral and foveal stimuli (the typical task of the visual system) is particularly strong, often near optimal [9–11]. The underlying neural mechanisms of trans-saccadic integration has been studied by fMRI, TMS, and EEG, implicating parietal and occipital cortices [12–21]. Some of this evidence also suggests that a memory signal is transferred across saccades (rather than to integration between early visual processing centers), with a crucial role of posterior parietal cortex in mediating the mnemonic performance [22]. In particular, the right parietal cortex is heavily involved for rightwards saccades during memory tasks.

Integration of pre- and post-saccadic information may involve similar mechanisms that subserve “serial dependence,” a recently described phenomenon that clearly demonstrates that prior visual information combines with the current stimulus to bias perception [23–25]. Manassi and Whitney [26] suggested that merging past with present perception helps preserve continuity by creating what they term “continuity fields.” Serial dependence occurs both for simple visual features, such as orientation or color, and complex features, such as face identity and gender, beauty, body weight, and quality of art [27–33]. Studies suggest that the bias induced by serial dependence is not a system flaw but rather leads to more efficient perception, by taking advantage of the temporal redundancy of natural scenes [34].

Serial dependence is usually measured in the laboratory with a sequence of images, examining the effect of the previous image on the current one. However, this is a very unnatural condition. A more ecological testbed for serial dependence is vision at the time of saccades, which continuously displace the retinal image, necessitating appropriate integration of past memory signals and present stimulus. The little available evidence suggests that serial dependence is particularly strong around the time of saccades, maximal at saccadic onset [35]. This is consistent with theoretical modeling of serial dependence [36], which predicts stronger integration of past stimuli when there is high uncertainty about the current stimuli: saccades are a time of maximal uncertainty for vision [37, 38].

A good deal of evidence has accumulated to suggest that neural oscillations in the alpha and low-beta ranges are implicated in serial dependence, and in predictive perception in general. Theoretical arguments suggest that generative feedback from higher to lower visual stations could create reverberations [39, 40], leading to neural oscillations in the alpha range [41, 42]. Ho et al. [43] showed that alpha-frequency oscillations in auditory judgements occurred only when the previous stimulus was presented to the same ear as the current stimulus. Similarly, judgments of the gender of faces showed strong oscillations in the low-beta frequency (synchronized to a voluntary hand movement), which depended on the gender of the previous face: 14 Hz if the previous face was female, 17 Hz if male [44]. Ranieri and colleagues [45] decoded an EEG signal associated with perceptual priors from past stimuli, suggesting coexistence in EEG scalp potential of prior and current information. The accuracy of decoding previous responses correlated with serial dependence effects, strongly implicating their role in predictive processes [46, 47]. More recently, they have repeated the study for the perception of faces [45], showing that the information about previous stimuli was mainly in the beta range, corroborating the psychophysical results of Bell et al. [44].

In these human studies, oscillations were revealed by behavioral measures, when stimulus presentation time is synchronized with action onset. Many actions can induce synchronization of endogenous rhythms. When measuring sensitivity, the predominant rhythms are at low frequencies in the theta range [48]. Saccadic eye movements, both during natural reading [49] and towards flashed targets [1, 50, 51], also synchronize oscillatory behavioral performance, again in theta or delta ranges for sensitivity, and in alpha ranges for criteria [51, 52]. More recently, Xie et al. [52] reported strong saccade-synchronized behavioral oscillations in the alpha range (~ 9 Hz), which were linked to serial dependence, but not to static priors such as “regression to the mean.” The synchronization of the endogenous rhythms leading to oscillatory behavioral performance are less well known. In monkeys, saccades induce modulation of local field potentials (LFP) in the primary visual cortex (V1), aligning neuronal spike timing with specific phases of these oscillations [53]. Neural oscillations in the alpha range have also been implicated in saccadic binding and remapping [54]: alpha oscillations in primate area V4 become coherent between cells whose receptive fields correspond to pre- and post-saccadic stimulation, similar to the process of remapping, suggesting a role of alpha oscillations in remapping from pre- to post-saccadic fixations.

To date, there is little electrophysiological data in humans associated with saccade-synchronized alpha oscillations. One problem is eliminating the strong 10-Hz oscillations generated by the stimulus presentations. The major evidence for saccade-synchronized alpha rhythms is a study during natural reading [49], where the power of the pre-saccadic synchronized alpha is modulated by the usage-frequency of the word targeted by the saccade, showing that alpha rhythms may play a role in perceptual processing, rather than only in the generation of the saccade. Again, the source of the modulations is in the parietal-motor area in the right hemisphere (Ba7).

The present study investigates the interrelationship between neural oscillations and serial dependence of orientation signals during saccadic eye movements. We analyzed pre-saccadic EEG of participants as they made visually driven saccadic eye movements, then reported the orientation of a briefly flashed Gabor patch. Alpha oscillations became synchronized with the onset of saccades. Importantly, the magnitude of alpha oscillations correlated (negatively) across participants with the magnitude of the serial dependence effect.

Results

Behavioral oscillations

Participants made 16° left–right saccades, then reproduced the perceived orientation of a small, briefly presented grating patch by rotating a visible line by mouse. Reproduction was not veridical, but tended to be biased systematically towards the previous stimulus, showing clear serial dependence, as previously reported [23, 34–36, 52]. The average bias towards the previous stimulus was 2.5°, averaged across all participants and presentation times. However, while the average is clearly positive (t(19) = 3.55, p = 0.002), there was considerable spread in the magnitude of serial dependence for individual participants, some showing repulsion rather than attraction, by as much as 5°. Such differences in serial dependence across participants are frequently observed and may suggest different perceptual strategies [55]. Discussion

This study investigated the neural mechanisms underlying serial dependence at the time of saccadic eye movements. We recorded EEG activity from 20 participants as they made left–right horizontal saccades. Grouping the electrodes into four regions of interest (ROIs)—two frontal and two parietal—clear saccade-synchronized alpha activity (average 11 Hz), significant in three out of the four (all except the left parietal ROI). Importantly, the amplitude of alpha oscillations correlated negatively with the magnitude of serial dependence across participants, strongly implicating alpha oscillations in serial dependence at the time of saccades. Behavioral measures (Fig. 2) also showed alpha-frequency oscillations in orientation judgments, reinforcing our previous study [52]. On the other hand, beta oscillations, while significant in three of the four ROIs, did not correlate with the magnitude of serial dependence and were not present in the behavioral measurements, suggesting that they are not involved in serial dependence.

That saccade-synchronized alpha oscillations are associated with memory integration across saccades agrees with much previous literature. Alpha (and low-beta) rhythms have been implicated in serial dependence (and predictive perception in general) in several studies. Ten-hertz EEG alpha rhythms clearly emerge in paradigms designed to maximize predictive feedback [41, 58, 59]. They also emerge in serial dependence auditory tasks [43] and criteria judgments during saccades [51]. Other oscillations, in the low-beta range, have been clearly linked to gender judgments in faces [44, 45]. Oscillations have also been associated with working memory [60], which has been linked to perceptual memory.

Saccade studies typically incorporate a fixation control. However, this was not possible with the current paradigm, as we report saccade-synchronized oscillations, both for the psychophysics and EEG analysis. Indeed the test of significance was to compare the saccade-synchronized oscillations with unsynchronized oscillations (Fig. 3). Without a saccade, it would be impossible to distinguish the two. An additional difficulty arises from the need to study pre-saccadic alpha, therefore restricting the data to stimuli presented in the post-saccadic interval. To exclude that the alpha could be associated to the strategy to allocate attention in time after the saccade, we presented the stimuli at random at all possible time, increasing the amount of recording time.

It is not completely clear why the correlation between magnitude of serial dependence and synchronized alpha oscillations should be negative. This implies that individuals with higher alpha oscillations will have lower serial dependence. However, this is consistent with the generally held idea that alpha frequencies are linked to inhibition. Alpha oscillations have been consistently linked with regulating visual cortex excitability and visual processing, in an inhibitory fashion [61–63]. There are also indications of involvement in attentional processes related to saccadic eye movements [64, 65]. It is therefore not unreasonable that the relationship with serial dependence should also be negative.

It should be stressed that we find the negative correlation between alpha amplitude and the average magnitude of serial dependence, an index of how much the judgements of each participant are influenced by immediate past perceptual experience. However, as Fig. 2 shows, the effect on orientation judgements is not constant, but oscillates at 11 Hz, virtually the same frequency as the saccade-synchronized alpha frequencies in the EEG. This strongly suggests that when the memory signal is strong, alpha diminishes allowing for more serial dependence to take place, as we have previously observed [52]. An obvious question is whether the amplitude of the behavioral modulation also correlated with the amplitude of alpha in the EEG? Is this correlation also negative? Unfortunately, to answer this question would require far more data than we could reasonably collect, nearly a tenfold increase on trials in the individual subject. Measuring average serial dependence requires far fewer trials than to obtain a reliable estimate of the oscillations, in individual data. We also found a non-significant negative correlation between the average levels of serial dependence and the 11-Hz modulation of serial dependence. Obviously, these non-significant correlations cannot be taken as firm evidence, but they do allow the possibility that average serial dependence varies independently from the alpha modulation of it, allowing the average to correlate negatively with EEG alpha.

Whatever the reasons behind the negative correlation of alpha rhythms and EEG, the fact that the correlations are clear and significant in two out of four ROIs shows that the two are highly interrelated, albeit in a non-straightforward manner. Beta rhythms (around 22 Hz) were also synchronized by the saccades, but these rhythms were not related to the magnitude of serial dependence, either positively or negatively. This lack of correlation reinforces the unique role of alpha rhythms in communicating past perceptual experiences, to bias current perception with saccades. Interestingly for other task (faces classification), beta oscillations are the most informative [44, 45].

The regional specificity of our results is interesting, with correlations strongest for the left frontal eye field (FEF) and the right parietal cortex. Given that saccades in our study were from left to right, the observed correlations in the left FEF align with its contralateral activation to the saccadic landing point [66], corroborating previous findings of the involvement of FEF in the trans-saccadic memory [17]. This finding underscores the potential involvement of alpha oscillations in modulating serial dependence within regions actively engaged in saccade planning and execution. Additionally, the correlation in the right occipital-parietal cortex may be expected since we selected trials where the stimulus appeared in the left visual field after the saccadic movement. As a result, activation of the serial dependence signal may be expected in the contralateral hemisphere, as participants were predicting both an attentional and visual field shift to the left after saccading towards the right.

Unfortunately, our results do not allow precise localization the brain areas that correlate with serial dependence: we had only 32 electrodes (not adequate for reliable source analysis) and needed to pool electrode output within broad ROIs to reach statistical significance. However, the location of the ROIs is broadly consistent with previous work identifying neural areas involved in trans-saccadic integration. For example, Prime et al. [22] showed that applying TMS to the right posterior parietal cortex (especially near electrode P4) heavily disrupts trans-saccadic memory during left-to-right saccades. Later studies [12–14, 19] confirmed this, and also implicated occipital cortex in memory for orientation and spatial frequency. Whereas frontoparietal cortex seems to be modulated more by task specificity, parietal cortex showed trans-saccadic feature modulations [15, 17, 19]. All these localizations are broadly consistent with the activity in our Fig. 8, where the highest correlations occur for superior parietal cortex between C4 and P4 electrodes. The strong correlation in the left frontal cortex ROI is also consistent with the involvement of these more frontal areas in trans-saccadic integration [17, 19].

The significant correlations between synchronized alpha amplitude and serial dependences, localized in occipital-parietal electrodes, are also consistent with MEG work from Jenson’s laboratory reporting saccade-synchronized alpha rhythms during reading, where the amplitude was informative of task performance [49]. The strongest activity was in BA7, mostly included within our region of significant correlation.

What is the functional role of alpha-mediated serial dependence at the time of saccades? Manassi and Whitney [26] argue that a major function of serial dependence is to promote the stability, accuracy, and efficiency of perceptual representations by continuity fields: spatiotemporal integration mechanisms that continuously bias perception and cognition towards previously encountered information, thereby smoothing representations to promote the stability, accuracy, and efficiency of experience. They claim that this mechanism allows cognition to compensate for variability in sensory input, and thus stabilizes what would otherwise be a noisy, jittery, and discontinuous experience. A major contributor to the discontinuity of retinal input are saccadic eye movements, causing large displacements of the retinal image. It is at this time when mechanisms to preserve continuity, such as the purported continuity fields created by serial dependence, are most needed. The current study implicates saccade-synchronized alpha rhythms in the process.

In summary, the correlation between serial dependence and pre-saccadic alpha power highlights the multifaceted nature of neural mechanisms underlying perceptual stability during saccadic eye movements. Future research should further investigate the specific mechanisms underlying this relationship to deepen our understanding of how the brain integrates sensory information to maintain perceptual continuity across dynamic visual contexts.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the role of neural oscillations in facilitating perceptual stability across saccadic eye movements. The observation of that pre-saccadic alpha oscillations correlate negatively with serial dependence suggests a predictive role for these oscillations, fundamental for perceptual continuity. Moreover, the predictive nature of the correlations, observed even before participants perceived the stimulus, underscores the anticipatory function of neural oscillations in modulating perceptual outcomes during dynamic visual processing tasks such as saccadic eye movements. The correlations observed between serial dependence and alpha—but not beta—oscillations highlight the distinct roles of these oscillatory rhythms in facilitating visual perception during dynamic visual processing tasks. Further research exploring the mechanisms underlying these correlations could deepen our understanding of the neural basis of perceptual stability in dynamic visual environments.

Methods

Participants

Twenty observers participated in the experiment (11 females, mean age = 26.3, SD = 4.0). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided written informed consent before taking part in the study. With the exception of the first author, all were naive to the purpose of the experiment. Our previous work measuring behavioral oscillations with saccades [52] shows reliable oscillations after pooling 24 participants. The sample size is also in line with previous electrophysiological work [67–69] in saccades and in EEG measuring serial dependence (12–29 participants). Experimental procedures were approved by the regional ethics committee (Comitato Etico Pediatrico Regionale, Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Meyer, Florence, Italy) and were in line with the declaration of Helsinki.

Apparatus

Experimental procedures were carried out in a quiet, dimly lit environment. Stimuli were generated using Psychtoolbox [70] in MATLAB R2021b (MathWorks®) and back-projected through a PROPixx projector (VPixx Technologies Inc., Canada) onto a matte white PVC screen (Epson ELP-SC21B, 180 × 100 cm) with resolution 1920 × 1080 pixels, refresh rate 60 Hz, and mean luminance 135 cd·m−2. Participants sat 1 m from the screen, which subtended 90 × 50 cm (48 × 28 deg of visual angle). Heads were stabilized with chin- and head-rest. Eye movements and concomitant EEG data were recorded (see below).

Stimuli and procedure

The test stimuli were Gabor patches (Gaussian-windowed sinusoidal gratings) displayed at screen center. The Gabor stimulus was 3 c/deg, 80% contrast, 1.36 deg standard deviation, and random phase for each presentation (Fig. 1). The orientation of the stimuli varied randomly between 6 possible orientations: ± 35, ± 45, and ± 55° (Fig. 1c), coded as anticlockwise difference from the horizontal.

Trials started with participants viewing a fixation dot (F0) positioned 8° left of screen center. After a random duration of 500 ms (± 250 ms), F0 disappeared, and the saccadic target (F1) immediately appeared 8° to the right of screen center. Participants saccaded to F1 on appearance and kept fixation on F1 until the next trial. After a random delay from the start of fixation on F0, a brief 17-ms Gabor patch (the test stimulus, two projector frames) was displayed at screen center (Fig. 1).

After completing the saccades, participants reproduced the perceived position of the stimulus on the screen by moving the mouse along the horizontal axis. They then reproduced the test orientation by rotating a bar presented on the screen (again, by horizontal mouse movement) and confirmed their responses by left click. If participants did not see the test, they could skip the trial without responding by right click.

In every block, a total of 60 trials were conducted (10 trials for each of the six orientations). Participants completed 15–20 blocks each.

Eye movements acquisition and pre-processing

Acquisition

Eye movement were collected with an EyeLink 1000 system (SR Research, Kanata, Canada). The position of the left eye was recorded at 1000 Hz. Nine-point calibration and validation were made for each participant at the start of the experiment and at the beginning of each session. Only “fair” and “good” validations, as automatically labeled by the EyeLink software, were accepted. Bad validations procedures were aborted and the calibration procedure re-run.

Pre-processing

For the analysis of the saccade data, we employed EyeLink parsing algorithms. Saccades were differentiated from fixations using specific thresholds: a saccadic velocity threshold of 35°/s, a saccadic acceleration threshold of 9500°/s2, and a saccadic motion threshold of 0.15°. Saccades with amplitude less than 1° were excluded to control for the effect of micro-saccades.

We identified valid trials through the evaluation of both saccade and response. By this procedure, we excluded 12.5% of total trials of the experiment across participants.

To optimize the number of trials, we included in the EEG analysis those trials where participants performed memory driven saccades to the target before the appearance of the F1 saccadic target. However, when those trials were excluded from the main analyses, the main results (alpha and serial dependence correlations and serial dependence correlation with saccadic latency) remain significant.

To analyze saccadic latency, we selected only saccades with latency greater than 100 ms, and similarly for the analysis of the behavioral oscillation.

Behavioral analysis

Orientation errors were defined as the difference between reproduced test stimulus orientation and actual stimulus orientation. Given the range of test orientations used (± 35°, ± 45°, ± 55°: Fig. 1c), the previous trial could either be similar to the current one (both positive or both negative), which we termed congruent, or very different (separated by more than 70°), which we termed incongruent. Incongruent trials and trials where the previous stimulus orientation was equal to the current stimulus orientation were excluded from the analysis of serial dependence (which has a limited range [23, 24]). The magnitude of serial dependence for each trial was defined as the amplitude of the reproduction error, scored positive if in the direction of the previous stimulus (attraction), and negative if in the opposite direction (repulsion).

EEG data acquisition, pre-processing, and analysis

Acquisition

Eye movements and EEG data were recorded simultaneously so that direct input from the EEG triggers would be sent directly to the EyeLink. EEG data was recorded using a Nautilus Research headset (g.tec) at a sampling rate of 250 Hz without online filtering with 32 gel-based active electrodes (utilizing g.LADYbird technology), arranged in accordance with the 10/20 system. Impedance levels were maintained below 50 kΩ and checked throughout the recording time (about 3 h).

Pre-processing

EEG were preprocessed offline using custom code in MATLAB 2021B (MathWorks®). They were bandpass filtered between 0.2 and 30 Hz with a zero-phase Butterworth filter (in EEGLab), then segmented into 2-s epochs locked to saccadic movement onset (± 1 s from saccadic onset). All channels were re-referenced to the average response.

Head and movement artifacts were rejected manually to eliminate bad channels and identify “bad” sections. Independent component analysis (ICA) was computed to identify artifactual ICs. Each IC identified as a blink was rejected and remaining ICs (associated presumably with saccades) were kept in the data. ICA was applied to the data, and “bad” sections of the data were all removed. Bad channels were interpolated (using a spherical spline). Data was labeled by condition depending on whether the stimulus appeared before saccadic onset (“PRE” trials) or after saccadic onset (“POST” trials).

Data analysis

For each participant, we selected the trial in the “POST” condition (stimulus delay > 0 ms) to yield a pre-saccadic period free of stimulus visual responses. As each participant completed a different number of trials, with a minimum of 300, we selected 300 trials without replacement from the total number of trials for 100 iterations.

We analyzed data in the pre-saccadic period spanning − 340 to 0 ms, to avoid both EEG contamination due to the saccadic movement. The 340-ms interval allowed at least three cycles at the frequency investigated (11 Hz).

Saccadic alpha and beta analysis.

We identified four regions of interest (ROIs), two frontocentral including the electrodes AF3, F3, F7, FC5, FC1, and C3 and the equivalent in the right hemisphere, and two parietal including electrodes PO7, P3, P5, and CP5 and the equivalent ROI on the right hemisphere. Previous work points to frontal cortex, in particular the FEF and DLPF and to posterior parietal cortex and IPS region in the parietal lobe as crucial hub for trans-saccadic memory integration [12–20]. Our ROIs include all those regions. The electrode signals inside each ROI were summed and used to generate a single ERP for the individual subject. ERPs of the 4 ROIs across participants, ERPs of individual electrodes were first linearly detrended and a normalization factor calculated by taking the absolute value of the maximum amplitude of voltage modulation in the − 340 to 0 ms across all electrodes. The electrodes within each ROI were then averaged. We transformed the ERP in the time window of − 340 to 0 ms into the frequency domain via the fast Fourier transform (FFT) and extracted the signal power at 11 Hz frequency as representative of the alpha range for each ROI and at 22 Hz as representative of the beta range for each ROI.

Significance of saccade-synchronized alpha and beta oscillations was tested by bootstrap (1000 repetitions). We created a null distribution by randomly perturbing the single saccadic onset, anticipating it by 0–180 ms, then averaging across trials and participants. We then averaged the surrogate ERPs of the channels within an ROI, selected the nominal pre-saccadic ERP of − 340 to 0 ms (which incorporates the random offset), linearly detrended it, normalizing, averaging, and computed the FFT as was performed with the original signals. The amplitudes in the alpha and beta range led to the null hypothesis distribution of amplitude for the desynchronized ERPs. We tested the real value of the alpha and beta amplitude locked to saccadic onset against the null distribution, correcting p values for multiple comparisons via FDR correction.

For initial statistical evaluation of the alpha power at each electrode, we used a t-test of the subject power against the extrapolated presumed alpha power obtained by fitting the average spectra with 1/f power density function. The fitting procedure excluded the 8–12 Hz range to avoid circular statistics. No individual electrodes reached statistical significance after Bonferroni or FDR correction.

For single trial alpha power evaluation, we calculate the spectra for the individual trial in the interval [− 340 0] ms after detrending, and we average the power in the interval 8–12 Hz across trials and subjects.

We then correlated serial dependence of each participant with the corresponding alpha and beta amplitdes for each electrode for the topomaps, and also for the 4 selected ROIs. For each region, we calculated the Spearman’s rho and p values, applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction to the p values.
