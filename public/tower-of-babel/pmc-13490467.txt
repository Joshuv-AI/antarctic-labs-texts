Impact of herbal medicine use on HbA1c levels among type 2 diabetes mellitus patients in northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Desalegn Getnet Demsie, Desye Gebrie, Ebrahim Abdela Siraj, Selamawit Yimer Kebede, Chernet Tafere, Abere Tilahun Bantie, Wondu Feyisa Balcha, Abrham Belachew, Gizachew Motbaynor Alene, Molla Dessalegn Nigus, Biset Asrade Mekonnen, Tefera Minwagaw Biadigilign, Wondim Ayenew, Segenet Zewdie, Kebede Feyisa, Yeniewa Kerie Anagaw, Zegaye Agmassie, Zenaw Debasu Addisu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herbal medicine use is widespread among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), yet its influence on glycemic control remains uncertain. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of herbal medicine use and its association with glycemic control among adults with T2DM. A cross-sectional study was conducted from January to June 2025 among 420 adults with T2DM selected through systematic random sampling. Data were collected using structured interviews and medical record reviews. Glycemic control was assessed based on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, with HbA1c < 7% considered good control. Descriptive statistics summarized sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Associations between herbal use and glycemic control were examined using multivariable logistic regression analyses, with p < 0.05 denoting statistical significance. Of 420 participants, 223 (53.0%) were female, and the majority resided in urban areas (67.4%). Most (74.8%) had lived with diabetes for more than five years, and one-third (33.3%) had comorbidities. Herbal medicine use was reported by 219 (52.1%) participants, predominantly Moringa oleifera (61.6%), Trigonella foenum-graecum (45.7%), and Allium sativum (38.8%). Overall, 228 (54.3%) participants achieved good glycemic control (HbA1c < 7.0%), while the remaining 192 (45.7%) exhibited poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7.0%). In multivariable logistic regression, urban residence (AOR = 10.5, 95% CI 4.9–22.7), shorter diabetes duration (< 5 years) (AOR = 10.18, 95% CI 4.46–23.23), absence of comorbidity (AOR = 2.58, 95% CI 1.58–4.21), and non-users (AOR = 3.89, 95% CI 2.38–6.38) were independently associated with good glycemic control. Literacy and age above 40 years were also significant predictors (p < 0.05). In conclusion, the prevalence of herbal medicine use was high among individuals with T2DM but was significantly associated with poor glycemic control.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and/or relative insulin deficiency, leading to persistent hyperglycemia1. It accounts for around 90% of all diabetes cases globally and has become a major public health challenge. The disease is responsible for an estimated 6.7 million deaths in 2021, causing significant disability and mortality across all age groups, sexes, and regions2,3. Its prevalence has risen dramatically over the past three decades, increasing from 7% in 1990 to 14% in 2022—a fourfold increase4. According to the International Diabetes Federation, 537 million adults aged 20–79 years currently live with diabetes, and this figure is projected to increase to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 20452. By 2050, the global prevalence is expected to reach 1.31 billion (Ong et al. 2023), with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) carrying the greatest share of the burden4.

Ethiopia is no exception to this growing crisis. The age-adjusted prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20–79 years increased from 3.4% in 2011 to 5% in 2021. Projections indicate that by 2030, about 2.74 million adults will be affected, rising to 4.76 million by 20455. A meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of T2DM at 6.5%6, and in psychiatric patients on antipsychotic medication, the prevalence is even higher at 24.9%, with 6.3% prediabetes7. Notably, 57.6% of diabetes cases remain undiagnosed in the country5. This increasing prevalence is strongly linked to risk factors such as alcohol use, family history of diabetes, older age, illiteracy, cigarette smoking, BMI ≥ 25, physical inactivity, and a low-vegetable diet6,8,9. If left unaddressed, these factors can accelerate the development of complications including chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension10. Evidence shows that 55% of Ethiopian T2DM patients also have hypertension11, and many suffer from macrovascular and microvascular complications such as stroke, nephropathy, retinopathy, and peripheral vascular disease5.

Amid this growing burden, many patients in Ethiopia use herbal medicine as an alternative or complementary therapy. Studies show that between 36% and 58.5% of people with diabetes use herbal remedies12,13, and the prevalence is even higher—up to 62%—among those with T2DM14. Despite its popularity, knowledge of patients regarding potential side effects and drug–herb interactions remains poor15. Herbal use has been associated with various adverse effects, including renal, liver, and cardiotoxicity, as well as genotoxicity and teratogenicity. Furthermore, drug-herb interactions may arise, potentially complicating standard diabetes management16–22.

Given that achieving optimal glycemic control remains a major challenge in Ethiopia (with studies reporting success in only 58% to 61% of T2DM patients), it is critical to investigate whether the prevalent and often unregulated use of herbal medicines serves as a contributing factor to these suboptimal therapeutic outcomes23,24. This is supported by evidence of high HbA1c levels despite ongoing treatment, a phenomenon partly attributed to poor medication adherence and a reliance on herbal therapies with limited or unproven glycemic benefits25, low self-management support, negative beliefs about conventional medicine, and increased diabetes distress26. Beliefs about alternative medicine have been shown to influence adherence in chronic conditions27–29, and a meta-analysis further identified smoking, illiteracy, comorbidities, and duration of illness as major determinants of poor glycemic control30.

Despite the widespread use of herbal remedies and the known challenges in glycemic control, the impact of herbal medicine on long-term glucose regulation—specifically on HbA1C levels—has not been evaluated in Ethiopia. This gap in knowledge has important clinical and public health implications. Understanding whether herbal medicine use improves, worsens, or has no significant effect on glycemic control is crucial for guiding evidence-based practice, informing patient education, and improving treatment adherence. It may also help policymakers design targeted interventions that address both biomedical and cultural dimensions of diabetes management.

In light of this, the present study aims to evaluate the impact of herbal medicine use on glycemic control among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus using HbA1c. As the gold-standard parameter for monitoring long-term glycemic status, HbA1c provides a robust measure of therapeutic outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study area, study design and period

This study was conducted at the outpatient clinics of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) and Tibebe Ghion Specialized Teaching Hospital (TGSH), both located in Bahir Dar, the capital city of the Amhara Region in northwest Ethiopia. Situated approximately 565 km from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital, Bahir Dar serves as a major urban center and a hub for healthcare services in the region.

Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) is a tertiary government teaching hospital established in 2019. It functions as a referral center for neighboring hospitals and health centers, providing specialized medical services to a catchment population of approximately seven million people in the region.

Tibebe Ghion Specialized Teaching Hospital (TGSH) is affiliated with the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Bahir Dar University, contributing to medical education and research. It is a prominent healthcare institution that delivers high-quality, patient-centered, and specialized health care services. It serves as a referral center for the Amhara Region and beyond, offering a wide range of medical services to the population. A retrospective cross-sectional design was employed, and the study was conducted over a six-month period, from January to June 2025.

Source and study population

The source population comprised all adult patients with T2DM attending the diabetes follow-up clinic at FHCSH and TGSH. The study population included T2DM patients from this source population who attended the clinic during the study period and met the eligibility criteria.

Sampling technique and sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, focusing on patients with T2DM who used herbal medicine and were available and willing to participate during the data collection period. A conservative estimate of p = 0.5 was used to maximize variability and ensure an adequate sample size. The calculation was based on a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a 5% margin of error. To account for possible nonresponses or incomplete data, a 10% contingency was added. In total, 420 participant records were included, with 210 from TGSH and 210 from FHCSH. Participants were randomly selected from the registries of patients attending diabetes clinics at each hospital.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were adult (≥ 20 years) outpatients with clinically diagnosed T2DM attending FHCSH and with a documented HbA1c measured within 3 months of interview. Herbal medicine (HM) use was defined as any self-reported use of herbal/traditional remedies in the preceding 3 months29. Pregnancy31, known haemoglobinopathies or recent blood transfusion, and acute severe illness32, current systemic corticosteroid therapy (unless stable and documented)33, health professionals, those unable to be interviewed reliably, or those who declined participation or had missing HbA1c or HM data were excluded.

Data collection

Data were collected through a combination of structured patient interviews and a retrospective review of medical records. A self-designed questionnaire was translated into the local language, Amharic, and back-translated into English to ensure linguistic consistency. This tool was specifically utilized to capture primary data on herbal medicine utilization, including the identification of specific medicinal plants such as Moringa oleifera, Trigonella foenum-graecum, and Allium sativum, as well as socio-demographic details including occupation, education level, and average monthly income.

A retrospective chart review was performed to extract documented clinical history from the patients’ medical records, including the duration of T2DM diagnosis, presence of comorbidities (such as hypertension or dyslipidemia), current conventional antidiabetic medications (oral agents or insulin), and the most recent laboratory-confirmed HbA1c values.

Definition of terms

Herbal medicine (HM) use was defined as any self-reported use of herbal/traditional remedies in the preceding 3 months29. According to the 2023 American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria, good glycemic control was defined as an HbA1c level below 7%, while poor glycemic control was defined as an HbA1c level of 7% or higher.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were employed to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics and patterns of medicinal plant use among study participants. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between glycemic control and various independent variables. In the bivariate analysis, factors with a p value less than 0.25 were selected for inclusion in the multivariable logistic analysis. In the final multivariable model, a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The fitness of the final multivariable model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, where a p value greater than 0.05 indicated an adequate fit. Additionally, multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance, with a VIF less than 5 used as the threshold for model stability.

Data quality management

Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample of patients with T2DM. Findings from the pretest were used to make necessary revisions to enhance clarity and consistency. During the data collection period, the completeness of all checklists was reviewed daily to ensure data quality. Data collectors received one day of training covering the study objectives, questionnaire administration procedures, and ethical considerations to minimize potential bias. The principal investigator conducted daily reviews to assess completeness, accuracy, and consistency. In addition, double data entry and random verification of 10% of the records were performed to identify and correct discrepancies.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 420 adults with T2DM, with mean age 42.6 ± 6.3 years were included. Slightly more than half were female (223, 53.0%). Most participants resided in urban areas (67.4%) and were married (304, 72.4%). In terms of education, 22.6% had completed TVET or higher, while 35.5% were unable to read or write. Occupation was dominated by merchants (27.1%), government employees (24.5%), and housewives (22.6%). Regarding income, a substantial proportion earned 5,000–10,000 birrs (45%) or less than 5,000 birrs (29.8%) per month, indicating low to moderate economic status (Table 1).

Effect of herbal medicine use with glycemic control

The logistic regression model demonstrated a robust goodness-of-fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: p = 0.087) and an absence of significant multicollinearity (all VIF < 3.1), suggesting that the reported predictors, including herbal medicine use, remain statistically reliable and independent (Table 4).

Patients residing in urban areas were about ten times more likely to achieve good glycemic control compared with those in rural settings (AOR = 10.5, 95% CI 4.9–22.7, p < 0.001). Similarly, individuals with a shorter duration of diabetes (< 5 years) had markedly higher odds of good glycemic control than those with longer disease duration (AOR = 10.18, 95% CI 4.46–23.23, p < 0.001). The absence of comorbidity was also significantly associated with good glycemic control (AOR = 2.58, 95% CI 1.58–4.21, p < 0.001). Participants who did not use herbal medicine had nearly fourfold higher odds of maintaining HbA1c < 7% compared with those who reported herbal use (AOR = 3.89, 95% CI 2.38–6.38, p < 0.001).

In addition, literate patients were more likely to have controlled HbA1c levels compared with their illiterate counterparts (AOR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.10–2.96, p = 0.019). Age also showed a significant relationship—participants aged above 40 years were more likely to achieve good glycemic control compared to younger adults (AOR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.28–7.68, p = 0.013). Conversely, monthly income did not exhibit a statistically significant association with glycemic control after adjustment (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of herbal medicine use and its effect on glycemic control, as measured by HbA1C, among patients with T2DM. Our findings revealed a 52.1% prevalence of herbal medicine use. This prevalence is very close to that reported in another Ethiopian study (51.7%)34 but lower than figures reported in Ethiopia (62%)14,35, Zambia (92%)36, and Nigeria (67.5%)37. Similarly, a higher proportion was observed in another Ethiopian study, where 58.5% of patients reported using herbal medicine13. On the other hand, the prevalence in our study was higher compared to reports from Ethiopia (48%) and Cameroon (24%)38. These variations across studies may be attributed to differences in study settings, cultural practices, availability of herbal products, and levels of integration of traditional and modern healthcare systems.

In our study, the most commonly reported herbal medicines among patients with type 2 diabetes were Moringa (Moringa oleifera), Fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum), Holy/Ethiopian Basil (Ocimum spp.), and Garlic (Allium sativum). These findings partly align with previous reports from Ethiopia, where Garlic, Caylusea abyssinica (Giesilla), Otostegia integrifolia (Tinjute), and Hagenia abyssinica (Kosso) were frequently used14,35. Other Ethiopian studies also documented high use of Moringa stenopetala, Nigella sativa, Zingiber officinale (Ginger), Aloe vera, Trigonella foenum-graecum, and Thymus schimperi14,34,35. The consistent reporting of certain plants—particularly Moringa, Fenugreek, Garlic, and Nigella sativa suggests they are widely trusted and culturally embedded remedies for diabetes management across different settings.

A key concern is that many patients use herbal medicines based on advice from family, friends, or fellow diabetic patients rather than seeking guidance from healthcare professionals14,35. This underscores the importance of integrating discussions about herbal medicine into routine diabetes care to ensure both safety and effectiveness.

Several factors have been shown to influence the use of herbal remedies, including patients’ knowledge, occupation, and income level39. Another study reported that individuals living in rural areas were nearly three times more likely to use herbal medicines. Likewise, patients with a longer duration of diabetes or those experiencing complications were significantly more likely to rely on herbal treatments40. These findings suggest that such variables may also influence glycemic control, as our study revealed that rural residence, longer disease duration, and lower educational level were significantly associated with higher HbA1C values.

Additionally, in our study, the high rate use of herbal medicine was associated with poor glycemic control. Notably, 58.9% of the participants in herbal medicine users had high HbA1C level. The result is consistent with findings which stated that up to 86% of herbal medicine users had uncontrolled blood glucose levels, compared to 66.8% among non-users, and herbal medicine use was significantly associated with higher mean HbA1c values (Lema et al., 2024).

Poor glycemic control suggest potential complications in patients with type diabetes mellitus41. In our study supplements such as Moringa Stenopatela may help control blood glucose levels, however plants with unconfirmed therapeutic effect may be used. This is supported by a meta-analysis discussing that on the face of having medicinal plants such as pomoea batatas, Silybum marianum, and Trigonella foenum-graecum may improve glycemic control in type 2 diabetes which can improve glycemic control25. Nevertheless, blood glucose may not be influenced in patients using supplements like Cinnamomum cassia25,42, a review described it may improve glycemic control43. A network-meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials also indicated. Apple cider vinegar (ACV), cinnamon, curcumin, and fenugreek significantly controlled FBG levels, with profound effect on HbA1C of herbs ( ACV-most effective and fenugreek seeds achieved reduced HbA1C44. Many people tend to seek advice from friends, family, or traditional healers rather than from physicians when it comes to herbal medicine. This reflects a hesitancy to consult healthcare professionals and creates barriers to the effective management of type 2 diabetes. As a result, treatment choices are often shaped by trial and error, as well as the availability, cost, and convenience of herbal and traditional remedies. Even when patients share their experiences with herbal medicine use, many healthcare providers lack sufficient knowledge about the safety and effectiveness of these remedies, further complicating patient care45.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that herbal medicine use is highly prevalent among patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in Northwest Ethiopia and is significantly associated with poor glycemic control. While factors such as urban residence, shorter disease duration, and the absence of comorbidities are linked to favorable clinical outcomes, the use of herbal medicine is strongly correlated with suboptimal HbA1c levels. These findings underscore a critical gap in current diabetes management, specifically the lack of clinical integration and open communication regarding traditional medicine use. Consequently, healthcare providers should routinely screen for herbal use and provide evidence-based counseling to mitigate potential therapeutic interference. While the cross-sectional nature of this study identifies a significant association, further prospective research is required to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and potential reverse causality within this population.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the few that have examined the relationship between herbal medicine use and glycemic control, particularly HbA1C levels, among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus in Ethiopia. The inclusion of a relatively large sample size (n = 420) and data collected from two major referral hospitals enhance the representativeness and generalizability of the findings to comparable urban and semiurban settings. The use of HbA1C, a widely accepted and objective biomarker of long-term glycemic control, strengthens the validity of the study outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences between herbal medicine use and glycemic control. Second, information regarding the specific dosage, frequency, preparation methods, duration of herbal medicine use, and herb–drug interactions was not standardized or quantitatively measured, potentially influencing their observed effects on glycemic outcomes. Further longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to clarify the causal relationship between herbal medicine use and glycemic control, and to evaluate the safety and efficacy of commonly used herbal preparations in diabetic care.

In addition, important confounding factors such as diet, physical activity, and adherence to prescribed antidiabetic medications were not fully controlled for. Finally, the study was conducted in two tertiary hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of the results to patients receiving care in rural or primary healthcare settings.

Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful insights into the real-world use of herbal medicines among Ethiopian patients with type 2 diabetes. It also underscores the importance of conducting longitudinal and interventional studies to better understand the causal relationships and safety profiles of commonly used herbal preparations in the management of diabetes.
