The effect of obesity on the coccoid form transformation of Helicobacter pylori and its relationship with gastric inflammation pattern: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Burcu Sanal Yılmaz, Sibel Acat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori) can transform from its spiral form into a coccoid (viable but non-culturable; VBNC) state under adverse environmental conditions. The coccoid form is associated with antibiotic resistance and eradication failure. This study compared coccoid and spiral form proportions, H. pylori localization patterns (epithelial vs. crypt), and gastric inflammation parameters across three BMI-stratified groups.Materials and methodsThis retrospective comparative study included 156 H. pylori-positive cases from the archives of Karamanoglu Mehmetbey University Faculty of Medicine (2016–2026). Groups: Group 1 (Severe obesity/Sleeve, BMI ≥ 35; sleeve gastrectomy, n = 55), Group 2 (Non-obese/Biopsy, BMI < 30; biopsy, n = 68), Group 3 (Obese/Biopsy, BMI ≥ 30; biopsy, n = 33). IHC with anti-H. pylori antibody was used for morphological assessment. Coccoid/spiral proportions, HP density, Sydney-criteria gastritis parameters, coccoid categorization (< 5%, 5–19%, 20–39%, ≥ 40%), and epithelial/crypt HP localization were analyzed (SPSS 25.0; p < 0.05).ResultsMedian corpus coccoid proportion was significantly higher in Group 1 (10%, IQR 5–30) vs. Group 2 (1%, IQR 1–1; p < 0.001), with strong BMI correlation (r = 0.639, p < 0.001). The ≥ 40% coccoid category was exclusively observed in the severe obesity/sleeve group (23.6%; p < 0.001). Corpus spiral proportion decreased in a stepwise inverse gradient across categories (99%→90%→80%→40%; H = 113.8, p < 0.001). Corpus HP density (Sydney score) differed significantly across groups (H = 13.8, p = 0.001) and correlated positively with coccoid proportion (r = 0.267, p = 0.003). Corpus crypt H. pylori involvement differed significantly across groups (Group 1 41.8% vs. Group 2 29.2% vs. Group 3 11.1%; overall p = 0.045). In antrum, crypt involvement was higher in non-obese (36.8%) vs. obese biopsy patients (9.1%; p = 0.011). Coccoid proportion showed no significant correlation with patient age or inflammation grade. Because specimen type differed between groups and eradication outcomes were not assessed, these findings represent associations rather than causal relationships. Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a Gram-negative microaerophilic bacterium colonizing the gastric mucosa of approximately 50% of the global population and is causally linked to chronic active gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric adenocarcinoma, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [1, 2]. Despite available eradication regimens, first-line eradication success rates have declined to 70–80% in some regions [3]. While antibiotic resistance is the principal mechanism of failure, the bacterial capacity to adopt a “viable but non-culturable” (VBNC) state — the coccoid form — is an increasingly recognized contributor [4, 5].

Under adverse environmental conditions including antibiotic exposure, extreme pH, elevated oxygen tension, and nutritional depletion, H. pylori transforms into spherical coccoid bodies through HdpA/Csd3-mediated progressive thinning of the lateral peptidoglycan (PGN) layer, leading to sacculus rupture and extrusion of the inner membrane-enclosed cytoplasm as a spheroplast-like structure [6, 7]. Viable coccoid bacteria (subtype B) retain expression of major virulence genes (ureA, ureB, vacA, cagA, cagE, BabA), display low urease activity, and can revert to spiral forms when conditions normalize [8, 9]. SEM studies confirm coccoid invasion of gastric epithelial cells and persistence within autophagosomes, creating a pharmacokinetic sanctuary from antibiotics [10].

Eradication failure linked to coccoid forms occurs via two mechanisms: (1) altered penicillin-binding protein profiles rendering β-lactams ineffective [11]; and (2) confinement within autophagosomes where drug concentrations may not reach bactericidal levels [12]. Clinical data corroborate this: eradication-failure strains exhibit higher invasiveness [13, 14], and murine studies show re-colonization from intracellular/intravacuolar bacteria 8 weeks after standard triple therapy [12].

Obesity alters the gastric microenvironment through chronic low-grade systemic inflammation (elevated IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, leptin), modified acid secretion, and changes in mucus composition and gastric microbiota. Mayer et al. [15] provided the first systematic clinical evidence that patients with severe obesity undergoing sleeve gastrectomy harbor a significantly higher median coccoid proportion (50%) than non-obese controls (10%; p < 0.001) and display predominantly chronic inactive rather than active gastritis. These findings raise the question of whether obesity-associated gastric alterations are associated with the VBNC transition and thereby with lower eradication success in obese populations.

The present study extends this question by examining H. pylori morphological distributions across three BMI-stratified groups, quantifying BMI–coccoid correlations, introducing a four-tier coccoid categorization (< 5%, 5–19%, 20–39%, ≥ 40%), and for the first time systematically analyzing intragastric HP localization patterns (epithelial surface vs. glandular crypt involvement) across BMI groups.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective comparative study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Karamanoglu Mehmetbey University Faculty of Medicine Local Scientific Medical Research Ethics Committee (application date 26.02.2025; approval decision No. 17-2025/20, dated 05.03.2025). Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis; no new interventions were performed.

Study population

The pathology archives were searched for H. pylori-positive gastric specimens processed between January 2016 and May 2026. A total of 156 cases fulfilling inclusion criteria were enrolled and stratified into three groups:

Group 1 (Severe obesity/Sleeve): 55 patients with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Only corpus (oxyntic) mucosa was available.

Group 1 (Severe obesity/Sleeve): 55 patients with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Only corpus (oxyntic) mucosa was available.

Group 2 (Non-obese/Biopsy): 68 patients with BMI < 30 kg/m²; both antrum and corpus biopsies available.

Group 2 (Non-obese/Biopsy): 68 patients with BMI < 30 kg/m²; both antrum and corpus biopsies available.

Group 3 (Obese/Biopsy): 33 patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²; both antrum and corpus biopsies available. Groups were defined primarily by specimen type and clinical context (bariatric sleeve resection vs. diagnostic endoscopic biopsy); Group 1 and Group 3 were therefore distinguished by specimen type rather than by mutually exclusive BMI ranges.

Group 3 (Obese/Biopsy): 33 patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²; both antrum and corpus biopsies available. Groups were defined primarily by specimen type and clinical context (bariatric sleeve resection vs. diagnostic endoscopic biopsy); Group 1 and Group 3 were therefore distinguished by specimen type rather than by mutually exclusive BMI ranges.

Exclusion criteria

Age > 60 years, gastric malignancy or premalignant lesions, prior documented H. pylori eradication therapy, and insufficient tissue.

Histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation

All FFPE tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm and stained with H&E and Giemsa. IHC staining was performed with polyclonal anti-H. pylori antibody (CellMarque 215 A-76, prediluted/ready-to-use format). Two experienced pathologists (B.S.Y. and S.A.) independently evaluated all slides while blinded to group assignment and clinical data. Each pathologist recorded all parameters separately without access to the other’s assessments. Discordance was defined as a difference of ≥ 10% points for continuous coccoid/spiral proportions or ≥ 1 grade for Sydney criteria ordinal scores. All discordant cases were re-examined jointly at a multihead microscope until consensus was reached. Interobserver agreement between the two pathologists was quantified using Cohen’s kappa (κ); agreement was substantial (κ = 0.81). Recorded parameters included: (1) Coccoid and spiral form proportions (% summing to 100%) in corpus (all groups) and antrum (Groups 2–3); (2) HP density (Sydney 0–3 scale); (3) Epithelial surface HP% and glandular crypt HP% in both corpus and antrum; crypt involvement was defined as any HP% >0% in the glandular crypt compartment; (4) Chronic inflammation grade (0–3); neutrophilic activation (0–3); intestinal metaplasia; glandular atrophy (all 0–3 per Sydney criteria) (Fig. 1). HP (bacterial) density, coccoid/spiral form proportions, and epithelial-surface versus glandular-crypt localization are distinct, non-interchangeable variables: HP density was graded semiquantitatively on the 0–3 Sydney scale, whereas the coccoid/spiral form proportions and the epithelial/crypt localization percentages were quantified separately on the IHC-stained sections. Corpus coccoid categorization

To identify clinically meaningful morphological thresholds, corpus coccoid proportions were stratified into four categories reflecting natural data breakpoints and clinical relevance: <5%, 5–19%, 20–39%, and ≥ 40%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0. Non-normal distribution of continuous variables was confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk test; results are presented as median (IQR) or mean ± SD. Group comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis with post-hoc Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed BMI–coccoid and HP density–coccoid relationships. To assess BMI as an independent predictor of elevated coccoid burden, univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed with corpus coccoid proportion ≥ 10% as the outcome variable. Predictors included BMI (continuous, per 1 kg/m²), age, and sex. Model discrimination was assessed by the C-statistic (area under the ROC curve) and calibration by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to evaluate multicollinearity. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To ensure denominator transparency, all analyses were performed on a complete-case basis with no imputation of missing values. BMI, age, and sex were available for all 156 enrolled patients, whereas corpus coccoid proportion was evaluable in 121 patients (Group 1, n = 55; Group 2, n = 48; Group 3, n = 18) and antrum proportions in the biopsy groups (Group 2, n = 68; Group 3, n = 33). Analyses using corpus coccoid proportion as an outcome or stratifying variable — the categorical analysis, the corpus spiral-form gradient, the ROC analyses, and the univariable and multivariable logistic regression — were accordingly restricted to the 121 corpus-evaluable cases; the applicable denominator is stated in each corresponding table and figure legend.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 156 H. pylori-positive patients were included: Group 1 (n = 55), Group 2 (n = 68), and Group 3 (n = 33). Mean BMI differed significantly across groups (Group 1: 47.4 ± 7.0; Group 2: 22.1 ± 2.7; Group 3: 42.8 ± 6.3 kg/m²; p < 0.001). Age distribution differed modestly (H = 9.28, p = 0.010). Female predominance was observed across all groups. Atrophy was absent in Group 1 and extremely rare in Groups 2–3 (≤ 2 cases each). Intestinal metaplasia was similarly rare in biopsy groups (Table 1).

Coccoid and spiral form proportions in corpus mucosa

Corpus coccoid proportions differed significantly across groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 64.43, p < 0.001). The severe obesity/sleeve group (Group 1) had a median corpus coccoid proportion of 10% (IQR: 5–30%), substantially higher than the non-obese/biopsy group (Group 2: 1%, IQR: 1–1%; U = 2372, p < 0.001). The obese/biopsy group (Group 3) showed an intermediate median of 10% (IQR: 5–17.5%), significantly higher than Group 2 (U = 52, p < 0.001) but not significantly different from Group 1 (U = 608, p = 0.145). Antrum coccoid and spiral form proportions

Antrum evaluation was restricted to Groups 2 and 3. The antrum coccoid proportion was significantly higher in Group 3 vs. Group 2: median 10% (IQR: 5–20%) vs. 1% (IQR: 1–1%; U = 112, p < 0.001). These findings corroborate the corpus results and reinforce a BMI-dependent shift in H. pylori morphology.

Correlation between BMI and coccoid form proportions

Spearman’s rank correlation revealed a strong positive association between BMI and corpus coccoid proportion (r = 0.639, p < 0.001) and antrum coccoid proportion (r = 0.676, p < 0.001) across the 121 corpus-evaluable cases for the corpus and the biopsy groups (Groups 2 and 3, n = 101) for the antrum.

Categorized analysis of corpus coccoid proportions

Corpus-based categorical analysis included 121 cases with evaluable corpus coccoid data (Group 1: n = 55, Group 2: n = 48, Group 3: n = 18). Corpus coccoid proportions were stratified into four categories (Table 3; Chi²=84.6, p < 0.001). The most striking finding was the ≥ 40% category: all 13 cases (23.6% of Group 1) were exclusively from the severe obesity/sleeve group; none of the 48 non-obese or 18 obese biopsy patients fell into this category within the present cohort (100% obesity-specificity). The 20–39% category also contained only severe obesity/sleeve and obese biopsy patients (0/48 non-obese). Conversely, the < 5% category was dominated by non-obese patients (47/48, 97.9%; Figs. 2 and 3).

Corpus spiral form gradient across coccoid categories

The corpus spiral proportion decreased in a precise stepwise gradient across increasing coccoid categories (Kruskal-Wallis H = 113.8, p < 0.001): <5% coccoid → spiral median 99% (IQR: 99–99%); 5–19% → 90% (IQR: 90–95%); 20–39% → 80% (IQR: 72–80%); ≥40% → 40% (IQR: 30–40%). All pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (all p < 0.001).

Corpus HP density across groups and correlation with coccoid form

Corpus HP density assessed by Sydney criteria differed significantly across groups (Kruskal-Wallis H = 13.8, p = 0.001): Group 1 mean 2.38 ± 0.71 vs. Group 2 mean 2.12 ± 0.82 vs. Group 3 mean 1.53 ± 0.80. Pairwise comparisons showed Group 1 and Group 2 did not differ significantly (p = 0.094), while Group 3 was significantly lower than both Group 1 (p < 0.001) and Group 2 (p = 0.010). Across all 121 cases with corpus coccoid data, HP density correlated positively with corpus coccoid proportion (Spearman r = 0.267, p = 0.003). Stratified by HP density category, coccoid medians were: density 1 → 1% (IQR: 0–1%), density 2 → 1% (IQR: 1–10%), density 3 → 10% (IQR: 1–20%; Kruskal-Wallis H = 9.6, p = 0.008). Although Group 3 exhibited the lowest mean HP density, its coccoid proportions were nonetheless elevated; the density–coccoid relationship is an individual-level correlation and does not preclude group-level differences driven by BMI.

In the corpus, crypt HP involvement (any HP% >0% in the crypt compartment) was significantly more frequent in Group 1 (23/55, 41.8%) compared to Group 2 (14/48, 29.2%) and Group 3 (2/18, 11.1%; Chi²=6.2, p = 0.045). Continuous corpus crypt HP% did not differ significantly across groups in Kruskal-Wallis analysis (H = 4.1, p = 0.126), owing to the low absolute crypt proportions; however, the binary presence of crypt involvement showed a clear BMI-associated gradient. The corpus epithelial surface HP% was uniformly high across all three groups (median 99–100%; p = 0.126).

In the antrum (Groups 2 and 3 only), an inverse pattern was observed: antrum crypt involvement was significantly more frequent in non-obese patients (Group 2: 25/68, 36.8%) than in obese biopsy patients (Group 3: 3/33, 9.1%; Chi²=6.4, p = 0.011). Correspondingly, antrum epithelial surface HP% was significantly higher in Group 3 than Group 2 (Mann-Whitney p = 0.011). This divergent pattern — deeper crypt involvement in non-obese antrum but predominantly surface epithelial localization in obese biopsy antrum — likely reflects the difference in gastritis type: Group 2 harbors active (neutrophilic) antral gastritis, which drives deeper HP infiltration, whereas the predominantly chronic inactive pattern in Group 3 is associated with more superficial colonization.

HP density correlation with coccoid proportion and absence of age effect

As noted in Corpus HP density across groups and correlation with coccoid form, corpus HP density correlated positively with coccoid proportion across all groups (r = 0.267, p = 0.003), with the strongest density–coccoid association in Group 2 (r = 0.426, p = 0.003). Notably, coccoid proportion showed no significant correlation with patient age in any group or overall (corpus: r = 0.008, p = 0.934; antrum: r = 0.151, p = 0.136), indicating that the elevated coccoid burden in obese and obese biopsy patients reflects BMI-dependent rather than age-dependent gastric changes.

Coccoid form and gastric inflammation pattern

Corpus coccoid proportion showed no significant correlation with the degree of neutrophilic activation (Spearman r = − 0.119, p = 0.198) or the grade of chronic inflammation (r = 0.077, p = 0.408) when analyzed across all cases. Comparison of patients with active (activation grade ≥ 1) vs. inactive (grade = 0) corpus gastritis revealed no significant difference in coccoid proportions (active: median 1%, IQR: 1–15% vs. inactive: median 5%, IQR: 1–15%; Mann-Whitney p = 0.676). This independence was also confirmed within Group 1 (active: median 20% vs. inactive: median 10%; p = 0.631). These findings indicate that coccoid burden does not track with the acute inflammatory response, consistent with the known property of the VBNC state to suppress pro-inflammatory virulence factor expression. The gastritis pattern (chronic inactive vs. active) is thus a group-level phenomenon driven by BMI rather than a function of coccoid proportion per se.

Gastritis parameters assessed by Sydney criteria are presented in Table 5. Corpus inflammation severity did not differ significantly across groups (H = 2.27, p = 0.321). ROC analysis: optimal BMI threshold for predicting corpus coccoid ≥ 5%

ROC analysis identified BMI as a strong predictor of corpus coccoid proportion ≥ 5% (AUC = 0.882, 95% CI: 0.814–0.943). The Youden-optimal BMI cut-off was ≥ 27 kg/m² (sensitivity 98.3%, specificity 77.0%, PPV 80.6%, NPV 97.9%, accuracy 87.5%, Youden index J = 0.754). Notably, a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² (international BMI ≥ 30 threshold) yielded near-equivalent performance (Sens 96.6%, Spec 77.0%), and a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² (obesity threshold) maintained high sensitivity (93.2%) with the same specificity. A secondary ROC analysis for corpus coccoid ≥ 10% yielded an AUC of 0.846 (95% CI: 0.770–0.914), with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² achieving sensitivity 98.0%, specificity 66.2%. Both ROC analyses were complete-case analyses restricted to the 121 patients with evaluable corpus coccoid data (Group 1, n = 55; Group 2, n = 48; Group 3, n = 18).

Multivariable logistic regression: BMI as an independent predictor of corpus coccoid ≥ 10%

To determine whether BMI independently predicts elevated coccoid burden after adjustment for potential confounders, univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed with corpus coccoid ≥ 10% as the outcome, on the 121 patients with an evaluable corpus coccoid proportion (complete-case analysis; the 35 patients without evaluable corpus coccoid data were excluded and no values were imputed; N = 121; Table 6). In univariable analysis, BMI was the only significant predictor (OR = 1.125, 95% CI: 1.081–1.171, p < 0.001), while age (OR = 0.980, p = 0.206) and sex (OR = 1.298, p = 0.466) were not. In the multivariable model adjusting simultaneously for all three predictors, BMI remained the sole independent predictor of corpus coccoid ≥ 10% (OR = 1.134, 95% CI: 1.088–1.183, p < 0.001), indicating that each 1 kg/m² increase in BMI confers a 13.4% increase in the odds of reaching the ≥ 10% coccoid threshold. Age (OR = 0.968, p = 0.107) and sex (OR = 2.361, p = 0.068) were not independently significant, though a marginal trend was observed for male sex. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (C-statistic = 0.849) and good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow χ²=9.971, p = 0.267), with Nagelkerke R²=0.438. No multicollinearity was detected (all VIF < 1.02). Because the analysis was limited to the variables available in the archival records, the model could adjust only for age and sex; unmeasured confounders — including prior antibiotic exposure, proton-pump inhibitor use, diabetes, smoking status, and other metabolic parameters — could not be accounted for, and the independence of BMI should therefore be interpreted with corresponding caution.

Discussion

The principal findings of this study are: (1) patients in the severe obesity/sleeve group harbor a significantly higher proportion of H. pylori coccoid form in the gastric corpus compared to non-obese individuals; (2) BMI correlates strongly and positively with coccoid form proportion (r = 0.639, p < 0.001); (3) the ≥ 40% coccoid category was found exclusively in the severe obesity/sleeve group within this cohort, an exploratory observation that may represent a candidate morphological correlate of severe obesity pending external validation; (4) corpus spiral proportions decrease in a precise stepwise gradient across coccoid categories; (5) corpus HP density (Sydney score) is significantly higher in the severe obesity/sleeve group and correlates positively with coccoid conversion; (6) corpus crypt H. pylori involvement is significantly more frequent in the severe obesity/sleeve group, while antrum crypt involvement is paradoxically higher in non-obese patients; (7) coccoid burden is independent of patient age and does not correlate with inflammation grade.

Our results are consistent with and extend the findings of Mayer et al. [15], who reported a median coccoid percentage of 50% in patients with severe obesity undergoing sleeve gastrectomy versus 10% in non-obese controls (p < 0.001). Our three-group design with quantitative BMI data indicates that the obesity–coccoid association is not a binary obese/non-obese phenomenon but follows a BMI gradient: obese biopsy patients (BMI ≥ 30) showed significantly higher coccoid proportions than non-obese patients (p < 0.001) but were statistically indistinguishable from the obesity/sleeve group (p = 0.145), suggesting the threshold for a meaningful coccoid shift may already begin at BMI ≥ 30. Importantly, the observation that obese biopsy patients showed coccoid proportions comparable to those observed in the severe obesity/sleeve group argues against specimen size alone accounting for the observed association. This finding suggests that obesity-related biological factors, rather than differences in tissue sampling alone, may contribute to the higher coccoid proportions observed; nevertheless, because Group 1 comprised sleeve gastrectomy specimens and Groups 2–3 comprised endoscopic biopsies, a contribution of differential sampling to the corpus comparisons cannot be fully excluded, and the corresponding conclusions are therefore framed as associations.

The categorical analysis introduced in this study provides additional clinical granularity. The ≥ 40% coccoid category was exclusively observed in the severe obesity/sleeve group, representing nearly one quarter of that cohort. In practical terms, a gastric specimen with ≥ 40% coccoid H. pylori may serve as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating histological correlate of severe obesity; this threshold has not been externally validated and should not be regarded as a definitive histological signature until confirmed in independent prospective cohorts.

A novel contribution of this study is the analysis of intragastric H. pylori localization. Corpus crypt colonization was significantly more frequent in the severe obesity/sleeve group than in the non-obese and obese biopsy groups (41.8% vs. 29.2% vs. 11.1%; overall three-group p = 0.045), suggesting that the obesity-associated gastric microenvironment may be associated with deeper mucosal localization of the pathogen. This deeper localization has clinical implications: bacteria residing in glandular crypts are more physically protected from luminal antibiotics and host immune surveillance, potentially contributing to eradication failure [16]. Paradoxically, antrum crypt involvement was most frequent in non-obese patients (36.8% vs. 9.1% in obese biopsy patients; p = 0.011). This inverse antrum pattern aligns with the predominantly active (neutrophilic) antral gastritis in Group 2: neutrophilic activity drives H. pylori into deeper tissue compartments, a well-recognized histopathological observation [15, 17]. In contrast, obese biopsy patients with their less active gastritis pattern show more superficial antrum colonization.

The positive correlation between corpus HP density and coccoid proportion (r = 0.267, p = 0.003) is consistent with a dose–response framework: a higher bacterial burden provides more organisms susceptible to VBNC-triggering environmental stress, amplifying coccoid conversion. Several mechanistic pathways may underlie the observed obesity-associated coccoid predominance. The chronic low-grade inflammatory state (elevated IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, leptin), altered gastric acid dynamics (including PPI use for reflux) [18], and adiposity-related changes in mucus composition collectively create a stressful intragastric microenvironment. From the ultrastructural perspective, Cheng et al. [6] demonstrated that the spheroplast-like coccoid structure is devoid of a complete PGN shell, rendering it inherently insensitive to β-lactam antibiotics. An amplification of this process in obesity could therefore compound the antibiotic pharmacological access problem, although this proposed mechanism was not directly tested in the present study.

Multivariable logistic regression further corroborated the independence of BMI: after simultaneous adjustment for age and sex, BMI remained the sole significant predictor of corpus coccoid ≥ 10% (OR = 1.134 per 1 kg/m², 95% CI: 1.088–1.183, p < 0.001), with the model demonstrating excellent discrimination (C-statistic = 0.849) and good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.267). The observation that coccoid proportion is entirely independent of patient age (corpus: r = 0.008, p = 0.934; antrum: r = 0.151, p = 0.136) confirms that the obesity–coccoid association is not confounded by age-related mucosal changes. Similarly, the absence of a correlation between coccoid burden and inflammation grade, including within the severe obesity/sleeve group itself, indicates that the inactive gastritis phenotype seen in this group is a group-level property of the gastric microenvironment rather than being directly determined by coccoid proportion. This suggests that the metabolic and hormonal milieu of obesity — rather than the coccoid form per se — may contribute to the attenuated inflammatory response. These interpretations remain associative: as a retrospective study we could not adjust for prior antibiotic exposure, PPI use, diabetes, smoking, or other metabolic variables, all of which may influence both BMI and coccoid transformation, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design precludes direct causal inference. Second, antrum evaluation was unavailable in sleeve gastrectomy specimens due to the surgical technique. Third, coccoid proportions were estimated visually rather than by quantitative image analysis. Fourth, several potentially important confounders — including prior antibiotic exposure, proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) use, eradication history, diabetes, smoking status, and other metabolic variables — were not systematically available in the archival records. Their absence limits causal interpretation, and although BMI was identified as an independent predictor in a model adjusted for age and sex, residual confounding by these unmeasured factors cannot be excluded; BMI should therefore be interpreted as an independent correlate rather than a proven causal determinant. Fifth, molecular VBNC confirmation (RT-PCR, flow cytometry) was not performed. Sixth, comparison between sleeve gastrectomy material and endoscopic biopsies poses an inherent methodological limitation: sleeve gastrectomy specimens provide a substantially larger mucosal surface area for assessment, potentially increasing the detection of minor HP populations compared to the limited surface area of pinch biopsies. We addressed this limitation by including a separate obese biopsy group (Group 3), whose comparison with the non-obese biopsy group (Group 2) provides a tissue-equivalent matched comparison unaffected by specimen size differences. The obese biopsy group thus serves both as an intermediate BMI category and as an internal methodological control. Nonetheless, direct corpus comparisons between the sleeve gastrectomy group and the biopsy groups should be interpreted in light of this sampling difference, and the corresponding conclusions are presented as associations rather than as definitive quantitative differences. Seventh, morphological scoring relied on semiquantitative visual estimation rather than automated image analysis; although interobserver agreement was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.81), reproducibility across multiple centres and observers remains to be established in prospective studies. Finally, the proposed coccoid categorization cut-offs (particularly the ≥ 40% obesity-specific threshold) require validation in independent prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that obesity is strongly associated with a coccoid-shifted H. pylori morphology (higher coccoid proportion; BMI–coccoid correlation r = 0.639, p < 0.001) and with deeper corpus crypt colonization (41.8% vs. 11.1% in obese biopsy patients; p = 0.045). The ≥ 40% corpus coccoid category was observed exclusively in the severe obesity/sleeve group; this cut-off should be regarded as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating candidate correlate that requires validation in independent prospective cohorts before it can be considered a severe-obesity-specific histological signature. Corpus HP density correlates positively with coccoid proportion (r = 0.267, p = 0.003), and corpus spiral proportions decrease in a stepwise gradient (99%→90%→80%→40%). Coccoid burden is independent of patient age and gastric inflammation grade. Despite high HP density and elevated coccoid proportions, patients in the severe obesity/sleeve group exhibited lower neutrophilic activity and lacked severe (grade 3) activation. Because eradication outcomes were not evaluated in this study, these findings should be regarded as associative and hypothesis-generating. They may support the selective use of IHC-based morphological assessment in obese H. pylori-positive patients and, importantly, warrant prospective studies — incorporating obese biopsy individuals and eradication-outcome endpoints — to determine whether coccoid burden and BMI can meaningfully inform individualized eradication strategies. In summary, these data support an association between obesity and coccoid H. pylori morphology and are not intended to define a treatment algorithm; because eradication outcomes, antibiotic exposure, PPI use, and molecular VBNC confirmation were not assessed, no change to eradication regimens is implied, and any such clinical application awaits prospective validation.
