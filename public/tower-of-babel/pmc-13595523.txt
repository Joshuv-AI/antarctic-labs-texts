Spatial and single-cell dissection of medulloblastoma identifies developmental hierarchies and a prognostic PPIA-BSG tumor-immune axis
Authors: Mingze Chen, Bailin Duan, Jiao Zhang, Craig Daniels, Cuiling Hu, Jingzhe Yuan, Jirong Guo, Michael D Taylor, Xiangjun Shi, Liwei Zhang, Deling Li, Tao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedulloblastoma (MB) in children is highly heterogeneous, leading to its classification into molecular subgroups and resulting in significant differences in clinical outcomes even within the same risk group. This heterogeneity severely hampers the clinical management of MB. Therefore, it is essential to characterize MB heterogeneity within its tumor microenvironment.MethodsDeep single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on tumor tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues from two WNT, three Group 4 (G4), and one Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) MB patients. Introduction

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the most common malignant brain tumor in children [1], arising in the posterior fossa and exhibiting pronounced molecular, developmental, and clinical heterogeneity. It comprises four molecular subgroups, Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), WNT, Group 3, and Group 4, which differ substantially in biology, treatment response, and prognosis [2]. Despite advances in molecular stratification and multimodal therapy, outcomes remain constrained by recurrence, therapy resistance, and long-term neurotoxicity [3]. The mechanisms linking developmental heterogeneity with immune reprogramming in the tumor microenvironment remain insufficiently understood, restricting the identification of reliable prognostic markers and therapeutic targets [4].

MB is considered an immunologically cold tumor, characterized by sparse lymphocyte infiltration and limited antigen-presenting capacity, resulting in a predominantly suppressive immune microenvironment [5]. Although T cells and macrophages constitute the principal immune populations in MB, their functional states and interactions display considerable heterogeneity [6]. A central unresolved question in MB is whether immune suppression represents an intrinsic consequence of oncogenic and developmental programs within tumor cells or is instead imposed by extrinsic cues from the surrounding cerebellar microenvironment [7]. Addressing this question requires spatially resolved approaches capable of dissecting tumor-immune interactions.

MB subgroups arise from distinct neural progenitor lineages, with SHH tumors originating from granule cell precursors (GCPs), Group 4 tumors from unipolar brush cell (UBC)-related lineages, and Group 3 tumors from neural stem or progenitor cells (NSCs) [8]. This developmental diversity implies intrinsic heterogeneity in tumor cell states and their microenvironmental interactions. Elucidating how these programs intersect with immune regulation requires single-cell analysis within a preserved spatial context. However, most existing studies focus on isolated tumor tissues and lack integration of adjacent cerebellar regions [9], limiting insight into tumor-brain interactions and early pathogenic events at the tumor-peritumoral interface.

This study applied single-cell and spatial transcriptomic profiling of freshly isolated MB tumors across molecular subgroups, integrating paired adjacent cerebellar tissues to define cellular diversity and immune context. Spatial transcriptomics resolved tumor and microenvironmental organization, while key cellular components and communication pathways were validated at the protein level in 249 pediatric cases using tissue microarrays (TMAs). These analyses provide an integrated framework for MB biology and identify clinically relevant therapeutic targets.

Methods

Ethics

This retrospective study at Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, was approved by the Ethics Committee (KY2014-021–02) per the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from guardians of all pediatric participants.

Clinical specimens

Six paired MB tumors and adjacent cerebellar tissues were collected from treatment-naïve pediatric patients at primary surgery for single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomic analyses. None of the patients had received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other antitumor treatments prior to surgery.

For validation, an independent cohort of 249 pediatric patients with primary MB who had surgical resection between 2014–2020 at Beijing Tiantan Hospital was included for multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and prognostic analyses. All patients were newly diagnosed, had complete clinical data and follow-up, and received standardized postoperative treatment per GPOH HIT-2000 protocol [10]. Clinical and pathological variables were retrieved from records. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were obtained from follow-up systems. Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics library preparation

Fresh tumor and adjacent tissues were processed for scRNA-seq using a standard enzymatic dissociation protocol. Single-cell suspensions exhibited cell viability exceeding 96%. scRNA-seq libraries were generated using the GEXSCOPE kit (Singleron Biotechnologies, China) per instructions and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at approximately 50,000 paired-end reads per cell.

For spatial transcriptomics, tissue specimens were embedded, snap-frozen, and cryosectioned at 10 µm thickness. Spatial transcriptomic libraries were prepared using the 10x Genomics Visium Spatial Gene Expression platform following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on HiSeq 3000 system (Illumina) at 250–270 million reads per sample.

Sequencing data processing and quality control

Sequencing results were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ format using Illumina bcl2fastq software (version 5.0.1). Sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3′ gene counting were performed using CeleScope (v1.9.0), and reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome. Downstream analyses were conducted using Seurat (version 4.4.0). Cells with fewer than 500 detected genes and cells with mitochondrial transcript percentages greater than 25% were excluded. Potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3). A total of 329,601 cells were initially captured, and 316,446 high-quality cells were retained after quality-control filtering and doublet removal. Data were normalized using the NormalizeData function, highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, and scaled using ScaleData. Principal component analysis was performed using RunPCA, followed by clustering with FindNeighbors and FindClusters. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (version 1.1.0). Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, and functional enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.12.6).

Spatial transcriptomic data from matched tumor and cerebellar tissue sections were generated using the 10× Genomics Visium platform (10× Genomics, Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA). Hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections were processed with Space Ranger (Genergy Inc., Shanghai, China). Downstream analyses were performed using Seurat (v5.3.0). Gene-spot matrices were normalized using sctransform, followed by principal component analysis and Louvain clustering based on 30 principal components at a resolution of 0.5. Differentially expressed genes for each cluster were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with adjusted p < 0.05 and logFC > 0.25 defining marker genes. Cell-type enrichment within clusters was determined by assessing the overlap between spatial transcriptomic and scRNA-seq marker genes using Fisher’s exact test. All detected genes were used as background to compute odds ratios and significance.

Computational identification of lineage trajectories and gene regulatory networks

Trajectory inference, regulon analysis, co-expression network modeling, and copy number variation (CNV) inference were integrated to characterize transcriptional programs underlying lineage deviation and regulatory architecture. Cell lineage trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle3 (version 1.3.7) [11]. For each analyzed cell population or partition, a principal graph was learned in the reduced-dimensional transcriptomic space following the standard Monocle3 workflow. Root nodes for pseudotime ordering were selected individually for each trajectory based on the biological context and the putative starting region of the corresponding cell population. Pseudotime values were calculated as graph-based distances from the selected root node(s). The biological interpretation of early and late pseudotime states was guided by cell-state annotation and marker-gene expression. Genes exhibiting significant pseudotime-dependent variation were identified using graph-based differential expression testing to characterize developmental progression and cell-state transitions.

To distinguish malignant from non-malignant transcriptional states and support lineage interpretation, large-scale CNV was inferred using the inferCNV package (version 1.20.0; https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV). Immune cells served as reference populations. Raw count matrices, cell annotations, and gene order information from Ensembl GRCh38 were used for CNV inference, retaining lowly expressed genes at a 0.05 cutoff. Samples were downsampled to reduce computational bias.

Gene regulatory networks were inferred using the SCENIC framework implemented in pySCENIC (v0.11.2) [12]. Regulon activity was quantified at the single-cell level using AUCell (version 1.16.0) [13]. To assess cell state specificity of inferred regulons, regulon specificity scores were calculated using the scFunctions package based on Jensen-Shannon divergence between binary regulon activity profiles and cell state assignments along pseudotime.

To identify gene modules associated with lineage progression, high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) was performed on scRNA-seq data using the hdWGCNA R package (v0.2.24) [14]. Gene expression matrices derived from Seurat objects were processed following the standard workflow. To reduce single-cell data sparsity, cells were aggregated into metacells before network construction. An optimal soft-thresholding power (β) was selected based on scale-free topology criteria, and a signed weighted co-expression network was constructed. All analyses followed the hdWGCNA pipeline with default parameters.

For transcriptomic characterization, differentially expressed genes were identified using FindMarkers in Seurat with the presto backend [15]. Pathway activity was quantified at the single-cell level using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) implemented in the GSVA package (v1.30.0) [16].

Cell-cell communication and ligand-receptor interaction analysis

Potential intercellular communication was inferred using CellChat (v2.1.2) based on normalized single-cell expression data [17]. Cell-cell communication networks were constructed using the CellChatDB.human ligand-receptor database, focusing on secreted signaling pathways. Communication probabilities were quantified at both interaction and pathway levels. Aggregated networks were used to compare global signaling patterns across cell types and conditions.

Full-spectrum flow cytometry of PBMCs

Peripheral blood samples were collected from medulloblastoma patients and healthy donors before anesthesia and prior to any medication or treatment that could affect peripheral immune status. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated and analyzed by full-spectrum flow cytometry to validate regulatory T-cell phenotypes identified by scRNA-seq. The flow cytometry panel included CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD25, FOXP3, PD-1, CD39, CD73, CCR4, CD45RA, and Zombie viability dye. After exclusion of debris, doublets, and dead cells, CD4+ T cells were gated for downstream analysis. Tregs were defined as CD4+CD25+FOXP3+ cells, and PD-1 expression was assessed within the Treg compartment. Tr1-like regulatory T-cell populations were identified using the corresponding CCR4/CD45RA and CD39/CD73 gating strategy. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Comparisons between medulloblastoma patients and healthy donors were performed using a two-sided unpaired t test with Welch’s correction.

Isolation and culture of medulloblastoma cell lines

Fresh medulloblastoma tissues were obtained immediately after surgical resection and processed under sterile conditions. Tumor tissues were mechanically minced and enzymatically dissociated using collagenase IV and DNase I to generate single-cell suspensions, which were filtered and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% N-2 supplement, 2% B-27 supplement, 20 ng/mL recombinant human fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and 20 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor (EGF). Cells were maintained on Matrigel-coated culture flasks at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂.Three patient-derived primary medulloblastoma cell lines, TTST0625 (WNT), TTST0219 (Group 3), and TTST0815 (Group 4), together with the commercial SHH medulloblastoma cell line DAOY, were used in this study. All cell lines were maintained under the same culture conditions described above.

The isolation and culture of primary medulloblastoma cells were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2014-021–02). Written informed consent for tissue collection was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

siRNA-mediated BSG knockdown

Two independent siRNAs targeting human BSG (si-BSG#1 and si-BSG#2) and a non-targeting negative control siRNA (si-NC) were used for transient gene silencing. Cells were seeded one day before transfection to reach approximately 50–70% confluence and transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, BSG knockdown efficiency was evaluated by western blotting. Based on knockdown efficiency, si-BSG#1 was selected for subsequent PBMC co-culture and ELISA experiments.

The siRNA sequences were as follows: si-BSG#1 sense, 5′-UCCCUCACCUGCUCCUUUGAAUGTT-3′; si-BSG#1antisense,5′-CAUUCAAGGAGCAGGUGAGGATT-3′;si-BSG#2sense,5′-GGCAGCACCAGAAUGACAAAGTT-3′; si-BSG#2 antisense, 5′-CUUUGUCAUUCUGGUGCUGCCTT-3′; si-NCsense,5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′;andsi-NCantisense,5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′.

Western blotting

Forty-eight hours after siRNA transfection, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of total protein (20–30 μg) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal antibodies against BSG (Immunoway, Cat. No. YM9272) and GAPDH (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab181602). Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and BSG expression was normalized to GAPDH.

PBMC co-culture and PPIA ELISA

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood using Ficoll density-gradient centrifugation. Forty-eight hours after siRNA transfection, medulloblastoma cells were co-cultured with PBMCs for an additional 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Extracellular PPIA concentrations in the culture supernatants were measured using a human Cyclophilin A/PPIA ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab314722) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and PPIA concentrations were calculated based on the manufacturer-provided standard curve.

External transcriptomic validation and deconvolution

To validate the clinical relevance of PPIA-BSG signaling, bulk RNA-seq data with matched clinical information from the independent MB cohort GSE85218 were analyzed [18, 19]. Log2-transformed TPM values of PPIA and BSG were dichotomized by median expression and jointly stratified into four groups (PPIAlow/BSGlow, PPIAhigh/BSGlow, PPIAlow/BSGhigh, PPIAhigh/BSGhigh). OS was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing in 511 patients with complete survival data.

To adjust for immune-related confounding in bulk transcriptomic data, immune cell abundance was estimated using xCell [20]. Immune populations identified as major PPIA sources in single-cell analyses, including CD4+ memory T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells, NK cells, and related subsets, were incorporated as covariates. A composite microglia score was derived from canonical marker genes (P2RY12, CX3CR1, TMEM119, SALL1) due to the absence of explicit microglia modeling in xCell. Multivariable Cox models incorporated PPIA-BSG expression groups, immune covariates, age, sex, and molecular subgroup. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

mIF and TMA analysis

To validate protein-level expression, mIF was performed on TMAs from 249 pediatric patients with primary MB. mIF staining was performed on the AlphaXTSA 7 platform (Alpha X Bio, Beijing, China) using tyramide signal amplification. Antibody panels were optimized by prior single-plex testing. The staining panel included PPIA (Cyclophilin A; CypA) and BSG (CD147) as the ligand-receptor pair of interest, and synaptophysin (SYN) as a tumor cell marker to distinguish tumor (SYN+) from stromal (SYN−) regions. Slides were processed in a single batch, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using a ZEISS Axioscan 7 system (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany). Quantitative single-cell analysis was performed using HALO software (Indica Labs, Corrales, NM, USA), including delineation of tumor and stromal compartments, calculation of marker-positive cell density, and assessment of combinatorial marker expression patterns for downstream spatial and survival analyses.

Spatial metrics and proximity analysis

Spatial proximity analyses were performed on mIF whole-slide images using QuPath (v0.3.0) (The Queen’s University of Belfast, Northern Ireland, UK). SYN+ tumor cells served as spatial reference points. Single cells were identified by nuclear segmentation and classified by optimized intensity thresholds. Spatial neighborhoods were defined using the nuclear centroid of each SYN+ cell as the reference. Immune cells (PPIA+ and/or BSG+) within concentric radii of 10, 20, 30, and 40 µm were quantified to assess distance-dependent spatial relationships. The spatial contact ratio was defined as the proportion of PPIA+SYN− cells within a 10 µm radius of BSG+PPIA+SYN+ tumor cells and was used for proximity-based survival analyses.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, depending on data distribution. Multiple subgroup comparisons applied one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank tests. Optimal stratification cutoffs were determined using survminer. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Role of funders

The funders had no involvement in the design, execution, analysis, or interpretation of the study, nor in the writing or decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Integrated single-cell and spatial atlas defines the diverse cellular ecosystem of MB and peritumoral tissues

To characterize the cellular ecosystem of MB, single-cell transcriptomes from tumor tissues of six newly diagnosed pediatric patients (3 Group 4, 1 SHH, and 2 WNT cases) and paired cerebellar controls were analyzed (Fig. 1A). After quality control and integration, 316,446 cells and 33,048 genes were retained, yielding 51 clusters annotated by canonical markers (Fig. 1B, S1A, Supplementary Data S1 and Table S2). Tumor-associated progenitor populations included GCPs, glial progenitors, neural progenitor-like cell, and mesenchymal-like cells. Neural lineage cells comprised excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, purkinje cells, NSCs, oligodendrocytes, UBCs and UBC precursors (UBC-P). Endothelial cells representing the tumor vasculature were also identified. Immune populations included microglia, monocytes, neutrophils, and T Cells. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed distinct spatial organization consistent with histology and CNV patterns (Fig. 1C). All annotated cell types exhibited high and specific expression of marker genes (Fig. 1D and S1B). Cell-type proportions varied markedly between tumor and cerebellar tissues and across clinical strata, indicating substantial compositional heterogeneity (Fig. S1C) and motivating lineage-specific analyses of malignant deviation.

GCP lineage deviation drives malignant transformation and structural remodeling in MB

Among tumor-associated progenitors, GCPs showed prominent expansion and transcriptional heterogeneity. Re-clustering of 20,374 GCPs identified three subpopulations, including Tumor-GCPs and two noncanonical states, tenascin (TNC)+ GCPs and CCDC68+ GCPs (Figs. 2A, B, S2A and Table S3). Tumor-GCPs were enriched for progenitor- and cell cycle-associated gene programs. In contrast, TNC+ GCPs showed higher expression of genes related to extracellular matrix, adhesion, and migration, including TNC, VIM, and PATJ, together with enrichment of cilia-associated genes such as IFT57. CCDC68+ GCPs displayed a distinct transcriptional profile linked to structural support and cellular homeostasis (Fig. 2C).

Spatial transcriptomic projection mapped CCDC68+ GCPs, TNC+ GCPs, and Tumor-GCPs, together with pseudotime states, onto tissue sections, revealing heterogeneous spatial and pseudotemporal patterns across cerebellar and MB tissues (Fig. 2D). Pseudotime analysis arranged GCPs along a continuous trajectory, with CCDC68+ GCPs predominantly occupying early states and Tumor-GCPs and TNC+ GCPs distributed toward later regions. Two trajectory branches were observed, one enriched for Tumor-GCPs and the other for TNC+ GCPs (Fig. 2E). Along pseudotime progression, MYCN and SOX2 expression increased toward later pseudotime, whereas OTX2 and ZIC1 showed higher expression at early states (Fig. S2B). Transcription factor regulon activity differed among GCP subpopulations (Fig. 2F and S2C). Pseudotime states were heterogeneously distributed across tissues and molecular subgroups (Fig. S2D). Transcription factors including NR2F1 and NEUROG1 were preferentially expressed in GCPs occupying early pseudotime-associated regions and showed reduced expression in Tumor-GCP-enriched areas (Fig. S2E and F).

hdWGCNA identified multiple transcriptional modules along the GCP trajectory. The MG-M1 module, containing genes such as HOXA9 and MYCN, was enriched in Tumor-GCP-associated regions and showed functional enrichment for cell proliferation and mesenchymal-associated programs. In contrast, the MG-M2 module showed higher expression in TNC+ and CCDC68+ GCPs and was enriched for neuronal lineage-related and synapse-associated biological processes, indicating partial retention of developmental programs in these subsets (Fig. 2G–I, S2G).

UBC-like populations exhibit abnormal developmental trajectories and synapse-like functions in MB

Given the abundance of UBC and UBC-P populations, lineage trajectory analysis was applied to characterize their developmental states and malignant deviation in MB. Integrated analysis of 145,860 UBC and UBC-P cells resolved three subpopulations, termed UBC-1, UBC-2, and UBC-3, with differential distributions across tumor and cerebellar tissues and molecular subgroups (Figs. 3A, B, S3A and Table S4). UBC-2 was enriched for cell cycle and mitotic programs, including UBE2C, TOP2A, CENPF, and PTTG1, consistent with a proliferative progenitor-like state, whereas UBC-3 showed elevated expression of neuronal and synapse-associated genes such as TRPM3, SYT4, RELN, CADPS, and DYNC1I1, indicating acquisition of neuron-like transcriptional features without evidence of normal differentiation (Fig. 3C).

Spatial transcriptomic projection mapped UBC subpopulations onto tissue sections and revealed consistent spatial patterning across cerebellar and MB samples (Fig. 3D). Pseudotime trajectory analysis reconstructed a continuous developmental progression, placing UBC-2 at the root of the trajectory, followed by an intermediate UBC-1 state and a terminal UBC-3 state, consistent with progressive malignant deviation (Fig. 3E). Along the pseudotemporal axis, expression of SOX2, MYCN, and GLI2 increased toward the terminal Tumor-UBC state, whereas BARHL1, OTX2, and ZIC1 were preferentially expressed at early stages and declined with progression (Fig. S3B). Transcription factor regulon analysis further identified SOX11, MEIS1, EZH2, ZEB1, and OTX2 as progressively activated along the trajectory and enriched in Tumor-UBC cells (Fig. 3F and S3C–E).

HdWGCNA identified that the tumor-enriched module (MG-M1) was preferentially expressed in UBC-3 cells and linked to synapse-related and post-transcriptional regulatory processes, whereas intermediate UBC-1 cells showed concurrent activation of metabolic and synapse-related modules, consistent with a bifurcation between developmental stalling and malignant pseudo-differentiation (Fig. 3G–I).

NSC subpopulations in the peritumoral niche exhibit inflammatory reactivity and divergent developmental trajectories

NSCs were analyzed because MB exhibits stem cell-like features and multilineage differentiation potential [21]. Re-clustering of 14,744 NSC cells resolved four subpopulations (NSC-1 to NSC-4) that were predominantly distributed in adjacent cerebellar tissues (Figs. 4A, B, S4A and Table S5). Marker gene expression and functional annotation distinguished these states: NSC-2 showed enrichment of mitochondrial and oxidative phosphorylation-related genes, NSC-3 was characterized by neuronal and synapse-associated programs, whereas NSC-4 selectively expressed immune and inflammatory response genes, including CD74, CCL3/4 family members, and related pathways (Fig. 4C). Spatial and subgroup-specific mapping showed heterogeneous distributions of NSC subpopulations and pseudotime states across cerebellar and MB tissues (Fig. 4D).

Pseudotime analysis placed NSC-2 at the developmental origin, followed by progression through NSC-1 toward the neuronal-like endpoint NSC-3, while NSC-4 diverged from the main trajectory (Fig. 4E). SOX2 expression was highest at early pseudotime and declined with progression, whereas neuronal differentiation-associated features were enriched toward the terminal NSC-3 state (Fig. S4B). Transcription factor activity analysis revealed that JUN, JUNB, and FOS were selectively enriched in NSC-4, consistent with its inflammatory-reactive identity, whereas NEUROD1 and LHX1 showed higher activity in NSC-3, aligning with its neuronal-like endpoint along pseudotime (Fig. 4F and S4C–F).

Integration of pseudotime with hdWGCNA showed that NSC-2 was enriched for MG-M2 (translation-related functions), NSC-1 for MG-M1 (neurodevelopmental programs), NSC-3 for MG-M1 and MG-M3 (neuronal and metabolic features), whereas NSC-4 showed preferential association with MG-M3 and partial overlap with MG-M2, consistent with a metabolically active stress-responsive state (Fig. 4G–I and S4G).

MB peritumoral environment is characterized by proliferative microglia and activated Tregs

In light of the pronounced lineage deviations across progenitor and neural compartments, the immune landscape was examined to assess coordinated immune reprogramming within the peritumoral niche. Microglia emerged as the dominant immune population in MB (Fig. 1B). Unsupervised analysis resolved homeostatic, disease-associated, interferon-responsive, and proliferating microglial states. Homeostatic microglia were enriched in adjacent tissue, whereas interferon-responsive and proliferative states were preferentially expanded in tumors, consistent with tumor-driven immune activation (Figs. S5A-C, S6A, S7). Pseudotime analysis revealed a trajectory originating from homeostatic microglia that bifurcated toward inflammatory and proliferative states, suggesting a microenvironment-induced state shift (Fig. S5D). Transcription factor analysis identified MAFB as a core regulator associated with activated microglial states, linking inflammatory signaling to microglial activation in tumors (Figs. S5E, F, S6D). hdWGCNA further demonstrated that inflammation-associated modules were enriched in disease-associated, interferon-responsive, and proliferating microglia, jointly delineating a shift from immune surveillance to chronic inflammatory activation (Fig. S5G, S5H, S6E). Infiltrating lymphocytes were sparse but exhibited pronounced functional polarization. Subclustering of T and NKT cells revealed a dominant FOXP3+ CD4 Treg population with elevated immunosuppressive and exhaustion-associated programs and reduced cytotoxic signatures, suggesting a disproportionate contribution to immune suppression within an otherwise lymphocyte-poor, immunologically cold tumor microenvironment (Figs. S8 and S9). These lineage-specific state transitions across progenitor, neural, and immune compartments prompted investigation of their coordination through intercellular communication within the tumor and peritumoral microenvironment.

To provide independent experimental validation of the regulatory T-cell programs identified by scRNA-seq, we performed full-spectrum flow cytometry using PBMCs from medulloblastoma patients and healthy donors (Fig. S8F). Representative gating strategies confirmed the identification of circulating Treg and Tr1-like populations. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant increase in PD-1+ Tregs in medulloblastoma patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. S8G). In addition, Tr1-like regulatory T-cell populations were also increased in medulloblastoma patients (Fig. S8H). These findings support the expansion of regulatory and exhaustion-associated T-cell phenotypes in medulloblastoma and provide independent phenotypic validation of the transcriptomic observations.

Spatial communication patterns reveal subgroup-specific signaling and immune evasion mechanisms

Intercellular communication was examined using CellChat at single-cell and spatial transcriptomic resolution. Despite medulloblastoma being considered immunologically cold, extensive cell-cell communication was observed across tumor and adjacent tissues (Figs. 5A, 6A, 7A, S11A–B, S12A–B, S13A–B). Across molecular subgroups, immune populations, particularly microglia and infiltrating immune cells, predominantly acted as signal receivers with higher incoming interaction strength, whereas putative tumor-origin cells, including GCPs, UBCs, and NSCs, functioned mainly as signal senders. In adjacent tissues, inhibitory neurons displayed globally reduced incoming and outgoing interactions (Fig. S10A). The overall signaling architecture in Group 4 tumors further confirmed this sender-receiver imbalance (Fig. 5B and S10B). Across the analyzed WNT, SHH, and Group 4 tumors, CypA, encoded by PPIA, emerged as a shared and dominant communication axis, being broadly active across most cell types in both tumor and adjacent regions (Figs. 5B, 6B, 7B, S10B–D, S11C–D, S12C–D, S13C–D and Table S6). This convergence suggests that PPIA may represent a shared signaling feature underlying intercellular communication in the analyzed WNT, SHH, and Group 4 tumors. Subtype-specific immune modulation was reflected by specific patterns of immune evasion and peritumoral defense. In Group 4 tumors, MHC-I-mediated interactions were reduced in tumor tissue compared with adjacent regions, particularly affecting T-cell-associated communication (Fig. 5C and S11E), consistent with impaired antigen presentation. Concurrently, APP signaling showed increased engagement of microglia within tumors (Fig. 5D and S11F). In WNT tumors, MHC-I communication was largely absent in tumor tissue but preserved in adjacent regions (Fig. 6C and S12E), while PSAP and NCAM signaling were preferentially enhanced in adjacent tissues (Figs. 6D, E, S12F, G), indicating a localized protective response. In SHH tumors, complement signaling was prominent in adjacent tissues but markedly attenuated within tumors (Fig. 7C and S13E), whereas SPP1 signaling was similarly reduced in tumor regions relative to surrounding tissue (Fig. 7D and S13F), highlighting subgroup-specific immune escape strategies accompanied by compensatory peritumoral signaling.

The PPIA-BSG signaling axis mediates tumor-immune crosstalk and serves as an independent prognostic factor

To resolve the molecular composition of the dominant PPIA signaling, ligand-receptor interactions were decomposed at the receptor level using CellChat. BSG (CD147) emerged as the primary receptor associated with PPIA-driven interactions among tumor cells, microglia, and infiltrating immune populations in the analyzed WNT, SHH, and Group 4 tumors. (Fig. 5E–H, 6F–I, 7E–H). These findings indicate that PPIA–BSG represents a candidate tumor-associated communication pathway shared among the analyzed WNT, SHH, and Group 4 tumors.

To experimentally validate the computationally inferred PPIA–BSG communication axis, we performed loss-of-function studies by silencing BSG in four medulloblastoma cell models, including the commercial SHH cell line DAOY and three patient-derived primary cell lines representing the WNT (TTST0625), Group 3 (TTST0219), and Group 4 (TTST0815) molecular subgroups. Western blotting confirmed efficient BSG knockdown in all four cell models following siRNA transfection (Fig. 8A). Among the two BSG-targeting siRNAs tested, si-BSG#1 consistently exhibited greater knockdown efficiency than si-BSG#2 and was therefore selected for subsequent functional experiments.To investigate whether BSG perturbation influences extracellular PPIA levels during tumor–immune interactions, BSG-silenced medulloblastoma cells were co-cultured with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) for 24 h, and extracellular PPIA concentrations in the culture supernatants were subsequently quantified by ELISA. Compared with the si-NC group, BSG knockdown significantly reduced extracellular PPIA levels across all four medulloblastoma models (Fig. 8B).

Protein-level validation using mIF in 249 pediatric MB cases (Figs. 8C and S14) showed that high densities of BSG+SYN+ cells and PPIA+SYN+ cells were associated with poorer OS (Fig. 8D and S15A). Similarly, in adjacent regions, elevated densities of PPIA+SYN− and BSG+SYN− cells predicted adverse prognosis (Figs. 8E and S15B). Spatial proximity analyses demonstrated that reduced distances between BSG+SYN+ tumor cells and PPIA+SYN− cells were associated with worse survival (Fig. S15C). Consistently, increased numbers of PPIA+SYN− cells within 10, 20, 30, and 40 μm neighborhoods centered on BSG+PPIA+SYN+ tumor cells predicted progressively poorer outcomes (Fig. S15D–G). Importantly, a higher spatial contact ratio emerged as a significant adverse prognostic indicator (Fig. S15H).

Lastly, the prognostic significance of PPIA-BSG signaling was independently validated in an external cohort of 511 pediatric MB patients. While high BSG expression alone was associated with poor survival, PPIA alone showed no significant prognostic effect (Fig. S16A–C). Stratification based on combined PPIA and BSG expression revealed significant survival differences among four groups (Figs. 8F and S16D). Multivariable Cox regression confirmed that high BSG expression and the combined high PPIA-high BSG state were independent adverse prognostic factors, after adjustment for clinical and immune variables (Figs. 8G and S16E). Together, these results support PPIA–BSG signaling as a clinically relevant tumor–immune communication axis in MB, with prognostic relevance further supported by external validation including Group 3 cases.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that MB biology extends beyond the tumor core into the adjacent cerebellar environment. Integrated single-cell and spatial analyses of matched tumor and non-tumor tissues revealed subgroup-specific developmental states and spatially organized immune architectures at the tumor-brain interface. These findings indicate that developmental context and local immune organization are integral to MB biology and provide a spatial framework for interpreting tumor-microenvironment interactions.

The UBC-related programs identified in Group 4 MB refine current models of tumor origin and progression by revealing a state of malignant pseudo-differentiation. Trajectory analyses indicated that UBC-like tumor cells did not proceed toward terminal differentiation but instead deviated into a transcriptional state characterized by partial acquisition of neuronal and synapse-associated features, including expression of RELN [22, ]SYT4 [23, ]CADPS [24], and other synaptic genes. This pattern suggests that Tumor-UBCs adopt neuron-like properties in form but not function, consistent with pseudo-differentiation. The enrichment of synapse-related transcriptional modules and post-transcriptional regulatory programs in terminal Tumor-UBC states further supports the presence of synaptic mimicry, in which tumor cells transcriptionally resemble neurons without engaging in normal circuit integration. This state may facilitate adaptation in the cerebellar environment by allowing tumor cells to coexist with surrounding neural tissue while avoiding normal developmental constraints. Importantly, the progressive activation of transcriptional regulators such as SOX11 and EZH2 along the UBC trajectory suggests a gated regulatory process that stabilizes this pseudo-differentiated state. SOX11 may promote neuronal gene expression programs [25], while EZH2-mediated chromatin regulation may simultaneously suppress terminal differentiation [26], thereby locking tumor cells into a synapse-like but proliferative phenotype. These findings suggest UBC-related pseudo-differentiation as a defining feature of Group 4 MB and highlight lineage-specific transcriptional control as a key determinant of malignant trajectory.

The identification of an inflammatory-reactive NSC subpopulation in peritumoral cerebellar tissue indicates that MB exerts biological influence beyond the tumor core. NSC-4 cells were transcriptionally distinct from developmental NSC trajectories, diverging from the main pseudotime path and showing selective activation of immune and stress-response programs marked by CD74, CCL3/4, and AP-1 family transcription factors. This transcriptional profile differs from canonical neurodevelopmental programs and instead resembles reactive NSC states under inflammatory or pathological conditions [27, 28]. These findings support the presence of a peritumoral “pre-activated zone”, in which resident NSCs are transcriptionally reprogrammed before overt tumor infiltration. This zone implies that MB is not a spatially confined mass but a biologically altered niche at its margins. From a clinical perspective, this has implications for marginal resection, as tissue that appears non-tumorous by conventional histology may already harbor inflammatory and stress-responsive cellular states shaped by tumor proximity [29]. Recognition of this peritumoral niche helps explain local recurrence and highlights the value of spatially informed approaches in surgical planning and therapy selection.

MB is commonly classified as an immunologically cold tumor due to its low lymphocyte content [30]. However, the present findings indicate that immune inactivity does not equate to immune quiescence. Instead, the MB microenvironment is dominated by highly activated immunosuppressive cell states. Microglia underwent marked tumor-driven reprogramming, shifting from homeostatic states in adjacent cerebellum toward interferon-responsive and proliferative phenotypes within tumors, consistent with chronic activation [31]. In addition, although lymphocyte infiltration is limited, infiltrating T cells are strongly polarized toward a FOXP3+ regulatory phenotype with elevated exhaustion and suppressive programs and diminished cytotoxic features [32]. These observations suggest that MB immune “coldness” reflects the presence of a dense suppressive barrier maintained by activated microglia and Tregs [33]. This globally suppressive immune architecture may account for the limited efficacy of conventional immune checkpoint blockade in MB and suggests that effective immunotherapeutic strategies require direct modulation of dominant suppressive immune cell states in addition to promoting lymphocyte infiltration [34].

Previous studies have implicated the PPIA-BSG axis in multiple cancer-related biological processes. PPIA, also known as cyclophilin A, can be secreted into the extracellular space under inflammatory or stress-related conditions, where it interacts with its major cell-surface receptor BSG [35, 36]. This axis has been reported in several tumour contexts, including glioma/glioblastoma and other solid tumours, and has been associated with tumour progression, invasion, therapeutic resistance, immune modulation, and poor prognosis [37–39]. Previous studies have also linked PPIA-BSG signalling to ERK/MAPK and NF-κB-related pathway activation, matrix metalloproteinase production, extracellular matrix remodelling, and metabolic adaptation through BSG- associated monocarboxylate transporter function [40]. These reported biological roles provide a plausible rationale for why PPIA-BSG signalling may be associated with MB progression and adverse clinical outcome. The spatial organization of PPIA-BSG signaling provides additional biological context for its prognostic impact. The association between poor survival and a high spatial contact ratio indicates that the adverse outcome is not driven by expression alone, but by proximity-dependent signaling between tumor cells and adjacent immune cells. Given that CypA is a secreted ligand and CD147 is a surface receptor with well-documented roles in metabolic support, matrix remodeling, and immune modulation, reduced physical distance likely facilitates sustained paracrine and juxtacrine signaling between tumor cells and neighboring immune or stromal cells [36, 41]. This proximity may facilitate local immunosuppression, metabolic adaptation, or survival signaling at the tumor-brain interface, creating spatially confined niches that favor tumor persistence and progression. Importantly, this interpretation is supported by the consistent prognostic value of spatial metrics across multiple distance thresholds and by independent validation in large clinical cohorts. These findings suggest that the PPIA-BSG axis is more than a passive biomarker, but a spatially actionable communication pathway. Targeting BSG, potentially in combination with cytotoxic therapy or emerging immune-modulatory strategies, may disrupt these proximity-dependent suppressive circuits and weaken the spatial shielding that characterizes aggressive MB lesions.

Despite identifying clinically relevant spatial tumor-microenvironment interactions, this study has several limitations. First, although the discovery dataset contained a large number of single cells, the number of patients was limited, and Group 3 tumors were not included in the single-cell and spatial discovery analyses due to sample availability. Therefore, lineage trajectory patterns and subtype-specific programs should be interpreted with caution, particularly with respect to patient-level heterogeneity and generalizability across all MB molecular subtypes. This limitation is partly mitigated by subtype-stratified CellChat analysis, wet-lab validation of the PPIA-BSG axis, and validation in the external bulk transcriptomic dataset GSE85218, which includes Group 3 tumors and supports the broader prognostic relevance of the PPIA-BSG axis. Second, spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed at the spot level, which may obscure fine-scale cellular interactions within densely cellular regions.Although our spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed regional distribution patterns of PPIA- and BSG-expressing spots, the HD spatial transcriptomic data used in this study do not provide strict single-cell resolution. This limitation is particularly relevant for evaluating immune-related ligand–receptor interactions in medulloblastoma, which is generally characterized by sparse immune-cell infiltration. Therefore, spot-level detection should not be interpreted as definitive single-cell-level colocalization or direct tumor–immune cell contact. Future studies incorporating higher-resolution spatial technologies, more extensive immune phenotyping, and single-cell/spatial mapping algorithms such as CellTrek will be valuable for systematically evaluating ligand–receptor spatial relationships and intercellular distances. Third, cell-cell communication pathways were inferred from transcriptomic ligand–receptor co-expression and spatial proximity analyses, which do not directly demonstrate protein-level interactions or downstream signaling activity. Therefore, the inferred ligand–receptor pairs should be interpreted as candidate communication features, and further studies are required to validate their biological roles in MB. Lastly, although additional flow-cytometric validation was performed for circulating Treg populations and exhaustion-associated immune markers, the functional states of immune cells, particularly tumor-associated microglia, were primarily inferred from transcriptional programs rather than direct functional assays. Furthermore, the retrospective design of the validation cohort may introduce selection bias. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts with improved subgroup coverage, higher-resolution spatial technologies, and mechanistic validation will be essential to extend and refine these findings.

In conclusion, this study defines spatially organized developmental and immune programs in medulloblastoma and identifies the PPIA–BSG axis as a clinically relevant candidate tumor–immune interaction that may inform prognostic stratification and guide targeted therapeutic intervention.
