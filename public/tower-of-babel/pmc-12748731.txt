Effect of the time interval between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty on the risk of postoperative stroke and death
Authors: Yi-Shiun Tsai, Neng-Chuan Tseng, Yeong-Ruey Chu, Pei-Tseng Kung, Wen-Chen Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough postoperative strokes after total hip arthroplasty are infrequent, they result in severe injury and can be fatal. This retrospective cohort study employed data from the nationwide research database. The study comprised 44,752 patients aged ≥ 40 years who received total hip arthroplasty from 2005 to 2017. The population were categorized into two groups based on the presence or absence of preoperative stroke. Patients with preoperative stroke exhibited a 4.37 times higher risk of postoperative stroke than did those without preoperative stroke (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.82–10.48). Moreover, patients with preoperative stroke exhibited a 33.19 times higher risk of postoperative stroke when the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and surgery was less than 180 days (95% CI: 9.12–120.77). However, when the elapsed time was more than 365 days, the risk of postoperative stroke was only 2.25 times higher (95% CI: 0.69–7.32). Furthermore, patients with preoperative stroke had a 1.22 times higher risk of postoperative death (95% CI: 0.68–2.15), but this result is not statistically significant. In conclusion, delaying total hip arthroplasty for at least 1 year after stroke may lower the risk of postoperative stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30111-4.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Preoperative stroke, Postoperative stroke, Postoperative death, Elapsed times between preoperative stroke and surgerySubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience, Risk factors

Introduction

The incidence of osteoarthritis is increasing as the population ages, leading to a projected rise in the number of patients with hip osteoarthritis. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been demonstrated to alleviate pain, improve function, and enhance the quality of life for patients with hip osteoarthritis1. Although the outcomes of total hip arthroplasty are generally satisfactory, postoperative complications can still occur. Stroke, although rare, is one such complication that can result in severe injury or even death2.

Despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative and postoperative care, the incidence of stroke has not decreased. Perioperative and postoperative stroke prolong hospitalization and increase both the disability rate and postoperative mortality rate3. Patients with a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack are at a greater risk of postoperative stroke4–6. Limited research has specifically examined postoperative stroke in total hip arthroplasty patients. Studies have indicated that patients who received THA have a longer life expectancy than those without THA7–9. However, total hip arthroplasty is associated with an increased short-term mortality rate10,11, with early postoperative mortality rates ranging from 0.3% to 1.0%12.

Cardiovascular diseases such as acute coronary syndrome and stroke increase the risk of postoperative cardiovascular complications and mortality. Determining the safest interval between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty is critical; however, research on this topic is limited and conclusions are varied. Jørgensen et al. reported that patients with a prior stroke undergoing non-cardiovascular surgery had a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, particularly when surgery occurred within nine months of the stroke13. Regarding the influence of timing on mortality among patients with preoperative cardiovascular disease undergoing joint replacement surgery, a shorter interval (< 12 months) between acute coronary syndrome and surgery is associated with higher mortality, whereas the interval between prior stroke and surgery appears to have no significant effect14.

Numerous studies have examined the incidence of recurrent stroke and postoperative complications, including stroke and death, following joint replacement surgery. This study determined whether patients with preoperative stroke have a significantly higher risk of postoperative stroke and death than individuals without such a history after total hip arthroplasty. Furthermore, the study assessed whether the interval between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty significantly influenced the risk of postoperative stroke and death.

Results

Descriptive statistics of patients with or without preoperative stroke who received total hip arthroplasty

The research population comprised 44,752 patients aged ≥ 40 years who received total hip arthroplasty from 2005 to 2017. As shown in Tables 1 and 2, 24,172 of the patients were female (54.01%) and 20,580 of the patients were male (45.99%). The majority of the patients were aged ≥ 75 years (18.54%), had a monthly salary of ≤ NT$22,800 (68.91%), and had a CCI score of 0 (96.68%). Moreover, the majority of the patients received their total hip arthroplasty from 2014 to 2017 (65.13%). Significant differences were found in demographic variables such as age, monthly salary, urbanization of residence, CCI score, and year of surgery between patients with and without preoperative stroke (p < 0.05). To mitigate selection bias, propensity score matching was performed for patients with and without preoperative stroke by using a 1:10 ratio. The matched groups included 1,113 patients with preoperative stroke and 11,130 patients without preoperative stroke. After matching, no significant differences were noted in the demographic variables between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Effect of preoperative stroke on the incidence of postoperative stroke after total hip arthroplasty

This study employed a log-rank test to assess the correlation between various factors and the incidence of postoperative stroke following total hip arthroplasty. The results revealed that the incidence rate of postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty was 0.81% for patients with preoperative stroke and 0.14% for those without (Table 3). The bivariate analysis indicated a significant difference in postoperative stroke incidence rates within 90 days between the two groups (p < 0.05). Further analysis, after controlling for additional variables, revealed that patients with preoperative stroke had a 4.37 times higher risk of experiencing a postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty compared with patients without preoperative stroke (HR = 4.37, 95% CI: 1.8–10.48), as detailed in Table 3; Fig. 1. Confounders in the bivariate analysis, such as liver disease and the use of anticoagulant therapy, were significantly associated with the occurrence of stroke within 90 days after THA (P < 0.05), as shown in supplementary Table 1. However, there are no statistically significant differences after multivariable adjustment (P > 0.05). The other confounders, such as gender, monthly salary, hypertension, and the type of anesthesia, had no statistically significant association with postoperative stroke incidence within 90 days after THA, as shown in supplementary Table 1 (P > 0.05). The elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty was statistically significant (p < 0.05) for postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 2. The risk of postoperative stroke decreased as the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty increased. Patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty within 180 days of preoperative stroke had a 33.19 times higher risk of postoperative stroke than those without preoperative stroke (HR = 33.19, 95% CI: 9.12–120.77). Conversely, patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty more than 365 days after preoperative stroke had only a 2.25 times higher risk of postoperative stroke than those without preoperative stroke (95% CI: 0.69–7.32), although this result was not significant (p > 0.05).

In a subgroup analysis, the results were comparable and are presented in the supplementary Table 3. Patients with a history of ischemic stroke had a significantly higher 90-day postoperative stroke risk after total hip arthroplasty (HR = 5.69, 95% CI: 1.69–19.22; P < 0.05). This risk was markedly elevated when the stroke-to-surgery interval was < 180 days (HR = 91.11, 95% CI: 13.61–609.89) or 181–365 days (HR = 31.50, 95% CI: 2.35–422.64; P < 0.05). With respect to preoperative hemorrhagic stroke, subgroup analysis was not feasible due to the limited number of postoperative stroke events (n = 3 within 90 days).

Effect of preoperative stroke on postoperative death after total hip arthroplasty

The results revealed that the mortality rate within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty was 0.75% for patients with preoperative stroke and 1.44% for those without (Table 4). The bivariate analysis revealed a significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.05). However, after other variables were controlled for, patients with preoperative stroke had a 1.22 times higher risk of death within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty compared with those without preoperative stroke (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.68–2.15), but this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Table 4 presents the effect of the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty on the relative risk of death within 90 days postsurgery. Although a longer interval between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty was associated with a reduced risk of postoperative death, the result was not significant (p > 0.05).

In a subgroup analysis based on stroke etiology, the interval between preoperative ischemic stroke and total hip arthroplasty (THA) was significantly associated with 90-day postoperative mortality. Patients with a stroke-to-surgery interval of less than 180 days and those with an interval between 180 and 365 days had 3.91 times (HR = 3.91, 95% CI: 1.06–14.41) and 5.35 times (HR = 5.35, 95% CI: 1.18–24.35) higher risks of mortality, respectively, compared with patients without prior stroke (P < 0.05). The results were presented in the supplementary Table 4. With respect to preoperative hemorrhagic stroke, subgroup analysis was not feasible due to the limited number of postoperative death events (n = 2 within 90 days).

Discussion

Most previous studies have identified a history of stroke as a significant risk factor for postoperative stroke following total hip arthroplasty4,5,15. A few studies have argued that preoperative stroke and postoperative stroke after total hip arthroplasty are unrelated16,17, possibly because patients received anticoagulant therapy prior to surgery, thereby reducing the risk of perioperative stroke. However, the present study adjusted for multiple variables, including anticoagulant use and still found a significant increase in the risk of postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty. Therefore, this study concluded that preoperative stroke is an independent risk factor for postoperative stroke after total hip arthroplasty, consistent with most studies. Many factors contribute to the increased risk of postoperative stroke in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty. Perioperative management including anticoagulation, blood pressure control, and even type of anesthesia could very well be the determinants of stroke risk after an operation. Anesthesia and the surgical procedure itself can induce physiological changes such as postoperative cardiac arrhythmia, hypotension, and hypercoagulation, all of which increase the risk of postoperative stroke18. In addition, THA can lead to fat embolism during surgery, which is particularly hazardous in patients with right-to-left cardiac shunts, thereby heightening the risk of postoperative stroke19. Antithrombotic therapy can prevent recurrent ischemic stroke20. In this study, preoperative anticoagulant use did not reduce postoperative stroke risk. However, the lack of perioperative medication details—such as discontinuation timing or dosing—may have influenced the results. Future research should explore these factors further. General anesthesia has been associated with an increased risk of postoperative stroke21, as it is more likely to induce intraoperative hypotension compared with regional anesthesia22. Theoretically, this may increase cerebrovascular risk; however, our findings did not support this association.

For patients with a history of stroke undergoing total hip arthroplasty, the operation can be performed electively after careful consideration of surgical risks; however, the optimal timing of surgery remains a matter of debate. Most studies suggest that extending the interval between preoperative stroke and surgery reduces the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, with risk stabilization observed between 90 days and 9 months post-stroke13,23. Current guidelines therefore recommend delaying elective non-cardiac surgery for at least 6 to 9 months24. However, a few studies have reported conflicting results, showing that in coronary artery bypass grafting, a longer interval between stroke and surgery may paradoxically increase the risk of postoperative stroke25. These varying conclusions and recommendations regarding the timing of surgery likely reflect differences in research subjects, surgical procedures, and comorbidity profiles across studies. We focused on a single surgical procedure—total hip arthroplasty—to evaluate the effect of the interval between preoperative stroke and surgery on the risk of postoperative stroke. This study found that patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty within 1 year after a prior stroke had a significantly higher risk of postoperative stroke within 90 days. This relationship may be explained by impaired cerebral autoregulation occurring shortly after an ischemic stroke, which begins to deteriorate within five days and gradually recovers over approximately three months26. Therefore, undergoing surgery during this period poses a heightened risk of secondary neuronal injury due to factors such as anesthesia, surgical stress, and postoperative care. Consequently, a shorter elapsed time between preoperative stroke and surgery is associated with a higher risk of postoperative stroke.

This study found that patients with a history of stroke undergoing total hip arthroplasty did not have an increased risk of mortality within 90 days postoperatively. Previous studies have reported that patients with prior stroke had a 1.4–1.7-fold higher 90-day mortality risk following THA14,27,28, which contrasts with our findings. Stroke is a cardiovascular disease and a risk factor of multiple diseases. It can complicate postoperative recovery by impairing physical function, reducing pain sensitivity, causing cognitive dysfunction, and delaying the diagnosis and treatment of complications, potentially leading to death29. Theoretically, patients with preoperative stroke are at a higher risk of postoperative stroke following total hip arthroplasty. Several factors may explain the discrepancies among studies. First, differences in the timing of a mortality assessment may contribute to the variation in findings. During this period, evaluated at 90 days postoperatively, most deaths result from cardiovascular and respiratory complications, with ischemic heart disease accounting for the largest proportion, while cancer becomes more common after 90 days post-surgery30. Similarly, Pedersen et al. reported an increased 30-day mortality but a decreased overall 90-day mortality following THA31. Second, the use of propensity score matching and multivariable regression models to adjust for various patient comorbidities, including other cardiovascular diseases, may have attenuated the specific effect of preoperative stroke on postoperative outcomes, resulting in statistically nonsignificant findings. Third, postoperative mortality risk may vary according to stroke subtype and the interval between stroke and surgery. Our subgroup analysis found that patients with ischemic stroke who underwent THA within 365 days of the stroke had a significantly higher risk of 90-day postoperative mortality.

Previous studies have shown mixed results regarding the impact of the stroke-to-surgery interval on postoperative mortality. Jørgensen and Liao et al. reported higher 30-day mortality with shorter intervals13,29, whereas Sanders et al. found no significant association14. We found that the risk of postoperative death within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty was lower if the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty was longer, although this trend did not reach significance. In subgroup analysis, when the interval between a preoperative ischemic stroke and total hip arthroplasty is less than one year, the risk of mortality within 90 days after surgery is significantly increased. The effect of preoperative stroke on postoperative mortality may have been attenuated by the inclusion of hemorrhagic stroke cases, resulting in a lack of statistically significant association with postoperative mortality.

The optimal interval between preoperative stroke and surgery to minimize the risk of postoperative stroke and mortality is an important issue. Patients with a history of stroke who undergo total hip arthroplasty have an increased risk of postoperative stroke compared with the general population, and recurrent strokes tend to present with more severe neurological deficits. Although total hip arthroplasty (THA) effectively alleviates pain and improves function in most patients, non-surgical interventions—such as physical therapy, pharmacologic management, and lifestyle modification—may serve as alternative or adjunctive options for pain control before surgery. Because THA is an elective procedure, surgical timing should be individualized according to each patient’s medical stability and cerebrovascular risk profile. In most cases, elective THA may be appropriately delayed for more than 12 months following a stroke to optimize neurological recovery and reduce perioperative risk.

Limitations

There are several limitations in the study. First, the primary limitation of this study lies in its reliance on secondary data sourced from the National Health Insurance database, which was originally compiled by medical institutions. These data lacked essential variables that could potentially influence outcomes, such as patients’ lifestyle habits (e.g., smoking or alcohol consumption), body mass index, other health behaviors, stroke severity, and rehabilitation level. Stroke severity (minor vs. major stroke) could strongly affect outcomes. Stroke severity (Baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores and modified Rankin Scale–adjusted deficits) could not be included in the present analysis because such information was not available in the National Health Insurance Research Database. Second, although preoperative anticoagulant use was included as a confounder, the actual perioperative administration of anticoagulants, including timing of discontinuation, resumption, and dosage, could not be assessed and may have influenced the outcomes. In addition, the database lacked detailed information on intraoperative hemodynamics and surgical duration, which could also have affected the results. Third, the study excluded simultaneous and staged bilateral THA cases. Whether unilateral and bilateral (staged or simultaneous) THA procedures result in different postoperative outcomes remains unclear and needs to be further investigated. Fourth, postoperative mortality included all-cause deaths except suicide and accidental causes, without stratification by specific cause in this study. Consequently, the association between preoperative stroke and cause-specific mortality could not be fully assessed. Future studies may further investigate the specific causes of postoperative death in this population. Finally, this study did not distinguish THA performed for acute femoral neck fracture from elective procedures for osteoarthritis or avascular necrosis. Fracture patients are typically older and frailer and undergo urgent surgery, whereas elective cases involve healthier, optimized patients. Combining these groups may lead to confounding by indication.

In conclusion, patients with preoperative stroke had a 4.37 times higher risk of postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty than patients without preoperative stroke. For patients with preoperative stroke, if the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty was less than 180 days, the risk of postoperative stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty was 33.19 times higher than that for patients without preoperative stroke. Longer elapsed times were associated with lower risks of postoperative stroke. Although patients with preoperative stroke did not exhibit a higher risk of death within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty, their mortality rate within 90 days of the surgery increased the closer their stroke occurred to the surgery date. These findings provide valuable insights for patients with preoperative stroke considering total hip arthroplasty, helping them and their health-care providers assess the associated risks more effectively.

Methods

Data sources and participants

This study employed a retrospective cohort design, drawing data from the National Health Insurance Research Database maintained by the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Taiwan, covering the years 2000 to 2018, and supplemented by Cause of Death Data. The study population comprised patients aged ≥ 40 years who received their first total hip arthroplasty from 2005 to 2017. Initially, data for 74,591 patients who received total hip arthroplasty during this period were collected. Simultaneous bilateral THA, staged bilateral THA, and unilateral THA may show different outcomes. Bilateral THA was defined as the presence of two THA procedure codes recorded during the same hospitalization episode. Therefore, we excluded patients from this dataset based on the following criteria: 1,222 patients who underwent simultaneous bilateral total hip arthroplasty, 407 patients who experienced two or more preoperative strokes and required hospitalization, 20,346 patients who had general or regional anesthesia within 1 year before or after their total hip arthroplasty, and 7,864 patients aged < 40 years. Consequently, the final cohort comprised data of 44,752 patients aged ≥ 40 years who received total hip arthroplasty. The selection process for the study population is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Selection of research participants.

All personal data were deidentified to ensure confidentiality and prevent identification of individual patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of China Medical University Hospital (IRB No: CMUH112-REC3-158).

Variables description

The primary independent variables included whether patients had been hospitalized due to stroke prior to their total hip arthroplasty and the elapsed time from preoperative stroke to total hip arthroplasty, categorized into four intervals: >365 days, 181–365 days, 0–180 days, and no preoperative stroke. In this study, preoperative stroke is defined as having a single occurrence of the following diagnosis codes prior to undergoing total hip arthroplasty: a primary inpatient diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease (ICD-9-CM 430.XX–438.XX, ICD-10-CM I60–I63). However, cases with a diagnosis of transient ischemic attack (TIA; ICD-9-CM 435.9, ICD-10-CM G45.9) are excluded. The study’s dependent variables were the occurrence of postoperative stroke or death within 90 days following total hip arthroplasty. The postoperative stroke is defined as having a single occurrence of the following diagnosis codes within 90 days following total hip arthroplasty: a primary inpatient diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease (ICD-9-CM 430.XX–438.XX, ICD-10-CM I60–I63). This study is based on the cause-of-death registry provided by the Ministry of Health and Welfare to identify the death date. If a study subject is recorded in the cause-of-death registry by the end of 2018, they are defined as dead. The control variables were patient demographics (namely sex and age), economic factors (namely monthly salary), environmental factors (namely urbanization of residence), health conditions (namely hypertension, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, transient ischemic attack, diabetes, renopathy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer, liver disease, peptic ulcer, rheumatic disease, and severity of other comorbidities measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] score), drug usage, anesthesia method, medical institution characteristics (namely medical institution level, category, and capacity), surgeon capacity, and year of surgery. The definition of the control variables are as follows. Monthly salary was divided into three levels: < NT$22,800, NT$22,801–NT$36,300, and > NT$36,301. The urbanization of residence was divided into seven levels in accordance with the urbanization levels of townships in Taiwan, with level one representing the highest urbanization and level seven representing the lowest urbanization32. The CCI was used to represent patients’ health conditions, derived from medical records within 2 years before death33. The Deyo CCI was employed to convert primary and secondary diagnoses into the CCI score. Because this study emphasized the impact of individual comorbidities, they were analyzed as associated variables. To avoid double-counting, comorbidities such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, diabetes, etc., which were listed as associated variables, were excluded from the CCI score calculation. Consequently, many patients had a CCI score of zero. Drug usage referred to whether patients used anticoagulants within 1 year before their total hip arthroplasty, based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code B01. Patients with a cumulative defined daily dose of > 30 were classified as anticoagulant users. Regarding the medical institution level and category, the primary medical institution was the one where the patients received their total hip arthroplasty. The medical institution capacity was classified into three levels, namely low (< 25th percentile), medium (25th–75th percentile), and high (> 75th percentile), and the levels were based on the total number of hip arthroplasty surgeries performed by the medical institution compared with other medical institutions nationwide. The medical institution levels were medical centers, regional hospitals, district hospitals, and clinics, and the institution categories were public or private ownership.

Statistical analysis

This study used SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) for data processing and statistical analysis. All tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at α = 0.05. The statistical analyses employed are detailed below:

First, patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty were classified on the basis of their history of preoperative stroke. To mitigate selection bias, propensity score matching was conducted using a 1:10 ratio. The matching methods employed were greedy nearest neighbor matching and the caliper method. After the group “patients with preoperative stroke who received total hip arthroplasty” and the group “patients without preoperative stroke who received total hip arthroplasty” were matched, a chi-square test was performed to determine significant differences between the groups in terms of patient demographics (sex and age), health conditions, socioeconomic status, environmental factor, health-care institution characteristics, and year of surgery.

A log-rank test was used to conduct bivariate analyses for postoperative stroke and various factors such as preoperative stroke before total hip arthroplasty, the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty, patient sex, patient age, patient monthly salary, urbanization of residence, health conditions (hypertension, transient ischemic attack, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, diabetes, renopathy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cancer, liver disease, peptic ulcer, rheumatic disease, and CCI score), drug usage, anesthesia method, surgeon capacity, medical institution level, and year of surgery. This test was performed to determine significant differences among these factors.

To investigate whether preoperative stroke influenced the risk of postoperative stroke following total hip arthroplasty, the study accounted for the fact that some research subjects died from unrelated causes, which prevented observation of recurrent stroke (relevant event). Thus, death was considered a competing risk. A stratified Cox proportional hazards model with a competing risk approach was used to examine the effect of preoperative stroke on the risk of stroke within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty and the effect of the elapsed time between preoperative stroke and total hip arthroplasty on the risk of stroke within the same period.

The stratified Cox proportional hazards model was employed to explore the effect of preoperative stroke on the risk of death within 90 days of total hip arthroplasty and the effect of the elapsed time between stroke and total hip arthroplasty on this risk. Control variables included patient demographics, health conditions, drug usage, anesthesia method, surgeon characteristics, medical institution characteristics, and year of surgery.

Stroke is a heterogeneous condition that encompasses both hemorrhagic and ischemic subtypes. The underlying etiology may differentially influence the risk of postoperative stroke and mortality following total hip arthroplasty, potentially introducing bias. To assess outcome differences by stroke type, we analyzed preoperative ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes separately for their associations with postoperative stroke and mortality. With respect to preoperative hemorrhagic stroke, analysis was not feasible due to the limited number of postoperative stroke events (n = 3 within 90 days) and postoperative mortality events (n = 2 within 90 days).

Data Availability Statement

Data are available from the Health and Welfare Data Science Center of the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) (https://www.mohw.gov.tw/mp-2.html (accessed on 1 May 2023), Taiwan. All interested researchers can apply to use the database managed by the MOHW. Due to legal restrictions imposed by the Taiwanese government related to the Personal Information Protection Act, the database cannot be made publicly available. Raw data from the Health and Welfare Data Science Center cannot be brought out. The restrictions prohibited the authors from making the minimal data set publicly available.
