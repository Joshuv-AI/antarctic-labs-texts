Orthopedic manifestations of acquired hemophilia: a case report on uncontrolled bleeding and progressive swelling post-phlebotomy- should fasciotomy be considered?
Authors: Parham Talebian, Mohammad Soleimani, Mahsa Mottahedi, Elham Nazar, Parmida Shahbazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcquired hemophilia A (AHA) is a rare bleeding disorder caused by autoantibodies against clotting factors, often resulting in spontaneous bleeding or bleeding after minor trauma, potentially leading to Acute Compartment Syndrome (ACS). Diagnosing and managing AHA-induced ACS is challenging due to its rarity and the risks associated with fasciotomy in bleeding disorders. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), ACS management is further complicated by the lack of intra-compartmental pressure monitoring devices, necessitating reliance on clinical symptoms.Case PresentationA 36-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with progressive swelling of the left antecubital area after phlebotomy with no trauma or bleeding disorder history. After hospitalization a venous color Doppler ultrasound showed superficial vein thrombosis, and the patient was discharged and referred to the orthopedic service for follow-up of clinical symptoms. Returned to the hospital after approximately six hours due to the development of generalized weakness and hemodynamic instability. Diagnostic challenges arose as initial laboratory results suggested possible acute leukemia due to leukocytosis and thrombocytosis. It also highlights the need to investigate any unexplained aPTT prolongation before surgical interventions. Prompt intervention, patient education, and tailored treatment strategies are crucial for preventing severe complications and optimizing patient outcomes.

Background

Acquired hemophilia A (AHA) is a rare, life-threatening bleeding disorder that can occur in patients with no previous history of coagulation issues [1]. It is characterized by the development of autoantibodies against clotting factors, especially factor VIII, which can result in spontaneous bleeding or bleeding after minor trauma [2]. It can happen to people of any age, but the incidence is higher in older adults [3]. Interestingly, the incidence rates for men and women appear to be similar [4, 5]. AHA can be idiopathic or associated with various underlying conditions, such as malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and pregnancy [6, 7].

Acute compartment syndrome (ACS), a serious condition that can result in tissue damage and permanent disability if left untreated, can be rarely caused by AHA [3, 8]. While ACS is recognized as a surgical emergency, fasciotomy as an intervention for AHA has been subject to debate in terms of its potential risk of bleeding and on the other hand preventing further tissue damage, thereby minimizing the risk of irreversible disabilities [2, 8].

Here, we report a case of AHA in a 36-year-old woman with no history of bleeding disorder developed ACS after a phlebotomy procedure. She initially presented with swelling and had flu-like symptoms but showed no signs of bleeding. However, her condition worsened, and she was found to have a high level of factor VIII inhibitor.

This case underscore the importance of considering AHA in patients presenting with ACS-like symptoms, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis, despite initial suspicion of acute leukemia. This case emphasizes the crucial role of elevated Partial Thromboplastin Time (PTT) analysis as the first step in achieving prompt diagnosis, timely intervention to prevent life-threatening complications and potentially unnecessary fasciotomy.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with progressive swelling of the left antecubital area six hours after repeated phlebotomy attempts.

The patient had a history of generalized weakness for two weeks and referred to a general practitioner. Laboratory tests were requested, including a complete blood count. Additionally, the patient reported flu-like symptoms for approximately three days and a history of giving birth 16 months ago and breastfeeding now. She had a normal vaginal delivery without any unusual heavy bleeding in prepartum and postpartum periods. Her beta-hCG was < 1.2 on the admission. The patient’s medical and drug history was insignificant and there was no history of trauma.

After hospitalization in the emergency room, the patient was evaluated by the vascular surgery service. A venous color Doppler ultrasound showed superficial vein thrombosis, and the patient was discharged and referred to the orthopedic service for follow-up of clinical symptoms. Initial laboratory results showed microcytic anemia, which could be attributed to the patient’s history of thalassemia minor.

The patient was discharged to outpatient orthopedic clinic for follow-up, but returned to the hospital after approximately six hours due to the development of generalized weakness and hemodynamic instability. The patient’s hemoglobin dropped unexpectedly from 8.4 to 5 (Table 1), and there was a progressive increase in edema of the patient’s upper limbs, chest, and left breast which drew increased attention.

Due to these symptoms, the patient was admitted to the orthopedic service for evaluation of ACS. Consultation with internal medicine and thoracic surgery services were requested. The thoracic surgery service excluded thoracic problems. The internal medicine service ordered packed cell transfusion due to hemoglobin drop and performed a peripheral blood smear (PBS) to investigate the cause of the disturbed blood test results. There was no previous history of gastrointestinal bleeding or epistaxis. However, due to leukocytosis with neutrophil predominance, thrombocytosis, bleeding symptoms, and cells suspicious for blasts observed in the PBS, further investigation was initiated to rule out acute leukemia. (Table 1) Subsequently, the patient’s condition deteriorated, the swelling progressed and wrist drop was occurred along with symptoms of ACS. As a result, the patient was transferred to the orthopedic operating room for a fasciotomy, while pack cell injection was initiated to improve her unstable hemodynamics.

During the fasciotomy, which involved the arm, forearm, and hand compartments, the surgical sites showed profuse bleeding. The patient received two additional pack cell units, in addition to the one received in the emergency room. Due to unstable vital signs and the negative result of Covid- tests conducted in admission the patient was transferred to the non-COVID patients’ Intensive Care Unit (ICU). After achieving relative stabilization of the patient’s vital signs in the ICU, Bone Marrow Aspiration (BMA) and flow cytometry were performed to investigate potential blood problems.

In the ICU, the patient’s condition did not improve. Weakness and lethargy continued, and bleeding from the operation site persisted, with the patient’s hemoglobin dropping to 4.1 and reaching 5.3 despite receiving another unit of packed cells in the ICU.

After 24 h, the BMA (Fig. 1) results indicated that the main differential diagnoses were erythroid hyperplasia or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) M6. Flow cytometry was performed to rule out leukemia. Another 24 h later, the final diagnosis was erythroid hyperplasia, suggesting another etiology, possible coagulation disorder due to prolonged PTT and a mixing test was requested.

The patient’s condition worsened due to persistent bleeding and the patient experienced a resuscitation following a cardiac arrest. After conducting mixing test, the initial result was positive and in an effort to improve the patient’s condition, activated factor VII was administered empirically postoperatively every 6 h at 1200 µg until PTT returned to normal. This treatment was given in the ICU following the administration of 4 units of packed cells up to that point. The patient’s bleeding decreased and their general condition slightly improved as a result of this treatment.

Further testing was performed to determine the levels of various factors, including factor VIII, factor VIII inhibitor, and factors II, V, VII, IX, X, XI, XII, and XIII. These subsequent tests indicated a low level of factor VIII with a high level of factor VIII inhibitor, leading to a diagnosis of AHA (Table 1). Treatment was initiated based on this diagnosis according to the Tiede et al. recommendations on AHA treatment guideline [9], which included activated factor VII, prednisolone (1 g/kg), and cyclophosphamide (100 mg/day). Additionally, due to the high level of factor VIII inhibitor and the lack of bleeding control, a regimen of rituximab (375 mg/m2) was prescribed, to be administered weekly for four weeks.

After approximately a month-long hospitalization, during which systemic lupus erythematosus and malignancies were ruled out, the patient’s clinical symptoms and laboratory results showed improvement. The bleeding gradually decreased, and the surgical wound was closed after several transfers to the operating room. Once the patient’s PTT levels returned to normal, activated factor VII was discontinued, and the patient was discharged from the hospital.

Post-discharge treatment consisted of oral prednisolone and endoxan 100 mg daily. At the one-week follow-up, the factor VIII inhibitor level had decreased from 19 to 1 Bethesda unit. This improvement allowed for a gradual tapering of prednisolone until its discontinuation, and the endoxan dosage was reduced to 50 mg daily. Presently, approximately three months later, although there has been clinical improvement, her wrist drop has not fully recovered to its previous state and continues to attend monthly follow-ups at both the hematology and hemophilia clinics for ongoing management and monitoring.

This case report is presented according to the SCARE guidelines 2020 [10].

Discussion and conclusions

ACS is a medical emergency that requires prompt recognition and appropriate management to prevent irreversible disability [11]. While fasciotomy works best if done within six hours post-injury and isn’t recommended after 36 h, it carries significant risks in the presence of hemophilia, particularly when inhibitors are present. In such cases, early hemostatic therapy, such as recombinant Factor VIIa (rFVIIa) or Activated Prothrombin Complex Concentrates (APCC), can prevent or reverse symptoms of developing compartment syndrome, potentially avoiding the need for fasciotomy. While ACS is well-described in individuals with hemophilia, it can pose a significant challenge for both orthopedic surgeons and hematologists. The absence of a clear underlying cause or evident traumatic event complicates diagnosis, and the potential for severe bleeding makes fasciotomy a risky procedure [8, 12, 13].

Management of ACS should be guided by intra-compartmental pressure (ICP) measurements, which need to be monitored regularly. Both invasive and non-invasive methods for measuring ICP have been described by Mortensen et al. [14]. For pressures between 20 and 30 mmHg, hospital admission and surgical consultation are necessary. An extensive fasciotomy is indicated for patients with clear clinical signs of ACS or when lower limb local perfusion pressure reaches 30 mmHg, measured every 1–2 h over a 12-hour period. Some clinicians prefer using the “delta pressure” method—the difference between diastolic (or mean) arterial pressure and compartment pressure—where a delta pressure below 30 mmHg is indicative of ACS [13]. In countries with limited medical resources, including many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and Iran, the diagnosis and management of ACS often face significant challenges. Intra-compartmental pressure monitoring systems, which are considered gold standard in many high-income countries, are frequently unavailable. As a result, clinicians in these settings must rely primarily on clinical symptoms for ACS diagnosis and follow-up which introduces additional complexities and potential delays in diagnosis. Therefore, it is recommended to monitor ICP in patients with high-responding inhibitors over an extended period to potentially avoid fasciotomy in cases where the adequacy of the hemostatic response is uncertain. However, once compartment syndrome is clinically established, performing fasciotomy is advised to prevent loss of function. In our case, we could not avoid fasciotomy, as the patient presented with clinically established compartment syndrome [15].

Despite advancements in hematological outcomes, there remains a lack of literature on the functional and orthopedic outcomes of patients with acquired hemophilia. Furthermore, clinicians frequently underreport unfavorable results. There are currently no standardized guidelines for managing ACS in patients with acquired hemophilia. Treatment recommendations are primarily based on case reports and reviews in the literature. ACS in individuals with hemophilia, with or without inhibitors, should be managed similarly. When ACS is suspected in persons with hemophilia, immediate replacement of clotting factor is critical. After administration of clotting factor concentrates, compartment pressure should be monitored hourly during an initial 2-hour observation period. If pressures begin to normalize, ongoing clinical and pressure assessments can continue. However, if no improvement is seen within this time frame, an extensive fasciotomy should be performed. In cases of AHA-related ACS, the decision to perform fasciotomy must be weighed carefully due to the high risk of bleeding and the uncertain functional outcomes [8].

This case report describes a 36-year-old woman with AHA who developed ACS in her forearm after undergoing phlebotomy. Two significant diagnostic challenges arose in this case. Firstly, healthcare providers often overlook isolated prolonged activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT) findings [5], as in this case where we initially suspected acute leukemia. Secondly, the presence of a factor inhibitor was missed and significantly complicated the management. Early diagnosis of AHA and administration of rFVIIa or APCC could have mitigated bleeding, preventing the progression of ACS and the subsequent need for fasciotomy.

Preventing irreversible outcomes, such as wrist drop, highlights the significance of considering AHA in patients with an acute or recent onset of bleeding, particularly those without a trauma history, who present with an unexplained prolonged aPTT and normal Prothrombin Time (PT). In such cases, fasciotomy may be necessary before making a definitive AHA diagnosis [2, 6].

Considering this viewpoint, it is crucial to approach diagnosis and treatment based on a suitable algorithm for non-traumatic ACS in patients with AHA. The rarity of AHA, combined with its non-specific symptoms and lack of awareness, often leads to delayed diagnosis and treatment. AHA should be suspected in cases of sudden bleeding accompanied by unexplained prolonged aPTT. An unexplained prolonged aPTT prior to surgery should not be ignored and must be investigated thoroughly. Causes may include coagulation factor deficiencies or inhibitors such as autoantibodies, lupus anticoagulants, or anticoagulant medications. Diagnosis of AHA is confirmed by measuring factor VIII activity and inhibitor levels using the Bethesda assay and/or an anti-FVIII ELISA. If FVIII: C levels are not immediately available, a mixing study may help differentiate between factor deficiency and inhibitor presence [2, 9].

In the absence of access to FVIII: C measurements, we utilized aPTT mixing studies with a 2-hour incubation period to distinguish between factor deficiency and the presence of an inhibitor. As highlighted by Clarke et al., [16] time- and temperature-dependent characteristics of FVIII inhibitors in AHA require careful considerations in diagnostic procedures. aPTT mixing studies are particularly useful in limited-resource settings to identify inhibitory activity. Furthermore, the literature emphasizes the utility of dilute Russell viper venom time (dRVVT) testing in mitigating confounding factors such as lupus anticoagulant as studies of aPTT mixing will be abnormal with both AHA and a lupus anticoagulant, highlighting a diagnostic approach that may further enhance the interpretation of mixing study results in future research or practice.

It is worth mentioning that the inhibitor titer offers limited value in guiding clinical decisions, as it does not align with the bleeding phenotype [17].

Regrettably, as previously stated in the literature review, in our particular case study, it should be noted that the patient had a factor inhibitor, which puts her at a higher risk of orthopedic complications, morbidity, and mortality compared to individuals with congenital hemophilia. Therefore, before discharging the patient, clinicians should educate hemophilia patients about compartment syndrome risks and symptoms to enable timely care-seeking [7]. In our case, we are not sure if pregnancy will cause another bleeding episode, as it may also be due to a possible COVID-19 pseudo-negative test. However, we have educated the patient that if she wants to become pregnant again, she should be closely followed up.

In conclusion, the diagnosis and management of ACS in patients with hemophilia, particularly those with AHA, are challenging and require prompt recognition based on elevated aPTT and interventions such as starting VIIa factor before surgery to prevent irreversible sequelae. An approach according to the algorithm for non-traumatic compartment syndrome with AHA is important and may improve patient outcomes. Education of patients regarding the risks and clinical presentation of compartment syndrome is crucial to allow for timely intervention. Orthopedic surgeons must work closely with hematologists to manage this complex condition and prevent poor outcomes.

Patient perspective

I am content with the medical care provided by the hospital staff. While my wrist drop has improved, it is not yet back to its previous state, and I still face some difficulties in performing my daily activities.
