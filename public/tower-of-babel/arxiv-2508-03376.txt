Abstract
Current quantum hardware presents a significant limitation in the number of available qubits compared to the requirements of practical quantum algorithms. Circuit knitting has been proposed as a solution to this issue by partitioning larger quantum circuits into smaller parts that can be executed by current devices. However, this approach often leads to a high sampling overhead, which increases exponentially with the number of cut points. In this paper, we introduce CKVQA, a framework that applies circuit knitting to variational quantum algorithms (VQAs). By employing a quantum circuit architecture search adapted to this scenario, CKVQA aims to minimize the sampling overhead by identifying parameterized quantum circuits that achieve a favorable balance between algorithmic performance and sampling overhead. Additionally, since circuit knitting generates multiple subcircuits, we have developed a subcircuit-level optimization method to accelerate the training of VQAs and reduce overall execution time. We apply this framework to two widely-used VQAs: the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and the Variational Quantum Eigensolver. Our numerical results demonstrate that the CKVQA framework significantly reduces the sampling overheads while maintaining comparable accuracy to conventional parameterized quantum circuit designs.

Background

II-A

Preliminaries of Quantum Computing

Quantum computing is a computational paradigm that leverages the principles of quantum mechanics to perform computations. Below, we provide a concise overview of essential quantum computing concepts relevant to this work.

Qubit and Quantum State.
The fundamental unit of information in quantum computing is the
quantum bit
or
qubit
. Unlike classical bits, which exist in one of two states (0 or 1), a qubit can exist in a
superposition
of both states.

A
quantum state
encapsulates all the relevant information about a quantum system and is mathematically represented by the
density matrix
, which is a positive semi-definite matrix with trace one. A quantum state is called
pure
if it has rank one; otherwise, it is
mixed
. An
N
-qubit pure state
\rho
can be expressed as a unit vector:
\rho=|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|
, where the
ket
notation
|{\psi}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}}
and the
bra
notation
\langle{\psi}|
denote the conjugate transpose of
|{\psi}\rangle
.

Quantum Gates and Quantum Circuits.
Quantum gates are unitary matrices that manipulate quantum states. Common single-qubit gates include the Pauli rotation gates, defined as
R_{P}(\theta)=\textnormal{e}^{-\textnormal{i}\theta P/2}
, where
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
is the rotation angle, and
P\in\{X,Y,Z\}
. The standard Pauli matrices are:

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\textnormal{i}\\
\textnormal{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

The CNOT gate is a common and fundamental two-qubit gate in quantum computation, which is defined as
\mathrm{CNOT}=|{0}\rangle\langle{0}|\otimes I+|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|\otimes X
. The evolution of a quantum state
\rho
under a quantum circuit is mathematically described as
\rho^{\prime}=U(\theta)\rho U^{\dagger}(\theta)
, where
U(\theta)
is a unitary transformation parameterized by a sequence of quantum gates. An observable is represented by a Hermitian matrix
O\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}\times 2^{N}}
, and quantum measurement is typically introduced at the end of the quantum circuit to extract classical information.

II-B

Variational Quantum Algorithms

Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) utilize classical optimizers to train Parameterized Quantum Circuits (PQCs), representing a promising paradigm for leveraging near-term quantum devices to solve complex computational problems
[
21
]
.

The core components of a VQA include (1) an initial quantum state
\rho
, (2) a parameterized ansatz circuit
U(\theta)
, and (3) an observable
O
. The objective of a VQA is to determine the optimal parameter set
\theta^{\star}
that minimizes the loss function, usually defined as
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\operatorname{tr}(OU(\theta)\rho U(\theta)^{\dagger})
.

A key challenge in designing effective VQAs is selecting an appropriate structure of the parameterized quantum circuit. Current PQC design strategies can be categorized into two main approaches: (1)
problem-inspired ansatzes
, which leverage domain-specific knowledge to construct circuits tailored to particular problem requirements
[
22
,
23
]
, and (2)
hardware-efficient ansatzes
, which optimize circuit designs for compatibility with specific quantum hardware constraints
[
31
]
.

A promising emerging research direction is Quantum Architecture Search (QAS), see, e.g.,
[
32
]
, which automates the discovery of optimal ansatz structures.
By integrating machine learning and optimization techniques, QAS explores the space of quantum circuit architectures to identify configurations that maximize computational efficiency while minimizing resource demands. This approach has significant potential for enhancing the scalability and practicality of VQAs as quantum hardware evolves.

II-C

Circuit Knitting

Executing large-scale quantum circuits on near-term quantum hardware is constrained by the limited number of available qubits. When a quantum circuit requires more qubits than the hardware can support, a practical approach is to partition the circuit into smaller, executable subcircuits. These subcircuits are processed sequentially or in parallel, and their results are combined to reconstruct the original computation.

Circuit Knitting

[
12
,
14
]
addresses this challenge by decomposing large quantum circuits into smaller subcircuits while preserving their computational fidelity. A key technique in CK is
Quasiprobability Decomposition

[
16
]
, which enables the decomposition of a unitary channel
\mathcal{U}
into a set of local operations
\{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\dots\}
, satisfying

\mathcal{U}(\rho)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\rho),

(1)

for any state
\rho
, where
\mathcal{F}_{i}
are operations that can be implemented locally.

A significant drawback of QPD is its associated sampling overhead, which scales as
C^{2}
, where
C:=\sum_{i}|p_{i}|
. This overhead increases exponentially with the number of partition points in the circuit
[
15
]
. To mitigate this issue, a common approach is to model the quantum circuit as a graph, with qubits represented as nodes and two-qubit gates as edges weighted by their frequency. By computing the graph’s minimum cut, circuit partitioning can be optimized to minimize cross-partition operations, thereby reducing the overall sampling overhead and improving computational efficiency.

III
Design Guideline and Motivation

III-A

Challenges in VQA Design

Variational quantum algorithms offer a practical framework for leveraging near-term quantum devices. However, two primary challenges hinder their scalability when employing circuit knitting: (1) the design of effective ansatz structures and (2) the optimization of partitioned subcircuits under circuit knitting. The choice of ansatz significantly impacts the performance, scalability, and resource efficiency of VQAs. However, with increasing complexity of quantum algorithms, the manual design of efficient quantum circuits becomes increasingly impractical.
QAS has emerged as a promising solution, automating the exploration of quantum circuit architectures to discover high-performing ansatzes. Nevertheless, existing QAS frameworks are not designed to handle the additional constraints imposed by circuit knitting, including sampling overhead and subcircuit isolation.

III-B

Reframing the Loss Function

When circuit knitting is applied, the original VQA circuit is decomposed into a set of independently executable subcircuits. This transformation impacts the training dynamics of the loss function. The objective of VQA optimization is to minimize the loss function:

L(\theta)=\operatorname{tr}(OU(\theta)|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|^{\otimes n}U^{\dagger}(\theta)).

(2)

where
U(\theta)
denotes the parameterized quantum circuit and
O
is the observable of interest. After implementing circuit knitting, the circuit is partitioned, and the loss function takes the form:

\sum_{i}p_{i}\operatorname{tr}(O_{i}\mathcal{F}_{i}(\theta^{(i)})(|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|^{\otimes n})).

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{F}_{i}(\theta^{(i)})
represents the subcircuit operations, and
\theta^{(i)}
are subsets of the full parameter set
\theta
and we set the initial state as
|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|^{\otimes n}
without loss of generality. This decomposition enables individual parameter updates within subcircuits, potentially reducing computational costs and improving convergence rates. By leveraging the structure of partitioned circuits, efficient optimization strategies can be developed to enhance the scalability of VQAs on near-term quantum hardware.

IV
Framework of CKVQA

We propose CKVQA, a framework that utilizes circuit knitting techniques to improve the performance of variational quantum algorithms on near-term intermediate-scale quantum devices.

Fig.
2
provides a high-level overview of the CKVQA design. This framework incorporates the quantum architecture search to identify an optimal ansatz that balances algorithmic performance with the sampling overhead introduced by circuit knitting. The selected ansatz is then subjected to circuit knitting, where the quantum circuit is partitioned into smaller subcircuits, each executed independently on available quantum hardware. The final computational result is reconstructed through post-processing techniques. To further enhance the efficiency of VQAs within the circuit knitting framework, CKVQA employs a parameter optimization strategy that updates the parameters of each subcircuit individually. This approach reduces computational costs by minimizing execution time and resource requirements, leading to a more efficient utilization of NISQ hardware.

IV-A

The structure of the ansatz, or parameterized quantum circuit, is a critical factor in variational quantum algorithms. It significantly influences algorithmic performance and determines the proposed method’s sampling overhead introduced by circuit knitting. Quantum Architecture Search is a systematic approach to identifying optimal ansatz structures for VQAs. While traditional QAS techniques primarily focus on improving algorithmic performance, specific approaches also optimize other properties, such as expressibility and trainability. This section discusses how QAS techniques can be integrated into the proposed CKVQA framework to identify ansatz structures that achieve high algorithmic efficiency and reduce sampling overhead.

IV-B

Circuit Execution via Circuit Knitting

This subsection describes how to execute a quantum circuit using circuit knitting techniques, specifically focusing on gate cutting. This technique decomposes two-qubit gates to facilitate circuit partitioning. To ensure compatibility with commonly available quantum hardware, we limit our implementation to two-qubit gates from the set
{CX,CZ,I\otimes I}
.

Given an
n
-qubit quantum circuit
U(\theta)
and a maximum qubit capacity of
m
on the quantum device, the
k

Once the partition positions are established, we decompose two-qubit gates that span different subsets using quasiprobability decomposition. The resulting subcircuits are then executed independently on the quantum device. After execution, we classically post-process the partial results from each subcircuit to reconstruct the final output of the original circuit. This reconstruction involves combining the partial results according to the quasiprobability coefficients and the partition structure, ensuring an accurate representation of the original quantum computation.

Optimization of Subcircuits in CKVQA

When a large parameterized quantum circuit is decomposed into smaller subcircuits via circuit knitting, each subcircuit retains a subset of the original parameters. This decomposition provides opportunities to optimize the training process of VQAs, as optimizing the complete parameter set is computationally expensive. By leveraging the structure of the partitioned subcircuits, the computational cost can be significantly reduced. This subsection presents a systematic approach for training VQAs on subcircuits generated through circuit knitting, focusing on efficient parameter updates and gradient computation.

The core principle of our optimization strategy is to exploit the locality of parameters within subcircuits. Specifically, each parameter
\theta_{i}
in the original circuit is associated with a subset of subcircuits that depend on it. Updating
\theta_{i}
using only the relevant subcircuits avoids redundant computations, thereby reducing the overall optimization cost. The proposed approach consists of two key components:

1.

Parameter Identification Module:
For each parameter
\theta_{i}

\theta_{i}
as part of their parameter set. This step ensures that only the relevant subcircuits are utilized for gradient computation and parameter updates, significantly reducing computational overhead.

2.

Subcircuit-Based Gradient Update Module:
Once the relevant subcircuits for
\theta_{i}
are identified, the module computes the gradient of the loss function with respect to
\theta_{i}
using only these subcircuits. The parameter is then updated using gradient-based optimization techniques (e.g., gradient descent or Adam).

V-C

Evaluation Metrics and Comparative Analysis

The performance of CKVQA is evaluated using benchmark-specific ground-truth metrics:

•

VQE Performance:
The accuracy of the ground-state energy estimation is measured, as it directly indicates the algorithm’s effectiveness in approximating electronic structure solutions.

•

QAOA Performance:
The approximation ratio is used to assess the solution quality for the Max-Cut problem. The approximation ratio is defined as the ratio of the cut value obtained by QAOA to the optimal cut value, serving as a quantitative measure of the algorithm’s optimization capabilities.

In addition to application-specific performance metrics, computational efficiency is also considered. The following factors are analyzed:

•

Sampling Overhead:
The additional measurements required due to circuit knitting techniques are quantified. This metric assesses the extent to which circuit partitioning affects the overall computational cost.

•

Execution Time:
The total runtime of the hybrid quantum-classical workflow is measured, encompassing both quantum execution and classical post-processing.

By integrating these metrics, the evaluation provides a comprehensive assessment of CKVQA, balancing accuracy, performance, and computational efficiency.

VIII
Conclusion

In the noisy intermediate-scale quantum era and beyond, a central challenge is extending the computational capabilities of current quantum devices to support larger-scale circuits.
This paper presents CKVQA, a framework that combines circuit knitting techniques with variational quantum algorithms. A key innovation of CKVQAis its quantum architecture search (QAS) module, which is explicitly designed to be knitting-aware. This module automatically discovers ansatz structures that adhere to user-specified sampling overhead constraints while preserving algorithmic performance. Additionally, CKVQAincorporates an efficient subcircuit optimization strategy that significantly reduces execution time without sacrificing solution accuracy.

Experimental results demonstrate that the QAS component can reduce the sampling overhead while maintaining the algorithmic performance, and the subcircuits optimization component can reduce the overall execution time, as well as our method’s robustness on the common quantum noises. The proposed approach enables more efficient utilization of quantum hardware while maintaining computational accuracy.

Future research could further explore integrating our CKVQA with complementary techniques to enhance the scalability and performance of NISQ-era quantum computing. These advancements bridge the gap between theoretical quantum algorithms and practical implementations on near-term quantum hardware.

Another interesting insight of our method lies in its ability to decouple the QAS process from reliance on quantum hardware. Similar to quantum compilers, our framework’s search for optimal ansatz structures is entirely conducted on classical hardware. Traditionally, evaluating circuit performance within the QAS search space requires execution on quantum devices, particularly when classical simulation becomes infeasible for large-scale circuits due to exponential resource demands. In contrast, our controlled sampling-based simulation of circuit knitting enables efficient and accurate performance estimation using classical computation, even for nontrivial circuits. This capability allows our framework to identify high-quality circuit structures without incurring the high cost or limited availability of quantum hardware—an especially beneficial feature in the NISQ era, where quantum resources are still constrained.
