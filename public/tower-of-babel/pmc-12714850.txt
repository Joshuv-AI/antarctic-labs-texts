NMRexp: A database of 3.3 million experimental NMR spectra
Authors: Jun-Jie Wang, Yongqi Jin, Chen-Yu Zhi, Yu-Jie Liu, Xu-Hao Huang, Fanjie Xu, Xiaohong Ji, Xi Fang, Haoyi Tao, Weinan E, Linfeng Zhang, Guolin Ke, Rong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although NMR spectroscopy is one of the most fundamental methods for structural characterization in organic chemistry, high-quality and large-scale experimental NMR datasets remain a critical bottleneck for training robust, generalizable spectral-prediction and structure-elucidation models. Here, we introduce NMRexp, an open, curated collection of 3.3 million experimental NMR records for six nuclei (1H, 13C, 19F, 31P, 29Si, 11B), extracted from nearly 200 thousand Supporting Information documents published between 2010 and 2024. Manual evaluation confirmed > 99% accuracy in metadata extraction (chemical shifts, multiplicities, J-couplings, solvents) and 98% correctness in molecular-skeleton assignment; internal consistency tests yielded mean absolute errors of 0.026 ppm for 1H and 0.206 ppm for 13C chemical shifts. NMRexp surpasses existing public NMR databases by over an order of magnitude in size, while providing rich spectral annotations and source-DOI traceability. We anticipate that NMRexp will accelerate the development of next-generation data-driven spectral prediction, automated structure elucidation, and related AI-enabled applications.

Background & Summary

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides rich, atom-level structure information and is one of the most powerful and fundamental analytical techniques in chemistry. Nearly every newly synthesized organic compound needs to be characterized by NMR, and most organic chemists interact with NMR spectra on a daily basis. In recent years, advances in computational chemistry and artificial intelligence (AI) have given rise to algorithm-assisted NMR analysis as a promising complement to manual interpretation1–8. However, the development of accurate, generalizable NMR models critically depends on access to large, high-quality experimental datasets9.

To date, publicly available NMR repositories remain small by modern deep learning standards. For example, AlphaFold 2’s multiple sequence alignment draws on over 2.2 billion sequences from the Big Fantastic Database10, and Uni-Mol2, one of the most advanced 3D molecular representation models for small molecules, was pretrained on about 800 million 3D conformations11. By comparison, in the field of NMR, the widely used nmrshiftdb2 contains only ~0.054 million entries12, several orders of magnitude fewer than what modern neural architectures typically require. While computational simulations can generate large quantities of synthetic spectra, predicted NMR spectra inevitably deviate from experimental values: state-of-the-art density functional theory (DFT) methods exhibit mean absolute errors (MAEs) of approximately 2.0 ppm for 13C and 0.15 ppm for 1H NMR (the typical spectral window spans ~0-220 ppm for 13C and ~0-12 ppm for 1H)13–16, which can degrade model performance in real-world applications. Consequently, the scarcity of experimental NMR data has become a major bottleneck for both predictive modeling and inverse-design workflows.

Here, we introduce NMRexp17, a curated dataset of over 3.3 million experimental NMR records extracted from the Supporting Information of peer-reviewed publications published between 2010 and 2024. By restricting our sources to the past 15 years, we minimize variability arising from outdated instrumentation or obsolete reporting conventions, ensuring data consistency and compatibility with modern analytical standards. Moreover, all data are derived from chemistry literature, ensuring the molecule highly representative of real-world researches.

In total, NMRexp comprises 1,667,135 1H, 1,455,670 13C, 197,186 19F, 33,456 31P, 17,143 11B and 2,397 29Si NMR spectra. Beyond its scale, NMRexp was designed to maximize data completeness and reliability. Each entry in NMRexp includes full-spectrum chemical shifts, solvent information, multiplicities, and J-coupling constants, which is expected to facilitate downstream studies. The accuracy and internal consistency of NMRexp were systematically evaluated through manual validation and replicate analysis. A random sample confirmed 100% correctness in metadata extraction and 98% correctness in molecular skeleton recognition. Moreover, internal consistency, measured as the MAE of replicate experimental data within NMRexp, stands at ~0.026 ppm for 1H and ~0.206 ppm for 13C, consistent with expectations for experimental NMR data18. In addition, NMRexp was generated via an automated rigorous multi-stage pipeline combining layout detection, optical chemical structure recognition, text normalization, precise pattern-based parsing, and chemical-consistency validation to ensure data reliability and reproducibility. The inclusion of source DOIs ensures precise traceability, and our fully automated, reproducible pipeline supports long-term scalability and future updates.

This combination of scale, accessibility, and data quality makes NMRexp a valuable resource for the development of next-generation, data-driven tools for spectral prediction, automated structure elucidation, and beyond. We invite the community to leverage NMRexp for machine-learning, cheminformatics, and analytical chemistry applications, and we remain committed to expanding and refining the dataset in future releases. If more advanced extraction algorithms and models become available, we will also consider reprocessing the underlying Supporting Information to maintain state-of-the-art data quality.

We also advocate for improved standardization in NMR reporting. Careful verification of reported chemical shifts, multiplicities, and coupling constants is strongly encouraged to ensure data accuracy. We recommend avoiding visually ambiguous or non-standard characters and facilitate automated processing.

Methods

Data preparation

In this work, all NMR records were extracted from the Supporting Information (SI) documents of peer-reviewed chemistry journals whose SI files are freely accessible from publishers’ websites. We focused on journals with high publication volumes in organic and general chemistry, including ACS Catalysis, ACS Central Science, Advanced Synthesis & Catalysis, Angewandte Chemie International Edition, Asian Journal of Organic Chemistry, Chemistry - An Asian Journal, Chemical Communications, Chemistry - A European Journal, Chemical Science, European Journal of Organic Chemistry, Green Chemistry, Journal of the American Chemical Society, Nature Catalysis, Nature Chemistry, Organic & Biomolecular Chemistry, Organic Chemistry Frontiers, Organic Letters, and Organometallics. Journals whose SI is not freely available to the public were excluded. In addition, journals where NMR data is primarily presented in the main article body, which is not open-access, were not considered. We processed nearly all available SI documents published between 2010 and 2024 from the aforementioned journals (approximately 200 thousand SI files).

Extraction pipeline overview

In the organic synthesis part of typical SI documents, molecular structures and corresponding NMR information appear next to each other (see the example19 in Fig. 2). Based on this pattern, we designed the following extraction pipeline: detecting molecular structures and surrounding text blocks in SI PDFs, converting molecular images into SMILES strings, classifying and parsing NMR text using a combination of regular expressions (REs) and language models, and then pairing and validating each molecule-spectrum instance based on spatial proximity and chemical consistency. Layout detection & structure recognition

First, layout detection was performed to identify all molecular structure diagrams and text blocks. This was done using the Uni-Finder tool developed by DP Technology20, which is based on the YOLO21 object detection architecture. Each molecular image was processed using MolParser22, an open-source OCSR (optical chemical structure recognition) tool, to generate SMILES representations.

Text normalization & text block pairing

Next, each recognized molecule was paired with the nearest text block based on reading order (top-to-bottom, left-to-right) to associate it with a potential NMR description. To verify and extract NMR-related content, we first applied strict REs to classify the block and extract NMR-specific metadata, such as nucleus type, solvent, and measurement frequency. Lines that failed to match these patterns were then processed by an auxiliary large language model (GPT-4o-mini) for classification and field extraction. During this stage, we also performed extensive text normalization to handle variations in character encoding and formatting. For example, visually similar characters from different scripts (e.g., Cyrillic Н vs. Latin H) were corrected. Approximately 100 REs were written for text normalization; these normalization rules are documented in detail in the codebase. Each molecule-text pair was then entered the following cleaning and extraction pipeline.

Signal parsing & filtering

While extracting individual NMR signals, we observed that NMR reporting styles vary widely across authors, and occasionally contain formatting errors or inconsistencies. Relaxed pattern-matching strategies (e.g., permissive regular expressions or fuzzy search) may achieve higher recall, but they often introduce erroneous extractions. To ensure strict correctness, we designed comprehensive set of approximately 60 highly specific regular expressions targeting different common NMR patterns, favoring precision over recall. Descriptions that deviated from standard formats, such as “7.39 (d, J = 1H)” (missing coupling constant) or “7.32 (d, J = 7..2 Hz, 1H)” (extra dot), were treated as malformed and filtered out. In these examples, both would be incorrectly accepted by a loose parser that simply searches for chemical shift patterns followed by splitting and integration, which would incorrectly extract these as valid NMR records and introduce noise into the dataset. We therefore encourage careful checking for numeric consistency and completeness of NMR parameters to support data accessibility and automated reuse. Flexible or annotated notations like “3.83 (s, 3H for major-4g, 3H for minor-4g)” were also excluded, as they are difficult to standardize reliably with rule-based extraction. As a result of these strict filtering criteria, approximately 10% of the entries were discarded at this stage to maintain high data integrity.

Chemical consistency validation

In the cleaning pipeline, we applied a series of validation rules grounded in fundamental NMR and organic-chemistry principles. First, consistency checks of proton count were performed. In CDCl3, exchangeable protons (e.g., OH, NH) were considered optional due to their potential absence from the experimental spectra; while in DMSO-d6 or DMF-d7, the number of protons reported in the NMR text must strictly match the molecule23–25. For 13C NMR data, we did not enforce such a strict match. Random overlaps, symmetry-equivalent carbon environments, and certain forms of structural tautomerism can all lead to deviations in the number of observed 13C peaks relative to the actual number of carbon atoms. These effects prevent reliable one-to-one mapping between carbon atoms and spectral peaks.

Second, we checked whether the extracted chemical shifts were ordered monotonically. In practice, most chemists record NMR signals in order of decreasing or increasing chemical shift. Non-monotonic sequences often indicate incorrectly recorded values, which were all excluded. Furthermore, we applied chemically informed filters based on basic organic chemistry knowledge. For instance, 1H-1H coupling constants greater than 30 Hz are typically implausible in molecules composed of C, H, O and N. We manually examined a sample of such molecules, which all indeed turned out to be data misinterpretation: for instance, some authors mistakenly treated the frequency difference of two coupled signals as a coupling constant, mislabeling two doublets as a double doublet with an abnormally large J value. Therefore, we excluded such entries. In cases involving isotopic nuclei (e.g., 1H-13C couplings), such large coupling constants may arise, but our OCR-based extraction method may not correctly identify the isotopic labels. Accordingly, these entries were excluded during data cleaning. For another example, any peak labeled as containing 0 protons was typically caused by impurity signals in the sample: these are often automatically included by NMR processing software (e.g., Mestrenova) and may be mistakenly copied into the record without manual verification. Other filtering rules include anomalous numbers of 13C peaks, missing coupling constants, missing multiplicity, abnormal SMILES strings, inconsistent fluorine atom numbers, and extreme or implausible chemical shifts in both 1H and 13C NMR. A complete list of cleaning rules is available in the code repository. The full cleaning rules can be found in the accompanying code repository.

We further observed that while the extracted molecular skeletons were highly accurate (~98%), OCR performance on stereochemistry was relatively poor, occasionally resulting in erroneous stereochemical assignments (for details, see Technical Validation section).

Data Records

This dataset is named NMRexp (NMR: an experimental database), reflecting its origin. To specify the publication period of the source documents, the current version is referred to as NMRexp_10to24_1, covering articles from 2010 to 2024. The suffix “_1” indicates the first release, and the dataset may be updated or expanded in the future. The dataset contains 3,372,987 experimental NMR records, including spectra for six nuclei: 1H, 13C, 19F, 31P, 29Si, and 11B.

Among the 300 sampled entries, only one entry had missing NMR solvent and only two entries had missing NMR frequencies due to recognition issues. All multiplicities, coupling constants, and chemical shifts were correctly extracted, thanks to the use of highly strict regular expressions during the parsing process.

With regard to molecular structures, 98% of the extracted molecular skeletons (i.e., atom connectivity without stereochemistry) were correct. The remaining 6 erroneous cases were all due to OCR misinterpretation of functional groups: for instance, mistaking –OOtBu as –OtBu, or –CF2CF2Br as –CF2CF3. These errors do not significantly affect the corresponding spectra, and hence are particularly difficult to detect or correct using rule-based logic. A small fraction of errors in a database of this scale is to be expected. For comparison, previous studies have shown that even manually curated datasets (such as ChEMBL and WOMBAT) from earlier years only achieved accuracy rates around 95%26. Although the error rate (~2%) is low, such cases may still influence models that are highly sensitive to the presence or absence of specific heteroatoms. Also, we are committed to continuously improving the quality of NMRexp, and will release all future versions of NMRexp at our project website (https://www.bohrium.com/materials-db/database/table?type=8).

OCR performance on stereochemistry proved less accurate. Among 87 molecules with a single stereocenter, only 72 were recognized correctly, yielding an accuracy of approximately 83%. The NMR spectra of a molecule and its enantiomer are identical in an achiral environment. Stereochemical influence on NMR arises mainly when multiple stereocenters exist, potentially leading to diastereomers with different spectra. Even so, in many practical tasks, spectral differences between diastereomers are much less pronounced as those between constitutional isomers. Because a wide range of NMR-modeling tasks do not require precise stereochemical annotation, and many entries lack accurate stereochemistry assignment in the original data sources, we retained all entries in the full dataset for general-purpose use. While these data would not be appropriate for training stereoselective models, we also provided a filtered subset corresponding to molecules with zero or one stereogenic carbon, thereby eliminating diastereomeric ambiguity. This subset still contains 2,853,590 entries, and is also available on Zenodo (filename NMRexp_10to24_1_1004_sc_less_than_1.parquet)17.

Furthermore, we performed an additional sampling validation on heteronuclear data by randomly selecting 50 entries each for 19F, 31P, 11B, and 29Si (200 entries in total, Table 2). The accuracy of these heteronuclear extractions was found to be consistent with that observed in the global random sampling, confirming the robustness of the extraction process across different nuclei.

In addition, the extraction of all 3.3 million entries was completed on a workstation equipped with an RTX 4090 GPU and a 16-core CPU, taking approximately two weeks. In contrast, manual extraction would require at least 3 minutes per entry, amounting to an estimated workload of 100 person-years.

Error analysis

We next performed a systematic evaluation of measurement error in the dataset. By comparing spectra of the same compound reported by different articles, we assessed the inter-study consistency, which serves as an estimation of the experimental variability inherent in the database. Exchangeable protons (e.g., –OH, –NH) were excluded from the analysis, as their chemical shifts are known to vary significantly, particularly in CDCl3. Error distributions are shown in Fig. 4A-C. When measured in the same solvent, the mean absolute error (MAE) for 1H NMR was approximately 0.026 ppm (based on 95,632 pairs), and 0.206 ppm for 13C NMR (based on 86,248 pairs). These values are substantially lower than those reported for NMRShiftDB (0.09 ppm for 1H shift and 0.51 ppm for 13C shift)12, and markedly lower than the MAEs of all currently known NMR prediction methods, including HOSE based27–30, DFT based13–16,31–33, and machine-learning based approaches3–5,7,34–36.Fig. 4Error analysis across studies and solvents. It is worth noting that solvent effects can significantly influence chemical shifts, which is not accurately reflected by simulated spectra. Chemical shift differences of identical molecules measured in different solvents are shown in Fig. 4D-F. Thanks to the large scale of NMRexp, we were able to gather sufficient matched spectra for such analysis. For instance, when comparing 1,744 instances where the same compound was measured in both CDCl3 and DMSO-d6, the 1H NMR MAE reached 0.141 ppm, comparable to the overall MAE of many predictive models. Similarly, for 2,050 matched 13C NMR pairs, the MAE was 0.894 ppm, more than four times higher than the within-solvent MAE. These findings emphasize the importance of treating solvent effects carefully in data-driven modeling. When training NMR prediction models using experimental data, it is essential to separate data by solvent. Since the majority of spectra in NMRexp were measured in CDCl3, we recommend using CDCl3 data to train the base model, followed by solvent-specific fine-tuning to accommodate other experimental conditions.

Additionally, during pairwise comparisons, we observed that some 1H NMR spectra showed a systematic offset of approximately 0.07 ppm between different report, likely due to contamination of silicone oil. Such systematic errors are difficult to detect or correct via automated extraction and are present in a small number of spectra in NMRexp.
