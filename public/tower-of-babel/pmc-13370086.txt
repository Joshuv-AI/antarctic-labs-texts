Serological and molecular assessment of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus infection in wild boars from two adjacent endemic prefectures in Japan
Authors: Hirohisa MEKATA, Kazunori KIMITSUKI, Mari YAMAMOTO, Kazumi UMEKI, Takaaki YAHIRO, Reiko MIYAHARA, Akira NISHIZONO, Tamaki OKABAYASHI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) is a tick-borne zoonotic virus endemic to western Japan. Miyazaki and Oita Prefectures are neighboring regions on Kyushu Island; however, the total number of reported human SFTS cases in 2013–2025 was nearly double in Miyazaki compared with Oita. To assess whether this difference is reflected in wildlife, we conducted a serological and molecular survey of SFTSV infection in wild boars. A total of 1,506 serum samples collected between November 2022 and May 2025 were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and screened for viral RNA using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). The ELISA positivity rate was significantly higher in Miyazaki (34.1%, n=1,045) than in Oita (11.9%, n=461) (P<0.001). A randomly selected subset of 150 samples was also examined by an 80% plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT80). In contrast, although both the PRNT80 positivity rate (44.2%, n=104 vs. 37.0%, n=46) and the viral RNA detection rate (0.57%, n=1,045 vs. 0%, n=461) were higher in Miyazaki, neither difference reached statistical significance. Furthermore, regional differences in ELISA seropositivity within each prefecture broadly corresponded with the distribution of human SFTS cases, suggesting that ELISA-based surveillance in wild boars may be useful for assessing broad regional patterns of SFTSV transmission.Keywords: ELISA, Japan, seroepidemiology, severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus, wild boar

INTRODUCTION

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick-borne viral zoonosis in East Asia. The disease is characterized by the sudden onset of a high fever, marked leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and gastrointestinal symptoms [13, 15, 41]. Many patients are elderly, and the case fatality rate in Japan is reported to range from 27 to 31% [13, 15]. Since the early 2010s, confirmed outbreaks of SFTS have been reported in China, Japan, and South Korea [27, 39, 42]. Although the extent of its prevalence remains unclear, SFTS cases have also been confirmed in other Asian countries, such as Thailand and Vietnam [28, 36]. In 2009, the SFTS virus (SFTSV), the causative agent of SFTS, was first isolated from patients with acute febrile illnesses in China [41]. In January 2013, the first case of human SFTS in Japan was identified in an individual with no history of overseas travel [32]. Subsequently, SFTS was designated as a nationally notifiable infectious disease under Japan’s Infectious Disease Control Law. Since then, the number of SFTS patients has increased steadily each year, with the highest number of cases reported in 2025 [11]. Furthermore, SFTS cases—previously reported only in western Japan—have spread eastward over time. Due to its high fatality rate, rising number of cases, and expanding endemic range, SFTS has become a major public health concern in Japan.

SFTSV is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus comprising three genomic segments (S, M, and L). It belongs to the genus Bandavirus, family Phenuiviridae, and species Bandavirus dabieense. The S segment encodes the nucleoprotein (NP) and the nonstructural protein NSs [1]. The M segment encodes the glycoproteins (GPs) Gn and Gc, and the L segment encodes RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). SFTS is a tick-borne disease, and its transmission is mediated by several tick species known to transmit the virus to humans and animals, including Haemaphysalis longicornis, Haemaphysalis flava, Haemaphysalis formosensis, and Amblyomma testudinarium [3, 19, 31]. In addition to humans, domestic cats and dogs can develop severe disease following SFTSV infection [9, 21]. In contrast, although many large animals, such as cattle, deer, horses, sheep, and wild boars, become infected with SFTSV, they generally remain asymptomatic, and sustained or high-level viremia is rarely observed [14, 22, 26, 35].

The gold standard for identifying SFTSV infections via serological investigations is the neutralization test [17]. However, this method is time-consuming, requiring up to a week to obtain results, and involves handling the infectious virus. In Japan, work with infectious SFTSV must be conducted in a Biosafety Level 3 facility, which limits the number of laboratories capable of performing neutralization tests. As an alternative, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is widely used because it enables high-throughput testing, does not require virus handling, and produces rapid results [37, 40]. In this study, ELISA using recombinant SFTSV NP was employed to detect anti-NP binding antibodies. In contrast, neutralization tests measure functional antibodies that inhibit viral entry, mainly targeting viral glycoproteins [33]. Anti-NP antibodies are generally more readily induced and may persist longer, whereas neutralizing antibodies reflect functional antiviral activity. Although ELISA does not measure neutralizing antibodies directly, its safety and efficiency make it particularly suitable for large-scale seroprevalence surveys in epidemiological studies.

Oita Prefecture and Miyazaki Prefecture are located in eastern Kyushu, Japan, bordering each other from north to south and separated by rugged mountain ranges (Fig. 1a). As of October 31, 2025, a total of 70 SFTS patients had been reported in Oita Prefecture and 124 in Miyazaki Prefecture (Table 1); Miyazaki has the highest number of SFTS cases nationwide [11]. Despite having similar population sizes—with an estimated 1.08 million residents in Oita Prefecture and 1.02 million in Miyazaki Prefecture—Oita has reported only about half as many SFTS cases as Miyazaki Prefecture. The reasons for this discrepancy remain unclear.

Since SFTSV infection primarily occurs through the bite of infected ticks, the regional infection risk is assumed to be proportional to the abundance of SFTSV-harboring ticks. However, even in endemic areas, the proportion of SFTSV-positive ticks is extremely low [7, 19, 31]. Consequently, assessing the regional infection risk through tick surveillance is inefficient as it requires the collection and testing of an enormous number of ticks. Seroprevalence surveys in wildlife may provide a more sensitive and efficient approach than tick collection for assessing the SFTSV infection risk. Several serological surveys in wildlife have been conducted in Japan, revealing an increased risk of SFTSV infection in specific regions. In Wakayama Prefecture, the seroprevalence of SFTSV antibodies in raccoons was 0% until March 2009 but increased annually thereafter, reaching approximately 50% after 2015 [34]. Similarly, between 2010 and 2013, the seroprevalence in wild boars in Yamaguchi Prefecture was approximately 10–20% but increased to 30–50% after 2014 [35]. However, differences in testing methods, target species, and survey periods complicate the interpretation of seroprevalence data and highlight the need for standardized approaches. Additionally, the interpretation of serological data in wild animals requires careful consideration. Antibody levels are typically measured at a single time point, and factors such as population turnover, uncertain antibody persistence, and repeated exposure to SFTSV may influence seroprevalence. Therefore, long-term observations are needed to establish seroprevalence as a reliable indicator of human exposure risk.

The aim of this study was to compare the serological and molecular evidence of SFTSV infection in wild boars from Oita and Miyazaki Prefectures using neutralization tests, ELISA, and qRT-PCR. To enable direct comparison between the two prefectures, serum samples collected during the same period were analyzed using identical testing methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

This study did not involve any experimental animal procedures, and therefore approval from an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was not required. All analyzed serum samples were residual specimens obtained from wild boars captured during routine nuisance wildlife control programs conducted by local authorities in Miyazaki and Oita Prefectures. Wild boars are designated as harmful wildlife species in Japan and are routinely culled by licensed hunters under prefectural wildlife management programs. In Miyazaki Prefecture, blood samples were collected by hunters as part of routine surveillance for the African swine fever virus and classical swine fever virus. In Oita Prefecture, hunters collected blood samples as part of the zoonosis surveillance related to the Wildlife Sanitary Promotion Project in Oita Prefecture. Residual sera were subsequently made available for this study. The use of these samples for research purposes was approved by the respective sample custodians.

Animals

A total of 1,045 serum samples from wild boars (Sus scrofa) in Miyazaki Prefecture and 461 from Oita Prefecture, Japan, collected between November 2022 and May 2025, were used in this study. These samples were subjected to ELISA and qRT-PCR analyses. Of these, 104 samples from Miyazaki Prefecture and 46 from Oita Prefecture were randomly selected for the neutralization test. Random numbers were assigned to all available samples using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel, and approximately 10% of the samples from each prefecture were selected. Individual metadata for all samples—including the capture location and date, age (juvenile or adult), and sex—were available. Both prefectures are conventionally divided into four geographical regions: northern, central, western, and southern (Fig. 1b and 1c). In this study, sampling locations were categorized according to these four regions for analytical purposes.

Neutralization test

An 80% plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT80) was performed to evaluate the presence of neutralizing antibodies against SFTSV. Vero cells stably expressing dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN), a receptor for SFTSV, were used for the following assays to enable clear plaque formation [23, 33]. The SFTSV strain A17, isolated from a tick in Miyazaki in 2016 [31], was adapted through 20 serial passages in cell culture until distinct cytopathic effects were observed within 7 days of incubation. This adapted virus and the DC-SIGN-expressing Vero cells were used for the PRNT80. Serum samples were diluted 10-fold in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min to inactivate complement. An equal volume of SFTSV adjusted to 500 plaque-forming units (PFUs)/mL was mixed with each diluted serum sample. The PBS without serum served as a control. After the incubation of the serum–virus mixtures at 37°C for 1 hr, 200 μL of each mixture was inoculated onto cell monolayers in 12-well plates and incubated for 2 hr. After washing, the cells were overlaid with a maintenance medium containing 1% methylcellulose and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 8–10 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 2% crystal violet, and the plaques were counted. The plaque reduction rate relative to the control was calculated for each sample. Samples were defined as positive when the plaque reduction rate exceeded 80% at a final serum dilution of 1:20.

ELISA

Anti-SFTSV NP antibodies were detected using a double-antigen sandwich ELISA, as described previously [22, 37]. Briefly, serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min, centrifuged, and diluted in Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). The diluted samples were applied to microplates coated with recombinant SFTSV NP and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated NP. The absorbance at 405 nm was then measured. Antibody concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic regression model. Cutoff values determined in previous studies were applied: ≥3,641 ng/mL as strongly positive, 1,096–3,641 ng/mL as weakly positive, and <1,096 ng/mL as negative [12, 22]. In this study, both strongly and weakly positive results were considered positive.

RNA extraction and detection of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus RNA

Viral RNA was extracted from 200 μL of wild boar serum using a fully automated nucleic acid extraction system (MagLEAD system, Precision System Science, Chiba, Japan). The One-Step PrimeScript III RT-qPCR Mix (TaKaRa Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) and PrimeTime qPCR assays (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) with gene-specific primers and probes were used to detect SFTSV and an internal control gene. Each sample was tested in duplicate (two wells per sample), and samples in which the internal control gene was not amplified in either well were retested. Primer and probe sets targeting SFTSV and a host internal control gene (GAPDH mRNA) were adapted from previous reports [24, 38]. The sequences were SFTSV forward primer: TGTCAGAGTGGTCCAGGATT, SFTSV reverse primer: ACCTGTCTCCTTCAGCTTCT, SFTSV probe: FAM-TGGAGTTTG/ZEN/GTGAGCAGCAGC-Iowa Black FQ, GAPDH forward primer: GCTGCCCAGAACATCATCC, GAPDH reverse primer: GTCAGATCCACRACBRAYAC, and GAPDH probe: ROX-TCACTGGCATGGCCTTCCGT-BHQ2.

Determination of partial nucleotide sequence of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus nucleoprotein

Samples that tested positive for SFTSV via qRT-PCR were subjected to conventional RT-PCR tests using the PrimeScript One Step RT-PCR Kit Ver.2 (TaKaRa Bio). The 10.0 μL reaction mixture contained 5.0 μL of 2 × 1 Step Buffer (Dye Plus), 0.4 μL of PrimeScript 1 Step Enzyme Mix, forward and reverse primers at a final concentration of 0.3 μM each, 2.0 μL of template RNA, and PCR-grade water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 50°C for 30 min and 94°C for 2 min followed by 50 cycles of 94°C for 30 sec, 66°C for 30 sec, and 72°C for 60 sec. Primer sequences were based on previous reports with minor modifications [12, 32]. A single amplicon of 458 bp was confirmed by electrophoresis using the E-Gel Power Snap Electrophoresis System and 2% E-Gel EX Double Comb Agarose Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). PCR products were purified using the ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent, sequenced using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit, and analyzed with a SeqStudio Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequence data were processed using ATGC-MAC version 7 (Genetyx, Tokyo, Japan), and a 419 bp region excluding the primer sequences was used for subsequent analyses. Partial viral sequences obtained from five animals were identical, and therefore, a single representative sequence was deposited in the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under accession number LC909674.

Phylogenetic analysis

The partial SFTSV NP sequences and reference sequences retrieved from GenBank were aligned using ClustalW. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA software version 12.1 [16]. Evolutionary distances were calculated using Kimura’s two-parameter model, and a bootstrap analysis was performed with 1,000 replicates.

Statistical analyses

Fisher’s exact test was used to compare PRNT80 and qRT-PCR positivity rates between Oita and Miyazaki Prefectures. For ELISA positivity rates, χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used to evaluate differences based on the prefecture, sex, region, season, and age. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Wilson score method. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

Serological survey using ELISA

We analyzed the ELISA positivity rates for SFTSV in wild boars using a total of 1,506 samples collected in Miyazaki and Oita Prefectures between November 2022 and May 2025 (Fig. 1b and 1c; Table 2). In Miyazaki Prefecture, weakly and strongly positive samples accounted for 21.7% (227/1,045) and 12.3% (129/1,045), respectively (Table 2). In Oita Prefecture, weakly positive and strongly positive samples accounted for 10.2% (47/461) and 1.7% (8/461), respectively. Significant differences in the overall ELISA positivity rate (including both weakly and strongly positive samples) were observed between the two prefectures (P<0.001, χ2 test).

Serological survey using the neutralization test

We performed a PRNT80 using 104 randomly selected serum samples from Miyazaki Prefecture and 46 from Oita Prefecture. Among the samples from Miyazaki, 46 tested positive, and 58 tested negative, yielding a positivity rate of 44.2% (Table 4). Among the samples from Oita, 17 tested positive, and 29 tested negative, corresponding to a positivity rate of 37.0%. Although the PRNT80 positivity rate was higher in Miyazaki Prefecture than in Oita Prefecture, the difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P=0.47).

Correlation between neutralization test and ELISA

A total of 150 serum samples from Miyazaki and Oita were subjected to both the neutralization test and ELISA in this study. The correlation between the ELISA and PRNT80 results is summarized in Table 4. When strongly and weakly positive ELISA results were considered positive, the sensitivity, specificity, and overall agreement of the ELISA relative to the PRNT80 were 46.0% (29/63), 88.5% (77/87), and 70.7% (106/150), respectively. Among the 11 samples classified as strongly positive by the ELISA, 10 (90.9%) were also positive via the neutralization test. Among the 28 samples classified as weakly positive by the ELISA, 19 (67.9%) were also positive via the neutralization test. In contrast, among the 111 ELISA-negative samples, 34 (30.6%) were positive in the PRNT80, indicating that the ELISA had a lower sensitivity compared to the neutralization test.

Virological survey using qRT-PCR

The prevalence of SFTSV RNA was also investigated via qRT-PCR. In Miyazaki Prefecture, six samples (0.57%, n=1,045) were positive based on qRT-PCR, with all Cq values ≥36 (Table 5). No reports were received from hunters indicating that any of the wild boars exhibited apparent signs of illness. The positive qRT-PCR samples were collected in November 2022, January 2023, June 2023, July 2023, October 2023, and June 2024, with one sample collected in each of these months. Regarding the ELISA results, one sample was strongly positive, two were weakly positive, and three were negative. As a result of random sampling, none of these samples were subjected to the neutralization test. Among the six wild boars, two were juveniles, four were adults, five were male, and one was female. With respect to collection sites within Miyazaki Prefecture, one sample originated from the northern region, three from the western region, and two from the central region. In contrast, all 461 samples from Oita (0%) were negative according to qRT-PCR. However, the difference in qRT-PCR positivity rates between the two prefectures was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P=0.19).

Phylogenetic analysis of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus in wild boars

For the six Miyazaki samples that tested positive by the qRT-PCR test, we successfully amplified a partial 458 bp sequence of the SFTSV NP gene in five of them via conventional RT-PCR. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed based on these partial NP gene sequences (Fig. 2). The SFTSV sequences detected in wild boars in Miyazaki Prefecture belonged to the J1 genotype and were closely related to strains previously identified in badgers from the same prefecture in 2020.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the prevalence of SFTSV infections in wild boars from Miyazaki and Oita Prefectures, two neighboring endemic regions in Japan that nevertheless exhibit a nearly two-fold difference in the number of reported human SFTS cases. Serum samples collected during the same period were analyzed to determine antibody positivity using both neutralization tests (PRNT80) and ELISAs, as well as viral RNA positivity using qRT-PCR. Consistent with the higher incidence of human SFTS in Miyazaki Prefecture, wild boars captured in Miyazaki demonstrated higher PRNT80, ELISA, and qRT-PCR positivity rates than those captured in Oita. Importantly, when compared with PRNT80, ELISA showed limited sensitivity for detecting neutralizing antibodies in wild boars at the individual level. Nevertheless, among the subset of samples tested by both methods, ELISA reactivity showed a degree of concordance with neutralizing antibody activity, suggesting that ELISA, while imperfect, partially reflects prior exposure to SFTSV. When applied to a sufficiently large sample set, such partial concordance may allow the detection of relative differences in seroresponse patterns between populations, even though ELISA is less suitable for individual-level classification. In the present study, ELISA was able to identify broad regional patterns in seroprevalence between the two prefectures. Given its advantages in biosafety and scalability, ELISA-based surveillance in wild boars is a practical approach for assessing regional SFTSV infection risk, although its limitations should be considered and, where possible, complemented by neutralization tests.

The ELISA positivity rate in the present study was 34.1% (356/1,045) in Miyazaki Prefecture (21.7% weakly positive and 12.3% strongly positive) and 11.9% (55/461) in Oita Prefecture (10.2% weakly positive and 1.7% strongly positive). These rates are comparable to those reported in previous ELISA-based serological surveys of wild boars, including 41.9% (44/105) in Miyazaki in 2009 and 12.3% (8/65) in Oita between 2010 and 2020 [6, 14]. Similar seropositivity rates have also been reported in neighboring prefectures, such as Kagoshima (53.9%, 55/102) and Nagasaki (18.9%, 36/190) [8, 20]. These findings suggest that the seroprevalence observed in the present study is broadly consistent with earlier reports. In Miyazaki Prefecture, the ELISA seropositivity tended to be higher in the southern region; however, the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, marked regional differences were observed within Oita Prefecture. Notably, no ELISA-positive samples were detected in the western region (0/56). However, a previous study reported a seropositivity rate of 28% (2/7) in this area [6], suggesting that it would be inappropriate to conclude that there is no possible SFTSV infection risk. Conversely, the southern region of Oita, which borders Miyazaki Prefecture, exhibited a relatively high ELISA positivity rate of 22.9% (8/35), consistent with previous findings (19%, 3/16). Although these earlier data were collected before 2020, previous studies have also reported that no human SFTS cases had been documented in the western region of Oita, whereas the southern region has shown a relatively high incidence when the population size was adjusted for [6]. Together, these findings suggest that the ELISA seropositivity in wild boars reflects the regional risk of infection with SFTSV. Although the cumulative number of reported human SFTS cases has been higher in Miyazaki Prefecture than in Oita Prefecture, recent annual data show that this difference has become less pronounced, and in the past two years, the number of cases in Oita has even exceeded that in Miyazaki (Table 1). Therefore, cumulative case numbers alone may not fully reflect the current infection risk. Nevertheless, seroprevalence in wild boars remained consistently higher in Miyazaki, suggesting that wildlife serosurveillance may capture longer-term transmission dynamics not reflected by short-term fluctuations in human case reports.

A seasonal trend of higher ELISA seropositivity during the summer (June–August) was observed among wild boars captured in Miyazaki Prefecture, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (P=0.053). In contrast, our previous investigation using the same sample set demonstrated a pronounced seasonal difference in seropositivity for the Oz virus, which is transmitted by ticks (P<0.0001) [24]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in vector ecology and host specificity. Oz virus is transmitted exclusively by A. testudinarium [2], whereas SFTSV is mainly transmitted by H. longicornis and A. testudinarium [30]. However, other tick species, such as H. flava, have also been suggested to contribute to SFTSV transmission [3, 31]. Adult and nymphal A. testudinarium, which preferentially parasitize wild boars, exhibit peak feeding activity during warmer months [4]. In contrast, although H. longicornis is mainly active during warm seasons, H. flava remains active even in winter, except during midsummer [10]. Consequently, wild boars are likely exposed to SFTSV throughout the year, which may obscure clear seasonal patterns. The absence of seasonal differences in Oita Prefecture remains unexplained but may be related to regional differences in the tick population abundance.

In this study, the PRNT80 positivity rates in wild boars were 44.2% in Miyazaki Prefecture and 37.0% in Oita Prefecture, with no statistically significant difference between the two prefectures. This lack of statistical significance may be partly attributable to the limited sample size, which may not have provided sufficient power to detect a moderate regional difference. In addition, comparisons of neutralization test results across studies or geographic areas are inherently difficult, owing to methodological variation among assays, such as differences in serum dilution factors and reduction criteria. Although neutralization tests are considered the gold standard for detecting SFTSV-specific antibodies, their application is constrained by biosafety requirements in Japan, limiting the number of facilities capable of performing these assays and making it difficult to standardize testing conditions across regions. Therefore, while PRNT80 provides reliable evidence of individual exposure, its utility for large-scale geographic comparisons of seroprevalence may be limited in practice.

Several serological surveys of wild boars have employed ELISAs for initial screening, followed by a confirmation using neutralization tests for ELISA-positive samples [8, 20]. While this approach is efficient, Tatemoto et al. reported a low correlation between ELISA and neutralization test results in wild boar samples [35]. Consequently, although ELISA has been used for other wildlife species [34], in their study the antibody positivity in wild boars was determined solely via the focus reduction neutralization test. In the present study, a comparison of ELISA and PRNT80 results revealed an ELISA sensitivity of 46.0%, a specificity of 88.5%, and an overall agreement of 70.7%, supporting the notion of a limited concordance between the two assays in wild boars. The precise reason why the concordance between ELISAs and neutralization tests is particularly low for SFTSV infection in wild boars remains unclear. Based on findings from this and previous studies, wild boars are thought to be susceptible to SFTSV infection; however, viral replication within the host appears to be minimal [14]. In endemic areas, wild boars are repeatedly exposed to SFTSV through tick bites. Consequently, neutralizing antibodies against the viral GPs, which constitute the viral envelope and play a major role in virus neutralization [5], may be readily induced, whereas antibody responses against the NP may be less readily elicited. This difference may reflect differences in antigen exposure or immune responses under conditions of limited viral replication in the host. However, the precise mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Thus, repeated exposure to SFTSV may be required for ELISA seropositivity in wild boars, whereas neutralizing antibodies may be induced after fewer exposures. This difference in antibody responses may influence the detection sensitivity of ELISA in wild boars. Given the lower sensitivity of the ELISA compared with the PRNT80, our decision to include weakly positive ELISA results as positive is considered reasonable. In particular, because the primary objective of this study was to assess the exposure to SFTSV at the population level rather than to establish individual assessments, the inclusion of weakly positive samples is justified. Nevertheless, among samples judged as strongly positive by ELISAs, 10 of 11 were also PRNT80-positive, indicating that the ELISA positivity—particularly strong positivity—provides meaningful information for population-level risk assessments, despite the reduced sensitivity.

The qRT-PCR positivity rate was low, with 0.57% of samples from Miyazaki Prefecture and none from Oita testing positive. Among the six wild boars that tested positive by qRT-PCR, three were also positive by ELISA; however, because a double-antigen sandwich ELISA was employed in this study, it was not possible to determine whether IgM levels were elevated in these animals. Previous studies have reported highly variable qRT-PCR positivity rates, ranging from <1% to >5% [14, 20, 29]. In the present study, qRT-PCR-positive samples were detected across different seasons, with no clear temporal clustering. Given the generally low viral loads detected in wild boars, the utility of qRT-PCR positivity alone as a marker of infection risk remains limited. In this study, qRT-PCR was performed as a complementary approach to serological assays, primarily to identify active infections and obtain viral sequences for phylogenetic analysis, using serum samples that were consistently available from routine surveillance programs. Partial NP gene sequences obtained in this study were identical to those previously detected in a badger captured in Miyazaki Prefecture [12], indicating the circulation of a genetically homogeneous SFTSV strain across multiple wildlife species and regions within the prefecture. According to a research report from the Miyazaki Prefectural Institute for Public Health and Environment, an SFTSV belonging to the J3 genotype has been detected in the southern region of Miyazaki Prefecture [25]. However, in the present study, all wild boar samples from the southern region tested negative by qRT-PCR tests; therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the J3 genotype is similarly circulating among wild boars in this region. Furthermore, only six samples among all specimens were positive by qRT-PCR, and all exhibited Cq values ≥36, indicating very low viral loads in serum. Contact transmission of SFTSV from animals to humans has been associated with high levels of viremia in the source animal; therefore, direct transmission of SFTSV from wild boars to humans is considered unlikely. On the other hand, in China, cases have been reported in which workers were potentially infected with SFTSV during the skinning of moribund minks and foxes [18]. Therefore, appropriate infection control measures are necessary.

Wild boar and deer are actively hunted throughout Japan, with the exception of Hokkaido. In addition, for wild boars, blood samples are routinely collected by local authorities for the surveillance of classical swine fever virus and African swine fever virus. The use of residual sera from these programs provides an efficient opportunity to assess SFTSV seroprevalence. Given the rapid expansion of the SFTSV distribution in Japan, serological surveys of wild boars may enable estimations of the SFTS infection risk even in regions where human cases have not yet been reported. Understanding the level of infection risk in a given region is important for both accurate diagnoses and effective prevention.

In conclusion, we demonstrated substantial differences in SFTSV seroprevalence and viral RNA detection rates between Miyazaki and Oita Prefectures using neutralization tests, ELISAs, and qRT-PCR tests. The positivity rates in Miyazaki and Oita were 44.2% and 37.0% via the neutralization test, 34.1% and 11.9% via the ELISA, and 0.57% and 0% via qRT-PCR, respectively. These findings suggest that SFTSV exposure patterns in wild boars differ between adjacent prefectures. However, because the sampling was not uniform across years and regions, the observed positivity rates should be interpreted with some caution. Considering the labor-intensive nature of neutralization assays and the low sensitivity of qRT-PCR tests in wildlife, ELISA-based surveillance using wild boar serum is a practical approach for detecting broad regional patterns in SFTSV seroprevalence, although its lower sensitivity relative to neutralization tests should be considered.
