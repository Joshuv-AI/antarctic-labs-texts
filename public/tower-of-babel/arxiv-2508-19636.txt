1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a smooth compact
2
-manifold (a surface).
Consider a natural right action of the group
\mathcal{D}(M)
of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphisms of
M
on the space of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
functions
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{D}(M)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle(f,h)\mapsto f\circ h.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
.
Then the corresponding stabilizer and orbit of
f
will be denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(f)

\displaystyle:=\{h\in\mathcal{D}(M)\mid f\circ h=f\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(f)

\displaystyle:=\{f\circ h\mid h\in\mathcal{D}(M)\}.

Let
\Delta_{f}:=\{f^{-1}(c)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
be the partition of
M
into level sets of
f
.
The connected components of elements of
\Delta_{f}
will be called
countours
.
A contour is
critical
if it contains a critical point of
f
, and
regular
otherwise.
Notice that

\Delta_{f\circ h}=\{h^{-1}(f^{-1}(c))\mid c\in\mathbb{R}\}=h^{-1}(\Delta_{f}),\qquad h\in\mathcal{D}(M).

Hence, one can study the action (
1.1
) by looking on how the diffeomorphisms of
M
perturb the partition
\Delta_{f}
.
In particular, every
h\in\mathcal{S}(f)
leaves invariant each element of
\Delta_{f}
, and therefore it yields a permutations of contours of each level set of
f
sending regular countours to regular, and critical to critical.
For that reason it will be convenient to say that diffeomorphisms from
\mathcal{S}(f)

preserve

f
.

We will endow all spaces of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
maps with the corresponding
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
Whitney topologies.
Then
\mathcal{D}(M)
and
\mathcal{S}(f)
are topological groups, and the above action is continuous.
In particular, path components of
\mathcal{D}(M)
(as well as of
\mathcal{S}(f)
and
\mathcal{O}(f)
) are pairwise homeomorphic.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M)
the identity path component of
\mathcal{D}(M)
(i.e. path component containing
\mathrm{id}_{M}
),
by
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{id}}(f)
the identity path component of
\mathcal{S}(f)
, and by
\mathcal{O}_{f}(f)
the path component of
\mathcal{O}(f)
containing
f
.

Let also
\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
be the subset of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
consisting of functions
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
being
locally constant and regular on
\partial M
, i.e.

(B)

f
takes constant values on every component of the boundary
\partial M
and has no critical points on it,

and
\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
be the subset of functions
f
satisfying additional
homogeneity
condition:

(H)

for every critical point
z\in M
of
f
, the germ of
f
at
z
is smoothly equivalent to the germ of some nonzero homogeneous polynomial
g_{z}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

without multiple factors
.

The latter condition easily implies that every critical point of
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})
is isolated.
Hence, due to compactness of
M
, every such
f
has only finitely many critical points.
Moreover, by Morse lemma, every non-degenerate critical point satisfies that condition (namely, it is equivalent to the polynomial
\pm x^{2}\pm y^{2}
), and therefore
\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})
contains the set
\mathcal{M}(M,\mathbb{R})
of all Morse functions, constituting an open and everywhere dense subset of
\mathcal{C}_{\partial}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
.
However,
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})
may have degenerate critical points.

\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})
, see

For the moment, those calculations do not completely cover only certain subclasses of functions on

, and Klein bottle

, and the aim of the present paper is to

.

Deformations of functions on surfaces by diffeomorphisms

In what follows

will be a compact surface,
S^{1}=\{|z|=1\}\subset\mathbb{C}
the unit circle in the complex plane,
\mathbb{M}
the Möbius band,
\mathbb{K}
the Klein bottle,

a one-point space, and

(a product of

Also the sign

We will discuss here known results about the homotopy types of stabilizers and orbits of functions from class
\mathcal{F}(M)

Furthermore, for

2.1.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}(M,\mathbb{R})
.
Recall that by a

of

A subset

One can observe that a compact

A typical example of a

Now if

-regular neighborhood
of

-adapted subsurface

Lemma 2.1.1

(
[
34
, Corollary 7.2]
)
.

Let

,

be an
f
-adapted submanifold, and

.
Then the inclusions

\mathcal{S}(f,U)\ \subset\ \mathcal{S}_{{\mathrm{nb}}}(f,X)\ \subset\ \mathcal{S}(f,X)

2.2.
Graph of a function

Consider the partition
\Gamma_{f}
of

into

, and let
p_{f}\colon M\to\Gamma_{f}
be the corresponding quotient map associating to each

containing

Endow
\Gamma_{f}
with the quotient topology with respect to

, so a subset

is open iff its inverse image

is open in

.
Then
\Gamma_{f}
is called the

of

Notice that we have the following decomposition of

f\colon M\xrightarrow{\penalty\ p_{f}\penalty\ }\Gamma_{f}\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \hat{f}\penalty\ }\mathbb{R},

for a unique continuous function
\hat{f}\colon\Gamma_{f}\to\mathbb{R}

Let

Then it is easy to see that

has structure of

(
0

(open
1

Moreover, the function
\hat{f}
is strictly monotone on open edges of

The following statement is well-known and follows from the easy observation that

is homotopic to

Lemma 2.2.1
.

For each
f\in\mathcal{F}(M)
, then induced homomorphism

In particular, if

is either a

(has no cycles) or

Let us briefly recall the proof.
Note that the inclusion

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X\cup\partial M)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(M,X)

Conversely, let

Since

\mathrm{id}_{M}

In particular,

In other words,

But then

2.5.
Special kinds of wreath products

Let
G
be a group with unit
e_{G}
, and
\phi\colon G\to G
be an automorphism.
Then we have the semidirect product
G\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
being by definition the cartesian product
G\times\mathbb{Z}
with respect to the following operation:

(g;k)(h;l)=(g\phi^{k}(h),k+l).

The unit element in this group is
(e_{G};0)
, and the inverse of
(g;k)
is
(\phi^{-k}(g^{-1});-k)
.

More generally, if
\phi,\psi\colon G\to G
are two commuting automorphisms, then we also have the semidirect product
G\rtimes_{\phi,\psi}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
being by definition the cartesian product
G\times\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with respect to the following operation:

(g;k,l)(h;k^{\prime},l^{\prime})=(g\,\phi^{k}(\psi^{l}(h));k+k^{\prime},l+l^{\prime}).

Define now the following three types of groups.

1) Let
a\geq 1
, and
\phi\colon G^{a}\to G^{a}
,
\phi(g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{a-1})=(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{a-1},g_{0})
, be the cyclic shift to the left.
Then the corresponding semidirect product
G\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
will be denoted by
G\wr_{a}\mathbb{Z}
.

2) Let
a,b\geq 1
.
Consider the product
G^{ab}
of
ab
copies of
G
.
Its elements can be regarded as
a\times b
matrices
\{g_{i,j}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{a},j\in\mathbb{Z}_{b}}
with entries in
G
.

Then we have the following two automorphisms of
G^{ab}
:

\displaystyle\phi,\psi\colon G^{ab}\to G^{ab},

\displaystyle\phi(\{g_{i,j}\})=\{g_{i+1,j}\},

\displaystyle\psi(\{g_{i,j}\})=\{g_{i,j+1}\},

making cyclic shifts of rows and columns respectively, where the first index is taken modulo
a
and the second one modulo
b
.
Evidently,
\phi
and
\psi
commute and therefore we have a semidirect product
G^{a,b}\rtimes_{\phi,\psi}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which we will denote by
G\wr_{a,b}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

3) Finally, let
H
be another group with unit
e_{H}
,
\gamma\colon H\to H
be an automorphism of order
2
, and
a\geq 1
.
Consider the following automorphism
\phi\colon G^{2a}\times H^{a}\to G^{2a}\times H^{a}
,

\phi(g_{0},\ldots,g_{2a-1};\ h_{0},\ldots,h_{a-1})=\bigl(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{0};\ h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{a-1},\gamma(h_{0})\bigr).

Evidently, it has order
2a
.
The corresponding semidirect product
(G^{2a}\times H^{a})\rtimes_{\phi}\mathbb{Z}
will be denoted by
(G,H)\wr_{a,\gamma}\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 2.5.1
.

If
G
is torsion free, then for every
a,b\geq 1
the groups
G\wr_{a}\mathbb{Z}
and
G\wr_{a,b}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
are torsion free as well.

If
G,H
are torsion free, then for every
a
and an automorphism
\gamma\colon H\to H
of order
2
, the group
(G,H)\wr_{a,\gamma}\mathbb{Z}
is torsion free as well.

Proof.

The proof for
G\wr_{a}\mathbb{Z}
is given in
[
34
, Lemma 2.2]
.
The proofs for
G\wr_{a,b}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
(G,H)\wr_{a,\gamma}\mathbb{Z}
is similar.
For the convenience of the reader let us present a proof for
(G,H)\wr_{a,\gamma}\mathbb{Z}
.

Suppose
q=(g_{0},\ldots,g_{2m-1};h_{0},\ldots,h_{a-1};k)\in(G,H)\wr_{a,\gamma}\mathbb{Z}
has finite order, say
l
.
Then

(e_{G},\ldots,e_{G};e_{H},\ldots,e_{H};0)=e=q^{l}=(g^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,g^{\prime}_{2m-1};h^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,h^{\prime}_{a-1};kl)

for some
g^{\prime}_{i}\in G
and
h^{\prime}_{j}\in H
.
Hence
kl=0
, and therefore
k=0
since
l\not=0
.
But then
g^{\prime}_{i}=g_{i}^{k}=e_{G}
and
h^{\prime}_{j}=h_{j}^{k}=e_{H}
.
Since
G
and
H
are torsion free,
g_{i}=e_{G}
and
h_{j}=e_{H}
, i.e.
q=e
.
∎

.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Diffeomorphism groups

All manifolds are assumed to be of class
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.
Let
M
be a manifold.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}(M)
the group of all
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
diffeomorphisms of
M
, and for a closed subset
X\subset M
define also the following subgroups of
\mathcal{D}(M)
.
Let

•

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M,X)
be the group of diffeomorphisms
h
of
M
leaving
X
invariant, i.e.
h(X)=X
;

•

\mathcal{D}(M,X)
be its subgroup fixing
X
pointwise;

•

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)
be another subgroup consisting of diffeomorphisms
h
each being fixed on some neighborhood of
X
(depending on
h)
.

Thus, we have the following inclusions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)\ \subset\ \mathcal{D}(M,X)\ \subset\ \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{inv}}(M,X)\ \subset\ \mathcal{D}(M).

A
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
map
\gamma\colon N\to M
between two manifolds is an
embedding
if the induced tangent map
T\gamma\colon TN\to TM
is injective.
Then by a
submanifold
of
M
we will mean the image of some embedding.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X)
the subgroup of
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
consisting of diffeomorphisms
h
which also “preserve the sides near
X
”, i.e. if
Y
is a connected component of
X
,
Y\subset U\subset E_{Y}
where
U
is any open neighborhood of
Y
and
E_{Y}
is some tubular neighborhood of
Y
,
E_{Y}^{0}
and
E_{Y}^{1}
are connected components of
E_{Y}\setminus Y
, with
h(U)\subset E_{Y}
, then
h(U\cap E_{Y}^{i})\subset E_{Y}^{i}
for
i=0,1
.

We will need the following theorem, which seems to be known however the authors did not find a formal proof in the literature:

Theorem 3.1.1
.

Let
X
be a compact submanifold of
M
such that every its connected component
Y
is

(1)

either a boundary component of
M
,

(2)

or a
proper two-sided of codimension
1
, that is

(a)

\partial Y=Y\cap\partial M
and that intersection is transversal;

(b)

any tubular neighborhood
E_{Y}\to Y
of
Y
is a trivial one-dimensional vector bundle.

Then the inclusion
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)\subset\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X)
is a homotopy equivalence.

This statement might be deduced from ambient isotopy extension theorem for smooth submanifolds and contractibility of the space of tubular neighborhoods of a submanifold, see
[
12
, Chapter A, Proposition 31]
.
A formal proof can be obtained as well from “linearization theorem” proved in the preprint
[
15
]
, see also discussions in
[
39
, Theorem 2.2.5]
.
For the convenience of the reader let us present the arguments.

Proof.

For simplicity assume that
X
is a closed submanifold of
\mathrm{Int\,}{M}
: for boundary components one should consider collars (“halves” of tubular neighborhoods).
Let
p\colon E\to X
be any tubular neighborhood of
X
and
\mathrm{GL}(E,X)
be the group of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
vector bundle automorphisms of
E
fixed on
X
.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}(M,X,p)
the subgroup of
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
consisting of diffeomorphisms
h
which coincide near
X
with some vector bundle automorphism of
E
, which in fact is uniquely determined by the following formula:

\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}h\colon E\to E,\qquad\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}h(x)=\lim_{t\to 0}\tfrac{1}{t}h(tx).

Here the multiplication by
t
and
1/t
should be understood in terms of the vector bundle structure on
E
, see e.g.
[
39
, Eq (2.1)]
.

Let
\mathcal{D}(M,X,p)
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
consisting of diffeomorphisms
h
which coincide near
X
with
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}h
.
Then under assumption
(2)
on
X
, the “linearization theorem” from
[
15
]
claims that the inclusion
\mathcal{D}(M,X,p)\subset\mathcal{D}(M,X)
is a homotopy equivalence.

Denote
\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p)=\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X)\cap\mathcal{D}(M,X,p)
,
Since
\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X)
is a union of path components of
\mathcal{D}(M,X)
, the inclusion

(3.1)

\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p)\subset\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X)

is still a homotopy equivalence.

Furthermore, we have a homomorphism
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}\colon\mathcal{D}(M,X,p)\to\mathrm{GL}(E,X)
,
h\mapsto\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}h
, whose kernel is
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)
.
It is shown in
[
15
]
, see also
[
39
, Corollary 7.1.3]
for another proof, that
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}
admits local cross sections, and therefore it is a locally trivial fibration
over its image
which is in turn a union of path components of
\mathrm{GL}(E,X)
.

Now let us use the assumption that
X
is “one-sided”.
Note that every vector bundle automorphism
g
of the trivial vector bundle
X\times\mathbb{R}\to X
is given by
g(x,v)=(x,\alpha(x)v)
, for some everywhere non-zero
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function
\alpha\colon X\to\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
.
Hence
\mathrm{GL}(E,X)
can be identified with
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
.

Moreover, then
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}(\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p))\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,(0;+\infty))
.
Evidently,
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,(0;+\infty))
is a path component of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
containing the constant function
X\mapsto 1
being the image
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}(\mathrm{id}_{M})
of
\mathrm{id}_{M}
.
Hence
\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}(\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p))=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,(0;+\infty))
, and the induced map

\mathsf{T}_{\!\mathsf{fib}}\colon\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,(0;+\infty))

is still a locally trivial fibration with fiber
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)
.
Since
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X,(0;+\infty))
is evidently contractible, we obtain that the inclusion of the fiber
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(M,X)\subset\mathcal{D}^{+}(M,X,p)
is a homotopy equivalence.
Together with the homotopy equivalence (
3.1
) this gives the desired result.
∎

3.2.
Klein bottle

Let
T^{2}=S^{1}\times S^{1}
be the
2
-torus.
It will be convenient to say that the curves
S^{1}\times\{w\}
,
w\in S^{1}
, are
longitudes
, while the curves
\{w\}\times S^{1}
are
meridians
.

Consider the following orientation reversing involution without fixed points:

\xi\colon T^{2}\to T^{2},\qquad\xi(z,w)=(-z,\bar{w}).

It defines an action of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on
T^{2}
, so that the corresponding quotient space
\mathbb{K}:=T^{2}/\xi
is a non-orientable surface called
Klein bottle
.
In turn, the respective quotient map
p\colon T^{2}\to\mathbb{K}
is then an oriented double covering of
\mathbb{K}
.

We recall here several facts (more detailed than Table
2.1
) on the homotopy type of the group
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
of diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{K}
and the generators of
\pi_{0}\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
.

Let
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
be connected components of
S^{1}\setminus\{\pm i\}
containing
-1
and
1
respectively.
Consider the following union
\tilde{\alpha}=S^{1}\times\{\pm i\}
of two longitudes.
Then
T^{2}\setminus\tilde{\alpha}
consists of two connected components whose closures are the following cylinders:

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=S^{1}\times J_{1},

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=S^{1}\times j_{2}.

Evidently,
\xi
exchanges the longitudes
S^{1}\times\{i\}
and
S^{1}\times\{-i\}
and also leaves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
invariant.
Denote
\alpha=p(\tilde{\alpha})
,
\mathbb{M}_{i}=p(C_{i})
,
i=1,2
.
Then
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
are Möbius bands in
\mathbb{K}
so that
\alpha=\partial\mathbb{M}_{1}=\partial\mathbb{M}_{2}=\mathbb{M}_{1}\cap\mathbb{M}_{2}
and
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{M}_{1}\cup\mathbb{M}_{2}
.

Then
\lambda_{1}=p(S^{1}\times\{1\})
and
\lambda_{2}=p(S^{1}\times\{-1\})
are the ‘‘
middle circles
” of the Möbius bands
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
respectively, while
\mu=p(\{1\}\times S^{1})
is the ‘‘
meridian
” of
\mathbb{K}
.

Define further the following diffeomorphisms
\hat{y},\hat{\tau}\colon T^{2}\to T^{2}
:

\hat{y}(z,w)=(\bar{z},w),\qquad\hat{\tau}(z,w)=(z,-\bar{w}).

Evidently, they both have order
2
and commute.
Moreover, they also commute with
\xi
, and therefore yield unique diffeomorphisms
y,\tau\colon\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{K}
, such that
p\circ\hat{y}=y\circ p
and
p\circ\hat{\tau}=\tau\circ p
.

Remark 3.2.1
.

Note that
\hat{y}
leaves invariant each longitude of
T^{2}
but reverses its orientation.
In particular,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are invariant under
\hat{y}
, whence
y
leaves invariant
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
but reverses orientation of their common boundary
\alpha
and the middle circles
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.

On the other hand,
\hat{\tau}
pointwise fixes
\tilde{\alpha}
and exchanges
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, whence
\tau
pointwise fixes
\alpha
and exchanges
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
.

In fact,
\tau
is isotopic to a Dehn twist along the meridian
\mu
, while
y
is the
Y
-homeomorphism
introduced by Lickorish
[
26
]
.

Remark 3.2.2
.

Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{K}
be a simple closed curve.
Then
\omega
is called
two-sided
or
orientation preserving
if it has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open cylinder.
Otherwise,
\omega
has a neighborhood homeomorphic with an open Möbius band, and is called in this case
one-sided
or
orientation reversing
.
Fix some orientation of
\omega
, and denote by
-\omega
the same curve but passed in the opposite direction.
Then by
[
26
, Lemma 1]
exactly one of the following cases is possible:

(a)

\mathbb{K}\setminus\omega
is a union of two open Möbius bands, and in this case
\omega
is isotopic to
\alpha
or
-\alpha
;

(b)

\omega
bounds a
2
-disk;

(c)

\omega
does not cut
\mathbb{K}
, and is isotopic to the
meridian

\mu
of
\mathbb{K}
.

Define further the following
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
action of
S^{1}
on
\mathbb{K}
by

(3.2)

\widehat{F}\colon T^{2}\times S^{1}\to T^{2},\qquad\widehat{F}(z,w,t)=(zt,w).

Evidently,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{t}\circ\xi(z,w)

\displaystyle=\widehat{F}_{t}(-z,\bar{w})=(-zt,\bar{w})=\xi(zt,w)=\xi\circ\widehat{F}_{t}(z,w),

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{t}\circ\hat{\tau}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\widehat{F}_{t}(z,-\bar{w})=(zt,-\bar{w})=\hat{\tau}(zt,w)=\hat{\tau}\circ\widehat{F}_{t}(z,w),

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{t}\circ\hat{y}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\widehat{F}_{t}(\bar{z},w)=(\bar{z}t,w)=\hat{y}(z\bar{t},w)=\hat{y}\circ\widehat{F}_{\bar{t}}(z,w).

In other words, this action commutes with
\xi
and therefore it yields a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
action
F_{t}\colon\mathbb{K}\times S^{1}\to\mathbb{K}
such that
p\circ\widehat{F}_{t}=F_{t}\circ p
.
Since
\bar{t}=t^{-1}
for all
t\in S^{1}
, the latter two identities in (
3.3
) imply that

\displaystyle F_{t}\circ\tau=\tau\circ F_{t},

\displaystyle F_{t}\circ y=y\circ F_{t}^{-1}.

Hence we have the following subgroups of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
:

\displaystyle SO(2)\cong\langle\{F_{t}\}_{t\in S^{1}}\rangle,

\displaystyle O(2)\cong\langle SO(2),\ y\rangle,

\displaystyle O(2)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\langle O(2),\tau\rangle.

Theorem 3.2.3
.

The inclusion
O(2)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\langle\{F_{t}\}_{t\in S^{1}},y,\tau\rangle\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
is a homotopy equivalence.
In particular,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})
is homotopy equivalent to
SO(2)
, and
\pi_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})\cong\langle y,\tau\rangle=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Let also
\mathcal{D}^{+}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
be the group of diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{K}
fixed on
\alpha
and leaving invariant
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
.
Then
\mathcal{D}^{+}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
is contractible and, in particular, coincides with
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
.

Proof.

The statement that the inclusion
SO(2)\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})
is a homotopy equivalence is independently proved in
[
3
, page 41, Corollary]
and
[
13
, Théorème 1]
.
The group
\pi_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})
and its generators
y
and
\tau
are described in
[
26
]
.

To prove contractibility of
\mathcal{D}^{+}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
note that by Theorem
3.1.1
the inclusions

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)\subset\mathcal{D}^{+}(\mathbb{K},\alpha),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{M}_{i},\alpha)\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{M}_{i},\alpha),\ i=1,2,

are homotopy equivalences, and by Table
2.1
(first line) the groups
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{M}_{i},\alpha)
are contractible.
Moreover, the map

\rho\colon\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{M}_{1},\alpha)\times\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{M}_{2},\alpha),\qquad\rho(h)=\bigl(h|_{\mathbb{M}_{1}},h|_{\mathbb{M}_{2}}\bigr),

is, evidently, an
isomorphism
of topological groups.
Hence,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
and therefore
\mathcal{D}^{+}(\mathbb{K},\alpha)
, are also contractible.
∎

Corollary 3.2.4
.

1)
The homomorphism
\mathsf{t}\colon\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})\to\mathrm{Aut}\bigl(H_{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z})\bigr)
, associating to each

the corresponding automorphism of the first integral homology group

, is surjective and has the kernel
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})
.

\pi_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})\cong\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})/\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathbb{K})\cong\langle\tau,y\rangle=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong\mathrm{Aut}\bigl(H_{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z})\bigr).

2)
Every diffeomorphism
h\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
is isotopic to a diffeomorphism
h^{\prime}
leaving invariant
\alpha
, and thus preserving or exchanging
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
.
In that case

•

h^{\prime}
preserves
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and
\mathbb{M}_{2}
iff it is isotopic either to

or to

;

.

3)
Let
\gamma\colon[0;1]\to\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
be a loop at
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{K}}
, i.e.
\gamma
is a continuous path with
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{K}}
.

and consider the loop

,

being the ‘‘

” of

.
Then the path

Proof.

1) Choose the standard counterclockwise orientation of

.
This gives orientations on

,

, and

,
\lambda_{2}
,
\alpha
and

Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=(1,0),

\displaystyle\mu=(0,1),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=(1,1),

\displaystyle\alpha=\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=(2,0).

isomorphic with

\displaystyle\mathsf{t}(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle\colon(n,\delta)\mapsto(n,\delta),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mathsf{t}(y)

\displaystyle\colon(n,\delta)\mapsto(-n,\delta),

and

2) By Theorem
3.2.3
every
h\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{K})
is isotopic to some diffeomorphism from the group

, each of whose elements leave

.
Then

preserves
\mathbb{M}_{1}
and

and

.

.

represents a loop in
