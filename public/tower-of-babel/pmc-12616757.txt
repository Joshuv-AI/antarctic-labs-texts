CBX6 induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion and tumor development in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma through SMARCD1-mediated CCL8 secretion and lactate efflux
Authors: Lihua Wang, Guoqing Liu, Qilan Huang, Haijing Wu, Xinhan Cheng, Li Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the functions of chromobox 6 (CBX6) in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and delves into its functional mechanisms. The bioinformatics insights suggested that CBX6 was overexpressed in ESCC and linked to dismal prognosis. Cbx6 knockdown was induced in mouse mEC25 cells. This procedure curbed the proliferation and migration of mEC25 cells and reduced exhaustion of the co-cultured CD8+ T cells. In vivo, Cbx6 knockdown in mEC25 cells reduced tumorigenesis while enhancing immune activity in mice. Further experiments showed that CBX6 reduced CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity by secreting C–C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CCL8) and promoting monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4)-mediated lactate transport. Regarding the mechanism, CBX6 regulated the expression of SWI/SNF related BAF chromatin remodeling complex subunit D1 (Smarcd1) to modulate chromatin remodeling, thus promoting transcription of Ccl8 and Slc16a3 (encoding MCT4). Smarcd1 overexpression restored metabolic activity in mEC25 cells, reduced activity of co-cultured CD8+ T cells, and promoted tumorigenesis in vivo. Tissue microarrays analysis suggested that CBX6 and SMARCD1 were linked to immunosuppression and poor prognosis in clinical samples. In conclusion, this study suggests that CBX6 induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion and tumor development in ESCC through SMARCD1-mediated CCL8 secretion and lactate efflux.Graphical abstract• CBX6 is highly expressed in ESCC and linked to poor prognosis.• Cbx6 knockdown in mouse cells reduces proliferation, migration, and tumor growth.• CBX6 suppresses CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity through CCL8 secretion and MCT4-mediated lactate transport.• SMARCD1 mediates CBX6’s effects on immune suppression and tumor progression in ESCC. Introduction

Cancer stands as one of the most pressing global health challenges (Sonkin et al. 2024). In China alone, roughly 4.8 million new cancer diagnoses were reported in 2022, leading to an estimated 3.2 million cancer-related fatalities that same year (Xia et al. 2022). Esophageal cancer (ESCA) represents the 11th most prevalent cancer and the 7th leading contributor to cancer-related deaths, which accounts for approximately 511,000 new diagnoses and 445,000 deaths gloabally (Bray et al. 2024). ESCA primarily contains two subtypes: esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which makes up for around 90% of cases (Rustgi and El-Serag 2014), and esophageal adenocarcinoma (Cancer Genome Atlas Research et al. 2017). ESCC is recognized for its aggressive nature, rapid growth, and a high tendency for lymph node metastasis, typically affecting the upper two-thirds of the esophagus (Morgan et al. 2022). Risk factors contributing to the progression of ESCC include tobacco use, alcohol consumption, thermal injury, and deficiencies in essential nutrients (Smyth et al. 2017). Surgery remains the most effective treatment for early-stage ESCC; however, most patients are diagnosed at later stages when surgery is no longer feasible. This leads to a dismal 5-year survival rate of around 18%, even with chemoradiotherapy (Codipilly and Wang 2022; Li et al. 2021). The advent of immunotherapies, especially immune checkpoint inhibitors has marked a breakthrough in treatment (Sun et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022). Despite these advancements, the overall survival rates for many patients with ESCC patients remain suboptimal. This is largely attributable to the intricate and often adaptive resistance mechanisms that constrain the long-term effectiveness of these therapies (Wang et al. 2024b).

In 2011, Hanahan and Weinberg broadened their framework of cancer hallmarks, introducing two new critical factors in tumor development: the reprogramming of energy metabolism and the ability of tumors to evade immune destruction (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011). A central player in cancer progression, including in ESCC, is the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Zheng et al. 2020). The TME is a dynamic and complex ecosystem composed of immune and stromal cells that surround and interact with tumor cells. This environment is integral to shaping the immune landscape, often promoting immunosuppressive conditions that inhibit effective therapeutic responses (Bejarano et al. 2021; Hinshaw and Shevde 2019). While immune cells that recognize and eliminate tumor cells, such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes, are present in the TME and can recognize tumor-specific antigens or neoantigens to mount anti-tumor responses (Keskin et al. 2019), the TME often fosters conditions that facilitate tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to treatment (Baba et al. 2020; Friedl and Alexander 2011).

Within the TME, CD8+ T cells that are specific to cancer cells are able to recognize and respond to antigens, selectively eliminating tumor cells (Philip and Schietinger 2022; Renkvist et al. 2001). However, despite their potential, most patients do not experience durable responses to immunotherapy. Even tumors that express highly immunogenic neoantigens can progress despite initial immune responses (Philip and Schietinger 2022). Chronic antigen exposure and persistent inflammation in the TME can induce exhaustion of CD8+ T cells, transforming them into exhausted T cells (Tex) (Philip and Schietinger 2022; Wang et al. 2022). This particular group of CD8+ T cells exhibited increased levels of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, TIM-3, CTLA-4, and LAG-3 (Liu et al. 2024a). These Tex exhibit a series of dysfunctional features, including heightened expression of inhibitory receptors, diminished self-renewal ability, and a progressive loss of effector functions, making them unable to effectively eliminate the tumor (Blank et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2022). Therefore, reinvigorating CD8+ T cell activity and restoring their cytotoxic potential may offer significant promise for enhancing anti-tumor immunity.

Epigenetic regulation is a crucial determinant in tumorigenesis and cancer progression (Dawson and Kouzarides 2012; Recillas-Targa 2022). Polycomb group (PcG) complexes, a key class of epigenetic regulatory complexes, have been implicated in the initiation and progression of various cancers (Casa and Gabellini 2012). Chromobox (CBX) proteins, integral components of PcG complexes, are involved in regulating essential biological processes such as stem cell self-renewal and cell differentiation (Gil and O'Loghlen 2014; Klauke et al. 2013). Abnormal expression of CBX proteins has been observed in numerous cancer types (Naqvi et al. 2023). Growing evidence suggests that CBXs are crucially implicated in regulating various aspects of tumor biology, including tumor stemness (Huang et al. 2021), the cell cycle (Wang et al. 2013), cellular metabolism (Iqbal et al. 2021), and resistance to treatments (Zhang et al. 2019). Moreover, these proteins are believed to influence the expression of genes within the TME (Zhang et al. 2022b).

Our bioinformatics analysis reveals that CBX6 is notably overexpressed in ESCC and is associated with CD8+ T cell dysfunction. CBX6 has been reported to exhibit unique expression patterns in different solid tumors (PMID: 36734270). However, its specific roles in the progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), especially in terms of energy metabolism and immune regulation, remain elusive. Grounded on these insights, this study seeks to further investigate CBX6's roles in ESCC, specifically focusing on its impacts on CD8+ T cell function and activity. Additionally, the molecular mechanisms driving these interactions will be explored to better understand how CBX6 contributes to immune evasion in the TME.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA-seq HTSeq-Counts data were downloaded from the TCGA-ESCA (esophageal cancer) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-ESCA) and GTEx (normal esophageal tissue) databases (https://gtexportal.org/). Differential expression was analyzed utilizing DESeq2 (v1.30.1), with the "median of ratios" method applied for normalization. A significance threshold of |log2FoldChange|> 1 and false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-values < 0.05 was applied. The expression level of CBX6 was presented in TPM (Transcripts Per Million). Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) data for patients were obtained from TCGA-ESCA. Patients were allocated into high/low expression groups according to the median expression of CBX6. The R package "survival" (v3.2–10) was employed to generate Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and significance was assessed using the log-rank test. The CIBERSORT (https://cibersort.stanford.edu/) algorithm was utilized to calculate the infiltration scores of CD8+ T cells (markers: CD8A, CD8B) and exhausted T cells (markers: PDCD1, HAVCR2, LAG3) in ESCC samples. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted using R (v4.0.3). Single-cell datasets GSE221561 (Two ESCC patients underwent general surgery alone while seven ESCC patients with neoadjuvant therapy) and GSE154763 (10X Genomics platform) (48 patients to investigate tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells) were queried from the GEO database. Quality control was conducted using Seurat (v4.0.4), retaining cells with gene expression > 200 and mitochondrial gene percentage < 10%. Data normalization was performed using "LogNormalize", and dimensionality reduction was done with principal component analysis followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection visualization. Cell types were annotated based on classic marker genes (T cells: CD3D, CD8A).

Cell culture and treatments

The mouse ESCC cell lines mEC25 (HTX3674, Otwo Biotech, Guangzhou, China) and AKR (VCM00123, ViCell Biotechnology, Zhengzhou, China) were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (11965092) containing10%FBS (10270106) and 1% antibiotics (15140122, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37℃. Cells were passaged every 2–3 d using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (25200056, Gibco) for digestion. Mycoplasma contamination was detected monthly by PCR, and experiments were continued only if the result was negative.

Three short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting mouse Cbx6 or Smarcd1 were designed, with a scramble shRNA used as the negative control. Transfection was carried out using lentiviral vectors. After 48 h, medium containing 2 μg/mL puromycin (P9620, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Germany) was used for selection over 7 d. Full-length mouse Smarcd1 CDS was cloned into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro vector (CD510B-1, System Biosciences, USA) and co-transfected with psPAX2 and pMD2.G into 293 T cells (CRL-3216, ATCC). The virus supernatant was filtered and applied to infect mEC25 cells, with 8 μg/mL polybrene (H9268, Sigma) added to enhance infection efficiency. After 72 h, stable clones were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin.

Lactate assays

Lactate concentrations were evaluated utilizing the Lactate Detection Kit (MAK064, Sigma). The cell supernatant was collected, and the reaction mixture, including lactate oxidase and horseradish peroxidase (HRP), was prepared following the kit's instructions. The cell supernatant and reaction mixture were incubated at 37℃. Optical density (OD) was measured at 492 nm utilizing a microplate reader. A standard curve (standard range: 0–10 nmol/μL) was generated for lactate concentration evaluation.

Seahorse glycolysis stress test

A total of 1 × 104 cells per well were plated in a Seahorse XF96 plate, using unbuffered DMEM (103575–100, Agilent, USA) as the base culture medium. Once cells adhered and reached over 90% density, metabolic analysis was started. Prior to the experiment, the Seahorse XF96 plate was pre-warmed and set at the appropriate temperature. In the experiment process, 1 μM oligomycin A (75351, Sigma), 10 mM glucose (G8769, Sigma), and 50 mM 2-DG (D8375, Sigma) were sequentially administered. Glucose was used to stimulate glycolysis, oligomycin to inhibit ATP synthesis for measuring basal glycolysis, and 2-DG as a competitive inhibitor of glycolysis to assess glycolytic capacity. Real-time monitoring of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, in mpH/min) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR, in pmol/min) was conducted to evaluate cellular metabolic activity.

Colony formation assays

Exponentially growing cells were plated in six-well plates (3516, Corning, USA) at 500 cells per well and cultured for 7 d. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (158127, Sigma) for 15 min and staining with 0.1% crystal violet (C3886, Sigma) for 30 min, colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays

Differentially treated cells were cultured in 96-well wells overnight. On day 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, each well was loaded with 10 μL CCK-8 reagent (Solarbio Science & Technology, China) to evaluate cell viability. After 2 h, OD at 450 nm was read.

Transwell migration assays

An Transwell chamber (8 μm pore, 3422, Corning) was used. A total of 5 × 104 cells dissolved in 200 μL serum-free DMEM were loaded to the upper chamber, while serum-containing complete DMEM was loaded to the lower chamber. After 24 h, migrating cells were fixed for crystal violet staining, and migration was assessed by counting cells in five random fields.

Co-culture assays

C57BL/6 mice (aged 6–8 weeks, 20 ± 2 g) were procured from Anburui Biotechnologies (Fujian, China) and used adhering to the guidelines approved by Mianyang Cancer Hospital. Lymphocytes were extracted from the thymus of mice. The MojoSort Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (480,008, BioLegend, USA) was employed to obtain highly purified CD8+ T cells (> 90% purity, verified by flow cytometry). The sorted CD8+ T cells were activated using αCD3/CD28 Dynabeads (11452D, Gibco) at a 1:1 ratio and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 50 U/mL IL-2 (PeproTech, 212–12) for 48 h.

These CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with mEC25 cells either directly or indirectly in Transwell systems. In the direct co-culture scenario, T cells were mixed with mEC25 cells (control or CBX6 knockdown) at ratios of 1:1000 to 1:1 in 96-well plates (3599, Corning) containing a 1:1 mixture of DMEM and RPMI 1640. In the non-contact setting, Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size, 3470, Corning) were used. After 48 h, the T cells were collected for flow cytometric analysis.

Additionally, the supernatant from mEC25 cells (control or Cbx6 knockdown) was filtered. The filtered conditioned medium (CM) was mixed 1:1 with activated CD8+ T cells for culture. Recombinant mouse Ccl8 (10 ng/mL, 250–15, PeproTech, USA) or the CCR5 antagonist BMS-813160 (HY-101482, 10 μM, MedChemExpress, USA) was supplemented to the experimental group.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) by flow cytometry

After co-culture, the cells were first washed to discard the medium and impurities. To prevent non-specific binding, Fc receptor blocking (101,320, BioLegend) was conducted by pre-treating the cells for 10 min. Next, an antibody mixture was added for surface staining. The specific antibody combination included: anti-CD8α-APC/Cy7 (100,714, 1:200), anti-PD-1-PE (135,206, 1:100), and anti-TIM-3-FITC (119,704, 1:50) (all from BioLegend). After adding the antibody mixture, the cells were incubated at 4℃, avoiding light exposure, for 30 min. After staining, the cells were fixed to maintain the stability of the staining and reduce variation between cells. For further analysis of functional markers, a Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit (554,714, BD Biosciences, USA) was employed to permeabilize the cells, allowing the intracellular antibodies to enter. After permeabilization, antibodies were added: anti-TNF-α-PE/Cy7 (506,324, 1:50) and anti-IL-2-Alexa Fluor 647 (503,810, 1:50), followed by incubation at 4℃ for 30 min. Finally, data were collected using the BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (v10.8.1), evaluating immune marker expression in different subpopulations and further analyzing the functional status of CD8+ T cells.

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated utilizing TRIzol reagent (15,596,026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher) adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. After measuring the RNA concentration and purity, cDNA was generated utilizing the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan) through reverse transcription. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (4,367,659, Applied Biosystems, USA) on the QuantStudio 6 real-time PCR system. Primers were synthesized by Tsingke, with GAPDH serving as the internal loading. Relative expression was evaluated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Cells or tissues were lysed utilizing RIPA lysis buffer. The concentration of the extracted protein concentration was assessed utilizing the BCA method (23,225, Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were then transferred to a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) following SDS-PAGE separation. After blocking, the membrane was probed overnight with primary antibodies [anti-CBX6, anti-SMARCD1 (SWI/SNF related BAF chromatin remodeling complex subunit D1), anti-MCT4 (monocarboxylate transporter 4), and anti-CCL8 (C–C motif chemokine ligand 8)]at 4℃. The other day, the membrane was incubated with IgG for 1 h and developed using ECL reagent (32,106, Thermo Fisher). Images were acquired using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon 5200) and analyzed for grayscale values using Image J.

Immunoprecipitation liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and the lysates were sonicated. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. Samples were reduced with dithiothreitol (10 mM, 56℃, 30 min) and alkylated with iodoacetamide (20 mM, 25℃, dark, 30 min), followed by overnight digestion with Trypsin (V5280, Sigma) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio. After digestion, peptides were desalted and purified using a C18 solid-phase extraction column. The peptides were then dissolved in 0.1% formic acid solution and injected into the LC system (EASY-nLC 1200, Thermo Fisher). Separation was performed using an Acclaim PepMap C18 column (75 μm × 25 cm, 2 μm). The mobile phase was prepared with solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), applying a linear gradient from 5 to 35% solvent B over a 90-min period. The MS analysis was performed using the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) in positive ion mode. The full scan mass range was set from 350 to 1,500 m/z with a resolution of 120,000. LC/MS data were acquired using the HCD fragmentation mode with a exclusion time of 30 s. Raw data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer (v2.4) software against the UniProt database for identification and quantification, with a threshold set for FDR < 1%.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&TAG) sequencing

The CUT&TAG experiment was conducted utilizing the Hyperactive CUT&TAG Assay Kit (TD903, Vazyme, China) to analyze chromatin in mEC25 cells. First, 1 × 106 mEC25 cells were combined with Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads. These cells were subsequently probed with anti-CBX6 antibody (3 × Flag tag, F1804, 1:50, Sigma) followed by secondary antibody (anti-Rabbit IgG, 1:100). Next, pA-Tn5 transposase was supplemented, followed by 1 h incubation at 37℃ to ensure transposase binding to the target DNA. After DNA extraction, PCR amplification was carried out, and a library was constructed. The library was subsequently subjected to paired-end 150 bp sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

The ChIP experiment was employed to detect the binding of specific proteins to chromatin. First, the cross-linked cells were sonicated using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) to fragment the chromatin into 200–500 bp fragments. The cell lysates were the probed with specific antibodies (anti-CBX6, anti-SMARCD1, anti-H3K27ac, anti-H3K9ac, and anti-H3K4me3). After immunoprecipitation, the complexes were captured using magnetic beads. DNA was extracted, and the enrichment of Ccl8 or Slc16a3 promoter was analyzed using qPCR analysis.

Animal experiments

Approximately 5 × 106 differentially transfected mouse mEC25 cells (control or gene-modified group) were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS/Matrigel (1:1, 356237, Corning) mixture, and then the cell suspension was injected into the right inguinal region of BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, Anburui) or C57BL/6 mice. Starting from day 7, the volume of the tumor was measured every 3 d as follows: Volume = π/6 × length × width2. After day 21, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation. The tumor tissues were then processed as follows: A portion of the tumor tissue was immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis; another portion of tumor tissue was used to prepare single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis.

Besides, another animal model to analyze tumor growth and metastasis model was established through tail vein injection of 1 × 106 Luciferase-labeled mEC25 cells into C57BL/6. D-luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg, GoldBio, LUCK-1G) was injected intraperitoneally twice a week after the experiment began. Ten min after injection, lung metastasis fluorescent signals were quantitatively detected using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system. Exposure time was set between 1–60 s to ensure clear fluorescent signal images.

These mice were allocated into IgG control group: Intraperitoneal injection of rat IgG1 (10 mg/kg, BioXCell BE0088); anti-PD-1 group: Intraperitoneal injection of anti-PD-1 antibody RMP1-14 (10 mg/kg, BioXCell BE0146); CBX6 siRNA group: Intratumoral injection of 25 μg CBX6 siRNA (Dharmacon L-040708–01), with 25 μg injected per dose; Combination therapy group: Simultaneous anti-PD-1 and CBX6 siRNA treatment, each containing 5 mice. These regimens were applied once every 3 d starting from day 5.

Tissue microarray (TMA) and IHC

The human ESCC TMA consisted of 119 pairs of paraffin-embedded samples from patients with various stages, grades, and prognostic information. Tumor tissues from mice were obtained from the aforementioned animal models, fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned to 4 μm thickness. Sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking, the prepared sections were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-cleaved-caspase-3 [C-Cas-3] for mouse tumor tissues, and anti-CBX6, anti-SMARCD1, anti-PDL1, anti-CCL8, and anti-CCR5 for human TMAs) overnight at 4℃. On the following day, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody, followed by DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. After dehydration and sealing, the sections were observed under a microscope. The staining score was calculated using the H-score based on the percentage of positive staining or the staining intensity, and evaluated by two independent pathologists.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normally distributed data from a minimal of six independent experiments were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test (for two groups) or analysis of variance (for multiple groups, Tukey’s correction). Data are expressed as independent dots on graphs. Correlation analysis was carried out using Spearman’s rank test. Statistical software used: GraphPad Prism 9.0 and R 4.0.3. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

CBX6 is linked to dismal prognosis in ESCC patients and related to T dysfunction

First, we obtained from the TCGA-ESCA data that CBX6 was upregulated in ESCA tumor tissues (Fig S1A), and it correlated with unfavoable prognosis in patients. Patients with high CBX6 expression exhibited worse OS and PFS (Fig S1B-C). Further analysis revealed that CBX6 was inversely correlated with the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in ESCC patients (Fig S1D), while it positively linked to T dysfunction (Fig S1E-F). This suggests that CBX6 may contribute to T cell dysfunction, leading to impaired survival of ESCC patients.

To investigate CBX6's effects on ESCC progression, we induced Cbx6 knockdown in the mouse ESCC cell line mEC25 (Fig S2A-B). The Cbx6 silencing significantly reduced proliferation (Fig S2C) and migration (Fig S2D) of mEC25 cells in vitro. In the TME, immune-suppressive surface ligands on tumor or stromal cells bind to receptors on CD8+ T cells, thus inhibiting their anti-tumor functions. The reported single cell RNA data from GSE221561 and GSE154763 datasets revealed that CBX6 is expressed in both tumor cells and T cells (Fig S2E-F). We hypothesized that CBX6 may influence the downstream immune-suppressive marker expression, thus limiting T cell function. Notably, a significant reduction was detected in the expression of Tim3, Pdl1, and Gal9 in mEC25 cells with Cbx6 knockdown (Fig S2G). Subsequently, we extracted leukocytes from mouse thymus and had them exposed to aCD3/CD28. These induced CD8+ T cells were directly co-cultured (contact) with mEC25 cells at different ratios. Notably, the Cbx6 silencing in mEC25 cells enhanced the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells (Fig S2H-I).

To further confirm whether Cbx6 influences CD8+ T cell function, the phenotypes of T cells after co-culture with mEC25 cells were analyzed using FACS. Notably, the proportions of CD8+PD1+TIM3– (progenitor T exhausted, Tpex) and CD8+PD1+TIM3+ (Terminal T exhausted, Tex) cells were significantly increased after co-culture with mEC25 cells, which were reduced upon Cbx6 knockdown in cells (Fig. 1A). The numbers of TNFα+ and IL-2+ (immunoreactive) CD8+ T cells were significantly increased after the direct co-culture as well, which were further enhanced upon Cbx6 knockdown (Fig. 1B-C). In the TME, tumor cells release "don't eat me" signals by secreting cytokines or through ligand-receptor interactions. To confirm how CBX6 affects T cell function, we generated another non-contact co-culture system of T cells and mEC25 cells in Transwell systems (Fig. 1D). In this setting, the populations of Tpex and Tex were increased after the indirect co-culture with mEC25 cells but decreased following Cbx6 knockdown (Fig. 1E), showing consistence with the contact models. Furthermore, the CM from mEC25 cells was harvested to treat activated CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1F). Still, after CM treatment, Tex and Tpex numbers increased, and CM from Cbx6 knockdown cells also reduced the promoting effect of CM on T exhaustion (Fig. 1G). To study further, we performed RNA-seq to analyze changes in cytokine-related gene expression in mEC25 cells after Cbx6 knockdown (Fig. 1H). Among these, Il6, Ccl8, and Il10 were the focus of our attention. We added mouse recombinant proteins mIL6, mCCL8, mIL10, or control IgG to mEC25 cells with Cbx6 knockdown (Fig. 1I). Only the mCCL8 treatment markedly increased the populations of Tpex and Tex (Fig. 1J). Notably, these effects were abolished by the further treatment of BMS-813160, an antagonist for CCR5, a receptor for CCL8 (Fig. 1K-L). In the direct contact co-culture system of CD8+ T cells and Cbx6-kd mEC25 cells, the addition of mCCL8 reduced the populations of TNFα+ and IL-2+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1M-O) and increased proliferation of the co-cultured cancer cells (Fig. 1P). Discussion

Identifying key molecular cascades implicated in ESCC progression is a challenging but significant mission to develop promising novel therapeutic strategies. This study focuses on the impacts of CBX6 in ESCC and identifies that it exerts critical tumor promoting functions in this context by augmenting malignant behavior of tumor cells and inducing CD8+ T cell exhaustion immunosuppressive cascades.

Emerging studies have indicated the complex and significant roles of CBX proteins in human malignancies (Naqvi et al. 2023). For instance, among the eight CBX proteins, CBX3/8 were found to be upregulated while CBX1/6/7 were found to be decreased in ovarian cancer, with CBX1/2/3 found to be linked to worse OS and PFS in patients (Hu et al. 2022). CBX6 and CBX7 have been suggested as prognostic factors in bladder cancer, with CBX6 linking to immunosuppression and poor prognosis while CBX7 more likely linking to a favorable outcome (Li et al. 2023). These publications, along with others (Xie et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2022b), suggest that the expression patterns and functions among the CBX proteins can be vary and context-dependent. CBX8 was the first CBX protein whose roles have been investigated in ESCC, where it suppressed epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and dissemination of tumor cells by repressing Snail (Wang et al. 2017). Later, a bioinformatics-grounded investigation by Hou and colleagues suggested that CBX1, CBX3, and CBX6 were upregulated in ESCA, but only CBX1 showed a significant negative correlation with the survival of patients (Hou et al. 2022). Nevertheless, our bioinformatics analysis incorporating the TCGA-ECGA data suggested that CBX6 was highly expressed in ESCA and linking to worse OS and PFS in patients. Existing evidence has also highlighted the context-dependent expression and function of CBX6 in human cancers. For instance, it is downregulated in breast cancer (Deng et al. 2019) and lung cancer (Chen et al. 2023), while upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (Pan et al. 2022) and skin cutaneous melanoma (Li et al. 2020). Hou et al. demonstrated the close correlation between CBX6 and the infiltration of B cells, macrophages, and CD4+ T cells in the ESCA (Hou et al. 2022). Here, our analysis further revealed that CBX6 presented an inverse correlation with CTL infiltration while a positive association with T cell dysfunction, suggesting its potential role in immunosuppression in ESCA.

Previous literature has indicated that CBX6 exerts tumor suppressive functions in breast cancer by repressing the transcription of pluripotency genes (SOX2 and NANOG) (Wang et al. 2024a) and BST2 (Deng et al. 2019). By contrast, upregulation of CBX6 in hepatocellular carcinoma has been associated with dismal survival (Zheng et al. 2017). Our experiments showed that Cbx6 knockdown in mouse mEC25 cells reduced cell growth and migration in vitro. Notably, this Cbx6 knockdown led to a reduction in the population of Tpex and Tex in the co-cultured CD8+ T cells. Parallelly, the Cbx6 knockdown reduced tumorigenic activity of mEC25 cells in mice, and a higher tumor inhibition rate observed in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice than that in nude mice. This ample evidence suggests that potential involvement of immune reactivation cascade following Cbx6 knockdown. Li and colleagues reported that CBX6 induced an immunosuppressive TME in bladder cancer by reducing M1 macrophages and recruiting regulatory T cells (Li et al. 2023), both of which are known to induce T cell exhaustion by employing metabolic mechanisms and upregulating PD-1 levels (Farhood et al. 2019; Vilbois et al. 2024). In this study, among the genes identified by RNA-seq in Cbx6-kd cells, Ccl8 was identified as a significant target. The supplementation of mCCL8 to the co-culture systems restored the number of Tpex and Tex, effects negated by the antagonist of CCR5. The CCL8/CCR5 axis is known to recruit immunosuppressive cells to the TME, augmenting tumor cell growth and dissemination (Halvorsen et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2023). Therefore, the CCL8/CCR5 axis is, at least in part, implicated in the oncogenic events mediated by CBX6.

Further analysis of the dysregulated genes upon Cbx6 knockdown in mEC25 cells suggested the enrichment of glycolysis and energy metabolism pathways. The metabolic conditions within the TME drive T cell exhaustion. Cancer cells often exploit these metabolic shifts to fuel their accelerated growth and sustain their rapid proliferation (Boroughs and DeBerardinis 2015; Wu et al. 2021). They predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis, even when their mitochondria are functional, a process referred to as the Warburg effect (Wang et al. 2024b). These metabolically active cancer cells often surpass T cells in acquiring vital nutrients while also releasing byproducts like lactate into the surrounding space (Chang et al. 2015). Notably, we observed that the ECAR in cells, as well as lactate and ATP production in mEC25 cells was decreased upon Cbx6 knockdown. Importantly, these metabolic byproducts, particularly lactate, contribute to an acidic TME, which not only inhibits the growth of healthy neighboring cells but also facilitates tumor dissemination (Chen et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2016). Additionally, lactate accumulation can facilitate the differentiation of Tregs and other immunosuppressive cells. These cells secrete factors that suppress the function of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, further driving tumor progression (Scott and Cleveland 2016; Zhang et al. 2022a). The lactate transport is primarily governed by MCTs, specifically MCT1-4, which are encoded by SLC16 genes (Felmlee et al. 2020; Halestrap 2013). MCTs, particularly MCT1 and MCT4, have emerged as key contributors to tumor aggressiveness (Payen et al. 2020). Among the MCTs, we observed that MCT4 inhibition showed the greatest suppressive effect or T cell exhaustion in the co-culture system with mEC25 cells. As Slc16a3 was substantially reduced in cells with Cbx6 knockdown, we opine Slc16a3 (MCT4) as another downstream effector responsible for the lactate efflux and immunosuppression in ESCC.

Disruption in genomic stability has been well recognized as a major contributor to tumorigenesis (Dong et al. 2025; Rasteh et al. 2024). Furthermore, our LC/MS analysis revealed SMARCD1 as an important binding protein of CBX6. Interestingly, the SMARCD1 levels were found to be reduced in cells with Cbx6 knockdown. As PcG factors, CBX6 and other CBX proteins are known to modulate chromatin structure for epigenetic modifications (van Wijnen et al. 2021). In parallel, SMARCD1, a member of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, influences gene expression by altering chromatin structure at specific target loci (Park et al. 2006). The SMARCD proteins have been found to regulate transcription of androgen receptor-driven genes, significantly impacting many aspects of prostate cancer progression (Ertl et al. 2022). Nevertheless, the roles of these protein family in ESCC remain largely unknown. Notably, we observed that Smarcd1 knockdown led to a reduction in the enrichment of open chromatin and active transcription markers near the promoters of Ccl8 and Slc16a3 in mEC25 cells, thus repressing their transcription. Additionally, Smarcd1 overexpression in mEC25 cells with Cbx6 knockdown was found to restore the population of Tpex and Tex while reducing active CD8+ T cells after co-culture, effects negated by inhibition of either CCL8 or MCT4. This ample evidence suggests that CBX6 incorporates SMARCD1 to regulate Ccl8 and Slc16a3 transcription, leading to CD8+ T cell exhaustion and tumor development in ESCC.

For final verification, we observed that silencing of Cbx6 enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment in mice bearing ESCC tumors. Additionally, the TMA analysis identified the positive correlation between CBX6 and SMARCD1 in clinical ESCC samples, both of which showed correlations with the immunosuppressive phenotypes (PD-L1) and the downstream effectors (CCL8, MCT4, and CCR5). These factors were also linked to resistance to anti-PD-1 treatment and poorer patient prognosis.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from the GSE221561 and GSE154763 datasets, indicating that CBX6 is primarily expressed in both tumor cells and T cells. To investigate cytokine-related gene expression changes following Cbx6 knockdown in mEC25 cells, we performed bulk RNA-seq. While bulk sequencing is cost-effective and robust for detecting average gene expression changes across large cohorts, it can obscure intra-tumoral heterogeneity and rare cell states (Liu et al. 2024, b). Integrating bulk and single-cell RNA-seq data would combine the sensitivity of bulk sequencing for global expression patterns with the ability to identify subpopulations exhibiting specific gene expression that may contribute to tumor progression (Li and Liu 2022; Liu et al. 2025a, b). However, technique and financial constrains prevented us from performing such integrated sequencing in this study. Furthermore, while we demonstrate CBX6 upregulation in ESCC tissues and its association with poor prognosis, the mechanisms underlying its aberrant expression remain unclear. Previous studies have identified potential regulatory pathways for CBX6. In breast cancer, CBX6 expression is repressed by Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 via a Polycomb Repressive Complex 2-dependent mechanism (Deng et al. 2019). In hepatocellular carcinoma, CBX6 is upregulated through competitive binding of the long non-coding RNA MIR100HG to microRNA-146b-5p, which normally targets the 3ʹ-UTR of CBX6 mRNA (Li et al. 2021a). These findings suggest that CBX6 expression is dynamically regulated through chromatin modifications, RNA-mediated mechanisms, or transcription factor networks. Nonetheless, the present study focuses primarily on the functional role of CBX6 in ESCC progression, and its upstream regulators in this context remain to be elucidated. In addition, our experiments predominantly employed genetic silencing approaches. Selective small-molecule or antibody inhibitors of CBX6 are currently limited, representing a constraint for translational validation. Most animal studies were conducted using allograft models, and due to financial and time limitations, we were unable to incorporate more physiologically relevant models, such as N-Nitrosomethylbenzylamine-induced primary ESCC in mice. We would like to address these limitations in future investigations. Given the emergence of targeted therapies as a promising strategy for cancer management (Joshi et al. 2024), developing effective blocking agents for CBX6 may favor ESCC treatment.

In summary, this study, which integrates bioinformatics analysis with abundant animal and cellular models, provides strong evidence that the CBX6/SMARCD1 cascade plays significant roles in immune evasion and ESCC progression by promoting CCL8 and MCT4-mediated lactate release. Targeting this axis could offer a promising therapeutic strategy for ESCC, particularly when combined with immune-based therapies.
