Effects of dietary supplementation with astragalus polysaccharides on growth performance, serum parameters, and rumen microbial function of yaks
Authors: Guowu Yang, Wenbin He, Bin Ma, Yongfu La, Xiaoming Ma, Xiaoyun Wu, Min Chu, Minhui Zhao, Xuemei Liu, Yuansheng Zhao, Xian Guo, Lei Wang, Chunnian Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAstragalus polysaccharide (APS) has become a natural feed additive that has attracted much attention in animal husbandry due to its significant immunomodulatory activity, low toxicity and promotion of animal growth performance. In this study, in order to explore the effects of dietary APS on the growth performance and rumen microorganisms of yaks, 20 male yaks aged 2–3 years were selected and randomly divided into two groups: experimental group (1.0 g/kg APS were added to the diet, AG) and control group (no APS were added to the diet, CG), with 10 yaks in each group. After 60 days of continuous feeding, 5 animals were randomly selected from each group to collect rumen fluid. Comparative analysis of rumen microbial composition, function, and metabolites was conducted using metagenomic and metabolomic approaches.ResultsThe analysis of yak body weight index showed that the body weight of yak in the AG group was significantly higher than that in the CG group on the 60 th day (P < 0.05). In addition, APS significantly increased the immune indexes of IgA, IgM, IgG, IL-10, IGF1, GH, and NOS in yaks (P < 0.05). Metagenomic analysis showed that the beneficial bacteria (such as Euryarchaeota, Methanobrevibacter and Butyrivibrio) in the rumen of yaks were significantly increased after the addition of astragalus polysaccharides in the experimental group. Differential metabolites were significantly enriched in pathways such as Purine metabolism, Galactose metabolism and Tryptophan metabolism that may have a positive impact on the growth and development of yaks. This may promote the production of immune and growth-related metabolites by regulation, and ultimately enhance the immune function and growth performance of yak.ConclusionsThe results showed that the addition of APS could significantly improve the growth performance and immune function of yak. It can also optimize the rumen microbial community. It shows that polysaccharides such as APS can be used as effective substitutes for antibiotics and other drugs for long-term improvement of yak growth performance and rumen health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04581-5.

Introduction

Yaks (Bos grunniens) are primarily distributed across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and its adjacent regions. Yak meat, renowned for its distinctive flavor and nutritional value, holds a unique position among meat products consumed in China’s plateau regions, serving as a key source of high-quality animal protein for local populations [1]. However, as the yak industry develops, a series of challenges and issues have gradually emerged. Traditional yak farming methods largely relied on natural grazing. Yet, with the rise of intensive and large-scale farming models, while production efficiency and economic benefits have improved, new problems have also arisen.

In the high-density breeding environment, the disease resistance of yaks has decreased, the product quality is uneven, and it is vulnerable to stress. These problems not only affect the health and production performance of yaks, but also restrict the sustainable development of yak breeding industry. In order to cope with these challenges, veterinary antibiotics have played a mitigating role to some extent. However, the abuse of antibiotics has brought potential threats to human health, such as enhanced drug resistance and drug residues [2]. Therefore, the ban on antibiotics issued by China makes it urgent to find alternatives to antibiotics. In this context, plant-derived feed additives and their extracts have attracted much attention due to their multiple advantages such as pure nature, no pollution, no residue, low toxic and side effects, and promotion of growth, and have become one of the ideal green substitutes [3]. Astragalus, a time-honored traditional Chinese medicinal herb, boasts abundant yields and superior quality in regions such as Gansu Province, China [4, 5]. Astragalus polysaccharides (APS) is a main active ingredient extracted from Astragalus membranaceus, which has many biological activities such as anti-oxidation, anti-inflammation, immune regulation, anti-virus and anti-tumor activity [6]. Studies have shown that APS can improve animal growth performance and immunity by stimulating the growth of beneficial bacteria in the intestine and inhibiting the reproduction of harmful bacteria [7]. At the same time, it can also promote the development of various tissues and organs, especially the spleen and intestinal mucosa [8, 9]. In addition, APS can also enhance the physical fitness and antioxidant capacity of yak [10, 11], and has a positive effect on meat quality, which can increase fat and protein deposition in muscle and improve meat quality and nutritional value [12]. Therefore, this experiment explored the effect of adding APS to the diet on improving the production performance of yaks and regulating the rumen microbial community, laying a scientific foundation for the application of APS in yak breeding and promoting the healthy and sustainable development of yak breeding industry.

As an efficient immune enhancer, APS can significantly improve the functional indicators of immune organs, promote the release of cytokines, accelerate the proliferation and differentiation of immune cells, and have a positive impact on the secretion of immunoglobulin and immune signal transmission [13]. Studies have shown that APS can regulate the activity of immune cells (macrophages, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and microglia) and immune organs (spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow, thymus, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue), and promote the release of immune active substances (such as IgA, IgG, and IgM) [14–17]. Adding APS can significantly improve the production performance of cattle. Adding APS to cattle feed can effectively reduce the incidence of rumen diseases and improve the rumen health of cattle [18]. The study also found that adding APS and Astragalus membranaceus root (AMT) to the diet enhanced antioxidant capacity and altered rumen fermentation patterns in lambs. Furthermore, supplementing the diet with 15 g/kg APS increased dry matter intake in lambs [19]. APS can promote the colonization of dominant flora in the rumen tract of cattle, and inhibit the reproduction of pathogenic flora, thus maintaining the diversity and balance of rumen flora [20]. This effect not only improves the proliferation ability of beneficial bacteria, but also further promotes the health status of the intestine, improves the physique and immunity of cattle, and provides strong support for its growth and development.

In this context, the development of green feed additives with both immunomodulatory and growth-promoting functions has become an important breakthrough in balancing intensive breeding efficiency and animal health. Moreover, more and more studies have shown that polysaccharides such as APS can regulate the physiological activities of mammalian hosts, and by regulating the rumen flora, it can not only enhance the immune function of the body, but also delay the development of the disease [21]. However, there are few reports on the effects of adding APS to the basic feed of yak on its production performance, rumen microbial community and the interaction between its metabolites. Therefore, in this study, metagenomics and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) non-targeted metabolomics techniques were used to systematically explore the effects of dietary supplementation of APS on rumen microbial community structure, functional characteristics and metabolites of yaks, so as to lay a foundation for clarifying the effects of dietary supplementation of APS on yak growth and rumen microbial.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and management

The test samples were collected from Liqiaru Livestock Farm in Luqu County, Gannan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Gansu Province (geographical coordinates: latitude 34°20’~34°58’, longitude 102°13’~102°46’). In this experiment, 10 healthy male yaks aged 2 years were selected as experimental animals. The test material was Astragalus Polysaccharides (APS) concentrated powder, which was provided by an animal health technology Co., Ltd., and its effective component content was detected to be 70%. Using R software (v.4.1.2), 20 yaks were randomly divided into two groups, 10 yaks in each group. The basal diet was a total mixed ration (TMR) with a concentrate-to-roughage ratio of 1:3 (supplementary material S1). The control group (CG) was fed a basal diet, and the experimental group (AG) was supplemented with 1.0 g/kg Astragalus polysaccharide in the concentrate of the basal diet. During the whole experiment, feeding was carried out at 08:00 and 16:00 every day, drinking water was free, and routine feeding management was carried out, including regular disinfection and cleaning of the enclosure. The experiment was divided into pre-test stage (15 days) and formal test stage (60 days). Before the test was officially carried out, the initial body weight of all test yaks was measured. During the experiment, the cattle were weighed at the end of each month (Weighing dates were August 26, 2024; September 24, 2024; and October 23, 2024). All measurements were completed in fasting state before morning feeding. At the end of the experiment, five yaks were randomly selected from each group to collect rumen fluid and blood samples, and stored and brought back to the laboratory for use.

Determination of immune performance

Blood samples were centrifuged at 4 °C, 2000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected for the detection of IgA, IgM, IgG, IL-10, IGF1, GH, NOS, and TP. The experimental operation strictly followed the instructions of biochemical kit or ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng and ELK Biotechnology).

DNA extraction, sequencing, and library construction

Total DNA was extracted from microbial samples in rumen fluid using the QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). This kit employs silica membrane spin-column technology to effectively remove PCR inhibitors while preserving high-purity DNA. Extracted products were quantified for concentration using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and simultaneously analyzed for integrity via agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA quality was verified through dual assessment of the A260/A280 ratio and band clarity. Subsequent processing involved DNA fragmentation using a Covaris S220 focused ultrasonicator (USA). For purification, Beckman Coulter’s Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads were employed. Based on the principle of solid-phase reversible immobilization, this method efficiently removed impurities and small DNA fragments. Finally, sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA).

The library sequencing used the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform to generate a 150 bp paired terminal read sequence. Fastp (v.0.20.1) software [22] was used to preprocess the original data, including removing the adaptor sequence and filtering the low-quality bases. Then bbMap (v.38.93-0.93) software [23] was used to compare the quality-controlled data to the host genome sequence and filter it. The splicing software MEGAHIT (v.1.2.9) [24] based on the principle of De-Brujin graph was used to splice and assemble the optimized sequences. According to the overlap relationship between kmers, the De-Brujin graph was constructed to obtain contigs. Contigs of more than 500 bp were screened for statistics and subsequent analysis. The ORF of the assembled Contigs sequence was predicted using Prodigal (v.2.6.3) software [25] and translated into an amino acid sequence. The ORF prediction results of each sample and mixed assembly were de-redundant by MMSeqs2 (v.13.45111) software to obtain non-redundant initial Unigene (non-redundant continuous gene-encoded nucleic acid sequence). Clustering was performed with 95% homology and 90% coverage, and the longest sequence was selected as the representative sequence. Using salmon (v.1.8.0) software, the clean reads of each sample were compared with non-redundant gene sets, and the abundance information of genes in the corresponding samples was counted. The genes with less than 2 reads in all samples were removed to obtain the final Unigene. Based on the number of reads and gene length on the alignment, the abundance information of each gene in each sample was calculated.

Bioinformatics analysis

Based on the taxonomic information of NR database, the abundance of species in each sample was calculated at each taxonomic level, such as Domain, Species, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus and Species, so as to construct the abundance spectrum at the corresponding taxonomic level. The carbohydrate active enzyme (CAZy) annotation information was obtained by comparing the hmmscan (v.3.1) tool with the CAZy database, and the enzyme abundance was calculated by the sum of the corresponding gene abundance. R (v.4.1.2) was used for multivariate statistical analysis and visualization, including principal component analysis (PCA), principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA), Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS), etc. At the same time, variance analysis (ANOVA), Kruskal-Wallis test, T test and Wilcoxon signed rank test were used to test the hypothesis of species abundance data between groups to obtain p value.

LC-MS metabolomics sequencing and analysis

The metabolomics detection platform was an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-Fourier transform mass spectrometry system (UHPLC, Q-Exactive HF-X, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for metabolomics analysis. The specific operation was as follows: 200 µL biological samples were accurately transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Add 800 µL pre-cooled extract (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v), vortex shake for 30 s and mix well. Then ultrasonic extraction was carried out at 40 kHz for 30 min at 5 °C. After sedimentation at-20 °C for 30 min, centrifuge at 4 °C, 13,000 g for 15 min (Eppendorf 5430R centrifuge). The supernatant was thoroughly dried by a nitrogen purge instrument (JXDC-20, Shanghai, China). A 120 µL acetonitrile-water (1:1, v/v) complex solution was added and ultrasonically treated at 5 °C for 5 min to fully dissolve. The supernatant was transferred to a special injection bottle after centrifugation for 10 min under the same conditions, and 20 µL mixed quality control sample (QC) was prepared. The injection volume was set to 3 µL, which was separated by HSS T3 chromatographic column (100 mm × 2.1 mm inner diameter, 1.8 μm) and detected by mass spectrometry. The mobile phase configuration was as follows: A mobile phase was composed of 95% water and 5% acetonitrile, containing 0.1% formic acid; b mobile phase contained 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol and 5% water, and added 0.1% formic acid. The positive and negative ion dual mode switching was used to collect data, and the spray voltage was set to ± 3500 V; the mass scan range is m/z 70–1050 with a resolution of 70,000 FWHM (measured at m/z 200). In terms of quality control, one quality control sample was inserted after each analysis of 5 to 15 samples for system performance monitoring. The original data of metabolomics were systematically processed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) platform, and finally a high-dimensional data matrix containing three-dimensional information of chromatographic retention time, molecular mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and ion peak intensity was constructed. First, the eigenvalues with missing values exceeding 20% in the group are removed, and the minimum values are used to fill the remaining missing values. The data are normalized and numerically summed. The QC metabolite characteristic samples with RSD ≤ 30% were retained. The filtered data were statistically analyzed after log10 conversion processing. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was further applied and a permutation test was performed to enhance the reliability of the results. In order to reveal the effect of dietary APS on metabolites, the differentially expressed metabolites were identified and verified by KEGG database.

Relevance analysis

The differential metabolites with VIP value > 2 and p < 0.05 were screened out. Based on the Bray-Curtis distance analysis, the dominant microorganisms, differential microbial genera and serum parameters (IgA, IgM, IgG, IL-10, IGF1, GH, NOS, TP) were analyzed by Spearman correlation analysis, and the correlation was visualized by cluster heat map.

Statistical analysis

Excel was used to summarize the data of yak body weight and serum parameters, and then SPSS25.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform independent sample T test on all data. The results are expressed as mean ± standard error. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Effects of APS on growth performance in yak

The results showed that there was no significant difference in body weight between the CG group and the AG group on the 30 th day (P > 0.05). On the 60 th day, there was a significant difference in body weight between the CG group and the AG group (P < 0.05). The average daily gain of yaks in the AG group was 0.53 kg, and the average daily gain of yaks in the CG group was 0.35 kg. The AG group was significantly higher than the CG group (P < 0.05). The results showed that the addition of APS in the diet significantly improved the growth performance of yak (Table 1).

Analysis of metagenomic data

To evaluate the effects of Astragalus polysaccharides on rumen microbiota in yak, we analyzed 10 samples via metagenomic sequencing (Supplementary Material S2). We obtained an average of 79,765,742.8 and 79,137,309.6 raw reads from the control and experimental groups, respectively. After quality control and removal of host genomic sequences, a final average of 79,735,633.6 and 79,100,826.4 cleaned reads were obtained from the control and experimental groups, respectively, with Q30 coverage exceeding 96.35%. After quality control of the sequences, we performed de novo assembly on the retained data to obtain more effective genomic information. An average of 317,226.6 and 491,599 contiguous sequences were obtained in the control and experimental groups, respectively, with GC contents of 45.36% and 44.52%. The average N50 and N90 lengths in the samples were 1669.5 bp and 594.5 bp, respectively. Gene prediction revealed 2,646,552 and 3,928,456 open reading frames (ORFs) in the control and experimental groups, respectively.

Effects of APS on the diversity of bacterial communities in the rumen of yak

In this study, the Alpha diversity of rumen microbial communities in CG group and AG group yaks was analyzed (Fig. 1A). The results showed that there was no significant difference in ACE index, Chao1 index, shannon index and Simpson index between CG group and AG group (P > 0.05). However, the shannon index and Simpson index of AG group were higher than those of CG group. Alpha diversity difference analysis showed that the species richness and community diversity of the two groups of samples were basically at a similar level. The β-diversity analysis of rumen microbiota revealed intra-group clustering and inter-group separation at the genus and phylum levels through PCA analysis (Fig. 1B and C). Points corresponding to CG group and AG group were relatively close, particularly at the phylum level, indicating similar rumen microbial species compositions between the two groups. Further validation at the genus and phylum levels was performed using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), a method that simplifies multidimensional data while preserving the original relationships between objects. The analysis results showed good clustering effects (Fig. 1D and E), enabling subsequent analysis. CAZyme composition

Rumen microbes produce diverse complex carbohydrate-degrading enzymes (CAZymes). Obtaining annotation information for CAZymes genes is crucial for elucidating the carbohydrate metabolism mechanisms of the yak microbiota under APS. This study identified a total of 207,927 CAZymes genes (Fig. 3A). These included 112,143 glycoside hydrolases (GHs), 57,592 glycosyltransferases (GTs), 5,042 polysaccharide lyases (PLs), 23,107 carbohydrate esterases (CEs), 430 auxiliary activities (AAs), and 9,613 carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). Differential analysis revealed (Fig. 3B) that GTs and PLs in AG group were significantly lower than in CG group (P < 0.05), while GHs, CEs, CBMs, and AAs showed no significant difference (P > 0.05). Differential functional heatmap analysis identified CAZyme families with significant functional differences based on p-value screening. The top 30 most abundant CAZyme families were visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 3C). Rumen fluid metabolomics analysis

To evaluate the effects of APS on yak rumen microbiota, this study subsequently performed metabolomic analysis on rumen fluid. First, supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was employed to analyze differences in metabolites between the AG group and CG group groups. Analysis revealed that individual samples from each group clustered closely together, indicating clear separation of metabolites between the two groups (Fig. 4A). Notably, in the OPLS-DA validation model, the regression line exhibited an upward trend, and the Q2 regression line had a negative intercept on the y-axis. This parameter characteristic reflects the model’s high stability and reliability, indicating no overfitting occurred (Fig. 4B). A total of 4,167 metabolites were identified through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics analysis. Analysis revealed 546 differentially expressed metabolites (230 cations and 316 anions), with 181 metabolites significantly upregulated and 365 metabolites significantly downregulated (Fig. 4C). To visually represent sample relationships and expression differences across samples, hierarchical clustering was performed on the top 50 most significantly differentially expressed metabolites ranked by p-value (Fig. 4D). Results revealed significant enrichment of these metabolites in metabolic pathways such as Purine metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism, Galactose metabolism, and Linoleic acid metabolism, as well as in the GnRH and cGMP-PKG signaling pathways (Fig. 4E). Correlation analysis of microorganisms and serum parameters with metabolites

The rumen is a unique organ in ruminant animals such as cattle, yak, and sheep, primarily serving as a storage site for ingested feed and the main location for microbial fermentation. Research indicates that approximately 55%–60% of rumen fluid metabolites are associated with the rumen microbial community [26]. Therefore, this study conducted correlation analyses between differential metabolites in rumen fluid and differential bacterial genera, as well as serum biochemical indicators. First, Spearman correlation coefficients were used to construct a correlation heatmap, analyzing the relationship between dominant differential bacterial genera and differential metabolites (Fig. 5A). Results revealed that bacterial genera such as Robinsoniella, Exiguobacterium, and Eggerthella were positively correlated with metabolites including 10-Deoxymethymycin, N-Hexanoylsphingosine, PGF2alpha-EA(d4), and Blumealactone B, while negatively correlated with Allantoin, 1-Isopropyl citrate, and Alendronic acid. Discussion

Growth data revealed that AG group yaks exhibited significantly higher body weights than CG group yaks at 60 days (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the experimental group showed significantly higher ADG than the control group (P < 0.05). The improvement of the growth performance of yaks by adding APS in the diet may be due to the positive effect of APS on the rumen health and immune system of yaks, thereby improving the growth performance of yaks. Previous studies have shown that adding APS to the basal diet can improve production performance and gut microbiota in weaned lambs and chickens [19, 27, 28], consistent with the findings of this study. Qiao et al.‘s. investigated the effects of APS supplementation in a basal diet on broiler growth performance, rumen health, and gut microbiota composition. They found that APS supplementation significantly increased average daily gain in broilers while also elevating immunoglobulin (IgA, IgG, IgM) levels [29]. Immunological parameter measurements in yak serum from this study indicate that APS significantly elevated immunoglobulin levels (P < 0.05), consistent with Qiao et al.‘s findings.

Metagenomic analysis revealed that Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, and Spirochaetota were the dominant bacterial phyla in the rumen. Bacteroidota primarily decompose dietary proteins and various carbohydrates [30], while Bacillota mainly degrade fiber [31]. Differential comparison analysis of the two groups at the phylum level revealed that Euryarchaeota was significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group (P < 0.05). Studies indicate that polysaccharides, such as those derived from Astragalus membranaceus, formed by the polymerization of various monosaccharides, can serve as carbon sources for rumen microorganisms [32]. It is speculated that methanogens within Euryarchaeota exhibit preferences for specific carbon sources, with astragalus polysaccharides and their degradation products potentially serving as high-quality carbon sources, providing energy and material foundations for growth. The increased abundance of Euryarchaeota contributes to enhanced rumen fermentation efficiency and increased short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, thereby supplying more energy to the host. Methanogens within Euryarchaeota produce methane by consuming hydrogen and carbon dioxide, thereby helping maintain stable rumen pH and preventing acidosis [33]. This suggests that APS not only alters the abundance of rumen bacterial phyla but also provides abundant carbon sources, regulates the rumen environment, and enhances microbial synergistic effects, ultimately improving rumen fermentation efficiency and host health. Pseudomonadota abundance was significantly lower in the AG group than in the CG group (P < 0.05). Research indicates that APS exhibits antibacterial effects against certain bacteria, particularly Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a common pseudomonad species [34]. At the genus level, the abundance of Methanobrevibacter and Butyrivibrio in the AG group was significantly higher than that in the CG group. Among these, Butyrivibrio is a key butyrate-producing bacterium commonly found in the rumen [35], and butyrate can reduce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [36]. Furthermore, certain strains of Butyrivibrio primarily function in the rumen by degrading cellulose [37], thereby releasing sugars from cellulose that can be utilized by yak as an energy source. Furthermore, Butyrivibrio participates in fatty acid biohydrogenation processes [38], and its increased abundance may contribute to enhancement fat metabolism in yak. This suggests that APS plays a significant role in yak digestive function. Methanobacterium, Methanobrevibacter, Methanomicrobium, and Methanosarcina are four widely prevalent methanogenic bacteria in the rumen of ruminants, with Methanobrevibacter being the most dominant genus [39]. Research indicates that Methanobrevibacter is the primary methanogen in the rumen, capable of utilizing hydrogen and carbon dioxide to produce methane [40]. Its increased abundance may indicate enhanced hydrogen utilization efficiency in the rumen, thereby reducing hydrogen accumulation and helping maintain redox balance. This may be related to the immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory bioactivities of APS, as well as its regulation of the rumen fermentation environment, suggesting that APS alters the microbial community structure in the rumen. Prevotella exhibited lower relative abundance in the AG group compared to the CG group. Research indicates that Prevotella primarily participates in the fermentation and utilization of complex polysaccharides within the rumen [41]. It is hypothesized that APS supplementation may have altered the animals’ pathways for nutrient absorption and utilization, reducing the availability of substrates fermentable by Prevotella in the rumen and consequently decreasing its abundance. This change may contribute to improved feed utilization and enhanced growth performance in animals. Bacteroidota and Firmicutes jointly play crucial roles in degrading cellulose and other complex carbohydrates [42]. For instance, Prevotella within Bacteroidota efficiently degrades starch and xylopolysaccharides while participating in protein and peptide metabolism [43]. These bacteria convert plant proteins and non-protein nitrogen into microbial protein for host utilization. Bacteroidota and other bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate during rumen fermentation. These SCFAs serve as one of the primary energy sources for ruminants.

The hydrolysis of plant polysaccharides is primarily accomplished by carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) secreted by microorganisms, which catalyze the degradation and assembly of polysaccharides [44]. PLs, CEs, and GHs are primarily responsible for polysaccharide breakdown, while GTs mainly participate in glycosidic bond formation, thereby contributing to the biosynthesis of complex carbohydrates [45]. Additionally, GTs and PLs regulate extracellular polysaccharide synthesis [46, 47]. In this experiment, the GHs family genes exhibited the highest expression levels, accounting for over 50% of the total. Research indicates that genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes in rumen microbes play a crucial role in the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose in ruminants. Astragalus polysaccharides may indirectly influence the abundance and expression of these functional genes by modulating the rumen microbial community [48]. Heatmap analysis revealed that the expression abundance of the top 30 CAZymes families was significantly lower in AG group than in CG group. Studies indicate that GT2 family enzymes participate in the synthesis and modification of polysaccharides, which may serve as components of the microbial cell wall itself or be utilized as metabolites by other microorganisms [49]. High GT2 gene abundance may correlate with rumen microbes’ efficient metabolism of complex carbohydrates (e.g., cellulose, hemicellulose), thereby enhancing rumen short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production to supply energy for both microbes and host animals [50]. Furthermore, high GT2 gene abundance correlates with microbial community stability and diversity [51]. These findings indicate that APS feeding influences carbohydrate enzyme gene abundance and activity, thereby regulating growth performance and immune response.

This study subsequently performed metabolomic analysis on rumen fluid. A total of 4,167 metabolites were identified through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics analysis. Analysis revealed 546 differentially expressed metabolites (230 cations and 316 anions). KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on these differential metabolites. Results showed significant enrichment of differential metabolites in metabolic pathways including Purine metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism, Galactose metabolism, and Linoleic acid metabolism. Simultaneously, it exhibits significant enrichment in the GnRH and cGMP-PKG signaling pathways. Among these, the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway plays a crucial role in animal growth and development, including regulating vasodilation and vasoconstriction, controlling cardiovascular development, modulating bone development, influencing muscle development, and regulating energy metabolism [52–54]. Furthermore, this pathway was found to play a crucial role in cardioprotection by monitoring cell death and maintaining intracellular acidosis [55], and it is also associated with antiviral effects [56]. In metabolic pathway research, pathway analysis is crucial for understanding physiological processes, disease mechanisms, and environmental influences in organisms [57]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that metabolic pathway alterations are closely linked to growth, development, and stress responses in organisms [58–60]. Galactose metabolism enables the conversion of galactose within organisms into energy or other compounds through various enzymes [61]. The synthesis and metabolism of glycolipids are crucial for cell membrane integrity and function, potentially indirectly influencing cell growth and development by regulating membrane permeability and stability [62]. Substances like adenine in the purine metabolism pathway participate in nucleic acid synthesis and metabolism, closely linked to energy metabolism and signal transduction. Nucleic acid synthesis is intrinsically related to cell proliferation, which forms the foundation of animal growth. At the cellular level, variations in adenine content influence cell proliferation and differentiation, thereby affecting tissue and organ growth and development [63]. Astragalus polysaccharides elevate metabolites associated with the rumen purine metabolism pathway in yak, potentially exerting positive effects on yak growth and development.

To gain a deeper understanding of the association between rumen microbiota, metabolites, and serum immune markers, we conducted a combined analysis. We found that the bacterial genera Prevotella and Segatella were significantly positively correlated with metabolites including 2,8-Quinolinediol, Atrinositol, Allantoin, 3-Methyl-1-butene, Alendronic acid, 2,4-Quinolinediol, and 4,1-Benzoxazepine. Microbial groups such as Exiguobacterium and Eggerthella showed significant positive correlations with metabolites including 10-Deoxymethymycin, N-Hexanoylsphingosine, and Blumealactone B. Prevotella is a dominant rumen bacterium that utilizes starch and protein to produce succinate and acetate. Furthermore, studies indicate that Exiguobacterium produces antimicrobial compounds exhibiting the broadest antibacterial spectrum [64]. Notably, the metabolite 10-Deoxymethymycin, positively correlated with Exiguobacterium, is also associated with antimicrobial activity [65]. Finally, an association analysis was conducted between serum biochemical indicators and differentially expressed metabolites. We found that the metabolite 10-Deoxymethymycin also showed a significant positive correlation with the immune markers IgA, IgM, and IgG.

Conclusions

The results showed that the addition of APS could significantly improve the growth performance and immune function of yak. It can also optimize the rumen microbial community. The abundance of Methanobrevibacter and Butyrivibrio, which are related to the functions of fiber degradation, anti-inflammatory and immune regulation, was significantly increased in rumen microorganisms of AG group yak. APS can also regulate metabolic pathways such as Purine metabolism, which are closely related to the growth, development and stress response of yak. Therefore, we speculate that the addition of APS in the diet can not only promote the growth of yak, change its rumen flora structure and improve digestive function, but also regulate the functional gene abundance of rumen microorganisms through various mechanisms, optimize the metabolism and immune function of microbial community, and have a positive impact on host health and nutrient absorption.
