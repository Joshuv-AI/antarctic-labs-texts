Integrated analysis reveals survival-related genes promoting cholangiocarcinoma progression via G2/M cell cycle, with validation of cyclin B1 in tumor proliferation
Authors: Qingliang Jiang, Xianglin Liu, Weichen Yu, Yangyang Li, Jiaqi Du, Xiaoqing Jiang, Hengyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) progression involves dysregulated cell cycle control, but core regulatory networks and their roles in tumor heterogeneity remain elusive. We integrated bulk transcriptomics (TCGA-CHOL and GEO cohorts) and single-cell RNA-seq (GSE138709) to identify survival-related genes (SRGs) driving CCA. Through limma, WGCNA, and PPI analysis, we identified seven SRGs (CDK1, ASPM, CCNB1, KIF2C, TOP2A, BUB1, DLGAP5) enriched in G2/M-phase pathways. Single-cell analysis revealed specific SRG upregulation in malignant epithelial subpopulations (PHGR1-Epi/UBE2C-Epi), with pseudotime trajectories demonstrating dynamically increased SRG expression during dedifferentiation. Functional validation confirmed CCNB1 as a core regulator: siRNA knockdown in HuCCT1/RBE cells significantly suppressed proliferation and clonogenicity. This study is the first to define a G2/M-phase gene module promoting CCA progression and map its single-cell dynamics, highlighting CCNB1 as a therapeutic target. Our results reveal SRGs as a coordinated oncogenic network underlying tumor aggressiveness, providing mechanistic insights for future SRG-targeted therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25299-4.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), originating from biliary epithelial cells, is a highly aggressive malignant tumor. The global incidence of CCA continues to rise, and the five-year survival rate remains below 20% for advanced-stage disease, making it the second most common malignancy of the hepatobiliary system1. Although surgical resection combined with chemoradiotherapy remains the primary treatment modality, more than 60% of patients are ineligible for surgery at diagnosis due to marked tumor heterogeneity, difficulty in early diagnosis, and frequent chemoresistance2. Recent studies have highlighted dysregulated cell-cycle control as a central mechanism driving CCA progression, with G2/M checkpoint disruption leading to genomic instability and enhanced tumor cell proliferation and metastasis3. Nevertheless, the key molecular regulators of the G2/M checkpoint in CCA and their dynamic associations with patient prognosis have not been systematically elucidated, significantly hindering the development of effective targeted therapies.

Traditional research has predominantly relied on bulk transcriptome data analysis or in vitro functional validation of individual genes, making it difficult to elucidate cell subset-specific regulatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Although cell cycle-related genes (e.g., CDKs, Cyclins families) have been widely reported in various solid tumors4,5, significant gaps remain in understanding their spatiotemporal expression patterns, molecular interaction networks, and clinical translational value in CCA. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology offers a novel perspective for resolving tumor heterogeneity. However, existing studies have yet to systematically integrate multi-omics data, resulting in the failure to precisely identify core gene clusters driving malignant progression and their associated regulatory pathways. Therefore, a critical scientific challenge in the field is to: By integrating cross-scale analysis (from population-level gene modules to single-cell dynamics), uncover key regulatory hubs at the G2/M checkpoint in CCA and validate their tumor-promoting mechanisms.

This study innovatively integrates Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), scRNA-seq, and functional experimental validation to establish, for the first time, a multi-omics molecular network governing the G2/M phase checkpoint in CCA. Through systematic screening, we identified 7 survival-related genes (SRGs: CDK1, ASPM, CCNB1, KIF2C, TOP2A, BUB1, DLGAP5). At the single-cell level, these SRGs were specifically enriched within the malignant epithelial subpopulations PHGR1-Epi and UBE2C-Epi. Pseudotime trajectory analysis further confirmed that the expression of these genes dynamically increased concomitantly with tumor cell dedifferentiation. Subsequent experimental validation demonstrated that knockdown of CCNB1 significantly inhibited CCA cell proliferation and clonogenic ability. This work not only provides a novel diagnostic biomarker panel for CCA but also reveals potential intervention nodes for cell cycle-targeted therapy from a multi-omics perspective, thereby providing a theoretical basis for overcoming current clinical therapeutic bottlenecks.

Methods

Dataset collection

This study systematically elucidates the molecular characteristics of CCA by integrating multi-omics data resources. Data were sourced from two authoritative bioinformatics databases: (1) the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) for transcriptome sequencing datasets (GSE762976, GSE265667) and scRNA-seq data (GSE1387098, GSE1718999); (2) The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) for standardized omics data from the TCGA-CHOL cohort. The specific characteristics of each dataset are as follows: GSE76297 contains 90 pairs of pathologically validated CCA and matched adjacent normal tissues, providing an ideal model for differential gene expression analysis; GSE26566 encompasses 104 primary tumor samples and 6 normal bile duct tissues, demonstrating significant advantages in clinical sample size; and the TCGA-CHOL cohort integrates multidimensional gene expression profiles from 35 tumor and 9 normal tissues, ensuring standardized data quality and reproducibility. For prognostic assessment of the top 10 critical candidate genes, the study additionally incorporated survival analyses using 169 tumor samples from the GSE24480710 cohort. Notably, the single-cell sequencing dataset GSE138709 includes 5 tumor and 3 matched adjacent normal tissue samples obtained from 5 patients, offering single-cell-level validation to support the elucidation of key molecular mechanisms.

Identification and analysis of DEGs

To identify DEGs between CCA and normal tissues, we performed differential expression analysis on the GSE76297 dataset, which comprises 90 pairs of CCA and matched normal tissue samples. The analysis was conducted using the limma package (v3.60.6) in R (v4.4.1)11. A mixed-effects model incorporating both group (tumor vs. normal) and individual covariates was constructed to eliminate inter-individual variability. The statistical significance of gene expression differences was adjusted using the empirical Bayes method. DEGs were filtered based on dual criteria: a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1. The ggplot2 package (v3.5.1)12 was employed to generate a volcano plot for visualizing the distribution of DEGs, while hierarchical clustering of gene expression profiles was illustrated using a heatmap constructed with the pheatmap package (v1.0.12)13.

WGCNA

To identify key gene modules associated with CCA progression, we performed WGCNA on the DEGs identified from the GSE76297 dataset. The analysis was conducted using the WGCNA package in R (v1.73)14,15. A soft-thresholding power of 6 was selected based on the criterion that the scale-free topology model fit index exceeds 0.9 while maintaining moderate mean connectivity. Using this threshold, a topological overlap matrix (TOM) was constructed, followed by hierarchical clustering to identify co-expression gene modules. Module detection was performed using the Dynamic Tree Cut algorithm, and modules with a similarity threshold below 0.25 were merged. Pearson correlation analysis between module eigengenes (MEs) and clinical traits was then conducted to determine the most CCA-associated module, identifying the “MEblue” as the key module of interest.

Functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the potential biological functions of key genes and co-expression modules, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.12.6)16–19. A Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05 was set as the significance threshold. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (v4.3.3) was performed on the top 10,000 DEGs to explore enrichment patterns across biological pathways, with enrichment curves used to depict the functional tendencies of gene expression changes. The results of multi-dimensional enrichment analyses were further visualized, integrated, and annotated using GraphPad Prism software (v9.0.0).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis and identification of SRGs

To further identify potential core regulatory factors, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (v12.0; https://string-db.org/), applying a combined interaction score threshold > 0.9 (highest confidence level). Cytoscape software (v3.10.2) was used for network topology analysis, and the top 10 hub genes were selected based on topological parameters such as node degree. Subsequently, survival data from the GSE244807 cohort were integrated, and univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (p < 0.05) was performed to identify genes significantly associated with patient prognosis. As a result, seven SRGs were identified: CDK1, ASPM, CCNB1, KIF2C, TOP2A, BUB1, and DLGAP5.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

To uncover the cell-specific distribution and dynamic expression patterns of SRGs, we analyzed the publicly available scRNA-seq dataset GSE138709, following standardized single-cell analysis workflows. During preprocessing, we performed stringent quality control using the Seurat package (v4.4.0), removing low-quality cells with mitochondrial gene content > 20% and cells expressing fewer than 200 genes. Log-normalization and identification of the top 2,000 highly variable genes were applied to optimize data features. To correct for batch effects across samples, the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.0) was used. After nonlinear dimensionality reduction via UMAP, six cell subpopulations were annotated based on the CellMarker 2.0 database, with the epithelial cell cluster selected as the focus for downstream analyses. To investigate the relationship between SRGs and tumor stemness regulation, CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) was used to quantify cellular differentiation potential. In parallel, gene expression dynamics were characterized using pseudotime trajectory inference constructed with Monocle2 (v2.28.0).

Cell culture and SiRNA transfection

The human CCA cell lines HuCCT1 and RBE used in this study were purchased from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were routinely cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Israel), maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cells were passaged at a 1:2 ratio every 2–3 days to ensure they remained in the logarithmic growth phase.

To investigate the role of CCNB1 in CCA cell proliferation, gene silencing experiments were performed using siRNAs designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Transfections were carried out with Lipofectamine® 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, at a working siRNA concentration of 50 nM. The specific siRNA sequences used were as follows:

siCCNB1#1: 5’-CCAUUAUUGAUCGGUUCAU-3’.

siCCNB1#2: 5’-CCACAUCAAGCAUGCUAA-3’.

Negative control siRNA (siNC): 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted 24 h post-transfection using TRIzol® reagent (Takara, Japan), and RNA quality was assessed using an ND5000 microvolume spectrophotometer (BioTeke, Beijing, China), ensuring A260/A280 ratios between 1.80 and 2.00. cDNA synthesis was performed in a reaction mixture containing 1× M-MLV buffer, 200 U of M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 0.4 mmol/L dNTP mix (Bio-Light, Shanghai, China), and 0.4 µmol/L Oligo(dT)18 primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

qPCR was conducted using Hieff® SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (No Rox) (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) on a LightCycler® 480 II real-time PCR system (Roche, Switzerland). The amplification protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end to verify amplification specificity. The specific primer sequences used were:

CCNB1-F: 5’-GCACTTTCCTCCTTCTCA-3’.

CCNB1-R: 5’-CGATGTGGCATACTTGTT-3’.

β-actin-F: 5’-CTACCTCATGAAGATCCTCACCGA-3’.

β-actin-R: 5’-TTCTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGATT-3’.

Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

At 24 h post-siRNA transfection, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well. Each experimental group included three technical replicates, and wells containing only culture medium served as blank controls. On days 1, 2, 3, and 4 after transfection, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (TargetMol, USA) was added to each well under light-protected conditions. Plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan™ FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). Background was corrected using the blank control, and cell proliferation activity was expressed as relative OD values (Relative OD = OD of experimental group - OD of blank control).

Colony formation assay

At 24 h post-siRNA transfection, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 7 days, with complete medium replaced every 3 days. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were sequentially washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 5 min. Excess dye was rinsed off with distilled water to remove background staining. Colonies were semi-automatically counted using ImageJ software (v1.54), and the number of colonies was quantified for each group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software and GraphPad Prism. Experimental data were analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way/two-way ANOVA, with assumptions of homogeneity of variance tested prior to analysis. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times, and results are presented as mean ± SEM. A p-value or adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the predictive performance of the CCNB1 gene, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the pROC package in R (v1.18.5)20, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.

The heatmap clearly demonstrated pronounced differences in expression profiles, with tumor and normal samples segregating into distinct clusters (Fig. 1A). A volcano plot further visualized these DEGs, highlighting significantly upregulated (coral red) and downregulated (cyan blue) genes, identified based on stringent criteria (|log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 1B).

Subsequently, WGCNA was performed on the identified DEGs to pinpoint key gene modules associated with CCA initiation and progression. To ensure a scale-free topology network, we selected a soft-thresholding power of 6, which yielded an optimal scale-free topology fit index while preserving suitable mean connectivity (Fig. 1C). Based on the TOM, hierarchical clustering categorized genes into distinct co-expression modules using the dynamic tree cut method. Modules displaying similar expression patterns were merged using a threshold of 0.25 to enhance biological interpretability (Fig. 1D).

To assess the relationship between gene modules and clinical traits, we examined correlations between MEs and tumor status. The eigengene of the blue module (MEblue) exhibited a robust positive correlation with tumor samples (correlation coefficient = 0.95, p = 3.18e-93) (Fig. 1E). Further validation using boxplot analysis confirmed significantly higher expression of MEblue in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that genes within the MEblue module likely play a pivotal role in CCA tumorigenesis.

Functional enrichment analysis of overlapping genes between DEGs and key WGCNA module, and identification of SRGs

To further elucidate the functional roles of DEGs in CCA tumorigenesis, we conducted enrichment and network analyses on genes shared between DEGs and key co-expression modules. A Venn diagram identified 1,701 overlapping genes between the DEGs and the MEblue module from WGCNA (Fig. 2A). GSEA demonstrated significant enrichment of these genes in biological pathways related to nucleosome organization (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.63), positive regulation of keratinocyte differentiation (NES = 1.63), and apical junction assembly (NES = 1.63). Conversely, metabolic pathways such as polysaccharide biosynthesis (NES = − 1.61) and carbohydrate metabolism regulation (NES = − 1.60) were notably downregulated (Fig. 2B). These findings underscore a general shift towards increased structural processes and decreased metabolic activity. KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted significant involvement of these overlapping genes in metabolic processes including xenobiotic metabolism, bile acid biosynthesis, and pyruvate metabolism, suggesting roles in reshaping the tumor microenvironment through metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 2C). Consistent with these observations, GO analysis indicated convergence on oxidoreductase activity (MF), apical and basal membrane localization (CC), and small molecule catabolic processes (BP) (Fig. 2D–F), reflecting metabolic adaptability and microenvironmental remodeling essential for tumor growth.

PPI network analysis further identified core regulatory genes among the overlapping set (Supplementary Fig. 1). Topological examination of the PPI network pinpointed 10 highly interconnected hub genes (Fig. 2H). Subsequent univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis determined seven genes significantly associated with patient survival: CDK1, ASPM, CCNB1, KIF2C, TOP2A, BUB1, and DLGAP5 (Fig. 2I). These SRGs are thus implicated as critical players in CCA pathogenesis and hold potential as prognostic biomarkers.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals that SRGs promote CCA progression by regulating the G2/M phase of the cell cycle

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which SRGs drive CCA progression, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from dataset GSE138709, examining SRG expression across various cell populations. UMAP dimensionality reduction identified distinct cellular clusters in both CCA and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 3A,B). Notably, epithelial cells comprised a significantly larger proportion in tumor tissues compared to normal samples (Fig. 3C). Cell-type annotation was confirmed using a dot plot displaying canonical marker gene expression (Fig. 3D).

Further analysis revealed that SRG expression was markedly higher in tumor epithelial cells compared to other cell populations, such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, and T cells (Fig. 3E,F). Functional enrichment analyses highlighted the primary involvement of SRGs in cell cycle regulation, particularly emphasizing their enrichment in G2/M phase-associated pathways (Fig. 3G,H). An independent scRNA-seq dataset (GSE171899) replicated these patterns at single-cell resolution (Supplementary Figs. 2 A–C).These observations strongly suggest that SRGs facilitate CCA malignant progression by modulating critical regulatory checkpoints during the G2/M transition.

Additional subclustering of tumor epithelial cells uncovered seven distinct epithelial subpopulations, emphasizing substantial cellular heterogeneity within the TME (Fig. 3I,J). Differential expression analysis via heatmap revealed elevated expression of SRGs, particularly within the PHGR1-Epi and UBE2C-Epi subpopulations (Fig. 3K). KEGG pathway enrichment analyses further indicated that these two subpopulations were significantly associated with enhanced cell cycle-related pathways (Fig. 3L,M), underscoring their potential roles in promoting tumor cell proliferation.

Pseudotime analysis reveals heterogeneity of malignant CCA cell subpopulations and dynamic expression of SRGs

To further delineate the heterogeneity among malignant epithelial subpopulations in CCA and explore dynamic expression patterns of SRGs during tumor progression, we performed pseudotime trajectory analyses using CytoTRACE and Monocle2 algorithms. CytoTRACE analysis uncovered significant heterogeneity in differentiation potential across malignant epithelial subclusters (Fig. 4A). Notably, the UBE2C-Epi and PHGR1-Epi subclusters displayed the lowest and third-lowest differentiation potentials, respectively (Fig. 4B), indicating these SRG-enriched populations exhibit strong progenitor-like and dedifferentiated characteristics.

Further correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive relationship between SRG expression and CytoTRACE differentiation scores (p < 2.2e−16; Fig. 4C–I), supporting their involvement in tumor cell dedifferentiation. Subsequently, we utilized Monocle2 to construct pseudotime trajectories, identifying five distinct cellular states that represented progressive differentiation stages of tumor cells (Fig. 4J,K). This analysis highlighted clear differences in differentiation trajectories associated with tissue origin (tumor versus adjacent normal; Fig. 4L) and specific cellular subpopulations (Fig. 4M).

Examination of SRG expression trends along the pseudotime trajectories revealed dynamic alterations during malignant progression, characterized by notable expression elevations at critical branching points (Fig. 4N–T). Collectively, these findings suggest that SRGs significantly contribute to CCA progression by orchestrating the dedifferentiation and proliferative potential of malignant epithelial cells.

Validation of CCNB1 overexpression in CCA tissues and its functional role in tumor cell proliferation

Based on prior analyses implicating CCNB1 as a critical regulator in CCA, we validated its expression using three independent datasets (GSE76297, GSE26566, and TCGA-CHOL). CCNB1 was consistently and significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (p < 0.001; Fig. 5A–C). ROC curve analyses confirmed the excellent diagnostic value of CCNB1, yielding AUC values of 0.99, 0.942, and 0.994, respectively (Fig. 5D–F), underscoring its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for CCA. We next assessed additional SRGs (CDK1, ASPM, KIF2C, TOP2A, BUB1, DLGAP5): all were upregulated in CCA versus non-tumor tissues. ROC analyses showed each gene discriminated tumor from non-tumor with informative AUCs (Supplementary Figs. 2D-E and 3).

To further define the biological function of CCNB1, we employed siRNAs (siCCNB1#1 and siCCNB1#2) to achieve targeted knockdown in CCA cell lines HuCCT1 and RBE. qRT-PCR analyses verified effective reduction of CCNB1 mRNA by both siRNAs, with siCCNB1#2 demonstrating consistently superior knockdown efficiency (Fig. 5G,H). Thus, siCCNB1#2 was utilized in subsequent functional assays.

CCK-8 showed that CCNB1 silencing significantly inhibited the growth of both HuCCT1 and RBE cells (Fig. 5I,J). Moreover, colony formation assays revealed a marked reduction in clonogenic capacity following CCNB1 knockdown (p < 0.05; Fig. 5K–M). Collectively, these data substantiate the oncogenic role of CCNB1 in CCA, positioning it as a promising candidate for diagnostic and therapeutic targeting strategies.

Discussion

CCA is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by a high recurrence rate and notable resistance to conventional radiotherapy and chemotherapy21. In this study, we employed an innovative multi-omics integration strategy to systematically elucidate the molecular regulatory network involving seven SRGs-including CDK1 and CCNB1-that participate in CCA progression through G2/M checkpoint control.Our analysis precisely identified UBE2C-Epi and PHGR1-Epi as two epithelial subpopulations with pronounced malignant features. Importantly, we revealed for the first time the dynamic expression patterns of key regulatory genes at single-cell resolution, offering deeper insights into tumor heterogeneity. Of particular significance, functional experiments confirmed for the first time in CCA models that knockdown of CCNB1 markedly inhibited both cell proliferation and clonogenic capacity. This finding aligns with prior studies showing that CCNB1 forms a complex with CDK1, phosphorylating downstream substrates to facilitate G2/M phase transition and promote cell cycle progression22. Notably, the coordinated overexpression of SRGs has been reported across multiple solid tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma23–26, suggesting that this regulatory pattern may function as a conserved oncogenic module across cancer types. From a systems biology perspective, our study highlights that these genes act not in isolation but as part of a functionally synergistic regulatory module that collectively drives cell cycle progression. This network-based regulatory model provides a compelling rationale for the development of combination targeted therapies in CCA and potentially other malignancies.

Studies on cell cycle regulation in CCA have provided important theoretical support for the development of novel antitumor strategies. Accumulating evidence suggests that targeting the G2/M phase checkpoint is an effective approach to inhibit CCA cell proliferation. Multiple studies have demonstrated that natural compounds and their derivatives exert antitumor effects by specifically interfering with G2/M phase-associated regulatory networks. For instance, β-elemene has been shown to induce G2/M phase arrest, enhance the chemosensitizing effect of doxorubicin, and significantly inhibit CCA progression by reducing γH2AX phosphorylation and reprogramming glutathione metabolism27. Similarly, flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol induce cell cycle arrest through modulation of key effector molecules involved in G2/M transition, and their activity is closely associated with poor prognosis in CCA patients28. Notably, Ulmus davidiana-derived condensed tannins (UCTs) have been reported to both activate caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways and synergistically induce G2/M phase arrest, offering strong theoretical support for the development of new natural antitumor agents29. In the context of the TME, Crenigacestat selectively suppresses G2/M-phase subpopulations within heterotypic tumor-stroma spheroids, underscoring the importance of targeting the TME in CCA therapy3. In the field of molecular targeted therapy, Rigosertib has been shown to induce G2/M arrest through dual inhibition of CDK1-Cyclin B complex activity and the p53 signaling axis30, while piperine mediates p21-dependent, p53-independent G2/M arrest via the ROS-JNK-ERK signaling cascade31. Future studies should aim to elucidate the spatiotemporal regulatory dynamics of core G2/M checkpoint components during CCA initiation and progression, which will be essential for advancing translational medicine and targeted therapeutic development in this highly aggressive cancer.

CCNB1, as a core regulatory factor of the G2/M phase transition, plays a pivotal role in the mitotic progression of CCA by forming a functional complex with CDK1. Recent studies have shown that PLK1 inhibitors (e.g., BI6727) can significantly induce G2/M arrest by stabilizing the CCNB1-CDK1 complex and increasing the phosphorylation level of histone H3 at serine 10 (pS10H3)32. Similarly, dioscin has been reported to inhibit the cell cycle progression of CCA by downregulating CCNB1 expression and upregulating p2133. In terms of epigenetic regulation, miR-494 has been shown to induce G2/M arrest by coordinately targeting CCNB1, PTTG1, and other key regulatory genes34. Clinicopathological analyses further demonstrate that 14-3-3σ expression is negatively correlated with CCNB1, and CCNB1 overexpression serves as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in patients with intrahepatic CCA35. Additionally, trans-(±)-TTPG-B exerts a pronounced synergistic antitumor effect by simultaneously inhibiting CCNB1-CDK1 complex activity and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway36. While these studies consistently underscore the central role of CCNB1 in G2/M transition in CCA, most prior investigations have been correlative or observational in nature, lacking direct functional validation. In this study, we provide the first experimental evidence through CCNB1 knockdown in CCA models, offering concrete support for the development of precision therapies targeting the CCNB1-CDK1 complex.

The findings of this study offer important implications for the clinical translation of future CCA therapies and provide a solid theoretical foundation for constructing a synergistic regulatory network model of SRGs. Integrating previous research with our current results, we propose that the CDK1-CCNB1 axis plays a central role in the initiation and progression of CCA30,32–36. Moreover, mitotic regulators such as ASPM and KIF2C may amplify this effect through cooperative interactions37–41. The SRGs identified in this study are closely associated with overall survival in CCA and exhibit an upregulated expression trend during tumor dedifferentiation. It is reasonable to hypothesize that this upregulation may be linked to adverse pathological features such as lymph node metastasis and vascular invasion, commonly observed during CCA dedifferentiation42. Notably, this study also highlights the diagnostic potential of CCNB1 in CCA. Across three independent datasets (GSE76297, GSE26566, and TCGA-CHOL), CCNB1 demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance with AUC values of 0.99, 0.942, and 0.994, respectively. These findings suggest that CCNB1 and other SRGs may serve as valuable biomarkers for the occurrence, progression, and metastasis of CCA43. Collectively, this study provides a strong theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies, particularly regarding the potential application of CCNB1 inhibitors in CCA treatment and the feasibility of targeting CCNB1 to overcome chemotherapy resistance44,45.

The primary limitation of this study lies in the heterogeneity of clinical samples, particularly the combined analysis of intrahepatic and extrahepatic CCA, which may obscure site-specific molecular mechanisms. CCA exhibits marked differences in biological behavior, immune microenvironment, and mutational landscape depending on its anatomical location46–48, and future studies should stratify intrahepatic and extrahepatic CCA more precisely to define their distinct molecular features. In addition, the functional validation of CCNB1 is preliminary. We did not quantify CCNB1 protein levels before and after gene silencing, evaluate its subcellular localization (nuclear vs. cytoplasmic), or formally correlate CCNB1 perturbation with cell-cycle distribution. We also did not assess tumor specificity by comparing effects in CCA cell lines with normal human cholangiocytes, and our specificity controls were limited (e.g., lack of multiple independent silencing reagents and rescue experiments). Finally, in vivo validation in animal models is lacking.

Looking ahead, future studies could utilize organoid platforms to further validate the spatiotemporal expression dynamics of SRGs during tumor progression-particularly across distinct intrahepatic and extrahepatic CCA models-in order to more accurately recapitulate the clinical tumor microenvironment and spatial architecture. As a central gene identified in this study, CCNB1 warrants further investigation, especially regarding its phosphorylation modifications and post-translational stability, which are likely to be crucial for understanding its pivotal role in tumorigenesis. Given the immune-evasive nature of CCA, exploring the potential of combining SRGs targeting with immune checkpoint inhibitors may offer novel strategies for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy in this malignancy. Therefore, future research should aim to deepen mechanistic exploration, strengthen validation in animal models, and focus on how the tumor microenvironment influences therapeutic response. These efforts will help lay a robust theoretical foundation for the development of precision therapies in CCA.
