Bridging autism and eating disorders: a scoping review on repetitive and restrictive behavioral patterns
Authors: Sara Passarini, Martina Parisi, Silvia Guerrera, Stefano Vicari, Elisa Fucà
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by difficulties in social communication and restricted and repetitive behaviors (RBs). Autistic individuals frequently exhibit co-occurring physical and mental health conditions, worsening long-term outcomes. Among these, feeding and eating disorders (FEDs) are particularly prevalent. Autistic traits may increase vulnerability to FEDs, and people with FEDs often display elevated autistic features. In particular, cognitive rigidity in FEDs seems to reflect RBs patterns typical of ASD. The current scoping review aimed to examine the relationship between autistic features and FEDs with specific reference to RBs, and to assess whether this association differs between pediatric and adult populations.MethodThe review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines for scoping reviews. A systematic search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and PsycArticles, with no time restrictions, yielded 204 records. Only peer-reviewed articles written in English were considered eligible. Consistent with our hypotheses, the review included research on autistic individuals as well as on individuals with FEDs or meeting established cut-off scores for FEDs. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria.ResultsThe reviewed studies consistently reported an association between ASD and FEDs with respect to RBs across the lifespan. Of them, four focused on pediatric populations while six on adults. While specific types of RBs associated with FED symptoms, such as repetitive or unusually intense interests, are present in both pediatric and adult age, others seem to manifest differently according to the individual’s age. Further, our findings suggest that RBs may play a role in predisposing autistic individuals in developing FED symptoms.ConclusionThe current scoping review shows that RBs may play a role in predisposing autistic individuals to FEDs but also it could potentially act as a feature of ASD in FED populations. Overall, our results highlight the need for tailored assessment, evaluation, and treatment approaches that can capture the unique presentation of eating disorder symptoms and autistic features.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01639-1.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by the early onset of social communication difficulties along with restricted interests and repetitive behaviors (RBs) which are an heterogenous group of behaviors varying among individuals. RBs can be distinguished into lower-level RBs, characterized by repetition of movement such as stereotyped movements, repetitive manipulation of objects, repetitive forms of self-injurious behavior, and higher-level RBs including insistence on the maintenance of sameness, circumscribed interests [1]. ASD shows heterogeneous presentations with a high rate of mental health co-occurrences [2] persisting from childhood to adulthood [3]. For instance, anxiety disorders and depressive disorder often co-occur with ASD with an estimated prevalence rate of 30–39% and 15–21% respectively [4, 5]. The presence of psychiatric comorbidities appears to intensify autistic traits. For example, higher levels of anxiety symptoms have been associated with more severe core features of ASD, particularly within the RBs domain [6]. Psychiatric co-occurrences in autistic individuals tend to increase from childhood to adulthood contributing to poorer long-term outcomes, such as reduced quality of life and elevated mortality risk [2]. Another common psychiatric co-occurrence associated with ASD are feeding and eating disorders (FEDs) [7], with an estimated prevalence of 20–46% in autistic population [4].

FEDs involve a broad group of disturbances or eating-related behaviors characterized by a persistent disturbance of eating or eating-related behavior resulting in the altered consumption or absorption of food and that significantly impairs individual health and functioning [8]. The most common conditions are anorexia nervosa (AN), bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder and avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) [8]. The onset of FEDs typically occurs from childhood to early adulthood, with around 15% of FEDs developing by age 14, around 50% by age 18, over 80% by age 25 [9]. Different forms of FEDs present both shared and distinct phenomenological features, etiological pathways, and treatment responses [9]. For example, AN and bulimia involve an excessive preoccupation with weight and body shape [8]. In contrast, ARFID involves disruptions in nutritional and caloric intake that go beyond typical variations in hunger, food preferences, or interest in eating, and occur in the absence of concerns about body weight or shape [8]. Prevalence rates indicate that approximately 11.41% of autistic individuals also have ARFID in co-occurrence [10] and 8.3% experience AN [11].

It has been proposed that autistic features are a risk factor implicated in both the onset and maintenance of FEDs [12]. Further, people with FEDs seem to exhibit more autistic traits with approximately 8–37% of individuals with AN and about 12.5% with ARFID showing autistic features [13]. ASD and FED co-occurrence may be explained referring to several factors. First, selective eating, with a strong preference for certain food (e.g. calorie-dense food) and typically based on foods’ features such as color, shape, texture, or temperature is frequently observed in autistic individuals [14], making them more likely to refuse certain foods [15]. Evidence suggests that this food selectivity may, at least in part, reflect autistic features related to restricted interest [15, 16]. Second, differences in sensory processing contribute to feeding problems, including fussy eating, a limited food repertoire, and reduced food intake [17, 18]. Sensory preference patterns may represent one of the causes of avoidant and restrictive eating behaviors in ASD [19]. Third, a cognitive profile characterized by weak central coherence, rigidity, and poor mentalizing ability has been observed in both autistic individuals and those with FEDs [20, 21]. Of note, cognitive rigidity in individuals with FEDs seems to mirror patterns of behaviors specific to ASD, specifically RBs [15]. For instance, the cognitive rigidity observed in both autistic individual and those with ANpose challenges for differential diagnosis. However, the temporal course of symptoms may aid clinicians in the diagnostic process: elevated levels of social and cognitive flexibility difficulties have been observed in women recovered from AN with autistic traits, whereas temporary rigidity appears more characteristic of AN without co-occurring ASD [20].

Despite this evidence, the relationship between autistic features, FEDs and RBs has not been systematically explored. While several literature reviews have focused on feeding problems in autistic population more broadly [22–24] or examined specific FEDs in autistic individuals, none have specifically investigated the relationship between autistic features, FEDs and RBs [25, 26]. To the best of our knowledge, only one book chapter has attempted to address both RBs and feeding problems - including FEDs - in ASD, though without adopting a systematic approach [15].

Therefore, the present scoping review aims at answering the following search question:

• Is there a relationship between autistic features and FEDs with respect to RBs?

Basing on the aforementioned literature, we hypothesize the presence of such relationship and more in detail we expected that: (i) autistic features such as restricted interests and sensory aspects could be factors linked to FEDs; (ii) FEDs related behaviors could resemble RBs as autistic features; (iii) specific kinds of RBs could be differently associated with different FEDs (e.g., difficulties in cognitive flexibility with AN and restricted interests for ARFID).

Moreover, since RBs follow a developmental trajectory across childhood and adulthood with motor stereotypes particularly observed in early childhood and insistence on sameness and ritualistic behaviors more prominent from early adolescence [27], it is of interest to examine whether the manifestation of FEDs also varies across the lifespan. Therefore, the second research question was:

Does this relationship differ between pediatric and adult populations when considered from a neurodevelopmental trajectory perspective?

Systematizing the available knowledge is therefore essential for improving our understanding of this relationship and guiding future research on the topic. Further, scoping reviews are essential for advancing clinical practice by providing insights that can inform the development of more effective intervention for individuals with autistic traits and FEDs, addressing both RBs and mental health co-occurrences.

Study selection

First, two authors (S.P and M.P.) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of each record against the eligibility criteria. In cases where the content of the title or abstract was ambiguous, the full text was evaluated, and in instances of disagreement between the authors, a third author (E.F) was consulted for resolution.

Data extraction

The second and third authors independently extracted data from articles to confirm accuracy. Any doubt was discussed together with the help of the other authors. For each selected study, sociodemographic, sample, and methodological variables were extracted.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 details the flowchart of the selection process. Database searching yielded 204 records (PubMed: n = 110; PsycInfo: n = 94; PsycArticles: n = 0). After the screening for title and abstract, 183 articles were excluded since they did not meet the eligibility criteria (for being duplicate records: n = 74; study type: n = 44; population included: n = 23; study topic: n = 42). The remaining 21 records underwent full text screening. Of them, 11 were further excluded (study type: n = 1; study topic: n = 10). A total of 10 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the present scoping review of the literature.

With reference to eating-related issues, the studies included in this review focused on recovered eating disorder (RED; n = 2), eating disorders (ED; n = 2), anorexia (AN; n = 5), and avoidant restrictive food intake (ARFID; n = 1). For evaluating eating symptoms, a variety of measures were adopted. Specifically, the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q) [32] (n = 6), the Swedish Eating Assessment for Autism Spectrum Disorders (SWEAA) [44] (n = 2), the Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ) [46] (n = 2), the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) [8] criteria (n = 1), the Body Mass Index (BMI) (n = 1), the Eating Attitude Test- 26 (EAT-26) [63] (n = 1), and the eating disorder section of the Development and Well-Being Assessment (DAWBA) [56] (n = 1). When considering autistic features, they were assessed using multiple approaches, comprising both standardized diagnostic instruments and clinical evaluations. Specifically, 2 studies utilized the Autism Diagnostic Schedule, second edition (ADOS-2) [36], while 4 studies relied on clinical diagnosis supported by standardized measures. Additional methods included the SPARKS database (n = 1) and the Developmental, Dimensional and Diagnostic Interview (3Di) [61] (n = 1). Finally, the assessment of RBs employed different standardized measures, including the Repetitive Behavior Scale Revised (RBS-R) [64] (n = 2), the Adult Repetitive Behaviours Questionnaire-2 (RBQ-2 A) [33] (n = 3), the RBs subscale of the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS) [41] (n = 2), the Autism–Tics, ADHD and other Comorbidities inventory (A-TAC) [62] (n = 1), and the ADOS-2 [36] (n = 2).

The association between RBs and FEDs in childhood and adolescence

The four studies discussed in this paragraph all examined the relationship between autistic features and FEDs with respect to RBs in pediatric populations. Of these, only one study employed a longitudinal design while the remaining studies were cross-sectional. Half of the studies investigated the development of FEDs in autistic individuals, whereas the other half focused on the manifestation of autistic traits in individuals with FEDs. Only one study included a control group.

Dinkler and colleagues [65] conducted a longitudinal study involving 5987 children and adolescents to examine the association between autistic traits and AN before (age 9) and after (age 18) its onset. At age 9, girls who later developed AN did not exhibit statistically significant higher autistic traits compared to girls without AN. However, the most frequently reported autistic features, as assessed by Child and Adolescent Twin Study in Sweden, were repetition of words and expressions, atypical language use, and difficulties in imitation and play. By age 18, girls with AN showed significantly higher autistic traits in social communication (1.48 times higher) and RBs (2.36 times higher) compared to girls without AN. A similar pattern was observed in boys. The most pronounced autistic features included becoming absorbed in routines, repetition of words, and repetitive or unusually intense interests. Furthermore, among 18-year-old girls, those with acute AN exhibited higher total CATSS-9 score for autistic traits than those with a history of AN, with statistically significant differences observed for RBs but not for the social communication domain. No significant differences were found between girls with a history of AN and girls without AN on either RBs or social communication. These findings suggest that autistic traits are elevated in individuals with AN, particularly during the acute phase of AN, with RBs being especially prominent.

Koomar and collaborators [66] conducted a cross-sectional study to investigate ARFID symptomatology in a cohort of 5157 autistic individuals and their parents. Results indicated that inflexible eating behaviors (e.g., rigidity regarding food presentation) and sensory sensitivities were more pronounced among autistic adolescents with elevated ARFID symptoms. ARFID severity showed strong positive associations with autistic traits as measured by the RBS-R and the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) [58], and negative associations with the adaptive functioning. The RBS-R exhibited the strongest relationships with ARFID symptomatology, particularly for the sameness and ritualistic subscales, as well as for the total RBS-R score. Moreover, strong associations were found between ARFID symptoms and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS) [59] internalizing problems. This study provides valuable insight for better understanding ARFID in autistic individuals, showing that specific RBs are associated with greater ARFID symptoms and offering clinical implications for both diagnostic and intervention processes.

Bitsika and Sharpley [67] investigated predictors of FEDs development in 37 school-aged autistic females using a cross-sectional design. Results showed that specific aspects of the children’s sensory profiles (e.g., oral, touch and visual processing) among with RBs explained 57.7% of the variance in the SWEAA total score. Specifically, oral processing alone accounting for 44.2% while RBs explained an additional 10.9% of the variance. When the SP oral processing items were divided into those overlapping and non-overlapping with the SWEAA items, the Sensory Profile (SP) Oral Processing Overlapping items explained 54.7% of the variance while RBs accounted for a further 15.5% of the variance. The SP Oral Processing non-overlapping items did not make a significant additional contribution to the SWEAA variance. With regard to the contribution of RBs, difficulties related to a narrow range of interests and rigid adherence to routines accounted for 23.2% and 9.5% of the variance in SWEAA scores, respectively. In contrast, unusual or bizarre behaviors and uneven performance across tasks did not explain a significant additional amount of variance in SWEAA scores. The current findings elucidate the role of RBs in the development of FEDs in young autistic females, identifying specific RBs that are particularly associated with the manifestation of FEDs in autistic population.

Another study conducted by Pooni and collaborators [68] investigated autistic features in 66 adolescents with and without FEDs (respectively 22 with FEDs, 20 autistics and 24 neurotypical controls). Results showed that half of the adolescents with FEDs had clinically significant total RBS-R scores. Adolescents with FEDs did not differ statistically from controls on the stereotypic behavior and restricted interests RBS-R subscales. However, they exhibited statistically significant higher scores on the RBS-R subscales assessing self-injurious behavior, compulsive behavior, and ritualistic/sameness behavior compared to controls, indicating that adolescents with FEDs mirrors autistic adolescents on these particular dimensions. Compared to autistic adolescents, those with FEDs scored lower on the ritualistic/sameness behavior subscale, although this difference was not statistically significant. This study highlights that RBs are frequently prominent also in adolescents with FEDs, closely resembling those observed in ASD. These findings emphasize the need for future research to better understand the etiology of RBs across both conditions and to determine whether features associated with RBs, such as cognitive inflexibility and difficulties with set-shifting, reflect true autistic traits or are specific to FEDs. Gaining such insights is crucial for improving differential diagnosis and for developing tailored interventions.

The association between RBs and FEDs in adulthood

The six studies discussed in this paragraph all examined the relationship between autistic features and FEDs with respect to RBs in adults, by adopting a cross-sectional design. Half of the studies explored the relationship between autistic features and FEDs symptomatology, whereas the other half focused on the manifestation of autistic traits in adults with FEDs.

Kirkovski and collaborators [69] conducted a cross-sectional study, involving 327 adults, to investigate if autistic features (i.e., RBs, shifting and mentalizing difficulties) could explain the overlapping profile of ASD and AN, also considering the potential effect of biological sex. Participants were divided into four groups according their diagnostic features, namely participants with AN, AN and co-occurring ASD (AN + ASD), ASD only and neurotypical controls. Results showed no statistically significant differences between participants with AN and with AN + ASD on EDE-Q scores while the ASD only group showed statistically significant lower EDE-Q scores compared to other clinical groups. Further, the control group showed lower scores compared to both AN and ASD groups. Additionally, the AN + ASD and ASD only groups obtained the highest scores on the Autism Quotient (AQ) [70], with no statistically significant differences. With refers to RBs, no statistically significant differences were found among all clinical groups on RBQ-2 A scores; all clinical groups scored significantly higher compared to controls. Further, EDE-Q, AQ, BMI and biological sex were found to predict RBs with higher AQ total score, EDE-Q scores and female sex significantly associated with higher RBQ-2a scores. This study highlighted that greater autistic traits and eating disorder psychopathology were associated with increased RBs, underscoring the importance of assessing autistic traits in individuals with AN to promote integrated interventions addressing both eating disorder and autistic behavioral aspects.

Brede and collaborators [71] conducted a cross-sectional study with the aim of characterizing FED symptoms and autistic features in a sample of 244 adults. Participants were divided into four groups: ASD with co-occurring restrictive eating disorders (REDs; ASD+REDs), women with high autistic traits and co-occurring REDs (HATs + REDs), ASD only, and REDs only. Findings showed that ASD+REDs had significantly higher FED symptoms (e.g., traditional eating disorder symptoms, body image issues, pride in eating pathology and autism-specific unusual eating behaviors) compared to the ASD-only group. Specifically, across the SWEAA subscales (i.e., Perception, Purchase of Food, Eating Behavior, Mealtime Surroundings, Social Situations at Mealtime, Other Disturbed Eating Behaviors, and Simultaneous Capacity), higher scores were observed for the ASD+REDs group compared to the ASD-only group. Conversely, the ASD+REDs group showed lower levels of FED symptoms compared to the REDs-only group, except for autism-specific unusual eating behaviors, in which they exhibited higher scores (e.g., SWEAA Social Situations at Mealtime, Mealtime Surroundings, Eating Behavior, Purchase of Food, Motor Control, and Perception subscales). In addition, the ASD and ASD+REDs groups had higher autistic features compared to REDs only, with ASD+REDs showing greater RBs as detected by the RBQ-2 A compared to the ASD only group. Current results highlight that the presence of autistic traits appears to exacerbate FED symptoms related to ritualistic behavior or sensory-driven food restriction, as well as the manifestation of RBs. Additionally, autistic features, such as RBs, seem to be implicated in the development and/or persistence of REDs.

In the same years, Saure and colleagues [72] investigated the presence of autistic traits in 16 women with AN, grouped by high and low levels of autistic traits. Results showed that autistic traits assessed using the ADOS-2 were significantly associated with the duration of AN symptoms, with higher autistic traits associated with a longer duration of AN. Furthermore, the duration of AN symptoms was significantly longer in women with high autistic traits compared to those with low autistic traits. Additionally, among women with high autistic traits, finger mannerisms, intense interests in specific topics, and routines related to eating (e.g., precise mealtimes) were observed. RBs were often accompanied by atypical sensory processing related to food. For instance, some women with high autistic traits and elevated RBs scores reported to organize foods by color. This study suggests that autistic features may contribute to a more severe and prolonged course of AN. In particular, routine-seeking behaviors, restricted interests, and atypical sensory processing should be carefully considered when planning assessment procedures and treatments for individuals with AN.

Another study conducted by Adamson and collaborators [73] explored the aspects that could discriminate between autistic and non-autistic individuals in a cohort with RED. Results showed that the autistic individuals with RED had significantly higher scores on AQ-10 items than the non-autistic adults. When considering specific autistic features, RBs were shown to discriminate between the two groups, with the autistic group with RED showing higher scores on the RBQ-2 A total scores and the repetitive motor behaviors and insistence on sameness subscales compared to low autistic traits group. Regarding RED symptoms severity, similar scores were found between the two groups. This study, highlighting the possibility of co-occurring RED and autistic features, underscores the importance of implementing screening procedures for autistic traits in individuals with RED to promote targeted treatments tailored to their specific needs.

Kerr-Gaffney and colleagues [74] examined the presence of autistic features in 218 females, grouped by their symptomatology as follows: acute AN, recovered AN (REC), autistic females (ASD), and neurotypical controls. Results indicated no statistically significant differences between the AN, REC, and ASD groups in social motivation and RBs as detected by the SRS-2. However, all clinical groups scored significantly higher than controls. The AN group showed the highest scores on RBs subscale of SRS-2, followed by the ASD and the REC group, and the control group. Further, the AN group had significantly higher scores on the ADOS-2/RBs subscale compared to the control group. The AN group statistically significant differences in ADOS-2 sensory abnormalities item compared to the ASD group while the REC group had intermediate scores. Concerning the ADOS-2/language abnormalities item, AN and ASD groups statistically significant differ from each other, while the AN and REC groups did not show statistically significant differences. This study highlights that RBs and social motivation difficulties are observed in both autistic individuals and those with AN, underscoring a potential exacerbation of RBs features in individuals with acute AN.

Westwood and colleagues [7] investigated the presence of autistic symptoms in a sample of 60 women with AN. Results revealed that individuals with AN exhibited different autistic profile according to their ADOS-2 scores, namely: high levels of autistic symptoms (HAS), subclinical autistic symptoms (SCAS), and no autistic symptoms (NAS). Specifically, 23.3% of women with AN scored above the ADOS-2 cutoff, representing the HAS group; 40% of participants comprised the SCAS group; and 36.6% did not exhibit autistic characteristics, defining the NAS group. Eight out of 14 women in the HAS group displayed an autistic profile characterized by the presence of specific RBs, such as speech abnormalities, stereotyped use of phrases, and excessive interest in unusual or highly specific topics or objects. The present study underscores the presence of autistic traits in women with AN, highlighting the manifestation of specific RBs in this population and providing valuable insights for both diagnosis and intervention.

Discussion

The current scoping review aimed to explore the relationship between autistic features and FEDs with respect to RBs. The included studies confirmed the existence of the association between autistic features and FEDs. Indeed, the reviewed studies consistently identified RBs as a predominant feature in both ASD and FEDs, contributing to a more severe and prolonged course of the eating disturbances [72]. The reviewed studies highlighted RBs as characteristic of both ASD and FEDs, suggesting that RBs may act as predisposing factors for the development of FEDs [66, 67]. These findings align with the hypothesis of Kelly and Kelly (2021) who emphasized that FEDs in autistic individuals are more closely linked to the need for control, routine, and rituals than to concerns about body weight or image [75]. This may explain why FEDs often persist in ASD even after weight restoration following treatment [75]. Hence, RBs could be viewed as a manifestation of the rigidity and restricted behavioral patterns characteristic of ASD.

The literature review confirmed the second hypothesis, namely that FEDs related behaviors could resemble RBs as autistic features [65, 68]. At the neurobiological level, a possible mechanism underlying the overlap between FEDs, specifically AN, and ASD involves the dopaminergic system. Indeed, alterations in dopaminergic signaling characterize both ASD and AN and they are associated with RBs [76–79]. In particular, dopamine plays a central role in modulating procedural learning and reinforcement mechanisms, contributing to the stabilization of rigid and repetitive behavioral sequences [76–79]. Hence, the manifestation of RBs sequences may differ between autistic individuals and individuals with eating disorders (e.g., AN), with behaviors such as eating and exercise rituals also serving to regulate mood states that can exacerbate these rigid behavioral patterns [78]. Another possible mechanism underlying therelationship between FEDs-ASD- RBs appears to be linked to sensory processes: sensory related behaviors were shown to underline the relationship between RBs and FEDs [72]. Further, the co-occurrence of FEDs and ASD has been found to exacerbate RBs, including ritualistic actions and sensory-driven food restrictions [71, 73].

In clinical practice, professionals assessing the presence of RBs in individuals with FEDs should consider whether these behaviors are specific to food-related contexts, likely reflecting the eating symptoms themselves, or extend more broadly across different areas of daily life, potentially indicating a co-occurrence with ASD traits.

Further, the scoping review provided support also for the third hypothesis. Indeed, an association was observed between RBs and the presence of FEDs in autistic children, especially regarding sameness and ritualistic RBs and ARFID symptomatology [66].

Biological sex seems to play a role in this association. Specifically, Kirkovski et al. [69] underscored that the relationship between RBs, autistic quotient and FED symptoms varies by sex, with males exhibiting higher RBs in presence of elevated eating disturbances and autistic quotient [69]. These findings align with the systematic review of Allely [80] and the study of Cary and collaborators [81], which reported that autistic females tend to display lower rates of RBs [80, 81]. Further, it is possible that RBs in autistic females with FEDs may exist along a spectrum of behaviors that current assessment tools fail to adequately capture [80]. Importantly, autistic females may exhibit specific RBs related to food, such as specific interests in diet or exercise [69].

It should be noted that the included studies focus solely on restrictive eating disorders, specifically ARFID and AN. No studies were identified that examined the relationship between RBs, ASD, and FEDs such as bulimia nervosa or binge eating disorder. This gap should be acknowledged as current literature indicates that both bulimia and binge eating are associated with cognitive rigidity. Bulimia is specifically associated with reduced mental flexibility [82, 83] while in binge-eating disorder, rigidity manifests as inflexible and dichotomous thinking (i.e., a cognitive distortion in which individuals perceive situations in binary terms, either good or bad, thereby oversimplifying reality and not considering intermediate possibilities) [84, 85]. Given the possible co-occurrence of bulimia (estimated prevalence 2.7%) [86] and binge eating in autistic individuals [86, 87], future research should explore these FEDs in relation to RBs and ASD.

In conclusion, the reviewed studies underscore that RBs are consistently linked to FEDs and ASD across development. Improving the assessment of RBs is therefore crucial for accurately identifying underlying ASD and informing targeted interventions.

The second research question guiding this scoping review aimed to characterize autistic features and FEDs in relation to RBs, with particular attention to differences between pediatric and adult populations.

The current review consistently identifies RBs as a characteristic feature among individuals with FEDs in both pediatric and adult populations [65, 68]. Further, the reviewed studies showed that, in both childhood and adulthood, the manifestation of RBs appears to vary depending on the stage of the FED. Specifically, an intensification of RBs was commonly observed during the acute phase of the eating pathology [65, 74]. The exacerbation during acute episodes of FEDs is particularly relevant for the differential diagnostic process, as it may complicate the distinction between behaviors primarily arising from ASD and those that are secondary to the eating disorder. Given this overlap, it is crucial to perform a comprehensive assessment of autistic features after the stabilization or treatment of FED symptoms. Indeed, eating disorders themselves may mirror or temporarily heighten RBs behaviors typically associated with ASD (e.g., rigid dietary routine). Consequently, ASD assessment should be conducted after the acute phase of a FED, avoiding the risk of overestimating autistic traits or misattributing FED-related rigidity to ASD. The persistence of RBs after the resolution of FED symptoms may serve as a strong indicator of an underlying ASD, whereas their reduction or disappearance may suggest that the behaviors were a transient consequence of FEDs. Hence, monitoring RBs is a crucial aspect as they are a key feature linked to both FEDs and ASD, regardlessof age. Investigating RBs may help clinicians during the diagnostic process, particularly when considering potential co-occurrences or making differential diagnoses.

The reviewed studies also revealed that specific types of RBs associated with FED symptoms are present in both pediatric and adult age. For instance, RBs such as absorbed in routines, speech anomalies (e.g., repetition of words), and repetitive or unusually intense interests were the most prevalent manifestations of RBs in FEDs during both the pediatric and the adulthood stage [7, 65]. Moreover, RBs ritualistic behavior have been documented in autistic individuals with FEDs regardless of age [66, 71]. Of note, the manifestation of RBs and FEDs in autistic individuals aligns with the timeline of RBs development across the lifespan, proposed by Comparan-Meza and colleagues [27]. Indeed, reviewed studies also showed that during the school-age period, when the ritualistic and sameness components are present along with the motor-sensory component of RBs [27], FEDs such as ARFID are documented. Over time, as the rigidity component of RBs becomes more prevalent, the reviewed studies predominantly report FEDs such as AN.

Of note, the manifestation of RBs in FEDs should be considered across the entire lifespan and regarded as a potentially relevant “red flag”. Specifically, RBs may be conceptualized in two ways: (i) an autistic feature per se within FEDs, and (ii) as a functional mechanism contributing to the maintenance of eating disorder symptomatology. While the first pathway conceptualizes RBs as core autistic features that may act as predisposing factors for the development of FED, the second pathway frames RBs as autism-like maintenance factors that sustain eating disorder symptomatology. Indeed, in individuals with FEDs, these behaviors may contribute to disorder persistence by eliciting positive reinforcement, enhancing predictability, and reducing unpleasant feelings and emotions [78, 88].

To better understand and confirm the pattern of association between sensory-motor and cognitive rigidity components of RBs and FEDs in ASD, future research should adopt a longitudinal approach including autistic preschoolers.

Overall, the reviewed studies reported a relationship between RBs and FEDs across the entire lifespan. The association appears to be particularly evident during the acute phase of FEDs, regardless of age. However, certain RBs, especially RBs related to cognitive rigidity, which are typically prominent in ASD during late childhood and adolescence [27], seem to be linked to specific FEDs such as AN, which tends to appear later in life, compared to ARFID, which in contrast typically has an earlier onset [89]. Further research is needed to clarify how this relationship unfolds and manifests over time, following its developmental trajectory, in order to inform earlier and more effective diagnosis and intervention.

The results of our scoping review should be considered in light of both its strengths and limitations. As for the strengths, we conducted a comprehensive and systematic search across multiple databases, without restrictions on publication date or the age of participants. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the relatively small number of longitudinal studies makes it difficult to clearly understand the trajectory of RBs in autistic population with FEDs. A further limitation concerns gender-related factors: most of the included studies involved solely females which could have influenced the findings. Moreover, many of the reviewed studies relied primarily on subjective and quantitative measures, without incorporating mixed methods that could provide a more comprehensive understanding of RB manifestations. The lack of literature examining FEDs such as bulimia nervosa or binge-eating disorder is another weakness of this review, alongside the predominant focus on restrictive eating disorders (ARFID and AN). Another limitation relates to feasibility constraints in conducting the literature search, which was restricted to a limited number of databases, excluding other relevant sources (e.g., the Scopus database) and gray literature sources, and may therefore have led to the omission of some pertinent studies. Furthermore, since only articles written in English were included, some potentially relevant studies may have been excluded. Finally, the lack of data on participants’ ethnicity, as this information was not reported in the included studies, potentially limits the generalizability of the findings.

In summary, the current scoping review examined 10 studies to improve the understanding of the relationship between RBs, ASD and FEDs. The results revealed that RBs may act as a predisposing factor for the development of FEDs in individuals with ASD, as well as serve as a potential marker of ASD within FED populations, depending on the nature and stage of eating-related symptoms.

Future research should adopt a longitudinal and developmentally based trajectory approach to better understand the relationship between RBs and FEDs, trying to provide valuable insights for the clinical practice helping in discriminating if RBs are a manifestation of ASD or related to FEDs.
