Subcutaneous administration of antibody–drug conjugates: current challenges, emerging opportunities, and future perspectives
Authors: Steven Ren, Shawn Shouye Wang, Frank Yunsong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) are a rapidly growing class of targeted therapeutics designed to deliver potent payloads while minimizing systemic toxicity. Despite significant progress, all approved ADCs are administered intravenously (IV). In contrast, subcutaneous (SC) delivery is well established for antibodies, offering shorter administration time, improved convenience, and the potential for outpatient or home-based treatment. Extending SC delivery to ADCs is therefore attractive but introduces unique challenges related to structural complexity, linker–payload instability, and cytotoxic payloads.This review evaluates the feasibility of subcutaneously administered ADCs by integrating insights from pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies, emerging clinical data, and advances in molecular design, formulation, and delivery technologies. Key challenges include limited clinical comparisons between IV and SC routes, uncertain bioavailability and tumor exposure, linker–payload instability, injection-site reactions, and high-concentration formulation constraints. Enabling strategies, such as rational molecular design, hyaluronidase-enabled delivery, polymer-based systems, and conversion of IV ADCs to SC dosing, are discussed.Recent advances, exemplified by JSKN033, demonstrate the feasibility of high-concentration liquid co-formulation approaches for SC administration and may represent a future direction. Evidence across oncology and non-oncology programs suggests that while SC delivery is achievable, success is highly molecule dependent. Overall, transitioning ADCs from IV to SC requires case-by-case evaluation and coordinated innovation across rational molecular design, formulation, and nonclinical and clinical development to realize the full patient-centric benefits of this approach.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) are an emerging class of biopharmaceuticals designed to selectively deliver cytotoxic payloads to malignant cells while leveraging antibody target specificity to minimize systemic toxicity. As of December 2025, 19 ADCs have received global regulatory approval [1]. The ADC development landscape continues to expand rapidly, with more than 370 new ADCs have entered the clinic [2].

Subcutaneous (SC) injection is a rapid, single-dose (bolus) method for administering medication into the hypodermis, the tissue layer between the dermis and muscle, using a short, small-gauge needle and a syringe. In contrast, subcutaneous infusion, also referred to as hypodermoclysis, provides slower, continuous, or controlled delivery of medication or fluids over an extended period. SC infusion delivers medication into the same SC tissue as SC injection but utilizes specialized equipment, including a catheter needle, tubing connected to a syringe or infusion bag, and an infusion pump to enable slow, continuous administration. The advantages and limitations of SC infusion, as well as its appropriate clinical applications, have been reviewed by Caccialanza [3] and Arcani [4]. In this review, SC refers to SC injection unless otherwise specified.

For antibody-based therapeutics, SC administration has become increasingly prevalent, with ~76% of high-concentration (≥ 100 mg/ml) antibody products delivered via the SC route [5] and ~34% (47 out of 138) for all approved antibody therapeutics [6]. In oncology, antibodies have historically been administered intravenously (IV); however, recent advances in formulation and delivery technologies, particularly co-formulation with hyaluronidase, have enabled a shift toward SC dosing [8]. Notable examples include KEYTRUDA QLEX (pembrolizumab with berahyaluronidase alfa-pmph) and OPDIVO QVANTIG (nivolumab with hyaluronidase-nvhy) [7, 8]. Compared with conventional IV infusion, SC administration offers greater convenience and substantially shorter administration times (e.g. 3–5 minutes for SC OPDIVO QVANTIG vs 30 minutes for IV OPDIVO [7]), enables self-administration in some settings or caregiver-supported dosing at home, and reduces the burden on healthcare systems. SC delivery also expands treatment access across diverse care environments, including physician offices and community clinics. Additionally, SC injection permits the treatment of patients with poor venous access and preserves venous capital in patients at risk of vascular exhaustion [9]. From a product lifecycle management perspective, IV to SC transitions may additionally provide opportunities to extend market exclusivity [8].

Liquid formulations are generally preferred for parenteral administration because they offer greater convenience, eliminate the need for reconstitution, and reduce the risk of preparation errors compared with lyophilized drug products. However, among the 19 ADCs currently approved, only two are formulated as ready-to-use liquid injections, while the remaining 17 are supplied as lyophilized powders [10]. This imbalance reflects the inherent physicochemical instability of ADCs in aqueous solution, driven primarily by linker hydrolysis, payload-induced hydrophobicity, and aggregation, which makes ADCs significantly less stable than their unconjugated monoclonal antibody (mAb) counterparts.

Lyophilization mitigates these degradation pathways by removing water and limiting molecular mobility; however, it introduces additional complexity in manufacturing and reconstitution for use [11]. Although these limitations are not insurmountable, they are particularly consequential for SC administration, which ideally requires a stable, ready-to-use, liquid formulation suitable for clinical use. As a result, with the exception of Akalux (cetuximab sarotalocan sodium) and Elahere (mirvetuximab soravtansine), the majority of currently approved ADCs, when formulated as lyophilized products, do not readily meet the practical requirements for SC injection [10].

Despite these advances, all currently approved ADCs are administered intravenously, and evaluation of SC delivery for ADCs remains limited in both nonclinical and clinical settings [10]. More recently, advances in ADC design, conjugation technologies, formulation science, and drug delivery platforms have enabled increasing interest in alternative routes of administration, including SC injection. From a pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) perspective, SC delivery is associated with slower absorption and lower peak plasma concentrations (Cmax), which may help mitigate acute systemic toxicities. However, SC administration of ADCs also presents distinct challenges, including linker–payload degradation, uncertain bioavailability and tumor exposure, injection-site reactions, and high-concentration formulation constraints [12–14].

This review aims to evaluate the feasibility of SC delivery of ADCs, summarize current non-clinical and clinical evidence, highlights key challenges, enabling strategies, and future directions for advancing patient-centric ADC delivery.

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of antibody-drug conjugates administered via subcutaneous route

Monoclonal antibodies have been administered subcutaneously for decades, however, many aspects of the PK/PD and bioavailability of SC-delivered mAbs remain incompletely understood, as comprehensively reviewed by Sánchez-Félix et al. [15]. Owing to their large molecular size, direct absorption into blood capillaries is unlikely, and systemic uptake occurs predominantly via the lymphatic system, with FcRn-mediated transcytosis potentially contributing to entry into the circulation. Consequently, SC bioavailability is typically less than 100%, with reported values ranging from ~43% to 88% for mAbs with available human bioavailability data. The reduced bioavailability has been attributed to local metabolism, degradation, or precipitation at the injection site, as well as elimination processes such as phagocytosis during lymphatic transit before reaching the systemic circulation. The rate and extent of absorption following SC injection are governed by a complex interplay of molecular properties, formulation characteristics, and injection-site physiology. Importantly, translation of SC bioavailability from preclinical models to humans remains challenging because of substantial interspecies differences as a major contributing factor [15].

The complexity and unique PK/PD challenges associated with intravenously administered ADCs have been comprehensively reviewed by Li et al. [16]. Owing to their multicomponent structure, ADCs require quantitative characterization of multiple different molecular species across systemic circulation and target tissues. The physiologically based pharmacokinetic and PK/PD modeling approaches discussed in this review [16] provide critical insights into clinical dose justification, inform key drug development decisions, and support the rational optimization of next-generation ADCs.

Whether the PK/PD behavior of unconjugated (a.k.a naked) antibodies or intravenously administered ADCs can be directly extrapolated to subcutaneously administered ADCs (SC ADCs) remains uncertain, largely due to the paucity of studies specifically investigating the PK/PD of SC ADCs. Although ADCs are likely to share broadly similar absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination mechanisms with their subcutaneously administered mAbs [17], their structural complexity introduces additional considerations. Compared with unconjugated antibodies, ADCs are heterogeneous molecules characterized by a distribution of drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) species and the presence of linker–payload components. Following administration, they exist as multiple circulating analytes, including total antibody (conjugated plus unconjugated), total ADC, total drug, free drug, and unconjugated antibody, each contributing differently to overall PK/PD behavior [17].

To date, no clinical PK/PD data have been reported for SC ADCs. The most comprehensive preclinical PK/PD comparison of SC versus IV ADC delivery was conducted by Chang et al. using trastuzumab–vc–monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) in NCI-N87 xenograft models [12]. In this study, SC administration resulted in substantially lower systemic and tumor exposure than IV dosing, with plasma and tumor area under the curve (AUC) values reaching only ~50%–80% of those achieved intravenously at the same dose. This reduced exposure translated into inferior antitumor efficacy, consistent with the limited SC bioavailability (~50%) observed in mice. In addition, SC dosing was associated with pronounced local injection-site toxicity, characterized by significant skin and tissue damage. This effect was primarily attributed to the MMAE payload rather than the antibody, likely driven by slow lymphatic absorption, prolonged local retention, and premature payload release in the protease-rich skin environment. Collectively, these findings imply that, for MMAE-based ADCs, SC administration may compromise efficacy while increasing the risk of local toxicity compared with IV delivery.

In contrast, Decker and colleagues demonstrated that SC administration of amatoxin-based ADCs can improve the therapeutic index relative to IV dosing [18]. Across multiple studies in mice and cynomolgus monkeys, SC dosing consistently reduced Cmax and extended systemic half-life while maintaining comparable overall exposure (AUC). As toxicity correlated primarily with Cmax and efficacy with AUC, SC administration resulted in higher maximum tolerated doses and improved tolerability without loss of antitumor activity across several tumor models. These data suggest that the suitability of SC delivery for ADCs is highly payload-dependent and that, for amatoxin-based conjugates, SC administration represents a promising and potentially translatable alternative to IV dosing.

Nonclinical research and emerging clinical evidence for subcutaneously administered antibody-drug conjugates

To date, 12 ADCs have entered preclinical or Phase I/II clinical evaluation via the SC route, as summarized in Table 1. Of these, eight programs remain active, while four have been discontinued or withdrawn during clinical development. Of the 12 ADCs, 10 are oncology indications, while two are developed for non-oncology indications. Strategies enabling SC administration of ADCs broadly fall into four categories: (i) Rational molecular design of ADCs, including site-specific conjugation, enhanced linker stability and reduced hydrophobicity, to improve local tolerability, systemic bioavailability and developability of high-concentration liquid formulations; (ii) co-formulation with hyaluronidase to enhance SC dispersion and absorption with increased injection volume; (iii) polymer-based delivery strategy; (iv) conversion of investigational or approved traditional ADCs from IV to SC administration.

Oncology subcutaneous antibody-drug conjugates

Rational molecular design

JSKN033, comprising JSKN003 and envafolimab, represents the first globally reported SC high-concentration liquid co-formulation of an ADC and an antibody, developed using the rational molecular design strategy [19–21]. Each 1 ml vial of JSKN033 contains 80 mg of JSKN003 and 100 mg of envafolimab [22]. JSKN033 is stable in liquid formulation primarily due to the inherent superior solubility and stability of its components (JSKN003 and envafolimab), and the use of site-specific conjugation technology for the ADC components [19].

The suitability of JSKN033 for SC administration are driven by several complementary factors [19]. First, JSKN033 is a co-formulation that includes envafolimab, a single-domain antibody that is the world’s first approved SC PD-L1 inhibitor. Single-domain antibodies are known to be highly soluble and stable compared to conventional full mAbs, which enables the high-concentration liquid formulation required for SC injection [23]. Second, the ADC entity, JSKN003, is generated using site-specific conjugation to the Fc glycans of the parental antibody anbenitamab (KN026), resulting in a homogeneous and stable ADC with a well-controlled DAR. This site-specific conjugation strategy mitigates the increased hydrophobicity and aggregation propensity commonly associated with heterogeneous conjugation methods, thereby enhancing stability in liquid formulations. In addition, JSKN-033’s developer, Alphamab Oncology, has established a high-concentration formulation platform specifically designed to address challenges related to protein stability and elevated viscosity in concentrated solutions, through the optimized selection of excipients and formulation technologies [19]. Together, these features enable JSKN033 to be formulated as a stable, high-concentration liquid co-formulation, supporting rapid SC administration and offering greater convenience compared with IV infusions or SC approaches that require prolonged administration or complex reconstitution.

Preclinical studies demonstrated favorable bioavailability and safety of JSKN033, supporting further clinical evaluation [20]. In the first-in-human (FIH) Phase I study (JSKN033–101, NCT06226766), which began in January 2024 and enrolled 11 heavily pretreated patients, subcutaneously administered JSKN033 exhibited a favorable safety profile and encouraging antitumor activity. These early clinical findings highlight the therapeutic potential of combining an ADC with immunotherapy in a single SC regimen and support continued clinical development [20–22]. A JSKN033 case study from the FIH also highlighted key limitations of SC ADC administration, most notably a high incidence of injection-site reactions (ISRs). In early dose-escalation data, ~90% of patients experienced ISRs, albeit predominantly mild and manageable [22]. This case study showed both localized reactions, pruritus, pain, induration, and skin changes, and systemic adverse events such as fatigue and diarrhea. Current clinical data indicates that ISRs are the most frequently reported adverse events associated with SC-administered JSKN033, primarily manifesting as localized erythema, swelling, pain and pruritus. Most ISRs are mild in severity and typically resolved without intervention or can be effectively managed with physician-directed antihistamine therapy when necessary [22].

A subsequent Phase I/II study (JSKN033–102, NCT06770881) evaluating the safety, tolerability, PK/PD, and antitumor activity of subcutaneously administered JSKN033 in Chinese patients with advanced malignant tumors was initiated in January 2025. Further clinical advancement includes a Phase II study in advanced non–small cell lung cancer, launched in September 2025 in China (JSKN033–201, CTR20253823). The dosing regimen consists of an initial weekly (QW) loading phase followed by biweekly (Q2W) administration at predefined doses (8.4 or 6.7 mg/kg). The primary objective is to assess safety and efficacy as the key endpoint, while secondary objectives include additional efficacy measures, PK, and immunogenicity. The clinical study outcomes are expected to become available in 2027, which will further inform the clinical utility of this novel subcutaneously administered bispecific ADC–immunotherapy combination.

JSKN016 is a first-in-class bispecific ADC developed by Alphamab Oncology that simultaneously targets TROP2 and HER3, two receptors frequently overexpressed in breast and lung cancers, and is administered intravenously. Built on proprietary single-domain antibody and bispecific antibody platforms, JSKN016 is conjugated via site-specific glycosylation to generate a homogeneous and stable ADC with a defined DAR of 4. Upon binding to TROP2 and HER3 on tumor cells, JSKN016 undergoes cellular endocytosis and releases a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, resulting in potent antitumor activity. As of early 2026, JSKN016 has advanced to Phase III clinical development, with a pivotal trial (JSKN016–301, NCT07533123) initiated in triple-negative breast cancer following strong efficacy observed in early-phase studies (JSKN016–101, NCT06592417), including high objective response rates in heavily pretreated HER2-negative breast cancer patients and a manageable safety profile without reported interstitial lung disease [24].

JSKN016HC is a subcutaneously injectable, high-concentration liquid formulation of JSKN016 designed to enable convenient, low-volume SC administration compared with IV infusion. The “HC” formulation is a single-agent, high-concentration liquid ADC optimized for SC delivery, rather than a co-formulated combination product, reflecting the molecule’s intrinsic dual-targeting architecture. By enabling SC administration, JSKN016HC has the potential to improve patient convenience and expand treatment flexibility while preserving the dual-targeting mechanism and payload delivery of the parent ADC. Collectively, JSKN016HC represents a formulation advancement intended to complement the clinical efficacy of JSKN016 and support broader application of bispecific ADCs in outpatient or non-hospital settings. A Phase I clinical study to evaluate the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics/ pharmacodynamics, and anti-tumor activity of JSKN016HC in subjects with advanced malignant solid tumors (JSKN016HC-101, NCT07475559) by SC administration was initiated in March 2026.

ADCs are frequently combined with mAbs, most commonly immune checkpoint inhibitors, to enhance therapeutic efficacy through synergistic mechanisms, overcome tumor resistance, and improve patient outcomes. By integrating targeted cytotoxic payload delivery with immune activation, these regimens attack tumors through complementary pathways while maintaining a manageable safety profile [25]. Numerous such combination regimens pairing an ADC with an antibody are currently under clinical investigation or approved by regulatory agencies. Examples include Enhertu plus Keytruda, Polivy plus Rituxan, Zynlonta plus Rituxan, and Elahere plus Avastin, and the FDA-approved combination of Padcev and Keytruda [7]. However, these ADC–antibody combination therapies to date rely on sequential- or co-administration of separate products. In contrast, JSKN033 represents the only clinically staged therapy globally that co-formulates an ADC and an antibody into a single high-concentration liquid product, highlighting a potentially transformative approach to combination therapy development. Co-formulated therapies are designed to enhance clinical benefit, improve patient convenience, and extend intellectual property protection, supporting long-term return on investment. The growing development of biologic-based co-formulations, such as mixed antibodies, insulin combinations, and antibody cocktails for hematologic disorders, reflects increasing industry interest in integrating multiple therapeutic agents into a single dosage form. By simplifying administration compared with separate dosing, co-formulated products can also improve patient adherence and overall treatment experience [26].

GQ1007 is a next generation HER2-targeting antibody-immune agonist conjugate developed using GeneQuantum’s proprietary, highly stable and hydrophilic linker together with site-specific, homogenous enzymatic conjugation technologies. It is designed to deliver a toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) agonist to tumors, enabling potent localized immune activation while minimizing systemic toxicity. Preclinical studies demonstrated strong antitumor activity, robust tumor-specific immune stimulation, excellent plasma stability with no detectable release of free agonist after 96 hours at 37 °C, and a favorable safety profile in GLP monkey toxicology studies, supporting its advancement into clinical testing [27]. GQ1007 entered a FIH Phase 1 study (ACTRN12622000513718) consisting of four sub-studies evaluating SC administration as monotherapy or in combination with the PD-L1 antibody envafolimab, with the primary objective of assessing safety and tolerability in patients with advanced HER2-expressing cancers. Despite its promising preclinical profile and rational clinical design, the study was withdrawn in September 2022 by the sponsor ~6 months after trial registration, due to a strategic shift in company priorities.

Co-formulation with hyaluronidase

Co-formulation with hyaluronidase has been used to enable large-volume SC delivery of antibody therapeutics since 2013, and nine antibody–hyaluronidase combination products have since been approved by the FDA and EMA [8]. These products were originally developed for IV administration and later adapted for SC delivery to enhance patient convenience and, in some cases, extend product life cycles. Building on this precedent, the first ADC–hyaluronidase co-formulation program was initiated in 2024 through a collaboration between Daiichi Sankyo and Alteogen [28]. Enhertu (fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan), a HER2-directed ADC combining trastuzumab with a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, was initially approved by the U.S. FDA in 2019 for IV administration [7]. To enable SC delivery, Daiichi Sankyo, in collaboration with Alteogen, is developing a formulation using the Hybrozyme platform incorporating recombinant human hyaluronidase (ALT-B4) to transiently degrade hyaluronan in the SC extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating larger injection volumes and enhancing absorption [28]. A clinical study evaluating SC Enhertu in patients with metastatic solid tumors (NCT07015697) was initiated in July 2025 by Daiichi Sankyo and AstraZeneca. If successful, this program would represent the first SC-administered ADC enabled by hyaluronidase technology, extending a strategy previously validated for unconjugated mAbs to more structurally complex ADCs.

Alteogen has recently reported that converting ADCs to SC administration using its hyaluronidase-based platform, ALT-B4, enables more convenient and rapid dosing while generating PK profiles characterized by a gradual increase in systemic ADC exposure, which may reduce adverse effects associated with the cytotoxic payload [29]. Similarly, preclinical studies involving three ADCs demonstrated that co-formulation with HyDIFFUZE™, a recombinant human hyaluronidase–based platform developed by Huonslab, supports its potential as a broadly applicable SC delivery platform for ADCs [30].

Polymer-based hydrogel

A polymer-based strategy for SC ADC delivery has been demonstrated in a nonclinical feasibility study by Detappe [31]. In this work, a zwitterionic chitosan-based hydrogel was developed to enable controlled and sustained SC delivery of ADCs, using Kadcyla (T-DM1, ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a HER2-targeted ADC, as a model. The hydrogel successfully encapsulated IV-formulated Kadcyla and enabled SC administration with systemic exposure (AUC) comparable to IV dosing. Preclinical evaluations in mice and human skin explants showed good biocompatibility and substantially reduced local inflammatory responses relative to direct SC injection of Kadcyla without a carrier. Importantly, in a HER2-positive xenograft model, SC administration of the hydrogel-formulated Kadcyla achieved antitumor efficacy comparable to the IV route, with ongoing studies focused on scalability and larger injection volumes in nonhuman models. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of reformulating IV-approved ADCs for SC delivery using polymer-based systems to mitigate local toxicity while preserving efficacy.

Conversion of investigational or approved traditional ADCs from IV to SC administration

Among the various approaches explored, conversion of investigational or approved traditional ADCs from IV to SC administration was one of the earliest strategies investigated. Two patents published in 2018 by Immunomedics (US10799597B2 and WO 2018/187074A1) disclosed methods to enable SC delivery of conventionally IV-administered ADCs, targeting antigens such as Trop-2, HER2, CD20, and CEACAM family members, with SN-38 used as the cytotoxic payload. SC sacituzumab govitecan (IMMU-132) was evaluated in patients with triple-negative breast cancer, metastatic colorectal cancer, and gastric cancer. Dosing regimens of 2–4 mg/kg, given daily for one week or two to three times weekly for two weeks, were generally well tolerated. SC administration of these ADCs was found to be at least as effective as IV dosing, without causing unmanageable injection-site toxicity, and local erythema observed in some patients resolved spontaneously within a few weeks [32, 33].

A Phase II clinical study (PHERGAIN-2; NCT04733118) converting IV Kadcyla to SC administration was initiated in Europe in 2021, with a primary completion date anticipated in March 2027. PHERGAIN-2 is a multicenter, open-label, single-arm, one-stage Phase II trial designed to assess the efficacy of a chemotherapy-free, pathological complete response–guided treatment strategy in patients with previously untreated HER2-positive early breast cancer. The regimen combines subcutaneously administered Phesgo (a co-formulation of trastuzumab, pertuzumab and hyaluronidase) with a separately administered subcutaneous Kadcyla, aiming to determine whether a pathological complete response-guided approach can achieve favorable clinical outcomes while avoiding conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy.

Avida (Trastuzumab rezetecan, SHR-A1811) is an ADC composed of the humanized anti-HER2 IgG1 mAb trastuzumab linked to a DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor (DXh, an exatecan derivative) via an enzyme-cleavable, maleimide-containing tetrapeptide linker. Incorporation of a chiral cyclopropyl group at the α-carbonyl position between the linker and payload enhances conjugate stability and limits premature payload release in circulation. In May 2025, Avida was approved by the China National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for adults with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic HER2-mutant non-small cell lung cancer who have received at least one prior systemic therapy, and is currently administered by IV infusion [34, 35]. To expand dosing options, an open-label, multi-center Phase I study (SHR-A1811(sc)-101) has been initiated in China to evaluate the PK, safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy of a SC formulation in patients with solid tumors (NCT07275242, CTR20254754).

Pertuzumab zuvotolimod (SBT6050) is a HER2-targeted immune agonist conjugate consisting of the anti-HER2 mAb pertuzumab linked to a selective TLR8 agonist. It was designed to activate myeloid cells within tumors expressing moderate to high levels of HER2, thereby secondarily engaging NK and T cells and amplifying antitumor immunity through enhanced antigen presentation, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis [36–38].

In the Phase 1/1b clinical studies (NCT04460456), 58 patients received SBT6050 as monotherapy or in combination with a checkpoint inhibitor at SC dose levels ranging from 0.15 to 1.2 mg/kg, with treatment durations of up to 41 weeks [37, 38]. Dose-dependent increases in systemic and intratumoral exposure and robust PD evidence of immune activation, including biomarker changes in on-treatment biopsies, were observed. However, antitumor activity as monotherapy was limited, and cytokine-related adverse events constrained dose escalation and combination with pembrolizumab. Following a comprehensive review of preclinical and clinical data, development of SBT6050, including subsequent Phase 1/2 studies of SC administration (NCT05091528), and the related SBT6290 program (a Nectin4-targeted conjugate sharing the same linker–payload) was discontinued [38–40]. Notably, the available data do not clearly attribute program termination to the SC route of administration.

Non-oncology subcutaneous antibody-drug conjugates

The importance of optimizing ADC design to enable SC administration is exemplified by the development of ABBV-3373 and its successor ABBV-154, as well as LFD-200, which represent notable examples of subcutaneously administered ADCs in non-oncology indications. ABBV-3373, a non-oncology ADC composed of the anti–tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF) mAb adalimumab conjugated to a glucocorticoid receptor modulator [41], demonstrated clinical efficacy in a Phase 2a arthritis study [42]. However, DAR4 conjugation substantially increased hydrophobicity, resulting in significant aggregation (up to 21% aggregate formation after 3 weeks at 40 °C) [43], which precluded high-concentration SC formulation and necessitated IV dosing. The findings highlight that identifying ADCs suitable for SC administration requires careful assessment of how conjugation chemistry impacts key physicochemical properties, particularly hydrophobicity, aggregation, and stability at high concentration [44].

Building on this proof of concept in payload–linker chemistry and clinical activity, AbbVie subsequently developed ABBV-154, a next-generation anti-TNF glucocorticoid receptor modulator ADC with substantially improved physicochemical properties. Through optimized linker design and incorporation of a phosphate prodrug payload, hydrophobicity and aggregation were minimized, enabling stable, liquid high-concentration SC dosing (up to 340 mg with exact formulation concentration undisclosed) [45].

In a Phase 2 study in glucocorticoid-dependent polymyalgia rheumatica (NCT04972968) and a Phase 2b study in rheumatoid arthritis refractory to prior biologic or targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) (NCT04888585), ABBV-154 demonstrated pharmacologic activity and superior efficacy versus placebo across multiple endpoints following SC administration, with treatment-emergent adverse event rates comparable to placebo and no new safety signals attributable to the SC route [46, 47].

Despite favorable efficacy and tolerability, clinical development of ABBV-154 was voluntarily discontinued following interim review due to insufficient clinical differentiation versus existing therapies across indications, rather than safety concerns related to the ADC construct or SC administration. Collectively, these programs demonstrate the technical feasibility and tolerability of SC-administered ADCs in non-oncology settings, while underscoring both the high bar for clinical differentiation and the critical role of ADC-level structure–activity relationship and rational drug–linker design in enabling stable, high-concentration formulations suitable for SC delivery [46, 47].

LFD-200 is a next-generation ADC targeting V-domain Ig suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA), a cell-surface protein highly expressed on immune cells and characterized by rapid internalization and intracellular accumulation. The ADC is designed to selectively deliver a potent glucocorticoid to immune cells for the treatment of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. LFD-200 incorporates a protease-cleavable linker that remains stable in circulation but is efficiently cleaved upon intracellular uptake, releasing the active glucocorticoid payload within target immune cells. This design enables targeted and sustained glucocorticoid delivery in immune tissues while minimizing the systemic toxicity commonly associated with conventional steroid therapy [48].

Nonclinical studies in mice and non-human primates demonstrated rapid immune-cell internalization, sustained glucocorticoid exposure in immune tissues for at least 7 days, and robust suppression of proinflammatory cytokines, with no evidence of systemic glucocorticoid toxicity, including no impact on cortisol levels after up to 13 weekly doses [48]. A randomized, double-blind Phase 1 clinical program is currently underway to evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, and PD of SC LFD-200, formulated as a high-concentration solution (150 mg/ml), in healthy participants and patients with moderate-to-severe rheumatoid arthritis using single- and multiple-ascending dose designs (NCT07207954). Enrollment and dosing are ongoing, with initial data from healthy participants anticipated in 2027. Compared with ABBV-154, LFD-200 targets VISTA rather than TNF, and its linker and payload chemistry have not been publicly disclosed [45, 48].

Compared with oncology, publications on SC ADC development in non-oncology indications remain very limited. To date, reported strategies in this area have primarily focused on rational molecular design combined with high-concentration liquid formulations. In contrast, enabling approaches such as polymer-based hydrogels or hyaluronidase co-formulation have not yet been reported in non-oncology ADC programs, reflecting the early stage of exploration in this field while also highlighting a potential opportunity for future innovation.

Current challenges, emerging opportunities, and future perspectives

SC administration of ADCs represents an attractive but technically challenging innovation in drug delivery. Compared with IV infusion, SC delivery offers clear patient-centric advantages, including reduced administration time, improved patient convenience, the potential for outpatient or home-based treatment, and reduced Cmax-related toxicity [8, 49]. The rationale and key considerations for SC ADC administration were comprehensively reviewed by Yaseen and colleagues [49]. However, clinical experience with subcutaneously administered ADCs remains limited and heterogeneous. Emerging data from early-phase programs, including HER2-directed ADCs such as JSKN033 suggest that SC administration is feasible and generally well tolerated, although injection-site reactions are frequently observed and warrant careful evaluation [12–49].

A fundamental limitation in the current field is the incomplete mechanistic understanding of SC PK and PD for ADCs, coupled with the absence of large-scale clinical trials directly comparing efficacy, injection-site reactions, and long-term safety between SC and IV administrations. As a result, it remains challenging to determine whether SC delivery represents a broadly viable option for ADCs or should be restricted to selected modalities and clinical indications. Improved understanding of how molecular properties, formulation parameters, and injection-site physiology interact to govern absorption, disposition, metabolism and elimination of SC-administered ADCs is therefore critical [12, 13, 16, 17].

Unlike unconjugated mAbs, ADCs present unique challenges owing to their cytotoxic payloads, linker-payload stability and hydrophobicity, and complex, multi-component PK and PD behaviors involving total antibody, total ADC, total drug, free drug, and unconjugated antibody [10, 12, 49]. Following SC administration, local precipitation, prolonged local exposure, and pre-systemic catabolism within SC and lymphatic tissues may reduce systemic bioavailability while increasing the risk of injection site toxicity [12, 13]. Proteolytic enzymes present in skin and lymphatic tissues can further promote premature payload release, leading to local tissue injury and inflammation [12, 13]. Differential absorption of ADC-related multiple species to IV dosing also complicates exposure–response relationships [12]. Preclinical studies have reported relatively low SC bioavailability (~50%) and greater local toxicity for ADCs compared with molar-equivalent free payload [12]. However, the translational relevance of these findings required further evaluation due to pronounced interspecies differences in SC PK and the limited predictability of human SC exposure based on current animal models [13, 15].

In addition to molecular and tissue-level factors, procedural and patient-specific variables likely contribute to interpatient variability in local tolerability. Injection volume, injection rate, and site selection or rotation, may influence local tolerability, while patient-related factors such as obesity, baseline inflammatory status, sex, and hormone-related differences in drug metabolism may further modulate the risk of SC injection site reactions [13]. Together, these multifactorial influences underscore the need for more systematic assessment of local tolerability in clinical development.

Formulation challenges constitute a particularly important barrier to SC ADC delivery. Traditional ADCs often exhibit limited aqueous solubility and relatively high hydrophobicity, increasing the risk of injection-site precipitation and impaired absorption following SC administration. Reducing ADC hydrophobicity is therefore essential not only to improve local tolerability and systemic bioavailability but also to enable the high-concentration formulations typically required for SC delivery. For example, the highest approved IV dose of Enhertu (trastuzumab deruxtecan) is 6.4 mg/kg administered every three weeks [7]. Converting this regimen to a 2-ml SC injection in a 75-kg patient would require delivery of ~480 mg at a concentration of ~240 mg/ml, assuming 100% bioavailability, posing substantial formulation and manufacture challenges [8]. One potential mitigation strategy is co-formulation with hyaluronidase to increase the allowable SC injection volume (up to 23 ml as demonstrated by approved co-formulations of naked antibody with hyaluronidase [8]), thereby reducing the required formulation concentration and potentially improving dispersion and absorption. Emerging high-concentration delivery technologies may further expand the feasibility of SC ADC administration [8]. Microparticle suspensions in both aqueous and non-aqueous vehicles have been explored to enable high-concentration protein delivery. Zheng et al. reported an aqueous platform using amorphous antibody-laden hydrogel microparticles, combining solid-state stability with injectability, enabling rapid release and potential application to other therapeutic modalities [50]. Similarly, Elektrofi’s non-aqueous microparticle suspensions (Elektroject™) achieve ultra-high concentrations (>400–500 mg/ml) of mAbs by forming dense, spherical microparticles that overcome viscosity and stability limitations, allowing small-volume, syringeable injections with high bioavailability [51]. Both approaches may be applicable to SC ADCs, providing strategies to increase formulation concentration and stabilize labile molecules for patient-centric SC administration.

The importance of formulation development for ADCs is comprehensively addressed in a recent review by Torres-Dias et al [52]. The authors systematically examine the susceptibility of ADCs to instability across the three key structural domains, the antibody, linker, and payload, and outline domain-specific mitigation strategies for each. They conclude that conventional mAb excipient systems are insufficient to address the unique physicochemical challenges of ADCs, and that ADC-specific, mechanism-driven formulation design, supported by advanced analytical characterization and AI-enabled predictive tools, is critical for improving stability, therapeutic index, and ultimately clinical outcomes.

Despite significant advances in ADC development, many candidates fail clinically due to excessive toxicities and unfavorable risk–benefit profiles, and even approved ADCs frequently require dose reductions or treatment discontinuation because of intolerable adverse effects [53, 54] Toxicities often arise from off-target effects in antigen-negative tissues or on-target off-tumor binding in normal tissues, and can be influenced by each component of ADCs (antibody, linker, and payload). Multiple strategies have been developed to improve therapeutic index, such as site-specific conjugation technologies to better control DAR and enhance stability, optimization of linker–payload chemistry to reduce hydrophobicity and premature drug release, refinement of payload structure, probody and bispecific antibody approaches to enhance tumor selectivity, fractional dosing regimens, and inverse targeting strategies using payload-binding antibody fragments to neutralize released drug in circulation [14]. These efforts highlight the central importance of rational molecular design, particularly linker–payload architecture, in improving ADC tolerability. In the context of SC administration, such optimization becomes even more critical, as linker stability, payload physicochemical properties, and controlled release kinetics directly influence lymphatic absorption, local tissue exposure, systemic pharmacokinetics, and injection-site tolerability. Detappe et al. [55] argue that the barriers to SC delivery of ADCs represent tractable engineering challenges rather than fundamental incompatibilities with the SC route. They identify multiple factors that render conventional mAb excipient systems and delivery devices insufficient for ADCs and outline a set of enabling strategies to overcome these limitations. The authors conclude that successful SC ADC development will require the co-design of the molecule, formulation, material, manufacturing process, and delivery device as a single, integrated platform. A balanced, mechanism-driven design is therefore essential to achieve adequate tumor delivery while minimizing both systemic and local toxicities.

Despite these challenges, available preclinical and early-phase clinical data indicate that SC administration of ADCs can achieve clinically meaningful efficacy with manageable safety profiles. Among the 12 ADCs evaluated for SC administration (see Table 1), three ADCs, Kadcyla, Enhertu, and Avida, are established commercial products originally developed for IV delivery and are now being investigated for SC feasibility. This trend mirrors the development trajectory previously observed for unconjugated antibodies and reflects growing interest in transitioning clinically validated IV ADCs to SC delivery [8]. In parallel, next-generation ADCs incorporating innovative, rational design features, exemplified by JSKN033, which combines site-specific conjugation with a high-concentration liquid co-formulation strategy, are advancing through early clinical development and may ultimately be approved for SC administration, potentially replacing regimens that currently require sequential- or co-administration of separate ADC and immuno-oncology antibodies.

Looking forward, the successful development of subcutaneously administered ADCs will require coordinated advances across molecular engineering (antibody, linker or payload modification), formulation science, and rigorous nonclinical and clinical investigations. Key opportunities include continued optimization of linker-payload chemistry to enhance stability and reduce hydrophobicity; incorporation of delivery technologies such as hyaluronidase to enhance absorption and enable larger injection volumes; application of formulation or polymer-based strategies to mitigate local payload exposure; and conversion of selected traditional ADCs from IV to SC administration. Ultimately, large-scale clinical studies will be critical to establish SC-specific exposure–response relationships, characterize long-term safety with repeated dosing, and compare efficacy and safety relative to IV administration.

Conversion of traditional ADCs from IV to SC administration is particularly challenging and generally requires a stable liquid formulation and/or high concentration (≥ 100 mg/ml) [5, 8]. Despite being well suited for liquid formulation and IV infusion, Elahere is not suitable for SC administration because of dose and injection volume constraints. At a clinical dose of 6 mg/kg, a 75-kg patient would require ~450 mg of mirvetuximab soravtansine, necessitating an impractical SC concentration of ~225 mg/ml assuming a maximum injection volume of 2 ml and 100% bioavailability. This example highlights a key limitation of SC ADC delivery: even when liquid stability is achieved, high therapeutic doses can render SC administration infeasible, underscoring the need for concurrent optimization of potency, dose, formulation concentration, and delivery strategy when transitioning ADCs from IV to SC routes. A similar constraint applies to another liquid ADC product, Akalux (cetuximab sarotalocan sodium) [7], where dose requirements and injection-volume limitations likewise restrict the feasibility of SC injection.

In contrast, Zynlonta (loncastuximab tesirine) is an exceptionally potent ADC with a low clinical dose of 0.15 mg/kg [7], eliminating the need for high-concentration formulations for SC administration. However, despite this favorable dosing profile, Zynlonta is not suitable for liquid formulation because of stability limitations, which necessitate lyophilization.

Taken together, available preclinical and clinical evidence suggests that transitioning ADCs from IV to SC administration is not straightforward and requires careful, case-by-case evaluation of benefit–risk profiles. Nevertheless, as ADC pipelines continue to expand, the integration of rational molecular design with mechanistic, translational, and clinical insights is expected to enable a new generation of ADCs that offer greater patient convenience, improved differentiation, and reduced healthcare burden, signaling a clear future direction for the field of subcutaneously administered ADCs.
