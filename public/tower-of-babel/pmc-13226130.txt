Primary traumatic cutaneous aspergillosis of the hand caused by Aspergillus terreus following a mould-contaminated penetrating injury
Authors: Shan Kai Ing, Yih Hoong Lee, Yuet Yang Tan, Munirah Binti Abdul Aziz, Andrew Kean Wei Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 39-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus developed progressive hand infection after a mould-contaminated penetrating steel injury. Operative debridement revealed sloughy infected tissue, and cultures from pus, tissue, and slough all yielded Aspergillus terreus. There was no evidence of disseminated infection. He improved with surgical source control, voriconazole therapy, and optimisation of glycaemic control. This case highlights traumatic inoculation as an important mechanism of primary cutaneous aspergillosis and the importance of early fungal-directed therapy.

Introduction

Primary cutaneous aspergillosis is an uncommon form of invasive fungal infection resulting from direct inoculation of Aspergillus into disrupted skin or soft tissue. It is most often associated with trauma, burns, occlusive dressings, intravenous access devices, or immunocompromised states [1,2]. However, it may also occur in patients without profound immunosuppression when local tissue injury and environmental contamination coexist [3,4]. Among pathogenic Aspergillus species, Aspergillus terreus is of particular clinical importance because of its invasive potential and reduced susceptibility to amphotericin B [5,6].

Primary traumatic cutaneous aspergillosis of the hand is rare and may initially mimic routine bacterial soft-tissue infection, resulting in delayed diagnosis and treatment. We report a case of primary traumatic cutaneous invasive aspergillosis caused by A. terreus following a mould-contaminated penetrating hand injury in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with a one-week history of progressive swelling and pain of the left hand following a penetrating injury over the third webspace caused by steel. He had defaulted diabetes treatment since 2023. Further history revealed that the steel had been contaminated with grease containing visible mould. He worked as a machine technician and also reported mould contamination in his home environment. He initially sought treatment at a private clinic, where radiography was performed and a one-week course of oral amoxicillin-clavulanate was prescribed. However, the wound continued to worsen.

On examination, there was diffuse swelling and erythema over the dorsum of the left hand, predominantly on the ulnar side, extending to the dorsal aspect of the ring finger. A puncture wound was noted over the third webspace with associated fluctuance (Fig. 1). Finger movement was limited by swelling. He underwent incision and drainage with debridement for a collar-button abscess involving the third webspace, with associated hand cellulitis. Intra-operatively, swelling and fluctuance were noted at the base of the fourth finger involving both the volar and dorsal aspects. Approximately 5 mL of pus was released on incision. The underlying tissue was sloughy, and purulent material tracked dorsally into the third and fourth webspaces. The tendon was intact, and the tendon sheath appeared healthy following release of the A1 pulley. Pending intra-operative culture results, he was treated empirically with intravenous cloxacillin.

Microbiological examination of three separate deep operative specimens yielded concordant results, with cultures from pus, tissue, and slough all growing Aspergillus terreus, identified by Bruker MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Lactophenol cotton blue mounts examined at ×40 magnification demonstrated characteristic biseriate phialides, with metulae and phialides of similar length arising from the upper half of the vesicle and forming compact columnar conidial heads (Fig. 3). On Sabouraud dextrose agar, the isolate showed tan to cinnamon-brown colonies with a velvety to powdery surface on the obverse view (Fig. 4A) and yellow to tan pigmentation on the reverse view (Fig. 4B). The contaminated grease and steel implicated in the injury were unfortunately unavailable for further microbiological analysis. On admission, the total white cell count was 14 × 109/L, platelet count 286 × 109/L, and renal and liver function tests were within normal limits. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with a C-reactive protein of 89.4 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 66 mm/h. Blood bacterial and fungal cultures remained sterile. Screening for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, and VDRL was negative, and the CD4 count was 1708 cells/μL. Formal ophthalmological assessment and abdominal ultrasound showed no evidence of ocular or disseminated fungal infection.

Taken together, the repeated isolation of A. terreus from multiple deep operative specimens, in the setting of a mould-contaminated penetrating injury and sloughy infected tissue, supported the diagnosis of primary traumatic cutaneous invasive aspergillosis. After the intra-operative culture results became available, intravenous voriconazole was initiated on day 5 of admission at 6 mg/kg twice daily for one day, followed by 4 mg/kg twice daily for 4 days, before stepping down to oral voriconazole 200 mg twice daily to complete 6 weeks of therapy. Antibacterial therapy was discontinued, and glycaemic control was optimised with insulin.

He received regular wound care and dressing changes in the ward. On day 8 of admission, a second-look wound debridement with secondary suturing of the left hand was performed. Intra-operatively, the wound was clean, with no necrosis or pus discharge. Repeated tissue culture was sterile. He improved clinically with surgical source control and antifungal treatment, and was discharged on day 11 of admission on oral voriconazole with interval improvement in wound appearance (Fig. 5A). At 6-week follow-up, he remained well, with no new complaints, improved glycaemic control, and a clean, well-healing wound (Fig. 5B). Discussion

This case illustrates primary traumatic cutaneous aspergillosis caused by direct inoculation of Aspergillus terreus into soft tissue following penetrating injury. A limitation of this report is the absence of histopathological confirmation, as no tissue specimen was submitted for histological examination at the time of debridement. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was strongly supported by repeated isolation of A. terreus from multiple deep operative specimens, together with compatible clinical, operative, and microbiological findings.

Cutaneous aspergillosis may occur either as a primary infection following direct inoculation or as a secondary manifestation of haematogenous dissemination [1,2]. In our patient, the absence of systemic features, sterile blood and fungal cultures, normal ophthalmological assessment, unremarkable abdominal ultrasound, and clinically localised disease all favoured primary traumatic inoculation. Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus likely acted as a predisposing factor by impairing host defence and wound healing.

Aspergillus terreus belongs to section Terrei within subgenus Circumdati. This taxonomic placement is relevant because accurate species-level identification supports appropriate antifungal selection [7]. Aspergillus terreus is therapeutically important because it is associated with reduced susceptibility to amphotericin B [5,6]. Accordingly, early recognition of the infecting species has direct treatment implications. Voriconazole remains a standard first-line agent for invasive aspergillosis, while surgical debridement is essential in localised traumatic infection to reduce fungal burden, establish the diagnosis, and achieve source control [6,[8], [9], [10]]. In the present case, combined debridement, voriconazole therapy, and optimisation of glycaemic control were associated with favourable early clinical response.

Traumatic cutaneous mould infection also raises the important differential diagnosis of cutaneous mucormycosis, which is a well-recognised post-traumatic fungal infection [11]. Trauma is a common mode of transmission in cutaneous mucormycosis, and both conditions may present with progressive soft tissue infection after penetrating injury. However, the repeated isolation of Aspergillus terreus from multiple deep operative specimens, together with the characteristic microscopic and colony morphology, supported cutaneous aspergillosis rather than mucormycosis in our patient. This distinction is clinically important because voriconazole is appropriate for aspergillosis but is not recommended for mucormycosis, for which amphotericin B-based therapy remains the preferred first-line treatment [6,12].

The initial presentation closely resembled bacterial hand infection, including abscess formation and cellulitis. This highlights an important diagnostic pitfall: invasive mould infection may be overlooked in post-traumatic wounds, particularly when clinicians anchor on common bacterial causes. Failure to improve with standard antibacterial therapy, together with a history of environmental contamination and the presence of necrotic or sloughy tissue, should prompt fungal investigation using deep operative specimens rather than superficial swabs.

Recent reports suggest that primary cutaneous aspergillosis can occur even outside the setting of profound immunosuppression, including after local trauma or skin disruption [3,4]. Our case adds to the limited literature on primary traumatic cutaneous aspergillosis caused by A. terreus by demonstrating repeated deep culture confirmation, supportive microscopic and colony morphology, exclusion of disseminated disease, and good early response to surgical source control plus voriconazole. These features strengthen the clinicomicrobiological evidence that this represented true invasive disease acquired through traumatic inoculation.
