Fulminant disseminated histoplasmosis in a pediatric patient with ataxia-telangiectasia and autoimmune hepatitis: A diagnostic and therapeutic challenge
Authors: Lía Esthefany Loredo Paz, Andrea Michelle Pineda Velasquez, María Regina Rodríguez Rosales, Sara Eloisa Rivera Molina, Scheybi Teresa Miralda Méndez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisseminated histoplasmosis (DH) is a common opportunistic mycosis in endemic regions, yet it remains underreported in pediatric patients with primary immunodeficiency or acquired immunosuppression. We present the clinical progression and fatal outcome of DH in a 12-year-old female from southeastern Honduras with an established diagnosis of Ataxia-Telangiectasia (AT) and Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) managed with daily prednisone (1 mg/kg/day) and azathioprine (2 mg/kg/day), presented with fever, non-productive cough and progressive hypoxemic respiratory failure. Despite broad-spectrum antimicrobials, she rapidly developed severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), culminating in irreversible cardiorespiratory arrest 24 h after ICU admission. Diagnosis of DH was confirmed via enzyme immunoassay (EIA) demonstrating high levels of antigenuria (15 ng/mL; threshold for high-level >5 ng/mL). This case highlights a critical therapeutic paradox where standard management for an autoimmune complication fully unmasked a lethal fungal infection in a vulnerable host. DH remains a notorious diagnostic mimicker in resource-limited settings where atypical presentation and delayed culture processing restrict timely polyene antifungal intervention. In endemic territories, proactive baseline screening for endemic mycoses and strict primary surveillance are mandatory before executing or maintaining intensive immunosuppression in pediatric patients with congenital immune dysregulation.Keywords: Histoplasmosis, Ataxia-Telangiectasia, Autoimmune Hepatitis, Immunosuppressive Therapy, Endemic, Congenital immune dysregulation

Introduction

Ataxia-Telangiectasia (AT) is a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of inmmunity (IEI) characterized by combined T- and B-lymphocyte dysfunction, progressive cerebellar ataxia, oculocutaneous telangiectasias and heightened susceptibility to opportunistic infections and malignancy [1], [2]. The underlying immune dysregulation in AT not only predisposes patients to severe infections, but also to autoimmune manifestations, such as autoimmune hepatitis, which frequently necessitate additional immunosuppressive therapy, further compounding infectious risk.

Histoplasmosis is a systemic fungal infection caused by the dimorphic fungus Histoplasma capsulatum, acquired primarily through inhalation of microconidia in contaminated soil. Honduras is among the countries in Latin America with the highest endemic burden of histoplasmosis, yet pediatric cases, particularly those occurring in the context of IEI, remain significantly underreported in the regional literature [3].

In endemic regions with limited diagnostic resources, disseminated histoplasmosis poses significant diagnostic challenges, as its clinical presentation closely mimics other conditions such as tuberculosis or Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. This diagnostic overlap, combined with aggressive clinical course seen in immunocompromised pediatric patients, often results in delayed targeted therapy and fatal outcomes [4], [5], [6].

We report a fatal case of disseminated histoplasmosis in a 12-year-old female with AT and autoimmune hepatitis on chronic immunosuppressive therapy, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, immunological complexity, and limited therapeutic options faced in resource-constrained endemic settings.

Case presentation

History and physical examination

A 12-year-old female patient from the southeastern region of Honduras with ataxia-telangiectasia (AT; diagnosed May 2025) and autoimmune hepatitis (AIH; diagnosed October 2025, characterized by elevated liver enzymes and positive anti-LKM−1 antibodies) (Table 1), receiving chronic immunosuppressive therapy with oral prednisone (1 mg/kg/daily) and azathioprine (2 mg/kg/daily). She was not receiving any antimicrobial prophylaxis against opportunistic infections. She presented to the pediatric emergency department with a 9-day history of fever (38.0°C) and a non-productive cough, with subsequent progression to respiratory distress.

Upon admission, physical examination revealed tachypnea, subcostal retractions, and bilateral fine crackles on lung auscultation. Neurological assessment confirmed oculocutaneous telangiectasias and progressive cerebellar ataxia. Following evaluation by the pediatric and pediatric immunology team, she was admitted to the general pediatric ward for close observation.

Diagnostic workup and clinical progression

Initial laboratory investigations showed leukopenia with neutropenia (Table 1), a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 24 mg/L, and a procalcitonin level of 0.276 ng/mL. Given the suspicion of community-acquired pneumonia, empirical antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone and azithromycin was initiated.

Within six hours of admission, her respiratory status deteriorated significantly, presenting with pulmonary hypoventilation and marked respiratory distress, necessitating transfer back to the emergency stabilization unit. Chest radiography demonstrated diffuse bilateral infiltrates (Fig. 1). Due to her profound immunosuppression and lack of clinical response, a strategy to address opportunistic pathogens is adopted. Tuberculosis screening with Xpert MTB/RIF (GeneXpert), fungal cultures, and urine Histoplasma antigen testing were requested. Further immunological profiling revealed severe hypogammaglobulinemia (Table 1).

Intensive care and outcome

Despite these interventions, on day 11 of illness (12 h after admission) the patient developed acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), requiring transfer to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) for non-invasive mechanical ventilation with escalation of antimicrobial therapy to piperacillin/tazobactam (100 mg/kg/dose). Histoplasma antigen screening revealed antigenuria of 15 ng/mL, prompting initiation of liposomal amphotericin B (3 mg/kg/day) on hospital day two (day 12 of illness).Twenty-four hours after PICU admission, the patient suffered rapid multi-organ failure followed by an irreversible cardio-respiratory arrest.

This case underscores the high mortality rate of disseminated histoplasmosis in patients with primary immunodeficiencies. It further highlights the diagnostic challenges in endemic regions where limited resources may hinder timely diagnosis and life-saving management.

Discussion and conclusions

Primary immunodeficiency, autoimmune comorbidity, and fatal opportunistic mycosis in this 12-year-old patient illustrates a profound clinical and therapeutic paradox. Disseminated histoplasmosis (DH) is an infection caused by the dimorphic fungus Histoplasma capsulatum, the severity of which depends on the patient’s immune status. In pediatrics, primary immunodeficiencies such as AT dramatically increase the risk of fatal forms of the disease. Patients with AT have a combined immunodeficiency (both T and B lymphocytes are deficient), which facilitates the simultaneous development of opportunistic infections and autoimmune diseases [2]. In our case, this was reflected in the coexistence of AT with autoimmune hepatitis and the subsequent fungal infection.

Clinically, our patient rapidly progressed to ARDS. Children with immune deficiencies present with much more severe disease in endemic areas. In this immunocompromised host, the manifestation of AIH created a dual-edged clinical dilemma. Management guidelines require aggressive immunosuppression, chronic corticosteroids and high-dose azathioprine contributed to debilitating her immunity [3].

Regarding the infectious diagnosis, DH is recognized as a 'great mimicker.' In Honduras, it is frequently misdiagnosed as tuberculosis due to overlapping clinical features, such as fever and pulmonary symptoms. In our patient, the diagnosis was confirmed by an antigenuria level of 15 ng/mL. According to the Antonio Vidal Research Institute in Honduras, high levels indicate a very large fungal load in the body and predicts a poor prognosis [3]. Although liposomal amphotericin B was initiated, the patient died, if treatment begins when the patient is already in septic shock, the chances of survival are very low [4].

The diagnostic trajectory of DH in pediatric patients with inborn errors of immunity (IEI) is frequently obscured by its clinical resemblance to other granulomatous diseases, particularly tuberculosis and community-acquired pneumonia [9]. In resource-limited endemic regions like Honduras, this diagnostic mimicry often leads to fatal delays in initiating targeted therapy [9]. Antigenuria detection has emerged as the preferred laboratory assay due to its high sensitivity (95%) and specificity (97%) in immunocompromised hosts, as it allows for rapid detection without the delays inherent in fungal cultures [10]. While the threshold for a "high-level" fungal load is typically defined as > 5 ng/mL, the exceptionally high antigenuria of 15 ng/mL observed in our patient serves as a potent clinical marker of an overwhelming fungal burden and is a strong predictor of poor prognosis. This extreme antigen load, coupled with the patient's underlying T- and B-cell deficiency, likely facilitated the rapid progression from a non-productive cough to fulminant multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) [10].

The management of severe DH in vulnerable pediatric populations necessitates a rapid, dual-phase approach, ideally starting with induction therapy using liposomal amphotericin B (3 mg/kg/day) for at least two weeks, followed by long-term maintenance with itraconazole for up to 12 months [7], [9], [10]. However, our case illustrates a critical therapeutic paradox: the aggressive use of high-dose corticosteroids and azathioprine for autoimmune hepatitis inadvertently disabled the host's cellular immunity, facilitating the "unmasking" of a lethal fungal infection [7]. Corticosteroids are sometimes used to manage ARDS or severe hypoxemia, their use alongside itraconazole creates a high risk for iatrogenic Cushing’s syndrome [7], [8]. This occurs because itraconazole potently inhibits the CYP3A4 pathway, thereby significantly decreasing steroid metabolism and increasing systemic exposure [8]. Ultimately, when targeted antifungal intervention is delayed until the patient has reached septic shock, survival rates remain distressingly low, reinforcing the imperative for proactive screening and early empirical treatment in endemic zones [9].

This case demonstrates the fatal synergy between congenital immune dysregulation, pharmacological immunosuppression and environmental exposure to dimorphic endemic fungi territories like Honduras. We strongly advocate for the implementation of baseline screening for endemic mycosis in vulnerable hosts, empirical antifungal intervention represents the sole barrier against a fulminant outcome.
