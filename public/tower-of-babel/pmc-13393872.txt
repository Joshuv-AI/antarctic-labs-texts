The effect of yoga on the level of burnout and happiness in midwifery students: randomized controlled study
Authors: Ayşe Çataloluk, Vasviye Eroğlu, Özgür Alparslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the demanding nature of midwifery education, it is important to examine whether yoga can positively influence students’ burnout and happiness levels. This study aimed to examine the levels of burnout and happiness among midwifery students who practice yoga and those who do not.MethodsThe research was conducted using a randomized controlled experimental design. A total of 74 s-year students from the Midwifery Department at Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University, who met the eligibility criteria, were randomly assigned to the experimental (n = 35) and control (n = 37) groups. The experimental group participated in a yoga program consisting of 18 sessions over six weeks, with three sessions per week. Data were collected using the Maslach Burnout Inventory – Student Scale (MBI-SS) and the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire short form-OHQ-SF. Parametric tests, correlation, and logistic regression methods were used for analysis.ResultsStudents practicing yoga had significantly lower scores in “Exhaustion” and “Cynicism”, and significantly higher scores in “Efficacy” and “Happiness” (p < 0.001). Yoga practice was found to significantly predict happiness levels (p = 0.001), while no direct effect on burnout levels was determined.ConclusionYoga practice increases happiness levels among midwifery students. It is recommended to integrate mindfulness-based practices such as yoga into educational programs to help students cope with academic and professional stress.Trial registrationNCT07321132. Introduction

The healthcare field is a highly stressful environment, not only for professionals but also for students continuing their education. Due to factors such as intensive theoretical knowledge, demanding practical training, shifts, clinical practice, and patient responsibility, students experience significant stress. This situation particularly increases burnout levels among midwifery students, who have high responsibilities regarding women’s health, childbirth, and neonatal care. It can also negatively affect their psychological well-being and happiness [1, 2].

Burnout was first defined by Freudenberger as physical and emotional exhaustion observed in healthcare workers under prolonged stress [3]. Maslach later conceptualized burnout in three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment [4]. Initially considered a condition specific to healthcare professionals, burnout has extended to other occupations, leading to the term “occupational burnout” [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes burnout as an occupational phenomenon and included it in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) [6]. Recently, burnout has also been studied in education, particularly among university students, under the term “school burnout.” This concept includes physical, emotional, and cognitive symptoms related to academic demands and school-related stress [7]. Burnout among midwifery students may result in decreased academic performance, reduced life satisfaction, and increased intention to leave the profession [8].

Conversely, higher happiness levels are associated with greater resilience, productivity, and adaptability in academic and social life [9]. Interventions aimed at supporting students’ mental health and enhancing coping skills often include mindfulness-based practices [10]. Yoga, a mindfulness-based practice, is not only physical exercise but also incorporates breathing techniques, relaxation, meditation, and mind-body awareness. Research demonstrates that yoga reduces stress, anxiety, depression, and burnout while increasing happiness, self-awareness, and life satisfaction [11–13].

Recent studies suggest that mindfulness-based interventions such as yoga and meditation effectively support midwifery and nursing students in managing occupational and academic stress, enhancing psychological resilience, and reducing anxiety [14–16]. However, randomized controlled trials on this topic remain limited in Turkey. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of yoga on burnout and happiness levels among midwifery students by establishing experimental and control groups, thereby contributing uniquely to the literature.

Hypotheses of the research

Hypothesis 1 (H10): Yoga has no effect on the burnout levels of midwifery students.

Hypothesis 1 (H11): Yoga has an effect on the burnout levels of midwifery students.

Hypothesis 2 (H20): Yoga has no effect on the happiness levels of midwifery students.

Hypothesis 2 (H21): Yoga has an effect on the happiness levels of midwifery students.

Materials and methods

Type of research

The research was conducted as a randomized controlled experimental study.

Place and time of the research

The research was conducted face-to-face with second-year students in the Midwifery Department of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University between October 23, 2023, and December 16, 2023, for a total of 18 sessions (6 weeks; 1 h each week, Monday, Wednesday, and Friday). Yoga sessions were scheduled according to the students’ class schedules and availability. Written permission was obtained from the relevant Department Head for the sessions to be conducted in the Pregnancy Education Laboratory of the Midwifery Department of the Faculty of Health Sciences at Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University.

Research population and sample

The study population consisted of second-year students (N = 93) studying in the Department of Midwifery at the Faculty of Health Sciences at Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University. Second-year students were specifically recruited because this period marks the commencement of clinical practice in the midwifery curriculum, a documented high-stress transition phase. By focusing on a single academic year, we also aimed to ensure a homogeneous sample regarding academic workload and clinical exposure, thereby reducing potential confounding variables.

The study sample consisted of second-year students who were attending face-to-face formal education during the study period and who agreed to participate in the study. Six students who declined to participate, six students who exercised regularly at least two days a week, and seven students who could not attend scheduled yoga practice times were excluded from the study.

The remaining eligible students (n = 74) were randomly assigned to the experimental and control groups. During the process, two students in the experimental group were excluded because they did not regularly participate in the yoga sessions. The study was completed with 35 students in the experimental group and 37 students in the control group (Fig. 1).

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

The randomization sequence was generated using Randomizer.org by an independent researcher who was not involved in the intervention or outcome assessment processes.

Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. These envelopes were prepared by the same independent researcher and were opened only after the participants had completed baseline assessments. Therefore, neither the participants nor the researchers were aware of the group assignments prior to allocation.

Due to the nature of the yoga intervention, blinding of participants and the instructor delivering the intervention was not feasible. However, to minimize potential bias, outcome assessors were kept unaware of the intervention stages during data collection, and the researcher performing the statistical analysis was blinded to group allocation (data-analyst blinding).

Inclusion criteria

Students who did not engage in physical exercise at least two days a week, were not practicing yoga or any other mindfulness-based activity, and were able to attend the scheduled yoga sessions at the designated location were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Students with musculoskeletal, neuromuscular, or chronic systemic conditions that could prevent them from practicing yoga; those who had undergone recent surgical procedures; and those who did not consent to participate in the study were excluded.

Withdrawal criteria

Students who initially agreed to participate but chose to withdraw during the study period, or who failed to attend the yoga sessions regularly (i.e., missed more than three sessions), were removed from the study.

Data collection tools

In this study, the data collection tools included a Socio-demographic Information Form, the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Student Survey (MBI-SS), and the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire–Short Form (OHQ-SF).

Socio-demographic information form

The form was developed by the researchers based on a review of the relevant literature. The form includes a total of 11 items addressing students’ age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), income level, place of residence, satisfaction with their department, reason for choosing the department, participation in social activities, average daily sleep duration, and academic performance [1, 2].

MBI-SS

The level of burnout was measured using the MBI-SS. Originally adapted from the MBI-General Survey by Schaufeli et al. [17] to the student context by Schaufeli et al. [18], the scale’s Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Çapri et al. [19]. The scale consists of 13 items and includes three subscales: exhaustion, cynicism, and efficacy. The exhaustion subscale assesses chronic fatigue and tension resulting from academic responsibilities; cynicism reflects indifference toward school-related activities and a detached, disengaged attitude toward academic tasks; and efficacy measures the tendency of students to evaluate their academic performance negatively and to perceive themselves as academically inadequate. In the original adaptation study, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients for the subscales were reported as 0.76 (exhaustion), 0.82 (cynicism), and 0.61 (efficacy). The efficacy subscale is reverse-scored, and high scores on exhaustion and cynicism, along with low scores on efficacy, indicate higher levels of burnout. For each participant, separate scores are calculated for all three subscales [19]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the exhaustion, cynicism, and efficacy subscales were found to be 0.94, 0.91, and 0.80, respectively. Official retrospective permission for the use of the MBI-SS was obtained from the publisher, Mind Garden, Inc.

OHQ-SF

The form is a shortened version of the original 29-item Oxford Happiness Inventory, developed by Argyle and Hills as a 4-point Likert-type scale (0–3) [20]. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was conducted by Doğan and Çötok to assess individuals’ levels of happiness [21]. The adapted version initially included 8 items; however, the 4th item was removed due to a low item-total correlation in item analysis. As a result, the Turkish version consists of 7 items. While the original scale used a 6-point Likert format, the Turkish version was modified to a 5-point Likert scale to improve clarity and ease of understanding. Response options range from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. Items 1 and 7 are reverse-coded. Higher scores on the scale indicate higher levels of happiness. The internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of the Turkish version was reported as 0.74, and the test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.85 [21]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the OHQ-SF was calculated as 0.74.

Yoga intervention process

One of the researchers, Dr. Vasviye EROĞLU, completed a 200-hour theoretical and practical Adult Yoga Instructor Training program. The yoga sessions were conducted face-to-face and interactively by the researcher with students in the experimental group, at the Pregnancy Education Laboratory of the Midwifery Department, Faculty of Health Sciences, Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University. The yoga intervention was designed based on core components of mindfulness-based yoga, including breathing techniques (pranayama), physical postures (asanas), and relaxation/meditation practices, which are widely reported to improve psychological well-being [11–16].

The sessions were structured as a combination of different breathing techniques and postures rather than a fixed sequence, in order to maintain participant engagement and address multiple aspects of physical and psychological health. Particular emphasis was placed on stress-reducing breathing techniques such as alternate nostril breathing (Nadi Shodhana), along with commonly used asanas that support relaxation and body awareness [11, 14].

Each session lasted approximately 60 min and consisted of 5 min of breathing exercises, 40 min of posture practice, and 5–10 min of relaxation and meditation. The intervention was conducted three times per week over six weeks, consistent with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of structured yoga programs [14–16].

All sessions were delivered face-to-face by a certified yoga instructor, ensuring consistency and safety of the intervention.

Written and verbal informed consent was obtained, and the socio-demographic information form was completed. Participants’ contact information was collected, and a WhatsApp group was created to communicate the schedule of yoga sessions. The benefits of yoga, how the sessions would be conducted, and the necessary materials were explained to the students in the experimental group.

Yoga Practice (Weeks 1–6): Each yoga session began with a 5-minute breathing exercise, which formed the first phase of the practice. This segment included a combination of different breathing techniques. Students were guided to breathe slowly and rhythmically while listening to calming background music, helping them first become aware of their natural breath and then regulate it consciously. Each session started with three selected breathing techniques, such as yogic breathing, alternate nostril breathing (nadi shodhana), and the 4-7-8 breathing method. Following the breathing exercises, participants engaged in a 40-minute asana (posture) practice, which included five standing poses, two transitional poses, and five seated poses, or the reverse order, depending on the session structure. Each yoga session concluded with 5 to 10 min of relaxation and meditation, promoting physical and mental calmness.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis of the collected data was performed using the SPSS 26.0 software package. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values, and percentages, were used. The data were determined to be normally distributed. For comparisons between two independent groups, the Independent Samples t-test was applied. For comparisons among more than two independent groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when homogeneity of variances was met to identify group differences. The Chi-square test was employed to assess differences between categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the direction and strength of relationships between variables. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify scales potentially affecting yoga outcomes. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Based on the post-test OHQ-SF scores (Effect size d = 1.25, α = 0.05, N = 72), the statistical power was determined to be 0.99.

Results

The mean age of the experimental group participants was 20.74 ± 0.91 years, mean height 162.60 ± 5.05 cm, mean weight 55.71 ± 7.99 kg, and mean BMI 21.04 ± 2.58 kg/m². Most participants reported having perceived their income-expenditure status as moderate (68.6%), a normal BMI range (82.9%), living in dormitories (85.7%), being satisfied with their department (77.1%), and sleeping 5–8 h daily (85.7%). The control group had a mean age of 20.75 ± 1.60 years, a mean height of 163.02 ± 5.16 cm, a mean weight of 59.24 ± 10.72 kg, and a mean BMI of 22.24 ± 3.64 kg/m². Similar proportions reported income equal to expenses (64.9%), normal BMI (56.8%), living in dormitories (83.8%), satisfaction with their department (83.8%), and sleeping 5–8 h daily (83.8%). There were significant differences between groups in BMI categories and participation in social activities (p < 0.05), while no significant differences were found in other characteristics (p > 0.05). Overall, the groups were considered homogeneous in terms of descriptive characteristics (Table 1).

Discussion

This study was conducted to determine the effects of yoga on burnout and happiness levels among midwifery students. According to the research findings, while the mean age of both the experimental and control groups was around 21, no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of height, weight, BMI, income level, place of residence, satisfaction with the department, choosing the department willingly, daily sleep duration, or perception of academic achievement (p > 0.05). It can be stated that the randomized groups included in the study were similar in these characteristics. Students in the experimental group who practiced yoga were found to have lower levels of burnout and higher levels of happiness compared to those in the control group who did not practice yoga. These results suggest that yoga enhances psychological well-being and reduces burnout levels in academic life.

In our study, it was observed that students who practiced yoga had significantly lower scores in the “Exhaustion” and “Cynicism” sub-dimensions of the MBI-SS compared to the control group, while their “Efficacy” scores were higher. Since lower scores in the “Efficacy” subscale indicate higher levels of burnout, these findings suggest that the overall burnout levels of students in the experimental group were significantly lower than those in the control group. This finding supports the idea that yoga helps reduce academic burnout by enhancing individuals’ ability to cope with stress. A review of the literature shows that yoga has positive effects on stress, exam anxiety, anxiety disorders, and burnout [22, 23]. In particular, studies conducted among students in the field of health sciences have demonstrated that yoga is effective in reducing stress levels [14, 16] and in enhancing academic motivation by alleviating burnout symptoms [13].

According to the research findings, students who practiced yoga had significantly higher levels of happiness based on the results of the OHQ-SF. This finding indicates that yoga practices enhance psychological well-being and happiness by increasing individuals’ positive emotions [11]. Yoga, through breath, body, and mind awareness, helps strengthen an individual’s inner balance and sense of peace. Consistent with the results of this study, existing literature also shows that yoga is effective in enhancing happiness levels among middle school [24], high school [25], and university students [26].

The present study also revealed that while the yoga intervention significantly improved burnout scores as shown in the group comparison analysis (t-test), the regression model focused on the predictive power of happiness on yoga practice. It is important to distinguish between the interventional efficacy of yoga and its predictive role in the regression model. While yoga practice significantly reduced burnout subscale scores (Exhaustion, Cynicism, and Efficacy), the lack of a predictive effect in the regression analysis may suggest that burnout is a multifaceted phenomenon influenced by various academic and environmental stressors that were not included in the predictive model. This indicates that yoga practices may be more directly effective in enhancing positive emotional states such as happiness, whereas their impact on the overall variance of burnout might be mediated by other factors or require a longer duration to manifest as a primary predictor. The logistic regression analysis showed that happiness levels significantly predicted the likelihood of participating in yoga practice (p = 0.001). This finding aligns with the existing literature suggesting that individuals who practice yoga report higher levels of life satisfaction and subjective well-being [12, 16].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a six-week yoga program was effective in increasing the happiness levels of midwifery students but did not produce a significant effect on their burnout levels. The findings suggest that yoga practices have the potential to support subjective well-being and positive mood, particularly in the short term. However, it is thought that more prolonged, intensive, or multifaceted intervention programs may be necessary to impact complex and chronic psychological conditions such as burnout. These results indicate that yoga can serve as a supportive tool for mental health, but may not be sufficient alone to address deeper structural issues like burnout. Further research with larger sample sizes, including follow-up measurements, is recommended to better understand the lasting effects of yoga on stress, happiness, and burnout.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the research was limited to midwifery students from a single university, and the sample size was relatively small; this restricts the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the six-week duration of the yoga intervention may have been insufficient to observe significant changes, especially in deeper and more chronic psychological processes such as burnout. Furthermore, the absence of follow-up measurements to assess the long-term effects of yoga practice represents an important limitation of the study.

A significant limitation of this study is its retrospective trial registration. While the data collection and intervention were conducted in 2023, the formal trial registration was completed in 2026. This delay may affect the perceived transparency of the trial reporting. However, it is important to note that the study was carried out in strict adherence to the original research protocol, and all outcomes were recorded and reported transparently to maintain scientific integrity.
