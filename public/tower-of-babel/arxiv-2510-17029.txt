Abstract
. The Böröczky configuration of lines and (multiple) points exhibits extremal behavior in commutative algebra and combinatorics. Examples of this appear in the context of the containment problem for ordinary and symbolic powers and the proof of the Dirac-Motzkin conjecture by Green and Tao. This paper studies the algebraic properties of Böröczky configurations for arbitrary values of n n . Our results compute the Waldschmit constant of the defining ideal of these configurations. Moreover, we use the weighted projective plane ℙ ⁡ ( 1 , 2 , 3 ) {\mathbb{P}}(1,2,3) to give an upper bound for the degree of the minimal generators of their ideal. Finally, this construction is applied to an elliptic curve in ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}^{2} to give a new counterexample to the containment I ( 3 ) ⊆ I 2 I^{(3)}\subseteq I^{2} .



1.
Introduction

The goal of this note is to study the algebraic properties of the Böröczky configuration of lines and points. The Böröczky configuration, defined for every
n\geq 3
, consists of lines, ordinary points, where exactly two lines meet, and triple points, where exactly three lines of the configuration meet; see
Section

4
for a precise definition. Let
B_{n}
denote the set of all lines and all intersection points of the Böröczky configuration.

There are two competing definitions of this configuration in the literature. One used by Green and Tao
[
GT13
]
in their proof of the Dirac-Motzkin conjecture, and one used by Füredi and Palásti
[
FP84
]
which, among other results, provides a negative answer to a question of Paul Erdös. An essential difference between the two is that Green and Tao consider this construction in the real
projective
plane while Füredi and Palásti make use of it in the real
affine
plane. The latter construction described in
[
FP84
]
, considered in the affine chart
U_{2}
of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, is the one of interest to us and henceforth is referred to “the Böröczky configuration.” It it notable that their construction is a special case of an earlier construction by Burr, Grünbaum and Sloane
[
SAB74
]
which uses elliptic integration; see
[
FP84
, Section 6]
. We draw another connection to elliptic curves in
Section

5
.

The algebraic context in which the Böröczky configuration arises is the containment problem of ordinary and symbolic powers. The specific problem related to this paper is for height
2
ideals in the standard graded polynomial ring
k[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}]
, namely those ideals that define points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.
Craig Huneke asked if the containment

(1.1)

I^{(3)}\subseteq I^{2}

always holds, where
I^{(3)}
denotes the
third symbolic power of
I
. Henceforth
Equation

1.1
is referred to as
the containment problem.
While
Equation

1.1
holds for general points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}

[
BH09
, Remark 4.3]
, the first counterexample to this containment was given in
[
DSTG13
]
. Sets of points that fail
Equation

1.1
are special and demonstrate high levels of symmetry. The containment problem, and its variants such as
[
HH11
, Conjecture 4.1.1]
and
[
Gri20
, Conjecture 2.1]
, have generated a plethora of research; see
[
DS21
,
DHN+15a
,
DHN+15b
,
CHM+16
,
HS15
,
MS18
,
Dra17
,
Ake17
]
. A survey of the results on the containment problem can be found in
[
SS16
]
.

Both real and projective Böröczky configurations exhibit extremal behavior in algebraic and combinatorial contexts.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{n}
denote the
set of triple points
of
B_{n}
with
I_{n}
as its homogenous defining ideal. The main observation of
[
CHM+16
]
is the fact that
I_{12}^{(3)}\not\subseteq I_{12}^{2}
; this was the first counterexample to the containment problem over the real numbers. It is noteworthy that Green and Tao’s interest in the projective Böröczky is its extreme behavior in the context of the Dirac-Motzkin conjecture
[
GT13
, p.10]
:

These (Böröczky) sets, it turns out, provide the example of non-colinear sets of
n
points with fewest number of ordinary lines, at least for large
n
.

In fact they are the only configurations, up to projective equivalence, that achieve the lower bound for the number of ordinary lines, which contain ordinary points only, of a configuration of lines and point as conjectured by Dirac and Motzkin; see
[
GT13
, Theorem 2.2]
.

We summarize the known results about the Böröczky configuration. The authors in
[
FKLBTG17
]
consider
\mathcal{B}_{12}
and another configuration of lines and points to show combinatorial data alone does not determine the containment
I^{(3)}\subseteq I^{2}
. For small values of
n
, the authors in
[
LBS16
,
FKLBTG18
]
consider a parameter space of

, in order to investigate other rational counterexamples to the containment problem, and
[
Kab19
]
studies some combinatorial aspects of Böröczky configuration. Finally,
[
Kab22
]
shows, albeit using Macaulay2, that

satisfies

1.1
for

.

This paper aims to start the first systematic study of the set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and the algebraic properties of

for
arbitrary values of

n
. The following is a summary of our results. Assuming that

,

shows the set of triple intersection points

admits an action of

computes the Waldschmit constant of

Remark 2.4
.

The following observations are straightforward to check.

(1)

Since the action fixes the hyperplane defined by
z
, identify
O
with its image in
U_{2}\cong{\mathbb{A}}^{2}
. If
|O|=3
or
6
then elements of
O
lie on a circle because elements of
D_{6}
are isometries which preserve distance. Therefore all elements of an orbit have the same distance from origin.

(2)

Suppose
O
is an orbit of size three. Then
O
has a stabilizer of size
2
, therefore it is fixed by some reflection. This implies that
O
lies on
V(p)
, the vanishing set of
p
, and any orbit of size
3
has a point on the
x
-axis. Moreover, each reflection line contains exactly one point of an orbit of size
3
. An orbit of size
6
can not have a point on

since its stabilizer has size
1
. In particular,
p
does not vanish on the points of any orbit of size
6
.

(3)

An invariant polynomial evaluates to the same value on the points of a single orbit. Let
\omega\in k[x,y,z]^{D_{6}}
,
O
be an orbit of any size,

with
a\neq b
, and

such that

. Then

\omega(a)=\omega(g\cdot b)=g^{-1}\cdot\omega(b)=\omega(b)

where the last equality follows because
\omega
is invariant. Therefore any invariant polynomial vanishing on a point of an orbit vanishes on the entire orbit.

Main Results

Let

be the defining ideal of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, the set of triple intersection points of

. The main results of this section are
Theorem

4.3
, which uses
Lemma

4.1
to compute the Waldschmit constant of
I_{n}
,
Corollary

4.6
which uses the weighted projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(1,2,3)
to bound the minimal degree of a generator of
I_{n}
, and
Proposition

4.8
which shows the product of the
n
configuration lines is the unique element of degree
n
in
I_{n}^{(3)}
.

Fix a non-negative integer
N
and let
k[{\mathbb{P}}^{N}]
denote the homogeneous coordinate ring of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Recall for a non-zero, homogeneous ideal
J\subsetneq k[{\mathbb{P}}^{N}]
, the
Waldschmit constant of
J
, denoted by
\widehat{\alpha}(J)
, is defined as the limit

\widehat{\alpha}(J)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\cfrac{\alpha(J^{(m)})}{m}=\inf_{m\geq 0}\cfrac{\alpha(J^{(m)})}{m},

where
\alpha(J^{(m)})
represents the smallest degree of a non-zero element of
J^{(m)}
, the
m
-th symbolic power of
J
. The existence of the limit and the second equality follow from
[
BH09
, Lemma 2.3.1]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\geq 8
be an even integer. Each line of
{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
contains at least
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor
configuration points.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.1
three lines
L_{i}
,
L_{j}
and
L_{k}
are concurrent if and only if
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Fix
2\leq i
and let
|L_{i}|
denote the number of points of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
on
L_{i}
. Since
0\leq i,j,k\leq n-1
, computing the quantity
|L_{i}|
is equivalent to counting the number of distinct non-negative integer solutions to the equations

(4.1)

j+k=n-i

(4.2)

j+k=2n-i

where
i,j,k
are pairwise distinct as no line
L_{i}
intersects itself at a configuration point. Moreover,
(j,k)
and
(k,j)
represent the same intersection. The assumption that
n
is even implies
n-i
and
2n-i
are even if and only if
i
is even. We first compute the number of solutions to
Equation

4.1
:

(4.3)

\begin{split}&i\text{ is even: }j\in\left[0,\frac{n-i}{2}\right]\text{ and }k\in\left[\frac{n-i}{2},n-i\right]\Rightarrow\frac{n-i}{2}\text{ solutions}\\
&i\text{ is odd: }j\in\left[0,\frac{n-i-1}{2}\right]\text{ and }k\in\left[\frac{n-i+1}{2},n-i\right]\Rightarrow\frac{n-i+1}{2}\text{ solutions}\end{split}

Since
j
and
k
must be distinct, the solution
\left(\frac{n-i}{2},\frac{n-i}{2}\right)
is excluded from the case
i
is even.

Similarly, to count the number of solutions to
Equation

4.2
one only needs to consider the range
0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2n-i}{2}\right\rfloor
.
Next, we claim that if
(j,k)
is a solution to
Equation

4.2
, then
n-i+1\leq j
. Assume to the contrary that
j<n-i+1
and observe the following contradicts that
k\leq n-1
.

j<n-i+1\iff j<2n-i-n+1\iff n-1<2n-i-j\underset{\ref{eq: 2n-i}}{=}k.

The claim follows. In
Equation

4.4
we compute the number of solutions to
Equation

4.2
. When
i
is even, the claim implies
j\in\left[n-i+1,n-\frac{i}{2}\right]
and the the pair
(n-\frac{i}{2},n-\frac{i}{2})
is excluded for distinctness.

(4.4)

\begin{split}&i\text{ is even: }j\in\left[n-i+1,n-\frac{i}{2}\right]\text{ and }k\in\left[n-\frac{i}{2},n-1\right]\Rightarrow\frac{i}{2}-1\text{ solutions}\\
&i\text{ is odd: }j\in\left[n-i+1,{\textstyle\frac{2n-i-1}{2}}\right]\text{ and }k\in\left[{\textstyle\frac{2n-i+1}{2}},n-1\right]\Rightarrow\frac{i-1}{2}\text{ solutions}\end{split}

Thus far there are a total of
\frac{n}{2}-1
solutions when
i
is even and
\frac{n}{2}
solutions when
i
is odd. As
i,j,k
are pairwise distinct, we need to count and subtract the cases when
i=j
or
i=k
. For a fixed
i
, the intervals in
Equation

4.3
and
Equation

4.4
are disjoint and they cover all integers from
0
to
n-1
, thus
i
will equal
j
or
k
for some solution pair
(j,k)
and we must subtract one more from each solution count. However, if:

•

\frac{n-i}{2}=i
which is true if and only if
i
is even and
i=\frac{n}{3}
, or

•

n-\frac{i}{2}=i
which is true if and only if
i
is even and
i=\frac{2n}{3}
,

then we have already accounted for the repeated solution and we don’t need to subtract anything. For
i=0,1
,
Equation

4.2
has no solutions and a simple counting shows
Equation

4.1
has
\frac{n}{2}-1
solutions.
To summarize, if
i\geq 2
and
i
is even, we have

|L_{i}|=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{2}-1&\text{ if }i=\frac{n}{3}\text{ or }i=\frac{2}{3}n,\\
\frac{n}{2}-2&\text{ otherwise .}\end{cases}

And if
i\in\{0,1\}
or
i
is an odd number we have

|L_{i}|=\frac{n}{2}-1.

Finally, the proof concludes since
\frac{n}{2}-2\leq|L_{i}|
for all cases of
i
and

(4.5)

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{n}{2}-2\text{ when }n\geq 8.\qed

Theorem 4.3
.

For any
n\geq 12
where

, we have
\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})=\frac{n}{3}.

Proof.

First we establish the inequality
\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})\leq\frac{n}{3}
. Observe the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}
is in
I_{n}^{(3)}
thus
\alpha(I_{n}^{(3)})\leq n
and
Equation

4.6
yields
\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})\leq\frac{n}{3}
.

(4.6)

\alpha(I_{n}^{(3m)})\leq m\alpha(I_{n}^{(3)})\leq mn\Rightarrow\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\alpha(I_{n}^{(3m)})}{3m}\leq\frac{n}{3}.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})<\frac{n}{3}
. By definition of the infimum there exists an
m
such that

\frac{\alpha(I_{n}^{(m)})}{m}<\frac{n}{3}\Rightarrow\alpha(I_{n}^{(m)})\leq m\frac{n}{3}-1.

Let
G\in I_{n}^{(m)}
such that
\deg(G)\leq m\frac{n}{3}-1
and note
G
vanishes on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to order at least
m
. Recall
L_{i}
denotes both the line
L_{i,\frac{n}{2}-i}
and its defining equation. By
Lemma

4.1
,
G
intersects
L_{i}
at at least
\frac{n}{3}
points thus
e(L_{i}\cap G)\geq\frac{n}{3}m
, where
e
denotes the intersection multiplicity. By Bezout’s theorem
L_{i}\mid G
for all
i
. Let
G^{\prime}=G\backslash(\prod_{i=1}^{n}L_{i})
which implies
\deg(G^{\prime})=\deg(G)-n
. Since
G^{\prime}
vanishes on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
to order at least
m-3
we get that
G^{\prime}\in I_{n}^{(m-3)}
. Thus

\alpha(I_{n}^{(m-3)})\leq\deg(G)-n\leq m\frac{n}{3}-1-n=\frac{n}{3}(m-3)-1

Continuing in this fashion we arrive at the cases

(4.7)

m-3=\begin{cases}1\\
2\\
3\end{cases}\Rightarrow\deg(F)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{n}{3}-1\\
\frac{2n}{3}-1\\
n-1\end{cases}

where
F
is an element of minimal degree in
I_{n}^{(m-3)}
. Let
\gamma\in\{1,2,3\}
. In each instance of
\gamma
,

4.1
implies

and thus

is divisible by all
n
lines of the configuration. Thus
\deg(F)\geq n
which contradicts

Equation

4.7
. Hence
\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})=\frac{n}{3}
.
∎

\widehat{\alpha}(I_{n})
