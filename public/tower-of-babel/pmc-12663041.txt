Barriers to the development of palliative care in Islamic countries: Navigating faith, culture, and ethics
Authors: Nasrin Karimi, Rasoul Goli
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Palliative care represents one of the most humane expressions of modern medicine, aiming to relieve suffering, uphold dignity, and provide comfort when curative treatment is no longer effective. 1 Despite its global recognition, palliative care remains underdeveloped and often misunderstood in many Islamic countries, where deeply rooted religious and cultural beliefs shape health decisions and end-of-life practices. 2

In Islam, life is considered sacred and divinely given; thus, its termination—by act or omission—is often viewed as interference with God’s will. 3 This theological stance makes practices such as Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR) orders, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, and open discussion about death ethically and emotionally challenging. 4 In Iran, for instance, even when patients are terminally ill and further medical interventions offer no benefit, families and clinicians may insist on continuing aggressive treatment, fearing moral guilt or religious condemnation. 5 The result is a paradox where the patient’s comfort and dignity are unintentionally compromised in the name of preserving life. 3

Cultural norms compound these challenges. In many Middle Eastern societies, talking openly about death is considered taboo or a sign of hopelessness. 6 Families often prefer to protect patients from “bad news,” limiting honest communication and shared decision-making. 7 Consequently, healthcare professionals face ethical conflicts between respecting family wishes, religious expectations, and the patient’s right to comfort and autonomy. 6

Structural and policy barriers further complicate the issue. Palliative care is not fully integrated into national health systems in most Islamic countries, and the absence of clear legal frameworks for end-of-life decisions leaves clinicians uncertain and vulnerable. 8 Moreover, a lack of education in palliative care ethics and insufficient collaboration between religious scholars and medical professionals perpetuate misunderstanding. 9

To advance palliative care in Islamic contexts, efforts must focus on developing culturally and theologically informed guidelines, strengthening interdisciplinary education, and fostering dialogue between healthcare providers, policymakers, and Islamic scholars. Compassion, mercy, and human dignity—core Islamic values—should form the moral foundation for modern palliative care models that honor both faith and patient comfort.

Addressing these issues requires courage, open conversation, and cultural humility. Only through such integration can palliative care flourish in Islamic societies, bridging the gap between faith and the fundamental human need for comfort at the end of life.
