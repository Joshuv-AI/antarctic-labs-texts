Synchronous Vaginal and Liver Metastases in Rectosigmoid Cancer: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Tamader Alyaarabi, Mahmood Al‐Dhaheri, Hamza A. Abdul‐Hafez, Ammar Aleter, Noof Al Naimi, Mohamed Kurer, Mohamed Abunada, Ali Toffaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaginal metastasis from colorectal cancer is extremely rare. We report the case of a 58‐year‐old postmenopausal woman with rectosigmoid adenocarcinoma and synchronous liver and vaginal metastases who initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms followed by vaginal bleeding. The diagnosis was established through colonoscopy, biopsy, CT, MRI, and FDG PET‐CT. After 6 cycles of FOLFOX chemotherapy, the liver metastases regressed, while the primary tumor and vaginal lesions progressed. She subsequently underwent pelvic exenteration with ileal conduit formation and right hepatectomy, followed by pulmonary metastasectomy and stereotactic body radiotherapy for subsequent lung metastases. At 41 months of follow‐up from diagnosis, the patient remains alive with disease and has demonstrated a favorable response to multimodal therapy. This case highlights the importance of gynecological evaluation in female patients with colorectal cancer and demonstrates that aggressive multimodal management can achieve prolonged disease control.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer‐related mortality [1]. Approximately 50%–60% of patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer will develop metastatic disease during the course of their illness [2].

Approximately 20% of patients with CRC have metastases at the time of diagnosis [3]. Patients with distant metastases have a median survival time of 9 months, and their 5‐year survival rate is only 9%–14% [4]. Metastasis is a major concern because it can be fatal, causing mass effect and disrupting physiological homeostasis [4].

The most common sites of distant CRC metastasis are the liver, lungs, and peritoneum [4]. Urogenital metastasis of colorectal origin is very rare, and only a few cases have been reported in the literature (Table 1). The first case of vaginal metastasis was reported by Whitelaw et al. in 1956, and only a limited number of cases have been reported since then [5].

Here, we report a rare case of a 58‐year‐old woman who presented with rectal bleeding and was found to have rectosigmoid adenocarcinoma with synchronous liver and vaginal/vulvar metastases. She received FOLFOX chemotherapy with a partial response, followed by pelvic exenteration, ileal conduit formation, and subsequent right hepatectomy. Later in her disease course, she underwent pulmonary metastasectomy, stereotactic body radiotherapy, and systemic therapy for recurrent pulmonary metastases. At the latest follow‐up, she remained alive with disease and demonstrated a favorable response to multimodal treatment.

Literature Review

A literature review was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar to identify previously reported cases of vaginal metastasis originating from sigmoid or colorectal cancer. The search was performed using the keywords “sigmoid cancer,” “colorectal cancer,” and “vaginal metastasis.” Only articles published in English and available in full text were included. Publications from the earliest available reports up to the time of manuscript preparation were reviewed. Cases were screened for relevance, and duplicate reports or articles lacking sufficient clinical information were excluded. A total of 29 cases meeting these criteria were identified and included in the review.

Case History and Examination

A 58‐year‐old woman initially presented on 22/11/2022 with bleeding per rectum and abdominal pain. She had been experiencing abdominal discomfort, constipation, bloating, abdominal distension, and an 8 kg weight loss over the preceding few months. She also reported fresh blood following defecation but denied loss of appetite, nausea, or vomiting. These symptoms were associated with vaginal bleeding despite being postmenopausal for 4 years. The vaginal bleeding began 1 month after the onset of her gastrointestinal symptoms. Initially, it consisted of daily spotting for 2 months but had increased in frequency over the preceding few weeks.

The patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and had previously undergone cesarean sections and knee surgery. There was no family history of colorectal or gynecological malignancies.

On abdominal examination, there was no distension, tenderness, or palpable mass. Digital rectal examination revealed hemorrhoids but no masses or blood. Pelvic examination revealed a 3 × 3 cm mass below the urethral meatus originating from the vagina. The lesion was friable and bled easily. A biopsy was obtained on 13/12/2022.

Colonoscopy performed on 01/12/2022 revealed a protruding ulcerative sigmoid mass extending from 18 to 25 cm from the anal verge and nearly obstructing the lumen. Tumor markers were as follows: CEA 5.6 and CA 19‐9 32.5. Biopsies from the rectosigmoid mass revealed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with intact MMR expression and no mutations in KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF. Biopsy of the urogenital mass confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma of colonic origin (CK20 positive, CDX‐2 positive, SATB‐2 positive, PR positive, CD15 positive, P16 positive, Napsin A negative, Vimentin negative, ER negative, PAX8 negative, MMR intact).

Investigation and Treatment

A CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis performed on 08/12/2022 revealed thickening of the rectosigmoid junction and three liver lesions suspicious for metastatic disease. An MRI of the liver performed on 13/12/2022 confirmed three focal lesions in segments VII and VIII, consistent with metastases. An MRI of the pelvis performed on 20/12/2022 demonstrated a solid mass with malignant features in the anterior lower vaginal wall extending toward the urethra. These findings were consistent with metastatic disease.

FDG PET‐CT performed on 28/12/2022 (Figure 1) demonstrated intense uptake within the sigmoid colonic wall thickening, intense uptake in the lower anterior vaginal wall (3 cm metabolic diameter), and increased uptake in the known liver metastases. The patient was diagnosed with sigmoid adenocarcinoma with synchronous liver and urogenital metastases.

At the time of diagnosis, the case was discussed at the colorectal multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting. Given the resectability of both the primary tumor and metastatic lesions, the initial MDT recommendation was staged curative‐intent treatment consisting of resection of the rectosigmoid primary tumor and vaginal metastasis, followed by systemic chemotherapy and subsequent liver resection. However, the patient sought a second opinion in Germany, where neoadjuvant chemotherapy was recommended before surgical intervention. The rationale was to achieve early systemic control of metastatic disease, assess tumor biology and treatment response, and evaluate for disease progression before undertaking extensive curative‐intent surgery. The patient subsequently returned to Qatar and continued the chemotherapy regimen that had been initiated in Germany. At the time of treatment planning, she maintained an ECOG performance status of 0.

The treatment plan involved 12 cycles of FOLFOX chemotherapy followed by surgical resection of the primary tumor, liver metastases, and urogenital metastases. Following the sixth cycle of FOLFOX on 02/05/2023, the liver metastases demonstrated a favorable response, whereas the sigmoid mass and vaginal metastasis showed no response. Imaging demonstrated regression of the liver lesions but progression of the vaginal wall thickening.

Following reassessment after 6 cycles of FOLFOX, the liver metastases remained technically resectable and demonstrated a favorable radiological response, whereas the primary tumor and vaginal metastasis showed persistent disease with progression of the vaginal lesion. Based on these findings, the MDT recommended proceeding with pelvic surgery to achieve local disease control and R0 resection of the primary tumor and vaginal metastasis before addressing the hepatic disease.

Following discussion at the colorectal multidisciplinary team meeting, the patient was scheduled for pelvic exenteration on 25/06/2023. Intraoperatively, the rectosigmoid tumor was found to be located above the peritoneal reflection, with a separate large metastatic lesion involving the anterior vaginal wall and vulvar vestibule and exerting pressure on the urethral meatus.

The patient successfully underwent low anterior resection with total mesorectal excision (TME), end colostomy, pelvic exenteration, and ileal conduit formation. Given the involvement of the anterior vaginal wall by metastatic disease, an en bloc resection including the involved vaginal tissue was performed to achieve complete oncological clearance and maximize the likelihood of an R0 resection.

Final histopathology revealed a grade 2 moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma, ypT3, ypN1a (1/20), ypM1a (AJCC/TNM 8th edition), completely excised with the closest radial margin measuring 1 cm. Metastatic involvement of the left and ventral vaginal wall was identified.

Following successful R0 resection, the patient completed the remaining six cycles of the planned 12‐cycle FOLFOX regimen, as six cycles had already been administered in the neoadjuvant setting. Completion of systemic therapy was intended to optimize treatment of potential micrometastatic disease and maintain systemic disease control. Given the favorable response of the liver metastases to chemotherapy, preservation of resectability, and the patient's excellent functional status (ECOG 0), right hepatectomy was subsequently performed 3 months later in Germany as part of the planned curative‐intent multimodal treatment strategy.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Follow‐up PET imaging performed on 11/08/2024 demonstrated a newly developed 9 mm subpleural round soft‐tissue nodule along the right horizontal fissure with detectable FDG uptake. Consequently, she underwent video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with right middle lobe wedge resection on 15/09/2024. Final histopathology confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma breaching the visceral pleura, consistent with a colorectal primary origin.

A subsequent PET scan performed on 17/04/2025 demonstrated increased uptake within a 14 mm nodule located medially in the right lower lobe adjacent to the esophagus, consistent with disease recurrence. The patient was offered the options of surgical resection or stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and elected to proceed with SBRT.

Following five treatment sessions, a repeat chest and thoracic CT scan performed on 28/08/2025 demonstrated significant regression of the right lower lobe paraoesophageal nodule, which measured 3.4 mm compared with 14 mm previously.

A follow‐up PET scan on 16/12/2025 demonstrated a new metastatic lesion in the right upper lobe of the lung, indicating further disease progression. The thoracic multidisciplinary team (MDT) recommended systemic chemotherapy with FOLFIRI plus cetuximab, and the first cycle was initiated on 20/01/2026 (Table 2).

Discussion

Vaginal metastasis from primary colorectal cancer is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 60 cases documented in the literature. The mechanisms underlying vaginal metastasis from a colorectal primary have been explained by four proposed theories [11]. The first theory involves hematogenous spread through the venous system, whereby cancer cells travel from the colon to the ovarian plexus or parametrial veins before reaching the vagina. The second theory suggests direct extension, with cancer cells spreading to the vagina through the superficial perineal space. The third mechanism involves retrograde lymphatic spread through lymphatic channels. The fourth theory proposes transcoelomic spread, whereby cancer cells pass from the colonic serosa through the fallopian tubes and uterus before reaching the vagina [6, 11].

We report the case of a 58‐year‐old woman with stage T3N1M1 sigmoid colon cancer who presented with synchronous metastases to the liver and vagina. Our review included 29 reported cases of women with colorectal cancer and genital metastases ranging in age from 44 to 87 years.

Most reported cases presented with gynecological symptoms. Vaginal bleeding was the most common presentation, reported in 20 cases, followed by vaginal masses in 9 cases. Three patients were diagnosed incidentally with vaginal metastasis during staging investigations for primary colorectal cancer and did not exhibit gynecological symptoms. In 10 cases, patients initially presented with gynecological symptoms attributable to vaginal metastasis, and subsequent investigations revealed a previously undiagnosed colorectal primary tumor. In our case, the patient presented with abdominal symptoms in addition to vaginal bleeding. This presentation is consistent with the majority of reported cases.

Across the reported cases, histopathological examination of the vaginal lesion was essential for confirming metastatic disease. Gynecological examination and biopsy established the diagnosis, while colonoscopy was crucial in detecting or confirming the colorectal primary tumor. CT imaging was the most frequently used modality for evaluating disease extent and excluding involvement of other organs, whereas MRI and PET‐CT were used selectively. Similarly, in our case, the diagnostic workup included colonoscopy with biopsy, biopsy of the vaginal lesion, CT imaging of the chest and abdomen, liver MRI, and PET‐CT.

The primary tumors originated from various regions of the colorectal tract: 11 cases arose from the rectum, 4 from the rectosigmoid junction, 10 from the sigmoid colon, 2 from the descending colon, and 1 from the ascending colon. Sixteen cases involved advanced‐stage tumors (T3N0 or higher). Vaginal metastasis was synchronous with the initial diagnosis of colorectal cancer in 14 cases, whereas it occurred metachronously in 15 cases, with intervals ranging from 3 weeks to 41 months after the primary diagnosis. Four cases presented with synchronous metastases to other organs, including the ovaries, uterus, peritoneum, liver, and lungs. Eight cases subsequently developed metastases to sites including the peritoneum, brain, lungs, liver, ovaries, small bowel, pelvis, and mediastinum. In our case, the patient was diagnosed with rectosigmoid cancer with synchronous vaginal and liver metastases (T3N1M1) and subsequently developed pulmonary metastases during follow‐up.

Vaginal metastasis from colorectal cancer is associated with a poor prognosis, and establishing a standard treatment approach remains challenging. In primary vaginal cancer, radiotherapy is generally considered the preferred treatment modality, with surgery reserved for selected cases [27]. Treatment strategies reported for vaginal metastases have included internal radiotherapy (IRT), external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), transvaginal excision (TVE), bilateral oophorectomy (BO), total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy (TAH/BSO), and chemotherapy (CTx).

Among the 29 cases reviewed, six patients underwent radiotherapy alone, two received chemotherapy alone, six underwent surgical excision alone, seven underwent surgical excision combined with radiotherapy, one underwent surgery combined with chemotherapy, two received chemotherapy and radiotherapy without surgery, and one underwent surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.

The sequencing of treatment in this case warrants particular discussion. Although the initial MDT recommendation at our institution was resection of the primary tumor and vaginal metastasis followed by systemic therapy and subsequent liver resection, the patient sought a second opinion in Germany, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy was initiated before surgery. This approach allowed early treatment of systemic disease, assessment of tumor biology, and evaluation of treatment response while ensuring that all sites of disease remained resectable. Reassessment after six cycles demonstrated a favorable response in the liver metastases but persistent progression of the vaginal lesion, which supported proceeding with radical pelvic surgery.

In our patient, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was initially administered. However, due to the poor response of both the primary tumor and the vaginal metastasis, surgery was performed after six cycles of chemotherapy. The procedure included low anterior resection with total mesorectal excision (TME), end colostomy, pelvic exenteration, and ileal conduit formation. The surgical approach included TME with en bloc resection of the involved vaginal tissue as part of the pelvic exenteration. This strategy was selected to achieve complete oncological clearance of both the primary rectosigmoid tumor and the adjacent metastatic vaginal disease while maintaining adherence to oncological resection principles. This achieved a successful R0 resection and was followed by completion of the planned 12‐cycle FOLFOX regimen and a right hepatectomy.

Completion of systemic therapy following R0 resection was undertaken to maximize treatment of potential micrometastatic disease and maintain systemic disease control. The decision to proceed with hepatectomy after completion of pelvic treatment was based on maintained resectability of the liver lesions, favorable response to chemotherapy, absence of extrahepatic progression at that stage, and the patient's excellent performance status (ECOG 0). This sequential multidisciplinary strategy ultimately enabled definitive treatment of all known sites of disease while preserving the opportunity for curative‐intent management.

Fifteen months after pelvic surgery, pulmonary metastasis was treated with VATS and right middle lobe wedge resection. Four months later, another pulmonary metastasis developed and was successfully treated with five sessions of stereotactic body radiotherapy, resulting in a favorable response.

Only one other reported case involved anterior pelvic exenteration. That patient was a 67‐year‐old woman with vaginal metastasis infiltrating the urethra, bladder wall, and perivaginal tissues following sigmoidectomy and chemotherapy for sigmoid cancer 3 years earlier [15]. In that case, pelvic exenteration was not followed by additional treatment, and the patient died 10 months after surgery due to brain metastases.

Of the 29 reported cases, five patients died within 10–39 months of diagnosis, 12 had no evidence of disease at follow‐up ranging from 8 to 48 months, and four were alive with disease at follow‐up ranging from 3 to 24 months. Our patient has remained alive with disease for 41 months. She developed a pulmonary metastasis 21 months after diagnosis, which was treated surgically, and subsequently developed another pulmonary metastasis 8 months later, which was treated with radiotherapy and demonstrated a favorable response.

Vaginal metastasis from colorectal cancer is an exceptionally rare manifestation of metastatic disease. This case highlights the importance of gynecological assessment in women with colorectal cancer and demonstrates that carefully selected patients may achieve prolonged survival through aggressive multimodal treatment incorporating systemic therapy, radical surgery, metastasectomy, and radiotherapy.
