Combined drought and heat stress intensifies antioxidant responses and gene expression in Persian walnut (Juglans regia L.)
Authors: Asaad Habibi, Saadat Sarikhani, Mohammad Mehdi Arab, Mohammad Soltani, Adamo Domenico Rombola, Mahmoud Reza Roozban, Kourosh Vahdati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Persian walnut (Juglans regia L.) frequently encounters simultaneous drought and heat stress in its natural habitat, impacting its physiology and biochemistry. Therefore, understanding the physiological and molecular responses to these combined stresses is essential for improving stress resilience in this species. The present study investigates the effects of drought stress (DS), heat stress (HS), combined drought-heat stress (DH) and control condition (CC) on the antioxidant defenses and gene expression in own-rooted walnut plants (cv. Chandler) under controlled greenhouse conditions. Combined stress significantly increased malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide by 2.55- and 2.05-fold, respectively, while reducing membrane stability index by 0.43-fold compared to CC. Total chlorophyll declined by 0.18- and 0.09-fold under DS and HS, respectively, with the greatest reduction (0.37-fold) under DH. Levels of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, polyphenol oxidase, superoxide dismutase, catalase, and guaiacol peroxidase rose 2.5- to sixfold under DH compared to CC. Similarly, phenolic compounds and antioxidant capacity increased between 1.49- and 3.55-fold relative to CC. Gene expression analyses of NCED6, PPO, and GST revealed 2- to sixfold upregulation under both individual and combined stress conditions compared to control. These results highlight the crucial roles of these genes in activating antioxidant defense pathways and detoxification processes that mitigate oxidative damage in walnut under stress. Following stress relief, walnut seedlings displayed notable recovery in photosynthetic function and antioxidant activity. These findings indicate that combined drought and heat stress exerts a stronger impact than either stress alone, underscoring key adaptive mechanisms that may enhance walnut resilience to climate variability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07625-0.

Introduction

Climate change due to its increasing intensity and frequency, is recognized as one of the major threats to walnut trees worldwide. Among these changes, water scarcity and heat stress are considered the most critical limiting factors for walnut productivity in current cultivation areas, especially in arid and semi-arid regions [1]. In Iran, walnut is extensively cultivated in 29 out of 31 provinces, including dry and tropical areas [2–4] predicted that, due to global warming, the walnut cultivation area in Iran will decrease by 11.39% between 2020 and 2049. This shift toward a hotter and drier climate, particularly in central provinces such as Yazd, Kerman, Semnan and Markazi key walnut producing regions will likely intensify the occurrence of prolonged droughts and frequent heatwaves, leading to reduced yield in walnut orchards. [4, 5] reported that heat stress negatively affects the yield and quality of perennial crops, including walnut, causing significant economic losses. Moreover, the combined effects of heat and drought stress, especially during the juvenile stage, severely disrupt walnut growth and ultimately limit its yield potential [2, 6]. The most severe impacts of heat and drought stress are observed during the late growing season (September), which coincides with the kernel filling and fruit ripening stage, these conditions can lead to kernel darkening and a reduction in kernel quality and size [7]. In addition, [8] reported that heat stress reduces flowering and fruit set in the following year. Overall, the above points highlight the urgent need for studies focused on understanding and improving stress tolerance mechanisms in this valuable crop.

Walnut trees are frequently exposed to multiple biotic and abiotic stressors over their extended lifespans, often encountering these stressors simultaneously or in sequence [9]. The response of long-lived plants like trees to concurrent stresses, particularly drought and heat, is complex and requires further exploration. When these stresses occur together, as they often do in nature, they can have amplified effects on plant physiology and growth [4, 10]. Under such conditions, where walnut orchards are expected to face more severe climate change in the future, studying the effects of individual and combined stresses becomes increasingly important.

Drought (DS) and heat stress (HS) induce oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) like hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which disrupts cellular homeostasis [11, 12]. Under DS, excess ROS can cause lipid peroxidation, damaging cell membranes, photosynthetic apparatus, and chlorophyll production in walnut,[13, 14]. Heatwaves further intensify oxidative damage by disrupting enzyme stability, compromising membrane integrity, and impairing mitochondrial respiration. [15] stated that HS, by disrupting the electron transport chain, impairs leaf function and reduces the physiological efficiency of walnut at high temperatures (30 to 40 °C). Moreover, Momayyezi et al. [4] reported that HS in walnut significantly decreases photosynthetic capacity by affecting key photosynthetic processes, including stomatal conductance, net photosynthetic rate, and the quantum yield of photosystem II. In contrast, the combination of drought and heat stress exacerbates negative effects by increasing evaporative demand, placing greater strain on the plant’s physiological systems [4, 16]. Studies indicate that combined drought and heat stress (DH) can trigger either antagonistic or synergistic physiological responses in plants [17, 18]. [15] reported that HS accelerated physiological changes in walnut seedlings subjected simultaneously to DH. [19] demonstrated that DH increases energy dissipation in Vitis vinifera, heightening the risk of cellular damage.

In response to these adverse conditions, plants activate diverse antioxidant systems to mitigate the damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS). These systems include enzymatic antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), guaiacol peroxidase (GPX), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants like phenolic compounds and carotenoids. These components play a critical role in scavenging ROS and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis, thereby contributing to the stabilization of plant performance under stress conditions [20]. Numerous studies have reported that simultaneous drought and heat stress enhances the antioxidant defense system in plants such as olive and eucalyptus [11, 17]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that these combined stresses stimulate abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis in grapevine [21] and induce the accumulation of defense-related compounds in citrus [22] and walnut [15]. Plants respond to drought and heat stress through a range of physiological and molecular mechanisms, including the regulation of stress-responsive genes. Under such stresses, abscisic acid (ABA) plays a central role in coordinating plant adaptation. Under normal conditions, ABA levels are maintained at low concentrations to ensure optimal plant growth and development. However, upon sensing stress, endogenous ABA levels rapidly increase, driven by the upregulation of ABA biosynthesis genes and downregulation of its catabolic genes, a process stimulated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [23]. Notably, the upregulation of the key ABA biosynthetic gene, cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED6), plays a decisive role in the rapid accumulation of endogenous ABA in roots and leaves following stress perception [24]. This hormone modulates stomatal closure, gene expression, and other protective responses critical for plant survival under water deficit and heat stress conditions.

In addition to ABA-related genes, enzymes involved in antioxidant defense are essential for mitigating oxidative damage caused by stress-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS). Polyphenol oxidases (PPOs) are plastid-localized enzymes that participate in detoxification and antioxidant processes, with increased expression under both biotic and abiotic stresses, including drought and heat [25, 26]. Furthermore, PPO expression is elevated in response to oxidative stress, contributing to detoxification processes and antioxidant defense mechanisms [4]. Glutathione S-transferase (GST) genes encode enzymes that function as part of the phase II detoxification system in plants. Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) further contribute by conjugating glutathione, a key antioxidant, to reactive compounds, maintaining cellular redox balance and enhancing tolerance [27, 28]. Numerous studies have highlighted the multifaceted roles of GSTs in plants, including in growth, metabolism, and responses to stresses such as cold, salinity, drought, and oxidative stress. Understanding the coordinated expression and function of NCED6, PPO, and GST genes offers valuable insight into the molecular basis of walnut resilience to combined drought and heat stresses.

According to official evaluations conducted in 2022 by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), the four main grafted cultivars used in the global walnut industry exhibit low resistance to environmental stresses, including extreme heat and drought, making them highly vulnerable (2022 California Walnut Objective Measurement Report, USDA). Among these, the popularity of the Chandler cultivar due to its high resistance to late spring frost, lateral bearing habit, and excellent kernel quality has led to its widespread adoption, particularly in commercial and modern orchards in Iran. Additionally, Chandler accounts for nearly 50% of walnut orchards in California [4] Despite the frequency of combined drought and heat stress in agricultural settings, little is known about how these conditions affect walnut (Juglans regia L.) physiology. This study investigates the individual and combined effects of drought and heat stress on oxidative damage, antioxidant responses, and gene expression in Persian walnut (Chandler. CV). Additionally, we assess recovery capacity after stress relief to better understand the resilience mechanisms that may support walnut adaptation under climate variability.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experimental design

The experiment utilized own-rooted Persian walnut (Juglans regia L. cv. Chandler) plants. Two-year-old plants were transferred and grown in 15 L polyethylene pots filled with a soil:sand whit sandy loam texture mixture in a 2:1:1 ratio. Prior to treatment application, the plants were cultivated under controlled environmental conditions in the research greenhouses of the Department of Horticulture, University of Tehran, Iran. The conditions included a 16/8 h light/dark cycle, a temperature range of 25/20 °C (day/night), and relative humidity of ~ 35–50%.

In this experiment, 48 uniform walnut seedlings were used and randomly assigned to four treatment groups. Data were collected at three time points: mild stress (16 days for DS and 4 days for HS), severe stress (at the end of the 24-day period), and recovery (14 days after stress relief) [29].

Physiological measurements

Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was extracted with 80% acetone, and the absorbance at 664, 680, and 470 nm was measured using a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 spectrophotometer. V: ml of volume of acetone used, W: weight of the sample (g).

Relative Water Content (RWC)

RWC was calculated using the equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RWC=\left(FW-DW\right)/\left(TW-DW\right)\times 100$$\end{document}where FW represents the fresh weight, TW the turgid weight (measured after rehydrating leaf discs for 24 h at 4 °C in the dark), and DW the dry weight (obtained after oven-drying the leaf discs at 75 °C for 72 h) [30].

Maximum photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (FV/FM)

The maximum photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/FM) was measured using Chl fluorescence transients (OJIP-test), following the method described by Arab et al. [13].

Membrane Stability Index (MSI)

MSI was measured by analyzing electrolyte leakage following the method of Karimi et al. [30]. Twenty leaf discs (8.9 mm in diameter) from fully expanded young leaves were used in this measurement.

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content

H₂O₂ was measured according to the method of Loreto and Velikova [4]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.25 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was mixed with potassium phosphate buffer and potassium iodide. After incubation in the dark for one hour, absorbance was read at 390 nm. The H₂O₂ content was reported in µmol g−1 FW.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

MDA content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was determined following Heath and Packer [31]. Fresh tissue (0.25 g) was homogenized with 2.5 mL of 0.1% TCA and centrifuged. The supernatant was reacted with TCA containing 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA), heated at 95 °C for 15 min, and the absorbance was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm. MDA concentration was calculated using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MDA=\left(A532-A600\right)/155\times 1000$$\end{document}

Finally, the MDA content was reported in µmol g−1 FW.

Total Phenolic Compounds (TPC) and Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC)

The total phenolic content (TPC) was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric assay, while total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was assessed via radical scavenging capacity using the DPPH assay [32]. The TAC results were expressed as percentages, and the quantity of phenolic compounds was quantified using a gallic acid standard curve [14].

Polyphenol oxidase activity

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO, EC 1.14.18.1) activity was determined following the method of [4, 33], using pyrogallol as the enzyme substrate. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.5 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7), 200 µL of 0.02 M pyrogallol, and 100 µL of enzyme extract. The absorbance was read at 420 nm after 3 min. Enzyme activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of pyrogallol (6.12 mM⁻1cm⁻1) and the equation A = εbc, where A is absorbance, ε is the extinction coefficient, b is the path length of the cuvette, and c is the concentration of pyrogallol. The results were expressed as enzyme units per µmol mg⁻1 protein.

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase activity

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) catalyzes the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamic acid. Enzyme activity was measured as described by D'cunha et al. [34], using phenylalanine as the substrate. The reaction mixture included 1 mL of extraction buffer, 0.5 mL of 10 mM phenylalanine, 0.4 mL of double-distilled water, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the reaction was stopped with 0.5 mL of 6 M hydrochloric acid, and absorbance was measured at 260 nm. One unit of PAL activity was defined as the amount of enzyme producing 1 µmol of cinnamic acid per minute per milligram of protein. The amount of cinnamic acid produced was determined using a standard curve [34]. The results were expressed as enzyme units per µmol mg⁻1 protein.

Total soluble protein

One gram of leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of extraction buffer containing 2 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.5 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), TritonX-100, 80% glycerol, and 1 M EDTT. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein content in the supernatant was determined using the Bradford [35] method. The protein content was expressed as mg g −1 of FW.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

Frozen leaf and root samples (0.5 g) were ground with liquid nitrogen and homogenized in extraction buffer (pH 7.5) containing 50 mM potassium phosphate, 1% PVP, and 1 mM EDTA.

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD (EC 1.15.1.1) activity was determined by monitoring the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction, following the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich [36]. The 3 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 2 µM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 100 µL of enzyme extract. Reaction mixtures were illuminated for 15 min at 5000 lx. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing 50% inhibition of NBT reduction, monitored at 560 nm using a spectrophotometer (U-2000, Hitachi Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). SOD activity was expressed as units per µmol mg⁻1 protein.

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT activity was measured by following the decrease in H₂O₂ absorbance at 240 nm (extinction coefficient 39.4 mM⁻1 cm⁻1) as described by [37], with slight modifications. The reaction mixture included 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 10 mM H₂O₂, and 200 µL of enzyme extract. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount that causes a 0.01 change in absorbance (using a spectrophotometer U-2000, Hitachi Instruments, Tokyo, Japan) per µmol mg⁻1 protein.

Guaiacol Peroxidase (GPX) activity

GPX activity was measured using guaiacol as the substrate, following the method of Plewa et al. [4]. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.77 mL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7), 100 µL of 1% H₂O₂, 100 µL of 4% guaiacol, and 30 µL of enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance due to guaiacol oxidation was measured at 470 nm for 3 min. Enzyme activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of tetraguaiacol (25.5 mM⁻1 cm⁻1) using a spectrophotometer (U-2000, Hitachi Instruments, Tokyo, Japan) and expressed as units per µmol mg⁻1 protein.

RNA preparation and cDNA synthesis

Leaf samples from walnut saplings control, drought, heat, and combined drought-heat treatments, each with three biological replicates (4 treatments × 3 replicates = 12 total samples), were collected for gene expression analysis. The total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of leaf tissue from walnut saplings using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA samples were treated with RNase-free DNase I to remove any potential genomic DNA contaminants. The quantity and quality assessment of RNA samples were performed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Subsequently, 1 µg of each RNA sample was reverse transcribed by using oligo dT primers and random hexamers in equal volume with the RevertAid™ First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer's guidelines. The quality and quantity of synthesized cDNA were evaluated by a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Gene-specific primer pairs (Table S1) were designed using Oligo7 software, based on available gene sequence information from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Then, the specificity of primers was verified using primer-BLAST tool on the NCBI website. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) reactions were performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK) on a LightCycler 96 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The reaction mixture, with a final volume of 20 μl, contained of 3 μl of diluted cDNA, 1 μl (50 ng) of each forward and reverse primer,10 μl of SYBR-Green PCR Master Mix Reagent, and 5 μl of nuclease-free water. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial activation of DNA polymerase at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Each qRT-PCR assay was conducted using three biological replicates, each with two technical replicates (total of 24 samples). The specificity of amplification for each primer pair was confirmed via melting curve analysis. Primer efficiencies were also calculated using LinRegPCR software based on the exponential phase of each amplification curve. The Actin gene was applied as a reference gene for normalization, and the relative gene expression level was computed by 2−ΔΔCt formula [38].

Statistical analysis

A before-after control-impact (BA-CI) analysis using linear mixed-effects models was conducted to assess treatment differences during and after stress. Treatments (CC, HS, DS, and DH) and time (stress and recovery) were treated as fixed factors, while trees were assigned as a random effect to account for repeated measures [4]. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed at each sampling point, and means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (P < 0.05) with software (R 4.3.2). Linear and nonlinear regression models were used to identify relationships between physiological and biochemical parameters. Pearson correlation matrix analysis was performed using the R package ggplot2. Stats models (Ver. 0.14.2) Python package [39] and SAS software (Ver. 9) were employed for statistical analysis.

Results

Total Chlorophyll (TChl), Carotenoid (Car) content

All treatments caused a significant decline in total chlorophyll (TChl) content during severe stress (SS). The reduction in TChl was more pronounced in combined drought and heat stress (DH) compared to heat stress (HS) and drought stress (DS), respectively (Fig. 2A). No significant differences were observed between treatments during mild stress (MS) and recovery (Re). Results from the linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) indicated significant differences only in the DH treatment during stress (St) (Table 1, S2). Carotenoid (Car) content varied between treatments throughout the experiment (Fig. 2B). A stress-dependent increase in Car content was observed with the intensity of stress in all treatments. DH exhibited a significant difference from other treatments during MS. During SS time, Car increased significantly in DH (2.11-fold) compared to HS (1.94-fold) and DS (1.73-fold). Discussion

RWC and photosynthetic

Significant progress has been made in understanding walnut responses to drought stress (DS) under controlled conditions [13], but limited data exist on the combined effects of heat stress (HS), drought and heat stress (DH), and recovery in Persian walnut. Plants naturally face multiple simultaneous stresses, with combined effects often more severe than individual ones [4, 15]. DH causes greater physiological and biochemical damage than DS or HS alone [15, 18]. This study uniquely evaluates DS, HS, DH, and recovery effects at multiple levels, showing most parameters recover after stress.

DS reduces relative water content (RWC) more than HS, especially with prolonged stress (Fig. 2C), consistent with previous findings linking RWC loss to impaired root growth and stomatal control [40]. In HS, RWC is maintained by irrigation, but DS and DH cause greater RWC declines, highlighting the importance of soil water availability during heat-induced high transpiration [18].

DH, or “hot drought,” is a major driver of tree mortality due to combined hydraulic and thermal damage disrupting physiological functions, causing crown growth reduction and chlorophyll degradation in walnut seedlings [41]. DH treatments visibly damaged crowns and significantly reduced total chlorophyll (TChl) content, likely due to thylakoid membrane damage and impaired electron transport [42]. This agrees with reports that DS and HS accelerate chlorophyll degradation and inhibit biosynthesis in walnut [4, 14] and grapevine [4]. The further TChl decline under DH may involve chlorophyllase activation degrading chlorophyll as a protective response against phototoxic pigments [4, 42].

Chlorophyll fluorescence (OJIP) curves, especially the FV/FM ratio, reflect PSII maximum quantum efficiency and are reliable indicators of drought stress in walnut [13, 43]. FV/FM decreased under DS and HS due to damage to the PSII reaction center, including the D1 protein, and disruption of the water-splitting complex [42, 43]. HS causes denaturation of PSII proteins and destabilizes light-harvesting complex II (LHCII), while DS limits CO₂ availability via RWC reduction and stomatal closure, inhibiting Calvin cycle carbon fixation [30, 44]. Both stresses reduce electron transport chain efficiency, decreasing ATP and NADPH production [42]. Previous studies confirmed FV/FM declines in response to DS [13] and heatwaves [4]in walnut. DH induced an even greater FV/FM reduction than either stress alone, highlighting the walnut PSII’s sensitivity to combined stress, consistent with findings in grapevine where HS primarily affected PSII under DH [4]. These synergistic thermal and hydraulic disruptions explain the amplified damage under combined stress [18].

Antioxidant defense

A clear decline in photosynthesis associated with heat and drought stress is often accompanied by an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. The generation of ROS results from the over-reduction of the photosynthetic electron transport chain, where excess electrons are transferred to O₂ at the PSI and PSII reaction centers [42, 43]. The results of this experiment showed that HS, DS, DH and the duration of stress significantly increased hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content. The accumulation of H₂O₂ under both individual and combined stress conditions was accompanied by elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and reduced membrane stability index (MSI), indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation, membrane damage, and increased water loss. These findings are consistent with studies on eucalyptus by Correia et al. [17], which demonstrated that disturbances in active oxygen metabolism, driven by the accumulation of free radicals such as H₂O₂, lead to increased damage to photosynthetic organs [4]. Moreover, in agreement with previous reports [18], we observed that walnut seedlings under combined stress exhibited reduced water availability and, consequently, lower hydraulic efficiency, which may lead to increased leaf temperatures during heatwave periods in walnut [15]. [45] stated that excessive leaf heating can reduce the stability of cellular membranes, leading to cellular disintegration and simultaneous electrolyte leakage in leaves. Furthermore, H₂O₂ showed a strong negative correlation with MSI (r2 = 0.83) and a positive correlation with MDA content (r2 = 0.91) (Fig. 7A).

Under normal conditions, a stable balance exists between the production and scavenging of ROS to maintain cellular homeostasis in plants. However, DS and HS disrupt this balance by reducing carbon assimilation, impairing PSII function, and increasing the NADPH to NADP⁺ ratio, which leads to electron transport chain disturbances and ultimately results in the overproduction of ROS within the chloroplasts [30, 42, 44]. In response to these adverse conditions, plants activate a complex network of defense mechanisms, particularly enzymatic antioxidant systems, to mitigate ROS-induced damage. Key enzymatic antioxidants include superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPX), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO). These molecules play a critical role in maintaining cellular redox balance, protecting biological structures from oxidative damage, and contributing significantly to metabolic stability and stress tolerance in plants [17, 46]. Our results demonstrated a marked increase in the activity of these antioxidant enzymes during both mild and severe stress conditions. Superoxide dismutase, by converting superoxide radicals into oxygen and H2O2, plays a pivotal role in the antioxidant defense system. In this study, a significant correlation was observed between the activities of SOD, GPX, and CAT, indicating a complementary function of these enzymes in decomposing H2O2 during the treatment period (Table 1). Recent studies have also confirmed such functional interplay between these enzymes [46]. The enhanced activities of SOD, GPX, and CAT in response to stress illustrate the plant's biochemical strategy to alleviate environmental stress, as these enzymes are key players in reducing H₂O₂ levels and minimizing oxidative damage [47],[42]. These findings align with previous reports indicating that antioxidant enzyme levels increase under drought and salinity stress in walnut [14, 30], and under DH in grapevine [48], Pinus elliottii [44], and eucalyptus [17].

Phenolic compounds, as secondary metabolites derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, are primarily synthesized and accumulated in response to environmental stresses through the induction of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) enzyme activity [49]. Our results demonstrated that the activities of PAL and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) enzymes were elevated in plants subjected to both individual and combined stress conditions. The PAL enzyme initiates the biosynthesis of flavonoids, which play a protective role against drought and salinity stresses in plants [4]. PPO, an oxidoreductase enzyme, catalyzes the oxidation of monophenols and diphenols into highly reactive quinones, which can subsequently react with oxygen to generate ROS. Both PAL and PPO enzymes play crucial roles in modulating and detoxifying ROS through the phenylpropanoid pathway, particularly under HS in olive [50] and under DH in tea [51].

Several studies have reported the pivotal role of non-enzymatic compounds, such as phenolics and carotenoids, in scavenging ROS and mitigating oxidative stress [46, 52]. The findings of the present study revealed that both individual and combined stress treatments significantly increased total phenolic content (TPC) and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) in walnut leaves. Phenolic acids contribute to osmotic adjustment by accumulating osmolytes such as sugars and polyols, thereby lowering osmotic potential [52]. Furthermore, TPC play a crucial role in detoxifying ROS and enhancing the antioxidant defense system, thereby protecting cellular structures under stress conditions [4, 53]. The increase in TAC and phenolic content observed in our study aligns with previous findings in olive trees subjected to DS [] and chestnut under HS [54]. Additionally, positive correlations between TAC and indices such as TPC (r2 = 0.80), PPO (r2 = 0.81), and PAL (r2 = 0.81) underscore the significant contribution of phenolic biosynthesis and antioxidant enzymes to the overall antioxidant capacity (Fig. 7A). Moreover, TAC exhibited significant correlations with SOD (r2 = 0.75) and CAT (r2 = 0.72), highlighting the role of enzymatic antioxidants in alleviating oxidative stress. We also observed that carotenoids (Car) exhibited an increasing trend under both individual and combined stress treatments throughout the experimental period. Carotenoids function as photoprotective pigments by absorbing excess light energy, thereby reducing photodamage [46]. It has been reported that Car possesses antioxidant properties that contribute to ROS scavenging and protect chlorophyll from degradation under stress conditions [11]. Additionally, carotenoids serve as precursors for the biosynthesis of phytohormones such as abscisic acid (ABA) and regulate genes involved in this biosynthetic pathway.

Gene expression

In the present study, the expression levels of NCED6, GST, and PPO genes were upregulated progressively under DS, HS, and DH. Drought and heat stress enhance ABA accumulation via activation of NCED, a key enzyme in ABA biosynthesis [6]. Under DS, decreased cellular water potential activates NCED promoters through transcription factors such as bZIP, MYB, and DREB by binding to DRE/CRE or ABRE elements [28]. Increased NCED expression enhances the production of 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase, which catalyzes the cleavage of carotenoids into xanthoxin, subsequently converted into ABA through oxidation by ZEP and AAO enzymes [55]. Once synthesized, ABA binds to PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors in the guard cells, initiating signaling cascades via SnRK2 and PP2C pathways, modulating calcium signaling, ion channels, and antioxidant-related gene expression. This process ultimately reduces stomatal conductance and transpiration, aiding in water conservation [21, 55]. Under HS, the accumulation of ROS and membrane damage activates NCED expression through ROS signaling and heat shock transcription factors (HSFs) [28]. ABA synthesized via NCED subsequently induces the expression of heat shock proteins (HSPs), which prevent protein denaturation under high temperatures [6, 42]. Moreover, ABA activates antioxidant pathways and modulates lipid metabolism and osmolyte (such as proline) accumulation, thereby facilitating ROS detoxification and maintaining membrane integrity [42]. Our results demonstrated that NCED6 expression significantly increased under DS, HS, and DH, findings that are consistent with studies conducted in grapevine (26) supporting its proposed roles in previous studies (Fig. 6A).

The PPO gene was also induced under DS, HS, and DH. The PPO gene plays a crucial role in modulating the oxidation of phenolic compounds to quinones and maintaining ROS homeostasis, thereby mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stresses. For instance, in Populus trichocarpa, PPO gene expression was significantly induced in leaves under drought conditions [56]. These authors suggested that PPO genes are part of a complex regulatory network involving ABA-dependent pathways, where cis-acting elements in their promoters enhance drought-responsive expression. It is believed that chloroplast-localized PPO genes, under heat stress conditions, regulate phenolic metabolism and interact with HSPs and DREB2A to prevent cellular damage by modulating stress-responsive gene expression [56]. Furthermore, PPO genes have been identified as indirect regulators of programmed cell death in walnut [57]. Similarly, [58] reported that several glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) are associated with ABA signaling and play essential roles in stress responses. Therefore, it can be concluded, the coordinated upregulation of NCED6, PPO, and GST under drought, heat, and combined stresses indicates an integrated ABA-mediated signaling and antioxidant defense mechanism. This transcriptional reprogramming enhances ROS detoxification, maintains cellular homeostasis, and contributes to stress adaptation in walnut at the molecular level.

Recovery patterns

The recovery phase is a complex process involving the reconstruction of metabolic pathways, whose role in stress adaptation is often more critical than the stress phase itself. We observed rapid recovery across all physiological processes in Persian walnut, including photosynthetic performance, pigment synthesis, RWC, and antioxidant detoxification. The reversible physiological processes during recovery effectively mitigated growth limitations by enhancing photoprotection and reducing oxidative stress through a coordinated response of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defense mechanisms. Overall, correlations among physiological traits were more pronounced during the stress period compared to the recovery phase (Fig. 7A and B). These findings align with previous studies investigating DS and recovery in adapted walnut genotypes [13], as well as DH in eucalyptus [17] and wheat [4]. Conversely, our results demonstrated that the levels of MDA and H₂O₂ under combined stress did not fully return to baseline and remained significantly different from the control (Fig. 3B and C). This observation is consistent with earlier reports showing elevated H₂O₂ and MDA levels post-recovery in various fenugreek genotypes [46] and olive [59]. These findings suggest that, beyond inducing oxidative stress, H₂O₂ also plays a signaling role, and its sustained elevation may be necessary to maintain active antioxidant systems and enable a rapid response to subsequent stresses [59]. Based on our results, it can be inferred that the incomplete return of H₂O₂ and MDA to basal levels is likely due to partial membrane repair under combined stress conditions, indicating that recovery is a long-term process requiring more time. Furthermore, our study showed that after rewatering, carotenoid levels and the activities of SOD, PPO, and GPX enzymes in the combined stress treatment remained significantly different from the control group (Fig. 2B, 4C, and 5B, D). It is plausible that plants employ different strategies for ROS scavenging during DS and recovery phases, specifically, maintaining high CAT and SOD activities post-rewatering may improve the efficiency of ROS detoxification during recovery. Additionally, it can be concluded that high stress sensitivity does not necessarily correlate with recovery potential in walnut. Environmental stress limitations and the plant’s recovery capacity are critical factors influencing photosynthetic efficiency and should be considered in walnut breeding programs aimed at enhancing stress tolerance.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined various physiological, biochemical, and molecular traits that enable Persian walnut to survive under DH, HS, and DH, as well as during the subsequent recovery phase. Although some mechanisms were common across all stress conditions, the intensity of responses was dependent on the stress type, with most pathways exhibiting stress-specific reactions. Rather than yielding an additive outcome, the combination of HS synergistically exacerbated the effects of DS. Our findings regarding water use behavior during HS clearly demonstrated that water availability is a critical factor influencing tree responses to thermal conditions. Moreover, seedlings subjected to combined stress exhibited a marked decline in photosynthetic performance and pigment content compared to those exposed to individual stresses, alongside a significant decrease in MSI an effect that persisted even throughout the recovery period. Additionally, plants improved membrane integrity through activation of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems and upregulation of genes involved in these pathways, notably NCED, GST, and PPO genes. The patterns of PPO enzyme concentration and PPO gene expression in leaves were well-aligned. Based on these findings, NCED, GST, and PPO genes may serve as suitable biomarkers for combined stress in walnut leaves. These results suggest that with the increasing intensity of future heatwaves, Persian walnut trees experiencing water limitations will be increasingly vulnerable to damage caused by the synergistic effects of DH. Therefore, there is a clear need for studies focusing on field-relevant stresses to better select plant genotypes with enhanced tolerance to natural environmental conditions.
