Ocular manifestations of hydatid disease: a case report
Authors: Xiaoyan Zhang, Kang Feng, Liang Han, Anadi Khatri, Jia-Horung Hung, Ying Li, Pei Zhang, Jinyu Liu, Quan Dong Nguyen, Zhizhong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHydatid disease, caused by Echinococcus species, is a parasitic infection that rarely affects the eye. However, reports of ocular lesions associated with systemic hydatid disease, in the absence of hydatid cysts in the eye, are scarce.Case presentationA 22-year-old male presented with blurred vision for one month. Instead, a dark green lesion was observed beneath the retina and within the choroidal tissue. Additionally, next-generation sequencing of samples from the vitreous and dark green lesion yielded negative results for Echinococcus DNA. Background

Intraocular hydatid cysts are extremely rare, comprising less than 1% of all human hydatid disease cases [1]. These cysts typically present as subretinal, choroidal, or vitreous lesions and can lead to serious complications, including retinal detachment, inflammation, and vision loss [2–7].

Although ocular involvement in hydatid disease has been reported, the case presented here is unique. Despite the presence of large lesions beneath the retina and within the choroid—similar to those seen in other reports—no hydatid cysts were identified within the eye. Additionally, the patient exhibited bilateral retinal vasculitis. Notably, this patient had a long-standing history of systemic hydatid disease and had undergone prolonged albendazole treatment, distinguishing this case from previously reported instances.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old male presented with a one-month history of blurred vision in his right eye. He lived in a rural area and had frequent contact with various animals, including dogs, horses, cats, and cattle. Seven years prior, he was diagnosed with hepatic, pulmonary, renal, and cerebral hydatid disease, and has since been undergoing long-term albendazole treatment.

At the time of presentation, the patient’s visual acuity was 20/500 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left. The right eye showed ciliary congestion (+++), 1 + cells in the anterior chamber, and vitreous haze (1+). Retinal examination of the right eye revealed scattered yellow dot-like exudates around the macula, a yellowish-white elevated lesion (approximately one disc diameter) in the inferior retina (Fig. 1), and two large choroidal elevations near the nasal ora serrata. The left eye appeared nearly normal on both anterior segment and retinal examination. Intraocular pressure was 7 mmHg in the right eye and 11 mmHg in the left.

Fundus fluorescein angiography revealed fluorescence leakage at the optic disc and retinal vessels (Fig. 1). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed subretinal fluid in the macula of the right eye (Fig. 1). OCT also revealed a small choroidal elevation in the inferior periphery, likely due to exudation from large and medium choroidal blood vessels in the right eye (Fig. 2). B-scan ultrasound and OCT further confirmed the presence of two large choroidal elevations in the nasal periphery of the right eye (Fig. 2).

Serological testing for hydatid disease (IgG) was positive. MRI imaging of the liver, lungs, kidneys, and brain confirmed the presence of hydatid cysts. Chest CT showed no signs of tuberculosis or sarcoidosis. Tests for T-SPOT tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum, and HIV were all negative. The complete blood count was largely within normal limits, with an eosinophil percentage of 2.8% and an absolute eosinophil count of 0.21 (cells/µL). However, C-reactive protein levels were elevated at 2.74 (mg/L). Blood cytokine testing revealed an increased IL-6 level (15.26 pg/mL), while other cytokines (IL-8, IL-10, IL-17, TNF-α, IFN-α, and IFN-γ) were within normal ranges.

The initial surgical procedure involved collecting samples of anterior chamber and vitreous fluid to assess inflammatory markers. A trans-scleral choroidal approach was then performed to address the choroidal elevations near the nasal ora serrata (Fig. 3). Following this, a pars plana vitrectomy was conducted, along with silicone oil injection and laser treatment around the yellowish-white elevated lesion located in the inferior retina, as well as the two choroidal elevations near the nasal ora serrata. Notably, no hydatid cysts were identified. Instead, a dark green lesion was observed beneath the retina and within the choroid, corresponding to the location of the choroidal elevations in the nasal periphery of the right eye.

The vitreous fluid samples and the dark green lesion were submitted for histopathological examination. No hydatid cysts, Echinococcus scolices, or hooklets were identified. However, the vitreous sample contained red blood cells and cellular debris without eosinophils, while the dark green lesion showed old red blood cells, pigment-containing cells, and macrophages (Fig. 3). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of both the vitreous and dark green lesion samples returned negative results for hydatid cyst DNA.

Inflammatory markers in the anterior chamber were elevated, with the following levels: IL-6 (58.69 pg/mL), IL-8 (218.62 pg/mL), IFN-γ (1.65 pg/mL), MCP-1 (4845.47 pg/mL), and IP-10 (395.97 pg/mL). Similarly, inflammatory markers in the vitreous were also elevated, including IL-6 (139.43 pg/mL), IL-8 (473.05 pg/mL), IFN-γ (2.18 pg/mL), MCP-1 (6352.60 pg/mL), IP-10 (1321.88 pg/mL), G-CSF (1.83 pg/mL), ICAM-1 (2390.49 pg/mL), and VCAM-1 (2721.13 pg/mL).

The diagnosis of bilateral retinal vasculitis, along with hepatic, pulmonary, renal, and cerebral hydatid disease, was established. Postoperatively, the patient continued the albendazole regimen to manage the systemic hydatid disease. Additionally, a retrobulbar injection of 20 mg triamcinolone acetonide and a subconjunctival injection of 2.5 mg dexamethasone were administered to the right eye. Acetate prednisolone eye drops were prescribed four times a day for the right eye. At the 3-month postoperative follow-up, the patient’s visual acuity in the right eye improved to 20/25. The two large choroidal elevations previously observed in the nasal periphery of the right eye had completely resolved, and OCT imaging confirmed the absence of macular edema (Fig. 4).

Discussion and conclusions

In this case, a patient with a long history of actively treated systemic hydatid disease presented with choroidal elevations and retinal vasculitis, but no intraocular hydatid cysts were detected. Surgical resection of the large choroidal lesions, followed by steroid therapy, resulted in significant improvement in the patient’s ocular condition.

Interestingly, our patient, with a long history of systemic hydatid disease, presented with only retinal vasculitis and choroidal lesions, without any evidence of hydatid cysts or their components. Tuberculosis and sarcoidosis—both of which can also present with choroidal lesions and concurrent vasculitis—were excluded. We hypothesize that ocular cysts were absent prior to the initiation of albendazole therapy. It is possible that the ocular inflammation originated from systemic hydatid disease. This hypothesis is supported by histopathological findings, which revealed large areas of old red blood cells within the choroid, surrounded by inflammatory cells. The dark green lesions were composed of old red blood cells and pigment granules. No hydatid cysts or their components, such as Echinococcus scolices or hooklets, were found. Additionally, NGS of both the vitreous and dark green lesion samples returned negative results for hydatid cyst DNA, further supporting the hypothesis.

Another possibility is that ocular hydatid cysts were initially present but were effectively eradicated by long-term albendazole therapy. Antiparasitic treatment can sometimes exacerbate ocular inflammation, especially if cyst rupture occurs [8]. The negative results from NGS of the choroidal lesions may be attributed to the sampling of only a portion of the vitreous and choroidal lesion.

In future cases, immunohistochemistry (IHC) could be a valuable adjunct, particularly when cyst elements are not readily identifiable. IHC plays a crucial role in confirming the parasitic origin of degenerated cysts by detecting specific parasite antigens, such as Antigen 5 and Antigen B, within the cyst wall, brood capsules, and protoscoleces [9]. Recent studies have also shown that IHC using monoclonal antibodies, mAbEmG3 and mAbEm2G11, is a reliable diagnostic tool for cystic echinococcosis [10]. Moving forward, we will conduct more tests and examinations when encountering similar cases.

The elevated cytokine levels in both the anterior chamber and vitreous, along with the fundus fluorescein angiography results, further support the diagnosis of retinal vasculitis in this patient. Cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 are commonly elevated in ocular hydatid disease [11]. In our case, not only were IFN-γ and IL-6 elevated, but additional inflammatory markers, including IL-8, MCP-1, and IP-10, were also increased in the anterior chamber. Similarly, elevated levels of IL-8, MCP-1, IP-10, G-CSF, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 were found in the vitreous. Additionally, macrophages were present in the choroidal lesions. These findings suggest that the ocular inflammation may be a secondary response to remnants of hydatid cysts, either from the eye or systemic infection. Given the elevated cytokine levels and persistent inflammation, steroid therapy was initiated, consistent with the standard treatment approach for ocular inflammation associated with hydatid disease.

Surgical exploration in this case did not reveal hydatid cysts; however, a large necrotic area in the choroid was excised. Histopathological examination of the two choroidal lesions showed a significant presence of old red blood cells and pigment cells, which were difficult to absorb. As seen in other cases of ocular hydatid disease, surgical intervention remains a key treatment modality [12]. For cysts located beneath the retina, a combination of vitrectomy, lesion excision, laser photocoagulation, and silicone oil tamponade may be necessary [2, 3, 5]. In this case, the condition was effectively managed through a combination of surgical intervention, albendazole therapy, and corticosteroids.

In this case, ocular manifestations of systemic hydatid disease in a patient on long-term albendazole therapy may present as ocular inflammation without intraocular cysts. Surgical removal of the ocular lesions, combined with steroid treatment, resulted in significant improvement in this case.
