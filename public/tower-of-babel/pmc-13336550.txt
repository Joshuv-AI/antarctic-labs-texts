Motivation and pleasure deficits reflect altered network embedding of normal brain deviations in schizophrenia☆
Authors: Jacob Holker, Aaron Clouse, Sierra Ann J Gilday, Jacob Jensen, Delaina Cimmino, Barrett Humble, Jacob Johnston, Kennedy Madrid, Madeleine Reading, Sam Johnson, Braydon Lee, Stefan Ehrlich, Matthew J Smith, Lei Wang, Derin Cobia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Negative symptoms in schizophrenia are associated with significantly worse outcomes, and their factor structure (i.e., diminished motivation and pleasure [MAP] and diminished expressivity [EXP]) has only recently been clarified. Studies investigating structural imaging correlates of negative symptoms have largely focused on cortical regions involved in motivation, reward processing, affect expression, and language. Structural covariance network modeling provides a framework for examining covariance among these cortical regions in a network-like manner.Methods:Clinical and imaging data from 203 individuals with schizophrenia were obtained from three independent data sites. Cortical thickness measures based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas were standardized using a normative modeling algorithm developed by the ENIGMA Lifespan Working Group. Structural covariance networks were constructed using a priori cortical regions of interest related to MAP and EXP. Linear regression models tested whether MAP and EXP severity predicted network embeddedness.Results:Both the MAP-related (F8,177 = 22.76, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.4848) and EXP-related networks (F8,1778 = 14.43, p = 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.3673) were significant. Howerver, only MAP severity predicted network embeddedness within the MAP network (β = 0.085, p = 0.03, 95% CI [0.009, 0.162]).Discussion:Results suggest that MAP is selectively associated with network-level over-coupling of cortical morphology, whereas EXP may reflect more distributed network properties. These findings underscore the potential role of cortical network rigidity in the pathophysiology of negative symptoms.Keywords: Psychosis, Structural covariance, Neuroimaging, Negative symptoms

Introduction

Despite longstanding debate surrounding its historical attribution (Berrios, 1985), the distinction between positive and negative symptoms in schizophrenia has become a cornerstone for identifying potential pathophysiological mechanisms and treatment targets. Negative symptoms in particular pose a challenge for clinicians and researchers, partly due to their initially nebulous operational definition (Arndt et al., 1991; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006), resistance to pharmacological intervention (Correll and Schooler, 2020), as well as a tendency toward insidious onset, contributing to consistently poor prognoses (Buchanan and Gold, 1996). Recently, studies have clarified the negative symptom structure using both exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, with hierarchical models providing a parsimonious description of its core substrates (Blanchard and Cohen, 2006; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; Lehoux et al., 2009; Strauss et al., 2012, 2018). In this model, the domains of anhedonia, avolition, and asociality comprise the higher order dimension of deficits in motivation and pleasure (MAP), whereas alogia and blunted affect comprise the higher-order expressivity domain (EXP) (Strauss et al., 2018).

Regarding the former dimension, basic science investigations into the neurobiological correlates of MAP have provided evidence for cognitive based models of avolition, anhedonia, and asociality, particularly in value representation (Brandl et al., 2023; Gold et al., 2012; Vignapiano et al., 2016) and its implementation in goal-directed behavior. These theories linking reward-based behaviors to the MAP factor are primarily based on observations of impaired reward evaluation in reinforcement learning paradigms. Gold et al. (2012) found that while equally motivated by loss aversion as their healthy counterparts, schizophrenia patients with high negative symptom ratings were less motivated by gains in a probabilistic reinforcement learning task. These findings were supported using electrophysiological methods by Vignapiano et al. (2016), where P3 waves (reputed to be associated with reward processing) did not parametrically increase with reward magnitude in schizophrenia during a monetary incentive task. Altogether, these findings denote a model of MAP that derives from dysfunction in maintaining mental representations of value (i.e., goals and rewarding behaviors).

Regarding the neurobiological bases of EXP, neuroimaging work has primarily focused on blunted affect and its brain-based substrates (Galderisi et al., 2015). In such studies, clinically rated severity of flat affect correlated with activity in the amygdala and parahippocampal regions in response to emotional faces (Lepage et al., 2011), with another study finding that, as opposed to schizophrenia patients with flat affect, those without flat affect showed significant activation in various cortical targets of the limbic system (Fahim et al., 2005). In a similar vein, hypoactivation of limbic regions during facial emotion identification was also observed in schizophrenia (Gur et al., 2007). Interestingly, amygdala activation in the schizophrenia group was predictive of misidentifying emotions, while the opposite relationship was found in healthy comparison participants. The authors of this study highlighted this counterintuitive finding and proposed that affective flattening in schizophrenia may result from a breakdown in integrating oncoming sensory information to generate phenomenological experiences of emotion (Gur et al., 2007). This is in contrast to current cognitive-based models of alogia that postulate its presentation arises from an individuals cognitive reserve not being able to keep up with demands of social interactions (Bègue et al., 2020). Through this lens, alogia may emerge through an interaction between alterations in affective circuity, as well as a global depletion of cognitive resources (Cohen et al., 2014a; Cohen et al., 2014b).

Given the prior literature establishing distinct neurobiological correlates in MAP and EXP, there is a strong rationale to hypothesize that these symptom dimensions relate to network-level changes in reward and affective circuitry. As an understanding of these networks continues to evolve, updated models may guide the development of meaningful treatment interventions for negative symptoms. For instance, models examining anhedonia in psychosis have now proposed cognitive remediation as a promising route for the treatment of motivation deficits in psychosis (Cella et al., 2017; Li et al., 2019). The distributed nature of these symptom dimensions suggests that negative symptoms may be better characterized at the level of coordinated neural systems rather than isolated brain regions. Structural covariance networks offer a powerful framework to examine large-scale patterns in neurodevelopment (Zielinski et al., 2010), particularly in the context of related, but dissociable, symptom profiles embedded within negative symptoms. Within this methodological framework (Rubinov and Sporns, 2011), inter-regional covariations in cortical morphometry (i.e., thickness, volume, surface area, etc.) are thought to represent coordinated developmental changes (Alexander-Bloch et al., 2013), but may also reflect shared influences of environmental factors, illness progression and treatment exposure (Evans, 2013). Thus, covariance patterns may likely represent coordinated developmental and disease processes that together relate to the expression and severity of negative symptoms. Furthermore, recent developments in normative modeling (Ge et al., 2024) have allowed researchers to measure morphology in the brain relative to population-based normative trajectories in various domains of neuromorphometrics, thereby enhancing sensitivity to pathology. By leveraging these two methodologies, deviations in network-level cortical maps can yield insight into the pathophysiology of negative symptom dimensions.

The overarching goal of the current study was to examine whether symptom profiles of MAP and EXP in a sample of chronic schizophrenia patients had corresponding cortical network-level morphological relationships, indexed using deviation scores derived from age- and sex-adjusted normative models. Based on prior research (Bègue et al., 2020), we hypothesized that structural covariance network embeddedness in cortical regions involved with reward processing (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, etc.) would be associated with MAP severity, and that the EXP factor would likewise be associated with embeddedness in cortical networks involved in affective processing and expressivity (e.g., ventrolateral prefrontal and superior temporal cortices). Characterizing these relationships could direct development of clinical and pharmacological interventions to target the neurobiological aspects of MAP and EXP, as well as create a model for using behavioral interventions to curtail the debilitating effects of MAP and EXP.

Methods

Participants

The current study used three public neuroimaging datasets drawn from the SchizConnect database (www.schizconnect.org) (L. Wang et al., 2016) to obtain demographic, clinical, and structural imaging data on patients with schizophrenia. In total, 203 schizophrenia (SCZ) participants were included in the final analysis based on available clinical variables and usable imaging data. Participants were drawn from the Mind Clinical Imaging Consortium (MCIC) (Gollub et al., 2013), the Northwestern University Schizophrenia Data and Software Tool (NUSDAST) (L. Wang et al., 2013) and the Neuromorphometry by Computer Algorithm Chicago data (NMorphCH) (Kogan et al., 2016). Participants from all cohorts underwent comprehensive clinical, neuropsychological, and imaging examination as part of larger studies of schizophrenia. Inclusion criteria for SCZ was a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia as assessed by the Structured Clinical Interview for DMS-IV, Axis I Disorders (SCID-IV) (First, 2002). Exclusion criteria for healthy control participants included: currently taking psychiatric medications, history of psychiatric hospitalization, and an immediate family member with a history of a schizophrenia spectrum disorder. Exclusion criteria for all groups included the following: a history of head trauma accompanied by a loss of consciousness, current (i.e., during the time of study recruitment) diagnoses of any neurological or unstable medical disorders, the possession of any medical device that would interfere with an MRI scan, as well as recent (i.e., within the previous month) substance abuse or dependence (according to the DSM-IV). All derived datasets included in this study received approval from their respective Institutional Review Boards.

Clinical assessments

Both positive and negative symptoms were measured in all cohorts using the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS) and Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), respectively (Andreasen, 1981, 1984). Both higher (MAP, EXP) and lower (anhedonia, asociality, avolition, blunted affect, alogia) order negative symptom factors were computed by summing the product of item-level scores on the SANS, and coefficients derived from previously conducted confirmatory factor analyses (Kamalakannan et al., 2025; Strauss et al., 2018); see supplementary material for the full formulae. All factor scores were linearly transformed to z-scores and then Winsorized at ±3 standard deviations. In total, 1.00% of EXP scores, 0.5% of blunted affect scores, and 1.98% of alogia scores were Winsorized. Medication exposure was calculated by converting each dosage to its chlorpromazine equivalent (Andreasen et al., 2010; Gollub et al., 2013), and duration of illness was measured in years.

Image preprocessing

All imaging data were submitted to cortical reconstruction using FreeSurfer version 7.3.2 (http://www.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). During this process, raw anatomical images were subjected to motion correction, corrections to non-uniform intensities, and registration to the Montreal Neurological Institute 305 atlas via affine transformations (Collins et al., 1994; Fischl, 2012). Non-brain tissue was then removed from each image using a hybrid approach to skull stripping (Ségonne et al., 2004). Morphometric measurements of the brain were then calculated using cortical parcellation and deep brain segmentation schemes (Fischl et al., 2002). All processed images were visually inspected for quality control by trained research assistants using established guidelines from the FreeSurfer development group (Segonne et al., 2007), which have been shown to improve cortical surface representation (Backhausen et al., 2016; Iscan et al., 2015). Due to poor image quality, one participant was excluded from the analyses. Measurements of cortical thickness were extracted from each subject and then converted to sex- and age-normed deviation scores (i.e., z-scores) for every cortical region from the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006) using a publicly available normative modeling algorithm created by the ENIGMA Lifespan Group (https://centilebrain.org) (Frangou et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2024). This algorithm uses normative modeling to estimate deviation scores across brain regions relative to population-based expectations for age and sex, thereby providing standardized metrics of regional structural atypicality. These deviation scores quantify the degree to which each individual’s regional cortical thickness departs from normative expectations. This allows covariance analyses to capture coordinated patterns of atypicality across individuals, rather than covariance of raw anatomical measures. All converted scores were then Winsorized at ±3 standard deviations to reduce inflated correlations and spurious covariance edges, fewer than 6% of observations were affected across all structures.

Statistical analyses

Structural covariance network construction

Based on prior work in anatomical networks (Sandini et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019), group-level structural covariance networks (SCNs) were constructed from the standardized cortical thickness deviation scores of all Desikan–Killiany cortical regions. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between all region pairs using pairwise complete observations, and diagonal elements of the correlation matrix were set to zero. Correlation coefficients were then Fisher z–transformed, and only positive values were retained for subsequent network construction. Negative correlations were excluded given in this context biological interpretation is less clear and mixed-sign weighted networks complicate graph theory interpretation (see Chen et al., 2023; Masuda et al., 2025). For this study, positive correlations represent coordinated abnormality patterns across individuals given the node values were derived normative deviation scores. Pairwise correlations ranged from r = − 0.19 to 0.90, with a mean z-transformed value of 0.43 ± 0.23.

Proportional thresholding was applied to the Fisher z–weighted adjacency matrix to control network density while preserving comparable edge density across graphs, with each threshold retaining the strongest edges and setting weaker edges to zero. To assess robustness of network expression across plausible network configurations, density was varied from 10% to 30% in 5% increments. Density-invariant summary measures were obtained by computing the area under the curve (AUC) across thresholds using the trapezoidal rule.

Participant-level SCN expression metrics were calculated by projecting each individual’s cortical thickness deviation profiles onto the group-level adjacency matrix. Global network expression was quantified as a normalized quadratic form of each participant’s thickness deviation vector and the adjacency matrix. This metric reflects the extent to which regions showing larger deviations from normative expectations are more strongly connected within the structural covariance network. Nodal embeddedness was calculated as the matrix product of the adjacency matrix and each participant’s thickness deviation vector, such that each region’s deviation score was weighted by its connectivity with other regions in the network. All expression metrics were integrated across thresholds using AUC computation.

Two a priori cortical networks were defined within the MAP/EXP neurobiological framework (Bègue et al., 2020). In accordance with models implicating cortico-striatal circuitry in reward processing (Barch and Dowd, 2010; Strauss and Cohen, 2017), the MAP network comprised bilateral medial/lateral orbitofrontal cortex, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, and insula. Similarly, with deficits in expressivity largely involving networks between prefrontal and temporal regions, the EXP network comprised bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis and triangularis), precentral gyrus, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, superior temporal gyrus, and banks of the superior temporal sulcus (see Fig. 1 for visualization of both networks). For each participant, nodal embeddedness values were averaged across ROIs within each network prior to AUC computation. Finally, SCNs were constructed using two alternative cortical thickness measurements: deviation scores derived from normative modeling (i.e., CentileBrain as described above), and raw cortical thickness values collected across data sites. CentileBrain utilizes CombatGAM to correct for site effects (Ge et al., 2024); however, to maintain analytic consistency across models, site was included as a covariate in both the raw and normative thickness analyses.

A priori cortical regions used to define diminished motivation and pleasure (MAP) and diminished expressivity (EXP) using the Desikan-Killiany Atlas (Desikan et al., 2006). The MAP network was comprised of bilateral medial/lateral orbitofrontal cortex, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, and insula. The EXP network was comprised of bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis and triangularis), precentral gyrus, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, superior temporal gyrus, and banks of the superior temporal sulcus.

Associations between SCNs and negative symptoms

Structural covariance networks can be constructed using different representations of cortical morphology (Liu et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2023). For this study, parallel analyses using both raw cortical thickness values and normative deviation scores were conducted to assess whether associations with negative symptom dimensions reflected network expression of absolute thickness or deviations from normative expectations.

For the primary analyses, three linear regression models were fitted with global SCN expression, MAP embeddedness, and EXP embeddedness (AUC) as dependent variables. MAP and EXP symptom scores were entered simultaneously as predictors of interest, with cumulative antipsychotic exposure (log-transformed chlorpromazine equivalents), duration of illness, age, data site, and sex included as covariates. Next, a second set of linear regression models were conducted in which raw cortical thickness values from MAP and EXP network ROIs were entered as dependent variables, with symptom scores as predictors and identical covariates as above.

Secondary analyses were also conducted using standardized cortical thickness SCNs to examine network expression of the five lower-level negative symptom domains (anhedonia, avolition, asociality, alogia, and blunted affect). Multiple regression models predicting embeddedness of the MAP network with anhedonia, avolition and asociality, and the EXP network with alogia and blunted affect, were performed also with the same covariates. Variance inflation factors were used to assess multicollinearity for all models using the car package (Fox and Weisberg, 2018). All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024).

Associations between SCNs and lower order negative symptom domains

For the secondary analyses, severity of alogia (β = −0.007, p = 0.461, 95% CI [0.002, 0.039]; standardized β = − 0.049; F8,177 = 14.42, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.3673) and blunted affect (β = −0.008, p = 0.413, 95% CI [− 0.028, 0.012]; standardized β = −0.055; F8,177 = 14.45, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.3678) did not predict embeddedness within the EXP network after covarying for MAP and demographic variables. Likewise, severity of anhedonia (β = 0.024, p = 0.512, 95% CI [−0.048, 0.096]; standardized β = 0.036; F8,182 = 22.77, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.4783) and asociality (β = 0.075, p = 0.051, 95% CI [−0.0004, 0.151]; standardized β = 0.112; F8,178 = 22.75, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.4833) did not predict embeddedness within the MAP network. However, scores of avolition severity were significant positive predictors of embeddedness within the MAP network (β = 0.08, p = 0.035, 95% CI [0.006, 0.155]; standardized β = 0.112; F8,181 = 23.56, p < 0.001, adjusted R2 = 0.4885); see Supplemental Tables 3 and 4 for details.

Discussion

The current study examined the neurobiological correlates of MAP and EXP negative symptom dimensions in schizophrenia as they relate to network-level covariance of normative cortical morphometric deviations. As hypothesized, MAP severity was significantly associated with network embeddedness in an a priori defined cortical network previously implicated in motivation and reward processing. In contrast, EXP was not associated with the predefined cortical network of regions implicated in affective expressivity. These models employed normative deviation scores derived from standardized cortical thickness measurements, which provide an individualized measure of cortical atypicality. In contrast, the models that used raw cortical thickness values found that MAP and EXP were not significantly predictive of their respective network embeddedness. Thus, distinct aspects of MAP negative symptomatology appear to better capture network models based on inter-individual deviations from normative cortical development. Results from the lower-order models further suggest that avolition may beis a particularly salient contributer within this domain. Moreover, the findings from this study highlight the importance of leveraging normative deviation approaches relative to raw biological data to identify subtle brain-based relationships in clinical symptomatology. In particular, the negative symptom association is not merely one of magnitude, but of organization of cortical deviation within a structural covariance architecture.

Another finding of note in this study was the positive direction of the relationship between MAP-network embeddedness and MAP severity. Given deviation cortical thickness scores reflect anatomy relative to expectation given age, sex, and site, they preserve inter-individual variability that group means often obscure. This suggests that the over-coupling of cortical regions observed in the MAP findings may reflect reduced network flexibility, thereby restricting such network’s ability to continually update value representations and motivational processes (Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). This is consistent with prior work suggesting that attitudinal rigidity is associated with negative symptoms. For example, in a recent meta-analysis (Saperia et al., 2025), defeatist performance beliefs were robustly correlated with negative symptom severity, specifically in motivational deficits, with an albeit smaller effect size. In terms of our findings, this suggests that rigid beliefs highlighted in Saperia et al. (2025) correspond to rigid networks, highlighting the importance of targeting these dysfunctional beliefs when treating negative symptoms.

The observed network-level dissociation between MAP and EXP may be explained in a variety of ways. Cognitive models of MAP have largely been based on studies linking MAP severity with deficits in reward processing and value representation (Gold et al., 2008). Indeed, the hierarchical structure of negative symptoms – particularly in MAP, which comprises anhedonia, asociality, and avolition – suggests that specific deficits in motivational processing and the phenomenological experience of pleasure underlie reduced drive to engage in a wide variety of socially-rewarding and goal-directed behaviors. This is underscored by the association avolition had with the MAP network in our secondary analyses. Given that these shared processes have strong network-level bases in cortico-limbic reward circuitry (Camara et al., 2009; Kandel et al., 2021), our models involving MAP-related networks may have been more comprehensive in terms of regional representation, and as a result, more sensitive to associations involving MAP. Conversely, the hierarchical structure of EXP in the negative symptom taxonomy comprises blunted affect and alogia, which both rely on similar but distinct neurobiological networks in affective and language processing (Bègue et al., 2020), respectively. As such, EXP may be selectively associated with more distributed cortical networks than defined in the current study, such as fronto-limbic circuits (Bègue et al., 2020; Gangl et al., 2023). Furthermore, EXP deficits may depend more strongly on focused functional alterations rather than large-scale structural covariance. Interestingly, in a prior study examining structural covariance in the medial orbitofrontal cortex (Yu et al., 2023), increased structural covariance was negatively correlated with measures of anhedonia. Although the findings contradict our own in terms of direction, Yu et al. (2023) used raw metrics of cortical thickness versus deviation values, therefore, higher thickness may have reflected the relative absence of pathology as opposed to the assumptions posed by normative modeling. Furthermore, recent evidence in schizophrenia samples supports these findings, where increased severity of negative symptoms exhibit less structural covariance within the left middle frontal gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus, and bilateral inferior frontal gyri (Zhou et al., 2025). Altogether, our results add nuance to the literature in that developmental changes in networks within the cortex are also associated with negative symptom severity.

Broadly speaking, recent work has highlighted the value of deriving and utilizing structural brain deviation scores in identifying unique developmental trajectories, as well as disease discrimination (Zhuo et al., 2026). In this work, deviations from normative brain organization showed promising utility in not only differentiating between various neurological diseases but also estimating disease propensity as well as predicting the cognitive and physical features of each disease group. The deviation scores outperformed raw structural brain measures in all categories, suggesting they may be more closely linked to disease pathology with greater predictive utility as imaging markers of neurological illness (Zhuo et al., 2026). Our findings extend this principle by highlighting how behavioral MAP deficits are selectively associated with embedding of deviation scores organized by known symptom networks, thus encouraging further work on their utility in discriminating and predicting features in psychiatric disorders.

A few limitations of this study should be considered. First, although MAP was significantly associated with network-level embeddedness, our predefined MAP-network only included cortical regions. Some models identify reward-related networks that also involve subcortical regions such as the striatum (Schultz, 1998). Nevertheless, our findings call attention to the importance of cortical-level organization in MAP. Second, raw cortical thickness values were used from different collection sites, which may have introduced data heterogeneity. Data site was entered as a covariate in all regression models, but this approach may not have entirely captured more complex site-related bias, thus leveraging more sophisticated models would be valuable. Importantly, the normative modeling algorithm used in our study (https://centilebrain.org) (Frangou et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2024) has been trained on several large-scaled data sites, mitigating some site-specific variability in the derived deviation scores.

Overall, this study highlights the role of cortical networks in characterizing the neurobiology of negative symptoms in SCZ, particularly deficits in reward processing and engagement in goal-directed behavior. Given current negative symptom models emphasize the role of cognition and beliefs (Correll and Schooler, 2020; Gold et al., 2008, 2012; Saperia et al., 2025), our results suggest such beliefs reflect over-coordination of cortical regions implicated in reward and motivational processing. Increased characterization of the pathophysiology of negative symptoms will encourage identification of meaningful treatment targets to mitigate their impact on long-term outcomes.
