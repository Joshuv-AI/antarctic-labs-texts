Six Years of Genetic Diagnosis of Severe Early‐Onset Obesity in a French Cohort
Authors: M. Rama, E. Blond, A. Janin, M. Di‐Filippo, C. Cuerq, O. Marmontel, P. A. Rollat‐Farnier, E. Dieterlen, B. Segrestin, M. Nicolino, E. Disse, K. Perge, D. Collin‐Chavagnac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveObesity is a multifactorial disease with a strong genetic component. It is imperative to enhance the identification of genetic variations in their early and severe manifestations in order to facilitate the development of personalized therapeutic strategies, informed clinical care, and the facilitation of genetic counseling. The objective of the study was to provide a comprehensive description of rare variations identified by next generation sequencing of a panel of genes.MethodsFrom 2018 to 2023, a panel of 22 genes was genotyped in 1066 probands (499 children and 567 adults) with severe early‐onset obesity.ResultsThe genetic study led to a molecular diagnosis in 34 probands (3.2%) and revealed variants of unknown significance (VUS) in 10.3% of cases. In the pediatric cohort, a genetic diagnosis was established in 19 probands (3.8%) and a VUS was identified in 67 (13.4%). A total of 152 rare single‐nucleotides variants (SNVs) were identified, of which 34 were classified as pathogenic, predominantly within the MC4R gene. Furthermore, 110 participants were carriers of one or two rare variants of uncertain significance (VUS). A total of three copy number variations (CNVs) were identified in the genes that were analyzed.ConclusionEarly genetic screening in severe obesity provides valuable diagnostic insights and identifies candidates for personalized treatment. The integration of genetics into clinical practice is imperative for the enhancement of care pathways and the facilitation of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Obesity is defined in adults by a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 30 kg/m2 and in children by a BMI z‐score of ≥ 2SD or a BMI exceeding the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF) 30 curve cut‐off. According to a recent international study, over one billion people worldwide are living with obesity (i.e., about one in eight people) [1]. In France, in 2020, it was estimated that obesity affected around 17% of adults, 6% of children aged 8–17 and 18% of children aged 2–7 [2], and 18.1% of adults in 2024 according to the OFEO study conducted by the Ligue Contre l'Obésité (League Against Obesity). The prevalence of weight‐related comorbidities increases significantly 10 years after diagnosis and almost half of them will develop complications [3, 4].

Data from twin and genome‐wide studies suggest that 30%–50% of the obesity risk is heritable [5, 6]. Rare forms of hypothalamic genetic obesity include “syndromic” and “monogenic” obesity [7, 8]. Syndromic obesity is associated with neurodevelopmental disorders and/or congenital malformations such as Prader–Willi, Bardet–Biedl (BBS), Alström syndromes, or 16p11.2 deletion [8, 9]. Monogenic obesity results from variants in genes encoding a protein involved in the hypothalamic leptin‐melanocortin pathway [10, 11]. These rare conditions share a common pathophysiology involving dysfunction of hypothalamic nuclei responsible for energy balance, eating behavior, and metabolism regulation.

Since the 1990s, several genes have been identified in the hypothalamic leptin‐melanocortin pathway [12]. The LEP gene codes for Leptin synthesized by adipocytes, which binds its receptor (LEPR) to POMC neurons in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, inducing expression of proopiomelanocortin (POMC). POMC is cleaved by a proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin 1 (PCSK1) into hormones, including α‐melanocyte stimulating hormone (αMSH) [13]. αMSH binds melanocortin receptors MC4R (α‐MSH and/or β‐MSH) and MC1R (α‐MSH) [8, 14], responsible for satiety and decreased food intake. Other genes like SH2B1 or MRAP2 code for regulators of LEPR or MC4R proteins. SIM1, ADCY3, NTRK2 and BDNF are genes involved in the development of neuronal populations in the hypothalamic region [14, 15]. Variants in these genes are associated with severe early‐onset obesity and endocrine abnormalities, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism for homozygous LEPR variants [16], malabsorption and corticotropic insufficiency for homozygous PCSK1 variants [13] and corticotropin insufficiency, and inconstant phototype 1 with fair skin and red hair for homozygous POMC variants [8]. MC4R Variants are the most widely described with heterozygous pathogenic variants present in about 3%–5% of individuals with severe childhood obesity [17].

With new personalized therapies targeting these genetic defects [18], a genetic diagnosis can provide access to tailored treatments. The MC4R agonist setmelanotide has been approved for treating obesity in patients with genetically confirmed Bardet Biedl Syndrome, biallelic POMC loss‐of‐function (including PCSK1 deficiency), and biallelic LEPR deficiency [19]. However, identifying patients who will benefit from these therapies is impeded by limited knowledge of all genetic variants [20], and the time‐consuming nature of such an analysis.

The present study was conducted with the objective of evaluating the diagnostic value of genetic testing in a large group of patients with severe early‐onset obesity. The primary aim of this study was to support the use of precision medicine in the care of patients with obesity. It is imperative to identify patients with a monogenic or syndromic form of obesity in order to adapt clinical management, including offering targeted therapies, tailoring nutritional and psychological interventions, and guiding family screening and genetic counseling. The study was conducted in a French laboratory affiliated with a Clinical Specialized Obesity Center. A customized gene panel was used to identify rare genetic variants, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variants (CNVs), in a cohort of 1066 patients with early‐onset severe obesity.

Methods

Cohort of Patients

From January 2018 to December 2023, blood samples were obtained from 1066 patients referred to French endocrinology departments for early‐onset obesity (12 university hospitals/specialized obesity centers and 10 hospitals). Genetic diagnosis of rare obesity was prescribed in cases of severe obesity in adults (BMI > 35) or children (IOTF > 35) or in cases of early‐onset obesity (obesity before the age of 5). When sending blood samples to the laboratory, clinicians provided the following clinical information on a completed request form: height, weight, BMI, country of origin, consanguinity, date of diagnosis, details of any eating disorders, intellectual disability, hypothalamic‐pituitary abnormalities, delayed puberty, or mental retardation. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and for diagnostic purposes. Written informed consent from the patients was obtained, according to the French bioethic laws. Clinicians suspecting genetic obesity used the Obsgen online tool designed by the French rare disease reference centers PRADORT (https://redc.integromics.fr/surveys/?s=XEFPD474YT). Genetic diagnosis was conducted at the Hospices Civils de Lyon by ISO15189‐certified staff from sample preparation to variant analysis. All genetic diagnoses were examined, as well as clinical data concerning the patients' families and the patients' provided medical history.

DNA Analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from EDTA peripheral blood using a Maxwell 16 LEV Blood DNA Kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, USA). A custom next‐generation sequencing (NGS) workflow including genes involved in rare genetic obesity was performed. The coding regions (including at least 30 base‐pairs of flanking intron of each exon) of the genes were sequenced. Sequencing libraries were prepared using a SeqCap‐EZ solution‐based enrichment strategy (Roche‐NimbleGen Madison, WI, USA). High‐speed sequencing was performed using a NextSeq500 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The gene panel includes the following genes: AGRP, ALMS1, BDNF, DYRK1B, FTO, KSR2, INSR, LEP, LEPR, MC4R, MYT1L, NTRK2, PCSK1, POMC, SIM1, TUB, and UCP3 (Table 1). In 2022 ADCY3, MRAP2, MAGEL2, SH2B1, and NCOA1 genes were added to the panel, which limits the 6‐years retrospective analysis for these genes. All variants reported were confirmed by a Sanger technique on Applied Biosystems 3500 XL Genetic Analyzer followed by analysis using SeqScape v3 software (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). When possible, a family segregation study was carried out on first‐degree relatives using targeted Sanger sequencing. All deletions/duplications highlighted by the analysis of relative coverage in NGS were confirmed using a real‐time qPCR technology (LightCycler 480, Roche‐Diagnostics, IN, USA).

Bioinformatics Analyses

The in‐house pipeline of the Hospices Civils de Lyon was used, which comprises a succession of processing modules on Nextflow pipeline generation software (https://www.nextflow.io/docs/latest/index.html) and follows recommendations from the Broad Institute (www.broadinstitute.org/). For each sample, read pairs were trimmed using Trimmomatic [21]. Reads were then aligned against the GRCh37 version of the human genome using BWA‐MEM [22], producing a Binary Alignment Map (BAM) file indexed and sorted with SAMtools [23]. The BAM files were preprocessed: (i) duplicate marked by PicardTools Mark Duplicates [24]; (ii) indel realignment and (iii) nucleotide recalibration were performed using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) IndelRealigner and GATK BaseRecalibrator [25]. FastQ and BAM metrics were collected using FastQC [26] and PicardTools [24]. The variant call was performed using GATK HaplotypeCaller [25], procuring a Variant Call Format (VCF) file per sample. Genotyping was performed using GATK GenotypeGVCFs [25], merging samples into one VCF. Variant normalization and annotation were handled by GATK LeftAlignAndTrimVariants [25] and the snpEff/SnpSift toolbox [27]. VCF metrics were collected using snpEff/SnpSift [27]. CNVs were called using DeCovA [28].

Variant Interpretation

Variant nomenclature followed the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) recommendations (https://varnomen.hgvs.org/). The allele frequencies of variants were checked in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomADV4; https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). For nonsynonymous coding variants, in silico analyses were performed using Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) to predict the deleteriousness of missense variants [29]. Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL) software was used as an ensemble method for predicting the pathogenicity of missense variants based on a combination of scores from 13 tools: MutPred, FATHMM v2.3, VEST 3.0, PolyPhen‐2, SIFT, PROVEAN, MutationAssessor, MutationTaster, LRT, GERP++, SiPhy, phyloP, and phastCons software [30]; these predict the possible impact of the genetic variant on the structure and function of the human protein. For splicing‐site variants, in silico analyses were performed using SPiP [31] and SpliceAI [32] algorithms via Mobidetails (https://mobidetails.iurc.montp.inserm.fr/MD/). Variants were classified according to the recommendation of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [29]. Only rare coding variants were included (minor allele frequency < 1%). Patients for whom a rare genetic variant was identified were seen in consultation in 23 French endocrinology departments (76 patients from a department of the Hospices Civils de Lyon, 67 from another French endocrinology department).

Minigene Assay

Minigene assay was performed as previously described [33]. A region spanning 363 pb in intron 5, exon 6 and 372 pb in intron 6 of LEPR gene was amplified from the patient DNA using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Evry FRANCE) and inserted in the pTB minigene vector using the In‐fusion‐HD PCR cloning kit (Clontech, Mountain View CA USA). HeLa cells were transfected with 1 μg of plasmid using Fugene (Promega WI USA) following the manufacturer's recommendations. Total RNA was extracted 48 h after transfection using RNAqueous‐4PCR Total RNA Isolation Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RT‐PCR was performed with the Transcriptor High Fidelity cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Diagnostics, IN, USA) and cDNA was amplified using vector‐specific primers surrounding the cloning site. PCR products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel and sequenced to identify splicing events.

Results

Characteristics of Patients

For this study, the results of the diagnostic gene panel analyzed in 1066 patients from January 2018 to December 2023 were reviewed. Patients from French endocrinology departments were included: 419 males (39.3%) and 647 females (60.7%); 499 children (mean ± standard error [SE] age 11.0 years ± 4.7; range 1–17 years) and 567 adults (mean ± SE age 33.0 years ± 11.5; range 18–67 years). 853 subjects (80%) were self‐ or caregiver‐reported to have overeating or binge eating. Genetic analysis was carried out in cases of obesity since childhood and severe obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2 or, for subjects age < 18 years, a BMI exceeding the IOTF cut‐off of 30 mg/kg2) with elements for a monogenic obesity: Eating disorder, central hypogonadism, corticotrope deficiency, pubertal delay, or failure of bariatric surgery.

Genetic Results

A total of 155 variants were identified, 152 SNVs (Table S1) and 3 CNVs (Table 2), that were either pathogenic, probably pathogenic, or of undetermined significance in 143 patients. Overall, 13.1% of patients (140/1066) had SNVs in target genes: 74 children (mean ± SE age 10.6 years ± 4.7; range 2–17 years; mean BMI 34.1 (SDS ± 8.8) kg/m2) and 66 adults (mean ± SE age 37.2 years ± 14.1; range 18–70 years; mean BMI 43.8 (SDS ± 10.5) kg/m2). Among them, 97 (67.8%) had SNVs in MC4R, LEPR, PCSK1, and POMC, with SNV in MC4R being the most common (36/140) (Figure 1).

Among the 152 rare SNVs detected, 34 have been previously reported in the literature and classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, considered causal for obesity; 118 novel variants were classified as VUS (Table S1), not used for patient management or genetic counseling. Familial segregation analysis was performed for 30 variants with the aim of reclassifying the variant as probably benign or probably pathogenic. All were inherited from the parents; no de novo variants were identified. Three patients inherited variants from a relative without obesity, raising questions about pathogenicity or penetrance. Other patients inherited the variant from a mother or father with obesity. Three segregation studies were inconclusive due to missing samples.

Considering that monogenic obesity may be associated with large deletions and duplications, CNVs were analyzed in target genes. Three CNSs were found in MC4R and LEPR genes in three patients, never described in the literature and classified as VUS (Table 2).

ADCY3, MRAP2, MAGEL2, SH2B1, and NCOA1 were recently added to the obesity panel, explaining why few or no variants were identified.

MC4R Variants

In this cohort, 36 heterozygous coding SNVs (23.7% of the total rare SNVs detected) were identified, including 32 missense, three frameshift, and one nonsense variant. Twenty‐eight (77.8%) were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic and eight as VUS. Family segregation study was conducted for eight patients, all with the MC4R variant inherited from a parent with severe obesity, confirming probable pathogenicity. All these variants had in silico prediction scores indicating a possible impact on the protein.

CNV analysis identified a complete heterozygous deletion of MC4R in a 21‐year‐old female patient with severe early‐onset obesity (BMI 41.1 kg/m2) (Figure 2; Table 2). She developed early‐onset obesity before age 3. She had gained weight progressively since childhood despite dieting since the age of 7 years, with alternating restrictive dieting and binge eating. Her leptin level was elevated to 48.4 ng/mL (reference interval 5.4–20.6 ng/mL). Unfortunately, a segregation study could not be carried out as the patient was lost to follow‐up.

LEPR Variants

Nineteen Class 3, 4, or 5 SNVs (12.5% of the total rare SNVs detected) were identified: 18 in the heterozygous state and one in the homozygous state; 14 missenses, one frameshift, one nonsense, and three intronic variants. A family segregation study was carried out for six patients. In five of them, the variant was identified in the mother, who had severe obesity. Only one SNV has been identified in the homozygous state LEPR:c.703+5G>A in an 11‐month‐old male referred to the Hospices Civils de Lyon for early severe obesity with insatiable hunger since birth [34]. Splicing‐prediction software indicates that this variant could have a strong impact on splicing because it reduces the strength of the natural site. A functional study using Minigene assay (Figure 3) was performed attesting to the skipping of exon 6, and therefore in a premature stop leading to premature truncation of the protein beyond the cytokine receptor homology domain 1 (p.(Pro166Glufs*3)). The patient weighed 7.2 kg (standard deviation score [SDS] 11.5) at 3 months of age, 20.5 kg (SDS 19.0) at 1 year of age, and 40.5 kg (SDS 117.5) at 26 months of age. The LEPR variant was found in the heterozygous state in both consanguineous healthy parents.

In this cohort, two CNVs for LEPR were reported: one heterozygous duplication and one homozygous deletion (Figure 2). The duplication of exons 3 and 4 of LEPR gene in the heterozygous state in one patient was confirmed by quantitative PCR. It has not been described in the literature; its role in the phenotype is unknown and it has been classified as VUS because it has not been demonstrated to be a tandem duplication. At 3 years of age, the female patient's BMI was 22.5 kg/m2 with a normal endocrine check‐up. Her BMI was 23.0 kg/m2 at 5 years of age and 26.8 kg/m2 at 9 years of age. The segregation study showed that this duplication was absent in the patient's mother (BMI 26.8 kg/m2) and present in her father (BMI 32.8 kg/m2). These segregation data were insufficient to conclude that this heterozygous duplication of the LEPR gene variant was pathogenic. A homozygous LEPR CNV was found in another patient with early‐onset severe obesity (BMI 65.7 kg/m2) with delayed puberty and a family history of obesity. This homozygous deletion of exons 6 and 7 and a large portion of exon 8 of the LEPR gene does not preserve the reading frame, and the predicted consequence is the synthesis of a non‐functional truncated protein. This deletion has not been described in the gnomAD database. This deletion was considered to be probably causal for genetic obesity. The study of symptomatic relatives could not be conducted.

PCSK1 Variants

Twenty‐three SNVs (15.1% of the total rare SNVs detected) were identified, four pathogenic and 19 of undetermined significance, all in the heterozygous state, all missense variants except one, which is a frameshift variant (Table S1). No CNVs were detected.

POMC Variants

Twenty coding SNVs were reported, including 17 missense variants, and three small duplications. The POMC: p.(Arg236Gly) variant was identified in seven patients. This variant located in the protein domain POMC/corticotropin ACTH leads to a missense coding effect. It was described as pathogenic by in silico prediction software, but, given its frequency in the gnomAD database V4 (h.f = 0.28%), this variant was considered as a susceptibility factor for obesity. Recently, functional studies were carried out on the eight POMC VUS [35]: two showed loss of function and six others showed gain of function. No CNV was identified.

SIM1 Variants

Ten rare heterozygous SNVs were identified, two associated with other gene variants (one with a MC4R variant, and the other one with a PCSK1 variant). Five adults harboring SIM1 SNVs had a mean BMI of 57.2 (SDS ± 13.8) kg/m2 and five children harboring SIM1 SNVs had a mean BMI of 34.7 (SDS ± 8.5) kg/m2 and eating disorders. None of the SIM1 SNVs found have been previously described in the literature, and no functional analyses were performed. Two familial segregation studies were carried out and concluded that the SIM1 variant was inherited from one obese parent (BMI 30 kg/m2). Considering that SIM1 is sensitive to haploinsufficiency, it is likely that the two frameshift variants located in the protein domain PAS (PER‐ARNT‐SIM) and single‐minded C‐terminal domain may be pathogenic.

Patients With Variants in Multiple Genes

The presence of SNVs in different genes was detected in four adults with severe early‐onset obesity (mean BMI of 45.9 [SDS ± 5.1] kg/m2) and seven children exceeding the IOTF 30 cut‐off. Ten patients had two missense‐variants; in MC4R and SIM1; in MC4R and PCSK1; in MC4R and LEPR; in MC4R and POMC; in LEPR and FTO; in PCSK1 and SIM1; in PCSK1 and KSR2; in PCSK1 and FTO; in AGRP and INSR, in MYT1L and SH2B1 (Table S1). Among them, a 24 year‐old‐male patient (BMI 43.2 kg/m2) with weight gain starting around the age of 8 and with both parents living with obesity, as well as his brother and sister who had undergone sleeve surgery, three missense variants were present: One in FTO gene, and two in POMC gene. For the two POMC variants, analysis of the bioinformatics data suggests that one variant is inherited from the father and the other one from the mother, confirming that the patient is a compound heterozygous. The severe phenotype suggests a cumulative effect for these variants. Genetic obesity probably involves complex heredity, both multifactorial and polygenic, with a cumulative effect of variants.

Discussion

In clinical practice, establishing a genetic diagnosis can substantially transform patient care. Furthermore, it can support the use of MC4R‐targeted therapies, such as setmelanotide, in eligible cases and guide decisions regarding intensive lifestyle management, endocrine follow‐up, or inclusion in clinical trials. For pediatric patients, an early diagnosis facilitates access to emerging therapies in a timely manner and supports the development of care pathways adapted to their needs from childhood to adulthood. SNVs and CNVs in a panel of well‐known genes for severe genetic obesity and potential targets for personalized medicine were described in this large multicentric cohort of patients with severe early‐onset obesity. Diagnostic yield of 3.2% was similar to that described in the literature when restricted to pathogenic and probably pathogenic variants. Adding VUS improved this diagnostic yield to 10.3%, even better in the pediatric cohort (definitive genetic diagnosis in 3.8% and VUS in 13.4%).

Rare genetic obesity linked to the presence of pathogenic variants in the MC4R gene was the most prevalent in this study as well as in the literature [17]. More than 200 SNVs have already been described (https://www.mc4r.org.uk/). One rare (frequency 0.094%) deletion of a 2.589 kb region in the MC4R gene was revealed in this study. Only three publications have previously reported large deletions: Macé et al. reported a 300 kb deletion [36]; Abdullah et al. reported a 6.46 Mb deletion in the long arm of chromosome 18q carrying MC4R and 31 other genes in a child with severe obesity [37]; and Turner et al. reported a 2.6 Mb deletion on the long arm of chromosome 18q21.31 carrying MC4R in a young boy with severe obesity and hyperphagia and who inherited the deletion from his father (normal BMI) [38]. MC4R is sensitive to haploinsufficiency. These three cases, along with the case of the patient described in this cohort, emphasized the importance of looking for CNVs as a rare cause of obesity.

Here, two other CNVs in the LEPR gene were highlighted. Literature already reports six carriers of LEPR deletion with an obese phenotype, with a homozygous deletion of exons 4 to 20 in a 7‐year‐old boy (consanguineous parents) with a BMI of 33.2 kg/m2 (z score + 5.4) upon presentation [39]. A study of patients with obesity from Reunion Island reported truncation of the LEPR protein by 172 amino acids in patients with deletions spanning exons 6 to 8 of the LEPR gene [40]. These patients had early‐onset severe obesity and hyperphagia. While CNV in the LEPR gene is rare, it requires verification.

Patients with POMC, PCSK1, and LEPR homozygous deficiency are known to have early‐onset obesity [41, 42, 43]. However, transmission close to the autosomal dominant mode is debated for class 3, 4 or 5 heterozygous variants of the PCSK1 and LEPR genes. Several previous studies have found that heterozygous, null, or complete loss‐of‐function variants cause monogenic obesity. Other studies have found that obesity can be explained by these variants in 68% of heterozygous carriers [44, 45]. The risk of developing obesity with a pathogenic variant in the heterozygous state appears to be high, but there is variability according to the patient's ethnic group and age, with a higher effect in children [41]. The broad analysis of this gene panel for diagnostic purposes has made it possible to identify VUS in genes that were previously rarely analyzed and described as being inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Patients carrying a heterozygous variant whose pathogenicity has been confirmed by functional studies [35] can be included in clinical studies. The inclusion of these patients in clinical trials should enable us to clarify the role of heterozygote variants in the phenotype. The EMANATE clinical trial (NCT05093634), a Phase 3, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study of setmelanotide in patients with POMC, PCSK1, LEPR, NCOA1, SH2B1 gene defects (RM‐493‐035), has enrolled patients with heterozygous variants of POMC, PCSK1, and LEPR, patients with homozygous, heterozygous, or compound heterozygous variants of NCOA1 and SH2B1, and patients with chromosomal 16p11.2 deletion in the SH2B1 gene. Seven patients from this cohort were included: Two patients with a heterozygous POMC variant, and five patients with a heterozygous PCSK1 variant. If transmission of the variant is autosomal recessive, whole genome sequencing could reveal a second deep intronic variant on this gene that cannot be identified by target short read NGS sequencing.

The large number of VUSs identified in this cohort underlines the need for functional studies such as Minigene assays, which demonstrated the pathogenicity of the LEPR splice variant.

In this cohort, in patients who did not have SNVs or CNVs in the gene panel, the following possible reasons might help explain the negative results. First, there might be no innate monogenic abnormality involved, with other factors such as the patient's environment playing a key role in the pathogenesis. Second, whatever the analysis technique, an important condition is to have a high pre‐test probability. The physician must check for consanguinity in the parents, and draw up a family tree to look for other cases of obesity; the physician should review patient's BMI since birth, search for eating disorders, assess intellectual development or other symptoms (short stature, microcephaly, neurosensory anomalies, dysmorphia, hypogonadism, pubertal development), and request a biochemical check‐up. Third, deep intronic and non‐coding variants in regulatory elements that alter gene expression contribute to the pathogenesis of severe obesity. Using analysis of a reference gene panel enabled us a rapid increase in ability to impute rare variants limited to the exons of the genes. Whole genome sequencing will enable us to analyze data from patients with severe obesity with a negative gene panel. Access to genome sequencing for obesity has been available in France only since 2025; therefore, we do not have data to present in this article [46]. We therefore expect that other rare variants will be identified, and that we will uncover other genes involved, other rare non‐coding variants, structural variants, or other complex genotypes that could explain the phenotype.

In conclusion, the NGS targeted gene panel analysis led to a definitive diagnosis of a monogenic obesity disorder in 3.2% of patients, and revealed VUS in 10.3% of patients requiring an extensive complementary genetic study of major or affected relatives and co‐segregation of the variant with the phenotype or further functional studies to confirm their pathogenicity. The highest yield was achieved in the pediatric subgroup, establishing a definitive diagnosis in 19 (3.8%) of the 499 children with severe early‐onset obesity, and revealing a VUS in 13.4% of this group. These findings gave a realistic insight into the early diagnostic yield of genetic testing for patients with obesity in a French hospital. As targeted therapy is currently available for the treatment of obesity associated with genetically confirmed BBS, biallelic POMC loss‐of‐function (including PCSK1 deficiency), and biallelic LEPR deficiency in adults and children, genetic diagnosis will become crucial for access to and early treatment with these new personalized therapeutics. The integration of genetic testing into routine clinical care for patients with severe early‐onset obesity is becoming increasingly important. This improves diagnostic accuracy, enables targeted treatment and helps to avoid misdiagnosis or inappropriate interventions. As new therapeutics emerge and the role of genetics in obesity management becomes more central, systematic testing should be considered in selected clinical settings.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was not possible to recover the precise phenotype of all patients, which made it impossible to perform genotype/phenotype correlations in all cases. Secondly, the study highlights the challenges involved in conducting genetic research on obesity. Common polygenic obesity is prevalent, which makes segregation difficult. There is also phenotypic variability, and very limited functional data exist to confirm the pathogenicity of variants. Thirdly, in addition to monogenic obesity, rare forms of hypothalamic obesity can result from syndromic obesity, such as BBS. This gene panel did not include the BBS genes. However, it has recently been expanded to include all 21 BBS genes, which will be examined in a follow‐up study.
