Does More Compute Make Every Adversarial Attack Transferable?
Greg Gluch, Shafi Goldwasser, Anastasiia Struss

Abstract
Transferable attacks, or transferable jailbreaks, are a particularly strong version of adversarial examples that fool all defenders in a class. In this work, we study when more compute enables transferable attacks. We show that, when there is a quadratic gap in computational resources between attacker and defender, every learnable classification task admits either a transferable attack or an adversarial defense. For generative tasks, this dichotomy fails below a quadratic gap, i.e., more compute for the attacker does not lead to a transferable attack. We prove a stronger result that implies this failure: a defense that spends additional computation after it sees the input can be provably strictly stronger than every defense that publishes a fixed model. This result provides formal evidence that increasing inference-time compute can improve robustness, as empirically observed by Zaremba et al. (2025) . We assume the existence of an array of cryptographic primitives: identity-based fully homomorphic encryption, publicly verifiable zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge, non-parallelizing languages, and incrementally verifiable computation.

Introduction

AI agents carry out user requests by processing documents, browsing webpages, and interacting with external tools. This access to information exposes them to adversarial content: instructions embedded in a document or tool response can redirect an agent away from its intended task. Such indirect prompt injection attacks have been demonstrated in applications that integrate large language models (LLMs) and studied systematically in tool-using AI agents
(
Greshake et al., 2023
;
Debenedetti et al., 2024
)
. These attacks bring renewed urgency to a fundamental theoretical question in adversarial robustness: can an AI model detect adversarial inputs on which it would potentially make a mistake? Detection has been studied for bounded perturbations
(
Tramer, 2022
)
, for arbitrary test examples with abstention
(
Goldwasser et al., 2020
)
, and for prompt filters of language models
(
Ball et al., 2025
)
.

Transferability is one of the most surprising features of adversarial attacks. Different models could have entirely different weaknesses, yet an input constructed to fool one model can also fool others. This phenomenon was observed in image classification and later in jailbreaks against aligned language models
(
Szegedy et al., 2014
;
Papernot et al., 2016
;
Liu et al., 2016
;
Zou et al., 2023
)
.
Bose et al. (2020)
cast the search for attacks that transfer to an entire hypothesis class as a min-max game. The observed transfer between particular models invites the following question:

Question 1.

When does the existence of an attack against each individual model imply the existence of a single attack that transfers to all models from a class?

In this work we ask how the answer depends on the computational resources available to the attacker and defender.
A Transferable Attack (TA) is a single attack procedure that succeeds against every defender within a specified resource bound.
An Adversarial Defense (AD) publishes a model that remains useful on natural inputs and withstands every attacker within the corresponding resource bound.

By a published model we mean the trained model made available (with white-box access) to the attacker.

For classification, we prove that every learnable task admits a TA or an AD, with a roughly quadratic resource tradeoff.
In this sense, this resolves
Question 1 affirmatively for classification tasks
at this gap: if every model can be attacked, then a single attack transfers to every defender with quadratically fewer resources.
We build on the framework of
Gluch et al. (2025)
, who established a trichotomy between TAs, ADs, and Watermarks (WMs) depending on computational power of the parties.
Our result shows that the WM alternative is not needed at the quadratic gap.
We note
Bubeck et al. (2019)
;
Degwekar et al. (2019)
;
Garg et al. (2020)
also considered computational constraints in the context of adversarial robustness.

We emphasize that the dichotomy between TAs and ADs, where it holds, is good news either way.
Either a defense exists, and we can use it.
Or a single attack wins against every defender within our resource bounds: no defender we could build at these resources would withstand it, so there is no point in trying harder.

We next consider generative tasks, where an input can have many valid answers.
We construct a learning task that, under cryptographic assumptions, admits
neither a TA nor an AD just below a quadratic resource gap.
This gives a
negative answer to Question 1
for generative tasks
in this resource regime.
To establish this result, we prove the following statement first: additional inference-time computation can improve robustness.

This statement is motivated by modern AI systems, which often reason,
search, or call tools after receiving a query, devoting additional computation
to that query.
Experiments with reasoning models show that such computation can improve robustness
(
Zaremba et al., 2025
)
;
see also
Wu et al. (2025)
and
McDonald et al. (2026)
.

We formalize defenses using inference-time resources as an Active Adversarial Defense (AAD): after the attacker sees the published model and chooses an attack, the defender uses additional computational resources to produce its final response.
Our constructed learning task, under cryptographic assumptions, admits an AAD but no AD within the specified resource bounds.
Thus it constitutes a formal evidence that increasing inference-time resources can improve robustness.

We also show that the inference phase of the AAD is, in particular, a defender against TAs
within the relevant resource bounds.
It follows that our task admits AAD but neither a TA nor an AD, and this proves that the answer to
Question 1
for generative tasks is negative as advertised.

Classification: A Dichotomy at a Quadratic Gap.

We establish a dichotomy answering
Question 1

We prove that a learnable classification task either has an attack that fools every sufficiently small defender, or there is a defense that holds up against every sufficiently small attacker. This is a win-win statement similar in spirit to

1.3
Discussion and Open Questions

We showed that every learnable classification task admits a TA or an AD once the stronger party has a quadratic resource advantage over its opponent. For generative tasks the picture changes. We constructed a task that admits neither at a similar resource gap, while there is a successful AAD that spends the square root of its training budget during inference.
Inference-time computation can thus improve robustness that no fixed model can provide, which is in line with
Zaremba et al. (2025)
.
These results taken together strongly suggest that the relative size exponent is a key parameter for determining whether a TA or an AD exists for a given task.
Two main questions whose answers would fill the empty parts of Figure
6
remain open.

Open Question 1.

Does every learnable generative task admit a TA or an AD at a quadratic, or at least some polynomial, resource advantage?

Open Question 2.

Is there a classification task that admits neither a TA nor an AD when the fixed party has less than a quadratic resource advantage?

In Section
2
we give a justification on why these questions cannot be answered by extending results in the classification setting to the generative case and vice versa.

Related Work

Robustness and Adversarial Examples.

The field of adversarial robustness has a rich literature

(
Szegedy et al., 2014
;
Gilmer et al., 2018
;
Raghunathan et al., 2018
;
Wong and Kolter, 2018
;
Engstrom et al., 2017
)
.
For classification tasks, adversarial training
(
Madry et al., 2018
)
, which trains the model on adversarial examples, has shown empirical promise, and certified defenses
(
Raghunathan et al., 2018
;
Wong and Kolter, 2018
)
provide provable guarantees within a specified perturbation bound.
Both address bounded perturbations of natural inputs, whereas the attacker in our model may submit arbitrary inputs.

A Computational Lens on Adversarial Examples.

The view that adversarial vulnerability can be a computational rather than a statistical phenomenon goes back to
Bubeck et al. (2019)
, who exhibit learning tasks on which a robust classifier exists but cannot be found by efficient learners
(
Bubeck et al., 2018
, see also)
, and to
Degwekar et al. (2019)
, who prove such separations under cryptographic assumptions and complement them with “win-win” results, in which the failure of efficient robust learning yields a cryptographic primitive.

Mahloujifar and Mahmoody (2019)
identify settings in which polynomial-time attackers are as strong as unbounded ones, so that computational hardness cannot help the defender, whereas
Garg et al. (2020)
use digital signatures to build classifiers that are robust against polynomial-time attackers although no classifier is robust against unbounded ones.
These works consider bounded perturbations of natural inputs and ask whether a robust classifier exists or can be found efficiently.
We consider arbitrary inputs, let the defender flag inputs, ask whether a single attack works against a whole class of bounded defenders, and measure the answer in explicit circuit-size exponents.
The dichotomy of Lemma
18
is a win-win statement in the sense of
Degwekar et al. (2019)
, with a transferable attack in place of a cryptographic primitive.
The resource-bounded protocol view that we adopt is due to
Gluch et al. (2025)
, who proved the trichotomy between TAs, ADs and WMs that Lemma
18
refines, constructed a TA from FHE, and showed that the TA alternative cannot be removed in general.

Transferability of Adversarial Examples.

One of the most intriguing facts about adversarial examples is their
transferability
: adversarial examples crafted for one model often transfer to other architectures or training sets.
This phenomenon was discovered early and studied extensively
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
;
Papernot et al., 2016
;
Liu et al., 2016
)
and more recently was observed in the context of LLMs
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
.

Ilyas et al. (2019)
hypothesize that adversarial examples exist because of highly predictive but non-robust features in the data, which would explain transferability.
Universal perturbations
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al., 2017
)
fool one model on most inputs. A transferable attack in our sense is the dual object, one input distribution that fools every model in a class.

Ball et al. (2025)
show, for concrete language models, that adversarial prompts can be computationally indistinguishable from benign ones for every efficient filter, which is the indistinguishability requirement of a transferable attack against filters.

Gluch et al. (2025)
showed that the existence of ADs and TAs depends critically on the computational resources of the defender and the attacker, treated in aggregate.
We separate training-time from inference-time resources.
For classification tasks, an attack with a quadratic resource advantage transfers to all bounded models unless a defense exists (Lemma
18
).
On our generative task, every published model with bounded evaluation cost can be attacked, yet no attack transfers to an active defender that spends about the square root of the training budget at inference (Corollary
25
).

Game-Theoretic Formulations.

Zero-sum games between attackers and classifiers have been used to characterize the minimax adversarial risk
(
Pydi and Jog, 2020
)
, to show that pure equilibria need not exist and that randomized classifiers help
(
Pinot et al., 2020
;
Meunier et al., 2021
)
, and to identify particular attack–defense pairs as equilibria
(
Pal and Vidal, 2020
)
.
Closest to our transferable attacks is the adversarial example game of
Bose et al. (2020)
, in which a generator is trained to fool every classifier in a hypothesis class and the optimal generator is characterized by a min-max equilibrium.
In those games the strategies range over perturbation sets and parametric model classes with a fixed loss.
In ours the pure strategies are all circuits of a given size, the payoff combines correctness with a distinguishing term, and the sparse approximate equilibria of
Lipton and Young (1994)
and
Althöfer (1994)
are what turn an equilibrium into a circuit of bounded size.
The uniform mixture of
k
attackers produced by Lemma
18
is the theoretical counterpart of the ensemble-based transfer attacks of
Liu et al. (2016)
.

Backdoors, Watermarks and Obfuscation.

Goldwasser et al. (2022)
plant backdoors in models that no efficient party can detect, and
Goldwasser et al. (2024)
show how to
mitigate
even such a backdoor by exploiting local self-correction properties of the ground truth for linear and low-degree polynomial regression tasks.
13
13

13

It is important to note that
Goldwasser et al. (2024)
and our work consider different models.
In our work, the party that returns the answer is the same as the one that produced the model, and aims to compute a model without introducing backdoors.
In contrast, in
Goldwasser et al. (2024)
, the model may be produced by a different party, one that is actively attempting to insert backdoors.
This key difference explains why the conclusions of the two works are compatible.

Christiano et al. (2024)
define defendability against backdoors as a game between an attacker who plants a backdoor and a defender who must detect it at evaluation time, and relate it to PAC learnability and obfuscation.

Gluch et al. (2025)
treat backdoor-based watermarks as a third alternative to ADs and TAs. Lemma
18
shows that at a quadratic resource gap this alternative is not needed.

Detection, Abstention and Defenses That Act at Test Time.

Selective classification lets a classifier abstain on some inputs
(
El-Yaniv and others, 2010
)
. Our flag plays that role, except that a flagged entry still counts as an error when it is answered incorrectly.

Goldwasser et al. (2020)
study the transductive setting in which the learner sees the unlabeled test batch, may abstain, and must have low error on the points it labels and a low abstention rate on natural data, against an unbounded adversary and for classes of bounded Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension.

Montasser et al. (2022)
give transductive robust learning guarantees with robust error linear in the VC dimension, and
Chen et al. (2021)
study transductive defenses empirically.
Our AD commits its model and its rule before seeing the input, is scored entry by entry, and bounds all parties computationally (see Section
3.6
).

Tramer (2022)
shows that in the bounded-perturbation model detecting adversarial examples is as hard as classifying them robustly, if computation is disregarded.
Our Robustness-or-Distinguishability alternative concerns arbitrary inputs, computationally bounded parties and a distributional flag rate rather than per-input detection, so that reduction does not apply. The coupling of the two alternatives in Lemma
18
is its computational counterpart.
Randomized smoothing
(
Cohen et al., 2019
)
certifies
\ell_{2}
robustness by averaging a base classifier over Gaussian noise at prediction time and, in our language, is (partially) active.
14
14

14

What we mean by partially active is that there are two ways to look at smoothing.
Smoothing can be seen as performed during inference time, thus making the defense active, but it can also be “hard-coded” into
f
and then the defense would be considered as static according to our definitions.

Adaptive test-time defenses such as adversarial purification
(
Nie et al., 2022
)
, test-time training
(
Sun et al., 2020
)
and runtime masking
(
Wu et al., 2020
)
spend computation on the received input.

Croce et al. (2022)
found that, under an adaptive evaluation
(
Tramer et al., 2020
)
, none of the defenses they tested improved on static defenses, while all of them raised the cost of inference.
An AAD is the formal counterpart of these defenses.
Theorem
24
exhibits a task on which computation after the input provably helps, and Section
2
explains the mechanism.

Inference-Time Computation and Robustness.

Zaremba et al. (2025)
report that giving reasoning models more inference-time computation improves their robustness across many attack families, with exceptions when the attacker can make the model reason less, divert its reasoning, or exploit ambiguous policies.

Wu et al. (2025)
find that these gains depend on the reasoning trace being hidden from the attacker and can reverse when it is exposed, and
McDonald et al. (2026)
find that inference-time computation helps mainly when training-time robustness already covers the attacked inputs.
Our notion of an AAD captures the abstract mechanism, computation spent after the input is known, and Theorem
24
shows that it can provably help.
The defenses we construct do not build on the published model at inference (Section
3.6
), so they model a separate certified computation rather than a model that reasons longer. Whether some task forces robust inference to reuse the trained model is an open question.

Cryptographic Tools.

Time-lock puzzles
(
Rivest et al., 1996
;
Bitansky et al., 2016
)
formalize computations that take
t
sequential steps, and verifiable delay functions
(
Boneh et al., 2018
)
and continuous verifiable delay functions
(
Ephraim et al., 2020
)
add that the result can be verified quickly.
A continuous verifiable delay function is iteratively sequential and iteratively verifiable, which is exactly properties 1 and 2 of Section
2
. The clear part of
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
is such a function, assembled from a non-parallelizing language
(
Bitansky et al., 2016
)
and incrementally verifiable computation
(
Valiant, 2008
;
Bitansky et al., 2013
)
.
We keep the two primitives separate because our proofs need average-case hardness against non-uniform circuits with sample access, and evaluation of the certificates under identity-based FHE
(
Gentry et al., 2013
)
.
Knowledge soundness of SNARKs with respect to arbitrary auxiliary input is implausible under indistinguishability obfuscation
(
Bitansky et al., 2014
;
Boyle and Pass, 2015
)
. Lemma
21
, and hence Theorem
22
, uses extraction only with respect to the benign distribution of fresh signatures under an independently generated key and uniform coins.
Fully homomorphic encryption implies collision-resistant hashing
(
Ishai et al., 2005
)
, and there is no black-box construction of collision-resistant hashing from identity-based encryption
(
Mahmoody and Mohammed, 2016
)
, and so none from public-key encryption, which has a black-box construction from identity-based encryption. Hence there is no black-box construction of FHE from public-key encryption. In this sense the assumption of Section
4.2
is weaker than that of
Gluch et al. (2025)
.

Alignment and Jailbreaking.

In the context of LLMs,
alignment
aims at making sure that the models act consistently with human goals, e.g., by not answering dangerous prompts or not using offensive language, etc.
There is a fast-growing literature about trying to achieve alignment
(
Amodei et al., 2016
;
Greenblatt et al., 2024
;
Ji et al., 2023
)
.
In consequence, there is a lot of research on trying to avoid alignment by designing
jailbreaks

(
Andriushchenko et al., 2024
;
Chao et al., 2023
;
Mehrotra et al., 2024
;
Wei et al., 2023
)
, which involves crafting prompts that bypass filters and protections embedded in the models.
There is also some interest in adversarial examples for LLMs
(
Zou et al., 2023
;
Carlini et al., 2023
;
Wen et al., 2023
)
.

Technical Overview

2.1
Dichotomy for Classification Tasks via a Zero-Sum Game

The proof of the dichotomy follows a game-theoretic approach. We consider a finite zero-sum game
(
von Neumann, 1928
)
between attackers and defenders and use sparse approximate optimal strategies
(
Lipton and Young, 1994
;
Althöfer, 1994
)
to obtain computationally bounded implementations.

Proof sketch of Lemma
6
.

We view the interaction as a zero-sum game. Rows are indexed by size
o(\sqrt{S})
attackers
A
, each producing an input
x
, and columns by size
o(\sqrt{S})
defenders
B
, each answering with a label
y
and a flag
r
. The entry
M_{A,B}
rewards the defender, being the sum of the probability that
B
labels
x\leftarrow A
correctly and of the distinguishability advantage of
B
against
A
. The attacker plays
\mathsf{Min}
and wants a small sum, while the defender plays
\mathsf{Max}
and wants it large. By the Min-Max Theorem
(
von Neumann, 1928
)
the game has a value, and by a result of
Lipton and Young (1994)
each player has a nearly optimal strategy that mixes only a few circuits and can therefore be implemented by a circuit of size
O(S)
. Which player wins determines which notion we obtain.

If the value is small, the attacker wins. The nearly optimal mixture
A_{N}
keeps both terms small against every small defender, which is exactly a TA.

If the value is large, the defender wins, and we turn the nearly optimal mixture
B_{N}
into a defense
B_{\mathsf{AD}}
, of size
O(S+S_{E})
since it also evaluates the learned model. Bob outputs the classifier
f
produced by the learner. Rule
R
tosses a fair coin, which he keeps private, to decide between two checks: flag
x
if
B_{N}
flags it, or flag
x
if the label of
B_{N}
disagrees with
f(x)
. A probabilistic analysis shows that
B_{\mathsf{AD}}
satisfies Distinguishability or Robustness.
∎

A careful analysis of the proof shows that uniqueness of labels is crucial for it to work. The defense flags a disagreement between
B_{N}
and
f
, which signals an error only because two correct labels must agree. In a generative task they may give different valid answers, and the defender cannot check validity itself since it only sees samples. It is therefore not obvious how to extend the dichotomy to generative tasks, and Corollary
25
rules it out only below a quadratic advantage.

2.2
Transferable Attacks from Public-Key Encryption

The construction of a TA in
Gluch et al. (2025)
relies on FHE. In this work, ordinary secure PKE suffices, which is believed to be a strictly weaker assumption (see Section
1.4
).

We construct a TA for a learning task
\mathbb{L}_{\mathsf{PKE}}
that is based on a PKE scheme
\mathsf{PKE}=(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
over the message space
\mathcal{M}=\{0,1\}
.

\mathsf{PKE}
is secure
(
Goldwasser and Micali, 1984
)
, if any efficient adversary
\mathsf{Adv}
knowing only the public key
\mathsf{pk}
cannot decrypt
c=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},y)
: its guess is wrong with probability close to
1/2
.

If we think of
(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},y))
as an adversarially chosen input
x
and of
y
as its true label, then security corresponds to the transferability of an attack producing such
x
. Remember that we would like to construct a TA for a task that can be learned.
An easy solution is to add the plain message
y
to the description of
x
, that is, to set
x:=(\mathsf{pk},y,\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},y))
and keep
y
as the true label. Now the task is learnable: the bit next to the public key is the true label of
x
. An adversary instead of appending
y
, outputs
x=(\mathsf{pk},y^{\prime},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},y))
, where
y
and
y^{\prime}
are independent uniformly random bits.
The modified attack is transferable, since the additional information
y^{\prime}
does not help a defender guess the label
y
of
x
. Moreover, an adversarially generated
x
looks like it came from the natural distribution by the security of
\mathsf{PKE}
. See Section
4.2
for a formal description of the task and the attack.

Now let
\mathbb{L}
be
any
learning task. Building upon the above construction, we provide a way to append additional dimensions to
x\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
without changing the labels of natural inputs, such that the resulting learning task
\mathbb{L}^{\prime}
admits a TA.

We continue working with a secure
\mathsf{PKE}
as before. Now let natural inputs be of the form
x^{\prime}:=(\mathsf{pk},x,\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},0))
, where
x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
. An arbitrary
x^{\prime}=(\mathsf{pk}^{\prime},x,c)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)}
receives the label
h_{\lambda}(x)\oplus\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk},c)
if
\mathsf{pk}^{\prime}=\mathsf{pk}
, and zero otherwise.
The TA samples one natural input
(\mathsf{pk},x,c)
and outputs
x^{\prime}:=(\mathsf{pk},x,\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},b))
, where
b
is a uniformly random bit. Transferability and Indistinguishability again follow from the security of the
\mathsf{PKE}
.

In Section
4.2
, we also provide a construction of a learning task that admits an AD.

How to Attack Any Adversarial Defense.

Let
f
be the model produced by a training algorithm of size
S

Starting from the input
x_{1}
of a clear sample, the attacker can call
f
on itself multiple times, that is,

x_{i}=f(f(\cdots f(x_{1})\cdots)).

By property 1,

\Theta(S)
.
By property 2, a model produced by training of size
S
cannot continue supplying valid
configurations once this iteration passes the amount of sequential work
available during training. Thus there is a first index
i=O(\sqrt{S})
for
which

is invalid.

The key challenge for the attacker is to compute an adversarial example
x

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}

If
x
is a clear input then it is obviously distinguishable by inspecting its level.
Thus, to construct an indistinguishable

x
for which it knows its level as well as the levels of
f(x),f(f(x)),\dots
.
In contrast, by property 3, the defender (model
f
) cannot compute the level of
x
, and so an AD does not exist.

How to Defend Using Inference Time.

Training uses a circuit of size
S
to compute the underlying sequential trajectory and
stores a few certified configurations above level
2n

n

n

After the attacker chooses
x

for
O(\sqrt{S})
steps.
An attacker of size

This is the

work extending precisely the configuration that the attacker
supplies.

\geq\ell+\sqrt{\ell}
.

2.

Levels Certify Samples.

It now says that observing
s
inputs up to level
\ell
does not help to produce proofs above level
\max\{s,\ell\}
.

3.

Level Hiding.
For opaque inputs, the level is hidden.

4.

Asymmetric Verifiability.
The attacker can create opaque inputs whose answers it can decrypt and check.

As before,
x_{1}
will be easy to construct from the samples and
x_{i}
is the result of iteratively applying a model
f
. The attacker again will be able to reach a level of order
K
on which
f
will fail.

An AAD will not fail by using fresh samples to modify
f
by making it work on inputs of higher levels.

How to Instantiate
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{data}}
Satisfying Properties 1–4.

We use strongly unforgeable signatures (Definition
31
), a zk-SNARK
(Definition
34
), and IB-FHE (Definition
30
).
Signatures supply information that a party can only obtain from samples.
The zk-SNARK attaches a short, publicly verifiable proof of knowledge of many signatures to each input without revealing them.
IB-FHE hides the level (number of signatures) while allowing both the learned model and the inference procedure to operate on encrypted inputs.

As before, we let the distribution
\mathcal{D}
be composed of two equally probable parts.
For every
(x,y)\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}})
,

x=\sigma\|\ell\|\pi,\ y=\sigma\|(\ell+\sqrt{\ell})\|\pi^{\prime}.

Let
(\mathsf{vk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{n})
be a key pair of a strongly unforgeable signature scheme.
Pair
(x,y)
satisfies: (i)
\sigma
is a signature of
0
under
\mathsf{vk}
, (ii)
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are zk-SNARK proofs of knowledge of
\ell
and
\ell+\sqrt{\ell}
distinct signatures of
0
, each computed from fresh signatures.
The level
\ell
is distributed as
\mathsf{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})
.

\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Enc}}
is defined as
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}
under the IB-FHE encryption.

How to Design an Active Adversarial Defense.

We define
B_{\mathsf{Train}}
that computes a model
f
that answers correctly for levels up to
K/8
.
Next, upon receiving an input
x
,
B_{\mathsf{Inference}}
computes a correct output by first parsing
x=\sigma\ \|\ \ell\ \|\ \pi
and joining the signatures of
O(\sqrt{K})
fresh samples with the
K/4
signatures kept from training.
It computes a zk-SNARK proof
\pi^{\prime}
of knowledge of these
K/4+\sqrt{K}/2
signatures and returns
\sigma\|(K/4+\sqrt{K}/2)\|\pi^{\prime}
.
If
x
is encrypted it runs the same procedure under the FHE.

The intuitive reason why
B_{\mathsf{Inference}}
is a valid AAD is that the attacker sees at most
K/4
samples and the trained model reveals only zero-knowledge proofs up to level
K/4
, and so, by Lemma
21
, they can produce valid proofs only up to level
K/4
, which implies that the highest level of
x
that
A
can create is
K/4
.
This implies that
O(\sqrt{K})
fresh samples of
B_{\mathsf{Inference}}
are enough.

How to Attack Any Adversarial Defense.

The attack is to send an adversarial input
x=\mathsf{Enc}(x_{i})
for some
i\leq O(\sqrt{K})
such that
f
fails on encryptions of
x_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
or a fresh sample from the marginal of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}
otherwise.
As before, such an input is indistinguishable from a natural sample by the properties of FHE.

By property 2,
f
can be correct only up to level
O(K)
, while by property 1,
O(\sqrt{K})
valid answers in a row starting from
x_{1}
reach a higher level. Hence such an
i
exists, the attacker finds it by property 4, and a defender producing such a model fails.
See Section
5.1
for the full proof.
∎

2.4
No Dichotomy for Generative Tasks

Proof sketch of Corollary
9
.

Item (ii) is item (ii) of Theorem
7
. For item (i), take the AAD of Theorem
7
. Its inference phase does not use
\mathsf{priv}
from the training phase, so it is a defender on its own, of size
\sqrt{S}\cdot\mathsf{poly}(n)
. An attacker of size
O(S)
in a TA sees no published model, so it is an attacker of the AAD that ignores
f
, and by item (i) of the theorem the inference phase answers its questions mostly correctly. Hence no attacker of size
O(S)
fools every defender of size
\sqrt{S}\cdot\mathsf{poly}(n)
, and none is a TA.
∎

The proof crucially uses that the task is generative. An AD could run the inference step inside its distinguishing rule
R
, but a disagreement between that answer and
f(x)
does not show that
f
errs. For classification tasks a disagreement does show an error. An AD can publish
f
and, according to a private fair coin drawn in training, flag an input if the AAD flags it, or flag it if the answer of the AAD disagrees with
f
, which turns an AAD into an AD of size about
S_{\mathsf{Train}}+S_{\mathsf{Inference}}
. If the AAD distinguishes an attack, the AD does so with half the gap. If the AAD answers it mostly correctly, the disagreement check flags it at a rate close to the error of
f
on it and flags natural inputs rarely, so
f
is robust or the AD distinguishes. Hence no separation as in Theorem
7
exists for classification tasks.

Similarly the proof of Corollary
9
does not work for classification tasks. This is because the AAD would turn into an AD of size
O(S)
as above and contradict item (ii). Whether the dichotomy is tight for classification tasks therefore remains open.

3
Preliminaries and Definitions

3.1
Notation

We write
\Delta(\cdot)
for the set of probability measures on a set, with respect to an implicitly fixed
\sigma
-algebra.
We write
x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}
for a draw from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and for a uniform draw when
\mathcal{D}
is a finite set, and
y\leftarrow C(x)
for the output of a randomized circuit or algorithm
C
on input
x
. We write
|\cdot|
for the length of a string, and
\ell^{+}:=\ell+\sqrt{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Whenever a number is part of an input to a party or an algorithm, it is written in binary, except for the unary strings
1^{n}
and
1^{\lambda}
.

A function
\mu:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is called negligible if for all
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all integers
n\geq n_{0}
, we have
|\mu(n)|<1/n^{c}
.

We write
\mathsf{negl}(n)
for an unspecified negligible function and
\mathsf{poly}(n)
for an unspecified polynomial. Parameters of our definitions, such as error bounds, size bounds and sample complexity bounds, are functions of
n
. Cryptographic primitives are instantiated with security parameter
\lambda=n
unless stated otherwise.

Our schemes are not required to succeed at every input length, and we measure how often they do.
A boolean sequence
a:\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
is true with frequency
\varphi\in[0,1]
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}a_{i}}{n}\geq\varphi.

We write
A\in_{\varphi}\mathsf{S}(\cdot)
to say that
A
implements the scheme
\mathsf{S}
with frequency
\varphi
, and
A\notin_{\varphi}\mathsf{S}(\cdot)
when this does not hold, and drop the subscript when the frequency is clear from the context.

3.2
Learning Tasks

We denote the input space by
\mathcal{X}_{n}
and the output space by
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
.
An error oracle is a function
h:\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n}\to\{0,1\}
,
i.e., it is a function that takes a pair
(x,y)
of input
x
and output
y
, and outputs
0
if
y
is a valid answer for
x
, and
1
otherwise.
Note that this captures both the classification and generation settings. If for every
x
there is exactly one
y
such that
h(x,y)=0
, then
h
corresponds to a classification task, but if there are many such
y
’s we deal with generation.
In the classification case we also write
h(x)
for the unique valid answer, slightly overloading the notation. Thus,
h(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{y\neq h(x)}
.

For a distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n})
we denote its marginal distribution over
\mathcal{X}_{n}
by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
.
For a classification task we assume that every sample is of the form
(x,h(x))
, so
\mathcal{D}
is determined by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
and
h
. In this case we identify
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
and write
x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 10

(Learning Task)
.

A learning task
\mathbb{L}
is a family
\{\mathbb{L}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where for every
n
,
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is an element of
\Delta\big(\Delta(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n})\times\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n}}\big)
, i.e., it is a distribution over pairs
(\mathcal{D},h)
, where
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n})
and
h:\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n}\to\{0,1\}
.

We note that Definition
10
, which is similar to the one used in
Gluch et al. (2025)
, is nonstandard. Tasks are indexed by the size parameter
n
so that the computational resources of the parties can be measured, and the outer distribution over
(\mathcal{D},h)
pairs represents the prior knowledge of the learner. Since a non-uniform circuit may hard-code advice for each
n

(
Karp and Lipton, 1980
)
, a task fixed to a single
(\mathcal{D},h)
would be settled by a circuit that simply stores the answers. The outer distribution is therefore what leaves something to be learned, and what lets a task be built from a cryptographic primitive and its security be stated as a bound on every party of a given size.

A model is a potentially randomized function
f:\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\mathcal{Y}_{n}
. A classifier is a deterministic model. A distinguishing rule is a potentially randomized function
R:\mathcal{X}_{n}\to\{0,1\}
. We call its output
r=R(x)
a flag. This rule will be used to distinguish natural and adversarially generated inputs.

For a distribution
\mathcal{D}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}_{n})
and an error oracle
h
we define the error of a model
f
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal{D},h}(f):=\Pr_{x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}}\big[h(x,f(x))=1\big],

where the probability also includes the potential randomness of
f
. We will omit the indices
\mathcal{D}
and
h
if they are clear from the context.
For
q\in\mathbb{N}
, a batch
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
, answers
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}^{q}
and flags
\mathbf{r}\in\{0,1\}^{q}
we also define the empirical error and the flag rate as

\operatorname{err}_{\mathbf{x},h}(\mathbf{y}):=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i\in[q]}h(x_{i},y_{i})\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{fl}(\mathbf{r}):=\frac{1}{q}|\{i\in[q]:r_{i}=1\}|.

3.3
Circuits, Sample Access, and Representations

Throughout, efficient computation is modeled by non-uniform families of Boolean circuits
C=\{C_{n}\}_{n}
. The size of a circuit is its number of gates including input gates. We write
|C|
for the size of a circuit
C
. A family has size
S:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
if
|C_{n}|\leq S(n)
for every
n
.
When we describe a party as an algorithm, its size is the size of a circuit implementing the algorithm. Up to a polylogarithmic factor, the size of a circuit implementing an algorithm is its running time
(
Arora and Barak, 2009
)
, and we write
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
to hide polylogarithmic factors. Polynomial bounds are unaffected by this factor, so for them we use running time and size interchangeably.

Our circuits have access to an example oracle
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})

(
Valiant, 1984
)
that samples
(x,y)\leftarrow\mathcal{D}
and returns
(x,y)
. For a classification task, where we identify
\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
, we write
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D},h)
for this oracle: it returns
(x,h(x))
with
x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}
.

Besides its size, a circuit family has sample and randomness complexity. A family has sample complexity
K:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and randomness complexity
J:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
if
C_{n}
makes at most
K(n)
queries to
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})
and reads at most
J(n)
random bits, for every
n
.
We sometimes explicitly write
C_{n}(\cdot\,,Z)
, where, given
(\mathcal{D},h)
,
Z\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{J(n)}\times\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})^{K(n)}
.

Models and distinguishing rules are exchanged between parties as strings.
The functions
f
and
R
that a defender produces belong to classes
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, whose members are described by strings
z_{f}
and
z_{R}
of length
m_{\mathcal{F}}(n)
and
m_{\mathcal{R}}(n)
.

A family of evaluation circuits
E=\{E_{n}\}_{n}
of size
S_{E}
for
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
E_{n}(z_{f},x)=f(x)
, so that a party holding
z_{f}
can compute
f
. For a randomized
f
, the circuit
E_{n}
also reads the random bits of
f
. The cost of evaluating
f
is thus
S_{E}
, while
m_{\mathcal{F}}
is only the cost of storing or transmitting
z_{f}
. Evaluation circuits for the class
\mathcal{R}
of distinguishing rules are defined in the same way.

3.4
Learnability

Recall the definition of PAC learnability
(
Valiant, 1984
;
Kearns and Vazirani, 1994
)
. Taking the computational aspect of learning algorithms into account,
Gluch et al. (2025)
defined computationally bounded learnability.

Definition 11

(Computationally Bounded Learnability)
.

For error and confidence parameters
\varepsilon,\delta:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
, a size bound
S_{L}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and a class of models
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n}
with description length
m_{\mathcal{F}}
, we say that a learning task
\mathbb{L}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta,S_{L},\mathcal{F})
-learnable, if there exists a circuit family
\{L_{n}\}_{n}
of size
S_{L}
, which outputs descriptions
z_{f}
of models
f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, such that for every
n
large enough

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal{D},h}(f)<\varepsilon(n)\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;z_{f}\leftarrow L_{n}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\end{array}\!\right]\geq 1-\delta(n).

3.5
Interaction

All attacks and defenses in this paper are with respect to some learning task
\mathbb{L}
. Before the interaction between a defender and an attacker starts,
(\mathcal{D},h)
is sampled from
\mathbb{L}_{n}
. The task
\mathbb{L}
is known to all the parties, but the particular
(\mathcal{D},h)
is unknown, i.e., it can be accessed only through the example oracle.

The notions studied in this paper are two- and three-round interactive protocols
(
Goldwasser et al., 1989
)
between Alice and Bob. Alice is the attacker
A=\{A_{n}\}_{n}
and Bob is the defender
B=\{B_{n}\}_{n}
. Both are circuit families with oracle access to
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})
, of sizes
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
and sample complexities
K_{A}
and
K_{B}
. Alice sends a batch
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
, where the batch size
q
is a polynomial known to all parties. Bob answers every entry
x_{i}
with some
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}
and flags it with some
r_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, either directly or through a published model and distinguishing rule. A defender may act in two phases
B=(B_{\mathsf{Train}},B_{\mathsf{Inference}})
. The training phase
B_{\mathsf{Train}}
runs before Alice’s message and the inference phase
B_{\mathsf{Inference}}
runs after it. Their sizes are
S_{\mathsf{Train}}
and
S_{\mathsf{Inference}}
, and their sample complexities are
K_{\mathsf{Train}}
and
K_{\mathsf{Inference}}
. We allow
B_{\mathsf{Train}}
to produce, during learning, a private string
\mathsf{priv}
that is passed to
B_{\mathsf{Inference}}
and is not sent to Alice. In Definitions
14
and
16
the defender also publishes a distinguishing rule, and
\mathsf{priv}
instantiates it: all randomness of the rule is in
\mathsf{priv}
, so
R(\mathsf{priv},\cdot)
is a deterministic function. Evaluating the model and the rule, with the evaluation circuits of Section
3.3
, is part of the defender, so its size bounds these evaluations.

3.6
Transferable Attacks and (Active) Adversarial Defenses

The definitions of this subsection apply to classification tasks and to generative tasks, where an input may have many valid answers. A defender may act in two phases, answering once more at inference time after the attacker has spoken, and the attacker sends a batch
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
.
A defender answers every entry of the batch and, separately, flags it, so its output is a pair
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}^{q}\times\{0,1\}^{q}
, scored by the empirical error and the flag rate.

Transferable Attacks are families of circuits that generate batches
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
that all computationally bounded defenders fail to answer correctly while also being unable to distinguish them from natural batches.

Definition 12

(Transferable Attack)
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a learning task. For a batch size
q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, size functions
S_{A},S_{B}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, transferability and indistinguishability bounds
c_{\mathsf{TA}},s_{\mathsf{TA}}:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
and a frequency
\varphi\in[0,1]
, we say that a family of circuits
A_{\mathsf{TA}}=\{A_{n,\mathsf{TA}}\}_{n}
of size
S_{A}
implements a
Transferable Attack
for
\mathbb{L}
with frequency
\varphi
, denoted by

A_{\mathsf{TA}}\in_{\varphi}\mathsf{TA}(\mathbb{L},q,S_{A},S_{B},c_{\mathsf{TA}},s_{\mathsf{TA}}),

if, with frequency
\varphi
over
n
, an interactive protocol in which
(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}
,
A^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n,\mathsf{TA}}
computes a batch
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
and an arbitrary
B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n}
based on
\mathbf{x}
outputs
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})\in\mathcal{Y}_{n}^{q}\times\{0,1\}^{q}
satisfies:

•

Transferability
(fast defenders answer the batch mostly incorrectly). For every circuit
B_{n}
of size
S_{B}(n)

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{err}_{\mathbf{x},h}(\mathbf{y})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;\mathbf{x}\leftarrow A^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n,\mathsf{TA}},\;(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\end{array}\!\right]\geq c_{\mathsf{TA}}(n).

•

Indistinguishability
(fast defenders flag the batch about as often as a natural batch). For every circuit
B_{n}
of size
S_{B}(n)

\left|\,\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{fl}(\mathbf{r})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}\\
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow A^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n,\mathsf{TA}}\\
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\end{array}\!\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{fl}(\mathbf{r})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}\\
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}})^{q}\\
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\end{array}\!\right]\right|<s_{\mathsf{TA}}(n).

Adversarial Defenses are counterparts of Transferable Attacks. An Adversarial Defense fixes its model before the attacker moves. A defender that may answer again at inference time is called active.

Remark 13
.

An attack for a fixed batch size
q
enables attacks of the same strength for all
q^{\prime}\leq q
.
Indeed, given an attack successful for
q
, one can construct an attack for any
q^{\prime}<q
by choosing a random subset of size
q^{\prime}
from the batch of
q
questions produced by the initial attack.
This new attack inherits at least as good
c_{\mathsf{TA}},s_{\mathsf{TA}}
as the initial one.

Definition 14

(Adversarial Defense)
.

Let
\mathbb{L}=\{\mathbb{L}_{n}\}_{n}
be a learning task. For a batch size
q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, size functions
S_{A},S_{B}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, an error parameter
\varepsilon:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
, a confidence parameter
\delta:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
, distinguishability and robustness parameters
c_{\mathsf{AD}},s_{\mathsf{AD}}:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
and a frequency
\varphi\in[0,1]
, we say that a circuit family
B=\{B_{n}\}_{n}
of size
S_{B}
implements an
Adversarial Defense
for
\mathbb{L}
with frequency
\varphi
, denoted by

B\in_{\varphi}\mathsf{AD}(\mathbb{L},q,S_{A},S_{B},\varepsilon,\delta,c_{\mathsf{AD}},s_{\mathsf{AD}}),

if, with frequency
\varphi
over
n
, an interactive protocol in which
(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}
,
B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}
computes descriptions
z_{f}
and
z_{R}
of a model
f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and of a distinguishing rule
R\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
, together with a private string
\mathsf{priv}
that instantiates
R
, sends
z_{f}
and
z_{R}
to
A_{n}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}
, which replies with a batch
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}^{q}
, and the defender outputs the answers
\mathbf{y}:=f(\mathbf{x})
and the flags
\mathbf{r}:=R(\mathsf{priv},\mathbf{x})
, both computed entry by entry, satisfies the conditions below.

•

Correctness
(
f
has low error).

\Pr\left[\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal{D},h}(f)<\varepsilon(n)\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;z_{f}\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\end{array}\!\right]\geq 1-\delta(n).

Moreover, for every circuit
A_{n}
of size
S_{A}(n)
at least one of the two conditions holds.

•

Robustness
(quickly generated adversarial batches are mostly answered correctly).

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{err}_{\mathbf{x},h}(f(\mathbf{x}))\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})},\;\mathbf{x}\leftarrow A_{n}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}(z_{f},z_{R})\end{array}\!\right]<s_{\mathsf{AD}}(n).

•

Distinguishability
(the published rule
R
, instantiated by the private string
\mathsf{priv}
, flags quickly generated adversarial batches at a different rate than natural batches).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{fl}(\mathbf{r})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{x}\leftarrow A_{n}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}(z_{f},z_{R}),\;\mathbf{r}\leftarrow R(\mathsf{priv},\mathbf{x})\end{array}\!\right]-\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{fl}(\mathbf{r})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{x}\leftarrow(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}})^{q},\;\mathbf{r}\leftarrow R(\mathsf{priv},\mathbf{x})\end{array}\!\right]\right|\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\end{array}\!\right]\geq c_{\mathsf{AD}}(n).

Remark 15
.

Our definition of Adversarial Defense is related to the guarantee for arbitrary test-time examples of
Goldwasser et al. (2020)
, where abstaining plays the role of flagging, but the two notions differ in three respects. First, their learner is transductive and sees the unlabeled test batch before it answers, whereas ours commits
f
and
R
before the batch is chosen. Second, their guarantee is conjunctive, low error on the points it labels together with a low abstention rate on natural inputs, whereas ours asks, for each attacker, for Robustness or Distinguishability and does not bound the flag rate on natural inputs. Third, their adversary is computationally unbounded and their classes have bounded VC dimension, whereas we bound all parties by circuit size.

Definition 16

(Active Adversarial Defense)
.

Let
\mathbb{L}=\{\mathbb{L}_{n}\}_{n}
be a learning task. For a batch size
q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, size functions
S_{A},S_{\mathsf{Train}},S_{\mathsf{Inference}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, an error parameter
\varepsilon:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
, a confidence parameter
\delta:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
, distinguishability and robustness parameters
c_{\mathsf{AAD}},s_{\mathsf{AAD}}:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
and a frequency

The following lemma loosely says that every learnable task either has a Transferable Attack or an Adversarial Defense.

Lemma 18

(Dichotomy)

Fix a size function

and constants

such that

17

\displaystyle\kappa((2-\alpha)(1-c)-s\alpha)>1+\varepsilon+\delta.

Assume that a learning task

Setup.

. Let

and

+\frac{1+\theta}{s}\big(\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma r_{Z_{B}}(x_{Z_{A}})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}Z_{A},Z_{B}\end{array}\!\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma r_{Z_{B}}(x)\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}x\leftarrow\mathcal{D},\;Z_{B}\end{array}\!\right]\big).

Game Theory Argument.

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k}M_{A_{i},(B,\sigma)}\leq\operatorname{val}(M)+\theta.

(1)

Let

\displaystyle 4kS_{L}O(\log k)=O(S_{L}^{2}\log^{2}S_{L})=O(S)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k}M_{A_{i},(B,\sigma)}

For each

Let

\displaystyle\mu:=\mathbb{E}\left[\Pr_{x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{n}}[R(\mathsf{priv},x)=1]\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D}_{n},h_{n})\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D}_{n},h_{n})^{\ell}\end{array}\!\right]

be the expected value of the probability that there are two functions in the hypothesis class

that are consistent with

. Then for all
\tau>\mu

21

21

It remains to show that the expected absolute flag-rate gap

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl|\Pr\left[R(\mathsf{priv},x_{A})=1\;\middle|\;x_{A}\leftarrow A_{n}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}(z_{f},z_{R})\right]-\Pr\left[R(\mathsf{priv},x)=1\;\middle|\;x\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}\right]\Bigr|\\
\;\Big|\;(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n},\;(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})\leftarrow B^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\Bigr],

is negligible. Fix the task instance and
(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})
. A
natural input is clear with probability
\frac{1}{2}
and otherwise

\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{id}_{1},x)\|\mathsf{id}_{1}\|\mathsf{id}_{2}\|\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{2}}

for a fresh natural clear question
x
, and
x_{A}
has the same
distribution with

in place of

if a round fails. The following

\mathsf{id}^{*}\leftarrow\mathcal{I}
before setup, simulates the task, the
training of
B
and the attack with
\mathsf{id}_{1}=\mathsf{id}^{*}
in

and with
\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id})
,
\mathsf{id}\neq\mathsf{id}^{*}
, from

and

\mathsf{negl}(n)
.

The attack uses

The Active Adversarial Defense.

K/4

clear examples. If there are fewer than

2.

For every

Let

\ell^{\prime}

4.

Let

Publish

Here

Correctness and Completeness.

By the Chernoff bound, training
and inference return

with negligible probability.
Otherwise, let

be a valid clear question. If

, then
\ell^{+}\leq K/4

, then
\ell^{+}\leq K/4+\sqrt{K}/2
, so

. An
encrypted natural question is inadmissible, and then

, or
h
decrypts
to a valid clear question

, and then

and

and

, and

, we obtain

\Pr\left[\operatorname{err}_{\mathcal{D},h}(f)>2^{-K/8}\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}\\
z_{f}\leftarrow B_{\mathsf{Train}}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\end{array}\!\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(n),

\Pr\left[\operatorname{err}_{x,h}(y)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}\\
(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})\leftarrow B_{\mathsf{Train}}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\\

As

, and

, these bounds give Correctness and
Completeness with negligible
\varepsilon
and
\delta
.

Robustness.

Fix an attacker
A
of polynomial size with

.
The published model contains only proofs at levels at
most
K/4
, computed on prefixes of the retained signatures, and the

contain a proof above

with
probability at most
K/4\cdot 2^{-K/8}=\mathsf{negl}(n)
, since

. We condition on this not happening.

By the analysis of the attack, with
\mathcal{O}_{K/4,K/4}
in place of

\mathcal{O}_{N,2N}
, the question of
x_{A}
, decrypted if
x_{A}
is encrypted, is valid with level above
K/4
only with negligible
probability. Here the circuit of Lemma
21
obtains the
published proofs as
\mathsf{prove}(\iota_{\mathsf{Train}},\ell)
under one
label
\iota_{\mathsf{Train}}
, since
f
depends on training only
through the retained
K/4
fresh signatures, so it makes at most
K/4

\mathsf{sig}
queries and only
\mathsf{prove}
queries of level at
most
K/4
.

If
x_{A}
is encrypted and inadmissible, or its question is

. Otherwise its question has level at
most
K/4
, and the argument of Correctness and Completeness gives

unless training or inference returns

, with the
admissibility in place of perfect correctness
when

is encrypted. Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[h(x_{A},y)\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathcal{D},h)\leftarrow\mathbb{L}_{n}\\
(z_{f},z_{R},\mathsf{priv})\leftarrow B_{\mathsf{Train}}^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}\\

applies to that family. As Correctness,

.

Efficiency.

Training uses
K
samples and inference uses

fresh samples. The model contains
\sqrt{K}/2
succinct proofs,
so its description length

is

, and so is the size

of its evaluation circuits, since on an encrypted input

adds only a factor

to the size of

.

5.2
Separation in Running Time

5.2.1
The Learning Task
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}

Let
\mathsf{NPL}=(\mathsf{Samp},M)
be an average-case NPL ensemble with gap
\eta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, and let
S
be a size parameter bounded by a polynomial in
n
. We use the ensemble at the difficulty
\tau=n\cdot S^{1/\eta}
, and since
\tau
is fixed throughout this section, we write
\mathsf{Samp}(1^{n})
for
\mathsf{Samp}(1^{n},\tau)
and
M(\chi)
for
M(\tau,\chi)
.

Let
\mathsf{IVC}=(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Update})
be an IVC scheme for the machine
M
, and write
M^{(\ell)}(\chi)
for its configuration on input
\chi
after
\ell
steps. Throughout, the prover
\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c))
is implemented as
\pi_{0}=\bot
followed by
\ell
calls to
\mathsf{Update}
along the computation, with fresh randomness for every proof, so that the proofs of the example distribution and those produced by the parties and reductions below have the same distribution.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define the learning task
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}_{n}
as the result of the following procedure:

\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}_{n}=\left\{(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},\chi},h_{\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},\chi})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{n})\\
(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{n})\\
\chi\leftarrow\mathsf{Samp}(1^{n})\end{array}\!\right\}.

As before, we omit the subscripts of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}
will have two equally probable parts
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Enc}}
.
We set

\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}=\left\{(c\|\ell\|\pi,c^{\prime}\|\ell^{+}\|\pi^{\prime})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\ell\leftarrow\mathsf{Geom}(\frac{1}{2})\text{ conditioned on }\ell\leq 2\tau\\
c\leftarrow M^{(\ell)}(\chi)\\
c^{\prime}\leftarrow M^{(\ell^{+})}(\chi)\\
\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c))\\
\pi^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell^{+},c^{\prime}))\text{ with independent randomness}\end{array}\!\right\}.

Conditioning
\ell\leq 2\tau
changes the distribution by
2^{-2\tau}
, which is negligible, and we ignore this term below.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Enc}}
is obtained from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}
exactly as in Section
5.1
.

Oracle
h
is defined via the following algorithm.

1.

Determine whether
x
has the clear or
encrypted input format. If neither, return
0
.

2.

If
x
is encrypted, parse

x=x^{\mathsf{enc}}\|\mathsf{id}_{1}\|\mathsf{id}_{2}\|\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{2}}.

If
\mathsf{Adm}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id}_{1},x^{\mathsf{enc}})=0
,
return
0
.
Otherwise, retrieve the fixed key
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{1}}
and replace
x
by

\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{1}},x^{\mathsf{enc}})
.
If decryption fails, return
0
.

3.

Decompose
x=c\|\ell\|\pi
.
If
x
has the wrong format or
\ell>2\tau
, return
0
.
If

\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c),\pi)=0,

return
0
.

4.

If the original input was encrypted, require
y

to have the prescribed ciphertext format and replace it by

\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{1}},y)
.
If its format is invalid or decryption fails, return
1
.

5.

Decompose
y=c^{\prime}\|\ell^{\prime}\|\pi^{\prime}
.
If
y
has the wrong format, return
1
.

6.

Verify that

\ell^{+}\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq 4\tau,\qquad\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell^{\prime},c^{\prime}),\pi^{\prime})=1.

If all checks succeed, return
0
,
and otherwise return
1
.

In other words,
h
decrypts encrypted inputs such that invalid inputs incur no error. For a valid input at level
\ell
,
an answer is correct if it contains a valid IVC proof for a
configuration at some level
\ell^{\prime}
with
\ell^{+}\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq 4\tau
.

In Theorem
24
we show that
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
is learnable: the training phase of its Active Adversarial Defense satisfies Correctness, so it learns
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
.

5.2.2
The Separation for
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}

Let
\eta\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, and assume that an average-case non-parallelizing language ensemble with gap
\eta
, an IVC scheme and an IB-FHE scheme with the admissibility property exist.

Let
g(n)\geq n
be a polynomial that bounds the length of the
inputs and answers of
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
and the size of each of the following
circuits: one step of
M
together with
\mathsf{Update}
,
\mathsf{G}
,

\mathsf{Setup}
,
\mathsf{KeyGen}
,
\mathsf{Enc}
,
\mathsf{Dec}
,
\mathsf{V}
,
sampling the level of an example, comparing two levels or identities, and
storing a certified configuration together with the test, in the clear or
under
\mathsf{Eval}
, whether it is a valid answer for an input.

The following lemma is the only place where the hardness of the non-parallelizing language enters.

Lemma 23

(Hardness of Reaching the Terminal Level)
.

Let
S
be polynomial in
n
and
\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
be the task of Section
5.2
, with
difficulty
\tau=n\cdot S^{1/\eta}
. Let
A
be a circuit of size
at most
\tau^{\eta}-2n\,g(n)
with access to
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})
, where
\mathcal{D}
is
the distribution of the task instance. Then

\Pr\left[\!\begin{array}[]{l}\tau\leq\ell\leq 4\tau,\\
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c),\pi)=1\end{array}\!\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{n})\\
(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{n})\\
\chi\leftarrow\mathsf{Samp}(1^{n})\\
(\ell,c,\pi)\leftarrow A^{\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})}(\chi)\end{array}\!\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(n),

and the same holds if
A
outputs

\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{id},c\|\ell\|\pi)
for an identity
\mathsf{id}
in
place of
(\ell,c,\pi)
.

Proof.

Let
p
be the probability in the lemma, and let
\mathsf{Adv}
be
the circuit that on input
\chi

1.

Samples
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{n})
and

(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{n})
.

2.

Answers all queries of
A
to
\operatorname{Ex}(\mathcal{D})
in parallel, as in

\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Clear}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{Enc}}
but with levels
truncated at
n
, so that each example takes at most

n^{+}
steps of
M
and of
\mathsf{Update}
.

3.

Runs
A
, decrypts its output with
\mathsf{MSK}
if it
is encrypted, and checks
\tau\leq\ell\leq 4\tau
and

\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c),\pi)=1
. If both hold, it
returns
1
if
c
is an accepting configuration and
0
otherwise.
Else it returns a uniform bit.

Truncation changes the view of
A
by at most

\mathsf{poly}(n)\,2^{-n}
. Step 1 has depth at most
2g(n)
,
step 2 at most
(n^{+}+2)\,g(n)
, since the two proof chains
of an example run in parallel, and the checks of step 3 at most

5g(n)
. So for
n\geq 16
,
\mathsf{Adv}
has depth at most

|A|+(n^{+}+9)\,g(n)\leq|A|+2n\,g(n)\leq\tau^{\eta}
.

In the experiment of the lemma,

\Pr\left[\!\begin{array}[]{l}\tau\leq\ell\leq 4\tau,\ c\neq M^{(\ell)}(\chi),\\
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c),\pi)=1\end{array}\!\right]\leq 4\tau\cdot\max_{\tau\leq\ell^{*}\leq 4\tau}\Pr\left[\!\begin{array}[]{l}\ell=\ell^{*},\ c\neq M^{(\ell)}(\chi),\\
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell,c),\pi)=1\end{array}\!\right].

For the maximizing
\ell^{*}
, the prover that runs this experiment and
outputs
((\chi,\ell,c),\pi)
only if
\ell=\ell^{*}
breaks the soundness of IVC
with the maximum, so the maximum is negligible, and so is the left side.
So, except with negligible probability,
c=M^{(\ell)}(\chi)
, which for

\ell\geq\tau
is halted, and accepting if and only if
M(\chi)=1
. Hence,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}(\chi)=M(\chi)\;\middle|\;\chi\leftarrow\mathsf{Samp}(1^{n})\right]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{p}{2}-\mathsf{negl}(n),

and hence
p\leq\mathsf{negl}(n)
.
∎

Theorem 24

(Running-Time Separation)
.

For every
S:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
bounded by a polynomial in

with
S(n)\geq 4n\,g(n)

\mathbb{L}
such that for every distinguishability parameter

(i)

(B_{\mathsf{Train}},B_{\mathsf{Inference}})

(ii)

For every
B
of size
S

,
\varepsilon,\delta:\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
and

Proof.

The task is
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{L}^{\mathsf{time}}
. As we discussed in Section
2
, the proof is very similar to that of Theorem

.

The Attack.

Let
m=5\sqrt{\tau}
.
A
performs the following

1.

Draw
\log^{2}n
samples from
\mathcal{D}
and let

be the input of one unencrypted sample. If no

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}

2.

Draw
4(2m\log^{2}n+2)
samples from
\mathcal{D}
. If fewer
than
2m\log^{2}n+2
of them are encrypted, return a fresh input
from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
. Otherwise take the identity–key pairs
from the auxiliary fields of the first

from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}
.

3.

For

\log^{2}n
trials, each as follows:

•

Take the next two unused pairs

(\mathsf{id}_{1},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{1}})
and

(\mathsf{id}_{2},\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{2}})
.

•

Let

x^{\prime}:=\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{id}_{1},x_{j})\|\mathsf{id}_{1}\|\mathsf{id}_{2}\|\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{2}},\qquad y_{j}:=\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{1}},f(x^{\prime})).

.
The trial succeeds if

has the right format,

\ell_{j}^{+}\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq 4\tau,\qquad\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{ivc}},(\chi,\ell^{\prime},c^{\prime}),\pi^{\prime})=1.

Decryption failures count as failed trials.

If any trial succeeds, let
x_{j+1}
be the output

y_{j}
of the first successful trial, and let
\ell_{j+1}
be its level. If all trials fail:

•

With probability
\frac{1}{2}
, take the
last two pairs and return

\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{fhe}},\mathsf{id}_{1},x_{j})\|\mathsf{id}_{1}\|\mathsf{id}_{2}\|\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}_{2}}.

•

Appendix B
Two-Player Zero-Sum Games

As in
Gluch et al. [2025]
, the dichotomy, Lemma
18
, is proved by framing interactions between Alice and Bob as zero-sum games. In this appendix we define such games and focus on statements crucial for our results.

Consider the following game specified by an
N_{r}\times N_{c}
matrix
M
. The row player
\mathsf{Min}
chooses a distribution
p
over rows. Analogously, we define a column player
\mathsf{Max}
that chooses a distribution
q
over columns. Then a row and a column are drawn randomly according to
p
and
q
, respectively, and
\mathsf{Min}
pays
M_{ij}
to
\mathsf{Max}
. As the name suggests, the goal of the row player is to minimize the expected payment

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}p(i)q(j)M_{ij}.

(7)

In the case when both players play optimally, the expected payment is called the value of the game denoted by
\operatorname{val}(M)
.

Now assume that the row player chooses
p
first and reveals it to
\mathsf{Max}
before they make their decision. To win the game,
\mathsf{Max}
will pick
q
that maximizes (
7
). Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}q(j)\sum_{i}p(i)M_{ij}\leq\max_{j}\sum_{i}p(i)M_{ij},

meaning that
\mathsf{Max}
picks a single column
j
that maximizes
\sum_{i}p(i)M_{ij}
.
The Min-Max Theorem
[
von Neumann, 1928
]
says that reversing the roles does not affect the value of the game:

\displaystyle\operatorname{val}(M)=\min_{p}\max_{j}\sum_{i}p(i)M_{ij}=\max_{q}\min_{i}\sum_{j}q(j)M_{ij}.

In our construction of Transferable Attacks and Adversarial Defenses we consider a game, where each row is represented by a computationally bounded attack
A
that generates adversarial
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and each column is basically represented by a defender
B
that on input
x
outputs a pair
(y,r)\in\{0,1\}^{2}
. The matrix value
M_{A,B}
corresponds to the ability of
B
to classify
x
correctly by
y
and to distinguish adversarial
x
from natural inputs by outputting
r=1
. If the value of this game is smaller than a certain threshold
t
, then there is a distribution
p
over
A
’s such that for all
B

\displaystyle\sum_{A}p(A)M_{A,B}<t.

We define the matrix
M
so that the attack
A_{N}
that samples
A
according to
p
has the (extended) matrix value
M_{A_{N},B}
smaller than
t
for all defenders
B
.

One caveat is that
A_{N}
might be a huge circuit since there is a large number of rows to choose from.
Lipton and Young [1994]
showed that we can approximate the value of the game by cleverly picking a strategy
p
that is a uniform distribution over
k
rows, where
k
is proportional to the logarithm of the number of columns: For any
\gamma>0
and
k=\ln(N_{c})/(2\gamma^{2})
there exists a uniform distribution
p
over
k
rows such that for all
B

\displaystyle\sum_{A}p(A)M_{A,B}<t+\gamma(\max_{A,B}M_{A,B}-\min_{A,B}M_{A,B}).

Similarly, if the value of the game is greater than or equal to
t
, one can show that for any
\gamma>0
and
k=\ln(N_{r})/(2\gamma^{2})
there exists a uniform distribution
q
over
k
columns such that for all
A

\displaystyle\sum_{B}q(B)M_{A,B}\geq t-\gamma(\max_{A,B}M_{A,B}-\min_{A,B}M_{A,B}).

Appendix C
Cryptographic Tools

C.1
Public-Key Encryption

A public-key encryption scheme (PKE)
[
Diffie and Hellman, 1976
,
Rivest et al., 1978
]
allows for secure communication without the need to exchange secret keys beforehand. If Charlie wants to receive a message from Dave, Charlie generates a pair of public and secret keys and publishes the public key. Dave then encrypts the message with Charlie’s public key and sends the ciphertext over a public channel. Correctness ensures that Charlie can decrypt Dave’s message with the secret key. Security means indistinguishability under chosen-plaintext attacks (IND-CPA)
[
Goldwasser and Micali, 1984
]
: a polynomial-size adversary that knows the public key cannot distinguish encryptions of zero from encryptions of one, except with a negligible advantage.

Definition 29

(PKE)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
be a security parameter. A public-key encryption scheme with message space
\{0,1\}
and ciphertext set family
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
consists of a tuple of probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms
(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec})
with the following properties:

•

Key Generation.

(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and outputs a public key
\mathsf{pk}
and a secret key
\mathsf{sk}
, both in
\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)}
.

•

Encryption.

c\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},m)
takes as input the public key
\mathsf{pk}
and a plaintext
m\in\{0,1\}
, and outputs a ciphertext
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}
.

•

Decryption.

m^{\prime}=\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk},c)
takes as input the secret key
\mathsf{sk}
and a ciphertext
c
, and outputs a plaintext
m^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
.

The scheme must satisfy the following properties:

•

Correctness.
For all
\lambda
and
m\in\{0,1\}
, decryption always succeeds:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},m))=m\;\middle|\;(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\right]=1.

•

IND-CPA Security.
For every polynomial-size adversary
\mathsf{Adv}
there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
such that for all
\lambda

\Big|\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},0))=1\;\middle|\;(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\right]\\
-\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},1))=1\;\middle|\;(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\right]\Big|\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

(8)

Throughout the paper without loss of generality we assume that after fixing security parameter
\lambda
, both
\mathsf{Gen}
and
\mathsf{Enc}
have fixed output length.

In the above definition the indistinguishability property (
8
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pk},y))\neq y\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda}),\;y\leftarrow\{0,1\}\end{array}\!\right]\geq 1/2-\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

We use both interpretations in our constructions of Transferable Attacks in Section
4.2
.

C.2
Identity-Based Fully Homomorphic Encryption

Identity-based encryption
[
Shamir, 1984
,
Boneh and Franklin, 2001
]
lets any string serve as a public key, and fully homomorphic encryption
[
Gentry, 2009
]
allows computing on ciphertexts. Identity-based fully homomorphic encryption (IB-FHE)
[
Gentry et al., 2013

]
combines the two. Its security is indistinguishability under selective-identity chosen-plaintext attacks (IND-sID-CPA)

Definition 30

(Identity-Based Fully Homomorphic Encryption)
.

An IB-FHE scheme with message space
\mathcal{M}
, identity space
\mathcal{I}
, and a class of circuits
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\{\mathcal{M}^{*}\to\mathcal{M}\}
consists of a tuple of probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms
\mathsf{IB\text{-}FHE}=(\mathsf{Setup},\mathsf{KeyGen},\mathsf{Enc},\mathsf{Dec},\mathsf{Eval})
with the following properties:

•

Setup.

(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and outputs public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
and a master secret key
\mathsf{MSK}
.

•

Key Generation.

\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}}\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id})
takes as input the master secret key
\mathsf{MSK}
and an identity
\mathsf{id}\in\mathcal{I}
, and outputs a secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}}
.

•

Encryption.

c\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{id},m)
takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
, an identity
\mathsf{id}
, and a message
m\in\mathcal{M}
, and outputs a ciphertext
c
.

•

Decryption.

m^{\prime}=\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}},c)
takes as input a secret key
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}}
and a ciphertext
c
, and outputs a plaintext
m^{\prime}
if
c
is a valid encryption under
\mathsf{id}
. Otherwise, it outputs
\bot
.

•

Evaluation.

c^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{pp},C,c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
, a circuit
C\in\mathfrak{C}
, and a collection of ciphertexts
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
, and outputs a new ciphertext
c^{\prime}
.

The scheme must satisfy the following properties:

•

Correctness.
For all
\lambda
, identities
\mathsf{id}\in\mathcal{I}
, circuits
C\in\mathfrak{C}
, and messages
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Dec}(\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}},\mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{pp},C,c_{1},\dots,c_{k}))=C(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\\
\mathsf{sk}_{\mathsf{id}}\leftarrow\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id})\\
c_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{id},m_{i}),\ i\in[k]\end{array}\!\right]=1.

•

Efficiency.
There is a polynomial
p_{\mathsf{Eval}}
such that for every circuit
C
,
\mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{pp},C,c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
runs in time
|C|\cdot p_{\mathsf{Eval}}(\lambda)
. The per-gate cost does not depend on
C
, in particular not on its depth.
26
26

26

Leveled schemes such as
Gentry et al. [2013]
have a per-gate cost polynomial in the depth bound, which would add a power of the depth to the cost of evaluating a deep circuit. Bootstrapping removes the dependence under a circular-security assumption. In our tasks the bootstrapping key of the identity of a sample can be supplied with the sample, since the example oracle holds the master secret key. We assume the depth-independent bound and do not pursue the construction.

•

Compactness.
There exists a polynomial
p
such that for all
\lambda
,
C
, and ciphertexts
c_{i}
,

\Pr\left[|\mathsf{Eval}(\mathsf{pp},C,c_{1},\dots,c_{k})|\leq p(\lambda)\;\middle|\;(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})\right]=1.

•

IND-sID-CPA Security.
For every polynomial-size adversary
\mathsf{Adv}
,

\left|\Pr\left[\mathsf{Game}_{0}^{\mathsf{Adv}}(\lambda)=1\right]-\Pr\left[\mathsf{Game}_{1}^{\mathsf{Adv}}(\lambda)=1\right]\right|\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda),

where
\mathsf{Game}_{b}^{\mathsf{Adv}}(\lambda)
is defined as follows:

1.

\mathsf{Adv}
chooses a challenge identity
\mathsf{id}^{*}
.

2.

(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{MSK})\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\lambda})
is generated, and
\mathsf{pp}
is given to
\mathsf{Adv}
.

3.

\mathsf{Adv}
may adaptively query
\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id})
for identities
\mathsf{id}\neq\mathsf{id}^{*}
.

4.

\mathsf{Adv}
submits
m_{0},m_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
of equal length.

5.

The challenger returns
c^{*}\leftarrow\mathsf{Enc}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{id}^{*},m_{b})
.

6.

\mathsf{Adv}
may again query
\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{MSK},\mathsf{id})
for identities
\mathsf{id}\neq\mathsf{id}^{*}
.

7.

\mathsf{Adv}
outputs a bit
b^{\prime}
, which is the output of the game.

Strongly Unforgeable Signatures

A strongly unforgeable signature scheme
[
Diffie and Hellman, 1976
,
Goldwasser et al., 1988
,
Boneh et al., 2006
]
allows generation of signatures that can be publically verified but are computationally hard to forge.

Definition 31

(Strongly Unforgeable Signature Scheme)
.

A strongly unforgeable signature scheme consists of a tuple of probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms
(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Sign},\mathsf{Verify})
with the following properties:

•

Key Generation.

(\mathsf{vk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and outputs a verification key
\mathsf{vk}
and a signing key
\mathsf{sk}
.

•

Signing.

\sigma\leftarrow\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{sk},m)
takes as input the signing key
\mathsf{sk}
and a message
m
, and outputs a signature
\sigma
.

•

Verification.

\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{vk},m,\sigma)\in\{0,1\}
takes as input the verification key
\mathsf{vk}
, a message
m
, and a signature
\sigma
, and outputs 1 if
\sigma
is a valid signature on
m
under
\mathsf{vk}
, and 0 otherwise.

The scheme must satisfy the following properties:

•

Correctness.
For all
\lambda
and all messages
m
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{vk},m,\sigma)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{vk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\\
\sigma\leftarrow\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{sk},m)\end{array}\!\right]=1.

•

Strong Unforgeability.
For every polynomial-size adversary
\mathsf{Adv}
, there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
such that

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Verify}(\mathsf{vk},m^{*},\sigma^{*})=1\text{ and }(m^{*},\sigma^{*})\notin\mathcal{Q}\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{vk},\mathsf{sk})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\\
(m^{*},\sigma^{*})\leftarrow\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{sk},\cdot)}(\mathsf{vk})\end{array}\!\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda),

where
\mathsf{Adv}
asks the signing oracle for signatures on polynomially many messages of its choice and
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of the message-signature pairs it receives.

C.4
Succinct Non-Interactive Arguments of Knowledge

First we define universal relations
\mathsf{R}_{U}

[
Barak and Goldreich, 2002
]
.

Definition 32

(Universal Relation)
.

A universal relation
\mathsf{R}_{U}
consists of all instance-witness pairs
(y,w)
where
y=(M,x,t)
and
|y|,|w|\leq t
, such that
M
is a random-access machine that accepts
(x,w)
within at most
t
steps.

A fully succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge (SNARK)
[
Bitansky et al., 2012
,
Bitansky et al., 2013
,
Bitansky et al., 2022
]
lets a prover convince a verifier with a short proof that it knows a witness for an instance of
\mathsf{R}_{U}
.

Definition 33

(SNARK)
.

A SNARK for a relation
\mathsf{R}\subseteq\mathsf{R}_{U}
consists of a triple of algorithms
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V})
with the following properties:

•

Parameter Generation.

\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda},T)
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and a time bound
T
, and outputs public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
.

•

Proving.

\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp},y,w)
takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
, an instance
y=(M,x,t)
with
t\leq T
and a witness
w
, and outputs a proof
\pi
.

•

Verification.

\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)\in\{0,1\}
takes as input the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
, an instance
y
and a proof
\pi
, and outputs
1
if it accepts
\pi
and
0
otherwise.

The scheme must satisfy the following properties:

•

Completeness.
For every large enough security parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, every time bound
T\in\mathbb{N}
, and every instance-witness pair
(y,w)\in\mathsf{R}
with
y=(M,x,t)
and
t\leq T
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda},T),\;\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp},y,w)\end{array}\!\right]=1.

•

Adaptive Proof of Knowledge.
For every deterministic polynomial-size prover
\mathsf{P}^{*}
, there exists a polynomial-size extractor
\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{P}^{*}}
such that for every large enough
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, all auxiliary inputs
z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)}
, and all time bounds
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)=1\text{ and }(y,w)\notin\mathsf{R}\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda},T)\\
(y,\pi)\leftarrow\mathsf{P}^{*}(\mathsf{pp},z)\\
w\leftarrow\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{P}^{*}}(\mathsf{pp},z)\end{array}\!\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

A randomized prover is covered by including its coins in
z
.

•

Efficiency.
There exists a polynomial
p
such that, for every large enough security parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, every time bound
T\in\mathbb{N}
, and every instance
y=(M,x,t)
with
t\leq T
, the following bounds hold:

–

Generator running time is
p(\lambda+\log T)
.

–

Prover running time is
p(\lambda+|M|+|x|+t+\log T)
.

–

Verifier running time is
p(\lambda+|M|+|x|+\log T)
.

–

Proof size is
p(\lambda+\log T)
.

Adaptive proof of knowledge is stated above for all auxiliary inputs
z
. Extractability with respect to arbitrary auxiliary input is implausible under indistinguishability obfuscation
[
Bitansky et al., 2014
,
Boyle and Pass, 2015
]
. Lemma
21
invokes the extractor only for the benign auxiliary-input distribution consisting of a verification key, fresh signatures under an independently generated signing key and uniform coins, and the assumption can be restricted to that distribution.

A zero-knowledge SNARK
[
Ben-Sasson et al., 2013
]
satisfies the zero-knowledge property
[
Goldwasser et al., 1989
]
in its non-interactive form
[
Blum et al., 1988
]
. That is, an honest prover can generate valid proofs without leaking any information beyond the truth of the statement, particularly without revealing any knowledge of the witness used to generate the proof. For zero-knowledge SNARKs, the public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
form a common reference string that is trusted by both the verifier and the prover.

Definition 34

(Zero-Knowledge SNARK)
.

A triple of algorithms
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V})
is a zero-knowledge SNARK for a relation
\mathsf{R}\subseteq\mathsf{R}_{U}
if it is a SNARK for
\mathsf{R}
and satisfies the following property.
There exists a stateful interactive polynomial-size simulator
\mathsf{Sim}
such that for every stateful interactive polynomial-size distinguisher
\mathsf{Adv}
there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
such that for every large enough security parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
and every time bound
T\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left|\Pr\left[t\leq T,\ (y,w)\in\mathsf{R},\ \mathsf{Adv}(\pi)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda},T)\\
(y,w)\leftarrow\mathsf{Adv}(\mathsf{pp})\\
\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp},y,w)\end{array}\!\right]\right.\\
\left.-\Pr\left[t\leq T,\ (y,w)\in\mathsf{R},\ \mathsf{Adv}(\pi)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathsf{pp},\mathsf{trap})\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(1^{\lambda},T)\\
(y,w)\leftarrow\mathsf{Adv}(\mathsf{pp})\\
\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{Sim}(\mathsf{pp},y,\mathsf{trap})\end{array}\!\right]\right|\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

C.5
Incrementally Verifiable Computation

Incrementally verifiable computation (IVC)
[
Valiant, 2008
]
lets a party perform a long deterministic computation step by step. After every step, there is a short proof that its current configuration is correct. Moreover, the proof can be updated to a proof of the next step.

Definition 35

(Incrementally Verifiable Computation)
.

An IVC scheme for a deterministic Turing machine
M
, with language
\mathsf{L}_{M}:=\{(\chi,t,c):c=M^{(t)}(\chi)\}
, where
M^{(t)}(\chi)
is the configuration of
M
on input
\chi
after
t
steps, and a halted machine stays in its final configuration, which is accepting if and only if
M
outputs
1
, consists of a tuple of algorithms
(\mathsf{G},\mathsf{P},\mathsf{V},\mathsf{Update})
with the following syntax:

•

Parameter Generation.

\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda})
takes as input the security parameter
\lambda
and outputs public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
.

•

Proving.

\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp},y)
, takes as input public parameter
\mathsf{pp}
and
y=(\chi,t,c)\in\mathsf{L}_{M}
and outputs a proof
\pi
.

•

Verification.

b=\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)
outputs
b\in\{0,1\}
indicating acceptance or rejection of the proof
\pi
for instance
y
.

•

Update.

\pi_{t}\leftarrow\mathsf{Update}(\mathsf{pp},(\chi,t-1,c_{t-1}),\pi_{t-1})
takes as input the public parameters, a previous statement
(\chi,t-1,c_{t-1})\in\mathsf{L}_{M}
, and the proof
\pi_{t-1}
, and outputs a new proof
\pi_{t}
for the statement
(\chi,t,c_{t})
.

The scheme must satisfy the following properties:

•

Completeness.
For every
y=(\chi,t,c)\in\mathsf{L}_{M}
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda}),\;\pi\leftarrow\mathsf{P}(\mathsf{pp},y)\end{array}\!\right]=1.

•

Efficiency.

\mathsf{G}
runs in time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
and
\mathsf{P}
in time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,|y|,t)
. The proof length is
|\pi|=\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,\log t)
, the verifier’s running time is
|y|\cdot\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,|\pi|)
, and
\mathsf{Update}
runs in time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,|\chi|+|c_{t-1}|,\log t)
.

•

Soundness.
For every polynomial-size cheating prover
\mathsf{P}^{*}
and every polynomial
t=t(\lambda)
, there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
such that

\Pr\left[y=(\chi,t,c)\notin\mathsf{L}_{M}\text{ and }\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},y,\pi)=1\;\middle|\;\!\begin{array}[]{l}\mathsf{pp}\leftarrow\mathsf{G}(1^{\lambda}),\;(y,\pi)\leftarrow\mathsf{P}^{*}(\mathsf{pp})\end{array}\!\right]\leq\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

•

Incrementality.
For any valid sequence of configurations
c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{t}
with
c_{0}
the initial configuration of
M(\chi)
, and
\pi_{0}=\bot
, the proofs
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{t}
can be computed iteratively using

\pi_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Update}(\mathsf{pp},(\chi,i-1,c_{i-1}),\pi_{i-1})\quad\text{for each }i\in[t].

Moreover,
\mathsf{Update}
extends every accepting proof, not only proofs it produced itself: for every
(\chi,t-1,c_{t-1})\in\mathsf{L}_{M}
and every
\pi_{t-1}
with
\mathsf{V}(\mathsf{pp},(\chi,t-1,c_{t-1}),\pi_{t-1})=1
, the proof
\mathsf{Update}(\mathsf{pp},(\chi,t-1,c_{t-1}),\pi_{t-1})
is accepted by
\mathsf{V}
for
(\chi,t,c_{t})
with probability
1
.

The last property is what lets a party continue a computation from a certified configuration that it did not produce, as the inference phase in Section
5.2
does with the input it receives. It holds for the schemes obtained by recursive composition of SNARKs
[
Valiant, 2008
,
Bitansky et al., 2013
]
, where the proof for step
t
is an argument of knowledge of an accepting proof for step
t-1
and of the transition, so that any accepting predecessor proof serves as the witness. It is not guaranteed by every IVC scheme. In particular,
Naor et al. [2019]
note that their construction from incremental probabilistically checkable proofs does not satisfy it.

C.6
Non-Parallelizing Languages with Average-Case Hardness

Average-case non-parallelizing languages (NPLs)
[
Bitansky et al., 2016
]
with difficulty parameter
\tau
are decidable in time
\tau
, but polynomial-size circuits of depth at most
\tau^{\eta}
, for a constant
\eta<1
, decide them on sampled instances with probability only negligibly higher than one half. We require an NPL for every difficulty parameter
\tau
and input length, together with a uniform way to decide these languages and to sample hard instances for every
\tau
.

Definition 36

(Average-Case Non-Parallelizing Language Ensemble)
.

An average-case NPL ensemble with gap
\eta<1
for a collection of languages
\{\mathsf{L}_{\lambda,\tau}\}_{\lambda,\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathsf{L}_{\lambda,\tau}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
is a pair of algorithms
\mathsf{NPL}=(\mathsf{Samp},M)
with the following properties:

•

Sampling.

\chi\leftarrow\mathsf{Samp}(1^{\lambda},\tau)
takes as input a length
\lambda
and a difficulty parameter
\tau
, runs in time
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda,\tau)
, and outputs an instance
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
.

•

Decision.

b=M(\tau,\chi)
takes as input a difficulty parameter
\tau
and an instance
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
, and outputs a bit
b\in\{0,1\}
.

The algorithms must satisfy the following properties:

•

Sequential Decidability.
For every
\lambda,\tau\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
,
M(\tau,\chi)
runs in time
\tau
and space
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
, and outputs
1
if and only if
\chi\in\mathsf{L}_{\lambda,\tau}
. In particular, every configuration of
M(\tau,\chi)
has length
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
, independently of
\tau
, and one step of
M
is computed by a circuit of size
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
.

•

Average-Case Non-Parallelizing.
For every family
\mathsf{Adv}=\{\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size circuits, there exists a negligible function
\mathsf{negl}
such that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\tau\geq\lambda
, if
\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}
has depth at most
\tau^{\eta}
, then

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}(\chi)=M(\tau,\chi)\;\middle|\;\chi\leftarrow\mathsf{Samp}(1^{\lambda},\tau)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

Since the depth of a circuit is at most its size, hardness against shallow circuits implies hardness against small ones. In particular, the second property applies to every family of polynomial-size circuits with
|\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}|\leq\tau^{\eta}
. This is how we use it, since our parties are measured by size.
