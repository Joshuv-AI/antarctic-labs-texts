Stable core-encapsulation of indium-111 in crosslinked polyionic complex micelles and assessment of its biodistribution
Authors: Hayato Laurence Mizuno, Sotaro Miyao, Hiroyuki Takahashi, Nobuyoshi Akimitsu, Yasutaka Anraku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadioactive isotopes, such as Indium-111 (111In), are pivotal for highly sensitive noninvasive imaging and targeted radiotherapy. However, achieving stable in vivo retention remains a critical barrier. Conventional nanocarriers typically rely on surface-bound chelators, exposing the isotopes to biological transchelation and detachment, while potentially altering the carrier’s surface properties and pharmacokinetics. To address these limitations, this study evaluates a polyionic complex micelle (PICm) platform designed to encapsulate 111In within a crosslinked core using DOTA chelation. This approach aims to enhance radiochemical stability while preserving the micelle’s native, engineered surface characteristics.ResultsDOTA-functionalized PICm were fabricated through electrostatic self-assembly of PEG-based polymers and subsequent core crosslinking. The resulting micelles exhibited a highly uniform size (32.6 ± 6.3 nm) and a modestly negative surface charge (− 4.9 ± 1.0 mV). Stability assays demonstrated that under the acidic conditions required for 111In chelation (pH 5.5), the micelles undergo reversible size swelling driven by polymer protonation without aggregating or collapsing, confirming that core crosslinking successfully maintains micellar integrity. In vivo biodistribution in CT26 tumor-bearing mice revealed that 111In-loaded PICm displayed prolonged systemic presence, with tumor accumulation peaking at 2.3 ± 0.4%ID/g at 24 h post-injection via the enhanced permeability and retention effect. Hepatic and splenic uptakes aligned with typical nanoparticle clearance pathways.ConclusionsThe core-loading of PICm may offer advantages over surface-chelated nanocarriers. By maintaining structural integrity during radiolabeling and extending systemic circulation in vivo, these results validate the platform’s robustness and passive tumor accumulation, highlighting its potential as a versatile vehicle for the delivery of diagnostic radionuclides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-026-00478-4.Keywords: Polyionic complex micelles, Radioactive isotopes, Radiopharmaceuticals, Biokinetics

Introduction

Radioactive isotopes (RIs), owing to their ability to emit ionizing radiation, are widely utilized in medical applications for theranostic purposes (Burkett et al. 2023; Shende and Gandhi 2021). When combined with positron emission spectroscopy (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), gamma rays emitted from RIs can be detected noninvasively with high sensitivity and spatial resolution from outside of the patient’s body (Rong et al. 2023; Müller et al. 2014; Alqahtani 2023). This capability enables precise localization of RI accumulation, thereby facilitating highly sensitive diagnosis of diseases such as cancer (Sgouros et al. 2020). In addition to diagnostic applications, RIs that emit high-energy radiation, such as beta or alpha particles, can induce single- and double-stranded DNA breaks, leading to selective cytotoxicity in diseased cells where the RIs are deposited (Khazaei Monfared et al. 2023). Compared with conventional external-beam radiotherapy, RI-based radiotherapy eliminates the need for an external radiation source, thereby reducing off-target exposure and minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues (Pouget et al. 2015). These combined diagnostic and therapeutic advantages have expanded the clinical applicability of RIs to the detection and treatment of a broad range of diseases, including various cancers, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases, with ongoing investigation aimed at extending their use to additional pathological conditions (Sgouros et al. 2020).

Among clinically established RIs, indium-111 (111In) possesses favorable nuclear properties, such as its relatively long half-life (2.8 days, Naik et al. 2017) and gamma emission energies suitable for detection. These characteristics have led to widespread use of 111In for labeling antibodies (Lamki et al. 1991; Gustavsson et al. 2024; Spahn et al. 2025; Loutfi et al. 1990), peptides (Pruller et al. 2023; Guan et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2004), and nanoparticle-based systems (Cao et al. 2013; Ng et al. 2014; Sun et al. 2014; Hsu et al. 2015). Its widespread clinical translation is evidenced by approved diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals, including 111In-Ibritumomab tiuxetan (Kume et al. 2021), 111In-capromab pendetide (Manyak 2008), and 111In-pentetreotide (Buscombe et al. 2003). Nevertheless, achieving stable in vivo retention of 111In remains critical, as dissociation of indium ions leads to nonspecific organ accumulation, thereby obscuring target detection and confounding biodistribution assessment.

Polyionic complex micelles (PICm) are a versatile platform for delivering various drug modalities to a variety of target tissues, owing to their tunable size, surface chemistry, and prolonged circulation. Their nanoscale dimensions allow preferential accumulation in tumors through leaky vasculature (Ideta et al. 2004; Matsumura and Kataoka 2009; Nishiyama et al. 2016). While many conventional 111In-labeled nanoparticle systems employ surface-bound chelators—exposing the RIs to biological transchelation and potentially altering surface properties—core-encapsulation strategies have also been explored, such as the widely reported ionophore-based trapping of radionuclides in liposomes (Essien and Hwang 1988; Chang et al. 2008; Gawne et al. 2018). However, achieving stable core-loading in polymeric systems remains a challenge.

Herein, we report a PICm system in which 111In is stably incorporated into the micellar core (111In-PICm) through chelation with 1,4,7,10-Tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA), a commonly applied indium chelator (Lever et al. 2019; Banerjee et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2020), employing a rationally designed synthetic strategy. Following intravenous administration in a mouse subcutaneous allograft tumor model, the in vivo biodistribution of the resulting 111In-PICm exhibited significant accumulation within tumor tissue. These results demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of core-loaded PICm as a platform for tumor-targeted RI delivery, and highlight their potential for diagnostic applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dimethyl sulfoxide, Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (D-PBS(−)), 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC), 6 M hydrogen chloride solution, 1 mol/L NaOH solution, sodium chloride, disodium hydrogenphosphate, and sodium dihydrogenphosphate were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). 1,5-Diaminopentane was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Sulfo-Cyanine5-NHS ester was purchased from Lumiprobe Corporation (Hunt Valley, MD, USA). DOTA-NHS was purchased from Macrocyclics, Inc. (Plano, TX, USA). 111In chloride solution was purchased from Nihon Medi-Physics Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan).

Experimental

Preparation of DOTA-PICm

Polyion complex micelles (PICm) were fabricated through the ionic complexation of a poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)-b-poly(aspartic acid) block copolymer (PEG-P(Asp)) and a poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly([5-aminopentyl]-α,β-aspartamide) block copolymer (PEG-P(Asp-AP)), as previously reported (Koide et al. 2006; Anraku et al. 2010, 2011; Tang et al. 2017). Briefly, the PEG-P(Asp) polymer was synthesized by deprotecting the benzyl l-aspartate groups of the PEG272-b-poly(β-benzyl-l-aspartate)81 (PEG-PBLA) precursor using a 1 M NaOH solution. The resulting deprotected PEG-PAsp was subsequently purified by dialysis against deionized water. Similarly, the PEG-P(Asp-AP) polycation was obtained via the aminolysis reaction of PEG272-PBLA81 using 1,5-diaminopentane (DAP). The crude PEG-P(Asp-AP) product was purified by sequential dialysis, first against a 0.01 N HCl solution, and then against deionized water.

To prepare the DOTA-PICm, the anionic polymer component was first formulated by dissolving lyophilized PEG-P(Asp) and Cy5-terminated PEG-P(Asp) (PEG-P(Asp)-Cy5) at a 9:1 molar ratio in 10 mM phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) to yield a final polymer concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. To prepare the cationic counterpart, the PEG-P(Asp-AP) polymer was dissolved in 10 mM PB (pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. PICm was prepared by mixing the anionic polymer solution with the cationic polymer solution at a 40:58 ratio then mixed vigorously using a vortex mixer (2,000 rpm, 5 min.). The uniform PICm size distribution was confirmed through dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement using the Zetasizer Nano DLS measurement apparatus (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The functional groups on the oppositely charged polymer side chains, e.g. the amino group and the carboxyl group, were then crosslinked by adding 10 eqv. EDC to the carboxyl groups of the PEG-PAsp side chain (10 mM PB) and allowed to react overnight at room temperature. The product PICm was purified through ultracentrifugation using the Vivaspin 6 membrane (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany) with a molecular weight cutoff of 30k, at 25 °C. The polymer concentration of the PICm solution was determined using the fluorescence of Cy5 conjugated to the PEG-P(Asp) terminus, and adjusted to 1 mg/mL for the subsequent DOTA installation.

DOTA-NHS 5 eqv. to the carboxyl groups within the PICm solution was added to the crosslinked PICm solution and left at room temperature for 8 h. The product DOTA-PICm was purified through ultracentrifugation using the Vivaspin 6 membrane (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany) with a molecular weight cutoff of 30k, at 25 °C. The final DOTA-PICm solution was adjusted to 5.0 mg/mL in D-PBS(−), and its size distribution was evaluated using the Zetasizer Nano DLS measurement apparatus (Malvern Panalytical), to validate its uniform size distribution. The schematic illustration of DOTA-PICm preparation is depicted in Fig. 1.

Evaluation of DOTA-PICm stability under acidic conditions

DOTA-PICm was suspended in acidic buffers to assess whether it can withstand such an environment, which mimics the condition used to chelate 111In to the DOTA group. DOTA-PICm solution (2 μL, 5.0 mg/mL in D-PBS(−)) was mixed with HEPES buffer (38 μL, 0.5 M) prepared at pH 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, or 7.4. HCl solution (10 μL, 0.05 M) was added to the solution, mixed, then incubated for 2 h at 40 °C. HEPES buffer (1 mL, 0.5 M, pH 5.5) was added to the DOTA-PICm solution, then transferred to Vivaspin 6 centrifugal filter units (MWCO 30k PES) and ultracentrifuged (2500 rpm, 25 °C) for 20 min, then again for 10 min after adding HEPES buffer (1 mL, 0.5 M, pH 5.5). DOTA-PICm was suspended in PBS for DLS measurement. Size distribution of the DOTA-PICm after incubation and after purification were evaluated via DLS measurement.

Radiolabeling of 111In-PICm

DOTA-PICm solution (40 μL, 5.0 mg/mL in D-PBS(−)) was mixed with HEPES buffer (760 μL, 0.5 M, pH 5.5) and [111In]InCl3 solution (80 μL, 74 MBq/mL in 0.05 M HCl; Nihon Medi-Physics Co., Ltd, Japan), then allowed to react for 2 h at 40 °C. Subsequently, 0.1 M HCl was added dropwise to adjust the reaction mixture to a final pH of 4.0–4.2, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at 40 ℃. After completion of the reaction, the entire reaction mixture was transferred to Vivaspin 6 centrifugal filter units (MWCO 30k PES) and ultrafiltered (2500 ×g, 25 ℃) for 20 min, then again for 10 min after adding HEPES buffer (1 mL, 0.5 M, pH 4.2) to ensure removal of free 111In, followed by four additional 10‑min ultrafiltration cycles under the same conditions. The radiochemical purity of the 111In-PICm solution was verified by evaluating the radioactivity of the flowthrough. The solution was deemed sufficiently pure when the flowthrough’s activity was below 5% of the total 111In-PICm radioactivity (Fig. S1). Finally, 1 mL saline solution was added and ultrafiltered (2500×g, 25 ℃) for 10 min for medium exchange. 50 μL of saline was added to the 111In-PICm solution to adjust the total volume to 150 μL, which was then immediately transferred to a syringe for use in the in vivo studies. The radiolabeling yield of the PICm was 59%. The schematic illustration of 111In-PICm preparation is depicted in Fig. 1.

In vitro stability of 111In-PICm

The stability of 111In‑PICm was evaluated in three media: saline, pre‑filtered fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 5 mM DTPA solution. FBS was passed through a Vivaspin 6 membrane (Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany) with a molecular weight cutoff of 30k, and the filtrate was used as the serum medium. The DTPA solution was prepared by diluting DTPA stock solution (adjusted to pH 7.4) with saline to a final concentration of 5 mM.

For each condition, 100 µL of 111In‑PICm (2.10–2.36 MBq) was mixed with 1.2 mL of the respective medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Stability assays were performed using three independent samples per condition (n = 3). After incubation, free 111In dissociated from 111In‑PICm was separated using Vivaspin 6 membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 30k.

Centrifugation yielded a retentate fraction containing intact 111In‑PICm and a filtrate fraction containing free 111In. Radioactivity in both fractions was quantified using a gamma counter (Cobra Quantum, PerkinElmer, USA). The percentage of released 111In was calculated by dividing the decay‑corrected radioactivity in the filtrate by the total radioactivity of the sample.

Establishment of tumor homograft mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Animal Research at the Isotope Science Center, The University of Tokyo, and were conducted in accordance with institutional (University of Tokyo Conduct no. 129: Code of Practice for Animal Research) and ARRIVE’s guidelines (Sert et al. 2020). Female BALB/c Slc-nu/nu nude mice (7 weeks old, weighing 16–21 g) were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Japan). All mice were housed under a cycle of 12 h of light and 12 h of dark with unrestricted access to food and water. Environmental conditions were maintained at 20–25°C and 50–60% relative humidity to support physiological stability.

CT26 cells, a colon adenocarcinoma cell line derived from female BALB/c mice, were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in air. The cultured cells were harvested using trypsin–EDTA (0.25% trypsin, 0.02% EDTA). Finally, CT26 cells (1.0 × 106 cells) suspended in 100 μL of 1×PBS were administered subcutaneously into the right shoulder of each mouse. Mice were then monitored for 12 days to allow for tumor development prior to administration of the 111In-PICm. Note that the CT26 cell line was employed because it is a widely utilized, well-characterized syngeneic model known to exhibit robust neovasculature, making it highly suitable for evaluating EPR-mediated passive accumulation.

Biodistribution evaluation of 111In-DOTA in tumor-bearing mice

111In‑DOTA was prepared by mixing 45 µL of 0.1 M sodium acetate/acetic acid buffer (pH 4.5) with 25 µL of [111In]InCl₃ solution, followed by the addition of 1.4 µL of DOTA solution (20 nmol/µL in DMSO). The reaction mixture was incubated at 80 °C for 1 h. After completion of the reaction, the solution was diluted with saline to a final volume of 200 µL, and the resulting 111In-DOTA solution (1.70–1.87 MBq per mouse) was intravenously administered via the tail vein to CT26-homograft model mice.

Biodistribution was evaluated at 5, 24, 48, and 72 h post‑injection, using three mice per time point (N = 3). At each time point, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia induced by 3–4% isoflurane delivered through a vaporizer system (AnesII, Biomachinery Co., Japan). Under anesthesia, cardiac puncture was performed to obtain blood samples, followed by perfusion with approximately 15 mL of 1×PBS via the left ventricle to remove residual blood from the organs. Immediately after PBS perfusion, cervical dislocation was conducted to ensure complete euthanasia. All procedures were performed by trained and experienced personnel in accordance with The University of Tokyo Manual for Animal Experiments. Blood, tumor, and major organs (liver, spleen, right kidney, stomach, large and small intestines, heart, lung, muscle, bone, and brain) were collected and weighed. The radioactivity in each sample was quantified using a gamma counter (Cobra Quantum, PerkinElmer, USA). Radioactive counts were decay‑corrected to determine the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g).

Biodistribution evaluation of the 111In-PICm in Mice

111In-PICm solution (2.36–2.86 MBq in 150 μL saline per mouse) was intravenously administered via tail vein to CT26-homograft model mice. Biodistribution studies were conducted at 5, 24, 48, and 72 h post‑injection, using three mice per time point (n = 3). At each designated time point, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia induced by 3–4% isoflurane administered via the vaporizer system (AnesII, Biomachinery Co., Japan). Under anesthesia, cardiac puncture was performed to obtain blood samples, followed by perfusion with approximately 15 mL of 1×PBS via the left ventricle to remove residual blood from the organs. Immediately after PBS-perfusion, cervical dislocation was performed to ensure complete euthanasia. This procedure was carried out by trained and experienced researchers in accordance with The University of Tokyo Manual for Animal Experiments. Blood, tumor, and organs (liver, spleen, right kidney, stomach, large and small intestines, heart, lung, muscle, bone and brain) were collected and weighed. The radioactivity in each sample was quantified using a gamma counter (Cobra Quantum, PerkinElmer, USA). Radioactive counts were decay-corrected to determine the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g).

Results

Preparation and characterization of DOTA-PICm

The DOTA-functionalized polyionic complex micelles (DOTA-PICm) were successfully fabricated through the initial electrostatic self-assembly of engineered PEG-polyanion and PEG-polycation polymers. Following self-assembly, the micellar core was stabilized by crosslinking the carboxyl and amine groups present on the polymer sidechains. Subsequently, DOTA-NHS was conjugated to the remaining residual amine groups. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis confirmed that the resulting DOTA-PICm exhibited a highly uniform size distribution, with an average diameter of 32.6 ± 6.3 nm and a polydispersity index (PdI) of 0.044 ± 0.031 (Fig. 2). These values were consistent with the PICm before DOTA installation, meaning that DOTA installation did not affect the size distribution of the micelles (Table S1). Additionally, zeta potential measurements indicated a value of − 4.9 ± 1.0 mV for the DOTA-PICm.

Size distribution of DOTA-PICm measured via dynamic light scattering. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3)

Stability assessment under acidic conditions

To evaluate their structural stability prior to radiolabeling, DOTA-PICm were incubated in a mixture of 0.05 M HCl and 0.5 M HEPES solution, adjusted to predetermined acidic pH levels, for 2 h at 40 °C. After the solutions were cooled to room temperature, subsequent DLS analysis revealed a moderate morphological shift, with the micellar size and PdI increasing to approximately 50 nm and 0.15, respectively (Fig. 3). However, following purification via ultracentrifugation, both the particle size and PdI fully recovered to approximately their initial as-prepared states.

Size distribution of DOTA-PICm incubated under acidic conditions and after purification. a Diameter of DOTA-PICm. b Polydispersity index of DOTA-PICm. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3)

In vitro radiochemical stability of 111In-PICm

To validate the efficacy of the stable core-encapsulation platform and distinguish its physical stability from its radiochemical stability, the structural integrity of 111In-PICm was evaluated under challenging biological and competitive conditions (Fig. 4). Following a 24-h incubation at 37 °C, 111In-PICm exhibited excellent radiochemical stability in both physiological saline and fetal bovine serum (FBS), with the percentage of intact core-encapsulated 111In remaining as high as 97% and 98%, respectively. Furthermore, to evaluate the resistance of the formulation against transchelation, a highly stringent competition assay was performed using a 5 mM DTPA solution. Even under this aggressive thermodynamic challenge, 70% of the 111In remained securely complexed within the PICm core after 24 h. Taken together, these results demonstrate that 111In-PICm possesses robust radiochemical stability and effectively prevents premature radiometal leakage, satisfying the prerequisites for subsequent in vivo applications.

In vitro stability of 111In-PICm. 111In-PICm was incubated in saline, serum, and DTPA solutions, and the amount retained after 24 h was evaluated via gamma counter. Data presented as mean ± standard deviation (n =3)

In vivo biodistribution kinetics in tumor-bearing mice

To evaluate the impact of macromolecular core-encapsulation on systemic pharmacokinetics and tumor targeting efficiency, a comparative in vivo biodistribution study was conducted. 111In-PICm and 111In-DOTA were intravenously administered to tumor-bearing mice, and tissue uptake was tracked over a 72-h period (Fig. 5). Crucially, 111In-PICm exhibited enhanced tumor delivery via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Tumor accumulation of 111In-PICm reached a peak of 2.3 ± 0.4 %ID/g at 24 h post-injection, followed by a gradual washout through 72 h (Fig. 5a, c). Conversely, 111In-DOTA, which serves as the small-molecular control, showed virtually no accumulation within the tumor tissue at any analyzed time point, demonstrating an approximately 50-fold lower peak tumor uptake than the micellar formulation.

Distribution of 111In-PICm and 111In-DOTA in tumor-bearing mice. a and b Broad organ bio distribution profiles following intravenous administration of a 111In-PICm and b 111In-DOTA to tumor-bearing mice sacrificed at 5, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection. Tissue uptake was quantified and expressed as percent injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g), representing radioactivity counts normalized by the injected dose per gram of tissue. c–f Time-activity curves showing detailed clearance and accumulation kinetics of 111In-PICm and 111In-DOTA in c tumor, d blood, e kidney, and f liver. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3)

This high tumor accumulation of 111In-PICm is partially attributable to its prolonged circulation lifetime, retaining a high concentration in systemic circulation (2.3 ± 1.1 %ID/g) at 5 h, which gradually cleared over 72 h (Fig. 5d). In contrast, 111In-DOTA, exhibited rapid renal clearance, with blood radioactivity levels dropping to near-negligible levels by 5 h post-injection. This fast systemic clearance was accompanied by a transient accumulation in the kidneys at 5 h (Fig. 5b, e). Due to its nanoparticle size, 111In-PICm successfully evaded immediate glomerular filtration, resulting in lower initial renal excretion, though a significant fraction exhibited anticipated accumulation in the liver (Fig. 5f). Collectively, these results confirm that stable loading of 111In into the PICm core radically alters the radiometal’s in vivo fate, dramatically extending circulation times and driving passive accumulation at the tumor site.

Discussion

The synthetic methodology utilized to install DOTA onto the residual functional groups after crosslinking the core was adapted from previous protocols developed for the fluorescent labeling of PICm in their cores (Nakagawa et al. 2025). The resulting DOTA-PICm demonstrated a modestly negative surface charge (− 4.9 ± 1.0 mV), which is considered ideal for the minimization of non-specific uptake by organs that have negatively charged blood-organ interfaces such as the liver and spleen. Such a negatively charged surface functioned to extend the systemic circulation lifetime of the nanocarriers (Lee et al. 2011).

Because the chelation of 111In to DOTA traditionally requires acidic conditions to maintain the solubility of 111In, confirmation of micellar stability in such an acidic environment was an essential requisite. The observed size expansion during acidic incubation proved to be entirely reversible upon purification. This reversibility indicates that the morphological change was not caused by permanent aggregation or the collapse of the micellar structure. Instead, the "swelling” is attributed to an alteration in the protonation state of the constituent polymers, which consequently induces elevated inter-polymer electrostatic repulsion (Ohara et al. 2019). This physical resilience highlights the efficacy of the core-crosslinking strategy, which successfully stabilizes the micellar architecture even under acidic conditions. Based on these findings, pH 5.5 was selected as an appropriate solvent condition, minimizing the swelling effect while maintaining the low pH necessary for efficient 111In loading.

The core-crosslinking and DOTA-functionalization strategy employed in the current study offers distinct advantages over alternative core-loading techniques, such as the ionophore-mediated trapping commonly used for liposomal formulations (Hwang et al. 1982). While ionophores enable highly efficient and rapid loading of 111In into an aqueous core, the subsequent retention of the isotope is heavily dependent on the internal environment of the nanocarrier and the physical integrity of the lipid bilayer. If the carrier membrane is destabilized in vivo, premature leakage of the radionuclide can occur. In contrast, the PICm platform utilizes DOTA, a macrocyclic chelator renowned for its high thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness with indium. By covalently anchoring DOTA within the crosslinked core, the 111In is chemically tethered rather than merely physically confined, thereby mitigating the risk of transchelation and dissociation during systemic circulation.

The in vivo biodistribution profile of the radiopharmaceuticals aligns closely with established nanocarrier paradigms, demonstrating that biological fate is heavily dictated by molecular size and structural stability. Small hydrophilic radiometal complexes like 111In-DOTA are rapidly cleared from the body through renal filtration, resulting in a short biological half-life that prevents efficient accumulation in target pathological tissues. In this study, the inclusion of 111In-DOTA as a control group clearly highlighted this limitation, presenting rapid renal clearance and an absence of tumor accumulation at all analyzed time points.

Conversely, by shielding the radiometal within a macromolecular architecture, the formulation effectively surpasses the renal filtration threshold (~ 30–50 kDa, Ruggiero et al. 2010), explaining the marked increase in blood residence time. The fact that a substantial fraction of 111In-PICm remained in the bloodstream even 48 h post-injection indicates a highly prolonged systemic presence. This extended systemic exposure likely facilitated efficient tumor delivery by enabling repeated interactions with the tumor vasculature, driving passive accumulation at the target site via the enhanced permeation and retention (EPR) effect (Shi et al. 2020; Ghezzi et al. 2021).

Our kinetic data reveal that tumor accumulation peaked precisely at 24 h post-injection before undergoing a slow clearance pathway. This specific profile confirms that the core–shell structure remains highly stable in the presence of competing serum components in vivo, allowing intact micelles to steadily extravasate into the tumor interstitial space. Meanwhile, the observed accumulation in the liver and spleen is typical for nanoparticle formulations undergoing systemic clearance. Hepatic uptake can be attributed to the liver’s robust filtration effect, which is characterized by high blood flow, slow sinusoidal circulation, and the direct exposure of the nanoparticles to liver macrophages, such as Kupffer cells (He et al. 2024). Similarly, the splenic accumulation is known to be a consequence of the organ’s phagocytic activity, which promotes further filtration of macromolecular carriers (Perrault et al. 2009; Alexis et al. 2008).

Importantly, the subsequent gradual decline in tumor radioactivity observed between 24 h and 72 h suggests a steady metabolic clearance of the carrier from the tumor mass rather than permanent, non-specific entrapment. This progressive washout profile represents an essential safety feature for translational development, as it demonstrates that the formulation does not cause irreversible tissue retention, thereby potentially minimizing long-term radiation doses to surrounding healthy tissues.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed DOTA-installed polyion complex micelles (DOTA-PICm) with controlled physicochemical properties suitable for radiolabeling and in vivo delivery. The DOTA-PICm exhibited a uniform nanoscale size, narrow dispersity, and a mildly negative surface charge—features favorable for extended systemic circulation and passive tumor accumulation. The DOTA-PICm maintained structural integrity under mildly acidic conditions required for In111 chelation with only reversible swelling observed, confirming that core crosslinking effectively stabilizes the architecture.

Following radiolabeling, the 111In-PICm demonstrated a biodistribution profile characteristic of long-circulating nanocarriers, with a substantial portion of the injected dose remaining in the bloodstream after 48 h, and another portion preferentially accumulating within the homograft tumor. These results collectively validate the structural robustness, radiolabeling compatibility, and favorable in vivo behavior of 111In-PICm, highlighting their potential as a versatile platform for diagnostic imaging.
