Research on the possibility of using olive pit biomass in recycling metals from slag
Authors: Albert Smalcerz, Jerzy Łabaj, Szymon Ptak, Robert Findorak, Tomasz Matuła, Marzena Półka, Tomasz Wojtal, Leszek Blacha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccording to the EU Sustainable Development Goals, it is necessary to search for new technologies that promote sustainable development and optimize the use of resources, raw materials and energy. This article presents the results of research on the use of biomass in metal recycling processes. Unlike fossil fuels, including the coke used in metallurgy, biomass is characterized by a closed carbon cycle. The research results prove that the use of biomass (e.g., olive pits) agents as reductants in the pyrometallurgical process of copper and lead recovery is effective and can be implemented in industrial practice. The significant reduction in copper content in waste slag allows this material to be classified as safe industrial waste, in accordance with standard legal requirements for waste management. This also has an impact on the carbon footprint of the pyrometallurgical process for processing secondary metal-bearing raw materials. As the research results show, the use of biomass led to a 98% reduction in copper and over 60% reduction in lead from the tested slag.Keywords: Biomass, Pyrometallurgy, Slag reduction, ThermogravimetrySubject terms: Bioenergy; Process chemistry; Reactive precursors; Design, synthesis and processing

Introduction

The circular economy model introduced in recent years, which aims to minimize the use of fossil raw materials and the amount of waste produced while limiting pollutant emissions, is based on the creation of technological systems in which waste from some processes is used as raw material in other processes. This alternative to the linear economy has a negative impact on the environment and is based on increasing the processing of natural resources and thus increasing the amount of waste produced in production1,2. The circular economy has forced broad actions aimed at improving the efficiency of the use of energy resources and the industrial use of renewable raw materials, including various types of biomass from agrofood production. This group of waste includes, among others, olive pit biomass.

Annually, more than 3 million tons of olives are harvested worldwide, and their main producers include Spain, Italy, Greece and Turkey. Thus, more than 300 thousand tons of olive pits are produced3. According to the literature, processing one ton of olives yields 27% oil and 73% pomace. Pomace contains 60% water and over 11% pits. Some pomace is involved in the secondary oil recovery process, whereas pits and extracted pomace are used in various technological processes3,4. The olive fruit can be divided into three parts in terms of structure (Fig. 2). The outer part, which is the skin of the fruit (epicarp), constitutes up to 3% of the fruit mass. The main part of the fruit is the pulp (mesocarp), which constitutes 70 to 80% of the fruit mass. The third part is the fruit pit (endocarp), which accounts for up to 20% of the total fruit mass5.

Olive pits consist mainly of hemicellulose (26.0 wt%), cellulose (20.9 wt%) and lignin (35.6 wt%). They are characterized by a relatively high heating value (heat of combustion), which makes them a valuable component of solid biofuels. The low ash content also significantly expands the possibilities for their use as fuel6. Currently, a technology that allows the use of olive pits for the production of bioethanol has also been developed7–9. When the possibility of using olive pits as solid fuel is analyzed, it should be emphasized that this waste unfortunately contains certain amounts of oil substances, which negatively affect their combustion as biofuel. This mainly concerns pits extracted in the oil production process. The content of oil substances causes, among other factors, increased emissions of carbon dioxide and nitrogen oxides compared with those of other solid biofuels10.

An important direction for the use of olive pits is the production of activated carbon. It is mainly used to remove dyes, odors or various types of impurities from aqueous solutions11–18.

The presented characteristics of olive pits, considering their basic physicochemical properties, indicate the possibility of their use for the production of solid fuel or as a component for the production of biofuels. Thus, this type of biomass can be an alternative raw material to the solid carbon fuels used in many industries. This work presents and discusses the possibility of using olive pits in the metallurgical industry as alternative reducing agents in the pyrometallurgical processing of metal recovered from metallurgical slag. Currently, this type of process uses mainly solid carbon materials, i.e., coke and coke breeze19. The solution proposed by the authors makes it possible to reduce the carbon footprint by using biomass and thus creates economically advantageous conditions for processing and limiting the deposition of environmentally harmful postindustrial solid waste. Notably, there are studies in the literature that present the research results on the use of other types of agrofood biomass in high-temperature metallurgical processes. This includes waste cooking oil, nut shells, and sunflower husks20–30.

In the article, the authors proposed a number of basic research (discussed in more detail in section “Research plan” Research plan) aimed at characterizing olive pit biomass for use as a reducing agent in the pyrometallurgical process.

The literature contains a number of data on the use of various types of biomass in metallurgical processes, but there are no research results on olive pits. Therefore, the research discussed in the article for energy materials, determining the behavior of biomass at high temperatures by conducting thermogravimetric tests and conducting basic tests of reductive slag melting are aimed at determining the degree of removal of metals i.e., Cu and Pb, and will demonstrate their usefulness.

Determination of the heat of combustion

The heat of combustion of the olive pits was determined in accordance with the EN ISO 1716 standard. The test consisted of complete combustion of the test sample in a calorimetric bomb under an oxygen atmosphere immersed in water. The temperature of the water jacket was recorded.

After the biomass sample (m = 1 ± 0.0005 g) was placed in the calorimetric bomb, oxygen was injected into the container to create an atmosphere enabling complete combustion of the sample. Since the container was placed in a double water jacket, it was possible to accurately determine the heat of combustion, considering the heat from the condensation of water vapor generated during the combustion process. The sample was ignited by a suitable electrical system enabling the flow of electric current through the ignition wire. All the data were recorded by a computerized measurement system. The heat of combustion of the material was calculated after corrections for additional thermal effects were determined. Three consecutive tests were performed, and the results were averaged. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the samples was performed with a thermal analyzer (Derivatograph-C, MOM Budapest) to study their thermal stability and reactivity with CO2. The sample was placed in an Al2O3 crucible, and in the first step, the heating mode was linear, with the sample being heated to 1160 °C at a heating rate of 15 K/min and subsequently heated to 1200 °C at a reduced heating rate of 0.5 K/min. The linear heating in the first step (pyrolysis) was performed in a N2 atmosphere, and the quasi-isothermal second step (for reactivity) was performed under CO2 conditions. The flow rates of the injected gases were held constant at 0.05 L/min.

Thermogravimetric analysis

During the TGA of the biomass, a TGA Q500 device manufactured by TA Instruments was used. This method allows studies to be conducted in both an oxidizing atmosphere and an inert atmosphere. In these studies, high-purity nitrogen was used as the inert atmosphere. The device allowed for studies with different sample heating rates from 0.01 to 100 K/min, with an estimated temperature measurement uncertainty of 1 K and a measurement resolution of 0.1 K. The sample mass was measured with a resolution of 0.1 µg and a measurement inaccuracy of 0.01% plus baseline drift not greater than 50 µg (for initial sample mass measurement). Calculated maximum weight uncertainty was equal to 3%. The sample mass ranged from 20 to 22 mg, and the sample heating rate was 100 K/min.

The variable parameter in the research was time, which ranged from 1 to 5 h. The maximum duration of the test was close to the actual time of the operations conducted under industrial conditions.

The analysis of the data presented in Table 3 reveals that the heat of combustion of olive pits is approximately 16,800 kJ/kg and is lower than the heat of combustion of other listed types of biomass. If olive pits were used in metallurgical technologies as fuel, then the production of the same amount of energy would require a larger mass supply than would other types of biomass. On the other hand, this parameter is even less favorable than that of metallurgical coke, which is the basic fuel used in pyrometallurgical processes. According to our study, the average heat of combustion of coke is 27 MJ/kg; thus, to produce a comparable amount of energy, more than 60% more olive pits than coke would be needed.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The results of the thermogravimetric analysis are presented in Figs. 7 and 8 in the form of a TGA curve. The test results indicate that the analyzed biomass samples were characterized by a transient moisture content of approximately 13–15% by mass. This is visible in the graphs presenting the test results in both the inert atmosphere (Fig. 7) and the oxidizing atmosphere (Fig. 8). The evaporation of transient moisture in the temperature range up to approximately 140 °C corresponds to the first peak of the DTG curve, i.e., the derivative of the sample mass changes over time. In both cases, the temperature of approximately 250 °C was determined to mark the beginning of thermal decomposition (pyrolysis), which corresponds to the point where the tangents to the TG curve intersect: 1) the tangent to the plateau after evaporation of transient moisture and 2) the section of the curve after the inflection point, resulting from the first phase of thermal decomposition (pyrolysis). In an inert atmosphere, two stages of pyrolysis are visible at temperatures of 260 °C and 350 °C. This is manifested by peaks of the DTG curve with values of 4.8–6%/min and most likely results from the thermal decomposition (pyrolysis) of hemicellulose and cellulose and, in the case of temperatures of approx. 350 °C, from the degradation of lignin33. The mass loss is similar in both cases, which is also evidenced by the temperature at which 50% mass is lost, i.e., 340–350 °C. In the case of testing in an inert atmosphere above approximately 400 °C, the phase of slow mass loss of the sample begins, which probably results from the process of sample coking. Moreover, the DTG curve obtained in an oxidizing atmosphere reveals an additional peak with a value of approximately 19.5%/min. This occurs at a temperature of approximately 460 °C, which corresponds to the self-ignition temperature of olive pit dust and may indicate the moment that flame combustion occurs in the sample.

Slag reduction

The copper slag reduction results obtained with olive pit biomass as a reducing agent (Table 6) and their graphical interpretation (Fig. 9) demonstrate the possibility of using the tested biomass as a potential reducing agent to recover copper (as well as other metals) in the slag decopperization process. With increasing processing time (from 1 to 5 h), the copper mass content in the waste slag decreased from 0.84% Cu to 0.21% Cu. This increase was accompanied by an increase in the degree of slag decopperization in the range from 92 to 98% (Fig. 10). Moreover, this phenomenon was accompanied by a decrease in the content of copper in the obtained alloy. This effect was related to an increase in the extent of the reduction of the iron contained in the slag. The iron content in the metallic phase increased from 15.5% to almost 24% with increasing test duration. However, there was no significant effect of processing time on the lead content in waste slag, and its concentration ranged from approximately 0.9 to 1.1% Pb by weight.

Changes in the copper and lead contents in postprocess (secondary) slag as a function of time.

Change in the postprocess (secondary) slag decopperization coefficient with time.

The analysis of the research results revealed that the use of olive pit biomass in the slag reduction process resulted in a copper content ≤ 0.5 wt% (in the secondary slag) after four hours of processing. In accordance with the applicable law, this slag can be considered safe for landfills or other purposes (e.g., construction)34.

The obtained results indicate that the products and substrates do not fully balance, as the oxygen is released during the reduction process. The second possible cause is the evaporation of some components contained in the initial slag sample.

Summary

When analyzing the feasibility of using olive pits in metallurgical processes as an alternative reducing agent to the carbon-bearing materials commonly used in this process, a certain limitation of this process must be noted. This concerns the variable supply of this type of biomass on the market throughout the year, i.e., the so-called seasonal availability. The seasonal availability of certain types of biomass, including olive pits, is an important aspect that should be considered when designing technologies that enable their use. The accumulation of such biomass outside the period of its vegetation and harvest is difficult in this case, which can clearly create problems in the raw material supply chain.

This research, which aimed to determine the possibility of using olive pits as an alternative reducing agent in the process of metal recovery from oxide slags, led to the following conclusions:

Olive pits are a material characterized by high carboxyreactivity, which confirms the purpose of using them as reducing agents in the discussed technological process. Nevertheless, the application of this technology on an industrial scale requires prior large-scale research.
