The Critical Beta-splitting Random Tree IV: Mellin analysis of Leaf Height Thanks: SJ supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation and the Swedish Research Council
David J. Aldous, Svante Janson

Abstract
. In the critical beta-splitting model of a random n n -leaf rooted tree, clades are recursively split into sub-clades, and a clade of m m leaves is split into sub-clades containing i i and m − i m-i leaves with probabilities ∝ 1 / ( i ⁡ ( m − i ) ) \propto 1/(i(m-i)) . The height of a uniform random leaf can be represented as the absorption time of a certain harmonic descent Markov chain. Recent work on these heights D n D_{n} and L n L_{n} (corresponding to discrete or continuous versions of the tree) has led to quite sharp expressions for their asymptotic distributions, based on their Markov chain description. This article gives even sharper expressions, based on an n → ∞ n\to\infty limit tree structure described via exchangeable random partitions in the style of Haas et al (2008). Within this structure, calculations of moments lead to expressions for Mellin transforms, and then via Mellin inversion we obtain sharp estimates for the expectation, variance, Normal approximation and large deviation behavior of D n D_{n} .



Keywords:

Exchangeable partition, Markov chain, Mellin transform, random tree, subordinator.

, with
s_{i}\in(-i,-(i-1))
for

i\geq 1
.
Numerically,

s_{1}\doteq-0.567
and

s_{2}\doteq-1.628
.

More generally, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the roots of
\psi(s)=a
are all real
and can be enumerated
as
s_{0}(a)=1>s_{1}(a)>s_{2}(a)>\dots
, with
s_{i}(a)\in(-i,-(i-1))
for

i\geq 1
.

We let
s_{i}
have this meaning throughout the paper.
See
[
19
, §5.4(iii)]
for

in the case

Proof.

Recall that
\psi(s)
is a meromorphic function of
s
, with poles at

0,-1,-2,\dots
. For any other complex
s
we have the standard formulas

[
19
, 5.7.6 and 5.15.1]

(3.9)

\displaystyle\psi(s)

\displaystyle=-\gamma+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+s}\Bigr),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+s)^{2}}.

If
\Im s>0
, then
\Im(1/(k+s))<0
for all
k
and thus (
3.9
)
implies

\Im\psi(s)>0
.
Similarly, if
\Im s<0
, then
\Im\psi(s)<0
.
Consequently, all roots of
\psi(t)=a\in\mathbb{R}
are real.

For real
s
, (
3.10
) shows that
\psi^{\prime}(s)>0
.
We can write
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\text{the poles}\}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}I_{i}
with

I_{0}:=(0,\infty)
and
I_{i}:=(-i,-(i-1))
for
i\geq 1
;
it then follows that
\psi(s)
is strictly increasing in each interval

.
Moreover, by (
3.9
) (or general principles),
at the poles we have the limits
\psi(-i-0)=+\infty
and

\psi(-i+0)=-\infty
(

),
and furthermore (by (

))

\psi(s)\to\infty
as
s\nearrow+\infty
, so
\psi
maps each interval
I_{i}
to
(-\infty,\infty)
.
Consequently,
\psi(s)=a

has exactly one root
s_{i}(a)
in each
I_{i}
.
(See also the graph of
\psi(s)
in
[
19
, Figure 5.3.3]
.)
In the special case

, obviously the positive root is

s_{0}=1
.

The numerical values are obtained by
Maple
.

3.3.
Mellin transforms

If
f
is a function defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=(0,\infty)
, then
its
Mellin transform

is defined by

(3.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}x

for all complex
s
such that the integral converges absolutely.
It is well known that the domain of all such
s
is a strip

\mathcal{D}=\{s:\Re s\in J_{f}\}

for some interval
J_{f}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(possibly empty or degenerate),
and that
\widetilde{f}(s)
is analytic in the interior
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
of
\mathcal{D}

(provided
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
is non-empty, i.e.,
J_{f}
is neither empty nor degenerate).

In analogy to (
3.11
),
if
\mu
is a (possibly complex) measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
its
Mellin transform

is defined by

(3.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

for all complex
s
such that the integral converges absolutely.
Again, the domain of all such
s
is a strip

\mathcal{D}=\{s:\Re s\in J_{\mu}\}

for some interval
J_{\mu}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
(possibly empty or degenerate),
and
\widetilde{\mu}(s)
is analytic in the interior
\mathcal{D}^{\circ}
of
\mathcal{D}
.
Note that the Mellin transform (
3.12
) for real
s
is just the
moments of the measure
\mu
, with a simple shift of the argument.

Remark 3.2
.

There are in the literature
also other definitions of the Mellin transform of measures;
see for example the Mellin-Stieltjes transform in
[
8
, Appendix D]
.
We use the definition above, which is convenient when we want to identify an
absolutely continuous measure with its density function.

4.
The central idea

Recall the limit

P_{t,1}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{K^{(n)}_{t,1}}{n}

from (
2.2
).
The central idea in this article is to define an infinite measure
\Upsilon
on

(0,1)
by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Upsilon:=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{L}}(P_{t,1})\,\mathrm{d}t.

This means that for any (measurable) function
f\geq 0
on
(0,1)
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{L}}(P_{t,1})(x)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[f(P_{t,1})]\,\mathrm{d}t.

This extends by linearity to every complex-valued
f
such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[|f(P_{t,1})|]\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
.
The identity (
4.2
) enables us to express several important
expectations in terms of the measure
\Upsilon
: these are listed
in Proposition
4.1
below.
As well as
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[D_{n}]
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[L_{n}]
, these involve
the total length
\Lambda_{n}
of
\operatorname{CTCS}(n)
, and
the “occupation probability”, that is

(4.3)

a(n,i):=
probability that the harmonic descent chain started at state
n
is ever in state
i
.

So
a(n,n)=a(n,1)=1
.

To illustrate the methodology we derive the first of these identities below
(the others will be proved in Section
5
).

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
n\geq 1
:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[D_{n}]=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-(1-x)^{n-1}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x).

(4.5)

\displaystyle a(n,j)=h_{j-1}\binom{n-1}{j-1}\int_{0}^{1}x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n-j}\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x),\quad 2\leq j\leq n.

(4.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[L_{n}]=\sum_{j=2}^{n}a(n,j)=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=2}^{n}h_{j-1}\binom{n-1}{j-1}x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n-j}\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x).

(4.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[\Lambda_{n}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{x}\Bigl(1-(1-x)^{n}-nx(1-x)^{n-1}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x).

But what is the measure
\Upsilon
, explicitly?
We first note that (
4.2
) and (
2.4
) tell us the
Mellin transform of the
measure
\Upsilon
:

(4.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Upsilon}(s):=\int_{0}^{1}x^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[P_{t,1}^{s-1}\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{\psi(s)-\psi(1)},\qquad\Re s>1.

The measure
\Upsilon
is determined by its Mellin transform.
We do not know how to invert the transform (
4.8
) to obtain a useful explicit formula for

\Upsilon
, but what is relevant to our asymptotics is mainly the behavior of
\Upsilon

near
0
, which will be given in the “inversion estimate”, Lemma
6.1
.

4.1.
An illustrative identity

To prove (
4.4
),
consider
\operatorname{CTCS}(n)
for an integer
n\geq 1
, and recall that the clades
at time
t
are precisely the blocks of the partition
\Pi(t)\cap[n]
.
Fix a non-empty subset
J\subseteq[n]
of size
j=|J|\geq 1
.
If we use the paintbox construction in Theorem
2.1

to reconstruct a copy
\Pi^{\prime}(t)
of
\Pi(t)
,
we see that

conditioned on
(P_{t,\ell})_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
,
the probability that
J
is a block in
\Pi^{\prime}(t)\cap[n]

equals
\sum_{\ell}P_{t,\ell}^{j}(1-P_{t,\ell})^{n-j}
.

We take the expectation;

\Pi^{\prime}(t)
has the same distribution as
\Pi(t)
,
so the distinction between them then disappears
and we obtain that
for any fixed non-empty set

J\subseteq[n]
of size
|J|=j
and
t>0
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(J\text{ is a clade at time }t\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\Bigl[\sum_{\ell}P_{t,\ell}^{j}(1-P_{t,\ell})^{n-j}\Bigr].

By (
2.3
), we can rewrite (

) as

(4.10)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(J\text{ is a clade at time }t\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[P_{t,1}^{j-1}(1-P_{t,1})^{n-j}\bigr].

In particular, since
D_{n}\leq t
exactly when
\{1\}
is a clade at time
t
,
(
4.10
) with
J=\{1\}
and thus
j=1
yields

(4.11)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}>t)=1-{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}\leq t)=1-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[(1-P_{t,1})^{n-1}\bigr]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[1-(1-P_{t,1})^{n-1}\bigr].

Consequently,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[D_{n}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(D_{n}>t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[1-(1-P_{t,1})^{n-1}\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t,

which by (
4.2
) yields our desired formula (
4.4
).

As with the other identities in Proposition
4.1
,
it is then
straightforward (but sometimes intricate) classical analysis to combine (
4.4
) with the inversion estimate (Lemma
6.1
) to obtain
an asymptotic expansion of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[D_{n}]
: see Sections
7
,
8
, and
9
.

Remark 4.2
.

We give in Section
10.1
a different argument,
not
using
Lemma

, where we obtain the same asymptotic expansion
by directly combining (
4.4
) and the Mellin transform
(
4.8
), using a version of Parseval’s formula.
This method is less intutive, but leads to exact formulas involving

Remark 4.3
.

We remark that (
4.4
) combined with (
4.8
)
also yields an exact formula for

.
By the binomial theorem we obtain, using (

(4.13)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[D_{n}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j-1}\binom{n-1}{j}x^{j}\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{1}{\psi(j+1)-\psi(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j-1}\binom{n-1}{j}\frac{1}{h_{j}}.

Remark 5.1

6.
The measure
\Upsilon

The following

will allow us to pass from the identities in Proposition

to sharp

asymptotics for expectations.
Part of this lemma is also given in

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Upsilon
be the infinite
measure on
(0,1)
having the Mellin transform
(
4.8
).
Then

\Upsilon
is absolutely continuous, with a continuous density
\upsilon(x)
on

(0,1)
.
Furthermore:

(i)

The density
\upsilon(x)
satisfies

(6.1)

\displaystyle\upsilon(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}x}+O\bigl(x^{-s_{1}}+x^{-s_{1}}|\log x|^{-1}\bigr),

uniformly for
x\in(0,1)
,
where
s_{1}\doteq-0.567
is the largest negative root of
\psi(s)=\psi(1)
.
In other words,
for
x\in(0,1)
,

(6.2)

\displaystyle\upsilon(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}x^{-1}+r(x)

where

(6.3)

\displaystyle r(x)=O\bigl(x^{-s_{1}}+x^{-s_{1}}|\log x|^{-1}\bigr)

and thus, in particular,
for
x\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
say,

(6.4)

\displaystyle r(x)=O\bigl(x^{|s_{1}|}\bigr).

Furthermore,

\Upsilon(\delta,1)<\infty

for every
\delta>0

and

(6.5)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|r(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

(ii)

More generally,
with
0>s_{1}>s_{2}>\dots
denoting the negative roots of
\psi(s)=\psi(1)
,
for any
N\geq 0
,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\upsilon(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}x^{-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(s_{i})}x^{|s_{i}|}+r_{N}(x),\qquad 0<x<1,

where

(6.7)

\displaystyle r_{N}(x)=O\bigl(x^{|s_{N+1}|}(1+|\log x|^{-1})\bigr)

and

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|r_{N}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

By (
6.4
)–(

), the Mellin transform

exists for

, and we obtain by (

) and (

\displaystyle\widetilde{r}(s)=\widetilde{\Upsilon}(s)-\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}x^{s-2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\psi(s)-\psi(1)}-\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{s-1},

first for
\Re s>1
and then by analytic continuation for

;
note that the right-hand side of (

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

We begin by noting,
as in the proof of Lemma

, that
the Mellin transform
1/\bigl(\psi(s)-\psi(1)\bigr)

in (
4.8
) extends to
a meromorphic function in the entire

\psi(s)=\psi(1)
.
As shown in Lemma

, besides the obvious pole

,
the other poles are real and negative, and thus can be ordered

. In particular,
there are no other poles than 1 in the half-plane

, with
s_{1}\doteq-0.567
.
The residue at the pole

is,
using (

),

(6.10)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\frac{1}{\psi(s)-\psi(1)}=\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(1)}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}.

We cannot immediately use standard results on Mellin inversion

[

because the Mellin transform in (

4
4

4

[

In fact, (

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\log s+o(1)=\log|s|+O(1)=\log|\Im s|+O(1)

We overcome this problem by differentiating the Mellin transform, but we
first subtract the leading term corresponding to the pole at 1.
Since
\Upsilon
is an infinite measure, we first replace it by

\nu

\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=x\,\mathrm{d}\Upsilon(x)

Next, define

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}(\log x)x^{s-1}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{\Delta}(x)

For any real

10.
Alternative proofs via a Parseval formula

We now show that one may skip the intermediate step of obtaining
asymptotics for the measure
\Upsilon
by using the following version of
Parseval’s formula (also called Plancherel’s formula)
for Mellin transforms: see Appendix
A
for a proof and references.

Recall that an integral
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
exists
conditionally

if
f
is locally integrable and the symmetric limit

\lim_{A\to\infty}\int_{-A}^{A}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
exists (and is finite); this limit
is then defined to be
the integral
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.
We use the same terminology for line integrals
\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty}
.

Lemma 10.1
.

Suppose that
f
is a locally integrable function and
\mu
a measure
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
and that
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\sigma-1}|f(x)|<\infty

and
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
, i.e., the
Mellin transforms
\widetilde{f}(s)
and
\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)
are defined when

\Re s=\sigma
.
Suppose also that the integral
\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s
converges
at least conditionally.
Suppose further that
x^{\sigma}f(x)
is bounded and that
f
is

\mu
-a.e. continuous.
Then

(10.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

We may apply this lemma to (
4.4
) and similar formulas,
and then obtain an asymptotic expansion from the complex line integral in
(
10.1
) by shifting the line of integration using residue calculus.
This gives an alternative method to obtain the asymptotic expansions found
above. The new method is perhaps less intuitive that the method used above,
and although it does not require Lemma
6.1
, it requires some technical
arguments and estimates in the complex plane, somewhat similar to the proof
of Lemma
6.1
.
On the other hand, granted these technical details,
the method is straightforward and presents the resulting
asymptotics in a more structured form as a sum of residues
(see for example (
10.15
)).
This is perhaps the main advantage of the method;
it is not important for the simple
cases studied so far, but it will be essential in Section
11
when we
look at the more complicated case of higher moments.

Parseval’s formula for Mellin transforms has long been used to derive
asymptotic expansions for various integrals and integral transforms
in a way similar to our use here, see for example

[
14
]
and
[
21
]
, but this is to our
knowledge the first application to combinatorial probability.
(Mellin transforms are used in other ways in many combinatorial problems,
see for example
[
9
]
.)

Lemma 12.3

Note also that

12.2.
A central limit theorem

As a corollary of Theorem
12.4
, we obtain a new proof of the following CLT.
As mentioned in Section

, this has been
proved in
[
5
, Theorem 1.7]
by analysing a recursion for the moment
generating function; other proofs by different methods are given in

[
2
]
,
[

]
, and

.

Theorem 12.5
.

(12.15)

\displaystyle\frac{D_{n}-\mu\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}\mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2})\ \mbox{ as }n\to\infty

where

(12.16)

\displaystyle\mu:=1/\zeta(2)=6/\pi^{2}\doteq 0.6079;\quad\sigma^{2}:=2\zeta(3)/\zeta(2)^{3}\doteq 0.5401.

Proof.

Let
z=z_{n}
be any sequence of real numbers with
z_{n}\to 0
.
Then (
12.12
) and (
12.10
) yield, as
{n\to\infty}
,

(12.17)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}[e^{z_{n}D_{n}}]=n^{\rho(z_{n})}\cdot\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)=e^{\rho(z_{n})\log n+o(1)}.

We have
\rho(0)=0
, and
the implicit function theorem shows that
\rho
is analytic at 0 (and
everywhere). Hence,

(12.18)

\displaystyle\rho(z_{n})=z_{n}\rho^{\prime}(0)+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{n}^{2}\bigl(\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)+o(1)\bigr).

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
and let
z_{n}:=t/\sqrt{\log n}
(for
n\geq 2
).
Then (
12.17
)–(
12.18
) yield

(12.19)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{t(D_{n}-\rho^{\prime}(0)\log n)/\sqrt{\log n}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{z_{n}(D_{n}-\rho^{\prime}(0)\log n)}

\displaystyle=\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}z_{n}^{2}(\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)+o(1))\log n+o(1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\to e^{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)}

as
{n\to\infty}
.
Since this holds for every real
t
, we thus have the CLT
(
12.15
) with
\mu=\rho^{\prime}(0)
and
\sigma^{2}=\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)
.

Finally,
differentiating (
12.6
) yields

\rho^{\prime}(z)=1/\psi^{\prime}(1+\rho(z))
and in particular

\rho^{\prime}(0)=1/\psi^{\prime}(1)=1/\zeta(2)

as already noted in (

),
and differentiating again yields

(12.20)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}(1)}{\psi^{\prime}(1)^{3}}=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\zeta(2)^{3}},

which completes the proof.
∎

12.3.
Large deviations

As another corollary of Theorem
12.4
, we obtain large deviation results
by the Gärtner–Ellis theorem.
We follow
[
7
, Section 2.3]
, but note that
n
there is replaced by the
“speed”
\log n
(see
[
7
, Remark (a) p. 44]
).
Thus we define (for
n\geq 2
)

(12.21)

\displaystyle Z_{n}:=\frac{1}{\log n}D_{n}

and note that (
12.12
) and Remark
12.1
yield as
{n\to\infty}
,
for any fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

(12.22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log n}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}\bigl[e^{(\log n)\lambda Z_{n}}\bigr]=\frac{1}{\log n}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{\lambda D_{n}}\to\Lambda(\lambda):=\begin{cases}\rho(\lambda),&\lambda<1,\\
+\infty,&\lambda\geq 1.\end{cases}

The Fenchel–Legendre transform
\Lambda^{*}
of
\Lambda

[
7
, Definition 2.2.2]

is defined by

(12.23)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl\{x\lambda-\Lambda(\lambda)\bigr\}=\sup_{-\infty<\lambda<1}\bigl\{x\lambda-\rho(\lambda)\bigr\}.

Recall that
z\mapsto\rho(z)
is a bijection of
(-\infty,1)
onto
(-1,1)
,
which by (
12.6
) is the inverse function of

(12.24)

\displaystyle\rho\mapsto g(\rho):=\psi(1+\rho)-\psi(1).

Hence, if we define

(12.25)

\displaystyle h(x,\rho):=xg(\rho)-\rho,

then
(
12.23
) yields

(12.26)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x)=\sup_{-1<\rho<1}\{xg(\rho)-\rho\}=\sup_{-1<\rho<1}{h(x,\rho)}=\sup_{-1<\rho\leq 1}{h(x,\rho)}

where the last equality holds by continuity.
We have

(12.27)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}h(x,\rho)=x\psi^{\prime}(1+\rho)-1

and for any fixed
x>0
, this is
by (
3.10
)
strictly decreasing from
+\infty
to
x\psi^{\prime}(2)-1=x(\zeta(2)-1)-1

as
\rho
grows from
-1
to 1.
Hence, for
x>0
,

h(x,\rho)
is a concave function of
\rho
, and the supremum in
(
12.26
) is attained at a unique
\rho_{*}(x)\in(-1,1]
given by

(12.28)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi^{\prime}(1+\rho_{*}(x))=1/x,&0<x\leq x_{1}:=(\zeta(2)-1)^{-1},\\
\rho_{*}(x)=1,&x\geq x_{1}.\end{cases}

For
x\leq 0
,
h(x,\rho)
is a decreasing function of
\rho
, and thus
the supremum in (
12.26
) is obtained by letting
\rho\to-1
,
and thus
g(\rho)\to-\infty
.
Thus we obtain, combining the cases,

(12.29)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}+\infty,&x<0,\\
1,&x=0,\\
xg(\rho_{*}(x))-\rho_{*}(x),&x>0.\end{cases}

In particular, for
x\geq x_{1}
, we have by (
12.28
) and (
12.29
)

(12.30)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}(x)=xg(1)-1=x-1.

It follows from (
12.23
) or (
12.26
) that
\Lambda^{*}
is convex
and that it is continuous on
[0,\infty)
.
Taking the derivative in (
12.29
) yields,
for
x>0
,

(12.31)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Lambda^{*}(x)=g(\rho_{*}(x))+\bigl(xg^{\prime}(\rho_{*}(x))-1\bigr)\rho_{*}^{\prime}(x)=g(\rho_{*}(x)),

since (
12.28
) implies both
xg^{\prime}(\rho_{*}(x))-1=0
for
0<x\leq x_{1}

and
\rho_{*}^{\prime}(x)=0
for
x\geq x_{1}
.
(In particular, we see that
\Lambda^{*}(x)
is continuously differentiable also at

x=x_{1}
.)
The derivative is thus strictly increasing for
0<x\leq x_{1}
, so
\Lambda^{*}
is
strictly convex on
[0,x_{1}]
, while
\Lambda^{*}(x)
is linear for
x\geq x_{1}
as shown
already in (
12.30
).
Hence, in the terminology of
[
7
, Definition 2.3.3]
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
is an

exposed point
of
\Lambda^{*}
if
0<y<x_{1}
.

We note that (
12.31
) yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Lambda^{*}(x)=0
when
\rho_{*}(x)=0

(since
g(0)=0
), which by (
12.28
) holds if and only if

x=x_{0}:=\psi^{\prime}(1)^{-1}=6/\pi^{2}
;
furthermore,
(
12.29
) shows that
\Lambda^{*}(x_{0})=0
.
Since
\Lambda^{*}
is convex, it thus attains its minimum at

, and the
minimum is 0 (as it is has to be).

The Gärtner–Ellis theorem
[

now implies Theorem

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
10.1

We repeat the statement of Lemma
10.1
.

Lemma
10.1
.

Suppose that
f
is a locally integrable function and
\mu
a measure
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
and that
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\sigma-1}|f(x)|<\infty

and
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
, i.e., the
Mellin transforms
\widetilde{f}(s)
and
\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)
are defined when

\Re s=\sigma
.
Suppose also that the integral
\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s
converges
at least conditionally.
Suppose further that
x^{\sigma}f(x)
is bounded and that
f
is

\mu
-a.e. continuous.
Then

(
10.1
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\sigma-\mathrm{i}\infty}^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)\widetilde{\mu}(1-s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Although we have not found Lemma
10.1
in the literature,
it is closely related to standard versions of Parseval’s formula for Mellin
transforms. These are usually stated for two functions, while our lemma is
stated for a function and a measure; however, for absolutely continuous

\mu
, our lemma reduces to a result for two functions.
In particular, in the case that
\mu
is absolutely continuous, Lemma
10.1

is a special case of the more general
[
22
, Theorem 43]
.
(Note that we have shown in Lemma
6.1
that our measure
\Upsilon
is absolutely
continuous, and as discussed in Section
11.2
, this extends to the measures

\Upsilon_{k}
considered there; however,
one advantage of our use of Lemma
10.1
is that we do not have to verify this
by a special argument.)
For completeness, we give a detailed proof below.

Note also that by a standard change of variables, as in the proof below,
any version of Parseval’s formula for Mellin transforms is equivalent to
a corresponding version of Parseval’s formula (also called Plancherel’s
formula) for the Fourier transform on the real line;
see e.g.
[
22
]
.
(From the point of view of abstract Harmonic analysis, the Mellin transform
is just the Fourier transform on the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{R}_{+},\cdot)

with Haar measure
\mathrm{d}x/x
.)

Proof of Lemma
10.1
.

We use a well-known transformation to the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}
,
which we do in two steps.
First, let
f_{\sigma}
and
\mu_{\sigma}
be the function and measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}

defined by

(A.1)

\displaystyle f_{\sigma}(x):=x^{\sigma}f(x),\qquad\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{-\sigma}(x):=x^{-\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

(i.e., the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mathrm{d}\mu_{-\sigma}/\,\mathrm{d}\mu=x^{-\sigma}
).
Note that
then
\widetilde{f}_{\sigma}(s)=\widetilde{f}(s+\sigma)
and
\widetilde{\mu}_{\sigma}(s)=\widetilde{\mu}(s-\sigma)
,
and that

f_{\sigma}
and
\mu_{\sigma}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
10.1
with

\sigma=0
, and
so we have reduced the lemma to this case.
(In particular,
\mu_{\sigma}
is a finite measure.)
Secondly, we change variables to
y\in\mathbb{R}
by the mapping
y=\log x
;
we let
F(y):=f_{\sigma}(e^{y})
and let
\nu
be the (finite) measure on
\mathbb{R}

corresponding to
\mu_{\sigma}
. Then the assumptions on
f
and
\mu
imply
that
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
F
is bounded,
F
is
\nu
-a.e. continuous,
and that
\nu
is a finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\sigma}(x)x^{\mathrm{i}\tau-1}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(y)e^{\mathrm{i}\tau y}\,\mathrm{d}y=:\widehat{F}(\tau),

the Fourier transform of
F
,
and

(A.3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(1-(\sigma+\mathrm{i}\tau))=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\sigma-\mathrm{i}\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-\mathrm{i}\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\mathrm{i}\tau y}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=:\widehat{\nu}(-\tau).

Hence, Lemma
10.1
follows from (and is equivalent to)
the following lemma for the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
f
is an integrable function on
\mathbb{R}

and that
\nu
is a finite
measure on
\mathbb{R}

such that the integral
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(t)\widehat{\nu}(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t
converges
at least conditionally.
Suppose further that
f
is bounded and
\nu
-a.e. continuous.
Then

(A.4)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(t)\widehat{\nu}(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

We begin by taking
the Féjer kernel on
\mathbb{R}
, for
s>0
,

(A.5)

\displaystyle k_{s}(x):=\frac{1-\cos(sx)}{\pi sx^{2}}=\frac{\sin^{2}(sx/2)}{2\pi s(x/2)^{2}},

which is integrable and
has the Fourier transform

(A.6)

\displaystyle\widehat{k_{s}}(t)=(1-|t|/s)_{+},

see e.g.
[
17
, VI.(1.8), p. 124]
.
Thus the convolution
k_{s}*f
has Fourier transform

(A.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{k_{s}*f}(t)=\widehat{f}(t)\widehat{k_{s}}(t)=(1-|t|/s)_{+}\widehat{f}(t).

The function
\widehat{f}(t)
is bounded, since
f\in L^{1}
, and thus
(
A.7
) shows that
\widehat{k_{s}*f}
is integrable.
Consequently, the Fourier inversion formula applies and yields,
for every
s>0
and a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(A.8)

\displaystyle k_{s}*f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}\widehat{f}(t)(1-|t|/s)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We obtain from (
A.8
)
by Fubini’s theorem (twice), since the double integrals are
absolutely convergent by the assumptions that
f\in L^{1}
and
\nu
is finite,
and thus
\widehat{f}
and
\widehat{\nu}
are bounded,

(A.9)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{s}*f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\mathrm{i}tx}\widehat{f}(t)(1-|t|/s)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-s}^{s}\widehat{\nu}(-t)\widehat{f}(t)(1-|t|/s)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-us}^{us}\widehat{\nu}(-t)\widehat{f}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}u.

The assumption that
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(t)\widehat{\nu}(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t
converges conditionally
means that as
s\to\infty
, the inner integral
on the last line of (
A.9
) tends to

J:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(t)\widehat{\nu}(-t)\,\mathrm{d}t
;
it follows also that
this inner integral is bounded uniformly in
u
and
s
.
Hence, (
A.9
) and dominated convergence show that as
{s\to\infty}
,

(A.10)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{s}*f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}J\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{2\pi}J.

Moreover, since
f
is bounded, it is easily seen (and well-known)
that, as
{s\to\infty}
,

k_{s}*f(x)\to f(x)
for every
x
such that
f
is continuous at
x
;
by assumption this holds for
\nu
-a.e.
x
.
Since furthermore
k_{s}*f
is bounded (by
\sup_{x}|f(x)|
),
it follows by dominated convergence that

(A.11)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{s}*f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\to\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x)\qquad\text{as }{s\to\infty}.

Finally, (
A.4
) follows from (
A.10
) and (
A.11
).
∎

Remark A.2
.

Lemma
A.1
extends, with the same proof, to complex measures
\nu
.

Remark A.3
.

As far as we know, it is unknown
(even in the absolutely continuous case)
whether
(
A.4
) holds for any integrable
f
and finite measure
\nu

such that both integrals in (
A.4
) converge.
