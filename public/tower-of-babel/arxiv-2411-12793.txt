Deep Boundary Perturbations at a Quantum Critical Point
Shang Liu

Abstract
In this work, we explore an unconventional class of problems in the study of (quantum) critical phenomena, termed “deep boundary criticality”. Traditionally, critical systems are analyzed with two types of perturbations: those uniformly distributed throughout the bulk, which can significantly alter the bulk criticality by triggering a nontrivial bulk renormalization group flow, and those confined to a boundary or subdimensional defect, which affect only the boundary or defect condition. Here, we go beyond this paradigm by studying quantum critical systems with boundary perturbations that decay algebraically (following a power law) into the bulk. By continuously varying the decay exponent, such perturbations can transition between having no effect on the bulk and strongly influencing bulk behavior. We investigate this regime using two prototypical models based on (1+1)D massless Dirac fermions. Through a combination of analytical and numerical approaches, we uncover exotic scaling laws in simple observables and observe qualitative changes in model behavior as the decay exponent varies.

\displaystyle{\lambda O(\tau,x)}/{x^{\alpha}},

3
Model Y: Dirac Fermions with a Particle-Hole Symmetric Perturbation

3.1
Unperturbed Theory

The unperturbed theory that we will consider is the (1+1)D massless Dirac fermion theory described by the following Euclidean Lagrangian density.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}=\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(\partial_{\tau}+i\partial_{x})\psi_{+}+\psi^{\dagger}_{-}(\partial_{\tau}-i\partial_{x})\psi_{-},

(7)

where
\psi_{\pm}(\tau,x)
is the left/right-moving complex fermion field.
We place the theory on the half-infinite space manifold
x\geq 0
, and impose the boundary condition

\displaystyle\psi_{+}(\tau,0)+\psi_{-}(\tau,0)=0.

(8)

The Lagrangian density (
7
) and the boundary condition (
8
) both preserve the particle-hole symmetry
\psi_{\pm}\leftrightarrow\psi_{\pm}^{\dagger}
.

Correlation functions in this free fermion theory can be computed using the doubling trick. For all
x\geq 0
, define
\psi_{+}(\tau,-x)=-\psi_{-}(\tau,x)
which respects the boundary condition (
8
). Then, we can map the original nonchiral theory with a spatial boundary to a chiral theory without spatial boundary:

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{x\geq 0}d\tau dx~\mathcal{L}_{0}=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}d\tau dx~\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(\partial_{\tau}+i\partial_{x})\psi_{+}.

(9)

The fermion two-point correlation function in this chiral theory can be obtained from the Schwinger-Dyson equation, and takes the following form.

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{+}(z_{1})\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(z_{2})\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi(z_{1}-z_{2})},

(10)

where the complex coordinate
z=\tau+ix
has been used and
\psi_{+}(z_{i})\equiv\psi_{+}(\tau_{i},x_{i})
.

The field theory (
7
) with the boundary condition (
8
) has a simple lattice regularization which enables numerical simulations. This lattice theory lives on a half-infinite chain with sites labeled by
j=1,2,3,\cdots
. There is a single flavor of complex fermions hopping on this chain, described by the following Hamiltonian.

\displaystyle H_{{\rm latt},0}=t\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+\textrm{h.c.}),

(11)

where
t>0
and
c_{j}
is the fermion annihilation operator on site
j
. Let
a
be the lattice constant, or equivalently, the spatial coordinate of the
j
-th site be
x_{j}=ja
. We take
t=1/(2a)
so that the fermi velocity equals to
1
. The lattice and continuum fermion operators can be identified as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{c_{x/a}}{\sqrt{a}}\simeq e^{ik_{F}x}\psi_{+}(x)+e^{-ik_{F}x}\psi_{-}(x),

(12)

where
k_{F}=\pi/(2a)
is the fermi momentum. For completeness, we derive this lattice-continuum correspondence in Appendix
A
. We observe that this lattice model also has an exact particle-hole symmetry
c_{j}\leftrightarrow(-1)^{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}
which corresponds to the aforementioned particle-hole symmetry in the continuum.

3.2
Deep Boundary Perturbation

The deep boundary perturbation that we will study in this section takes the following form.

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{L}=\frac{\lambda}{x^{\alpha}}m_{Y}(\tau,x),\quad m_{Y}:=-i(\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{-}-\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{+}).

(13)

We may cut off
\Delta\mathcal{L}
at a small distance
\delta
near the boundary, so that the total action takes the form

\displaystyle S=\int_{x\geq 0}d\tau dx~\mathcal{L}_{0}+\int_{x\geq\delta}d\tau dx~\Delta\mathcal{L}.

(14)

This is referred to as Model Y throughout this paper.
Note that
m_{Y}
is a mass operator for the Dirac fermions; that is, the fermions will become massive if
m_{Y}
is uniformly added to the Lagrangian. An alternative mass operator is
m_{X}:=\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{-}+\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{+}
, and in the next section, we will discuss the effect of a deep boundary
m_{X}
perturbation. Under the particle-hole transformation,
(m_{Y},m_{X})\leftrightarrow(m_{Y},-m_{X})
.

The lattice counterpart of
m_{Y}
is as follows.

\displaystyle m_{Y}(x)\simeq\frac{M_{Y,x/a}}{a},

(15)

\displaystyle M_{Y,j}:=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{M}_{Y,j-1/2}+\tilde{M}_{Y,j+1/2}),

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{Y,j+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{j}\left(c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j+1}+c^{\dagger}_{j+1}c_{j}\right).

(17)

See Appendix
A
for the derivation. This correspondence enables us to numerically simulate the perturbed theory. More precisely, we can add the following term to the unperturbed lattice Hamiltonian in (
11
).

\displaystyle\Delta H_{\rm latt}=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda a^{1-\alpha}}{j^{\alpha}}M_{Y,j}.

(18)

Here, the sum over
j
starts from
j=2
because
M_{Y,j}
is only well-defined for
j\geq 2
. In actual numerical computations, we need to consider a finite lattice with
N
sites, the
j
sum is then also constrained by the upper bound
j\leq N-1
. All numerical results presented in this paper have
N=2\times 10^{4}
.

Here comes a technical remark about our numerical simulation. If we wish to precisely reproduce the field theory model (
14
), what we should really do is the following: (1) Choose the summation lower bound in (
18
) to be
j_{0}
and then (2) take the limit
a\rightarrow 0
,
N\rightarrow\infty
,
j_{0}\rightarrow\infty
while fixing
L=Na
and
\delta=j_{0}a
. Moreover, in order to ensure that the unperturbed fermion dispersion is strictly linear, as discussed in Appendix
A
, we need to also (3) manually enforce a fixed cutoff
\Lambda
on the allowed single-particle momenta while taking the aforementioned limit. However, this set of procedures is too complicated to implement in practice, so we have instead fixed
a
,
N
,
j_{0}
and did not impose any other UV cutoff other than the intrinsic lattice regularization. As a result (see Appendix
A.3
), the effective continuum Lagrangian we simulate will also contain many higher derivative terms which are irrelevant by power-counting. We are expecting that those terms will not affect the long-distance physics we care about, especially given that our models are quadratic. The good match between field theory and numerics (to be presented below) also verifies this expectation.

The scaling dimension of
m_{Y}
is
1
. Therefore,
just by naïve power counting, we expect the perturbation to be relevant (irrelevant) for the bulk criticality when
\alpha<1
(
\alpha>1
), and
\alpha=1
is the marginal case. This is reflected in the energy spectrum of the system on a finite space. Let
L
be the length of the space. In the absence of any perturbation, the finite-size energy gap is of the order
1/L
. After we turn on the perturbation, the Dirac fermions acquire a spatially varying mass that is everywhere no less than
\lambda/L^{\alpha}
. Hence, if
\alpha<1
, there should be an energy gap of the order
\lambda/L^{\alpha}
. On the contrary, if
\alpha\geq 1
, we expect the energy gap to be still of the order
1/L
. We have verified these claims numerically.

In the rest of this section, we investigate the effect of the deep boundary perturbation on the mass expectation value
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle

3.3.1
Irrelevant Case:
\alpha>1

In the absence of any perturbation,
m_{Y}
already has a nonzero vacuum expectation value due to the boundary condition we choose. Using the doubling trick discussed in Section
3.1
, one can check that

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi x},

(20)

where the subscript
0
represents the unperturbed theory.

Now if we turn on the deep boundary perturbation (
13
) with
\alpha>1
, since it is irrelevant by power counting, a
naïve expectation will be that
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
approaches
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle_{0}
for large
x
, or more precisely

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle/\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle_{0}=1.

(21)

We have indeed confirmed this result numerically, but the above argument is not reliable for the following reason: Although the perturbation is irrelevant
to the bulk
, it may still have a nontrivial effect on the boundary condition. The boundary condition is actually crucial for determining the expectation value of
m_{Y}
; for example, if we instead took
\psi_{+}+e^{i\gamma}\psi_{-}=0
at the spatial boundary,
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
would become
\cos(\gamma)/(2\pi x)
. Therefore, we need to carefully examine the effect of
\Delta\mathcal{L}
on the boundary condition.

We will take an RG approach, which is nothing but a scale transformation for a free fermion field theory. We write the action as
S=S_{0}+\Delta S
where
S_{0}
is the unperturbed theory and

\displaystyle\Delta S=\int_{x\geq\delta}d\tau dx~\frac{\lambda}{x^{\alpha}}m(\tau,x).

(22)

Here, for later convenience, we allow
m
to represent either
m_{Y}
(as considered here) or
m_{X}
. In fact,
m
can even be a linear combination of these two. To implement a scale transformation, we define

\tau^{\prime}=\tau/b
,
\psi_{\pm}^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime},x^{\prime})=b^{1/2}\psi_{\pm}(\tau,x)

S
in terms of the primed coordinates and fields, and finally remove the primes by renaming, we find that
S_{0}
is invariant, while
\Delta S
changes to

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\prime}=\int_{x\geq\delta/b}d\tau dx~\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{x^{\alpha}}m(\tau,x),

(23)

where
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda b^{1-\alpha}
.
To deal with the change in the short-distance cutoff, we utilize the bulk-to-boundary operator expansion:

\displaystyle m(\tau,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}C_{k}x^{\Delta_{k}-1}\mathcal{O}_{k}(\tau),

(24)

where
\{\mathcal{O}_{k}\}
is a set of independent local operators living on the boundary with scaling dimensions
\{\Delta_{k}\}
. We assume that (1)
\mathcal{O}_{0}=1

\mathcal{O}_{1}(\tau)=\,:\psi_{+}^{\dagger}\psi_{+}:(\tau,0)

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(z_{1})\psi_{+}(z_{2})=\,:\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(z_{1})\psi_{+}(z_{2}):+\frac{1}{2\pi(z_{1}-z_{2})},

(25)

and (3)
\Delta_{k\geq 2}>1
. Applying this expansion to the region

, we get

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{x\geq\delta}d\tau dx~\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{x^{\alpha}}m(\tau,x)+\sum_{k}C_{k}\int d\tau~\lambda^{\prime}\mathcal{O}_{k}(\tau)\left[\frac{\delta^{\Delta_{k}-\alpha}-(\delta/b)^{\Delta_{k}-\alpha}}{\Delta_{k}-\alpha}\right].

(26)

In the special case where
\Delta_{k}=\alpha
, the term in square bracket should be replaced by
\log b
. Now we take the

\alpha>1
. One can check that

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\prime}\rightarrow\frac{C_{1}\lambda\delta^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}\int d\tau~\mathcal{O}_{1}(\tau)+\text{const}.

(27)

We observe that only an exactly marginal boundary perturbation is left. Next, we need to understand the implication on the mass expectation value. For convenience, we use an enriched notation
\left\langle m(x)\right\rangle[S,\delta^{\prime}]
to denote the vacuum expectation value of
m(x)
under the action
S
and the UV cutoff
\delta^{\prime}
at the operator insertion point

. Note that
\delta^{\prime}
is a new parameter, not to be confused with
\delta/b
.
The necessity of
\delta^{\prime}
can be seen, for example, by perturbatively computing

. Our RG analysis implies that

\displaystyle\left\langle m(x)\right\rangle[S_{0}+\Delta S,\delta^{\prime}]=b^{-1}\left\langle m(x/b)\right\rangle[S_{0}+\Delta S^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}/b].

(28)

We choose a
b
such that

is satisfied
5
5

5

If

, the bulk-to-boundary operator expansion may no longer be valid.
. Now, if we can ignore

In the case of
m=m_{Y}
, one can check using the doubling trick that
C_{1}=0
. Hence the deep boundary perturbation indeed has no effect at long distance, and we can expect
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
to approach
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle_{0}
. On the other hand, when
m=m_{X}
,
C_{1}\neq 0
and we will see the consequence in the next section.

3.3.2
Marginal Case:
\alpha=1

When
\alpha=1
, the deep boundary perturbation is marginal to the bulk, and starts to have a nontrivial effect.
Given that the full Lagrangian is still quadratic, i.e. the perturbed theory is still a free fermion theory, we can try to exactly compute
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
from single-particle wave functions.
In the following, we will present some key steps of this calculation and leave further details to Appendix
B
.
Let
\phi(x)=(\phi_{+}(x),\phi_{-}(x))^{T}
be a single-particle energy eigenstate wave function with energy
E
.
\phi
satisfies the following time-independent Schrödinger equation.

\displaystyle[i\sigma_{z}\partial_{x}+f(x)\sigma_{y}]\phi=E\phi,

(29)

where
f(x)
is the mass profile:
f(x)=\lambda/x
for
x>\delta
and
f(x)=0
for
0<x<\delta
.
\phi
is also subject to the boundary condition
\phi_{+}(0)+\phi_{-}(0)=0
by Eq.
8
.
This differential equation happens to be solvable. Let

\displaystyle u(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\phi(x).

(30)

The general solution of
u(x)
for
x>\delta
and generic
\lambda
(
\lambda\notin\mathbb{Z}+1/2
) is

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=Au_{1}+Bu_{2},

(31)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{-\pi Ex}\begin{pmatrix}J_{\lambda-1/2}(-Ex)\\
iJ_{\lambda+1/2}(-Ex)\end{pmatrix},

(32)

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{-\pi Ex}\begin{pmatrix}J_{-(\lambda-1/2)}(-Ex)\\
-iJ_{-(\lambda+1/2)}(-Ex)\end{pmatrix},

(33)

where
J_{\nu}(z)
is the standard Bessel function. The coefficients
A
and
B
, possibly depending on the energy
E
, need to be determined by the boundary condition and the normalization condition
\int dx~u^{\dagger}_{E}(x)u_{E^{\prime}}(x)=2\pi\delta(E-E^{\prime})
where the subscripts indicate energy dependence. Details of this analysis are given in Appendix
B
, and we found that
in the
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit
, the coefficients take a very simple form:

\displaystyle(A,B)=\begin{cases}(0,1)&(\lambda<1/2)\\
(1,0)&(\lambda>1/2)\end{cases}.

(34)

The
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit is supposed to be the IR fixed point of our theory, which can be seen by applying scale transformations to the action. Therefore, the leading long-wavelength physics should be captured by this simplifying limit. The case of nonzero
\delta
will be discussed later in this section. We can try to compute
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
with the above solution by integrating over the contributions of all single-particle states with
E<0
. A UV cutoff is needed to regularize the divergent integral. As a result, we have found that at large
x
,

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle\sim\frac{-\lambda\log(x)}{\pi x}

(35)

for both
\lambda<1/2
and
\lambda>1/2
. This is a special case of our previous claim (
19
).

The
\log(x)
factor in the above result is interesting and somewhat surprising. If we naïvely perform scale transformations to the path integral expression of
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
, without worrying about any UV cutoff, we would conclude that
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle\propto 1/x
which is incorrect. To get a clue about the subtle effect of UV cutoff, we may try to compute
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
by a perturbative expansion in
\lambda
, again in the
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit. The result turns out to be

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi x}-\frac{\lambda\log(2x/\delta^{\prime})}{\pi x}+O(\lambda^{2}).

(36)

The logarithm appears at the first order due to the divergent integral

\displaystyle\int d\tau dy\left\langle m_{Y}(\tau,y)m_{Y}(0,x)\right\rangle_{0,{\rm c}},

(37)

where the subscript
{\rm c}
stands for connected correlation function, and
\delta^{\prime}
is a short-distance cutoff as the two
m_{Y}
insertions approach.
This perturbation theory result already suggests that at large
x
,
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
may decay slower than
1/x
. We note that in this special case, the first-order perturbation theory happens to reproduce the exact asymptotic result, but
a priori
, we lack a strong reason to trust the perturbation theory even for small values of
\lambda
. For instance, a term proportional to
\lambda^{2}\log^{2}(x)/x
might emerge at second order and dominate for large
x
, or the entire series might resum into a term proportional to
1/x^{1+2\lambda}
. Furthermore, the perturbation series may fail to converge, as is likely the case for
\alpha<1
, which will be discussed below.

We have seen that the expectation value of the “bare” mass operator
m_{Y}(x)
contains UV divergence at the first order of the perturbation theory. One may wonder whether we can introduce some counter term to cancel the divergence and perhaps use RG to resum some higher order corrections as in conventional renormalized perturbation theories. An attempt to address this question is given in Appendix
C
.

As a side comment, by analyzing the near-zero-energy behavior of the single-particle wave functions, we can also extract information about the boundary operator spectrum or temporal correlation functions. Since this is not the main focus of our work, we leave a more detailed discussion of this matter to the last paragraph of Appendix
B.1.2
. The result is that for
\lambda<1/2
and
\lambda>1/2
, there should exist a boundary operator with the scaling dimension
n(1/2-\lambda)
and
n(1/2+\lambda)
, respectively, for any positive integer
n
. Something exotic must happen when
\lambda=1/2
and we leave a careful study of this point to future works.

3.3.3
Relevant Case:
\alpha<1

For
\alpha<1
, unfortunately, we are unable to solve the single-particle Schrödinger equation. Nonetheless, we may try directly extracting useful information about the observable
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
that we care about. To this end, we define two observables at the single-particle level:

\displaystyle F(E;x)

\displaystyle:=\phi^{\dagger}_{E}(x)\sigma_{y}\phi_{E}(x),

(38)

\displaystyle G(E;x)

\displaystyle:=\phi^{\dagger}_{E}(x)\sigma_{x}\phi_{E}(x),

(39)

where
\phi_{E}(x)
is the single-particle energy eigenstate wave function with eigenvalue

is nothing but an integral of

over

and

\displaystyle=2EG,

\displaystyle=-2EF+2f|\phi|^{2},

To make progress, let us consider the regime where
|E|
is much larger than
|f(x)|
. In this case, we expect the effect of

to be negligible, and thus

\displaystyle\phi\approx\begin{pmatrix}C_{+}(E)e^{-iEx}\\
C_{-}(E)e^{iEx}\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

The conservation of probability current

We propose that the many-body observable
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
can be computed as follows.

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle\approx\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dE}{2\pi}~F_{\rm approx}(E;x)(e^{E/\Lambda}-e^{E/E0}),

(45)

where

\displaystyle x^{\alpha}\mathcal{I}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dz}{2\pi}~

\displaystyle C(z/x^{\alpha})\cos[2zx^{1-\alpha}-\theta(z/x^{\alpha})][e^{z/(\Lambda x^{\alpha})}-e^{z/(c|\lambda|)}].

We thus expect

which is exactly the result we claimed at the beginning of this section.

It may be interesting to compare with the result from the perturbative expansion in

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi x}-\frac{\lambda}{\pi x^{\alpha}}[\log(x)+{\rm const}]+O(\lambda^{2}).

(49)

We see that the first-order term correctly predicts the

Mass Expectation Value: Higher-Order Corrections for
\alpha=1

For simplicity, we restrict our discussion to

3.5
Topological Edge State

There is an interesting connection to SPT physics which we shall briefly discuss. If we gap out the Dirac fermion theory by adding the uniform mass term
\lambda m_{Y}
to the Lagrangian density (namely
\alpha=0
), the two signs of
\lambda
correspond to distinct SPT phases protected by the particle-hole symmetry and the charge U(1) symmetry. For the boundary condition we choose, the
\lambda>0
phase has an edge zero mode while the
\lambda<0
phase does not. We wonder what is the fate of this topological edge mode in the presence of the symmetric deep boundary perturbation, i.e. in Model Y.

The
E=0
energy eigenstate is solvable for arbitrary
\alpha
and
\lambda
. After incorporating the boundary condition, we have found that

\displaystyle u_{E=0}\propto\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{1-\alpha}x^{1-\alpha}\right)\end{pmatrix}\quad(\alpha\neq 1).

(51)

For
\alpha=1
, we have instead
u_{E=0}\propto(0,x^{-\lambda})^{T}
. We observe that for all
\alpha<1
, including
\alpha=0
, the solution is normalizable (unnormalizable) for
\lambda>0
(
\lambda\leq 0
), and hence a zero-energy bound state exists (does not exist). However, for
\alpha>1
, the solution is not normalizable regardless of the sign of
\lambda
. The
\alpha=1
case is most interesting: There is a valid bound state for
\lambda>1/2
but not for
\lambda\leq 1/2
. See Appendix
B.1.2
for details.

Notice that
u_{E=0}
has no contribution to
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
because it has a vanishing component. Therefore, whether or not the bound state is occupied does not affect our previous results.

Similar to Model Y, there are interesting higher-order corrections as well. Focusing on
0<\beta:=|2\lambda|<1
, we have found that
\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle
contains a
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling term, and it is the most significant component other than the
\log(x)/x
- and
1/x
-scaling terms. To test this term is not easy. In Fig.
5
, we plot the lattice version of
x\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle+(\lambda/\pi)\log(x)
against
x^{-\beta}
for two values of
\lambda
, expecting straight lines. The blue dots are computed using the lattice model in Eq.
56
, and we see that the linearity is good for
\lambda=0.1
but not satisfactory for
\lambda=0.3
. We then tried terminating the lattice perturbation at a larger value of
r
(increasing
r_{\rm min}
in Eq.
56
), effectively increasing
\delta
. This can enhance the
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling term because it has a
\delta^{\beta}
factor, potentially suppressing
\delta
-independent finite-size effect. However, this will also enhance the
1/x^{1+2\beta}
-scaling term more significantly, potentially bending the curve. The result turns out to be good here, as shown by the red dots; the linearity looks much better. On the other hand, the same trick does not seem to help for the previous Model Y case.

It is interesting to compare again with the result from perturbation theory:

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle=-\frac{\lambda}{\pi x}[\log(2x/\delta^{\prime})+\log(x/\delta)]+O(\lambda^{2}),

(70)

where
\delta^{\prime}
is a short-distance cutoff near the operator insertion point and
\delta
is the cutoff near the boundary. The first logarithm leads to the correct
\log(x)/x
-scaling term, while the second logarithm is resummed into those terms with
\lambda
-dependent exponents such as the
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling term. Therefore, unlike in Model Y, we would not be able to correctly guess the leading asymptote if we only had the first-order perturbation theory.

4.2.3
Relevant Case:
\alpha<1

Finally, for
\alpha<1
, in terms of the tilded fields
\tilde{\psi}_{\pm}
, the same derivation as that in Model Y can be carried over. The derivation is insensitive to the boundary condition, and we get the same result:

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle\sim\frac{-\alpha\lambda\log(x)}{\pi x^{\alpha}}.

(71)

A numerical test is shown in Fig.
3
a and we find good agreement.

5
Conclusion

In summary, we have investigated the effects of deep boundary perturbations in the (1+1)D massless Dirac fermion theory, uncovering nontrivial behaviors as the decay exponent and coupling constant vary. Through this study, we aim to bring attention to an unconventional class of problems within (quantum) critical phenomena. We hope that our findings will inspire further research and deepen our understanding of boundary effects in critical systems. We suggest a few possible directions for future exploration below.

•

There are remaining interesting questions for the two models studied in this work. For example, how do more general correction functions behave?
It is also natural to explore the properties of ground state entanglement entropy.

•

One can analyze many other models with deep boundary/defect perturbations, especially interacting systems, such as (1+1)D Ising CFT perturbed by a deep boundary magnetic field that explicitly break the Ising
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.

•

Another interesting possibility is to turn on multiple deep boundary perturbations and study the interplay between them.

As a final remark, the type of quantum systems studied here may be experimentally realized using superconducting qubits or other quantum simulation platforms. Exploring this possibility is meaningful, as generic strongly interacting critical systems with deep boundary perturbations are difficult to solve exactly using conventional analytical and numerical approaches.

Lattice Regularization of Dirac Fermions

A.1
Periodic Boundary Condition

Before considering any boundary effect, let us first try to understand the continuum limit of the model on a closed chain with perodic boundary condition. Recall the Hamiltonian takes the following form,

\displaystyle H_{{\rm latt},0}=t\sum_{j}(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+\textrm{h.c.})\quad(t>0).

(72)

In the case of periodic boundary condition, we identify
j
with
j+N
where
N
is the total number of sites. This Hamiltonian can be easily diagonalized by Fourier transform:

\displaystyle c_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j}e^{-ikx_{j}}c_{j}~\Leftrightarrow~c_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}e^{ikx_{j}}c_{k},

(73)

where
x_{j}=ja
,
a
is the lattice constant,
k\in(\Delta k)\mathbb{Z}
,
\Delta k=2\pi/(Na)
, and
k
is identified with
k+2\pi/a
. Be warned that we are somewhat lazy about the notations: We simply use the subscripts (
j
or
k
) to distinguish operators in the real and momentum spaces.
In terms of the Fourier modes,

\displaystyle H_{{\rm latt},0}=2t\sum_{k}\cos(ka)c^{\dagger}_{k}c_{k}.

(74)

We define the vacuum state as the (many-body) state with all negative-energy modes occupied. Given that
t>0
, the unoccupied modes are in the range
-k_{F}\leq k\leq k_{F}
, where
k_{F}=\pi/(2a)
. The Fermi velocity is
v_{F}=2ta
.

The continuum limit more precisely means the following:
N\rightarrow\infty
,
a\rightarrow 0
,
t\rightarrow\infty
, while fixing
L=Na
that defines the length of the space manifold, and fixing
v_{F}=2ta
.
In order to take this limit, we introduce fermion field operators
\psi_{\pm}(k)
in the momentum space as follows:

\displaystyle c_{\pm k_{F}+k}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\Delta k}{2\pi}}\psi_{\pm}(k)\quad\text{for}\quad|k|<\Lambda\ll k_{F}.

(75)

Note that a cutoff scale
\Lambda
has been introduced. We will fix the value of
\Lambda
when taking the continuum limit
8
8

8

One may further take the
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
limit on top of this continuum limit.
, and it is assumed that only fermion modes closed to
k=\pm k_{F}
are important for the physical quantities that we are interested in. The normalization factor is chosen to reproduce the conventional anticommutation relations as
L\rightarrow\infty
(
\Delta k=2\pi/L\rightarrow 0
), e.g.
\{\psi_{+}(k_{1}),\psi^{\dagger}_{+}(k_{2})\}=(2\pi/\Delta k)\delta_{k_{1},k_{2}}\rightarrow 2\pi\delta(k_{1}-k_{2})
. We further define the real-space fermion field operators
\psi_{\pm}(x)
as follows:

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(x)=\sum_{k,|k|<\Lambda}\frac{\Delta k}{2\pi}e^{ikx}\psi_{\pm}(k)\overset{L\rightarrow\infty}{=}\int_{-\Lambda}^{\Lambda}\frac{dk}{2\pi}~e^{ikx}\psi_{\pm}(k),

(76)

which implies, in the continuum limit,

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(k)=\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}dx~e^{-ikx}\psi_{\pm}(x).

(77)

We can now also establish the lattice-continuum correspondence between real-space operators. Using the Fourier expansion of
c_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle c_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}e^{ikx_{j}}c_{k}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k,|k|<\Lambda}\left(e^{i(k_{F}+k)x_{j}}c_{k_{F}+k}+e^{i(-k_{F}+k)x_{j}}c_{-k_{F}+k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{a}\sum_{k,|k|<\Lambda}\frac{\Delta k}{2\pi}~e^{ikx_{j}}\left(e^{ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{+}(k)+e^{-ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{-}(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{a}\left(e^{ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{+}(x_{j})+e^{-ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{-}(x_{j})\right),

(78)

which is nothing but (
12
) in the main text.

We can now derive the continuum version of the Hamiltonian. One possible approach is to plug the relation (
78
) into the real-space lattice Hamiltonian. After dropping the rapidly oscillating terms and doing Taylor expansions, we arrive at

\displaystyle H_{0}=v_{F}\int dx~\left(i\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\partial_{x}\psi_{+}-i\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\partial_{x}\psi_{-}\right).

(79)

After setting
v_{F}=1
, this Hamiltonian corresponds to the standard Dirac Lagrangian (
7
) in the main text. One can also check that when
N\in 4\mathbb{Z}
, the continuum boundary condition is periodic,
\psi_{\pm}(x+L)=\psi_{\pm}(x)
, while when
N\in 4\mathbb{Z}+2
, the continuum boundary condition becomes antiperiodic,
\psi_{\pm}(x+L)=-\psi_{\pm}(x)
.

We can use (
78
) to derive the lattice-continuum correspondence between other operators as well. For example,

\displaystyle(-1)^{j}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j}/a\simeq

\displaystyle\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{-}+\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{+}\right)(x_{j})+e^{2ik_{F}x_{j}}\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{+}+\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{-}\right)(x_{j}).

(80)

Note that the second term on the right-hand side contains an oscillatory factor
e^{2ik_{F}x_{j}}=(-1)^{j}
. Such a term can be removed if we average over two sites, or smear the operator with any function that is smooth in the continuum limit. Therefore, if we define

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{X,j}

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{j}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j},

(81)

\displaystyle m_{X}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{-}+\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{+}\right)(x),

(82)

then

\displaystyle M_{X,j+1/2}:=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{M}_{X,j}+\tilde{M}_{X,j+1})\simeq am_{X}(x_{j+1/2}).

(83)

There is actually an implicit subtlety in the above derivation. When we derive (
78
), we have applied a projection
\mathcal{P}
onto the low-energy subspace defined by
\Lambda
. In other words, (
78
) should be more precisely written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}c_{j}\mathcal{P}=\sqrt{a}\left(e^{ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{+}(x_{j})+e^{-ik_{F}x_{j}}\psi_{-}(x_{j})\right).

(84)

When we computed the continuum limit of fermion bilinear operators such as the Hamiltonian or
\tilde{M}_{X,j}
, we should have applied the projection
\mathcal{P}
on the whole operator, instead of on each individual fermion creation/annihilation operator which is what we have done. The net effect is that our results above may be off by (possibly divergent) additive constants. Fortunately, this subtlety does not cause any issue. The Hamiltonian is fine because we do not care about any additive constant there. For (
83
), there is no additive constant because both the lattice and continuum sides have vanishing expectation values on a closed chain: The lattice side can be seen from the lattice translation symmetry, while the continuum side can be seen from the chiral U(1) symmetry whose partial origin is the lattice translation. We assume that the lattice-continuum correspondence of local operators does not depend on the boundary condition.

Similarly, if we define

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{Y,j+1/2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{j}\left(c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j+1}+c^{\dagger}_{j+1}c_{j}\right),

(85)

\displaystyle m_{Y}(x)

\displaystyle:=-i\left(\psi^{\dagger}_{+}\psi_{-}-\psi^{\dagger}_{-}\psi_{+}\right)(x),

(86)

then one can check that

\displaystyle M_{Y,j}:=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{M}_{Y,j-1/2}+\tilde{M}_{Y,j+1/2})\simeq am_{Y}(x_{j}).

(87)

A.2
Open Boundary Condition

Now suppose the lattice model lives on an open chain with sites labeled by

The exact single-particle energy eigenstates have the following wave functions:

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(x_{j})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}}\sin(kx_{j}),\quad\frac{k(N+1)a}{\pi}=1,2,\cdots,N,

(88)

where
x_{j}=ja
as before, and the energy eigenvalues are again
\epsilon_{k}=2t\cos(ka)
.
These wave functions are nothing but standing waves with nodes at the nonexisting sites

and

Given that the single-particle wave functions vanish at

\displaystyle\psi_{+}(0)+\psi_{-}(0)=0,

(89)

as claimed in the main text. For the right end or

\displaystyle\psi_{+}(L)+(-1)^{N+1}\psi_{-}(L)=0,

(90)

where

. As a sanity check, let us compare the many-body ground state degeneracies of the lattice and continuum models. When

A.3
Comment about our numerical approach

Thus far, we have introduced a precise continuum limit of a lattice model which exactly reproduces the field theory defined in the main text (with a hard momentum cutoff
\Lambda
). In our numerical calculations, however, we did not take any
a\rightarrow 0
limit, or impose any Hilbert space cutoff
\Lambda
, then how should we interpret these numerical results?

There is actually an alternative way of thinking about the lattice continuum correspondence. Without taking the
a\rightarrow 0
limit or imposing the
\Lambda
cutoff on the allowed momenta, the momentum integration in Eq.
76
will be from
-\pi/(2a)
to
\pi/(2a)

78
still holds, but the expressions for the Hamiltonian

as well as the mass operators

and
M_{Y}
will contain higher derivative terms accompanied by higher powers of the lattice constant

. We expect that these higher derivative terms do not affect the long-distance behavior of our models, as also verifid by the agreement between numerics and our field theoretic predictions.

B.1.2

One may wonder whether there are

We may directly solve Eq.

\displaystyle u_{E=0}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{+}x^{\lambda}\\

The boundary condition (either

\displaystyle\lim_{E\rightarrow 0}(-E)^{\lambda}u_{2,E<0}(x)=-\frac{i\sqrt{\pi}2^{\lambda+1/2}}{\Gamma\left(1/2-\lambda\right)}\begin{pmatrix}0\\

1/2
which is the interesting special value. More precisely, we consider either
0<\lambda<1/2
or
1/2<\lambda<1
.
Let
\zeta=-E\delta>0
. We have

\displaystyle\chi=\zeta^{2\lambda-1}[M_{0}+O(\zeta^{2})],\quad M_{0}=\frac{-2^{-2\lambda}\Gamma(3/2-\lambda)}{\lambda\Gamma(1/2+\lambda)}.

(114)

The leading-order terms in
A
and
B
for small
\zeta
are the following:

\displaystyle 0<\lambda<1/2:~\begin{cases}A\approx M_{0}^{-1}\zeta^{1-2\lambda}&\\
B\approx 1-M_{0}^{-1}\sin(\pi\lambda)\zeta^{1-2\lambda}&\end{cases},

(115)

\displaystyle 1/2<\lambda<1:~\begin{cases}A\approx 1-M_{0}\sin(\pi\lambda)\zeta^{2\lambda-1}&\\
B\approx M_{0}\zeta^{2\lambda-1}&\end{cases},

(116)

where an unimportant overall sign has been dropped in the first case.
Recall that

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dE}{2\pi}~e^{E/\Lambda}(-u^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u)_{E}(x).

(117)

u^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u
can now be expanded as

\displaystyle A^{2}u_{1}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{1}+AB(u_{1}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{2}+u_{2}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{1})+B^{2}u_{2}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{2}.

(118)

The
O(\zeta^{0})
terms of the
A,B
coefficients lead to our previous result of
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
in the
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit. Our new task now is to analyze the corrections due to nonzero
\zeta
.

As an example, let us focus on the middle (cross) term in Eq.
118
. It will contribute the following term to the expectation value of
m_{Y}
:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}{dE}~e^{E/\Lambda}\zeta^{\beta}f(z),

(119)

where
z:=-Ex
,
\beta=|2\lambda-1|\in(0,1)
, and

\displaystyle f(z)=-(2\pi)^{-1}M_{0}^{\sign(2\lambda-1)}(u_{1}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{2}+u_{2}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{1}).

(120)

By a change of variable, this integral becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{\beta}}{x^{1+\beta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\beta}f(z)e^{-z/(\Lambda x)}dz.

(121)

If this integral is convergent in the
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
limit, then the result will consist of a
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling term plus faster-decaying corrections due to finite
\Lambda
. However, the integral may actually contain UV divergence, so a more careful analysis is needed. We first note that at small
z
,
(u_{1}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{2}+u_{2}^{\dagger}\sigma_{y}u_{1})
scales as
z^{0}
, hence the integral has no IR divergence. In fact, one may similarly check that the whole integral for
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
has no IR divergence. Thererfore, we may split the above integral by
\int_{0}^{\infty}=\int_{0}^{1}+\int_{1}^{\infty}
. The integral for
0<z<1
is finite and gives a
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling term plus higher corrections. To analyze the integral in the range
1<z<\infty
, we may expand
f(z)
for large
z
. Using the known asymptotic expansion of Bessel functions (see Eq.
104
which is already enough for our purpose), we observe that the large-
z
expansion of
f(z)
only contains the following two types of terms: (1)
{\sin(2z+\theta)}/{z^{m}}
for
m=0,1,2,\cdots
, and (2)
{1}/{z^{n}}
for
n=1,2,3,\cdots
; note the absence of
n=0
. The same actually holds for all three components of Eq.
118
. The first type of terms will not lead to any divergence. In fact,
\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0+}\int_{1}^{\infty}dz~z^{\gamma}\sin(2z+\theta)e^{-\eta z}
is finite for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, the second type of terms may lead to divegence if
n-\beta\leq 1
which is actually only possible for
n=1
since
0<\beta<1
. In this case, the result consists of
1/x
-scaling,
1/x^{1+\beta}
-scaling, and faster-decaying terms.

Next, we shall analyze other corrections from nonzero
\zeta
, and the same line of reasoning as above can essentially be carried over. In general, those corrections come from integrals of the following form:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}{dE}~e^{E/\Lambda}\zeta^{\Delta}g(z),

(122)

where
\Delta\geq\beta
. There is no IR divergence, so the integral can be split as before. The large
z
behavior of
g(z)
is also similar: There can only be (1)
{\sin(2z+\theta)}/{z^{m}}
for integer
m\geq 0
, and (2)
{1}/{z^{n}}
for positive integer
n\geq 1
. The result will consist of
1/x^{1+\Delta}
-scaling or faster-decaying terms, as well as
1/x^{n}
-scaling terms with positive integer
n\geq 1
. For special values of
\Delta
such that
n-\Delta=1
for some
n
, there can also be terms scaling as
\log(x)/x^{1+\Delta}=\log(x)/x^{n}
, but this is only possible for
n\geq 2
.

We also note that the zero-energy bound state found in Section
B.1.2
has no contribution to
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
.

To summarize, with all these efforts, we have found that for
\lambda\in(0,1/2)\cup(1/2,1)
,
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle
should contain a term that scales as
1/x^{1+\beta}
(
\beta=|2\lambda-1|
) at large
x
unless some magical cancellation happens. Moreover, this is the most significant component other than the
\log(x)/x
- and
1/x
-scaling terms. There also exist higher-order terms such as one that scales as
1/x^{1+2\beta}
.

B.2
Model X

For Model X, it is convenient to define

\displaystyle(\psi_{+},\psi_{-})=(\tilde{\psi}_{+},-i\tilde{\psi}_{-}),

(123)

namely a chiral transformation. We then have

\displaystyle H_{0}=\tilde{H}_{0},\quad m_{X}=\tilde{m}_{Y}.

(124)

Therefore, in terms of the tilded fields, the Model X Hamiltonian takes the same form as that in Model Y, but the boundary condition becomes
\tilde{\psi}_{+}(0)-i\tilde{\psi}_{-}(0)=0
.

For generic
\lambda
, the wave function
\tilde{u}
has the same form as that in Eq.
98
, with coefficients
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{B}
. If we turn off the deep boundary perturbation in the region
0<x<\delta
, the boundary condition dictates that

\displaystyle b_{X}:=

\displaystyle\cos(E\delta)e^{-i\pi/4}(\tilde{u}_{+}+i\tilde{u}_{-})(\delta)+i\sin(E\delta)e^{i\pi/4}(\tilde{u}_{+}-i\tilde{u}_{-})(\delta)=0.

(125)

Analogous to
b_{Y}^{\prime}
introduced previously, we may also define an alternative boundary condition

\displaystyle b_{X}^{\prime}:=e^{-i\pi/4}(\tilde{u}_{+}+i\tilde{u}_{-})(\delta)=0,

(126)

which is equivalent to terminating the space at
x=\delta
. We are interested in universal properties of the system that does not depend on the choice between
b_{X}
and
b_{X}^{\prime}
.

One can check that at small
x
,
b_{X,1}\sim x^{\lambda}
and
b_{X,2}\sim x^{-\lambda}
, where nonzero factors have been omitted. In fact, the same holds for
b_{X,1}^{\prime}
and
b_{X,2}^{\prime}
as well. Hence, in the
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit, we have
\tilde{u}=u_{1}
for
\lambda>0
and
\tilde{u}=u_{2}
for
\lambda<0
. Computing the mass expectation value gives

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle=\left\langle\tilde{m}_{Y}(x)\right\rangle\sim\frac{-\lambda\log(x)}{\pi x},

(127)

same as in Model Y.

Next, let us consider the case
\delta>0
. From the small-
x
expansions of
b_{X,1}
and
b_{X,2}
, we know that
\tilde{\chi}:=\tilde{B}/\tilde{A}=\tilde{M}_{0}\zeta^{2\lambda}+\cdots
for small
\zeta=-E\delta
and some nonzero
\tilde{M}_{0}
. The same analysis as that for Model Y suggests the existence of a
1/x^{|2\lambda|}
-scaling term in
\left\langle m_{X}(x)\right\rangle
. When
0<|2\lambda|<1
, this is the most significant component other than the
\log(x)/x
- and
1/x
-scaling terms.

What about zero-energy states? Demanding either
b_{X}=0
or
b_{X}^{\prime}=0
which are the same for
E=0
, the unnormalized zero-energy solution takes the following form,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{E=0}=\begin{pmatrix}(x/\delta)^{\lambda}\\
i(x/\delta)^{-\lambda}\end{pmatrix}.

(128)

This is never normalizable. Moreover, it is a limit of the
E<0
wave function
\tilde{u}_{E<0}
; this is simplest to check in the
\delta\rightarrow 0
limit but holds more generally. Therefore, we believe there is no independent
E=0
solution.

Appendix C
Counter Term for the Observable

In the main text, we have briefly touched on the perturbative expansion of the mass expectation value, where the bare mass operator has been used. One may wonder whether we can define a renormalized operator to remove the UV divergence and whether this could help resum certain higher order corrections by integrating some RG equation. This section serves as an attempt to explore this question.

We will focus on Model Y with
\alpha=1
.
Since we have a free fermion field theory, it appears reasonable to assume that the renormalization of the action is equivalent to dimension analysis, i.e. the RG equation for
\lambda
is trivial, though we do not have a rigorous proof.
However, although the renormalization of the action is trivial, composite operators inserted into correlation functions may still have nontrivial renormalization.
Recall that perturbation theory gives

\displaystyle\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi x}-\frac{\lambda\log(\Lambda x)}{\pi x}+O(\lambda^{2}),

(129)

where
\Lambda
is some UV cutoff
10
10

10

The
\Lambda
here is not necessarily equal to other uses of the same symbol in this paper.
. The UV divergence at the first order needs to be canceled by some counter term. Here comes two choices:

1.

We can define an
additive
counter term such that the renomalized operator
m_{Y,R}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle m_{Y,R}(x)=m_{Y}(x)+\delta_{m},\quad\delta_{m}=\frac{\lambda}{\pi x}\log(\Lambda/\mu)+\cdots

(130)

where
\mu
is the renomalization scale. In this case, we are actually not sure whether the term “renormalization” is appropriate, as the effect of
\delta_{m}
is more similar to normal ordering, but we will use this term anyway.

2.

Alternatively, we may define a
multiplicative
renomalization factor such that

\displaystyle m_{Y,R}(x)=m_{Y}(x)Z_{m},\quad Z_{m}=1+2\lambda\log(\Lambda/\mu)+\cdots

(131)

We will try to resum higher order corrections using both approaches to renormalization and compare the results with the exact answer.

Let us start with the second approach, namely that with a multiplicative renormalization factor. Given that the bare operator has no dependence on the renomalization scale
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{d\log\mu}\log\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\log\mu}\log[Z_{m}^{-1}\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle]

(132)

\displaystyle=2\lambda+\frac{d}{d\log\mu}\log\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle+O(\lambda^{2}).

(133)

Let us now integrate
\mu
from the low-energy scale
x^{-1}
all the way to
\Lambda
. This gives

\displaystyle\log\left[\frac{\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle_{\Lambda}}{\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle_{x^{-1}}}\right]=-2\lambda\log\left(\Lambda x\right)+O(\lambda^{2}).

(134)

We know that
\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle_{x^{-1}}=w(\lambda,\Lambda x)/x
for some dimensionless function
w
that is finite in the
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
limit. Moreover, when
\mu
is set to be
\Lambda
, the renormalization factor
Z_{m}
evaluates to a dimensionless function of
\lambda
, denoted as
z(\lambda)
. We then have

\displaystyle z(\lambda)\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{w(\lambda,\Lambda x)}{x^{1+2\lambda}\Lambda^{2\lambda}}[1+O(\lambda^{2})].

(135)

If ignoring the higher order corrections, we have found that
\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle\propto 1/x^{1+2\lambda}
at large
x
, and this is inconsistent with our exact solution.

Next, consider the first approach where the counter term is additive. We have

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{d\log\mu}\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{d}{d\log\mu}\left\langle m_{Y,R}(x)\right\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{\pi x}+O(\lambda^{2}).

(136)

Again integrating over
\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle z(\lambda)\left\langle m_{Y}(x)\right\rangle=\frac{w(\lambda,\Lambda x)}{x}-\frac{\lambda}{\pi x}\log(\Lambda x)+O(\lambda^{2}).

(137)

The result has nothing new, but is indeed consistent with our exact solution.
