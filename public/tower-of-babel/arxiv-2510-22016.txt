Cost-Sensitive Evaluation for Binary Classifiers
Pierangelo Lombardo, Antonio Casoli, Cristian Cingolani, Shola Oshodi, Michele Zanatta

Abstract
Selecting an appropriate evaluation metric for classifiers is crucial for model comparison and parameter optimization, yet there is not consensus on a universally accepted metric that serves as a definitive standard. Moreover, there is often a misconception about the perceived need to mitigate imbalance in datasets used to train classification models. Since the final goal in classifier optimization is typically maximizing the return of investment or, equivalently, minimizing the Total Classification Cost (TCC), we define Weighted Accuracy (WA), an evaluation metric for binary classifiers with a straightforward interpretation as a weighted version of the well-known accuracy metric, coherent with the need of minimizing TCC. We clarify the conceptual framework for handling class imbalance in cost-sensitive scenarios, providing an alternative to rebalancing techniques. This framework can be applied to any metric that, like WA, can be expressed as a linear combination of example-dependent quantities and allows for comparing the results obtained in different datasets and for addressing discrepancies between the development dataset, used to train and validate the model, and the target dataset, where the model will be deployed. It also specifies in which scenarios using UCCs-unaware class rebalancing techniques or rebalancing metrics aligns with TCC minimization and when it is instead counterproductive. Finally, we propose a procedure to estimate the WA weight parameter in the absence of fully specified UCCs and demonstrate the robustness of WA by analyzing its correlation with TCC in example-dependent scenarios.



Keywords—

Machine Learning, Binary Classification, Imbalanced Dataset, Cost-Sensitive Learning, Return of Investment,
Total Classification Cost, Unit Classification Cost, Weighted Accuracy, Evaluation Metric, Example-Dependent Cost

this framework allows for comparing the results obtained in different datasets and for addressing discrepancies between class distributions in the development and target datasets;
(iii) we propose a procedure to estimate the weight parameter of WA in the absence of a fully specified cost matrix;
and (iv) we demonstrate the robustness of WA by analyzing its correlation with TCC in several example-dependent scenarios.

2.2
Costs Associated with Classification

As discussed in Section
1
, the ideal strategy for selecting the optimal classifier in a given use case -- particularly when the goal is maximizing the RoI -- is to minimize the Total Classification Cost (TCC). This cost reflects the consequences of actions taken based on the classifier’s predictions.
In general, TCC may depend on the specific examples that are misclassified
1
1

1

For instance, in churn prediction, the cost of a false negative may vary depending on the customer misclassified as non-churn.

[
41
]
and for binary classification can be defined as

{\mathrm{T}CC}=\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{+}}\left(d_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}\delta_{o(a),-1}+d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}\delta_{o(a),1}\right)+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{-}}\left(e_{a}^{\mathrm{T}N}\delta_{o(a),-1}+e_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}\delta_{o(a),1}\right),

(1)

where
\mathbf{S}_{+}
(
\mathbf{S}_{-}
) is the set of positive (negative) examples,
d_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}
(
d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}
) is the UCC for incorrect (correct) classification of positive example
a
,
e_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}
(
e_{a}^{\mathrm{T}N}

a

In most literature, UCCs are assumed to be example-independent, i.e.,
d_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}=c_{\mathrm{F}N}

d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}=c_{\mathrm{T}P}

Despite extensive research in classifier evaluation, there is still no universally accepted metric that serves as a definitive standard (see, for instance,
[

]
).
Among the existing metrics, C-score, MSU, and H stand out for their linear relation with TCC, allowing for model ranking coherent with TCC. However, each has limitations. C-score, while dimensionless,
lacks an upper bound, which can complicate comparisons
across different datasets, as its magnitude depends on dataset-specific properties, such as the number of examples and the proportion of actual positives. MSU and H are normalized within the
[0,1]

{\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{min}}=0

As an alternative to the metrics described in Table
2
, in the next section we propose a weighted version of the well-known accuracy metric, normalized between 0 and 1.
In the case of example-independent Unit Classification Costs (UCCs), this metric not only ranks classifiers consistently with the Total Classification Cost (TCC), but also enables performance comparison across different datasets and supports TCC minimization on the target dataset—provided the ratio of positive examples (or the prior probability of a positive outcome) can be estimated.

3
Weighted Accuracy

Accuracy, as defined in Table
2
, is a standard KPI, widely used to evaluate classifiers due to its simplicity: it measures the proportion of correct predictions over the total number of examples. However, it assumes equal importance for all types of classification outcomes, deviating from TCC
in the presence of unequal misclassification costs and it is often considered inappropriate in imbalanced scenarios.

To address these limitations, we define a
Weighted Accuracy
(WA), which assigns different importance to positive and negative examples

\mathrm{WA}=\frac{w\,\mathrm{TP}+(1-w)\mathrm{TN}}{w\,\mathrm{P}+(1-w)\mathrm{N}},

(3)

where
w
is the normalized weight assigned to actual positives (true positives and false negative), and
1-w
is the weight assigned to actual negatives (false positives and true negatives).
If we choose the weight
w
as the UCC ratio

r_{C}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{F}N}}{C_{\mathrm{F}N}+C_{\mathrm{F}P}},

(4)

then WA becomes linearly related to the TCC defined in Eq.
2
. Substituting Eq.
4
into Eq.
3
, we obtain
\mathrm{WA}=1-({\mathrm{T}CC}-{\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{min}})/({\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{max}}-{\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{min}}),

where
{\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{max}}=C_{\mathrm{FN}}\mathrm{P}+C_{\mathrm{F}P}\mathrm{N}
is the maximum possible classification cost.
This shows that WA is analogous to the metrics C-score, MSU, and H discussed in Section
2.2
, but it provides a different interpretation and a more consistent normalization scheme. It also demonstrates that Eq.
3
offers a more principled way to introduce cost-based weighting into accuracy than, for example, WCA (see Table
2
).

For example-independent UCCs, WA with the weight in Eq.
4
ranks confusion matrices in the exact reverse order of TCC. Therefore, maximizing WA is equivalent to minimizing TCC and, by extension, maximizing RoI. By construction, WA is normalized in the interval
[0,1]
and is independent of the test set size and this makes it suitable for comparing models evaluated on different datasets, as long as the class distribution (i.e.,
\mathrm{P}/N_{\mathrm{tot}}
) remains the same.
In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
,
we show how WA can be adapted straightforwardly to compare results across datasets with different class distributions, by appropriately adjusting the weight
w
.

3.1
Handling Class Imbalance

Here we propose an approach to handle class imbalance that avoids under or oversampling, thereby preserving the original dataset and its statistical properties.

Suppose we want to compute a metric that can be expressed as a weighted average:

K=\frac{\sum_{a}w_{a}k_{a}}{\sum_{a}w_{a}},

(5)

where
k_{a}
is the contribution of example
a
, and
w_{a}
is its weight. WA, for instance, fits this form with
k_{a}=1
(
0
) if
a
is correctly (incorrectly) classified, and
w_{a}=w
(
1-w
) if
a\in\mathbf{S}_{+}
(
\mathbf{S}_{-}
).

To estimate the value of
K
after resampling
2
2

2

We assume here that resampling aims to equalize the number of positive and negative examples. Generalization to arbitrary class ratios is discussed in the next section.
, we can rescale the weights within each class according to their relative frequencies:

w_{a}^{\mathrm{b}alanced}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\frac{w_{a}}{\mathrm{P}}&\text{if }a\in\mathbf{S}_{+}\\
\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\frac{w_{a}}{\mathrm{N}}&\text{if }a\in\mathbf{S}_{-}\end{cases},

(6)

where
Z
is a normalization constant. For WA, we require
w_{+}^{\mathrm{b}alanced}+w_{-}^{\mathrm{b}alanced}=1
, where
w_{+}^{\mathrm{b}alanced}
(
w_{-}^{\mathrm{b}alanced}
) is the average rescaled weight for positive (negative) examples.
This formulation is consistent with findings in
[
19
,
30
,
31
,
21
,
39
]
, and can be applied to any metric of the form in Eq.
5
. For WA, the rescaled weight becomes
w_{\mathrm{b}alanced}=r_{C}\mathrm{N}/[r_{C}\mathrm{N}+(1-r_{C})\mathrm{P}]
, while for standard accuracy
w_{\mathrm{b}alanced}=\mathrm{N}/N_{\mathrm{t}ot}
.
This implies that rebalancing techniques and metrics designed to correct class imbalance implicitly assume
r_{C}\simeq\mathrm{N}/N_{\mathrm{t}ot}
and may become counterproductive if this condition is not satisfied.

3.1.1
Target Dataset: Generalizing the Balancing

The weight defined in Eq.
6
aims at correcting the class imbalance, or equivalently estimating the metric after both classes + and - are balanced to the same fraction of examples.
However, for cost-sensitive evaluation, we must consider the actual class distribution in the
target dataset
, i.e., the dataset on which the classifier will be deployed and decisions will be made. This could correspond to a production environment in business or a patient population in healthcare.
Let us then distinguish between:
(
i
) the
development dataset
, with
N_{\mathrm{tot}}
examples and ratio of positive examples
r_{+}=\mathrm{P}/N_{\mathrm{tot}}
, used for training, validation, and testing and
(
ii
) the
target dataset
, with
\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{t}}
examples and ratio of positive examples
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}=\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{t}}/N_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{t}}
, which may differ from
r_{+}
.
If the target dataset is not directly accessible,
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}
can be interpreted as a
base rate
, i.e., the prior probability of a positive outcome
[
25
]
.

To estimate a metric on the target dataset using the development dataset, we generalize Eq.
6
as:

w_{a}^{\mathrm{t}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\frac{w_{a}\cdot r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}}{\mathrm{P}}&\text{if }a\in\mathbf{S}_{+}\\
\frac{1}{Z}\cdot\frac{w_{a}\cdot(1-r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}})}{\mathrm{N}}&\text{if }a\in\mathbf{S}_{-}\end{cases},

(7)

where
Z
is a normalization factor. As expected, Eq.
7
reduces to Eq.
6
when
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}=0.5
.
This rescaling can also be used to compare metrics across datasets with different class distributions: we select one dataset as the target and apply Eq.
7
to the others.

3.2
WA on the Target Dataset with unequal UCCs

Here we discuss how to estimate WA on the target dataset in presence of unequal misclassification costs. If we had direct access to the target dataset we would use the weight defined in Eq.
4
; if we only have access to the development dataset, by applying the transformation in Eq.
7
to WA (Eq.
3
) and normalizing appropriately, we obtain

w_{\mathrm{t}}=\frac{r_{C}\cdot r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}/r_{+}}{r_{C}\cdot r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}/r_{+}+(1-r_{C})\cdot(1-r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}})/(1-r_{+})},

(8)

where
w
is defined in Eq.
4
.
Equation
8
provides the most general form of the weight to be used in WA when accounting for both non-uniform UCCs and class imbalance.
It encompasses several important special cases:
(i) if the target dataset has the same positive rate as the development dataset (
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}=r_{+}
), then
w_{\mathrm{t}}=r_{C}
;
(ii) if misclassification costs are equal, then
r_{C}=1/2
, and WA reduces to standard accuracy evaluated on the target dataset;
(iii) if we wish to estimate WA on a rebalanced dataset, then
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}=1/2
, yielding the same result described in Section
3.1
.

Equation
8
shows that a weighting scheme for accuracy (or, in alternative, class rebalancing) is required unless
r_{+}=r_{C}r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}/(1-r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}-r_{C}+2r_{C}r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}})
, which is therefore the condition for the standard accuracy to be equivalent to TCC and includes the special case of the target dataset with the same positive rate as the development dataset (
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}=r_{+}
) and equal UCCs
r_{C}=1/2
.

3.3
Expected Weighted Accuracy

When the value of
w
cannot be precisely determined, a probabilistic approach may be adopted, similar to that proposed in
[
40
,
12
]
. We introduce a probability density function
u(w)
over the weight
w
, allowing us to define the
Expected Weighted Accuracy
(EWA)

\mathrm{EWA}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}w\,\mathrm{WA}(w)\,u(w).

(9)

Although this formulation shares similarities with the H-measure introduced in
[
40
,
12
]
, it is not equivalent, since in the H-measure, the integrals of
{\mathrm{T}CC}(c)
and
{\mathrm{T}CC}_{\mathrm{max}}(c)
are computed separately, as shown in Table
2
.

Depending on the available information about the cost distribution,
u(w)
can be modeled using a Beta distribution (as discussed in Section
2.2
), or constructed as a custom distribution over a plausible range of
w
values. If the support of
u(w)
is narrow – e.g.,
u(w)>0
only within a small interval – then EWA can be approximated by WA evaluated at the expected value of
w
.
More precisely, for a narrow interval
[\bar{w}-\delta,\bar{w}+\delta]
, we have

\mathrm{EWA}=\mathrm{WA}(\bar{w})+O(\delta^{2}),

which implies that EWA can be well approximated by evaluating WA at the midpoint
\bar{w}
of the interval.

4
Example-Dependent Classification Cost

Although most confusion-matrix-based literature assumes that UCCs are example-independent, in many real-world use cases they actually depend on the individual example
a

[
42
,
41
]
.
In such cases, evaluating the TCC (see Eq.
1
) requires specifying the set
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{FN}}
(
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}P}
) of misclassified positive (negative) examples, rather than simply their count
\mathrm{FN}
(
\mathrm{FP}
). This implies that the same confusion matrix may correspond to different TCC values depending on which examples are misclassified.

We can extend the shifted UCCs defined in Section
2.2
to the example-dependent case as
E_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}=e_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}-e_{a}^{\mathrm{T}N}
and
D_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}=d_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}-d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}
; this allows us to rewrite Eq.
1
as

\mathrm{TCC}=\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}N}}D_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}P}}E_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}+\mathrm{TCC}_{\mathrm{min}}
,
where
\mathrm{TCC}_{\mathrm{min}}=\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{+}}d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{-}}e_{a}^{\mathrm{T}N}
is the minimum achievable cost, corresponding to perfect classification.
If we
express the UCCs as
D_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}=C_{\mathrm{F}N}+\delta_{a}
and
E_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}=C_{\mathrm{F}P}+\epsilon_{a}
– where
C_{\mathrm{FN}}
and
C_{\mathrm{F}P}
are the average costs over the target dataset
3
3

3

If these averages are not known for the target dataset, they may be estimated from the development dataset.
, and
\delta_{a}
and
\epsilon_{a}
represent example-dependent deviations – we obtain

\mathrm{TCC}=C_{\mathrm{F}N}\cdot\mathrm{FN}+C_{\mathrm{F}P}\cdot\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TCC}_{\mathrm{min}}+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}N}}\delta_{a}+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}P}}\epsilon_{a}.

(10)

Equation
10
extends Eq.
2
, with additional terms that depend on the specific misclassified examples. If we assume that
\delta_{a}
and
\epsilon_{a}
are random variables with zero mean, i.e., their distributions are the same for misclassified and correctly classified examples, their contribution becomes negligible when the number of misclassified examples is sufficiently large.

4.1
Example-Dependent Churn Prediction

A representative use case involving example-dependent costs is churn prediction,
where the model predicts whether a customer will churn within a forthcoming time window.
Based on this prediction, the commercial team contacts customers identified as potential churners and implements retention measures.

•

Each customer
a
predicted as positive causes a cost
M
for the retention measure, which includes the time and effort of the commercial team to contact the customer and implement the measure. Here we assume that this retention cost is example-independent, so
e_{a}^{\mathrm{F}P}=d_{a}^{\mathrm{T}P}=M
.

•

Conversely, for a customer
a
predicted as negative, the cost (or missing income) is
d_{a}^{\mathrm{F}N}=R_{a}P_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
e_{a}^{\mathrm{T}N}=0
, where
P_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the probability of effectiveness of the retention measure (assumed example-independent), and
R_{a}
is the revenue the company would have earned from
a
if the customer had not churned.
R_{a}
may be estimated as the customer revenue rate multiplied by the expected customer lifetime.

Under these assumptions, we have
C_{\mathrm{F}P}=M
and
C_{\mathrm{F}N}=R_{\mathrm{avg}}P_{\mathrm{eff}}-M
, where
R_{\mathrm{avg}}
is the expected value of
R_{a}
over the target dataset. Assuming zero cost for correct classifications, Eq.
10
simplifies to

\mathrm{TCC}=C_{\mathrm{F}N}\cdot\mathrm{FN}+C_{\mathrm{F}P}\cdot\mathrm{FP}+\sum_{a\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{F}N}}\delta_{a},

(11)

with
\delta_{a}=R_{a}-R_{\mathrm{avg}}
.

5
Weight Estimation

In an ideal scenario, the UCCs defined in Section
2.2
can be assessed by estimating the cost associated to the actions taken in response to each type of classification outcome (false positive, true positive, etc.). Note that the cost matrix may include opportunity costs, i.e., foregone benefits due to missed opportunities, as in the example described in Section
4.1
; costs or benefits can be measured relatively to any baseline. However, the baseline must remain fixed, i.e., the reference point for zero cost should not change
[
7
]
.

In practice, estimating the full confusion matrix may be challenging. Therefore, we often focus on the more attainable goal of estimating the weight
w
in Eq.
3
, or equivalently, the UCCs ratio
v=C_{\mathrm{F}N}/C_{\mathrm{F}P}
, which suffices for computing WA in Eq.
3
.
We propose therefore a procedure to estimate the weight range
w_{\mathrm{min}}\leq w\leq w_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Here, we assume that the development dataset have the same ratio of positive examples; the generalization to
r_{+}\neq r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}
is described in Eq.
8
.

5.1
Unit Misclassification Costs Ratio

The first approach to estimating the weight range is via the UCCs ratio
v
– i.e., estimating how many false positives are, on average, equivalent to a single false negative – and using the equivalence
w=v/(v+1)
.
For instance, in bankruptcy prediction, a false negative – where an auditor incorrectly assesses a company as solvent – may result in liability to creditors and shareholders. Conversely, a false positive – where a solvent company is incorrectly assessed as insolvent – may lead to reduced access to credit and increased uncertainty. Based on these considerations,
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
estimated
v\simeq 35
(i.e.,
w\simeq 0.97
) and
10\lesssim v\lesssim 50
(i.e.,
0.91\lesssim w\lesssim 0.98
), respectively.

5.2
Constraints from Ranking of Emblematic Models

If the previous approach is not feasible, an alternative method is to infer constraints on
w
by ranking a set of emblematic classification results. Even if we cannot estimate the costs
C_{\mathrm{F}P}
and
C_{\mathrm{F}N}
(or their ratio), we may still be able to determine whether one specific outcome is more costly than another.

5.2.1
Define a Set of Emblematic Models

The choice of emblematic models may depend on the use case and the ratio of positive examples. Ideally, we should select a small set of models, sufficiently simple to avoid overcomplexity in the ranking of the outcomes, yet diverse enough to yield meaningful constraints on
w
. As a baseline, we consider the models described in Table
3
:
(i)
\mathcal{M}_{+}
and
\mathcal{M}_{-}
, which always predict the same class;
(ii)
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad}}
, which misclassifies a fraction
\alpha
of examples in both classes;
(iii)
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad+}}
, which misclassify a fraction
\alpha
of negatives or positives, respectively, while perfectly classifying the other class.

5.2.2
Ranking the Emblematic Models

To rank the models previously identified, we must understand the use case from a business perspective. For example, is it preferable to correctly identify all positives (as in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}}
) or all negatives (as in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad+}}
)?

Let us consider a churn prediction use case as depicted in Section
4.1
.
In such scenarios, we typically observe both class imbalance (
\mathrm{P}\ll\mathrm{N}
) and cost imbalance (
C_{\mathrm{F}N}\gg C_{\mathrm{F}P}
).
For illustative purpose, let us assume a ration
r_{+}=0.05
of positive examples, and let us use the models from Table
3
with
\alpha=0.6
. Suppose it is impractical to react to a large number of predicted positives (e.g., contacting many customers in churn prediction, treating many patients in cancer detection, etc.). In this scenario, the worst outcome is likely
\mathcal{M}_{+}

A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad+}})

Moreover,
\mathcal{M}_{-}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}}
is preferable to
\mathcal{M}_{-}

\alpha

In summary, we obtain the following ranking:

A(\mathcal{M}_{+})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad}})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{-})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad+}}).

For

A(\mathcal{M}_{+})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad}})

A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad-}})\lesssim A(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bad+}})
.

Metrics Comparison with Total Classification Cost

In this section, we compare WA, EWA (introduced in Section
3
) and each of the metrics described in Table
2
, using as a reference the TCC in an example-dependent scenario.
As a practical example, we consider the churn prediction scenario with example-dependent costs described in Section
4
.
The target dataset is assumed to be equivalent to the development dataset in terms of ratio of positives (
r_{+}=r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}
), with the latter containing 200 examples (
N_{\mathrm{tot}}=200
).
The retention measure has a 25% probability of effectiveness (
P_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.25
), while the revenues

are sampled uniformly and without repetition from the monthly charges in the Telco Customer Churn dataset from Kaggle
4
4

4

https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/blastchar/telco-customer-churn
.

The TCC is computed according to Eq.
11
, while the cost-sensitive metrics WCA, WRA, ACD, WA, EWA are calculated using average UCCs.
The H metric is computed using a Beta distribution with
\alpha=\beta=2

MSU and C-score are not explicitly computed, as they rank classification outcomes identically to WA, differing only in their normalization schemes.

To quantify the similarity between each metric and TCC, we identify a sample of classification outcomes and rank them according to the metric and TCC, respectively; hence we compute the correlation between the two rankings, using:

•

•

r_{+}

For each pair

Anti-Diagonal Metrics

and

are both small or both large): in this case balancing techniques are counterproductive.
Indeed, in these regions, accuracy outperforms many sophisticated metrics, particularly those designed to handle class imbalance

Besides standard accuracy, also CBA, IAM, WCA, and H exhibit strong correlation with TCC close to the anti-diagonal of the heatmap (i.e., the line

More precisely, accuracy, CBA, IAM, and H diverge significantly from TCC in two regions: (i)

Main Diagonal Metrics

Several metrics exhibit qualitatively symmetric or antisymmetric correlation patterns with respect to reflection along the main diagonal (

According to the findings in Section

Recall, F1, and B-ROC show instead strong correlation with TCC above the main diagonal and strong anticorrelation below (less pronounced for F1 in the standard correlation), while specificity shows the opposite behavior.

ACD, H-informed, WA, and EWA are the top-performing metrics, showing strong correlation with TCC across nearly all scenarios. ACD performs slightly worse than the others in this group, particularly under the weighted correlation (Figure

6.3
Conclusion

The analysis of correlation with TCC
confirms that, while
rebalancing metrics
described in Section
6.2.2
outperform accuracy in imbalanced scenarios where
r_{C}\simeq 1-r_{+}
, the opposite happen when UCCs are balanced (
r_{C}=1/2)
or when the majoirity class has the larger UCC (
r_{C}\simeq r_{+}
).
Here we consider
development
and
target
datasets with the same ratio of positives, but the extension to the general case
r_{+}^{\mathrm{t}}\neq r_{+}
can be carried out as described in Section
3.2
.
Moreover, the analysis demonstrates that the example-independent approximation of TCC, i.e., ignoring the fluctuation term in Eq.
11
, is consistent and reliable in all examined scenarios.
This property is fundamental, as it is implicitly assumed by any evaluation framework based on the confusion matrix and cost matrix.

Finally, this experiment identifies H-informed, EWA, WA, C-score, and MSU as the only confusion-matrix-based metrics with robust correlation with TCC across all tested scenarios, thereby avoiding misleading model evaluations.
Among these, WA has the already discussed advantages of a straightforward interpretation and the capability to compare models among different datasets and to consistently evaluate a classifier even when the
target
and
development
datasets differ.
