Background

Omphalocele is a central abdominal wall defect characterized by viscera protruding through the umbilical ring, which is covered by a three-layered membrane. Its estimated incidence ranges from 2 to 4 per 10,000 live births [1]. Comorbid anomalies are common and include cardiac malformations, gastrointestinal anomalies, neural tube defects, genitourinary anomalies, chromosomal abnormalities, and associated congenital syndromes. Cantrell syndrome (CS), also known as Pentalogy of Cantrell (POC), is an extremely rare condition, occurring in approximately 1 in 65,000 to 1 in 200,000 live births, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.35:1 [2]. It comprises five anomalies: a midline supraumbilical abdominal wall defect, an anterior diaphragmatic defect, a diaphragmatic pericardium defect, a lower sternal defect, and various congenital heart malformations [3]. The perinatal mortality rates are typically high and depend on the severity of associated anomalies [4, 5]. Nevertheless, experiences related to the diagnosis and management of the disease remain limited due to its rarity.

Here, we report a newborn with an abdominal mass initially thought to be an omphalocele but ultimately diagnosed with Cantrell syndrome, who underwent an eventful treatment process.

Case presentation

A female neonate was born at 40 weeks and 3 days of gestation via vaginal delivery and was subsequently found to have an abdominal mass in the center of the abdominal wall, covered with rufous skin (Fig. 1). The infant was G1P1, a test-tube baby, with a birth weight of 3150 g. The Apgar scores were eight at one minute and ten at five minutes. Both her parents were aged 29 years and had no relevant family history. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a midline abdominal wall defect with portions of the liver and bowel loops herniated (Fig. 2). The chest radiograph showed an apparent cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) of 0.68 (Fig. 3). Echocardiography revealed a ventricular septal defect (VSD), an atrial septal defect (ASD), a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), pulmonary arterial hypertension, dextroversion of the heart, a persistent left superior vena cava, and a dilated coronary sinus. Consequently, the initial diagnosis was an omphalocele accompanied by congenital heart disease.

A surgical intervention for omphalocele was performed on the fifth day following her birth. A pulsatile heart and a defect in the diaphragm were observed after the hernial sac was opened (Fig. 4A-C, Additional file 1). Based on the clinical presentation, preoperative assessments, and intraoperative findings, the diagnosis of Cantrell syndrome was conclusively established. The omphalocele, pericardial defect, and diaphragmatic defect were subsequently repaired. During the procedure, an episode of pulseless electrical activity (PEA) occurred, necessitating immediate intracardiac compressions, which restored cardiac activity. The abdominal wall was reconstructed utilizing an acellular dermal matrix patch, and the umbilicus was repositioned (Fig. 4D-E). Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for mechanical ventilation and continuous monitoring.

(A) Preoperative photographs. (B-C) The ventral diaphragmatic defect and the diaphragmatic pericardium protruding into the abdominal cavity were identified intraoperatively. (D) The abdominal wall was reconstructed using an acellular dermal matrix patch, and the umbilicus was repositioned. (E) Postoperative appearance

The cardiovascular surgery was performed at 25 days of age. The PDA (3 mm) was ligated, and the VSD (12 mm) and ASD (10 mm) were repaired with Gore-Tex patches under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). During the operation and postoperatively, episodes of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) were frequent and reverted to sinus rhythm with synchronous electrical cardioversion. The chest was left open and closed on postoperative day 3. Amiodarone and propafenone were administered for arrhythmias. There were several failures in weaning from the ventilator. Echocardiography demonstrated an interventricular residual shunt, leading to a secondary VSD repair on her 54th day of life. Afterward, she was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation on postoperative day 3. Complications that arose during hospitalization included SVT, sepsis, pneumonia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and hepatic dysfunction. The patient ultimately improved after a series of supportive treatments and was discharged. Hospitalization lasted 79 days, with a total of 49 days on mechanical ventilation.

During the twenty months of follow-up after discharge, the child exhibited good recovery, but SVT remained frequent. The electrocardiogram (ECG) during the episode revealed paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) with intraventricular conduction block, while the ECG during sinus rhythm showed ventricular pre-excitation. Nonetheless, due to the refusal of further examinations, the precise classification of SVT remains undetermined. The administration of adenosine during acute episodes was effective in terminating SVT. The patient’s long-term management regimen includes oral propafenone and sotalol.

Discussion and conclusions

Omphalocele can be readily diagnosed at birth by its features. Notably, concomitant anomalies should always be considered once an omphalocele is identified, with incidence rates from 25% to 97% [1]. Cantrell syndrome is defined by five congenital anomalies involving the abdominal wall, sternum, diaphragm, pericardium, and heart, first described by Cantrell and colleagues in 1958 [3]. Common intracardiac anomalies include VSD, ASD, tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary stenosis, and left ventricular diverticulum [6]. In 1972, Toyama classified Cantrell syndrome into complete (all five defects), probable (four defects including intracardiac and abdominal wall anomalies), and incomplete forms (various combinations, including sternal abnormalities) [7, 8].

In this case, we report a neonate initially diagnosed with omphalocele by appearance and CT was later identified with Cantrell syndrome during operation, as the ventral diaphragmatic defect caused the diaphragmatic pericardium to protrude into the hernia. From the lateral view of the preoperative appearance and CT scan, we could find the lower sternum missing, and the hernia sac was positioned higher than the usual omphalocele. In addition, the VSD, ASD, and PDA were confirmed via echocardiography. Consequently, this patient exhibited the complete form.

The diagnosis of Cantrell syndrome is established according to the five anomalies. However, not all patients exhibit all symptoms, and severity varies, complicating diagnosis. Particularly, some non-obvious defects may be easily overlooked, such as minor diaphragmatic and pericardial defects, as well as sternal defects in the neonatal period, leading to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. One pitfall is to diagnose an isolated omphalocele, as in our case. To distinguish, note that the abdominal defect is higher, presenting as epigastric omphalocele, and a pulsatile mass below the costal margin may indicate a herniating heart through a diaphragmatic defect. Additionally, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers advantages in soft tissue visualization, which may assist in diagnosis. Undoubtedly, an accurate and early diagnosis contributes to making a strategic treatment plan, which may improve the prognosis.

Treatment of Cantrell syndrome is tailored depending on the diversity and severity of related anomalies. Stable neonates may benefit from primary conservative management [9], but surgical correction of anomalies remains primary. One-stage or multi-stage procedures can be employed following a thorough assessment of the severity of intracardiac and extracardiac anomalies, including the size of the abdominal wall defect, the type of cardiac anomalies, and the patient’s tolerance ability [10]. Both anatomical integrity and hemodynamic stability should be taken into account when devising the operative plan [11]. It is crucial to avoid high intra-abdominal or thoracic pressures during thoraco-abdominal reconstruction. Early cardiac intervention is controversial; in this case, surgery was urgent due to progressive hemodynamic compromise (pulmonary arterial hypertension and enlarged right heart) unresponsive to medical management. Delaying surgery carried a risk of irreversible end-organ damage.

The prognosis of Cantrell syndrome is extremely poor, with documented surgical mortality rates approximating 52% [12]. Ectopia cordis, complex intracardiac defects, pulmonary hypoplasia, or the complete form are linked to elevated mortality rates [13, 14]. Complications include tachyarrhythmias, bradycardia, hypotension, rupture of diverticula, and heart failure [11], each of which can substantially contribute to patient mortality; thus, meticulous management and timely intervention are imperative [15]. Consequently, the involvement of a multidisciplinary team comprising neonatologists, pediatric surgeons, pediatric cardiologists, and anesthesiologists is essential for the effective management of patients with Cantrell syndrome.

Our case presented several limitations. Firstly, the absence of genetic testing was noted. Although no specific genetic variant has been identified in most sporadic cases of Cantrell syndrome, emerging evidence indicates a potential association between genetic anomalies and the syndrome [11]. We therefore recommend that future cases undergo comprehensive genomic evaluation. Additionally, no electrophysiological study was conducted to definitively determine the type of SVT, and the etiology of the arrhythmia remains unknown. Furthermore, the follow-up period has been only 20 months to date, so the long-term prognosis and outcomes for this patient remain unclear.

In conclusion, comorbid anomalies should be considered in infants with omphalocele. Cantrell syndrome, though rare, should be suspected when an epigastric omphalocele is observed. Further detailed examinations and comprehensive assessments are essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis and formulating an appropriate treatment strategy. In cases of Cantrell syndrome, a multidisciplinary team is imperative. Complications, especially life-threatening ones such as tachyarrhythmias, necessitate long-term follow-up and management.
