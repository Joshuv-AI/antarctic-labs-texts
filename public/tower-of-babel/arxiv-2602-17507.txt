High Order Semi‐implicit Rosenbrock type and Multistep Methods for Evolutionary Partial Differential Equations with Higher Order Derivatives
Boscarino Sebastiano, Giuseppe Izzo

Abstract
The aim of this work is to apply a semi-implicit (SI) strategy within a Rosenbrock-type and IMEX linear multistep (LM) framework to a sequence of 1D time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs) with high order spatial derivatives. This strategy provides great flexibility to treat these equations, and allows the construction of simple linearly implicit schemes without any Newton iteration. Furthermore, the SI schemes so designed do not require the severe time-step restrictions typically encountered when using explicit methods for stability, i.e. Δ ​ t = 𝒪 ⁡ ( Δ ​ x k ) \Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{k}) for the k k -th order PDEs with k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . For space discretization, this strategy is combined with finite difference schemes. We provide examples of methods up to order p = 4 p=4 , and we illustrate the effectiveness of the schemes with applications to dissipative, dispersive, and biharmonic-type equations. Numerical experiments show that the proposed schemes are stable and achieve the expected orders of accuracy. Keywords : Time dependent partial differential equations, semi-implicit (SI) strategy, Rosenbrock type methods, Multistep methods, IMEX-RK methods, finite difference schemes. Introduction

Many time-dependent PDEs which arise in physics or engineering involve the computation
of high order spatial derivatives.
In this paper, we consider a sequence of such PDEs with increasingly higher order derivatives. To simplify the presentation, the PDE examples below are restricted to the one-dimensional case.

Many PDEs of the form (

In this paper we also consider a one-dimensional version of the convection-diffusion equation

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}-(a(u)u_{x})_{x}=0.

For all these equations suitable initial conditions and boundary conditions will be set.

It is well known that the time discretization is a very important issue for time dependent PDEs. For the
k
-th (
k\geq 2
) order PDEs, explicit methods always suffer from stringent and severe time step restriction, i.e.,
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^{k})
, for the stability where
\Delta t
is the time step and
\Delta x
is the mesh size. This time step is too small, resulting in excessive computational cost and rendering the explicit schemes impractical.
On the other hand, implicit methods can overcome the drastic time step size restriction imposed by the stability condition for explicit schemes. However, a nonlinear algebraic system must be solved (e.g. by Newton iteration) at each time step.

In order to apply the SI formulation, assume that the semi-discrete formulation of (
2

6

8

\frac{dU(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{F}(U(t))+\frac{1}{\Delta x^{k}}\mathcal{B}(U(t))U(t),

(9)

where
U(t)=(U_{1}(t),U_{2}(t),...,U_{M}(t))^{T}
, and
U_{i}(t)
is the approximate solution at spatial position
x_{i}
for
i=1,...,M
, i.e.,
U_{i}(t)\approx u(x_{i},t)
, for
i=1,...,M
, and uniform grid spacing
\Delta x=x_{i+1}-x_{i}
, where
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\mathcal{B}(U(t))
is an

As an example, taking the convection nonlinear diffusion equation (
1

is a vector arising from the spatial discretization of

\mathcal{B}(U(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

(a(u)u_{x})_{x}

The semi-implicit (SI) method
[

23

combines implicit and explicit time discretizations: in the term
\mathcal{B}(U)U
, only the factor
U
is treated implicitly, while the factor
\mathcal{B}(U)
and the term
\mathcal{F}(U(t))
are explicit, thus the resulting scheme can be solved without any iterative solvers. Within the IMEX Runge–Kutta framework, the SI approach is particularly convenient and effective when the problem involves a linearly implicit treatment of the unknown variable in terms with high-order spatial derivatives, as in Eq. (
9
) (see
[
23
]
). Its main advantage is its flexibility, as it can be integrated with various space discretization schemes.

In this work, we develop an SI strategy based on both a Rosenbrock-type formulation and linear multistep schemes. We present several examples of equations with high-order spatial derivatives of the form (
1
), (
2
), (
3
), (
7

8

The starting point for the SI Rosenbrock-type formulation is the work in
[
32
]
, where the so-called additive semi-implicit Runge–Kutta schemes were introduced and Rosenbrock linearized Runge–Kutta methods were developed. The theoretical framework established in
[
32

provides a natural basis and can be extended in a straightforward manner to the present semi-implicit setting.

On the other hand, the starting point of the SI linear multistep approach is the work in
[
1
]
, which develops a general setting for the construction of high order SI linear multistep methods for time-dependent PDEs of the form (
9
), combining an explicit predictor with an implicit corrector.
However, when the order of the high-order spatial derivative increases beyond
k\geq 2
the SI linear multistep scheme tends to become unstable and inaccurate due to the explicit predictor step. To avoid this drawback, we introduce a
modified
class of SI linear multistep schemes that turn out to be more efficient, due to a semi-implicit corrector, and more stable and accurate, since it is based on a predictor that is itself semi-implicit (see Sect.
4
).
As usual in the context of predictor-corrector (PC) techniques based on linear multistep methods, an order
p^{*}
predictor provides a preliminary approximation, and an order
p
corrector, with
p\geq p^{*}
, refines it through successive iterations. In order to achieve the expected order of convergence
p
of the scheme, typically
p-p^{*}
correction steps are required.

When applying modified semi-implicit linear multistep (SI-LM) methods, each correction step can improve the accuracy of the solution. In practice, performing approximately
p-p^{*}
correction steps per time step, i.e. solving about
p
linear systems, is sufficient to gradually recover the desired convergence order
p
of the method.

Finally, we coupled high order finite difference schemes
[
25

with high-order SI time discretization for solving system (
9

We choose the finite difference schemes to discretize the space because of its simplicity in design and coding. For a detailed description of the spatial discretization used in the numerical section, we refer the reader to
[
23
]
.
Nevertheless, other types of spatial discretizations can also be considered, such as local discontinuous Galerkin schemes
[

with application to various high order PDEs,
[

,
27
,
28

30

The structure of the paper is as follows. In the next section, we introduce the SI strategy, which is developed using the approach proposed in
[

In section (

2
SI methods

Starting from system (
9
) we define a multivalued function
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
that satisfies
\mathcal{H}(U,U)=\mathcal{F}(U(t))+\mathcal{B}(U(t))U(t)
so that (
9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{dU}{dt}(t)=\mathcal{H}(U(t),U(t)),\quad\forall\,\,t\geq t_{0}.

(10)

In
[
4
]
the authors introduced a class of SI Runge-Kutta (RK) methods that can be directly applied to (
10
). The SI-RK framework proposed in
[
4
]
assumes that function
\mathcal{H}
has a non stiff dependence on the first variable and a stiff dependence on the second variable.

Therefore the problem (
10
) can be rewritten as a partitioned form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{dU}{dt}(t)\,=\,\mathcal{H}(U(t),V(t)),\\
\\
\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}(t)\,=\,\mathcal{H}(U(t),V(t)),\end{array}\right.

(11)

with
\mathcal{H}(U(t),V(t))=\mathcal{F}(U(t))+\mathcal{B}(U(t))V(t)
and initial conditions
U(t_{0})=V(t_{0})
,
where the variable
U(t)
appearing as the first argument of
\mathcal{H}
will be treated explicitly, while
V(t)
appearing as the second argument will be treated implicitly. In such a case the solution of system (
11
) satisfies

U(t)=V(t)
for any
t\geq t_{0}
and is also a solution of Equation
(
10
). System (
11
) is a particular case of
partitioned system apparently with an additional computational cost with respect to (
10
) since we
double the number of variables.

In this section we review the concept of SI RK methods introduced in
[
4
]
and next we derive new classes of SI-Rosenbrock methods.

A general SI RK method for solving (
11
) is a partitioned RK method defined by a double Butcher tableaux with equal weights
b=\tilde{b}
, where the resulting method treats the variable
U(t)
explicitly using
(\tilde{A},b)
and the variable
V(t)
implicitly using
({A},b)
. Then the SI RK method applied to (
11
) is implemented as follows. Setting
U^{n}=V^{n}
, we compute the stage values for
i=1,...,s
,

\displaystyle U^{i}=U^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{a}_{ij}K^{j},\quad{V}^{i}=V^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}{a}_{ij}K^{j}+a_{ii}K^{i},

(12)

and the numerical solution

\displaystyle V^{n+1}=V^{n}+{\sum_{i=1}^{s}}b_{i}K^{i},

(13)

where
K_{i}=\Delta t\mathcal{H}(U^{i},V^{i})
, are the RK fluxes.

We observe that, since
\tilde{b}_{i}=b_{i}
, for
i=1,...,s
, the numerical solutions coincide, i.e., if
U^{0}=V^{0}
then
U^{{n}}=V^{n}
, for all
n\geq 0
. Hence, the duplication of the system is only apparent and not necessary if we adopt the RK fluxes
K_{i}=\mathcal{H}(U^{i},V^{i})
as basic unknowns. In this case, the scheme can be equivalently rewritten in the form

\displaystyle K^{i}=\Delta t\mathcal{H}(U^{i},\,U^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}{a}_{ij}K^{j}+\Delta ta_{ii}K^{i}),

(14)

for
i=1,...,s
, with the numerical solution (
13
).
In light of the previous discussion, the main advantage of using the SI RK approach to compute numerical solutions is to solve a linear system. In the case of system (
9
), Eq. (
14
) can be expressed as linear equations in terms of
K_{i}
:

K^{i}=\Delta t\mathcal{B}(U^{i})\left(\bar{V}^{i}+\Delta ta_{ii}K^{i}\right),

(15)

where
\bar{V}^{i}=U^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}{a}_{ij}K^{j}
and the matrix
\mathcal{B}(U^{i})
are computed explicitly.

SI Rosenbrock-type methods.

A more computationally efficient SI-RK method is a semi-implicit extension of the Rosenbrock-type Runge-Kutta method
[
29
]
. The idea is to consider a semi-implicit scheme (
12
)-(
13
) with (
14
)
and by linearizing the terms

K^{i}=\Delta t\mathcal{H}(U^{i},\bar{V}^{i}+\Delta a_{ii}K^{i}),\;\;\;i=1,\ldots,s,

(16)

with respect to the second variable, we get,

\left[{\bf I}-\Delta ta_{ii}{\bf J}(U^{i},U^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}d_{ij}K^{j})\right]K^{i}\,=\,\,\Delta t\,\mathcal{H}\left(U^{i},\bar{V}_{i}\right),\;\;\;i=1,\ldots,s,

(17)

with
U^{i}=U^{n}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{a}_{ij}K^{j}
, and

\displaystyle V^{n+1}\,=\,V^{n}\,+\,\sum_{i=1}^{s}{b}_{i}\,K^{i},

where
d_{ij}
constitute an additional set of parameters,
{\bf J}=\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial V\equiv\mathcal{H}_{V}
is the Jacobian matrix with respect to the second argument in (
16
), once fixed the first argument. Here
I
denotes the
M\times M
identity matrix.

Note that, in the literature, to compute the Jacobian, in classical Rosenbrock methods, it is common to use the same value
a_{ii}=\gamma
for all

are set to 0, so that the Jacobian

17

In particular, from (
9

{\bf J}=\partial\mathcal{H}/\partial V
, we obtain
{\bf J}=\mathcal{B}(U(t))
with
{\bf J}(U^{n})=\mathcal{B}(U^{n})
. Thus, SI Rosenbrock type methods use the same matrix computed at the time
t_{n}
, namely
{\bf I}-\Delta t\gamma{\bf J}(U^{n})
to solve the linear system for each evaluation of
K^{i}
, for
i=1,...,s
. In contrast, in (
15

{\bf I}-\Delta t\gamma\mathcal{B}(U^{i})
to solve the linear system at every stage
K^{i}
, for every
i=1,...,s
.

[
32
]

\mathcal{H}(U,V)
has the additive form

Following now the idea in

As a first step, we compute the

Stability function.

We consider the simplified one-dimensional linear model equation of the form (

U^{\prime}=\lambda U+\mu U,

with
\lambda,\,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, and the corresponding partitioned form (

U^{\prime}=\lambda U+\mu V,\quad V^{\prime}=\lambda U+\mu V,

(34)

with

\mathcal{H}(U,V)=\lambda U+\mu V,\quad U(0)=V(0)=1.

Here
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{0}={\bf J}(U^{0})=\mu
, and applying the method (
18
)-(
20
) and (
21
) to (

U^{1}=R(\tilde{z},z)
with

R(\tilde{z},z)=1+(\tilde{z}+z)b^{T}(I-\tilde{z}\tilde{A}-zB)^{-1}{\bf e},

(35)

where
b^{T}=(b_{1},...,b_{s})
and
B=(\beta_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{s}
. The stability region of the method is defined as

S=\{(\tilde{z},z):\tilde{z},z\in\mathbb{C}\quad\textrm{such that}\quad|R(\tilde{z},z)|<1\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}.

(36)

Note that, in (

(37)

where we denote with
\omega_{ij}
the coefficients of the inverse of the matrix
(\beta_{ij})
.

Whenever the coefficients of the Rosenbrock method are chosen such
that
[
29
]
,

\beta_{si}=\alpha_{si}+\gamma_{si}=b_{i},\quad i=1,...,s,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\alpha_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}\alpha_{sj}=1,

(38)

they are called
stiffly accurate
. Since in this case
b^{T}=e^{T}_{s}B
, (or
b^{T}B^{-1}=e^{T}_{s}
) with
e^{T}_{s}=(0,...,0,1)
, from (
37
) we get
R(\tilde{z},\infty)=0
, that is, for
fixed
\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}
, this ensures
L
-stability for the Rosenbrock method.

Note that conditions (
38
) together with
\sum_{i}^{s}b_{i}=1
, implies
\gamma_{s}=\sum_{j}^{s-1}\gamma_{sj}+\gamma=0
.

The SI-Rosenbrock type methods with
\gamma>0
possess the stability functions of the form

R(\tilde{z},z)=\frac{P(\tilde{z},z)}{(1-\gamma z)^{s}},

(39)

with
P(\tilde{z},z)
polynomial of degree
\geq s
similar to the IMEX-RK methods
[
5
]
.
For instance, the one-stage method, (
18
)-(
21
) reads as:

V^{n+1}=V^{n}+K^{1},\quad K^{1}=(\tilde{z}+z)V^{n}+\gamma zK^{1},

with stability function

R(\tilde{z},z)=\frac{1+\tilde{z}+(1-\gamma)z}{1-\gamma z}.

For
\gamma=1
we obtain the stability function of the IMEX Euler method. Similarly, for the two-stage SI Rosenbrock-type method (
18
)–(
21
),
satisfying the order conditions (
29
), namely

b_{1}=1-b_{2},\quad b_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2\tilde{c}_{2}},\quad b_{2}\beta_{21}^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma,

with
b_{2}\neq 0
and for any choice of
\gamma>0
, the method is of order two.
The corresponding stability function is the rational function
[
5
]

R(\tilde{z},z)=\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{z}^{2}+\tilde{z}+1\;+\;z(1-2\gamma)(1+\tilde{z})\;+\;z^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-2\gamma+\gamma^{2}\right)}{(1-z\gamma)^{2}},

which coincides with that of the second-order two-stage IMEX-RK scheme.
By this analogy, we choose in the following
\gamma\geq\tfrac{1}{4}
in order to guarantee
A
-stability for the method,
[
5
]
.

Finally, to understand the geometrical structure of the region
S
(
36
) associated with a SI Rosenbrock-type method, we follow the idea in
[
21
,
5
]
. Then we define the region
\tilde{S}
as the largest stability region for
\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}
such that the scheme is still
A
-stable. Such region is formally defined as

\tilde{S}=\{\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}:\quad|R(\tilde{z},z)|\leq 1,z\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\}.

Explicit expressions for the set
\tilde{S}
have been given in
[
21
]
.
In particular, to compute
\tilde{S}
, we consider
z=iy
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and use the maximum modulus principle, then we can rewrite
S
as

\tilde{S}=\{\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}\quad\textrm{such that}\quad\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}|R(\tilde{z},iy)|\leq 1\},

and the boundary of this region (boundary locus) is obtained as

\partial\tilde{S}=\{\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}\quad\textrm{such that}\quad\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}|R(\tilde{z},iy)|=1\}.

(40)

Partitioned semi-implicit Multistep methods.

In
[
1
]
, partitioned semi-implicit multistep methods were developed by applying distinct multistep schemes to the different components of system (
11
).
Specifically, for the partitioned system (
11
), we consider schemes based on solving first component with an explicit linear multistep method and an implicit one for the second. For the sake of notation simplicity, we assume the system (
11
) is autonomous, such that the function
\mathcal{H}
is independent of time
t
. Therefore, we obtain the semi-implicit linear multistep (SI-LM) solver as

\displaystyle u^{n+1}=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\tilde{a}_{j}u^{n-j}+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\tilde{b}_{j}{\mathcal{H}}\left(u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right)

(52)

\displaystyle v^{n+1}=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v^{n-j}+{\Delta t}\sum_{j=-1}^{s-1}b_{j}{\mathcal{H}}\left(u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right),

where
b_{-1}\neq 0
and initially we assume
v^{n-j}=u^{n-j}
,
j=0,\ldots,s-1
. Implicit linear multistep methods for which
b_{j}=0
,
j=0,\ldots,s-1
are referred to as backward differentiation formula (BDF).
Another important class is represented by Adams methods, for which
\tilde{a}_{0}=-1
,
a_{0}=-1
,
\tilde{a}_{j}=0
,
a_{j}=0
,
j=1,\ldots,s-1
.
Let us point out that even if the scheme doubles the number of unknown, the number of evaluations of the function
{\mathcal{H}}(u(t),v(t))
is not doubled since both schemes use the same time levels.

Now, based on equation (
9
), the function
\mathcal{H}
has a linear dependence on its second argument, as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}\left(u(t),v(t)\right)={\mathcal{F}}(u(t))+\mathcal{B}(u(t))v(t).

(53)

This particular choice allows for the resulting scheme to be solved without requiring an iterative solver.
In fact, the second equation in (
52
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle v^{n+1}=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v^{n-j}+{\Delta t}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}{\mathcal{H}}\left(u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right)+{\Delta t}b_{-1}\left({\mathcal{F}}\left(u^{n+1}\right)+{\mathcal{B}}(u^{n+1})v^{n+1}\right).

or equivalently in explicit form

\displaystyle\begin{split}v^{n+1}=&\left(I-{b_{-1}\Delta t}{\mathcal{B}}(u^{n+1})\right)^{-1}\left(-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v^{n-j}+{\Delta t}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}{\mathcal{H}}\left(u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right)\right.\\
&+{\Delta t}b_{-1}\left({\mathcal{F}}\left(u^{n+1}\right)\right)\Bigg),\end{split}

(54)

since
u^{n+1}
is computed from the first equation in (
52
).

At variance with semi-implicit Runge-Kutta methods,
multistep semi-implicit
scheme provides two distinct numerical solutions
u^{n+1}
and

v^{n+1}
approximations of the true solution
u(t^{n+1})
of problem
(
11
). Note, however, that both these solutions are used by the scheme to advance in time. In order to define a unique solution, it is natural to consider the scheme (
52
) as a predictor–corrector multistep method for the non stiff component, where an explicit scheme can be used to predict
u^{n+1}
which is then used by the implicit solver as a corrector for
v^{n+1}
.

Remark 4.1

As a consequence of the above predictor-corrector formulation for the non stiff component, if the implicit solver has order
p
it is typically enough to consider an explicit solver of order
p-1
,
[
1
]
.

However, the semi-implicit approach proposed in the paper
[
1
]
, following the idea in remark (
4.1
), does not ensure, in general, the stability of the method. Specifically, this lack of stability arises from the explicit predictor step, which becomes problematic when the spatial discretization involves higher-order derivatives (e.g., a fourth derivative), thereby increasing the stiffness of the system.

For instance, we consider problem (
9
) with (
53
) and set
\mathcal{F}(u(t))=0
. We choose the simplest explicit predictor with
p=1
(namely, the explicit Euler scheme),
and starting with
u^{n}=v^{n},
we get for the numerical solution

\displaystyle u^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^{k}}\mathcal{B}(u^{n})v^{n}=\left(I+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^{k}}\mathcal{B}(u^{n})\right)u^{n}.

(55)

This equation involves an explicit evaluation of
u^{n+1}
, making the scheme conditionally stable, i.e.,

\|u^{n+1}\|\leq\|u^{n}\|,

for a certain (semi-)norm, provided that a suitable time step restriction
\Delta t\leq\Delta t_{0}
is satisfied. For instance, in this case, we can choose
\Delta t_{0}=C\Delta x^{k}/\left(\max_{u^{n}}(\mathcal{B}(u^{n}))\right)
.
This implies that a stability constraint is imposed on the time step, which becomes increasingly restrictive as
k>2
, due to its dependence on
\Delta x^{k}
.

However, this suggests that, in order to restore stability in the predictor step, and thereby making the scheme unconditionally stable, one can compute an
u^{*}
using a semi-implicit approach of the form

\displaystyle u^{*}

\displaystyle=v^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^{k}}\mathcal{B}(u^{n})u^{*}.

(56)

This scheme corresponds to a backward Euler step and is unconditionally stable, in the sense that
\|u^{*}\|\leq\|u^{n}\|
for all
n
and for any positive time-step
\Delta t>0
. Indeed, the backward Euler method in (
56
) is unconditionally stable if the spectral radius
\rho(B)
of the iteration matrix
B=\left(I-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x^{k}}\mathcal{B}(u^{n})\right)^{-1}
satisfies
\rho(B)<1
. This condition holds whenever
\mathcal{B}(u^{n})
is nonsingular and negative definite (see, for instance,
[
23
]
for details).

Therefore, we will employ the SI approach even in the predictor step to restore the stability of the method, using a suitable SI predictor solver of order
p-1
together with an SI corrector of order
p
. In the next section, we propose a
modified
SI-LM method to ensure both stability and accuracy.

4.1
Semi-implicit predictor-corrector LM methods of order
p

For the purpose of this section, assuming that the past values
u^{n-s},...,u^{n}
are available, and suppose that
v^{n-j}=u^{n-j}
, for
j=0,...,s-1
, we shall define a modified
s
-step SI-LM scheme. In practice, its implementation proceeds through the following steps:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textrm{predictor (P):}\\
u^{*}+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{a}_{j}u^{n-j}&=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{b}_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u^{n-j-1},v^{n-j}\right)+{\Delta t}\bar{b}_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u^{n},u^{*}\right),\\[5.69054pt]
&\textrm{(SI-LM predictor of order $p^{*}$)},\\[5.69054pt]
\textrm{corrector (C):}\\
v^{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v^{n-j}&={\Delta t}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right)+{\Delta t}b_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u^{*},v^{n+1}\right),\\[5.69054pt]
&\textrm{(SI-LM corrector of order $p$)}\end{split}

(57)

Finally we update the solution
u^{n+1}=v^{n+1}
.

The coefficients
\bar{a}_{j}^{(k)}
,
\bar{b}_{j}^{(k)}
, depend on the specific linear multistep scheme of order
p^{*}
in the predictor step and
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
depend on the specific linear multistep scheme of order
p
for the corrector one.

Compared to implicit methods, in the semi-implicit predictor–corrector (SI-PC) approach (
57
), a more practical procedure is usually to specify in advance how many iterations of the corrector are needed at each step to achieve the desired order of convergence. In this way, the scheme (
57
) is both accurate and stable. We will apply the SI-PC pair (
57
) in order to correct the order of convergence.
Usually, the number of iterations of the corrector depends on the difference between orders
p^{*}
and
p
.

In order to validate that our SI-PC scheme gets the corrector order, we start to introduce the local truncation error (LTE).
Following
[
18
]
, for a given linear multistep method

v^{n+1}=-\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v^{n-j}+{\Delta t}\sum_{j=-1}^{s-1}b_{j}{\mathcal{H}}\left(t^{n-j},u^{n-j},v^{n-j}\right)

(58)

we introduce the
linear difference operator

\mathcal{L}
associated with (
58
), defined by

\mathcal{L}(z(t);{\Delta t})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left[a_{j}z(t+j{\Delta t})-{\Delta t}b_{j}z^{\prime}(t+j{\Delta t}))\right],

(59)

where
z(t)\in C^{1}[a,b]
is an arbitrary function.
Hence for an order
p
method, it holds:

\mathcal{L}(z(t);\Delta t)=C_{p+1}\Delta t^{p+1}z^{(p+1)}(t)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+2}).

Let the predictor and corrector defined in (
57
) have associated linear difference
operator
\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}
, of order
p^{*}
,
p
and error constant
C^{*}_{p^{*}+1}
and
C_{p+1}
, respectively.

Now we assume that all the past values are exact, that is,

u^{n-j}=v^{n-j}=u(t_{n-j}),

(60)

for
j=0,1,..,s
. Let us denote by
{u}^{*}_{n+1}
and
{v}_{n+1}
the values at time
t_{n+1}
generated by the method when condition (
60
) is in force.
We assume that
u(t),v(t)\in\textrm{C}^{\hat{p}}[a,b]
, where
\hat{p}=\max(p^{*},p)
.
Then one has for the LTE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{*}(u(t_{n});\Delta t)&=C_{p^{*}+1}^{*}\Delta t^{p^{*}+1}u^{(p^{*}+1)}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p^{*}+2}),\\
\mathcal{L}(v(t_{n});\Delta t)&=C_{p+1}\Delta t^{p+1}v^{(p+1)}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+2}).\end{split}

(61)

From (
59
), we have for the predictor step

u(t_{n+1})+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{a}_{j}u(t_{n-j})=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{b}_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u(t_{n-j-1}),v(t_{n-j})\right)+{\Delta t}\bar{b}_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u(t_{n}),u(t_{n+1})\right)+\mathcal{L}^{*}(u(t_{n});\Delta t).

and, similarly, for the corrector one

v(t_{n+1})+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}v(t_{n-j})=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u(t_{n-j}),v(t_{n-j})\right)+{\Delta t}{b}_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u(t_{n+1}),v(t_{n+1})\right)+\mathcal{L}(v(t_{n});\Delta t).

The values
{u}^{*}_{n+1}
and
{v}_{n+1}
given by the method satisfy

u^{*}_{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{a}_{j}u_{n-j}=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\bar{b}_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u_{n-j},{v}_{n-j}\right)+{\Delta t}\bar{b}_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u_{n},u^{*}_{n+1}\right),

and

{v}_{n+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{j}{v}_{n-j}=\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}b_{j}\mathcal{H}\left(u_{n-j},{v}_{n-j}\right)+{\Delta t}{b}_{-1}\mathcal{H}\left(u^{*}_{n+1},{v}_{n+1}\right).

(62)

Subtracting and using the condition (
60
) we obtain

u(t_{n+1})-{u}_{n+1}=\Delta t\bar{b}_{-1}\left(\mathcal{H}(u(t_{n}),u(t_{n+1}))-\mathcal{H}(u_{n},u^{*}_{n+1})\right)+\mathcal{L}^{*}(u(t_{n});\Delta t),

(63)

and

v(t_{n+1})-{v}_{n+1}=\Delta tb_{-1}\left(\mathcal{H}(u(t_{n+1}),v(t_{n+1}))-\mathcal{H}(u^{*}_{n+1},v_{n+1})\right)+\mathcal{L}(v(t_{n});\Delta t).

(64)

Now, from (
60
) with
u(t_{n})=u_{n}
, we have

\mathcal{H}(u(t_{n}),u(t_{n+1}))-\mathcal{H}(u_{n},u^{*}_{n+1})=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial u}[u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}],

where the derivative
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial u}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\mathcal{H}
with respect to
u
evaluated at a point
\eta_{n+1}
lying in the interior of the segment joining
u(t_{n+1})
to
u^{*}_{n+1}
.
Equation (
63
) now yields

[I-\Delta t\bar{b}_{-1}\partial{\mathcal{H}}/\partial u][u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}]=\mathcal{L}^{*}(u(t_{n});\Delta t).

(65)

Proceeding for the
v
-component, we have

[I-\Delta t\,b_{-1}\partial{\mathcal{H}}/\partial v][v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}]=\Delta t\,b_{-1}\partial{\mathcal{H}}/\partial u\,[u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}]+\mathcal{L}(v(t_{n});\Delta t).

(66)

In (
65
) to a first-order approximation, that is, neglecting the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
term on the left-side, the LTE is simply the difference between the exact solution and the numerical one, subject to the condition (
60
), i.e.,

u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}=\mathcal{L}^{*}(u(t_{n});\Delta t).

(67)

Denoting by
\mu
the number of correction steps, we first set
\mu=1
. That is, we use the predictor value
u^{*}_{n+1}
to compute
v_{n+1}
, and then evaluate the order of the SI–PC method after a single correction by analyzing the local truncation error LTE, under condition (
60
).

Therefore, from (
67
), for the
u
-component we have

\displaystyle u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}=C_{p^{*}+1}^{*}\Delta t^{p^{*}+1}u^{(p^{*}+1)}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p^{*}+2}).

(68)

Whereas in order to evaluate the LTE for the
v
-component, we have to make the following considerations.

First consider the case
p^{*}\geq p
. On substituting (
65
) into (
66
) we get

\displaystyle v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}=C_{p+1}\Delta t^{p+1}v^{(p+1)}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+2}).

(69)

Thus, if
p^{*}\geq p
and
\mu=1
, the LTE for the
v
-component is precisely the order of the corrector step.

Now we consider the case
p^{*}=p-1
and denote by
v_{n+1}^{(\mu)}
the numerical solution obtained from (
62
) after the
\mu
-th correction step. For
\mu=1
, on substituting again (
65
) into (
66
), from (
61
) we obtain that

v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}^{(1)}=\left(b_{-1}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial u^{*}}C^{*}_{p}u^{(p)}(t_{n})+C_{p+1}v^{(p+1)}(t_{n})\right)\Delta t^{p+1}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+2}).

(70)

Thus, after one correction
\mu=1
, in the case
p^{*}=p-1
, the LTE of the SI-PC method is not identical with that of the corrector one even though it has the same order of accuracy as the corrector, since the leading error constant is different.

Now, setting
u^{*}_{n+1}=v_{n+1}^{(1)}
, with

u(t_{n+1})-u^{*}_{n+1}=v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}^{(1)}
,
we recompute with a second correction
\mu=2
,
v_{n+1}^{(2)}
from (
62
),
and we find for the LTE that

v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}^{(2)}=\mathcal{L}(v(t_{n});\Delta t)=C_{p+1}\Delta t^{p+1}v^{(p+1)}(t_{n})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+2}),

namely, the SI-PC method has the same LTE of the corrector alone with the same leading error constant.

Finally, we consider the case
p^{*}=p-2
and
\mu=1
. On substituting (
65
) into (
66
), and from (

), we get that

v(t_{n+1})-v_{n+1}^{(1)}=b_{-1}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{H}}}{\partial u^{*}}C^{*}_{p-1}u^{(p-1)}(t_{n})\Delta t^{p}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{p+1}).

(71)

Thus, if
\mu=1
the order of the SI-PC method is only
p-1
.

Now, proceeding as before, we recompute
v_{n+1}^{(2)}
from (
62
)
and on substituting (
71
) into (

) we obtain an order
p+1
for the LTE of the SI-PC method, which is identical to that of the corrector. Nevertheless, also in this case, the corresponding leading error constant is not the same. Continuing this process iteratively, for
\mu\geq 3
, and using again (

) with

the SI-PC method achieves the same LTE of the corrector alone.

We therefore state the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1

Consider the differential equation (
11
), with
\mathcal{H}(t,u,v)
continuously differentiable and let
v(t)=u(t)
be its solution. Assume condition (
60
) and consider a SI-PC method (
57
), where the predictor method (P) has order
p^{*}
and the corrector one has order
p
. Then we have the following cases:

•

if
p^{*}\geq p
, for all
\mu\leq 1
the LTE for the numerical solution
v_{n+1}
is precisely that of the corrector (C) alone.

•

if
p^{*}=p-1
, with
\mu=1
the LTE of the SI-PC method (
57
) is not identical with that of the corrector even though it has the same order of accuracy as the corrector. With
\mu\geq 2
, the LTE for the numerical solution
v_{n+1}
is precisely that of the corrector (C) alone.

•

if
p^{*}<p
, with
\mu=p-p^{*}
, the SI-PC method and corrector have the same order but different error constants. With
\mu>p-p^{*}
the order is that of the corrector step.

In order to derive our semi-implicit predictor-corrector method, we choose as a predictor step the SI-Euler method with
p^{*}=1
and correct the numerical solution
v_{n+1}^{(\mu)}
in (
62
) by a fixed number
\mu
of suitable iterations in order to reach the correct order of convergence. For instance,
let us choose a SI-BDF method of order
p
for the correction steps in (
62
). We call this type of predictor-corrector schemes SI-PC
μ
BDF
p
.

Example 4.1

For example if we use a SI-BDF3 as corrector,
in order to achieve the correct order of convergence, i.e.
p=3
, we require at least
\mu=2
.
Note that with
\mu=3
we can improve the accuracy because the third iteration also allows one to recover the error constant of the corrector method, while
\mu>3

does not provide further improvement.

Below we provide the algorithm with SI-Euler method as predictor and SI-BDF3 as corrector, in order to achieve the third order with
\mu=2
(or
\mu=3
).

Algorithm: SI-PC
{}^{\,\mu}
BDF3

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\textbf{Predictor (P):}\\
&\quad u^{*,(0)}=u^{n}+\Delta t\,\mathcal{H}(u^{n},u^{*,(0)}),\qquad\text{(SI-Euler, order }p=1\text{)}\\[5.69054pt]
&\textbf{Corrector (C):}\\[-2.84526pt]
&\quad\text{for }\nu=0,1,...,\mu-1\text{ do}\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad u^{*}=u^{*,(\nu)},\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad u^{*,(\nu+1)}=\frac{18}{11}u^{n}-\frac{9}{11}u^{n-1}+\frac{2}{11}u^{n-2}+\frac{6}{11}\,\Delta t\,\mathcal{H}(u^{*},u^{*,(\nu+1)}),\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\text{(SI-BDF3, order }p=3\text{)}\\[2.84526pt]
&\quad\text{end for}\\[5.69054pt]
&\textbf{Set:}\quad v^{n+1}=u^{*,(\nu+1)}.\end{split}

(72)

In the numerical tests section, we have chosen BDF methods as our linear multistep schemes because of their good stability properties.

In (
72
), to perform the predictor step and the
\mu
correction steps, a certain number of linear systems must be solved.
Specifically, one linear system is required for the predictor (the SI-Euler method) and
\mu
systems for the corrections, resulting in a total of
\mu+1
linear systems per time step.

For instance, to achieve third-order accuracy in (
72
), three linear systems must be solved. However, by solving a fourth linear system, one can get the same error constant of the corrector method.
Similarly, if the corrector is replaced by a SI-BDF4 scheme, to obtain fourth-order accuracy at least four linear systems are required. In general, by means of this strategy, a BDF of order
p
requires solving
p
or
p+1
linear systems per time step.
This behavior will be illustrated in the numerical tests presented in Section
4.3
.

In any case, once we choose an order
p
corrector method, we could choose any suitable order
p-1
predictor method, such as for example
a
chain
of semi-implicit linear multistep methods of order
1
to
p-1
. Of course, this choice can affect the computational costs of the overall method.

4.2
Computational Aspects.

In order to show the effective benefit of these methods, we remark again that in general these semi-implicit predictor-corrector schemes provide
a way to efficiently compute an accurate solution
of problem (
10
) by means of a fixed number of linear systems solve, even when the problem presents stiffness
due to high order spatial derivatives.

Indeed, a semi-implicit predictor-corrector BDF scheme of order
p
requires solving
p
or
p+1
linear systems per time step,
and does not suffer from instability as in the case when using an explicit predictor.

The semi-implicit predictor-corrector BDFs have computational costs per step
comparable to SI-RK methods
[
23
]
that have order
p\leq 3
,
but provide a simple and effective way to obtain higher order schemes,
since they are based on regular linear multistep methods. Indeed, semi-implicit RK schemes are based on existing additive IMEX methods
[
4
]
,
so, for methods of order
p\geq 3
are needed at least
p+1
stages and hence
p+1
linear systems to solve.
Furthermore, only a few high-order (order
p>3
) additive IMEX-RK schemes exist in the literature (e.g.,
Carpenter et al.,
[
16
]
), and they typically require 6 to 8 stages to reach fourth- or fifth-order
accuracy. Finally, existing IMEX RK schemes have low stage order, and hence they can suffer from order reduction.

In any case, since the proposed methods are multistep, a suitable
starting procedure
is needed
[
18
,
6
,
14
]
.
As usual in the context of multistep linear methods,
the starting values can be approximated by means of a
SI-PC
μ
BDF method of order
p-1
applied with a reduced
stepsize
\Delta t/m
, with an integer, suitably chosen
m
.
In the numerical tests here reported a maximum value of
m=4
has been used for Test 1 and 2, while a maximum value of
m=16
has been used for Test 3 and 4.

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper, following the ideas developed in
[
23
]
we have developed a SI strategy for Rosenbrock type and multistep methods based on an implicit-explicit setting for solving high order dissipative, dispersive and special biharmonic-type equations in one dimension. The SI schemes so designed for high order time-dependent PDEs
do not require nonlinear iterative solvers and avoid the severe time-step restrictions
typically encountered when using explicit methods. Numerical experiments show that the schemes are stable and achieve the expected orders of accuracy for large time step.

Since the main issue of this paper is to design an appropriate high-order time discretization for time-dependent PDEs, the numerical experiments are only performed for one-dimensional equations. For the space discretization, we considered only classical finite difference spatial discretization because of its simplicity in design and coding and it is straightforward to extend to higher-dimensional equations. However, other types of space discretization can be used.
