Implementing a surveillance and prevention program for post-caesarean surgical site infections in Kenya
Authors: Anne Njoroge, Matthew Westercamp, Loyce Kihungi, Mary Ndinda, Evelyn Wesangula, Catherine Mwangi, Faith Muthoni, George Owiso, Linus Ndegwa, John Lynch, Peter Rabinowitz, Elizabeth Bancroft
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical Site Infection (SSI) surveillance efforts in sub-Saharan Africa have largely been documented in research settings. Such programs need to be institutionalized within routine healthcare settings for sustainability. We evaluate the feasibility of setting up an SSI surveillance and prevention program within public settings in Kenya.MethodsFacility infection prevention and control (IPC) committees were established, trained, and resourced on SSI surveillance and prevention in two large hospitals in Kenya. A surgical checklist and monitoring form assessing surgical care bundle implementation and wound status before discharge was included in the medical charts of women who had cesarean section (CS) deliveries. Post-discharge wound assessment interviews were targeted for completion 14–30 days after surgery. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests (χ2) for differences.ResultsThe program enrolled 1,039 women undergoing CS. Only 65% (675/1039) were reached for any post-discharge wound assessment, with 28% (186/675) reached within the targeted 30 days. Of these, 7% (12/186) had an SSI. Six of these women (6/37 = 16%) were identified within 14 days post-operatively while the remaining six (6/149 = 4%) were identified 15–30 days post-operatively. Surgical care bundle implementation differed by site, with pre-operative antibiotic use at 100% in Thika vs. 66% in Kitale, with variation in the antibiotics used. Blood glucose monitoring at 23 vs. 32% respectively. Hair removal was low overall at 2%.ConclusionWhile setting up an SSI surveillance and prevention program is feasible, efforts and resources targeting post-discharge follow-up and case finding should be prioritized. National guidelines standardizing surgical antibiotic prophylaxis are needed as part of antimicrobial stewardship programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01633-3.Keywords: Surgical site infection (SSI), Surgical care bundle, Sub-Saharan africa, Infection prevention and control (IPC)

Introduction

Nearly two-thirds of the global maternal deaths are estimated to occur in sub-Saharan Africa [1–3]. Caesarean section (CS) delivery is one of the most common operative procedures performed in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for as much as 80% of the surgical workload. While CSs are a critical intervention to prevent maternal mortality [4], post-operative complication following CS in sub-Saharan Africa are the highest in the world with surgical site infection (SSI) contributing to significant morbidity, mortality and additional healthcare system costs [5–7]. The World Health Organization (WHO) provides global evidence-based recommendations for the prevention of SSIs and recognizes SSI surveillance as a cornerstone of SSI prevention [8, 9].

Across Sub-Saharan Africa there is marked variability in SSI rates between hospitals nationally and across countries [10]. In Tanzania, reported SSI rates after CS have ranged from 11% in a tertiary care research and teaching hospital [11], to 24% in a district hospital [12], and up to 48% in a regional referral hospital [13]. SSI rates following CS ranging from 4 to 10% have been reported in the same hospital in Kenya [14–16]. Such variability may be explained by differences in the surveillance methods and lack of standardized definitions of SSI used. However, differences in infection prevention and control practices likely also play some role [17]. Despite the limited and variable data available, the burden of SSI post-CS in lower resource settings is consistently and substantially higher than that in high resource settings [18].

In the absence of laboratory confirmation for SSI, surveillance strategies based on patient symptoms need to include screening questions with optimum sensitivity and interrater reliability to improve SSI ascertainment [16, 19]. With overstretched staff, surveillance data collection processes need to be simple and easy to integrate into routine surgical practice to minimize additional workload and enhance sustainability.

To assess feasibility of integrating healthcare-acquired infection (HAI) surveillance and prevention activities into the healthcare system in Kenya, the Ministry of Health prioritized post CS SSI surveillance as a pilot initiative. Post CS SSIs were selected for surveillance because the procedure is common, the burden and impact of infection is substantial [3], and evidence based prevention strategies are well described and generally feasible [1]. Furthermore, CS SSIs can be identified by symptoms without requiring laboratory confirmation which allows for routine surveillance of this HAI in facilities with limited or no microbiology laboratory capacity.

The development of Kenya’s post CS SSI surveillance and prevention strategy was guided by WHO’s recommendation that multimodal strategies for improving infection prevention and control be embedded into routine service delivery activities with limited external resource requirements [4]. This manuscript evaluates the feasibility of implementing an SSI surveillance and prevention program within routine healthcare settings at two regional referral hospitals in Kenya. The interventions were framed using the WHO Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) multimodal improvement strategy, a quality improvement framework specific to IPC [20]. Feasibility as an outcome in implementation research [21], was defined as the extent to which the surveillance and infection prevention components of the program would be executed within routine service delivery activities.

Methods

Infection prevention activities, including introduction of the surgical care bundle for CS SSI prevention, began in September 2019, while the post CS SSI surveillance component was initiated in November 2019.

Location

Two public regional hospitals participated in the pilot: Thika Level 5 Hospital and Kitale County Referral Hospital. Both pilot facilities have engaged in prior quality improvement initiatives organized by the core study team [22]. Thika town is located approximately 30 miles (48 km) northwest of Nairobi and has an official population of 280,000. Thika hospital serves the greater Thika area with a total bed capacity of 467, performs approximately 160 CS per month, and is managed by the Kiambu County government [23]. Kitale is an agricultural town located in western Kenya with a population of approximately 162,000. Kitale hospital has a bed capacity of 250 and performed approximately 100 CS per month in 2019. Kitale Hospital is managed by the Trans Nzoia County government, which is in the process of upgrading the facility to provide updated health services and alleviate overcrowding [24]. In public facilities in Kenya, one nurse typically attends to an average of 19 patients per shift across various service delivery areas [25].

Infection Prevention Activities.

Project activities focused on three of the five elements (system change, training and education, monitoring and feedback) of the WHO infection prevention and control (IPC) multimodal improvement strategy due to time and resource restrictions [20]. (Fig. 1). At the beginning of the project, emphasis was placed on system change (step 1) and training and education (step 2).

WHO multimodal improvement framework as applied to the post CS delivery SSI surveillance and prevention project

System change included establishing and strengthening facility IPC committees. In Thika, there was an existing IPC committee, but it was dormant (did not have regular meetings). In Kitale, the project facilitated the appointment of an IPC committee chair (a nurse trained in the national IPC curriculum) and other committee members, derived from various departments. The project provided technical guidance to ensure key departments were represented, such as pharmacy, the laboratory and health records departments. The project supported regular meetings, through provision of refreshments during the meetings, a standing agenda and standardized meeting format at the beginning before the IPC committees got a rhythm but with time, meeting formats evolved organically. Besides this project, other issues discussed included hand hygiene monitoring and supplies, waste management and orientation of student trainees in IPC. System change also included provision of material resources needed for post-discharge follow-up by phone interview (i.e., dedicated phones and call minutes) to facilitate surveillance activities.

Training and education (step 2) activities focused on introducing SSI surveillance methods and pre-identified evidence-based best practices (i.e., surgical care bundles) for the prevention of SSIs [9]. Surgical care bundles included pre-operative surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) with guidance on recommended antibiotics and timing of antibiotic administration, monitoring and control of perioperative blood glucose, and avoidance of pre-operative shaving for hair removal. Materials were developed for each topic and delivered in dedicated educational sessions to obstetricians, pharmacists, nurses, laboratory technicians and health records officers involved in caesarean delivery services at the two hospitals. Formal training was conducted at the start of the project, but educational reminders were included in the monitoring and feedback sessions.

Monitoring and feedback (step 3) activities were a central component of this pilot and were operationalized through the introduction of a surgical checklist and monitoring form in the patient’s medical chart. This form was used as a best practice reference by the surgical team, practice record, and standardized surveillance data collection instrument. Review of the surgical checklist and monitoring form was the principal method for monitoring the progress and impact of the initiative and results were periodically discussed with care teams. The surgical checklist and monitoring form was designed to be minimally disruptive, including just eight data elements required at the point of the procedure (Supplement Table S.1). Each facility identified staff persons that were responsible for completing the form, which was also required for cases admitted at night. Feedback was provided through the sharing of summary data including surgical care bundle compliance and follow-up calls, presented as simple bar charts comparing month to month trends. There were no consequences tied to the data, as it did not indicate which staff member had completed the form.

The hospital administration played a supportive role in the project. Under system change, they formally designated the IPC committee, including appointing the IPC committee chair with a formal letter, which in turn relieved the chair of other duties. They also provided space and time for regular IPC team meetings to discuss project progress, setbacks next steps, as well as other IPC-related issues. Under training, the administration allowed trainees to be excused from their regular duties on training days and arranged for alternate staff coverage. For monitoring and feedback, they approved the integration of the surgical checklist and monitoring form into patient medical charts. The administration remained informed through quarterly reports provided by the IPC committee chair.

The surveillance component ended in July 2020; but the use of the surgical care bundle, led and managed by the facility, continues.

Surveillance for CS SSI.

Pilot CS SSI surveillance activities were divided into three steps: (1) monitoring the practices recorded in the surgical checklist, (2) recording of wound assessment at patient discharge and (3) post-discharge patient phone wound assessment interview. Post-discharge wound assessment interviews were conducted by the IPC focal nurse as semi-structured interviews using a standardized script with a defined set of definitional elements used to determine SSI case status. Wound assessment interviews represented added responsibility for hospital staff without additional remuneration and were to be conducted on day14 and day 30 after surgery. If the SSI case definition was met at the first contact, the participant was not contacted again.

The SSI case definition used included either the report of pus (i.e., cloudy and/or foul-smelling discharge) from the wound OR report of fever (measured > 38 C or consistent report of symptoms) with noted erythema (discoloration/redness) in the skin around the wound and/or pain beyond that which is normally expected after surgery [26]. Post-discharge wound assessments did not include classification of wound depth and individuals reporting signs suggestive of active infection were asked to return to the healthcare facility for care.

Descriptive statistics and data visualizations were completed and shared with facilities regularly throughout the pilot period. Routine facility electronic surveillance records available within Kenya’s District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) system were used to obtain the total number of CSs in the facility during the pilot period. Overall and stratified proportions were compared using confidence intervals from binomial exact calculations. (Kohn MA, Senyak J. Sample Size Calculators [website]. UCSF CTSI. 28 September 2024. Results

During the surveillance period between November 2019 and July 2020, 1,039 caesarean deliveries were enrolled in the surveillance system: 59% (610/1,039; CI:54−60%) from Thika and 41% (429/1,039; CI:37–43%) from Kitale. Per facility records during this period, a total of 2,179 CS occurred, indicating that less than half (48%; CI:46−50%) of CSs were enrolled. 51% (429/848; CI:47−54%) at Kitale and 46% (610/1,331; CI:43−49%) at Thika. (Fig. 2) There was no change in the enrollment rate over time.

Prophylactic post-operative IV antibiotic use, which was not part of the surgical care bundles, was documented in 38% (231/610; CI:34% − 42%) of patients at Thika and 100% (429/429) at Kitale. Antibiotics used included ceftriaxone/metronidazole (97%), ampicillin or flucloxacillin used alone or in combination with metronidazole (2%). Oral antibiotics for prophylaxis at discharge (not recommended) were documented for 59% (360/610; CI:55% − 63%) of patients at Thika and 90% (387/429; CI:87% -93%) at Kitale. IV antibiotics were typically administered for 72 hours. Common antibiotics prescribed at discharge for five to seven days included cefuroxime (34%) and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid combined with metronidazole (34%). In total, 24% (146/610; CI:21% -27%) of patients at Thika and 56% (240/429; CI:51% − 61%) at Kitale had documented antibiotic receipt at all three time-points: pre-operative, post-operative on the wards, and at discharge. Use of prophylactic post-operative antibiotics did not change with time. Of note, there are no national guidelines on choice of antibiotics for surgical prophylaxis and the two hospitals did not have hospital-specific SAP guidelines.

Results from pre-operative blood glucose monitoring were documented in 22.5% (137/610; CI:19% − 26%) of procedures at Thika and 31.9% (137/429; CI:28% − 36%) at Kitale, while use of a razor for pre-operative hair removal was documented in just 2% (22/1039; CI:1% − 3%) of procedures at Kitale and was not documented at Thika.

There were no SSIs identified during the wound assessment before discharge. In total, 65% (675/1039; CI:62% − 68%) of patients had a post discharge wound assessment; 21% (214/1039; CI:18% − 23%) were called but not reached, and 14% (150/1039; CI:12% − 17%) had missing data (i.e., no documentation regarding follow-up calls). (Fig. 2). Of those that had a post discharge wound assessment, 92% (620/675; CI:90% − 94%) included a date of interview and 28% (186/675; CI:24% − 31%) had the assessment during the defined 30-day follow-up period. The median number of days between surgery and follow-up contact was 30 days (IQR:27–33), with no statistically significant difference comparing those who were reached vs. those who were not reached (OR: 0.99, 95% CI:0.99 -1.00).

Among the 186 women reached within thirty days of their procedure (per protocol), 20% (37/186; CI:14% − 26%) were contacted within 14 days, with a follow-up yield (women identified to have an SSI) of 16% (6/37; CI:4% − 28%). Among those contacted between 15 and 30 days, follow-up yield was 4% (6/149; CI:1% − 7%). Over the nine-month surveillance period, 12 total infections were detected per protocol (elicitation of symptoms within 30 days post CS) for an overall post-caesarean delivery SSI rate of 6.5% (12/186; CI:3% − 10%). No participant was contacted and reached twice i.e. day 14 and day 30 post-op, as per protocol.

In addition to lack of documentation on the date of follow-up, other missing data included whether they were called but not reached vs. not contacted at 14% (150/1039; CI:12% − 17%) and date of attempted contact for those who were not reached at 88% (188/214; CI:83% − 92%). Noted data errors included date of discharge prior to date of CS (123/1039, 12%; CI:9% − 14%) and dates of follow-up prior to discharge (384/1039, 37%; CI:34% − 40%). When observed, evident transposition of day and month was manually corrected.

Discussion

This pilot project to implement SSI surveillance after CS in Kenya has important lessons learned for future implementation projects within routine healthcare services in resource limited settings. It demonstrated feasibility in implementation of CS SSI surveillance and surgical bundle for SSI prevention, including the implementation of IPC surgical checklists. It also identified a noteworthy overuse of post-surgical antibiotics that could lead to antibiotic resistance. However, this pilot initiative only captured about half of the procedures performed (48%; 1,039/2,179) and only 65% (675/1,039) of these had a post-discharge wound assessment documented. While the lack of complete outcome data is unfortunate, the description of challenges and experience-based considerations are useful both for Kenya, as pilot activities are expanded to other sites, and to other institutions considering CS SSI surveillance and prevention initiatives. Overall implementation challenges included limitations in enrollment and timeliness of follow-up, poor data quality, and the absence of guidelines and policies needed to guide the use of antibiotics for surgical prophylaxis.

In this pilot, post discharge patient surveillance and case finding were resource intensive and logistically challenging. Despite dedicated efforts, staff were able to contact only a fourth of women with any post-discharge wound assessment (28%; 186/675) within the specified follow-up period introducing substantial increase in the risk of recall bias. The limitation in total enrolment and timeliness of follow-up, lack of documentation, and overall lack of adherence to the surveillance protocol represent critical points of failure that dramatically impact the value and utility of results. The effort required for timely, systematic, and complete case finding should not be underestimated and must be prioritized in early planning and resource allocation. Such efforts could include designating specific staff to conduct follow-up activities and ensuring that these responsibilities are formally documented as part of their official roles, preventing the perception of additional uncompensated work. Additionally, integrating the surgical checklist and monitoring form into the medical chart as a required component of the patient record could promote its consistent use.

Lack of additional staff or remuneration in this project could explain why the monitoring and feedback did not lead to improvements in the enrolment and follow-up rates.

While the US CDC’s National Healthcare Safety Network surveillance period for SSI following caesarean section is 30 days [27], there is need to consider a shorter surveillance period in lower-resource settings where patient follow-up may be a challenge. Our finding of decreased yield in SSI identification with longer follow-up is supported by other studies in similar settings. In Burundi, the median time to onset of SSI was day 6 post-operatively (IQR 2–17) [28], in Sierra Leone 4 days [29] and in Tanzania, all the SSIs occurred between post-op day 3 and day 22 [11]. These results suggest that shorter ascertainment periods, when majority of the post-CS SSIs are identified, may be an efficient way to optimize limited follow-up resources.

The data errors were attributable to use of spreadsheets for data transcription and management. Spreadsheets allowed for easy implementation and minimal staff training; however, data entry errors were common, and our data collection methods allowed limited options for ongoing validity checking or centralized data management. To the extent that resources and process flow at hospitals allow, dedicated electronic data capture designed with logic checks and data value restrictions would be helpful in improving data completeness and validity. Data visualization mechanisms for continuous feedback to and data use by facility staff should also be considered.

Evidence-based bundles for preventing infection implemented in parallel to this surveillance project focused on increasing appropriate use of preoperative antibiotics, decreasing shaving for preoperative hair removal, and increasing perioperative blood glucose monitoring. Preoperative skin antisepsis was not considered in the infection prevention bundles addressed; a notable omission given the high-quality evidence supporting this intervention [30]. In general, data on surgical and clinical practices associated with infection risk before surgery and in the in-patient setting were both more consistently collected and had fewer errors compared to follow-up data. Additionally, results were directly linked to the desired set of actions targeted for behavior change (e.g., increasing the proportion of patients with peri-surgical blood glucose monitoring). Where follow-up resources are limited, SSI surveillance and IPC initiatives should consider the systematic monitoring of practices with strong evidence of association with reduced infection risk [31].

While it is beyond the scope of this pilot project to characterize the documented antibiotic use as appropriate or inappropriate, we observed substantial deviation from the WHO guidelines of a single dose of a first-generation cephalosporin or penicillin prior to skin incision [32]. In this study, both pilot facilities had a high use of third generation cephalosporins and metronidazole, administered pre- and post-operatively. Third generation cephalosporins are in the “Watch” category of the WHO AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) antibiotics classification and are recommended as second choice empiric treatment options for a limited number of specific infectious syndromes due to their high resistance potential [33]. This is in keeping with similar studies in Kenya [14, 34]. Our findings support conclusions from other studies in Kenya that discipline-specific clinical guidelines for antibiotic use are needed [35]. Such guidelines would facilitate institutionalization of appropriate antibiotic use for surgical prophylaxis, including the availability of appropriate medicines, staff training, monitoring of usage, and feedback mechanisms.

Our results show that preoperative shaving was uncommon in this population at the baseline and thus we didn’t observe any changes in this practice. While both sites had additional blood glucose meters provided at the start of the project, blood glucose monitoring was documented in just over a quarter of the population, with no change over time. That it was unspecified whose role it was to monitor blood glucose peri-operatively (theatre nurse or anaesthetist) was cited as a reason why no change was observed in this practice over time. We highlight this as a lesson learnt: even standard of care practices sometimes fail to be implemented effectively without clear role delineation. Process mapping and analysis are essential in such quality improvement programs to clarify roles and responsibilities, for successful implementation.

Our ability to assess or measure the fourth (“sell it”) and fifth (“live it”) components of WHO’s multimodal strategy was limited. Additionally, the lack of detailed process data – such as the specific number and timing of feedback sessions – posed a limitation. Such data could have supported inferences about why certain components, such as monitoring and feedback, did not work as expected, particularly in improving data quality over time.

Conclusion

The implementation challenges encountered in this SSI surveillance pilot project highlight the need for prioritizing resources and planning for post-discharge follow-up and case finding for successful SSI surveillance in Kenya. Proper data management systems and data use support, including data on risk-lowering practices, could trigger behavior changes that reduces the risk of surgical site infection.

Contextually specific and evidence-based policy regarding preoperative and postoperative antibiotics remains a cornerstone for antimicrobial resistance prevention. National surgical prophylaxis antibiotic use guidance should be developed and implemented as part of antimicrobial stewardship programs.
