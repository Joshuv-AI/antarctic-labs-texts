1.
Introduction

The study of polynomial Diophantine equations of the form

(1)

f(x)=g(y)

have been of interest to many number theorists. Observe that a defining equation of an elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
with
a,b\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Q}$}
,
4a^{3}+27b^{2}\neq 0
is an example of such equation. The essential question concerning (
1
) is whether there exist finitely or infinitely many integral solutions of (
1
) in
S
-integers
x
and
y
. By Siegel’s classical theorem, it follows that an irreducible algebraic curve defined over a number field can have only finitely many
S
-integral points, unless it has genus zero and at most two points at infinity. However, this approach is ineffective since it does not provide explicit bounds for the size of the solutions
(x,y)
. Over the years, many authors such as Baker, Sprindžuk, Brindza, Bugeaud, and others developed effective versions of this finiteness result, especially for particular classes of equations (see for example
[
1
]
,
[
20
]
). A major advancement in this direction was made by Bilu and Tichy
[
2
]
, who established precise conditions under which (
1
) has infinitely many
S
-integer solutions with a bounded denominator. Their work marked a major step forward in the study of Diophantine equations of the type (
1
). It turns out to be more convenient to investigate a slightly more general version of the problem.

One well-known problem involving the Diophantine equations with linear recurrences is to determine how many zeros occur in such a sequence, or more generally, to estimate the number of indices
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying the equation
G_{n}(x)=a
for a given
a
in a function field (see
[
8
]
). Glass, Loxton, and van der Poorten
[
12
]
proved that if the sequence
(G_{n}(x))_{n=0}^{\infty}
is nonperiodic and nondegenerate,
then there exist only finitely many pairs of integers
m,n
with
m>n\geq 0
such that

G_{n}(x)=G_{m}(x).

This result has generalized to an equation of the form

(2)

G_{n}(x)=G_{m}(P(x)),

where
P(x)\in K[x]
(see
[
5
]
).
In
[
4
]
, Dujella and Tichy investigated the family of linear recurrence sequences defined by

G_{n+1}(x)=xG_{n}(x)+BG_{n-1}(x),\quad G_{0}(x)=0,\quad G_{1}(x)=1,

where
B
is a nonzero integer. They proved that for such sequences, there exist no polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
satisfying (
2
)
whenever
m,n\geq 3
and
m\neq n
. As a continuation of this, Fuchs
[
7
]
obtained conditions under which the Diophantine equation (
2
) has finitely many solutions in
n
and
m
for a third order linear recurrence. Moreover, Fuchs et al.
[
6
]
have generalized this result for an arbitrary degree recurrence.
By applying the result due to Bilu and Tichy
[
2
]
, these findings led to the conclusion that the Diophantine equation

G_{n}(x)=G_{m}(y)

admits only finitely many integer solutions in
n,m,x,y
with
n\neq m
. Further, considering simple linear recurrences of polynomials, Fuchs and Heintze
[
11
]
examined the Diophantine equation

G_{n}(x)=H_{m}(y)

and proved that it admits infinitely many rational solutions with a bounded denominator only when certain specific identity hold.
In
[
13
]
, Kreso has given a similar result concerning (
1
) when
f
and
g
are lacunary polynomial. By analyzing the properties of monodromy group of a polynomial, Kreso and Tichy have given a generalized result in
[
14
]
.

The theory of functional decomposition
f=f_{1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{m}
of a complex polynomial
f
into indecomposable complex polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
was developed by J. F. Ritt
[
19
]
, where a complex polynomial
f
is said to be
indecomposable if
\deg f>1
and it cannot be represented as a composition of two lower
degree polynomials. Several authors have investigated the structure and properties of decomposable polynomial. The decomposability among terms of second-order linear recurrence sequences has been studied under some assumptions (see
[
10
,
15
]
). The complete decomposition of linear combinations
of the Bernoulli and Euler polynomial has been studied (see
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
).

In this paper, firstly, we extend a result of Fuchs and Heintze
[
11
]
to the case of polynomial sequences defined by a polynomial recurrence relation over a number field. Secondly, we extend the main result of
[
10
]
for a third order polynomial recurrence. Further, we study the decomposition properties of
sum of two terms of a binary recurrence sequences of polynomials. In Section
2
, we give the required notation and state our main results. In Section
3
, we describe finiteness criterion proposed by Bilu and Tichy, which is essential to prove one of our result. We define projective height over a function field and state some properties of height functions. Also, we state important results like Brownawell-Maser theorem, Castelnuovo’s inequality, and Riemann’s inequality which are essential to prove main theorems. Finally, in Section
4
, we prove main theorems. Our proof closely follow the proofs in
[
11
]
and
[
10
]
.

2.
Notation and results

Let
K
be a number field,
\mathcal{O}_{S}
be the ring of integers of
K
and
(U_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be the recurrence sequence of polynomials of order
d\geq 2
defined by

(3)

U_{n}(x)=a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{n}+\cdots+a_{d}(x)\alpha_{d}(x)^{n},

where
a_{1}(x),\ldots,a_{d}(x)\in K[x]
and the polynomial characteristic roots
\alpha_{1}(x),\ldots,\alpha_{d}(x)\in K[x]
. Again, we rewrite
a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{n}
as follows:

(4)

a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{n}=\eta_{\ell}x^{\ell}+\eta_{\ell-1}x^{\ell-1}+\eta_{\ell-2}x^{\ell-2}+\cdots+\eta_{0},

where
\ell=n\deg\alpha_{1}+\deg a_{1}
and
\eta_{i}\in K,0\leq i\leq\ell
. Furthermore, we assume the following:

(i)

U_{n}(x)
satisfy the dominant root condition, that is
\deg\alpha_{1}>\max_{i=2,\ldots,d}\deg\alpha_{i}
and having at most one constant characteristic root.

(ii)

\deg a_{1}\geq\max_{i=2,\ldots,d}\deg a_{i}
.

(iii)

U_{n}(x)
cannot be written in the form
\hat{a_{1}}\hat{\alpha_{1}}(x)^{n}+\hat{a_{2}}\hat{\alpha_{2}}^{n}
for
\hat{\alpha_{1}}(x)\in K[x]
a perfect power of a linear polynomial and
\hat{a_{1}},\hat{a_{2}},\hat{\alpha_{2}}\in K
.

(iv)

The coefficients
\eta_{\ell-1}
and
\eta_{\ell-2}
in (
4
) are zero.

We will refer to the assumptions (i)-(iv) by saying
U_{n}(x)=a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{n}+\cdots+a_{d}(x)\alpha_{d}(x)^{n}

n-th polynomial in a linear recurrence sequence of the desired structure.

Remark 2.1
.

It is possible to choose the coefficients of
a_{1}(x)
so that the assumption (iv) holds. For that, let

,
a_{1}(x)=b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}
,
\alpha_{1}(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^{2}
, where

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(x)^{3}=c_{0}^{3}

\displaystyle+3c_{0}^{2}c_{1}x+(3c_{0}c_{1}^{2}+3c_{0}^{2}c_{2})x^{2}+(6c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}^{3})x^{3}

\displaystyle+(3c_{0}c_{2}^{2}+3c_{1}^{2}c_{0})x^{4}+3c_{1}c_{2}^{2}x^{5}+c_{2}^{3}x^{6}.

Let

(5)

a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{3}=\sum_{i=0}^{8}\eta_{i}x^{i}.

Observe that
\ell=8
. We now determine the conditions under which the coefficients
\eta_{7}
and
\eta_{6}
are zero. From (

)

\eta_{7}=b_{1}c_{2}^{3}+3b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}^{2}=0

implies

and

\eta_{6}=b_{0}c_{2}^{3}+b_{1}(3c_{1}c_{2}^{2})+b_{2}(3c_{0}c_{2}^{2}+3c_{1}^{2}c_{2})=0

For simplification and verification, we use Mathematica to compute the general formulas for the coefficients and determine the conditions under which they vanish. The code is written specifically for the given degrees of

and

, and the value of

We say that a polynomial
f
of degree at least 2 is decomposable if it can be written as
f=g\circ h
, where
g
and
h
are polynomials with degrees both at least 2. If no such representation exists, then
f
is said to be indecomposable. Let
S
be a finite set of places of
K
containing all Archimedean places. We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{S}
the ring of
S
-integers of the field
K
. We say that the equation
f(x)=g(y)
has infinitely many solutions with bounded
\mathcal{O}_{S}
-denominator if there exists
\delta\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
such that
f(x)=g(y)
has infinitely many solutions
(x,y)\in K\times K
with
\delta x,\delta y\in\mathcal{O}_{S}
.

The first result of this paper is stated as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
K
be the number field,
S
be a finite set of places of
K
containing all Archimedean places,
(U_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
and
(V_{m}(y))_{m\geq 0}
be linear recurrence sequences of the desired structure with power sum representation
U_{n}(x)=a_{1}(x)\alpha_{1}(x)^{n}+\cdots+a_{d}(x)\alpha_{d}(x)^{n}
and
V_{m}(y)=b_{1}(y)\beta_{1}(y)^{m}+\cdots+b_{s}(y)\beta_{s}(y)^{m}
respectively, where
a_{i}(x),

b_{j}(x),\alpha_{i}(x),\beta_{j}(x)\in K[x]
. If
n,m\geq 3
and
U_{n}(x)
is indecomposable, then

(6)

U_{n}(x)=V_{m}(y)

has infinitely many solutions
(x,y)\in K\times K
with a bounded
\mathcal{O}_{S}
-denominator if and only if there exists a polynomial

such that
V_{m}(y)=U_{n}(Q(y))
holds identically.

Moreover, if
V_{m}(y)
is also indecomposable, then in the above statement we can restrict

to be linear.

Let
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
be the polynomial in the indeterminate
x

\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
, that is,
\{W_{n}(x):n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}\}
that consist of elements of a simple non-degenerate third order linear recurrence sequence
(W_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
given by

(7)

W_{n+3}(x)=a(x)W_{n+2}(x)+b(x)W_{n+1}(x)+c(x)W_{n}(x)\quad\text{for }n\geq 0,

with
a(x),b(x),c(x),W_{0}(x),W_{1}(x),W_{2}(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
.
We denote
\lambda_{1}(x),\lambda_{2}(x),\lambda_{3}(x)
as the roots of the characteristic polynomial

T^{3}-a(x)T^{2}-b(x)T-c(x).

Setting
S=T-\frac{1}{3}a(x)
the characteristic polynomial becomes

S^{3}-p(x)S-q(x),

where

p(x)=\tfrac{1}{3}a(x)^{2}+b(x),\quad q(x)=\tfrac{2}{27}a(x)^{3}+\tfrac{1}{3}a(x)b(x)+c(x).

Let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(x)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{q(x)}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{p(x)}{3}\right)^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{27}a(x)^{3}c(x)-\tfrac{1}{108}a(x)^{2}b(x)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}a(x)b(x)c(x)+\tfrac{1}{4}c(x)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{27}b(x)^{3}.

Moreover, let

u(x)=\sqrt[3]{\frac{q(x)}{2}+\sqrt{\mathfrak{D}(x)}},\qquad v(x)=\sqrt[3]{\frac{q(x)}{2}-\sqrt{\mathfrak{D}(x)}}.

Then we have by Cardano’s formulae

(8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)+v(x)+\tfrac{1}{3}a(x),

(9)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{u(x)+v(x)}{2}+i\sqrt{3}\,\frac{u(x)-v(x)}{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}a(x),

(10)

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{u(x)+v(x)}{2}-i\sqrt{3}\,\frac{u(x)-v(x)}{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}a(x).

Since
(W_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
is simple, we have for
n\geq 0

(11)

W_{n}(x)=w_{1}(x)\lambda_{1}(x)^{n}+w_{2}(x)\lambda_{2}(x)^{n}+w_{3}(x)\lambda_{3}(x)^{n},

where

w_{1}(x),w_{2}(x),w_{3}(x)\in K(i\sqrt{3})\left(x,\sqrt{\mathfrak{D}(x)},u(x),v(x)\right).

A polynomial
h
is said to be

if

h(x)=\ell_{1}(x)\circ x^{n}\circ\ell_{2}(x)

for some
n\geq 2
and linear polynomials
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
. A polynomial

is said to be

h(x)=\ell_{1}(x)\circ T_{n}(x)\circ\ell_{2}(x)

for some

and linear polynomials
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[x]
, where
T_{n}(x)
is the
n

T_{n+2}(x)=2xT_{n+1}(x)-T_{n}(x),\quad T_{0}(x)=1,\;T_{1}(x)=x,\quad\text{for }n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}.

It is well known that
T_{mn}=T_{m}\circ T_{n}
for any
m,n\in\mbox{$\mathbb{N}$}
. Assume that
W_{n}
is decomposable for some

and write
W_{n}(x)=g(h(x))
, where

is indecomposable, and thus
\deg h\geq 2
. By Gauss’s lemma it follows that the polynomial
h(T)-h(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(h(x))[T]
is irreducible. Since
\deg h\geq 2
, there exists a root
y\neq x
in its splitting field over
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(h(x))
. Clearly,
h(x)=h(y)
.
Conjugating over
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(h(x))

, we get a sequence
(W_{n}(y))_{n\geq 0}
with
W_{n}(y)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}[y]
, which satisfies the same non-degenerate simple recurrence relation as

with

replaced by
y
. We note that

W_{n}(y)=t_{1}\mu_{1}^{n}+t_{2}\mu_{2}^{n}+t_{3}\mu_{3}^{n},

where
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in K(i\sqrt{3})(y,\sqrt{\mathfrak{D}(y)},u(y),v(y))
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}
are distinct roots of the characteristic polynomial of
(W_{n}(y))
in its splitting field over
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(y)
. Since

, we get
W_{n}(x)=W_{n}(y)
, that is

(12)

w_{1}\lambda_{1}^{n}+w_{2}\lambda_{2}^{n}+w_{3}\lambda_{3}^{n}=t_{1}\mu_{1}^{n}+t_{2}\mu_{2}^{n}+t_{3}\mu_{3}^{n}.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(u_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of polynomials defined as in (
16

C=C(\{a_{i},u_{i}:i=0,1\})

Remark 2.5
.

For

, we have

(18)

T_{nk}(x)\,T_{mk}(x)\;=\;\frac{T_{nk+mk}(x)+T_{nk-mk}(x)}{2}.

Using the identity

T_{nk+mk}(x)+T_{nk-mk}(x)=2\,(T_{n}\circ T_{k})(x)\,(T_{m}\circ T_{k})(x)\;=\;2\,(T_{n}T_{m})\circ T_{k}(x).

It follows that the degree of

necessarily depends on

in Theorem

cannot be bounded.

Remark 2.6

Let

Note that

is a cyclic polynomial whose degree varies with

is not cyclic in Theorem

3.
Auxiliary results

In this section,we collect some important results which we will need in our proofs. First we outline the finiteness criterion proposed by Bilu and Tichy in

Let

Theorem 3.1

\mathcal{O}_{S}
-denominator.

Let

be a finite algebraic extension of function field
\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(x)

define the valuation
\nu_{a}

\nu_{\infty}
be the infinite valuation which is defined by

\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(x)

and again in this way one obtains all discrete valuations on
L
. Furthermore, for

holds, where the sum is taken over all discrete valuations on

\mathbb{C}
with function field

In the following lemma, we establish that the right hand side inequality of

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

. Then

(i)

\mathcal{H}(f+g)=\mathcal{H}(f)+\mathcal{H}(g)
, if
\nu(f)\neq\nu(g)
and
\max\{\nu(f),\nu(g)\}>0
for all valuations
\nu
over
L
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}(fg)=\mathcal{H}(f)+\mathcal{H}(g)
, if
\nu(f)\nu(g)>0
for all valuations
\nu
over
L
.

Proof.

(
i
)

Recall that

\mathcal{H}(f+g)=-\sum_{\nu}\min\{0,\nu(f+g)\}.

Therefore, it suffices to verify that for every valuation
\nu
,

(20)

\min\{0,\nu(f+g)\}=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}.

Since

\nu(f+g)\geq\min\{\nu(f),\nu(g)\}.

and because we assume
\nu(f)\neq\nu(g)
, we infact have

\nu(f+g)=\min\{\nu(f),\nu(g)\}.

Without loss of generality assume that
\nu(f)<\nu(g)
; hence
\nu(f+g)=\nu(f)
. Fix a valuation
\nu
. Now we consider the following three cases.

Case 1:
Suppose
\nu(f+g)=0.
Then
\nu(f)=0
and
\nu(g)>0
. Thus

\min\{0,\nu(f+g)\}=0=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}.

So (
20
) holds.

Case 2:
Suppose
\nu(f+g)>0
. Then
\nu(f)>0
and since
\nu(f)<\nu(g)
, also
\nu(g)>0
. Hence, in (
20
)
\nu(f+g),\nu(f),\nu(g)
are positive, and therefore is zero and thus equality holds.

Case 3:
Suppose
\nu(f+g)<0
. Then
\nu(f)<0
. Also note that either
\nu(g)<0
or
\nu(g)>0
. Since by our assumption
\max\{\nu(f),\nu(g)\}=\nu(g)>0
and therefore
\min\{0,\nu(g)\}=0
. Thus,

\min\{0,\nu(f+g)\}=\nu(f)=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}

and(
20
) holds. This completes the proof of part
(i)
.

(
ii
)

We have
\nu(fg)=\nu(f)+\nu(g)
. Therefore,

\mathcal{H}(fg)=-\sum_{\nu}\min\{0,\nu(fg)\}=-\sum_{\nu}\min\{0,\nu(f)+\nu(g)\}.

So it is suffices to verify that for every valuation
\nu
,

(21)

\min\{0,\nu(f)+\nu(g)\}=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}.

We check this by considering all possible sign combination of
\nu(f)
and
\nu(g)
.

Case 1:
Suppose
\nu(f)=\nu(g)=0
. Then,
\nu(f)+\nu(g)=0
, and the identity (
21
) holds trivially.

Case 2:
Suppose
\nu(f)>0
and
\nu(g)=0
. Then
\nu(f)+\nu(g)=\nu(f)
and hence all the minimum in
(
21
)
are zero and the identity holds.

Case 3:
Suppose
\nu(f)>0
and
\nu(g)>0
. Then

\min\{0,\nu(f+g)\}=0=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}.

Case 4:
Suppose
\nu(f)<0
and
\nu(g)<0
. Then
\nu(f)+\nu(g)<0
. Thus,

\min\{0,\nu(f)+\nu(g)\}=\nu(f)+\nu(g)=\min\{0,\nu(f)\}+\min\{0,\nu(g)\}.

Similarly, if we interchange the roles of
f
and
g
in all the above cases, we get the same conclusion. This completes the proof of Lemma
3.3
.

1-\frac{\sigma_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n_{1}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}-\frac{\sigma_{2}\alpha_{2}^{n_{1}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}-\frac{\sigma_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}-\frac{\sigma_{2}\alpha_{2}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}+\frac{\delta_{1}\beta_{1}^{n_{1}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}+\frac{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{1}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}+\frac{\delta_{1}\beta_{1}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}=0.

Now put

p_{1}=-\frac{\sigma_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}},\quad p_{2}=\frac{\sigma_{2}\alpha_{2}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}},\quad p_{3}=\frac{\delta_{1}\beta_{1}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}},

and also

q_{1}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\beta_{2}},~q_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\beta_{2}},~q_{3}=~\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}},~r_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\delta_{2}},~r_{2}=\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\delta_{2}},~r_{3}=\frac{\delta_{1}}{\delta_{2}}.

Consider the field
K=\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(x,y,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
and define the projective height
\mathcal{H}
on
K/\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}(x)
and we obtain the estimate

\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\sigma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}\right)\geq n_{2}\cdot\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{2}}\right)-\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\delta_{2}}\right),\quad i=1,2.

Similarly we have

\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\delta_{1}\beta_{1}^{n_{2}}}{\delta_{2}\beta_{2}^{n_{2}}}\right)\geq n_{2}\cdot\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}}\right)-\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{\delta_{1}}{\delta_{2}}\right).

That is,

\mathcal{H}(p_{i})\geq n_{2}\cdot\mathcal{H}(q_{i})-\mathcal{H}(r_{i}),

for
i=1,2,3
. Since
(u_{n}(y))_{n\geq 0}
is non-degenerate, we have
q_{3}\notin\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}
. It then follows that
\mathcal{H}(q_{3})\neq 0
. So,

(34)

n_{2}\leq(\mathcal{H}(p_{3})+\mathcal{H}(r_{3}))\cdot\mathcal{H}(q_{3})^{-1}.

Alternatively, we derive the following estimate for height of
u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x)
;

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n_{1}}+\sigma_{2}\alpha_{2}^{n_{1}}+\sigma_{1}\alpha_{1}^{n_{2}}+\sigma_{2}\alpha_{2}^{n_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1})+2\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{2})+n_{1}\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{1})+n_{1}\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle+n_{2}\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{1})+n_{2}\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2n_{2}(\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1})+\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{2})+\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{1})+\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{2})).

From (
34

\mathcal{H}(u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x))\leq 2(\mathcal{H}(p_{3})+\mathcal{H}(r_{3}))\cdot\mathcal{H}(q_{3})^{-1}(\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1})

+\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{2})+\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{1})+\mathcal{H}(\alpha_{2})).

and hence we omit the details.

5.1.
Proof of Corollary
2.7
:

Assume that
u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x)=v_{m_{1}}(y)+v_{m_{2}}(y)
has infinitely many solutions with
x,y\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
. By Theorem
3.1
,

(35)

u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x)=\phi(f_{1}(\lambda(x)))

and

(36)

v_{m_{1}}(x)+v_{m_{2}}(x)=\phi(g_{1}(\mu(x))),

where
\phi(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Q}$}[x]
,
\lambda(x),\mu(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Q}$}[x]
are linear polynomials, and
(f_{1},g_{1})
is a standard pair over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
u_{n}
and
v_{m}
are non constant, we have
\phi
is non constant. Now suppose that
\deg f_{1}>1
and
\deg g_{1}>1
. Then either
u_{n_{1}}+u_{n_{2}}
or
v_{m_{1}}+v_{m_{2}}
is a composite of either cyclic or dihedral polynomial, a contradiction.

If
\deg g_{1}=1
and
\deg f_{1}=1
then from (
36
) we get
(v_{m_{1}}(x)+v_{m_{2}}(x))\circ\ell_{1}(x)=\phi(x)
, where
\ell_{1}(x)
is a linear polynomial. So then we have,

u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x)=(v_{m_{1}}\circ\ell_{1}+v_{m_{2}}\circ\ell_{1})\circ f_{1}\circ\lambda(x)=v_{m_{1}}\circ\ell(x)+v_{m_{2}}\circ\ell(x),

where
\ell(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Q}$}[x]
is linear. Now if
\deg g_{1}=1
and
\deg f_{1}>1
, then we have
v_{m_{1}}(\ell(x))+v_{m_{2}}(\ell(x))=\phi(x)
, for linear
\ell(x)\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Q}$}[x]
. So that we obtain

(37)

u_{n_{1}}(x)+u_{n_{2}}(x)=(v_{m_{1}}+v_{m_{2}})(\ell(f_{1}(\lambda(x)))).

Since by assumption (
37
) has no proper vanishing subsum and that
u_{n_{1}}+u_{n_{2}}
is also not a composite of a cyclic or a dihedral polynomial, by Theorem
2.4
we get
\deg(v_{m_{1}}+v_{m_{2}})<C
.
