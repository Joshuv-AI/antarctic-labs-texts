Bellman Calibration for V V -Learning in Offline Reinforcement Learning
Lars van der Laan, Nathan Kallus

Abstract
We introduce Iterated Bellman Calibration , a simple, model-agnostic, post-hoc procedure for calibrating off-policy value predictions in infinite-horizon Markov decision processes. Bellman calibration requires that states with similar predicted long-term returns exhibit one-step returns consistent with the Bellman equation under the target policy. We adapt classical histogram and isotonic calibration to the dynamic, counterfactual setting by repeatedly regressing fitted Bellman targets onto a model’s predictions, using a doubly robust pseudo-outcome to handle off-policy data. This yields a one-dimensional fitted value iteration scheme that can be applied to any value estimator. Our analysis provides finite-sample guarantees for both calibration and prediction under weak assumptions, and—critically— without requiring Bellman completeness or realizability.

Introduction

Many applications require predicting the long-term consequences of a decision
policy in a sequential, stochastic environment. We consider settings modeled as
a Markov decision process (MDP) and aim to forecast the long-run returns that
would occur
under a target policy
\pi
, which may differ from the behavior
policy that generated the data. This problem is widespread: clinicians anticipate
long-term health outcomes under proposed treatment rules
(
62
)
;
online platforms estimate customer lifetime value and retention under alternative
recommendation strategies
(
55
;
41
;
80
)
;
and economists assess the downstream impact of counterfactual programs

(
51
;
14
)
. In all these settings, practitioners
rely not only on correct rankings but also on
accurate
numerical predictions,
since value estimates must reflect the long-term outcomes individuals actually
experience. As a result, value calibration is essential for personalized forecasting,
long-term prediction, and reliable policy evaluation, as miscalibrated estimates
can distort policy comparisons and undermine decision-making.

Modern machine learning models such as neural networks and gradient-boosted
trees often produce predictions that deviate from realized outcomes due to
model misspecification, distribution shift, or limited data

(
82
;
46
;
7
;
26
)
. Similar issues arise in reinforcement learning, where
value estimates can exhibit systematic overestimation, instability, and
off-policy bias
(
56
;
75
;
23
)
. A
natural requirement in this setting is that value estimates be

calibrated
: individuals with similar predicted returns should, on
average, realize long-term outcomes that match those predictions under the
target policy. Calibration improves uncertainty quantification,
counterfactual evaluation, and the interpretability and reliability of value
estimates. It has been extensively studied in classification and regression,
where calibrated predictors are constructed to match empirical outcome
frequencies or conditional means

(
38
;
49
;
82
;
76
)
. However, calibration of long-term values remains largely
unexplored in reinforcement learning.

Although long-horizon prediction resembles supervised learning, it introduces
challenges absent from standard regression. Value prediction is inherently
counterfactual: it concerns the long-run outcomes that would unfold under a
target policy
\pi
rather than the behavior policy that generated the data.
Long-term returns are never directly observed – only one-step transitions are
seen – so multi-step returns must be reconstructed through a model or the
Bellman equation. The state distribution also shifts with
\pi
, and small
modeling errors propagate through the transition dynamics, causing even mild
misspecification to compound

(
25
;
42
;
20
;
1
)
. Moreover, fitted value and
Q
-iteration are
iterative regression procedures, and stopping after only finitely many
iterations may introduce bias, while function approximation error and
optimization instability can further distort the iterates in practice. As a
result, common value-function estimators such as Monte Carlo prediction,
temporal-difference methods, and fitted value or
Q
-iteration may produce
systematically biased or unstable predictions

(
56
;
58
;
23
;
2
)
.

These empirical challenges reflect deeper theoretical limitations. Classical
analyses of fitted value or
Q
-iteration rely on strong assumptions such as
(Bellman) completeness, realizability, or low-dimensional state spaces

(
5
;
58
;
42
;
19
;
30
)
. Completeness ensures that the Bellman target
lies within the function class, so each regression step is well specified. When
completeness fails, the Bellman target falls outside the class, the regression
becomes misspecified, and projection errors accumulate – potentially compounding
exponentially with the horizon and yielding arbitrarily poor estimates

(
13
;
77
;
22
;
3
)
. Recent work replaces Bellman completeness with min–max or adversarial
formulations, but these approaches require highly expressive critics, dual
realizability, and partial coverage

(
60
;

;

)
.

Contributions.

We study calibration of the
value function
associated with a target
policy
\pi
. We show classical calibration tools – histogram binning and isotonic
regression – can be adapted to the dynamic, counterfactual setting of
reinforcement learning when combined with off-policy fitted value iteration and
a doubly robust Bellman target. Building on these components, we develop

iterated Bellman calibration
, a simple and model-agnostic post-hoc
procedure that corrects systematic biases in long-horizon value prediction and
produces calibrated estimates of long-run returns.

Our main contributions are:

1.

We formalize weak and strong notions of
Bellman calibration
,
analogous to regression calibration but tailored to the fixed-point
structure of infinite-horizon value functions.

2.

We introduce histogram- and isotonic-based
calibration
algorithms
applicable to any value-function estimator, including those
produced by fitted value and Q iteration, and construct a novel doubly robust
Bellman target for off-policy data.

3.

We provide
finite-sample guarantees
for iterated Bellman
calibration, bounding calibration error through finite-iteration,
statistical, and nuisance-estimation terms. We obtain guarantees for both
calibration and prediction error, showing that calibration does not degrade
accuracy and can strictly improve it – all
without requiring Bellman
completeness
.

Calibration for Value Functions

2.1
Setup and Notation

We consider an MDP with continuous state space
\mathcal{S}
, discrete action
space
\mathcal{A}
, initial state distribution
\rho
, transition kernel

P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
, reward function
r_{0}(s,a)
, and discount factor

\gamma\in[0,1)
. Data are collected under a behavior policy

b_{0}(a\mid s)
, and each sample consists of a transition

(S,A,R,S^{\prime})
, where
(S,A)\sim\rho\times b_{0}
,
S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid S,A)
,
and
R=r_{0}(S,A)+\varepsilon
with
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid S,A]=0
.
We observe a calibration dataset of
n
i.i.d. transitions,

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\{(S_{i},A_{i},R_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Let
\pi(a\mid s)
denote a target policy and

w_{\pi}:=\pi/b_{0}
the corresponding importance ratio. Define

(\pi f)(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\mid s)\,f(s,a)
and

(Pv)(s,a):=\mathbb{E}[v(S^{\prime})\mid S=s,A=a]
. The policy-marginalized
quantities are
P_{\pi}:=\pi P
and
r_{0,\pi}:=\pi r_{0}
, with

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(f):=r_{0,\pi}+\gamma\,P_{\pi}f
the associated
Bellman operator. A measure

\mu
is
stationary
for a Markov operator
P
if
\mu P=\mu
.

The
value function
under policy
\pi
is the expected discounted return
starting in state
s
and following
\pi
:

v_{0}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\,\bigg|\,S_{0}=s\right].

Here
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
denotes expectation over
A\sim\pi(\cdot\mid S)
and

S^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot\mid S,A)
. It is the unique bounded fixed point of the Bellman
equation
(
9
;
10
)

v_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v_{0}),\quad\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v_{0})(s)=r_{0,\pi}(s)+\gamma\,(P_{\pi}v_{0})(s).

(1)

Let
\|f\|:=\{\mathbb{E}_{b_{0}}[f(A,S)^{2}]\}^{1/2}
denote the
L^{2}
norm under
the behavior distribution, and for any measure
\mu
, let

\|f\|_{2,\mu}:=\{\int f(s,a)^{2}\,\mu(ds,da)\}^{1/2}
. Let
\|f\|_{n,S,A}
and
\|g\|_{n,S^{\prime}}
denote the
empirical
L^{2}
norms over the samples
\{(S_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and

\{S_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, respectively. We write
\lesssim
to denote inequalities
holding up to absolute constants, and use
[K]:=\{0,1,\dots,K\}
.

2.2
Bellman Calibration: Weak and Strong Forms

Let
\hat{v}
denote an estimated value function, which we treat as a predictor
of long-term discounted returns under the target policy
\pi
. We assume

\hat{v}
is trained on data independent of the calibration set

\mathcal{C}_{n}
.

For each
v
, define the
Bellman-calibration map

\Gamma_{0}(v)(s):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[R+\gamma\,v(S^{\prime})\mid v(S)=v(s)\right],

(2)

which returns the expected one-step reward plus continuation value implied by

v
under policy
\pi
, conditional on
v(S)=v(s)
. Equivalently,

\Gamma_{0}(v)(s)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v)(S)\mid v(S)=v(s)\right].

(3)

The true value function is a fixed point of this map, satisfying the
coarsened Bellman equation
v_{0}(s)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v_{0})(S)\mid v_{0}(S)=v_{0}(s)]=\Gamma_{0}(v_{0})(s)
.

Bellman calibration requires that states with similar predicted long-term
returns exhibit one-step returns consistent with the Bellman equation under the
target policy. We say that
\hat{v}
is
perfectly

Bellman calibrated
if

\hat{v}(S)=\Gamma_{0}(\hat{v})(S)
almost surely. The associated

Bellman calibration error
is

\mathrm{Cal}_{\ell^{2}}(\hat{v}):=\big\|\hat{v}-\Gamma_{0}(\hat{v})\big\|.

(4)

Equivalently, perfect calibration means that
\hat{v}(S)
is conditionally
unbiased for the Bellman target
R+\gamma\,\hat{v}(S^{\prime})
under
\pi
. In other
words, among individuals with identical predicted long-term returns, the
realized reward plus continuation value
\hat{v}(S^{\prime})
matches their shared
prediction on average.

This condition can also be expressed in terms of the implied reward model

\hat{r}_{\pi,\hat{v}}(s):=\hat{v}(s)-\gamma\,P_{\pi}\hat{v}(s)
. Perfect Bellman
calibration is equivalent to requiring that this reward model is calibrated for
the true reward:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R\mid\hat{v}(S)]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_{\pi,\hat{v}}(S)\mid\hat{v}(S)].

This is the natural dynamic analogue of regression calibration (recovering the
classical case when
\gamma=0
and
\pi=b_{0}
)
(
47
)
. Since the true value
function is itself Bellman calibrated, imposing this property on
\hat{v}
is a
minimal form of self-consistency.

Strong Bellman calibration.

The definition above calibrates
\hat{v}
only with respect to its own
Bellman target
r_{0,\pi}+P_{\pi}\hat{v}
. A stricter notion requires
calibration with respect to the
true
Bellman target

r_{0,\pi}+P_{\pi}v_{0}
, that is, with respect to the value function
v_{0}
itself. We say that
\hat{v}
is
strongly
Bellman
calibrated if
\hat{v}(s)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[v_{0}(S)\mid\hat{v}(S)=\hat{v}(s)\right].

Equivalently, individuals with identical predicted long-term returns realize,
on average, the
true
long-term returns under
\pi
:

\hat{v}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\;\middle|\;\hat{v}(S)=\hat{v}(s)\right].

This stronger notion is generally unattainable without accurate estimation of
either the full
Q
-function or the discounted occupancy ratio, both of which
are typically more difficult than value prediction itself. In particular,
achieving strong calibration is at least as hard as efficient estimation of
average policy values, which involves estimation of both nuisances

(
32
;
33
)
. For this reason, we focus on
the weaker but practically achievable notion of Bellman calibration introduced
above.

2.3
Bellman calibration reduces estimation error

We now link Bellman calibration to estimation error, showing that calibration
removes one error component and tightens the overall bound.

Define the
L^{2}(\rho)
projection onto functions of
\hat{v}
by

\Pi_{\hat{v}}q:=\arg\min_{\theta\circ\hat{v}}\|q-\theta\circ\hat{v}\|.

Let
P_{\pi,\hat{v}}:=\Pi_{\hat{v}}P_{\pi}
denote the coarsened transition
operator. This is a valid Markov operator, given explicitly by

P_{\pi,\hat{v}}f(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[f(S^{\prime})\mid\hat{v}(S)=\hat{v}(s)\right].

Let
\hat{v}_{0}=\theta\circ\hat{v}
be the fixed point of the coarsened
Bellman operator

\hat{v}_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v}}\hat{v}_{0},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v}}q:=\Pi_{\hat{v}}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}q.

(5)

Since
\hat{v}_{0}
is a transformation of
\hat{v}
, conditioning on

\hat{v}_{0}(S)
is a coarsening of conditioning on
\hat{v}(S)
, and therefore

\Gamma_{0}(\hat{v}_{0})(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[R+\gamma\,\hat{v}_{0}(S^{\prime})\mid\hat{v}_{0}(S)=\hat{v}_{0}(s)]=\hat{v}_{0}(s).

Thus
\hat{v}_{0}
is perfectly Bellman calibrated.

A1

There exists a stationary measure
\mu_{\hat{v}}
for
P_{\pi,\hat{v}}
.

Theorem 1

(Calibration–Refinement Bound)
.

Under
A1
,

\|\hat{v}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v}}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\|\Pi_{\hat{v}}v_{0}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v}}}\;+\;\|\hat{v}-\hat{v}_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v}}}.

The estimation error decomposes into two components: a
refinement error

\|\Pi_{\hat{v}}v_{0}-v_{0}\|
, which measures the best approximation to
v_{0}

using only the scalar representation
\hat{v}
, and a
calibration error

\|\hat{v}-\hat{v}_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v}}}
, which measures how far
\hat{v}
is
from the fixed point of the coarsened Bellman operator. If
\hat{v}
is perfectly
Bellman calibrated, then
\hat{v}=\hat{v}_{0}
and the calibration error
vanishes. In this case, the estimation error (under the stationary norm) is as
small as the
L^{2}(\rho b_{0})
-optimal transformation
\Pi_{\hat{v}}v_{0}
of

\hat{v}
, up to a factor
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
. This mirrors classical
calibration–refinement decompositions in classification and regression

(
44
;
16
;
72
;
78
)
.

Condition
A1
ensures that
\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v}}
is a

\gamma
-contraction on
L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v}})

(Appendix
A
). When the behavior distribution
\rho
is
stationary for
P_{\pi}
, it is also stationary for the coarsened kernel

P_{\pi,\hat{v}}
. In this case we may take
\mu_{\hat{v}}=\rho
, yielding

\|\Pi_{\hat{v}}v_{0}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v}}}=\min_{\theta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}}\|\theta\circ\hat{v}-v_{0}\|.

2.4
A Doubly Robust Bellman Target

Off-policy prediction requires an (approximately) unbiased estimate of

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v)
despite data being generated by the behavior policy
b_{0}
. Importance
weighting with
w_{\pi}=\pi/b_{0}
corrects this mismatch but is highly sensitive to
estimation error in the weights. To obtain a more stable update, we use a

doubly robust
Bellman pseudo-outcome that combines importance weighting
with estimates of the reward function and transition kernel. This target is
unbiased if either the weights or the reward–transition model is correctly
specified.

Let
\widehat{w}_{\pi}
estimate the importance ratio

w_{\pi}:=\pi/b_{0}
, let
\hat{r}
estimate the reward function
r_{0}
, and let

\widehat{P}
estimate the Markov operator
P
. For any function
v
, we define the
doubly robust

fitted Bellman
target

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}(v)(S,A,R,S^{\prime})
as

(\pi\hat{q}_{v})(S)+\widehat{w}_{\pi}(A\mid S)\big[R+\gamma\,v(S^{\prime})-\hat{q}_{v}(S,A)\big],

where the true and estimated
Q
-functions under
v
are

q_{v}:=r_{0}+\gamma\,Pv,\qquad\hat{q}_{v}:=\hat{r}+\gamma\,\widehat{P}v.

When
\gamma=0
, this pseudo-outcome reduces to the standard doubly
robust scores used in static treatment-effect estimation

(
50
;
72
;
34
)
.

Recall that
(b_{0}f)(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}b_{0}(a\mid s)\,f(s,a)
and define the denoised Bellman operator

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(v)(s)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}(v)(S,A,R,S^{\prime})\,\middle|\,S=s\right].

Theorem 2

(Doubly robust errors)
.

For any
v
,

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(v)-\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(v)=b_{0}\!\left\{(w_{\pi}-\widehat{w}_{\pi})(\hat{q}_{v}-q_{v})\right\}.

The fitted Bellman target is
doubly robust
: it is unbiased whenever

\widehat{w}_{\pi}=w_{\pi}
or
\hat{q}_{v}=q_{v}
(e.g., if

\widehat{r}=r
and
\widehat{P}=P
).
For instance,
w_{\pi}
is known whenever the behavior policy
b_{0}
is known or
when
b_{0}=\pi
, in which case
w_{\pi}\equiv 1
.
In this case the pseudo-outcome remains valid even with trivial models

\widehat{r}=0
and
\widehat{P}v=0
, reducing to the standard
importance-weighted target

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}(v)(S,A,R,S^{\prime})=w_{\pi}(A\mid S)\,\big(R+\gamma v(S^{\prime})\big).

(6)

Conversely, in robotics and simulation settings the transition kernel is often
known (
\widehat{P}=P
), in which case the error simplifies to

b_{0}\{(\widehat{w}_{\pi}-w_{\pi})(\widehat{r}-r)\}
and doubly robustness holds
with respect to the weight and reward estimators.

Algorithms and Theory

3.1
Iterated Bellman Calibration Algorithm

Algorithm
1
introduces a simple post-hoc calibration method,

Iterated Bellman Calibration
, which transforms a given value predictor

\hat{v}
into a Bellman-calibrated predictor of the form

\hat{v}^{\mathrm{cal}}:=\theta_{n}\circ\hat{v},

where the calibrator
\theta_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is learned from the
calibration dataset
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. At a high level, we learn
\theta_{n}
by
iteratively regressing the doubly robust targets

\{\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}(\hat{v}^{(k)})(S_{i},A_{i},R_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\}_{i=1}^{n}

onto the initial value predictions
\{\hat{v}(S_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
using a regression
class
\mathcal{F}
. The procedure follows the structure of fitted value
iteration
(
43
;
42
)
, but crucially avoids
high-dimensional function approximation by restricting
\hat{v}^{(k+1)}
to lie
in the one-dimensional class
\{\theta\circ\hat{v}:\theta\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Because the finite-iteration error of fitted value
iteration decays at a geometric rate
\gamma^{K}

(
42
)
,
only a small number of iterations (
K\asymp\log n
) are needed. As a result,
Algorithm
1
is computationally efficient.

based

3.2
Histogram Calibration

Histogram binning constructs a piecewise-constant calibrator by grouping
examples with similar predicted values. We partition the initial value
predictions
\{\hat{v}(S_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
into
B
nondecreasing bins

\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{B}\}
, formed either by empirical quantiles (
equal-mass

binning) or by discretizing the prediction range into uniform intervals
(
equal-width
binning). We implement
histogram iterated Bellman
calibration
by applying Algorithm
1
with the calibrator class

\mathcal{F}_{B}:=\mathrm{span}\{1_{I_{b}}:b\in[B]\}
, consisting of
piecewise-constant functions that are constant within each bin. The bins may be
data-adaptive (e.g., empirical quantiles) provided that the bin count
B
is
deterministic, possibly growing with
n
. Histogram calibration is
computationally efficient as it simply involves computing empirical means within bins.

Histogram calibration enforces approximate Bellman calibration by requiring that,
within each bin, the fitted value equals the empirical average of the Bellman target. In Step 4, the resulting calibrator has the step-function form

\theta_{n}^{(K)}(t)=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{m}_{b}^{(K)}\,\mathbf{1}\{t\in I_{b}\},

where
\hat{m}_{b}^{(K)}=|I_{b}|^{-1}\sum_{i:\,\hat{v}(S_{i})\in I_{b}}\hat{\chi}_{i}^{(K)}

is the empirical mean of the fitted Bellman target in bin
b
. This binwise
representation yields the empirical fixed-point relation

\hat{v}^{(K)}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\!\left[\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}(\hat{v}^{(K-1)})(S,A,R,S^{\prime})\,\middle|\,\hat{v}^{(K)}(S)=\hat{v}^{(K)}(s)\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{n}
denotes the empirical conditional expectation over the
calibration sample
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Hence, at convergence
(
\hat{v}^{(K)}\approx\hat{v}^{(K-1)}
), the calibrated predictor is an
approximate empirical fixed point of (
2
), ensuring
approximate Bellman calibration.

Theorem 3

(Calibration Error for Histogram Binning)
.

Assume
C1
-
C3
. Then, there exists a
C\in(0,\infty)
such that, for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cal}_{\ell^{2}}(\hat{v}^{(K)})\;\leq\;

\displaystyle\;C\left(\sqrt{\frac{B}{n}\,\log\Big(\frac{n}{B}\Big)}\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\;\big\|(\widehat{w}_{\pi}-w_{\pi})\,(\widehat{q}_{\hat{v}^{(K)}}-q_{\hat{v}^{(K)}})\big\|

\displaystyle\;+\;\big\|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(\hat{v}^{(K)})-\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(\hat{v}^{(K-1)})\big\|.

The first two terms represent the oracle calibration error that would be
achievable by regression calibration if the full return

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}
were observed under
\pi
, matching classical
bounds for histogram binning
(
27
;
28
;
64
)
. The third term reflects the doubly robust nuisance
estimation error from Theorem
2
. The final term is the
finite-iteration error, which decays at the geometric rate
\gamma^{K}
as

K\to\infty
under mild conditions (Appendix
F.4
),
consistent with the theory of fitted value iteration
(
42
)
.

Interestingly, the calibration bounds do not depend on the discount factor

\gamma
(except indirectly through the finite-iteration term). This reflects
that calibration is fundamentally a one-step prediction problem: unlike value
estimation, calibration error does not compound over future timesteps, making
it substantially easier to control statistically. By Theorem
1
, however, the extent to which calibration
controls estimation error weakens as
\gamma
increases and coverage decreases,
as we now formalize.

Estimation error.
The next theorem shows that calibration preserves –
and can even improve – the estimation error of the original predictor
\hat{v}
.
Thus, Bellman calibration can be applied post hoc without sacrificing
predictive accuracy, and may be especially beneficial under model
misspecification.

Define the
L^{2}(\rho)
projection induced by
\mathcal{F}_{B}

\Pi_{\hat{v},B}q:=\arg\min_{\theta\circ\hat{v}:\theta\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\|q-\theta\circ\hat{v}\|.

Let
\hat{v}_{0,B}\in\{\theta\circ\hat{v}:\theta\in\mathcal{F}_{B}\}
be the
unique fixed point of the corresponding coarsened Bellman equation

\hat{v}_{0,B}=\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}\hat{v}_{0,B},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}q:=\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}q.

The corresponding Markov operator is

P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}:=\Pi_{\hat{v},B}P_{\pi}
, satisfying

P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}f(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[f(S^{\prime})\mid\hat{v}(S)\in I_{\hat{b}(s)}\right],

with
\hat{b}(s)=b
whenever
\hat{v}(s)\in I_{b}
.

C4

(
Stationary coverage
) There exists a stationary measure
\mu_{\hat{v},B}
for

P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
and
\hat{\kappa}_{B}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\|h\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}\leq\hat{\kappa}_{B}\,\|h\|
for all
h:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 4

(Estimation Error for Histogram Binning)
.

Assume
C1
-
C4
. Then, there exists a
C\in(0,\infty)
such that, for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|\hat{v}^{(K)}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\|\Pi_{\hat{v},B}v_{0}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}

\displaystyle+\gamma^{K}\|\hat{v}-\hat{v}_{0,B}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}

\displaystyle+\frac{C\hat{\kappa}_{B}}{1-\gamma}\left(\sqrt{\frac{B}{n}\log\!\frac{n}{B}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(K/\delta)}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\hat{\kappa}_{B}}{1-\gamma}\max_{j\in[K]}\big\|(\hat{w}_{\pi}-w_{\pi})(\hat{q}_{\hat{v}^{(j)}}-q_{\hat{v}^{(j)}})\big\|.

Remark.
The proof also shows that the same bound holds for

\|\hat{v}^{(K)}-\hat{v}_{0,B}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}
, but without the
approximation term

\|\Pi_{\hat{v},B}v_{0}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}
, implying that
the algorithm converges rapidly to
\hat{v}_{0,B}
even when
\hat{v}
is
a poor initial fit.

Discussion.
Neither Theorem
3
nor
Theorem
4
requires any Bellman completeness
assumptions. The key observation is that Bellman completeness
does
hold
for the coarsened Bellman operator
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
. Defining

\mathcal{F}_{B,\hat{v}}:=\{\theta\circ\hat{v}:\theta\in\mathcal{F}_{B}\}
, we have
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}f\in\mathcal{F}_{B,\widehat{v}}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{B,\hat{v}}
, since
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}

averages
P_{\pi}
over the bins of
\hat{v}
and therefore yields a function
that is constant on those bins. Our proof first shows that
\hat{v}^{(K)}

converges to the fixed point
\hat{v}_{0,B}
of this coarsened operator, and
then bounds the discrepancy
\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}
using a modification of
Theorem
1
.

When the importance weights
w_{\pi}
are known and
K
is sufficiently large,
Theorem
3
shows that the calibration error vanishes at
rate
\sqrt{(B/n)\log(n/B)}
. In contrast, Theorem
4

shows that the estimation error consists of an approximation term

(1-\gamma)^{-1}\|\Pi_{\hat{v},B}v_{0}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}
(reflecting lack
of realizability) plus the same
\sqrt{(B/n)\log(n/B)}
term. Thus the choice
of bin number
B
induces the usual bias–variance tradeoff: if
B
is too
small, the calibrator is overly coarse and may distort
\hat{v}
, while if

B
is too large, bins contain too few samples, increasing variance and harming
calibration. To preserve the predictive structure of
\hat{v}
,
B
should
be large enough that
\mathcal{F}_{B}
approximates the identity map well. A simple bound illustrates this:

\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\|\theta\circ\hat{v}-v_{0}\|\;\leq\;\|\hat{v}-v_{0}\|+\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\|\theta\circ\hat{v}-\hat{v}\|.

(7)

For uniform-mass binning, choosing
B\asymp n^{1/3}

yields the worst-case guarantee
(
29
)

\|\hat{v}^{(K)}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}\;\leq\;\frac{\hat{\kappa}_{B}}{1-\gamma}\|\hat{v}-v_{0}\|+O_{p}((\log n/n)^{1/3}).

Under the same scaling, the calibration error produced by our procedure also
vanishes at rate
(\log n/n)^{1/3}
. This suggests that selecting
B
based
on predictive performance – for example, via cross-validation – typically yields
good calibration in practice.

3.3
Isotonic Calibration

Histogram binning requires choosing the number of bins
B
to manage the
bias–variance tradeoff. As a tuning-free alternative, we consider

isotonic iterated Bellman calibration
, implemented by setting

\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{iso}}
in
Algorithm
1
, the class of monotone nondecreasing functions on
the real line. In this case, Step 4 performs isotonic regression of the Bellman
target to obtain
\theta_{n}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{iso}}
, which can be
computed in near-linear time using the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm

(
11
)
. Each isotonic regression step is
equivalent to histogram regression over an outcome-adaptive partition of the
predicted values
(
64
)
. The monotonicity
constraint regularizes the calibrator and mitigates overfitting to small or
noisy bins, and because the identity map is monotone, isotonic calibration
tends not to distort already well-calibrated predictors.

We next establish finite-sample calibration guarantees.

C5

(
Finite variation of the calibrated target
)
There exists
C\in(0,\infty)
such that, almost surely, the function
t\mapsto\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{T}_{\pi}}(\hat{v}^{(K)})(S)\mid\hat{v}(S)=t,\mathcal{C}_{n}]

has total variation at most
C
.

Theorem 5

(Calibration Error for Isotonic Calibration)
.

Assume
C1
-
C5
for
\mathcal{F}_{iso}
. Then, there exists a
C\in(0,\infty)
such that, for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cal}_{\ell^{2}}(\hat{v}^{(K)})\;\leq\;

\displaystyle\;C\left(n^{-1/3}\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\;+\;\big\|(\widehat{w}_{\pi}-w_{\pi})\,(\widehat{q}_{\hat{v}^{(K)}}-q_{\hat{v}^{(K)}})\big\|

\displaystyle\;+\;\big\|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(\hat{v}^{(K)})-\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0,\pi}(\hat{v}^{(K-1)})\big\|.

The isotonic case parallels the histogram-binning analysis but uses an
adaptively chosen partition determined by the pool-adjacent-violators
algorithm. Importantly, calibration does not require any monotonicity
assumptions; monotonicity simply provides a data-adaptive partition of

\hat{v}
. The resulting
n^{-1/3}
term matches classical isotonic regression
rates
(
12
;
70
;
64
)
. In
effect, isotonic calibration achieves the calibration error that histogram
binning would obtain with
B\asymp n^{1/3}
bins.

3.4
Hybrid Isotonic–Histogram Calibration

While isotonic iterated Bellman calibration guarantees finite-sample Bellman
calibration, extending the analysis to obtain an analogue of
Theorem
4
– showing that calibration does not worsen the
estimation error – is challenging. The difficulty is that the class of monotone
functions is not Bellman complete, so an argument based on approximate Bellman
completeness
(
42
)
would introduce additional approximation
error. Although isotonic regression can be viewed as outcome-adaptive histogram
binning, the analysis in Section
3.2
relies on the partition
remaining fixed across iterations. In contrast, applying
Algorithm
1
with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{iso}}

produces a
new
, data-dependent partition at each iteration, so each step
targets the fixed point of a different coarsened Bellman operator. This
instability obstructs a direct extension of the histogram argument.
Nevertheless, when
\hat{v}
is already a good estimator of
v_{0}
, it lies near a
fixed point of
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
, so the Bellman iterates modify it only
slightly. We therefore expect predictive performance to be preserved in this
regime, even though a formal analysis is left for future work.

To obtain formal estimation guarantees while still avoiding manual tuning of
the binning, we introduce a hybrid isotonic–histogram method
(Algorithm
2
). The procedure performs a
single

isotonic regression step to learn an outcome-adaptive partition of the range of

\hat{v}
, and then applies histogram-based calibration over this fixed
partition. Specifically, we regress the initial Bellman target on
\hat{v}

using isotonic regression to obtain a data-adaptive binning, and then run
Algorithm
1
using this partition for all subsequent
iterations. Similar ideas—using isotonic regression to learn data-adaptive
partitions—have been used to construct conformal prediction intervals

(
48
;
63
)
.

Given a deterministic bound on the number of bins learned in the isotonic
regression step, the theoretical guarantees of Section
3.2
for
histogram binning apply directly.

Theorem 6

(Calibration and Estimation Error for Alg.
2
)
.

Let
\hat{v}^{(K)}
denote the output of Algorithm
2
. Suppose
the isotonic regression step produces at most
\hat{B}
bins, where

\hat{B}\leq B_{n}
for some deterministic sequence
B_{n}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Assume
C1
-
C5
with
B=B_{n}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-2\delta
, the guarantees in
Theorems
3
and
4
hold for

\hat{v}^{(K)}
with
B:=B_{n}
.

Existing theory for isotonic regression suggests that the effective number of
bins satisfies
B_{n}=O(n^{1/3})

(
17
)
, implying that the

B
–dependent error term in Theorem
4
scales as

O_{p}(n^{-1/3})
, consistent with Theorem
5
.

For the approximation term in Theorem
4
to be no larger
than the initial error
\|\hat{v}-v_{0}\|
(up to constants), the histogram class
must approximate the identity map on the range of
\hat{v}
. In particular, we
require
\min_{\theta\in\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\widehat{B}}}\|\theta\circ\hat{v}-\hat{v}\|\approx 0,

so that step functions in
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{\widehat{B}}
nearly preserve
the original predictions. This approximation term is small when
\hat{v}
is consistent for
v_{0}
: in this
case,
\hat{v}
lies close to a Bellman fixed point, and isotonic regression
typically outputs a near-identity transformation. When
\hat{v}
is
miscalibrated, however, isotonic regression induces a coarse partition,
collapsing regions of poor prediction into a single calibrated value.

4
Experiments

4.1
Synthetic CRM environment

We evaluate all methods in a synthetic customer-relationship-management (CRM)
Markov decision process (MDP) that mimics monthly retention and revenue dynamics
for a subscription service. The state
s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
encodes tenure in
months, engagement, fatigue, value segment, price sensitivity, and an indicator
of whether the customer is active. At each time
t
, the agent selects one of
three actions: no promotion, light promotion, or strong promotion. Rewards
correspond to monthly revenue after discounts and are zero after churn.

Transitions capture key CRM effects. Churn probability depends on tenure,
engagement, fatigue, and action via a logistic model; visit probability depends
on engagement, fatigue, and action; and revenue conditional on visit scales a
baseline value segment by an action-dependent uplift and a price-sensitivity
effect, with log-normal noise. Engagement decays over time but is boosted by
successful promotions, while fatigue increases with promotion intensity and
decays slowly otherwise. Once a customer churns or reaches a maximum tenure of
60 months, the process enters an absorbing state.

We simulate offline datasets of
n_{\text{cust}}=50{,}000
customers over a
horizon of
T=24
months with discount factor
\gamma=0.99
. Behavior data are
generated by a fixed heuristic policy that sends light promotions by default,
suppresses promotions for highly engaged and fatigued customers, and
occasionally sends strong promotions to low-engagement, high-value customers. We
evaluate off-policy value estimation for a deterministic
aggressive

revenue-seeking target policy that sends strong promotions to low-engagement,
high-sensitivity customers and avoids promotions for highly engaged, high-value
customers.

Ground-truth values
V^{\pi}(s_{0})
are estimated via Monte Carlo rollouts in the
same environment, and we report
(1-\gamma)
-scaled RMSE between the estimated and
Monte Carlo values at initial states, averaged across 50 independent runs.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced Iterated Bellman Calibration as a simple, model-agnostic

post-hoc
procedure for calibrating off-policy value predictions in
infinite-horizon MDPs. The method operates on top of any existing value
estimator by iteratively regressing Bellman targets onto its
one-dimensional output. It produces calibrated value estimates satisfying a
coarsened Bellman self-consistency condition, with finite-sample guarantees for
both calibration and prediction error under weak assumptions. We further show
that the calibrated iterates converge to the fixed point of a coarsened Bellman
operator, without requiring Bellman completeness or realizability.

We propose several algorithms based on isotonic calibration and histogram binning. Based on our theory and experiments, we recommend the hybrid isotonic–histogram calibration method (Algorithm
2
), as it retains the tuning-free advantages of isotonic calibration while inheriting the calibration guarantees of fixed-bin histogram regression, making it a practical and robust choice for post-hoc value calibration.

Beyond these guarantees, Iterated Bellman Calibration provides a practical post-hoc correction for value estimates that are miscalibrated due to finite-iteration bias, early stopping under compute constraints, or unstable and diverging training dynamics. Because calibration reduces to a cheap one-dimensional regression, it can be applied using small, targeted datasets – including recent historical data or a limited amount of on-policy interaction – enabling rapid deployment-time recalibration and targeted correction on subpopulations. Our guarantees are, however, inherently distribution-dependent. Calibration error in Theorem
3
is controlled in
L^{2}(\rho)
under the behavior distribution, which need not control error off distribution. Similarly, the prediction error in Theorem
4
is measured under the stationary distribution
\mu_{\hat{v}}
, and nuisance and statistical estimation errors are amplified by the factor
\hat{\kappa}_{B}
when the behavior distribution differs from the stationary distribution of the target policy. When overlap is limited, predictions may therefore generalize poorly to under-represented regions of the state space. Thus, Iterated Bellman Calibration does not fully resolve distribution shift, but it does provide calibration and convergence guarantees under minimal assumptions, without requiring realizability or Bellman completeness.

A natural direction for future work is to explicitly target this remaining
distribution mismatch by calibrating under distributions closer to the target
policy’s stationary distribution, or by reweighting the calibration sample to
better align with it, as suggested by recent work on stationary reweighting for
fitted
Q
-evaluation and control
(
68
;
69
)
.
Density-ratio estimators for stationary distributions and discounted occupancy
measures
(
35
;
84
;
45
;
37
)
provide promising tools for this purpose.

Stationary Measures and Contraction Results

Lemma 1

(Bellman contraction under a stationary measure)
.

Let
P
be a Markov operator with stationary distribution
\mu
(i.e.,
\mu=\mu P
),
and define the Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}f:=r+\gamma Pf
.
Then
P
is nonexpansive in
L^{2}(\mu)
:

\|P(f-g)\|_{2,\mu}\;\leq\;\|f-g\|_{2,\mu}.

Consequently,
\mathcal{T}
is a
\gamma
-contraction:

\|\mathcal{T}(f-g)\|_{2,\mu}\;\leq\;\gamma\,\|f-g\|_{2,\mu}.

Proof.

Because
\mu
is stationary for
P
, the operator
P
is nonexpansive in

L^{2}(\mu)
. Jensen’s inequality implies

(Ph)^{2}\leq P(h^{2})\qquad\text{pointwise}.

Integrating both sides with respect to
\mu
and using
\mu=\mu P
yields

\|Ph\|_{2,\mu}^{2}=\int(Ph)^{2}\,d\mu\leq\int P(h^{2})\,d\mu=\int h^{2}\,d\mu=\|h\|_{2,\mu}^{2}.

Now apply this with
h=f-g
. Since the reward function
r
cancels,

\mathcal{T}f-\mathcal{T}g=\gamma\,P(f-g).

Therefore,

\|\mathcal{T}f-\mathcal{T}g\|_{2,\mu}=\gamma\|P(f-g)\|_{2,\mu}\leq\gamma\|f-g\|_{2,\mu},

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2

(Stationarity under coarsening)
.

Let
P
be a Markov kernel on
\mathcal{S}
and let
\rho
be stationary for
P

(i.e.,
\rho P=\rho
).
For any measurable coarsening map
g:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

P_{g}f(s):=\mathbb{E}[\,f(S^{\prime})\mid g(S)=g(s)\,],

where
(S,S^{\prime})
has joint law
\rho(ds)\,P(s,ds^{\prime})
.
Then
\rho
is stationary for
P_{g}
.

Proof.

Let
(S,S^{\prime})\sim\rho(ds)\,P(s,ds^{\prime})
, so
S\sim\rho
and
S^{\prime}\mid S\sim P(S,\cdot)
.
Then

\displaystyle\int P_{g}f(s)\,\rho(ds)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho,P}\!\left[P_{g}f(S)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho,P}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\rho,P}\!\left[f(S^{\prime})\mid g(S)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho,P}[f(S^{\prime})].

Since
\rho
is stationary for
P
, the marginal of
S^{\prime}
is again
\rho
, hence

\mathbb{E}_{\rho,P}[f(S^{\prime})]=\int f(s)\,\rho(ds)
.
∎

F.1
Additional notation

Define the bin–index map
\hat{b}(s)
by setting
\hat{b}(s)=b
whenever

\hat{v}(s)\in I_{b}
. Let

\Pi_{\hat{v},B}
denote the binning projection

(\Pi_{\hat{v},B}g)(s):=E\!\left[g(S)\,\middle|\,\hat{v}(S)\in I_{\hat{b}(s)}\right].

Denote

\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(g)(s):=(\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(g)(s)

Define
\hat{v}_{0,B}
denote the fixed point satisfying

\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(\hat{v}_{0,B})=\hat{v}_{0,B}.

Such a fixed point exists by Banach’s fixed point theorem because

\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(g)\in\mathcal{F}_{B,\hat{v}}
whenever

g\in\mathcal{F}_{B,\hat{v}}
, and the operator is a
\gamma
-contraction in the
sup-norm.

Recall the projected
transition operator

(P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}h)(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[h(S^{\prime})\,\middle|\,\hat{v}(S)\in I_{\hat{b}(s)}\right].

It holds that

P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}\;:=\;\Pi_{\hat{v},B}P_{\pi},

and

\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(f)-\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(g)=\gamma\,P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(f-g).

F.2
Supporting lemmas

We begin by bounding the error between the projected fixed point

\hat{v}_{0,B}
and the true value function
v_{0}
.

Lemma 6

(Approximation error for the projected fixed point)
.

Assume that
\mu_{\hat{v},B}
is stationary for
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
. Then

\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\|(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}.

Proof.

Because
v_{0}
and
\hat{v}_{0,B}
satisfy

v_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{\pi}v_{0}

and

\hat{v}_{0,B}=\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\hat{v}_{0,B},

we have

\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}=\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\hat{v}_{0,B}-\mathcal{T}_{\pi}v_{0}\bigr)+(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})\mathcal{T}_{\pi}v_{0}.

Since
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(f)-\mathcal{T}_{\pi}(g)=\gamma P_{\pi}(f-g)
and

\Pi_{\hat{v},B}
is affine but linear on differences, the first term equals

\displaystyle\Pi_{\hat{v},B}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\hat{v}_{0,B}-\mathcal{T}_{\pi}v_{0}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\gamma\,\Pi_{\hat{v},B}P_{\pi}(\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma\,P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}).

Using
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}v_{0}=v_{0}
, we therefore obtain the error identity

\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}=\gamma\,P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0})+(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})v_{0}.

Taking
L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})
norms and applying the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\,\|P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0})\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}

\displaystyle\quad+\;\|(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})v_{0}\|_{2,\mu_{\hat{v},B}}.

Because
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
is a Markov operator and
\mu_{\hat{v},B}
is stationary for

P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
, it is nonexpansive in
L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})
.
By Jensen’s inequality,

(P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}h)^{2}\leq P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(h^{2})
pointwise, hence

\displaystyle\|P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}h\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int(P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}h)^{2}\,d\mu_{\hat{v},B}

\displaystyle\leq\int P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(h^{2})\,d\mu_{\hat{v},B}

\displaystyle=\int h^{2}\,d\mu_{\hat{v},B}

\displaystyle=\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}^{2}.

where the equality uses stationarity of
\mu_{\hat{v},B}
.
Applying this with
h=\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}
gives

\|P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}(\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}\leq\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}
.
Therefore,

\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}\leq\gamma\,\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}+\|(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}.

Rearranging completes the proof:

\|\hat{v}_{0,B}-v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,\|(I-\Pi_{\hat{v},B})v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\hat{v},B})}.

∎

Lemma 7

(Inexact iterations of
\mathcal{T}_{\pi,\hat{v},B}

.

Assume
\mu_{\hat{v},B}
is stationary for
P_{\pi,\hat{v},B}
