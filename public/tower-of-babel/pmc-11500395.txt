A review of temporal interference, nanoparticles, ultrasound, gene therapy, and designer receptors for Parkinson disease
Authors: A. D. Currie, J. K. Wong, M. S. Okun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this review, we summarize preclinical and clinical trials investigating innovative neuromodulatory approaches for Parkinson disease (PD) motor symptom management. We highlight the following technologies: temporal interference, nanoparticles for drug delivery, blood-brain barrier opening, gene therapy, optogenetics, upconversion nanoparticles, magnetothermal nanoparticles, magnetoelectric nanoparticles, ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles, and designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs. These studies establish the basis for novel and promising neuromodulatory treatments for PD motor symptoms.

Introduction

Parkinson disease (PD) is the second-most common neurodegenerative syndrome, with an estimated global prevalence in 2019 of 8.5 million people1. The cardinal motor features include bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability2. Non-motor features of the syndrome contribute to significant disability and commonly include constipation, REM sleep behavior disorder, depression, anxiety, orthostatic hypotension, and psychosis3. A pathological hallmark of PD is the death of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which leads to a hypodopaminergic state. PD is not just a disease of dopamine; it also affects the serotonin, acetylcholine, and norepinephrine systems, leading to dysfunction in multiple motor and non-motor pathways4.

Oral dopamine replacement therapy has been the mainstay of symptomatic treatment since the introduction of levodopa in the 1960s5; however, the associated side effects such as nausea, dyskinesias, and psychosis limit tolerability, especially as the disease progresses. The United States Food and Drug Administration approved deep brain stimulation (DBS) for the treatment of motor symptoms of advanced PD in 20026, revolutionizing advanced PD management. DBS is a highly effective therapeutic option for select patients7–10; however, this therapy can be limited by neurosurgical and device-related complications11, and it is not suitable for people with some advanced complications of PD including dementia and severe gait impairment. Therapeutic lesioning (pallidotomy, thalamotomy and subthalamotomy) has been re-emerging for PD treatment after the recent publication of trials of MRI-guided high-frequency focused ultrasound therapy12–15. Limitations of this therapy include speech- and gait-related side effects12,14,15, lack of approval for treatment of bilateral symptoms, and inability to titrate or adjust the therapy over time. Therefore, there is urgency to develop novel treatment approaches for PD, and innovative neuromodulatory approaches are emerging.

This review will focus on emerging neuromodulation strategies targeting the symptomatic management of PD. We define neuromodulation as “the targeted manipulation of neural activity through the direct application of chemical, genetic, or physical interventions, including electrical, magnetic, optical, mechanical, or thermal mechanisms”. We will review clinical trials and preclinical trials in rodents and in non-human primate populations. The less-invasive techniques reviewed in this article include temporal interference, nanoparticles for drug delivery, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) opening with focused ultrasound as a method to facilitate more efficient drug delivery. The surgical techniques reviewed will also include gene therapy for PD motor symptom management, optogenetics, nanoparticles (upconversion nanoparticles, magnetothermal nanoparticles, magnetoelectric nanoparticles, and ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles), as well as designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs.

Less-invasive techniques

Surgical treatment options for PD such as DBS are well-established and highly effective, but less-invasive techniques applied for neuromodulation maintain several advantages over more traditional surgical options. Surgery carries the risks of infection, hemorrhage, and stroke. Additionally, surgical techniques require advanced training and technology, thus limiting access, especially in under-resourced communities or in low-income countries. Finally, some patients with PD are not ideal surgical candidates, particularly as the disease progresses and there is development of more comorbidities. Less-invasive treatment options are therefore an integral component of the PD treatment armamentarium and are a central focus of ongoing neuromodulation research.

Temporal interference

Temporal interference is a technique that has recently gained traction in preclinical and clinical trials. The mechanism underpinning this technique is the summation of two high-frequency electric fields (E1 and E2) with frequencies f1 and f2. When performed correctly, each field in isolation does not modulate the underlying tissue since its frequency is too high to affect neuromodulation16. The electric field formed by summation creates a new envelope field with a lower frequency (Δf), which can modulate tissue in the location where the two electric fields overlap (Fig. 1). Grossman and colleagues first investigated temporal interference in mice16. The authors applied two electric fields with frequencies of 2.00 kHz and 2.01 kHz and demonstrated neuronal firing at a frequency of 10 Hz in the targeted hippocampal cortex. Their applied technique did not modulate the overlying cortex. This work has been considered seminal, as it raised the possibility of non-invasive neuromodulation for deep brain regions. There are, however, several barriers which remain for translation to PD treatment for humans. Lingering questions include determining whether the spatial resolution will be high enough to facilitate millimeter-level precision within human deep brain structures. Humans will require deeper tissue penetration than mice. Whether the efficacy of the technique can be maintained at therapeutic frequency (60–180 Hz) or amplitude (0–6 mA) ranges similar to those utilized in conventional DBS therapy also remains unknown17.

The first studies using temporal interference for deep brain neuromodulation in human subjects were published in 2023 in Nature Neuroscience. Violante and colleagues showed they could focally modulate hippocampal activity (measured as a decrease in brain blood oxygen level-dependent activity on functional MRI) and could enhance the accuracy of episodic memories in healthy human subjects participating in a sham-controlled face-name learning task18. In another study, Wessel and colleagues applied theta-burst stimulation to the striatum and demonstrated increases in striatal blood oxygen level-dependent activity in the group receiving temporal interference stimulation compared to the high-frequency control stimulation group. This activity correlated with improvements in a motor learning task19. The Wessel study had important translational potential because the Δf of 100 Hz that was used was within the therapeutic range commonly applied for human DBS for PD, and the authors were able to target a node in the motor network known to be affected by PD. Future studies in people with PD should focus on identifying optimal treatment parameters, defining the duration of benefit after a treatment, and developing practical methods to apply this treatment in the long term.

Nanoparticles for drug delivery

The BBB presents a significant challenge for drug delivery in PD. The BBB limits the types and sizes of potential drug therapies capable of reaching the central nervous system (CNS). The BBB thus limits the therapeutic efficacy of many drugs and indirectly contributes to side effects occurring as a result of drug activity outside the CNS. Nanoparticles are an emerging technology designed to circumvent the BBB and can be used for brain-targeted drug delivery. Poly (D,L-lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles are components of several FDA-approved drugs that target the CNS, including depot formulations of naltrexone and risperidone20. Human clinical trials using this technology for PD have not yet begun; however, rodent models of PD have revealed proof of concept. Much of the ongoing work in PD has focused on loading nanoparticles with dopamine and other currently available drug therapies in an effort to increase their potency and to decrease their off-target effects.

Recent work in murine preclinical models of PD has paved a possible road for clinical trials of dopamine-loaded nanoparticles. In early translational studies, Pahuja and colleagues showed that intravenously-administered dopamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles were associated with improvements in motor function, and these improvements lasted at least 7 days following injection21. These experiments suggested a more durable benefit compared to current levodopa therapy. This could reduce the medication burden for people with PD, which often includes frequent redosing and complex cocktails of drugs. Monge-Fuentes and colleagues hypothesized that modifying the coating of the nanoparticles could facilitate drug delivery across the BBB. Using a mouse model of PD, they showed that mice receiving dopamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles coated with albumin had improved coordination and balance compared to mice treated with dopamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles without albumin and also compared to standard levodopa without the use of nanoparticles22. Further studies of co-modifications with borneol, which enhances passage across the nasal mucosa and BBB permeability, and lactoferrin, which can aid in targeting the striatum, have opened the door to intranasal administration of nanoparticles23. When tested via intranasal administration in a rat model of PD, these nanoparticles manifested better therapeutic effectiveness than unmodified dopamine-loaded PLGA nanoparticles23. Wheat germ aglutunin co-modification of levodopa-loaded PLGA nanoparticles has also been shown to increase nasal absorption and to optimize motor improvements in mice24. Many investigators have commented that the development and use of intranasally-administered nanoparticles will likely increase access to therapy.

Nanoparticles have been heralded by some scientists as the “revolutionary therapeutic of the 21st century”25 and thus have been considered a promising non-invasive approach for PD neuromodulation. Nanoparticles for PD drug delivery remain in a preclinical stage, though there are plans underway for human trials. Additionally, there are alternative and more invasive applications of nanoparticles which have been proposed, and we will review these in the Surgical Techniques section of this review.

Blood-brain barrier opening for targeted drug delivery

Drug delivery across the BBB can be assisted by using low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU). Whereas high-intensity focused ultrasound has been used to create brain lesions in PD, lower-intensity focused ultrasound opens the potential to modulate the underlying tissues without permanent damage. The introduction of microbubbles coincident with focused ultrasound has the additive effect of amplifying ultrasonic waves26,27, thereby lowering the energy requirement for generating a biological effect (Fig. 2). There are several explanations for the mechanism(s) of LIFU to open the BBB. These include mechanical disruption of tight junctions due to expansion and compression of the microbubbles during each ultrasonic wave28,29, carrier- as well as receptor-mediated transcytosis, and caveolin-mediated endocytosis30.Fig. 2Focused ultrasound for blood-brain barrier opening.Following intravenous injection of microbubbles (grey spheres), sonication with low-intensity focused ultrasound results in transient opening of the blood-brain barrier. This technique facilitates diffusion of drugs (depicted by green spheres) into the brain parenchyma in the sonicated areas.

Preclinical studies of BBB opening have been recently reviewed31. In clinical trials, LIFU for BBB opening has been primarily applied in studies of neuro-oncology32–37 and in Alzheimer’s disease38–41. We will focus on the updates in clinical trials for BBB opening in PD. In a proof-of-concept safety and feasibility study in seven patients with PD dementia, recently it was demonstrated that successful, transient opening of the striatal BBB could be accomplished using LIFU along with microbubbles42. This procedure was well-tolerated; however, five subjects had persistent susceptibility-weighted imaging changes in the sonicated region. This finding could reveal a propensity for future hemorrhage in a susceptible region; however, given the small sample size and limited follow-up of 6 months, this will need to be clarified. Meng and colleagues recently investigated LIFU-mediated BBB opening for the delivery of glucocerebrosidase into the putamen in four patients with GBA1 mutations43. These authors demonstrated successful opening of the BBB and a 12% improvement in the blinded Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) OFF motor score at 6-month follow-up. Treatment-related adverse events included transient dyskinesias in two subjects and transient T2 hypointensities in three others. Ongoing clinical trials in PD include a phase I/II trial of MR-guided focused ultrasound delivery of glucocerebrosidase to the putamen in patients with GBA1 mutations and idiopathic PD (NCT05565443) and a randomized blinded trial of MR-guided focused ultrasound for BBB opening without delivery of a drug (NCT04593875).

There are several shortcomings when applying LIFU BBB opening. As with high-frequency focused ultrasound for lesioning, low skull density could preclude some people from receiving this therapy. Focused ultrasound therapies currently require shaving the head of hair, which for some patients is not desirable. A recent study in mice suggested that using oil as a coupling medium could be a solution to head shaving44, though this requires more extensive investigation in preclinical trials before moving to clinical applications. Additionally, LIFU requires patients to travel to centers with focused ultrasound machines, which creates burden, especially for repeated treatments. The T2 and susceptibility-weighted imaging changes associated with LIFU will warrant further investigation in larger samples, as these could have important clinical significance. The PD field should take notice of the amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) in Alzheimer’s disease monoclonal antibody therapies, including areas of edema (ARIA-E) and hemosiderin deposits/microhemorrhages (ARIA-H)45, as examples of clinically-relevant imaging changes which could suggest a need to modify or discontinue treatment. APOE genotype status appears to modulate susceptibility to these adverse events46–48. It may be critical to understand whether in PD similar genetic predispositions will potentially confer either protection or danger from LIFU BBB opening. Finally, consideration should be given to the theoretical immunologic risks of disrupting the BBB.

Surgical techniques

The most well-known and established surgical neuromodulation techniques for PD include creating permanent lesions49–53 and applying DBS7–10. More recently, high-intensity focused ultrasound has emerged as a less-invasive technique which can be used to lesion brain tissue12–15. Newer neuromodulation techniques have built upon the successes and failures of these foundational approaches. In the following sections, we will review several of the emerging techniques that require neurosurgical intervention for neuromodulation in the treatment of PD.

Gene therapy

Early viral vector-based gene therapy trials resulted in safety concerns. Some of these trials were associated with deaths due to systemic inflammatory reactions and to fatal cancers. In the case of the cancers, the cause was thought to be insertion of the transgene into unintended oncogene promoter sites54. The safety of these therapies has, however, rapidly advanced. In 2017, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first gene editing therapy to treat a human disease. Approved gene therapy options continue to grow, and nine gene therapies are currently FDA-approved. Available therapies for neurologic conditions include treatments for spinal muscular atrophy (onasemnogene abeparvovec-xioi) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (delandistrogene moxeparvovec-rokl). The allure of this type of technology is in its ability to specifically target abnormal genes and proteins by replacing, silencing, or correcting dysfunctional DNA. Promoters further increase the specificity of this type of therapy by targeting the transgenes to specific cell populations. This review will focus on clinical trials intended for the symptomatic treatment of PD; however, several ongoing trials have been investigating this approach for disease modification.

Oral dopamine supplementation in the form of levodopa is the mainstay of symptomatic treatment for PD. Some of the earliest gene therapy clinical trials for PD were built upon principles of increasing CNS dopamine through modification of various steps in the dopamine biosynthesis pathway. We will summarize a handful of these efforts.

Aromatic acid decarboxylase (AADC) converts L-DOPA to dopamine and is one of the best-characterized targets in clinical trials for PD gene therapy. In 2008, Eberling and colleagues published a Phase I trial in humans with PD, and they used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) to overexpress AADC via one-time intra-striatal infusion. Among the five subjects included, the safety profile was felt to be favorable, without any significant adverse events attributed to the viral vector, but with one asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhage attributed to the surgical procedure55. In the follow-up dose-ranging study which included two cohorts (five subjects per cohort), there were one symptomatic and two asymptomatic intracranial hemorrhages, and the authors reported an improvement of ~30% in UPDRS total and motor sub-scores in the on and off medication states for both cohorts56. In a subsequent Phase I dose-ranging study involving 15 subjects and three dose cohorts, the only serious adverse event was deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolus, and atrial fibrillation in one subject; this adverse event was attributed to immobility during the surgery. The most common vector-related side effect was dyskinesia, consistent with the proposed mechanism of action for the therapy. This adverse event improved with reduction in oral levodopa dose. Signals for therapeutic benefit included dose-dependent improvements in UPDRS motor scores in the on and off dopaminergic medication states, reductions in daily levodopa dose, and reduction in daily off time and on time with troublesome dyskinesia when measured at 12 months of follow-up57. The three-year follow-up results from this cohort demonstrated stable or improved motor function58. A double-blind Phase II trial including 14 subjects is currently ongoing (NCT03562494).

Palfi and colleagues in France took a slightly different approach to gene therapy for symptomatic treatment of PD by administering genes encoding three enzymes in the dopamine synthesis pathway. Their product, known as ProSavin, encodes genes for GTP cyclohydroxylase 1, tyrosine hydroxylase, and AADC, using a lentiviral vector. In their first Phase I/II study, they divided the 15 subjects into three dose cohorts and injected the vector into the bilateral putamina. No severe drug-related adverse events were identified; the most common adverse events were on-medication dyskinesia in 11 subjects, which improved with levodopa dose reductions, and on-off phenomena in nine subjects. Across all cohorts, off-medication UPDRS motor scores improved 11 points on average at 12 months of follow-up59. In the subsequent five-year long-term follow-up study, dyskinesia and on-off phenomena continued to be the most common treatment-related adverse events (in 11 subjects each); dyskinesia was rated as severe in one subject. Eight subjects received standard-of-care subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS and were excluded from the efficacy analysis. The majority of the remaining patients manifested continued improvement in off-medication UPDRS motor scores for the duration of follow-up60. A subsequent Phase II double-blind study (NCT03720418) was halted due to resource constraints61.

The other major line of investigation in gene therapy for symptomatic treatment of PD was the expression of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), an enzyme that catalyzes GABA synthesis62. Pathological STN overactivity in PD has been associated with a lack of GABA-mediated inhibition from the globus pallidus63, so restoration of GABAergic tone to the STN was a logical approach for symptomatic PD treatment. This approach was designed to circumvent the canonical dopamine supplementation approach to PD management, potentially avoiding hyperdopaminergic side effects such as dyskinesias, psychosis, and orthostasis. In the first clinical trials, Kaplitt and colleagues injected AAV-GAD into the unilateral STN in 12 subjects with PD. The investigators identified no treatment-related adverse events during the 12-month follow up. Statistically significant improvements were manifested in the motor scores in the on- and off-dopaminergic medication states, and they persisted in follow-up visits64. In a subsequent double-blind trial, 37 subjects were randomized to receive injection of the AAV-GAD vector into the bilateral STN or to receive sham surgery. At six months, off-medication UPDRS motor sub-scores improved significantly in both groups, with greater improvement in the AAV-GAD group65. After a hiatus due to dissolution of the sponsoring company, a subsequent randomized controlled trial is now underway (NCT05603312).

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based gene editing strategies are emerging as an exciting gene therapy technique. The premise of this therapy rests upon identifying the target gene using guide RNA and then making a highly specific cut in genomic DNA using an endonuclease (most commonly CRISPR-associated protein 9, or Cas9)66. This technique has not yet been tested in clinical trials for CNS disorders, but it has been investigated in clinical trials for cancer and other disorders since 201667. As a treatment for PD, CRISPR could be used for both non-genetic and monogenic forms of PD, and it can also be used to answer the question of the importance of alpha-synuclein in PD pathogenesis. The barriers to translation into clinical trials for PD are similar to the other gene therapy techniques discussed above, including the need to deliver the therapy across the BBB, minimize immunogenicity, and avoid off-target gene editing66.

Gene therapy is, therefore, an area of active clinical investigation and an emerging neuromodulation technique that has demonstrated a favorable safety profile and has shown encouraging improvements in various PD symptoms. Gene therapy techniques are also being combined with other approaches to develop innovative new therapeutics, as discussed in the following sections and as summarized in Fig. 3. Their limitations include the need for neurosurgery to inject the vector into the target structure, inability to titrate the treatment as symptoms fluctuate and as the disease progresses, and the uncertainty of the durability of benefit. Non-viral gene delivery mechanisms have also been recently investigated; nanoparticles, nanodisks, and hydrogels are all leading techniques68–70. Another challenge for this therapy was that the improvement was less than in DBS cohorts, and this “high bar” for DBS therapy has been an added challenge for gene therapy researchers. Recent experience with antisense oligonucleotide gene therapy for Huntington’s disease and other disorders has demonstrated to the field that “more delivery” is not necessarily optimal or better and can potentially increase the risk of adverse effects71. Optogenetics: cell type-specific neuromodulation

Optogenetic neuromodulation techniques use genetic modification to express light-sensitive proteins called opsins on the cell surface and modulate defined cell populations. There are three key components in the optogenetic toolkit: opsins, vector delivery, and a light source72. Opsins derive from a family of light-sensitive transmembrane proteins and are modulated by specific wavelengths of visible light. Opsins can have excitatory or inhibitory effects. Vectors deliver the transgenes that encode the opsins into the cell population of interest. The transgenes integrate into the host cell’s DNA, and the photoreceptors are expressed on the cell surface. Light sources can then selectively modulate these genetically modified cells with a high degree of spatial and temporal specificity (Fig. 3). The canonical target nuclei for DBS are collectively comprised of several populations of neurons and other cell bodies, axons, and glia. Optogenetics offers a method to modulate each of these populations with more specificity, thereby potentially minimizing stimulation-induced side effects. In contrast to nonspecific electric current-based neuromodulation, which relies heavily on accurate electrode placement, optogenetics could maximize clinical benefit in cases of suboptimal light source placement by allowing an increase in light intensity to capture the targeted cell population without modulating other nearby eloquent structures. Thus, although optogenetics has been most commonly used to elucidate basic concepts in neuroscience, recently there has been growing interest in both preclinical and clinical applications.

Preclinical work in PD models has identified therapeutic benefit that could serve as a basis for future clinical trials using optogenetics. Kravitz and colleagues applied optogenetics to modulate medium spiny neurons in the striatum of mice and demonstrated that activation of the direct basal ganglia pathway neurons could rescue Parkinsonian motor behavior73. Two groups have subsequently demonstrated that optogenetic stimulation of parvalbumin neurons in the globus pallidus externa (GPe) of Parkinsonian mice can also reverse motor symptoms in the animal model of the disease74,75. Thus, these studies are paving the foundation for therapeutic applications by identifying the cell populations that may mediate improvement in Parkinsonian symptoms.

While the cell type specificity of optogenetics opens limitless therapeutic potential, it also introduces nuances that could prove to be formidable translational challenges. For instance, Gradinaru and colleagues demonstrated that inhibition of glutamatergic neurons and astroglial cells in the STN did not improve Parkinsonian symptoms; however, high-frequency stimulation of afferent axons in the STN did result in a marked therapeutic benefit76. Similarly, indiscriminate stimulation of the GPe had no effect on Parkinsonian symptoms, but both selective activation of the parvalbumin-expressing subpopulation and inhibition of the Lim homeobox 6-expressing neurons restored movement in a murine PD model74. Thus, a fundamental challenge in applying optogenetics for PD neuromodulation will be to determine which cellular components of the network will mediate the clinical improvements (e.g., specific subpopulations of neurons, axon projections, and glial modulators).

The term “optogenetics” was coined less than 20 years ago, in 200677, so the scientific community’s experience with optogenetic techniques is in its early stages. Thus, a major challenge for optogenetics will be to better characterize the methodological nuances of this technique. Yu and colleagues recently hypothesized that the lack of effect derived from STN glutamatergic cell body stimulation previously reported by Gradinaru and colleagues76 may have been due to the ‘slow’ channelrhodopsins that were applied78. In fact, the Yu group used a faster opsin in a similar protocol to Gradinaru and colleagues and demonstrated reversal of parkinsonian symptoms. Similarly, a recent paper by Di Bisceglie Caballero and colleagues reported that indiscriminate optogenetic stimulation of the GPe improved parkinsonian behaviors, in contrast with earlier published work74,75. The authors hypothesized that the discrepancy could be due to differences in photostimulation protocols, PD models, or timing of the behavioral testing. As the scientific community gains more experience with applying and interpreting optogenetic techniques, this technique could yield important insights for future neuromodulation in PD.

Each of the three components of the optogenetic toolkit (opsins, vectors, and light source) present unique translational challenges. Opsin immunogenicity presents a potential risk since the opsins are derived from microbes or cells that are not native to the CNS72. Choosing the optimal cell population for opsin expression could be the difference between success and failure of the technique, and the degree of homology in cell populations between animal models and humans is a pertinent consideration. Genetic modification is necessary to express the opsins in the cell populations of interest, and safety of the viral vectors is a concern. Yet another challenge is how to deliver the vector to the intended location. For neurological disorders like PD, this method will likely require stereotactically guided injections, introducing the risks of neurosurgical intervention. Finally, light sources must be surgically implanted and can restrict movement. Recent advances in miniaturization and remote control of light sources have facilitated freer movement of animals79,80, especially when compared to tethered optical fibers that are commonly used for this technique. Upconversion nanoparticles, discussed in greater detail below, are another emerging solution that could eliminate the need for an implanted light source. Thus, several barriers remain to translating optogenetic techniques to clinical trials for complex neurological diseases such as PD, but researchers are actively pursuing creative solutions.

The first clinical trials of optogenetics are investigating treatments for ocular and retinal diseases. Three trials for retinitis pigmentosa (NCT04945772, NCT02556736, NCT04919473, NCT03326336) and one in Stargardt Disease (NCT05417126) are ongoing and should help address some of the barriers to human translation for disorders of the CNS. It remains unclear if optogenetics will play a role in the future PD treatment arsenal. If the formidable translational barriers can be overcome, optogenetics could be a powerful tool for neuromodulation with a high degree of temporal and cellular specificity.

Nanoparticles

The nanoparticles reviewed in this section all transduce a biologically inactive or subtherapeutic energy source such as light, magnetic fields, or ultrasound into an alternative energy source which can be used to drive neuromodulation. Most nanoparticles require invasive surgical techniques to inject them into the intended target structure or region. As a neurotherapeutic, nanoparticles have been designed as a potentially more precise technique compared to current methods for neuromodulation, only modulating the cells near the particles.

Upconversion nanoparticles for optogenetic stimulation

Upconversion nanoparticles can potentially harness capabilities of optogenetics while eliminating the need for an implanted or tethered light source. Similar to optogenetics, this technique requires neurosurgical injection of a viral vector to generate opsin expression, and it additionally requires administration of the nanoparticles, most commonly through stereotactic injection. The nanoparticles can then convert near-infrared light, which scatters less than visible light and can penetrate the skull, into visible light. Lin and colleagues first investigated upconversion nanoparticles in vivo by packaging them into a glass optrode. These investigators implanted the optrode into the occipital cortex of anesthetized mice81. The authors then were able to measure an increase in spiking activity when near-infrared light pulses were delivered. Chen and colleagues extended the Lin technique to include freely moving mice. Chen primed cells in the ventral tegmental area to express opsins, and then stereotactically injected the nanoparticles in the brain. The investigators then applied a transcranial near-infrared laser, which triggered the nanoparticles to emit blue light. The blue light excited hippocampal granule cells and triggered a learned freezing behavior82. Liu and colleagues have recently moved beyond single-wavelength nanoparticles and pioneered tricolor upconversion nanoparticle-mediated optogenetics83. These nanoparticles emit different colors dependent on the wavelength of the incident near-infrared light. The Liu technique thus has facilitated selective modulation of three neuronal populations. The authors subsequently showed that changes in movement within their mouse model paralleled the changes in the different cells that were excited.

Future avenues for upconversion nanoparticle research will need to include testing in PD models, characterizing the immunogenicity of the particles, determining long-term durability, and developing refined stimulation protocols. Additional important translational steps will involve engineering practical methods to deliver the infrared light and developing a portable power source for this light. The use of nonhuman primate studies could aid in determining whether near-infrared light could penetrate several centimeters of skull and brain tissue. Penetration of the skull will be necessary for deep brain neuromodulation in humans, and it will be important to determine the safety of such a technique, with special emphasis on documenting potential adverse skin effects.

Magnetothermal nanoparticles

Magnetothermal nanoparticles transduce energy from an external magnetic field and convert the energy into thermal energy. The thermal energy is then used to modulate cells that have been genetically modified to express heat-sensitive ion channels. To date the most commonly-used thermal receptor is the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) nociceptor. Magnetothermal nanoparticle techniques thus require stereotactic injection of both a viral vector to encode the thermosensitive ion channel and the nanoparticles (Figs. 3 and 4).

In the first mammalian trial of this technique, Chen and colleagues expressed TRPV1 channels and then injected magnetothermal nanoparticles into the ventral tegmental area of mice and measured trains of action potentials following magnetic stimulation84. Munshi and colleagues applied this technique in awake, freely moving mice, testing one location in the motor cortex and two in the striatum. These authors demonstrated reliable changes in rotational behavior as well as improvements in freezing of gait85. Hescham and colleagues then further replicated the success of the technique by performing injections into the STN of healthy mice and demonstrating behavioral modification upon application of an external magnetic field. These investigators were also able to demonstrate reversal of Parkinsonian motor symptoms in two different murine models of PD86.

Limitations of magnetothermal nanoparticles include the need for stereotactic injection of the vectors and nanoparticles, as well as the requirement for genetic modification of cells to express the appropriate ion channels. Since this approach relies upon generation of thermal energy, careful safety testing to define the therapeutic window will be crucial. Some experts have hypothesized that frequencies lower than those currently used for DBS may be required to avoid damaging tissues87. Magnetothermal DBS also requires a strong magnetic energy source, and this could limit movement. Advances in miniaturization and portability of the magnetic coils could facilitate trials in nonhuman primates and possibly one day trials in humans.

Magnetoelectric nanoparticles

Magnetoelectric nanoparticles also rely upon a magnetic energy source; however, these nanoparticles transduce the energy into electric fields. The advantage of this technique is that it can modulate local neuronal activity without the need for genetic modification. Most protocols still require neurosurgical injection of the nanoparticles (Fig. 4), though studies are beginning to investigate alternative, less-invasive means of delivering the nanoparticles to the intended sites of neuromodulation.

Guduru and colleagues performed the first in vivo studies with magnetoelectric nanoparticles. The authors used a transcranial electromagnetic coil as the energy source and measured the neuromodulation output with an electroencephalogram (EEG)88. The investigators then injected the nanoparticles intravenously and used a magnetic field to drive the nanoparticles across the BBB. Their technique avoided the need for neurosurgical injection of the nanoparticles. Guduru also used relatively small, battery-driven electromagnetic coils to generate the magnetic field. This technique operated with lower power requirements due to the more efficient magnetoelectric coupling, especially when compared to magnetothermal coupling. Nguyen and colleagues also used the intravenous nanoparticle administration approach, and they were able to refine the distribution of the nanoparticles to the cortex by steering the particles to the desired location through the use of a magnetic field89. Nguyen measured an increase in somatic calcium spiking when delivering magnetic stimulation, but the effect waned 72 h following nanoparticle administration. Finally, in a return to more conventional nanoparticle delivery, Kozielski and colleagues stereotactically injected magnetoelectric nanoparticles into the STN region of healthy mice and demonstrated improvement in dynamic gait90.

Magnetoelectric nanoparticles offer two distinct advantages compared to the other nanoparticles in this review: genetic modification is not required, and some of the particles can be administered peripherally. Barriers to translation include investigating this therapy in animal models of PD and further characterizing the safety and effectiveness of this approach. How to apply the magnetic field required to activate the particles without imposing restrictions on movement will also need to be addressed to move this therapy toward clinical trials.

Ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles

Ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles marry LIFU and nanoparticles to accomplish drug delivery. The nanoparticles are delivered systemically, then they are activated or “uncaged” by ultrasound focused on the intended site of action (Fig. 4). The cargo of the nanoparticles is then free to accomplish neuromodulation of the surrounding cells. This technique offers a high degree of spatial selectivity by limiting the distribution of the active drug to the area that is sonicated.

Airan and colleagues first investigated ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles in vivo. The authors administered propofol-loaded nanoparticles intravenously in a rat model of status epilepticus and demonstrated improvement in EEG in the animals that received the propofol-loaded particles91. A follow-up study revealed that this approach could achieve millimeter-level precision92. Similar work showed that focused ultrasound-mediated release of pentobarbital from nanodroplets in the motor cortex of rats led to transient ipsilateral stumbling93. Kim and colleagues engineered nanoparticles that generated nitric oxide upon sonication; this nitric oxide transiently opened the BBB and allowed escape of the particles into the parenchyma. Subsequent sonication of the piezoelectric particles generated electric current that triggered voltage-gated ion channels on dopaminergic neurons to open, leading to endogenous dopamine release. The investigators administered the particles intravenously, and they subsequently sonicated the STN daily for 8 days and demonstrated improvements in Parkinsonian symptoms94.

The therapeutic potential of ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles is only beginning to be unlocked. The spectrum of ultrasound-responsive nanoparticles is wide and expands beyond the drug release domain that has been the focus of recent preclinical studies. Potential effects of sonication on the nanoparticles can include microbubble cavitation, phase change (from liquid to gas, wherein this phase change releases the cargo of the nanoparticle), production of thermal energy, piezoelectric activation, and generation of free radical species as methods to accomplish neuromodulation95.

Nevertheless, extensive work will be needed to ensure the biocompatibility of the particles and the safety of the sonication parameters. Notably, prior studies of this technique have focused on releasing drugs that easily cross the BBB. If the particles can be engineered in a way that allows administration of larger or less lipophilic drugs, this could amplify the potential applications for this technique. Another crucial focus of future studies should be defining practical methods for delivering the ultrasound. Preclinical trials will also need to establish effectiveness before translation to human clinical trials.

Designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs

Off-target drug effects in PD arise from stimulation of dopamine receptors outside of the intended targets in the CNS. These effects, including nausea, orthostatic hypotension, hallucinations, and dyskinesias, can significantly limit the therapeutic benefit patients may derive from dopamine replacement therapies. Designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD) is a novel neuromodulation approach that attempts to circumvent this conundrum and is a topic of active investigation for PD treatment. DREADD techniques specifically modulate a particular area or cell population, and they often use viral vectors with promoters specific for the intended target. The transgenes delivered by these vectors insert synthetic G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) that are activated by biologically inert substances into the plasma membrane. Clozapine-N-Oxide (CNO), an inactive derivative of the antipsychotic drug clozapine, is the most commonly-used ligand for these receptors96. This technique requires neurosurgical injection of the viral vector to encode the novel receptors, but the drugs that activate these receptors can be administered orally or parenterally (Fig. 3).

In the first in vivo studies of DREADDs in awake and freely moving mice, Alexander and colleagues expressed synthetic GPCRs in forebrain principal neurons. These authors administered CNO via intraperitoneal injection and were able to demonstrate increased locomotion and the induction of stereotypies97. In the first studies in a PD mouse model, dopaminergic neurons expressing synthetic GPCRs were transplanted into the striatum. Systemic administration of CNO led to improvements in Parkinsonian behaviors including apomorphine-induced rotations and improvements on a stepping test98. In another demonstration of a potential therapeutic application in PD, Alcacer and colleagues used DREADDs in a PD mouse model to stimulate direct pathway striatal projection neurons with CNO. They demonstrated behavioral improvements which mimicked the administration of levodopa. Similarly, the authors demonstrated that inhibition of the indirect pathway striatal projection neurons attenuated levodopa-induced dyskinesia99.

Several limitations and barriers remain in the translation of DREADDs into human PD therapeutics. The technique requires transgenic modification to induce expression of the designer receptor, which may raise safety concerns. We included this technique in the surgical neuromodulation section because stereotactic injections of the viral vectors are required in current iterations of the technique. However, novel mechanisms for peripheral gene delivery are being developed, such as delivering the genes in nanoparticles100. Perhaps in this way DREADDs could be developed into a less-invasive application. The temporal resolution of the therapy is also limited by the pharmacokinetics of the drug, preventing the real-time adjustments in therapy that are possible with many other techniques described in this review. On the other hand, there are distinct advantages to using an oral drug to activate the receptors, including the convenience of administration and that an external device or energy source is not required. While most investigations of the use of DREADDs for PD have used CNO, Gomez and colleagues showed that the active metabolite clozapine, rather than the inert CNO, is the compound that actually crosses the BBB and acts as the effector for this therapy101. This could limit therapeutic applications in humans due to off-target effects of clozapine, including somnolence, tachycardia, constipation, sialorrhea, and hematologic effects. A recent focus in this field has been on identifying alternative ligands for these receptors, including deschloroclozapine102, JHU37152103, and JHU37160103. While there have been no studies of DREADDs in non-human primate models of PD, this technique has been used in non-human primate studies of reward pathways104. This work could thus open possibilities for future studies using PD models in nonhuman primates, and eventually for clinical trials in humans.

Conclusions

As the prevalence of PD continues to grow, there is increasing need to develop novel, safer, and more effective PD treatments. DBS is a well-established and highly effective neuromodulatory therapy that is standard-of-care for medication-refractory motor symptoms in PD7–10,105. The landscape of emerging neuromodulation technologies for the treatment of PD is broad and promising, extending far beyond DBS therapy.

Among the less-invasive techniques presented in this review, temporal interference and LIFU for BBB opening offer a high degree of structural precision and temporal resolution for modulating deep brain structures; however, they require additional extracranial devices to deliver the therapy. In the case of LIFU for BBB opening, head shaving is also required. Nanoparticles for drug delivery offer less precision in targeting and less real-time titration of the therapy; however, these may be superior in ease of administration, with intravenous and intranasal formulations being investigated.

Among the more invasive techniques, optogenetics, magnetothermal stimulation, DREADDs, and gene therapies all offer the highest level of potential targeting precision, with preclinical studies of the former three therapies facilitating cell population-specific neuromodulation. Temporal precision is highest for the therapies requiring an external energy source (optogenetics, magnetothermal nanoparticles, magnetoelectric nanoparticles, and ultrasound-gated nanoparticles); however, the logistical challenges of delivering these energy sources without constraining movement remain formidable barriers to human translation. While gene therapy is the least titratable of these options, clinical trials have demonstrated the potential for durable benefit on the order of several years, possibly without the need for retreatment.

While these therapies hold promise for the treatment of PD motor symptoms, there are several barriers to translation into the clinic that merit mention. Most of the studies reviewed herein are preclinical, with supporting evidence limited to rodents. Animal models of PD motor symptoms are important but imperfect, and historically, attempts to replicate the benefits identified in preclinical studies have been met with limited success106. In this sense, BBB opening for targeted drug delivery and gene therapy are two of the more promising therapies presented in this review, since they have been investigated in clinical trials for persons with PD.

The utility of newer neuromodulation strategies will inevitably be judged by the ability to improve upon existing therapeutics or whether they can fill a niche not met by existing treatment options. Thus, the high degree of efficacy and relatively favorable safety profile of existing treatments such as DBS present another challenge for translation of these therapies. The less-invasive techniques presented in this review could contribute to the therapeutic landscape by offering non-surgical alternatives for people who are not ideal surgical candidates or for those who prefer not to undergo neurosurgery. The more-invasive techniques presented in this review could prevent the hardware- and device-related complications of DBS, and they could facilitate more precise therapeutic targeting, in some cases down to a specific cell population of interest. Finally, rather than replacing DBS, these therapies could be used in conjunction with DBS to amplify or provide additive, or even synergistic, effects. These new therapies could target different nodes in the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuits when compared to those traditionally targeted by DBS, or they could tie in new motor or non-motor networks107. If these techniques can address symptoms that are not treated by DBS, such as gait dysfunction, dysarthria, or nonmotor symptoms, this would be an important advancement in the field.

Collectively, the studies discussed in this review have established the scientific basis for novel and promising neuromodulatory treatments for PD motor symptoms. These therapies could be used instead of, or in conjunction with, existing therapies such as DBS. The scope of this review was limited to the motor symptoms of PD, but exciting work is also ongoing for the treatment of nonmotor symptoms, which holds potential for significant impacts on quality of life108,109. Perhaps even more exciting is the pursuit of disease modification, which could treat, or even reverse, motor and nonmotor symptoms. Future work will need to more directly address the logistical and methodological barriers to bringing these therapies to the bedside. As research in neuromodulation for PD continues to advance, the central focus should remain on improving symptoms and quality of life for persons with PD.
