Biological Patience in Dental Trauma: An Emerging Clinical Competency
Authors: Mohammed Turky, Paul V Abbott, Venkateshbabu Nagendrababu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The management of traumatic dental injuries has greatly advanced due to significant innovations in diagnostic processes.1 The advent of cone-beam computed tomography allows for three-dimensional imaging, providing detailed insights into the dental and surrounding anatomical structures.2,3 Additionally, other digital technologies and enhanced pulp status assessment methods have emerged, enabling clinicians to evaluate the health of dental pulp more accurately.4,5 Moreover, the introduction of artificial intelligence applications offers potential to recognise post-traumatic changes with unparalleled ability, paving the way for improvements in diagnostic accuracy and in monitoring the progress of healing.1,6,7

Nevertheless, as the ability to discern fine abnormalities in dental trauma increases, it raises an important concern: are clinicians becoming less comfortable with the natural biological processes of healing that take time?

Dental traumatology holds a distinct position among the various dental specialties, primarily because achieving optimal outcomes largely hinges on the time factor.8,9 Processes such as pulp revascularisation, continued root development, periodontal healing, and the resolution of transient inflammatory changes are all inherently dynamic biological processes. These processes frequently necessitate careful observation rather than hasty treatment decisions, as definitive conclusions regarding the outcomes can only emerge throughout the natural healing timeline. In many instances, allowing time to transpire is not a delay in treatment; it is an essential element of an effective management strategy.

Historically, clinicians have relied on observational approaches because they faced constraints related to diagnostic information. They were compelled to observe wounds and healing processes due to the limited availability of imaging modalities. In contrast, the contemporary landscape of dental trauma management offers a wealth of diagnostic data.1,2 However, this accessibility to detailed information has shifted the paradigm from passive observation to deliberate decision-making. Clinicians may now identify minor abnormalities, including subtle areas of surface resorption, slight pulp changes, and insignificant periodontal alterations that – while detectable – may not be clinically significant. Such early-stage findings can be detected much sooner than previously, which, although valuable, can lead to improper inclinations to intervene prematurely, before the biological healing trajectory is thoroughly understood.

This issue extends beyond the realm of dental traumatology; it reflects a broader trend in healthcare where the heightened sensitivity of diagnostic techniques and devices raises concerns about overdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions.10 The progression of the ability to identify abnormalities has frequently outstripped clinicians’ comprehension of the clinical significance of those findings, leading them to feel obligated to take action solely based on the identification of a change seen on an image.10

In the context of dental trauma, recognising the importance of observation becomes increasingly critical.8 Many post-traumatic findings represent transitional stages within the healing journey and should not be mistaken for signs of irreversible damage or pathosis. Engaging in premature intervention may disrupt the natural recovery processes, expose patients to procedures that may be unnecessary, or trigger treatment pathways that lead to unnecessary complications.

What may be increasingly required in clinical practice is an attribute that could be termed ‘biological patience’. Biological patience should not be misconstrued as passive inaction or disregard for therapeutic needs; rather, it embodies a conscious and deliberate willingness to allow the necessary time for biological healing processes to manifest clearly before initiating interventions. This concept necessitates a robust understanding of biological principles, a commitment to ongoing monitoring, and an acceptance that uncertainty is an inherent aspect of trauma management.

It is also essential to clarify that advocating for biological patience does not equate to dismissing the role of technological advancements in clinical practice. Modern imaging capabilities and diagnostic tools have revolutionised the field of dental traumatology, significantly enhancing patient care.2,4,5 Still, these technologies must serve to augment clinical judgment, not erode the capacity for careful observation. The mere detection of a change on a diagnostic image does not inherently necessitate immediate treatment. Similarly, choosing to refrain from intervention does not signify a lack of vigilance or care.

As diagnostic technologies advance and become more sophisticated, one of the paramount competencies for clinicians in dental traumatology will be to adeptly discern which clinical findings genuinely require prompt action and which simply necessitate the need for patience to allow the natural healing process to unfold. The future challenge, then, may not solely lie in acquiring the ability to recognise more – rather, it may fundamentally rest in cultivating the patience to wait and observe the intricate dynamics of biological healing following an injury.
