Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) testing of high-grade serous carcinoma using cytological samples
Authors: Izadora Demmer, Michael Bento Schmid, Diana Born, Gabriel Handschin, Wolfram Jochum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHomologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is associated with increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitor therapy in various cancer types, including high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC). Current guidelines recommend HRD testing to guide treatment decisions in HGSG. The majority of HRD assays rely on tumour tissue analysis. The main objective of this study was to assess cytological HGSC samples for HRD testing.MethodsNext generation sequencing using the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCAP) panel was performed on 110 matched cytological and histological samples from 55 treatment-naïve HGSC patients to determine the mutational status of 15 homologous recombination repair (HRR)-associated genes including BRCA1/2, and the HRD status based on BRCA1/2 mutational status and genomic instability metric (GIM). Concordance analysis was performed.ResultsCompared to matched histological samples, cytological HGSC samples revealed an overall concordance rate of 89.7% for HRR-associated gene mutations and of 100% for BRCA1/2 mutations, respectively. A moderate correlation between GIM values (Pearson correlation coefficient 0.48), but an overall concordance rate of 90.9% for the HRD status was observed.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that cytological HGSC samples can be employed for HRR-associated gene mutation and HRD testing using next generation sequencing with the OCAP panel.

Introduction

High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) is the most frequent and lethal subtype of ovarian, fallopian tube, and peritoneal cancer, accounting for 70% of cases and 80% of deaths [1, 2]. It is commonly diagnosed at a late stage, contributing to the poor five-year survival rate of 31% for advanced HGSC [3]. Standard treatment involves cytoreductive surgery and platinum- and taxane-based chemotherapy. Despite adjuvant maintenance therapy, relapses are common [2, 3]. Early-stage cases, comprising 10–15% of patients, often have better outcomes with curative surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy [2].

Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), attributable to defects in homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathways, is present in approximately 50% of HGSC [3–5]. In HGSC, the most frequent causes of HRD are germline or somatic loss-of-function mutations in the BRCA1/2 genes [6]. Mutations in other HRR-genes including PALB2, RAD51C and RAD51D are infrequent. HRD sensitizes tumours to DNA-damaging agents like platinum-based chemotherapy and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) through synthetic lethality in various cancer types, including HGSC [7]. PARP inhibition has become an established treatment option in advanced HGSC and various PARPi have achieved regulatory approval in many countries [8]. As a result, current guidelines recommend HRD testing to guide treatment decisions in newly diagnosed HGSC [1, 9].

The HRD status of HGSC can be assessed by various assays, which employ array comparative genomic hybridization, array-based copy number alteration analysis, or next-generation sequencing (NGS) of targeted gene panels [10]. The Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay (Myriad Genetics, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA), which represents the most frequently used HRD assay in clinical trials, assesses genomic instability through metrics such as loss of heterozygosity (LOH), large-scale state transitions (LSTs), and telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI) [7]. Importantly, all currently available HRD assays predominantly rely on tumour tissue, lacking established validation for cytological samples.

The Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCAP, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) is a NGS panel designed to analyse more than 500 cancer-related genes. It simultaneously detects multiple types of variants in a single workflow, including single nucleotide variants, insertions and deletions, copy number variants, microsatellite instability, tumour mutational burden, and LOH. In addition, HRD status can be assessed by the genomic instability metric (GIM) based on LOH quantification.

While cytological samples, such as ascites or pleural effusions, are frequently used for the initial diagnosis of HGSC, HRD testing is mainly performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens from surgical resections or tissue biopsies. Consequently, patients with advanced, inoperable disease (19.9% of patients according to the RESPONSE trial) [11] often require additional invasive procedures to facilitate HRD testing. Cytological samples offer a readily accessible, less invasive alternative for HRD assessment. However, technical challenges, such as low DNA yield and low tumour cell content, may hamper analysis [12], and a significant fraction of cytological samples completely lack neoplastic cells (23% according to a recent meta-analysis) [13]. Despite these obstacles, prior studies have successfully used cytological samples for mutational profiling, including analysis of BRCA1/2 variants [12, 14, 15]. By leveraging advancements in NGS and optimized sample processing, cytological HRD testing could enhance patient stratification and expand access to targeted therapies.

The main objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and performance of HRD testing using HGSC cytological samples compared to matched tissue samples.

Materials and methods

Study design and tumour samples

For local verification of the OCAP, histologically confirmed HGSC tissue samples were used for which Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus (hereinafter referred to as “reference assay”) assay results and at least one paraffin block with residual tumour tissue were available (Institute of Pathology, Cantonal Hospital St. Gallen).

To build a cohort of matched cytological and histological HGSC samples, the laboratory information system of the Institute of Pathology, Cantonal Hospital St. Gallen was then searched for HGSC patients diagnosed between 2015 and 2023, for whom paired cytological and histological tumour samples were available that had been collected within a period of 4 weeks. All cytological samples were obtained prior to primary treatment of newly diagnosed HGSC. Samples obtained after neo-adjuvant treatment were excluded from the analysis, as were cytological cell blocks. Demographic and clinical data were collected by chart review. Specimen preparation, histological and cytological analysis

HGSC biopsy/resection specimens and cytological smears were processed using standard methods [16]. Carcinomas were classified according to the World Health Organization Classification of Female Genital Tumors (2020). Tissue sections were reviewed by a board-certified pathologist to confirm the pathological diagnosis of HGSC, to select one representative tissue block for molecular analysis, and to determine tumour cell content. Papanicolaou-stained cytological smears were reviewed to confirm the presence of carcinoma cells and to determine tumour cell content. For both histological and cytological samples, a minimum of 30% tumour cell content was required to be eligible for molecular testing.

Nucleic acid extraction, library preparation and next-generation sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from H&E-stained sections and Papanicolaou-stained slides as previously described [16]. Briefly, DNA was obtained from unstained 3 μm FFPE tissue sections or from cells scraped from stained cytological specimens without destaining. Extraction was performed using the Maxwell® CSC or RSC DNA FFPE Kits (Promega, Fitchburg, WI, USA) on the corresponding automated instruments, following the manufacturer’s instructions with in-house protocol optimizations. DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water, quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA Assay on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and stored at 4–8 °C for up to one week or at −20 °C for long-term storage. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed with the OCAP, which targets 13,473 amplicons in 517 genes covering approximately 1.50 million base pairs of the human genome, of which 1.05 million are located within exonic regions. The assay also includes the 15 HRR pathway genes covered by the reference assay (ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCL, PALB2, PPP2R2A, RAD51B, RAD51C, RAD51D, and RAD54L). NGS was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions using the following instruments: Ion Chef™ for templating preparation and Ion GeneStudio™ S5 System for sequencing. Initial quality control and data visualization were conducted using the Torrent Suite™ software, which assessed key metrics including chip loading density, median read length, total number of mapped reads, and mean sequencing depth. Variant calling was performed using the Ion Reporter® analysis software, applying the assay-specific workflow.

Data analysis

In-house HGSC sequencing data was analysed with the Ion ReporterTM Software version 5.20 using the “Oncomine Comprehensive Plus - w3.0 – DNA - Single Sample analysis” workflow for variant calling, which included the detection of SNVs, long deletions, exon deletions/duplications and copy number variations (CNVs, gains and losses). Analysis of DNA sequence variants was restricted to the 15 HRR pathway genes covered by the reference assay (ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCL, PALB2, PPP2R2A, RAD51B, RAD51C, RAD51D, and RAD54L). Sequence variants were reported according to the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) nomenclature and classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign according to the ACMG/AMP/CAP joint consensus 2015 [17] using multiple databases (including ClinVar, Varsome, Franklin and OncoKB). Variants in genes not listed above were not reported in order to preserve comparability with the reference assay. In addition, CNVs and long deletions were not evaluated in this study because they are not currently used for determination the HRD status using the OCAP. For the assessment of the HRD status, the OCAP uses the GIM, a proprietary measurement that quantifies genomic scarring associated with HRD. The GIM is a numeric value between 0 and 100 that summarizes unbalanced copy number changes in autosomes using genomic segmentation. The Ion ReporterTM OCAP analysis workflow utilizes heterozygous population single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to determine ploidy levels of genomic segments. For this purpose, the genome is divided into contiguous segments of similar ploidy levels using a circular binary segmentation algorithm. Log odds ratios for variant allele frequency of observed population SNPs and copy number ratios (by CNV pipeline) for each segment are calculated and used to infer loss of heterozygosity (LOH) for each genomic segment. Segment-level LOH events are intersected with targeted gene boundaries to determine LOH events in selected genes and are aggregated to determine genomic LOH percentage (%LOH). A GIM equal or greater than 16 as per the manufacturer’s recommendation, was used as cut-off for HRD positivity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using PRISM 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Presence of a pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variant and HRD status were considered categorical variables (positive vs. negative). The reference assay defined positive HRD status as the presence of a clinically significant BRCA1/2 variant and/or a genomic instability score (GIS) of 42 or greater. Analogously, we defined OCAP-based HRD positivity as the detection of a pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 sequence variant and/or a genomic instability metric (GIM) value of 16 or greater. Interassay concordance of categorical variables was analysed with the Fisher’s exact test. In addition, sensitivity and specificity values were calculated. Concordance analysis between histological and cytological samples included all sequence variants regardless of their pathogenicity classification (benign/likely benign variants, variants of unknown significance, pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants). Pearson correlation was used to analyse the association between the continuous GIS and GIM variables. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Verification of the OCAP for HRD testing

We retrospectively identified 27 HGSC tissue samples for which reference assay results were available, including data on BRCA1/2 sequence variants, GIS and HRD status. The reference assay detected pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants in three samples (11.1%). Two variants were detected in BRCA1, namely the truncating variant p.Trp372Ter and the splice donor variant c.5193+1del; the frameshift variant p.Ser1248ArgfsTer10 was detected in BRCA2. OCAP identified all three BRCA1/2 variants resulting in an overall concordance rate, sensitivity, and specificity of 100%, respectively (Fig. 2a, Suppl. Table S1).

Next, we compared the genomic instability measures of both assays (i.e., GIS for the reference assay, GIM for the OCAP). The distribution of GIS ranged from 5 to 79 with a median of 45, and the distribution of GIM ranged from 0-46 with a median of 11. We observed a strong correlation between GIS and GIM (Pearson correlation coefficient 0.77, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2b).

Among the 27 HGSC tissue samples, the reference assay classified 13 cases (48.1%) as HRD-positive, based on the detection of pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 sequence variants or GIS values of 42 or greater (Fig. 2c). Using a similar definition of HRD positivity (detection of pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 sequence variants or GIM values of 16 or greater), OCAP identified 12 cases (44.4%) as HRD-positive. Reference assay and OCAP revealed concordant HRD categories in 26 out of 27 samples, resulting in a concordance rate of 96.3%, a sensitivity of 92.3%, and a specificity of 100% (Suppl. Table S2). The single discrepant case had a positive HRD status with the reference assay (GIS 65), but not with the OCAP (GIM 11), in the absence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants.

Comparison of cytological and histological samples for HRD testing using the OCAP

The study cohort included 55 patients with matched cytological and histological HGSC samples, totalling 110 samples. Mean patient age was 65.4 years. Cytoreductive surgery was performed in 46 patients (83.6%). The majority of patients had International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage III (45.5%) or stage IV (36.4%) disease. Among the 34 patients with surgical resection and known R status, all visible tumour could be removed in 73.5% of patients (i.e., R0). Resection specimens represented the majority (83.6%) of histological samples. Most histological samples originated from the ovary (34.5%) and omentum (30.9%), while the majority of cytological samples were obtained from ascites (47.3%) or peritoneal lavage fluid (32.7%). Tumour cell content ranged from 40 to 90% for histological and from 30 to 100% for cytological samples. The clinicopathological characteristics of the study cohort are shown in Table 1. All 110 analysed samples (100%) met the predefined sequencing quality control parameters, ensuring reliable variant detection. Quality metrics of cytological samples, including on-target reads, uniformity, and mean coverage were highly comparable to those of tissue samples (Suppl. Fig. S1).

Tissue-based analysis using OCAP identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic TP53 variants in 49 of 55 cases (89.1%). Of those, 25 (51.0%) were missense variants, 23 (47.0%) were nonsense variants, and one (2.0%) was a splice variant (c.97-2_98del). Genomic profiling of cytological samples identified TP53 variants in the same 49 of 55 patients. Of those, 48 variants (98.0%) were concordant with tissue-based analysis. In one patient (2.0%), however, a different TP53 variant (p.Glu51AsnfsTer72) was detected in the cytological sample from the ascites fluid when compared to the tissue sample from the ovary (p.Val225LeufsTer22).

Tissue-based analysis revealed a total of 29 sequence variants in 10 of the 15 HRR pathway genes of interest (Table 2). Among the 29 HRR gene variants, 26 (89.7%) were also detected in matched cytological samples (Fig. 3a). BRCA1/2 variants were detected in 17 of 55 samples (30.9%), with 16 of them (94.1%) classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic, and one (3.3%; the BRCA1 variant p.Leu1154Ile) as a VUS. Most BRCA1/2 variants were nonsense variants (12/17, 70.6%), while missense variants (3/17, 17.6%) and splice variants (2/17, 11.8%) were less common. Of note, all 13 BRCA1 and all 4 BRCA2 variants (100%) detected in tissue were correctly identified in cytological samples, resulting in a 100% concordance rate for BRCA1/2 variants (Table 2). A total of 12 non-BRCA1/2 HRR gene variants were detected in 10 of 55 samples (18.2%). Perfect concordance (100%) was observed for variants in PALB2 (n = 1), BRIP1 (n = 2), CDK12 (n = 2), ATM (n = 1) and FANCL (n = 1). In three patients, HRR gene variants identified in tissue were not detectable in the corresponding cytological samples, resulting in an overall concordance rate of 75.0% for non-BRCA1/2 HRR gene variants (Table 2). The discordant variants included the benign/likely benign RAD51B variant p.Lys243Arg, the BARD1 VUS p.Leu480Phe and the CHEK2 VUS p.Gly370Arg (tumour cell content was 80%, 60% and 60% for the three cytological samples, respectively), yielding concordance rates of 50, 0 and 50% for RAD51B, BARD1 and CHEK2 sequence variants, respectively.

We performed a detailed analysis of the five HRD discordant cases (Suppl. Table S4). In all cases, discrepant HRD status was due to discrepant GIM values, whereas BRCA1 and BRCA2 sequencing results were concordant. In three cases, GIM values were higher than the cut-off for HRD positivity in HGSC tissue samples, but lower than the cut-off in cytological samples. In all discordant cases, the quality control metric MAPD met the predefined quality criterion, with all values remaining below the cut-off of 0.4, indicating acceptable signal quality for copy number analysis. In contrast, three cases failed the on-target rate (OTR) quality criterion, as the observed OTR values were below the expected minimum threshold of 90%. This finding suggests suboptimal target enrichment, potentially due to impaired DNA quality, as a possible cause of the discordant GIM results.

Discussion

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility and performance of cytological samples for HRD testing in HGSC patients. To verify the OCAP assay for HRD testing, we first compared the OCAP-based HRD status of HGSC tissue samples with the HRD results obtained with the Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay, the most extensively applied commercial HRD assay. The OCAP demonstrated a perfect concordance regarding the BRCA1/2 mutation status, a strong correlation between the GIM (the genomic instability measure used by the OCAP) and the GIS (the genomic instability measure used by the Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay), and a very high HRD concordance rate (96.3%). These findings are in line with the recent literature. For instance, Schejbel et al. analysed ovarian cancer tissue samples with the OCAP and reported a concordance rate of 89% compared to the Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay for cases with a tumour fraction ≥ 30% [18]. In cases with a tumour fraction of <30%, however, the concordance rate dropped to 60% [18], highlighting the importance of careful selection of suitable material for HRD testing. Results from the MITO16A/MaNGO-OV2 trial show concordance rates of 95.8% for BRCA1/2 mutational status and 90.7% for HRD status in ovarian cancer tissue samples analysed by OCAP, using the Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay as the reference [19]. These results verify the OCAP for HRD testing of HGSC tissue samples and suggest that LOH alone, when properly modelled, can serve as a robust surrogate marker for HRD, as opposed to the combination of LOH, LST, and TAI used to derive the GIS as per the Myriad MyChoice® CDx Plus assay. The main limitations of this assay are its inability to be performed locally, its lacking validation for cytological samples, and its inability to concurrently analyse other clinically relevant biomarkers, such as targetable mutations in non-HRR genes as well as clinically relevant biomarkers such as microsatellite instability and tumour mutational burden.

Only a few studies have performed comparative genomic profiling of HGSC in matched cytological and tissue samples so far [12, 14, 15, 20, 21]. The majority of these studies have focused on BRCA1/2 mutation testing. In our cohort we observed pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants in 30.9% of HGSC samples, which well agrees with the reported prevalence of germline and somatic BRCA1/2 variants in approximately 25% and 5% of HGSC patients, respectively [22]. For BRCA1/2 variants, we also found a perfect concordance (100%) between matched cytological and histological samples. In contrast to BRCA1/2, the average concordance rate for non-BRCA1/2 HRR gene variants was noticeably lower (75.0%). Whereas cell blocks [23] and cell-free tumour DNA from peritoneal fluid [24] have been used for HRD testing in ovarian cancer, data for cytological samples are limited. We therefore compared the OCAP-based HRD status of matched cytological and tissue HGSC samples. In our cohort, 54.5% of HGSC tissue samples were HRD positive, consistent with findings of the Cancer Genome Atlas project [3]. We observed a high HRD concordance rate (90.9%) between cytological and histological samples. Our results are consistent with findings by Planisamy et al., who performed HRD testing on 11 ascitic/pleural fluid samples using the SOPHiA DDM™ HRD Solution panel (SOPHiA Genetics, Rolle, Switzerland) or the Illumina TruSight™ Oncology 500 panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and reported valid HRD results in all cases [25]. Together, these findings indicate that HRD testing using cytological HGSC samples is feasible and reliable. Cytology-based HRD testing may facilitate treatment decision-making in clinical scenarios such as inoperable patients or cases where cytological specimens are the only material available.

Discrepant HRD results were observed in 5 of the 55 cases (9.1%). Further investigation indicated that these discrepancies were due to discrepant GIM values and, in the majority of cases, were likely attributable to low on-target rates. These findings highlight the need for strict and standardized preanalytical quality control in cytology-based HRD testing, in addition to sufficient tumour cell content of samples. When compared to tissue-based testing, cytological samples offer the advantage of reduced sampling invasiveness and higher-quality nucleic acids without FFPE-related artifacts. Limitations include variable and often low tumour cell content, limited material, and potential challenges in bioinformatics analysis, necessitating validation and adjustments of algorithms, including but not limited to HRD testing.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and the limited sample size from a single institution limit the generalizability across ethnic and geographic populations. Second, only treatment-naïve patients were included. Therefore, the findings may not be transferable to post-treatment or recurrent disease settings, where clonal evolution and DNA damage responses may be different. Additionally, not all patients present with ascites or pleural effusions, limiting the clinical utility of cytological HRD testing to a subgroup of HGSC patients. Third, our concordance analysis was limited to HRR gene variants, BRCA1/2 mutation status, and GIM. Due to methodological limitations, the OCAP does not allow for the analysis of BRCA1 promoter methylation, which can also induce HRD and was reported to be present in 16.3% of ovarian cancers in a recent meta-analysis [26]. In addition, age-related genomic alterations in tumour suppressor genes and increased genomic instability may affect the interpretation and accuracy of HRD testing in older patients. Finally, we did not assess patient outcome or response to platinum-based chemotherapy or PARPi, which are clinically relevant endpoints in HRD testing.

In conclusion, the present study shows that cytological samples are a feasible alternative source for HRR-associated gene and HRD testing in HGSC, with potential implications for patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making. Analysis of cytological samples may facilitate molecular testing even in cases where histological tissue is unavailable or difficult to obtain, improving patient management, treatment and care.
