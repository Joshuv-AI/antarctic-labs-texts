Aligning leadership incentives with patient outcomes in Somalia’s public hospitals
Authors: Abdirasak Sharif Ali, Mohamed Mustaf Ahmed
Published: 2026

Somalia’s public hospital system is increasingly being asked to do more with less and to be seen doing so. Given the severe resource and capacity constraints described below, this commentary does not assume that incentives are the sole or primary cause of underperformance in the health sector. We argue that heavy external financing may create an additional, underexamined accountability problem: highly visible and easily reported activities can receive more institutional rewards than slower work on supply chains, infection prevention, supervision, and clinical quality.1 Therefore, the argument developed here is a hypothesis about how capacity and incentives interact, not a settled diagnosis.

Somalia has an estimated population of 19.4 million people. Recurrent conflict, climate shocks, large-scale displacement, and hard-to-reach rural and nomadic communities complicate access to healthcare. The major current health needs include maternal and newborn mortality, acute malnutrition, low immunization coverage, and recurrent outbreaks of cholera/acute watery diarrhea, measles, and diphtheria.2 Service delivery is decentralized and pluralistic: federal and state health authorities set policy and stewardship priorities, while public facilities, non-governmental organizations, private providers, and development partners deliver substantial shares of care. The 2020 Essential Package of Health Services provides a common service framework, but access remains uneven because of insecurity, distance, supply disruptions, workforce shortages, and household costs.2–4 The policy ambition is not in doubt. Somalia’s Health Sector Strategic Plan III for 2022–2026 articulates a credible agenda for governance, equitable service delivery, workforce development, and progressive realization of universal health coverage.2 However, outcomes continue to lag. The most recent national survey estimates a maternal mortality ratio of 692 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2020, down from 1,044 in 2006, leaving Somalia among the highest burden countries globally despite a 44 percent reduction over fourteen years.5 However, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously. The 2006 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey and the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey are survey-based secondary data with substantially different sample sizes, and the source study itself notes constraints in comparing the two surveys. Therefore, the figure is better read as an indicator of the order of magnitude and direction of change than as a precise routine surveillance measure.5 For regional context, South Sudan, another nearby East African fragile and strongly donor-dependent health system, reported a maternal mortality ratio of 789 per 100,000 live births in the evidence reviewed by Widdig et al.; its donor-dominated priority-setting shows that severe outcome gaps under external financing are not unique to Somalia, although Somalia’s burden remains exceptionally serious.6 The health workforce remains both undersized and maldistributed, with a recent national mapping documenting 112 health profession schools concentrated in urban centers such as Benadir and a density of skilled professionals below the World Health Organization-recommended threshold.4 Households absorb the consequences directly: out-of-pocket payments comprise 44.9 percent of total health expenditure, more than twice the global average of 18.6 percent, and 24 percent of households incur catastrophic health spending.5 Persistent stockouts in public facilities push patients toward private pharmacies, transforming a notionally subsidized system into one financed at the point of need by the poorest households.7

These outcomes cannot be attributed to incentive design alone: insecurity, workforce shortages, weak information systems, fragmented financing, and limited regulatory capacity remain fundamental constraints. However, these same constraints can make accountability relationships especially consequential.8 A recent analysis of Somalia’s federal health system documents that the Federal Ministry of Health holds nominal stewardship responsibility but operates under capacity constraints and an evolving federal arrangement, with communication between tiers often being suboptimal and quality oversight inconsistent across regions.8 Principal-agent theory helps specify the proposed mechanism rather than merely label it. In performance-based financing, ministries or donors can act as principals, providers as agents, and patients as ‘benefiting principals’; the design of contracts, verification arrangements, and indicator selection shapes which behaviors are rewarded.9 When reporting systems reward outputs that are easy to document, while continuity, technical quality, and patient experience are harder to measure, managers may rationally prioritize the former. The same literature cautions that poorly designed incentives can produce gaming, task substitution, information manipulation, or misdirected accountability, making independent verification and patient participation essential rather than optional.9,10 In Somalia, heavy donor dependence, short funding cycles, and fragmented oversight create conditions in which the mechanism is plausible. However, the available evidence does not demonstrate that visibility causes poor outcomes. The narrower claim is that misaligned accountability may compound existing capacity limitations.1,4,8 The 2020 revision of the Essential Package of Health Services secured broad stakeholder agreement on the progressive realization of high-impact, cost-effective services; however, it operates against a backdrop of fragmented and often unregulated provision that has lacked coordination and stewardship from the health ministry.3 Accordingly, visible events such as launches, delegations, and media coverage should not be treated as evidence of system strengthening unless accompanied by independently verified gains in service reliability, safety, clinical outcomes, and patient experience.

Constructive examples show that coordinated governance arrangements are feasible, but they should not be read as causal proof that incentive alignment alone improves the outcomes. The recently completed rehabilitation of Bakool Regional Hospital, supported by the Ministry of Health, World Health Organization, and Italian Agency for Development Cooperation, rebuilt and equipped the outpatient department, operating theatre, and radiology unit, trained health workers in outbreak detection and emergency response, and reshaped access to care for an estimated 7,266 households across South West State.11 Crucially, the 30-month intervention was coordinated across the Ministry of Health, WHO, and AICS, strengthened referral pathways, and relied on hospital leadership supported by Action Against Hunger to coordinate partner inputs, which created clearer roles and accountability alongside capital investment.11 At the system level, the Damal Caafimaad project, financed through a US$75 million International Development Association grant and a further US$25 million from the Global Financing Facility for Women, Children, and Adolescents, represents the World Bank’s first health sector investment in Somalia in three decades and is designed to expand the essential package of health services and strengthen ministerial stewardship across five disadvantaged regions.4 Its governance model similarly couples federal and state stewardship with government contracting of service delivery to non-governmental organizations and explicit alignment of partner support behind the national Essential Package of Health Services.4 These cases illustrate feasible governance mechanisms: coordination, contracting, stewardship, and role clarity, rather than proving that incentives were the principal cause of any observed improvement. Their patient-level effects should be evaluated with clinical outcomes and patient experience data rather than inferred from inputs alone.

Three concrete reforms would shift incentives from optics to outcomes while acknowledging the current state capacity. First, the Federal Ministry of Health and Federal Member State ministries should introduce a phased hospital accountability compact piloted in selected referral hospitals before a national scale-up. Each participating hospital published a quarterly dashboard covering outpatient and inpatient volumes, essential medicine stockout days, staff vacancy rates, institutional delivery, perinatal mortality, routine immunization, selected safety indicators, and patient-reported measures of experience and outcome, such as respectful communication, complaint resolution, symptom improvement, or functional recovery, where clinically appropriate. During the initial phase, compliance should be tied to eligibility for pooled operating support or contract renewal, not operating licensure, until regulatory capacity is demonstrably adequate. Verification should be separated from hospital management and commissioned to an independent third party or a multi-stakeholder verification unit reporting to both federal and state authorities.9,10,12 Second, development partners should consolidate fragmented disbursements into pooled, results-based financing, in which a defined share of payments is contingent on independently verified outcomes and quality indicators rather than activity reports alone. Payments should not be triggered until minimum data quality thresholds are met; verification should triangulate HMIS/DHIS2 submissions with source registers, pharmacy stock cards, random chart review, and sampled patient or community follow-back, with periodic external re-audits to detect gaming and reporting drift.9,10 Third, hospital governance should be anchored domestically through chartered hospital boards with transparent and time-limited appointments. A practical board of approximately 9–11 members could include federal or state health authorities, clinicians, finance or legal experts, local government, women, civil society, and representatives of internally displaced, nomadic, or other underserved communities, with conflict-of-interest declarations and staggered terms. Boards should approve annual plans and budgets, review dashboards, audits, complaints, and patient-reported data, require corrective action plans, participate in senior administrator appraisal, and escalate serious safety or procurement failures while remaining outside individual clinical decisions. Annual community scorecards and public reporting should complement these formal powers.8,9 Together, these reforms align financing, verification, managerial accountability, and patient voice without assuming that the ministry can immediately exercise the enforcement capacity it does not yet possess.

Somali patients have endured decades of fragility with notable resilience, and they are owed a public hospital system that matches their resilience with institutional integrity. The evidence reviewed here does not establish incentives as the principal cause of poor performance. Its contribution beyond existing analyses of federalism and stewardship is to translate system-level weaknesses into a testable hospital-level governance hypothesis: capacity building is less likely to produce sustained gains when financing, verification, managerial accountability, and patient voice point in different directions.8,9 This hypothesis can be tested prospectively by pairing routine clinical outcomes with patient-reported experience and outcome measures and examining whether hospitals exposed to clearer contracts, independent verification, and stronger governance boards improve relative to their own baselines and comparable facilities. Substantial international financing continues to flow into the sector; the operative question is whether it will be governed in ways that prioritize measurable improvements in care over public visibility.1 Therefore, recalibrating leadership incentives should not be treated as a low-cost certainty but as a pragmatic governance reform that must be implemented alongside capacity building, independent verification, and transparent evaluation.
