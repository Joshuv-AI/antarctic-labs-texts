Type B Digital Mucous Cysts Can Present with Cuticle Thickening as a Clinical Clue
Authors: Benjamin Y. Li, Megan Hauptman, Aaron J. Russell, Julia O. Baltz, Nathaniel J. Jellinek, Julie E. Mervak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType B digital mucous cysts (DMCs) exert pressure over the nail matrix, leading to longitudinal nail plate groove. In the authors’ experience, focal hyperkeratosis of the proximal nail fold (PNF) in type B DMC is frequently observed. We analyzed clinical images in the literature to assess how often this feature was present, if noted in the figure caption, and aimed for clinical description of this finding.MethodsWe performed a search of DMC images from PubMed literature and nail atlases. Consensus for DMC type, presence of PNF hyperkeratosis, hyperkeratosis described in the figure caption, and etiology of hyperkeratosis was performed.ResultsOf available images, 33.9% were determined to be type B DMC; of these, 53.8% had presence of PNF hyperkeratosis. There was consensus that a majority (61.9%) of the hyperkeratotic findings were due to cuticle thickening, and only one figure caption mentioned PNF hyperkeratosis.ConclusionType B DMCs often feature focal hyperkeratosis at the PNF. A review of type B DMC images revealed that this hyperkeratosis was mostly due to cuticle thickening, suggesting a reaction of the eponychium in response to the mass effect within the PNF. Bringing this characteristic to the attention of practicing physicians may aid in clinical diagnosis.

Introduction

Digital mucous cysts (DMCs), also known as digital myxoid cysts or myxoid pseudocysts, are benign pseudocysts of the fingers and, less commonly, toes [1]. They can cause pain, tenderness, repetitive leakage of fluid, and nail dystrophy. The etiology of DMCs remains uncertain, although history of trauma, occupation-related repetitive stress to the area, and comorbidity of osteoarthritis may be elicited in the patient history [1]. In particular, communication with the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint via a pedicle has been observed, making this a ganglion, a pseudocyst without a formal cell lining [2, 3]. Treatment is guided by patient symptoms and can include excision, incision and drainage, and cryotherapy among myriad treatment options [2].

De Berker [2] originally classified DMC types (A, B, and C) by the location of the lesion in the nail unit. The most common subtype, type A (classic), presents as a superficial swelling most frequently noted between the transverse line of the distal interphalangeal and proximal nail fold (PNF) [1, 2]. Type A DMCs often present as a translucent, dome-shaped papule with history of episodic drainage of clear gel [2], making this DMC type easily recognized by the clinician without a broad differential diagnosis. The second most common, type B (pseudofibromatous), is located deeper within the PNF, leading to fluctuating pressure on the underlying nail matrix depending on the size and location of the pseudocyst. This pressure on the matrix can lead to the characteristic “waves sign” of longitudinal groove along the nail plate [2, 4, 5]. In the text of his chapter on DMCs in Baran’s textbook, Advances in Nail Disease and Management, De Berker [2] described “a small crusted or keratinous papule at the margin of the cuticle” in these lesions. Type C (subungual) lesions present beneath the nail matrix, present quite differently with increased curvature of the nail, and most commonly appear on the thumb [2].

DMCs can fluctuate in volume and sometimes discharge gelatinous material, mainly glycosaminoglycans such as hyaluronic acid [6]. This leads to the question of whether the hyperkeratotic PNF appearance [7] can be attributed entirely to dried hyaluronic acid and crusting or is due to other factors such as thickening of the cuticle. In the authors’ experience, when these focal hyperkeratotic PNF changes are observed, patients are often referred for evaluation of potential nail unit squamous cell carcinoma or periungual verruca vulgaris. Further classification of these findings in DMC may allow for physician education and prevent unnecessary referrals and procedures. In this study, we collected DMC images from a structured literature search and key nail textbooks for review by four dermatologists with expertise in nail disorders to better delineate the frequency and suspected etiology of type B DMC PNF hyperkeratosis.

Methods

Image Collection

We collated type A, B, and C DMC images from a PubMed query of recent literature for review by four dermatologists with expertise in nail disorders. We used the following PubMed query: (((((((((((((((digital mucous cyst[Book]) OR (digital mucous cyst[Text Word])) OR (digital mucous cyst[Title])) OR (digital mucous cyst[Title/Abstract])) OR (digital myxoid cyst[Book])) OR (digital myxoid cyst[Text Word])) OR (digital myxoid cyst[Title/Abstract])) OR (digital myxoid cyst[Title])) OR (digital pseudocyst[Title/Abstract])) OR (digital pseudocyst[Title])) OR (digital pseudocyst[Text Word])) OR (digital pseudocyst[Book])) NOT (pancreas)) NOT (pulmonary)) NOT (CSF)) NOT (pancreatic), which returned 35 relevant papers with DMC images allowing for analysis of 62 unique images. We also included 53 DMC images from 16 key nail textbooks and atlases which yielded a total of 115 DMC images for expert review.

Image Review and Analysis

DMC images were organized in a secure spreadsheet and a separate spreadsheet was shared with each reviewer. Each reviewer made a diagnosis of type A, B, or C DMC for each photograph; and if it was a type B lesion, the presence of PNF hyperkeratosis was evaluated. If the reviewer decided it was a type B lesion and it featured hyperkeratosis, they were then asked whether the figure caption commented on the presence of hyperkeratosis and whether they thought the hyperkeratosis represented gelatinous drainage, cuticle thickening, or both.

Reviewer responses were integrated using consensus analysis. For DMC diagnosis (type A, B, or C), consensus was defined as at least 3/4 reviewers agreeing on the DMC type. For presence of hyperkeratosis (yes or no), results were first filtered to consensus type B DMC diagnoses and consensus was again defined as at least 3/4 reviewers agreeing on presence or absence of PNF hyperkeratosis. For etiology of hyperkeratosis (cuticle thickening, gelatinous drainage, or both), results were first filtered to type B DMC diagnoses with consensus for presence of hyperkeratosis. Consensus was defined as at least 3/4 reviewers agreeing on the etiology of hyperkeratosis. In limited cases of no consensus, we counted “both” as a vote for “cuticle thickening” and “gelatinous drainage” as this yielded additional insights and improved the rate of consensus (e.g., two votes for “both” and two votes for “cuticle thickening” would be translated to a consensus of “cuticle thickening” since all reviewers agreed on that component). Analysis was performed with custom R scripts (version 4.3.3).

Results

We analyzed 115 DMC images. First categorizing images by DMC diagnosis type, 54 (47.0%) images had a consensus diagnosis (at least 3/4) of type A, 39 (33.9%) were type B, and 8 (7.0%) were type C. A total of 12.2% of images did not have a majority diagnosis and these were excluded from further analyses.

Of type B DMC diagnoses (n = 39), we investigated whether there was a consensus either for the presence or absence of PNF hyperkeratosis. In 53.8% of cases, there was a consensus for the presence of hyperkeratosis and in 35.9% of cases, there was consensus for absence of hyperkeratosis. Of type B diagnoses with consensus agreement for the presence of hyperkeratosis (n = 21), we asked whether the DMC hyperkeratosis represented cuticle thickening, gelatinous drainage, or both. In this case, consensus had a higher bar since there were three options. Nonetheless, reviewers thought the hyperkeratosis represented cuticle thickening in 61.9% of cases (Fig. 1), gelatinous drainage in 19.0% of cases, and both cuticle thickening and gelatinous drainage in 14.3% of cases.

Of 39 type B DMC diagnoses, it was then reviewed whether the figure caption commented on the presence of hyperkeratosis. For only one figure’s caption did reviewers agree that the figure caption discussed hyperkeratosis, in which a “finger-like protruding nodule emerging from the PNF” was noted [8].

Discussion

In this study, we have demonstrated that type B DMCs frequently present with PNF hyperkeratosis, and these features are most often attributed to cuticle thickening. This feature of type B DMCs is not often discussed and is very rarely mentioned in captions of DMC images. However, in Baran’s textbook, Advances in Nail Disease and Management, in the chapter text written by De Berker [2], there is mention of a “small keratinous papule protruding from the cuticle margin in line with a longitudinal groove of the nail” as part of the appearance of type B DMCs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to catalog this cuticle thickening as a clinical sign of type B DMCs among many reviewers. We postulate that the etiology of this thickening may stem from a reaction of the eponychium in response to the mass effect by the type B DMC’s location within the PNF.

This study is limited by the largely image-based analysis of DMCs. This allowed us to review a much greater number of cases than could be done by in-person examination in the same amount of time. Consensus analysis also allowed us to report only the most robust results. We acknowledge the limitation that a large series of histology of these lesions is needed to confirm the clinical impression. Clinicopathologic correlation of what the PNF hyperkeratosis represents was not specifically explored in this study; however, anecdotally, in the authors’ practice, this has revealed hyperkeratosis and parakeratosis. This is consistent with our clinical impression of cuticle thickening. Although the PNF is not often excised as part of standard DMC treatment and therefore available for histologic review, it is important to recognize these findings to differentiate from other diagnoses such as periungual verruca vulgaris or periungual fibroma. We acknowledge that there are some DMCs in practice which have characteristics of both type A and type B, making compartmentalization imperfect.

Taken together, we present cuticle thickening as a clinical sign of type B DMCs with hyperkeratosis of the PNF. Particularly when monodactylous, focal, and seen in conjunction with other clinical features such as longitudinal nail plate groove or “waves sign,” this can lead to a correct clinical diagnosis of type B DMC. Although present in many of the type B DMC clinical images in the literature, the PNF hyperkeratosis is not included in the clinical description, except in one instance. Bringing this characteristic to the attention of practicing physicians will inform clinicians and may limit unnecessary referrals with concern for malignancy and procedures for these patients.
