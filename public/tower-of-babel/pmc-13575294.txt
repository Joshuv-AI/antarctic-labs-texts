Quantitative SPECT imaging of 225Ac and its daughters in mice in vivo
Authors: Shixian Du, Kondapa Naidu Bobba, Robin Peter, Grant T. Gullberg, Robert R. Flavell, Javier Caravaca, Youngho Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted alpha therapy (TAT) using actinium‐225 (225Ac) holds great promise for cancer treatment, and to advance 225Ac therapy it is required to develop accurate dosimetry method. 225Ac daughters, especially 213Bi, may redistribute and cause renal toxicity. Currently, direct imaging of 225Ac‐emitted photons at low injected activity levels remains a technical challenge for image‐based 225Ac dosimetry.PurposeThis study aims to assess the feasibility and accuracy of quantitative in vivo SPECT/CT imaging for 225Ac and its decay daughters at activity levels similar to those in small animal studies.MethodsA state‐of‐the‐art commercial preclinical SPECT/CT scanner was used to image healthy nude mice in vivo and ex vivo after injection of unlabeled 225Ac from the United States Department of Energy isotope program, at activities below 48.8 kBq. Each in‐vivo imaging was 1.5 h and ex‐vivo imaging was 12 h, at approximately 2 h after injection of 225Ac, and all SPECT images were reconstructed at multi‐energy windows (82, 218, and 440 keV). Mouse‐like phantom imaging for 1 h at 0.078 MBq and 12 h at 0.496 MBq were also performed to validate quantitative accuracy.ResultsIn vivo SPECT/CT images clearly showed abundant liver uptake, and significant kidney uptake was also observed in 440 keV images for 213Bi, consistent with known renal toxicity from daughter redistribution. However, quantitative analysis of in vivo, ex vivo, and low‐activity phantom images revealed suboptimal quantitative accuracy, with high background recovery coefficients and variations in measured activity across the energy windows.ConclusionsWe demonstrated the ability to image 225Ac and its daughters in vivo at low activity levels. However, the quantitative results confirmed that, even with the state‐of‐the‐art preclinical SPECT/CT system, current methods and reconstruction algorithms may not provide sufficient accuracy for accurate dosimetry at typical activity used in small animal studies.

INTRODUCTION

Targeted alpha therapy (TAT) using actinium‐225 (225Ac) has shown great promise in treating both solid and liquid tumors in clinical and preclinical studies. TAT achieves high potency of tumor cell killing from the high linear energy transfer (LET) of alpha emitters. Alpha particles are known for their effectiveness in inducing DNA double strand breaks (DSB), a main mechanism leading to cancer cell death. 1 , 2 , 3 A major challenge in advancing TAT is to perform accurate dosimetry evaluations after injection of the radiopharmaceutical. Image‐based dosimetry using single photon computed tomography (SPECT) would be preferred if the alpha emitter used in TAT emits detectable photons, but direct imaging has remained a technical challenge due to the low injected activities involved.

To perform quantitative in vivo SPECT imaging of 225Ac, two challenging technical tasks exist: SPECT imaging at low activity levels that are not toxic to mice (below around 18.5 kBq), and multi‐energy imaging of 225Ac together with its main decay daughters 221Fr and 213Bi. 225Ac has a physical half‐life of 9.92 days and can be labeled with promising tumor targeting vectors such as prostate‐specific membrane antigen (PSMA) and other tumor‐targeting biomarkers. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, potential de‐chelation of labeled 225Ac due to recoil during decay will release nontargeted 225Ac daughters into the body that may induce toxicity in healthy tissue. 10 , 11 213Bi, for example, has a longer physical half‐life of 45 min that allows migration and is found to result in kidney toxicity. 12 , 13

Several quantitative SPECT imaging methods of labeled and unlabeled 225Ac of very high activity samples (1.56 MBq/mL and 0.235 MBq/mL) in 3D phantoms have been reported. 14 , 15 Du Y et al. 16 also reported images of 225Ac daughters 221Fr and 213Bi in rabbits in vivo using a clinical SPECT scanner. Zannoni et al. 17 built a CdTe‐based mini‐SPECT detector to image 225Ac‐filled phantom at a high activity concentration of 13.69 kBq/uL, or 13.69 MBq/mL. Das et al. 18 explored Compton imaging of 225Ac using mouse‐like phantoms at reduced time and activity. However, SPECT imaging techniques have not been developed to quantify labeled or unlabeled 225Ac in small animals in vivo, where imaging and dosimetry are needed for further TAT development and optimization. In this study, we assess the performance and feasibility of in vivo quantitative imaging for realistic activities of unlabeled 225Ac and its decay daughters in mice using a widely available, advanced preclinical SPECT/CT scanner.

METHODS

A commercial preclinical SPECT/CT scanner VECTor4CT (MILabs, Netherlands) was used to image unlabeled 225Ac injected to three healthy nude mice (Jackson laboratory) (at 48.76, 48.84, and 49.21 kBq) in vivo. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the UCSF IACUC protocol and standard. The scanner was configured for low‐activity, very high sensitivity imaging. We used the sodium iodide (NaI) detector of customized thickness 19.05 mm. We also used a high energy, general purpose, small animal imaging collimator (HE‐GP‐RM), which has 2.0% sensitivity at 2.1 mm full‐width half‐maximum (FWHM) resolution for 140 keV and 3.2% sensitivity at 2.4 mm FWHM resolution for 511 keV. The HE‐GP‐RM collimator has 156 pinholes facing toward a common field‐of‐view (FOV), an axial FOV of 18 mm, and a transverse FOV of 28 mm. The NaI thickness higher than standard configuration is designed to capture more high energy photons and increase detection efficiency. The collimator HE‐GP‐RM is designed for imaging of high energy photons and therefore allows imaging at 440 keV in this study.

The VECTor4CT SPECT scanner was first calibrated by imaging at 82, 218, and 440 keV using a sealed vial of 643 kBq unlabeled 225Ac‐nitrate (1.07 mL), which was received from Oak Ridge National Laboratory and dissolved in 0.2 M HCl aqueous solution. A CT‐based volume of interest (VOI) was manually selected to cover the entire vial to compute the whole activity of 225Ac, and the calibration factor was derived by comparing the computed activity to the true activity of 225Ac in the vial.

Imaging of 225Ac is complex due to its decay chain, which consists of 4 alpha and 2 beta decays before reaching stable daughter 209Bi. In this study, the total imaging window width is 25% for each photopeak. The imaging window of 82 keV ± 12.5%, 218 keV ± 12.5%, and 440 keV ± 12.5% were set to image multiple x‐ray and gamma ray emissions from 60 to 100 keV by 225Ac, the 11.44% gamma ray emission at 218 keV by daughter 221Fr, and the 25.94% gamma ray emission at 440 keV by daughter 213Bi. 15 An imaging window of ± 12.5% was not from optimization in the present study. The relative narrow window setting was chosen as a compromise between retaining sufficient counts under low activity in vivo imaging condition and limiting the crosstalk effect. FWHM values of the three imaging peaks were estimated from the acquired energy spectrum by fitting each local peak region with a Gaussian function and a linear background term.

Scatter correction was implemented for all SPECT imaging to correct down‐scattering using a triple energy window technique implemented in the scanner vendor‐provided reconstruction software. 19 Automatic background weighting was also enabled in the vendor‐provided software. Scattering correction window width was set to be 15% of the imaging window width. Lower and upper scatter windows were placed immediately adjacent to each primary imaging energy window, and the counts acquired in these scatter windows were used by the vendor reconstruction software to estimate the scatter contribution to the corresponding primary imaging energy window. In vivo and ex vivo SPECT/CT imaging of mice was subsequently performed. Additionally, a mouse‐like compartmental phantom (BIOEMTECH, Athens, Greece) was imaged to validate the quantification. The healthy mice (nu/nu, n = 3) were imaged in vivo under 2% isoflurane anesthesia for approximately 1.5 h at 2.5 h post‐intravenous administration of unlabeled 2 2 5Ac. The SPECT images were reconstructed with energy windows at 82 keV ± 12.5%, 218 keV ± 12.5%, and 440 keV ± 12.5% using the scanner vendor‐provided reconstruction algorithm, similarity regulated ordered‐subsets expectation maximization (SROSEM) with 4 subsets and 25 iterations. The SROSEM algorithm aims to accelerate image reconstruction by automatically and locally adapting the number of subsets based on similarity of update terms for different subsets. 19 Reconstruction with 4 subsets and 25 iterations were the optimal choice considering image quality and reconstruction time by Robertson et al. 15

Ex vivo biodistribution was performed immediately after imaging. The mice were euthanized, and each organ was collected and counted in an individual well of a gamma counter (HIDEX), which acquired the full gamma energy spectrum from 0 to 2047 keV. The same energy windows as the SPECT imaging at 82, 218, and 440 keV were used in the gamma spectra. The counts of each organ in each energy window were integrated and converted to activity in kBq using the corresponding calibration equations. Then the quantified activities were decay corrected to the start of imaging before being normalized by the injected activity and divided by the organ volume as percentage of injected activity per mL (%IA/mL). For the ex vivo imaging, the healthy mice (nu/nu, n = 3) were sacrificed 2.5 h after 55.50, 41.96, and 48.47 kBq of unlabeled 225Ac administration and immediately imaged for 12 h in the scanner. Reconstruction with count reduction was also performed to emulate 1.5‐h ex vivo imaging.

For the mouse‐like phantom imaging, approximately total activity 0.50 MBq and activity concentration of about 0.12 MBq/mL of unlabeled 225Ac was filled between the phantom compartments (total volume of 4.2 mL) and imaged for 12 h. Then the mouse‐like phantom was imaged in SPECT/CT for 1 h when the total activity was decayed to 0.078 MBq and the activity concentration of 0.019 MBq/mL of unlabeled 225Ac in each phantom compartment. For all image reconstruction, Gaussian filtering with a kernel size of 23 pixels and FWHM of 2.4 mm was applied, matched with the collimator's known spatial resolution at 511 keV. Then, VOIs were selected for quantitative analysis using AMIDE. Multiple VOIs were manually selected per each mouse and phantom SPECT and averaged to avoid the partial volume effect (PVE). In mice, standard small VOIs within each organ of interest were used. In the phantom, large VOIs covering each compartment entirely were chosen to reduce the uncertainties from non‐uniform distribution of unlabeled 225Ac. A whole‐phantom VOI (30 × 30 × 80 mm cylinder) was used to enclose the entire phantom. Example choice of VOIs were displayed in Figure 1a,b. For quantitative image analysis, all activities were decay‐corrected to the start of imaging time. Recovery coefficient (RC) is the ratio of measured activity concentration to true activity concentration. RCs were calculated for each phantom compartment, background, entire phantom. All CT images were processed with a 3 × 3 median filter for better visualization.

RESULTS

In vivo mouse SPECT/CT imaging

The energy spectrum acquired from 1.5‐h SPECT/CT in vivo imaging of a mouse injected with 48.76 kBq unlabeled 225Ac is shown in Figure 2. FWHM values of 82, 218, and 440 keV imaging peaks were 20.6, 28.3, and 40.2 keV, respectively. Figure 3 shows 1.5‐h SPECT/CT MIP images of three in vivo healthy nude mice reconstructed at 82, 218, and 440 KeV. All in vivo SPECT images showed abundant liver uptake. Kidney uptake was obvious in most SPECT images and especially apparent at 440 keV, which aligned with previous findings that nuclear recoil would release 213Bi daughters and cause kidney toxicity. 12 , 13 Quantitative analysis of in vivo imaging with reference to biodistribution result after in vivo imaging is shown in Figure 4. In both left and right kidney VOIs, the uptakes in 440 keV were significantly higher than uptakes at 82 and 218 keV.

Ex vivo mouse SPECT/CT imaging

Figure 5 shows 12‐h and count reduced (1.5‐h) SPECT/CT MIP images of three ex vivo healthy nude mice reconstructed at 82, 218, and 440 keV. Liver uptake was abundant. Activities in proximity to the abdominal regions were also apparent in SPECT images at 440 keV. Quantitative analysis of ex vivo imaging with reference to biodistribution result after ex vivo imaging is also shown in Figure 4. In both 12‐h and count reduced (1.5‐h) ex vivo imaging, left kidney VOIs, named kidney2, had higher uptake in 440 keV than uptake at 82 and 218 keV. The ex vivo %IA/mL showed higher agreement between three energy windows than in vivo %IA/mL, consistent with the fact that 225Ac and 213Bi begin to equilibrate and reduce visible 213Bi redistribution after euthanasia.

Mouse‐like phantom SPECT/CT imaging

For total activity of 0.5 MBq filled in the mouse‐like phantom and imaged for 12 h, the recovery coefficients across 10 phantom compartments at 82, 218, and 440 keV had a range of 62.48%–104.45%, 65.02%–111.95%, and 72.06%–119.08% respectively. For total activity of 0.078 MBq and imaged for 1 h, the average recovery coefficients across 10 phantom compartments at 82, 218, and 440 keV were 67.40%–151.04%, 67.94%–124.31%, and 77.27%–125.66% respectively. DISCUSSION

The ability to quantitatively image 225Ac and its decay daughters quantitatively and in vivo is desired to advance 225Ac treatment studies in small animals. While there have been reported 225Ac imaging studies at activities around 37 MBq, therapeutic effects of 225Ac radiopharmaceuticals are also being observed and studied at much lower activities (below 37 kBq), which are still challenging for quantitative imaging in vivo. Therefore, in our study, we investigated the capabilities of an advanced SPECT/CT scanner for small animal imaging and previously validated image reconstruction algorithm to perform in vivo, ex vivo and mouse‐like phantom imaging in this low activity range.

While the SPECT/CT images were able to show kidney activity distribution in mouse in vivo at an imaging time of 1.5 h and activity just below 48.8 kBq, the image qualities at 82 keV were not optimal. Both in vivo and ex vivo imaging showed excessive activities at different locations outside the mouse body at 82 keV and in some 218 keV images, as shown in Figures 3 and 5. The measured spectral shape is complex near 82 keV peak, with potential contamination from photon scattering and characteristic x‐rays from tungsten in the SPECT collimator at around 67.5 keV and lead at 72–75 and 85–87 keV. The vendor‐implemented triple energy window scatter correction does not explicitly model tungsten or lead fluorescence. Activities were found outside the mouse liver region from the reconstructed SPECT images in Figures 3 and 5, particularly at 440 keV, could be caused by insufficient thickness for pinhole apertures. These artifacts highlighted that improvement in SPECT image reconstruction algorithm and collimator design is needed to achieve optimal image quality and quantitative accuracies.

Quantitative accuracies of in vivo, ex vivo, and phantom images were also suboptimal. Current SPECT/CT imaging of 225Ac has been demonstrated previously, but at a much higher activity level than the requirement to perform small animal treatment studies. Previous therapy studies of 225Ac in mouse have found that even activities as low as 37 kBq are toxic. 12 , 20 We reduced the 225Ac activity level to 78 kBq and imaged in phantom for an hour. The very high background and entire VOI recovery coefficients indicated that this imaging method could not recover the true activity prepared in the phantom, though the average RC per compartment was close to 1. At 1‐h imaging of mouse‐like phantom with total activity of 78 kBq, the very high RCs suggested lack of quantitative accuracies from insufficient number of photons using current image reconstruction method. If we image in vivo at even lower activity that is typical dosage in small animal studies, it is reasonable to predict that image quality and quantitative accuracy will deteriorate. In Figure 4, imaging at 82, 218, and 440 keV showed slightly different %IA/mL measurements. For example, x‐ray fluorescence from tungsten and lead near 82 keV photopeak could impact the scatter correction and therefore reduce quantitative accuracy of SPECT imaging of 225Ac at 82 keV. This observation suggests that additional improvement in multi‐energy window imaging and reconstruction of 225Ac is needed for full optimization of this imaging protocol. In that case, an optimized scatter correction method should be applied considering the spectral characteristics around the photopeaks to improve accuracies.

We have applied several adaptations to a commercial preclinical SPECT/CT imaging system to meet the 225Ac imaging requirements. However, our results demonstrated that the current system remains limited for quantitatively accurate 225Ac imaging at low activity levels required for TAT small animal studies. Quantitative accuracy of low activity 225Ac imaging could be affected by low‐count statistics, down‐scattering and crosstalk, as well as high energy photon penetrations. Therefore, further development is needed to improve quantitative accuracy such as an automated small animal VOI segmentation method, specific aperture and collimator designs for higher energy photons, and a Monte Carlo‐based scatter correction method.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we investigated the feasibility of quantitative SPECT/CT imaging of 225Ac and its daughters in vivo at a lower activity. We injected unlabeled 225Ac below 48.8 kBq for in vivo and ex vivo mouse imaging and imaged the mouse‐like phantom for 1 h at 78 kBq. We demonstrated with in vivo SPECT imaging the kidney uptakes in 440 keV, which was consistent with previous findings that daughter 213Bi causes renal toxicity. However, the quantitative results suggested that imaging with an advanced micro‐SPECT/CT scanner VECTor4CT (MILabs, Netherlands) and reconstruction will not provide sufficient accuracy for quantitative dosimetry at typical activity levels in routine small animal studies.
