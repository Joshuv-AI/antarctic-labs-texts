Clustered random forests with correlated data for optimal estimation and inference under potential covariate shift
Elliot H. Young, Peter Bühlmann

Abstract
We develop Clustered Random Forests , a random forests algorithm for clustered data, arising from independent groups that exhibit within-cluster dependence. The leaf-wise predictions for each decision tree making up clustered random forests takes the form of a weighted least squares estimator, which leverage correlations between observations for improved prediction accuracy and tighter confidence intervals when performing inference. We show that approximately linear time algorithms exist for fitting classes of clustered random forests, matching the computational complexity of standard random forests. Further, we observe that the optimality of a clustered random forest, with regards to how optimal weights are chosen within this framework i.e. those that minimise mean squared prediction error, vary under covariate distribution shift. In light of this, we advocate weight estimation to be determined by a user-chosen covariate distribution, or test dataset of covariates, with respect to which optimal prediction or inference is desired. This highlights a key distinction between correlated and independent data with regards to optimality of nonparametric conditional mean estimation under covariate shift. We demonstrate our theoretical findings numerically in a number of simulated and real-world settings.

Introduction

Clustered structures are omnipresent in real-world datasets, arising from repeated measurements, longitudinal and geographical studies, or through data augmentation.
A key challenge in such settings is accounting for potential correlations between intra-cluster components of the response. In (partially) parametric models this is often achieved through an (iteratively re-) weighted least squares estimator for the parameter of interest. Although such approaches perform well when the assumed parametric mean model is correctly specified, model misspecification can introduce substantial bias in the parametric estimates, leading to inconsistent estimation.

The random forest
(
Breiman, 2001
)
is one example of a nonparametric regression method that circumvents the need to impose such a (partially) parametric structure on the data, instead assuming smoothness assumptions on the conditional mean.
Random forests are constructed by bagging decision trees, each of which can be cast as a two-step procedure: first obtaining a data-driven partition of the covariate support, onto which predictions take the form of an ordinary least squares estimator corresponding to the model of piecewise constant mean with respect to the (high-dimensional) data-driven partition.
Meinshausen (2006)
;
Biau (2012)
;
Wager and Athey (2018)
among others show that pointwise consistency and asymptotic normality of this estimator follows under Lipschitz smoothness of the mean function, and when forests are constructed using independent, identically distributed data.
Extending the random forest methodology and theory to optimally account for correlations between observations is however non-trivial. Methodologically one natural approach, that we adopt, adapts the decision tree algorithm described above so that instead of performing an ordinary least squares regression with respect to a piecewise constant mean ‘working model’ given the data-driven leaves constructed, a weighted least squares regression is performed, with weights that account for correlations between observations.
A number of existing methods have been introduced that aim to model correlations between data in decision trees and random forests
(
Sela and Simonoff, 2012
;
Hajjem et al., 2014
;
Hu and Szymczak, 2023
)
.
Such strategies posit a specific, rigid parametric structure on the conditional covariance, such as those implied by a random effects model, and use a likelihood-based loss function to estimate the covariance model.
As we will see, these approaches can be computationally intractable, and need not even guarantee variance reduction over standard random forests.

We propose a different approach, still estimating predictions via a weighted least squares based decision tree mechanism, but estimating the optimal parameters for estimation in a covariance model free fashion.
For such a procedure to be computationally competitive with random forests it may be desirable to restrict the class of weights to a restricted, potentially parametric class; we will see that we can achieve approximately linear time clustered random forest fitting for some of the parametric classes we study. The natural question that arises is therefore how to select data-driven optimal weights for such a clustered random forest.
Here we refer to optimality with respect to minimal mean squared prediction error under a covariate shifted distribution.
Such covariate distribution shift problems apply naturally to optimal pointwise prediction and inference, in the settings of for example personalized decision making and precision medicine, as well as in supervised learning problems, for example in optimal treatment strategies between geographical areas with different population characteristics, or in natural language processing problems where models may be deployed in settings different to that of the training dataset.
We observe that with correlated data the optimal clustered random forest weights are a function of the covariate shifted distribution with respect to which the performance benchmark is defined, and that not accounting for these weights in a covariate shift adaptive fashion, as would be the case for e.g. likelihood based or cross-validation based methods, can cause arbitrarily poor performance under covariate shifted testing.

1.1
Our contributions

The main contributions of our clustered random forest proposal are as follows:

Clustered random forest methodology and theory:
We introduce a practical extension of the popular random forest algorithm to clustered data, that in particular provides more accurate predictions by accounting for correlations between observations. We develop asymptotic theory for a flexible class of these clustered random forests.

Minimax optimal rates:
We introduce a class of decision tree splitting rules within which there exists a splitting rule that is minimax rate optimal for pointwise estimation of Lipschitz mean functions. Such results also apply to standard, unclustered honest random forests.

Inference with clustered random forests:
A class of these clustered random forests can be shown to be variance-dominating and asymptotically Gaussian, allowing for inference on a broad class of functionals of the conditional mean. Clustered random forests necessarily enjoy (potentially arbitrarily) tighter confidence intervals compared to both standard random forests and pre-existing mixed effects models based random forests.

Computational feasibility:
Despite gains that hierarchical or longitudinal random forests may exhibit over standard, unclustered random forests, existing methods have cubic-order computational complexity in the data used to build a decision tree.
Clustered random forests on the other and are computable in approximately linear time, matching the favorable computational complexity, scalability and parallelizability of standard random forests.

Covariate distribution shift with correlated data:
When modeling correlations for improved accuracy in clustered random forests, we see that optimal accuracy in terms of mean squared prediction error (MSPE) can intricately depend on the covariate distribution to which the MSPE is defined. Consequently we see that likelihood-based and cross-validation based methods can be arbitrarily poor under covariate shift.
We introduce an important connection between covariate distribution shift problems and correlated data settings. Our results accommodate the covariate shift to be a point mass, thus encompassing optimal pointwise MSE results and tight confidence interval constructions.

1.2
Some related literature

There is a rich history in acknowledging and modeling correlations between observations for more accurate estimation of parameters of interest, particularly in the setting of parametric multilevel models. Mixed effects models
(
Pinheiro and Bates, 2000
)
have proved popular for hierarchical clustered data in addition to ARMA models for longitudinal data
(
Box, 1976
;
Brockwell and Davis, 1991
)
. In each case a specific parametric structure is imposed on the conditional covariance of the response. Historically these methods were introduced in fully parametric models, where the conditional expectation of the response is also assumed to follow a specific parametric form, although they have also been incorporated into semiparametric models that ask the conditional mean to take a semi or non parametric form.
Emmenegger and Bühlmann (2023)
for example considers a partially linear mixed effects model. Within the context of decision trees (and by extension random forests) random effects expectation maximisation (REEM) trees
(
Sela and Simonoff, 2012
)
and mixed effects random forests (MERF)
(
Hajjem et al., 2014
)
have each been introduced, both of which assume that each data cluster
(Y_{i},X_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times d}
, for some
n_{i},d\in\mathbb{N}
, follows the semiparametric model

Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i}\sim N_{n_{i}}\big(\mu(X_{i})\,,\;X_{i}^{\text{(sub)}}\mathcal{V}X_{i}^{\text{(sub)}\top}+\sigma^{2}\text{I}_{n_{i}}\big),

(1)

where
\mu
is a smooth mean function that acts row-wise on the clustered data
X_{i}
, that satisfies with a slight abuse of notation
(\mu(X_{i}))_{j}=\mu(X_{ij})
, and
X_{i}^{\text{(sub)}}
is a set of random effects that takes the form of a subset of the covariates that make up
X_{i}
, and
(\mathcal{V},\sigma)
is a Euclidean parameter to be estimated. Loosely speaking, both REEM and MERF strategies grow trees by partitioning the covariate support and then fitting a parametric mixed effects model with fixed effects given by a piecewise constant mean working model on the partition, in addition to the random effects covariance structure as in (
1
). Such a model is then high-dimensional in the conditional mean, but relies on a rigid low dimensional structure for the conditional covariance; in Section
3.1
we will see that this covariance model reliance can be potentially costly with regards to accuracy when misspecified.

In recent work
Young and Shah (2024)
consider estimating a linear component in a partially linear model, where they propose an estimation strategy within a constrained class of estimators that is optimal regardless of the form of the true covariance structure. Our work follows a similar robust ‘covariance model free’ ethos, but additionally considers the further relaxed setting of a fully nonparametric conditional mean function.

Our clustered random forest algorithm naturally relates to a wide literature on random forests
(
Breiman, 2001
)
, which have proved popular due to their success when deployed on modern datasets.
Random forests results have shown
(
Meinshausen, 2006
;
Biau, 2012
;
Cevid et al., 2022
)
and asymptotic normality of variance-dominating random forests
(
Wager and Walther, 2016
;
Wager and Athey, 2018
;
Näf et al., 2023
)
. Our theoretical results draw on some of these ideas.

One of the contributions of this work highlights a connection between covariate distribution shift problems and correlated data. The setting of optimal estimation in covariate shift problems for independent, uncorrelated data has been studied by
Shimodaira (2000)
;
Sugiyama and Kawanabe (2012)
;
Ma et al. (2023)
;
Pathak et al. (2024)
;
Kpotufe and Martinet (2021)
;
Koh et al. (2021)
. Much less attention has been paid however to correlated data settings, where we see differences in behaviour under covariate shift to the independence settings.

Weight classes to model intra-cluster correlation:

An important distinguishing feature of the clustered random forest algorithm is the added flexibility resulting from our weighting scheme, that in turn models the intra-cluster correlations between observations for more accurate estimation of the mean function
\mu
.

Algorithm
1
involves a user-chosen parametric class of cluster-wise weight matrices
\{W_{i}(\rho):\rho\in\Gamma\}
(where by default we take
\rho=0
to correspond to
W_{i}(0)=I_{n_{i}}
). The restriction to specifically a parametric class allows for computationally simple weight estimation, in particular in light of our desire for minimal additional computational burden over standard random forests. Our numerical experiments (see Section
4
to follow) explore two concrete examples of possible weight classes, which are also implemented in the
corrRF
package: the equicorrelated (exchangeable); and
\text{AR}(1)
weight structures, which are both popular weighting methods in parametric clustered models (such as mixed effects models and generalised estimating equations) and take the forms

W_{i}(\rho)=\big(\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{j=k\}}+\rho\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{j\neq k\}}\big)_{j,k\in[n_{i}]}\big)^{-1},\quad\text{and}\quad W_{i}(\rho)=\big(\big(\rho^{|j-k|}\big)_{j,k\in[n_{i}]}\big)^{-1},

respectively. These two structures are shown to allow for approximately linear time (in the number of observations used to build a tree) fitting of clustered decision trees (see Section
3.4
to follow). Whilst our theoretical results are shown to hold for classes of weight structures beyond these two examples, we treat these two examples as popular running examples throughout.

Estimation of weights:

Given a user-specified weight matrix class (parametrised by some Euclidean parameter
\rho\in\Gamma
) we will see that the estimator
\hat{\rho}
that defines these weights may be estimated via any method that need only converge at logarithmic rates to a deterministic weight parameter
\rho^{*}\in\Gamma
, with optimality under potentially covariate shifted MSPE controlled by the covariate shift integrated variance of an individual
\rho^{*}
-weighted tree (see Theorem
1
).

There are a number of natural strategies, not necessarily optimal, one may choose to estimate weights; we discuss some of these methods and highlight their drawbacks. One natural approach would be to fit a random effects model on the residual error terms
(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij})_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}},j\in[n_{i}]}
(e.g. for the residuals defined in Algorithm
1
). This follows the ethos of
(
Sela and Simonoff, 2012
;
Hajjem et al., 2014
)
, which builds on standard parametric theory in multilevel modeling
(
Pinheiro and Bates, 2000
;
Fahrmeir and Tutz, 2001
;
Diggle et al., 2013
)
. An alternative strategy could be to estimate weights that minimise an empirical estimator of the mean squared training error of a clustered decision tree weighted by the parameter
\rho
. Adopting the notation of Algorithm
1
, a simple empirical estimator of the integrated variance over the training covariate distribution is given by

\underset{\rho\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigg\{\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}\chi_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)^{-1}\\
\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)^{-1}\bigg\}.

(3)

We will see later in Section
3.1
that (see Theorem
2
) both of these two strategies can select arbitrarily suboptimal MSPE under covariate shift. Because clustered random forest performance is controlled by the individual tree-level covariate shift integrated variance (Theorem
1
) a natural optimal approach would be to directly target this quantity. Given a covariate shift distribution
Q
, consider the empirical estimator of this quantity (again adopting the notation of Algorithm
1
) via

\underset{\rho\in\Gamma}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigg\{\textrm{diag}\Big(\big(Q(L_{m})\big)_{m\in[M]}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)^{-1}\\
\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{corr}}}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\Big)^{-1}\bigg\},

(4)

where
Q(L_{m})=\int_{L_{m}}dQ
may be replaced by an estimator for this quantity e.g. the empirical frequency using a new testing dataset consisting only of covariates drawn from
Q
.
Theory for this empirical estimator (
4
) is derived in Section
3
.
Alternatively
Q
can be directly taken as the empirical distribution of the covariates a test set, and so it may contain only a few unlabeled test points.

Honesty property for decision trees:

The clustered decision trees of Algorithm
1
satisfy a principle termed as honesty, following the naming scheme of
Wager and Athey (2018)
, in that for each individual decision tree a single cluster is used in determining at most one of the decision tree splits, working correlation (weight structure) estimation, and predictions (evaluations); in practice this is achieved through a tree-wise sample splitting of the subsampled data for each tree. As we see in Section
3
this allows for guaranteed theoretical results such as asymptotic normality of the pointwise mean function estimator and functionals of the mean. In generality however, an analogous clustered random forest could easily be introduced that does not satisfy honesty (obtained by omitting the tree-wise sample split step) and that therefore more closely mimics the original random forests of
Breiman (2001)
; see Appendix
B
for more details, which also allows for instance the option to treat the weight parameters as additional hyperparameters to be tuned via cross validation.

Decision tree splitting rule:

The clustered decision tree Algorithm
1
also notably allows for an arbitrary user-chosen splitting rule. Whilst it is likely that different splitting mechanisms will differ in behaviour in practice, the asymptotic results for clustered random forests we present only require the splitting rule used to satisfy certain minimal properties (see Assumption
A1
to follow). For these reasons, the precise choice of splitting rule here we leave to further work. As a default in our numerical simulations we will use the CART criterion of
Breiman (2001)
, in part due to its popularity and computationally attractive properties in practice.

Subsampling to generate random forests:

Algorithm
2
constructs clustered random forests, which aggregates a number of clustered decision trees generated with subsamples of the clustered data.
In practice, we aggregate
R\times B
clustered decision trees, where the subsampling process is structured into two sample splits; for each
r\in[R]
a subsample of the data (of size approximately half the full data) is taken, and then
B
decision trees are constructed from a further subsampling, in which a
s_{I}
clusters are used in the evaluation step for each tree. The rate at which
s_{I}
diverges will dictate the convergence rate of the final random forests (see Section
3
). The former part of this subsampling mechanism, which does not appear in the random forests of
Breiman (2001)
, is introduced to construct a so-called ‘bootstrap of little bags’ estimator of the variance
(
Sexton and Laake, 2009
;
Athey et al., 2019
;
Näf et al., 2023
)
, allowing for confidence interval construction of the mean function for variance dominating random forests (see Theorem
5
). Note however if this estimator for the variance is not desired, for example if one merely wishes to obtain a more accurate estimator for the mean function (see Theorem
1
), then Algorithm
2
can simply be taken with
R=1
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\mathcal{I}
.

3
Asymptotic theory

Here we present asymptotic theory of our clustered random forests algorithm, in a regime where the number of clusters
I
diverges. For simplicity of exposition we will present our results in the case where the group size
n_{i}\equiv n_{\mathrm{c}}
is constant across clusters, though our results extend to differing group sizes.
First, we present an asymptotic upper bound on the general
Q
-covariate shifted MSPE, and thus by corollary an upper bound on the pointwise MSE (that for certain splitting rules coincide with the minimax rate). We then study optimality with respect to
Q
-covariate shifted MSPE to motivate the
Q
-adjusted weight estimation method of Algorithm
2
. Finally, we prove uniform asymptotic Gaussianity results for variance dominating clustered random forests, which in turn can be used to show asymptotic coverage of the pointwise
\alpha
-level confidence intervals
\hat{C}_{\alpha}(x)
presented in Algorithm
2
.
We conclude by discussing the linear time computational comparability of clustered random forests to standard random forests.
Proofs of all the results presented in this section are given in Appendix
C
.

;
Cevid et al. (2022)
. In particular, our extensions to clustered data involves a number of additional non-trivial technicalities. To indicate how these arise observe that the predictor at a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
of a standard decision tree is a function of only the local data in the leaf
L(x)
of that tree. In contrast a clustered decision tree’s prediction at
x\in\mathcal{X}
is also a function of (potentially) all the data, due to the inclusion of information from not only the local datapoints contained in the leaf
L(x)
but also the data correlated with observations in
L(x)
. A number of common techniques used to analyse random forests in the aforementioned references consequently don’t follow in correlated data settings, and must be adapted to accommodate these extra intricacies; see Appendix
C
for further details.

Throughout this section, our theoretical results are presented for ‘Monte-Carlo limit’ random forests
(
Wager and Athey, 2018
;
Näf et al., 2023
)
constructed from trees generated via all possible subsamplings of the data, and take the form

\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\text{MC}}(x):=\binom{I}{\bar{s}_{I}}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\bar{s}_{I}}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\Xi}\bigl[T(x,\xi;Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{\bar{s}_{I}}})\,\big|\,Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{\bar{s}_{I}}}\bigr],

(5)

where
\bar{s}:=3s_{I}
, and where
T(x,\xi;Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{\bar{s}}})
denotes the prediction at the point
x\in\mathcal{X}
of a decision tree
T
constructed from the data
\{Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{\bar{s}}}\}
, with
Z_{i}:=(Y_{i},X_{i})
and
\xi\sim\Xi
denoting a random variable that encapsulates the randomness in the decision tree splitting procedure.
In practice our clustered random forests (Algorithm
2
) approximate their ‘Monte-Carlo limit’ for a sufficiently large
B\times R
, and in our empirical experiments of Section
4.1
we take
(B,R)=(500,1)
, which suffices as a sufficient approximation to the Monte-Carlo limit (see Appendix
D.3
).
The following assumptions are made on the tree splitting mechanism and random forest construction of Algorithm
2
.

Assumption A1

(Forest properties)
.

Suppose each tree of Algorithm
2
satisfies the following properties:

(A1.1)

(
Honesty
) Each group-wise datapoint
(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
used to build a single tree is used to determine at most one of the splits of the tree, the ‘random effects’
\hat{\rho}
, the ‘fixed effects’ evaluation (see Algorithm
2
for a splitting technique that enforces this property).

(A1.2)

(
Symmetry
) The distribution of the randomised tree splits are independent of the ordering of the training samples, for orderings preserving the group structure i.e. interchanging the order of clusters and interchanging intra-cluster observations.

(A1.3)

(
Regularity
) Each tree split leaves a fraction
\alpha\in(0,0.5]
of the available training data in each child node. Further, trees are grown until every leaf contains between
k_{I}
and
2k_{I}-1
observations for some
k_{I}\in\mathbb{N}
.

(A1.4)

(
Feature splits
) Each path from the root node to a terminal node (leaf), and for each covariate feature
j\in[d]
, a proportion
d^{-1}\pi
of the tree splits along the path are along the
j
th feature space almost surely, for some fixed
\pi\in(0,1]
.

(A1.5)

(
Subsampling sizes
) The subsampling sizes
s_{I},s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}}\to\infty
as
I\to\infty
.

These restrictions to the splitting procedure are commonplace in pre-existing results on distributional limits of random forests
(
Wager and Athey, 2018
;
Athey et al., 2019
;
Näf et al., 2023
)
.
Our assumption
(A1.4)
differs to
Wager and Athey (2018)
who assume each tree split is on the
j
th feature with probability at least
d^{-1}\pi
, although notably the two assumptions are equivalent in the
d=1
case.
This however allows us to strengthen our theoretical results. Whilst the aforementioned requires require
\alpha\in(0,0.2]
in Assumption
(A1.3)
we may accommodate
\alpha\in(0,0.5]
. Subsequently we obtain faster rates that approach minimax rates for certain splitting rules (see Remark
4
to follow), and we obtain variance dominating, asymptotically normal random forests for smaller subsampling regimes, and thus allowing us to form confidence intervals for the pointwise conditional mean function with widths that converge at faster rates.
Assumption
A1
does restrict the splitting procedures that we provide our theoretical guarantees over, but the above assumptions can be enforced for popular splitting criteria such as CART, provided the candidate splits being assessed are restricted so as not to include sufficiently extreme splits at each iteration.

Our theoretical results are also shown to hold for a broad class of weight functions.
It will help to define for symmetric matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the diagonally dominant function

\zeta(M):=\min_{j\in[n]}\biggl(M_{jj}-\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[n]\setminus\{j\}}|M_{jj^{\prime}}|\biggr).

Assumption A2

(Weight estimation)
.

.

(A2.1)

(
Admissible weight classes
)
The weight class
(W_{i}(\rho))_{\rho\in\Gamma}
defined over the compact set
\Gamma
in Algorithm
1
is symmetric and satisfies

\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\|W_{i}(\rho)\|_{1}\leq C_{W},\quad\inf_{\rho\in\Gamma}\zeta(W_{i}(\rho))\geq c_{W},\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}\bigg)\leq C_{W}^{\prime},

for finite constants
c_{W},C_{W},C_{W}^{\prime}>0
and for every
i\in[I]
.

(A2.2)

(
Weight estimation
) Suppose that
\hat{\rho}=\rho^{*}+o_{\mathcal{P}}\bigl((\log I)^{-d}\bigr)
of some deterministic weight limit
\rho^{*}
.

Assumption
(A2.1)
is satisfied for two common diagonally dominant weight structures used in multilevel modeling: the inverse equicorrelated structure with finite cluster size; and the inverse
\text{AR}(1)
structure with potentially diverging cluster size (see Appendix
C.3
), both of which are implemented in
corrRF
. The second condition of
(A2.1)
implies a lower bound on the minimum eigenvalue
\Lambda_{\min}(W_{i}(\rho))\geq c_{W}
via the Gershgorin circle theorem.

We also make the following assumptions on the training data generating mechanism.

Assumption A3

(Data generating procedure)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a class of distributions on i.i.d. clusters
(Y_{i},X_{i})
with the properties that for each
P\in\mathcal{P}
the following hold:

(A3.1)

(
Covariate Density
)
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
with i.i.d. cluster-level covariates
(X_{i})_{i\in[I]}
, with each entry
X_{ij}
admitting a marginal density
f
with respect to Lebesgue measure, satisfying
\nu^{-1}\leq f(x)\leq\nu
for some
\nu\geq 1
, and either: (i) covariates
(X_{ij})_{j\in[n_{i}]}
are i.i.d and we allow the cluster size
n_{i}
to diverge with rates
\log n_{i}=O(\log I)
; or
(ii)
X_{ij}=X_{ij^{\prime}}
almost surely for all
j,j^{\prime}\in[n_{i}]
,
n_{i}
is finite, and
k/n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.

(A3.2)

(
Lipschitz continuity
) The functions
x\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y_{ij}\,|\,X_{ij}=x]=:\mu(x)
and
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(Y_{ij},Y_{ik}\,|\,X_{ij}=x,X_{ik}=x^{\prime})

are bounded and Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constants
L_{\mu}
and
L_{v}
respectively.

(A3.3)

(
Bounded covariance
) The covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}(Y_{i}\,|\,X_{i})

has eigenvalues in the interval
[\lambda^{-1},\lambda]
almost surely for some
\lambda\geq 1
.

(A3.4)

(
Bounded moments
) There exists some
\delta,\tau>0
such that for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
and
x\in[0,1]^{d}
we have
\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[|a^{T}(Y_{i}-\mu(x))|^{2+\delta}\,|\,X_{i}=x\big]\leq\tau\,\mathbb{E}_{P}[\{a^{T}(Y_{i}-\mu(x))\}^{2}\,|\,X_{i}=x]^{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}
.

The condition
(A3.1)
allows for an extreme case when the joint distribution of the cluster-level covariate
X_{i}
is degenerate, which occurs for example when one has repeated measures with the same cluster-level covariates.
Equipped with Assumption
A3
, we may prove the following bound on a general
Q
-covariate shifted MSPE

\mathrm{MSPE}_{Q}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}\bigr):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\bigr)^{2}\,dQ(x).

Theorem 1
.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}
be a clustered random forest estimator (
5
) satisfying
A1
and
A2
, trained on
I
clusters drawn from a law
P
satisfying
A3
,
with
k_{I}^{-1}I^{-1}s_{I}\to 0
and
k_{I}^{-1}n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}\to\infty
as
I\to\infty
.
Also fix an arbitrarily small constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and covariate distribution
Q
, there exists some
I_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
that does not depend on
(P,Q)
such that, for all
I\geq I_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{MSPE}_{Q}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}\bigr)\bigr]\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{2}\biggl(\frac{2k_{I}}{n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}}\biggr)^{2(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\frac{\pi}{d}}+\,\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})\biggl(\frac{s_{I}}{k_{I}I}\biggr),

where
C_{\mathrm{bias}}:=\frac{6C_{W}L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\nu}{c_{W}}
, and

\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*}):=k_{I}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(T_{\rho^{*}}(x)\bigr)\,dQ(x),

is the
Q
-integrated variance of an individual clustered decision tree
T_{\rho^{*}}(x)
with fixed correlation weighting parameter
\rho^{*}
.

The two terms in the above bound correspond to the integrated squared bias and variance respectively, and dictate the bias-variance tradeoff with respect to the parameters
(s_{I},k_{I})
.
Taking
Q
as the marginal law of the training covariate distribution, the above is a bound on the mean squared training error. When
Q=\delta_{x}
is a point mass at some
x\in\mathcal{X}
we obtain pointwise MSE convergence rates

\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\big)^{2}\Big]\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{2}\biggl(\frac{2k_{I}}{n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}}\biggr)^{2(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\frac{\pi}{d}}+\,\mathcal{V}_{\delta_{x}}(\rho^{*})\biggl(\frac{s_{I}}{k_{I}I}\biggr),

where
\mathcal{V}_{\delta_{x}}(\rho^{*})=k_{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(T_{\rho^{*}}(x)\bigr)

Moreover, the quantity
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)
converges to some finite non-zero deterministic limit as
I
diverges, that in particular does not depend on
(s_{I},k_{I})
; the pointwise variance of a tree with order
k_{I}
observations per leaf scales as
k_{I}^{-1}
. Because
I_{0}
in Theorem
1
does not depend on
P\in\mathcal{P}
this result is a uniform result in
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem
1
also holds for standard random forests (with cluster-wise subsampling) applied to clustered data by taking
\hat{\rho}=\rho^{*}=0
in the above. Thus, if a clustered random forest with weights
\rho^{*}
satisfy
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})\leq V_{Q}(0)
then the clustered random forest improves on the standard random forest regardless of the choice of hyperparameters
(s_{I},k_{I})
. Whilst not necessarily optimally tuned, if a user had access to a standard random forest already fit and hyperparameter tuned, then asymptotic improvements are achieved by adding in the weight structure of clustered random forests without having to re-tune the other hyperparameters in a ‘greedy’ final post-hoc evaluation step.

3.1
Optimal weights under covariate distribution shift

The
Q
-covariate shifted MSPE in Theorem
2
is controlled monotonically by
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})

Thus, given a target covariate distribution
Q
, we are interested in obtaining the minimiser of the function
\rho\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)
. The following theorem shows that this minimiser can depend critically on the covariate shift
Q
.

Theorem 2

(Covariate shift)
.

Consider a clustered random forest with properties
A1
and
A2
with equicorrelated weight structure, trained on
I
clusters drawn from law satisfying
A3
with
d=1
, and
with leaf size
k_{I}^{-1}=o(1)
,
k_{I}=o\bigl(s_{I}^{2\phi/(2\phi+1)}\bigr)
where
\phi:=(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\pi
.

Fix an arbitrarily small
\varrho\in(0,1)
, and arbitrarily large
\kappa\geq 1
.
Then there exists a distribution
P
on the training data satisfying Assumption
A3
with

\mathrm{Corr}_{P}(Y_{ij},Y_{ij^{\prime}}\,|\,X_{i})=\varrho,

almost surely for
j\neq j^{\prime}
, and there exists covariate shifted distributions
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
such that for sufficiently large
I
,

\frac{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{1}}(\rho_{Q_{2}\text{-}\mathrm{opt}})}{\mathcal{V}_{Q_{1}}(\rho_{Q_{1}\text{-}\mathrm{opt}})}\geq\kappa,

where

\rho_{Q_{j}\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}:=\underset{\rho\in[0,1)}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\mathcal{V}_{Q_{j}}(\rho),\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

Essentially Theorem
2
shows that for correlated data
\mathrm{argmin}_{\rho\in\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)
is a function of
Q
, and thus the optimal weights vary depending on covariate shift; an empirical realisation of this observation is given in Section
4.1
(see also Figure
3
). Moreover, the optimal clustered random forest under one covariate distribution
Q_{1}
can be arbitrarily suboptimal under another covariate distribution
Q_{2}
. The result of Theorem
2
holds when restricting
Q_{2}
to be the marginal distribution of
X
under
P
, in which case
\rho_{Q_{2}\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}
is the asymptotic limit of weights estimated via cross-validation. Theorem
2
also holds when restricting
Q_{1}
to a point mass e.g. when performing pointwise inference.

The consequences listed below follow from the above result, with comparisons between standard random forests, clustered random forests (CRF), REEM (likelihood based) and MERF (likelihood based) random forests given for the same parameter choices
(s_{I},k_{I})
and (with the exception of standard random forests that don’t have a weight class)
fit with identical weight classes:

(i)

Q
-shift optimised clustered random forests are optimal in the sense of Theorem
1
in the sense that their weights directly target the minimal
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\cdot)
within the user-specified weight class.

(ii)

Likelihood based random forests (e.g. REEM and MERF) are suboptimal in the sense that they can select weights that see an arbitrarily larger variance than the optimal weights within the user-specified weight class, i.e. there exists distributions
P
and covariate shifts
Q
for which REEM and MERF random forests perform arbitrarily worse than
both
clustered random forests and even standard random forests.

(iii)

An approach based on estimating the weights by minimising the MSPE under the training covariate distribution (as is targeted by e.g. cross validation) can exhibit arbitrarily suboptimal performance on a covariate shifted test set compared to
both
clustered random forests and even standard random forests.

All the above points are studied empirically in Section
4.1
(see also Figure
3
).

In light of Theorem
2
, our clustered random forest that adapts to
Q
-covariate shift minimises an empirical estimator of
\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\cdot)
via (
4
) as advocated in Algorithm
1
to estimate the optimal parameter
\rho^{*}_{Q\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{\rho\in\Gamma}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)
.
The following proposition justifies this empirical estimator (
4
).

Proposition 3
.

Consider the weight estimator
\hat{\rho}
that minimises the empirical
Q
-adjusted loss function (
4
), as in Algorithm
1
for the weight class satisfying Assumption
(A2.1)
, alongside the additional identifiability condition that

\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho)\geq\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*}_{Q\text{-}\mathrm{opt}})+c\,\|\rho-\rho^{*}_{Q\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}\|_{2}^{2},

for all
\rho\in\Gamma
with
\|\rho-\rho^{*}_{Q\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}\|_{2}\leq\Delta
and for some
c,\Delta>0
.
Also suppose the minimum node size is chosen to satisfy
k_{I}^{-1}n_{\mathrm{c}}=o(1)
. Then

\hat{\rho}=\rho_{Q\text{-}\mathrm{opt}}^{*}+O_{P}\Bigl(n_{\mathrm{c}}k_{I}^{-1/2}+(s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}})^{-(\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}\wedge\frac{\phi}{d})}\Bigr),

where
\phi:=(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\pi
.

The correlation weighting estimation scheme (
4
) therefore satisfies the rate requirement
(A2.2)
for Theorem
2
for example if the subsampling rate
s_{I}
is taken to be polynomial in
I
, cluster size
n_{\mathrm{c}}
is finite and
k_{I}\sim(\log I)^{2d+1}
. Proposition
3
also accommodates diverging cluster sizes at sufficiently slow polynomial rates by taking the asymptotic regime of
k_{I}
to diverge sufficiently fast.

3.1.1
Distinction between correlated and uncorrelated data when adjusting for covariate shift

Our result of Theorem
2
directly contrasts uncorrelated data settings e.g. when the data are independent. In misspecified parametric mean models with independent observations, it is well known that improvements can be achieved (under covariate shift from
Q_{1}
to
Q_{2}
) by the re-weighting scheme given by
dQ_{2}/dQ_{1}

(
Shimodaira, 2000
;
Sugiyama and Kawanabe, 2012
)
, with no improvements under model well specification
(
Ge et al., 2024
)
.
Note that for a random forest however provided the conditional mean is indeed Lipschitz smooth, the conditional mean is not model misspecified, and consequently the reweighting
dQ_{2}/dQ_{1}
applied to a standard random forest effectively leaves the random forest predictor and hence covariate shifted MSPE unchanged. For a single decision tree this can be seen by noticing that the leaves are sufficiently localised, with diameters converging to zero, and so the weights
dQ_{2}/dQ_{1}
are effectively constant on the local region on which we do estimation, giving asymptotically identical estimation to standard decision trees.

3.2
Optimality over subsampling rates

We now turn our attention to the dependence of clustered random forests on the other hyperparameters, most notably those of
(s_{I},k_{I})
. The results of this section give rise to analogous immediate consequences for hyperparameter selection in standard random forests, which may be of independent interest.

Recall that by Theorem
1
we obtain that for sufficiently large
I
,

\mathbb{E}_{P}\biggl[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\bigr)^{2}dQ(x)\biggr]\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{2}\biggl(\frac{2k_{I}}{n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}}\biggr)^{\phi/d}+\frac{s_{I}}{k_{I}}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})}{I},

for
C_{\mathrm{bias}}:=\frac{6C_{W}L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\nu}{c_{W}}
,
which is minimised when

k_{I}^{-1}s_{I}=2\Biggl(\frac{C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{2}\phi I}{n_{\mathrm{c}}^{2\phi/d}\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})}\Biggr)^{\frac{d}{2\phi+d}},\qquad\phi:=(1-\epsilon)\pi\,\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}

(6)

whence

\mathbb{E}_{P}\biggl[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\bigr)^{2}dQ(x)\biggr]\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{\frac{2d}{2\phi+d}}\biggl(\frac{2\phi+d}{d}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{\mathcal{V}_{Q}(\rho^{*})}{\phi N}\biggr)^{\frac{2\phi}{2\phi+d}},

(7)

where

constitutes the number of individual-level observations.

Note therefore the system of hyperparameters is essentially overparametrised with regards to
Q
-shifted MSPE; we only require the ratio

to diverge as in (

) to obtain the above rate of convergence for

-covariate shifted MSPE, thus giving a ‘valley’ of optimal hyperparameters along

for an optimal constant

(see Figure

).
This allows us to accommodate forest splitting hyperparameter setups where
k_{I}
diverges sufficiently slowly without incurring an asymptotic cost to MSPE.
If for example
s_{I}=3^{-1}I^{\beta}
and
k_{I}=I^{\kappa}
for constants
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\kappa>0
then to achieve the rate of (

) we require
\beta=\kappa+\phi
, implying the restriction on
k_{I}
divergence via
\kappa<1-\phi
.
In practice will grow particularly deep trees, such that we consider either

as fixed and finite, or
k_{I}
diverging at a poly-logarithmic rate, and in all our empirical experiments we take

.

Note in particular (

) allows for minimax rates under certain splitting rules.

Remark 4

(Approaching minimax rates)
.

Taking Theorem
1
with splitting procedure satisfying

with

as in (

Taking

By taking Algorithm

the faster rates of Theorem

and minimax rates of Remark

We also observe the curse of dimensionality in the dependence of

. Studying the performance of random forests, and by virtue clustered random forests, in the high dimensional case with e.g. further sparsity assumptions on

.

3.3
Inference

The subsampling rate (
6
) used to achieve minimax optimality in Theorem
1
corresponds to a random forest that balances the bounds obtained for the squared bias and variance. For larger subsampling rates we obtain variance-dominating random forests, for which we can show uniform (over
\mathcal{P}
) asymptotic Gaussianity results. For the following result we take an asymptotic regime with subsampling size
s_{I}=3^{-1}I^{\beta}
diverging at a subsampling rate
\beta
.

Theorem 5

(Asymptotic Normality of variance-dominating CRFs)
.

Suppose Assumption
A3
holds, and let
\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{I}
denote a clustered random forest estimator (
5
) with tree splitting procedure satisfying
A1
with
k=O(\log I)
, and
s_{I}=3^{-1}I^{\beta}
with subsampling rate satisfying
\beta>\beta_{\min}:=1-\Big(1+\frac{d}{2\pi}\frac{\log(\alpha^{-1})}{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}\Big)^{-1}
. Suppose also the weighting scheme
(W_{i}(\rho))_{\rho\in\Gamma}
satisfies
A2
.
Now fix an
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then there exists a sequence of positive deterministic functions
(\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*}))_{I\in\mathbb{N}}
with

\sigma_{P,I}^{2}(x;\rho^{*})=O_{\mathcal{P}}\big(I^{-(1-\beta)}\big)
and

\lim_{I\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})^{-1}\big(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\big)\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\big|=0.

(8)

Theorem
5
shows that the variance–dominated clustered random forests are asymptotically unbiased and Gaussian pointwise over
\mathcal{X}
and uniformly in probability for the class of distributions
\mathcal{P}
as defined by Assumption
A3
.
Moreover, a similar result holds for estimating the integrated mean squared error over a fixed covariate distribution
Q
with
\textrm{supp}\,Q\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, in that there exists a sequence of deterministic positive functions
(\sigma^{(Q)}_{I}(\rho^{*}))_{I\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\sigma_{I}^{(Q)\,2}(\rho^{*})=O_{\mathcal{P}}\big(I^{-(1-\beta)}\big)
and

\lim_{I\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sigma^{(Q)}_{I}(\rho^{*})^{-1}\Big(\int_{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)d{Q}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mu(x)d{Q}(x)\Big)\leq t\bigg)-\Phi(t)\bigg|=0.

An empirical realisation of this result is seen in Appendix
D.1
.
Also note that the subsampling rates we accommodate of
\beta>1-\bigl(1+\frac{d}{2\pi}\frac{\log(\alpha^{-1})}{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}\bigr)^{-1}
allow for smaller subsampling rates than e.g.
Wager and Athey (2018)
;
Athey et al. (2019)
;
Näf et al. (2023)
who require
\beta>1-\bigl(1+\frac{d}{\pi}\bigl\{\frac{\log(\alpha^{-1})}{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}\vee\frac{\log 5}{\log(5/4)}\bigr\}\bigr)^{-1}
. As an example, in the case
(d,\pi,\alpha)=(2,1,0.5)
we may take
\beta>0.5
, whereas the results of the aforementioned references require
\beta>0.93
. This wider range of permissible subsampling rates allows for us to obtain confidence intervals whose widths converge at faster rates too. As our non-i.i.d. data setting collapses to i.i.d. data in special cases, these strengthened subsampling rates can also be applied in this simpler setting.

Regarding testing based on the pointwise mean function, note in particular that, with a sequence of estimators
(\hat{V}_{I}(x))_{I\in\mathbb{N}}
of the variances of the sequence of clustered random forest estimators
(\hat{\mu}_{I}(x))_{I\in\mathbb{N}}
we can construct asymptotically valid confidence intervals for
\mu(x)
using (
8
). To construct such an estimator a number of bootstrap aggregation procedures
(
Efron, 1982
)
have been proposed for standard honest random forests, and that naturally extend to clustered settings. These include the infinitesimal jackknife
(
Efron, 2014
;
Wager and Athey, 2018
)
the bias corrected infinitesimal jackknife
(
Wager et al., 2014
)
, and ‘bootstrap of little bags’ estimators
(
Sexton and Laake, 2009
;
Athey et al., 2019
;
Näf et al., 2023
)
. As a benchmark, in the construction of Algorithm
2
, in addition to the numerical results of Section
4
, we consider the latter ‘bootstrap of little bags’ estimator
\hat{V}_{I}(x)
of the variance, and obtain nominal coverage of the
\tilde{\alpha}
-level confidence interval
\hat{C}_{\tilde{\alpha}}(x)
for
\mu(x)
for a fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}
(as in Algorithm
2
) i.e.

\lim_{I\to\infty}\big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\mu(x)\in\hat{C}_{\tilde{\alpha}}(x)\big)-(1-\tilde{\alpha})\big|=0,

following from Theorem
5
and the consistency of the ‘bootstrap of little bags’ estimator
(
Athey et al., 2019
, Section 4.1)
. Note that as one can cast pointwise inference as a covariate distribution shift problem, from the covariate distribution of the training data to a point mass at the point of interest, the optimality results of clustered random forests, and the corresponding suboptimalities of pre-existing alternative methods, under covariate shift (Section
3.1
) directly apply in this context with regards to obtaining tight confidence intervals for functionals of the conditional mean.

3.4
Approximately linear time computation of clustered random forests

A key practical advantage of standard random forests is their decision trees can be trained in linear time
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
with the number of observations used for splitting and evaluation in the tree. Using the CART criterion for splitting requires
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
operations. For the evaluation step, using the notation of Algorithm
1
, we recover ordinary decision trees by setting
\hat{\rho}=\rho=0
. In this case, the matrix in the evaluation step simplifies to

\sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(0)\chi_{i}\;=\;\sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{\top}\chi_{i},

which is diagonal and therefore can be inverted in
O(M)
operations. Since
M
scales like
n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}/k_{I}
, a standard decision tree is fit in linear time
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
with respect to the number of data points.

For clustered decision trees (Algorithm
1
) the evaluation step is more involved.
For general
\rho\neq 0
the matrix

\sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

is typically dense, and a naive inversion would require
O(M^{3})
operations—prohibitively expensive as the number of leaves grows with sample size. Nonetheless, although this matrix is not sparse, certain choices of working-correlation weights yield structure that enables efficient computation via conjugate gradient descent. In particular, for the equicorrelated and AR
(1)
weight classes used in our running examples, we show that the fitted values

\bigl(\sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(\sum_{i}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)Y_{i}\bigr)

can still be computed in linear time through a lightweight application of conjugate gradient descent.

Proposition 6

(Approximate linear time clustered random forest fitting)
.

Suppose
W_{i}(\rho)
takes the equicorrelated or
\mathrm{AR}(1)
working correlation weight structure. Then:

(i)

Evaluation complexity:
There exists a computational strategy to fit a clustered decision tree with fixed
\rho
that computes fitted values to an
\epsilon
-accuracy in Euclidean norm in
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
operations.

(ii)

Correlation weight estimation complexity:
For a given covariate distribution
Q
and tree splits giving rise to leaves
(L_{m}:m\in[M])
define
c_{Q}=|\{m\in[M]:L_{m}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,Q\neq\emptyset\}|
. Then there exists a computational strategy that estimates the optimal correlation parameter via (
4
) such that Algorithm
1
requires
O(c_{Q}n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}})
operations.

Case
(i)
motivates a linear time fitting procedure for a fixed correlation parameter
\rho
, e.g. if
\rho
were to be treated as a hyperparameter for tuning alongside the standard random forest hyperparameters, which could be estimated in non-covariate shift settings e.g. by cross-validation.
Note in the case of
(ii)
when considering a covariate shift to a point mass - e.g. targeting optimal pointwise MSE or pointwise inference (see also Section
3.3
) - the quantity
c_{Q}=1
. At the other extreme when
\mathrm{supp}\,Q=\mathcal{X}
then
c_{Q}=M\sim k_{I}^{-1}n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}}
and thus the computational order of our proposed strategy is of order
O(k_{I}^{-1}n_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}(s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}})^{2})
. Because we only require slow rates for
\rho
-estimation (of which arbitrarily slow polynomial rates suffices; see Assumption
(A2.2)
), taking an asymptotic regime for
s_{I}^{\mathrm{corr}}=o\bigl((n_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}k_{I}s_{I})^{1/2}\bigr)
allows the correlation weight estimation to add only an asymptotically negligible excess computational cost over standard random forests.

Together
(i)
and
(ii)
allow for clustered random forests to be fit in approximately linear time, effectively matching the favourable scalability properties of standard random forests.
We remark that the pre-existing random forests algorithms for clustered data via REEM and MERF random forests are cubic-time procedures of order
O(M^{3})
(even with oracle knowledge of the correlation) and subsequently accommodating correlations with these procedures come at a high computational cost.

5
Discussion

In this work we have introduced a new random forest algorithm for clustered data that utilises intra-cluster correlations for lower variance prediction error. We extend the theory of random forests to obtain asymptotic Gaussianity results for ‘Monte-Carlo limit’ variance-dominating clustered random forests as well as the existence of splitting rules and subsampling mechanisms for which minimax rates are obtained.

A main contribution of this work is the recognition that when data are correlated the optimal weighting parameters in clustered random forests depend intricately on the covariate distribution with which one wishes to obtain optimal MSPE, and consequently the lack of optimality of standard random forests in these settings (Theorem
2
). We implement a computationally competitive weight estimation strategy that aims to target the optimal MSPE with respect to a user-specified covariate shift, allowing for optimal covariate shift adaptation in these settings. Given the growing recognition of covariate shift being a potential factor for poor practical performance in a range of machine learning tasks, these results offer a fresh perspective on aspects of the covariate shift problem, specifically through the lens of correlated data.

This work naturally offers a number of interesting directions for further study. One is to extend the class of ‘working correlations’ that clustered random forests may accommodate, both theoretically with regards to valid inference (i.e. relaxing the property
A2
) and practically in terms of computational considerations.
Another helpful direction would be to extend estimation beyond functionals of the conditional mean to other functionals of the conditional distribution, such as conditional quantiles.

It would also be of interest to further study the higher dimensional properties of clustered random forests; in particular we see empirical evidence in Section
4.2
that clustered random forests still maintain good performance and confidence intervals remain valid even in moderate dimensions with a sparse mean function, albeit with a cost to variance. A further theoretical treatment of Theorem
5
to high dimensional settings, for example with sparsity, would also be a natural extension.

Adapted (dishonest) clustered random forests

In Section

; subsampling fraction

\rho
.

for

Z_{\mathrm{corr}}:=(Z_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{corr}})
, as well as the analogous
X_{\mathrm{split}},Y_{\mathrm{split}},X_{\mathrm{eval}}
,
Y_{\mathrm{eval}}
.

For sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
when there exists a constant
c>0
that does not depend on
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
or
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
n^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, that does not depend on
x\in\mathcal{X}
nor
P\in\mathcal{P}
, such that for all
n\geq n^{*}
we have
a_{n}\leq cb_{n}
, and analogously for
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
. Note in particular the lack of dependence on
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}
in
c
here allows us to obtain the uniform convergence results where relevant. Also write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
when
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
.

C.2
Asymptotic unbiasedness of clustered random forests

In this section we prove the asymptotic unbiasedness of the estimator in Algorithm
2
.

Lemma 7

(Asymptotic unbiasedness of clustered random forests)
.

Suppose Assumption
A3
holds, and let
\hat{\mu}_{I}
denote a clustered random forest estimator (Algorithm
2
) with properties
A1
and
A2
. Fix an arbitrarily small constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Then for sufficiently large
I
,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}_{I}(x)]-\mu(x)\big|\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}\,\Bigl(\frac{s_{N}}{2k_{I}-1}\Bigr)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\frac{\pi}{d}\beta},

for the finite positive constant
C_{\mathrm{bias}}:=\frac{6C_{W}L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\nu}{c_{W}}
.

The result of Lemma
7
holds for clustered random forests consisting of an arbitrary number of clustered decision trees, and so applies to both a single decision tree as well as
\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\text{MC}}
as in (
5
). We observe that for our splitting properties
A1
- that are equivalent in the
d=1
case - we obtain an extra factor of two in the bias convergence rate over the analogous result of
Wager and Athey (2018, Lemma 2)
.
This faster bias convergence rate, alongside an upper bound on the variance of the random forest estimator, allows us to obtain minimax optimal rates in pointwise mean squared error for Lipschitz functions
(
Stone, 1982
)
for certain decision tree splitting regimes for (clustered) random forests
\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\text{MC}}
in unclustered i.i.d. settings (see Remark
4
).

Proof of Lemma
7
.

Let
B\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of trees that make up the random forest
\hat{\mu}_{I}
. We begin by considering an individual tree
T(x)
, for which we drop the notational dependence on
b\in[B]
for brevity.
For an individual tree
T(x)
, let
M
be the number of leaves of the tree, and
(L_{m})_{m\in[M]}
be the rectangular region that makes up each leaf. Also for a given
x\in\mathcal{X}
define
L(x)
to be the leaf to which
x
belongs, and let
J(x)
be the index for the leaf
L(x)
i.e.
J(x)
is the unique value of
m
satisfying
L(x)=L_{m}
.

Then the tree predictor at
x\in\mathcal{X}
is given by

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}\big(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\big)^{-1}\big(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\mathcal{Y}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{I}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\hat{\rho})\chi_{i}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{I}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\hat{\rho})Y_{i}\Big),

where

\chi:=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\chi_{I}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{Y}:=\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
Y_{I}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{W}:=\textrm{diag}\big(W_{i}(\hat{\rho})\mathbbm{1}_{(i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}})}\big)_{i\in[I]}.

(\chi_{i})_{j,m}=\mathbbm{1}_{(X_{ij}\in L_{m})}\mathbbm{1}_{(i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}})},\quad(i\in[I],j\in[n_{i}],m\in[M]).

Now for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define
\gamma(x):=\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{*}\,|\,X^{*}\in L(x),Z_{\text{split}}]
, where the data
(Y^{*},X^{*})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}
is such that
(Y^{*},X^{*})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z_{\text{split}}
. Note therefore by construction
\gamma(x^{\prime})=\gamma(x^{\prime\prime})
for all
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in J(x)
.
Now for each
m\in[M]
define
\gamma_{m}:=\gamma(x)
for any
x\in L_{m}
. We then have

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{\text{split}},Z_{\text{corr}},X_{\text{eval}}]-\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}\big(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\{\mu(X_{\text{eval}})-\chi\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}\big)+\{\gamma(x)-\mu(x)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)\theta_{ij}+\{\gamma(x)-\mu(x)\},

(9)

where

\boldsymbol{\gamma}:=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{M})
, and

\displaystyle\omega_{ij}(x)

\displaystyle:=e_{ij}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)},

(10)

\displaystyle\theta_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\mu(X_{ij})-\gamma_{J(X_{ij})}.

We work on the event
\Omega_{I}
given by Lemma
8
which holds with probability at least
1-s_{N}^{-3}
(see also Lemma 12 of
Wager and Walther (2016)
).
By Lemma
8
on
\Omega_{I}
and for sufficiently large
I
,

\big|\gamma(x)-\mu(x)\big|=\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{*}\,|\,X^{*}\in L(x),Z_{\text{split}}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{*}\,|\,X^{*}=x]\big|\\
\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\,\text{diam}\,L(x)\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

(11)

which follows by Lemma
8
.

The former term of (
9
) that arises is precisely zero for standard random forests, but typically non-zero for clustered random forests. First, note that

\displaystyle|\theta_{ij}|

\displaystyle=\big|\mu(X_{ij})-\gamma_{J(X_{ij})}\big|

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{*}\,|\,X^{*}=X_{ij}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{*}\,|\,X^{*}\in L(X_{ij}),Z_{\text{split}}]|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\,\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij}),

and so, also on the event
\Omega_{I}
,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)\theta_{ij}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)\theta_{ij}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)||\theta_{ij}|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|\,\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}C_{1}\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

(12)

where the penultimate inequality follows by Lemma
8
, and the final inequality by Lemma
9
, where
C_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is as given in Lemma
9
.
Combining (
11
) and (
12
) with the triangle inequality we therefore have that

\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{\text{split}},X_{\text{eval}}]-\mu(x)\big|\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}(1+C_{1})\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

(13)

Now consider the clustered random forest consisting of
B
trees i.e.
\hat{\mu}_{I}(x)=B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_{b}(x)
, and where we denote
Z_{b,\text{split}}
to be the split data for specifically the
b
th tree, and similarly
X_{b,\text{eval}}
etc. Then for each tree
T_{b}
we have

\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{b}(x)\,|\,Z_{b,\text{split}},X_{b,\text{eval}}]-\mu(x)\big|\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}(1+C_{1})\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

(14)

almost surely by the above. Therefore

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]-\mu(x)\big|

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{b}(x)]-\mu(x)\big|

\displaystyle=B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{b}(x)\,|\,Z_{b,\text{split}},X_{b,\text{eval}}]-\mu(x)\big]\big|

\displaystyle\leq B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{b}(x)\,|\,Z_{b,\text{split}},X_{b,\text{eval}}]-\mu(x)\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}(1+C_{1})\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

where the penultimate inequality follows by Jensen’s inequality and the final inequality by applying (
14
). The universal constants
L_{\mu}d^{1/2},C_{1},k,\alpha,\pi
carry no
x\in\mathcal{X}
or
P\in\mathcal{P}
dependence, so

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]-\mu(x)\big|\leq L_{\mu}d^{1/2}(1+C_{1})\Big(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Fix an abitrarily small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
T
be a tree satisfying Assumption
A1
, built such that
s
observations are used in the evaluation step. Let
L(x)
be the leaf of
T
that contains the point
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then with probability at least
1-s^{-3}
and for large enough
s
,

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{diam}\,L(x)\leq\Big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}.

Proof.

Fix an
x\in\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
. Let
c(x)
be the number of splits leading to leaf
L(x)
, and
c_{j}(x)
be the number of these splits along the
j\text{th}
feature.
Also let
L_{j}(x)
be the interval in the
j
th covariate direction of the rectangular leaf
L(x)
, so that
L(x)=\Pi_{j=1}^{d}L_{j}(x)
.
By Assumption
(A1.3)
we have that
s\alpha^{\frac{d}{\pi}c(x)}\leq 2k-1
, and thus
c(x)\geq\big(\log(\alpha^{-1})\big)^{-1}\log\big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\big)
almost surely. Then by Assumption
(A1.4)
it follows
c_{j}(x)\geq\frac{\pi}{d}c(x)\geq\frac{\pi}{d}\big(\log(\alpha^{-1})\big)^{-1}\log\big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\big)
almost surely. Further, by Lemma 12 of
Wager and Walther (2016)
(see also Lemma 2 of
Wager and Athey (2018)
), for large enough
s
and with probability at least
1-s^{-3}
we have
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\nu^{-1}\text{diam}\,L_{j}(x)}{(1-\alpha)^{(1-\epsilon)c_{j}(x)}}\leq 1
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-s^{-3}
and for large enough
s
,

\text{diam}\,L_{j}(x)\leq\nu(1-\alpha)^{(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log(\frac{s}{2k-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}=\nu\Big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}.

As this upper bound is independent of
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
j\in[d]
, the result follows;

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\text{diam}\,L(x)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{j\in[d]}\text{diam}\,L_{j}(x)\leq\nu\Big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}.

∎

Lemma 9
.

Let the weights
\big(\omega_{ij}(x)\big)_{i\in[I],j\in[n_{i}]}
be as defined in (
10
) in the proof of Lemma
7
for a clustered decision tree satisfying property
A2
. Then there exists a universal constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|\leq C_{1}.

Proof.

Fix some
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
. We first show that
{\bf 1}^{\top}\omega(x)=1
. This holds as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{N}^{\top}\omega(x)

\displaystyle=({\bf 1}_{N}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{M}^{\top}(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}

\displaystyle={\bf 1}_{M}^{\top}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}=1,

where in the penultimate line we use that
{\bf 1}_{N}=\chi{\bf 1}_{M}
, and where
M
is the number of leaves that make up the tree defining
\omega_{ij}
. Now define
(d_{ij}(x))_{i\in[I],j\in[n_{i}]}
by
d_{ij}(x)=\frac{1-\mathrm{sgn}(\omega_{ij}(x))}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(\omega_{ij}(x)\neq 0)}
. Note therefore that
d_{ij}(x)\in\{0,1\}
with
d_{ij}(x)=0
if
i\notin\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)\mathrm{sgn}(\omega_{ij}(x))\mathbbm{1}_{(\omega_{ij}(x)\neq 0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)\big(\mathrm{sgn}(\omega_{ij}(x))-1\big)\mathbbm{1}_{(\omega_{ij}(x)\neq 0)}

\displaystyle=1-2\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)d_{ij}(x)

\displaystyle=1+2\,\bigg|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)d_{ij}(x)\bigg|.

Now define
\bar{\chi}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{i}\times M}
for each
i\in[I]
and
\bar{\chi}
by

\big(\bar{\chi}_{i}\big)_{j,m}=\mathbbm{1}_{(X_{ij}\in L_{m})}\mathbbm{1}_{(\omega_{ij}(x)<0)}\mathbbm{1}_{(i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\chi}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\chi}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bar{\chi}_{I}\end{pmatrix}.

Then by construction we have that
\text{vec}(d(x))=\bar{\chi}{\bf{1}}_{M}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)d_{ij}(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\big|\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\bar{\chi}){\bf 1}_{M}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|{\bf 1}_{M}\|_{\infty}}_{=1}\big\|(\bar{\chi}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|(\bar{\chi}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\big\|_{1}\underbrace{\|\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\|_{1}}_{=1}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\bar{\chi}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\big\|_{1}\big\|(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\big\|_{1}.

Now

\big\|\bar{\chi}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\big\|_{1}\leq\underbrace{\|\bar{\chi}\|_{1}}_{\in\{0,1\}}\|\hat{W}\|_{1}\underbrace{\|\chi\|_{\infty}}_{\leq 2k-1}\leq(2k-1)\|\hat{W}\|_{1},

following by Hölder’s inequality and Assumption
(A1.3)
, and where for a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write the norm
\|v\|_{p}:=(\sum_{i}|v_{i}|^{p})^{1/p}
and for a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
write the operator norm
\|M\|_{p}:=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|v\|_{p}=1}\|Mv\|_{p}
. Now denote the entries of the block diagonal matrix
\hat{W}
by
\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}
, so that

\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}=(W_{i}(\hat{\rho}))_{jj^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{(i=i^{\prime})}

(15)

Then we also have

\displaystyle\big\|(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\big\|(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\big\|_{\infty}

(16)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\min_{m\in[M]}\Big(\big|\mathrm{e}_{m}^{\top}\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\mathrm{e}_{m}\big|-\Big|\sum_{m^{\prime}\neq m}e_{m^{\prime}}^{\top}\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\mathrm{e}_{m}\Big|\Big)\Big)^{-1}

(17)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\min_{m\in[M]}\Big(\Big|\sum_{(i,j)}\mathbbm{1}_{(X_{ij}\in L_{m})}\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i,j)}\Big|-\Big|\sum_{(i,j):X_{ij}\in L_{m}}\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)}\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\Big|\Big)\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\min_{m\in[M]}\Big(\sum_{(i,j):X_{ij}\in L_{m}}\Big\{\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i,j)}-\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j)}\big|\hat{W}_{(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\big|\Big\}\Big)\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k\,\tilde{\nu}(\hat{W})}\leq k^{-1}c_{W}^{-1},

(18)

where each step in the above is justified as follows: (
16
) follows by symmetry of
(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}
; (
17
) follows by the Ahlberg–Nilson–Varah bound for diagonally dominant matrices, which can be applied as

\tilde{\nu}(\hat{W}):=\min_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{eval}},j\in[n_{i}]}\Big((W_{i}(\hat{\rho}))_{jj^{\prime}}-\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\big|(W_{i}(\hat{\rho}))_{jj^{\prime}}\big|\Big)\geq c_{W},

by Assumption
A2
(see also Lemma
11
); and (
18
) follows by Assumption
(A1.3)
.
Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|=1+2\|\omega(x)^{\top}\bar{\chi}{\bf 1}_{M}\|\leq 1+2\|\bar{\chi}^{\top}\hat{W}\chi\|_{1}\|(\chi^{\top}\hat{W}\chi)^{-1}\|_{1}\\
\leq 1+\frac{2(2k-1)}{k}\cdot\frac{\|\hat{W}\|_{1}}{\zeta\hat{W})}\leq 1+\frac{2(2k-1)}{k}\cdot\frac{C_{W}}{c_{W}}\leq\frac{5C_{W}}{c_{W}}=:C_{1},

completing the proof. Note in particular that
C_{1}
by construction is a universal constant i.e. carries no
x\in\mathcal{X}
nor
P\in\mathcal{P}
dependence.

∎

Remark 10
.

Note that Assumption
A2
on the weight structure is satisfied for inverse correlation structures that take the form of equicorrelated structures with finite group size, and inverse
\text{AR}(1)
processes of potentially diverging group sizes (see Lemma
11
).
Note however the assumption
A2
may be relaxed in a number of cases. In particular in the case
d=1
and
k\sim 1

the condition on weights
W
can be weakened to the
\ell_{2}
condition number of the weight matrix
\kappa_{2}(W):=\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{\Lambda_{\min}(W)}
being bounded. This relaxation follows by observing

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}|\omega_{ij}(x)|\,\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\omega_{ij}(x)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\omega\|_{2}^{2}\bigg(\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{k\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\bigg(\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{k\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}\bigg(\sum_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\text{diam}\,L(X_{ij})\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{k\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{(i,j):X_{ij}\in L_{m}}\text{diam}\,L_{m}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2k-1}\,\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{k\,\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\text{diam}\,L_{m}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2k-1}\,\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{k\,\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\left(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}},

which makes use of the inequality

\|\omega(x)\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}(\chi^{\top}W\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}W^{2}\chi)(\chi^{\top}W\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\leq\Lambda_{\max}(W)\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}(\chi^{\top}W\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\\
\leq\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{\Lambda_{\min}(W)\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\chi^{\top})}\leq\frac{\Lambda_{\max}(W)}{\Lambda_{\min}(W)}\cdot\frac{1}{k}.

(19)

Note however that whilst these weight structures still provide control over the bias, this is at a slower rate than in Lemma
7
by a factor of two and so the result of Theorem
1
only would be shown here at a cost of two in the bias convergence rate, and the results of Theorem
5
follow for this wider weight class for
\beta>1-\Big(1+\frac{d}{2\pi}\frac{\log(\alpha^{-1})}{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}\Big)^{-1}
.

C.3
Interpreting Assumption
A2
in the context of popular working correlation models

In the following we show that the general weight structure property of Assumption
A2
holds for equicorrelated and
\text{AR}(1)
working correlation structures.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose the clustered random forest weight structure
(W_{i}(\rho))_{\rho\in\Gamma}
take either of the forms:

(i)

Equicorrelated structure, with finite group size
i.e.
W_{i}(\rho)=\big(I_{n_{i}}+\rho{\bf 1}_{n_{i}}{\bf 1}_{n_{i}}^{\top}\big)^{-1}
with
\Gamma\subset[0,\infty)
bounded.

(ii)

Autoregressive
\mathrm{AR}(1)
structure, with potentially diverging group size
i.e.
W_{i}(\rho)=\big(\rho^{|j-k|}\big)^{-1}_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]\times[n_{i}]}
with
\Gamma\subset(-1+t,1-t)
for some
t>0
.

Then in either case there exists universal constants
c_{W},C_{W},C_{W}^{\prime}>0
such that

\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\|W_{i}(\rho)\|_{1}\leq C_{W},\qquad\inf_{\rho\in\Gamma}\zeta(W_{i}(\rho))\geq c_{W},\quad\;\text{and}\quad\;\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg)\leq C_{W}^{\prime}

Recall the function
\nu
is as defined in Assumption
A2
.

Proof.

As
W=\textrm{diag}(W_{i}(\rho))
is block diagonal, with blocks
W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}=W_{i}(\rho)
for each
i\in[I]
(recall the notation as in (
15
)), it follows that

\frac{\|W\|_{1}}{\zeta(W)}\leq\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}\|W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\|_{1}}{\min_{i\in[I]}\nu\big(W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\big)}=\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}\|W_{i}(\rho)\|_{1}}{\min_{i\in[I]}\nu\big(W_{i}(\rho)\big)}.

We consider each case in turn.

Case (i): Equicorrelated, finite group size:

For the equicorrelated structure, whose correlation matrix takes the form
\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{j=k\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{j\neq k\}}\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}\big)_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}
, the resulting weights (proportional to the inverse of the correlation matrix) takes the form

W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{j=k\}}-\frac{\rho}{1+\rho n_{i}}\right)_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\|W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{|1+(n_{i}-1)\rho|+(n_{i}-1)|\rho|}{|1+n_{i}\rho|}=\frac{1+2(n_{i}-1)\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho>0\}}}{|1+n_{i}\rho|},

\displaystyle\nu\big(W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{|1+(n_{i}-1)\rho|-(n_{i}-1)|\rho|}{|1+n_{i}\rho|}=\frac{1+2(n_{i}-1)\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho<0\}}}{|1+n_{i}\rho|},

noting that for
W
to be positive definite it is necessary that
1+n_{i}\rho>0
, and so
1+(n_{i}-1)\rho>0
.
Then

\frac{\|W\|_{1}}{\zeta(W)}\leq\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}\|W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\|_{1}}{\min_{i\in[I]}\nu\big(W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\big)}\leq\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}|1+n_{i}\rho|}{\min_{i\in[I]}|1+n_{i}\rho|}\cdot\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}\big(1+2(n_{i}-1)\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho>0\}}\big)}{\min_{i\in[I]}\big(1+2(n_{i}-1)\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{\rho<0\}}\big)},

which is finite whenever
\rho>0
and
n_{i}
is finite.
Further,

\bigg\{\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}=\frac{n_{i}}{(1+\rho n_{i})^{4}}{\bf 1}_{n_{i}}{\bf 1}_{n_{i}}^{\top},

and so

\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{\partial W}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}\bigg)\leq\max_{i\in[I]}\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}\bigg)=\frac{n_{i}^{2}}{(1+\rho n_{i})^{4}},

which is finite.

Case (ii): Autoregressive
\text{AR}(1)
, potentially diverging group size:

For the autocorrelated structure whose correlation matrix takes the form
(\rho^{|j-k|})_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}
, the resulting weights (proportional to the inverse of the correlation matrix) takes the form

(W_{(i,j),(i,k)})_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}=\begin{cases}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{j=k\}}+\rho^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{1<j=k<n_{i}\}}-\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{|j-k|=1\}}\right)_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}&\text{if }n_{i}>1\\
(1-\rho^{2})&\text{if }n_{i}=1\end{cases},

and so

\|W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\|_{1}=\begin{cases}\big(1+|\rho|\big)^{2}&\text{if }n_{i}>2\\
1+|\rho|&\text{if }n_{i}=2\\
1-\rho^{2}&\text{if }n_{i}=1\end{cases},\qquad\nu\big(W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\big)=\begin{cases}\big(1-|\rho|\big)^{2}&\text{if }n_{i}>2\\
1-|\rho|&\text{if }n_{i}=2\\
1-\rho^{2}&\text{if }n_{i}=1\end{cases},

and thus

\frac{\|W\|_{1}}{\zeta(W)}\leq\frac{\max_{i\in[I]}\|W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\|_{1}}{\min_{i\in[I]}\nu\big(W_{(i,\cdot),(i,\cdot)}\big)}\leq\frac{\max\big((1+|\rho|)^{2},\,1+|\rho|,\,1-\rho^{2}\big)}{\min\big((1-|\rho|)^{2},\,1-|\rho|,\,1-\rho^{2}\big)}\leq\bigg(\frac{1+|\rho|}{1-|\rho|}\bigg)^{2},

in all cases. Note this result holds irrespective of cluster sizes
n_{i}
, and thus clusters can be of diverging size. Further

\bigg(\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg)_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}=\begin{cases}\big(2\rho\mathbbm{1}_{\{1<j=k<n_{i}\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{|j-k|=1\}}\big)_{(j,k)\in[n_{i}]^{2}}&\quad\text{if $n_{i}>1$}\\
(-2\rho)&\quad\text{if $n_{i}=1$}\end{cases},

and so, by the Gershgorin circle theorem

\bigg|\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg)\bigg|\vee\bigg|\Lambda_{\min}\bigg(\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg)\bigg|\leq 1+2|\rho|\leq 3,

and thus

\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{\partial W}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}\bigg)\leq\max_{i\in[I]}\Lambda_{\max}\bigg(\bigg\{\frac{\partial W_{i}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\bigg\}^{2}\bigg)\leq 9,

which again is finite.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

Recall the bias–variance decomposition

\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big(\hat{\mu}(x)-\mu(x)\big)^{2}\Big]=\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]-\mu(x)\big\}^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(\hat{\mu}(x)\big).

Let
\Omega_{I}
be the event of Lemma
7
for which on
\Omega_{I}
we have for sufficiently large
I

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]-\mu(x)\big\}^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{bias}}^{2}\biggl(\frac{n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}}{k}\biggr)I^{-2(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\frac{\pi}{d}\beta},

and
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(\Omega_{I}^{c})\leq s_{N}^{-3}
for sufficiently large
I
. It thus remains to prove
the relevant upper bound on the variance quantity, which follows by standard results from
Efron and Stein (1981)
; we include an overview here for completeness. Fix a
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Then each individual decision tree prediction
T(x)
making up
\hat{\mu}(x)
admits the Efron–Stein ANOVA decomposition
(
Efron, 2014
)

T(x)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]+\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}}),

for some symmetric functions
(T^{(k)})_{k=1,\ldots,s}
such that
\big(T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\big)_{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,s\}}
are all mean-zero, uncorrelated functions.
Note in particular that

T^{(1)}(Z_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)].

(20)

Similarly the Efron–Stein ANOVA decomposition for the aggregated random forest predictor
\hat{\mu}(x)
is

\hat{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]+\binom{I}{s_{I}}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\binom{I-k}{s_{I}-k}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}}).

Note in particular, such a decomposition holds even if the tree
T
is not symmetric and
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{I})
are independent but not identically distributed (see
Efron and Stein (1981, Comment 4)
). Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(\hat{\mu}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\binom{I}{s_{I}}^{-2}\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\binom{I-k}{s_{I}}^{2}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{I}T^{(1)}(Z_{i})\bigg)+\binom{I}{s_{I}}^{-2}\sum_{k=2}^{s_{I}}\binom{I-k}{s_{I}-k}^{2}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{I}T^{(1)}(Z_{i})\bigg)+\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\sum_{k=2}^{s_{I}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{I}T^{(1)}(Z_{i})\bigg)+\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{I}T^{(1)}(Z_{i})\bigg)+\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}T^{(k)}(Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\big)+\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(T(x)\big).

For identically distributed data, and by symmetry of the decision trees, we have that

\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\big)\leq\frac{s_{I}}{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(T(x)\big),

see for example
Dynkin and Mandelbaum (1983)
;
Vitale (1992)

Cevid et al. (2022, Lemma 9)
.
Therefore

\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(\hat{\mu}(x)\big)\leq\bigg(\frac{s_{I}}{I}+\frac{s_{I}^{2}}{I^{2}}\bigg)\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(T(x)\big)\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon^{\prime})s_{I}}{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big(T(x)\big),

for some arbitrarily small
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
and for sufficiently large
I
.

We now claim that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\hat{\rho}}(x))}{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\rho^{*}}(x))}-1\Bigr|=o(1)
. For every
\rho\in\Gamma
, and writing

,

\displaystyle\quad\;\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\bigl|\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(T_{\rho}(x)\bigr)-\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(T_{\rho}(x)\,\big|\,X\bigr)\bigr]\bigr|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{\rho}(x)\,|\,X]\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl(\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{\rho}(x)-\mu(x)\,|\,X]\}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mu}^{2}d(1+C_{1})^{2}\Bigl(\frac{s_{N}}{2k-1}\Bigr)^{-2(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}.

Moreover

\displaystyle\quad\;\biggl|\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigl(T_{\rho}(x)\,\big|\,X\bigr)\bigr]\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}(\chi^{\top}W\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}W\Sigma W\chi)(\chi^{\top}W\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\bigr]\biggr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}BA^{-1}CA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}+\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}CA^{-1}BA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}DA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\bigr]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}(B+C)A^{-1}(B+C)A^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}+\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}BA^{-1}BA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}CA^{-1}CA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}+\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}^{\top}A^{-1}DA^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{J(x)}\bigr]

Thus by Lemmas

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\biggl|\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\hat{\rho}}(x))}{\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T_{\rho^{*}}(x))}-1\biggr|=o(1).

where the term involving

Finally for

(note that

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\Bigl[\bigl(\hat{\mu}_{I}^{\mathrm{MC}}(x)-\mu(x)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr]dQ(x)\\

where

and

First define

\sigma_{P,I}^{2}(x;\rho^{*}):=\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big]\\
=\mathrm{Var}_{P}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}_{\rho^{*}}(x)]\big\}\bigg]=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\Big],

and consider the decomposition

\frac{\hat{\mu}(x)-\mu(x)}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}=\underbrace{\frac{\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big]}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}}_{=:A_{P}}+\underbrace{\frac{\hat{\mu}(x)-\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}(x)}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}}_{=:B_{P}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]-\mu(x)}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}}_{=:C_{P}}\\
+\underbrace{\frac{\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}(x)\big]-\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)+\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big]}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}}_{=:D_{P}},

noting that
\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}(x)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]

In brief, Term
A_{P}
is a sum of independent, mean zero terms which is unit variance, and so under Lyapunov-type conditions can be shown to be asymptotically standard Gaussian. The additional terms will all be shown to be
o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)
. Term
B_{P}
relates to the projection of the random forest predictor onto the orthogonal complement of the image of the Hájek projection. Term
C_{P}
is a (scaled) bias term, already shown to be sufficiently small (see Lemma
7
); for the range of
\beta
as given in Assumption
A1
the Term
C_{P}
is controlled to be
o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)
. Finally, Term
D_{P}
would be precisely zero were one to set
\hat{\rho}=\rho^{*}
a deterministic parameter. When estimated as in Algorithm
2
, Term
D_{P}
is mean zero, with variance of order
(\hat{\rho}-\rho^{*})^{2}

\hat{\rho}

\rho^{*}

Part 1:
Showing

\lim_{I\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}_{P}(A_{P}\leq t)-\Phi(t)|=0

Recall that

\displaystyle\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}(x)-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\hat{\mu}(x)]\big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{I}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]\big\}.

We proceed to show the Lyapunov-style condition

\lim_{I\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]\big|^{2+\delta}\Big]}{\big(\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\big]\big)^{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}}=0.

Decompose

\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,X_{i}]}_{=:A^{\textup{I}}_{i}(x)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,X_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]}_{=:A^{\textup{II}}_{i}(x)}.

A_{i}^{\textup{I}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\\

(30)

using the honesty property
(A1.1)

\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\big\}\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\big\}\big]=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{i}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]^{\top}\sigma_{P,I}(x_{i})\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{i}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big\|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{i}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

Also

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[|A_{i}^{\textup{I}}(x)|^{2+\delta}\big]

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[|A^{\textup{II}}_{i}(x)|^{2+\delta}\big]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[T(x)]\big|^{2+\delta}\Big]

A single decision tree

Also for each tree, and for arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
, by Lemma
8
for sufficiently large

\displaystyle\big|\bar{T}(x)-\bar{T}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\big(\bar{T}(x)-\mu(x)\big)-\big(\bar{T}_{\rho^{*}}(x)-\mu(x)\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{\mu}d^{1/2}(1+C_{1})\Big(\frac{s}{2k-1}\Big)^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}}

and so, as
\bar{\mu}
is an average of trees
\bar{T}

|\bar{\mu}(x)-\bar{\mu}_{\rho^{*}}(x)|=O_{\mathcal{P}}\Big(I^{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}\beta}\Big).

Also recalling (again by Lemma
13
)
\sigma_{P,I}^{2}(x;\rho^{*})\gtrsim I^{\beta-1-\epsilon^{\prime}}
for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0

\frac{|\bar{\mu}(x)-\bar{\mu}_{\rho^{*}}(x)|}{\sigma_{P,I}(x;\rho^{*})}=O_{\mathcal{P}}\bigg(I^{\frac{1}{2}\big({1-\beta+\epsilon^{\prime}-2(1-\epsilon)\frac{\log((1-\alpha)^{-1})}{\log(\alpha^{-1})}\cdot\frac{\pi}{d}\beta}\big)}\bigg)=o_{\mathcal{P}}(1),

for any

(i.e. obtained by also taking

sufficiently small). Therefore it follows by Markov’s inequality that

.

Similarly, for the term

Lemma 13
.

\sigma_{P,I}^{2}(x;\rho^{*})\gtrsim\frac{s_{I}}{I(\log(n_{\mathrm{c}}\vee I))^{d}}.

Proof.

First, note that as

\sigma_{P,I}^{2}(x;\rho^{*})=\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{\hat{\mu}}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big]=\frac{s}{I}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{T}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big],

it suffices to show that

\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\accentset{\circ}{T}_{\rho^{*}}(x)\big]\gtrsim(\log I\vee n_{\mathrm{c}})^{-d}.

To prove this claim, first note that by Lemma
17
,

\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{\rho^{*}}(x)\,|\,Z_{i}]\big]\gtrsim\mathbb{E}_{P}\Big[\big\|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{i}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\gtrsim s_{I}^{-1}k(\log(n_{\mathrm{c}}\vee I))^{-d}.

Then it follows that

\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[T_{\rho^{*}}(x;Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{s}})\big]\gtrsim\sum_{k=1}^{s_{I}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[T_{\rho^{*}}(x)\,|\,Z_{i_{k}}]\big]\gtrsim k(\log(n_{\mathrm{c}}\vee I))^{-d},

completing the proof.

satisfies

Proof.

We begin by showing that

7
, and define the block diagonal matrix
\Sigma:=\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\mathcal{Y}\,|\,X)

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{P}\,T(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\mathrm{Var}_{P}(T(x)\,|\,X)\big]

Lemma 16
.

Suppose
A
is a non-negative random variable. Then for any
c>0

\mathbb{E}\big[A^{2}\big]\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[A\big]^{2}-\mathbb{E}\big[A\mathbbm{1}_{(A<c)}\big]^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)}.

Proof.

Note that

\mathbb{E}\big[A^{2}\big]\geq\mathbb{E}\big[A^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{(A\geq c)}\big]=\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)\mathbb{E}\big[A^{2}\,|\,A\geq c\big]\\
\geq\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)\mathbb{E}[A\,|\,A>c]^{2}=\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)\bigg(\frac{\mathbb{E}[A\mathbbm{1}_{(A\geq c)}]}{\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[A\mathbbm{1}_{(A\geq c)}]^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)}\\
=\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[A]-\mathbb{E}[A\mathbbm{1}_{(A<c)}]\big)^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[A]^{2}-\mathbb{E}[A\mathbbm{1}_{(A<c)}]^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(A\geq c)},

where the second inequality follows by Jensen’s inequality, and the final inequality follows as
(a-b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}-b^{2}
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle=\bigg(n_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{ij}(x)\,|\,X_{ij}]+n_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\mathbbm{1}_{L(X_{i1})}(X_{i^{\prime}1})\omega_{i^{\prime}1}(x)\,\bigg|\,X_{i1}\bigg]\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2n_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{ij}(x)\,|\,X_{ij}]^{2}+\frac{4n_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}(k/n_{\mathrm{c}})^{2}C_{1}^{2}}{(s_{I}-n_{\mathrm{c}})^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2n_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{ij}(x)\,|\,X_{ij}]^{2}+\frac{4k^{2}C_{1}^{2}}{s_{I}^{2}},

and so

\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{ij}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]^{2}\big]\gtrsim\frac{1}{2n_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\mathbb{P}_{P}(X_{i1}\in L(x)\,|\,X_{i1})^{2}\big]-\frac{2k^{2}C_{1}^{2}}{s_{N}^{2}},

and so for finite
n_{\mathrm{c}}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\omega_{i}(x)\,|\,X_{i}]\|_{2}^{2}\big]\gtrsim n_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}k^{-1}Is_{I}^{-1}(\log s_{I})^{-d}\sim n_{\mathrm{c}}^{-1}k^{-1}I^{1-\beta}(\log I)^{-d},

and the result follows in case (ii) by the same arguments.

∎

Lemma 18
.

Suppose
(A_{P,I}(x):P\in\mathcal{P},x\in\mathcal{X},I\in\mathbb{N})
and
(B_{P,I}(x):P\in\mathcal{P},x\in\mathcal{X},I\in\mathbb{N})
are sequences of non-negative random variables satisfying
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathrm{Var}_{P}(A_{P,I}(x)\,|\,B_{P,I}(x))-\omega(x)|=o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)
for some bounded
\omega(\cdot)
, and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}(A_{P,I}(x)\,|\,B_{P,I}(x))\leq C
almost surely for some constant
C
. Then
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathrm{Var}_{P}(A_{P,I}(x)\,|\,B_{P,I}(x))]-\omega(x)|=o(1)
. Moreover, if also
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\mathbb{E}_{P}[A_{P,I}(x)\,|\,B_{P,I}(x)])=o(1)
then

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathrm{Var}_{P}(A_{P,I}(x))-\omega(x)|=o(1).

Proof.

It suffices to show that for any sequence of random variables
(X_{P,I}(x):P\in\mathcal{P},x\in\mathcal{X},I\in\mathbb{N})
if
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|X_{P,I}(x)|=o_{\mathcal{P}}(1)
and
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|X_{P,I}(x)|\leq C^{\prime}
almost surely for some constant
C^{\prime}
, then
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[|X_{P,I}|]=o(1)
. For arbitrary
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
I\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[|X_{P,I}(x)|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[|X_{P,I}(x)|\mathbbm{1}_{(|X_{P,I}(x)|>\epsilon)}\bigr]+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigl[X_{P,I}^{2}(x)\bigr]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}_{P}(|X_{P,I}(x)|>\epsilon)^{1/2}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\,\mathbb{P}_{P}(|X_{P,I}(x)|>\epsilon)^{1/2}+\epsilon.

Taking suprema over
P\in\mathcal{P}
, followed by limits as
I\to\infty
and then
\epsilon\downarrow 0
gives the result.
∎

Appendix E
Proof of Proposition
6

The algorithm proposed for Proposition
6
simply performs conjugate gradient descent to evaluate the quantity
(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)Y)
in case
(i)
and in case
(ii)
calculates
(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)^{-1}\mathrm{e}_{m}
for all
m\in[M]
satisfying
L_{m}\cap\,\text{supp}\,Q\neq\emptyset
. The conjugate gradient descent method is known to be fast when calculating
A^{-1}b
where
A
is a sparse matrix. In our case
\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi
is in general a dense matrix, although for certain weight classes we can decompose
\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi
in such a way the conjugate gradient descent iterations can be perform in a fast manner.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

The number of conjugate gradient descent iterations required for an
\epsilon>0
accurate solution in the Euclidean norm is
O\Bigl(\sqrt{\kappa(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)}\log(\sqrt{\kappa(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)}/\epsilon)\Bigr)
, where
\kappa
denotes the condition number. Because
\kappa\bigl(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi\bigr)\leq\frac{C_{W}(2k-1)}{c_{W}k}\leq 2c_{W}^{-1}C_{W}
the number of conjugate gradient iterations required is
O\bigl(\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr)
.

Equicorrelated working correlation weights:

For the equicorrelated weight structure

W_{i}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl(\mathrm{I}_{n_{i}}-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{i}}^{\top}\biggr),

where

\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl(\chi^{\top}\chi-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\chi_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{i}}^{\top}\chi_{i}\biggr),

Define
v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
via
v_{m}:=|\{(i,j):i\in[I],j\in[n_{i}],X_{ij}\in L_{m}\}|
for
m\in[M]
, and for each
i\in[I]
define
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
via
(u_{i})_{m}:=|\{j\in[n_{i}]:X_{ij}\in L_{m}\}|
for
m\in[M]
. Note therefore
\chi^{\top}\chi=\textrm{diag}(v)
and
k_{I}\leq v_{m}\leq 2k_{I}-1
for each
m\in[M]
, and also that
\|u_{i}\|_{0}\leq n_{i}\leq 2k_{I}-1
is sparse.
Then for any
b\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
the number of iterations it takes to calculate

\displaystyle(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl(\chi^{\top}\chi-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{eval}}}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}\biggr)b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl(\textrm{diag}(v_{m}b_{m}:m\in[M])-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{eval}}}(u_{i}^{\top}b)u_{i}\biggr),

is
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
because calculating
\textrm{diag}(v_{m}b_{m}:m\in[M])
takes
O(M)
operations, calculating
u_{i}^{\top}b
takes
O(n_{i})
operations as
\|u_{i}\|_{0}\leq n_{i}
, and so calculating
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{eval}}}(u_{i}^{\top}b)u_{i}
takes
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
operations. Together therefore calculating
(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)b
takes
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
operations. Thus calculating
(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)^{-1}z
for some vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
up to order
\epsilon>0
accuracy in Euclidean norm is

O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))

For fitted values, calculating

\displaystyle\chi^{\top}W(\rho)Y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl(\chi^{\top}Y-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{eval}}}u_{i}(u_{i}^{\top}Y)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{(i,j):X_{ij}\in L_{m}}Y_{ij}:m\in[M]\biggr)-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\biggl\{\sum_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}):u_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\neq 0}u_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}Y_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\biggr\}u_{i}\biggr],

which is an order
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{eval}}}\|u_{i}\|_{0})=O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
time calculation.
Therefore calculating
(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)\chi)^{-1}(\chi^{\top}W(\rho)Y)
via conjugate gradient descent, to
\epsilon
accuracy is
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I}\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
. Case (ii) follows by analogous arguments.

AR
\boldsymbol{(1)}
working correlation weights:

As each row of
\chi_{i}
has precisely one non-zero value, calculating
\chi_{i}b
requires
n_{i}
operations, with
\|\chi_{i}b\|_{0}=n_{i}
. Because the inverse of an
\text{AR}(1)
covariance matrix is tri-diagonal,
each non-zero entry of
\chi_{i}b
contributes to
W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}b
via at most 3 multiplications with entries of
W_{i}(\rho)
, thus
W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}b
can be computed in at most
3n_{i}
operations, and moreover
\|W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}b\|_{0}\leq 3n_{i}
.
As precisely one entry of each row of
\chi_{i}
is non-zero,
\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}b
can be calculated in
O(n_{i})
operations, and so
\sum_{i=1}^{I}\chi_{i}^{\top}W_{i}(\rho)\chi_{i}b
can be calculated in
O(n_{\mathrm{c}}s_{I})
operations. The results then follow by identical arguments to those used in the equicorrelated case.

∎
