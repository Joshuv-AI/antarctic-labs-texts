Are We Prepared for Agentic Dentistry? Exploring the Impact of Autonomous Artificial Intelligence in Dental Care
Authors: Mohammed Turky, Paul M.H. Dummer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autonomous artificial intelligence (AI), such as Medical Intelligence for Reasoning and Action and Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer, represents an evolution in healthcare AI, extending its capabilities beyond task-specific decision support towards longitudinal clinical reasoning and patient management. Although these systems have been developed in medicine, they raise important questions regarding the future of oral healthcare. This perspective proposes agentic dentistry as a conceptual framework for considering how autonomous AI could eventually influence dental practice. We discuss the potential opportunities of agentic AI to enhance clinical decision-making, care coordination, continuity of care, and personalised treatment, while critically examining the scientific, ethical, educational, regulatory, and professional challenges associated with its translation into dentistry. Finally, we propose a research and policy agenda to support the responsible development of autonomous AI in oral healthcare. By initiating this discussion before widespread clinical adoption, dentistry can proactively shape the future integration of agentic AI.

Medicine has entered the era of agentic AI – Should dentistry prepare?

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become one of the most transformative technologies in healthcare, with applications now extending across diagnosis, prognosis, treatment planning, and clinical workflow optimisation.1 In dentistry, AI has similarly gained considerable momentum, demonstrating promising performance in tasks such as radiographic interpretation, detection of dental caries, periodontal assessment, implant planning, orthodontic analysis, and prediction of treatment outcomes.2,3 These developments have established AI as a valuable clinical decision-support tool, assisting clinicians in performing well-defined tasks with increasing accuracy and efficiency.4,5 However, despite these advances, contemporary dental AI remains predominantly task-specific, with most systems designed to address individual clinical problems rather than coordinate patient care across the entire treatment journey.

Recent developments in medicine suggest that this paradigm may be evolving. Two autonomous AI systems, Medical Intelligence for Reasoning and Action and Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer, have demonstrated capabilities that extend beyond conventional diagnostic assistance towards longitudinal clinical reasoning and patient management.6,7 Unlike current AI applications that generate isolated predictions or classifications, these systems can gather information iteratively, formulate differential diagnoses, recommend investigations, develop management plans, monitor clinical progress, and adapt their recommendations as new information becomes available.6,7 Although both systems have thus far been evaluated only in simulated medical environments, they represent an important conceptual shift from task-oriented AI towards agentic AI.

The emergence of agentic AI in medicine raises an important question for the dental profession: could a similar transformation eventually occur in oral healthcare? At present, no autonomous agentic AI system has been developed or validated specifically for dentistry. Nevertheless, the rapid pace of AI innovation suggests that dentistry may benefit from considering the implications of this emerging paradigm before such technologies become clinically available. Anticipating future developments would allow researchers, educators, clinicians, professional organisations, and regulators to shape their implementation proactively rather than responding only after widespread adoption.

Accordingly, this perspective proposes the concept of agentic dentistry as a forward-looking framework for discussing how autonomous AI systems could eventually influence dental practice. Rather than suggesting that agentic dentistry is already a clinical reality, we use the term to describe a potential future model in which interconnected AI agents extend beyond isolated diagnostic tasks to support longitudinal clinical reasoning, treatment planning, care coordination, and patient follow-up. By examining the opportunities, challenges, and research priorities associated with this emerging concept, we aim to stimulate discussion regarding how the dental profession can prepare for the next generation of AI-enabled oral healthcare.

Towards agentic dentistry: A conceptual framework

The transition from task-specific AI to autonomous clinical agents represents more than a technological advancement; rather, it reflects a potential shift in how healthcare may be organised and delivered. While current dental AI systems generally perform isolated tasks, such as detecting evidence of disease on radiographs or estimating treatment outcomes, agentic AI introduces the possibility of systems capable of integrating information, reasoning across multiple clinical domains, and supporting patient management over time.6,7 Although such systems have not yet been developed or validated in dentistry, the experience from medicine provides an opportunity to consider how similar technologies might eventually influence oral healthcare.

In this context, we propose the term ‘agentic dentistry’ to describe a potential future paradigm in which autonomous AI systems extend beyond individual decision-support applications to assist with longitudinal clinical reasoning and care coordination. Importantly, agentic dentistry should not be interpreted as replacing the clinician. Rather, it represents a conceptual framework through which AI agents may augment clinical decision-making by integrating diverse sources of information, identifying knowledge gaps, recommending evidence-informed actions, and continuously updating recommendations as new clinical information becomes available.

One can envisage future agentic systems functioning as coordinated ecosystems of specialised AI agents rather than as a single monolithic model. Individual agents might focus on analysing conventional radiographs and cone-beam computed tomography images, interpreting electronic health records, assessing systemic and behavioural risk factors, retrieving the latest evidence-based recommendations, monitoring treatment progress, or facilitating communication between patients and clinicians. A supervisory clinical agent could then synthesise these individual outputs to generate transparent and context-specific recommendations while maintaining the dentist as the final decision-maker.

Such an approach could be particularly relevant for managing conditions that require complex decision-making over extended periods. For example, in endodontics, an autonomous system might integrate patient-reported symptoms, pulp sensibility tests, microbiological assays, histological findings, pulpal biomarkers, clinical photographs, radiographic findings, including CBCT data, medical history, and previous treatment records to generate a differential diagnosis and propose personalised management strategies. As additional information becomes available during treatment or follow-up, recommendations could be refined dynamically rather than remaining static.

Similarly, long-term management of chronic oral diseases, including periodontitis, temporomandibular disorders, potential oral malignant disorders, and dental trauma, may eventually benefit from these advanced AI systems. Such capabilities could improve continuity of care, an area that remains challenging because dental treatment frequently occurs across multiple appointments, providers, and healthcare settings.

Nevertheless, it is important to emphasise that these examples remain conceptual rather than predictive. Considerable scientific, technical, regulatory, and ethical challenges must be addressed before agentic AI can be responsibly translated into routine dental practice. Rather than advocating immediate implementation, we propose agentic dentistry as a framework for anticipating future developments and guiding research, policy, and professional discussion before these technologies become clinically available.

Potential opportunities for oral healthcare

Although agentic AI remains a conceptual proposition within dentistry, reflecting on its potential applications may help identify areas where future research and innovation could be directed. If autonomous AI systems eventually become feasible for oral healthcare, their greatest contribution may lie not in replacing existing diagnostic tools but in supporting the continuity, coordination, and personalisation of patient care.

One potential advantage of agentic AI is its ability to integrate heterogeneous sources of clinical information that are traditionally reviewed separately. Contemporary dental care often requires clinicians to synthesise medical histories, medication records, clinical examination findings, radiographic and three-dimensional imaging, laboratory investigations, patient-reported symptoms, and evolving scientific evidence before arriving at an appropriate management strategy. Future autonomous AI agents may assist clinicians by continuously integrating these diverse data streams, identifying inconsistencies or missing information, and generating transparent recommendations that evolve as additional patient-centred, clinical, or laboratory information becomes available.

Agentic AI may also contribute to more personalised oral healthcare. Rather than relying solely on disease-centred treatment protocols, future systems could incorporate individual patient characteristics, including systemic health, behavioural risk factors, previous treatment experiences, socioeconomic circumstances, and personal preferences when supporting clinical decision-making. Such an approach could facilitate treatment strategies that are not only evidence-informed but also more closely aligned with the principles of patient-centred care.

Another promising application lies in enhancing continuity of care for various oral diseases, such as periodontitis, temporomandibular disorders, oral malignancies, and traumatic dental injuries, all of which necessitate extensive monitoring and ongoing clinical evaluation. Future agentic systems could play a crucial role by systematically tracking disease progression, detecting deviations from anticipated healing pathways, generating automated reminders for follow-up appointments, and facilitating timely referrals for specialist interventions when required. These advanced functionalities have the potential to mitigate fragmented care pathways and improve overall patient management over the long term.

Beyond individual patient care, agentic AI could potentially strengthen population oral health. By analysing large volumes of anonymised clinical data, future systems may assist in identifying high-risk individuals and population groups, supporting preventive strategies, monitoring quality of care, and informing resource allocation within public oral health programmes. These capabilities could be particularly valuable in regions where access to specialist dental services remains limited and where efficient allocation of healthcare resources is essential.

Importantly, the greatest value of agentic AI may not arise from autonomous decision-making alone but from effective collaboration between clinicians and intelligent systems.8 Similar to the concept of a ‘copilot’ increasingly adopted in other healthcare settings,9,10 future dental AI agents could function as cognitive partners that enhance diagnostic reasoning, support evidence-based decision-making, reduce administrative burden, and enable clinicians to devote greater attention to communication, empathy, and patient-centred care. In this model, AI would augment, rather than replace, the professional judgement and clinical expertise of dental practitioners.

Challenges and considerations for responsible translation

While the emergence of agentic AI presents exciting possibilities, its translation into dentistry is likely to be considerably more challenging than simply adapting existing medical systems. The encouraging performance of Medical Intelligence for Reasoning and Action and Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer was demonstrated under carefully controlled experimental conditions using structured clinical scenarios.6,7 In contrast, routine dental practice is characterised by incomplete information, diagnostic uncertainty, heterogeneous data sources, and highly individualised treatment decisions made by clinicians with a wide range of knowledge, experience and competence. Consequently, the successful implementation of agentic AI in dentistry will require substantially more than technological advancement alone.

Unlike many current medical applications that rely primarily on text-based clinical information, dental decision-making is inherently multimodal.11 Clinicians routinely integrate findings from clinical examination, intraoral photographs, periapical and panoramic radiographs, cone-beam computed tomography, intraoral scans, periodontal charting, occlusal analysis, and patient-reported symptoms. These diverse data sources must then be interpreted alongside medical history, behavioural risk factors, patient expectations, financial considerations, and aesthetic preferences. Developing autonomous systems capable of synthesising such heterogeneous information while maintaining clinical reliability represents a considerable scientific challenge.

Equally important are the human dimensions of dental care. Successful treatment frequently depends not only on selecting the most appropriate clinical intervention but also on effective communication, shared decision-making, management of dental anxiety, and understanding patients’ personal values and expectations. Moreover, clinicians’ knowledge, training, and competence, alongside available facilities, could impact treatment outcomes. These aspects of care are difficult to capture within computational models and highlight the continuing importance of human empathy, professional judgement, and the therapeutic relationship between clinician and patient.

The increasing sophistication of autonomous AI systems also raises important questions regarding professional accountability. If future agentic systems recommend diagnostic or therapeutic pathways that subsequently prove inappropriate, determining responsibility may become increasingly complex. Issues relating to legal liability, regulatory approval, informed consent, data privacy, cybersecurity, and algorithmic transparency remain largely unresolved and will require careful consideration before clinical implementation.

Another important consideration is automation bias, whereby clinicians may place excessive confidence in AI-generated recommendations despite contradictory clinical evidence. Preserving critical thinking and independent clinical judgement will therefore remain essential. Agentic AI should be viewed as a decision-support partner rather than an autonomous decision-maker, with responsibility for patient care remaining firmly under the supervision of appropriately trained dental professionals.

Ultimately, the successful translation of agentic AI into dentistry will depend not only on technological capability but also on the establishment of robust scientific evidence, appropriate regulatory oversight, ethical governance, and continued public trust.

Shaping the future: A research and policy agenda

Although agentic AI has not yet entered routine dental practice, its emergence in medicine provides a timely opportunity for the dental profession to prepare proactively rather than reactively. Anticipating future developments before widespread implementation may enable researchers, educators, professional organisations, industry, and regulators to shape the responsible evolution of autonomous AI within oral healthcare.

From a scientific perspective, future research should move beyond evaluating diagnostic accuracy alone. Studies should investigate whether agentic AI can improve clinically meaningful outcomes, including treatment quality, patient safety, continuity of care, oral health-related quality of life, healthcare efficiency, and equitable access to dental services. Prospective clinical studies conducted in real-world environments will ultimately be required to determine whether agentic systems provide measurable benefits beyond those achieved through conventional clinical decision-support tools.

The development of trustworthy agentic AI will also require advances in explainability, transparency, robustness, and validation. Clinicians should be able to understand the reasoning underpinning AI-generated recommendations, recognise situations in which uncertainty exists, and identify circumstances requiring additional human judgement.12,13 Establishing internationally accepted standards for validation and reporting will be essential before autonomous systems can be safely integrated into routine dental care.

Educational frameworks should evolve in parallel with technological innovation. Future dental curricula will need to move beyond teaching students how to use AI tools towards developing competencies in supervising, critically evaluating, and appropriately integrating AI into clinical decision-making. Maintaining diagnostic reasoning, ethical judgement, and patient communication skills will remain fundamental irrespective of future technological advances.

Professional organisations and regulatory authorities will likewise have an important role in developing governance frameworks that address accountability, transparency, data governance, cybersecurity, and patient safety. International collaboration between researchers, clinicians, educators, regulators, and industry will be essential to ensure that innovation proceeds responsibly and consistently across healthcare systems.

At present, agentic dentistry remains a conceptual vision rather than an established clinical reality. Nevertheless, the rapid evolution of autonomous AI in medicine suggests that similar technologies may eventually have a major impact on oral healthcare. The question is therefore not simply whether agentic AI will become relevant to dentistry, but whether the profession will be adequately prepared to guide its safe, ethical, and evidence-based implementation. By initiating this discussion now, dentistry has a unique opportunity to shape the future of autonomous AI before that future arrives.

Conclusion

The emergence of agentic AI represents a significant conceptual shift in healthcare, moving beyond task-specific algorithms towards autonomous systems capable of longitudinal clinical reasoning and patient management. Although these technologies have thus far been developed and evaluated only within medicine, they offer an opportunity for dentistry to anticipate rather than react to the next generation of AI innovation.

Throughout this perspective, we have proposed agentic dentistry as a conceptual framework for exploring how autonomous AI could eventually support oral healthcare. Importantly, this concept should not be interpreted as evidence that agentic AI has already arrived in dentistry. Rather, it serves as a call for the profession to begin considering the scientific, ethical, educational, regulatory, and clinical foundations that would be required should such systems become feasible.

At present, the answer to the question posed in the title is probably no. Dentistry is not yet fully prepared for the responsible integration of autonomous AI systems because important questions regarding validation, governance, accountability, transparency, professional education, and patient trust remain unresolved. However, this should not be viewed as a limitation but as an opportunity. By engaging with these issues now, the dental profession can help shape the future development of agentic AI in a manner that is safe, evidence-based, ethically sound, and centred on patients’ needs. Ultimately, the future of agentic dentistry will depend not on the sophistication of AI alone, but on how wisely the profession chooses to guide its evolution.
