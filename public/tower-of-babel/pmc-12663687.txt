Integrative Diagnostic Model Combining Urinary Biomarkers and Clinical Parameters to Improve Diagnostic Performance in Interstitial Cystitis/Bladder Pain Syndrome
Authors: Yu-Chen Chen, Jing-Hui Tian, Hann-Chorng Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageAn integrative model combining urinary biomarkers and validated pain scores diagnoses interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome accurately. This noninvasive tool assists clinicians in diagnosing this challenging condition and reduces delays in initiating appropriate treatment.Keywords: Interstitial cystitis, Bladder pain syndrome, Urinary biomarkers, Functional bladder capacity, Bladder pain severity, Diagnosis

Introduction

Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic, debilitating condition characterized by pelvic pain, bladder discomfort, and urinary symptoms in the absence of other identifiable causes [1]. Despite significant advancements in understanding IC/BPS, accurate diagnosis remains challenging due to its multifactorial nature and symptom overlap with other lower urinary tract disorders [2,3]. Several validated questionnaires have been developed to improve diagnostic standardization. For example, the O’Leary-Sant Interstitial Cystitis Symptom and Problem Index (ICSI/ICPI) is a comprehensive tool, with a score of ≥12 yielding sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 65% [4]. However, it lacks precision and remains dependent on subjective patient reporting. The visual analog scale (VAS) for bladder pain, while simpler and more practical (VAS score ≥7: diagnostic accuracy = 0.94, positive predictive value [PPV] = 0.82, negative predictive value [NPV] = 0.97), is also limited by individual variability in pain perception and becomes less reliable in patients with scores below 7 [5]. As a result, although these tools offer partial improvements in diagnostic consistency, their reliance on subjective symptomatology limits overall accuracy and contributes to the typical diagnostic delay. The current diagnostic approach continues to depend largely on patient-reported symptoms, cystoscopic findings, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses—factors that often delay treatment initiation and impact patient outcomes negatively [6].

Recent research has focused on the identification of objective biomarkers that could provide more reliable and noninvasive diagnostic options for IC/BPS [2,3]. Inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, neurogenic growth factor–related markers, and oxidative stress markers in urine have emerged as promising candidates due to their involvement in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying IC/BPS [7]. Additionally, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and voiding diary data may have the potential to provide valuable clinical parameters to further support diagnosis [8,9].

However, individual biomarkers alone have shown limited sensitivity and specificity [3,7], suggesting that a comprehensive, integrative approach incorporating both urinary biomarkers and clinical parameters (including PROs and information from voiding diary) could enhance diagnostic accuracy. Accordingly, the present study (1) compares existing symptom-based diagnostic tools with biomarker-based approaches, and (2) develops and validates a multivariable diagnostic prediction model integrating urinary biomarkers with clinical parameters, to enhance diagnostic performance for IC/BPS.

Patients and methods

Study population and diagnostic definition

This study follows the principles of multivariable diagnostic prediction model development, and the reporting adheres to the TRIPOD guidelines where applicable. As a retrospective study utilizing existing biospecimens and clinical data, no formal prospective study registration or protocol identifier was applicable.

This retrospective study ultimately included 385 participants: 344 patients with clinically confirmed IC/BPS and 41 non-IC/BPS controls. A detailed breakdown of the patient selection process is presented in Fig. 1. Participants were identified from an institutional database consisting of patients evaluated for IC/BPS at Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital. The inclusion criteria were the following:

IC/BPS diagnoses were established according to the European Association of Urology guidelines, defined as pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived to be related to the urinary bladder, accompanied by at least one additional symptom such as increased daytime and/or night-time urinary frequency [1]. Confusable conditions were excluded based on the criteria outlined by the European Society for the Study of Interstitial Cystitis (ESSIC) [[9], [10], [11]]. To ensure diagnostic consistency and enable phenotypic characterization, all participants underwent standardized cystoscopic hydrodistension under anesthesia and videourodynamic studies [12]. The diagnosis of IC/BPS was further supported by cystoscopic findings in accordance with the Asian guidelines [13], which emphasize the presence and grading of glomerulations following hydrodistension as the diagnostic criteria. Patients with normal cystoscopic findings after hydrodistension were excluded from the IC/BPS cohort. IC/BPS diagnosis was based on the ESSIC and Asian clinical practice guidelines, which include cystoscopic hydrodistension findings and are routinely used in our institution. This approach was preferred to the American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines [14], which are applied less commonly in our local context.

Control phenotype

Control participants were individuals presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms but without bladder or pelvic pain. They were stringently screened to exclude any identifiable lower urinary tract dysfunction, as confirmed by videourodynamic studies, including detrusor overactivity, bladder outlet obstruction, dysfunctional voiding, or neurogenic bladder. Additionally, participants with active urinary tract infections at the time of enrollment were excluded.

All participants had previously been enrolled in our institutional review board–approved clinical studies focused on IC/BPS (approval no. 113-151-B), and urine samples were collected with prior informed consent [9]. Given the retrospective nature of the current study and the prior consent for a biomarker analysis, the requirement for additional informed consent was formally waived.

Clinical parameters

Two key clinical parameters were incorporated into the diagnostic model. Bladder pain severity was assessed using a standardized VAS, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain) [5]. Functional bladder capacity (FBC) was defined as the maximum voided volume recorded over a continuous 3-d period using a detailed patient-completed voiding diary [15]. These parameters were selected based on their established clinical relevance in reflecting symptom burden and bladder function in IC/BPS patients [5,16]. Both pain scores and 3-d bladder diaries were collected at the initial patient evaluation visit, prior to urine biomarker sample collection and cystoscopic hydrodistension.

Urine biomarkers

All biomarker measurements were performed in a blinded fashion, without any knowledge of the participants’ clinical diagnosis. Urine samples were collected immediately prior to cystoscopic hydrodistension, ensuring minimal delay between biomarker sampling and diagnostic confirmation.

Midstream urine samples (50 ml) were obtained via spontaneous voiding at full bladder capacity, prior to cystoscopic hydrodistension. Immediately after collection, samples were placed on ice and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at –80 °C. Prior to the biomarker analysis, samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged again at 12 000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to ensure removal of cellular debris [2].

Urinary biomarkers were quantified using multiplex bead-based immunoassays and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [9]. Proinflammatory and regulatory cytokines/chemokines—including interleukin (IL)-8; C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10; monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1); eotaxin; IL-6; macrophage inflammatory protein-1β; regulated on activation, normal T-cell expressed and secreted; tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α); brain-derived neurotrophic factor; and total antioxidant capacity—were measured using the Milliplex Human Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Oxidative stress biomarkers, including 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and 8-isoprostane, were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (K4160-100; Biovision, and DI-900-010; Enzo Life Sciences). Levels of 8-isoprostane were normalized to urinary creatinine concentration using a creatinine assay kit (ADI-907-030A; Enzo Life Sciences). Biomarker concentrations were calculated based on median fluorescence intensity or optical density in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. No adverse events were reported during urine collection or cystoscopic hydrodistension.

Statistical analysis

All included participants (n = 385) had complete biomarker and clinical data, allowing for a complete-case analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using deidentified data, independent of clinical label information.

We developed and internally validated a multivariable diagnostic prediction model using logistic regression with stepwise forward selection to identify the most discriminative combination of urinary biomarkers and clinical parameters. All candidate variables, including urinary biomarkers, FBC, and VAS pain scores (0–10 scale), were treated as continuous variables in the model. No categorization, binarization, or thresholding was performed. The full dataset was split randomly into a training cohort (70%) and a testing cohort (30%). Biomarkers and clinical parameters with a low discriminative ability in a univariate analysis (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] <0.6) were excluded from multivariable modeling. No formal sample size calculation was conducted, as this was a retrospective study using all available eligible data. Model construction was performed exclusively within the training cohort. The final models were then validated independently in the testing cohort to assess generalizability.

The classification threshold for sensitivity and specificity was selected using Youden’s index based on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve in the training set. These thresholds were derived for internal validation purposes and are not intended as clinically validated cutoffs. Diagnostic performance metrics included AUROC, overall accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.x (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as urinary biomarker distributions, were comparable between the training cohort (n = 269, 69.9%) and the testing cohort (n = 116, 30.1%), with no statistically significant differences observed across key variables (Table 1). This comparability minimized baseline confounding and validated the reliability of subsequent model training and evaluation.

In the training dataset, the diagnostic utility of each urinary biomarker was assessed via an ROC curve analysis (Fig. 2). To further refine the model, clinical parameters were incorporated. The addition of patient-reported bladder pain severity, assessed via the VAS, improved diagnostic performance markedly. The final integrative model, which included 8-OHdG, TNF-α, and VAS, achieved an AUROC of 0.997 in the training cohort and 0.997 in the testing cohort (Table 2). This model demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy (95.7%), sensitivity (96.2%), and specificity (91.7%), highlighting its potential as a noninvasive, high-performing diagnostic tool for IC/BPS applicable in real-world clinical settings.

Discussion

This study presents the development and internal validation of a multivariable diagnostic prediction model for IC/BPS, integrating both urinary biomarkers and clinical parameters. Our modeling approach follows the established frameworks for diagnostic prediction research, rather than classical diagnostic test accuracy studies. We identified several novel and important findings: (1) the integrative model combining urinary 8-OHdG, TNF-α, and VAS score yielded the highest diagnostic performance; (2) among models based solely on urinary biomarkers, the combination of 8-OHdG and TNF-α demonstrated the strongest performance; and (3) if limited to a single urinary biomarker, 8-OHdG outperformed other inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and neurogenic markers in diagnostic accuracy.

Compared with our previous study [9], which focused solely on clusters of urinary biomarkers for the identification of IC/BPS and its subtypes, the current work represents a significant advance by integrating both key urinary biomarkers and clinically relevant parameters (pain score and FBC) into a single diagnostic model. This integrated approach resulted in superior diagnostic performance and greater potential for practical clinical application.

Although single biomarker strategies are attractive due to their cost effectiveness and simplicity, these often fail to differentiate IC/BPS from other overlapping urological conditions [17]. Our integrative model overcomes this limitation by combining objective biochemical signals with clinically relevant symptom assessments, achieving superior diagnostic discrimination.

Oxidative stress is a well-established contributor to IC/BPS pathology [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. The consistent diagnostic performance of 8-OHdG, a robust marker of oxidative DNA damage, highlights its potential role as a core biomarker in clinical diagnosis [18]. Although previous studies have suggested its involvement in IC/BPS, few have successfully embedded it into a validated diagnostic algorithm. Our findings reaffirm the relevance of oxidative stress and support the clinical applicability of 8-OHdG.

The inclusion of TNF-α, a proinflammatory cytokine involved in IC/BPS-related inflammation [23,24], improved model performance further. The established role of TNF-α in inflammation, immune activation, and bladder pain mechanisms enhances the biological rationale for its inclusion [[25], [26], [27]]. The dual-biomarker model (8-OHdG + TNF-α) alone achieved an AUROC of 0.96 in the training cohort and 0.95 in the testing cohort, offering an optimal balance of sensitivity and specificity for purely biomarker-based screening.

Crucially, the integration of patient-reported bladder pain severity via the VAS elevated model performance to near-perfect levels (AUROC = 0.997 in the training and 0.997 in the testing cohort). This reflects the clinical reality that symptom severity often correlates with underlying pathophysiological changes. While VAS is subjective and categorized as a PRO, our data also demonstrate that substitution of VAS with a purely objective clinical parameter such as FBC—when combined with 8-OHdG and TNF-α—still yielded strong diagnostic accuracy (AUROC = 0.947, accuracy = 96.6%, sensitivity = 98.1%, and specificity = 83.3%). Thus, clinicians may flexibly tailor the diagnostic model based on available resources and patient preferences.

Importantly, the robustness of our model was confirmed through consistent results in independent training and testing subsets, supporting its generalizability across clinical environments. The model’s high specificity is especially valuable for IC/BPS, where misdiagnosis often leads to delayed and inappropriate treatments.

Clinically, this model has several advantages: it is entirely noninvasive, relies on standard and scalable assays, and incorporates straightforward clinical metrics. Compared with validated questionnaires such as the ICSI/ICPI (AUROC = 0.85), or the Pain, Urgency, Frequency Symptom Scale (AUROC = 0.92) [4,7], our model demonstrated significantly greater discriminative power (AUROC = 1.00), with greater PPV and NPV. These attributes make it feasible for use in outpatient and telemedicine settings. Given the economic burden associated with testing multiple biomarkers, our model also provides guidance for cost-effective decision-making—offering clinicians high-performance options based on one, two, or three variables. Moreover, the implications of our model may extend beyond diagnosis. Both 8-OHdG and TNF-α are implicated in chronic disease progression and may serve as prognostic markers in IC/BPS. Future studies could investigate whether longitudinal monitoring of these markers provides actionable insight into disease trajectory or therapeutic responsiveness.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Variables such as diet, medication use, or comorbidities may influence urinary biomarker levels. Although age and the prevalence of hypertension were not significantly different between groups in our study, both factors may affect oxidative stress and could confound urinary biomarker measurements [28,29]. The absence of histological data also limits correlation with tissue-level pathology. Imbalances in sex and age in our study may have introduced a bias and limited generalizability; a meaningful sex-specific analysis was not possible due to the small number of male cases. Future studies with a more balanced sex distribution, age-matched groups, and larger male cohorts are needed to validate our findings and assess sex-specific diagnostic performance.

Although the AUA guidelines do not recommend routine cystoscopy, hydrodistension, or urodynamic studies for the diagnosis of IC/BPS [14], all patients in our study underwent standardized cystoscopic hydrodistension and videourodynamic assessments to enhance diagnostic precision. This rigorous diagnostic approach was primarily based on Asian guidelines, which emphasize the importance of cystoscopy or hydrodistension as an essential component for the definitive diagnosis of IC/BPS [13]. By adopting this approach, we aimed to ensure a homogeneous disease cohort and minimize the misclassification bias during biomarker model development. While this strategy strengthens the internal validity of our findings, it may limit their generalizability to broader clinical settings where less invasive diagnostic criteria, such as those recommended by the AUA, are applied. Future studies should evaluate the performance of our biomarker model in more diverse populations diagnosed under varying international standards. Additionally, the small number of patients with Hunner lesions in our cohort limited the feasibility of a meaningful subgroup analysis. Larger and more heterogeneous samples will be necessary to validate the model across IC/BPS subtypes and to assess its applicability in distinguishing IC/BPS from other pelvic pain syndromes.

As a single-center retrospective study, a selection bias cannot be excluded, which may limit generalizability. Since only patients who underwent cystoscopic hydrodistension and videourodynamic studies were included, an ascertainment bias is possible, as this cohort may not represent the broader IC/BPS population. This study used a single random 70/30 split-sample internal validation strategy, which may yield an overly optimistic estimate of model performance due to sampling variability. The high AUROC values may reflect dataset-specific patterns rather than true generalizability, and optimism-corrected estimates should be considered in future work. More robust internal validation methods, such as cross-validation or bootstrapping, should be applied in future studies. The use of stepwise logistic regression for model construction may also increase the risk of overfitting and result in unstable models. Penalized regression techniques (eg, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator, ridge, or elastic net) may offer greater robustness and should be considered in future model development. Stepwise selection was used due to the exploratory nature of this retrospective study, but we acknowledge its limitations. Furthermore, calibration assessments such as calibration plots or Brier scores were not performed, limiting the ability to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities.

The classification thresholds used to calculate sensitivity and specificity were derived from ROC curves using Youden’s index within the training dataset. These thresholds are data driven and may be prone to overfitting; therefore, these are intended solely for internal evaluation and are not proposed as clinically validated cutoffs. Additionally, the PPV of our biomarker panel was evaluated in a retrospectively identified cohort with established diagnoses, rather than in newly presenting, undiagnosed patients. The lack of a priori sample size calculation represents a limitation and may affect statistical power estimation. Prospective multicenter studies enrolling patients based on clinical suspicion and evaluating model performance against the final clinical diagnosis in real-world settings are needed to validate the panel’s predictive performance and to confirm its true clinical utility and generalizability. Only internal validation using a random split-sample method was performed. Although this provides initial support for model performance, external validation in independent and multicenter cohorts is necessary to confirm generalizability.

While this study focuses primarily on the development of a noninvasive diagnostic model for IC/BPS, it does not address treatment response or prognostic prediction. As the urine samples and clinical data were collected prior to therapeutic interventions, we were not able to assess whether the identified biomarkers can discriminate between responders and nonresponders. We fully acknowledge this limitation, and future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal follow-up are warranted to determine the discriminant validity of these biomarkers in monitoring treatment outcomes.

Additionally, future work should explore additional biomarkers such as serum or stool-based indicators [7,30], and integrate the model into clinical decision support tools. Sympathetic nervous system assessment may also represent a promising direction for objective biomarkers in future research [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Furthermore, incorporation of machine learning frameworks and mobile health platforms may enhance the model’s scalability and utility. Finally, creatinine normalization for urinary 8-OHdG, although used widely, remains controversial for bladder-derived biomarkers due to potential variability from inflammation. Alternative normalization strategies, including the use of bladder-specific markers such as hyaluronic acid, might be considered in future research [35].

The proposed integrative diagnostic model has the potential to improve clinical care by providing a noninvasive tool that complements existing clinical evaluation. In practice, this model could support earlier identification of IC/BPS, especially in settings where cystoscopic hydrodistension is not feasible or available. By reducing dependence on invasive procedures, the model may streamline diagnostic workflows and reduce patient burden. Nonetheless, we recognize the potential risks of false positives or negatives, which could lead to over- or undertreatment. Therefore, external validation and prospective studies are essential to evaluate the clinical consequences, including impacts on diagnostic accuracy, treatment decision-making, and patient outcomes.

In summary, this discussion underscores the biological significance, clinical relevance, and translational potential of an integrative diagnostic strategy for IC/BPS. By combining urinary 8-OHdG, TNF-α, and symptom-based metrics, we offer a high-performance, accessible tool that bridges molecular diagnostics and patient-centered care.

Conclusions

We developed and internally validated a clinically actionable diagnostic model for IC/BPS that integrates urinary 8-OHdG, TNF-α, and patient-reported pain scores. This model demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in both the training and the testing cohort, indicating potential clinical utility. Our findings reinforce the value of combining biological data with clinical insights to improve the accuracy and efficiency of IC/BPS diagnosis. By reducing reliance on subjective reporting, this approach may help address diagnostic delays and facilitate more timely, appropriate treatment. However, further prospective and multicenter validation is required before clinical implementation. With such additional evidence, this model may ultimately contribute to advancing precision diagnostics in urology and improving care for patients with chronic bladder pain.
