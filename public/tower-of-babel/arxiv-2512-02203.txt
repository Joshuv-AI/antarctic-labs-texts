Abstract
Maximum likelihood estimators, such as the Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood (PPML), suffer from the incidental parameter problem: a bias in the estimation of structural parameters that arises from the joint estimation of structural and nuisance parameters. To address this issue in multi-way gravity models, we propose a novel, asymptotically unbiased estimator. Our method reframes the estimation as a series of classification tasks and is agnostic to both the number and structure of fixed effects. In sparse data environments — common in the network formation literature — it is also computationally faster than PPML. We provide empirical evidence that our estimator yields more accurate point estimates and confidence intervals than PPML and its bias-correction strategies. These improvements hold even under model misspecification and are more pronounced in sparse settings. While PPML remains competitive in dense, low-dimensional data, our approach offers a robust alternative for multi-way models that scales efficiently with sparsity. The method is applied to estimate the effect of a policy reform on spatial accessibility to health care in France.

Three-way gravity models, with

As the structure of fixed effects becomes more complex,

Specifically, as the sample size grows, so too does the number of nuisance parameters representing the fixed effects, possibly creating bias in standard maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters of interest, as first described by

In this paper, we tackle the incidental parameter problem in multi-way gravity models by proposing a novel, asymptotically unbiased estimator. In the spirit of
19
, we regard data through the lens of graph theory.
The key insight is that certain configurations of count outcomes within subgroups of observations, which we call
polyads
, have identical sufficient statistics for the fixed effects, making their relative likelihoods independent of the fixed effects.
Our framework differs from
19
in two important dimensions.
First, while Graham models undirected graphs, we consider multipartite graphs. Second, Graham’s network formation model considers only the extensive margin, i.e., the probability that potential links are realized. By contrast, we also consider the intensive margin, i.e., the strength of connections.

Our method compares favorably with existing approaches for gravity models along several dimensions.
First, it accommodates multi-way models with an arbitrary number of node groups,
in contrast to debiasing methods such as
16
and

44

and to two-way approaches like
19
,

7

24

Second, unlike PPML estimation, our estimator is bias-free by construction. Even relative to bias-corrected PPML procedures, our approach remains advantageous: the corrections may themselves be biased in finite
samples, as
44

47

Third, compared with approaches such as
7

We proceed the same way for count data and gravity models. The polyad estimator can be thought of as a nonlinear version of a difference-in-differences estimator.
Contrary to the above cited literature,
the polyad method handles count data and recovers both the existence and intensity of relationships. In the special case where the count data is right-censored at one, i.e., where the intensive margin is ignored, the polyad estimator maximizes the same conditional likelihood as

For researchers working with sparse networks — i.e., where most potential connections are not realized —, our approach offers a distinct computational advantage: polyads can be constructed by looping over pairs of edges with strictly positive counts (i.e., realized connections), allowing the estimation procedure to scale with the number of observed relationships rather than the number of potential relationships. This is a significant step forward, since the usage of tetrad-based methods has been limited by its computational cost: the available methods for computing tetrad-based statistics, which work only for two- or three-way models, either (i) require looping over all pairs of edges, including unrealized connections (e.g.,
19
;
30

24

5.5

We establish consistency and asymptotic normality under mild assumptions, extending the current theoretical framework in two main directions: relaxing sampling assumptions and better exploiting convexity. First, we do not require the existence of a distribution to sample the nodes, thus treating the fixed effects as true parameters and revealing fundamental geometrical properties that a graph should satisfy for consistency and asymptotic normality (see Assumptions
2
and
4

31

38
and asymptotic statistics results from
2

20

34

The practical limitations of our method merit clear statement. First, we do not consider interdependencies between observations beyond those captured by fixed effects and observed covariates. Second, our approach is designed specifically for count data, rather than continuous weights. Third, the method becomes computationally inefficient in dense networks where most potential relationships are realized. Within these constraints, however, our estimator provides a powerful tool for estimating gravity models with high-dimensional fixed effects structures.

Assumption 1
.

Let

The distribution of

(2)

involves a potentially high number of fixed effects. The MLE

3.2
A classification problem on polyads and the associated estimator of

We introduce the loss function at the level of the polyad
\xi
:

\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=-\ln\mathbb{P}_{\beta}\left(\,Y=y\,|\,X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\,\right).

(10)

A polyad
\xi
is not informative if the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
is a singleton, i.e., if both
m_{\xi}(Y)
and
M_{\xi}(Y)
are zero. For non-informative polyads, we have
\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=0
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We can thus restrict attention to informative (or “active”) polyads
\xi
for which the corresponding orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
has room for potential variation in the data, i.e., contains at least two distinct elements. Formally, active polyads satisfy:
|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|=m_{\xi}(Y)+M_{\xi}(Y)+1\geq 2
. To simplify notations, we denote the transformed graph
T^{r}_{\xi}(y)
by
y^{r}
, hence
y^{r}_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i}+rs_{\xi}({\bf i})
. The same computation as in (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\beta}\left(\,Y=y\,|\,X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\,\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{\bf i}y_{\bf i}\beta^{\top}X_{\bf i}-\ln y_{\bf i}!\right\}}{\displaystyle\sum_{r=-m_{\xi}(y)}^{M_{\xi}(y)}\exp\left\{\sum_{\bf i}y_{\bf i}^{r}\beta^{\top}X_{\bf i}-\ln y_{\bf i}^{r}!\right\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{r=-m_{\xi}(y)}^{M_{\xi}(y)}\exp\left\{\sum_{\bf i}rs_{\xi}({\bf i})\beta^{\top}X_{\bf i}+\ln\frac{y_{\bf i}!}{y_{\bf i}^{r}!}\right\}\right]^{-1}.

As already explained, all graphs in the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
coincide with
Y
except in the subgraph induced by the polyad
\xi
. Formally, for
{\bf i}\notin\mathcal{E}(\xi)
, we have
s_{\xi}({\bf i})=0
and hence
y_{\bf i}^{r}=y_{\bf i}
for all
r
between
-m_{\xi}
and
M_{\xi}
. In the above sums over edges
{\bf i}
, we can thus restrict attention to edges
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
. From (
10
),
the loss function associated with the polyad
\xi
is

\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\ln\left(\sum_{r=-m_{\xi}(y)}^{M_{\xi}(y)}\exp\left\{r\beta^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}+\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)}\ln\frac{y_{\bf i}!}{y^{r}_{\bf i}!}\right\}\right),

(11)

where the generalized “difference-in-differences” (DiD) operator
\widetilde{X}_{\xi}
is defined as

\widetilde{X}_{\xi}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}}s_{\xi}({\bf i})X_{\bf i}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)}s_{\xi}({\bf i})X_{\bf i}.

(12)

In Example
2
, consider the polyad

\xi=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
2&2\\
\end{pmatrix}.

In this two-way example, we get the usual DiD formula:

\widetilde{X}_{\xi}=X_{22}-X_{21}-(X_{12}-X_{11})=X_{22}-X_{21}-X_{12}+X_{11}.

In Example
3
, consider the polyad

\xi=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
2&2&2\\
\end{pmatrix}.

In this three-way example, we get (the opposite of) a “triple difference” formula:

\displaystyle-\widetilde{X}_{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[X_{222}-X_{221}-(X_{212}-X_{211})]-[X_{122}-X_{121}-(X_{112}-X_{111})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{222}-X_{221}-X_{212}+X_{211}-X_{122}+X_{121}+X_{112}-X_{111}.

Remark 1

(Difference-in-differences (DiD))
.

For a given polyad
\xi
, we may look at the sign
s_{\xi}=(s_{\xi}({\bf i}))_{\bf i}
as a DiD operator acting on the observed graph
y
and the observed feature vector
(X_{\bf i})_{\bf i}
either in an additive or a multiplicative way leading to the polyad transformation
T_{\xi}(y)=y+s_{\xi}
and the polyad feature
\widetilde{X}_{\xi}=\bigl<s_{\xi},(X_{\bf i})_{\bf i}\bigr>
. The tensor
s_{\xi}=(s_{\xi}({\bf i}))_{\bf i}
provides the sign in a DiD approach.

Finally, we call
\Xi_{a}
the set of informative (or active) polyads and form the loss function taking
y=Y
:

\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(Y|X,\beta)=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{a}}\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta).

(13)

Two immediate observations will play an important role in our analysis. First, because the LogSumExp function is convex,
6
6

6

Recall that the LogSumExp function
\mathbb{R}_{+*}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by
\mbox{LSE}(U_{0},\cdots,U_{S-1};S)=\ln\left(\sum_{s=0}^{S-1}e^{U_{s}}\right)
.
the function
\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)
is convex in
\beta
for any polyad
\xi
and hence the loss function is convex. Second, given two active polyads
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
, the terms
\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)
and
\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y|X,\beta)
in the above some are not independent if the two polyads share at least one edge, i.e., if
|\mathcal{E}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{E}(\xi^{\prime})|\geq 1
.

We are now in a position to define our loss function and the associated estimator.

Definition 4
.

Given the observed graph
Y
, our loss function is
\beta\to\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(Y|X,\beta)
and the polyads estimator of the parameter of interest
\beta_{\star}
is given by

\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi}=\argmin_{\beta}\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(Y|X,\beta).

(14)

The above polyad estimator can be interpreted in relation to a logit classification problem. Specifically, given a polyad
\xi
and the observed orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
, the minimal rank
m_{\xi}(Y)
is distributed according to a conditional Logit model, see
29
. To see this, consider the transformed graph

\underline{y}=T^{-m_{\xi}(Y)}(Y)
, which can be thought of as the “minimal” graph in the orbit of the observed graph
y
. In the example of Figure
1
, the graph
\underline{y}
is the second graph among the six graphs shown on the bottom line (that is for
r=-m_{\xi}(y)=-1
). The observed orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
can thus be represented as

\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y):=\left\{\,T_{\xi}^{m}(\underline{y}):0\leq m\leq|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|-1\,\right\}.

If the polyad
\xi
is active, the size of the orbit
|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|=m_{\xi}(Y)+M_{\xi}(Y)+1
is greater than 2.
Changing the indices
m=r+m_{\xi}(Y)
in (
3.2
) yields the conditional logit structure for the distribution of
m_{\xi}(Y)

\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(m_{\xi}(Y)=m_{\xi}(y)\,|\,X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y))=\frac{e^{v(m_{\xi}(y),\xi;\beta)}}{\sum_{m=0}^{|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|-1}e^{v(m,\xi;\beta)}}\ \ \mbox{ with }\ \ v(m,\xi;\beta)=m\beta^{\top}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}-\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)}\ln\underline{y}_{\bf i}^{m}!

(15)

Remark 2
.

(Analogy with conditional likelihood methods used in panel and network data)
For any active polyads
\xi\in\Xi_{a}
, the tensor
(s_{\xi}({\bf i}))_{\bf i}
, whose entries belong to
\{-1,0,1\}
,
is a “differencing vector” in the sense of
11
.
It applies linearly to the independent features
X
to deliver the generalized difference-in-differences term
\widetilde{X}_{\xi}
.
The corresponding transformation
T_{\xi}
plays the same role for the count data
Y
. Conditionally on the observed graph
y
belonging to the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)
, we thus obtain a conditional logit classification problem, with the number of alternatives,

|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|-1
, being polyad-specific.

The conditional logit distribution of
m_{\xi}(Y)
yields the following result.

Lemma 1
.

For every polyad
\xi
and
y=(y_{\bf i})_{{\bf i}}
, the function
\beta\to\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)
has gradient

\nabla_{\beta}\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\left(\Ex_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]-m_{\xi}(y)\right)\widetilde{X}_{\xi}

(16)

and its Hessian is non-negative since it is given by

\nabla_{\beta}^{2}\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\mathbb{V}_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}^{\top}

(17)

Proof.

Denote by
p_{\xi}(m;\beta)
the conditional probability that
m_{\xi}(Y)=m
given by (
15
). Notice that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\beta}p_{\xi}(m;\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle mp_{\xi}(m;\beta)\widetilde{X}_{\xi}-\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)|-1}m^{\prime}p_{\xi}(m;\beta)p_{\xi}(m^{\prime};\beta)\widetilde{X}_{\xi}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{\xi}(m;\beta)\left\{m-\Ex_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]\right\}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}.

Using
\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=-\ln p_{\xi}(m_{\xi}(y);\beta)
, we get

\nabla_{\beta}\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\sum_{m=0}^{|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)|-1}p_{\xi}(m;\beta)\,(m-m_{\xi}(y)),

which gives (
16
). Differentiating the above equality and using (
18
) yields

\nabla_{\beta}^{2}\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}^{\top}\sum_{m=0}^{|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)|-1}p_{\xi}(m;\beta)\,(m-m_{\xi}(y))\left\{m-\Ex_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]\right\},

and hence (
17
).
∎

As mentioned above, the loss function
L(y|X,\beta)
defined by (
13
) is convex in
\beta
.
Thanks to Lemma
1
, we can now compute its Hessian

\nabla^{2}_{\beta}\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(y|X,\beta)=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{a}}\mathbb{V}_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}^{\top}

and discuss strict convexity.

Lemma 2
.

The loss function
\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(y|X,\beta)
defined by (
13
) is strictly convex if and only if

\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{a}}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}^{\top}\succ 0
.

In other words,
\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(y|X,\beta)
is strictly convex in
\beta
if the DiD-features vary in the data.

Proof.

Since
|\Xi_{a}|<\infty
for any finite sample,
|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)|>1
for all
\xi\in\Xi_{a}
and the shape of the distribution of
m_{\xi}(Y)
in (
15
), the minimum
\underline{\sigma}^{2}(\beta):=\min_{\xi\in\Xi_{a}}\mathbb{V}_{\beta}\left[m_{\xi}(Y)|Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)\right]
is strictly positive for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Therefore, for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\nabla^{2}_{\beta}L(y|X,\beta)\succeq\underline{\sigma}^{2}(\beta)\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{a}}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}\widetilde{X}_{\xi}^{\top}.

∎

4
Large sample properties of the polyads estimator

In this section we establish consistency and asymptotic normality of the polyads estimator introduced in Section
3
. The concept of an active polyad is central to both our theoretical results and the implementation of the method (see Section
5
). Recall that a polyad
\xi
is active when its orbit satisfies
|\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(Y)|>1
, meaning it exhibits variation from which the parameters can be identified. This observation motivates normalizing the loss function by the expected number of active polyads and suggests deriving limiting behavior as the number of active polyads increases.

Let
\widehat{N}_{a}=|\Xi_{a}|
denote the observed number of active polyads and let
N_{a}
denote its conditional expectation given
X
under Assumption
1
. Formally,

\widehat{N}_{a}:=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}\mathbb{I}\{\xi\text{ is active}\}\text{ and }N_{a}:={\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}[\widehat{N}_{a}|X]=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star}}(\xi\text{ is active}|X).

(19)

As mentioned in Remark
2
,
N_{a}
plays the role of the average of number of data, we therefore normalize the loss function by
N_{a}
, defining

\widehat{Q}_{\Xi}(\beta)=\frac{1}{N_{a}}\widehat{L}_{\Xi}(Y|X,\beta)\text{ and }Q_{\Xi}(\beta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\widehat{Q}_{\Xi}(\beta)\mid X\right].

(20)

In machine learning
\beta\to Q_{\Xi}(\beta)
is refered as the risk function.

Recall that
n=\prod_{d=1}^{D}n_{d}
is the size of the graph indexed by
\mathcal{I}=[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times\dots\times[n_{D}]
and that if
\Xi
is the set of polyads given by the indices
\mathcal{I}
, then
|\Xi|
has order
n^{2}
. Through this section, one is given a sequence
\left(\Xi^{(1)},\Xi^{(2)},\dots\right)
of families of polyads, defined over growing sets of indices. Let
n_{d}^{(k)}
be the size of the
d
-th dimension associated with
\Xi^{(k)}
. We impose no restrictions on how each dimension
n_{d}^{(k)}
grows, requiring only that
n^{(k)}=\prod_{d=1}^{D}n_{d}^{(k)}\to\infty
— or, equivalently,
|\Xi^{(k)}|\to\infty
. Notably, our results accommodate short panels where one dimension remains bounded while others diverge. For three-way models, this includes settings where the PPML estimator suffers from the incidental parameter problem
(
44
)
. We also do not assume the existence of a limiting function
Q_{\infty}
, thus avoiding restrictive assumptions on the asymptotic behavior of fixed effects and covariates. In particular, we do not impose that fixed effects or covariates are drawn from any probability distribution. To circumvent a convoluted notation we use
(k)
as index in place of
\Xi^{(k)}
, for instance,
\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi^{(k)}}
is written as
\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}
. Also, notice that associated with each
(k)
we also have covariates
X^{(k)}
, fixed effects
\theta^{(k)}
and random variables
Y^{(k)}
.

Finally, unlike
19
;
25
, we do not require the components of
\left[n_{d}^{(k)}\right]
to arise from random sampling. This permits a more general interpretation of the data generating process. Consider a two-way model of doctor-patient interactions. Under the framework of
19
;
25
, one assumes the existence of a large population graph containing all doctors and patients, from which patients and doctors are randomly sampled, inducing distributions on both fixed effects and covariates. Our approach is more constructive: for each
(k)
, the distribution of fixed effects and covariates may differ entirely. In the doctor-patient example, our framework accommodates data collection that expands geographically. For instance, initially observing patients and doctors in Paris only, then adding doctors from Marseilles, then adding also patients from Marseilles, and so forth. Imposing a random sampling structure would limit this possibility, as any finite sample could contain doctors from Marseilles with positive probability.

4.1
Consistency

The polyads estimator (
14
) is defined as the minimizer of a loss function that is itself the sum of possibly non-independent terms. Our consistency result proceeds in two steps. First, we show that the normalized empirical loss
\widehat{Q}^{(k)}
converges to its expectation
Q^{(k)}
as
k\to\infty
. Second, we establish regularity of
Q^{(k)}
around its minimum
\beta_{\star}
. Note that at no point do we require the existence of a limit risk function
Q_{\infty}
.

Assumption
2
controls the approximation between the empirical and expected versions of
\widehat{Q}^{(k)}
. Observe that
\ell_{\xi}
and
\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}
are independent as long as
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
share no edges. Moreover, a polyad
\xi
contributes to the loss only when it is active. Thus, Assumption
2
controls the amount of dependence across the terms of
\widehat{Q}^{(k)}
, enabling a law of large numbers for the normalized losses.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that, as
k\to\infty
,

\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}\mathbf{1}_{\xi\text{ and }\xi^{\prime}\text{ are active}}\right|X^{(k)}\right]\gg\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}\mathbf{1}_{\xi\text{ and }\xi^{\prime}\text{ are active and }|\mathcal{E}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{E}(\xi^{\prime})|\geq 1}\right|X^{(k)}\right]

Assumption
2
is not restrictive: as
n^{(k)}
grows, the number of positive entries
Y_{\bf i}^{(k)}
with disjoint indices should also increase, and therefore, the number of pairs of active polyads sharing no edge should outnumber those sharing at least one edge. Notice that if one assumes a distribution on the fixed effects and on the covariates this assumption is automatically true, as in this case the probability that
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
are both active is always upper and lower bounded by a constant, thus it is just a matter of comparing the number of pairs of polyads – which has order
(n^{(k)})^{4}
– and the number of pairs of polyads sharing an edge – which has order
(n^{(k)})^{3}
. This last approach is used by
19
and
25
.

Next, Assumption
3
ensures enough regularity of
Q^{(k)}
around the minimum
\beta_{\star}
so that it can be identified.

Assumption 3
.

We assume that as
k\to\infty
, the polyads estimator
\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}
defined in (
14
) is the unique minimizer of
\widehat{Q}^{(k)}(\beta)
. We assume that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

•

there exists
L_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\inf_{k}Q^{(k)}(\beta_{\star})\geq L_{\star}
and for all
\beta\in\beta_{\star}+3\varepsilon_{0}B_{2}
, there exists
L
satisfying
\sup_{k}Q^{(k)}(\beta)\leq L
;

•

for all
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
there exists
\eta>0
and
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, if
\left\|\beta-\beta_{\star}\right\|_{2}=\varepsilon
then
Q^{(k)}(\beta)-Q^{(k)}(\beta_{\star})\geq\eta
.

We are now in position to state the following consistency result:

Theorem 1

(Consistency of the polyads estimator)
.

Grant Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
. Then, for almost all
\left(X^{(k)}\right)_{k}
and for all
\varepsilon>0
, if
n^{(k)}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, then

\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}^{\left.Y^{(k)}\right|X^{(k)}}\left[\left\|\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}\right\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right]\to 0.

Proof.

The proof can be found in Section
C.3
. The proof uses the convexity of the loss function as a key ingredient. It is based on a slight modification of Theorem 2.7 of
31
that requires revisiting classical results in convex analysis (such as those from Chapter 10 in
38
), as well as some results in asymptotic statistics from
31
and
2
.
∎

4.2
Asymptotic normality

Before introducing the assumptions and main result on the asymptotic normality of the polyads estimator we first investigate the error
\beta^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}
. A Taylor approximation yields, for some
\overline{\beta}^{(k)}
between
\beta_{\star}
and
\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}
,

\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)-\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}\right)=\nabla^{2}\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\overline{\beta}^{(k)}\right)\left(\beta_{\star}-\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}\right),

which implies

\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}=-\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\overline{\beta}^{(k)}\right)\right)^{-1}\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right).

(21)

If
\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}
is close to
\beta_{\star}
,
\widehat{Q}^{(k)}
is a good approximation of
Q^{(k)}
and
\nabla^{2}Q^{(k)}(\beta_{\star})\to\Gamma
for some invertible
\Gamma
we may approximate

\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}\approx-\Gamma^{-1}\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right).

The last approximation shows that to control the fluctuations of
\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}
we need to control the fluctuations of
\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)
, which is not a sum of independent random variables. Following
19
;
25
, we use Hájek projections to approximate
\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)
by a sum of independent random variables for which classical central limit theorems hold. The following assumption is useful to control the error of this approximation:

Assumption 4
.

Given
c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, define
w_{\xi}=\bigl<\nabla\ell_{\xi}(\beta_{\star}),c\bigr>
.
We assume that, as
k\to\infty
,

\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}w_{\xi}w_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{E}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{E}(\xi^{\prime})|=1}\right|X^{(k)}\right]\gg\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}|w_{\xi}w_{\xi^{\prime}}|\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{E}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{E}(\xi^{\prime})|\geq 2}\right|X^{(k)}\right].

Assumption
4
bears resemblance to Assumption
2
. Assumption
2
asks for the active polyads to be distributed among the edges of
Y_{\bf i}
in a way that no edge
{\bf i}
has a substantial amount of active polyads
\xi
such that
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
. Meanwhile and up to the weights
w_{\xi}
, which are zero when
\xi
is not active, Assumption
4
looks at the cases where two polyads share one edge and requires that, up to some weights, in most of these cases only one edge is being shared.

Assumption
5
below is a technical assumption to control the convergence of the Hessian:

Assumption 5
.

We assume that

•

\left(Q^{(k)}\right)_{k}
is equicontinuous, i.e., for all
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
k_{1}
and
\eta>0
such that for all
k\geq k_{1}
and all
\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, if
\left\|\beta_{1}-\beta_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta
then
|Q^{(k)}(\beta_{0})-Q^{(k)}(\beta_{1})|\leq\varepsilon
;

•

\nabla^{2}Q^{(k)}(\beta_{\star})\to\Gamma
is invertible;

•

the sequence of Hessian of
\left(Q^{(k)}\right)_{k}
is equicontinuous over
B_{2}(\beta_{\star},1)
.

Moreover, we assume that the fixed effects and the covariates are uniformly bounded as
k\to\infty
.

In order to present the main result we introduce the quantity
\Sigma^{(k)}
, which is the covariance of the Hájek projection of
\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)
and is given by

\Sigma^{(k)}=\left(N_{a}^{(k)}\Gamma\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\left.\bar{s}^{(k)}_{{\bf i}}\bar{s}^{(k)\top}_{{\bf i}}\right|X^{(k)}\right]\right)\left(N_{a}^{(k)}\Gamma\right)^{-1},

(22)

where

\bar{s}_{{\bf i}}^{(k)}=\sum_{\xi:{\bf i}\in{\cal E}(\xi)}{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta^{*}}\left[\nabla\ell_{\xi}\left(Y^{(k)}|X^{(k)},\beta_{\star}\right)\left|X^{(k)},Y^{(k)}_{\bf i}\right.\right].

Under Assumption
4
,
\Sigma^{(k)}
will play the role of the asymptotic variance of the polyads estimator if it does not vanish to zero as
k\to+\infty
and if a third moment condition holds. We gather these last conditions in Assumption
6
below:

Assumption 6
.

Given
c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We assume that

N_{a}^{-1}\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}\mathbf{1}_{\xi\text{ and }\xi^{\prime}\text{ are active}}\right|X^{(k)}\right]\not\gg\Ex_{\beta_{\star},\theta^{(k)}}\left[\left.\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}w_{\xi}w_{\xi^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{E}(\xi)\cap\mathcal{E}(\xi^{\prime})|=1}\right|X^{(k)}\right].

(23)

For
m\in\{2,3\}
, we define
\widehat{S}_{m}^{(k)}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}}|Z_{\bf i}|^{m}
where
Z_{\bf i}=\bigl<\bar{s}_{\bf i},\Gamma^{-1}c\bigr>
,
S_{m}^{(k)}={\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\left.\widehat{S}_{m}^{(k)}\right|X^{(k)}\right]
and
V_{m}^{(k)}=\sum_{\bf i}{\mathbb{V}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[|Z_{\bf i}|^{m}\left|X^{(k)}\right.\right]
. Assume that exists a sequence
\left(a^{(k)}\right)_{k}
such that as
k\to\infty
,
a^{(k)}\to\infty
and

S_{3}^{(k)}+a^{(k)}\sqrt{V_{3}^{(k)}}\ll\left(S_{2}^{(k)}-a^{(k)}\sqrt{V_{2}^{(k)}}\right)^{3/2}.

(24)

Assumption (
24
) is useful to bound the third moment of the Hájek projection of
\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)
in a central limit theorem and is not restrictive. It is satisfied as long as the quantities
\bar{s}_{\bf i}
(and their variances) are not concentrated on a limited number of edges
{\bf i}
as
k\to\infty

5
Computational implementation

Lemma
1
provides the tools to solve the optimization problem (
14
) defining the polyads estimator
\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi}
. Since each term in the loss function is convex and we have expressions for both their gradients and their Hessians, we can efficiently solve the minimization problem using Newton’s method. However, computing the gradient and Hessian involves summing over all polyads
\xi\in\Xi
, which naively requires looping over
\prod_{d=1}^{D}n_{d}(n_{d}-1)
terms, an operation that quickly becomes computationally expensive. The main goal of this section is to reduce this complexity by avoiding unnecessary iterations, which is done characterizing the set of active polyads
\Xi_{a}
. Besides that, we also discuss the implementation of two approximations of the variance. These approximations are essential to construct confidence intervals. The methods presented in this section are of special interest when
Y
is sparse. In particular, the computational complexity of our methods outperforms the PPML alternative
10
when the size of
E=\{{\bf i}:Y_{\bf i}>0\}
is of order smaller than
\sqrt{n}
.

5.1
Permutating polyads

A key tool that we explore to obtain efficient computational implementations of the polyads estimator is their invariance to permutations. A permutation of a polyad defined by
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
is obtained by flipping some indices between
{\bf i}
and
{\bf i}^{\prime}
. For instance,

\xi^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\
2&1&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\text{ is a permutation of }\xi=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
2&2&2\\
\end{pmatrix}\text{ on indices }d\in\{2,3\}.

We say that a permutation is odd when an odd number of indices are flipped and even when an even number of indices are flipped.
Notice that each polyad
\xi
has a total of
2^{D}
unique permutations, including itself. Lemma
3
below collects the main tools that will be necessary in this section:

Lemma 3
.

It holds that:

(i)

If
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
for some
\xi
, then
{\bf i}
belongs to all
2^{D}
permutations of
\xi
and exists exactly one permutation that can be written as
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
for some
{\bf i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
.

(ii)

Let
\xi^{\prime}
be any permutation of
\xi
. Then
s_{\xi^{\prime}}({\bf i})=s_{\xi}({\bf i}),\forall{\bf i}
iff the permutation is even and
s_{\xi^{\prime}}({\bf i})=-s_{\xi}({\bf i}),\forall{\bf i}
iff the permutation is odd. In particular, for odd permutations
m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)=M_{\xi}(y)
and
M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)=m_{\xi}(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{I}}
and for even permutations
m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)=m_{\xi}(y)
and
M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)=M_{\xi}(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathcal{I}}
.

(iii)

If
\xi^{\prime}
is a permutation of
\xi
, then
\ell_{\xi}(y|X,\beta)=\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(y|X,\beta)
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Proof.

To see (i), notice that flipping the
d
-th index of
{\bf i}
with the
d
-th index of
{\bf i}^{\prime}
produces a new edge that still belongs to
\mathcal{E}(\xi)
. Repeating this operation for all possible combinations of indices produces all
2^{D}
permutations. Also, given
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
, to find the unique
{\bf i}^{\prime}
such that
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
is a permutation of
\xi
one just needs to flip the
d
-th index of
{\bf i}
with the
d
-th index of
{\bf i}^{\prime}
if and only if
i_{d}\neq i_{d}^{\prime}
.

To see (ii), notice that flipping one index changes the sign of
s_{\xi}({\bf i})
, thus flipping an even number of indices preserves the sign while flipping an odd number of indices changes it. The expressions for
m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)
and
M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)
follow directly from the definition.

Finally, (iii) follows observing that
\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)=\mathcal{O}_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)
and so

\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(m_{\xi}(Y)=m_{\xi}(y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y))=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(m_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y)=m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi^{\prime}}(y))\text{, if the permutation is even}\\
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}(M_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y)=M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)|X,Y\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi^{\prime}}(y))\text{, if the permutation is odd}\end{cases}.

Since the event
M_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y)=M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)
is equivalent to
m_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y)=m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)
we are done.
∎

5.4
Evaluating the variance

We now discuss two approaches for evaluating the covariance matrix of the polyads estimator. To shorten notation let
\nabla\ell_{\xi}\left(Y|X,\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi}\right)
be denoted by
\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi}
and define

\widehat{\Gamma}=\nabla^{2}L_{\Xi}\left(Y|X,\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi}\right).

In Theorem
2
, the asymptotic normality is written in terms of
\Sigma^{(k)}
, which can not be directly evaluated from the sample. In practice, (
22
) suggest to approximate it by
\widehat{\Sigma}
given by

\widehat{\Sigma}=\widehat{\Gamma}^{-1}\widehat{\Omega}\widehat{\Gamma}^{-1},\text{ where }\widehat{\Omega}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}}\left(\sum_{\xi:{\bf i}\in{\cal E}(\xi)}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi}\right)\left(\sum_{\xi:{\bf i}\in{\cal E}(\xi)}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi}\right)^{\top}.

We also implement and empirically verify the performance of another variance estimator. Equation (
21
) suggests approximating the covariance of
\left(\nabla^{2}\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\overline{\beta}^{(k)}\right)\right)\left(\widehat{\beta}^{(k)}-\beta_{\star}\right)
by the expectation of

\left(\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)\right)\left(\nabla\widehat{Q}^{(k)}\left(\beta_{\star}\right)\right)^{\top}=\left(\widehat{N}_{a}^{(k)}\right)^{-2}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi^{(k)}}\left(\nabla\ell_{\xi}\left(Y^{(k)}|X^{(k)},\beta_{\star}\right)\right)\left(\nabla\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}\left(Y^{(k)}|X^{(k)},\beta_{\star}\right)\right)^{\top}.

Notice that if
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
share no edges, the expectation of their corresponding term is zero since it is the product of independent quantities with zero mean. This suggests approximating
\Sigma^{(k)}
by

\widehat{\Sigma}^{\prime}=\widehat{\Gamma}^{-1}\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}\widehat{\Gamma}^{-1},\text{ where }\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}=\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\text{ sharing edges}}\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi}\right)\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi^{\prime}}\right)^{\top}.

The difference between
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
is subtle. The next lemma illuminates this difference and provides computationally tractable expressions for
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let

\mathcal{I}_{a}=\{{\bf i}:\exists\xi\in\Xi_{a}\text{ such that }{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)\}

be the set of all edges that belong to at least one active polyad. It holds that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{a}}\left(\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}2^{D}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})}\right)\left(\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}2^{D}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})}\right)^{\top}

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{a}}\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})}\right)\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\top}2^{2D}

and

\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{a}}\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})}\right)\left(\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})}\right)^{\top}2^{D+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{1}\{i_{d}^{\prime}\neq i_{d}^{\prime\prime}\}}.

To see (

{\bf i}\not\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
, then its corresponding term is zero. Thus, the first sum can be taken only over
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
. Now recall from Lemma
3
that if
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
for some
\xi
, then
{\bf i}
belongs to all
2^{D}
permutations of
\xi
and there exists exactly one permutation that can be written as
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
for some
{\bf i}^{\prime}
. Since each
\ell_{\xi}
is invariant by permutation we have

\sum_{\xi:{\bf i}\in{\cal E}(\xi)}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{\xi}=\sum_{{\bf i}^{\prime}:({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}}2^{D}\nabla\widehat{\ell}_{({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})}

and so (

The proof of (
27

\xi,\xi^{\prime}
share one edge we can put this edge “in evidence” to obtain a permutation
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
of
\xi
and a permutation
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})
of
\xi^{\prime}
. If
\xi,\xi^{\prime}
share exactly one edge this representation is unique and a total of
2^{D}\times 2^{D}
permutations (

for each polyad in the pair) will be represented by the pair
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime}),({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})
. Now assume that the polyads share exactly
m

m

{\bf i}

{\bf i}

\frac{2^{D}\times 2^{D}}{m}

({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime}),({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})

i_{d}^{\prime}=i_{d}^{\prime\prime}
, then we can simultaneously flip the
d

{\bf i}
with the
d
-th coordinate of
{\bf i}^{\prime}
and
{\bf i}^{\prime\prime}
to obtain a new shared edge. It can not be done if
i_{d}^{\prime}\neq i_{d}^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, the number of shared edges is
m=2^{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{1}\{i_{d}^{\prime}=i_{d}^{\prime\prime}\}}
, which yields

\frac{2^{D}\times 2^{D}}{2^{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{1}\{i_{d}^{\prime}=i_{d}^{\prime\prime}\}}}=2^{D+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\mathbf{1}\{i_{d}^{\prime}\neq i_{d}^{\prime\prime}\}}

permutations counted for each pair
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime}),({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime\prime})
.∎

Indeed, (
26

\widehat{\Omega}
and
\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}
. First,
\widehat{\Omega}
contains duplicates of certain pairs of polyads. A closer inspection of the proof reveals that these duplicates arise precisely on pairs that share strictly more than one edge. This clarifies the role of Assumption
4

— which is approximately
\widehat{N}_{a}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\,\widehat{\Omega}

\widehat{N}_{a}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\,\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}
— must converge to each other. Second, computing
\widehat{\Omega}

\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}

For each
\mathbf{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
, evaluating
\widehat{\Omega}
requires only a single pass over each
\mathbf{i}^{\prime}
such that
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}
. In contrast, computing
\widehat{\Omega}^{\prime}
requires an extra loop over
{\bf i}^{\prime\prime}
such that
(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{i}^{\prime\prime})\in\Xi_{a}
.

We now use equations (
26
) and (
27
) to obtain an algorithm for computing
\widehat{\Sigma}
and
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\prime}
. We need to be able to loop over all
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{I}_{a}
and, given
{\bf i}
, to efficiently loop over all
{\bf i}^{\prime}
such that
({\bf i},{\bf i}^{\prime})\in\Xi_{a}
. Recall that
\Xi_{a}^{\star}
contains exactly one permutation of each active polyad
\xi\in\Xi_{a}
, in fact,
\widehat{N}_{a}=|\Xi_{a}|=2^{D}|\Xi_{a}^{\star}|
. One can loop over each
\xi\in\Xi_{a}^{\star}
and compute all permutations of
\xi
. By updating a dictionary containing for each key
{\bf i}
the corresponding set of
{\bf i}^{\prime}
s we can easily construct the data structure needed to evaluate the covariances. In practice we also store a pointer to the original
\xi
so that we can profit from the already evaluated
\widehat{X}_{\xi}
and
\{Y_{\bf i}:{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)\}
. The following result gives the computational complexity of computing each variance alternative.

Theorem 5
.

Computing
\widehat{\Sigma}
requires
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
operations and computing
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\prime}
requires
O(\widehat{N}_{a}m_{a})
operations, where
m_{a}
is the maximum over all edges
{\bf i}
of the number of active polyads
\xi
such that
{\bf i}\in\mathcal{E}(\xi)
.

Proof.

Since the cost of updating and consulting a dictionary is constant one can construct the dictionary that maps
{\bf i}
to
{\bf i}^{\prime}
in
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
. For evaluating
\widehat{\Omega}
one goes through each key
{\bf i}
and each set of
{\bf i}^{\prime}
s once, thus yielding
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
. Evaluating
\widehat{\Omega}
requires a double loop over the
{\bf i}^{\prime}
s, thus
O(\widehat{N}_{a}m_{a})
. The corresponding
\widehat{\Gamma}
is just the Hessian of the loss, which is evaluated in
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
.
∎

5.5
Final cost analysis and practical considerations

As a consequence of this section’s discussion, we can provide a complete computational cost analysis of our method:

Theorem 6
.

Assume that
m_{a}
is bounded. When
D
is odd, assume also that there exists
c\geq 1
that
|E_{i_{1}}|<c\frac{|E|}{n_{1}}
for all
i_{d}\in[n_{d}]
. Thus, evaluating
\widehat{\beta}_{\Xi}
and estimating its variance requires
O(T|E|^{2})
operations, where
T
is the number of iterations of Newton’s method.

Proof.

First notice that constructing
\Xi_{a}^{\star}
has cost
O(|E|^{2})
, thus the size of
\Xi_{a}
, i.e.
\widehat{N}_{a}
, must be of order at most
|E|^{2}
. Each Newton’s method update has cost
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
and, under the assumption that
m_{a}
is bounded, both variance estimates have cost
O(\widehat{N}_{a})
. Thus, the total cost is driven by the number of iterations of Newton’s method times
|E|^{2}
.
∎

In practice, Newton’s method converges in less than
10
iterations, yielding
O(|E|^{2})
operations. This quantity is to be compared with the fast implementation of PPML from
10
, which is
O(n)
. Our analysis suggests that our method is faster when
|E|\ll\sqrt{n}
and competitive when
|E|
is of order
\sqrt{n}
. Although being the standard practice when reporting the complexity of algorithms, the big-O notation hides a constant that matters to practitioners. In the next section, we empirically verify that, as predicted by our cost analysis, our method outperforms PPML in terms of computational time when
|E|
is smaller than
\sqrt{n}
and remains competitive as
|E|
grows. Indeed, in our computational setup (see Section
6
) the running time of our method is shorter than that of PPML as long as
|E|\leq 15\sqrt{n}
. This is, for example, the case of a bipartite network with
n_{1}=n_{2}
such that the average degree of a node is at most
15
.

Remark 4
.

We also note that the improvements developed in this section, and particularly the construction of
\Xi_{a}^{\star}
, may be useful for other polyads-based methods. For example,
24
proposes a generalized method of moments for two-way models with
n_{1}=n_{2}=\sqrt{n}
. His tetrad-based estimator leverages matrix-multiplication tricks and achieves a computational complexity of order
O(n^{1.1877})
when using the best available theoretical matrix-multiplication algorithm. In contrast, our
O(|E|^{2})
complexity can yield substantial gains in sparse regimes.

Consistency of

\displaystyle\Delta_{q,N}(X)

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathbb{E}}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\bigl<\nabla\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(\beta_{\star}),c\bigr>\bigl<\nabla\ell_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}}(\beta_{\star}),c\bigr>I\left(|{\cal E}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\cap{\cal E}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime})|=q\right)|X\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\xi,\xi^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\bigl<\nabla\ell_{\xi}(\beta_{\star}),c\bigr>\bigl<\nabla\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(\beta_{\star}),c\bigr>I\left(|{\cal E}(\xi)\cap{\cal E}(\xi^{\prime})|=q\right)|X\right]

.

\hat{\theta}_{n}

.

Then
\hat{\theta}_{n}\overset{p}{\to}\theta_{0}
.

{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[(\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta))(\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y|X,\beta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y|X,\beta))|X\right]\leq C_{1}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\xi\mbox{ and }\xi^{\prime}\mbox{ are both active}|X\right].

According to Lemma

(A.1)

where

,

\bar{\lambda}=\max_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}}\left(\exp\left(\bigl<X_{\bf i},\beta_{\star}\bigr>+L_{N}+\sum_{g\in{\cal G}}\theta^{g}_{g({\bf i})}\right)\right)\mbox{ and }\underline{\lambda}=\min_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}}\left(\exp\left(\bigl<X_{\bf i},\beta_{\star}\bigr>-L_{N}+\sum_{g\in{\cal G}}\theta^{g}_{g({\bf i})}\right)\right)

(note that we used

and

).

As a consequence, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[(\hat{Q}_{N}(\beta)-Q_{N}(\beta))^{2}|X\right]\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N_{a}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi\\
{\cal E}(\xi)\cap{\cal E}(\xi^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\xi\mbox{ and }\xi^{\prime}\mbox{ are both active}|X\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{N^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{P}}}_{\beta_{\star}}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\text{ are active and they share at least one edge in common }]}{N_{a}^{2}}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{P}}}_{\beta_{\star}}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\prime}\text{ are active and they share at least one edge in common }]}{{\boldsymbol{\mathbb{P}}}_{\beta_{\star}}[{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\text{ is active}]^{2}}.

(A.3)

∎

Appendix B
Asymptotic normality of the polyads estimator

We first prove an asymptotic normality result with a theoretical asymptotic covariance matrix that show that the polyads estimator does not suffer an incidental parameter problem (IPP) unlike PPML. We then show an a.n. result with an empirical asymptotic covariance matrix that will be useful to derive data-driven confidence sets for the polyads estimator. We will use these confidence sets in our simulation studies.

B.1
Main theorem for asymptotic normality under convexity assumption

The following result is adapted from Victor-Emmanuel Brunel lecture notes
5
who cites
20
and
34
as classical references for asymptotic statistics under convexity. However, all the later results use a loss function which is a sum of independent variables. This is not our case here since our loss function sums over all polyads that are not necessarily independent, moreover, as mentioned previously in the section on the consistency result above, we do not assume the existence of a limiting risk function; we only require the existence of a limiting covariance matrix of the risk in
\beta_{\star}
. We therefore need to adapt the classical proof of asymptotic normality under convexity assumption to our setup. The proof of asymptotic normality of the polyads estimator will rely on the following general result.

Theorem 7
.

Let

For all

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=(y_{\bf i})_{\bf i}}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star}}[Y=y|X]\left(\ln\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi}(y)}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y=y^{\prime}|X]}{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y=y|X]}\right)\left(\ln\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y=y^{\prime}|X]}{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y=y|X]}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(y_{\bf i})_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}\cup{\cal I}_{\xi^{\prime}}}}\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{\star}}[Y_{{\bf i}}=y_{\bf i},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}\cup{\cal I}_{\xi^{\prime}}|X]\left(\ln\left(\sum_{r=-m_{\xi}(y)}^{M_{\xi}(y)}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i}^{r},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}|X]}{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}|X]}\right)\right)\left(\ln\left(\sum_{r=-m_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)}^{M_{\xi^{\prime}}(y)}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i}^{r},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi^{\prime}}|X]}{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi^{\prime}}|X]}\right)\right)

where we recall that for all polyads
\xi
, all
{\bf i}\in{\cal I}
and all
r\in\{-m_{\xi}(y),\ldots,M_{\xi}(y)\},y_{\bf i}^{r}=y_{\bf i}+rs_{\xi}({\bf i})
. Next, we use the independence of the

’s conditionally on

to get that for all

,

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i}^{r},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}|X]}{\mathbb{P}_{\beta}[Y_{\bf i}=y_{\bf i},\forall{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}|X]}=\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}\frac{\lambda_{\bf i}^{y_{\bf i}^{r}}y_{\bf i}!}{\lambda_{\bf i}^{y_{\bf i}}y_{\bf i}^{r}!}=\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}\lambda_{\bf i}^{rs_{\xi}({\bf i})}\frac{y_{\bf i}!}{y_{\bf i}^{r}!}

where
\lambda_{\bf i}=\exp\left(\bigl<X_{\bf i},\beta\bigr>+\sum_{g\in{\cal G}}\theta_{g({\bf i})}^{g})\right)
.
By the diff-in-diff property of the sign function
s_{\xi}
from Proposition

\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}\lambda_{\bf i}^{rs_{\xi}({\bf i})}=\exp(r\bigl<\tilde{X}_{\xi},\beta\bigr>):=w_{\xi,r}

v_{\xi,r}(y):=\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}\frac{y_{\bf i}!}{y_{\bf i}^{r}!}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\prod_{{\bf i}:s_{\xi}({\bf i})=-1}y_{\bf i}(y_{\bf i}-1)\cdots(y_{\bf i}-r+1)}{\prod_{{\bf i}:s_{\xi}({\bf i})=1}(y_{\bf i}+r)\cdots(y_{\bf i}+1)}&\mbox{ if }r>0\\
\frac{\prod_{{\bf i}:s_{\xi}({\bf i})=1}y_{\bf i}(y_{\bf i}-1)\cdots(y_{\bf i}+r+1)}{\prod_{{\bf i}:s_{\xi}({\bf i})=-1}(y_{\bf i}-r)\cdots(y_{\bf i}+1)}&\mbox{ if }r<0\\
1&\mbox{ if }r=0.\end{array}\right.

We have, for
M_{\xi}=M_{\xi}(Y)
,
m_{\xi}=m_{\xi}(Y)
and
V_{\xi,r}:=v_{\xi,r}(Y)
for all

{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)|X\right]={\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\ln\left(\sum_{r=-m_{\xi}}^{M_{\xi}}w_{\xi,r}V_{\xi,r}\right)\ln\left(\sum_{r=-m_{\xi^{\prime}}}^{M_{\xi^{\prime}}}w_{\xi^{\prime},r}V_{\xi^{\prime},r}\right)|X\right].

Next, we use that
|\ln(t)|\leq\ln(a)
for all
a^{-1}\leq t\leq a
and that for polyads
\zeta
and all
r\in\{-m_{\zeta},\cdots,M_{\zeta}\}\backslash\{0\}
,

\displaystyle w_{\zeta,r},w_{\zeta,r}^{-1}\leq\exp\left[(M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta})|\bigl<\tilde{X}_{\zeta},\beta\bigr>|\right]\mbox{ and }V_{\zeta,r},V_{\zeta,r}^{-1}\leq\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\zeta}}(2Y_{\bf i}+1)^{M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta}}

to get the following bound:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y|X,\beta)|X\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\prod_{\zeta\in\{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\}}\left((M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta})|\bigl<\tilde{X}_{\zeta},\beta\bigr>|+\ln\left((M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta})\prod_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}(2Y_{\bf i}+1)^{M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta}}\right)I(M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta}\geq 1)\right)|X\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\prod_{\zeta\in\{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\}}\left((M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta})|\left(\bigl<\tilde{X}_{\zeta},\beta\bigr>|+1\right)+2^{D}(M_{\zeta}+m_{\zeta})\left(\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\xi}}\ln(Y_{\bf i}+1)\right)\right)|X\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[(M_{\xi}+m_{\xi})(M_{\xi^{\prime}}+m_{\xi^{\prime}})\prod_{\zeta\in\{\xi,\xi^{\prime}\}}\left(L_{N}\left\|\beta\right\|_{2}+1+2^{D}\sum_{{\bf i}\in{\cal I}_{\zeta}}\ln\left(Y_{\bf i}+1\right)\right)|X\right]

(C.7)

where
L_{N}=\max_{\xi}\left\|\tilde{X}_{\xi}\right\|_{2}
. Next, we remark that all of the four terms in the product
(M_{\xi}+m_{\xi})(M_{\xi^{\prime}}+m_{\xi^{\prime}})
are product of two minimum of Poisson variables. To handle (
C.4
), we rely on the next lemma.

Lemma C.10
.

Let
(U_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent Poisson variables with intensities
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Let
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},I_{4}\subset{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
I_{1}\cap(I_{2}\cup I_{4})=\emptyset
and
I_{3}\cap(I_{2}\cup I_{4})=\emptyset
(but possibly
I_{1}\cap I_{3}\neq\emptyset
and
I_{2}\cap I_{4}\neq\emptyset
). Let
\underline{\lambda}
and
\bar{\lambda}
be such that
\underline{\lambda}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq\bar{\lambda}
for all
i\in\cup_{k=1,2,3,4}I_{k}
. We have for all
i,j\in\cup_{k=1,2,3,4}I_{k}

{\mathbb{E}}\left[(m_{1}+m_{2})(m_{3}+m_{4})\ln(U_{i}+1)\ln(U_{j}+1)\right]\leq C_{0}\mathbb{P}\left[m_{1}+m_{2}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{3}+m_{4}\geq 1\right]

where
m_{k}=\wedge_{i\in I_{k}}U_{i}
and
C_{0}
is given by

C_{0}=4\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1,2\\
s=3,4\end{subarray}}\left(2e^{8}(\bar{\lambda}+1)^{4}+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{r}|}}+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{s}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{s}|}}\right).

For all interger
r\geq 1
, we also have

{\mathbb{E}}[m_{1}^{r}]\leq C_{|I_{1}|,r}\mathbb{P}[m_{1}\geq 1]\mbox{ where for all }n,C_{n,r}=(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}+\frac{e\exp(-ne^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{n}}.

Proof.

Let
I\subset\cup_{k=1,2,3,4}I_{k}
and
i\in\cup_{k=1,2,3,4}I_{k}
. Let
M=\wedge_{i\in I}U_{i}
. We first establish some preliminary results on
M
. It follows from a Chernoff bound that for all
i
and all
k\geq e^{2}\lambda_{i}
,
\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq k]\leq\exp(-k)
. As a consequence, we have for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[M^{r}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[M\geq k^{1/r}]=\sum_{k=1}^{(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}-1}\mathbb{P}[M\geq k^{1/r}]+\sum_{k=(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[M\geq k^{1/r}]

\displaystyle\leq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]+\sum_{k=(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}}^{\infty}\exp(-k)\leq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]+e\exp(-ne^{2}\bar{\lambda})

where
n=|I|
. We also have
\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]=\prod_{i}(1-\mathbb{P}[U_{i}=0])\geq(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{n}
. As a consequence,

{\mathbb{E}}[M^{r}]\leq C_{n,r}(\underline{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]\mbox{ where }C_{n,r}=C_{n,r}(\underline{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})=(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{r}+\frac{e\exp(-ne^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{n}}.

(C.8)

If
i\notin I
then by independence,

{\mathbb{E}}[M\ln(U_{i}+1)]={\mathbb{E}}[M]{\mathbb{E}}[\ln(U_{i}+1)]\leq\bar{\lambda}C_{n,1}(\underline{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]

If
i\in I
then we use that

M\ln(U_{i}+1)\leq M^{2}+\ln^{2}(U_{i}+1)I(M\geq 1)=M^{2}+\ln^{2}(U_{i}+1)I(U_{i}\geq 1)I(U_{j}\geq 1,\forall j\neq i)\leq M^{2}+U_{i}I(U_{j}\geq 1,\forall j\neq i),

that
{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i}]=C(\lambda_{i})\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]
where
C(\lambda_{i})=\lambda_{i}(1-\exp(-\lambda_{i}))^{-1}
and
\ln^{2}(x+1)\leq x,\forall x\geq 1
to get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[M\ln(U_{i}+1)]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}[M^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i}]\mathbb{P}[U_{j}\geq 1,\forall j\neq i]\leq C_{n,2}(\underline{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1]+C(\lambda_{i})\prod_{i}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{n,2}(\underline{\lambda},\bar{\lambda})+C(\lambda_{i})\right)\mathbb{P}[M\geq 1].

We have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[(m_{1}+m_{2})(m_{3}+m_{4})\ln(U_{i}+e)\ln(U_{j}+e)\right]=\sum_{r=1,2;s=3,4}{\mathbb{E}}\left[m_{r}m_{s}\ln(U_{i}+1)\ln(U_{j}+1)\right]

The four terms in the right-hand side of the equality above are of the same ’type’, the only difference we need to take care of is the relation between
I_{s}
,
I_{r}
,
i
and
j
. Let us consider the hardest situation when
I_{s}
and
I_{r}
intersect and
i
and
j
are different and both in this intersection. The other situations are easier using independence and can be handled using similar technics as below. We are therefore considering now the situation where
i\neq j\in I:=I_{r}\cap I_{s}
. We use the following decomposition:

\displaystyle m_{r}m_{s}\ln(U_{i}+1)\ln(U_{j}+1)

\displaystyle\leq m_{r}^{2}m_{s}^{2}+\ln^{2}(U_{i}+1)\ln^{2}(U_{j}+1)I(m_{r}m_{s}\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq m_{r}^{2}m_{s}^{2}+U_{i}U_{j}I(\forall k\in I_{r}\cup I_{s}\backslash\{i,j\},U_{k}\geq 1).

Let us handle the two terms in the right-hand side separately. We have for

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[m_{r}^{2}m_{s}^{2}]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[m_{r}^{2}m_{s}^{2}\geq k]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[m_{r}^{2}\geq\sqrt{k}\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1]+\mathbb{P}[m_{s}^{2}\geq\sqrt{k}\mbox{ and }m_{r}\geq 1]

\displaystyle\leq 2(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}\mathbb{P}[m_{s}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{r}\geq 1]+\sum_{k\geq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}}\mathbb{P}[m_{s}\geq k^{1/4}\mbox{ and }m_{r}\geq 1]+\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq k^{1/4}\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1]

and using independence and the Chernoff bound above we have for all
k\geq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq k^{1/4}\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in I_{r}}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq k^{1/4}]\prod_{i\in I_{s}\backslash I}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]\leq\exp(-|I_{r}|k^{1/4})\prod_{i\in I_{s}\backslash I}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]

and so

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}}\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq k^{1/4}\mbox{ and }m_{r}\geq 1]\leq\sum_{k\geq(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}}\exp(-|I_{r}|k^{1/4})\prod_{i\in I_{s}\backslash I}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]\leq e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})\prod_{i\in I_{s}\backslash I}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{r}|}}\mathbb{P}[\forall i\in I_{r},U_{i}\geq 1]\prod_{i\in I_{s}\backslash I}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]=\frac{e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{r}|}}\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1].

Gathering the results above, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[m_{r}^{2}m_{s}^{2}]\leq\left(2(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{r}|}}+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{s}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{s}|}}\right)\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1].

Next, using that
{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i}^{2}]=2\lambda_{i}(1-\exp(-\lambda_{i}))^{-1}\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]\leq 4(\bar{\lambda}+1)\mathbb{P}[U_{i}\geq 1]
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i}U_{j}I(\forall k\in I_{r}\cup I_{s}\backslash\{i,j\},U_{k}\geq 1)]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[U_{i}^{2}]}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[U_{j}^{2}]}\mathbb{P}[\forall k\in I_{r}\cup I_{s}\backslash\{i,j\},U_{k}\geq 1]

\displaystyle\leq 8(\bar{\lambda}+1)\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1].

Collecting, the results above, we conclude that

{\mathbb{E}}[m_{r}m_{s}\ln(U_{i}+1)\ln(U_{j}+1)]\leq\left(2(e^{2}\bar{\lambda})^{4}+8(\bar{\lambda}+1)+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{r}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{r}|}}+\frac{e\exp(-|I_{s}|e^{2}\bar{\lambda})}{(1-\exp(-\underline{\lambda}))^{|I_{s}|}}\right)\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1].

Finally, the result follows since we have for all
r\in\{1,2\}
and
s\in\{3,4\}
that

\mathbb{P}[m_{r}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{s}\geq 1]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[m_{1}+m_{2}\geq 1\mbox{ and }m_{3}+m_{4}\geq 1\right]

∎

Using Lemma
C.10
in (
C.4

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\beta_{\star}}\left[\ell_{\xi}(Y|X,\beta)\ell_{\xi^{\prime}}(Y|X,\beta)|X\right]\leq C_{D}(L_{N}\left\|\beta\right\|_{2}+1)^{2}C_{1}\mathbb{P}[M_{\xi}+m_{\xi}\geq 1\mbox{ and }M_{\xi^{\prime}}+m_{\xi^{\prime}}\geq 1].

∎

C.5
A result on the expectation of the empirical
L_{3}/L_{2}
ratio and other probability results

In this section, we provide the tools we used in (
B.14
) to show that as
n\to\infty

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{\bf i}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|R_{\bf i}|^{3}|X\right]=o(\sqrt{n}).

(C.11)

Since we can write the mean above as the expectation of the ratio to the power three of the third empirical moment over the second one of independent variables, we provide upper bound for such a quantity in this section.

Proposition 5
.

Let
((X_{i,n})_{i\in[n]})_{n}
be a triangular array of real-valued random variables. We assume that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
(X_{i,n})_{i\in[n]}
are independent. For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we consider for
k=2,3
,

\hat{S}_{k,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i,n}|^{k},S_{k,n}={\mathbb{E}}\hat{S}_{k,n}\mbox{ and }V_{k,n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{V}}\left(|X_{i,n}|^{k}\right).

We assume that

\frac{S_{3,n}+u_{n}\sqrt{V_{3,n}}}{\left(S_{2,n}-u_{n}\sqrt{V_{2,n}}\right)^{3/2}}=o\left(\sqrt{n}\right)

(C.12)

for some
u_{n}\sim a_{n}/\sqrt{n}
and
a_{n}\to+\infty
when
n\to\infty
. Under this assumption, as
n
tends to
\infty
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\hat{S}_{3,n}}{\left(\hat{S}_{2,n}\right)^{3/2}}\right]=o\left(\sqrt{n}\right).

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and denot
\hat{S}_{k}=\hat{S}_{k,n}
,
S_{k}=S_{k,n}
and
V_{k}=V_{k,n}
.
We consider the following decomposition:

Lemma C.13
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be random variables such that
Y
and
Z
are independent conditionally on

. Then for all measurable functions
g
and
f
, we have

•

{\mathbb{E}}[f(X,Y)g(X,Z)|X]={\mathbb{E}}[f(X,Y)|X]{\mathbb{E}}[g(X,Z)|X]
, in other words, conditionally on
X
,
(X,Y)
and

•

Proof.

For the first item, we check the result for all tensor functions
g=g_{1}\otimes g_{2}
and
f=f_{1}\otimes f_{2}
for which the result is trivial. For the second item, it is enough to show that
\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)|(X,Z)}=\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)|X}
. we have
\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)|(X,Z)}=\mathbb{P}^{X|(X,Z)}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y|(X,Z)}

and for all measurable sets
