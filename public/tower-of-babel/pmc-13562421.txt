A case of pulmonary Mycobacterium kansasii presenting with an atypical extensive consolidation involving the entire right lower lobe
Authors: Konjo Cho, Hirofumi Kamata, Yoshitaka Uchida, Koji Okudela, Fumikazu Sakai, Makoto Nagata, Hidetoshi Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 76-year-old man with a 3-year history of total gastrectomy presented with extensive consolidation involving the entire right lower lobe on chest CT. Pulmonary Mycobacterium kansasii infection was diagnosed based on sputum and bronchoscopic specimen analyses. Treatment with rifampicin, ethambutol, and clarithromycin was initiated, resulting in improvement of symptoms and radiography findings. M. kansasii infection typically presents with thin-walled cavities in the upper lung lobes. We report a rare case of atypical radiological findings characterized by extensive consolidation in the right lower lobe.

Introduction

Mycobacterium kansasii infection most commonly occurs in the upper lung lobes and is frequently associated with cavitary lesions; lower lobe involvement is uncommon [1,2]. Reports of extensive consolidation involving an entire lower lobe are rare. In contrast, in patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, pulmonary M. kansasii infection often presents with infiltrates or nodules in the middle and lower lung fields, differing from the typical upper-lobe cavitary lesions seen in those without HIV [3]. Here, we report a case of pulmonary M. kansasii infection in a patient without apparent immunodeficiency, who presented with extensive consolidation in a lower lobe, along with a review of the literature.

Case report

A 76-year-old Japanese man presented with a 1-month history of cough, sputum production, and fever (up to 38 °C). Chest radiography performed at a local clinic revealed extensive consolidation in the right lower lung field, accompanied by hypoxemia. He was referred to our hospital with suspected bacterial pneumonia. His medical history included total gastrectomy for gastric cancer 3 years earlier. He had no history of smoking or lung disease. Alcohol consumption was occasional.

On admission, his vital signs were as follows: height, 170.0 cm; weight, 50.5 kg (body mass index, 17.5 kg/m2); temperature, 37.7 °C; pulse rate, 100 beats/min (regular); blood pressure, 149/79 mmHg; respiratory rate, 20 breaths/min; and peripheral oxygen saturation, 90% on room air. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 5570/μL (neutrophils, 84.9%; lymphocytes, 5.6%; monocytes, 8.4%; and eosinophils, 0.7%) and a C-reactive protein level of 3.41 mg/dL, indicating inflammation (Table 1A). The serum albumin and total protein levels were 2.8 g/dL and 6.4 g/dL, respectively, indicating nutritional impairment. Chest radiography revealed consolidation, predominantly in the right lower lung field (Fig. 1A). Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed extensive consolidation with air bronchograms throughout the right lower lobe and a small cavitary lesion in segment 8 (Fig. 1B). Given the 1-month duration of fever, history of gastrectomy, and cavitation on CT, pulmonary tuberculosis was considered. Three consecutive sputum samples were collected for acid-fast bacillus (AFB) testing. On hospital day 2, sputum AFB smear was positive (1+: 1-9 AFB per 10 fields), but polymerase chain reaction was negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, M. avium, and M. intracellulare (Table 1B). Based on the clinical history and imaging findings, empirical treatment for bacterial pneumonia was initiated with ampicillin/sulbactam (days 1–9), followed by tazobactam/piperacillin (days 10–14). However, the fever persisted, and chest radiography findings showed only minimal improvement.

The differential diagnoses for antibiotic-resistant pneumonia included organizing pneumonia (elevated KL-6 and SP-D levels), pulmonary aspergillosis (positive antigen and antibody tests), and nontuberculous mycobacterial infection (positive AFB smear). No bacteria, other than AFB, were detected in the sputum. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and transbronchial lung biopsy were performed on hospital day 18. BAL culture yielded M. kansasii (Table 2), and histopathology revealed epithelioid granulomas with multinucleated giant cells, caseous necrosis, and lymphocytic infiltration (Fig. 2A). Ziehl–Neelsen staining confirmed the presence of AFB (Fig. 2B). Thus, the pulmonary infiltrates were attributed to M. kansasii. Cultures from three sputum samples were positive for M. kansasii. On hospital day 22, treatment with rifampicin (600 mg/day), ethambutol (750 mg/day), and clarithromycin (800 mg/day) was initiated. Drug susceptibility testing showed a minimum inhibitory concentration of 0.25 μg/mL for rifampicin, indicating susceptibility. The patient was discharged on day 32 without adverse effects. Discussion

M. kansasii is a nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) found in soil, dust, and water, but it is most often isolated from treated water sources [4]. Untreated pulmonary infection typically progresses to chronic disease, leading to destruction of the lung structure; therefore, most patients require antimicrobial therapy [5]. Risk factors for M. kansasii infection include heavy smoking, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, alcoholism, prior gastrectomy, immunodeficiency, and prior tuberculosis [6]. Although gastrectomy has been associated with an increased risk of pulmonary tuberculosis, Kim et al. reported that the standardized incidences of NTM lung infection and disease after gastrectomy were not significantly different from those in the general population [7]. Therefore, the history of total gastrectomy in the present case should be regarded as a possible contributing factor through its association with nutritional impairment, rather than as an established risk factor for pulmonary M. kansasii infection.

The diagnosis, which was based on radiological findings, positive sputum cultures, and positive BAL culture, fulfilled the diagnostic criteria of the American Thoracic Society/Infectious Diseases Society of America released in 2007 [8]. Bronchoscopy excluded pulmonary aspergillosis and organizing pneumonia. The marked improvement with rifampicin, ethambutol, and clarithromycin without antifungal or steroid therapy further supported the diagnosis of M. kansasii as the sole pathogen.

Typically, pulmonary M. kansasii infection manifests as cavitary lesions in the upper lung lobes [1,2]. A Japanese study of 29 cases reported cavities in 82.8% of cases, predominantly in the upper lobes [9], although lower lobe involvement was noted. However, none of the patients exhibited extensive consolidation involving an entire lower lobe. Similarly, a Brazilian study of 19 cases reported large infiltrates but not in the lower lobes [10]. Notably, HIV-associated cases often exhibit middle and lower lung involvement [3]. Our patient had no evidence of HIV infection or immunodeficiency.

A literature search did not identify any non-HIV cases of M. kansasii infection presenting with extensive consolidation of an entire lower lobe. Although similar findings have been reported in disseminated disease with anti-interferon-γ autoantibodies and mixed infection [11], our case did not have systemic features.

Although Aspergillus antigen and antibody tests were positive, no fungal organisms were identified, and radiological evidence of improvement was noted without antifungal therapy. However, post-mycobacterial lung abnormalities may predispose individuals to chronic pulmonary aspergillosis [12], warranting continuous surveillance.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of pulmonary M. kansasii infection presenting with extensive consolidation involving an entire lower lobe. Treatment with rifampicin, ethambutol, and clarithromycin resulted in marked improvement. Bronchoscopy excluded other conditions such as organizing pneumonia and pulmonary aspergillosis. Although prior gastrectomy and nutritional impairment may have contributed to host susceptibility, a direct causal relationship with pulmonary M. kansasii infection could not be established, and there was no evidence of concurrent HIV infection or disseminated disease. To our knowledge, such presentation is rare in immunocompetent patients.
