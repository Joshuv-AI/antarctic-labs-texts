Abstract
. In this paper we study non-degeneracy properties of Σ \Sigma via the Jacobi operator J Σ := Δ Σ + | A Σ | 2 J_{\Sigma}:=\Delta_{\Sigma}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2} of a given minimal hypersurface Σ \Sigma asymptotic to a cone C ⊂ ℝ N + 1 C\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1} of co-dimension one. Here Δ Σ \Delta_{\Sigma} is the Laplace Beltrami operator of Σ \Sigma and | A Σ | |A_{\Sigma}| is the norm of the second fundamental form of Σ \Sigma . We also construct a right inverse of J Σ J_{\Sigma} , that is, we prove that the Jacobi equation J Σ ​ ϕ = f J_{\Sigma}\phi=f is solvable in Σ \Sigma , at least under some suitable non-degeneracy assumptions about Σ \Sigma and about the asymptotic behavior of f f at infinity. We also discuss some examples where our results can be applied.



1.
introduction

Throughout this work we assume that
N\geq 3
and we denote by
\mathbb{S}^{N}
the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
centered at the origin.

Let us start by introducing some notation. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
O(m)
denotes the group of orthogonal transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
S
be a subgroup of
O(N+1)
A set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is
S-
invariant if for any
\rho\in S
,
\rho(D)\subset D
. If
D
is an
S-
invariant set, a function
f:D\to\mathbb{R}
is
S-
invariant if for any
\rho\in S
,
f\circ\rho=f
in
D
.

Next, let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{S}^{N}
be a minimal submanifold of
\mathbb{S}^{N}
of codimension one and consider the minimal cone

C:=\{r\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\,:\,r>0\quad\hbox{and}\quad\theta\in\Gamma\}.

We will refer to
C
as a trivial cone, whenever
\Gamma
lies in a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
of dimension
N
. In this case
C
coincides with this subspace.

The vector function
\nu_{C}:C\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{S}^{N}
will denote a fixed and continuous choice of the unit normal vector to
C\setminus\{0\}
. In view of the homogeneity of
C
with respect to the variable
r
,
\nu_{C}
is independent of
r
, i.e.,
\nu_{C}(r\theta)=\nu_{C}(\theta)
for any
r>0
and
\theta\in\Gamma
.

Now, let
\Sigma
be a smooth minimal hypersurface of codimension one. In what follows, we assume the following hypotheses on
C
and
\Sigma
:

(H1)

C
is a nontrivial and
S-
invariant cone;

(H2)

\Sigma
is
S-
invariant;

(H3)

\Sigma
is asymptotic to the cone
C
, that is: there exist
R>0
and a smooth function
w:(R,\infty)\times\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\|w(r,\cdot)\|_{C^{2}(\Gamma)}=0
and such that

(1.1)

\Sigma_{R}:=\{r\theta+w(r,\theta)\nu_{C}(\theta):\,\theta\in\Gamma,\,r>R\}.

and
K_{R}:=\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{R}
is compact.

(H4)

S
is maximal with respect to property (H1). In other words, if
\rho\in O(N+1)
is such that
\rho(C)\subset C
, then
\rho\in S
.

The invariance assumption in (H1) is equivalent to saying that
\Gamma
is
S-
invariant. This hypothesis is not restrictive, since
S
may be the trivial subgroup. However, in the case that
C
(or
\Sigma
) features certain symmetries, it is possible to find examples of such nontrivial subgroups
S
. For instance, when
m,n\geq 2
and
C=C_{m,n}
is the well-known Lawson cone, then we may consider
S=O(m)\times O(n)
(see Theorems 1.4, 1.5 below and the works
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
). We refer the reader to
[
5
,
14
]
for notions related to this invariance. Hypothesis (H3), is motivated by the Definition 2.1 and the developments in
[
3
]
. Up to modifying
S
by means of the Zorn lemma, if necessary, it is not restrictive to assume that (H4) holds. This fact, together with hypothesis (H2), yields that
\Sigma
inherits the symmetries of
C
. In the present work, we need a finer topology in order to extract more precise information of the geometric objects hereby studied. We believe that Definition 2.1 in
[
3
]
and the statement in (H3) are equivalent.

Let
J_{\Sigma}
denote the Jacobi operator of
\Sigma
, i.e.,

(1.2)

J_{\Sigma}:=\Delta_{\Sigma}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2},

where
\Delta_{\Sigma}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator over
\Sigma
and
|A_{\Sigma}|
corresponds to the norm of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma
.

Let
\mathcal{D}(\Sigma)=(C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma))^{\prime}
denote the space of distributions on
\Sigma
(see, e.g., Chapter 5 in
[
13
]
). We consider the
Jacobi equation

J_{\Sigma}\phi=0
in
\mathcal{D}(\Sigma)
. In this sense, we allow for singular solutions in
\mathcal{D}(\Sigma)
of the Jacobi equation (see e.g., section 5 in
[
3
]
). The solutions to the Jacobi equation will be referred to as
Jacobi fields
of
\Sigma
.

As it is known from standard regularity theory and the smoothness of
\Sigma
,
Jacobi fields

\phi\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\Sigma)
, actually belong to
C^{2}(\Sigma)
and solve the Jacobi equation in the classical sense.

Following the definitions and conventions in
[
3
]
we recall that
G(\Sigma)
denotes the space of geometric Jacobi fields in
\Sigma
, that is, the space of locally bounded solutions of the Jacobi equation generated by the invariances of the minimal surface
\Sigma
. These invariances arise from translations, dilations and rotations of
\Sigma
(see Introduction in
[
3
]
). Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N+1}\}
be the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and consider the Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
generated by the invariance of
\Sigma
under translations, i.e.,

(1.3)

\zeta_{j}(y):=e_{j}\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\quad\hbox{for }y\in\Sigma,\,1\leq j\leq N+1.

Observe that for any
j=1,\ldots,N+1
,
\zeta_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\Sigma)
and
\zeta_{j}
is bounded away from zero at infinity.

Let also
\zeta_{0}
be the Jacobi field of
\Sigma
generated by the invariance under dilations of
\Sigma
, i.e,

(1.4)

\zeta_{0}(y):=y\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\quad\hbox{for }y\in\Sigma.

Let

(1.5)

\mathcal{A}_{N+1}:=\{M\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{N+1}:\,M=-M^{t}\}

be the space of antisymmetric
(N+1)\times(N+1)
-matrices. We observe that
{\rm dim}(\mathcal{A}_{N+1})=\frac{N(N+1)}{2}
. Then the space of the Jacobi fields arising from rotations is given by
\{\zeta_{M}\}_{M\in\mathcal{A}_{N+1}}
, where

(1.6)

\zeta_{M}(y):=My\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\quad\hbox{for }y\in\Sigma.

Observe that for any matrix
M\in\mathcal{A}_{N+1}
such that the corresponding rotation
\rho\in SO(N+1)
fulfills
\rho(\Sigma)\neq\Sigma
, the Jacobi field
\zeta_{M}
is unbounded (see Lemma
3.1
).

Since
G(\Sigma)={\rm span}\{\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N+1}\}\oplus\{\zeta_{M}\}_{M\in\mathcal{A}_{N+1}}
,

{\rm dim}(G(\Sigma))\leq\frac{N(N+1)}{2}+N+2.

For future reference, denote also by
G(\Sigma,S)
the subspace of
G(\Sigma)
of
S
-invariant geometric Jacobi fields.

In view of (H3) and the fact that the Jacobi field of
C
associated to dilations is the trivial one,
\zeta_{0}
decays to zero at infinity, as we will prove in Lemma
3.1
. We recall that the decay of
\zeta_{0}
is polynomial if the underlying cone is strictly stable, however for more general cones we cannot expect this decay to be polynomial, even in the case of stable, but not strictly stable underlying cones (see Remark
3.2
of
[
3
]
).

Set

(1.7)

\bar{\nu}:=\inf\{\nu\in\mathbb{R}:\,\limsup_{|y|\to\infty}|y|^{-\nu}|\zeta_{0}(y)|=0\}.

The infimum in (
1.7
) is well defined in view of Lemma
3.1
. Observe also that
\bar{\nu}\leq 0
.

Next, for any
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
define

(1.8)

D_{\nu}(\Sigma):=\{\phi\in C^{2}(\Sigma):J_{\Sigma}\phi=0,\,\phi(1+|y|)^{-\nu}\in L^{\infty}(\Sigma)\}

and

D(\Sigma,S)_{\nu}:=\{\phi\in D_{\nu}(\Sigma):\,\phi\text{ is $S$-invariant}\}.

Our first result states that when the underlying cone
C
is nontrivial, there are no nontrivial bounded geometric Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
, which decay at a polynomial rate faster than
\nu=\bar{\nu}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C
and
\Sigma
satisfy (H1)-(H4). Then for any
\nu<\bar{\nu}
,
G(\Sigma)\cap D_{\nu}(\Sigma)=\{0\}
.

In the limit case
\nu=\bar{\nu}
, the result in Theorem
1.1
might not be true. For instance, if the underlying cone is strictly stable,
G(\Sigma)\cap D_{\nu}(\Sigma)={\rm span}\{\zeta_{0}\}
. However,
G(\Sigma)\cap D_{\nu}(\Sigma)
may be trivial as well. This can happen, for instance, if the underlying cone is stable but not strictly stable and
\zeta_{0}(y)=O(|y|^{-\frac{N-2}{2}}\log|y|)
as
|y|\to\infty
(see Remark
3.2
of
[
3
]
).

Next, we introduce a notion of
nondegeneracy
of
\Sigma
through the operator
J_{\Sigma}
.

Definition 1.1
.

i.

The minimal surface
\Sigma
is dilation nondegenerate if
\bar{\nu}>2-N
and
D_{\nu}(\Sigma)=\{0\}
for any
\nu<\bar{\nu}
.

ii.

The minimal surface
\Sigma
is
S
-dilation nondegenerate if
\bar{\nu}>2-N
and
D_{\nu}(\Sigma,S)=\{0\}
for any
\nu<\bar{\nu}
.

Roughly speaking,
\Sigma
is
S
-dilation-nondegenerate if the Jacobi field
\zeta_{0}
does not decay
too fast
and if there are no nontrivial
S
-invariant Jacobi fields which decay faster than
\zeta_{0}
.

In view of Theorem
1.1
, when
C
is non-trivial, Definition
1.1
is violated if and only if either
\bar{\nu}\leq 2-N
or there are nongeometric bounded Jacobi fields which decay faster than
\zeta_{0}
.

In general, it is a difficult task to exhibit minimal hypersurfaces
\Sigma
which are
dilation degenerate
, or in other words, that violate one of the properties required in Definition
1.1
. This suggest that Definition
1.1
might be an appropriate tool in the classification of surfaces
\Sigma
satisfying (H1)-(H4).

Remark 1.1
.

Assume that
\bar{\nu}>2-N
and let
S
be a subgroup
of

O(N+1)
. If
\Sigma
is
S-
nondegenerate in the sense introduced in Definitions 1.3 and 1.4 in
[
3
]
, then
\Sigma
is also
S-
dilation nondegenerate.

When the underlying cone
C
is trivial, we are able to construct an example of dilation degenerate minimal hypersurface asymptotic to
C
in the sense of (H3). However, the complement of such a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is connected.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\geq 3
. Then there exists a dilation-degenerate minimal hypersurface
\Sigma
asymptotic to
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\{0\}
such that
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\setminus\Sigma
is connected.

Now we focus on developing a linear theory to solve the Jacobi equation

(1.9)

J_{\Sigma}\phi=f\qquad\text{in }\Sigma

under suitable assumptions on
f
.

We start by introducing the functional analytic setting we use for solving equation (
1.9
). Let
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and
S
be a subgroup of
O(N+1)
. The space
C^{0,\alpha}_{\nu}(\Sigma,S)
denotes the space of
S
-invariant functions

with finite norm

\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{\nu}(\Sigma)}:=\sup_{y\in\Sigma}(|y|+1)^{-\nu}\|f\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1}(y))},

where
B_{1}(y)\subset\Sigma
is the geodesic ball in
\Sigma
of radius one and centered at
y\in\Sigma
.

In what follows, we use the notation

\|f\|_{\infty,\nu}:=\|(|y|+1)^{-\nu}f\|_{\infty}.

The space
C^{2,\alpha}_{\nu}(\Sigma,S)
denotes the space of
S
-invariant functions
\phi\in C^{2,\alpha}_{loc}(\Sigma)
with finite norm

\|\phi\|_{C^{2,\alpha}_{\nu}(\Sigma)}:=\sup_{y\in\Sigma}(|y|+1)^{-\nu+2}\|\nabla^{2}_{\Sigma}\phi\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1}(y))}+\|\nabla_{\Sigma}\phi\|_{\infty,\nu-1}+\|\phi\|_{\infty,\nu}.

Recall that
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{S}^{N}
is a smooth minimal submanifold of
\mathbb{S}^{N}
. Let
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}
be the sequence of eigenvalues of the operator
-J_{\Gamma}
in

, where
J_{\Gamma}:=\Delta_{\Gamma}+|A_{\Gamma}|^{2}
and
\Delta_{\Gamma}
and

correspond to the Laplace-Beltrami operator and the norm of the second fundamental form in
\Gamma
, respectively. It is known that

(1.10)

\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{j}\leq\dots,\qquad\lambda_{j}\to\infty\qquad j\to\infty.

Recalling the notations in
[
2
]
, we set

j_{0}:=\min\left\{j\geq 0:\,\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{j}\geq 0\right\}.

The authors in
[
9
]
proved that
j_{0}=0
if and only if the cone

is stable. For

, we set

(1.11)

\Lambda_{j}:=\sqrt{\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{j}}\geq 0.

If
C
is not stable, for

0\leq j<j_{0}
, we set

\Lambda_{j}:=i\sqrt{-\left(\left(\frac{N-2}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{j}\right)}.

The numbers
-\frac{N-2}{2}\pm{\rm Re}(\Lambda_{j})
are known as the
indicial roots
of
C
.

2.
Fredholm Theory for the Jacobi equation

Consider the
Jacobi equation

J_{\Sigma}\phi=f
in
\Sigma
, where
f:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is given. Let us solve for
\phi
in the form

(2.1)

\phi(y):=|y|^{-\frac{N-2}{2}}u(y)\quad\hbox{for}\quad y\in\Sigma\setminus\{0\}.

From Lemma 3.1 in
[
3
]
, the equation for
u
reads as

(2.2)

\mathcal{L}u=g\qquad\text{in $\Sigma\setminus\{0\}$,}

where

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}u:=|y|^{N}\text{div}(|y|^{2-N}\nabla_{\Sigma}u)+\left(|y|^{2}|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}+|y|^{\frac{N+2}{2}}\Delta_{\Sigma}(|y|^{-\frac{N-2}{2}})\right)u

and

g(y):=|y|^{\frac{N+2}{2}}f(y).

The strategy to solve the Jacobi equation for
\phi
is motivated by the developments in
[
11
]
and those in section 3 in
[
3
]
. The idea consists, first on solving the equation (
2.2
) for
u
and then translate the properties to the corresponding solution
\phi
by pulling back the change of variable (
2.1
). We begin by introducing the functional analytic setting with which we work.

Consider the space

L^{2}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}):=\{v\in L^{2}_{loc}(\Sigma)\,:\,|y|^{\frac{-N}{2}}v\in L^{2}(\Sigma)\}.

Observe that
L^{2}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
is a Hilbert space with the scalar product

(2.4)

\langle v,u\rangle:=\int_{\Sigma}vu|y|^{-N}d\sigma,\qquad v,u\in L^{2}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}).

Next, for
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
arbitrary but fixed we set
\Gamma_{\delta}(y):=(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{\delta}{2}}
for
y\in\Sigma
. Observe that
\Gamma_{\delta}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function such that
\Gamma_{\delta}^{-1}=\Gamma_{-\delta}
and
\Gamma_{0}=1
.

Consider also the space

L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}):=\{v\in L^{2}_{loc}(\Sigma)\,:\,\Gamma_{-\delta}|y|^{\frac{-N}{2}}v\in L^{2}(\Sigma)\}

with the inner product

(2.5)

\langle v,u\rangle_{\delta}:=\int_{\Sigma}\Gamma_{-2\delta}vu|y|^{-N}d\sigma,\qquad v,u\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})

and the norm
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})}:=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\delta}
. Observe that
L^{2}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})=L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
.

In what follows,
X^{\prime}
denotes the topological dual space of a given Banach space
X
. We now verify that the dual space of
L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
is the space
L^{2}_{-\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
. In the above notations,
(L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}))^{\prime}=L^{2}_{-\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}))
and the duality product is given by (
2.4
).

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\big(L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})\big)^{\prime}
and consider
l_{\psi}\in\big(L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})\big)^{\prime}
defined by

<l_{\psi},v>:=\psi(v\Gamma_{\delta})
for
v\in L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
. The Riesz representation Theorem implies that there exists a unique
w\in L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
such that

\psi(v\Gamma_{\delta})=<l_{\psi},v>=\langle w,v\rangle\quad\hbox{for }v\in L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N}).

Set
u:=w\Gamma_{-\delta}\in L^{2}_{-\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
and for any
z\in L^{2}_{\delta}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})
write

v:=\Gamma_{-\delta}z\in L^{2}_{0}(\Sigma,|y|^{-N})

and evaluate

3.
S-dilation nondegeneracy

3.1.
Asymptotic behaviour of the geometric Jacobi fields

In this subsection we discuss the asymptotic behaviour of geometric Jacobi fields.

Recall that
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{N+1}\}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and that the functions

(3.1)

\zeta_{j}(y):=e_{j}\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\quad\hbox{for }y\in\Sigma,\,1\leq j\leq N+1,

span the Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
arising from invariances under translations.

Recall also that

(3.2)

\zeta_{0}(y):=y\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\quad\hbox{for }y\in\Sigma,

\zeta_{0}
generates the Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
arising from the invariance under dilations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S=\{0\}
and assume (H1)-(H4). If
\zeta\in{\rm span}\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N+1}\}\setminus\{0\}
, then

(3.3)

\limsup_{|y|\to\infty}|\zeta(y)|>0.

Moreover,
\lim_{|y|\to\infty}\zeta_{0}(y)=0
and the nontrivial Jacobi fields
\zeta
of
\Sigma
coming from rotations have linear growth at infinity.

Proof.

First we show (
3.3
). Performing a change of basis, if necessary, we only need to prove the statement when
\zeta=\zeta_{j}
. Let
R>0
be arbitrary and large, but fixed. Fix particular choices of
\nu_{\Sigma}
and
\nu_{C}
, the unit normal vector fields of
\Sigma
and
C
, respectively, so that any
y\in\Sigma_{R}
can be written as

y=r\theta+w(r,\theta)\nu_{C}(r\theta)\qquad\hbox{ for }r>R\,\hbox{ and }\theta\in\Gamma.

It holds that

|\nu_{\Sigma}\big(r\theta+w(r,\theta)\nu_{C}(r\theta)\big)-\nu_{C}(r\theta)|\to 0\qquad r\to\infty,

uniformly in
\theta\in\Gamma
. Consequently, for any
1\leq j\leq N+1
,
\zeta_{j}=\nu_{\Sigma}\cdot e_{j}
and the corresponding Jacobi field of the cone
\bar{\zeta}_{j}(r\theta):=\nu_{C}(r\theta)\cdotp e_{j}
satisfy that

(3.4)

|\zeta_{j}\big(r\theta+w(r,\theta)\nu_{C}(r\theta)\big)-\bar{\zeta}_{j}(r\theta)|\to 0\qquad r\to\infty

uniformly in
\theta\in\Gamma
.

We claim that the Jacobi field
\bar{\zeta}_{j}
of
C
does not vanish on
C
. Once the claim is proven, then (
3.3
) follows for
\zeta_{j}
.

In fact, if we assume by contradiction that there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
such that
\bar{\zeta}_{j}=0
, then
e_{j}\in T_{p}C
for any
p\in C\setminus{\rm sing}(C)
, where
{\rm sing}(C)
denotes the singular set of
C
.

We claim that this fact yields that
C
is a cylinder, in the sense that there exists a cone
\tilde{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
of codimension
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
C={\rm span}\{e_{j}\}\times\tilde{C}
.

In order to prove the claim, we fix
y\in C\setminus{\rm sing}(C)
and a parametrisation
\varphi
around
y
. Differentiating the relation
\nu_{C}(y)\cdotp e_{N+1}=0
with respect to the local coordinates
x_{i}
we can see that

d\nu_{C}(y)\partial_{i}\varphi\cdotp e_{N+1}=d\nu_{C}(y)e_{N+1}\cdotp\partial_{i}\varphi=0\qquad\forall\,i=1,\dots,N,

which gives
d\nu_{C}(y)e_{N+1}=0
. This yields that the principal curvature
\kappa_{N}
vanishes identically, since
e_{N+1}
is an eigenvector of
d\nu_{C}(y)
with eigenvalue
0
. Since
y\in C
is arbitrary, this proves the claim.

If
\tilde{C}=\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, then
C=\mathbb{R}^{N}
, a contradiction with (H1). If
\tilde{C}
is nontrivial, then the singular set of
C
contains the straight line
{\rm span}\{e_{j}\}
, therefore there exists no hypersurface asymptotic to
C
, since there exists no
R>0
such that a unit normal vector field is defined in
C\setminus B_{R}(0)
.

A similar argument shows that the Jacobi fields coming from nontrivial rotations are unbounded. In fact, let
M\in\mathcal{A}_{N+1}
be an antisymmetric matrix (see (
1.5
)) and assume that the Jacobi field
\zeta_{M}(y):=My\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma}(y)
is bounded. Then, arguing as in (
3.4
), together with (
1.6
), we see that the corresponding Jacobi field
\bar{\zeta}_{M}
of
C
is bounded. However,
\bar{\zeta}_{M}(r\theta)=rM\theta\cdotp\nu_{C}(\theta)
, and hence
\bar{\zeta}_{M}\equiv 0
. This yields that
C
is invariant under the rotation
\rho\in SO(N+1)
associated to
M
. The maximality assumption on
S
, that is (H4), yields that
\rho\in S
. Thus, from assumption (H2), we have
\rho(\Sigma)\subset\Sigma
, which in turn yields that
\zeta_{M}\equiv 0
. We remark that the previous argument and (
3.4
) yield that the nontrivial Jacobi fields of
\Sigma
coming from rotations have linear growth.

Finally, since the cone is homogeneous, its Jacobi field coming from dilations fulfills
\bar{\zeta}_{0}(r\theta):=r\theta\cdotp\nu_{C}(\theta)=0

For

.

Let

for any

Proof.

∎

For

\displaystyle Y_{\delta}

Lemma 3.3
.

In the above notations, we have

i.

ii.

Proof.

i.

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}(u-u\circ\rho)v|y|^{-N}d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}uv|y|^{-N}d\sigma-\int_{\Sigma}u(y^{\prime})(v\circ\rho^{-1})(y^{\prime})|\rho^{-1}(y^{\prime})|^{-N}|\det(\rho)|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}uv|y|^{-N}d\sigma-\int_{\Sigma}u(v\circ\rho^{-1})|y|^{-N}d\sigma

\displaystyle=0,

since

Next, we show that

and for any

ii. If

4.3.
An example of dilation degenerate hypersurface

In order to construct a dilation degenerate minimal hypersurface, we prove the following Proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

For any
R>0
, there exists
\alpha=\alpha(R)>0
such that the Plateau problem

(4.6)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{in}\,\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{B}_{R}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\alpha(R)\qquad\text{on}\,\partial B_{R}

has a positive radial nonconstant solution
u_{R}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{B}_{R})
such that. More precisely, there exists
v_{R}\in C^{\infty}(R,\infty)
such that
u_{R}(x)=v_{R}(|x|)>0
and

(4.7)

v_{R}(r)=cr^{2-N}(1+o(1)),\qquad v_{R}^{\prime}(r)=-c(N-2)r^{1-N}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{as}\,r\to\infty

for some constant
c>0
.

Proof.

If we look for a radial solution
u(x)=v(|x|)
to (
4.6
),
v
has to satisfy equation

(4.8)

\frac{1}{r^{N-1}}\left(r^{N-1}\frac{v^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+(v^{\prime})^{2}}}\right)^{\prime}=0\qquad\forall\,r>R,

or equivalently

(4.9)

r^{N-1}\frac{v^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+(v^{\prime})^{2}}}=\kappa\qquad\forall\,r>R,

for some constant
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\kappa=0
we have the constant solution, hence we are interested in the case
\kappa\neq 0
. Since we are looking for a positive solution, we take
\kappa=-c<0
. As a consequence (
4.9
) becomes

\frac{v^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1+(v^{\prime})^{2}}}=-\frac{c}{r^{N-1}}\qquad\forall\,r>R,

or equivalently

(4.10)

v^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{cr^{1-N}}{\sqrt{1-((cr)^{1-N})^{2}}}\qquad\forall r>R

provided
c
is such that
cR^{1-N}=1
, that is
c=R^{N-1}
. As a consequence, by the fundamental Theorem of calculus, the function

v(r):=\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{1-N}}{\sqrt{R^{2-2N}-\rho^{2-2N}}}\,d\rho>0,\qquad\forall\,r>R

is smooth in
(R,\infty)
and fulfills (
4.8
). Hence the smooth function
u(x):=v(|x|)
is a solution to the Plateau problem (
4.6
) provided we set

\alpha(R):=\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{1-N}}{\sqrt{R^{2-2N}-\rho^{2-2N}}}\,d\rho\in(0,\infty).

The asymptotic behaviour of
v^{\prime}
as
r\to\infty
is directly given by (
4.10
) and the one of
v
follows from the de l’Hopital Theorem.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

From the proof of Proposition
4.3
, it follows that the solution to the Plateau problem constructed in Proposition

fulfills

in the sense of (H3) with

The Jacobi field coming from dilations satisfies

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\langle(x,u_{R}(x)),\frac{(-\nabla u_{R}(x),1)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u_{R}(x)|^{2}}}\rangle=\frac{-rv_{R}^{\prime}(r)+v_{R}(r)}{\sqrt{1+(v^{\prime}_{R}(r))^{2}}}

∎

Remark 4.4

The behaviour of the solution at infinity
