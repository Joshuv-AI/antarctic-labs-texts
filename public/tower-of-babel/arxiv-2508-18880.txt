Judicial Requirements for Generative AI in Legal Reasoning
Eljas Linna, Tuula Linna

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are being integrated into professional domains, yet their limitations in high-stakes fields like law remain poorly understood. This paper defines the core capabilities that an AI system must possess to function as a reliable reasoning tool in judicial decision-making. Using the IRAC (Issue-Rule-Application-Conclusion) model as an analytical framework, the study focuses on the most challenging phases of legal adjudication: determining the applicable Rule (R) and performing the Application (A) of that rule to the facts of a case. From a judicial perspective, the analysis deconstructs legal reasoning into a series of core requirements, including the ability to select the correct legal framework across jurisdictions, generate sound arguments based on the doctrine of legal sources, distinguish ratio decidendi from obiter dictum in case law, resolve ambiguity arising from general clauses like "reasonableness", manage conflicting legal provisions, and correctly apply the burden of proof. The paper then maps various AI enhancement mechanisms, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), multi-agent systems, and neuro-symbolic AI, to these requirements, assessing their potential to bridge the gap between the probabilistic nature of LLMs and the rigorous, choice-driven demands of legal interpretation. The findings indicate that while these techniques can address specific challenges, significant challenges remain, particularly in tasks requiring discretion and transparent, justifiable reasoning. Our paper concludes that the most effective current role for AI in law is a dual one: as a high-volume assistant for simple, repetitive cases and as a sophisticated "sparring partner" for human experts in complex matters.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs), machine learning systems that produce remarkably human-like responses, are rapidly being integrated into a wide array of professional domains
[
1
]
. These anthropomorphic AI tools can, when assessed externally, appear to discuss, explain, and reason like human experts. This ability is fascinating, useful, and entertaining, but also fraught with risk when used carelessly. Shortly after the launch of the original ChatGPT in November 2022, embarrassing examples have emerged of advocates who have used artificially manufactured legal citations
[
2
]
, most famously in the case of
Mata v. Avianca, Inc.

[
3
]
. By today, we are well aware that LLMs have severe limitations in their ability to reason and produce truthful responses. What is still not well understood however, is where exactly the boundaries of these limitations lie in different domains.

This paper seeks to define these boundaries within one of the most demanding and high-stakes fields: the legal domain. Here, the standards for reasoning and truthfulness are not abstract ideals but concrete requirements that determine people’s rights and obligations. When speaking of AI in the legal context, it’s useful to separate at least the following five aspects: (1) the regulation of AI, (2) AI-assisted research in legal theory, (3) AI’s impact on access to justice, (4) AI-supported legislative drafting, and (5) AI-assisted or automated legal decision-making.

Firstly, the
regulation of AI
encompasses many important legal questions, such as copyright and privacy. For example,
The New York Times
has sued OpenAI and Microsoft for copyright infringements
[
4
]
. In response to such issues, international bodies and national administrations are acting; the EU has passed Regulation (EU) 2024/1689
[
5
]
, the first binding AI regulation in the bloc, while in the USA, action plans and executive orders have been issued to steer AI development
[
6
]
. This paper assumes such legal problems will be regulated in the coming years and leaves them aside.

The second aspect refers to
legal theory and deontic logic
, which is an abstract normative science that studies the structure of normative systems and notions like "permissible", "obligatory", "optional", "must" and their counterparts. Symbolic AI has shown some promise to be used in context of such theoretical patterns
[
7
]
, and novel approaches have been presented for using LLMs to translate legal texts into formal deontic logic representations
[
8
]
.

Third, AI has a significant impact on
access to justice
. LLMs themselves are unlikely to create a competitive advantage between law firms as all companies have access to the same leading commercial LLMs and open-source models. Large law firms may gain an edge with proprietary data however, especially in arbitration proceedings where the data is not public. A vast proprietary knowledge base allows utilizing techniques like RAG to ground LLM responses with examples and reference cases, leading to higher quality responses from LLMs and thus higher quality service to customers. On the other hand, AI can dramatically diminish the cost of legal aid; it has been noted that in high-volume litigation, a complaint response system reduced associate time from 16 hours to 3-4 minutes, and productivity gains greater than 100 times have been observed
[
9
]
.

One emergent area for AI is in
law drafting
, the fourth aspect. AI can streamline and optimize law drafting processes, offer cyclical interaction between LLMs and human legislative drafters, deepen expert work and user engagement and understanding of legal material, and enhance transparency in the law drafting process. The European Union has already started assessing incorporating AI into legislation editing software
[
10
]
.

This paper focuses exclusively on the challenges for AI in relation to the fifth aspect,
AI-assisted or automated legal decision-making
, while contract interpretation is left aside as there are special requirements compared to the interpretation of statutes and case law
[
11
]
. The central aim is not to test how reliably different LLM-based systems perform a given task, but rather to discuss from a
judicial perspective
what is required for an AI to succeed in the nuanced work of legal interpretation. The approach is not one of computer science in a technical sense, but of a legal regime setting forth its expectations for generative AI to work properly in a high-risk judicial context. The core question is not whether LLMs can be reliable AI judges, but what capabilities they must possess to become reliable reasoning machines in the legal domain.

To explore this question, this paper will focus on the process of legal reasoning from the standpoint of an impartial judge adjudicating according to law. We will use the well-established IRAC (Issue–Rule–Application–Conclusion) model as our analytical framework
[
12
]
, concentrating on the most challenging phases for any legal reasoner, human or artificial: determining the applicable
Rule (R)
and performing the
Application (A)
of that rule to the facts of a case
[
13
]
.

To build this analysis, the paper is structured as follows. Chapter 2 will establish a foundational framework for legal reasoning, outlining the core principles that govern judicial decision-making. Chapter 3 will deconstruct legal interpretation into a series of specific and demanding judicial requirements, from selecting the correct legal framework to generating sound and defensible arguments in ambiguous situations. Chapter 4 will then map the various AI enhancement mechanisms such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), multi-agent systems and neuro-symbolic AI to these requirements, assessing their potential to meet the identified challenges. Finally, the paper will offer a discussion and conclusion on the current state of AI in law, clarifying its most effective role today and looking forward to a future where human expertise and artificial intelligence might work in symbiosis to uphold the rule of law.

2
The Landscape of Legal Reasoning: A Foundational Framework

Before examining the specific challenges that artificial intelligence faces in the legal domain, it is essential to establish a foundational understanding of the framework that governs judicial work. Legal reasoning is not an unstructured exercise in logic; it is a disciplined process guided by core principles and established patterns of analysis. These elements set the standard for any adjudicator, human or machine, and provide the lens through which we must evaluate the potential of AI in law.

At its heart, the judicial process is bound by a set of imperatives designed to ensure fairness and uphold the rule of law. These include the principles of
impartiality
, requiring that decisions are made without bias;
legal certainty
, ensuring that outcomes are predictable and consistent; and
transparency
, demanding that the reasoning behind a judgment is accessible and understandable. Crucially, every step in the legal decision-making process must be
justifiable and controllable
, allowing the parties to a dispute, appellate courts and the public to scrutinize and validate the outcome. These principles are not mere aspirations; they are functional requirements for a legitimate legal system.

The key elements of legal work—legal search, argument creation, and legal writing—are typically structured around a common pattern of reasoning. While no single formula can capture the full complexity of legal analysis, the IRAC model provides a widely recognized anatomy of the process
[
12
]
. This model breaks down legal reasoning into four distinct phases:

•

Issue (I):
Determining the core legal questions raised by the facts of a case.

•

Rule (R):
Identifying the relevant legal rules, from statutes and regulations to binding case law, that govern the issue.

•

Application (A):
Analyzing how the identified rules apply to the specific facts of the case. This is the heart of legal interpretation, where arguments are weighed, and conclusions are drawn.

•

Conclusion (C):
Stating the outcome of the analysis and the resolution of the legal issue.

This paper uses the IRAC model as its primary analytical framework. While AI can be a powerful tool in the initial "Issue" discovery phase, its greatest challenges and potential lie in the subsequent stages. The reasoning in courts resembles that of
legal dogmatics
, with the major difference being that a judge has a legal obligation to deliver a judgment; a judge cannot say "
non liquet
" (it is not clear), whereas a scholar may leave a question open. Therefore, our analysis will concentrate on the two most demanding phases of legal reasoning: the selection of the correct
Rule (R)
and the nuanced
Application (A)
of that rule to the facts. It is here, in the interpretation and application of law, that the highest standards of judicial reasoning are required.

Finally, alongside the interpretation of legal norms, the equally critical task of evidence evaluation forms another pillar of the judicial process. A significant part of any trial involves assessing evidence—witness testimonies, documents, and forensic data—to establish a factual record. This process presents a distinct set of challenges for AI. Evidence evaluation is difficult to improve with techniques like RAG because legal evidence pertains to the truthfulness of everyday facts; fine-tuning an AI with official law texts doesn’t help it answer whether a certain person was in a certain place at a certain time. LLMs struggle to evaluate the truthfulness of real-world facts
[
14
]
, a task that often relies on human experience and credibility assessments. Furthermore, legal systems employ complex and often unwritten
burden of proof
rules to resolve cases where evidence is insufficient. An AI that attempts to decide a case on weak evidence without correctly applying these rules would inadvertently bypass a cornerstone of procedural justice.

3
Core Judicial Requirements in Complex Legal Scenarios

Having established the foundational framework of legal reasoning, we now turn to the specific and demanding tasks that comprise the judicial process. The selection of rules, their application to facts, and the evaluation of evidence are not simple procedural steps but a series of complex
legal choices
that require deep domain knowledge, nuanced interpretation, and the ability to navigate ambiguity. Resolving ambiguity involves formalism (formalistically applying existing rules to new facts), analogy (applying the existing rules by analogy), and realism (fashioning new rules by means of a realistic balancing of policies and interests)
[
15
]
. This chapter deconstructs this process into five core judicial requirements that AI systems must meet to function reliably in legal decision-making in different scenarios.

3.1
Requirement: Selecting the Correct Legal Framework

Before any legal question can be answered, the correct body of law must be identified. This initial selection process is fraught with complexity, particularly in cases with cross-border implications or intricate national legal systems.

3.1.1
Navigating International and Cross-Border Jurisdictions

In an increasingly globalized world, legal disputes often transcend national borders. An AI must first be able to distinguish between
jurisdiction
(which court has the authority to hear a case) and applicable law (which country’s laws govern the dispute). This distinction is a foundational concept, the first aspect (jurisdiction) being a part of procedural law, the latter (applicable law) belonging to private international law. Traditionally, these two inquiries are treated as separate and sequential stages: a court must first determine whether it has the authority to adjudicate the matter before it proceeds to the second stage of deciding which substantive law to apply. This separation is particularly pronounced in legal systems that employ rigid, non-discretionary rules for jurisdiction, such as those within the European Union, where a court, once its jurisdiction is established, generally has no discretionary power to decline it on the grounds that a foreign court might be a more appropriate (
forum non conveniens
)
[
16
]
. Correctly distinguishing the two sides requires the correct application of international instruments, such as the European Union’s
Regulation (EU) 1215/2012 (Brussels I Recast)
on jurisdiction and the
Regulation (EC) 593/2008 (Rome I)
on applicable law.

Furthermore, the AI must recognize the fundamental differences between legal traditions. For instance, if private international law rules determine that the applicable substantive law is from a common law jurisdiction, the AI must reason within that framework, respecting the binding effect of precedent under the doctrine of
stare decisis
. Conversely, when dealing with a case governed by the law of a civil law system, it must recognize that while there is no formal rule of binding precedent, a consistent line of judicial decisions (
jurisprudence constante
) from superior courts carries highly persuasive weight and is a crucial source for interpretation
[
17
]
. In some domains, like international arbitration, it may even need to reason from non-codified sources, such as established trade practices (
Lex Mercatoria
) or on the basis of fairness (
ex aequo et bono
).

While knowledge retrieval systems can find the relevant regulations, AI may struggle to interpret the connecting factors that determine jurisdiction, such as a defendant’s habitual residence or where the damage occurred which normally allow for judicial discretion and context-sensitive application
[
18
]
. A multi-agent system could break the problem into sub-tasks (agent 1 finds jurisdiction, agent 2 finds applicable law, agent 3 reviews, etc.), but this still relies on the agents’ ability to correctly interpret the often ambiguous and complex web of logical dependencies.

3.1.2
Respecting National Legal Hierarchies and Rules

Even within a single country’s legal system, choosing the right provision is a multi-layered task. An AI must be able to navigate several critical dimensions:

•

Hierarchy:
It must respect the hierarchical system of legal norms, prioritizing international treaties and constitutional law over ordinary statutes and decrees. Legal systems are structured with a clear hierarchy of norms, where rules from a higher source prevail over rules from a lower one. In most civil and common law states, international treaties (which are often considered to have primacy over domestic law upon ratification) stand at the apex of this hierarchy, followed by the constitutions, federal laws or statutes coming next, and finally, subordinate legislation such as decrees and regulations. In the European Union context, this includes understanding the difference between the
direct applicability
of EU Regulations and, on the other hand,
interpretive effect of EU Directives on national legislation
.

•

Time:
It must correctly handle the
time dimension
. Laws are frequently amended, and the AI must apply the version of the law that was in force when the events in question occurred. This concept, known as the temporal scope of law, is fundamental to legal certainty. The general principle is that of non-retroactivity. This requires checking for
transitional provisions
that may suspend or modify the application of new laws and correctly identifying whether a law applies retroactively. Non-retroactivity is especially important in criminal cases (
nullum crimen sine lege
), meaning an act can only be an offense if it was defined as such by law at the time of its commission.

•

Specificity:
It must correctly apply the general maxim of
lex specialis derogat legi generali
, whereby a special law (e.g., a consumer protection act) supersedes a more general one (e.g., a general trade act). This principle is not used to solve contradictions but is applied to ensure that the rule tailored to the specific situation is the one that governs it. This is not a simple keyword match; it requires the AI to first correctly identify the legal status of the parties involved (e.g., consumer, employee) to determine if a special law is applicable.

•

Procedural Choice:
Within a single statute, an AI must be able to choose the correct internal proceeding. For example, a Restructuring Act may contain separate frameworks for preventive, normal, or simplified procedures, each with different purposes and rules. The AI must be able to select the appropriate procedure based on the goals of the law, such as process economics or rescuing a viable business.

These requirements present a systemic reasoning challenge. An AI must simultaneously consider multiple constraints, a task difficult to program with simple prompts. For instance, determining the applicable version of a law (time dimension) while also checking for a superseding special law (specificity) and its place in the legal hierarchy requires a complex dependency graph. This challenge highlights a fundamental tension in legal AI: the need to bridge the unstructured, natural language of legal texts with the highly structured, formal logic of legal doctrine. A neuro-symbolic AI system, which combines LLMs with rule-based systems, could model these hierarchies, but defining all the rules and their exceptions for an entire legal system is a monumental undertaking
[
7
]
.

3.2
Requirement: Generating Sound and Defensible Legal Arguments

Once the correct legal text is identified, the core task of interpretation begins. This is not a matter of deductive logic but of constructing a persuasive argument grounded in accepted sources of law, generally accepted legal principles and the rule of law.

3.2.1
Adhering to the Doctrine of the Sources of Law

Legal argumentation follows a generally accepted, though sometimes flexible, hierarchy of sources
[
19
]
. A reliable AI must be able to construct its reasoning by starting with the literal
wording
of the provision and interpreting it in light of the
legislator’s purpose
. The judicial task is to ascertain and act upon the "true intention of the legislature"
[
20
]
. This is a potential pitfall, as the AI must correctly source this purpose from preparatory works (e.g., government bills) and, crucially, verify if the final law was amended by Parliament. If so, the reasoning in a parliamentary committee report supersedes the original preamble.

The AI must then integrate relevant
case law
and non-binding sources like
legal literature
according to their authoritative weight. This entire process must be conducted while respecting overarching legal doctrines, such as the
principle of legality
and the rule of lenity (
in dubio mitius
) in criminal law. This is a problem of synthesis and validation. An AI must not only retrieve preparatory works but also verify if the final law was amended, a step that requires comparing multiple documents and understanding the implications of any changes. This task demands more than simple information retrieval; it requires a structured comparison and a logical inference about the significance of textual differences between legislative drafts, a higher-order reasoning capability that current LLMs often lack, leading to a risk of overlooking critical changes or hallucinating their importance
[
21
]
.

3.2.2
Interpreting Case Law

Using precedents requires more than simply finding cases with similar keywords. An AI must be able to perform a sophisticated analysis of case law by distinguishing the
ratio decidendi
(the core, binding legal rule of a judgment) from
obiter dictum
(incidental remarks that are not binding)—a particularly difficult task since the original court does not explicitly label them.

Furthermore, it must be able to conduct a
legally relevant comparison of the facts
between a precedent and the current case. A precedent is only binding if the cases are legally similar in terms of the key facts or at least sufficiently analogous on legally significant points, a form of analogical reasoning that is influenced by societal values and goes far beyond matching semantic similarities via RAG. Legal analogy is not a mechanical process of counting factual similarities but an evaluative judgment about whether the facts of two cases are comparable in
legally relevant
ways. This determination of relevance requires the identification of an underlying normative principle that connects the two cases, a step that moves from the specific facts to an abstract rule. This is one of the most difficult tasks for AI because
ratio decidendi
is an abstract legal concept, not an explicit piece of text. Current AI systems, which excel at identifying semantic patterns in text, struggle to make the independent, evaluative judgments needed to formulate or apply these normative principles
[
22
]
.

3.3
Requirement: Resolving Ambiguity and Incompleteness

Law is not always clear or comprehensive. A key judicial function is to resolve ambiguity and fill gaps in the law, tasks that demand reasoning beyond a formal application of rules.

3.3.1
Adjudicating with General Clauses

Legislators often employ open-textured terms like "reasonable", "fair", or "unconscionable" to delegate discretion to the courts, allowing for flexible, case-by-case adjudication. These provisions, known as
general clauses
, are the legal rules that are deliberately formulated in a non-precise manner. They empower judges to declare cases based on broad principles such as good faith, fairness or public policy when the strict application of a specific rule would lead to an unjust result—or when a specific rule does not exist.

To apply such general clauses, an AI cannot be a mere
legal formalist
that rigidly follows rules while disregarding non-legal factors. They require an understanding of social norms, ethics, and common sense—knowledge that is not explicitly codified. These general clauses are the antithesis of what LLMs do well. They cannot be solved with just better information retrieval since there is no reference material for general clauses in every context, nor with agentic systems since the definition of the open-textured terminology cannot be broken down to well-defined factors.

3.3.2
Addressing Conflicting Provisions and Gaps in the Law

In cases of a
lacuna in law
(a gap where no specific rule exists), the AI must be able to reason by analogy from related provisions or derive a solution from the general principles that underpin that area of law. This requires a deep, systemic understanding of the entire legal framework. While an LLM can process vast amounts of text, it struggles to track the dependencies and interactions across a large number of factors such as laws and principles simultaneously
[
23
]
. This is a critical point of failure for AI.

3.4
Requirement: Performing Reliable Evidence Evaluation

Distinct from interpreting legal norms is the foundational task of establishing the facts of the case. Reliability and accuracy are of utmost importance in evidence evaluation as countless other procedures in law depend on the facts of the case. Even though LLMs have shown promising early results in evaluating evidence, considerable challenges remain unsolved in reliability and explainability
[
24
]
.

3.4.1
Assessing Factual Truthfulness

The ability to reason about law is meaningless if one cannot ground that reasoning in a correct understanding of the facts. LLMs face a fundamental challenge in evaluating the truthfulness of information such as evidence
[
25
]
. Their knowledge is derived from their training data and provided context, not from lived experience or an innate sense of real-world plausibility. Determining whether a specific witness is credible or whether a sequence of events described in a document is physically possible often cannot be inferred from the textual case documents alone. Assessing witness credibility involves evaluating non-verbal cues, demeanor, consistency, and potential biases—factors that extend far beyond the analysis of a written transcript. While AI tools are being developed to assist lawyers in witness preparation by analyzing speech patterns, emotional tone, and even micro-expressions, these are assistive technologies and are not capable of making definitive judgments about deception or truthfulness
[
26
]
. Evaluating real-world plausibility requires common-sense reasoning, an area where LLMs continue to show significant limitations, as they lack the embodied experience and intuitive understanding of the physical and social world that humans possess
[
27
]
. Evidence evaluation involves piecing together scraps of information with no authoritative source of truth available for the LLM’s reference, increasing the risk of hallucination
[
28
]
.

3.4.2
Applying the Burden of Proof

When the evidence presented is insufficient or contradictory, a case is not left undecided. Instead, it is adjudicated according to the
burden of proof
, which prescribes which party bears the negative consequences of an unproven fact. The burden of proof is a fundamental legal principle for resolving uncertainty when the facts of a case cannot be definitively established. An AI must be able to navigate these critical rules. In criminal cases, it must understand the high standard of
"beyond reasonable doubt"
and acquit if the prosecution fails to meet it. In civil cases, it must apply the more complex, often
unwritten principles
that govern which party must prove a given claim.

An AI system that attempts to render a decision based on insufficient evidence, rather than correctly identifying the issue as "unproven" and applying the burden of proof, would fundamentally undermine procedural justice. The legal concept of the burden of proof is, at its core, a sophisticated mechanism for managing epistemic uncertainty. To apply it correctly, a decision-maker must first be able to assess its own level of certainty and recognize when the evidence is insufficient to meet the required threshold. This points to a critical technical limitation of current models as LLMs are notoriously poor at this kind of self-assessment. Their confidence scores are often misaligned with their actual accuracy, a problem known as poor calibration, and models frequently exhibit overconfidence, expressing high certainty in answers that are factually incorrect
[
29
]
.

3.5
Requirement: Upholding Procedural Fairness

Finally, the legitimacy of a judicial decision rests not only on the quality of its reasoning but also on the fairness of the process used to reach it.

3.5.1
Ensuring the Right to Be Heard (
Audiatur et Altera Pars
)

A cornerstone of a fair trial is the principle that both parties must have an opportunity to be heard and to contest the evidence and arguments presented by the other side. The use of AI introduces a new layer to this requirement. Prompt engineering is a critical skill where subtle changes in wording, context or structure can lead to vastly different outcomes
[
30
]
, and with the exact same prompt, different models produce different outcomes depending on their training data
[
31
]
. In an adversarial system, these choices would be subject to intense scrutiny. The principle of procedural fairness dictates that if an AI’s output is to be used as evidence or to support a judicial decision, the process that generated that output must be transparent and open to challenge. The specific prompt used to generate a legal analysis is no longer just a query; it becomes a piece of discoverable evidence, and the choice of a particular AI model is a methodological decision comparable to an expert witness selecting a specific scientific instrument for an analysis. This necessitates a new level of transparency, extending beyond the final output to the entire generative process. A reliable and fair AI-assisted process must therefore be transparent enough to allow for such adversarial probing, ensuring that its methodological choices can be justified and challenged.

4
AI Mechanisms and Their Application to Legal Requirements

Having deconstructed the complex requirements of the judicial process in the previous chapter, we now turn to the technological solutions designed to enhance the capabilities of Large Language Models. The gap between the probabilistic nature of LLMs and the rigorous demands of legal reasoning is significant, but a growing suite of techniques aims to bridge it. This chapter maps these AI enhancement mechanisms to the specific judicial requirements identified, assessing their potential to bring greater structure, reliability, and transparency to AI-assisted legal work. It is important to note that no single technique is a silver bullet; rather, a thoughtful combination of methods targeted at each specific scenario is necessary.

4.1
Domain Adaptation and Factual Grounding

Before an LLM can reason about the law, it must first have reliable access to it. This requires techniques that either adapt the model’s internal parameters to the nuances of the legal domain or ground its responses in external, authoritative sources, thereby enhancing factual accuracy and reducing the risk of hallucination.

•

Fine-tuning:
This method continues the training of a pre-trained model to adapt it to the legal domain’s specific vocabulary, style, and nuances. Fine-tuning with legal domain data has been demonstrated to have "remarkable improvements in various metrics, including the newly proposed Non-Hallucinated Statute Rate, Statute Relevance Rate, Legal Claim Truthfulness"
[
32
]
. It allows an LLM to obtain a deeper foundation in legal domain knowledge and is useful specifically in grasping the structure and typical language for
3.3.1
Adjudicating with General Clauses
(
3.3.1
)
. However, fine-tuning by itself is not enough to resolve the requirements discussed due to the variable and open-textured nature of legal context which often lacks authoritative single truth. It also requires a high-quality dataset and risks "catastrophic forgetting" which degrades the model’s performance on other capabilities that were not in focus of the fine-tuning process
[
33
]
.

•

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):
This is the foundational technology for grounding an LLM’s responses with factual context and is an active subject of research in the legal domain
[
34
]
. It uses various methods to locate relevant information in an external knowledge base (e.g., statutes, case law) and provides the retrieved content to the LLM as context, making it a powerful tool in scenarios that require pinpointing relevant documents, cases or other information from a large corpus. RAG is essential for tasks such as
3.1
Requirement: Selecting the Correct Legal Framework
(
3.1
)
and
3.2.1
Adhering to the Doctrine of the Sources of Law
(
3.2.1
)
which require collecting a large number of relevant documents before a decision on e.g. the right jurisdiction can be made. It also significantly reduces hallucinations (though does not eliminate them)
[
35
]
. On the downside, its effectiveness is only as good as the quality of the retrieval system and the knowledge base content. Additionally, the most common RAG methods rely on semantic similarity rather than legal similarity which may cause it to overlook relevant documents.

4.2
Structured Reasoning and Deliberation

A legally defensible argument requires not only access to correct information but also a transparent and logical application of it. This can be supported with methods that instill this discipline by structuring the LLM’s cognitive process. They guide the model to move from simple probabilistic text generation toward more systematic and multifaceted reasoning, thereby producing a more explicit and verifiable logical trail for legal scrutiny.

•

Task Decomposition and Chained Prompts:
This technique breaks a complex problem into a series of narrower sub-tasks which are then sequentially processed using a chain of carefully crafted prompts. This increases accuracy and reduces hallucinations by allowing the model to take more manageable steps and has been shown to improve performance in legal reasoning tasks
[
36
]
. This is effective for processes that have a clear, sequential logic. For example, it can be used in
3.1.1
Navigating International and Cross-Border Jurisdictions
(
3.1.1
)
by first prompting the AI to determine jurisdiction, then to identify the applicable law in a separate step. This approach fails when a legal problem requires a holistic evaluation and different factors must be weighed simultaneously, without the ability to break them down into narrower, sequential steps.

•

Tree of Thoughts (ToT):
When faced with a general clause like "reasonable" this technique encourages the model to explore and merge multiple branches of reasoning simultaneously. It creates a more reliable and comprehensive reasoning structure by allowing the AI to map out pro and contra arguments for different interpretations, making Tree of Thoughts useful in e.g.
3.3.1
Adjudicating with General Clauses
(
3.3.1
)
which requires thorough consideration rather than referring to authoritative sources of truth
[
37
]
. However, this method is only useful when the correct response can be achieved with reasoning alone.

4.3
Hybrid and Agentic Architectures

While the previously mentioned techniques enhance a single model, some legal challenges require capabilities that a monolithic LLM cannot provide alone, requiring more complex systems.

•

Neuro-Symbolic AI:
In this hybrid approach, an LLM acts as an "observation engine" to extract facts and rules, which are then processed by a classic, rule-based symbolic AI. This brings consistency and controllability to decisions by combining the LLM’s powerful language understanding with the deterministic logic of a rule-based system. For example, in
3.1.2
Respecting National Legal Hierarchies and Rules
(
3.1.2
)
, the system can first use an LLM to extract the jurisdiction and date from conflicting documents, then use manually crafted if-else rules to determine which document prevails. However, it requires the scenario to be one where the symbolic rules can be comprehensively and deterministically defined, and a human expert to rigorously craft those rules for different scenarios. The rules could also be created with the LLM itself if they can be confirmed to be correct with a validated dataset.

•

Multi-Agent Systems:
This collaborative approach deploys multiple specialized LLM-based agents that delegate and complete tasks as a "team". This architecture can handle highly complex tasks by automatically orchestrating iterations involving information retrieval, reasoning, and self-review. One agent could be tasked with finding jurisdictional rules, another with substantive law, and a third with reviewing for conflicts, addressing many of the challenges related to
3.1
Requirement: Selecting the Correct Legal Framework
(
3.1
)
. It could also simulate an adversarial process to strengthen arguments, contributing to
3.5
Requirement: Upholding Procedural Fairness
(
3.5
)

Output Verification and Refinement

Given the probabilistic nature of LLMs, their outputs cannot be accepted without scrutiny. The final and perhaps most critical step in ensuring reliability is the verification of the generated content. This requires methods that enable the system to automatically review its own work for factual consistency or to assess its own confidence in a response, providing a crucial safeguard against error and a necessary component for responsible deployment in legal practice.

•

Structured Self-Evaluation:
This involves prompting the LLM to create a list of evaluation factors to determine if its own response is consistent and truthful against provided source material and then evaluating those factors individually. 5
Conclusion

The preceding chapters have deconstructed the intricate process of judicial reasoning, pointing out that in complicated cases, the "Rule" and "Application" phases of the IRAC pattern encompass tasks that are challenging for LLMs due to the wide discretion required on many levels. The analysis reveals gaps between the fundamental probabilistic nature of LLMs and the rigorous, choice-driven nature of legal adjudication. The crucial question is whether LLMs can make justified legal choices—in accordance with choice of law rules, between several interpretation alternatives, or to fill a lacuna in law—and, critically, express the reasoning chain, showing how arguments and counterarguments were weighted. Without this transparency, it is impossible to ascertain the due course of reasoning.

5.1
The Evolving Symbiosis of Law and AI

This paper has focused on the most challenging situations and aspects of law, a review that may seem unfair to the broader potential of LLMs, as many judicial cases are in practice rather simple. It is more productive to view AI not as a monolithic replacement for human legal interpreters, but as a powerful tool whose application must be carefully tailored to the complexity of the task. The most effective current role for AI in law can therefore be understood through a dual-application model: as a high-volume assistant for simple cases and as a sophisticated "sparring partner" for experts in complex matters.

First, in simple high-volume legal cases where only a few points must be considered, LLMs can work well. Matters such as small claims procedures or consumer disputes concerning product defects often feature repetitive fact patterns and are governed by an extensive body of case law. For example, small claims procedures with disputes between a consumer and a vendor concerning a product defect in goods (with a value of no more than, e.g., €10,000), are an ideal environment for e.g. Neuro-Symbolic AI systems, allowing for high quality automation with safeguards in place. If a party wants to challenge the response of the system, a human operator can easily validate the details extracted by the LLM and whether the symbolic logic is correctly applied.

Second, regarding most complex civil and criminal litigation, there arises a deep barrier and the fundamental mismatch between the peculiar character of legal reasoning and the probabilistic nature of the core generator, LLM. Yet, even in these tricky cases, LLMs can assist legal decision-making by saving time in document management, digging for arguments, and enhancing efficiency in many ways. Here, the LLM is not an adjudicator but an invaluable collaborator for the human lawyer or judge, helping to challenge arguments and enhance the coherence of a legal strategy.

5.2
Synthesis and Future Directions

Putting it straightforwardly, the current generation of LLMs excels when legal tasks can be distilled into sophisticated information retrieval and pattern recognition. They serve best in cases that have abundant reference material, rely on clearly defined concepts as opposed to open-textured standards, and can be broken down into narrow, sequential sub-tasks. When these conditions are not met, a fundamental barrier remains: the gap between the probabilistic nature of language models and the principled, choice-driven nature of judicial reasoning.

This is not a poor outcome. The successful automation of high-volume, procedurally simple cases has potential to free up significant human resources for more complex legal challenges. In any case, the path toward broader application in complex litigation requires a focused effort to develop systems capable of justifiable reasoning. Future research needs to prioritize creating AI with auditable decision chains, reliable mechanisms for self-assessing uncertainty to respect legal principles, and hybrid architectures that can enforce the rigid hierarchies and non-negotiable rules of legal doctrine. Despite the challenges, we remain optimistic that the current trajectory of technological advancement will bring these capabilities within reach in the future.
