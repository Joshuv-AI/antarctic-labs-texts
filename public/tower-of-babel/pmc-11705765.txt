Development of fluorescence lifetime biosensors for ATP, cAMP, citrate, and glucose using the mTurquoise2-based platform
Authors: Chongxia Zhong, Satoshi Arai, Yasushi Okada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle-fluorescent protein (FP)-based FLIM (fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy) biosensors can visualize intracellular processes quantitatively. They require a single wavelength for detection, which facilitates multi-color imaging. However, their development has been limited by the absence of a general design framework and complex screening processes. In this study, we engineered FLIM biosensors for ATP (adenosine triphosphate), cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate), citrate, and glucose by inserting each sensing domain into mTurquoise2 (mTQ2) between Tyr-145 and Phe-146 using peptide linkers. Fluorescence intensity-based screening yielded FLIM biosensors with a 0.5 to 1.0 ns dynamic range upon analyte binding, demonstrating that the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone is a versatile platform for FLIM biosensors, allowing for simple intensity-based screening while providing dual-functional biosensors for both FLIM and intensity-based imaging. As a proof of concept, we monitored cAMP and Ca2+ dynamics simultaneously in living cells by dual-color imaging. Our results complement recent studies, establishing mTQ2 as a valuable framework for developing FLIM biosensors.

Introduction

Fluorescent protein (FP)-based biosensors play a crucial role in cell biology by offering dynamic insights into the localization and quantity of signaling molecules and metabolites. Currently, fluorescence intensity-based biosensors are widely used in two designs: single-FP-based intensiometric biosensors and two-FP-based fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) biosensors. The single-FP-based intensiometric biosensors report their target analytes based on changes in fluorescence intensity.1,2 However, intensiometric biosensors are susceptible to various experimental factors affecting the fluorescent signal intensity, such as photobleaching, probe concentration, excitation intensity, and sample thickness, compromising measurement accuracy. FRET-based biosensors use the emission ratio of two FPs to avoid these issues and report the analyte more quantitatively.3 Cyan FP (CFP) and yellow FP (YFP) pairs have been versatile platforms for FRET-based biosensors for various analytes or reactions.4,5,6 However, these FRET-based biosensors use two FPs for one biosensor, which limits the multiplex measurement with multiple biosensors for different analytes. Moreover, the differences in the maturation and degradation kinetics of the two FPs can bias the measurements.7

Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) provides a more robust quantitative imaging approach, as fluorescence lifetime (τ)—an intrinsic property of the fluorophore—is not affected by the aforementioned experimental factors.8 Genetically encoded FLIM biosensors fall into two categories: FRET-FLIM biosensors and single-FP-based FLIM biosensors. FRET-FLIM biosensors utilize changes in fluorescence lifetime caused by energy transfer between a donor and an acceptor fluorophore to detect and quantify analytes.9 However, their design using two FP pairs poses similar limitations to the intensity-based FRET biosensors mentioned above: a potential bias due to the differences in maturation and degradation rates between the two FPs and limitations in multiplex measurements.

Single-FP-based FLIM biosensors can overcome these drawbacks of the FRET-FLIM biosensors.10 Several single-FP-based FLIM biosensors have been engineered so far, such as FLIM biosensors for sensing the NADH/NAD+ ratio,11 adenosine triphosphate (ATP),12 glucose,13 Ca2+,14 and lactate.15 However, the number of single-FP-based FLIM biosensors remains limited compared to that of intensity-based biosensors.

This study aims to expand the FLIM biosensor toolbox by developing single-FP-based FLIM biosensors through an easy-to-implement screening approach combined with a versatile platform. We chose a CFP, mTQ2 (mTurquoise2), as the scaffold for constructing FLIM biosensors to sense intracellular signaling molecules. mTQ2 boasts a long mono-exponential fluorescence lifetime (4.0 ns) and a high quantum yield (QY; 0.93).16 The long mono-exponential fluorescence lifetime of mTQ2 was achieved by ensuring the planarity of the chromophore and restricting its rotational freedom through strong van der Waals interactions with surrounding amino acids.16 Based on these features and insights from two preceding studies,14,15 we inferred that perturbations to the beta-can amino acid residues surrounding the chromophore would increase the non-radiative relaxation, thus decreasing the lifetime and QY.

Additionally, the fluorescence of mTQ2 remains stable across a wide pH range due to its low pKa of 3.1, suggesting that mTQ2-based fluorescent biosensors may show high stability against physiological pH fluctuations. Reported mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors, such as Tq-Ca-FLITS and Lilac, alter both intensity and lifetime in response to analytes.14,15 This likely reflects the fact that mTQ2 has a minimal non-radiative decay (long lifetime and high QY). For other FPs, changes in fluorescence intensity and lifetime may not necessarily coincide, as their non-radiative decay rates might be higher and more variable. Lifetime changes do not correlate well with the intensity changes in other fluorescent biosensors, such as the green FP (GFP)-based GCaMP6 and jGCaMP7 (Dana et al.17) and the red FP (RFP)-based R-GECO1.18

In this paper, we propose mTQ2 as a versatile platform for single-FP-based FLIM biosensors suitable for a fluorescence intensity-based screening approach. As a proof of concept, we first examined the properties of an mTQ2-based FLIM biosensor for ATP. Subsequently, we validated our biosensor design platform and screening strategy by developing FLIM biosensors for sensing cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), citrate, and glucose.

Results

Construction, characterization, and validation of the FLIM biosensor for ATP

ATP provides energy to drive many essential biological processes in living organisms. ATP biosensors are valuable tools for evaluating the spatiotemporal dynamics of ATP within a single cell. We decided to begin with ATP FLIM biosensor construction because our team has previously established methods for engineering intensiometric ATP biosensors,19 and a YFP-based FLIM biosensor for ATP detection was reported recently by our group.12 An mTQ2-based ATP FLIM biosensor would expand the color variants and offer potential for in vivo imaging using two-photon microscopy.

We constructed the ATP FLIM biosensor by inserting the epsilon subunit of B. subtilis FoF1 ATP synthase into the mTQ2 protein between Tyr-145 and Phe-146 and designated it as qmTQ2-ATP-0 (quantitative mTurquoise2-based ATP biosensor v.0) (Figure S1A). The epsilon subunit of FoF1 ATP synthase specifically binds to ATP without hydrolyzing it and undergoes a significant conformational change upon ATP binding.19,20 The insertion site was determined according to previous studies21,22 to ensure that the sensing domain would be in close proximity to the chromophore of mTQ2 upon folding into its tertiary structure. We expected that the ATP-dependent conformational change in the epsilon subunit would influence the chromophore’s environment, resulting in alterations in its fluorescence properties, including fluorescence lifetime.

The fluorescence intensity or lifetime of bacterial lysate of qmTQ2-ATP-0 did not change in the presence of 10 mM ATP. Previous studies indicated that modifying the linker length and/or the type of amino acids in the linker sequence between the sensing domain and the FP could enhance the dynamic range of fluorescent indicators.23 Therefore, we performed a linker screening on the qmTQ2-ATP-0 construct.

The amino acid sequence (ALKKEL) from the NZ leucine zipper motif24 was adopted as the basis of the N-terminal linker (linker 1) and C-terminal linker (linker 2). We anticipated that the strong ⍺ helical structure of the NZ leucine zipper would facilitate a more efficient transfer of the conformational change of the epsilon subunit to the chromophore, thereby improving the dynamic range of the ATP FLIM biosensor.19 Linker libraries were created with 0- to 6-amino-acid insertions in both linkers (Figure S1A).

Screening the bacterial lysates from the linker library for ΔF/F0 using a fluorescence plate reader led to the identification of a mutant, which showed a 5% fluorescence intensity change in response to 10 mM ATP (Figure S1C). This mutant, named qmTQ2-ATP-0.1, contains a 5-amino-acid insertion (ALKKE) in linker 2 (Figure S1B).

Previous studies reported that Tyr-145 in GFP plays an important role in enhancing the performance of fluorescent biosensors since it is sterically adjacent to the chromophore.21 To expand the dynamic range of the qmTQ2-ATP-0.1 mutant, further optimization was pursued through site-saturation mutagenesis targeting Tyr-145. Initially, we screened the bacterial lysates for ΔF/F0 using a fluorescence plate reader. Subsequently, we measured the fluorescence lifetime change (Δτ) of the bacterial lysates from the mutants showing significant ΔF/F0.

The screening yielded a mutant displaying a 19% reduction in fluorescence intensity and a 0.6 ns decrease in fluorescence lifetime in response to 10 mM ATP. This mutant, designated as qmTQ2-ATP-0.2, featured the Y145K substitution (Figures S1D–S1G).

Subsequent site-saturation mutagenesis targeting the glycine residue of linker 1 resulted in the identification of qmTQ2-ATP-0.3, which showed a 25% decrease in fluorescence intensity and a 0.9 ns reduction in fluorescence lifetime upon exposure to 10 mM ATP (Figures S1H–S1K). In qmTQ2-ATP-0.3, the glycine of linker 1 was mutated to proline alongside the Y145K substitution.

Using qmTQ2-ATP-0.3 as the template, a third round of site-saturation mutagenesis targeting the threonine residue of linker 1 was conducted, and a mutant with a 39% fluorescence intensity decrease and a 1.1 ns decrease in fluorescence lifetime in the presence of 10 mM ATP was obtained. Furthermore, the QY of the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor decreased from 0.71 to 0.47 upon ATP addition, while the molar extinction coefficient (ε) showed only a slight change (Table 1). The fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor altered in response to ATP in a dose-dependent manner, with a dissociation constant (KD) estimated at 1.3 ± 0.2 mM (Figures 1E and S2A). Additionally, the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor remained insensitive to pH changes within the range of 6.0–9.0 (Figure 1F), indicating its suitability for quantitative intracellular imaging. Moreover, qmTQ2-ATP showed specificity for ATP compared to other nucleotides (Figure 1G).

Next, we validated the performance of the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor by expressing it in HeLa cells. Thirty minutes following treatment with 20 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), a glycolysis inhibitor, the fluorescence lifetime of qmTQ2-ATP increased by approximately 0.1 ns compared to the control group, indicating that qmTQ2-ATP could detect changes in intracellular ATP levels induced by glycolysis inhibition (Figures 1H and 1I).

The ATP level in HeLa cells was estimated to be 3.4 mM.12,26 The KD of qmTQ2-ATP is not optimal for detecting ATP levels in HeLa cells, which explains the relatively small changes in the fluorescence lifetime of qmTQ2-ATP in HeLa cells treated with 2-DG. Affinity tuning for specific applications (temperature and ATP concentration range) would be required by introducing mutations to the ATP sensor domain of the epsilon subunit, as described in previous reports.7,26,27

Similar to the circular permutation mutant mTQ2-based Tq-Ca-FLITS previously reported,14 the qmTQ2-ATP design, which inserts the sensor domain between amino acids 145 and 146 of mTQ2, also exhibited a nearly linear correlation between changes in fluorescence intensity and fluorescence lifetime (Table S2; Figure 2). To explore the utility of this insertion design—specifically, the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone (where X represents the sensing domain)—as a generalizable platform for engineering a suite of mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors, we investigated its versatility by substituting the X region with sensing domains for other signaling molecules and metabolites.

Construction, characterization, and validation of the cAMP FLIM biosensor

cAMP is a widely conserved intracellular second messenger. Its concentration is tightly controlled by the balance of adenylyl cyclase (AC) and phosphodiesterase (PDE) activities in mammalian cells.28 Jalink’s research group engineered Epac (the exchange protein directly activated by cAMP)-based FRET-FLIM biosensors to measure changes in cellular cAMP levels.5,9,29 To our knowledge, a single-FP-based FLIM indicator for cAMP has not been reported.

We engineered an mTQ2-based cAMP FLIM biosensor by inserting the cAMP-binding domain of mouse EPAC123 into mTQ2 between Tyr-145 and Phe-146. The resulting construct, qmTQ2-cAMP-0 (quantitative mTurquoise2-based cAMP biosensor v.0) (Figure S3A), showed a 1.4% increase in fluorescence intensity upon adding 1 mM cAMP. By screening the bacterial lysate of the ALKKEL-motif-based linker library for ΔF/F0, a mutant that showed an 8% decrease in fluorescence intensity in response to 1 mM cAMP was found (Figure S3B). This mutant, designated as qmTQ2-cAMP-0.1, contains a 2-amino-acid (AL) insertion in linker 2 (Figure S3A).

Subsequent site-saturation mutagenesis aimed at Tyr-145 of qmTQ2-cAMP-0.1 led to the identification of a mutant displaying a 23% elevation in fluorescence intensity and a 0.6 ns increase in fluorescence lifetime upon exposure to 1 mM cAMP. The emission and excitation spectra of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor increased in amplitude by 23% upon the addition of 1 mM cAMP (Figure 3B), accompanied by a fluorescence lifetime change from 3.0 (cAMP-free state) to 3.6 ns (cAMP-bound state) (Figure 3D). The alteration in the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor absorption spectra was small between the presence and absence of cAMP (Figure 3C). The specificity of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor for cAMP was examined by performing cAMP and cGMP (cyclic guanosine monophosphate) titration (Figure 3E). The determined KD values for cAMP and cGMP were 5.8 ± 1.2 and 228.9 ± 28.3 μM, respectively (Figures S2B and S2C), indicating the high specificity of the qmTQ2-cAMP sensor for cAMP and the low affinity for cGMP.

The range of cAMP concentrations detected by this indicator spanned from 1 to 100 μM (Figure 3E), covering both basal and elevated cAMP concentrations reported in mammalian cells.28,30 Furthermore, the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor remained insensitive to pH changes between 7.0 and 8.0, suggesting its suitability for quantitative intracellular imaging (Figure 3F). We calibrated the qmTQ2-cAMP sensor in cellular environments using permeabilized HeLa cells (Figure S2D). The sensor exhibited a larger lifetime response from 2.8 (without cAMP) to 3.6 ns (with cAMP) and a lower-affinity KD = 6.5 ± 3.2 μM, reflecting the effects of the cellular environment. This result exemplifies the importance of in situ calibration for accurate quantitative measurements.

Subsequently, we evaluated the intracellular performance of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor. In COS7 cells expressing qmTQ2-cAMP, the fluorescence was uniformly distributed in the cytoplasm but excluded from the nucleus (Figure 3G). The pseudo-colored images in Figure 3G illustrate the FLIM response of the biosensor before and at different time points post-stimulation with 50 μM forskolin. The fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor increased significantly shortly after forskolin application (Figure 3H), as forskolin activates adenylate cyclase, leading to an elevation in intracellular cAMP concentrations.

Furthermore, we evaluated the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor in COS7 cells using a β-adrenergic receptor agonist, isoproterenol. The results indicated an increase in the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor upon 100 μM isoproterenol stimulation (Figures 3I and 3J), demonstrating its capability to monitor cAMP dynamics in live cells under physiological conditions. We found no correlation between basal fluorescence intensity and lifetime of qmTQ2-cAMP in COS7 cells (Figure S4), suggesting that fluorescent lifetime is not influenced by the expression level of qmTQ2-cAMP. This finding highlights its suitability as a quantitative biosensor.

The results of qmTQ2-ATP and qmTQ2-cAMP indicate that we have identified an insertion site (between Tyr-145 and Phe-146) within mTQ2 that has the capability to transform the interaction signal between the sensing domain and the analyte into a change in the fluorescence lifetime of the chromophore.

Construction and characterization of FLIM biosensors for citrate and glucose

To further prove the versatility of our engineering approach for mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors, we developed FLIM biosensors for citrate and glucose using the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone.

To engineer a citrate FLIM biosensor, the sensing domain for citrate (the citrate-binding periplasmic domain [CitAP] of Klebsiella pneumoniae CitA)31 was integrated into mTQ2 between Tyr-145 and Phe-146. The resulting qmTQ2-citrate-0 (quantitative mTurquoise2-based citrate biosensor v.0) showed a 5% increase in fluorescence intensity in the presence of 20 mM citrate. Facile linker optimization based on the ALKKEL motif was conducted to improve the dynamic range, resulting in a mutant that exhibited a 15% increase in fluorescence intensity and a 0.7 ns fluorescence lifetime change upon exposure to 20 mM citrate. The emission and excitation spectra of the qmTQ2-citrate biosensor increased in amplitude by 19% upon citrate saturation (Figure 4B), with the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-citrate biosensor increasing from 2.2 (citrate-free state) to 3.0 ns (citrate-bound state) (Figure 4D). A minimal change in the absorption spectra of the qmTQ2-citrate biosensor was observed with or without citrate (Figure 4C). The qmTQ2-citrate biosensor changes its fluorescence lifetime in response to citrate in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 4E), with the estimated KD value being 4.6 ± 0.29 mM (Figure S2E). Notably, the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-citrate biosensor remains insensitive to pH between 6.5 and 7.5 (Figure 4F). Moreover, the qmTQ2-citrate biosensor exhibited specificity to citrate over other metabolites (Figure 4G).

The glucose-sensing domain bacterial D-galactose-binding periplasmic protein (MglB)32 was inserted into the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone to construct the glucose FLIM biosensor. Subsequent ALKKEL-motif-based linker screening performed on the resulting qmTQ2-glucose-0 (quantitative mTurquoise2-based glucose biosensor v.0) yielded a mutant with a 16% decrease in fluorescence intensity and a 0.3 ns fluorescence lifetime change in response to 10 mM glucose. This mutant, featuring a 2-amino-acid (AL) insertion in linker 2, was designated as the qmTQ2-glucose biosensor (Figure 4H).

Upon the addition of 10 mM glucose, the emission and excitation spectra of the qmTQ2-glucose biosensor decreased in amplitude by 40% (Figure 4I), accompanied by a fluorescence lifetime change from 3.0 (glucose-free state) to 2.5 ns (glucose-bound state) (Figure 4K). However, the change in the absorption spectra of the qmTQ2-glucose biosensor was minimal with or without glucose (Figure 4J). The qmTQ2-glucose biosensor changes its fluorescence lifetime in response to glucose in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 4L), and the KD value was estimated to be 13.8 ± 2 μM (Figure S2F). Additionally, the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-glucose biosensor remains insensitive to pH between 7.0 and 7.5 (Figure 4M). Furthermore, the qmTQ2-glucose biosensor showed specificity to glucose and galactose over other monosaccharides and glucose-metabolism-related molecules (Figure 4N).

In the case of the citrate and glucose biosensors, a sufficient dynamic range was achieved through linker screening alone, without the need for site-saturation mutagenesis of Tyr-145. However, it is worth noting that further optimization through Tyr-145 site-saturation mutagenesis could potentially yield biosensors with even greater dynamic ranges. This underscores the versatility and potential for improvement inherent in our approach. By replacing the X region of the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone with analyte-sensing domains, combined with linker screening and site-saturation mutagenesis targeting Tyr-145 and linker 1, mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors for various analytes could be developed with optimized performance characteristics.

Dual-color imaging with qmTQ2-cAMP and RCaMP1h biosensors

A single-FP FLIM biosensor has a significant advantage over a FRET-FLIM biosensor in multi-color imaging, requiring only a single wavelength for detection. To explore the potential of dual-color imaging, we prepared HeLa cells that co-expressed the qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor and a red calcium FLIM biosensor, RCaMP1h.33 Upon simultaneous treatment of the HeLa cells with 100 μM isoproterenol and 50 μM histamine dihydrochloride, the fluorescence lifetimes of both qmTQ2-cAMP and RCaMP1h increased (Figures 5A–5C). The increase in RCaMP1h lifetime indicated an elevated Ca2+ concentration following histamine stimulation, while the elevation in qmTQ2-cAMP lifetime reflected an augmented cAMP level induced by isoproterenol treatment. Histamine induces calcium oscillation via H1 receptor binding, leading to the generation of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 then binds to IP3 receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, prompting the release of calcium ions from the ER lumen into the cytoplasm. Our results suggest that the surge in cAMP levels might inhibit the Ca2+ signals triggered by histamine, as evidenced by the simultaneous increase in qmTQ2-cAMP lifetime and decrease in RCaMP1h lifetime (Figure S5). Previous studies have shown that isoproterenol suppresses histamine-evoked Ca2+ signals in bronchial airway smooth muscle cells through the cAMP and protein kinase A (PKA) pathway.34

To further validate the inhibitory effect of isoproterenol on histamine-induced calcium oscillation in HeLa cells, we performed sequential treatments on HeLa cells co-expressing qmTQ2-cAMP and RCaMP1h biosensors with 100 μM isoproterenol followed by 10 μM histamine. In cells treated solely with histamine, the fluorescence lifetime of RCaMP1h at its maximal peak was 1.5 ± 0.3 ns. However, pre-treatment with isoproterenol significantly reduced this peak lifetime to approximately 1.04 ± 0.2 ns (Figures 5D and 5E). This result suggests that isoproterenol inhibited histamine-evoked Ca2+ signals by reducing their maximal peak rise in HeLa cells. Based on the in-cell calibration information of the qmTQ2-cAMP sensor (Figure S2D), the basal cAMP concentration in the group treated simultaneously with histamine and isoproterenol was estimated to be 0.17 ± 0.003 μM. Following the stimulation, the cAMP concentration increased to 2.42 ± 0.13 μM. In the sequential treatment group, the basal cAMP level was 0.24 ± 0.005 μM, which rose to 5.87 ± 0.21 μM after stimulation. Given the known crosstalk between Ca2+ and cAMP signaling pathways at multiple levels,35 simultaneously monitoring both molecules will offer deeper insights into the intricate crosstalk between these two messengers.

Discussion

In this paper, by adopting the conventional fluorescence intensity-based biosensor screening strategy, we successfully developed mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors for ATP, cAMP, glucose, and citrate. We validated qmTQ2-ATP and qmTQ2-cAMP biosensors in live-cell applications. Additionally, we conducted dual-color imaging using qmTQ2-cAMP and RCaMP1h, demonstrating their efficacy. Notably, our results indicated that isoproterenol attenuated histamine-induced Ca2+ signals in HeLa cells by reducing their maximum peak rise.

The interplay between Ca2+ and cAMP signaling is a commonly discussed topic in various research fields, including neurobiology and physiology.36 The quantitative imaging method presented in this study will significantly contribute to elucidating the reciprocal regulation between Ca2+ and cAMP signaling circuits. Recent advancements in multiplexing, especially with colorful intensiometric biosensors, facilitate the simultaneous observation of the spatiotemporal dynamics of signaling molecules within the same cell.18,19 Despite the expanded availability of biosensors, their findings have only captured relative changes in signaling molecules over time. Conversely, multiplex FLIM imaging enables the concurrent quantification of various cellular signaling molecules, providing reliable concentration information.

In addition, mTQ2-based biosensors would be suitable for in vivo imaging using two-photon excitation. Our mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors could offer quantitative insights both at the single-cell level and in vivo. Furthermore, since fluorescence lifetime is a parameter insensitive to changes in expression levels and photobleaching, FLIM biosensors are eligible for long-term imaging, provided that sufficient photons can be acquired.

We further addressed the underlying sensing mechanism of mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors. Using fluorescence lifetime and QY, we estimated the radiative rate constant (kr) and the non-radiative rate constant (knr) of the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor, along with two mutants, qmTQ2-ATP-0.2 and qmTQ2-ATP-0.3 (Table S1), according to the two formulas kr = QY/τ and knr = (1 − QY)/τ. Following ATP binding, the knr of the qmTQ2-ATP mutants increased significantly, while minimal changes were observed in the kr, indicating that an ATP-induced conformational change would increase the non-radiative decay, with little effect on the radiative pathway. Consequently, the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-ATP mutants decreases upon ATP binding, as fluorescence lifetime is inversely proportional to the sum of the knr and kr of the chromophore. Notably, similar trends were observed with other mTQ2-based biosensors for detecting cAMP, glucose, and citrate (Table 1; Figure 2). In these instances, alterations in fluorescence lifetime induced by analyte binding were primarily driven by changes in knr, while kr and the molar absorption coefficient were barely affected.

To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanism behind the fluorescence lifetime responsiveness of mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors, understanding the structure of mTQ2 is crucial. The long mono-exponential fluorescence lifetime of mTQ2 was achieved by ensuring the planarity of the chromophore and restricting its rotational freedom through strong van der Waals interactions with surrounding amino acids.16 Moreover, mTQ2 has an exceptionally low pKa of 3.1, so the protonation state of its chromophore is unlikely to be influenced by the analyte-induced conformational changes in the biosensor.14 Consequently, the changes in the ε would be minimal upon analyte binding, as supported by our experimental data (Tables 1 and S1).

Here, the citrate biosensor exhibited slightly larger changes in its excitation spectrum and molar absorption coefficient compared to other biosensors developed in this study. This observation suggests that depending on the structure of the sensor domain, there may be some influence on the chromophore structure of mTQ2. A more detailed investigation based on molecular structure analysis would be necessary to elucidate the precise mechanisms behind these subtle differences.

These features lead to the strong correlation between the fluorescence intensity and the lifetime (Figure 2), making mTQ2 an ideal scaffold for developing FLIM biosensors, either as a circular permutation-based design (van der Linden et al.14) or the insertion design in this study.

Taking these features into account, a key aspect of the sensing mechanism in mTQ2-based turn-off FLIM biosensors, such as qmTQ2-ATP and qmTQ2-glucose, could be attributed to the increased mobility of the chromophore upon analyte binding. Specifically, the accelerated free rotation of the chromophore, without concurrent alteration in its charged states, facilitates the dissipation of energy released from the excited state through a vibrational relaxation process. Conversely, for mTQ2-based turn-on biosensors like qmTQ2-cAMP and qmTQ2-citrate, the sensing mechanism involves a reduction in chromophore mobility upon analyte binding.

These photochemical attributes of mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors, wherein fluorescence intensity changes occur while the ε value (absorption) remains unaffected, could serve as a distinguishing feature for identifying FLIM-based biosensors. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that mTQ2, with its long fluorescence lifetime, might have the potential for developing FLIM biosensors with expanded dynamic ranges. This is attributed to the unique molecular environment surrounding the chromophore,16 offering a versatile platform for the development of various FLIM biosensors.

It should be noted that the biosensors developed by this strategy have dual functionality. They respond to the analytes through changes in both fluorescence intensity and lifetime. An FLIM setup is advised but not required to use these biosensors. The same biosensor can be used with conventional fluorescence microscopes for simpler intensiometric experiments. Researchers can then use an FLIM setup for more precise or quantitative analyses. Furthermore, combining intensity-based high-speed imaging with FLIM-based quantitative imaging could enable applications such as quantitative measurements with high temporal resolution.

These observations suggest that other FPs with high QYs would also serve as good scaffolds for designing FLIM-based biosensors. Recently reported bright and photostable YFP variants, StayGold and its derivatives,37,38,39 with a reported QY of 0.93, show promise in this regard. StayGold, with its spectral separation from mTQ2, is well suited for simultaneous measurements, and its further development is highly anticipated.

Overall, the mTQ2-based FLIM biosensors developed in this study provide tools for visualizing and quantitatively assessing the intracellular dynamics of ATP, cAMP, citrate, and glucose. Additionally, we demonstrated that the mTQ2(1-145)-GT-X-EF-mTQ2(146-238) backbone can be adopted for developing multiple mTQ2-based biosensors. Moreover, we proposed a facile screening strategy for constructing fluorescence lifetime-based biosensors. Our research will facilitate the development of FLIM biosensors for quantitative fluorescence imaging in the future.

Limitations of the study

While this study successfully demonstrated the versatility of mTQ2 as a common backbone for various biosensors, further fine-tuning of each sensor’s sensitivity (KD) is desirable for optimal performance in specific applications. For example, the ATP sensor requires an increased KD for accurate intracellular ATP measurements in animal cells, and the citrate sensor would benefit from a 10-fold increase in affinity to detect physiological concentration changes, while the glucose sensor needs a 10-fold decrease in affinity. These refinements can likely be achieved through targeted mutations in the sensor domains. This approach maintains the integrity of the mTQ2 backbone while allowing for tailored improvements, highlighting the potential for future research to enhance the applicability of these biosensors in diverse cellular environments.

Fluorescence lifetime-based biosensors are sensitive to pH, viscosity, temperature, and refractive index. Accurate quantification requires proper calibration using membrane-permeabilized cells, which can effectively mimic the intracellular conditions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture and expression of DNA constructs

HeLa cells (HeLa Kyoto, RRID: CVCL 1922, kindly provided by Dr. Fumiko Toyoshima, Kyoto University) and COS-7 cells (obtained from RIKEN Cell Bank, RCN0539) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HeLa cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM (041–29775, Wako Pure Chemical, Osaka, Japan). COS7 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (043–30085, Wako Pure Chemical). For imaging experiments, the cells were dissociated with TrypLE Express (12604021, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and then seeded onto 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (P35G-1.5-14-C, MatTek Life Sciences, Massachusetts, USA) coated with collagen I (Cellmatrix Type I-C, 631–00771, Wako Pure Chemical). Once the cells reached 70% confluency, they were transfected with plasmid in the pcDNA3.1(−) vector (0.2 μg plasmid DNA per dish) using TransFectin Lipid Reagent (#170350, Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., California, USA) and OptiMEM (31985070, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was exchanged 4 h after transfection, and the cells were cultured at 37°C for 24 h before imaging.

Method details

Plasmid construction

The cDNA fragment for mTurquoise2 (a generous gift from Dr. Joachim Goedhart, University of Amsterdam) was modified by overlapping extension PCR (polymerase chain reaction) to include KpnI and EcoRI restriction sites between Tyr-145 and Phe-146 of mTQ2. The resultant DNA was cloned into the XhoI/HindIII site of the pRSET-A vector. The resulting construct was named the mTQ2-pRSET-A plasmid.

For the qmTQ2-ATP biosensor cDNA construction, the epsilon subunit of the bacterial FoF1-ATP synthase cDNA (Addgene plasmid #113906) was inserted into mTQ2-pRSET-A plasmid at Tyr-145 (between the KpnI and EcoRI restriction sites) through various peptide linkers generated by PCR, using In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix. A similar strategy was adopted for constructing the plasmids of the qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose biosensors.

The sensing domain for cAMP comprised residues 199–358 of mouse Epac1 (Addgene plasmid #73938). The sensing domain for citrate comprised residues 4–133 of the CitAP domain from Klebsiella pneumoniae CitA protein (Addgene plasmid #134301). The sensing domain for glucose comprised residues 24–328 of the bacterial D-galactose-binding periplasmic protein (MglB) (Addgene plasmid #163115). Site saturation mutagenesis was carried out according to previous protocols.40,41 Briefly, sense and antisense primers were created for the targeted amino acid, and the codon was replaced with NNK and MNN, respectively.

For mammalian expression, the Kozak sequence was added to the cDNA of qmTQ2-ATP and qmTQ2-cAMP by PCR, and the resulting PCR fragments were cloned into pcDNA3.1(−) vectors using In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix. Kozak sequence was added to RCaMP1h by PCR using RCaMP1h cDNA from pRSET-RCaMP1h plasmid (Addgene plasmid #42874) as the template, and the result PCR product was inserted into the mammalian expression pN1 vector.

Screening

The mutants for fluorescence biosensor candidates containing plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli JM109 (DE3) competent cells and cultured by shaking at 200 rpm for 3 days at 20°C. Subsequently, the bacterial suspension was harvested by centrifugation at 4440g for 10 min at 4°C, and the pellet was resuspended in HEPES buffer (20 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, pH 7.2). This was followed by sonication for 30 s (amplitude 60%) using an ultrasonic disruptor (UD-100, TOMY Digital Biology Co., LTD, Tokyo, Japan). The supernatant obtained after centrifugation (17,750 g, 20 min) was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube.

The fluorescence property of each candidate without or with 10 mM ATP (final concentration) was examined using a microplate fluorescence reader (VarioSkan Lux, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the emission spectra, the excitation wavelength was set to 450 nm, and the fluorescence emission range was from 470 nm to 600 nm at a 1 nm step size. For the excitation spectra, the emission wavelength was set at 520 nm, with the range of excitation from 350 nm to 500 nm at 1 nm step size. The fluorescence lifetime response of the mutants that showed changes in fluorescence intensity upon ATP addition was measured under a Leica TCS-SP8 FALCON confocal FLIM microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Samples were excited with a 440 nm pulsed diode laser (PicoQuant, Berlin, Germany), and photon arrival times were recorded with a HyD detector, with emission ranging from 470 nm to 530 nm.

The same approach was adopted in creating qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose biosensors. The buffer for resuspending the bacterial pellets was PBS for both qmTQ2-cAMP biosensor and qmTQ2-glucose biosensor, and TBS (Tris-buffered saline) for qmTQ2-citrate biosensor.

Fluorescent protein expression, purification, and in vitro characterization

For protein expression, qmTQ2-ATP, qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose in the pRSET-A vector were transformed into JM109 (DE3) competent cells, respectively. The cells were then cultured by shaking at 200 rpm for 3 days at 20°C and harvested by centrifugation at 4440g for 10 min. The harvested cells were resuspended in PBS, and the cell suspensions were sonicated, centrifuged, and supernatants were collected. The supernatants containing the His-tagged fluorescent biosensors were purified with TALON Metal Affinity Resins (Takara Bio USA, Inc., California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s procedure. Proteins were eluted with a buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 300 mM NaCl, and 200 mM imidazole.

Subsequently, the proteins were subjected to buffer exchange to 20 mM HEPES (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.4) (for qmTQ2-ATP), PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4. pH 7.4) (for qmTQ2-cAMP and qmTQ2-glucose biosensors), and TBS (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) (for qmTQ2-citrate biosensor) using the PD-10 gel filtration column (GE Healthcare, Illinois, USA) to remove imidazole.

The absorption spectra of the purified fluorescent biosensors were measured between 350 nm and 600 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with the buffer as a reference. The emission and excitation spectra were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FP-8500, JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). For the excitation spectra, the emission wavelength was set at 520 nm, and the range of excitation was from 350 nm to 500 nm. For the emission spectra, the excitation wavelength was 450 nm, and the range of fluorescence emission was from 470 nm to 600 nm.

To measure the pH sensitivity of qmTQ2-ATP, qmTQ2-cAMP, and qmTQ2-glucose, buffers ranging from pH 5.5 to 9.0 were prepared using 30 mM sodium citrate/Borax buffer (pH 5.5–6.0, pH 8.0–9.0) and MOPS buffer (pH 6.5–7.9). The pH sensitivity measurement of qmTQ2-citrate was performed with 30 mM sodium acetate/Borax buffer (pH 5.5–6.0, pH 8.0–9.0) and MOPS buffer (pH 6.5–7.9). Purified qmTQ2-ATP, qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose sensors were diluted 100× in the prepared buffers. All stock solutions of ATP, cAMP, glucose, and citrate were pre-adjusted to pH 7.4 before use.

In cell calibration

The in-cell calibration was conducted according to a previously established protocol.13 HeLa cells, seeded on a 35-mm glass-bottom dish, were transfected with the qmTQ2-cAMP plasmid using Transfectin lipid reagent. In-cell calibration buffer was prepared (consisting of 140 mM K-Gluconate, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, 1.3 mM MgCl2, and 0.34 mM CaCl2, pH 7.2).

Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the HeLa cells were washed twice with the in-cell calibration buffer. Next, the cells were incubated in a calibration buffer containing 50 μM β-escin (157941, MP Biomedicals) for 5 min. The β-escin buffer was then replaced with fresh calibration buffer containing varying concentrations of cAMP, and an in-cell calibration curve correlating cAMP concentration with the fluorescence lifetime of the qmTQ2-cAMP sensor was plotted.

Quantum yield and molar absorption coefficient measurements

The quantum yields (QY) of the purified qmTQ2-ATP, qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose proteins were measured with the absolute PL quantum yield spectrometer (C9920-02, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Shizuoka, Japan) with the excitation wavelength set at 450 nm.

The molar absorption coefficient (ε) was determined through the alkaline denaturation method. To achieve this, the concentration of the purified qmTQ2-ATP, qmTQ2-cAMP, qmTQ2-citrate, and qmTQ2-glucose proteins in the presence or absence of the analytes was adjusted to attain a native optical density (OD) of at least 0.2 after 1:1 dilution with buffer. The absorbance spectra were measured before and more than 5 min after adding 2 M NaOH (at a 1:1 ratio) across the range of 350–650 nm with a 1 nm step size, using the corresponding buffer as a reference.

The concentration of the unfolded protein was determined using the Beer-Lambert law, assuming the molar absorption coefficient at 462 nm of 46,000 M−1cm−1 for the free cyan chromophore.42 Subsequently, utilizing the concentration derived from the NaOH-denatured sample, the peak extinction coefficient value for the native samples, in the presence or absence of the analytes at 450 nm, was determined.

Fluorescence lifetime imaging

FLIM experiments were performed at room temperature using a Leica TCS-SP8 FALCON confocal FLIM microscope with LAS-X version 3.5 software and a 93× glycerol-immersion objective lens (HC PL APO CS2 93 × 1.30 GLYC). Samples were excited with a 440 nm pulsed diode laser, and photon arrival times were recorded using the HyD detector, with the emission ranging from 470 nm to 530 nm. Images were acquired every 5 s for 20 min in the forskolin and isoproterenol treatment experiments. Images were acquired every 10 s for 15 min for the qmTQ2-cAMP and RCaMP1h dual-color imaging experiments. The initial treatment commenced 2 min after starting image acquisition.

Fluorescence lifetimes were calculated using a standardized “tau8” value proposed by the Yellen group.13 The fluorescence lifetime decay histogram was fitted with a two-exponential function, and the photon arrival time was fixed to 0–8 ns from the start of the pulse. Subsequently, the intensity-weighted average lifetime (Tau int) was reported.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Time-lapse results of fluorescence lifetime in cells were analyzed using ImageJ. GraphPad Prism 10 was used to plot the generated data and perform statistical analysis. Statistical details of the experiments are described in the figure legends.
