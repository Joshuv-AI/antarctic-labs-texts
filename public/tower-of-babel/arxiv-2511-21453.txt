Long-Range Antiferromagnetic Order in the AKLT Model on Trees and Treelike Graphs
Thomas Jackson

Abstract




Introduction

The AKLT model
[
1
]
is the first example of a proven Haldane phase
[
4
]
and of a matrix product state (MPS)
[
7
]
and consequently of a tensor network state (TNS)
[
6
]
. However, basic questions about its behavior in higher dimensions remain unanswered. The ground state has the highly unusual property
[
2
]
that expectations of observables can be given by a classical partition function of a system in the same number of variables. This led Affleck, Kennedy, Lieb, and Tasaki to conjecture that it does not have a unique ground state and that the ground states have antiferromagnetic long-range order (LRO) for high-dimensional lattices and high-degree graphs. This was later shown for the Cayley trees (i.e. Bethe lattices) in
[
3
]
, with some evidence for further results
[
8
]
, but otherwise a proof has remained elusive for general lattices. We extend the result of
[
3
]
to include various trees and tree-like graphs that obey certain conditions. Our examples split into three cases: Cayley-like tree-like graphs generated by a finite subgraph, for which we have a simple condition; arbitrary trees with a prescribed growth rate of their volume; and bilayer Cayley trees.
This paper is organized as follows:

In
Section 1
we introduce the AKLT Hamiltonian, prove it is unique up to boundary conditions, give our definition of our infinite-volume ground states, and the graphical language with which we will describe our trees and tree-like graphs.

In
Section 2
we introduce the graphical language and compute the transfer operator for a single site; we compute explicitly the transfer function and give relevant properties and bounds which will be instrumental for our proof.

In
Section 3
we prove the result of
[
3
]
that Cayley trees of degree
5
or more have degenerate ground state space in infinite volume in two ways which generalize in two separate ways as well.

In
Section 4
we define the transfer operator on any finite graph with one outgoing index and finitely many ingoing indices. We define our tree-like graphs and show using the graphical picture a condition for a non-unique ground state.

In
Section 5
we prove for a type of irregular tree that a geometric mean condition on the degree of the vertices is enough to ensure a non-unique ground state.

In
Section 6
we discuss the bilayer Cayley trees and show the degree
d=5
bilayer Cayley tree does not have a unique ground state. This shows that local degree determines uniqueness even when the graph has a similar overall structure.

1.
The AKLT Hamiltonian and ground state on a general tree

Let
T=({\mathcal{V}}_{T},{\mathcal{E}}_{T})
be an infinite tree with vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}_{T}
and edge set
{\mathcal{E}}_{T}
and distinguished root
\overline{0}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{T}
, with the property that there are no vertices such that
\deg(v)=1
and
|v-\overline{0}|<\infty
, meaning the leaves are infinitely far away. Define the sequence of vertex sets
\{{\mathcal{V}}^{n}_{T}\}_{n\geq 0}
with
{\mathcal{V}}^{n}_{T}=\{x\in{\mathcal{V}}_{T}:\>|x-\overline{0}|=n\}
where
|\cdot|
denotes the familiar graph distance; we call
{\mathcal{V}}_{T}^{n}
the
n
th layer of the graph
T
, and call the corresponding
n
-layer tree
T_{n}=(\bigcup_{i\leq n}{\mathcal{V}}_{T}^{i},\bigcup_{i\leq n}{\mathcal{E}}_{T}^{i})
where
{\mathcal{E}}_{T}^{n}=\{e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{T}:e\cap{\mathcal{V}}_{T}^{n}\neq\emptyset\}
. We define the AKLT Hamiltonian on
T
via the formal Hamiltonian

(1.1)

H=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{V}}_{T}}P^{(\deg(x)/2)}

introduced in
[
1
]
. This Hamiltonian is a sum of projectors, and we show that
\ker(H)\neq\emptyset
and that the vectors
\psi\in\ker(H)
are unique up to a choice of boundary condition; moreover, we show the Hamiltonian is frustration-free.

1.1.
The Weyl Representation and Uniqueness of Finite-Volume States

We follow the proof of
[
5
]
to construct the AKLT ground state on an arbitrary graph.
Consider the representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
on the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
(\deg(x)+\deg(y))/2=2S_{e}
, denoted

(1.2)

{\mathcal{S}}=\mathrm{span}\{u^{S_{e}-k}v^{k}\>|\>0\leq k\leq 2S_{e}\}

{\mathcal{S}}
is a
2S_{e}+1
dimensional vector space; note that our spin operators correspond to

\displaystyle S^{+}=S^{x}+iS^{y}=u\frac{\partial}{\partial v}

\displaystyle S^{-}=S^{x}-iS^{y}=v\frac{\partial}{\partial u}

\displaystyle S^{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}-v\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)

And note that the eigenstates of
S^{z}
are the polynomials
u^{j}v^{S_{e}-j}
for
0\leq j\leq 2S_{e}
; we define an inner product so that the polynomials

(1.3)

u^{j}v^{2S_{e}-j}\left[\binom{2S_{e}}{j}(2S_{e}+1)\right]^{1/2}

are an orthonormal basis. This gives a
2S_{e}+1
-dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. The inner product can be made explicit via a change of basis where

(1.4)

u=e^{i\phi/2}\cos(\theta/2)

and

(1.5)

v=e^{-i\phi/2}\sin(\theta/2)

with
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
and
0\leq\phi<2\pi
. Then we can define

(1.6)

\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=\int d\Omega\overline{\psi(u,v)}\phi(u,v)

where

(1.7)

d\Omega=(4\pi)^{-1}\sin\theta d\theta d\phi

It was shown by
[
2
]
that for each local observable
A
there exists a unique polynomial in
\theta
and
\phi
labeled
A(\Omega)
called the symbol such that

(1.8)

\langle\psi,A\phi\rangle=\int d\Omega\overline{\psi(u,v)}\phi(u,v)A(\Omega)

At each site
x\in{\mathcal{V}}
we associate the variables
u_{x}
and
v_{x}
and the spin-
S_{x}
subspace is given by the span of homogeneous polynomials of degree
S_{x}
. We then show the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
5
]

Let
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
H\psi=0
; then there exists a unique
\phi
such that
\phi
is a polynomial in
u_{x},v_{x}
for
x\in\partial\Lambda
and such that

(1.9)

\psi=\phi\prod_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}}(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{x}v_{y})

Conversely if the last equation holds then
H\psi=0

Proof.

We first prove two lemmata.
For any edge
(x,y)
denote the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{x}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{y}
and label the subspaces

(1.10)

S_{1}=\{(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})\phi(u_{x},v_{x},u_{y},v_{y})\}

(1.11)

S_{2}=\{\psi(u_{x},v_{x},u_{y},v_{y})\>:\>P^{2S_{e}}_{x,y}\psi=0\}

Lemma 1.1
.

[
5
]

S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}

Proof.

Define
T^{\alpha}=S^{\alpha}_{x}+S^{\alpha}_{y}
for
\alpha=x,y,z
. Then

(1.12)

T^{\alpha}(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})=0

for all
\alpha
and so

(1.13)

T^{\alpha}\psi=(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})T^{\alpha}\phi

and thus

(1.14)

T^{2}\psi=(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})T^{2}\phi

We note that
\phi
can be written as a sum of eigenvectors of
T^{2}
so that
\phi=\sum_{j}\phi_{j}
where
T^{2}\phi_{j}=j(j+1)\phi_{j}
and thus

(1.15)

T^{2}(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})\phi_{j}=j(j+1)(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})\phi_{j}

and so
(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})\phi_{j}
is an eigenstate of
T^{2}
; by assumption
\phi_{2S_{e}}=0
because it is in the ground state of
P^{2S_{e}}
and so
\psi\in S_{2}
so we know
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}
.

Next note that
\dim S_{2}=(2S_{e}+1)^{2}-(4S_{e}+1)=(2S_{e})^{2}
because we have every eigenspace
(2S_{e}+1)^{2}
minus the ones corresponding to highest spin
4S_{e}+1
. Note also that
S_{1}
is
(2S_{e})^{2}
dimensional and thus
S_{1}=S_{2}

∎

Lemma 1.2
.

[
5
]

If
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}=\bigotimes_{k}{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
satisfies
P^{2S_{e}}_{(x,y)}\psi=0
then
\psi=(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})\phi
; the converse also holds.

Proof.

The space of all
\psi
satisfying

(1.16)

P^{2S_{e}}_{(x,y)}\psi=0

is
S_{2}\otimes(\bigotimes_{k\neq x,y}{\mathcal{H}}_{k})
and similarly all
\psi
such that
\psi=(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})\phi
is

(1.17)

S_{1}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{k\neq x,y}{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\right)

and thus from the previous lemma we have that the two conditions are the same.
∎

Now let
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
H\psi=0
, Then
(u_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}u_{y})
is a factor of
\psi
for every
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}
. The polynomial ring these are contained in is a unique factorization domain, so if
A
and
B
are factors of
\psi
then
\psi=AB\gamma
for some unique
\gamma
. Then
\psi=\phi\prod_{(x,y)\in{\mathcal{E}}}(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})
and the converse also holds. Thus
\psi
is unique up to a polynomial of the remaining boundary variables
\phi
.
∎

Now letting
T_{n}
be the previously defined increasing and exhaustive sequence of subtrees, we can associate a finite volume state
\psi_{n}(B)
of the Hamiltonian defined on
T_{n}
which is dependent on the boundary condition
B_{n}\in M_{2}^{\otimes|\partial T_{n}|}
, where
\partial T_{n}=\{x\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n+1}\setminus{\mathcal{V}}_{n}\}
. We define the infinite-volume ground states of the model as weak limits of finite volume states where

(1.18)

\omega^{n}_{B_{n}}(A)=\langle\psi_{n}(B_{n}),A\psi_{n}(B_{n})\rangle

where
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
is a quasi-local observable and
B_{n}
is a sequence of boundary conditions on
T_{n}
. We say the infinite volume state
\omega:{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

(1.19)

\omega(A):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\omega^{n}_{B_{n}}(A)

is unique if the limit is independent of the sequence of boundary conditions
B_{n}
. One way to compute whether the infinite volume state is independent of the boundary variables taken in the limit is to compute the spectra of the transfer operators, as in the
1
-dimensional case.

In order to understand the AKLT model further, we fully describe the transfer operator on a single site of degree
d
.

AKLT model transfer operator on a single site

.
Vertex of degree
d=7
with one outgoing index and six ingoing indices

First we describe each site operator on the lattice. From the valence bond solid picture we have that we can view each physical site as composed of
d
spin-
\frac{1}{2}
entities living on the edges projected onto a physical spin-
\frac{d}{2}
space. Note we have a physical spin-
d/2
representation
{\mathcal{P}}_{(d/2)}
of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
of dimension
d+1
which can be uniquely found as a subspace, meaning

(2.1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{(d/2)}\subset\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}{\mathcal{P}}_{(1/2)}

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{(1/2)}
is the
2
-dimensional spin representation.
We have then that the uniqueness of this subspace implies the existence of an SU(2) intertwiner

(2.2)

V^{*}:\mathbb{C}^{d+1}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes d-1}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}

which maps the ingoing space and physical
space
\mathbb{C}^{d+1}\otimes(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes d-1}
to the outgoing auxiliary space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and satisfies

(2.3)

V(S_{i})=(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{tp}}\otimes S^{d-1}_{i}+S_{i}^{\mathrm{tp}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d-1})

where
S^{d}_{i}
is the
i
th SU(2) generator in
{\mathcal{P}}_{(d/2)}
and
S^{\mathrm{tp}}_{i}
is the
i
th SU(2) generator on
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}{\mathcal{P}}_{(1/2)}
. We can construct an associated non-normalized transfer operator
\mathbb{F}:M_{2}(\mathbbm{C})^{\otimes d-1}\to M_{2}(\mathbbm{C})
given by

(2.4)

\mathbb{F}(X\otimes Y)=V^{*}(X\otimes Y)V

where we normalize so that
\mathbb{F}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})=c\mathbbm{1}
. We will refer to this unnormalized transfer operator for ease of computation in some places. To compute infinite volume expectations on the tree, we will need to know how
\mathbb{F}
evaluates when

X=\mathbbm{1}
and
Y
is arbitrary; in this case we have that the transfer operator breaks down into spin subspaces labeled by
j
as

(2.5)

\mathbb{F}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes Y)=\mathbb{F}(Y)=\sum_{j=-\frac{d-1}{2}}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}W_{j}YW_{j}^{*}

where

(2.6)

S=|\uparrow\rangle\langle\downarrow|-|\downarrow\rangle\langle\uparrow|,\qquad W_{j}=SP_{j}

where
P_{j}
is the projection onto the subspace of spin
j
; note we drop the dependence on the identity since it will be taken for granted. We choose our boundary indices to be written in the Hilbert-Schmidt basis, that is, as sums of tensor products of Pauli matrices, and we will see that we can provide a formula for each term in this basis. We can compute these weights by choosing a basis of unnormalized symmetric states in the following way.

Let
|w_{k}\rangle
be the symmetric state with
k
ups so that the total spin is
j=k-(d-1)/2
(for example
|w_{1}\rangle
for
d=3
would be
|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle
). On a site of degree
d
one can write the unnormalized projection as

(2.7)

P_{j}^{\prime}=|\uparrow\rangle\langle w_{k-1}|+|\downarrow\rangle\langle w_{k}|

We then normalize by total number of terms which is the length of
w_{k-1}
and
w_{k}
added together, which gives
\binom{d-1}{k-1}+\binom{d-1}{k}=\binom{d}{k}
so that

(2.8)

P_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{d}{k}}}(|\uparrow\rangle\langle w_{k-1}|+|\downarrow\rangle\langle w_{k}|)

as this ensures the normalization condition
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbbm{1})=\mathbbm{1}
; note we differentiate our normalized transfer operator
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}
from our unnormalized transfer operator
\mathbb{F}
. Thus we have

(2.9)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(M)=\frac{2}{d+1}\sum_{k}W_{j}MW_{j}^{*}

where
M\in M_{2}(\mathbbm{C})^{\otimes d-1}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}:M_{2}(\mathbbm{C})^{\otimes d-1}\to M_{2}(\mathbbm{C})
. Note that our normalization now insures

(2.10)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes d-1})=\frac{2}{d+1}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{\binom{d-1}{k}}{\binom{d}{k}}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{2}{d+1}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{d-k}{d}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{2}{d+1}\frac{d+1}{2}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}

In order to know how
this operator acts on a general matrix we look at how it acts on a tensor product of Pauli matrices. We have explicitly

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}=\sigma_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}

Letting

(2.11)

M=(\sigma_{1})^{\otimes k_{1}}\otimes(\sigma_{2})^{\otimes k_{2}}\otimes(\sigma_{3})^{\otimes k_{3}}\otimes(\mathbbm{1})^{\otimes d-1-k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}}

where this tensor product is taken in any order we have the following identity

Theorem 2.1
.

For our unnormalized transfer operator defined in
2.5
, if all the
k_{i}
’s are even in expression
2.11
we have

(2.12)

\mathbb{F}(M)=\frac{1}{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}+1}\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}}{\displaystyle\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}}\mathbbm{1}

Proof.

We provide an elementary proof by providing some intuition using the spin-flip operator
\sigma_{1}
.
First we assume
k:=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}
is even.
Starting with the case of
M=(\sigma_{1})^{\otimes k}\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(d-1-k)}
we note that in order for the term
W_{j}MW_{j}^{*}
will be nonzero for the number of terms in
W_{j}
which have the same spin after flipping the first
k
of them. This implies that the first
k
of the total string of length
d
are an equal number of ups and downs. Thus the number of ways to choose such a configuration with total number of
j
spin ups is

(2.13)

{\binom{k}{k/2}}\binom{d-k}{j-k/2}

Noting that the normalization of
W_{j}
is
\binom{d}{j}
and summing over all possible values of
j
we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(M)=\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j\geq k/2}^{d-k/2}\frac{{\binom{k}{k/2}}\binom{d-k}{j-k/2}}{{\binom{d}{j}}}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j\geq k/2}^{d-k/2}\frac{{\binom{j}{k/2}}\binom{d-j}{k/2}}{{\binom{d}{k}}}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{d+1}\frac{\binom{d+1}{k+1}}{\binom{d}{k}}\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{k+1}\mathbbm{1}

where we have rewritten the sum on the second line and used Vandermonde’s identity to get a closed form for the sum, and divided by two since we have two total outputs
\langle\uparrow|
and
\langle\downarrow|
.
A similar calculation shows that for
k
odd we have

(2.14)

\mathbb{F}(M)=-\frac{1}{k+2}\sigma_{1}

By invariance these numbers will be the same for
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
with the same output for odd with a different index.

For the case of

(2.15)

M=(\sigma_{1})^{\otimes k_{1}}\otimes(\sigma_{2})^{\otimes k_{2}}\otimes(\sigma_{3})^{\otimes k_{3}}\otimes(\mathbbm{1})^{\otimes d-1-k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}}

we make the same counting argument and notice that the number of configurations is

(2.16)

\sum_{j\geq(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}^{d-(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}\binom{k_{1}}{k_{1}/2}\binom{k_{2}}{k_{2}/2}\binom{k_{3}}{k_{3}/2}\binom{d-k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}}{j-k_{1}/2-k_{2}/2-k_{3}/2}

and so by normalization we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(M)=\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j\geq(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}^{d-(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}\frac{\binom{k_{1}}{k_{1}/2}\binom{k_{2}}{k_{2}/2}\binom{k_{3}}{k_{3}/2}\binom{d-k_{1}-k_{2}-k_{3}}{j-k_{1}/2-k_{2}/2-k_{3}/2}}{{\binom{d}{j}}}\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{j\geq(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}^{d-(k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3})/2}\frac{\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{k_{1}/2,k_{2}/2,k_{3}/2}\binom{d-j}{k_{1}/2+k_{2}/2+k_{3}/2}\binom{j}{k_{1}/2,k_{2}/2,k_{3}/2}}{\binom{k}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\binom{d}{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}}\mathbbm{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}+1}\frac{\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{k_{1}/2,k_{2}/2,k_{3}/2}}{\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}}\mathbbm{1}

where again we have used Vandermonde’s identity
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Letting
k^{\prime}_{i}:=k_{i}+k_{i}\mod 2
, if at most one
k_{i}
is such that
k_{i}\neq k_{i}^{\prime}
then

(2.17)

\mathbb{F}(M)=\frac{1}{k^{\prime}_{1}+k^{\prime}_{2}+k^{\prime}_{3}+1}\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k^{\prime}_{1}+k^{\prime}_{2}+k^{\prime}_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k^{\prime}_{1}}{2},\frac{k^{\prime}_{2}}{2},\frac{k^{\prime}_{3}}{2}}}{\displaystyle\binom{k^{\prime}_{1}+k^{\prime}_{2}+k^{\prime}_{3}}{k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2},k^{\prime}_{3}}}\prod_{i}(-\sigma_{i})^{k^{\prime}_{i}-k_{i}}

Proof.

Similar as above, using Vandermonde’s identity.
∎

Note that because of normalization the coefficients in for the transfer operator are not dependent on the degree of the site
d
, only on the number of occupied edges.

We will choose a simple boundary condition to help understand how the concatenations of these operators work on trees, for which the product boundary condition is an invariant subspace of
\mathbb{F}
.
Denote the vector
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
with
||\mathbf{x}||\leq 1
and denote
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}]
We will explore the action of the transfer operator on the vector-parametrized boundary condition

(2.18)

B(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{\otimes(d-1)}

We evaluate

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{F}(B(\mathbf{x})))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{i}\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\binom{d-1}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},d-1-k}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}}{\displaystyle\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}+1}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}}\cdot x_{1}^{k_{1}}x_{2}^{k_{2}}x_{3}^{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{i}\>\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{d-1}{k}\cdot\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}\cdot x_{1}^{k_{1}}x_{2}^{k_{2}}x_{3}^{k_{3}}=\sum_{k\>\mathrm{even}}\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{d-1}{k}\cdot||\mathbf{x}||^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+||\mathbf{x}||\right)^{d}-\left(1-||\mathbf{x}||\right)^{d}}{2d\cdot||\mathbf{x}||}

We will call this function
f_{d}(\mathbf{x})=f_{d}(||\mathbf{x}||)
for convenience.

Similarly we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{F}(B(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\sigma_{i})=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=k\\
k_{1}\mathrm{odd},k_{2},k_{3}\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\binom{d-1}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},d-2-k}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+1+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}+1}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}}{\displaystyle\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1}+1,k_{2},k_{3}}}\cdot x_{1}^{k_{1}}x_{2}^{k_{2}}x_{3}^{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=k\neq 0\\
k_{i}\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\binom{d-1}{k_{1}-1,k_{2},k_{3},d-k}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}}{\displaystyle\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}}\cdot x_{1}^{k_{1}-1}x_{2}^{k_{2}}x_{3}^{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=k\neq 0\\
k_{i}\mathrm{even}\end{subarray}}\frac{k_{1}}{d}\binom{d}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},d-k}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\binom{\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{2}}{\frac{k_{1}}{2},\frac{k_{2}}{2},\frac{k_{3}}{2}}}{\binom{k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}}\cdot x_{1}^{k_{1}-1}x_{2}^{k_{2}}x_{3}^{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}{f_{d+1}}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{x_{i}}{d||\mathbf{x}||}f^{\prime}_{d+1}(||\mathbf{x}||)

Lastly we note that our trace normalized operator
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}

Following our second proof of the Cayley-tree result using part (g) of Theorem
2.3

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
d_{i}
be a sequence of real numbers with
d_{i}\geq 2
. If the partial products

(5.1)

a_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{3}{(d_{i}-1)}

is summable, such that
\sum_{n}a_{n}<\infty
then the infinite functional composition
\bigcirc_{i=1}^{\infty}F_{d_{i}}(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,1]
. Moreover if the sequence of partial products is such that
a_{n}\to\infty
then the infinite functional composition
\bigcirc_{i=1}^{\infty}F_{d_{i}}(x)=0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Note that from the bound
F_{d}(x)\geq\left(\frac{3}{(d-1)x}+1\right)^{-1}
we have that the iterates

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigcirc_{i=1}^{N}F_{d_{i}}(x)\geq\left(\frac{1}{x}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{3}{d_{i}-1}+1+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{3}{d_{i}-1}\right)^{-1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}\cdot a_{N}+1+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sum_{k}a_{k}\right)^{-1}>0

since
a_{N}\to 0
and
\sum_{k}a_{k}<\infty
.

For the second implication, notice that if the sequence
a_{n}\to\infty
then

(5.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigcirc_{i=1}^{n}F_{d_{i}}(x)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}x\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d_{i}-1}{3}=\lim_{n\to\infty}x\cdot\frac{1}{a_{n}}=0

for all
x\in[0,1]

∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
{d_{i}}
be a sequence of real numbers with
d_{i}\geq 2
; if

(5.3)

\ln(\mu):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln((d_{i}-1)/3)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln(a_{n})>0

then the infinite function composition

(5.4)

\bigcirc_{i=1}^{\infty}F_{d_{i}}(x)>0

for all
x\in(0,1]
, and
if

(5.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln((d_{i}-1)/3)\leq 0

then

(5.6)

\bigcirc_{i=1}^{\infty}F_{d_{i}}(x)=0

for all
x\in[0,1]

Proof.

Note that since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln((d_{i}-1)/3)=\ln(\mu)>0
we have that for any
\epsilon>0
we have that there exists an
N
such that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln((d_{i}-1)/3)>(1-\epsilon)\ln(\mu)>0
for all
n\geq N
; thus we have that
a_{n}\geq\mu^{(1-\epsilon)n}
; thus

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigcirc_{i=1}^{N}F_{d_{i}}(x)\geq\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{3}{d_{i}-1}+\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{3}{d_{i}-1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{3}{d_{i}-1}+\frac{1-\mu^{(\epsilon-1)(N+2)}}{1-\mu^{\epsilon-1}}\right)^{-1}>0

Similarly note that if
\ln(\mu)\leq 0
then
\frac{1}{a_{n}}\leq\mu^{(1-\epsilon)n}
for large enough

, so we have

(5.7)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\bigcirc_{i=1}^{N}F_{d_{i}}(x)\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{a_{N}}\cdot x=0

for all
x\in[0,1]

∎

Note that we do not have a uniform lower bound on the functional composition in the first part of the proof of Corollary
5.1
. One tantalizing generalization is if we can uniformly lower bound this for trees that are not characterized by a single sequence
\{d_{i}\}
of degrees uniformly radiating from the origin. However we have a counterexample to this.

Counterexample 5.1
.

For any
d\geq 5
define the following tree via the sequence of degrees
\{d_{i}\}
as
d_{i}=2
for all
i\leq N
and
d_{i}=5
for all
i>N
. Then we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigcirc_{i=1}^{n}F_{d_{i}}(x)=\frac{1}{(-3)^{N}}\cdot x_{5}
where
x_{5}
is the positive solution to
F_{5}(x_{5})=-x_{5}
which can be made as small as we like while maintaining that
\mu=\frac{4}{3}
.

This prevents us from using a simple macroscopic condition (such as all sequences obeying the
\mu
condition) to obtain a bound for trees with arbitrary degrees; however we can still make this work for irregular trees with some added assumptions.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree with distinguished root
\overline{0}
such that
\deg(x)=d_{i}
for all
x
such that
|x-\overline{0}|=i
. If

(5.8)

\ln(\mu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln((d_{i}-1)/3)>0

then the AKLT ground state on this tree is not unique.

Proof.

We note that this condition immediately implies that the infinite composition
\bigcirc_{i=1}^{\infty}F_{d_{i}}(x)>0
for
x\in(0,1]
by the previous lemma; let
N
be such that for all
n\geq N
we have that
\bigcirc_{i=1}^{n}F_{d_{i}}(x)=-x
has at least one, solution; let
x_{n}
be the smallest solution of
\bigcirc_{i=1}^{n}F_{d_{i}}(x)=-x
; this sequence is bounded on
(\epsilon,1]
for some
\epsilon>0
and so has a convergent subsequence
x_{n_{k}}\to x
with
x\neq 0
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}=x_{n_{k}}\sigma_{1}
; then the ground state expectations of an observable
A
with support on
\overline{0}
of a sequence of boundary conditions

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\psi_{n_{k}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}})),A\psi_{n_{k}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}))\rangle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\psi_{0}(\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{T_{n_{k}}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}))),A\psi_{0}(\tilde{\mathbb{F}}_{T_{n_{k}}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{x}_{n_{k}}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{0}(\mathbbm{1}+x\sigma_{1}),A\psi_{0}(\mathbbm{1}+x\sigma_{1})\rangle\neq\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle\psi_{n_{k}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{0})),A\psi_{n_{k}}(B_{T_{n_{k}}}(\mathbf{0})\rangle=\langle\psi_{0}(\mathbbm{1}),A\psi_{0}(\mathbbm{1})\rangle

∎

We can also use the above method to give a result for a general irregular tree with a simple condition.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree such that there exists a
\mu>1
and
C>0
such that for every sequence
\{d_{i_{k}}\}
such that
|d_{i_{k}}-\overline{0}|=k
we have that
\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d_{i_{k}}-1}{3}\geq C\mu^{n}
. Then the ground state of this model is not unique.

Proof.

For each
n
let
\alpha_{k}
be the index of the leaf corresponding to the minimal sequence

(5.9)

\min_{\{i_{k}\}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d_{i_{k}}-1}{3}\right)

use the bound that
f_{d}(x_{1},...,x_{n})\geq f_{d}(x_{\alpha})
and thus the composition

(5.10)

f_{d_{i_{0}}}(f_{d_{i_{1}}}(f_{d_{i_{2}}}(...)))\geq\bigcirc_{k=1}^{n}f_{d_{\alpha_{k}}}(x)

and from the above condition we know that from the bounds previously used that

(5.11)

\bigcirc_{k=1}^{n}f_{d_{\alpha_{k}}}(x)\geq\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{m}\frac{3}{d_{a_{k}}-1}+1\right)^{-1}\geq\left(C\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mu^{-m}+1\right)^{-1}\geq\left(\frac{C\mu^{-1}}{1-\mu^{-1}}+1\right)^{-1}>0

Thus the concatenations of the transfer functions are uniformly bounded away from zero so there exists a convergent subsequence that is positive and one that is negative, so the ground state is not unique.
∎

Note that in this last proof we could not use the geometric mean condition for the minimal sequence, as the functional compositions associated to the set of such sequences is not uniformly bounded below as was shown in Counterexample 1.

One can see the constant
3\mu
as something of a ”connective” constant in that it quantifies the exponential growth of all the self-avoiding paths from the root to the leaves in the tree.

6.
Bilayer trees

The case of bilayer trees is significantly different, and requires a slightly different analysis; however the same general fixed-point type argument exists.

Bilayer Cayley tree

Figure 6
.
First layer of bilayer Cayley tree of degree
d=6
which has splitting number
g=d-2=4

The bilayer tree is substantially different. This is because we have non-trivial operators which commute with representations of SU
(2)
. Note that in the bilayer tree, if we have the transfer operator at each (bilayer) node in the tree
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}:M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2(d-1)}\to M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2}
we look for a pair of density matrices
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2}
such that
T(\rho_{i}^{\otimes(d-1)})=\rho_{1-i}
. Note that within
M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2}
we have the Casimir
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{i}
which commutes with the
SU(2)
representation so we cannot have that
T(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1})=\mathbbm{1}
; however
T
is still trace-preserving. Thus if we define
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=[\sigma_{lk}]
where
\sigma_{lk}=\sigma_{l}\otimes\sigma_{k}
with
l,k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
; then our boundary conditions
B
can be written as
\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. We find fixed points of the transfer operator, the coefficients of which are rational functions of this vector.
For simplicity of notation, for a bilayer tree of degree
d
we call
g-2
the
splitting number
of the tree.

Theorem 6.1
.

The bilayer Cayley tree has a unique ground state for
g=1,2
.

Proof.

The case of
g=1
is the case of a bilayer AKLT chain which has a unique ground state
[
1
]
. However, by way of an example, we prove this using a fixed point method. Note that the transfer operator can be fully diagonalized so that, starting with a boundary condition

(6.1)

B=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{i}x_{0i}\mathbbm{1}\otimes\sigma_{i}+\sum_{i}x_{i0}\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{i\neq j}x_{ij}\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}+\sum_{i}x_{ii}\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}

then we have

(6.2)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(B)=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{i}f_{i0}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1})+\sum_{i}f_{0i}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\sigma_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j})+\sum_{i}f_{ii}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{i})

where

(6.3)

f_{0i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{-\frac{1}{3}x_{0i}-\frac{1}{9}x_{i0}}{\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)},\qquad f_{i0}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{-\frac{1}{3}x_{i0}-\frac{1}{9}x_{0i}}{\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)}

(6.4)

f_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\frac{1}{9}x_{ij}}{\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)},\qquad f_{ii}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1+\frac{x_{ii}}{9}}{\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)}

we search for a fixed point
f_{kl}(\mathbf{x})=\pm x_{kl}
and so we have the set of equations

(6.5)

\pm x_{0i}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)=-\frac{1}{3}x_{0i}-\frac{1}{9}x_{i0},\qquad\pm x_{i0}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)=-\frac{1}{3}x_{i0}-\frac{1}{9}x_{0i}

(6.6)

\pm x_{ij}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)=\frac{1}{9}x_{ij},\qquad\pm x_{ii}\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)=1+\frac{x_{ii}}{9}

which has solution
x_{i0}=x_{0i}=x_{ij}=0
and

(6.7)

x_{ii}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{9}+\frac{x_{ii}}{9}\right)}{\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)}

or

(6.8)

\left(\frac{1}{9}+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)=\left(\sum_{i}x_{ii}\right)\left(1+\frac{\sum_{i}x_{ii}}{9}\right)

which has solution
\left(\sum_{i}x_{ii}\right)^{2}+8\sum_{i}x_{ii}-3=0
and thus
\sum_{i}x_{ii}=-4+\sqrt{19}
which is within the unit sphere. This value, while not being nonzero, is a fixed point of
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}
that does not break the SU
(2)
symmetry; moreover, the functions
f
converge exponentially quickly to this fixed point, so we have a unique ground state.

For
g=2
, like before, we have our bilayer transfer operator
\tilde{E}:M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 4}\to M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2}
where we can explicitly calculate for
B=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\sigma
that we have

(6.9)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(B)=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{i}f_{i0}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1})+\sum_{i}f_{0i}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\sigma_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j})+\sum_{i}f_{ii}(\mathbf{x})(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{i})

where each
f
is a rational function of the vector
\mathbf{x}
.
We have that we are solving the equation

(6.10)

\pm x_{kl}=f_{kl}(\mathbf{x})

and so we get that we need to solve

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{2}{3}x_{i0}-\frac{2}{9}x_{0i}-\frac{4}{15}x_{ii}x_{i0}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{45}x_{j0}y_{ij}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{9}x_{i0}x_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle\pm x_{i0}\left(1+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{3}x_{i0}^{2}+\frac{1}{9}x_{i0}x_{0i}+\sum_{i}\frac{2}{9}x_{ii}+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{9}x_{ii}^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{9}x_{ij}^{2}\right)=0

and

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{2}{3}x_{0i}-\frac{2}{9}x_{i0}-\frac{4}{15}x_{ii}x_{0i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{45}x_{0j}y_{ji}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{9}x_{0i}x_{ji}\right)

\displaystyle\pm x_{0i}\left(1+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{3}x_{i0}^{2}+\frac{1}{9}x_{i0}x_{0i}+\sum_{i}\frac{2}{9}x_{ii}+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{9}x_{ii}^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{9}x_{ij}^{2}\right)=0

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{9}x_{ij}+\frac{2}{9}x_{i0}x_{0j}+\frac{2}{45}x_{i0}x_{j0}+\frac{2}{75}x_{ij}x_{ii}+\frac{2}{75}x_{ij}x_{jj}+\frac{4}{225}x_{ij}x_{kk}+\frac{2}{225}x_{ik}x_{kj}\right)

\displaystyle\pm x_{ij}\left(1+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{3}x_{i0}^{2}+\frac{1}{9}x_{i0}x_{0i}+\sum_{i}\frac{2}{9}x_{ii}+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{9}x_{ii}^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{9}x_{ij}^{2}\right)=0

and

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{9}+\frac{2}{9}x_{ii}+\frac{2}{9}x_{i0}x_{0i}+\frac{2}{15}x_{i0}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{45}x_{i0}x_{j0}+\frac{1}{25}x_{ii}^{2}+\frac{2}{75}x_{ij}x_{ij}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{225}x_{ij}^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{2}{225}x_{ii}x_{jj}\right)

∎

Next we show that we have non-uniqueness for splitting number
g=3

Theorem 6.2
.

The AKLT Hamiltonian defined on the
g=3
bilayer Cayley tree does not have a unique ground state.

Proof.

We start with the ansatz that we have a boundary condition of the form

(6.11)

B(\mathbf{x})=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}

where we note that the space of boundary conditions such that
x_{1}=x_{0i}=x_{i0}
and
x_{2}=x_{ij}=x_{kl}
and
x_{3}=x_{ii}=x_{jj}
is a fixed subspace, so we assume
\mathbf{x}
satisfies these and search for two boundary conditions
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
that are solutions to

(6.12)

\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(B_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 3})=B_{\mp}(\mathbf{x})

which means

(6.13)

f_{0i}(\mathbf{x})=f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=-x_{1}

(6.14)

f_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{2}

(6.15)

f_{ii}(\mathbf{x})=f_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{3}

where
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
are rational functions; define
f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{F}(B_{\pm}(\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 3}))
, so we can rearrange by searching for a root of the following polynomials:

\displaystyle f_{1}(\mathbf{x})+x_{1}\cdot f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=-3x_{1}-\frac{37}{15}x_{1}^{3}-\frac{2}{5}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}-\frac{8}{15}x_{1}x_{3}-\frac{4}{25}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}-\frac{14}{75}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+\frac{8}{75}x_{1}x_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{25}x_{1}x_{3}^{2}+\frac{22}{25}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+\frac{3}{25}x_{3}^{3}+\frac{4}{75}x_{2}^{3}+\frac{8}{25}x_{2}^{2}+2x_{2}^{2}+2x_{3}+6x_{1}^{2}=0

and

\displaystyle f_{2}(\mathbf{x})-x_{2}\cdot f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{2}{3}+\frac{7}{5}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{14}{75}x_{2}^{2}+\frac{6}{25}x_{3}+\frac{1}{15}x_{3}^{2}+\frac{11}{75}x_{2}x_{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{22}{75}x_{2}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{4}{25}x_{3}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{4}{25}x_{2}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{4}{75}x_{3}x_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{25}x_{2}^{3}+\frac{1}{25}x_{3}^{3}=0

and

(6.16)

f_{3}(\mathbf{x})-x_{3}\cdot f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{8}{9}+\frac{142}{75}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{8}{25}x_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}x_{3}+\frac{13}{75}x_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{15}x_{3}^{3}+\frac{16}{75}x_{2}x_{1}^{2}+\frac{8}{25}x_{3}x_{2}^{2}=0

From a simple check with a graphing calculator we find there is a pair of solutions
\mathbf{x}_{\pm}\approx[\pm 0.3020,0.0466,0.1754]
such that
\tilde{\mathbb{F}}(B(\mathbf{x}_{\pm})^{\otimes 3})=B(\mathbf{x}_{\mp})
.
∎
