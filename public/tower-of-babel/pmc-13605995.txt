Comparative Efficacy of Cyclosporine, Tacrolimus, and Neurostimulatory Nasal Sprays for Moderate-to-Severe Dry Eye Disease: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Asim Marwan Bogari, Nada Omar Taher, Reem Omar Nughays, Mohammed Albadri, Hind Mohammed Aljabri, Mohammed Abdulaziz Taha, Mahmood Jameel Showail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo compare the efficacy and tolerability of topical cyclosporine A (CsA) 0.05%, 0.09%, and 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; and neurostimulatory nasal sprays (varenicline and simpinicline) in moderate-to-severe dry eye disease (DED), focusing on tear production, ocular surface integrity, and tear film stability.Clinical RelevanceDED management remains complex due to heterogeneous presentation and variable therapeutic responses. While immunomodulators like CsA and tacrolimus are widely used, novel neuromodulatory agents such as varenicline nasal spray are emerging. Comparative data across these treatment classes remain scarce.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, and CENTRAL was performed up to January 2025. Randomized controlled trials assessing CsA 0.05%, 0.09%, or 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; or nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist nasal sprays in moderate-to-severe DED were included. Primary endpoints included Schirmer test score (STS), tear break-up time (TBUT), corneal fluorescein staining (CFS), and ocular surface disease index (OSDI). A Bayesian network meta-analysis estimated comparative efficacy using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).ResultsTwenty-nine RCTs (n = 8453 patients) were included in the systematic review; fourteen qualified for network meta-analysis. CsA 0.09% and 0.1% ranked highest for CFS improvement at the evaluated time points. Tacrolimus 0.03% showed favorable early STS and TBUT trends but did not consistently improve longer-term ocular surface outcomes. Varenicline improved tear production but lacked CFS data. Placebo ranked favorably for STS and TBUT at 3 months and beyond, possibly because several vehicles had lubricating or tear-stabilizing effects. CsA 0.05% showed modest efficacy.ConclusionCsA 0.09% and 0.1% showed the most favorable corneal healing outcomes, while tacrolimus 0.03% showed an early tear-stimulating effect. Varenicline offers a distinct neurostimulatory mechanism but requires further comparative validation. These findings support phenotype-guided therapy and future head-to-head trials.Keywords: dry eye disease, cyclosporine a, tacrolimus, varenicline nasal spray, network meta-analysis, Schirmer test, corneal fluorescein staining, tear break-up time

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface, defined by the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society (TFOS) as a loss of tear film homeostasis, involving tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation, and neurosensory abnormalities.1 It is one of the most common reasons for ophthalmic consultations, ranking as the fifth most prevalent ocular condition in women and ninth in men, with the third highest incidence after refractive errors and cataracts.2 Global DED prevalence varies widely—from approximately 5% to over 40%—depending on diagnostic criteria and geographic region, with a pooled estimate of 29.5% using the TFOS DEWS II definition;3,4 prevalence in Saudi Arabia has been reported at 21.7% for mild, 13.1% for moderate, and 32% for severe disease.5 Despite the availability of multiple therapeutic classes for DED, direct head-to-head comparisons between them remain scarce, leaving considerable uncertainty regarding optimal treatment selection for moderate-to-severe disease. DED pathophysiology is broadly classified into aqueous-deficient and evaporative subtypes, which frequently coexist. Tear film instability and hyperosmolarity initiate ocular surface inflammation, epithelial damage, and sensory dysregulation. This feedback loop exacerbates symptoms such as dryness, ocular pain, and visual disturbances, significantly impairing quality of life.6,7 The clinical manifestations of DED vary depending on the dominant subtype. Evaporative DED, most commonly associated with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), primarily compromises the lipid layer of the tear film, resulting in shortened tear breakup time (TBUT).8 In contrast, aqueous-deficient DED, particularly in the setting of Sjögren’s syndrome, is characterized by impaired lacrimal gland secretion, reflected in reduced anesthetized Schirmer test scores (STS).9 Regardless of subtype, corneal fluorescein staining (CFS) is frequently elevated due to chronic epithelial injury, serving as a sensitive and reliable indicator of disease severity across the DED spectrum.10 Management remains challenging due to disease heterogeneity, chronic progression, and inconsistent therapeutic response. First-line treatments include artificial tears and lifestyle modifications. However, many patients exhibit persistent symptoms, particularly when MGD, the leading cause of evaporative DED, is inadequately managed. Subclinical inflammation often persists even after MGD-directed therapy and may not correlate with conventional clinical metrics like TBUT, contributing to the frequently observed symptom–sign discordance.11,12 Topical anti-inflammatory therapies such as corticosteroids, CsA 0.05–0.1%, tacrolimus (off-label), and secretagogues like diquafosol or rebamipide (where available) play a central role in modulating the inflammatory cascade.12 However, newer therapeutic classes have emerged with distinct mechanisms. Varenicline and simpinicline nasal sprays, for example, lack direct anti-inflammatory properties. Instead, their mechanism is centered on neurostimulation of the nasolacrimal reflex via nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonism, leading to increased tear production without immunomodulation.13,14 Notably, varenicline nasal spray (VNS) has demonstrated more rapid and robust improvements in tear production and comparable or superior symptom relief compared to CsA or lifitegrast, all while maintaining a favorable safety profile.15,16 The recently published TFOS DEWS III Management and Therapy Report (2025) further emphasizes the importance of individualized, phenotype-based management of dry eye disease, recognizing the diverse contributions of tear film instability, inflammation, meibomian gland dysfunction, aqueous deficiency, and neurosensory abnormalities to disease expression and treatment response.12 This evolving framework highlights the need for comparative evidence across therapies with distinct mechanisms of action to better inform treatment selection and optimize patient outcomes. Therefore, this systematic review and network meta-analysis aimed to critically compare the efficacy and tolerability of topical CsA 0.05%, 0.09%, and 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist nasal sprays (varenicline and simpinicline) in the treatment of moderate to severe DED.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

This study was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42025643221) prior to the preliminary search and was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Ethical approval was not required, as all data included in this study were previously published. All data is available within this article. We systematically searched the MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases from their inception until January 25, 2025, without restrictions on language or publication date. The search strategy included keywords and controlled vocabulary related to “tacrolimus OR “cyclosporin” OR “calcineurin inhibitors” OR “nicotine replacement therapy” OR “varenicline” OR “OC-01” OR “varenicline nasal spray” OR “varenicline solution” OR “tyrvaya” OR “simpinicline” OR “OC-02” AND “dry eye disease” OR “dry eye” OR “Sjögren’s syndrome” OR “meibomian gland dysfunction” OR “evaporative dry eye” OR “aqueous deficient dry eye” OR “keratoconjunctivitis sicca” AND “RCTs” OR “randomized controlled trial” OR “clinical trial”. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and included studies were manually screened to identify any additional eligible articles.

Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review and network meta-analysis included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the efficacy and tolerability of topical CsA 0.05%, 0.09%, and 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist nasal sprays (VNS and simpinicline) for moderate-to-severe DED. Eligible studies assessed mean changes in anesthetized STS, tear film stability measured by TBUT, OSDI, CFS, and adverse events. Anesthetized rather than non-anesthetized STS was selected to better reflect basal tear production while minimizing reflex tearing, thereby providing a more consistent measure of lacrimal gland function. Studies lacking a placebo or comparator arm, as well as single-arm trials, were excluded from the analysis.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

After removing duplicates, two authors independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction was conducted using a predefined Excel spreadsheet, collecting information on study characteristics, design, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, participant demographics, baseline characteristics, intervention and control details, as well as treatment outcomes at 1 month, 3 months, and >3 months (ie, STS, TBUT, OSDI, and CFS). Also, secondary outcomes (ie, ocular and systemic adverse events and patient-reported outcomes) were collected. All extracted and calculated data underwent a double-checking process by the reviewers to maintain data integrity.

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias in all included studies using the Revised Cochrane risk of bias tool.45 Any discrepancies in judgment were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical Analysis

A network meta-analysis was performed to compare the efficacy and tolerability of CsA 0.05%, 0.09%, and 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; and nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist nasal sprays (VNS and simpinicline) for moderate-to-severe DED. Both direct and indirect evidence were integrated using a Bayesian random-effects model to estimate comparative treatment effects across multiple outcomes. The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated for each intervention to assess the probability of being the most effective treatment, with values ranging from 0% (least effective) to 100% (most effective). Treatment effects for OSDI, TBUT, CFS, and anesthetized STS were analyzed as continuous variables and summarized using mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrIs). A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates by iteratively excluding individual studies. All statistical analyses were performed using MetaInsight v6.2.1, leveraging the “netmeta” and “gemtc” packages in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Search Result and Study Selection

The initial database search using the specified keywords yielded a total of 1260 articles. After removing duplicates, the number of articles was reduced to 935. Following the title and abstract screening, 178 studies were selected for further evaluation through full-text screening. Eventually, 29 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the systematic review; however, only 14 were eligible for inclusion in the network meta-analysis, as shown in Figure 1.

Study Baseline Characteristics

A total of 29 RCTs, involving over 8453 patients and approximately 9860 treated eyes, were included in the systematic review. Detailed characteristics of these studies are summarized in Tables 1–3. The interventions evaluated included 15 studies investigating CsA 0.05%, 3 studies assessing CsA 0.09%, 9 studies evaluating CsA 0.1%, 3 studies on tacrolimus 0.03%, 6 studies examining varenicline solution nasal spray, and 1 study assessing simpinicline nasal spray. Of note, Sheppard et al 202138 is a worse-eye subgroup analysis of the same Phase 3 OTX-101 trial reported by Goldberg et al 2019;29 to avoid double-counting this population, only Goldberg et al 201929 was retained for the CsA 0.09% network meta-analysis comparison, while Sheppard et al 202138 is included in the descriptive synthesis only. These studies were conducted across a broad geographic spectrum, including North America, Asia, Europe, and the Middle East, and ranged from small-scale pilot trials to large, multicenter investigations. Participants were typically aged between the mid-40s and late 60s. A predominance of female subjects was reported in many trials, consistent with the known gender distribution of DED. Several studies specifically recruited patients with moderate-to-severe DED or Sjögren’s syndrome. However, the criteria and tools used to assess disease severity varied across trials, leading to some heterogeneity in baseline characteristics among the studies. Follow-up durations ranged from 4 weeks to 6 months, allowing for assessment of both short- and intermediate-term outcomes. Of the 29 eligible RCTs, 14 studies reported sufficient quantitative data on outcomes such as STS, TBUT, and CFS and were included in the network meta-analysis.17,18,20–24,28,29,31,34,37,42,44 Specifically, the network meta-analysis included 7 studies evaluating CsA 0.05%,17,18,20,21,23,24,44 3 studies on CsA 0.1%,28,31,37 1 study investigating CsA 0.09%,29 2 studies on VNS,34,42 and 2 studies evaluating tacrolimus 0.03%.20,22 Reporting of OSDI scores was limited, restricting network meta-analysis feasibility for that specific outcome. The remaining 15 studies, which lacked complete outcome data or network-compatible comparators, were incorporated only into the descriptive synthesis.

Bayesian Network Meta-Analysis

Figure 4 illustrates the network structure of the included interventions across the evaluated outcomes and follow-up periods. The network plots show how treatments are connected through shared comparators, allowing both direct and indirect comparisons within the Bayesian network meta-analysis. In these plots, each node represents a treatment, and the connecting lines indicate available head-to-head comparisons between interventions. The size of the nodes reflects the number of participants assigned to each treatment, while the thickness of the connecting lines represents the number of studies contributing to that comparison. Together, these networks provide an overview of the available evidence for the analyzed outcomes—Schirmer test score (STS), tear break-up time (TBUT), and corneal fluorescein staining (CFS)—at different follow-up time points (1 month, 3 months, and beyond 3 months), forming the basis for the subsequent comparative analyses.

Network plots of included interventions across outcomes and time points. Network plots illustrate the structure of treatment comparisons included in the Bayesian network meta-analysis across all evaluated outcomes and follow-up periods. Each panel represents a specific outcome and time point: (A) corneal fluorescein staining (CFS) at 1 month, (B) CFS at 3 months, (C) CFS beyond 3 months, (D) Schirmer test score (STS) at 1 month, (E) STS at 3 months, (F) STS beyond 3 months, (G) tear break-up time (TBUT) at 3 months, and (H) TBUT beyond 3 months. Nodes represent individual interventions, including cyclosporine a (0.05%, 0.09%, 0.1%), tacrolimus 0.03%, varenicline nasal spray, and placebo.

CFS at 1 Month

The network meta-analysis evaluating CFS at 1 month included two studies,23,29 covering 1721 eyes. As shown in Figure 5A, the SUCRA ranking plot identified CsA 0.09% as the highest-ranked treatment for improving CFS at this time point. Placebo ranked intermediate (SUCRA ≈ 43%), while CsA 0.05% had the lowest probability of being the most effective. The forest plot in Figure 5B showed a favorable trend for CsA 0.09% relative to CsA 0.05% (MD −0.359; 95% CrI, −0.867 to 0.144). Placebo also showed a favorable trend relative to CsA 0.05% (MD −0.161; 95% CrI, −0.576 to 0.254).

(A) Surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) plot showing the probability of each treatment being the most effective in improving CFS outcomes. Higher SUCRA values indicate greater likelihood of treatment efficacy. Cyclosporine a (CsA) 0.09% demonstrated the highest probability of effectiveness, followed by placebo, while CsA 0.05% ranked lowest. CFS at 3 Months

The network meta-analysis evaluating CFS at 3 months included five studies, with one four-arm study contributing two comparisons, and covered 1925 eyes.21–24,29 As shown in Figure 6A, CsA 0.09% ranked highest for reducing CFS scores. Placebo followed with a SUCRA value close to 70%, while CsA 0.05% and tacrolimus 0.03% ranked lower. The forest plot in Figure 6B showed a favorable trend for CsA 0.09% Relative to CsA 0.05% (MD −0.482; 95% CrI, −2.68 to 1.11), as did placebo (MD −0.322; 95% CrI, −1.53 to 0.360). Tacrolimus 0.03% had an MD of 0.829 (95% CrI, −1.62 to 2.89) relative to CsA 0.05%, indicating a less favorable point estimate, although the credible interval was wide.

TBUT at 1 Month

Because only two treatment arms were available, a pairwise rather than network meta-analysis was conducted for TBUT at 1 month. Three RCTs involving 357 eyes were included.18,21,23 Placebo showed a small, non-significant reduction in TBUT relative to CsA 0.05% (MD −0.71 s; 95% CI, −2.38 to 0.95; P = 0.40), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 80%; P = 0.006 for the heterogeneity test) (Figure 11A). After excluding Perry et al (2006),21 heterogeneity was fully resolved (I2 = 0%) and the direction of effect reversed, with placebo showing a small, non-significant increase in TBUT relative to CsA 0.05% (MD 0.20 s; 95% CI, −0.15 to 0.54; P = 0.26) (Figure 11B).

(B) Forest plot presenting mean differences in TBUT compared with CsA 0.05%, expressed as mean differences with 95% credible intervals (CrI) derived from the Bayesian random-effects network meta-analysis. Negative mean differences indicate greater improvement in tear film stability relative to CsA 0.05%. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that, among all evaluated interventions for moderate to severe DED, CsA 0.09% and 0.1% consistently emerged as the most effective treatments in reducing CFS. Tacrolimus 0.03% showed favorable early STS and TBUT trends but ranked lower for CFS at 3 months and did not consistently outperform CsA across outcomes or longer follow-up. In terms of tear production and stability, placebo performed unexpectedly well, especially at 3 months and beyond, occasionally outperforming low-dose CsA and VNS. These observations reflect the heterogeneous nature of DED and the variability in treatment response, likely influenced by disease subtype, ocular surface inflammation, and neural feedback integrity. With the growing adoption of neuromodulators and novel immunomodulatory agents, elucidating their comparative efficacy is critical for optimizing care. Effective DED management, especially in moderate to severe cases, necessitates individualized therapeutic strategies, patient education, and the careful balancing of efficacy, tolerability, and onset of action in treatment planning. Prior studies have underscored the clinical value of higher-concentration CsA formulations. Fahmy et al (2025) demonstrated that CsA 0.1% in a semi-fluorinated alkane vehicle yielded the most rapid and consistent improvements in corneal CFS among FDA-approved topical agents.46 Similarly, Jee et al (2025) reported superior efficacy of CsA 0.1% in patients with inadequate response to CsA 0.05%.47 Notably, White et al (2023) observed faster short-term tear production gains with varenicline nasal spray compared to CsA 0.05% in an indirect comparison.16 In line with these results, our network meta-analysis identified CsA 0.09% and 0.1% as the most effective treatments, particularly for improving CFS, reinforcing their anti-inflammatory activity across both aqueous-deficient and evaporative dry eye phenotypes. Interestingly, CsA 0.1% ranked lower than placebo for TBUT improvement beyond 3 months, a finding that may appear counterintuitive. This result is primarily attributable to the design and outcomes of the SANSIKA trial, the source of this data point.28,31 In one study, CsA CE yielded a modest TBUT improvement (+0.8 seconds), which was not statistically significant compared with the vehicle (+0.3 seconds; p = 0.298).47 Importantly, several placebo comparators used in the included trials were not entirely inactive. For example, Chung et al used 0.9% normal saline, while the SANSIKA studies employed a cationic vehicle emulsion with known lubricating and tear film–stabilizing properties.17 These vehicle effects may partly explain the favorable performance of placebo in some STS and TBUT analyses. This observation also aligns with the TFOS DEWS III framework, which emphasizes phenotype-guided management and recognizes the therapeutic value of lubrication-based strategies in selected DED phenotypes. In our network meta-analysis, VNS showed a moderate improvement in STS at 3 months, suggesting a tear-stimulating effect. However, since none of the included trials evaluated its impact on CFS, its role in improving ocular surface health remains uncertain. Given its mechanism—neurostimulation of the nasolacrimal reflex without direct anti-inflammatory action—varenicline may be better suited for early-stage or non-inflammatory aqueous-deficient DED. This interpretation is supported by a matching-adjusted indirect comparison by White et al (2023), which demonstrated significantly faster and greater short-term STS improvements with varenicline compared to CsA 0.05%, highlighting its rapid onset in stimulating tear production.16 Varenicline solution nasal spray (VNS) is generally well tolerated and offers a non-ocular route of administration. Its proposed mechanism involves activation of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors within the nasal mucosa, stimulating the trigeminal–parasympathetic pathway and lacrimal functional unit to increase tear secretion, thereby bypassing ocular surface stimulation.33 Given that neurosensory abnormalities are recognized as contributory to dry eye disease pathophysiology, this neurostimulatory approach may be particularly attractive in patients in whom conventional reflex tearing pathways are dysregulated, although phenotype-specific efficacy data remain limited. These findings suggest that varenicline may complement, rather than replace, conventional anti-inflammatory therapies. In clinical practice, its distinct mechanism may be particularly valuable in patients with predominant aqueous deficiency or neurosensory dysfunction, whereas patients with substantial ocular surface inflammation may require concomitant immunomodulatory treatment. This observation further supports an emerging phenotype-guided approach to dry eye disease management.6 Clinically, these findings support tailoring treatment selection to the predominant DED phenotype, with anti-inflammatory therapies favored in inflammation-driven disease and tear-stimulating agents potentially benefiting patients with aqueous deficiency or neurosensory dysfunction. CsA 0.05% has historically served as a benchmark topical immunomodulatory therapy for DED, supported by extensive clinical experience, a well-characterized safety profile, and regulatory approval for increasing tear production in inflammation-associated keratoconjunctivitis sicca.48 However, our network meta-analysis revealed that its clinical efficacy is relatively modest, particularly when compared to higher concentrations such as CsA 0.09% and 0.1%. These differences were particularly pronounced in both short- and long-term outcomes, particularly CFS, where CsA 0.05% consistently demonstrated inferior efficacy relative to higher-concentration formulations (CsA 0.09% and 0.1%), and in some instances, even underperformed compared to placebo. This may be attributable to its lower immunosuppressive potency, which limits its effectiveness in more severe or refractory disease states. While Wan et al (2015) reported significant improvements in CFS, TBUT, and STS with CsA 0.05%, these findings were derived from placebo-controlled studies lacking active comparators, potentially inflating perceived benefit.49 Additionally, Zhou et al (2014) demonstrated that both low- and high-concentration CsA formulations significantly improved STS, especially in patients with Sjögren’s syndrome.50 However, our meta-analysis included a broader DED population comprising both Sjögren’s and non-Sjögren’s subtypes, which may have diluted the specific benefits observed in autoimmune-mediated disease. Collectively, these findings suggest that while CsA 0.05% remains a widely used and well-tolerated agent, its efficacy may be suboptimal in moderate-to-severe DED, and clinicians should consider higher-dose or alternative therapies when targeting tear production and ocular surface repair in more advanced cases. Tacrolimus 0.03% has been investigated as an alternative to CsA 0.05% in the management of moderate-to-severe DED, particularly in immune-mediated subtypes such as Sjögren’s syndrome.30 Mechanistically, tacrolimus is a lipophilic calcineurin inhibitor with high potency, which may contribute to clinically observed early improvements in some studies, although comparative pharmacodynamic explanations remain indirect in ocular-surface disease.51 In our network meta-analysis, tacrolimus 0.03% showed favorable early trends relative to CsA 0.05%, particularly for STS and TBUT at 1 month, but it did not consistently demonstrate superiority across outcomes or later time points. These findings are consistent with previous clinical data. Moscovici et al (2015, 2012) reported significant improvements in STS and symptom relief in patients with Sjögren’s syndrome after three months of tacrolimus therapy.30,52 Preclinical and formulation studies by Luaces-Rodríguez et al (2018) confirmed favorable ocular tolerability and effective drug delivery.53 Despite these strengths, tacrolimus 0.03% ranked lowest in our meta-analysis for CFS improvement in three months. This suggests that while tacrolimus may offer a rapid and robust anti-inflammatory effect in aqueous-deficient or early-phase DED, its longer-term benefits on epithelial integrity may be more limited. Variability in vehicle composition, patient population, and trial design could partly explain the observed differences in long-term outcomes. Nonetheless, our findings, alongside existing literature, support further consideration of tacrolimus 0.03% as an early-phase option, particularly in patients who exhibit suboptimal responses to CsA 0.05%. This study’s primary strength lies in its rigorous design and comprehensive scope. By integrating both direct and indirect comparisons through a Bayesian network meta-analysis, we were able to generate comparative efficacy rankings across multiple time points and clinical outcomes for topical CsA 0.05%, 0.09%, and 0.1%; tacrolimus 0.03%; and neurostimulatory nasal sprays. The inclusion of anesthetized STS as a primary measure adds methodological rigor by minimizing reflex tearing and improving accuracy in assessing basal tear production. Furthermore, by incorporating studies across diverse populations and DED subtypes—including aqueous-deficient and evaporative forms—we provide a broader generalizability to real-world practice. The systematic review also incorporated newer agents such as varenicline, expanding the therapeutic landscape beyond conventional immunomodulation. Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, outcome heterogeneity across included trials arising from differences in disease severity, usage of various disease severity assessment scores, and formulation vehicles, may have introduced variability in treatment effects. Second, although the Bayesian framework enables robust indirect comparisons, its conclusions are inherently dependent on the quality and consistency of included data. Notably, six included studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias and six had an unclear risk of bias. Therefore, the treatment rankings and SUCRA estimates should be interpreted in the context of the methodological limitations of the included evidence. For example, the superior performance of placebo in some outcomes may reflect potent vehicle effects or methodological artifacts, rather than true therapeutic superiority. Third, data on long-term outcomes, adverse events, and patient-reported symptom relief were variably reported, limiting comprehensive assessment. Lastly, OSDI, a critical patient-reported outcome measure, could not be included in the network meta-analysis because of insufficient and inconsistent reporting across the included studies. Given the well-recognized symptom–sign discordance in dry eye disease, improvements in objective clinical signs such as STS, TBUT, and CFS do not necessarily correspond to improvements in patient-reported symptoms. Consequently, while the present analysis provides robust comparative evidence regarding clinical efficacy, it cannot definitively determine which intervention offers the greatest symptomatic benefit from the patient’s perspective.

Conclusion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis identified CsA 0.09% and 0.1% as the highest-ranked therapies for improving ocular surface integrity in moderate-to-severe DED, while tacrolimus 0.03% showed a rapid but potentially transient benefit in tear production. Varenicline nasal spray offered a promising neurostimulatory effect but lacked data on CFS. These findings support individualized treatment based on DED phenotype, disease chronicity, and symptom–sign concordance. Future research should prioritize head-to-head trials, standardized disease phenotyping, and long-term patient-reported outcomes.
