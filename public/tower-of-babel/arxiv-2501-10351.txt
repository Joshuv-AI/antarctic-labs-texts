Purcell-Enhanced, Directional Light–Matter Interaction in a Waveguide-Coupled Nanocavity
Nicholas J. Martin, Dominic Hallett, Mateusz Duda, Luke Hallacy, Elena Callus, Luke Brunswick, René Dost, Edmund Clarke, Pallavi K. Patil, Pieter Kok, Maurice S. Skolnick, Luke R. Wilson

Abstract
We demonstrate electrically tunable, spin-dependent, directional coupling of single photons by embedding quantum dots (QDs) in a waveguide-coupled nanocavity. The directional behavior arises from direction-dependent interference between two cavity modes when coupled to the device waveguides. The small mode volume cavity enables simultaneous Purcell enhancement ( 10.8 ± 0.7 {10.8\pm 0.7} ) and peak directional contrast ( 88 ± 1 % {88\pm 1\%} ), exceeding current state-of-the-art waveguide-only systems. We also present a scattering matrix model for the transmission through this structure, alongside a quantum trajectory-based model for predicting the system’s directionality, which we use to explain the observed asymmetry in directional contrast seen in QD devices. Furthermore, the nanocavity enables wide-range electrical tuning of the emitter’s directional contrast. We present results showing precise tuning of a QD emission line from a directional contrast of 2 % {2\%} to 96 % {96\%} . In combination, these characteristics make this cavity-waveguide approach promising for use as a building block in directional nanophotonic circuits.

III
Theory

To accurately capture the behavior of the device, our theory must account for realistic QD properties, including fine structure splitting, imperfect crystalline symmetry, and dipole misalignment. These factors lead to deviations from ideal circular polarization and to coherent population transfer between the QD’s excited states. A simple two-level model of a perfectly aligned emitter cannot reproduce the asymmetric directional emission that we observed in our experiments. By including a three-level QD structure, complex coupling rates, and the associated interference effects, our model provides a faithful representation of the QD-cavity-waveguide system.

In this section, we provide an overview of the theoretical models used to describe the waveguide-coupled nanocavity system and to predict the device’s performance. We present the key equations below; more details are provided in the appendices, with detailed derivations given in the Supplementary Information.

As shown in Fig.
2
, we consider a QD with fine structure splitting, modeled as a three-level V-type emitter
\{\ket{g},\ket{e_1},\ket{e_2}\}
, coupled to two orthogonal cavity modes
\{H,V\}
, and further coupled to left (
L
) and right (
R
) waveguides. The frequency of the
\ket{g}\leftrightarrow\ket{e_j}
transition is
\omega_{e,j}
and the emitter-cavity coupling rates are denoted by
g_{\alpha,j}
, where
j\in\{1,2\}
labels the QD transitions and
\alpha\in\{H,V\}
labels the two cavity modes. The mode frequency of the
H
(
V
) mode is
\omega_{c,H}
(
\omega_{c,V}
). Furthermore, the cavity-waveguide coupling rates are
V_{\mu,\alpha}
, where
\mu\in\{L,R\}
labels the left and right waveguides, respectively. The cavity modes are coupled to the two waveguides with equal strength, so we calculate these coupling rates as
{V_{R,\alpha}=V_{L,\alpha}=\omega_{c,\alpha}/2Q_{\alpha}}
, where
Q_{\alpha}
is the quality (
Q
) factor of mode
\alpha
.

The total Hamiltonian for the system takes the form:

H=H_{e}+H_{c}+H_{\text{wg}}+H_{e\text{-}c}+H_{c\text{-wg}},

(1)

where the emitter Hamiltonian
H_{e}
, the cavity Hamiltonian
H_{c}
, the waveguide Hamiltonian
H_{\text{wg}}
, the emitter-cavity interaction
H_{e\text{-}c}
, and the cavity-waveguide interaction
H_{c\text{-wg}}
are given in Appendix A. Using the input-output formalism
[
24
,
25
]
, we derive the single-photon scattering matrix elements for the waveguide-to-waveguide transmission, e.g.,

S_{pk}^{RL}=t_{RL}\,\delta(p-k),

(2)

where
k
and
p
denote the input and output photon frequencies, respectively, and
t_{RL}
is the transmission coefficient for left-to-right transmission (see Appendix B, and Supplementary Information Section S1 for the derivation and result). From this, we obtain the transmission probability
|t_{RL}|^{2}
, and use it to fit experimental data from our transmission measurements.

To predict the directional contrast of the system, we use a space-discretized waveguide model based on quantum trajectory theory
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
, which allows us to simulate the time evolution of the system and calculate the probability of a photon being emitted into each of the waveguides. In the model, each waveguide is discretized into a series of
N
boxes. At each time step in the quantum trajectory algorithm, the cavity interacts with the first box in each waveguide (box
0
), photon number measurements are simulated on the final box in each waveguide (box
{N-1}
), the quantum state of the system is projected according to the measurement results, and the boxes are moved along by one (see Appendix C and Supplementary Information Section S2 for more details). Each trajectory is a stochastic evolution process, so we take an average over many trajectories to compute the expected time evolution of observables (conditioned on photon detection events at the waveguide ends). The contrast
C
is then given by the normalized difference between the final waveguide box populations at the end of the trajectories (i.e., at time
{t=t_{\text{end}}}
):

C=\left|\frac{\bigl\langle A_{R,N-1}^{{\dagger}}A_{R,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\bigr\rangle_{t=t_{\text{end}}}-\bigl\langle A_{L,N-1}^{{\dagger}}A_{L,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\bigr\rangle_{t=t_{\text{end}}}}{\bigl\langle A_{R,N-1}^{{\dagger}}A_{R,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\bigr\rangle_{t=t_{\text{end}}}+\bigl\langle A_{L,N-1}^{{\dagger}}A_{L,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\bigr\rangle_{t=t_{\text{end}}}}\right|,

(3)

where
A_{\mu,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}
(
A_{\mu,N-1}^{{\dagger}}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator for the final box of waveguide
\mu
(box
{N-1}
). The end time
t_{\text{end}}
is chosen such that the emitter has fully decayed to its ground state
\ket{g}
by the end of the simulations, and all of the photon population has been transferred to the ends of the waveguides. Eq. (
3
) allows us to predict the directional contrast of our system for a given initial state and chosen parameters, and therefore to study how the energy level structure of the QD and the coupling rates affect the degree of directionality.

From our experimental results, we can extract the contrast using the relative power transmitted through the waveguides when exciting the QD in the cavity:

C_{\sigma^{+}/\sigma^{-}}=\left|\frac{P_{L/R}-P_{R/L}}{P_{L/R}+P_{R/L}}\right|,

(4)

where
P_{R}
(
P_{L}
) is the power transmitted to the right (left). This enables us to quantify the directional performance of our device and to compare the experimental results with theoretical predictions from the quantum trajectory model.

As shown in Fig.

V
Conclusion

In conclusion, our work has successfully demonstrated a waveguide-coupled cavity nanophotonic device that achieves a remarkable balance between near-unity directional coupling and significant Purcell enhancement for an embedded circularly polarized emitter. The low mode volume of the H1 cavity, approximately
{V\approx 0.6(\lambda/n)^{3}}

[
32
]
, is critical in achieving a large emitter-cavity coupling strength within a relatively low-
Q
(
{Q\approx 400\text{-}1000}
) cavity environment. The performance of the device is robust against both spectral and spatial perturbations of the embedded emitter, a significant advance over alternative approaches
[
23
]
. In particular, as shown in Ref
[
23
]
the directional contrast of our device remains exceptionally high even with emitter displacements up to
60
nm from the center of the cavity. This feature underlines the potential for the device in practical applications where precise emitter positioning can be challenging.

Furthermore, our experiments on two devices reveal that the directional contrast strongly depends on the wavelengths of the cavity modes and the emitter. This sensitivity allows post-fabrication tuning and optimization
[
33
]
, demonstrating the device’s adaptability to varying emitter properties while maintaining high performance. We present results showing precise tuning of a QD emission line from a directional contrast of
2\%
to
96\%
. Notably, our quantum trajectory model explains for the first time the observed asymmetry in directional contrast seen in QD-waveguide devices and provides a clear pathway for optimizing QD-based directional light–matter interfaces, even in the face of imperfect polarization.

Lastly, the general mechanism behind our directional light–matter interaction is not confined to our specific cavity design. It extends to other cavity architectures that support degenerate orthogonal modes, such as micropillars
[
34
]
, and would be effective for systems operating in the telecom bands
[
35
,
36
]
. This broad applicability could pave the way for a range of high-performance applications in quantum nanophotonics, including reconfigurable phase shifters
[
37
]
, quantum routers and switches
[
38
]
, and compact optical circulators
[
19
]
.

Appendix A: System Hamiltonian

We consider the system depicted in Fig.
2
in the main text. In Eq. (
1
), we have the free emitter Hamiltonian (
{\hbar=1}
)

H_{e}=\sum_{j=1,2}\omega_{e,j}\sigma_{j}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}

(5)

(where
\sigma_{j}^{+}=\outerproduct{e_j}{g}
and
\sigma_{j}^{-}=\outerproduct{g}{e_j}
are the raising and lowering operators for transition
{j\in\{1,2\}}
), the free cavity Hamiltonian

H_{c}=\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\omega_{c,\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

(6)

(where
c_{\alpha}
and
c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}
are the annihilation and creation operators for mode
{\alpha\in\{H,V\}}
), and the free waveguide Hamiltonian

H_{\text{wg}}=\sum_{\mu=L,R}\int\omega(k)a_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(k)a_{\mu}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(k)dk,

(7)

where the operators
a_{\mu}(k)
and
a_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(k)
annihilate and create photons with wave number
k
in waveguide
{\mu\in\{L,R\}}
. This Hamiltonian assumes identical dispersion relations
\omega(k)
for both waveguides.

The emitter-cavity interaction is of the Jaynes-Cummings form:

H_{e\text{-}c}=\sum_{j=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\left(g_{\alpha,j}^{\vphantom{*}}\sigma_{j}^{+}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+g_{\alpha,j}^{*}\sigma_{j}^{-}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}\right),

and the cavity-waveguide interaction, under the Markov approximation
[
24
]
(assuming coupling independent of

), is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{c\text{-wg}}=\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{\mu=L,R}&\int\biggl[\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(k)c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\\
&+\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(k)c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\biggr]dk.\end{split}

(9)

The phase factors
e^{\pm i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}
reflect the anti-symmetric coupling of the cavity modes into the left and right waveguides, which gives rise to the interference that enables directional emission in our device

Appendix B: Scattering Matrix Model

The single-photon scattering matrix elements are given by:

S_{pk}^{\mu\nu}=\langle p_{\mu}|S|k_{\nu}\rangle=\innerproduct{p_{\mu}^-}{k_{\nu}^+}=\expectationvalue{a_{\mu,\text{out}}(p) a_{\nu,\text{in}}^{\dag}(k)}{0},

(10)

where
k
is the frequency of the input photon in waveguide
\nu
,
p
is the frequency of the output photon in waveguide
\mu
,
{\ket{k_{\nu}^+}=a_{\nu,\text{in}}^{{\dagger}}(k)\ket{0}}
and
{\ket{p_{\mu}^-}=a_{\mu,\text{out}}^{{\dagger}}(p)\ket{0}}
are scattering eigenstates, and
\ket{0}
is the vacuum state of the system. For transmission, we set
{\mu\neq\nu}
, such that the output photon leaves in the opposite waveguide to the input photon. For example, for left-to-right transmission,
{\nu=L}
and
{\mu=R}
, and the corresponding transmission matrix element has the form:

S_{pk}^{RL}=\expectationvalue{a_{R,\text{out}}(p) a_{L,\text{in}}^{\dag}(k)}{0}=t_{RL}\,\delta(p-k),

(11)

where
t_{RL}
is the transmission coefficient, and
{\delta(p-k)}
enforces energy conservation. The derivation of the scattering matrix is presented in Supplementary Information Section S1, where we obtain the transmission coefficient for both left-to-right and right-to-left propagation, and demonstrate that the directional light–matter interaction in our cavity can give rise to nonreciprocal phase shifts.

Appendix C: Quantum Trajectory Model

In the quantum trajectory model, each waveguide is discretized into a series of
N
spatial bins of width
\Delta t
in time (labeled with the index
{n\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}}

\smash{a_{\mu}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(k)}
,
\smash{a_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(k)}

\smash{a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}
,
\smash{a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}}

A_{R,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{R,k}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}

(12)

and

A_{L,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{L,k}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}

(13)

to transform from
k
-space to position space (

is the discretized waveguide dispersion relation). The operator

(
A_{\mu,n}^{{\dagger}}
) annihilates (creates) a photon in spatial bin
n
of waveguide
\mu
. After discretizing the Hamiltonian, we express all operators as matrices by choosing the following basis consisting of states with at most one photon in the system (since we consider single-photon emission from the cavity):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Bigl\{&\ket{g, 0_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},\\

&\ket{e_2, 0_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},\\[3.61371pt]
&\ket{g, 1_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},\\[3.61371pt]
&\ket{g, 0_H, 1_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},\\[3.61371pt]
&\ket{g, 0_H, 0_V, 1_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},\\
&\hskip 93.95122pt\vdots\\
&\ket{g, 0_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 1_{L,0}}\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(14)

The basis states are tensor products of states from the emitter basis, the cavity basis, and the discrete position-space waveguide basis, where
\ket{0_\alpha}
(
\ket{1_\alpha}
) corresponds to no photons (one photon) in cavity mode
\alpha

\ket{0_{\mu,n}}
(
\ket{1_{\mu,n}}
) corresponds to no photons (one photon) in spatial bin
n

\mu

At the start of each trajectory simulation, we set the emitter to be in a superposition of its excited states
\ket{e_1}
and
\ket{e_2}

\ket{\psi(0)}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ket{\psi(0)}&=\alpha\ket{e_1,0_H,0_V,0_{R,N-1},\dots,0_{L,0}}\\

(15)

with the normalization condition

At each time step in the quantum trajectory algorithm, the cavity interacts with the first box (
{n=0}

for

at each time step, which are the populations of the final waveguide boxes. From these, we calculate

at the end of the trajectories (

), once the emitter has fully decayed and all the photon population has reached the ends of the waveguides.

Appendix D: Purcell Enhancement

Considering the two modes of our cavity system, a circularly polarized emitter will emit into the modes as a proportion of the Purcell factor of that mode (
{\alpha\in\{H,V\}}
):

f_{\alpha}=\frac{F_{\alpha}}{F_{H}+F_{V}}.

(17)

The total Purcell factor for a circularly polarized emitter in the cavity is given by:

F_{\text{tot}}=\frac{F_{H}^{2}+F_{V}^{2}}{F_{H}+F_{V}},

(18)

where, for
{\alpha\in\{H,V\}}
,

F_{\alpha}=\frac{{3Q_{\alpha}}}{{4{\mathrm{\pi}}^{2}V_{\alpha}}}\frac{{(2\kappa_{\alpha})^{2}}}{{4\left({\omega_{e}-\omega_{c,\alpha}}\right)^{2}+(2\kappa_{\alpha})^{2}}}\frac{\left|{\bm{\mu}}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}({\mathrm{r}_{0}})\right|^{2}}{\left|{\bm{\mu}}\right|^{2}\left|{{\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathrm{max}}}}\right|^{2}}.

(19)

Here,
Q_{\alpha}
is the
Q
factor of cavity mode
\alpha
, and
V_{\alpha}
is the mode volume in cubic wavelengths. In addition,
\omega_{e}
is the emitter transition frequency,
\omega_{c,\alpha}
is the mode frequency of cavity mode
\alpha
, and
2\kappa_{\alpha}
is the full width at half-maximum of the cavity mode. Furthermore,
\bm{\mu}
,
{\mathbf{E}}({\mathrm{r}_{0}})
, and
{\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathrm{max}}}
are the transition dipole moment, electric field at the emitter’s position, and the maximum electric field in the cavity, respectively.

\alpha
, and we used the commutator
{[c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t),c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}(t)]=1}
(all other equal-time commutators involving the cavity operators are zero). We also used Eq. (
S15a
) to eliminate the integral in the second line. Note that adding the Lindblad terms is equivalent to replacing the cavity mode frequencies
\omega_{c,\alpha}
with
{\omega_{c,\alpha}-i\kappa_{\alpha}/2}
, effectively making the frequencies complex. Next, we perform a Fourier transform from time
t
to frequency
p
, allowing us to eliminate the time derivative:

\displaystyle\begin{split}-ipc_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(p)=&-i\left(\omega_{c,\alpha}-\frac{i\kappa_{\alpha}}{2}\right)c_{\alpha}(p)-i\sum_{j=1,2}g_{\alpha,j}^{*}\sigma_{j}^{-}(p)-i\sum_{\mu=L,R}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\sqrt{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}a_{\mu,\text{in}}(p)\\[3.61371pt]
&-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{\beta=H,V}e^{i\pi\delta_{\mu L}(\delta_{\beta H}-\delta_{\alpha H})}\sqrt{V_{\mu,\beta}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}c_{\beta}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(p).\end{split}

(S20)

If we pre-multiply by
\bra{0}
and post-multiply by
\ket{k_{\nu}^+}
, and use
{\matrixelement{0}{a_{\mu,\text{in}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}=\expectationvalue{a_{\mu,\text{in}}(p) a_{\nu,\text{in}}^{\dag}(k)}{0}=\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta(p-k)}
, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}-i\left(\Delta_{c,\alpha}+\frac{i\kappa_{\alpha}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}=&-i\sum_{j=1,2}g_{\alpha,j}^{*}\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}-ie^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\nu L}}\sqrt{V_{\nu,\alpha}^{*}}\delta(p-k)\\[3.61371pt]
&-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{\beta=H,V}e^{i\pi\delta_{\mu L}(\delta_{\beta H}-\delta_{\alpha H})}\sqrt{V_{\mu,\beta}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}\matrixelement{0}{c_{\beta}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+},\end{split}

(S21)

where we have also defined the frequency detuning
\Delta_{c,\alpha}=p-\omega_{c,\alpha}
. In order to find the matrix elements
\matrixelement{0}{c_H^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}
and
\matrixelement{0}{c_V^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}
[and hence the scattering matrix elements in Eq. (
S18
)], we therefore need to find
\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}
for
{j\in\{1,2\}}
. We do this using the Heisenberg equation for
\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)
, where we include Lindblad terms corresponding to photon loss from transition
j
of the emitter at rate
\gamma_{j}
(corresponding to emission into non-cavity modes):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)&=i\Bigl[H,\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)\Bigr]+L_{e,j}^{{\dagger}}\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)L_{e,j}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\{L_{e,j}^{{\dagger}}L_{e,j}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}},\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)\Bigr\}\\[3.61371pt]
&=-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)+i\sum_{k=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,k}e^{iHt}\outerproduct{e_k}{e_j}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t)-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}e^{iHt}\outerproduct{g}{g}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t),\end{split}

(S22)

where
{L_{e,j}=\sqrt{\gamma_{j}}\sigma_{j}^{-}}
is the Lindblad operator for loss from transition
j
, and we used the time-evolved operators
\sigma_{j}^{-}(t)={e^{iHt}\sigma_{j}^{-}(0)e^{-iHt}}={e^{iHt}\outerproduct{g}{e_j}e^{-iHt}}
and
\sigma_{j}^{+}(t)={e^{iHt}\sigma_{j}^{+}(0)e^{-iHt}}={e^{iHt}\outerproduct{e_j}{g}e^{-iHt}}
to evaluate the commutators and operator products. Note again that including the Lindblad terms is equivalent to replacing
\omega_{e,j}
with
{\omega_{e,j}-i\gamma_{j}/2}
, effectively making the emitter transition frequencies complex. We now pre-multiply by
\bra{0}
and post-multiply by
\ket{k_{\nu}^+}
to obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}=&-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}+i\sum_{k=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,k}\bra{0}e^{iHt}\ket{e_k}\bra{e_j}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t)|k_{\nu}^{+}\rangle\\[3.61371pt]
&-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\bra{0}e^{iHt}\ket{g}\bra{g}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t)|k_{\nu}^{+}\rangle\\[3.61371pt]
=&-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}+i\sum_{k=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,k}\innerproduct{0}{e_k}\bra{e_j}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t)|k_{\nu}^{+}\rangle\\[3.61371pt]
&-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\innerproduct{0}{g}\bra{g}e^{-iHt}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(t)|k_{\nu}^{+}\rangle\\[3.61371pt]
=&-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\matrixelement{0}{e^{-iHt} c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}\\[3.61371pt]
=&-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(t)}{k_{\nu}^+}-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\matrixelement{0}{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(t)}{k_{\nu}^+},\end{split}

(S23)

where we used
{e^{\pm iHt}\ket{0}=\ket{0}}
(which follows from
{H\ket{0}=0}
), as well as
{\innerproduct{0}{e_k}=0}
and
{\innerproduct{0}{g}\bra{g}=\bra{0}}
. As before, we Fourier transform from time to frequency to eliminate the time derivative and obtain an algebraic equation involving the matrix elements:

-ip\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}=-i\left(\omega_{e,j}-\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}-i\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\matrixelement{0}{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}.

(S24)

Solving for
\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}

\matrixelement{0}{\sigma_j^-(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{e,j}+\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}g_{\alpha,j}\matrixelement{0}{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+},

(S25)

where we have defined the detuning
\Delta_{e,j}=p-\omega_{e,j}
. Substituting the result in Eq. (

\displaystyle\begin{split}-i\left(\Delta_{c,\alpha}+\frac{i\kappa_{\alpha}}{2}\right)\matrixelement{0}{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}=&-\sum_{\beta=H,V}\Biggl[\hskip 1.4457pt\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{ig_{\beta,j}^{\vphantom{*}}g_{\alpha,j}^{*}}{\Delta_{e,j}+\frac{i\gamma_{j}}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=L,R}e^{i\pi\delta_{\mu L}(\delta_{\beta H}-\delta_{\alpha H})}\sqrt{V_{\mu,\beta}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}\Biggr]\matrixelement{0}{c_{\beta}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}\\[3.61371pt]

(S26)

Since

{\alpha=H}

{\alpha=V}

\matrixelement{0}{c_H^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}

(S27a)

(S27b)

where we have defined

(S28a)

(S28b)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{pk}^{\mu\nu}=&\;\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta(p-k)-ie^{i\pi\delta_{\mu L}}\sqrt{V_{\mu,H}^{\vphantom{*}}}\matrixelement{0}{c_{H}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}-i\sqrt{V_{\mu,V}^{\vphantom{*}}}\matrixelement{0}{c_{V}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(p)}{k_{\nu}^+}\\[3.61371pt]

&\times\delta(p-k).\end{split}

(S29)

For transmission from the left waveguide to the right waveguide, we set
{\nu=L}
and
{\mu=R}
(since
\nu
labels the waveguide of the input photon and

(S31)

is the transmission coefficient for left-to-right (
{L\rightarrow R}
) propagation. From this result, we can calculate the phase acquired by a transmitted photon, as this is given by the argument of the complex amplitude
t_{RL}

\phi_{RL}=\text{arg}\bigl(t_{RL}\bigr)=\text{atan2}\bigl(\imaginary\{t_{RL}\},\real\{t_{RL}\}\bigr),

(S32)

where

By setting
{\nu=R}
and

S_{pk}^{LR}=t_{LR}\delta(p-k),

(S33)

where

t_{LR}=\frac{i\left(-B_{V,H}\sqrt{V_{L,H}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{R,V}^{*}}+B_{H,V}\sqrt{V_{L,V}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{R,H}^{*}}+A_{V}\sqrt{V_{L,H}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{R,H}^{*}}-A_{H}\sqrt{V_{L,V}^{\vphantom{*}}V_{R,V}^{*}}\right)}{A_{H}A_{V}-B_{H,V}B_{V,H}}

(S34)

is the transmission coefficient for right-to-left (
{R\rightarrow L}
) propagation, from which we can calculate the corresponding transmission phase:

\phi_{LR}=\text{arg}\bigl(t_{LR}\bigr)=\text{atan2}\bigl(\imaginary\{t_{LR}\},\real\{t_{LR}\}\bigr).

(S35)

The fits to the transmission data in the main text were obtained by calculating the transmission

with the experimentally measured system parameters (equivalently, we could calculate

, as the cavity mode spectra from the main text are the same for

and

and

S2
Quantum Trajectory Model for Emission Directionality

In this section, we present the quantum trajectory model that we use to predict the directional contrast of our system. As mentioned in the main text, this model is based on a space-discretized waveguide approach
[
27
]
. First, in Section
S2.1
we discretize our Hamiltonian from the main text [see Eq. (
S1
)]. We then outline how this discretization allows us to simulate the time evolution of our system using quantum trajectory theory in Section
S2.2
. Some example trajectory results are shown in Section
S2.3
, corresponding to Device 1 and Device 2 from the main text.

S2.1
Hamiltonian Discretization

Figure S3:
The waveguide-coupled cavity system treated within the space-discretized quantum trajectory model. Each waveguide is divided into
N
spatial bins of width
\Delta t
in time. The boxes are labeled with the index
{n\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}}
. At each time step in the quantum trajectory algorithm, the cavity interacts with box
0
in each waveguide, photon number measurements are simulated on the final waveguide boxes, and the boxes are moved along by one (in the direction indicated by the red arrows).

As mentioned in the main text, each of the waveguides is discretized into a series of
N
spatial bins/boxes, see Fig.
S3
. This discretization enables the time evolution of the system to be solved numerically using quantum trajectories. In order to discretize the
k
-space Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
S1
), we replace the integrals over
k
with summations over a finite number
N
of
k
-space modes, and we replace the continuum waveguide mode operators
\smash{a_{\mu}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(k)}
,
\smash{a_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(k)}
with discrete mode operators
\smash{a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}
,
\smash{a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}}

[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H\rightarrow&\sum_{j=1,2}\omega_{e,j}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\sigma_{j}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}+\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\omega_{c,\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega_{k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sum_{j=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\Bigl(g_{\alpha,j}^{\vphantom{*}}\sigma_{j}^{+}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+g_{\alpha,j}^{*}\sigma_{j}^{-}c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\Bigr)\\[3.61371pt]
&+\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\Biggr),\end{split}

(S36)

where
{L_{0}=N\Delta t}
is the length of the discretized waveguides. This allows us to perform discrete Fourier transforms that replace the
k
-space waveguide operators with position-space operators describing the spatial bins (see Section
S2.2
):

A_{R,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{R,k}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t},\quad\text{and}\quad A_{L,n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}a_{L,k}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t},

(S37)

as given in Appendix C.

In order to perform numerical simulations of our system, we need to express the Hamiltonian (and any other observable of interest) as a matrix by choosing an appropriate basis. Since we look at single-photon emission from the cavity, the basis will only contain states with at most one photon in the system (higher photon number states are never occupied in this situation). At any given time, the photon can either exist as an excitation in the three-level emitter (
\ket{e_1}
or
\ket{e_2}
), one of the two cavity modes, or one of the
2N
spatial bins of the waveguides. Including also the ground state of the system, this amounts to a basis with
{2N+5}
elements, as given in Eq. (14) in Appendix C. As there are
{2N+5}
basis elements, all observables are represented by
{(2N+5)\times(2N+5)}
matrices, and the state of the system is represented by a vector of length
{2N+5}
.

S2.2
Quantum Trajectory Algorithm

After discretizing the Hamiltonian, we can use it to solve for the time evolution of the system numerically. This allows us to calculate the probability that an emitted photon ends up in the left waveguide or the right waveguide, from which we can extract the directional contrast. To obtain the time evolution, we follow the quantum trajectory approach presented in ref.
[
27
]
. First, we divide the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
S36
) into three parts:

H=H_{S}+H_{W}+H_{I},

(S38)

where

H_{S}=\sum_{j=1,2}\omega_{e,j}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\sigma_{j}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}+\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\omega_{c,\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sum_{j=1,2}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\Bigl(g_{\alpha,j}^{\vphantom{*}}\sigma_{j}^{+}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+g_{\alpha,j}^{*}\sigma_{j}^{-}c_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\Bigr)

(S39)

is the Hamiltonian for the emitter-cavity system,

H_{W}=\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\omega_{k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

(S40)

is the free waveguide Hamiltonian, and

H_{I}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{\mu=L,R}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu,k}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}a_{\mu,k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\Biggr)

(S41)

is the interaction between the cavity and the waveguides. As mentioned in Appendix C, we set the initial system state
\ket{\psi(0)}
to the case where the emitter in the cavity is excited in some superposition of its excited states,
\ket{e_1}
and
\ket{e_2}
:

\ket{\psi(0)}=\alpha\ket{e_1, 0_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}}+\beta\ket{e_2, 0_H, 0_V, 0_{R,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{R,0}, 0_{L,N-1}, \dotsc, 0_{L,0}},

(S42)

where
{|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1}
. Since
\alpha
is the coefficient of basis element
2
and
\beta
is the coefficient of basis element
3
[see Eq. (14) in the Appendix], the initial state is represented by a vector where the second element is
\alpha
and the third element is
\beta
, and all other elements are zero. The time evolution of the state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is then obtained by following the algorithm below at each time step:

(1) Calculate observables of interest at the beginning of the time step [for observable
O
, compute the expectation value
{\expectationvalue{O}=\expectationvalue{O}{\psi(t)}}
for the current state
\ket{\psi(t)}
].

(2) Evolve the state
\ket{\psi(t)}
by one time step. If a loss occurs, apply the corresponding Lindblad jump operator
L_{k}
and renormalize:

\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}=\frac{L_{k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\ket{\psi(t)}}{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{L_k^{\dag} L_k^{\vphantom{\dag}}}{\psi(t)}}}.

(S43)

If a loss does not occur, evolve the state under the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
{H_{\text{eff}}=H_{S}+H_{I}-i/2\sum_{k}L_{k}^{{\dagger}}L_{k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}
by directly applying the time evolution operator
{U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)=e^{-iH_{\text{eff}}\Delta t}}
[and renormalize as
U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)
is not unitary when losses are included in the model]:

\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}=\frac{U_{\text{eff}}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}(\Delta t)\ket{\psi(t)}}{\sqrt{\expectationvalue{U_{\text{eff}}^{\dag}(\Delta t) U_{\text{eff}}^{\vphantom{\dag}}(\Delta t)}{\psi(t)}}}.

(S44)

(3) Simulate a photon number measurement on the final waveguide boxes, and project the state according to the measurement outcome.

(4) Shift the waveguide boxes along by one. This corresponds to evolving the system under the free waveguide Hamiltonian
H_{W}
in Eq. (
S40
) for a time
\Delta t
.

(5) Renormalize the state before the next iteration:

\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}\rightarrow\frac{\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{\psi(t+\Delta t)}{\psi(t+\Delta t)}}}.

(S45)

In step (1), we calculate the populations of the final waveguide boxes (labeled with index
{N-1}
, see Fig.
S3
), as these give the probabilities for the photon being emitted into the respective waveguides. These populations are calculated using the position-space waveguide operators as (for
{\mu\in\{L,R\}}
):

\bigl\langle A_{\mu,N-1}^{{\dagger}}A_{\mu,N-1}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\bigr\rangle=\expectationvalue{A_{\mu,N-1}^{\dag} A_{\mu,N-1}^{\vphantom{\dag}}}{\psi(t)}.

(S46)

In addition to the waveguide box populations, we calculate the populations of the excited states
\ket{e_j}
of the emitter (for
{j\in\{1,2\}}
),

\expectationvalue{\sigma_j^+ \sigma_j^-}=\expectationvalue{\sigma_j^+ \sigma_j^-}{\psi(t)}=\left|\innerproduct{e_j}{\psi(t)}\right|^{2},

(S47)

as well as the populations of the two cavity modes (
{\alpha\in\{H,V\}}
):

\expectationvalue{c_{\alpha}^{\dag} c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}}=\expectationvalue{c_{\alpha}^{\dag} c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{\dag}}}{\psi(t)}.

(S48)

In order to implement step (2), we first need to determine if a loss occurs in the current time step. The losses we consider are the same as in the scattering matrix model, described by the Lindblad jump operators

L_{c,\alpha}=\sqrt{\kappa_{\alpha}}c_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{e,j}=\sqrt{\gamma_{j}}\sigma_{j}^{-},

(S49)

for
{\alpha\in\{H,V\}}
(cavity losses) and
{j\in\{1,2\}}
(emitter losses). We simulate whether a loss occurs by computing the total loss probability

P_{\text{loss}}(t)=\Delta t\sum_{k}\expectationvalue{L_k^{\dag} L_k^{\vphantom{\dag}}}{\psi(t)},

(S50)

and comparing it to a uniformly-distributed random number between
0
and
1
,
{\epsilon\in(0,1)}
. If
{\epsilon\leq P_{\text{loss}}}
then a loss occurs, and we evolve the state according to Eq. (
S43
). We choose which of the Lindblad jump operators
L_{k}
to apply by comparing the individual loss probabilities [given by
{P_{k}=\Delta t\expectationvalue{L_k^{\dag} L_k^{\vphantom{\dag}}}{\psi(t)}}
] against uniformly-distributed random numbers. On the other hand, if
{\epsilon>P_{\text{loss}}}
then no loss occurs, and we evolve the state under the effective Hamiltonian
{H_{\text{eff}}=H_{S}+H_{I}-i/2\sum_{k}L_{k}^{{\dagger}}L_{k}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}
[Eq. (
S44
)].

To be able to implement step (2), we need to find matrix representations for the Lindblad operators
L_{k}
and the time evolution operator
{U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)=e^{-iH_{\text{eff}}\Delta t}}
in the chosen basis, such that they can act on the vector representation of
\ket{\psi(t)}
. For the Lindblad operators [Eq. (
S49
)] and the emitter-cavity Hamiltonian
H_{S}
[Eq. (
S39
)] that goes into
U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)
, the matrix elements can immediately be found using the way in which the emitter operators
\sigma_{j}^{\pm}
and the cavity operators
\smash{c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}
,
\smash{c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}}
act on the basis states, as well as using the orthonormality of the basis elements. What then remains is to find the matrix for the cavity-waveguide interaction Hamiltonian
H_{I}
[Eq. (
S41
)], as this is also needed to find the matrix for
U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)
. Here, the
k
-space waveguide operators must first be replaced with the position-space operators for the spatial bins using the inverse of the Fourier transforms in Eq. (
S37
), i.e.,

a_{R,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{R,n}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t},\quad\text{and}\quad a_{L,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{L,n}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}.

(S51)

Substituting these into the
k
-space Hamiltonian in Eq. (
S41
) leads to the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{I}&=\;\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{L_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Biggl[\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{R,n}^{{\dagger}}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{R,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}\\[3.61371pt]
&\hskip 79.49744pt+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{L,n}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A_{L,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}\Biggr]\\[3.61371pt]
&=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{NL_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Biggl[\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}A_{R,n}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)+\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}A_{R,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)\\[3.61371pt]
&\hskip 90.3375pt+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}A_{L,n}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{-i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}A_{L,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}\biggr)\Biggr].\end{split}

(S52)

Using the (discrete) linear dispersion approximation,
{\omega_{k}=2\pi k/L_{0}}
, means that

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{\pm i\omega_{k}n\Delta t}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{\pm 2\pi ikn\Delta t/L_{0}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{\pm 2\pi ikn/N}=N\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{\pm 2\pi ik(n-0)/N}\right]=N\delta_{n0},

(S53)

where we have used
{L_{0}=N\Delta t}
, as well as the identity

\delta_{nm}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}e^{\pm 2\pi ik(n-m)/N}.

(S54)

Substituting the result in Eq. (
S53
) into
H_{I}
allows the sum over
n
to be eliminated, leading to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{I}&=\;\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{NL_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}N\delta_{n0}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}A_{R,n}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{R,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}A_{R,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{2\pi}}A_{L,n}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{L,\alpha}^{*}}{2\pi}}A_{L,n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}\Biggr)\\[3.61371pt]
&=\sqrt{\frac{N}{L_{0}}}\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\biggl(\sqrt{V_{R,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}A_{R,0}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}+\sqrt{V_{R,\alpha}^{*}}A_{R,0}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}+\sqrt{V_{L,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}A_{L,0}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}+\sqrt{V_{L,\alpha}^{*}}A_{L,0}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}}\biggr)\\[3.61371pt]
&=\sum_{\alpha=H,V}\sum_{\mu=L,R}\Biggl(\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{\vphantom{*}}}{\Delta t}}A_{\mu,0}^{{\dagger}}c_{\alpha}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}e^{i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{\mu,\alpha}^{*}}{\Delta t}}A_{\mu,0}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}e^{-i\pi\delta_{\alpha H}\delta_{\mu L}}\Biggr).\end{split}

(S55)

The matrix elements of
H_{I}
can now be found using the way in which the cavity operators and the position-space waveguide operators act on the basis elements in Eq. (14) in Appendix C (e.g., using that
{c_{\alpha}\ket{1_{\alpha}}=\ket{0_{\alpha}}}
,
{c_{\alpha}^{{\dagger}}\ket{0_{\alpha}}=\ket{1_{\alpha}}}
,
{A_{\mu,n}\ket{1_{\mu,n}}=\ket{0_{\mu,n}}}
, and
{A_{\mu,n}^{{\dagger}}\ket{0_{\mu,n}}=\ket{1_{\mu,n}}}
). Note that this Hamiltonian describes a local interaction between the cavity and the waveguide boxes labeled with
{n=0}
(see Fig.
S3
), which arises from the fact that the cavity-waveguide coupling is uniform in
k
-space (since it was assumed to be independent of
k
through the Markov approximation
[
24
]
).

In step (3), a photon number measurement is simulated on the final waveguide boxes to determine whether a photon was detected at one of the waveguide ends in a given time step. First, the probabilities of measuring a photon in one of the final boxes must be calculated:

P_{\mu}=\innerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_\mu},

(S56)

where
\ket{\psi_\mu}
is the projected state where all amplitudes except that of the basis state containing
\ket{1_{\mu,N-1}}
are set to zero (corresponding to a photon being present in the final box of waveguide
\mu
). For
{\mu=R}
this is basis element
6
and for
{\mu=L}
this is basis element
{N+6}
.
P_{\mu}
is then the probability that a photon is detected at the end of waveguide
\mu
in the current time step. Next, the total detection probability
{P_{\text{tot}}=P_{R}+P_{L}}
is compared against a uniformly-distributed random number
{\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)}
, in the same manner as losses are simulated in step (2). If
{\epsilon^{\prime}\leq P_{\text{tot}}}
, then a photon is detected at the end of one of the waveguides, and the current state is projected as

\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}\rightarrow\frac{\ket{\psi_\mu}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_\mu}}},

(S57)

where the waveguide in which the photon is detected (
{\mu=R}
or
{\mu=L}
) is determined by comparing the relative detection probabilities
P_{R}
and
P_{L}
against a second uniformly-distributed random number,
{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\in(0,P_{\text{tot}})}
(if
{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}\leq P_{R}}
project onto
\ket{\psi_R}
, else project onto
\ket{\psi_L}
). On the other hand, if
{\epsilon^{\prime}>P_{\text{tot}}}
, then no photon is detected and the state projection is:

\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}\rightarrow\frac{\ket{\psi_0}}{\sqrt{\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}}},

(S58)

where
\ket{\psi_0}
is the projected state with the amplitudes of the basis states containing
\ket{1_{R,N-1}}
and
\ket{1_{L,N-1}}
set to zero (elements
6
and
{N+6}
).

In step (4), the waveguide boxes are shifted along by one. This is implemented by moving the amplitudes in the vector
{\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}}
accordingly, such that in each waveguide the amplitudes are moved away from box
0
and towards box
{N-1}
(the amplitude of the basis state with
\ket{1_{\mu,0}}
moves to the state with
\ket{1_{\mu,1}}
, the amplitude of the state with
\ket{1_{\mu,1}}
moves to the state with
\ket{1_{\mu,2}}
, etc.). Once the boxes are shifted in this way, the amplitudes of the states with
\ket{1_{\mu,0}}
are set to zero, corresponding to a new empty box entering at the original position of box
0
in both waveguides. This process does not conserve the norm of the state
{\ket{\psi(t+\Delta t)}}
, so the state must be renormalized at the end of the iteration in step (5) before the next time step begins [as in Eq. (
S45
)].

Steps (1)-(5) can be repeated to simulate the time evolution of the system until a desired end time is reached. Such a simulation is called a quantum trajectory. Since the simulation of losses in step (2) and the measurement simulation in step (3) are stochastic processes, an average of many trajectories must be obtained to compute the expected time evolution of observables, conditioned on photon detection events at the waveguide ends.

S2.3
Trajectory Results

In this section, we present example trajectory results for Device 1 and Device 2 from the main text. In all the results presented here, we use a time step
{\Delta t=2\times 10^{-7}}
ns, and each waveguide is divided into
{N=2}
boxes. Hence, the cavity-waveguide interaction takes place at box
0
, and photon number measurements are simulated at box
1
in each waveguide (see Fig.
S3
). We use a small number of waveguide boxes to keep the size of our matrices and vectors small in order to make the computations faster (the choice of
N
does not affect our average trajectory results, as this effectively just determines the length of the waveguides). We also neglect losses in our trajectory simulations, i.e., we set
{\kappa_{\alpha}=\gamma_{j}=0}
, and in step (2) of the trajectory algorithm we always apply the time evolution operator
U_{\text{eff}}(\Delta t)
at each time step [Eq. (
S44
)]. Neglecting losses does not have a significant effect on the directional contrast and allows us to speed up the computations.

S5
Wafer Details

The wafer used to fabricate the devices is grown via molecular beam epitaxy. A GaAs substrate is used, on which a
1.05\,\mu
m AlGaAs sacrificial layer is grown. The
170
nm GaAs membrane is then grown on top of this AlGaAs layer. The membrane is engineered to form a p-i-n diode, with
30
nm p-doped GaAs at the top and
30
nm n-doped GaAs at the bottom, sandwiching an intrinsic region containing a layer of InAs QDs. AlGaAs tunneling barriers are incorporated on either side of the QD layer to improve charge confinement.

After growth, the AlGaAs sacrificial layer is selectively etched to produce a free-standing GaAs membrane. The photonic crystal pattern is then defined by electron beam lithography and transferred into the membrane via dry etching, creating the triangular lattice of air holes and the H1 cavity and W1 waveguides. The resulting structure supports high-quality optical modes in the desired wavelength range, enabling efficient coupling to and from the embedded QDs.

S6
Relationship between Left- and Right-Circular Polarization Coefficients under Misalignment

In this section, we discuss the effect of emitter misalignment on the complex polarization coefficients corresponding to left‐ and right‐circularly polarized transitions of a QD.

S6.1
Circular Polarization States

An ideal QD emits photons in pure right‐circular (
\ket{R}
) or left‐circular (
\ket{L}
) polarization states. In the absence of misalignment, these states can be written in terms of the horizontal (
\ket{H}
) and vertical (
\ket{V}
) linear polarization basis as:

\displaystyle\ket{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\ket{H}+i\,\ket{V}\Big),

(S60)

\displaystyle\ket{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\ket{H}-i\,\ket{V}\Big).

(S61)

The coefficients for
\ket{L}
are the complex conjugates of those for
\ket{R}
. Explicitly, if we write

\displaystyle\ket{R}

\displaystyle=\;c_{H}^{R}\,\ket{H}+c_{V}^{R}\,\ket{V},

then

\displaystyle\ket{L}

\displaystyle=\;c_{H}^{L}\,\ket{H}+c_{V}^{L}\,\ket{V},

with the relationships

\displaystyle c_{H}^{L}

\displaystyle=(c_{H}^{R})^{*},

(S62)

\displaystyle c_{V}^{L}

\displaystyle=(c_{V}^{R})^{*}.

(S63)

S6.2
Effect of Real 3D Rotations (Misalignment)

When the emitter or cavity is misaligned relative to the lab frame, the state vectors undergo a real three-dimensional rotation described by a rotation matrix
R
. Importantly, such a rotation is a linear transformation that does not introduce additional complex phases. Instead, it mixes the components of the polarization vector while preserving conjugate relationships.

Under this real rotation, the coefficients for the right‐circular state transform as

\begin{pmatrix}c_{H}^{R}\\
c_{V}^{R}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}c_{H}^{R^{\prime}}\\
c_{V}^{R^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}=R\begin{pmatrix}c_{H}^{R}\\
c_{V}^{R}\end{pmatrix},

where
c_{H}^{R^{\prime}}
and
c_{V}^{R^{\prime}}
are the rotated coefficients. Since
R
is a real matrix, the relationship between the left‐ and right‐circular polarization coefficients remains preserved under the transformation:

\displaystyle c_{H}^{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(c_{H}^{R^{\prime}})^{*},

(S64)

\displaystyle c_{V}^{L^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(c_{V}^{R^{\prime}})^{*},

(S65)

where
c_{\alpha}^{L^{\prime}}
and
c_{\alpha}^{R^{\prime}}
(
\alpha\in\{H,V\}
) are the coefficients for the rotated
\ket{L}
and
\ket{R}
states, respectively.

S6.3
Impact of Quantum Dot Symmetry on Polarization

The above analysis assumes that the QD emits pure circularly polarized light under ideal conditions. However, imperfections in the symmetry of the QD can alter its fine structure and the polarization properties of its transitions. Such symmetry deviations may lead to:

•

Mixed polarization states, where the emitted light is elliptical rather than purely circular.

•

Variations in the intrinsic dipole orientations of the emitter transitions, affecting how they couple to the cavity modes.

When these symmetry imperfections are significant, the straightforward complex conjugate relationship between the
\ket{L}
and
\ket{R}
coefficients may no longer hold perfectly. Instead, deviations from pure circular polarization introduce additional complexities in the coupling rates
g_{\alpha,j}
and the observed directional emission characteristics. Accurate modeling in such cases requires accounting for the modified polarization states and potentially nonideal phase relationships arising from the QD’s broken symmetry. Nonetheless, the core impact of real rotations on preserving conjugate relationships provides a foundational understanding, which can be adjusted to incorporate symmetry-induced polarization effects when necessary.
