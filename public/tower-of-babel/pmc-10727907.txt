Extranodal NK/T-Cell Lymphoma, Nasal Type, Extranasal and Ulcerative Blister Variant, Case Report
Authors: Yadira Noemí Chávez Dávila, Víctor Hugo Pinos León, Sonia Tello Astudillo, Grace Margreth Loza Erazo, Jennyfer Daniela Granizo Rubio
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The extranodal natural killer (NK) T-cell lymphoma of nasal type is a form of lymphoma that falls within the WHO/EORTC 2018 classification of cutaneous T-cell lymphomas. It is characterized for being aggressive, infrequent, and destroying midline facial structures; however, it can also be in primary or secondary form at extranasal sites, such as the skin or the gastrointestinal tract, among others. We report the case of an 18-year-old patient with an extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma located in an extranasal site. The clinical presentation is characterized for being multifocal and with erythematous-violaceous plaques that progress to hemorrhagic blisters and necrotic ulcers. Although this type of lymphoma has been reported previously by others, the presence of blisters is an atypical finding, which we believe has been described only in one case in the medical literature.

INTRODUCTION

Lymphomas are traditionally classified into Hodgkin’s lymphoma and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL). Lymphomas can occur within or outside the lymphatic system, known as nodal and extranodal, respectively1.

Primary cutaneous lymphomas are defined as NHL that occur on the skin without evidence of extracutaneous disease at the moment of diagnosis. These lymphomas are divided into two large groups: T-cell lymphomas, which account for 75% to 80% of cases, and B-cell lymphomas2.

The most common form of T-cell lymphoma is mycosis fungoides and its variants2. Extranodal natural killer (NK)/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) constitutes 3% to 10% of NHL2. ENKTL prevails in Asia and Central and South America, with a male-female relationship of 2 to 1. Epstein Barr virus (EBV) has been found in almost all of the cases described3.

New studies have reported a different prognosis according to the initial site of ENKT presentation. But the current WHO classification makes no distinction between the site of primary involvement of ENKTL4.

ENKTL occurs most frequently (60%~90%) in the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, paranasal sinuses or palate, hypopharynx, larynx, and oral cavity and is designated as ENKTL nasal type. Less frequently, ENKTL, nasal type, can occur in extranasal locations such as skin, gastrointestinal tract, central nervous system, soft tissues, and other tissues156.

Extranasal cutaneous ENKTL can be subdivided into primary or secondary involvement, Primary involvement include those tumors that appear initially on the extranasal skin whereas secondary involvement occurs by the spread of a nasal-origin lymphoma into extranasal skin6.

We present the case of a patient with extranasal primary cutaneous ENKTL of aggressive behavior with blisters. Blisters are an atypical clinical presentation in this condition and, to the best of our knowledge, it has been described only once in the medical literature.

CASE REPORT

An 18-year-old woman, with no significant medical history, seeks care for a one-year history of polymorphic and multifocal lesions with alternating periods of remission and exacerbation. A month before her current hospital admission, she had lesions in her lower limbs, which grew progressively and became superinfected, leading to a septic shock that was managed in an intensive care unit.

At admission, the physical examination showed disseminated dermatosis with predominance in the chest, abdomen, and extremities. These lesions were characterized by multiple erythematous-violaceous plaques. Some of these plaques presented a central vesicle, hemorrhagic blisters, as well as hypo and hyperpigmented spots (Fig. 1). In addition, in the lower limbs, she presented ulcers of different sizes with well-delimited violet edges, necrotic background, and some perilesional blisters (Fig. 2).

Biopsies were taken from an infiltrated plaque with a central vesicle (first sample), a fully developed blister (second sample), and one of the ulcers (third sample). The first sample shows a subepidermal vesicle, numerous apoptotic and dyskeratotic cells corresponding to epidermal necrosis. An atypical lymphocytic lichenoid infiltrate (Fig. 3A, B), neoplastic infiltrate around eccrine adnexa and fat cells (Fig. 3C, D), and angiotropism (Fig. 3E, F) were also evident. In a second sample, a subepidermal vesicle with a necrotic roof was evidenced (Fig. 3G). And, finally, in the third sample, there were similar findings but accompanied by fibrosis and areas of necrosis in the subcutaneous tissue.

The study of genetic reordering of T-cell receptors and an in-situ hybridization for EBV are not available in our country. Therefore, these tests were not performed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed cell positivity for CD3, CD45, CD56, granzyme B, EBV-encoded latent membrane protein (LMP)-1 expression (Fig. 3H~K), and negativity for CD 20, CD5, CD4 and CD8. An EBV-encoded LMP-1 expression was performed in a different institution because it was not available in the hospital. The result of that test was positive.

Complementary tests performed included: 1) a full body computed tomography scan that reported infiltrative-looking cervical, thoracic, and abdominal nodes; and, 2) a bone marrow aspirate where no malignant infiltrate was found. Additionally, indicators of poor prognosis were found, such as: the viral EBV viral load at 125,000.00 copies/ml and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 832 U/L. The clinical presentation, histopathological and IHC study led us to make the diagnosis of primary cutaneous ENKTL.

According to the TNM classification system for primary cutaneous lymphomas, the tumor stage was IV A (T3b, NX, M0)7. However, a few days later, a right hard hemifacial edema was identified. The nuclear magnetic resonance showed an ill-defined mass on the right cheek with extension to subcutaneous fat and facial muscles. Center facial involvement was ruled out. Based on these findings, the tumor stage was redefined as IV B.

The ulcers in the right lower limb were superinfected with multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii and significantly increased in size. Amputation of the right lower limb was decided prior to the initiation with SMILE therapy (L-asparaginase, methotrexate, ifosfamide, etoposide, and dexamethasone). Three cycles of SMILE were administered. The patient did not survive this clinical episode. Prior to publication, the patient’s consent was obtained for publication of this clinical case.

DISCUSSION

ENKTL is a subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that occurs primarily in young men from Asia and Central and South America189. These lymphomas originate from NK cells expressing cytoplasmic CD3 and CD56 or in T cells8.

Although ENKTL’S pathogenesis is not clear, there is a relationship between EBV infection and the development of ENKTL. A higher ENKT incidence has been observed in regions with high childhood prevalence of EBV (3% to 5.2%) as compared with just 0.3% in North American and European countries381011.

The ENKTL was initially known as “lethal midline granuloma.” It destroys midline facial structures involving the nasal cavity, hard palate, paranasal sinuses, facial skin, oropharynx, orbits, and other organs and even in advanced stages lymph nodes (Table 1)341213141516. It is accompanied by obstructive symptoms, epistaxis and/or destructive mass and b symptoms (fever, night sweats and weight loss).

Only 20% of ENKTL have a extranasal site involvement. Some authors mention skin as the most frequent site of development of extranasal ENKTL; however, some authors report that extranasal ENKTL is more frequent in lungs and the gastrointestinal tract than it is on skin16.

Skin-localized extranasal ENKTL has a wide clinical variability, with more frequent reports of plaques, ulcers, nodules, tumors, edema (Table 1), as well as nodules and plaques that have been described as abscess-like lesions and cellulitis-like, respectively. In addition, it may also occur with necrotic background ulcers and a violet border69.

In this paper, we describe an atypical clinical variant characterized by the presence of hemorrhagic blisters. The mechanism of blister formation has not been established previously, since there is only one similar case published in the medical literature in 20198.

In 1987, Maeda et al.10 described a case of bullous mycosis fungoides and their possible pathogenesis. The formation of a blister in the context of a cutaneous lymphoma may be secondary to the loss of adhesion between the basal lamina and basal keratinocytes, due to the accumulation of neoplastic cells in the dermo-epidermal junction plus the presence of inflammatory mediators. In addition, we believe that activation of signaling pathways in the inflammatory response by EBV11 and degradation caused by cytotoxic enzymes such as granzyme B could be additional causes17.

In terms of management, SMILE therapy is currently the standard treatment. This regimen contains drugs not dependent on P-glycoprotein, which are usually effective against ENKTL18.

The mean survival time of ENKTL is 13 to 42 months and at 5 years only 20% to 65% survive1920. It has been described that ENKTL nasal type with secondary skin spread is more aggressive than primary extranasal cutaneous lymphomas, except when the latter have a large involvement of the body surface area12. Elevated LDH titers at the time of initial staging have also been considered as a factor of poor prognosis, as well as EBV positivity, although some studies do not support this latter data6.

In conclusion, primary cutaneous extranasal ENKTL is a rare form of lymphoma, even more so in its blistering variant. Considering this pathology within the differential diagnoses of blistering skin diseases can lead to its early identification and treatment.
