Coffee ground-derived carbon dots reinforced zein/PVDF electrospun nanofiber membranes with antioxidant and photodynamic antibacterial activities
Authors: Fei Yao, Kaixin Niu, Yishuang Dong, Chan Jin, Zihao Li, Changhong Liu, Fusheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coffee grounds were utilized as a sustainable biomass precursor to synthesize carbon dots (CG-CDs), providing a value-added route for agro-industrial waste reutilization. The as-prepared CG-CDs were incorporated into a zein/polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) matrix, and multifunctional composite nanofiber membranes were fabricated by electrospinning. The influences of CG-CDs incorporation on membrane morphology, thermal behavior, mechanical performance, surface wettability, antioxidant activity, and photodynamic antibacterial properties were systematically evaluated. The results indicated that the incorporation of CG-CDs increased fiber diameter while maintaining a continuous fibrous morphology. In addition, the composite membranes exhibited improved thermal stability and increased hydrophilicity, while mechanical testing revealed a clear strength–flexibility trade-off, characterized by decreased tensile strength and increased elongation at break. More importantly, CG-CDs significantly strengthened the radical-scavenging activity of the membranes and enhanced the light-responsive antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus. ESR spin-trapping analysis further confirmed the light-induced generation of O₂•− and •OH by CG-CDs, providing direct evidence for their ROS-generating capability. These findings indicate that coffee ground-derived carbon dots can simultaneously modulate the physicochemical and functional properties of Zein/PVDF electrospun membranes, providing a resource-valorization strategy for the development of multifunctional fibrous materials.

Introduction

Despite advancements in the modern food sector, food products are inherently prone to spoilage via oxidation and pathogen proliferation during the supply chain, leading to quality deterioration, reduced shelf life, and potential food safety risks (Sun et al., 2022; Zhao, Zhang, Mujumdar, et al., 2022). Therefore, engineering high-performance packaging materials with integrated antioxidant and antibacterial functions is critical for extending food shelf life and ensuring consumer safety. In recent years, research on eco-friendly, functional, and intelligent food packaging has intensified. Among these strategies, electrospun nanofiber membranes offer unique advantages for active and intelligent packaging. Their distinctive architecture offers a high surface-to-volume ratio, excellent breathability, and controlled release of active substances (Min et al., 2022; Sree et al., 2025; Venezia et al., 2024).

However, it is often challenging for a single natural or synthetic polymer to simultaneously achieve both environmental sustainability and satisfactory mechanical and barrier properties. Zein is an abundant, renewable, and biodegradable plant-derived protein. Its exceptional capacity for film synthesis and inherent biological compatibility have facilitated its widespread application in food-contact materials (Fan et al., 2025; Ranjan et al., 2020). However, zein-based films are inherently brittle and exhibit poor mechanical strength and stability; thus, they fail to meet the complex requirements of modern food packaging (Niu et al., 2020; S. Wang et al., 2024).

Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) is a semi-crystalline fluoropolymer with excellent barrier properties, thermal stability, and mechanical strength (Tang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, it can elevate the chain entanglement density of the solution, consequently improving its electrospinning performance. The combination of zein with PVDF not only alleviates the brittleness and poor stability of natural protein-based films but also markedly augments the functional integrity of the composite membranes while retaining the contribution of Zein as a renewable bio-based component.

Recently, the integration of nanomaterials as functional additives in biopolymer packaging films has proven effective in enhancing their mechanical and physical attributes. Carbon dots (CDs), carbon-based nanoparticles with dimensions typically under 10 nm, have been utilized as active fillers in food packaging films due to their good water solubility, biocompatibility, stable fluorescence, and promising antibacterial and antioxidant activities (Riahi et al., 2025; L. Zhao, Zhang, Wang, & Devahastin, 2022). In food-packaging applications, CDs have been employed to impart enhanced antioxidant, UV-barrier, antibacterial, and other functional properties to films, thereby extending the storage period of foods (Chen et al., 2024; Kousheh et al., 2020; Salimi et al., 2020).Ezati et al. (Ezati & Rhim, 2022)prepared a pectin-based film incorporated with CDs, which significantly enhanced its antioxidant, antifungal, and UV-shielding properties. Pillai et al. (Pillai et al., 2025) prepared CD-loaded EC/PEO electrospun nanofiber membranes, which demonstrated a substantial enhancement in the antioxidant activity of the matrix and effectively prolonged the shelf life of walnut kernels. In addition, it has been established that CDs possess the capability to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) under light stimulation, and the oxidative stress induced by excessive ROS accumulation contributes to antibacterial effects (Ghate et al., 2019). Photodynamic sterilization, in contrast to conventional antibacterial therapies, offers benefits such as superior selectivity, heightened efficacy, and reduced toxicity. Light-excited CDs produce ROS via redox processes, which induce the inactivation and oxidation of intracellular components, damaging intracellular organelles and cellular functions, ultimately resulting in bacterial death (Chen et al., 2023). Biomass-derived carbon dots (B-CDs) are synthesized from biomass materials that serve as carbon sources. In addition to their photoluminescent properties, B-CDs typically demonstrate enhanced biocompatibility, low biotoxicity, and environmental sustainability (Meng et al., 2019). Coffee grounds, a biomass waste rich in crude fiber, organic functional groups, and minerals, offer new avenues for high-value valorization and improved resource utilization (Rawangkan et al., 2022).

Although biomass-derived carbon dots and carbon dot-containing food packaging materials have been previously reported, the integration of coffee ground-derived carbon dots into electrospun protein/polymer nanofibrous matrices and the systematic investigation of their loading-dependent structure–property–function relationships remain relatively limited. In this study, coffee grounds were employed as a sustainable precursor for the synthesis of carbon dots (CG-CDs). The obtained CG-CDs were subsequently incorporated into a zein/PVDF matrix to construct multifunctional CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes via electrospinning. Unlike conventional dense polymer films, the electrospun fibrous architecture provides a high-surface-area functional platform for the incorporation and exposure of CG-CDs. The functional and physicochemical characteristics of the CG-CDs were systematically examined. Furthermore, the influence of CG-CD incorporation on the morphology, intermolecular interactions, crystalline structure, thermal stability, surface wettability, mechanical properties, antioxidant activity, and light-responsive antibacterial performance of zein/PVDF composite membranes was systematically evaluated. Through this approach, the loading-dependent relationships among CG-CD incorporation, nanofiber structure, physicochemical properties, and functional performance were investigated, providing a basis for the further development of CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF membranes as multifunctional candidate materials for active food-packaging applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zein (Z3625) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 761) was purchased from Arkema, France. Waste coffee residue was obtained from a franchise café and used as a carbon source. 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was obtained from Shanghai MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. LB nutrient agar and LB broth were sourced from Beijing Aoboxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Caco-2 cells were obtained from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Guangzhou Kamo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Methods

Preparation of coffee-ground-derived carbon dots (CG-CDs)

Approximately 2.0 g of dried coffee ground powder was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water and thoroughly stirred. The suspension was transferred into a sealed autoclave and subjected to a hydrothermal reaction at 200 °C for 6 h. Following the reaction, the mixture was allowed to reach ambient temperature, yielding a dark brown carbon dot (CDs) dispersion. The resulting liquid was subjected to sequential filtration through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm microporous membranes to eliminate unreacted residues and large impurities. To eliminate low-molecular-weight impurities, the filtrate was dialyzed in deionized water for 24 h (molecular weight cutoff: 1000 Da) using a dialysis membrane. Finally, the purified solution was lyophilized to yield coffee-ground-derived carbon dots (CG-CDs) in a solid powder form.

Preparation of zein/PVDF spinning solutions and nanofiber membranes with different amounts of CG-CDs

Coffee-ground-derived carbon dots (CG-CDs) were dispersed in a 3:2 (v/v) mixture of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and acetone at loadings of 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 wt% relative to the total mass of Zein and PVDF and stirred continuously at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate dispersion. Subsequently, predetermined amounts of zein and PVDF powders were gradually added under gentle stirring to obtain a total polymer concentration of 15% (w/v) at a zein/PVDF mass ratio of 1:1. The resulting mixtures were subsequently maintained in a 45 °C water bath under magnetic stirring for 12 h until the polymers were completely dissolved, yielding stable composite spinning solutions containing different CG-CD loadings.

Each spinning solution was loaded into a syringe fitted with a 23-gauge stainless-steel needle. Electrospinning was performed using an electrospinning system (E05, Foshan Light Industry Precision Measurement and Control Technology Co., Ltd., China) under the following conditions: a flow rate of 0.5 mL h−1, an applied voltage of 18 kV, a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm, an ambient temperature of 25 °C, and a relative humidity of 50 ± 5%. During electrospinning, the spinneret was moved laterally in a reciprocating manner at a traverse speed of 20 cm min−1, while the fibers were collected on a rotating drum operating at 400 rpm, as illustrated in Fig. 1. After electrospinning, the obtained nanofiber membranes were vacuum-dried at 50 °C for 12 h to remove residual solvents and subsequently sealed for storage.Fig. 1Flow chart of the electrospinning process.Fig. 1

Characterization of CG-CDs

Structure and performance characterization of CG-CDs

The particle size and morphology of the synthesized CG-CDs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-200, JEOL, Japan). The crystalline nature of the samples was scrutinized via X-ray diffraction (XRD; SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan), while their surface chemical states and elemental profiles were established through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA). To probe the optical characteristics, both ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy (TU-1900, Persee, China) and fluorescence spectroscopy (FL970 Plus, Techcomp, China) were utilized. Finally, the surface-bound functional groups were identified using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Activity test

(1) Antioxidant Activity:

DPPH free radical scavenging ability (Sachdev & Gopinath, 2015) was determined following the method described by Sachdev, with slight modifications. Initially, a 0.1 mM DPPH solution was prepared using ethanol. Subsequently, 1 mL of this radical solution was blended with 1 mL of CG-CDs solution at different concentrations. The mixtures were then incubated in the dark for 20 min. Following incubation, the optical density (absorbance) was measured at 517 nm. The percentage of DPPH radical inhibition was quantified using the following Eq. (1).(1)%DPPH scavenging capacity=1−AiA0×100%where A₀ denotes the absorbance of the blank control (without sample) and Aᵢ denotes the absorbance of the CG-CD sample solution at each tested concentration.

ABTS•+ radical scavenging: CG-CDs aqueous solutions at different concentrations were placed in centrifuge tubes and mixed with 2.0 mL ABTS•+ working solution (the ABTS•+ stock was diluted to an absorbance level of A₀ = 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm). Following a reaction period of 10–30 min in a dark environment with continuous agitation, the final absorbance of the mixture (Aₛ) was recorded at 734 nm. An ABTS•+ working solution without CG-CDs served as the control (A₀), and the ABTS•+ scavenging percentage was subsequently calculated using the following Eq. (2).(2)%ABTS•⁺scavenging capacity=1−AsA0×100%

(2) Antibacterial activity.

The antibacterial properties of the carbon dots were evaluated using solid culture media, following a protocol adapted from Wang et al. (2022) with minor adjustments (Wang et al., 2022). Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli were inoculated into sterile Luria–Bertani (LB) broth and cultured in a shaking incubator at 37 ± 1 °C for 12 h. The bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a final concentration of 10^5–10^6 CFU/mL. Subsequently, the suspensions were treated with different concentrations of carbon dots (0, 0.12, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg/mL) and exposed to a 50 W full-spectrum LED light source with a spectral range of 380–780 nm, positioned 10 cm above the samples, for 4 h. The same source-to-sample distance and irradiation conditions were maintained for all light-treated groups. Dark and blank controls were included for comparison. After treatment, 100 μL of each bacterial suspension was evenly distributed across plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The resulting bacterial colonies were photographed and quantitatively counted. All experiments were independently performed in triplicate. The growth inhibition rate under light irradiation was calculated according to Eq. (3).(3)Growth inhibition%=1−NtN0×100%where N0 and Nt represent the viable colony counts of the light-irradiated blank control and the corresponding CG-CD-treated group, respectively.

(3) ESR spin-trapping analysis of photogenerated ROS.

The photogenerated reactive oxygen species (ROS) of CG-CDs were analyzed using an electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometer (EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, Germany) in combination with spin-trapping techniques. CG-CD suspensions containing the corresponding spin-trapping agents were subjected to light irradiation or maintained under dark conditions, followed by ESR spectral acquisition. O₂•−, •OH, and 1O₂ were separately examined. 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was used as the spin-trapping agent for O₂•− and •OH detection, whereas 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) was used for 1O₂ detection.

Morphology observation of CG-CDs@zein/PVDF nanofibers

The surface topography of the electrospun Zein/PVDF composite nanofibers was examined following the protocol established by Yao et al. (Yao et al., 2022). To enhance the conductivity for imaging, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer. The morphology was subsequently visualized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA LMS, Czech Republic) operated at an acceleration potential of 10 kV. The fiber diameters were determined through statistical analysis using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy of CG-CDs@zein/PVDF nanofibers

Following the method of Zheng et al. (Zheng et al., 2022), the structure of CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes with different CG-CDs concentrations was acquired using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Spectra were collected in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, with an accumulation of 32 scans per sample.

X-ray diffraction

Following the methodology established by Yao et al. (Yao et al., 2022), the crystal structures of CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes with different CG-CD concentrations were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). The instrument was operated at an acceleration voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA, Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm), and a scanning range of 5–90° (2θ), utilizing a scanning rate of 10°/min.

Thermal performance test

The thermal stabilities of the CG-CDs powder and CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes with different CG-CDs concentrations were evaluated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA, Q600, TA Instruments, USA) following the method described by Yao et al. (Yao et al., 2022). Approximately 5 mg of each sample was loaded into a platinum crucible and heated from 30 to 600 °C at a linear heating rate of 20 °C/min under a continuous nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL/min).

Contact angle measurement

Following the methodology described by Zheng et al. (Zheng et al., 2022), the hydrophilicity of the nanofiber membranes was evaluated using a contact angle goniometer (DSA25, KRÜSS, Germany). The composite membranes were cut into square specimens (≈10 mm × 10 mm). A single droplet of deionized water was deposited on the membrane surface. The droplet profile was automatically recorded by the instrument, and the corresponding contact angle was determined. Measurements were taken at three distinct positions on each sample surface, and the calculated mean values were noted.

Mechanical properties testing

Following the method of Yao et al. (Yao et al., 2023), the thickness of the central section of dumbbell-shaped nanofiber membrane specimens was determined using a thickness gauge, Five random points were selected on each specimen for measurement, with the resultant arithmetic mean designated as the representative thickness for calculations. Mechanical evaluation was conducted at ambient temperature using an electronic universal testing machine (AGX-100 N, Shimadzu, Japan). Stress-strain curves were recorded, and the mechanical parameters were calculated. For each material, specimens were cut from three random areas of the membrane and tested three times. The final data were then averaged.

Characterization of the bioactivity of CG-CDs@zein/PVDF nanofiber membrane

(1) Determination of antioxidant properties.

To evaluate radical quenching, a 0.1 mM DPPH ethanol solution was combined with the specimen dispersions, and the resulting mixtures were kept in a light-shielded environment for 30 min. Subsequently, the optical density was recorded at 517 nm. Scavenging activity was calculated as described in Section 2.2.3.2.

ABTS•+ radical scavenging: The ABTS•+ working solution (prepared by diluting the ABTS•+ stock to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm) was mixed with the sample solution and allowed to undergo a light-shielded reaction for 10–30 min at room temperature, followed by the recording of the optical density at 734 nm. Scavenging activity was calculated in accordance with the protocol detailed in Section 2.2.3.2.

(2) Determination of antibacterial properties.

According to the experimental framework established by Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2022), the antibacterial activity of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes was evaluated on a solid culture medium. Briefly, approximately 6 mg of nanofiber membranes with different CG-CDs loadings was added to 0.4 mL of bacterial suspension (approximately 10^6 CFU/mL). A bacterial suspension without a membrane was used as the negative control. The mixtures were incubated under the same full-spectrum LED irradiation conditions described above (50 W, 380–780 nm, source-to-sample distance of 10 cm) or in the dark for 4 h, after which aliquots (100 μL) from each sample were uniformly distributed across the surface of nutrient agar media and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The resulting colonies were photographed, and the antibacterial performance was quantified by comparing the colony counts between the control and experimental groups. All assays were conducted in triplicate to ensure statistical consistency. The growth inhibition rate was calculated according to Eq. (3), where N0 represents the viable colony count of the corresponding light-irradiated no-membrane control, and Nt represents that of the membrane-treated group.

Cytocompatibility assay

To evaluate the effect of CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes on the viability of Caco-2 cells, a CCK-8 assay was performed (Yao et al., 2023). The Zein/PVDF blank membrane and 7% CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite membrane were selected as test samples. The membranes were cut into 2 cm × 2 cm pieces, sterilized, and completely immersed in equal volumes of complete culture medium, followed by extraction at 37 °C for 24 h. The resulting membrane extracts were collected for subsequent cell treatment. Caco-2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 24 h. After cell attachment, the cells were washed with PBS and the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, the extract of the Zein/PVDF blank membrane, or the extract of the 7% CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite membrane, followed by further incubation for 24 h. After treatment, the medium was removed and the cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of fresh culture medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h. The absorbance of each well was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Three treatment groups were established: cells cultured in fresh complete medium without membrane extract were used as the control group (ODcontrol); cells treated with the extracts of the Zein/PVDF blank membrane or 7% CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite membrane were used as the experimental groups (ODsample); and wells containing culture medium without cells were used as the blank group (ODblank). All experiments were independently performed in triplicate. Cell viability was calculated according to the following Eq. (4).(4)Cell viability%=ODsample−ODblankODcontrol−ODblank×100%where ODsample represents the absorbance of the corresponding membrane-extract-treated group, ODcontrol represents the absorbance of the untreated cell control group, and ODblank represents the absorbance of the cell-free blank wells.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, quantitative experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA). Differences among treatment groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences among treatment groups (p < 0.05), whereas groups sharing the same lowercase letter are not significantly different (p ≥ 0.05). Fiber diameters were measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0, colony counts were analyzed using ImageJ, and graphical representations and data plotting were generated using Origin 2021.

Results and discussion

Characterization of CG-CDs

As depicted in the TEM micrograph (Fig. 2a), the synthesized CG-CDs exhibited a uniform dispersion without aggregation, showing a quasi-spherical morphology with sizes ranging from 0.91 to 3.00 nm and an average diameter of 1.88 ± 0.43 nm (Fig. 2b). Clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.21 nm were observed in localized regions of the CG-CDs, indicating the presence of locally ordered carbon domains, which is consistent with structural characteristics commonly reported for carbon dots (Fan et al., 2019). Meanwhile, the XRD pattern exhibited a broad diffraction feature centered at approximately 2θ = 20.2°, generally associated with the (002) reflection of disordered carbon, suggesting a low degree of long-range structural ordering in the bulk CG-CD sample. Combined with the locally resolved lattice fringes observed by HRTEM, these results indicate that the CG-CDs possess a largely disordered carbon framework containing locally ordered sp2-rich carbon domains. The surface-bound functional moieties of the CG-CDs were further identified using FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2d). In the resulting spectrum, the absorption band at 3418.7 cm−1 represents the vibrational signatures of the –OH groups. The surface elemental states and functional groups of the CG-CDs were scrutinized using XPS (Fig. 2e). The wide-scan survey spectrum (Fig. 2e) displays three main electronic signals centered at 284.83, 399.94, and 531.78 eV, which are assigned to C 1 s, N 1 s, and O 1 s, respectively (Li et al., 2025). The atomic percentages of C, N, and O in the CG-CDs were 70.28, 6.18, and 23.54%, respectively. The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 2f) was resolved into three components at 284.83, 286.17, and 288.45 eV, which were assigned to C=C/C–C, C—N, and C–O/C=O bonds, respectively. Deconvolution of the N 1 s spectrum (Fig. 2g) shows three specific peaks at 398.68, 399.94, and 401.45 eV, identifying the presence of pyridinic N, pyrrolic N, and graphitic N. The O 1 s spectrum (Fig. 2h) was fitted with two components at 531.78 and 532.92 eV, representing the C—O and CO species, respectively. These findings corroborate the FTIR data, validating that the CG-CDs are N-doped carbon dots with sp2/sp3-hybridized carbon cores bearing hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups, which confer good water solubility.

The UV–Vis spectra of the CG-CDs are shown in Fig. 3a. The synthesized CG-CDs exhibited a weak shoulder peak centered near 280 nm, which is linked to the π–π* electronic transition of CC aromatic systems alongside n–π* transitions from CO bonds within the CG-CDs structure, indicating the presence of sp2-hybridized carbon domains (De & Karak, 2013; Zhu et al., 2019). The photophysical characteristics of the carbon dots are closely linked to these sp2 domains, where radiative recombination from the carbon core gives rise to fluorescence under UV excitation (Alafeef et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3b, the CG-CDs displayed a maximum excitation wavelength of 350 nm and a maximum emission wavelength of 430 nm. The emission intensity and peak position are excitation-dependent; when the excitation wavelength is varied from 280 to 500 nm, the emission peak gradually red-shifts, and the fluorescence intensity first increases and then decreases. This behavior is likely associated with the complex structure of CG-CDs, where multiple defect centers and surface states provide diverse emissive sites, leading to excitation-dependent photoluminescence (Alafeef et al., 2023; De & Karak, 2013).Fig. 3Optical properties of CG-CDs: (a) UV–Vis absorption, fluorescence excitation, and emission spectra; (b) excitation-dependent fluorescence emission spectra.Fig. 3

Activity analysis of CG-CDs

Scavenging ability of DPPH free radicals

The DPPH radical-scavenging activity of the CG-CDs solutions was evaluated at various concentrations. Fig. 4a shows that the DPPH scavenging rate increased with increasing CG-CDs concentration and gradually approached a plateau, indicating a dose-dependent response. With the CG-CDs concentration increasing from 0 to 128 μg/mL, the scavenging rate increased from 0.75% to 93.41% (Fig. 4b), accompanied by a visible color change of the DPPH ethanol solution, shifting from dark purple to yellow (inset), suggesting efficient radical quenching. This phenomenon is attributed to the abundant surface-active sites on the CG-CDs, which facilitate the interaction and neutralization of free radicals. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, the UV–Vis absorption of the ABTS•+ reagent gradually decreased as the CG-CD concentration increased from 0 to 200 μg/mL, with a more pronounced decline at 734 nm. This result indicates that CG-CDs effectively reduced ABTS•+ and caused visible decolorization. Consistently, Fig. 4e shows a significant increase in ABTS•+ scavenging efficiency with increasing CG-CDs concentration, suggesting a clear concentration-dependent behavior that approached saturation at higher concentrations.

Antibacterial activity of CG-CDs

The experimental findings demonstrate that the antibacterial efficacy of CG-CDs was dependent on both their concentration and light exposure (Fig. 5). Under illumination, the antibacterial efficacy increased progressively with increasing CG-CDs concentration from 0.12 mg/mL to 2.0 mg/mL. Quantitative colony-counting analysis further confirmed a significant concentration-dependent increase in growth inhibition under light irradiation (Fig. 5b, p < 0.05). S. aureus exhibited greater susceptibility to CG-CDs than E. coli, particularly at low and intermediate concentrations, whereas strong inhibition of both bacterial strains was observed at higher CG-CDs concentrations. In contrast, the antibacterial effect remained limited under dark conditions, as indicated by the corresponding agar-plate images (Fig. 5a), demonstrating that the pronounced enhancement of antibacterial activity was strongly dependent on light irradiation. To directly investigate the photogenerated ROS associated with the light-enhanced antibacterial activity, ESR spin-trapping analysis was performed at the solution level under light and dark conditions (Fig. 6). Clear characteristic ESR signals attributable to O₂•− and •OH were detected under light irradiation, whereas no discernible corresponding signals were observed under dark conditions. In contrast, no detectable 1O₂ signal was observed under the present experimental conditions. Morphology observation of different amounts of CG-CDs@zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes

Fig. 7 shows the diameter distributions and SEM images of the electrospun CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite nanofibers prepared at a total zein/PVDF concentration of 15% (w/v) at various CG-CDs loadings. The SEM images revealed that the addition of CG-CDs minimally impacted the overall surface morphology of the zein/PVDF nanofibers, which remained continuous and smooth without bead formation, albeit with slight adhesion between the fibers. The diameter distributions indicate that, at a constant total polymer concentration, the average fiber diameter increased gradually with increasing CG-CDs content. The gradual increase in fiber diameter suggests that CG-CD incorporation affected the electrospinning process and subsequent fiber formation. Nevertheless, SEM primarily reveals fiber-level morphology and does not directly provide information on the nanoscale distribution of CG-CDs within the fibers. This behavior may be attributed to the molecular associations between the surface functional moieties of the CG-CDs and the zein/PVDF matrix, resulting in elevated solution viscosity, enhanced chain entanglement, and increased effective solid content, while reducing jet stretchability. Additionally, carbon dots may influence charge distribution within the spinning solution, decreasing the charge density and weakening the electric-field-induced stretching of the jet (Zhao et al., 2020). Fig. 8c shows the normalized XRD patterns of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes. All profiles were normalized to the main peak to exclude the influence of film thickness and sample inhomogeneities. Characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 18.4°, 20.0°, and 26.6° aligned with the (020), (021), and (110) planes of the α-phase PVDF were observed for all samples, indicating that CG-CDs doping did not induce crystal phase transformation (Wang et al., 2016). Zein contributes a broad halo at 2θ ≈ 18–22°, reflecting its amorphous nature and partially overlapping with PVDF crystalline peaks. With an increase in the CG-CDs content from 0% to 5%, the characteristic diffraction features became sharper and narrower, suggesting increased structural ordering within the composite matrix. However, at 7% CG-CDs, the diffraction peak broadened, suggesting a reduction in crystalline order at the higher CG-CD loading. This change may be associated with local CG-CDs aggregation, increased disruption of polymer-chain packing, or a combination of both. These results indicate that the observed crystalline features are primarily associated with PVDF, while Zein contributes an amorphous background and CG-CDs incorporation modulates the degree of crystalline ordering.

Fig. 8d and e show the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of the CG-CDs powder and CG-CDs-doped Zein/PVDF composite nanofiber membranes, respectively, to investigate how the incorporation of CG-CDs influences the thermal decomposition behavior of the composite system. According to the profiles in Fig. 8d, the mass loss below 200 °C was mainly associated with water evaporation. The CG-CDs powder exhibited a single major mass-loss stage in the range of approximately 200–400 °C, indicating that its thermal decomposition was primarily associated with thermally labile surface functional groups, which resulted in a relatively high carbon residue and typical thermal decomposition characteristics of carbonaceous nanomaterials.

The undoped sample (0% CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF) exhibited a distinct two-stage thermal degradation process: the first stage (approximately 280–380 °C) was attributed to cleavage and deamination of the zein molecular chains, whereas the second stage (approximately 430–490 °C) corresponded to thermal decomposition of the PVDF backbone, reflecting the typical degradation behavior of a semi-crystalline polymer. With increasing CG-CDs loading, the initial degradation temperature (T_onset) and the point of peak weight-loss rate (T_max) increased slightly. At 3% and 5% CG-CDs, the DTG peak became less pronounced, the mass loss rate decreased, and the final residual mass increased slightly, indicating that moderate CG-CDs incorporation helped suppress the pyrolysis rate and promote carbonization.

The enhancement in thermal stability was associated with the thermal stability of CG-CDs and the interfacial interactions between their surface polar functional groups and the polymer matrix, which microscopically restricted the mobility of polymer segments and delayed thermal decomposition. However, when the CG-CDs loading was further increased to 7%, the degradation rate peak became more pronounced again, although the residual char yield remained higher than that of the undoped sample. This behavior may be associated with less favorable interfacial organization at higher CG-CD loading, which could weaken the stabilizing interactions between CG-CDs and the polymer matrix. Local CG-CD aggregation may also contribute to this behavior. Overall, these results provide theoretical support for the construction of functional membrane materials with tunable thermal stability based on natural–synthetic polymer composites.

The contact angle is crucial for evaluating the wettability of a material surface. Surfaces with a contact angle (θ) of less than 90° are categorized as hydrophilic, whereas θ > 90° indicates hydrophobicity. As illustrated in Fig. 8f, the static water contact angle of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes decreased markedly with increasing CG-CDs loading, declining from approximately 118° at 0% to approximately 85° at 7%. This result demonstrates that the surface hydrophilicity of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite membranes was enhanced as the CG-CDs content increased. This improvement was attributed to the abundant hydrophilic oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., carboxyl and hydroxyl groups) on the CG-CDs surface, which were exposed on the fiber surface and strengthened the interactions between the membranes and water molecules. In addition, changes in fiber surface roughness at higher CG-CDs loading may have further contributed to the increased wettability. Overall, these results indicate that tuning the CG-CDs content effectively regulates the surface wettability of the composite membranes, providing useful information for the further evaluation of these membranes in active food-packaging systems.

Fig. 8g and h display the tensile strength, stress–strain curves, and elongation at break of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes with different loadings of CG-CDs. The results indicated a clear modulation of mechanical behavior with the incorporation of CG-CDs: the tensile strength decreased from approximately 12 MPa at 0% to approximately 6 MPa at 7%, while the elongation at break increased from approximately 45% to 72%. These results indicate that increasing CG-CD loading enhanced the flexibility and extensibility of the membranes, but at the expense of tensile strength, demonstrating a clear strength–flexibility trade-off rather than an overall improvement in mechanical performance. This trend was likely associated with changes in interfacial interactions, polymer-chain organization, and stress-transfer efficiency with increasing CG-CD loading. At higher loadings, possible local aggregation of CG-CDs may also contribute to reduced stress-transfer efficiency. Moreover, the fiber diameter of the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF composite nanofibers increased with increasing CG-CDs content, which may alter inter-fiber contact and entanglement within the fibrous network. Such morphological changes may facilitate fiber slippage during tensile deformation and thereby contribute to the reduced tensile strength but enhanced elongation at break at higher CG-CDs loadings (Yao et al., 2023).

As shown in Fig. 10a,b, the CG-CDs@Zein/PVDF membranes demonstrated only limited suppression of E. coli and S. aureus in the absence of light. This residual inhibition suggests a limited light-independent antibacterial contribution of the composite membranes. In contrast, their antibacterial activity was substantially enhanced under light irradiation. This light-enhanced antibacterial behavior is consistent with the solution-level ESR results of isolated CG-CDs, which directly confirmed their light-induced generation of O₂•− and •OH. Conclusion

In this study, CG-CDs were synthesized from waste coffee grounds and incorporated into zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes via electrospinning. Increasing CG-CD loading increased fiber diameter, modulated crystalline ordering and surface wettability, improved thermal stability, and produced a clear strength–flexibility trade-off, with decreased tensile strength but increased elongation at break. The composite membranes exhibited strong free-radical-scavenging activity and concentration-dependent light-responsive antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus. ESR analysis confirmed the light-induced generation of O₂•− and •OH, whereas no detectable 1O₂ signal was observed, suggesting that the photodynamic behavior was more consistent with a Type I radical-mediated pathway than with a 1O₂-dominated Type II pathway. Overall, CG-CDs incorporation effectively regulated the physicochemical and functional properties of the zein/PVDF nanofiber membranes without altering the characteristic α-phase crystal structure of PVDF, while the amorphous structural contribution of Zein was retained. The favorable preliminary cytocompatibility further supports their potential as multifunctional candidate materials for active food-packaging applications, although practical performance and food-contact safety require further evaluation.
