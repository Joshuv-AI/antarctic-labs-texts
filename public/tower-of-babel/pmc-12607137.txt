Unequal access in a universal health system: COVID-19 vaccination and health services utilization among international migrants in Brazil
Authors: Juliana Soares Tenório De Araújo, Letícia Perticarrara Ferezin, Heriederson Sávio Dias Moura, Rander Junior Rosa, Ariela Fehr Tártaro, Ione Carvalho Pinto, Mônica Chiodi Toscano de Campos, Ricardo Alexandre Arcêncio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic has intensified existing health inequities, particularly among international migrants, who often face barriers to healthcare access and vaccination. In Brazil, where the Unified Health System (SUS) provides universal coverage, little is known about COVID-19 vaccination coverage and healthcare utilization among international migrants. This study aimed to analyze COVID-19 vaccination coverage, associated factors, and the use of SUS services among international migrants in Brazil during the pandemic. MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted through face-to-face interviews with 374 international migrants residing in Brazil for at least six months. Participants were recruited between May 2022 and October 2023 through community networks and organizations in 26 of Brazil’s 27 federative units. COVID-19 vaccines were provided exclusively through SUS infrastructure and facilities. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using a culturally adapted questionnaire covering sociodemographic characteristics, vaccination status, healthcare utilization, and trust in health services. Descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination and SUS utilization. Analyses were conducted using RStudio software.ResultsAmong participants, 82.4% reported receiving a COVID-19 vaccine, and 93.0% reported using SUS services during the pandemic. Lower likelihood of vaccination was observed among migrants who identified as White (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.42; 95% CI: 0.21–0.89) and those with completed elementary education (aOR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.19–0.86). Migrants living with older adults (aOR: 2.54; 95% CI: 1.12–6.64) and those tested for COVID-19 prior to vaccination (aOR: 3.67; 95% CI: 2.00–6.96) were more likely to be vaccinated. Increased utilization of the SUS was positively associated with trust in hospitals and healthcare professionals (aOR: 3.22; 95% CI: 1.25–8.89), while higher educational attainment (aOR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.06–1.09) and residing in the Northeast (adjusted OR: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.09–0.99; p = 0.047) were associated with lower utilization of the SUS.ConclusionsInternational migrants in Brazil experienced significant barriers to COVID-19 vaccination and healthcare access, influenced by race, education, household composition, and trust in health institutions. These findings reveal critical gaps in universal health coverage during pandemics and highlight the need for targeted, culturally sensitive public health strategies to promote equity in vaccination and healthcare delivery for migrant populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25198-3.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has starkly exposed and intensified longstanding health inequities worldwide, disproportionately affecting socially marginalized populations such as people experiencing homelessness, incarcerated individuals, residents of urban peripheries, and international migrants [1–3].

Among these groups, international migrants face unique and compounded barriers — including legal, linguistic, and institutional obstacles — that restrict their access to health information, preventive measures, and essential healthcare services [4–6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has acknowledged that migrants are among the most vulnerable groups to the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic due to their mobility status, social insecurity, and higher risks of exposure to the virus [7]. However, available data indicate that migrants and refugees were precisely the subgroups with the lowest COVID-19 vaccination coverage, highlighting major gaps in public health inclusion [8–10].

Although direct national data on migrants are lacking, studies show that similar vulnerable groups—such as Indigenous people and residents of lower-middle-income communities—exhibited substantially lower full vaccination coverage compared to the general population. For instance, in March 2022, Indigenous populations in Brazil had a full vaccination rate of 48.7%, in contrast to a 74.8% rate in the overall population, despite similar vaccine effectiveness [11]. Spatial analyses reveal lower coverage in the North and Northeast regions, which concentrate larger vulnerable populations, including international migrants [12, 13]. These patterns suggest that migrants likely experienced similar or even greater barriers, including administrative, informational, and logistical obstacles [14, 15].

In Brazil, the Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS) is constitutionally mandated to provide free and universal healthcare to all individuals, regardless of migratory status. During the pandemic, the Ministry of Health coordinated the national COVID-19 vaccination campaign through SUS infrastructure, achieving over 80% coverage with at least one dose by late 2022 [16]. However, international migrants were not explicitly prioritized or supported by tailored strategies, raising concerns about equitable access.

Evidence from other countries and limited Brazilian studies suggests that international migrants often experience lower vaccination coverage and reduced access to health services due to administrative, informational, and social barriers [17–20]. A literature review identified only two studies relevant to this population: one focused on Brazilians living abroad (in French Guiana), and another analyzing Latin American vaccination strategies that included Brazil only in a regional context [21]. Despite the growing literature on COVID-19 vaccination among migrant populations globally, there is a notable lack of empirical research focusing specifically on international migrants in Brazil—particularly regarding their access to vaccination and public health services—which remains underexplored.

Access to COVID-19 vaccination and the use of public healthcare services represent two independent yet complementary dimensions of health access. While this study does not seek to investigate a causal relationship between these two outcomes, analyzing them together provides a more comprehensive understanding of how international migrants navigated the Brazilian public health system during the pandemic. Considering that COVID-19 vaccination was exclusively delivered through SUS infrastructure, evaluating both indicators sheds light on structural barriers and contributes to health equity monitoring.

Given these gaps, this study aimed to analyze COVID-19 vaccination coverage, associated factors, and use of SUS services among international migrants in Brazil during the pandemic. By examining these two complementary indicators, we seek to inform targeted, culturally sensitive public health strategies that advance health equity and strengthen preparedness for future health emergencies.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This is a cross-sectional, descriptive, and analytical study [22, 23] conducted in Brazil between May 2022 and October 2023. Data were collected via field interviews in 26 of the country’s 27 federative units, covering all five geographic regions and representing over 96% of the national territory. The study is part of the matrix project “COVID-19 Social Thermometer in Brazil.”

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Ribeirão Preto School of Nursing, University of São Paulo (CAAE: 32210320.1.3001.5393) and conducted in accordance with Brazilian ethical guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Study population and sampling

Eligible participants were international migrants aged 18 years or older, residing in Brazil for at least six months at the time of data collection. Brazilian-born internal migrants and individuals under 18 were excluded to focus on adult autonomy in health decision-making and due to ethical considerations. Participants who did not report their nationality were also excluded.

Given the social vulnerability and geographic dispersion of the migrant population in Brazil, sequential, non-probabilistic sampling was conducted through community networks, migrant organizations, and common gathering places, with recruitment continuing until operational resources were exhausted. This approach is appropriate for hard-to-reach populations and does not aim for statistical representativeness.

A sample size calculation—based on a finite population formula (95% confidence level, 80% statistical power, 8% margin of error, 50% estimated proportion, and 10% for potential losses)—indicated a minimum of 151 participants, referencing an estimated 1,085,673 international migrants in Brazil between 2011 and 2019, according to the International Migration Observatory (OBMigra) [24]. Ultimately, 374 individuals were included. This calculation considered COVID-19 vaccination coverage as the primary outcome, with the 50% prevalence used as a conservative estimate due to the lack of prior national data for migrants.

The sample size calculated for the primary outcome (vaccination) provides sufficient statistical power for additional analyses, despite the absence of a designated primary outcome for the logistic regression models. Furthermore, the final sample size of 374—more than double the minimum required 151—strengthens the robustness of the findings across multiple independent variables and accommodates variability in prevalence estimates.

Instruments and data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using the culturally adapted and validated “Termômetro Social COVID-19: Opinião Social” questionnaire (10.6084/m9.figshare.25917571), developed by the National School of Public Health at Universidade Nova de Lisboa (ENSP-UNL). The instrument, based on World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines [25] and the Portuguese National Health Survey [26], covered sociodemographic characteristics, COVID-19 vaccination status, healthcare utilization, and trust in health services [27–29].

The questionnaire was adapted to the Brazilian context using the Delphi technique and was administered in Brazilian Portuguese by trained interviewers using mobile devices and the REDCap platform [30]. Each interview lasted 20–30 min. Questions about COVID-19 vaccination referred to the participant’s immunization history during the pandemic (2021–2022).

Statistical analysis

Data were reviewed for consistency and standardized using Microsoft Excel, then imported into RStudio (version 4.3.1) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study participants through absolute and relative frequency analyses. The data were subsequently imported into RStudio (version 4.3.1) [30] for statistical modeling.

To construct the binary logistic regression models—one with COVID-19 vaccination status and the other with use of SUS services as the dependent variable—we first conducted univariate logistic regression analyses, in which each independent variable was analyzed separately in relation to the binary outcome. This step allowed us to identify variables significantly associated with each dependent variable (p < 0.05), which were then selected for inclusion in the final models.

During multivariate modeling, refinement procedures were applied to ensure statistical robustness and interpretability. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and only variables with VIF values below 10 were retained. Final model selection was based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), consistent with an exploratory approach appropriate for research involving socially vulnerable and heterogeneous populations. As both outcomes and independent variables were binary or dichotomized, binary logistic regression was deemed the appropriate method.

For the final models, we calculated adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Model validation included the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, the likelihood ratio test, and assessment of discriminatory capacity using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A complete list and operational definitions of all variables included in the final models are available in the supplementary materials (Table S1), facilitating interpretation of the regression results.

Results

Participants characteristics

A total of 374 migrants participated in the study (Table 1). Most were male (n = 219; 58.6%), aged 30–59 years (n = 252; 67.4%), and self-identified as Black or Brown (n = 220; 58.8%). Regarding socioeconomic status, 153 migrants (40.9%) reported no monthly family income, 32.4% had completed high school, and 6.7% had no formal schooling.

During the pandemic, 93.0% (n = 347) of migrants reported using the SUS, and 82.4% (n = 308) reported receiving at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine, which was provided exclusively through SUS infrastructure and facilities.

Factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination status (Table 3).

Migrants who identified as White (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.21–0.89) and those who had completed primary education (aOR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.19–0.86) were less likely to be vaccinated. Conversely, migrants living with people aged 60 years or older were more likely to be vaccinated (aOR = 2.54; 95% CI: 1.12–6.64). Additionally, those who had been tested for COVID-19 prior to vaccination were more likely to be vaccinated (aOR = 3.67; 95% CI: 2.00–6.96).

Model performance

Both final regression models demonstrated good fit and discriminatory capacity (Tables 3 and 4). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated adequate calibration (p = 0.572 for vaccination; p = 0.161 for SUS utilization), and the area under the ROC curve was 0.75 for the vaccination model and 0.82 for the SUS utilization model.

Discussion

This study provides new evidence on the factors influencing COVID-19 vaccination coverage and access to public health services among international migrants in Brazil during the pandemic. Our findings reveal that, despite the universal mandate of the SUS, significant barriers persist for migrants—particularly related to race, education, trust in health services, and living conditions—mirroring and amplifying broader health inequities observed globally.

The study sample, made up of 374 migrants, was predominantly male and aged between 30 and 59. Ethnic diversity was striking, with a predominance of Venezuelan migrants, followed by Haitians and representatives of other nationalities. These findings show the heterogeneity of this population, highlighting the need for adaptable public health policies that integrate cultural, socioeconomic, and geographical particularities to promote equitable and effective care.

COVID-19 vaccination and associated factors

Vaccination coverage among international migrants in this study was lower than the national average for the general population, corroborating previous evidence of persistent disparities in COVID-19 vaccine uptake between migrant and non-migrant populations [31]. Such disparities often stem from structural barriers, including limited healthcare access and documentation challenges, as well as socioeconomic disadvantages and variable engagement with health systems [32, 33].

Our finding that migrants living with older adults and those tested for COVID-19 prior to vaccination had higher odds of being vaccinated aligns with the role of perceived risk and direct experience in promoting vaccine uptake. Multigenerational households, where the vulnerability of older adults is salient, can increase motivation to vaccinate to protect at-risk family members. Likewise, prior COVID-19 testing may indicate greater health-seeking behavior and facilitate access to vaccination services [34].

Conversely, the lower likelihood of vaccination among migrants identifying as White and those with only elementary education aligns with existing literature linking low educational attainment to reduced vaccine uptake, potentially due to lower health literacy and increased susceptibility to misinformation [35]. In our sample, White-identifying migrants were predominantly from Venezuela, China, and Haiti, and experienced considerable socioeconomic vulnerabilities, including high unemployment, low household income, and residence in informal settlements or refugee settings. These findings suggest that the typical socioeconomic advantages associated with whiteness in Brazil may not apply to migrant subgroups, whose vaccination behavior is shaped by migration status, country of origin, and local structural barriers.

This observation underscores the heterogeneity within White-identifying migrant populations and highlights the importance of disaggregating data by region of origin and other sociodemographic variables to better identify and address specific barriers to vaccination [32, 33]. Although the small sample size limits the precision of estimates and precludes formal subgroup analyses, the patterns observed are consistent with global evidence that the association between socioeconomic status and vaccine uptake is context-dependent and mediated by education and health literacy [35].

These findings highlight the complex interplay of sociodemographic, behavioral, and health system factors influencing COVID-19 vaccination uptake among migrants. Interventions tailored to address educational gaps, targeted outreach to vulnerable subgroups, and leveraging health system interactions—such as testing—represent promising strategies to improve vaccine coverage in these populations [33].

Health services utilization and the role of trust

The high use of SUS services by migrants during the COVID-19 pandemic in this study highlights the central role of Brazil’s public health system in providing care to socially vulnerable populations, including international migrants. This finding reflects SUS’s function as a critical safety net, particularly during public health crises that exacerbate preexisting inequalities [36].

A significant positive association was observed between trust in hospitals and health professionals and increased SUS, corroborating existing evidence that institutional trust is a key determinant of healthcare engagement among marginalized groups. Trust likely reflects perceived quality, accessibility, and reliability of health services, which facilitate migrants’ navigation of structural and social barriers to care [37].

Conversely, migrants with higher educational attainment exhibited significantly lower odds of SUS use. This pattern is consistent with the socioeconomic stratification of healthcare access in Brazil, where individuals with greater education and resources more frequently rely on private healthcare providers [38, 39]. Despite advances in SUS coverage, these findings highlight persistent disparities, with lower-educated and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations remaining disproportionately dependent on public health services.

The lower likelihood of healthcare utilization among individuals residing in the Northeast of Brazil, as observed in Table 4, reflects a multifactorial disparity influenced by both healthcare system constraints and socioeconomic status. System-level limitations in the Northeast—such as deficiencies in the primary care work process, inadequate human resources, and suboptimal organization of care delivery—have been shown to reduce access to both preventive and curative services [40, 41]. Socioeconomic factors, including higher poverty rates, lower educational attainment, and greater social vulnerability, further compound these disparities. Migrant populations in the region may face additional barriers, including unstable employment, limited social support networks, and unfamiliarity with the local healthcare system, which can exacerbate inequities in access. This finding underscores the importance of considering both structural and social determinants when addressing healthcare utilization among residents and migrants in the Northeast [40, 41].

Collectively, these results emphasize SUS’s indispensable role in ensuring healthcare access for vulnerable migrant populations during the pandemic, particularly among those facing heightened social vulnerabilities. Moreover, the findings underscore the necessity of strengthening trust in health institutions as a strategy to enhance service utilization. Addressing educational and socioeconomic inequalities remains imperative to achieving equitable healthcare access within Brazil’s diverse migrant communities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample may not fully capture the heterogeneity of Brazil’s migrant population, as it was predominantly composed of Venezuelans and Haitians, and mainly included men aged 30–59. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for recall and social desirability bias, particularly regarding vaccination and health service use. Third, the study did not capture key contextual variables such as legal status, type of migration (economic, humanitarian), or length of stay in Brazil—factors known to influence health access and behaviors. Fourth, some of the odds ratios in our analysis had wide confidence intervals, which may indicate statistical imprecision resulting from substantial variability or limited sample size within certain subgroups, such as White migrants and residents of the Northeast region. It is important to acknowledge these potential limitations in the precision of estimates for smaller subgroups. This imprecision can affect the reliability and generalizability of the associations observed. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and may not reflect changes in attitudes or behaviors over time.

Implications for policy and practice and future studies

Our results reinforce the urgent need for public health strategies that are both inclusive and culturally sensitive. Effective approaches should include multilingual and culturally adapted health communication campaigns to improve vaccine coverage among migrants; outreach through trusted community leaders and networks; simplification of documentation requirements for health service access; and integration of vaccination and testing services, with active linkage and follow-up. Additionally, targeted interventions are needed in regions with historically lower coverage and greater vulnerability. Such strategies are supported by international guidelines and have shown effectiveness in improving vaccine uptake and health service utilization among migrants in other Latin American and global contexts [42, 43].

Future research should employ longitudinal designs and include more diverse migrant subgroups, as well as internal migrants, to better understand the evolving dynamics of health access. Given the wide confidence intervals observed in some estimates, future studies should employ larger and more representative samples to enhance result precision and reduce uncertainty. Studies should also examine the impact of legal status, social integration, and targeted interventions on health outcomes. Adopting an intersectional framework will be crucial to disentangle the overlapping vulnerabilities that shape migrants’ experiences with public health systems.

This study advances knowledge by demonstrating that COVID-19 vaccination and access to the SUS among international migrants in Brazil are influenced by factors such as age, education, race/color, and trust in health institutions. The findings highlight the need for targeted, culturally sensitive strategies to address barriers and promote equity in healthcare for migrants. Adopting an intersectional approach is essential to understand and effectively respond to the unique challenges faced by these populations during public health crises.

Conclusion

This study offers important insights into the complex factors influencing COVID-19 vaccination coverage and access to Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS) among international migrants during the pandemic. Despite the SUS’s universal mandate, migrants exhibited lower vaccination rates than the general population, highlighting persistent structural barriers linked to sociodemographic characteristics, living conditions, and trust in health services.

Despite the high utilization of SUS services among migrants, trust in health institutions proved to be a central factor shaping both access to and adherence with care. Ensuring transparent communication and promoting active community participation are vital strategies for fostering this trust, especially in the context of public health emergencies and other sanitary crises.

These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted, culturally sensitive public health strategies that address the structural and social barriers faced by migrant populations. Adopting an intersectional approach is essential to promote equity in healthcare access and strengthen the resilience of health systems in future public health emergencies.
