Reasoning Models are Test Exploiters: Rethinking Multiple Choice
Narun K. Raman, Taylor Lundy, Kevin Leyton-Brown

Abstract
When evaluating Large Language Models (LLMs) in question-answering domains, it is common to ask the model to choose among a fixed set of choices (so-called multiple-choice question-answering, or MCQA). Although downstream tasks of interest typically do not provide systems with explicit options among which to choose, this approach is nevertheless widely used because it makes it makes automatic grading straightforward and has tended to produce challenging benchmarks that correlate sufficiently well with downstream performance. This paper investigates the extent to which this trend continues to hold for state-of-the-art reasoning models, describing a systematic evaluation of 15 15 different question-answering benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HLE, GSM8K, MATH) and 25 different LLMs (including small models such as Qwen-2.5 7B Instruct and relatively large open-source models such as Llama-3.3 70B Instruct). For each model–benchmark pair, we considered 5 5 ways of presenting the model with questions, including variations on whether multiple choices were offered to the model at all; whether “none of the above” sometimes replaced the right answer; and whether the model was permitted to perform chain-of-thought reasoning before and/or after the choices were presented. MCQA remained a good proxy for the downstream performance of models as long as they were allowed to perform chain-of-thought reasoning only before being presented with the options among which they had to select. On the other hand, large models that were able to perform reasoning after being given a set of options tended to significantly outperform their free-text performance due to exploiting option artifacts, label priors, and elimination heuristics. We conclude that MCQA is no longer a good proxy for assessing downstream performance of state-of-the-art models, and offer practical guidelines for designing more robust, bias-resistant benchmarks that better reflect LLMs’ genuine reasoning capabilities.

Methodology

3.1
Evaluation Formats

The question of how to present MCQA and FTQA questions to LLMs gives rise to a large design space. We focus on two key dimensions of this space: how the question is formatted and what form the LLM’s response is allowed to take.

Question formats

We present MCQA questions to to the model in two formats. In the first format we present the question stem (i.e. “What is 2+2?”) followed by
k
options. Most of our benchmarks set
k=4
; in these cases, we drop

Following our work in
Raman et al. 2025

\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}
of the questions, we replaced the
correct
answer

with NOTA. In the remaining questions, we replaced one
incorrect
answer

with NOTA, chosen uniformly at random. Let
o_{\text{NOTA }}
be the set of options with NOTA replacing an option.
Note that an LLM that always picks NOTA now performs at the random-guess baseline, making any systematic improvement over this baseline evidence of comprehension.

A notable limitation is that because the second stage reintroduces the options to the same model that generated the chain-of-thought, reasoning models can still exploit option artifacts or apply elimination heuristics when selecting their final label. This residual vulnerability means
CoT-MC-CoT
mitigates, but does not entirely eliminate, the shortcuts afforded by multiple-choice formats.

However,
CoT-MCNA-CoT
potentially mitigates this issue for reasoning models. The model only gets to reason on

3.2
Question Format Conversion

A core aspect of our methodology is asking LLMs questions on multiple-choice and free-text formats to examine how format alone affects performance. In this section, we describe how we converted the benchmarks listed in
Section

MCQA
\rightarrow

We start with the datasets within

The filtering process they used kept many MCQA questions that would not be viable FTQA questions. After this filtering process,

Furthermore, given that we are not using an LLM or other model-based tool to evaluate the free-text answer, there are many MCQA questions that pass the filtering steps that cannot be evaluated in free-text. For example, when a question asks for an answer and a reason: “Should the state court look to federal or state law to decide the effect of the judgment?” With possible completions: “State law, because X…” or “State law, because Y…” For those questions, we only evaluate correctness on formats where the model gets to see the options (e.g,

3.3
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate LLM performance and behavior using several metrics:

Accuracy:

The primary metric is the percentage of questions answered correctly. For MCQA this is simple: a response is correct if the model’s chosen option letter matches the correct option letter. For FTQA, a response is correct if it matches the known correct answer. In the case of numeric answers, we require numerical equivalence after rounding the correct answer to the number of significant figures the model reports. This penalizes overprecision: if an LLM reports more significant figures than necessary and is incorrect, that discrepancy is treated as an error. For functional answers, we convert the text into sympy and simplify, testing equivalence through sympy’s built-in functionality.

Calibration:

We measure how different formats affect the confidence the LLM places on its answer and how well that aligns with its accuracy. For multiple-choice, we can obtain a confidence score by looking at the LLM’s probability (or logit) for each option. Concretely, we extract the probability it assigns to each option token (after normalizing by either RPM strategy). We then compute the expected calibration error (ECE) by comparing the LLM’s confidence in its answer with the empirical accuracy
(
Naeini et al. 2015
)
. This metric helps assess whether multiple-choice formats lead to overconfident guessing. Indeed, if an LLM is merely guessing among the choices, a well-calibrated LLM’s confidence should not be high; overconfidence in MCQA would indicate it is “fooled” by the presence of an answer it recognizes.

, indicating it rarely over-selects NOTA when a correct option exists. In other words, when the model does choose NOTA it is usually correct (high precision), but it only identifies
58\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of NOTA in when it is the correct answer (moderate recall). The fact that precision substantially exceeds recall shows the model is not unduly drawn to NOTA as a salient choice; rather, it applies NOTA selectively when its reasoning trace does not map to another valid option.

5
Limitations

We touch on some of the limitations in this work. The primary limitation to our results is that it is limited to a suite of questions that permit answer extraction through regex. While this still leaves a large swathe of questions, it remains to be seen that this methodology can be used to spot option exploitation in other less amenable questions.

Benchmarks like MMLU or STEER-ME often permit simple numeric or algebraic equivalence checks, but fully open-ended responses (e.g., essays) can’t be automatically adjudicated. Our analysis thus omits these more complex tasks.

Furthermore, performance changes on NOTA answer replacement may not be entirely due to an inability to reason about the problem, but that there is not necessarily an unambiguously correct answer. While we investigated a set of questions which contain a gold answer, this leaves open the question of more open-ended reasoning tasks.

6
Conclusions

Our investigation reveals three intertwined lessons for the design and interpretation of LLM evaluations: (1) Modern reasoning models do not merely solve questions—they dynamically leverage option sets to shortcut deep inference. The gap between MCQA and free-text performance is not noise but a window into how LLMs ground their “thoughts” in readily available cues. (2) Decoupling is essential. By separating CoT from selection—via
CoT-MC-1T
and
CoT-MCNA-CoT
—we can expose latent reasoning ability and distinguish genuine comprehension from test exploitation. This synthesis suggests that future benchmarks should treat reasoning and answer recall as orthogonal axes of evaluation. (3) Construct questions that never reference the answer choices (e.g., avoid “which of the following”), and favor prompts whose free-form answers can be parsed or mapped post-hoc. Steer clear of validation-style MCQA questions in reasoning benchmarks, and filter or transform any questions that hinge on distractor semantics. Such option-agnostic question design ensures that chain-of-thought traces reflect true problem solving rather than option exploitation.

Together, these insights call for a paradigm shift: evaluation formats must evolve in lockstep with model capabilities. As LLMs grow more adept at test-taking, our benchmarks must outpace superficial heuristics and faithfully track the emergence of genuine reasoning.
