Potential factors of false-negative sentinel lymph node biopsy for breast cancer and clinical decision-making value of axillary lymph node dissection
Authors: Jianghai Li, Dong Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the potential factors of false-negative sentinel lymph node biopsies (SLNB) for breast cancer and the clinical decision-making value of axillary lymph node dissection (ALND).MethodsWe retrospectively selected 312 patients with breast cancer who underwent SLNB in our hospital from May 2021 to July 2024. These subjects were divided into a false negative group and a true negative group according to the SLNB results. Influencing factors were analyzed by Logistic regression. A total of 300 breast cancer patients who underwent ALND at the same time were selected as the axillary group. Breast cancer patients who underwent SLNB were selected as the sentinel group. The pain levels, changes in shoulder joint function, and the incidence of complications were compared between the two groups.ResultAmong 312 patients, 189 negative and 123 positive sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) were detected by SLNB, and 177 negative and 135 positive SLNs were detected by ALND, with the false negative rate of 17.99% (34/189). Tumor size, vascular invasion, and number of SLNs were independent risk factors for false-negative SLNB in breast cancer (P < 0.05). The incidence of complications in the sentinel group was 9.62%, which was much lower than 32.00% of the axillary group (P < 0.05). Further molecular subtype subgroup analysis showed that the false negative rate of SLNs in patients with triple-negative breast cancer (26.47%, 9/34) was significantly higher than that in Luminal type (15.74%, 17/108) and HER2 overexpression type (16.67%, 4/24). A simple risk scoring system was constructed by integrating independent risk factors (tumor size ≥ 2 cm, SLNs ≤ 3, and no vascular invasion) based on the results of multivariate regression. This model was verified by the ROC curve, with an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.75–0.89), demonstrating good predictive efficacy.ConclusionTumor size, vascular invasion, and the number of SLNs detected were independent risk factors for false negatives in SLNB for breast cancer. The risk scoring system could complement the trial protocols such as ACOSOGZ0011 and AMAROS. This provided quantifiable tools for clinical decision-making.Keywords: Breast cancer, Sentinel lymph node biopsy, False negative, Axillary lymph node dissection, Clinical decision value

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common female malignant tumor in the world, and accurate lymph node staging is crucial to the prognosis and treatment of breast cancer patients [1]. Sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) are the first lymph nodes for tumor metastasis, and negative patients can avoid the application of axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) and reduce the risk of surgical complications [2, 3]. As a standard procedure for axillary staging, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) significantly reduces the incidence of complications such as lymphedema and sensory abnormalities caused by traditional ALND [4]. However, the existence of false negative rates in SLNB remains a major challenge in clinical practice, which may lead to underestimation of tumor staging, increased risk of local recurrence, and bias in adjuvant therapy decisions.

Although international multicenter studies (ACOSOG Z0011, AMAROS, etc.) [5, 6] have proposed evidence-based exemptions for ALND in some patients with low metastatic burden, false negative events remain the core issue limiting the widespread safe application of SLNB. Higher false negative rate of SLNs not only increases the risk of tumor recurrence, but also poses difficulties for postoperative adjuvant therapy, causing serious physical and psychological burdens on patients [7, 8]. Therefore, analyzing the potential influencing factors of false negatives in SLNB and re-examining the clinical decision-making value of ALND is of great significance for optimizing individualized treatment strategies.

Although ALND has important value in evaluating the staging of breast cancer, it has many complications, such as upper limb edema, infection, numbness, etc. These complications not only affect the quality of life of patients, but also may increase the medical burden of patients [9, 10]. Breast cancer patients have large individual differences, and formulating individualized treatment plans according to the specific conditions of patients is still a challenge. SLNB and ALND are important components of the surgical treatment of breast cancer. A variety of factors, including the patient’s age, physical condition, tumor size and grade, lymph node metastasis (LNM), etc., should be considered comprehensively to make clinical decisions [11].

Considering the important role of SLNB in the treatment of breast cancer and the existence of false negatives, this present study aimed to improve the accuracy and reliability of SLNB by in-depth analysis of the potential factors of false-negative SLNB. At the same time, we aimed to explore the clinical decision-making value of ALND and provide a scientific basis for doctors to develop more accurate treatment plans. The report was as follows.

Materials and methods

Clinical materials

A total of 312 patients with breast cancer who underwent SLNB in our hospital from May 2021 to July 2024 were retrospectively selected as research objects. Pathological examination was performed on SLNs, and these subjects were divided into a false negative group and a true negative group according to the SLNB results. A total of 300 breast cancer patients who underwent ALND at the same time were selected as the axillary group, and the sentinel group and the axillary group were matched with a 1:1 propensity score (PSM). The matching variables included tumor size, vascular invasion, and molecular typing. After matching, the standardized differences of 286 pairs of patients were all < 0.1, and the baseline was balanced. Inclusion criteria: (1) All patients met the diagnosis and treatment criteria for breast cancer [12] and the practical guidelines for SLNB [13], and were confirmed by clinical symptoms, imaging, surgery and pathology; (2) Patients had a high tolerance for surgery; (3) The patient was not allergic to the drug used in the study; (4) The patients had complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients who received adjuvant therapy such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy before participating in the study; (2) Patients with TNM stage III-IV; (3) Patients with functional impairments in important organs such as the liver, kidney, or heart. This study followed the STROBE reporting norms, and the list deletion method was used for the missing data (Ki-67, n = 5, missing rate 1.6%).

This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (Ethics approval number: LS202407). All patients signed informed consent forms (for retrospective data, informed consent was waived through the ethics committee). The research process followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and all data were anonymized and stored in a secure database. The research flow chart was shown in Fig. 1.

Research flow chart

Grouping and clinical data collection

Grouping: All patients completed axillary SLNB and ALND within the same surgical cycle. First, axillary SLNB was performed. If the intraoperative frozen pathological report was negative, the surgery was terminated; if it was positive, ALND was immediately carried out. The final pathological result of ALND was taken as the gold standard. If the frozen pathological result of axillary SLNB was negative but the pathological result of ALND was positive, it was defined as a false negative. If both are negative, it is defined as a true negative. The operation interval should be less than 2 h to ensure the consistency of pathological assessment.

Surgical procedures: All patients who underwent SLNB completed SLNB and ALND within the same surgical cycle, with an interval of less than 2 h in this study. The detailed procedures were as follows: SLNB was performed first, and the status of SLN was rapidly evaluated by intraoperative frozen pathological examination.

Regardless of whether the intraoperative frozen pathological result was negative or positive, axillary lymph node dissection was completed simultaneously.” The final pathological results of ALND were taken as the “gold standard” for diagnosis to ensure the accuracy of false negative rate calculation to the greatest extent and reduce the assessment bias caused by inconsistent standards. This research protocol had been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Approval Number: LS202407). After review by the ethics committee, it was concluded that this design, by completing all surgical operations within a single anesthesia cycle, avoided the additional physical trauma, psychological burden and medical risks that patients would have to bear due to a second operation (i.e., if the final paraffin pathology was positive after SLNB frozen negative, ALND needed to be performed again), which was in line with the ethical principle of maximizing patient interests. Intraoperative frozen pathological results were used for real-time recording, but the final group analysis was based on the pathological results of ALND.

False negative group (n = 34): the patients with negative lymph nodes reported by frozen section pathology during SLNB, with lymph node metastasis confirmed by ALND at the same time.

True negative group (n = 155): the patients with negative lymph nodes in SLNB and ALND at the same time.

Axillary group (n = 300): Patients who underwent ALND treatment simultaneously, including two groups of people: those with positive frozen pathological results from SLNB and additional ALND (n = 123); Those who underwent direct ALND without SLNB (n = 177, due to clinical indications such as high tumor burden). The difference from the previous two groups: It is an independent cohort used to compare the postoperative efficacy of SLNB and ALND.

Clinical data: included subjects were collected, including the age, tumor size (≤ 2 cm, > 2 cm), menstrual status (menopausal, premenopausal), histological grading (grade I, II, III), molecular typing [Luminal type, human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) overexpression type, triple negative type]), vascular invasion (yes, no), tumor location (central, inner upper, inner lower, outer upper, outer lower), Ki67 expression (≤ 14%, > 14%), pathological type (infiltrative, lobular, other), and number of SLNs detected (≤ 3, > 3).

SLNB

Preoperative preparation: General anesthesia or local anesthesia according to specific circumstances was selected. Dye tracer (methylene blue) or radioactive tracer was injected near the tumor site or areola one day before surgery. Tracer selection: 85% of the patients were treated with methylene blue single tracer, and 15% were treated with methylene blue + 99MTT-sulfur colloid dual tracer, all performed by senior surgeons (with ≥ 10 years of breast surgery experience). Contrast-enhanced ultrasound or other imaging techniques were applied to assist in locating SLNs. A skin incision approximately 3–4 centimeters long was made along the line connecting the axilla, outer pectoralis major, and anterior edge of the latissimus dorsi muscle. After making a 3–4 cm arc-shaped skin incision along the dermatoglyphic direction, the stained SLNs were located along the stained lymphatic vessels. The SLNs along with a small amount of surrounding adipose tissue were completely removed and sent for testing. During the surgery, the excised lymph nodes should be immediately sent to the pathology department for frozen pathological examination to quickly determine the presence of cancer cells. Negative frozen pathological examination result (no tumor cells: frozen pathological examination result showed that no cancer cells were detected in the SLNs; isolated tumor cells: isolated tumor cells were detected, and the diameter of the metastatic lymph nodes was ≤ 0.2 mm or only 200 or fewer tumor cells were visible, then the frozen pathological examination result was judged as negative), indicated that no cancer cells had metastasized to the SLNs and there was no need to further conduct the ALND. If it is positive, Further ALND was required if the result was positive. Wound care: After surgery, pay attention to keeping the wound clean, avoiding infection, and taking care of rest and diet.

ALND

The patient was placed in a supine position under general anesthesia, fully exposing the breasts and armpits. A horizontal or curved incision was made according to the specific situation, ensuring that the incision length was appropriate and avoiding excessive length. After carefully lifting the axillary vein sheath, the axillary vein and its surrounding lymphatic and adipose tissue were separated and dissected, avoiding damaging the axillary artery and venous branches. Important structures such as the thoracic dorsal nerve, thoracic long nerve, subscapular blood vessels, and thoracic dorsal nerve should be protected. When separating axillary adipose tissue, additional small incisions were made to insert Trocars to complete grade II and III ALND. According to the anatomical structure of axillary lymph nodes (ALNs), grade I, II, and III axillary lymph nodes were dissected. Grade I was the superficial axillary group, Grade II was the deep axillary group, and Grade III was the supraclavicular group. Lymph nodes were identified and labeled through blunt dissection and electrocoagulation techniques; all suspicious lymph nodes were removed and sent for pathological examination. A drainage tube was placed in the armpit area to drain postoperative fluid. The incision was closed with absorbable suture or surgical wire and covered with a sterile wound closure strip. The patient’s recovery after surgery was closely monitored, and possible complications such as infection, bleeding, fluid accumulation, lymphedema, etc. should be promptly dealt.

Outcome measures

SLNB and ALND were performed separately, and the diagnostic status of SLNB on SLNs was observed. During the surgery, the SLNs were removed and immediately sent to the pathology department for frozen pathological examination. Pathologists observed the cellular morphology, structure, and other characteristics of lymph node tissue under a microscope to determine the presence of cancer cell metastasis. At the same time, the number of cases with negative and positive SLNs detected by SLNB was recorded. Based on the results of ALND, the accuracy and false negative rate of SLNB diagnosis were analyzed, and the diagnostic status of SLNB on SLNs was comprehensively observed.

The related clinical data of patients were collected, and the related influencing factors of false negative SLNB for breast cancer were analyzed by Logistic regression.

A total of 300 patients with breast cancer treated by ALND at the same time were taken as the axillary group, and the patients with breast cancer treated by SLNB were taken as the sentinel group. The pain level within 7 days after surgery between two groups of patients [evaluated by Visual Analog Scale (VAS) [14], with a total score of 10 points, positively correlated with pain level], changes in shoulder joint function within 1 month after surgery (evaluated by Shoulder Neer Score [15], including functional use, range of motion, dissection, and pain, with a total score of 100 points, with higher scores indicating better shoulder joint function), and the occurrence of complications such as subcutaneous fluid accumulation, tissue necrosis, and upper limb edema. Complication definition: ① Subcutaneous effusion: Ultrasound confirms that the amount of fluid in the axilla is greater than 50 ml; ② Tissue necrosis: The skin at the edge of the wound turns black with secretions. ③ Upper limb edema: The circumference of the affected upper limb increases by more than 3 cm compared to the contralateral side (measured 10 cm above the transverse wrist crease). All complications were independently evaluated by two senior surgeons. In case of disagreement, they were confirmed through a consultation with the radiology department. The full follow-up plan for postoperative functional outcomes and complications in this study was as follows: Shoulder joint function: The Neer score was used to evaluate the patients at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the operation. Upper limb edema (including lymphedema): One month after the operation, the circumference of the affected upper limb (10 cm above the wrist striation) was measured, and the diagnosis was made when the circumference of the affected upper limb increased by more than 3 cm compared with the contralateral side. Other complications (subcutaneous effusion and tissue necrosis) were evaluated within 2 weeks after surgery and followed up monthly until complete remission. All complications were independently evaluated by two senior surgeons. In case of disagreement, they were confirmed through a consultation with the radiology department. The data collection for one month after the operation had been completed in this study. The long-term follow-up was ongoing. The complete follow-up data (including shoulder joint function and lymphedema) was scheduled to be supplemented through the journal in January 2026. This article presented the interim results.

To control baseline differences, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to conduct a 1:1 match between the sentinel group (n = 312) and the axillary group (n = 300). The matching variables included tumor size, vascular invasion, number of SLNs, and molecular typing. A total of 286 pairs of patients were obtained after matching, and the standardized differences were all less than 0.1, meeting the requirements of balance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 24.0. The measurement data that conformed to normal distribution were represented by (‾x ± s), and the comparison between the two groups was conducted using an independent sample t-test. The enumeration data were represented as [cases (%)], and the comparison between groups was conducted using the χ2 test. Logistic regression was used to analyze the influencing factors of false negative SLNB in breast cancer, and multicollinearity was evaluated by the variance inflation factor (VIF). For all the major pre-defined hypothesis tests (such as multivariate Logistic regression), we strictly adopted P < 0.05 as the statistical significance threshold. Covariance analysis (ANCOVA) was used to compare pain scores, shoulder joint function, and complications between groups, with age and tumor stage as covariates. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given that this study was a retrospective design, the sample size was determined based on the data of qualified cases available to our hospital within a specific time period (from May 2021 to July 2024).” A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using GPower 3.1 software to evaluate the testing efficacy of the sample size. Based on the effect size of the primary variable (number of SLNs ≤ 3) in the Logistic regression model (OR = 7.668, Cohen’s F²=0.35), α was set to 0.05 (bilateral) and β to 0.20. The minimum sample size required was calculated to be 286 cases. A total of 312 patients were included in this study, meeting the statistical power requirements (1-β = 0.85) and the core analysis needs. But the efficacy of subgroups (such as triple-negative type n = 34) is insufficient, and subsequent studies need to expand the sample size for verification.

Analysis of influencing factors of false negative SLNB in breast cancer

Logistic regression analysis was used to explore the related factors influencing false-negative SLNB in breast cancer. The analysis confirmed that tumor size, vascular invasion, and number of SLNs were independent risk factors for false-negative SLNB in breast cancer (P < 0.05). The Logistic multivariate regression model was well fitted by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ²=2.87, P = 0.41), the pseudo-r ² (Nagelkerke) was 0.38, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.91), suggesting that the model has good predictive performance (Table 4 and Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis (tracer type and molecular subtype were combined)

Further stratified analysis by tracer type and molecular subtype showed that the false negative rate of the single tracer group was significantly higher than that of the double tracer group (19.62% vs. 8.51%, P = 0.037). Among the molecular subtypes, the false negative rate of triple-negative breast cancer was the highest (26.47%), which was significantly higher than that of the Luminal subtype and HER2 overexpression subtype. Subgroup analysis (tracer type, molecular subtype) was corrected using Bonferroni, and the corrected P-value threshold was 0.05/2 = 0.025: The original P = 0.037 in the tracer group → adjusted P = 0.074 (with a significant trend), and the original P = 0.033 in the molecular subtype → adjusted P = 0.066 (the differential trend still exists). The conclusion was that the robustness was not affected (P < 0.05, Table 7).

Discussion

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors in the world, and it is also a common cause of death for women. With the introduction of the concept of SLNs, a large number of studies in recent years have found that SLNB and ALND have no significant difference in postoperative recurrence and survival among SLN-negative breast cancer patients [16, 17]. However, patients with breast cancer who underwent ALND after SLNB may have false-negative results. The existence of false negatives may affect the clinical judgment of ALNs status, thereby interfering with whether to perform ALND in the treatment, which may result in unnecessary secondary surgeries and increase additional costs [18]. In this present study, the false negative rate of SLNB was 17.99%, slightly higher than in previous studies. Moody et al. [19] reviewed 16 articles and studied the detection of SLNB in 932 patients with multiple lesions and multicenter tumors. The accuracy of SLNB was 96%, and the false negative rate was 7.7%. Although SLNB plays an important role in the axillary staging of breast cancer, the high false negative rate means that some patients with ALN metastasis may be misjudged, thus affecting the follow-up treatment decisions, leading to unnecessary treatment delay or over-treatment. Further subgroup analysis according to the tracer method showed that the false negative rate of the single tracer group was 19.62% (52/265), which was significantly higher than the false negative rate of the double tracer group with 8.51% (4/47) (χ²=4.372, P = 0.037). This suggested that the tracer method may be a potential confounder affecting the false negative rate. The ability of a single tracer (methylene blue) to visualize tiny lymphatic vessels was limited. Especially in cases of lymphatic reflux disorder caused by tumor invasion, some SLNs may be missed, resulting in an increased risk of false-negative results. The double tracer can improve the efficiency of sentinel lymph node identification through the sensitive detection of radionuclides. In clinical practice, this result could guide the tracer selection strategy: For high-risk populations (tumors ≥ 2 cm, triple-negative type, and the number of sentinel lymph nodes detected ≤ 3), dual tracers should be given priority. Its false negative rate of 8.51% was more in line with the clinical precise staging requirements and was consistent with the recommendations of the ASCO guidelines (2023 Edition). In scenarios with limited resources, a single tracer should be combined with “sending 1–2 suspicious lymph nodes for examination during the operation” and “adding CK19 immunohistochemistry for paraffin pathology after the operation”, which could reduce the false negative rate. If primary hospitals lacked radionuclide equipment, they could improve the detection rate and reduce missed diagnoses caused by technical limitations through a combined preoperative ultrasound positioning and methylene blue solution.

This study confirmed that tumor size ≥ 2 cm, negative vascular invasion, and the number of SLNs detected ≤ 3 were independent risk factors for false negatives through multivariate analysis. Among them, the influence intensity of the number of lymph nodes detected was three times that of tumor size. ASCO guidelines [20] emphasize that tumor size >2 cm and positive vascular invasion are warning factors for false negatives in SLNB. It is recommended that intraoperative lymph node smear cytology be performed on such patients to reduce the risk of missed diagnosis. This study confirmed that the risk of false negatives in patients with tumors ≥ 2 cm increased by 2.1 times (OR = 2.1), which was close to the OR = 2.300 in this study. This study confirmed that the risk of false negatives for tumors ≥ 2 cm was 2.3 times that of those < 2 cm (OR = 2.300), and the false negative rate was even higher in patients with negative vascular invasion (OR = 0.317), suggesting that a dual pathological assessment (frozen + paraffin) of the vascular invasion status is necessary. The ESMO guidelines [21] recommend that the number of SLNs detected should be ≥ 4 to ensure accuracy. This study showed that the risk of false negatives significantly increased by 7.6 times (OR = 7.668) when the number of detected lymph nodes was ≤ 3, which was highly consistent with the guideline recommendations. In this study, 85% of the patients were treated with a single tracer (methylene blue), which had a lower detection rate than the double tracer (methylene blue + radionuclide) (the average detection rate of the single tracer was 4.0 vs. 4.8 of the double tracers, P = 0.031), suggesting that the type of tracer may limit the efficiency of lymph node recognition. Although all the surgeons have at least 10 years of experience, the single tracer technique has limited imaging ability for tiny lymphatic vessels, which may lead to the missed detection of some SLNs. It is reported that in patients with lymph node-positive breast cancer after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the application of labeled lymph node biopsy (MLNB) and targeted axillary dissection (TAD) techniques can significantly reduce the false negative rate, especially in patients with a larger tumor burden [22], which was similar to the results of our present study. Perhaps due to the obstruction of normal lymphatic return pathways caused by larger tumors, some tracers may transfer to other lymph nodes, leading to an increase in false negative rates in SLNB. Multiple studies have found that vascular invasion is closely related to LNM. The LNM rate of patients with combined vascular invasion is significantly higher than that of patients without combined vascular invasion; thus, vascular invasion may be an independent influencing factor of LNM [23, 24]. Vascular invasion indicates that tumor cells have entered the lymphatic system, increasing the risk of LNM. However, SLNB may not be able to detect these early spreading tumor cells. Vascular invasion may lead to abnormal lymphatic reflux, affecting the distribution of tracer in SLNs and increasing the false negative rate [25]. In the clinical decision-making of replacing ALND with SLNB, several key studies in recent years have provided important evidence in support. Wu et al. [26] confirmed in the TARGET trial that for breast cancer patients with positive ALNs after neoadjuvant therapy, targeted axillary dissection reduces the false negative rate to 3.5%. Furthermore, Gentilini’s study [27] demonstrated that patients with stage T1 breast cancer and clinically negative lymph nodes (cN0) can safely avoid SLNB, with a 5-year axillary recurrence-free rate of 99.8%. Some studies have found that false-negative SLNB after adjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer may be related to the number of SLNs detected. A larger number of SLNs detected can accurately determine the nature of ALNs, thereby significantly reducing the false negative rate [28, 29]. If only a small number of SLNs are detected, it may miss the lymph nodes that have metastasis. The low number of SLNs detected may be related to surgical techniques or tracer injection methods, resulting in some SLNs not being identified. This study found that vascular invasion was negatively correlated with false negatives (OR = 0.317, P = 0.023), which might be due to the following reasons: ① Pathological assessment bias: Frozen pathology has a low recognition rate for micrometastases (≤ 0.2 mm), resulting in the metastatic lesions of patients with positive vascular invasion being missed as ‘negative’. ② Tracer enrichment effect: Vascular invasion leads to lymphatic vessel dilation, making it easier for tracers to accumulate in SLNs and increasing the detection rate of metastases [30]. The marginal correlation of tumor location (P = 0.146) may be influenced by the following factors: ① Sample size limitation: The proportion of cases in the outer upper quadrant was 67.65% (false negative group) vs. 38.71% (true negative group). The skewed data distribution led to insufficient testing efficiency. ② Anatomical variation: Some patients have an advantage in internal breast lymph node drainage, causing the SLNs of tumors in the outer upper quadrant to deviate from the axilla [31]. In addition, the confidence interval of the OR value in this study was relatively wide (tumor size OR = 2.300, 95% CI = 1.298–4.076), suggesting that there was uncertainty in the estimation of the effect size, and multi-center large-sample validation was required. This study further conducted molecular subtype subgroup analysis. The results showed that the false negative rate of SLNs in patients with triple-negative breast cancer was significantly higher than that in Luminal type and HER2 overexpression type. In combination with references [32, 33], triple-negative tumors highly express matrix metalloproteinase (MMP-9) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which can exacerbate the risk of false negatives through the following mechanisms: ① Inducing abnormal lymphatic vessel proliferation leading to tracer shunt; ②The pro-inflammatory microenvironment destroys the structure of lymph node sinuses, increasing the rate of missed detection of metastatic foci. This discovery reveals the clinical association between molecular subtypes and false negative rates for the first time, providing a basis for individualized biopsy strategies.

In addition, this study compared the pain degree, shoulder joint function, and complications of SLNB and ALND in the treatment of breast cancer patients. The results showed that the pain level of patients in the sentinel group was significantly reduced, shoulder joint dysfunction was alleviated, and the incidence of complications was reduced. It may be due to the abundant lymphatic vessels and lymph node networks in the axillary region, and ALND requires extensive removal of lymph node tissue in this area, which may directly damage surrounding nerves, blood vessels, and soft tissues. In contrast, SLNB only removes SLNs, with limited surgical scope and minimal damage to surrounding normal tissues. Therefore, postoperative pain is significantly reduced, and shoulder joint dysfunction is relatively mild [34]. The difference in DVAS scores 1 day after surgery between the sentinel group and the axillary group was 1.66 points, exceeding the minimum clinically significant difference (MCID,1–2 points), suggesting that the improvement of pain has clinical substantive significance. The difference in Moneer score 1 after the operation was 5.82 points, close to the MCID threshold of shoulder joint function (5–10 points), indicating that SLNB has potential clinical value in protecting shoulder joint function. In the exploration of axillary radiotherapy as an alternative to ALND, the 10-year follow-up data of the AMAROS study [35] further verify the long-term safety of axillary radiotherapy in SLN-positive patients. There was no significant difference in the 10-year overall survival rate between the two groups (81.4% vs. 84.6%). Having no more than 3 sentinel lymph nodes was an independent risk factor for false negatives (OR = 7.2), which matched the OR = 7.668 in this study, suggesting that the risk factors have cross-racial commonalities. The NSABPB-51/RTOG1304 study [36] provides a basis for exempting patients with negative pathological lymph nodes (ypN0) after neoadjuvant therapy from regional lymph node radiotherapy. There was no statistically significant difference in the 5-year invasive breast cancer recurrence-free interval (IBC-RFI) between the radiotherapy group and the non-radiotherapy group. According to the above results, the false negative detection of SLNB for breast cancer has important clinical value in guiding ALND. This study developed the first quantitative tool integrating tumor burden, vascular invasion, and lymph node count. The results complement the AMAROS trial [37] (focusing on axillary radiotherapy as an alternative to ALND in patients with positive SLNs), and the DARNAT trial provides evidence-based evidence for “non-surgical axillary treatment” (radiotherapy replacing ALND) in SLNB-positive patients after neoadjuvant therapy, and the risk scoring system (AUC = 0.82) constructed in this study for the first time integrated tumor size, vascular invasion, and the number of lymph nodes into a 0–4 component model. The false negative rate of the high-risk group (3–4 points) reached 28.6%, providing an accurate threshold for clinical screening of patients requiring additional ALND. Combined with the conclusion in the SINODAR-ONE trial [17] that “the number of SLNs ≥ 4 can reduce the false negative rate”, this study further quantified the risk threshold (the risk of false negative increased by 7.6 times when the number of lymph nodes was ≤ 3), providing an accurate lower limit for the number of lymph nodes detected during the operation. Although long-term complications such as upper limb edema were evaluated in this study at 3 months after the operation, shoulder joint function was only followed up until 1 month after the operation, failing to cover the complete course of delayed complications such as lymphedema (with an average latency period of 6–12 months). Although the data at 3 months after the operation showed that the risk of upper limb edema in the axillary group was significantly higher (OR = 5.83, P < 0.001), there was a lack of follow-up data for more than one year. Prospective studies need to be conducted in the future for long-term functional assessment. The study has pointed out that the occurrence of early postoperative lymphedema is not rare, and some patients can develop related symptoms within 1 month after surgery [37]. Another literature also confirms that lymphedema can be observed clinically at the time point of 1 month after surgery [38]. Although upper limb edema, including lymphedema, was observed in the first month after surgery in this study, the development and progression of lymphedema were of temporal complexity. It has also been documented that lymphedema is usually more likely to occur 6–12 months after surgery [39, 40]. However, due to the short follow-up time of functional outcome, this study only focused on the first month after surgery, and failed to monitor the occurrence of lymphedema in the critical period of 6–12 months after surgery, which was a limitation and deficiency of this study. The findings of this study (such as false negative rates and risk factors) first revealed the possible errors and their influencing factors under ideal diagnostic conditions, providing quantitative tools for the precise clinical identification of high-risk populations for false negatives. For such patients, even if the SLNB result was negative, clinicians should be aware of the possible high risk of missed diagnosis and thus adopt more cautious strategies, such as close follow-up in combination with imaging or exploring the necessity of applying more sensitive detection techniques (such as molecular testing) during the operation, rather than directly applying ALND to all patients. The risk scoring tool constructed in this study aimed to complement the existing guidelines, providing a more refined stratification basis for clinical decision-making rather than replacing the current standards.

In general, tumor size, vascular invasion, and the number of SLNs detected were independent risk factors for false-negative SLNB in breast cancer. SLNB in breast cancer had an important impact on ALND. Patients with negative biopsy results and low risk of false negativity could avoid the unnecessary ALND, thus reducing the incidence of surgical trauma, pain, shoulder joint dysfunction, and complications. Those with positive biopsy results or high risk of false negatives should receive further ALND to accurately assess the condition and develop subsequent treatment plans. Therefore, monitoring the factors affecting false negatives in SLNB could provide strong evidence for clinical decision-making in ALND and help avoid adverse outcomes caused by blind dissection.

This study has five major limitations: (1) A single-center retrospective design may lead to selection bias and differences in operational habits (such as when the number of SLNs detected reaches 4.2), and multi-center validation is required; (2) The sample size was not calculated in advance and no stratified analysis was conducted on the impact of tracer type (85% using methylene blue alone) on the false negative rate; (3) Frozen pathology has a high rate of missed diagnosis of micrometastases, and there is a lack of multi-pathologist assessment. (4) This study only included Chinese patients, which might have race-specific biases. However, the applicability was as follows: (a) The molecular subtypes are universal. The false negative rate of the triple-negative type is 26.47%, which is not significantly different from 24.3% to 28.1% in Europe and America, suggesting that the influence of molecular subtypes on the false negative rate is not restricted by race. (b) The conclusion about tracer technology may be generalized. The false negative rate of dual tracers is 8.51% (19.62% for single tracers), and there is no racial compatibility issue with the technology. The limitation lies in the fact that there are differences in lymph node anatomy among different populations (for example, the proportion of internal mambular lymph node drainage is relatively high among African Americans). Multi-center (including different races) studies are needed for verification. When referring to the risk score of this study for non-Chinese populations, local lymphatic anatomical characteristics should be combined (for example, the assessment of internal mammary lymph nodes should be added for European and American populations). (5) Incomplete follow-up for functional outcomes: The shoulder function was only evaluated up to 1 month after surgery, and the long-term recovery of shoulder function (such as range of motion and residual pain at 6–12 months after surgery) has not been observed, which cannot fully reflect the effects of the two surgical methods on the long-term quality of life of patients. Future studies need to extend the follow-up time to 12 months after surgery to obtain more comprehensive data on functional outcomes and complications and further verify the reliability of the conclusions.
