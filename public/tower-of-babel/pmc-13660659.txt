Cross species single-nucleus characterization of mammillary body
Authors: Xinrui Lin, Lanfang Li, Yuhang Shen, Xiaojing Chu, Aodi He, Xinyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As distinct nuclei within the posterior hypothalamus, the mammillary body (MB) is an essential component of the central nervous system and plays a critical role in memory processing in mammals. To date, however, its cellular heterogeneity across different species has yet to be systematically characterized. Here, leveraging single-nucleus transcriptomics of MB in mice, humans, and pigs, we delineated the cellular composition, transcriptional regulation and neuronal functional potentials in the MB. In mice, we identified five glutamatergic neuronal subtypes marked by Tachykinin 2 (Tac2) in lateral mammillary nucleus (LM), parvalbumin (PV), dopamine receptor D2 (Drd2), and neurotensin (Nts) in medial part of the medial mammillary nucleus, and nitric oxide synthase 1 (Nos1) in lateral part of the medial mammillary nucleus, and the spatial distribution of these markers was validated at RNA level. Additionally, we showed that Tac2 neurons possess functional potentials related to ionotropic signaling, synaptic transmission, and plasticity, and Nos1 neurons were inferred to the most cell interactions in the MB. Marker genes that showed partial discriminative power in human and pig data, together with heterogeneity comparisons within cell types between human and pig, revealed both potential cross-species heterogeneity and convergence in the MB. To sum up, our cross-species single-nucleus transcriptomics presents a cell-specific gene expression portrait of the mammalian MB across different species, which may have implications for neural signaling and disease vulnerability.

Introduction

Mammillary body (MB), a subcortical structure located on the ventral surface of the posterior inferior hypothalamus, constitutes an integral node within the Papez circuit and the limbic system (Barbizet, 1963; Papez, 1995). Through dense connections, it integrates information within the hippocampal-fornix-anterior thalamic system (Petrof & Sherman, 2009), playing a central role in spatial navigation (Lei et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Waye et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025), episodic memory (Tsivilis et al., 2008), and emotional regulation (Dillingham et al., 2015; Vann & Nelson, 2015). Anatomically, the MB can be subdivided into three distinct nuclei across species: the lateral mammillary nucleus (LM) and two components of the medial mammillary nucleus (MM), the medial (MM-M) and lateral (MM-L) parts (Vann, 2010; Vann & Aggleton, 2004). However, constrained by traditional research paradigms, early studies on MB function and mechanism had always regarded it as a single functional unit. Damage to the MB can lead to cognitive deficits (Kahn & Crosby, 1972; Papez, 1995), and its volumetric atrophy is significantly associated with cognitive decline in the Alzheimer's disease (Huang et al., 2023; Thierry et al., 2020), mood disorders (Bernstein et al., 2012; Nowak et al., 2023), and schizophrenia (Briess et al., 1998; Brisch et al., 2008; Milczarek et al., 2023). Considering the importance of the MB, a few studies have examined its cellular composition and transcriptomic features, leaving questions about whether these features are conserved across species.

In recent years, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) technologies have provided a new perspective for dissecting the cellular composition of brain regions (García-Marín et al., 2024). This has greatly facilitated analysis of molecular characteristics across multiple brain regions, including the hippocampus (Ayhan et al., 2021; Hilla et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), prefrontal cortex (Chu et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2018), claustrum (Lei et al., 2025), and thalamus (Wang et al., 2024). The latest breakthrough in cross-species snRNA-seq studies has further provided novel insights into the evolutionary conservation of brain regions. Existing studies have confirmed that cell types and molecular pathways in the hippocampus, neocortex, and cerebellum are highly conserved across mammals (Yu et al., 2023), which provides strong support for the application of rodent models in human neurological disease research. Although snRNA-seq studies of the mammillary body (MB) have recently been reported (Li et al., 2025; Mickelsen et al., 2020), the similarities and differences in the cellular composition of the MB at the single-nucleus level across species have yet to be systematically analyzed.

These studies have resolved the cellular composition and spatial organization of the mouse MB. Nevertheless, how MB cellular components are conserved (or diversified) across species at single-nucleus resolution remains unexplored. To address this gap, we performed cross-species single-nucleus transcriptomic profiling of the MB in three species: mouse, human, and domestic pig. Pigs were included as a representative large mammal with brain size and neuroanatomical features comparable to those of humans. By constructing a high-resolution single-nucleus atlas, we systematically characterized the cellular composition of MB, cell interactions and key functional gene signatures among neuronal subsets in these species. Then by scoring human and pig cell subsets with biomarkers identified in mouse, and comparing heterogeneity between pig and human, we discovered the consistent transcriptional features across species, providing insights into shared cell characteristics of MB.

Results

A single-nucleus spectrum of mammalian mammillary body

Distinguished by both their locations and the enriched types of glutamatergic projection neurons, the mammillary body (MB) comprises the medial (MM) and lateral (LM) mammillary nuclei, with the MM further subdivided into medial (MM-M) and lateral (MM-L) components. To elucidate the structural variations of the MB across species, we employed immunofluorescence staining using parvalbumin (PV) and calbindin-D28k (Calb1), two calcium-binding proteins that label distinct neuronal populations (Küçükerden et al., 2022), to delineate the anatomical compartments of the MB in a range of mammalian species, including mouse, rat, pig, monkey, and human (Fig. 1(A)). In the MB of mice and rats, the MM-M, MM-L, and LM, distributed medial to lateral, were clearly delineated in coronal sections by distinct PV and Calb1 expression patterns. Notably, while PV completely occupied the MM-M in rats, its expression was restricted to the dorsal portion of the MM-M in mice, with the ventral MM-M exhibiting predominant Calb1 expression. In pigs, unlike the singular and unified MM-M observed in rodents, the MM-M is divided into two bilaterally symmetrical subnuclei connected by a slender isthmus. In monkeys, the MM-M subnuclei show progressive segregation, retaining only a tenuous connection, whereas in humans, this vestigial linkage becomes indiscernible through unilateral MB immunostaining analysis. Nevertheless, the distribution patterns of PV and Calb1 in pigs and monkeys remain consistent with those in rodents, showing predominant PV expression in the LM and more prominent Calb1 expression in the MM-L, whereas PV and Calb1 expression in the MM-M is not unified. In summary, the immunostaining results across species reveal a certain degree of evolutionary conservation alongside interspecies heterogeneity. More specifically, the structural complexity of the MB tends to increase with brain size. To further investigate the single-nucleus landscape of the mammalian mammillary body, pooled MB nuclei from 35 mice (processed as a single sample using the sCelluFlu-A platform and sequenced on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7) and MB nuclei from 2 pigs and 3 humans (processed using the MobiCube/MobiNova platform and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000) were sequenced in our previous study (Li et al., 2025) and in this study (Fig. 1(B)). After stringent quality control, 6 685, 41 522, and 27 775 high-quality nuclei were retained from mouse, human, and pig, respectively, and subjected to unsupervised clustering (Fig. 1(C), Fig. S1A–B). Based on the expression patterns of well-known (i. e., SNAP25 for neurons) and newly identified marker genes, cells were classified into seven to nine major cell types, covering glial cell populations, neuronal cell populations, mural cell populations as well as very few immune cells (Fig. 1(C–D), Fig. S1C–E, and Table S1–S3). Among them, neurons constitute the majority (Fig. S1F–H). Our data provides a valuable resource for in-depth cross-species studies on both the spatial and transcriptomic characterization of mammal MB.

The transcriptomic and spatial characterization of neurons in mouse mammillary body

To further explore the neuronal heterogeneity within MB, we comprehensively dissected the neuronal populations from the mice. Based on the expression patterns of reprimo (Rprm) and tachykinin 2 (Tac2), we distinguished the MM and LM neurons (Fig. 2(A–B), and Table S4). In contrast to the adjacent neurons outside the MB, neurons within MB exhibited higher excitability, as indicated by the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) enrichment analysis on the upregulated genes of MB neurons compared to other neurons (Fig. 2(C), Fig. S2A, and Table S5). Consistent with a previous report (Huang et al., 2023), mammillary body neurons of LM, MM-L and MM-M exhibited higher evoked spiking frequencies upon injection of a rectangular depolarizing current pulse compared to neurons of the adjacent SuM (Supramammillary nucleus) (Fig. 2(D)). Focusing on the MB neurons of mice, the five subtypes were robustly delineated by the specific expression of Tac2, PV, dopamine receptor D2 (Drd2), neurotensin (Nts), and nitric oxide synthase 1 (Nos1) (Fig. 2(E–F), Fig. S2B–C, and Table S6). To assign these transcriptomic subtypes to their corresponding anatomical subregions within the mammillary body, we performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and compared the spatial distribution of each marker with the mouse brain atlas. PV was predominantly distributed in the dorsal MM-M and LM; Drd2 was localized to the basal side of MM-M; Nts was symmetrically distributed on both sides of MM-M; and Nos1 was symmetrically distributed in MM-L. This approach directly linked each neuronal subtype to its respective subregion. Given the known distribution of Calb1 protein in the mouse MB, we examined Calb1 expression across the five neuronal subtypes. The expression level was relatively higher in c1_Drd2 and c2_Nos1, moderate in c3_Nts, and lower in c4_PV and c5_Tac2 (Fig. S2D–E), consistent with the patterns observed in Figs. 1(A) and 2(G). By evaluating the transcription factors specifically activated in each MB neuronal subtype, we demonstrated distinct transcriptional regulations within different neuronal subtypes (Fig. 2(H) and Fig. S2G). For example, c5_Tac2 highly activated and expressed the transcription factors involved in cell development and differentiation (Patz1, Runx2, Pou2f1, Tcf7l1, and Tcf7l2), lipid metabolism (Pparg and Esrrg), and stress response (Bach1, Bach2, and Nfib). c2_Nos1 highly activated stress response factors (Jun, Rela, Elk4, Atf1, and E2f6), cell development and differentiation factors (Etv3, Zfp639, and Nfyb). In c1_Drd2, the highly activated are mainly related to neuronal development and differentiation, such as Klf7, Ikzf2, and Rfx1.

Next, we tried reclustering the c2_Nos1 cluster (MM-L), resulting in four subclusters (Fig. S2H). Labeling the marker genes Tenm3 and Tnc from clusters 0 and 3, we observed the partition of Tenm3+ and Tnc+ cells in the Nos1+ (MM-L) region, with Tenm3+ cells localized near the MM-M and Tnc+ cells near the LM region (Fig. 2(I) and Fig. S2I–J). Altogether, our single-nucleus data combined with our fluorescence staining indicate the distinct transcriptional and distribution characteristics of MB neurons in mouse.

Ligand-receptor pairs characterization revealing potential neural and paracrine regulations in mouse mammillary body

To infer the neural signaling reflected by the expression of neurotransmitters and their receptors in mouse MB, we first examined the expression of the two major excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters (glutamate and GABA) receptor genes to study the signal reception in each of the MB cell subtypes. Glutamatergic receptors are categorized into two types: ionotropic (iGluRs) and metabotropic (mGluRs), each playing distinct roles in the nervous system. iGluRs are primarily divided into 3 types: AMPA receptors, NMDA receptors, and kainate receptors and they mediate excitatory current signals through rapid ion channel opening, while mGluRs comprise 8 types, mGlu1-8, and regulate cellular activities via G protein signaling cascades (Reiner et al., 2015). GABA, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, is mainly mediated by two types of receptors, ionotropic GABAA and metabotropic GABAB. These receptors regulate the activity of neurons within the brain.

We observed substantial transcriptional heterogeneities in both glutamate and GABA receptors across these subtypes, with the largest differences between LM and other medial regions (Fig. 3(A–D) and Fig. S3A–B). In general, higher levels of ionotropic glutamate and lower levels of metabotropic glutamate receptor genes signature scores were observed for LM (c5_Tac2) neurons than other region neurons (Fig. 3(A)). The c2_Nos1 and the c4_PV neurons exhibit higher expression of GABAA and GABAB genes respectively, whereas the expression of both receptor genes in the LM region is comparatively lower (Fig. 3(B)). Additionally, different receptor genes from the same pathway also exhibited varying expression patterns (Fig. 3(C–D)). For example, Gria1 and Gria4 were specifically and widely expressed in LM neurons which encodes GluA1 and 4 subunits which plays an important role in maintaining synaptic and cognitive functions (Diering & Huganir, 2018). In MM, the specific high expression of Grin3a in c1_Drd2 cluster suggests the encoding of NMDA receptor subunit and its role in delaying synapse maturation and pruning non-used synapses, as well as its association with addiction, neurodegenerative diseases, and other major brain disorders (Pérez-Otaño et al., 2016).Fig. 3Ligand-receptor pairs characterization revealing potential neural and paracrine regulations in mouse mammillary body.(A) Signature scores of ionotropic and metabotropic glutamate receptor genes in mouse MB neuronal subtypes. Similarly, genes related to glutamate production and release also showed obvious heterogeneities between different subtypes. Neurons in LM region (c5_Tac2) expressed the highest level of glutamate production-related gene signals (Fig. 3(E), top panel), while c2_Nos1 and c3_Nts neurons exhibited higher levels of glutamate release-related gene signatures (Fig. 3(E), bottom panel) than neurons of other subtypes. It was observed that Gls, highly expressed in c5_Tac2, and Slc1a2 highly expressed in c2_Nos1 and c3_Nts neurons (Fig. 3(F)). Specifically, Slc1a2 encodes EAAT2, which plays a dominant role in the rapid clearance of glutamate from the nervous system, preventing potential neurotoxicity caused by excessive glutamate (Gan et al., 2024; Kato et al., 2022).

We subsequently inferred the cell-cell interactions to gain insights into the paracrine regulations among these MB neuronal clusters. We observed that different regions exhibited varying strengths as signaling sources and targets (Fig. 3(G) and Fig. S3C). Neurons in the MM-L region (c2_Nos1) had predicted strong outgoing interactions with neurons in all other MB regions but weak incoming interactions, suggesting their potential role as predicted senders in MB. In contrast, neurons in the LM region (c5_Tac2) exhibited strong incoming interaction strength with neurons in all other regions, computationally suggesting their role in receiving signals within the MB network. Additionally, the signaling maintained from c4_PV to c3_Nts and from c1_Drd2 to c3_Nts were also predicted based on their incoming and outgoing information flows and also supported by their adjacent anatomical positions. Surprisingly, signals from MM-M regions (c4_PV, c3_Nts and c1_Drd2) to LM region (c5_Tac2) were also predicted, discordant with their neuronal locations in the MB, raising the possibility of the additional synaptic communications through other brain regions. Specifically, the significant receptor-ligand (L-R) interactions observed between different regions highlighted their potential functional significance (Fig. 3(H) and Fig. S3D–F). For instance, c2_Nos1 and c4_PV showed expression of Slit2-Robo1/2 L-R pairs, which is known to play a critical role in axon guidance and neuronal migration (Brose et al., 1999; Wong et al., 2001). This suggests that MM-L might be involved in regulating neuronal positioning and network formation. Meanwhile, c3_Nts, located at the center of MM-M, and c5_Tac2, located at LM, as targets for the Nrg1/2/3-Erbb4 signaling axis, are likely associated with synaptic plasticity and neural development, given that the Nrg-Erbb pathway is crucial for synapse formation, neuron differentiation, and the maintenance of neuronal function (Mei & Nave, 2014). Together, these results specifically highlight the region-specificity of neural signal transmission and neuronal interaction within the MB.

The transcriptional heterogeneity of neurons in human mammillary body

Next, we aimed to investigate the characterization of human MB by clustering and gene signals analyses. Considering that excitatory neurons dominated MB, we first extracted MB neurons from all detected neurons of samples from 3 individuals based on their inhibitory or excitatory gene signals (Huang et al., 2023) (Fig. 4(A–B), and Fig. S4A–B). Subsequently, the neurons were grouped into two categories, MM and LM, based on a set of well-known markers (Fig. 4(C–D), Fig. S4C, and Table S7), such as FOXB1, ONECUT2, and MYO5B in MM and UNC5D, TAC3, and HTR2C in LM (Huang et al., 2023). The MM neurons were further subdivided into seven subclusters (Fig. 4(E)), presenting distinct upregulated genes (Fig. 4(F) and Table S8). Functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional characteristics across the MM subclusters (Fig. 4(G) and Table S9). MM0 were enriched in terms related to axonogenesis and regulation of neuron projection development, and MM1 was primarily enriched in terms related to cellular respiration and oxidative phosphorylation. Meanwhile, both MM3 and MM4 were enriched in small GTPase-mediated signaling and actin filament organization. Additionally, MM2, MM5 and MM6 showed enrichments in synapse organization and regulation of trans-synaptic signaling. To explore potential similarities between the neuronal subtypes in mouse MM and those in human MM, we scored human MM subclusters with the top 30 homologous upregulated genes from each mouse MM subtype and found higher mouse MM gene signatures in MM3, MM4 and MM5, but no clear one-to-one correspondence (Fig. 4(H)). Consistently, we detected the expression patterns of the top ten homologous gene markers of each mouse MM subtype across the seven human MM subclusters. However, we found no obvious corresponding relationships (Fig. S4D). Additionally, using the mouse data as a reference for cell annotation based on gene expression similarity of signature genes (Methods), we found the expression relationships among cell-type signature genes of the mouse MM subtypes did not effectively distinguish the human MM subclusters, resulting in most human MM subcluster cells being annotated as c3_Nts (Fig. S4E–F, and Fig. S4G–H). This may suggest that although certain cell-type markers are conserved across species, the inter-gene relationships within the same cell type are not necessarily conserved between species.

Ligand-receptor pairs characterization revealing potential neural and paracrine regulations in human mammillary body

To elucidate the neural regulatory mechanisms within human MB, we first characterized the transcriptional heterogeneity of excitatory (glutamate) and inhibitory (GABA) neurotransmitter systems (Fig. 5(A–D)). IGluR and mGluR gene scores revealed distinct expression patterns across MB subclusters (Fig. 5(A)). Consistent with Fig. 3(A), the LM region (c5_Tac2) exhibited higher iGluR scores and lower mGluR scores, suggesting potential differences in synaptic transmission and synaptic plasticity compared to MM neurons (Habrian et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2021; Reiner et al., 2015; Reiner & Levitz, 2018). The human MM5 subcluster exhibited relatively high gene scores for both GABAA and GABAB receptors. In contrast, the LM subcluster showed lower gene scores specifically for GABAB receptors, while their GABAA receptor scores remained comparable to other groups diverging from observations in mouse (Fig. 5(B)). At the gene level, LM highly expressed GRIA1, GRIA4, GRID1, and GRID2 (Fig. 5(C)). Among these, GRIA1 and GRIA4, which encode AMPA-type receptor subunits essential for synaptic plasticity and cognitive function (Diering & Huganir, 2018; Li et al., 2023), along with GRID1, which encodes the GluD1 receptor crucial for sensory, cognitive, and motor processing (Ung et al., 2024) were all upregulated in mouse LM (Fig. 3(C)). MM2 showed high expression of GRIK2 and GRM1 (Fig. 5(C)), respectively encode the ionotropic (iGluR) and metabotropic (mGluR) glutamate receptors, similar to their upregulation in mouse MM-L region (c2_Nos1). Further analysis of genes involved in glutamate production and release (Fig. 5(D–E)) revealed interspecies differences: human LM exhibited lower glutamate production activity compared to mouse LM, while MM6 displayed higher glutamate production scores. Meanwhile, MM4 showed the highest glutamate release activity, resembling the mouse MM-L region (c2_Nos1) (Fig. 5(D)). At the gene level, human MM4 highly expressed SLC1A2 and SLC1A3 that showed distinct expression patterns in mouse subtypes, with SLC1A2 enriched in MM-L region (c2_Nos1) and SLC1A3 in LM c5_Tac2. SLC1A2 and SLC1A3 encode EAAT2 and EAAT1, respectively (Fig. 5(E)). Meanwhile, human MM6 exhibited high expression of SLC38A1, a gene that was broadly expressed across multiple mouse subtypes including c5_Tac2, c4_PV, and c2_Nos1 (Fig. 5(E)). The aforementioned results demonstrate both predicted conserved and divergent functional and transcriptional characteristics in human and mouse MB subtypes.

Cell-cell communication analysis inferred heterogeneous interaction strengths among MB subregions, suggesting neuron-specific paracrine regulatory networks. LM engaged in fewer interactions than MM subregions, with MM0, MM1, and MM3 predominantly functioning as signal sources, while MM4 acted primarily as a target (Fig. 5(F) and Fig. S5). Two ligand-receptor pairs identified in both human and mouse MB data were SLIT2-ROBO1/2 and NRG1/2/3/4-ERBB4. SLIT2-ROBO1/2 signaling axis critical for axon guidance and neuronal migration (Brose et al., 1999; Wong et al., 2001) originates from MM0, MM1, and MM3 and targets most subclusters except MM4 and MM6. While the NRG-ERBB4 signaling pathway, associated with synaptic plasticity and neural development (Mei & Nave, 2014), sourced from MM3, MM4 and LM subclusters, exhibits pan-subcluster targeting (Fig. 5(G)).

Aligning pig MB neurons with mouse MB neurons, we scored the pig subclusters based on the expression of the top 30 homologous marker genes for mouse MM subtypes (c1_Drd2, c2_Nos1, c3_Nts, c4_PV). We observed that pig subclusters 3, 6, 2, and 4 exhibited higher scores of the mouse subtypes c1_Drd2, c2_Nos1, c3_Nts, and c4_PV, respectively (Fig. 6(G)), in line with the gene expression patterns (Fig. S6D). In addition, a part of the cells in cluster 2 were computationally annotated as c3_Nts, while some cells in subclusters 3 and 6 were computationally annotated as c2_Nos1 (Fig. S6E). This partial alignment points to potential shared signature genes among a minority of cell subclusters between pig and mouse. However, the broader gene expression relationships within the same cell type are generally not preserved across species, though direct cross-species comparisons are limited by the small sample size (n = 2 pigs) and platform differences.

To systematically evaluate the cellular heterogeneity between human and pig MB by an integration approach (Fig. S6H, Methods), we observed that most cell types from different species overlapped (Fig. 6(H)), with oligodendrocytes, microglia and neurons presenting the highest discrepancy, in line with the cell type clustering analysis (Fig. S6I). These results suggested greater transcriptomic differences across species in these three cell types than in others.

Next, we investigated the inter-cellular transcriptomic heterogeneity in each cell type in human and pig MB, respectively. The ratio of Global Unshifted Entropy (ROGUE) statistic was calculated to quantify the purity of each neuronal cell type. Indicated by the lowest ROGUE values, neurons presented the least purity, consistent with the most complex functionality of neurons compared to other cell types. Additionally, a comparison between species showed that neuronal cell types in humans contained in general higher inter-cellular heterogeneity than that in pigs (Fig. 6(I–J) for all cells, and Fig. S6J for 500 sampled cells).

Cellular heterogeneity of mammillary body neurons in cognitive memory functions

The role of the MB in cognitive and memory functions has long been recognized (Sziklas & Petrides, 1998); however, the variation of different MB regions in these functions remains unclear. Previous studies have identified neuronal-specific transcriptional changes after fear learning in mice, ending up with a set of genes associated with remote memory (Chen et al., 2020). We found that in our data, these genes exhibited different expression patterns across various mouse MB subtypes (Fig. 7(A) and Fig. S7A). For example, high expression of Gpm6a, which is associated with synaptic transmission and plasticity, and transmembrane transport (Chen et al., 2020), was observed for c1_Drd2 subtype, suggesting a potential involvement of the c1_Drd2 subtype in neurotransmitter transmission. Additionally, Pls3 is associated with dendritic spine density, and its higher expression in the c4_PV subtype may relate to enhanced neuronal signal reception (Chen et al., 2020). The c5_Tac2 subtype showed high expression of Nsf, Stx1b, Syt13, and Rab15, which are associated with vesicle exocytosis (Chen et al., 2020). In addition to the differential functions of mouse MB subtypes in cognitive and memory processes, we examined the signatures of genes related to cognitive function identified in a previous study (Hu et al., 2021) in each human MB subcluster. We observed that the human MM4 subcluster exhibited a high signature of midbrain development, cellular catabolic processes, and the neurotransmitter release cycle (Fig. 7(B) and Fig. S7B). Additionally, MM4 also showed high expression of FOXO3, EXOC4 and ARHGAP15, which were reported with relevance to cognitive level (de la Fuente et al., 2021) (Fig. S7C). These observations are consistent with the results shown in Fig. 4(G), highlighting the enrichments of GTPase-mediated signal transduction, regulation of neuron projection development, and axonogenesis-related genes in MM4 neurons, and thus highlight MM4 as a candidate subpopulation for further functional studies on human cognitive function.

Discussion

Previous studies have confirmed that as a key node in the Papez circuit, the MB serves as a core support for mediating memory encoding and emotional regulation and is also involved in neurodegenerative diseases (Vann & Aggleton, 2004). This study establishes a cross-species single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of the MB in mouse, human, and pig, resolving its cellular components, spatial locations and their transcriptional regulations related to functional relevance of different neuronal subsets. At a descriptive level, the cross-species molecular atlas generated in this study reveals the complexity of the MB across species with both neuronal subtypes’ heterogeneity and shared functional potentials. This observation could help explain the functional complexity (heterogeneity versus conservation) of MB in the Papez circuit across mammals. However, owing to the small sample sizes and technical platform differences, these observations remain preliminary and require further validation in larger cohorts with standardized protocols.

Notably, we refined the classification of MB neurons based on their transcriptional heterogeneities and validated subtype-specific spatial distributions via protein levels, and ultimately classified five neuronal subtypes in either LM, MM-M, or MM-L. Additionally, we identified transcription factors with specific regulations on each neuronal subset, largely extending our understanding on cellular characterization of mammalian MB neurons. Lower expression levels of both GABAA and GABAB receptors were observed in the LM (c5_Tac2) neurons relative to those in the MM and MM-L subregions, a transcriptomic pattern that may contribute to distinctive characteristics in inhibitory synaptic transmission and plasticity in LM neurons (Habrian et al., 2023; Hansen et al., 2021). Additionally, a higher level of glutamate production-related gene signals raises the possibility that these neurons may be involved in maintaining the viability of senescent cells associated with neurodegenerative diseases (Johmura et al., 2021; McDuff & Sumi, 1985). These observations collectively provide a cellular and molecular foundation for understanding the functional organization and connectivity of the MB within broader limbic and hypothalamic circuits, rather than characterizing MB neurons in isolation, as the MB serves critical roles in spatial memory, emotional regulation, and limbic circuit integration—core functions that are tightly linked to its connectivity with the hippocampus, thalamus, and hypothalamus (Nitzan & Buzsáki, 2024).

Our investigations on neurotransmitters and their receptors imply distinct functional potentials in neuronal regulations. For instance, the high expression of ionotropic glutamate receptors and low expression of metabotropic glutamate receptors of LM neurons (c5_Tac2) suggests stronger ionotropic regulations than metabolic regulations on this subset. Meanwhile, the low expression of genes related to glutamate release is consistent with the possibility that LM neurons may preferentially act as signal-receiving rather than signal-releasing neurons. This neurotransmitter expression profile is further reflected in our cell-cell interaction analysis, which infers that LM neurons (c5_Tac2) may predominantly serve as signal receivers in paracrine regulation. In contrast, MM-L neurons (c2_Nos1) may serve as the upstream paracrine regulators in mouse MB. Therefore, interactions between MM neurons and LM neurons were also proposed. Interestingly, while four distinct neuronal subtypes were identified within the MM-L neurons (c2_Nos1), only two were consistently observed in standard coronal sections of the MB. This discrepancy likely arises from spatial distribution biases along the anterior-posterior (A-P) axis, suggesting that the remaining two subtypes may be localized outside the sampled regions—either more rostrally or caudally. These results imply an anatomical connection between MM-M and LM through MM-L or other regions at three-dimension (3D) level. Future investigations employing serial sectioning across multiple anatomical planes (coronal, sagittal, and horizontal) or advanced 3D imaging modalities would provide a more comprehensive understanding of MB subregional organization.

Our data suggest that the mammalian MB displays both shared and species-specific characteristics across the examined species. This dual pattern of cross-species conservation and diversification is not unique to the MB, but has also been observed in several hypothalamic, thalamic, and other diencephalic structures, which maintain conserved core architectures while acquiring species-specific specializations—findings that have been supported by studies on mammalian hypothalamic development and transcriptional profiles (Chen et al., 2025; Xie & Dorsky, 2017). At the structural level, we observed preservation of the fundamental organization into LM and MM regions across the three species, with these two principal subdivisions maintaining their distinct identities. This conserved architecture was mirrored at the cellular level, where the main cell types within MB are conserved. Moreover, human and mouse LM region shared similar transcriptional profiles for iGluRs and mGluRs. Their ligand-receptor interaction patterns showed overlap, particularly pathways mediating synaptic modulation and neuronal excitability. However, this preliminary evidence of divergence also emerged when attempting to annotate human and pig MM subclusters based on mouse references, confirming the presence of species-specific molecular distinctions. Notably, one human sample exhibited distinct neuronal transcriptomic patterns compared to the other two replicates. This difference could be attributed to inter-individual variation suggesting a larger sample size in future studies. Additionally, compared to pigs, humans exhibit a higher diversity of neural cell types, which may be linked to the more complex functional structure of the human brain. Across mouse, pig, and human, the relative sizes and proportions of the lateral and medial mammillary subregions differ notably, as also observed in other mammalian brain regions (Równiak & Bogus-Nowakowska, 2020). Whether these differences reflect species-specific scaling or functional specialization is unclear. Future studies combining single-nucleus sequencing with behavioral assays are needed to explore this possibility.

An intriguing observation of this study is that small but distinct inhibitory clusters can be identified in both pig and human MB, whereas there is no GABAergic interneuron in mouse MB. This pattern raises the speculative possibility that local inhibitory microcircuits within the MB may be more prominent in larger-brained species, potentially supporting more complex temporal coding or feedback regulation. The lack of such interneurons in rodents implies that inhibitory modulation of MB output in mice relies entirely on extrinsic inputs, whereas in primates and artiodactyls, intrinsic inhibition may contribute to refined information processing within the Papez circuit. However, this cross-species difference in cell populations cannot exclude the contribution of several technical factors: dissection precision (gross visual dissection for human and pig MB), insufficient sample sizes and different sequencing platforms. Formal evolutionary interpretations are not warranted at this stage and require validation with larger cohorts and consistent methodologies across species.

A limitation of this study includes that whereas we obtained both cellular transcriptional profiles and spatial validation for mouse, only transcriptional profiles were available for pig and human and different snRNA-seq platforms were employed, which limited direct comparisons between mouse and pig/human. Additionally, the human samples were limited to three male donors (aged 62, 77, and 89), and donor effects were observed in human data largely within LM subpopulation (Fig. S4A). Therefore, the reported human subclusters may be influenced by donor-specific effects, and the exclusive use of elderly male donors limits the generalizability of our human MB atlas. Future studies with larger, age-matched, and sex-balanced cohorts are needed to validate these findings.

We performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing and profiled the MB in different mammals by characterizing their cellular composition and functional signatures, and validated the spatial localization of several neuronal subsets in mouse. Our data revealed potential species divergence and convergence, as well as relevance between MB genes and diseases at the transcriptional level.

Methods

Animal

The C57 mice, rats, and brains of monkey and pig were purchased from GENEPAX BIOTECH (NANJING) CO., LTD, China. All experiments were done in accordance with guidelines from the National Natural Science Foundation of China and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the animal core facility at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China ([2023] IACUC Number: 2753). Only male animals were included to control for sex-based variability.

Human brains

Postmortem human brain tissues (three males with 89, 62, and 77 years old) were obtained from the department of Anatomy, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No.: 2024-S124) and performed in accordance with the principle of the Helsinki Declaration II. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq)

The snRNA-seq data of mouse MB were derived from our previous study (Li et al., 2025), and have been deposited in the SRA database (accession number: SRR24902043). In brief, brain sections containing the mammillary body were cut using a vibratome. Under a stereomicroscope, the mammillary bodies were dissected based on anatomical landmarks defined in the mouse brain atlas. Pooled MB nuclei from 35 mice were processed as a single sample using the sCelluFlu-A platform and sequenced on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 at approximately 20 000 reads per cell.

For pig and human samples, the mammillary bodies from 2 male pigs and 3 male humans (aged 62, 77, and 89) were dissected based on gross visual inspection, with well-defined anatomical borders guided by the appropriate brain atlases (Atlas of the Human Brain 4th Edition, and (Félix et al., 1999)). For nuclei capture and cDNA synthesis using the 3′ Library and MobiCube v2.0 kit, the nuclei suspension (700-1 200 nuclei per microliter, as determined by Count Star) was loaded onto the MobiNova (MOBIDROP) to generate single-nuclei gel beads in emulsion according to the manufacturer's protocol. In short, single nuclei were suspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA. Approximately 20 000 nuclei were added to each channel, and the estimated number of recovered nuclei was about 10 000. Captured nuclei were lysed, and the released RNA were barcoded during reverse transcription within individual GEMs. Reverse transcription was performed on a S1000TM Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) at 53 °C for 45 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 min, and then held at 4 °C. The cDNA was generated and then amplified, and the quality was assessed using an Agilent 4200 (performed by CapitalBio Technology, Beijing). Single-nucleus RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Single Cell 3′ Library and MobiCube v2.0 kit. The libraries were finally sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer with a sequencing depth of at least 40 000 reads per cell, using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy (performed by CapitalBio Technology, Beijing). The MB snRNA-seq data of human and pigs were deposited in the GSA database (accession number: CRA029730 and HRA013195 respectively).

Immunofluorescence and fluorescence in situ hybridization

Mice and rats were sacrificed via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium. Subsequently, they were transcardially perfused with 100 mL PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were harvested and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. All brain sections (30 μm) were prepared using a Leica microtome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Human, monkey, and pig brains were not perfused but were fixed and dehydrated in the same manner before being sliced. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on free-floating brain sections according to established protocols. Briefly, 30 μm coronal sections were blocked in 3% normal donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated at 4 °C for 24 h in primary antibodies diluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer containing 3% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies used were mouse anti-parvalbumin (PV; 1:1000, Swant PV235) and mouse anti-D28k (Calb1; 1:500, Proteintech, 14479-1-AP). Following washes, sections were incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, sections were rinsed, air-dried, and coverslipped using fluorescence mounting medium (Invitrogen, P36961).

For fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), brain sections (20 μm) were made using a cryostat tissue slicer and mounted on glass slides. Slides were subsequently stored at −80 °C until use. RNA probes for PV (421931-C3), Drd2 (406501-C4), Nts (420441-C1), Nos1 (437651-C2), Tenm3 (411951-C1), and Tnc (465021-C3) as well as all other reagents for FISH, were ordered from Advanced Cell Diagnostics (ACD, Hayward, CA). As described below, retrieval, pretreatment, hybridization, amplification, and detection were performed according to User Manual for Fixed Frozen Tissue (ACD). Sections were incubated in DAPI for 30 s, and slides were coverslipped with fluorescent mounting medium.

Electrophysiology in vitro

Voltage-clamp recordings were performed using a potassium-based intracellular solution. Mammillary nucleus slices (300 μm) were prepared from 2-month-old male mice and maintained in a holding chamber containing oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; composition in mM: 124 NaCl, 3 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 1.2 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4·2H2O, 10 C6H12O6, 2 CaCl2; pH 7.4, 305 mOsm) at 32 °C for 30 min. Individual slices (300 μm) were made and then transferred to a recording chamber under continuous perfusion with oxygenated ACSF (2 mL/min). Intracellular and whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were obtained from visualized neurons using an Axioskop 2FS upright microscope equipped with infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) optics and a Hamamatsu C2400-07E infrared camera. Recordings included neurons from 3 mice per experimental group, with 4-5 cells sampled per mouse. Signals were low-pass filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 10-20 kHz using a Digidata 1440A interface (Molecular Devices). Series resistance (RS) was continuously monitored throughout recordings; data were excluded from analysis if RS changed by >20%.

Intrinsic physiological properties of neurons in MM-M, MM-L, LM, and SuM (Supramammillary nucleus) neurons were assessed using sharp microelectrodes. Resting membrane potential (RMP) was measured within 2 min of membrane rupture. Steady-state potentials were maintained at either −65 mV or −90 mV via intracellular current injection to evaluate tonic firing properties. Repetitive spiking was characterized in tonic and burst modes using 500 ms depolarizing current steps (50-300 pA increments, 50 pA steps).

snRNA-seq data preprocessing

Raw sequencing reads were processed using Cell Ranger (v9.0) with default parameters for alignment (GRCh38 reference genome for human, GRCm38 for mouse, Sscrofa11.1 for pig), unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting, and feature-barcode matrix generation. Initial quality control and downstream analyses were performed in Seurat (v.5.1.0) (Hao et al., 2024). Cells were filtered for three species data, respectively, based on the following criteria: 1) cells with low detected genes were removed (200 for mice, 1000 for human and 200 for pig), as human samples exhibited substantially higher gene detection numbers than mouse and pig samples. 2) human cells expressing of mitochondrial genes >5%, as mitochondrial genes were largely undetectable in mouse samples (75% of cells exhibiting percentages below 0.122%) and completely undetectable in pig samples. 3) potential doublets, expressing marker genes of different cell types, were removed. Specifically, we removed cells with counts >3 for at least one gene in each of any two of the following marker gene sets simultaneously: endothelial lineage (VWF), neuronal lineage (SNAP25, SLC17A6, GAD1), immune lineage (PTPRC, CD14, CD3E), astrocytic lineage (GFAP), oligodendrocyte lineage (MBP, OLIG1, OLIG2, PDGFRA), mural/perivascular lineage (ABCC9, COL23A1); cells co-expressing both myeloid lineage (CD14) and T cell lineage (CD3E) marker genes with counts >3 were also excluded. Gene expression matrices were log-normalized, and 2 000 highly variable genes were selected and scaled. Then, harmony (v1.2.0) (Korsunsky et al., 2019) was applied on human data for integration using the top 30 principal components to regress donor effects. Cell clusters were identified based on established K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) graph with Louvain algorithm. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Marker-based cell type annotation

Cell clusters were annotated based on the expression patterns of common marker genes. Canonical markers for mouse cell types or clusters are summarized below: Endothelial cells (Vwf), Microglia (Ptprc), Tanycyte (Dnah6), Mural (Abcc9), Neuron (Snap25), OPC (Olig1), Astrocyte (Gja1), Oligo (Mbp), LM (Tac2), MM (Rprm). Key markers for human cell types or clusters are summarized below: Endo/Fib (VWF, DCN), Microglia (PTPRC, CD14), T cell (PTPRC, CD3E), Neuron (SNAP25), OPC (OLIG2), Astrocyte (GJA1), Oligo (MBP), Excitatory neurons (SLC17A6), Inhibitory neurons (GAD1), LM (HTR2C), MM(FOXB1). Key markers for pig cell types or clusters are summarized below: Microglia (PTPRC), Astrocyte (GFAP), T cell (PTPRC, CD3E), Neuron (SNAP25), OPC and Oligo (OLIG1), Endothelial cells (VWF), OPC (PDGFRA), Mural1 (COL23A1), Mural2 (FN1), Excitatory neurons (SLC17A6), Inhibitory neurons (GAD1), LM (HTR2C), MM (FOXB1).

Based on these marker annotations, microglia (PTPRC), neurons (SNAP25), endothelial cells (VWF), and astrocytes (GJA1) were consistently detected across all three species, whereas oligodendrocytes and OPCs were also detected across species but showed partially shared or species-specific marker usage (Table S12).

Differential gene expression analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes across cell types and subclusters, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed. Differentially expressed genes were defined as those with an absolute log-fold change ≥ 0.25, expressed in at least 25% of cells in either cluster, and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). Differentially expressed genes across cell types and subclusters were identified after preprocessing and clustering of the single-nucleus data. Analysis was performed using Seurat's FindAllMarkers function, which employs a Wilcoxon rank-sum test as the default method.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) terms enriched in cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1) (Wu et al., 2021). Enriched terms were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Gene regulatory network inference

Transcriptional regulatory networks were reconstructed using SCENIC (v1.3.1) (Aibar et al., 2017). To identify transcription factor (TF)-driven regulatory modules. Co-expression modules were first inferred from cluster-specific marker genes, followed by motif enrichment analysis against the cisTarget database to link TFs to their target genes. Regulon activity scores were quantified per cell using the AUCell algorithm, which evaluates the enrichment of regulon member genes in individual transcriptional profiles.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Intercellular signaling networks were inferred using CellChat (v1.6.1) (Jin et al., 2021) based on ligand-receptor co-expression across clusters. The CellChatDB provided curated ligand-receptor pairs. Communication probabilities were calculated by integrating ligand expression in predicted sender cells with receptor expression in predicted receiver cells, weighted by pathway-specific interaction potentials. Statistical significance was assessed through permutation testing (FDR < 0.05).

To validate the CellChat results, we retrieved section C57BL6J-638850.32 from the Allen Brain Cell Atlas (Yao et al., 2023; https://brain-map.org/) dataset “MERFISH-C57BL6J-638850 with Imputed Genes + Reconstructed Coordinates” and subsequently exported the gene expression distribution maps for the MB region.

Cross-species medial mammillary nucleus subtype annotation

MM subtype scores for human and pig MM subclusters were calculated using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. Gene signatures were derived from the top 30 upregulated genes in mouse MM subclusters with the largest fold changes. Meanwhile, human and pig MM subclusters were annotated using SingleR (v2.4.1) (Aran et al., 2019) with mouse MM single-nucleus data as a reference. We tested marker sets comprising the top 10, 30 and 50 upregulated genes from each mouse MM subcluster as the SingleR reference feature sets, and found that the top 30-gene signatures produced the most robust and parsimonious annotations. To ensure accurate cross-species annotation, homologous genes were identified using Ensembl BioMart. Mouse genes were queried from the Ensembl gene dataset, and one-to-one orthologous gene mappings between mouse–human and mouse–pig were extracted by retaining only entries annotated as “Unique results only” in BioMart. Then, mouse reference labels were mapped to human/pig cells by matching conserved gene symbols (e.g., Cd44 → CD44) based on direct nomenclature homology, excluding genes with ambiguous or species-specific names (Table S13).

To verify whether the SingleR results were influenced by differences in cell number and sequencing depth, we performed subsampling validation. Specifically, 1 500 cells were randomly sampled per species, downsampled to 3000 UMIs, and the analysis was repeated three times. Pseudobulk gene expression was computed for each donor within each species using AverageExpression, and cosine similarity was calculated between pseudobulk profiles across replicates and donors (Table S14). Taking the mouse–human analysis as an example, for each replicate we first used the top 30 marker genes of mouse neuronal subclusters (c1_Drd2, c2_Nos1, c3_Nts, c4_PV) to annotate human cells (forward), then used the top 30 upregulated genes of human subclusters (MM0-MM6) to annotate mouse neuronal cells (reverse). Annotation confidence was reported using SingleR's delta.next metric. The mouse-pig analysis was performed analogously.

Cell type purity assessment

The transcriptional purity of annotated neuronal cell types was quantified by ROGUE package (Liu et al., 2020). An entropy-based metric for assessing the purity of single cell populations using the ROGUE statistic, an entropy-based metric measuring population homogeneity. Expression matrices were pre-filtered to exclude low-abundance genes. ROGUE values were computed for each human and pig cell type using the CalculateRogue function, which evaluates the uniformity of gene expression distributions across cells. To ensure robustness of the results against varying cell numbers and depth sequencing, we analyzed the ROGUE results by imposing an upper limit of 500 cells per cell type per sample, and then assessed between-group differences using t-test.

Hierarchical clustering of cross-species cell types

To evaluate transcriptional similarity between human and porcine cell types, a hierarchical clustering tree was constructed using the BuildClusterTree function in Seurat. And the resulting dendrogram was visualized with PlotClusterTree in Seurat.
