Assessment of MR‐Induced Heating Effects in Four Common Veterinary Implants at 1.5 and 3.0 T: A Phantom Study
Authors: Manabu Kurihara, Taichi Kimura, Kenji Yodo, Shuhei Shibukawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQuantitative evaluation of implant‐related heating in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is well standardized in human medicine, whereas evidence in veterinary medicine remains scarce. To address this gap, we measured radiofrequency (RF)‐induced heating of four used veterinary implants under controlled conditions on clinical 3.0 and 1.5 T MRI, using an American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) F2182‐19e2‐informed gel phantom manufactured to approximate a 10 kg dog. The test set comprised an identification microchip, a titanium alloy tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) plate, a stainless‐steel TPLO plate, and an ameroid constrictor. Temperature–time data were acquired with fiber‐optic probes, and temperature change was calculated relative to baseline for each replicate run. Maximum temperature rise and heating rate were derived at the run level, with heating rate estimated from the slope of the linear regression of ΔT over time. At 3.0 T, the mean maximum ΔT was 0.300°C for the titanium alloy TPLO plate, 0.300°C for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, 0.467°C for the ameroid constrictor, and 0.500°C for the microchip. At 1.5 T, the corresponding values were 0.067°C, 0.067°C, 0.100°C, and 0.200°C. All individual run‐level maximum ΔT values remained ≤0.8°C. Heating rates were small in absolute magnitude, with the microchip ranking highest across field strengths. These findings indicate that, when appropriate imaging parameters are used, MRI‐induced heating for the implants examined is unlikely to pose a clinical risk. Future work should broaden device coverage, evaluate off‐center placements, vary B 1 conditions, test higher load sequences, and extend assessment to other safety endpoints to refine veterinary‐specific MRI safety guidance.Keywords: ameroid constrictor, microchip, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radiofrequency (RF)‐induced implant heating, tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO)‐plate

Introduction

Patients with metallic implants undergoing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) face four main risks: (1) translational forces arising from spatial gradients of the static field (dB/dx) and torques that tend to align ferromagnetic objects with B 0; (2) Lenz forces and related torques generated when conductive objects move within B 0; (3) malfunction of active devices such as pacemakers in strong electromagnetic fields, with potential life‐threatening consequences; and (4) localized heating and tissue injury from currents induced by radiofrequency (RF) fields or, for bulky metal, by rapidly switched gradients [1]. Implant heating in MRI arises from two mechanisms: RF electromagnetic fields predominantly heat peri‐implant tissues through induced electric fields. Rapidly switched gradient fields induce eddy currents within the implant, and the resulting heat is then conducted into adjacent tissues. In clinical practice, RF‐induced heating is generally the dominant hazard; its magnitude depends strongly on implant geometry (length, loops, and sharp tips), orientation relative to B 0/B 1, and position within the transmit coil [1]. The biological effects of RF fields are commonly categorized as thermal and nonthermal; nonthermal effects in clinical MRI are considered minimal. Thermal mechanisms, primarily resistive (ohmic) and dielectric heating, predominate [2, 3]. Sequences that keep the RF transmission on for a long fraction of the repetition time and use high flip angles, such as turbo/fast spin echo (TSE/FSE), generally deposit more RF power and tend to produce greater RF heating, although variable‐flip‐angle refocusing and specific absorption rate (SAR) management can mitigate this. In contrast, sequences with very rapid gradient switching and a high duty cycle, such as echo‐planar imaging (EPI), are most relevant for gradient‐driven heating [1]. Because metallic or otherwise conductive components can undergo RF‐induced heating, MRI safety in patients with implanted medical devices remains a key concern [4, 5]. International guidelines and standards, including ICNIRP exposure limits and the ASTM (American Society for Testing and Materials) F2182 bench test, provide a framework for risk assessment, although implant‐specific evidence in veterinary practice remains limited.

In human medicine, several international standards organizations and regulators have published methods and standards to quantify implant‐related heating in the MRI environment [1, 6]. These guidelines have enabled standardized MR safety labeling for clinical implants, thereby allowing clinicians to make informed decisions about MRI eligibility and protocol selection. Among the most influential bodies are ASTM International, the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). These standards address related but distinct aspects of MRI safety: ASTM F2182 provides a bench‐test method for passive implant heating, IEC 60601‐2‐33 defines MRI system safety requirements and operating limits, and ISO/TS 10974 provides a broader risk‐assessment framework, particularly for active implantable medical devices. ASTM F2182 is one of the most widely adopted and recognized testing standards for assessing RF‐induced heating of passive implants in cylindrical magnets at 1.5 and 3.0 T [7]. The method uses a tissue‐mimicking saline gel phantom whose electrical and thermal properties approximate those of human tissue. The implant is placed in the phantom and exposed to continuous RF such that the whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR is maintained at 2.0 W/kg for about 15 min. Temperature rise in and around the device is then measured, typically with fiber‐optic temperature probes. Although details can vary by body region and test scenario, F2182 functions as the reference standard for RF‐heating assessment and underpins the MR conditional labeling used by many manufacturers, playing a central role in clinical MRI safety evaluations. IEC 60601‐2‐33 is a comprehensive standard addressing multiple aspects of MRI safety [8]. An important advance for RF exposure management is the introduction of B 1 +rms as an alternative primary metric alongside SAR. B 1 +rms represents the root‐mean‐square amplitude of the effective transmit RF magnetic field (B 1 +) over the RF pulse train of an MRI sequence and is reported in microtesla (µT). B 1 + refers to the circularly polarized component of the transmit RF magnetic field that contributes to spin excitation. Because B 1 +rms is determined by the RF pulse amplitude and duty cycle required to achieve a prescribed flip angle, it can be computed directly from transmitter voltages, making it practical and comparable across scanners. In principle, B 1 +rms is largely independent of B 0; by contrast, SAR scales approximately with (B 1 +)2 and RF frequency and is more system‐ and subject‐dependent [9]. ISO/TS 10974 provides a tiered framework (Tiers 1–4) for evaluating RF‐ and gradient‐induced effects, particularly for active implants [10]. The upper tiers include coupled‐field analysis and co‐modeling of the patient and device, followed by verification using ISO‐specified bench and phantom tests. ASTM F2182 is used primarily for passive implants and is not a mandatory step within ISO/TS 10974, although it is often used for complementary validation.

In contrast to human medicine, the evaluation of MRI‐related heating in veterinary medicine remains extremely limited. Several publications have examined susceptibility artifacts from veterinary implants [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. A case of RF‐induced thermal burn injury has also been reported in a dog after MRI, despite scanner‐reported SAR remaining within safety limits; in that case, two bottles of warm water wrapped in a blanket had been placed next to the abdomen [18]. A few studies have assessed microchip function after MRI [19, 20], microchip MR safety [21], and histopathologic tissue responses in dogs [22]. Nevertheless, the MRI safety profiles of these devices, particularly regarding RF‐induced heating, remain largely unknown. Moreover, existing international testing standards were developed primarily for human implants, human anatomy, and human MRI use. Veterinary patients vary widely in body size, body conformation, and implant‐to‐body‐size ratio, ranging from cats and small dogs to large‐breed dogs. Therefore, heating behavior measured in a phantom approximating a 10 kg dog may not fully represent other clinically relevant scenarios, such as cats or large dogs, in which coil loading, implant coupling, and heat dissipation may differ. Differences in body size, anatomical configuration, implant placement, and tissue properties between humans and animals may alter heating behavior, so direct application of human‐based safety data to veterinary patients may be misleading or inadequate. To our knowledge, no veterinary‐specific guideline for implant heating assessment currently exists, and published studies are scarce, leaving clinicians with limited guidance when MRI is indicated in patients with implanted devices.

The present study addresses this gap by systematically evaluating RF‐induced heating of commonly used veterinary implants, including microchips, titanium alloy tibial plateau leveling osteotomy (TPLO) plates, stainless‐steel TPLO plates, and ameroid constrictors, under controlled experimental conditions. Our aim is to generate veterinary‐relevant data to inform MRI safety guidelines and support clinical decision‐making, thereby enhancing patient safety in veterinary imaging.

Materials and Methods

Selection and Description of Subjects

All experiments and measurements were performed by a single MRI technologist (M.K.) with more than 10 years of experience in MRI acquisition. The technologist was not blinded to implant identity, and probe placement was not independently verified by a second observer. An ASTM F2182‐19e2‐informed gel phantom (BioView Inc., Tokyo, Japan) designed to approximate the body size of a 10 kg dog was used. According to the manufacturer, the gel density was 1.0113 × 103 kg/m3; based on this density and the nominal 10 kg body‐size approximation, the estimated gel volume was approximately 0.00989 m3, equivalent to 9.89 L (9888 cm3). The gel consisted of polyacrylic acid, sodium chloride, and deionized water, and its electrical conductivity was adjusted to 0.47 S/m ±10% at the test temperature, as specified in ASTM F2182 [7].

Each implant was positioned within the phantom at the magnet isocenter and fully immersed in the gel. To avoid regions of elevated electric field near the boundaries, ample clearance from all phantom walls was maintained. All four implants were positioned with a minimum pairwise separation of 5 cm. Implant orientation was standardized by aligning the long axis parallel to the bore axis (B 0, z‐axis), which kept the orientation relative to the transmit B 1 and electric‐field distribution constant and minimized variability in RF coupling (Figure 1A). Temperature was measured with fiber‐optic probes (H201, Rugged Monitoring, Quebec City, Canada; accuracy ±0.1°C) placed on the implant surface at the anticipated hotspot (Figure 1B). Anticipated hotspot locations were selected a priori on the basis of implant geometry and general principles of RF‐induced heating, rather than pilot scans or electromagnetic simulations. For the TPLO plates, the probe was positioned on the central portion of the proximal plate head, near the clustered screw holes and adjacent plate edge, where local electric‐field concentration and RF coupling were expected to be greatest. For the ameroid constrictor, the probe was positioned on the central ring surface, taking into account the inner aperture and case discontinuity where induced current concentration was considered likely because of the closed‐loop geometry. For the microchip, the probe was positioned on the central portion of the microchip body, corresponding to the internal coil and ferrite region (Figure 1C). Probes were secured with nonmetallic anchors to prevent movement during scanning. Before each RF exposure, the phantom was allowed to passively stabilize, and the baseline temperature was recorded immediately before scanning. No predefined numerical equilibration criterion was used.

Data Analysis and Recording

MRI experiments were performed on two clinical scanners: a 3.0 T system (Ingenia Elition X 7700, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) and a 1.5 T system (Vantage Gracian, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan), each using the integrated body transmit coil in Normal Operating Mode. RF exposure followed an ASTM F2182‐19e2‐informed protocol; each experiment consisted of 15 min of continuous scanning with a target whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR of 2.0 W/kg, and the achieved SAR was recorded from the scanner console. Imaging parameters were selected from standard T2‐weighted TSE/FSE sequence settings available on each scanner and modified as needed for the 15‐min heating experiment. Transverse T2‐weighted images were acquired on both systems using spin‐echo‐based TSE/FSE sequences (Figure 2A,B): at 3.0 T: TR 5931 ms; TE 100 ms; ETL 25; echo spacing not available; flip angle 90° (refocusing flip angle not available); FOV 220 mm; matrix 368 × 250 (reconstructed 480 × 480); in‐plane resolution 0.46 × 0.46 mm2; slice thickness 10 mm with 1 mm gap; about 10 slices; averages 8; pixel bandwidth 343 Hz/pixel; acquisition time 960.8 s; B 1 +rms 2.33 µT. At 1.5 T: TR 4416 ms; TE 97.5 ms; ETL 25; echo spacing not available; flip angle 90° (refocusing flip angle not available); FOV 220 mm; matrix 352 × 352 (reconstructed 704 × 704); in‐plane resolution 0.3125 × 0.3125 mm2; slice thickness 10 mm with 1 mm gap; about 10 slices; echo number 1; averages 16; pixel bandwidth 326 Hz/pixel; acquisition time 994 s; B 1 +rms 3.71 µT. The relatively high number of averages was selected to maintain continuous scanning and sustained RF exposure during the 15‐min heating experiment and to approximate an ASTM‐style high‐RF‐burden test condition. These settings were not intended to represent a routine clinical imaging protocol. Temperature was sampled every 5 s during RF exposure, and values were expressed as increases from each implant's baseline at time zero to enable direct comparison of heating behavior. Following RF exposure, measurements were halted, and the phantom was allowed to cool passively to the pre‐exposure baseline temperature before the next run. Each implant type was tested in triplicate on both scanners. For each run, the maximum temperature during exposure (T_max) and the baseline temperature (T_0) were recorded; the maximum temperature rise was then calculated as ΔT = T_max − T_0.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2024) by a single investigator (S.S.). Analyses were performed separately for the 3.0 and 1.5 T datasets. Temperature change (ΔT) at each time point was calculated relative to the baseline temperature at time zero for each replicate run. Heating rate was first estimated for each individual replicate run by fitting a simple linear regression of ΔT against time, and the slope and R 2 value were recorded to assess the adequacy of the linear approximation. Run‐level slopes and maximum ΔT values were then summarized by implant material. To compare heating rates among implant materials while accounting for repeated measurements within replicate runs, linear mixed‐effects models were fitted with ΔT as the outcome and time, implant material, and their interaction as fixed effects; replicate run was included as a random effect within each implant material. Pairwise comparisons of material‐specific time slopes were performed using estimated marginal trends with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons. Maximum ΔT values were compared among materials using Kruskal–Wallis tests; however, because only three replicate runs were available per material, these comparisons were considered exploratory. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, but p values were interpreted in conjunction with absolute ΔT values, run‐level variability, R 2 values, and the stated accuracy of the fiber‐optic temperature probes.

Results

On the 3.0 T system, the console‐reported whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR during the 15‐min heating run was 3.21 W/kg; on the 1.5 T system, it was 1.23 W/kg.

Run‐level linear regression showed that temperature–time relationships were approximately linear for some implant materials, although fit adequacy varied among materials and replicate runs (Table 2). At 3.0 T (Figure 3A), the mean run‐level heating slope was highest for the microchip (4.91 × 10−4°C/s; 95% CI, 3.03–6.79 × 10−4), followed by the ameroid constrictor (4.34 × 10−4°C/s; 95% CI, −3.62 to 12.30 × 10−4), stainless‐steel TPLO plate (3.05 × 10−4°C/s; 95% CI, −2.33 to 8.43 × 10−4), and titanium TPLO plate (2.15 × 10−4°C/s; 95% CI, −8.25 to 12.54 × 10−4). Mean R 2 values were 0.921 for the microchip, 0.831 for the ameroid constrictor, 0.699 for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, and 0.352 for the titanium TPLO plate. At 1.5 T (Figure 3B), the microchip again showed the highest mean run‐level heating slope (2.21 × 10−4°C/s; 95% CI, −0.87 to 5.29 × 10−4). The other implants showed minimal or inconsistent slopes: 0.24 × 10−4°C/s for the ameroid constrictor (95% CI, −2.14 to 2.62 × 10−4), −0.74 × 10−4°C/s for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate (95% CI, −1.46 to −0.02 × 10−4), and −0.14 × 10−4°C/s for the titanium TPLO plate (95% CI, −1.63 to 1.35 × 10−4). The small negative slopes observed in some replicate runs were interpreted as measurement fluctuation or baseline drift rather than true cooling. Mean R 2 values were 0.662 for the microchip, 0.193 for the ameroid constrictor, 0.221 for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, and 0.053 for the titanium TPLO plate.

At 3.0 T, the mean maximum ΔT was 0.300°C for the titanium TPLO plate, 0.300°C for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, 0.467°C for the ameroid constrictor, and 0.500°C for the microchip (Table 2). At 1.5 T, the mean maximum ΔT was 0.067°C for the titanium TPLO plate, 0.067°C for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, 0.100°C for the ameroid constrictor, and 0.200°C for the microchip, with corresponding ranges of 0.000–0.100°C, 0.000–0.100°C, 0.000–0.200°C, and 0.100–0.300°C, respectively.

Linear mixed‐effects models, with replicate runs included as a random effect, detected material‐specific differences in temperature–time slopes at both field strengths. However, these statistical differences were interpreted cautiously because the absolute slope magnitudes and mean maximum ΔT values were small, and run‐level variability was present. Kruskal–Wallis tests comparing maximum ΔT among materials were exploratory because only three replicate runs were available per material and showed no statistically significant differences at either 3.0 T (χ 2 = 1.782, p = 0.619) or 1.5 T (χ 2 = 2.065, p = 0.559).

Discussion

In this study, run‐level analysis showed that RF‐induced temperature increases around the tested veterinary implants were small under the tested phantom conditions. At 3.0 T, the microchip showed the most consistent positive heating slope and the highest mean maximum ΔT, whereas the ameroid constrictor showed a similar mean maximum ΔT but greater run‐level variability. Stainless‐steel and titanium TPLO plates showed lower and less consistent heating slopes. At 1.5 T, the microchip again showed the highest mean maximum ΔT, whereas the other implants showed minimal or inconsistent slopes, including small negative values in some replicate runs. These negative slopes were interpreted as measurement fluctuation or baseline drift rather than true cooling. Although mixed‐effects modeling detected material‐specific differences in temperature–time slopes, these statistical differences should not be interpreted as clinically meaningful heating because the absolute slope magnitudes and mean maximum ΔT values were small, and several measurements were within or close to the stated accuracy of the fiber‐optic temperature probes (±0.1°C). Prior work on animal identification microchips reported no safety risk at 1.0 T under their test conditions [21]. Histopathologically, microchips were well encapsulated by granulation tissue; in dogs exposed to 1.5 T MRI, only minor cellular degeneration and debris were noted and judged clinically insignificant [22]. Moreover, under clinical conditions, tissue perfusion and conductive heat removal are expected to further reduce in vivo temperature rise relative to our phantom setting [23]. Because the observed temperature rises were small relative to clinically relevant thermal injury thresholds, these findings suggest that the risk of thermal injury for the tested implants under typical imaging conditions is very low.

SAR quantifies RF power deposition per unit tissue mass (W/kg). On clinical scanners, SAR is an estimated value derived from the selected protocol and the system's safety model; because the underlying algorithms are vendor‐specific, absolute numbers can differ across platforms. Accordingly, direct numerical comparison between our 1.5 and 3.0 T systems (different manufacturers) should be interpreted cautiously. Electrodynamically, SAR is proportional to tissue electrical conductivity and the square of the local electric field, so it tends to be higher in high‐water‐content tissues and near conductive implants, and lower in bone or fat. It also depends strongly on body size and geometry, with peripheral deposition often accentuated [1, 24]. For a given subject and sequence, SAR increases approximately with the square of the main field (∝B 0 2 via RF frequency), with the square of the flip angle (∝α 2), and with RF duty cycle; therefore, long‐duty, large‐flip‐angle TSE/FSE protocols tend to maximize RF heating [1, 5]. For implant testing, ASTM F2182 specifies test conditions and scaling methods; in common practice, experiments are performed for about 15 min at a whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR of 2.0 W/kg, with results reported or scaled accordingly [7]. In the present study, however, the achieved SAR differed between scanners, as described below. Per IEC 60601‐2‐33, the first‐level controlled operating mode permits a whole‐body‐averaged SAR of up to 4.0 W/kg, averaged over 6 min [8, 25]. This upper limit is often taken to correspond to a maximum whole‐body temperature rise on the order of about 1°C [8, 26]. Notably, local RF burns have been reported at scanner‐reported SAR values well below regulatory limits, likely due to factors such as skin‐to‐skin conductive loops, breed‐ or patient‐specific skin morphology, and the inability of an anesthetized subject to signal discomfort [18]. Differences in vendors and software versions mean safety models and sequence‐dependent calculation methods can vary, so the SAR values shown on the console may not match. Therefore, scanner‐reported SAR values should not be directly compared across systems without caution [27]. Consistent with recent guidance, reporting and managing B 1 +rms alongside SAR is increasingly recommended, as B 1 +rms is obtained directly from flip‐angle calibration and facilitates comparison across systems [8].

Although mixed‐effects modeling detected material‐specific differences in temperature–time slopes, the absolute magnitude of heating remained small. Mean maximum ΔT values were ≤0.5°C for all implant materials, and all individual run‐level maximum ΔT values remained ≤0.8°C. These temperature rises are far below commonly referenced thermal dose benchmarks based on cumulative equivalent minutes at 43°C (CEM43), which consider both absolute tissue temperature and exposure duration [28]. Nevertheless, under high SAR conditions (e.g., TSE/FSE with long echo trains and 3D TSE/FLAIR sequences such as SPACE, VISTA, or CUBE), prolonged or repeated scanning can increase heating, underscoring the importance of pre‐scan safety assessment [1, 5, 23, 29]. From a clinical perspective, these findings support continued MRI safety screening and protocol review rather than automatic exclusion of patients with the tested implants. Greater caution may be warranted when prolonged or repeated high‐RF‐burden sequences are planned, particularly long echo‐train TSE/FSE, 3D TSE or FLAIR‐type sequences, and protocols using additional RF pulses such as spectral fat saturation [1, 5, 23, 26]. Diffusion‐weighted or EPI should also be considered during protocol review because high gradient duty cycles may contribute to implant‐related heating, although this mechanism differs from RF‐induced heating [1, 26]. Small patients, patients with a high implant‐to‐body‐size ratio, brachycephalic or obese patients with abundant subcutaneous fat near the implant, and patients with superficially located implants such as subcutaneous microchips may warrant additional caution because local heat dissipation may be less favorable and RF‐related burns can occur despite scanner‐reported SAR remaining within regulatory limits [18, 26, 30, 31]. Therefore, microchip location should be considered during MRI planning, particularly when the microchip is located within or near the imaging region or transmit coil and when high‐RF‐burden sequences are required.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior systematic reports evaluating RF‐induced heating of veterinary implants such as TPLO plates and ameroid constrictors. Although in practice small metallic objects (<2 cm) are often considered unlikely to pose RF‐heating problems during clinical MRI [32, 33, 34], actual heating depends continuously on conductor length, geometry (loops, sharp edges, and tips), configuration and orientation, and position within the transmit coil. Accordingly, no universal size threshold is defined in international standards (IEC 60601‐2‐33; ISO/TS 10974) [8, 10]. Contrary to this common assumption, the microchip showed the most consistent positive heating slope and the highest mean maximum ΔT, whereas the larger TPLO plates showed lower and less consistent heating slopes. This finding likely reflects the internal antenna coil and ferrite rod within the microchip, which can enhance RF coupling despite the small external device size, whereas the larger TPLO plates do not form a compact coil‐like structure. The ameroid constrictor also showed a comparable mean maximum ΔT and greater run‐level variability, likely reflecting its closed‐loop geometry and potential for induced circulating currents. The interdevice differences in ΔT observed in this study can be explained primarily by variations in electromagnetic coupling, geometry, and electrical continuity; heat diffusion and heat capacity related to implant location; and material properties. From an electromagnetic‐coupling standpoint, electric fields tend to concentrate at the device tip, and capacitive coupling at the metal–tissue interface can further intensify local displacement currents; accordingly, even without resonance, focal heating may occur in tissue adjacent to the tip. In addition, prior work indicates that an internal coil or ferrite structure and the device's orientation relative to B 1 can augment coupling [1, 10, 35]. By contrast, TPLO plates, owing to their multiple screw holes and curved geometry, are unlikely to behave as long continuous conductors or to form large conductive loops; circulating currents are, therefore, dispersed, and focal hotspots are less likely to develop. The ameroid constrictor, by virtue of its closed‐loop geometry, readily supports induced circulating currents that can concentrate at constrictions or contact points and promote local temperature rise. In our experiments, the finding that a small‐caliber ring produced a ΔT comparable to the microchip suggests that coupling was enhanced by device orientation and the local B 1 field distribution [1, 36, 37]. Clinically, microchips are typically placed in subcutaneous fat, where RF electric fields are often relatively stronger near the surface, and adipose tissue has low perfusion and low thermal conductivity, so heat removal is limited and local temperature rise can be amplified [30, 31]. In contrast, TPLO plates lie along periosteal and muscular planes, where higher perfusion and higher thermal conductivity help transport and dissipate heat more efficiently, so the temperature rise can be reduced under the same RF exposure [30]. Importantly, tissue temperature is determined not by SAR alone but by the interplay of RF power deposition with heat removal through perfusion and thermal conduction. The titanium‐alloy TPLO plate showed lower and less consistent heating slopes than the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, consistent with the lower electrical conductivity of titanium alloys relative to stainless steel, which may diminish RF‐induced currents. However, the mean maximum ΔT values for titanium and stainless‐steel TPLO plates were similar in this study. Titanium alloys also have lower magnetic susceptibility than stainless steel, a property generally associated with reduced susceptibility‐related image distortion; however, artifact size was not quantitatively evaluated in the present study [1, 5, 17, 38].

This study has several limitations. First, a 3.0 T scanner (Philips Healthcare, Ingenia Elition X 7700, Best, the Netherlands) and a 1.5 T scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Vantage Gracian, Otawara, Japan) were used, and differences in body transmit coil design and B 1 field distribution between vendors may have influenced heating behavior. On the 1.5 T system, we could not achieve the ASTM target whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR of 2.0 W/kg in Normal Operating Mode; the maximum attainable value was 1.23 W/kg. This reflected scanner‐side SAR management and sequence constraints under Normal Operating Mode, despite adjustment of sequence parameters to increase RF exposure. Accordingly, the 1.5 T heating measurements likely underestimate worst‐case RF heating and should be interpreted as conservative. This SAR discrepancy also limits direct comparison of ΔT values and heating‐rate slopes between the 1.5 and 3.0 T systems, because the lower temperature rise observed at 1.5 T may reflect both lower field strength and lower RF power deposition. Assuming an approximately linear relationship between scanner‐reported SAR and temperature rise, the 1.5 T mean maximum ΔT values would be expected to increase by a factor of approximately 1.63 if normalized to 2.0 W/kg. Even with this simple scaling, the estimated mean maximum ΔT values would remain small: 0.109°C for the titanium TPLO plate, 0.109°C for the stainless‐steel TPLO plate, 0.163°C for the ameroid constrictor, and 0.326°C for the microchip. However, these scaled values should be interpreted cautiously as approximate estimates rather than precise corrected measurements, because SAR estimates are vendor‐specific and do not fully reflect local B 1 and electric‐field distributions around implants. Second, the phantom was designed to approximate the body size of a 10 kg dog rather than the standard ASTM F2182 human torso phantom. Exact external phantom dimensions were not available, and differences in phantom geometry and gel volume may have influenced RF coupling and whole‐phantom‐averaged SAR estimation. In addition, all four implants were tested simultaneously within the same phantom rather than individually, which differs from the standard ASTM F2182 approach. Although a minimum pairwise separation of 5 cm was maintained, this configuration may have altered the local electric‐field distribution or introduced inter‐implant coupling. Therefore, the measured ΔT values should be interpreted as specific to the tested configuration. Third, all implants were positioned at the isocenter; off‐center placement along the z‐ or x‐axis can introduce B 1 and electric‐field inhomogeneity, potentially increasing local heating near coil edges or regions of electric‐field concentration. Implant orientation was standardized to reduce experimental variability, and the effect of different orientations relative to the transmit B 1 field was not evaluated. Future studies should assess multiple clinically relevant implant orientations. Probe placement was also based on anticipated hotspot locations selected from implant geometry and general RF‐heating principles rather than systematic hotspot mapping or electromagnetic simulation; therefore, the measured ΔT values should be interpreted as representative of the tested configuration rather than as absolute worst‐case heating values. Fourth, only three replicate runs were available per implant material. Therefore, comparisons of maximum ΔT among materials were considered exploratory, and confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously. The adequacy of the linear approximation also varied among materials and replicate runs, as reflected by the R 2 values. A no‐RF control run was not performed; therefore, slow baseline drift during the 15‐min measurement period could not be quantified. This is particularly relevant to the smallest 1.5 T mean maximum ΔT values, which should be interpreted as no clinically meaningful measurable heating rather than definitive RF‐induced temperature increases. Finally, only a limited selection of implants was evaluated, and the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to the wide variety of commercially available veterinary devices. TPLO plates vary in thickness, screw‐hole configuration, curvature, and alloy composition; microchips differ in dimensions, internal conductive component design, and encapsulation materials; and ameroid constrictors may vary in ring diameter, case material, and structural configuration. Because these device‐specific features can influence RF coupling and local heating behavior, the results should be interpreted as specific to the tested implants rather than generalizable to all products within each implant category. The study was also performed under phantom conditions, without the cooling effects of tissue perfusion that would be present in vivo; conversely, in clinical practice, prolonged scanning or repeated high‐SAR sequences may increase heating risk. Other safety endpoints, including image artifact [39], magnetically induced torque [40], and magnetically induced displacement force [41], were beyond the scope of this study and warrant evaluation in future work.

In conclusion, this study provides systematic, empirical measurements of RF heating around common veterinary implants under ASTM‐style phantom conditions with a high RF burden at 3.0 and 1.5 T. The observed temperature rises were small in absolute terms, with mean maximum ΔT values peaking at about 0.5°C near the microchip at 3.0 T and remaining at 0.2°C or less at 1.5 T; all individual run‐level maximum ΔT values remained ≤0.8°C. Accordingly, when appropriate imaging parameters are used, the clinical risk of MRI‐induced heating for the implants examined appears to be very low. Future work that includes a broader range of devices, off‐center placements, varied B 1 conditions, and sequences with higher RF burden should further refine veterinary‐specific safety guidance.
