Disentangling by random pulses
Henryk Gzyl

Abstract
Here we propose an analytically tractable model of an entangled quantum state subject to random shocks and prove that disentanglement occurs asymptotically. The system consists of two identical, two level state systems, say two entangled qubits or two entangled two state molecules, and the heat bath consists of the occurrence of state switching shocks, which happen at a Poisson rate. The time dependence of such system can be explicitly obtained and the decay rate to disentanglement happens to be twice the frequency of the occurrence of shocks.



Keywords
: Entangled quantum systems subject to random pulses, Disentanglement produced by random shocks
Quantum systems in random environments.

Introduction and Preliminaries

If you type “disentanglement” in your favorite browser, you will be provided with links to almost any type of human endeavor. If you refine your search to “disentanglement in quantum mechanics”, you will find references to works in very many domains of application of quantum mechanics. It thus makes sense to recall the meaning of entanglement and what do we mean by disentanglement.

When dealing with a system composed of two subsystems, in the Schrödinger description one says that the state
\Psi
of the whole system is an entangled state when there do not exit two states
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
such that
\Psi=|Psi_{1}\otimes\Psi_{2}.

In this work, we are interested in entangled states in the Heisenberg formalism of quantum mechanics, where states correspond to density matrices. In this case, a state
\rho
of a composite system is an entangled state if it can not be written (factored) as
\rho=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},
where the
rho_{i}
denote states of the two sub-systems. When the state can be factored into two individual states, we say that the system is in a product, separable or disentangled state.

In this work, we shall consider two identical, non-interacting, two level sub-systems (qubits, say), in and initial state (density matrix) describing an entangled state, and immersed in a heat bath. The effect heat bath consists of a state switching perturbation, occurring at the arrival times of a Poisson process. We show that, under this perturbation, the system evolves into a pure state. Thus disentanglement will mean the evolution of the initial entangled state into a pure state.

One of the worries of quantum computer design is the presence of thermal nose destroying the entanglement necessary for the quantum codes, thus a simple model displaying this phenomenon might be of interest

The basic assumptions

The assumptions underlying the model are the following.

•

The two sub-systems do not interact. That is, each of them evolves according to the Hamiltonian in (
1.1

•

The heat bath affects each of them independently of the other. That is,the occurrence of the shock that each suffers are independent of each other (we may assume that they are equally distributed).

The second assumption is natural if we think of the subsystems as spatially separated. Thus even if they respond similarly to the heat bath, their responses are independent of each other.

The Blume-Clauser model for the time evolution of the density matrix

The starting point is the time evolution operator in the Schrödinger picture. As mentioned above, the random time evolution goes as follows: Let
U_{0}(t)=\exp(-it\bH/h)
describe the time evolution of the unperturbed system. We shall use
U(t)
to describe the time evolution operator of the perturbed system. Suppose that a random time
T_{1}
a random shock occurs. Because of continuity we have

U(T_{1}-)=lim_{t\,\uparrow\,T_{1}}U(t)=U_{0}(T_{1}).

(2.1)

Right after the (instantaneous) shock at
T_{1}
the time evolution operator of the random system is

U(T_{1})={\boldsymbol{V}}U(T_{1}-)={\boldsymbol{V}}U_{0}(T_{1}).

(2.2)

Next, during the interval
T_{1}\leq t<T_{2},
the system again evolves according to
U_{0},
that is

U(t)=U_{0}(t-T_{1})U(T_{1})=U_{0}(t-T_{1}){\boldsymbol{V}}U_{0}(T_{1}).

(2.3)

And so on. These comments can be summarized as

U(T)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}EvolutionOperator&NumberofShocks&Probability\\

(2.4)
