Real-World Treatment Patterns for Waldenstrom Macroglobulinemia: Implications for Treatment Sequencing
Authors: Seo Yoon Jang, Ja Min Byun, Sung-Soo Park, Youngju Kim, Jeongmin Park, Dong-Yeop Shin, Youngil Koh, Inho Kim, Chang Ki Min, Sung-Soo Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDue to the rarity of Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia (WM) and scarcity of direct comparative data, there remains uncertainty regarding the optimal therapeutic approach for treatment-naïve patients and subsequent treatment sequence. To bridge such knowledge gap and generate data on most appropriate treatment in relatively resource constraint setting, we conducted this study with 168 WM patients.MethodsDue to the diversity of treatment regimens, patients were categorized into four groups: cytotoxic regimen, rituximab-based regimen, proteasome inhibitor (PI)/immunomodulatory drug (IMiD)-based regimen, and bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi). Treatment patterns and efficacy outcomes were analyzed.ResultsThe overall response rate (ORR) between rituximab-based vs. PI/IMiD based regimens were similar (74.2% vs. 80.0%, P = 0.750), but rituximab-based regimens were associated with longer response (median progression free survival [PFS] 44.3 vs. 8.5 months, P < 0.001), while PI/IMiD based regimens were associated with faster response (median time to initial response 1.8 vs. 0.9 months, P = 0.040). Among rituximab-based regimens, bendamustine-rituximab was associated with better response (ORR, 83.3%) and significantly longer PFS compared to rituximab + cyclophosphamide-based regimen group (median PFS 61.3 months vs. 18.2 months, P < 0.001). Ultimately 56% underwent second line treatment. Prior treatment did not seem to affect subsequent BTKi efficacy.ConclusionOur findings underscore the importance of optimizing treatment strategies and offer real-world data to support informed decision-making in settings with constrained resources.

INTRODUCTION

Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia (WM) is a rare B cell lymphoma characterized by the presence of lymphoplasmacytic cells in the bone marrow and the overproduction of IgM.1 The clinical manifestations of WM are heterogeneous ranging from indolent to rapidly progressing. Increased IgM causes symptoms such as hyperviscosity, neuropathy, retinopathy, cryoglobulinemia, while the bone marrow infiltration of lymphoplasmacytic cells causes cytopenia.2

As WM tumor cells typically express CD20 and CD38, treatment options include both anti-myeloma drugs and anti-lymphoma drugs.34 For example, proteasome inhibitors (PI), which actions by inhibiting nuclear factor-kB pathway, are used in various combinations to induce rapid response.567 Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, also constitutes a main pillar of WM treatment and is often paired with alkylating agents.8910

More recently, with the success of iNNOVATE trial (ibrutinib)111213 and ASPEN trial (zanubrutinib),1415 bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi) made ways into WM treatment scene and have altered the course of the disease altogether. However, these drugs are not without faults. Due to their indefinite treatment duration which ultimately tantamount to exponential financial burden and increased adverse events, upfront BTKi treatment is not readily accessible in many parts of the world. The same situation applies to Korea, where ibrutinib frontline is available but not reimbursed and zanubrutinib is only available as second line treatment.

Due to the rarity of WM and the scarcity of direct comparative studies, there remains uncertainty regarding the optimal therapeutic approach for treatment-naïve patients and subsequent treatment sequence. To bridge such knowledge gap, we conducted this study with substantial number of 168 WM patients.

METHODS

Patients

This was a longitudinal cohort study conducted between January 2003 and January 2023 at Seoul National University Hospital and Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital. WM was diagnosed according to the IWWM (International Workshop on Waldenstrom Macroglobulinemia) criteria,16 and patients aged 18 years or older were retrospectively identified and included. Their medical records were reviewed and analyzed for demographics, baseline disease characteristics, details of treatment, treatment outcomes, and survival. When available, results from cytogenetic and mutational analyses for MYD88 and CXCR4 mutations were collected. The revised International Prognostic Scoring System for WM (rIPSS-WM) was used for risk stratification.17

Treatment and response evaluation

Due to the diversity of the treatment regimens, we categorized them into 4 treatment groups; cytotoxic regimen, rituximab-based regimen, PI/immunomodulatory drug (IMiD)-based regimen, and BTKi (Fig. 1). The specific regimens in each treatment groups are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Study enrollment.

PI = proteasome inhibitor, IMiD = immunomodulatory drug, BTKi = bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

The overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the sum of minor response (MR; ≥ 25% but < 50% reduction in serum monoclonal gammopathy level), partial response (PR; ≥ 50% but < 90% reduction), very good partial response (VGPR; ≥ 90% reduction or within normal range), and complete remission (CR; within normal range, negative immunofixation and absence of bone marrow and extramedullary disease). Major response rate (MRR) was defined as composite of PR, VGPR and CR.1819

Statistical analysis

Summaries of response rates were presented using descriptive statistics. Comparisons of response rates between groups were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Progression free survival (PFS; time from the start of treatment to disease progression or death from any cause), overall survival (OS; time from diagnosis to death from any cause), and time to next treatment (TTNT; time from the initiation of frontline treatment to the start of the subsequent treatment) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between frontline regimen and PFS. Variables that were clinically relevant were included in the multivariable model to adjust for potential confounders. A two-sided P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Time to response (time from the start of treatment to achieving a response of MR or better), treatment duration (time from the start of treatment to the end of treatment), and follow-up time (time from diagnosis to the last follow-up) were summarized using the median and range. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH IRB No. H-2306-021-1437). The IRB waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and its minimal risk to participants.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Among 179 consecutively identified patients (Fig. 1), 11 were excluded due to misdiagnosis or insufficient medical records, resulting in a final cohort of 168 patients (Table 1), of whom 34 (20.2%) were not treated. For the 134 patients undergoing at least 1 line of therapy, the median time from diagnosis to treatment was 0.7 month with wide range of 0–133.5 months. The most common reason for initiating treatment was cytopenia (110/134, 82.1%), followed by symptomatic or bulky adenopathy (5.2%), neuropathy (4.5%), and hyperviscosity (3.0%). Of the 47 patients with available MYD88 mutational data, 33 (70.2%) harbored the L265P mutation, while 14 (29.8%) were wildtype. CXCR4 mutational status was available in six patients, all of whom were wildtype.

Frontline treatment and outcomes

For frontline treatment, 54 patients received a cytotoxic regimen, 62 patients received a rituximab-based regimen, 15 patients received a PI/IMiD-based regimen, and 3 patients received a BTKi. The efficacy outcomes according to frontline treatment groups are shown in Table 2. Excluding BTKi group, which only included three patients, rituximab-based regimens demonstrated deeper response, with a VGPR/CR rate of 40.3%. However, in terms of response, rituximab-based regimens (MRR, 69.4%; ORR, 74.2%) and PI/IMiD-based regimens (MRR, 66.7%; ORR, 80.0%) were similar with no significant differences observed (MRR: P = 1.000; ORR: P = 0.750).

Interestingly, PI/IMiD-based regimens induced the most rapid responses with a median time to initial response of 0.9 months and time to best response of 3.2 months. However, this early response did not translate into durable disease control, as these regimens were associated with the shortest PFS among all treatment groups, with a median PFS of 8.5 months (Fig. 2).

In a multivariable cox regression analysis adjusting for age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, and rIPSS-WM risk group, frontline PI/IMiD-based regimens were associated with significantly inferior PFS compared to frontline rituximab-based regimens (hazard ratio [HR], 3.17; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.43–7.04; P = 0.005) (Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, cytotoxic regimens were also associated with significantly shorter PFS (HR, 3.55; 95% CI, 1.81–6.98; P < 0.001). The BTKi group showed no progression events during follow-up, precluding reliable HR estimation. Age > 65 years, ECOG performance status, and rIPSS-WM risk group were not independently associated with PFS.

The rituximab-based regimen group comprised 30 patients who received bendamustine-rituximab (BR), 24 patients who treated with rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen, and 8 patients treated with rituximab in combination with other agents (Supplementary Table 1). Due to the small sample size of the rituximab in combination with other agents group, comparisons were limited to BR and rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen. Baseline characteristics and efficacy outcomes for these two subgroups are presented in Supplementary Table 4. The response rates were higher in the BR group (VGPR/CR, 53.3%; MRR, 80.0%; ORR, 83.3%) compared to the rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen group (VGPR/CR, 37.5%; MRR, 62.5%; ORR, 70.8%), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.283, 0.223, and 0.333, respectively). However, BR was associated with significantly longer PFS compared to the rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen group (median PFS 61.3 months vs. 18.2 months, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

As second line treatments, PFS did not differ between the cytotoxic, rituximab-based, and PI/IMiD-based regimen groups, excluding the BTKi group (median PFS 10.6, 10.2, and 12.5 months, respectively, P = 0.922) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Among the 62 patients who received a rituximab-based regimen as frontline treatment, 4 (6.5%) were rechallenged with a rituximab-based regimen, 5 (8.1%) were subsequently treated with BTKi, and 35 (56.5%) did not receive subsequent treatment. Among the 34 patients who received up to three lines of treatment, 16 (47.1%) received cytotoxic regimen, while 8 (23.5%) received a rituximab-based regimen.

Since more patients underwent BTKi treatment as second line therapy and beyond, we analyzed all patients treated with BTKi as shown in Table 3. Twenty-two patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) WM were treated with BTKi. Of these, 12 patients had received at least two prior lines of treatment. In the R/R WM cohort, with a median follow-up of 19.6 months (range 0.5–32.7 months), MRR and ORR were 72.7% and 90.9%, respectively.

DISCUSSION

This study represents one of the largest real-world cohorts of patients with WM reported to date, with a comprehensive evaluation of treatment patterns, response kinetics, and survival data across frontline and subsequent lines of therapy. Our findings provide practical insights for guiding WM management in routine clinical practice, especially in regions with limited access to and/or reimbursement restrictions for novel agents.

Approximately 20% of patients in our cohort remained treatment-free during the study period, and their OS was comparable to that of treated patients. These findings underscore that immediate treatment is not universally necessary and supports a risk-adapted, observation-first approach for selected low risk, asymptomatic patients. Among patients who required systemic therapy, rituximab-based regimens yielded the highest CR rate, despite their relatively short and finite duration of administration. In our cohort, patients treated with rituximab-based regimens achieved CR in 25.8%, which was higher than those treated with PI/IMiD-based regimens (6.7%) and BTK inhibitors (0%). This suggests that, despite the theoretical advantage of continuous BTKi exposure, fixed-duration rituximab-based approaches may offer favorable early disease control, particularly in treatment-naïve settings.

Among the rituximab-based regimens, BR demonstrated the most favorable therapeutic profile. BR was associated with a shorter time to initial response (median 1.7 vs. 2.7 months), a higher MRR (80.0% vs. 62.5%), and a significantly longer PFS (61.3 vs. 18.2 months, P < 0.001) compared to rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen. The PFS of the BR group in our cohort aligns with the PFS seen in the BR group in the StiL NHL-1-2003 study (69.5 months).8 It should be noted, however, that StiL NHL-1-2003 was not WM-specific and included only a small subset of patients with WM (8%), thus its findings offer limited disease-specific conclusions. Our study reinforces these results within a larger, dedicated WM population and provides real-world evidence supporting BR as a preferred frontline regimen. The efficacy of BR was also confirmed by the FILO study, which reported an ORR of 97.0% at 18 months in a cohort of 69 WM patients treated with BR.20 Long-term follow-up of the same cohort demonstrated a five-year PFS of 66.6% and a five-year OS of 80.0% after a median follow-up of 76.1 months.21

It is also important to note that our study offers a direct head-to-head evaluation of BR and rituximab plus cyclophosphamide-based regimen (mainly rituximab-cyclophosphamide-dexamethasone [RCD]) within a uniform WM population. While RCD has traditionally been favored for its perceived tolerability, especially in older adults, our data suggest that BR offers greater efficacy without compromising its use in this population. Our findings align with those of Paludo et al.,22 who reported comparable efficacy and toxicity between BR and RCD, with BR showing a trend towards better PFS.

PI/IMiD-based regimens achieved the most rapid responses, with a median time to initial response of 0.9 months. While this may be valuable in scenarios requiring urgent disease control, these regimens were associated with shorter PFS and remain limited by potential toxicities such as peripheral neuropathy—commonly seen with bortezomib but not completely captured in our dataset due to the retrospective design. On the other hand, 56.5% of the patients who received rituximab-based frontline treatment did not require subsequent treatment, highlighting its potential as a durable option. This was further supported by the observation that rituximab-based regimens demonstrated a markedly longer median TTNT of 60.8 months compared with cytotoxic or PI/IMiD-based regimens, indicating that disease control was maintained for a prolonged period before additional treatment was required. These findings align with the StiL NHL7-2008 MAINTAIN trial, which showed BR induction followed by observation to be highly effective.23 The biologically complementary features of these agents support the rationale for combination approaches. Recent findings from the ECWM-2 trial position the bortezomib, ibrutinib, and rituximab triplet as a promising frontline option for WM, and its clinical potential is highly anticipated.24

The phase III ASPEN trial comparing zanubrutinib with ibrutinib included a subset of treatment-naïve patients (19 zanubrutinib, 18 ibrutinib).14 In this subgroup, the 18-month MRR was 74% with zanubrutinib and 67% with ibrutinib, while the ORR was 95% and 89%, respectively. These outcomes are comparable to the ORR 97% reported with BR in the FILO study, although long-term comparative data are still needed. BTKi, particularly ibrutinib, is associated with potentially serious adverse effects, including atrial fibrillation and hypertension. In the final analysis of the ASPEN trial, the incidence of any grade atrial fibrillation was 21.4% (grade ≥ 3, 6.1%) in the ibrutinib arm compared with 6.9% (grade ≥ 3, 2.0%) in the zanubrutinib arm.15 Differences in toxicity profiles, together with issues of treatment accessibility, support the use of BR as a reasonable frontline option in specific clinical contexts.

In the R/R setting, BTKi therapy produced favorable response rates and durable disease control, even in heavily pretreated patients. In the ASPEN15 trial, which included previously treated patients, the ORR at 18 months was 94% with zanubrutinib and 93% with ibrutinib—comparable to the 92% ORR observed in our cohort at a median follow-up of 20 months. Such findings suggest that sequential strategies starting with rituximab-based therapy and reserving BTKi for relapse may be particularly effective in practice settings where upfront BTKi is inaccessible. However, longer-term follow-up is needed to determine whether these favorable responses translate into sustained survival benefits.

The lack of mutation data is the most obvious pitfall of our study. MYD88 status was available in only 47 patients, and CXCR4 in 6, limiting genotype-response correlation. Although MYD88 mutated patients showed a trend of longer OS compared to wildtype (median OS 8.3 years vs. not estimable, P = 0.190; Supplementary Fig. 4), the difference did not reach statistical significance. Nonetheless, this limitation also reflects clinical reality where routine genomic profiling may not be universally available and thus makes our findings more generalizable to real-world practice. Another limitation is that our study cohort was collected over an extended period, during which treatment regimens evolved in line with changes in clinical practice. Cytotoxic regimens were predominantly used before 2010, whereas rituximab-based regimens became the preferred frontline approach thereafter. Therefore, differences in treatment era may have influenced the observed outcomes. In addition, treatment-related toxicity data were not systematically collected in this study due to the retrospective design, precluding a comprehensive analysis of regimen tolerability and safety profiles in real-world clinical practice.

All in all, our data fill an important evidence gap and provide support for individualized treatment strategies in WM, with potential implications for treatment sequencing combination approaches.
