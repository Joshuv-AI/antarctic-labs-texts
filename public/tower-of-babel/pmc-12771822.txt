Is the serratus posterior superior intercostal plane block a viable alternative to the erector spinae plane block for postoperative analgesia in breast surgery? A prospective, randomized trial
Authors: Emine Arık, Musa Zengin, Gaye Şeker, Onur Küçük, Ezgi Güngördü, Yusuf Özgüner, Ali Alagöz, Jülide Ergil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective management of postoperative pain remains a significant challenge after breast surgery. Among the available strategies, regional nerve blocks play a key role in alleviating surgical pain in these patients. This study was aimed to evaluate and compare the analgesic efficacy of two regional techniques—erector spinae plane block (ESPB) and serratus posterior superior intercostal plane block (SPSIPB)—in patients undergoing breast surgery.MethodsThis study included 50 female patients aged 18 to 65 years, classified as ASA physical status I-III, with a body mass index ranging from 18 to 35 kg/m², who were scheduled to undergo unilateral breast cancer surgery. Based on random group allocation, either ESPB or SPSIPB was administered before the induction of general anesthesia. Postoperative analgesia was provided using a tramadol-based patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) system. Pain intensity was assessed with the visual analog scale (VAS) at specific predetermined time points: immediately postoperatively (0 h), and at 1, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h. The primary outcome was postoperative pain intensity assessed using the VAS at predetermined time points. Secondary outcomes included total opioid consumption via PCA, requirement for rescue analgesia, incidence of postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV), block performance time, side effects, and patient satisfaction.ResultsThere were no significant differences in VAS scores at rest or during coughing between the two groups at any of the assessed postoperative time points (0, 1, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h) (p > 0.05). A statistically significant difference in postoperative tramadol consumption was observed between the groups, with the ESPB group receiving 137.6 ± 124 mg and the SPSIPB group receiving 82.4 ± 102 mg via PCA, corresponding to an average reduction of 55.2 mg in the SPSIPB group (p = 0.044). The difference in consumption was due to the difference in consumption between 4 and 8 h. In the ESPB group, the average tramadol consumption during this period was 75.2 mg, while in the SPSIPB group, it was 36.8 mg (p = 0.007). No significant differences were found between the groups regarding PONV, side effects, patient satisfaction, and duration of blocks.ConclusionsThe SPSIPB may provide postoperative analgesia that is comparable in efficacy to the ESPB technique in breast surgery. Based on the findings of this study, SPSIPB provided postoperative analgesia comparable to ESPB and was associated with lower opioid consumption within the first 24 h after surgery. Furthermore, the ESPB group experienced pain relief earlier after surgery compared to the SPSIPB group, while pain relief in the SPSIPB group occurred later and lasted longer.Keywords: Acute pain, Breast surgery, Erector spinae plane block, Plane blocks, Serratus posterior superior intercostal plane block

Background

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy affecting women globally, and surgical procedures are still commonly performed [1]. Postoperative pain management in breast surgery is complex due to the extensive nature of the procedure and the complex innervation of the breast. A comprehensive perioperative multimodal analgesia plan that includes regional techniques is essential [2, 3]. Various analgesic techniques—such as local anesthetic (LA) infiltration, intercostal nerve block, pectoral nerve block, rhomboid intercostal block, erector spinae plane block (ESPB), costotransverse block, paravertebral block, and thoracic epidural anesthesia)—have been described to reduce acute postoperative pain [4–8].

Among these techniques, the ESPB has gained widespread acceptance due to its effectiveness in blocking thoracic and abdominal dermatomes, ease of application, and safety profile [9, 10]. Multiple meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials in patients undergoing breast surgery have shown that ESPB significantly reduces pain scores, decreases opioid consumption, and lowers the incidence of postoperative nausea/vomiting (PONV) [10, 11]. However, it should be noted that ESPB is not without its limitations. It should be recognized that the success rate may be reduced in patients with spinal deformities or when transverse process is difficult to visualize under ultrasound (US) guidance [12]. These limitations have increased interest in alternative block techniques that are more superficial, easier to perform, and capable of providing similar analgesic effects. In this context, the serratus posterior superior intercostal plane block (SPSIPB) has recently been introduced as a novel regional anesthesia technique for thoracic analgesia. Initially described by Tulgar et al., this approach involves administering LA into the plane between the serratus posterior superior muscle (SPSm) and the ribs, effectively targeting the intercostal nerve branches from T2 to T6 [13, 14]. Clinical and anatomical studies have demonstrated that SPSIPB can provide effective analgesia in the upper hemithorax and the axillary regions [15, 16]. Furthermore, the ability to administer this block without visualizing the transverse process makes it particularly advantageous in cases with anatomical difficulties [12, 16].

However, no randomized controlled trials have been published comparing SPSIPB and ESPB directly in terms of postoperative analgesia after breast surgery. This limitation prevents the assessment of SPSIPB’s clinical efficacy and restricts its acceptance as an alternative method. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of SPSIPB and ESPB for postoperative analgesia in female patients undergoing breast surgery and to determine whether SPSIPB provides analgesic effects comparable to those of ESPB.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective, randomized, and assessor-blinded study was initiated after obtaining approval from from the Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Etlik City Hospital School of Medicine (approval number: AEŞH-EK1-2023-824; dated 27/12/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT06407037) on 06/05/2024, and patient enrollment began subsequently. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth by the institutional review board and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), including the 2013 amendment and any subsequent revisions. This randomized clinical trial was conducted and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 Statement for randomized controlled trials [17].

Participants

The study was conducted between May 2024 and February 2025 and included 50 women aged 18–65 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, a body mass index (BMI) 18–35 kg/m², and elective unilateral surgery planned for breast cancer (breast-conserving surgery with sentinel lymph node biopsy; mastectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy). Exclusion criteria included a BMI < 18 or > 35 kg/m², skin infection at the needle insertion site, known allergy to any of the study medications, coagulopathy, and recent opioid use.

Interventions

Participants were randomly assigned to either the ESPB or SPSIPB group using a computerized randomization sequence (Research Randomizer; Urbaniak G.C. & Plous S.), generated by a researcher not involved in patient care or data analysis. To maintain blinding, each participant received a random identification code sealed in an opaque envelope. The anesthesiologist responsible for intraoperative care opened the envelope immediately before block administration to determine the assigned intervention. Neither the patient, the surgical team, nor the personnel conducting postoperative pain assessments were aware of the group allocation.

Upon arrival in the designated block area, patients underwent standard anesthetic monitoring including noninvasive blood pressure, heart rate, electrocardiography, and peripheral oxygen saturation. Intravenous access was established with a 20-G catheter, and sedation was achieved using intravenous midazolam at a dose of 0.03 mg/kg. All block procedures were performed by a single anesthesiologist experienced in US-guided regional techniques. The start time of the block procedure was defined as the moment the US probe contacted the skin, and the duration was measured as the time between needle insertion and withdrawal. A high-frequency linear probe (6–15 MHz) and a 22-G, 80-mm block needle (Echoplex, Vygon, Ecouen, France) were used. Blocks were administered approximately 20 min before induction of general anesthesia.

In the ESPB group, the block was performed unilaterally at the T4 level in the lateral decubitus position using an in-plane approach. A high-frequency linear probe and a 22-G, 80-mm block needle were used. After confirming negative aspiration, 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered deep to the erector spinae muscle.

In the SPSIPB group, the block was also performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. After identifying the target region, 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was administered between the SPSm and the underlying rib. Following block administration, patients were transferred to the operating room. The timing of anesthesia induction was recorded. After preoxygenation, anesthesia was induced with propofol (2 mg/kg), fentanyl (1.5 µ/kg), and vecuronium (0.1 mg/kg) intravenously. After induction, all patients were intubated with an appropriately sized endotracheal tube. Maintenance of anesthesia was achieved using 1%–1.5% sevoflurane in a 50:50 oxygen-air mixture and continuous remifentanil infusion (0.01–0.20 mcg/kg/min), titrated according to hemodynamic responses. Thirty minutes before the end of surgery, all patients received dexketoprofen (50 mg), tramadol (1 mg/kg), and ondansetron (0.1 mg/kg) intravenously. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed using neostigmine, and patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) was initiated in the recovery unit. During the postoperative period, multimodal analgesia was administered to all patients with a paracetamol dose of 1 g every 8 h, a dexketoprofen dose of 50 mg (rescue analgesia), and PCA with tramadol. The PCA protocol included tramadol (5 mg/mL), a 20 mg bolus, a 20-minute lockout, and a maximum dose of 200 mg per 4 h without continuous infusion.

Postoperative pain was assessed by an anesthesiologist blinded to the group allocation. Pain intensity was measured at rest and during coughing using a 100 mm visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 represented no pain and 100 indicated worst imaginable pain. Intravenous dexketoprofen (50 mg) was administered as rescue analgesia when the resting VAS ≥ 40 mm at the scheduled assessment times (0, 1, 4, 8, 12, 24 h), with a minimum interval of 8 h between doses and a maximum cumulative dose of 150 mg within 24 h. PONV were graded using a 4-point scale: (1) none, (2) nausea without vomiting, (3) a single episode of emesis or persistent nausea, and (4) repeated vomiting or severe retching. VAS scores were recorded, and PONV assessments were conducted at 0, 1, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h postoperatively. The initial VAS score, recorded in the recovery room once the Aldrete Score reached ≥ 9, was defined as the baseline (0-hour) measurement. Ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg IV was administered to patients with PONV scores of 3 or higher. Patient satisfaction was evaluated 24 h after block administration using a 5-point Likert scale: 1 – very dissatisfied, 2 – dissatisfied, 3 – neutral, 4 – satisfied, 5 – very satisfied. Additional parameters including age, height, weight, BMI, diagnosis, surgical procedure type, intraoperative/postoperative adverse effects (pruritus, sedation, PONV), VAS scores, and additional analgesic use were recorded. All postoperative assessments were performed by an observer anesthesiologist who remained blinded to the block technique used.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was pain scores assessed using the VAS measured at predetermined time intervals during the first 24 h postoperatively in patients who underwent ESPB and SPSIPB. Secondary outcomes included total opioid consumption via the PCA device, documentation of rescue analgesics administration, and patient satisfaction assessed with a Likert-type scale at the end of the 24th postoperative hour.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6) based on the postoperative VAS scores reported by Seelam et al. in patients who underwent ESPB after breast cancer surgery [18]. In that study, the highest standard deviation observed was 13.2 mm, at the 1-hour measurement point. Using the clinically equivalent limit of ± 13 mm accepted in the literature [19], the power analysis indicated an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.98. Accordingly, a minimum of 21 patients per group was required to detect a clinically significant difference between the SPSIPB and ESPB groups with a type I error of 0.05 and 80% statistical power. To account for potential protocol deviations, a 20% confidence margin was added, resulting in 25 patients being included in each treatment group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software for Windows (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The distribution normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR), unless stated otherwise. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages.

For intergroup comparisons, the Student’s t-test was used to analyze normally distributed continuous variables. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed for non-normally distributed variables. Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate differences between categorical variables, depending on expected frequency conditions. For repeated measures involving a single factor, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, and adjustments were made based on Greenhouse–Geisser epsilon (ε) values where necessary. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type I error in cases involving multiple comparisons. A p-value below 0.05 was accepted as the threshold for statistical significance throughout the analyses.

Graphical visualizations were created using Jamovi statistical software (version 2.3.21.0; Sydney, Australia).

When VAS scores during coughing were evaluated, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups at any of the assessed time points: 0 h (p = 0.053), 1 h (p = 0.114), 4 h (p = 0.125), 8 h (p = 0.204), 12 h (p = 0.221), and 24 h (p = 0.109). Additionally, delta VAS values—reflecting the change in both resting and coughing scores relative to baseline (0-hour) measurements—showed no significant intergroup differences. Collectively, these findings indicate that neither resting nor coughing pain scores differed significantly between the groups at any postoperative time point.

When changes in resting VAS scores over time were analyzed within both groups, a statistically significant difference was observed. In the ESPB group, significant differences were detected between the scores at the 4th hour and those at the 8th, 12th, and 24th hours. Specifically, the resting VAS score at the 4th hour was 6 mm higher than at the 8th hour (p = 0.042), 7 mm higher than at the 12th hour (p = 0.015), and 12 mm higher than at the 24th hour (p = 0.017; Greenhouse–Geisser, ε = 0.546).

In the SPSIPB group, significant differences were also observed: there was a 6 mm difference between the 0th hour and 1 st hour (p < 0.001), a 7 mm difference between the 0th hour and 4th hour (p = 0.012), a 9 mm difference between the 1 st hour and 24th hour (p = 0.001), and a 10 mm difference between the 4th hour and 24th hour (p < 0.001; Greenhouse–Geisser, ε = 0.638).

When changes in VAS cough scores over time were analyzed between the groups, statistically significant differences were observed in both groups. In the ESPB group, a significant difference was recorded between the 4th and 12th hours (p = 0.012, Greenhouse–Geisser, ε = 0.481), with scores being 8 mm higher at the 4-hours. In the SPSIPB group, significant differences were found between 0th and 1 st hour (10 mm, p < 0.001), 0th and 4th hours (8 mm, p = 0.008), and 1 st and 24th hours (9 mm, p = 0.044; Greenhouse–Geisser, ε = 0.525). These findings underscore the critical impact of time on cough scores in both treatment groups.

The average amount of tramadol administered via PCA after surgery was 137.6 mg in the ESPB group and 82.4 mg in the SPSIPB group. There was a statistically significant difference in postoperative tramadol requests via PCA between the two groups, with the SPSIPB group requiring an average of 55.2 mg less (p = 0.044, Table 3). When examining tramadol consumption at different time points, a significant difference was observed between the groups during the 4th to 8th hours post-surgery. The ESPB group consumed an average of 75.2 mg of tramadol during this interval, while the SPSIPB group consumed 36.8 mg (p = 0.007). At other time intervals, PCA tramadol consumption was similar between the groups. Within the first 24 h after surgery, 9 patients (36%) in the ESPB group and 4 patients (16%) in the SPSIPB group required additional rescue analgesia (p = 0.107, Table 3). Although the need for rescue analgesia was lower in the SPSIPB group, this difference was not statistically significant.

Discussion

This randomized trial compared the postoperative analgesic effects of ESPB and SPSIPB in patients undergoing breast surgery. ESPB and SPSIPB demonstrated comparable VAS scores both at rest and during coughing. This similarity was also reflected in delta VAS values. Furthermore, the reduced 24-hour opioid consumption observed in the SPSIPB group suggests that it may provide effective postoperative analgesia. Another noteworthy finding was that, within the ESPB group, VAS scores decreased from baseline, especially during the early postoperative period. This observation was consistent with the findings from the evaluation of tramadol consumption.

Breast cancer surgery is a commonly performed procedure that is frequently associated with significant postoperative pain, which may impair early recovery and overall quality of life [20, 21]. Acute postoperative pain after breast surgery is highly prevalent, and existing literature has highlighted its clinical relevance due to the potential impact on long-term functional outcomes [22]. Although chronic pain was not an outcome assessed in the present study, prior studies have shown that inadequate early analgesia may contribute to the development of persistent postoperative pain, underscoring the importance of optimizing acute pain management in this patient population [23, 24]. Therefore, an effective perioperative analgesic strategy remains essential not only for improving immediate postoperative comfort but also for supporting psychological well-being in patients undergoing surgery for malignancy [25]. In this context, the evaluation of regional block techniques capable of providing reliable postoperative analgesia has become an important area of ongoing research.

Breast surgery, especially procedures involving axillary dissection, affects multiple somatic and visceral nerve structures originating from the thoracic spinal nerves and brachial plexus, resulting in complex and multifactorial pain [21, 26]. This widespread pain distribution is considered one of the primary factors contributing to the development of persistent post-mastectomy pain syndrome over time, particularly when acute postoperative pain is not adequately controlled [22]. Therefore, the implementing an effective and comprehensive analgesic strategy from the early stages of breast surgery is critical not only for pain control but also for the prevention of chronic pain [22]. Regional anesthesia techniques, one of the key components of this strategy, are widely used in the management of analgesia after breast surgery [24]. In particular, fascial plane blocks, which have been introduced in recent years, are increasingly utilized in clinical practice because of their simplicity, safe implementation under US guidance, and low complication rates [13, 24, 27].

The ESPB, first described by Forero and colleagues for managing thoracic neuropathic pain, is an interfascial plane block performed under US guidance and has been used as an effective analgesic method in many surgical procedures [18, 28]. ESPB is widely accepted as an adjunctive component of multimodal analgesia strategies, and its efficacy in breast cancer surgery is increasingly emphasized [29, 30]. The effectiveness of the block is influenced by various factors, including the needle entry point, level of application, injectate volume, and LA concentration. Cadaveric dissections and radiological studies have demonstrated that the LA solution may spread through the costotransverse foramen, affecting the dorsal and ventral branches of the spinal nerves and the rami communicantes carrying sympathetic fibers [29]. Seelam et al. reported that an ESPB administered at the T3 level provided superior analgesia during the early postoperative period compared with a control group of breast cancer patients who underwent surgery [18]. The same study emphasized that analgesic efficacy may be related to the level of administration and the volume injected; it was noted that additional blocks or volume adjustments may be required due to limited access to the supraclavicular region [3, 18]. In our study, we demonstrated that an ESPB administered at the T4 level with 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine provided adequate analgesic efficacy. This finding once again highlights the decisive effect of technical parameters such as application level, volume, and concentration on the success of postoperative analgesia. Regional anesthesia techniques for breast surgery remain an active area of research, with several newly described plane blocks now emerging in the literature.

The SPSIPB, a novel technique first described by Tulgar et al., is suggested to offer a theoretical benefit by facilitating LA spread to the lateral cutaneous branches of the dorsal rami and intercostal nerves spanning the C3 to T7 levels [13]. In the same cadaveric study, it was noted that the injected contrast solution spread over a wide area from C7 to T7 and that consistent sensory blockade covering the C3–T10 dermatomes could be achieved in clinical observations [13]. Due to this anatomical distribution, it is thought that the SPSIPB may provide effective analgesia in areas such as the posterior neck, shoulder, axilla, and hemithorax, which are important sources of pain in breast surgery. Due to its newly defined nature, the limited number of case reports and clinical studies on the SPSIPB have reported that this technique provides effective postoperative analgesia [16, 27, 31–33]. Additionally, recent case reports on patients undergoing open-heart surgery have shown that bilateral SPSIPB provides effective postoperative analgesia while requiring low levels of opioids [34]. This finding further supports the clinical applicability of this technique in surgeries involving extensive thoracic nociception. In a prospective randomized trial by Köksal et al., SPSIPB was reported to significantly reduce opioid consumption and enhance recovery quality in patients who underwent modified radical mastectomy compared to the control group [15]. In the present study, although pain scores were similar, lower opioid consumption (statistically significant) and a lower incidence of PONV (although not statistically significant) in the SPSIPB group support the effective analgesia provided by this block due to its extensive dermatomal spread.

One of the noteworthy findings of our study concerns temporal changes in intra-group analgesic effects. In the early postoperative period, the ESPB group demonstrated a rapid decrease in VAS scores, whereas this rapid decrease was observed during the later postoperative period in the SPSIPB group (compared to ESPB). This result may be associated with the proximity of ESPB to neural structures within the paravertebral area, allowing the LA to spread more quickly from the injection site to the paravertebral space, thereby improving pain control in the early postoperative phase. The findings regarding SPSIPB, on the other hand, can be explained by the delayed onset of effect in the paravertebral area due to the wider spread of the LA, as also reported by Tulgar et al. [13]. Considering that many factors, such as injection site, volume, and concentration of the LA, influence its spread, as noted in numerous studies [35–37], further extensive studies are needed to fully understand this matter.

This study has several limitations. First, it was performed at a single center with a relatively small sample size. Second, the absence of a control group may have limited our capacity to draw more definitive conclusions. Additionally, to avoid patient discomfort, dermatomal mapping was not performed, thus limiting the assessment of block spread. Another potential limitation of the study is that rescue analgesia was limited to a non-opioid NSAID (dexketoprofen), which was intentionally chosen to evaluate the block’s isolated efficacy and to minimize opioid exposure. This may have limited comparison with studies using opioid-based rescue regimens. Finally, the assessment of acute pain was limited to 24 h, and long-term follow-up for chronic pain development was not performed, which should also be considered a limitation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the SPSIPB, a relatively recent regional anesthesia technique, may provide effective postoperative analgesia in surgeries involving extensive nerve networks, such as breast surgery, due to its broad dermatomal spread demonstrated in cadaveric studies. The reduced opioid requirement observed in clinical studies supports the analgesic efficacy of this block and represents a significant advantage in reducing opioid-related complications.

Conversely, while the ESPB appears to be more effective in the early postoperative period, its analgesic efficacy diminishes over time in comparison with SPSIPB. This finding highlights the need for further research into the optimal application technique and parameters of this block.

In this context, prospective, randomised controlled trials with larger patient populations across multiple centres are required to confirm the efficacy of SPSIPB and to better elucidate the limitations of ESPB.
