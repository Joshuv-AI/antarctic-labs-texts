Entire solutions to a quasilinear purely critical competitive system
Mónica Clapp, Víctor A. Vicente-Benítez

Abstract
We establish the existence of a fully nontrivial solution with nonnegative components for a weakly coupled competitive system for the p p -Laplacian in ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} whose nonlinear terms are purely critical. We also show that the purely critical equation for the p p -Laplacian in ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} has infinitely many nodal solutions. Keywords: p p -Laplacian, critical quasilinear equation, critical quasilinear system, competitive system, entire solutions, variational methods. MSC2020: 35J92, 35J47, 35B08, 35B33, 35J20.

Introduction

We consider the weakly coupled competitive critical quasilinear system

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\beta_{ij}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}u_{i},\\
u_{i}\in D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,\end{cases}

where
\Delta_{p}u:=\text{div}\left(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)
is the
p
-Laplacian of
u
,
\frac{2N}{N+2}<p<N
,
\beta_{ii}>0
,
\beta_{ij}<0
if
i\neq j
,
p^{*}:=\frac{Np}{N-p}
is the critical Sobolev exponent,
\ell\geq 2
, and

D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\{u\in L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\partial_{i}u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\,i=1,\dots,N\}.

We also consider the critical quasilinear equation

(1.2)

-\Delta_{p}w=|w|^{p^{*}-2}w,\qquad w\in D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

For
p=2
this equation is conformally equivalent to the Yamabe equation on the standard
N
-sphere, by means of the stereographic projection.

Damascelli, Merchán, Montoro, and Sciunzi
[
16
]
, Sciunzi
[
29
]
and Vétois
[
30
]
showed that, for any
p\in(1,N)
, the problem (
1.2
) has a unique positive solution up to translations and dilations, which is the least energy solution. The existence of nodal solutions was established in
[
11
]
, where it is shown that (
1.2
) has a finite number of nonradial solutions that change sign, which depends on the dimension
N
.

The existence of infinitely many nodal solutions to (
1.2
) is known only for
p=2
. It was first proved by W. Ding in
[
4
]
using the invariance of the problem under conformal transformations. Del Pino, Musso, Pacard and Pistoia exhibited in
[
3
]
another family of sign-changing solutions, using the Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction method. None of these methods apply to the quasilinear case. As shown in
[
23
]
, the
p
-Laplacian is not invariant under conformal transformations when
p\neq 2
. On the other hand, the Lyapunov–Schmidt procedure cannot be used because the linearized operator for the
p
-Laplacian is not well understood.

In
[
7
]
another type of nodal solutions for
p=2
was obtained, using only the invariance of the problem under linear isometries. Inspired by this approach we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\geq 4
. Then, for any
p\in(1,N)
, the quasilinear problem (
1.2
) has an infinite sequence of nonradial sign-changing solutions that are not equivalent under translation and dilation.

Next, we look at the system. Motivated by their physical applications, weakly coupled elliptic systems have received much attention in recent years, specially in the semilinear case (
p=2
).

Note that every solution
w
to (
1.2
) gives rise to a solution of the system (
1.1
) whose
i
-th component is
\beta_{ii}^{(p-N)/p^{2}}w
and all other components are trivial. The interesting question is whether (
1.1
) has a
fully nontrivial
solution, i.e., a solution
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
such that all components
u_{i}
are nontrivial. This is not an easy matter.

First, we observe that the system cannot be solved by minimization.

Theorem 1.2
.

The system (
1.1
) does not have a least energy fully nontrivial solution.

This is a consequence of the invariance of the system under dilations and the strong maximum principle for the
p
-Laplacian. In fact, the same is true for any domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; see Theorem
2.1
below. For a system of two equations this was proved by Chen and Zou in
[
6
, Theorem 1.6]
when
p=2
, and by Guo, Perera and Zou in
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
for general
p
.

The question now is whether there are higher energy fully nontrivial solutions. Some existence results are known for
p=2
. Guo, Li and Wei
[
20
]
studied the system (
1.1
) of two equations in dimension
N=3
and established the existence of nonnegative solutions with
k
peaks for sufficiently large
k
using the Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction method. The existence of infinitely many solutions to (
1.1
) was established in
[
12
,
8
,
15
]
by exploiting the fact that the system is invariant under conformal transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
when
p=2
. As we mentioned above, none of these methods apply if
p\neq 2
. Furthermore, even for
p=2
, the Lyapunov-Schmidt procedure can only be used for small dimensions where the variational functional has enough regularity.

Cooperative quasilinear systems in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with critical nonlinear terms have been considered in
[
1
,
32
,
21
]
, and results in bounded domains are given, for example, in
[
5
,
17
,
18
,
24
]
. But we know of no existence result for the
competitive
critical system (
1.1
) with
p\neq 2
.

Our goal is to use symmetries to produce solutions. Therefore, we consider the symmetric system

(1.3)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u_{i}=|u_{i}|^{p^{*}-2}u_{i}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\beta|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}u_{i},\\
u_{i}\in D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,\end{cases}

where
\frac{2N}{N+2}<p<N
,
N\geq 4
,
\beta<0
,
p^{*}:=\frac{Np}{N-p}
and
\ell\geq 2
.

We now describe the symmetries we consider. We write
\mathbb{R}^{N}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
and a point in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as
x=(z,y)
with
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
. Let
\tau:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be given by

\tau(z_{1},z_{2}):=(-\overline{z}_{2},\overline{z}_{1}),

where
\overline{z}
is the complex conjugate of
z
. We define
G
to be the group whose elements are the unit complex numbers acting on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

(1.4)

gx:=(gz_{1},\overline{g}z_{2},y),\qquad\text{for \ }g\in G,\ \ x=(z_{1},z_{2},y)\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4},

and take
\varrho_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
to be

(1.5)

\varrho_{\ell}(z,y):=\left(\Big(\cos\frac{\pi}{\ell}\Big)z+\Big(\sin\frac{\pi}{\ell}\Big)\tau z,\,y\right)\qquad\text{for \ }(z,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}.

We shall look for solutions
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
that satisfy

(\boldsymbol{P_{1}})

u_{j}
is
G
-invariant for every
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, i.e.,
u_{j}(gx)=u_{j}(x)
for any
g\in G
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

(\boldsymbol{P_{2}})

u_{j+1}=u_{j}\circ\varrho_{\ell}
for every
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, where
u_{\ell+1}:=u_{1}
.

They are called
pinwheel solutions
. We prove the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

The system (
1.3
) has a nontrivial pinwheel solution which has least energy among all nontrivial pinwheel solutions and whose components are nonnegative.

For
p=2
this result was recently proved in
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
. The symmetries used there are the same as those just described and the basic strategy of the proof is formally the same. It is based on a careful analysis of the behavior of minimizing sequences for the variational functional associated with the system in the unit ball; see Theorem
4.2
. But here we find a substancial difference from the semilinear case. When
p=2
one can use a unique continuation principle for systems, which was established in
[
10
]
, to rule out the existence of nontrivial pinwheel solutions in a ball and in a half-space, leaving as the only option the existence of a pinwheel solution in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; see Remark
4.3
. Unfortunately, a unique continuation principle is not available for
p\neq 2
, not even for the single equation (
1.2
); see
[
19
]
. We avoid this difficulty by showing that a least energy pinwheel solution in a ball or a half-space gives rise to a least energy pinwheel solution in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Theorem
1.3
appears to be the first existence result for the purely critical competitive quasilinear system (
1.1
).

We note that pinwheel solutions for subcritical Schrödinger systems have been exhibited in
[
13
,
14
,
27
,
28
,
31
]
.

We close this introduction with an open question. It is well known that the
p
-Laplacian satisfies a strong maximum principle; see
[
26
, Theorem 2.5.1]
. Since we are assuming that
\beta_{ij}<0
if
i\neq j
, this principle cannot be applied to each equation of the system (
1.1
). But perhaps a weak unique continuation property for systems, similar to the one obtained in
[
10
]
, holds true for solutions with
nonnegative
components. This would allow us to exclude the existence of a least energy pinwheel solution in a ball and in a half-space, as happens in the semilinear case. Our precise question is the following:

Question 1.4
.

Is it true that, if
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
is a solution to the system (
1.1
) with
u_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
and
\boldsymbol{u}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
in some open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then
\boldsymbol{u}\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
in all of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
?

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove a general nonexistence result that includes Theorem
1.2
. The variational setting for pinwheel solutions is discussed in Section
3
. In Section
4
we analize the behavior of minimizing sequences for the symmetric system in the unit ball and we prove Theorem
1.3
. Section
5
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2
Nonexistence of ground state solutions

Consider the system

(2.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\ell}\beta_{ij}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}u_{i},\\
u_{i}\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\frac{2N}{N+2}<p<N
,
\beta_{ii}>0
,
\beta_{ij}<0
if
i\neq j
and
p^{*}:=\frac{Np}{N-p}
.

The space
D_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the closure of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
in the Banach space

D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\{u\in L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):\partial_{i}u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\,i=1,\dots,N\}

equipped with its standard norm

\|u\|_{p}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u(x)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

We write
|\cdot|_{q}
for the usual norm of
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
q\geq 1
, and denote by

S_{p}:=\inf_{\overset{u\in D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}{u\neq 0}}\frac{\|u\|^{p}_{p}}{|u|_{p^{*}}^{p}}

the best constant for the Sobolev embedding
D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{p^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Consider the
\ell
-fold cartesian product
(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}
with the norm

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\qquad\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}),

and the functional
\mathcal{J}:(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}-\frac{1}{p^{*}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}\beta_{ij}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}.

Since, by assumption,
p^{*}>2
, this is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functional. Its
i
-th partial derivative at each
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell})\in(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}
is given by

\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u_{i}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla v-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\beta_{ij}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}u_{i}v\qquad\text{for all \ }v\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

The solutions
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell})
of the system (
2.1
) are the critical points of
\mathcal{J}
. The fully nontrivial ones, i.e., those whose components are nontrivial, belong to the Nehari-type set

(2.2)

\mathcal{M}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}:u_{i}\neq 0\text{ \ and \ }\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})u_{i}=0\text{ \ for all \ }i=1,\ldots,N\}.

Define

\mu(\Omega):=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\mathcal{J}(u).

A function
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
such that
\mathcal{J}(u)=\mu(\Omega)
is called a
least energy fully nontrivial solution
.

In order to estimate
\mu(\Omega)
, for each
i=1,\ldots,\ell
we consider the critical quasilinear equation

(2.3)

-\Delta_{p}w=\beta_{ii}|w|^{p^{*}-2}w,\qquad w\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),

The nontrivial solutions of (
2.3
) are the critical points of the restriction of the functional
J_{i}:D_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

J_{i}(w):=\frac{1}{p}\|w\|_{p}^{p}-\frac{\beta_{ii}}{p^{*}}|w|^{p^{*}}_{p^{*}},

to the Nehari manifold

\displaystyle M_{i}(\Omega):=\{w\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega):w\neq 0\text{ \ and \ }\|w\|_{p}^{p}=\beta_{ii}|w|^{p^{*}}_{p^{*}}\}.

Using the invariance under dilations, one easily sees that

(2.4)

m_{i}:=\inf_{w\in M_{i}(\Omega)}J_{i}(w)=\frac{1}{N}\beta_{ii}^{-\frac{N-p}{p}}S_{p}^{\frac{N}{p}}.

Thus,
m_{i}
does not depend on
\Omega
.

It is well known that
m_{i}
is not attained if
\mathbb{R}^{N}\smallsetminus\Omega
has nonempty interior. A stronger result is true for the system.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

\mu(\Omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i},

and
\mu(\Omega)
is not attained.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
. Then
u_{i}\neq 0
and

\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}=\beta_{ii}|u_{i}|_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}}+\sum_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq i}}^{\ell}\beta_{ij}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}\leq\beta_{ii}|u_{i}|_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}}.

Setting
t_{i}:=\Big(\frac{\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}}{\beta_{ii}|u_{i}|_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}}}\Big)^{1/(p^{*}-p)}
we have that
t_{i}\in(0,1]
and
t_{i}u_{i}\in M_{i}(\Omega)
. Therefore,

(2.5)

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|t_{i}u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})\quad\text{for every \ }\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega).

As a consequence,
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}\leq\mu(\Omega).

To prove the opposite inequality, for each
i=1,\ldots,\ell
we take a sequence
(\varphi_{i,k})
in
\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap M_{i}(\Omega)
such that
J_{i}(\varphi_{i,k})\to m_{i}
as
k\to\infty
. We choose
\ell
distinct points
x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\in\Omega
and
r>0
such that
B_{r}(x_{i})\subset\Omega
and
B_{r}(x_{i})\cap B_{r}(x_{j})=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
. Since
\varphi_{i,k}
has compact support, we may choose
\varepsilon_{i,k}>0
such that the support of the function

\psi_{i,k}(x)=\varepsilon_{i,k}^{\frac{p-N}{p}}\varphi_{i,k}\Big(\frac{x-x_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i,k}}\Big)

is contained in
B_{r}(x_{i})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\|\psi_{i,k}\|_{p}=\|\varphi_{i,k}\|_{p}
and
|\psi_{i,k}|_{p^{*}}=|\varphi_{i,k}|_{p^{*}}
, we have that
\psi_{i,k}\in M_{i}(\Omega)
and, since
\psi_{i,k}
and
\psi_{j,k}
have disjoint supports whenever
i\neq j
, the function
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\psi_{1,k},\ldots,\psi_{\ell,k})
belongs to
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mu(\Omega)\leq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\psi_{i,k}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\varphi_{i,k}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}J_{i}(\varphi_{i,k}).

Passing to the limit we get
\mu(\Omega)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}.
This proves that

\mu(\Omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}.

Finally, we show that
\mu(\Omega)
is not attained. Arguing by contradiction, assume there exists
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
such that
\mu(\Omega)=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})
. Then, it follows from (
2.5
) that

(2.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}m_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|t_{i}u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p},

where
t_{i}\in(0,1]
is such that
t_{i}u_{i}\in M_{i}(\Omega)
. This identity implies that
t_{i}=1
. Hence,
u_{i}\in M_{i}(\Omega)
and, as a consequence,
m_{i}\leq J_{i}(u_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. These inequalities, together with (
2.6
), yield
m_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, i.e.,
u_{i}
is a least energy solution of (
2.3
). Since
|u_{i}|
is also a least energy solution, we may assume that
u_{i}\geq 0
. On the other hand, since
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
,
u_{i}\in M_{i}(\Omega)
and
\beta_{ij}<0
, we get that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}=0\quad\text{if \ }j\neq i.

This implies that
u_{i}u_{j}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. Since
\ell\geq 2
there exists
j\neq i
and, since
u_{j}\neq 0
, we have that
u_{i}=0
in a set of positive measure. This contradicts the maximum principle
[
26
, Theorem 2.5.1]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Apply Theorem
2.1
to
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

3
The symmetric variational setting

Next we consider the system

(3.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u_{i}=|u_{i}|^{p^{*}-2}u_{i}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\beta|u_{j}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|u_{i}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}u_{i},\\
u_{i}\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,\end{cases}

where
\Omega
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 4
,
\frac{2N}{N+2}<p<N
,
\beta<0
.

We describe the variational setting for solutions that satisfy
(P_{1})
and
(P_{2})
.

First, we consider the group
G:=\{g\in\mathbb{C}:|g|=1\}
of unit complex numbers acting on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
by

(3.2)

gx:=(gz_{1},\overline{g}z_{2},y)\qquad\text{for every }g\in G\text{ and }x=(z_{1},z_{2},y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4},

where
\overline{g}
is the complex conjugate of
g
, and we assume that
\Omega
is
G
-invariant (i.e.,
gx\in\Omega
for all
g\in G,\ x\in\Omega
). For each
g\in G
and
u\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
we define
gu\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
by
(gu)(x):=u(g^{-1}x)
. Then the map
\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto g\boldsymbol{u}:=(gu_{1},\dots,gu_{\ell})
is a linear isometry of
(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}
and the functional
\mathcal{J}
is
G
-invariant, i.e.,
\mathcal{J}(g\boldsymbol{u})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})
for every
g\in G,\ \boldsymbol{u}\in(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}
. The
G
-fixed point space of
(D_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\ell}
is the subspace

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Omega):=

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{u}\in(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}:g\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}\text{ for all }g\in G\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{u}\in(D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega))^{\ell}:u_{j}\text{ is }G\text{-invariant for each }j=1,\dots,\ell\}.

Since
(D_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega))^{\ell}
is reflexive and strictly convex, by the principle of symmetric criticality for Banach spaces
[
22
, Theorem 2.2]
, the critical points of the restriction of
\mathcal{J}
to
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
are the critical points of
\mathcal{J}
that satisfy
(P_{1})
.

Next, we define an action of
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}:=\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-1\}
, the additive group of integers modulo
\ell
, on the space
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
as follows: Let
\tau:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be given by
\tau(z_{1},z_{2})=(-\overline{z}_{1},\overline{z}_{2})
and, for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
, define

\varrho_{\ell}^{j}x:=\left(\Big(\cos\frac{\pi j}{\ell}\Big)z+\Big(\sin\frac{\pi j}{\ell}\Big)\tau z,\,y\right),\qquad\text{where \ }x=(z,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}.

Since
\tau z
is orthogonal to
z
and
|\tau z|=|z|
we have that
\varrho_{\ell}^{j}\in O(N)
. Note that
\varrho_{\ell}^{k}\varrho_{\ell}^{j}=\varrho_{\ell}^{k+j}
. Furthermore, as
\tau g=g\tau
for every
g\in G
, we have that
\varrho_{\ell}^{j}g=g\varrho_{\ell}^{j}
for every
g\in G
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
.

From now on we assume that
\Omega
is invariant under the action of the subgroup of
O(N)
generated by
G\cup\{\varrho_{\ell}^{1}\}
.
We denote by
\sigma^{j}:\{1,\ldots,\ell\}\to\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
the permutation
\sigma^{j}(m):=m+j\mod\ell
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
. As in
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
, it is readily seen that the function
\varrho_{\ell}^{j}:\mathcal{D}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
given by

\varrho_{\ell}^{j}\boldsymbol{u}(x):=(u_{\sigma^{j}(1)}(\varrho_{\ell}^{-j}x),\ldots,u_{\sigma^{j}(\ell)}(\varrho_{\ell}^{-j}x)),\quad\text{where \ }\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}),

is a well-defined linear isometry, and that
j\mapsto\varrho_{\ell}^{j}
is a well-defined homomorphism from
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
into the group of linear isometries of
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
. The functional
\mathcal{J}|_{\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}
is, clearly,
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
-invariant. So by the principle of symmetric criticality
[
22
, Theorem 2.2]
, the critical points of the restriction of
\mathcal{J}
to the
\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}
-fixed point space of
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{D}(\Omega)^{\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}}=\{u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega):\varrho_{\ell}^{j}\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}\}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in D^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega):u_{j}\text{ is }G\text{-invariant and }u_{j+1}=u_{j}\circ\varrho_{\ell}\text{ for each }j=1,\dots,\ell\},

are critical points of
\mathcal{J}|_{\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}
and, hence, of
\mathcal{J}
. They are the solutions to the system (
3.1
) that satisfy
(P_{1})
and
(P_{2})
. We call them
pinwheel solutions
.

Abusing notation, we shall write

\mathcal{J}:=\mathcal{J}|_{\mathscr{D}(\Omega)}:\mathscr{D}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}.

Note that, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then every component of
\boldsymbol{u}
is nontrivial. Hence, the fully nontrivial critical points of
\mathcal{J}
belong to the Nehari manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\text{ and }\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{u}\neq 0\}.

Note also that

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})v_{i}=\ell\partial_{j}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})v_{j}\quad\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega)\text{ and }j=1,\dots,\ell.

Therefore
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)=\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\cap\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
, where
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
is as in (
2.2
). Set

c(\Omega):=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega)}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}).

Then
c(\Omega)\geq\mu(\Omega)
and it follows from Theorem
2.1
and equation (
2.4
) that

(3.3)

c(\Omega)\geq\frac{\ell}{N}S_{p}^{\frac{N}{p}}.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathcal{N}(\Omega)\neq\emptyset
, and it is a closed
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-submanifold of
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
and a natural constraint for
\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

The proof is easy; see
[
9
, Lemma 2.3]
.
∎

The proof of the following lemma is identical to that of
[
9
, Proposition 2.5
(i)
]
. We give the details for the benefit of the reader.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\Omega\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})\neq\emptyset
, then
c(\Omega)=c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
via trivial extension, we have that
c(\Omega)\geq c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. To prove the opposite inequality we chose a sequence
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}=(\varphi_{1,k},\ldots,\varphi_{\ell,k})\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
\varphi_{j,k}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k})\to c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
\xi\in\Omega\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
and
r>0
be such that
B_{r}(\xi)\subset\Omega
, where
B_{r}(\xi)
is the ball centered at
\xi
of radius
r
. Fix
\varepsilon_{k}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{k}z\in B_{r}(0)
for every
z\in\text{supp}(\varphi_{1,k})
. Since
\varphi_{j+1,k}=\varphi_{1,k}\circ\varrho_{\ell}^{j}
for every
j=0,\ldots,\ell-1
, we have that
\varepsilon_{k}x\in B_{r}(0)
for every
x\in\text{supp}(\varphi_{j,k})
and
j=1,\ldots,\ell
. Define

\widetilde{\varphi}_{j,k}(x):=\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{p-N}{p}}\varphi_{j,k}\Big(\frac{x-\xi}{\varepsilon_{k}}\Big).

Then
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j,k}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and, since
g\xi=\xi
for every
g\in G
and
\varrho_{\ell}^{j}\xi=\xi
, we have that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{k}=(\widetilde{\varphi}_{1,k},\ldots,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\ell,k})\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, as

\|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j,k}\|^{p}_{p}=\|\varphi_{j,k}\|^{p}_{p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\varphi}_{j,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|\widetilde{\varphi}_{i,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\varphi_{j,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|\varphi_{i,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}},

we have that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{J}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{k})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k})\to c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. This shows that
c(\Omega)\leq c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

When
p=2
more can be said. Namely, as shown in
[
9
, Proposition 2.5
(ii)
]
, if

and

) does not have a least energy pinwheel solution. This follows from the unique continuation property for systems proved in

]
, which is not available for

.

4
Existence of a pinwheel solution

Our aim is to analyse the behavior of minimizing sequences for
\mathcal{J}
on
\mathcal{N}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is the unit ball. The existence of a pinwheel solution to the system (
1.3
) will follow from this analysis. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
act on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
as in (
1.4
). Then any given sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})
in
(0,\infty)
and
(\zeta_{k})
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
contain subsequences that satisfy one of the following statements:

(i)

either there exist
\eta_{k}\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
and
C_{1}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}|\zeta_{k}-\eta_{k}|<C_{1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(ii)

or, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in G
such that
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}|g_{i}\zeta_{k}-g_{j}\zeta_{k}|\rightarrow\infty
for any
i\neq j
.

Proof.

See
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{B}
be the unit ball centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})
be a sequence in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{B})
such that
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\to c(\mathbb{B})
. Then, after passing to a subsequence, one of the following three statements holds true:

(I)

(u_{k})
converges strongly in
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{B})
to a least energy pinwheel solution of the system (
3.1
) in the ball
\mathbb{B}
.

(II)

There exist sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})
in
(0,\infty)
and
(\xi_{k})
in
\partial\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
such that
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
and
\xi_{k}\to\xi
, and a least energy pinwheel solution
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell})
to the system (
3.1
) in the half-space

\mathbb{H}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\xi\cdot x<0\}

such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|=0
, where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=(\widehat{w}_{1,k},\ldots,\widehat{w}_{\ell,k})
is given by

\widehat{w}_{i,k}(x)=\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{p-N}{p}}w_{i}\Big(\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\Big)\qquad\text{for every \ }k\in\mathbb{N}\text{ \ and \ }i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(III)

There exist sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})
in
(0,\infty)
and
(\xi_{k})
in
\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
and a least energy pinwheel solution
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell})
to the system (
1.3
) in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}\mathrm{dist}(\xi_{k},\partial\mathbb{B})\to\infty,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|=0,

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=(\widehat{w}_{1,k},\ldots,\widehat{w}_{\ell,k})
is defined as above.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=(u_{1,k},\ldots,u_{\ell,k})\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{B})
be such that
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\rightarrow c(\mathbb{B})
. By Ekeland’s variational principle, we may assume that
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\rightarrow 0
in
[(D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}))^{\ell}]^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\|_{p}^{p}
,
(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})
is bounded in
(D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}))^{\ell}
. Hence, it contains a subsequence that converges weakly to some
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
in
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{B})
. Following an argument similar to that presented in
[
11
, Appendix A]
, we see that
\boldsymbol{u}
is a solution of the system (
3.1
).

If
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
then
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{B})
and

c(\mathbb{B})\leq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|u_{i,k}\|_{p}^{p}=c(\mathbb{B}).

Hence,
c(\mathbb{B})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})
and
\boldsymbol{u}
is a least energy solution of (
3.1
) in
\mathbb{B}
. Furthermore, since
\|u_{i,k}\|_{p}\rightarrow\|u_{k}\|_{p}
and
D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B})
is a uniformly convex Banach space,
u_{i,k}\rightarrow u_{k}
strongly in
D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B})
for all
i=1,\dots,\ell
; see
[
2
, Proposition 3.32]
. This shows that statement
(I)
holds true if
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.

Now assume that
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Fix
0<2\delta<S_{p}^{\frac{N}{p}}
. Since
\beta<0
, using (
3.3
) we see that

\frac{\ell}{N}|u_{1,k}|_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}}\geq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})=c(\mathbb{B})+o(1)\geq\frac{\ell}{N}S_{p}^{\frac{N}{p}}+o(1)>\frac{\ell}{N}\delta,

for large enough
k
. Hence, there exist bounded sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})
and
(\zeta_{k})
in
(0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively, such that

\delta=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x)}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}=\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\quad\text{for all }\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

Applying Lemma
4.1
to the sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})
and
(\zeta_{k})
we have two options. Let us see that option
(ii)
yields a contradiction. Indeed, suppose that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G

such that
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}|g_{i}\zeta_{k}-g_{j}\zeta_{k}|\rightarrow\infty
, if
i\neq j
. Then, for
k
large enough,

B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(g_{i}\zeta_{k})\cap B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(g_{j}\zeta_{k})=\emptyset\quad\text{if \ }i\neq j.

Since
u_{1,k}
is
G
-invariant, we have

m\delta=m\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(g_{i}\zeta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\leq|u_{1,k}|_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}}\quad\text{for all }m\in\mathbb{N}.

This is a contradiction, because
(u_{1,k})
is bounded in
D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B})
. Therefore, option
(i)
in Lemma
4.1
must hold true, i.e., after passing to a subsequence, there exist
\eta_{k}\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
and
C_{1}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}|\zeta_{k}-\eta_{k}|<C_{1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})\subset B_{(C_{1}+1)\varepsilon_{k}}(\eta_{k})
and

(4.1)

0<\delta=\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\leq\int_{B_{(C_{1}+1)\varepsilon_{k}}(\eta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}.

Passing to a subsequence we have

d_{k}=\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}\operatorname{dist}(\eta_{k},\mathbb{B})\rightarrow d\in[0,\infty].

If
0\leq d<\infty
, for each
k
we choose
\xi_{k}\in\partial\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
such that
|\eta_{k}-\xi_{k}|=\operatorname{dist}(\eta_{k},\mathbb{B})
. On the other hand, if
d=\infty
, we set
\xi_{k}=\eta_{k}
. Then, inequality (
4.1
) implies that
\operatorname{dist}(\xi_{k},\mathbb{B})\leq(C_{1}+1)\varepsilon_{k}
and, since
d=\infty
, necessarily
\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{B}
. Summing up, there are two possibilities:

(1)

either
\xi_{k}\in\partial\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
,

(2)

or
\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}\operatorname{dist}(\xi_{k},\partial\mathbb{B})\rightarrow\infty
.

In both cases there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}|\zeta_{k}-\xi_{k}|<C_{0}
. Thus,
B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})\subset B_{(C_{0}+1)\varepsilon_{k}}(\xi_{k})
for
k
large enough and as a consequence

(4.2)

0<\delta=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x)}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}=\int_{B_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\zeta_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\leq\int_{B_{(C_{0}+1)\varepsilon_{k}}(\xi_{k})}|u_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}.

Let
\Omega_{k}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\varepsilon_{k}x+\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{B}\}
and consider the function
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}=(w_{1,k},\dots,w_{\ell,k})
defined by

w_{i,k}(x):=\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{N-p}{p}}u_{i,k}(\varepsilon_{k}x+\xi_{k})\;\text{ if }\;x\in\Omega_{k},\qquad w_{i,k}(x):=0\;\text{ otherwise.}

Since
\xi_{k}\in\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}
we have that
g\xi_{k}=\xi_{k}
for every
g\in G
and
\rho_{\ell}\xi_{k}=\xi_{k}
. Therefore
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
k
and, as
\|w_{i,k}\|_{p}=\|u_{i,k}\|_{p}
for
i=1,\dots,\ell
, after passing to a subsequence we have that

\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{w}\text{ weakly in }\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\quad\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{w}\text{ in }(L_{loc}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))^{\ell},\quad\text{and }\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{w}\text{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Additionally, (
4.2
) yields

(4.3)

\delta=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\int_{B_{1}(x)}|w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\leq\int_{B_{C_{0}+1}(0)}|w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}.

Next we show that
\boldsymbol{w}\neq 0
.

Arguing by contradiction, assume that
\boldsymbol{w}=0
. Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\varphi\geq 0
, and set
\varphi_{k}(x):=\varphi\big(\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\big)
. Then,
(\varphi_{k}^{p}u_{1,k})
is a bounded sequence in
D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B})
. Since
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\rightarrow 0
in
[(D_{0}^{1,p}(\mathbb{B}))^{\ell}]^{\prime}
, a direct computation shows that

(4.4)

\displaystyle o(1)

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})[\varphi_{k}^{p}u_{1,k}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla(\varphi^{p}w_{1,k})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\varphi^{p}-\beta\sum_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq 1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|w_{j,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|w_{1,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}\varphi^{p}.

Now, since
|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla(\varphi^{p}w_{1,k})=|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}+p\varphi^{p-1}w_{1,k}|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla\varphi
and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\varphi^{p-1}w_{1,k}|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\varphi^{p-1}|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-1}|w_{1,k}\nabla\varphi|

\displaystyle\leq C\|w_{1,k}\|_{p}^{p-1}\Big(\int_{\text{supp}(\varphi)}|w_{1,k}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}=o(1),

(because
w_{1,k}\rightarrow 0
in
L_{loc}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
), we have that

(4.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla(\varphi^{p}w_{1,k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}+o(1).

Using the mean value theorem we see that

\displaystyle\Big||\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}-|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big||\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}+w_{1,k}\nabla\varphi|^{p}-|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq p\Big(|w_{1,k}||\nabla\varphi|+|\varphi||\nabla w_{1,k}|\Big)^{p-1}|w_{1,k}\nabla\varphi|.

So integrating and using
w_{1,k}\rightarrow 0
in
L_{loc}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
again we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\text{supp}(\varphi)}\Big(|w_{1,k}|+|\nabla w_{k,1}|\Big)^{p-1}|w_{1,k}|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\text{supp}(\varphi)}\Big||w_{1,k}|+|\nabla w_{1,k}|\Big|^{p}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\left(\int_{\text{supp}(\varphi)}|w_{1,k}|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=o(1).

Thus,

(4.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\varphi\nabla w_{1,k}|^{p}+o(1).

Now assume that, in addition,
\text{supp}(\varphi)\subset B_{1}(x)
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then, since
\beta<0
, from (
4.4
), (
4.5
), (
4.6
) and our choice of
\delta
we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\varphi^{p}+o(1)\leq\Big(\int_{B_{1}(x)}|w_{1,k}|^{p*}\Big)^{\frac{p^{*}-p}{p^{*}}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\varphi w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}}\Big)^{\frac{p}{p^{*}}}+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\delta S_{p}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}+o(1)<\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{\frac{N}{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla(\varphi w_{1,k})|^{p}+o(1).

It follows that
\|\varphi w_{1,k}\|_{p}=o(1)
and, hence, that
|\varphi w_{1,k}|_{p^{*}}=o(1)
for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1}(x))
such that
\varphi\geq 0
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This implies that
w_{1,k}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{p^{*}}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, contradicting (
4.3
). Therefore,
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, as claimed.

Note that, as
u_{k,1}\rightharpoonup 0
and
w_{k,1}\rightharpoonup w_{1}\neq 0
weakly in
D^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we have
\varepsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
.

Let us now analyze the alternatives (1) and (2).

If (1) holds true, passing to a subsequence we have that
\xi_{k}\rightarrow\xi\in\partial\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
. Then
\mathbb{H}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,:\,\xi\cdot x<0\}
is invariant under the action of the group generated by
G\cup\{\rho_{\ell}\}
) and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{H})
. If
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})
, then
\text{supp}(\varphi)\subset\Omega_{k}
for
k
large enough. Hence, the support of the function
\varphi_{k}(x):=\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{p-N}{p}}\varphi\big(\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\big)
is contained in
\mathbb{B}
and performing a change of variables we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})\varphi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla w_{k,1}|^{p-2}\nabla w_{1,k}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|w_{1,k}|^{p^{*}-2}w_{1,k}\varphi-\beta\sum_{\overset{j=1}{j\neq 1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|w_{j,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}}|w_{1,k}|^{\frac{p^{*}}{2}-2}w_{1,k}\varphi

\displaystyle=\partial_{1}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\varphi_{k}=o(1).

As
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightharpoonup\boldsymbol{w}
weakly in
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, following an argument similar to that presented in
[
11
, Appendix A]
we derive that

\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w})\varphi=\lim_{k\to\infty}\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})\varphi=0\quad\text{for all }\;\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}),\;\;i=1,\dots,\ell.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{H})
, and using Lemma
3.2
, we have

c(\mathbb{H})\leq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i,k}\|_{p}^{p}=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i,k}\|_{p}^{p}=c(\mathbb{B})=c(\mathbb{H}).

This shows that
\boldsymbol{w}
is a least energy pinwheel solution of (
3.1
) in
\mathbb{H}
and that
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{w}
strongly in
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{H})
. Setting
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=(\widehat{w}_{1,k},\dots,\widehat{w}_{\ell,k})
with

\widehat{w}_{i,k}(x)=\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{p-N}{p}}w_{i}\Big(\frac{x-\xi_{k}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\Big)

we get that
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|_{p}=\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{p}=o(1)
. Therefore, if (1) is true, statement (II) is necessarily true.

Assume now alternative (2), i.e.,
\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{B}\cap(\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4})
and
\varepsilon_{k}^{-1}\operatorname{dist}(\xi_{k},\partial\mathbb{B})\rightarrow\infty
. Then, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we have that
\text{supp}(\varphi)\subset\Omega_{k}
if
k
is large enough. Arguing as before we see that

\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w})\varphi=\lim_{k\to\infty}\partial_{i}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})\varphi=0\quad\text{for all }\;\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\;\;i=1,\dots,\ell.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and using Lemma
3.2
we obtain

c(\mathbb{R}^{N})\leq\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k,i}\|_{p}^{p}=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k,i}\|_{p}^{p}=c(\mathbb{B})=c(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

This shows that
\boldsymbol{w}
is a least energy pinwheel solution of (
3.1
) and that
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{w}
strongly in
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. So, setting
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}=(\widehat{w}_{1,k},\dots,\widehat{w}_{\ell,k})
with
\widehat{w}_{i,k}
as above, we get that
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|_{p}=\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{p}=o(1)
. Therefore, if (2) is true, statement (III) is necessarily true.

The proof is now complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Since
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{B})\neq\emptyset
by Lemma
3.1
, there is a sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})
in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{B})
such that
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\to c(\mathbb{B})
. It follows from Theorem
4.2
that the system (
3.1
) has a least energy pinwheel solution either in
\mathbb{B}
, or in
\mathbb{H}
, or in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. From Lemma
3.2
we get that the energy of any one of them is
c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore, if the system has a least energy pinwheel solution either in
\mathbb{B}
or in
\mathbb{H}
, its trivial extension to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a least energy pinwheel solution to the system (
3.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This proves that, in all three cases, there exists a least energy pinwheel solution
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})
to the system (
1.3
). Then,
\boldsymbol{u}=(|v_{1}|,\ldots,|v_{\ell}|)\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{u})=c(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. So
\boldsymbol{u}
is a least energy pinwheel solution to (
1.3
) with nonnegative components.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

When
p=2

(I)
and
(II)

4.2

5
Infinitely many solutions to the quasilinear equation

To prove Theorem
1.1
we use the following result from
[
11
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a closed subgroup of
O(N)
and
\phi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}:=\{1,-1\}
be a continuous homomorphism of groups with the following properties:

(S1)

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, either
\#\Gamma x=\infty
or
\#\Gamma x=1
, where
\Gamma x:=\{\gamma x:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
is the
\Gamma
-orbit of
x
and
\#\Gamma x
is its cardinality.

(S2)

\phi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is surjective.

(S3)

There exists
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\xi=\xi\}\subset\ker\phi
.

Then, the problem (
1.2
) has a nontrivial solution
w
that satisfies

w(\gamma x)=\phi(\gamma)w(x)\quad\text{for all \ }\gamma\in\Gamma,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

In particular,
w
is nonradial and changes sign.

Proof.

See
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
G
act on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as in (
1.4
) and
\varrho_{\ell}
be as defined in (
1.5
). Let
\Gamma_{\ell}
be the subgroup of
O(N)
generated by
G\cup\{\varrho_{\ell}\}
and
\phi_{\ell}:\Gamma_{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{2}:=\{-1,1\}
be the homomorphism of groups given by
\phi_{\ell}(g)=1
if
g\in G
and
\phi_{\ell}(\varrho_{\ell})=-1
. Since
\varrho_{\ell}
has order
2\ell
, this homomorphism is well defined and it is clearly surjective. The
\Gamma_{\ell}
-orbit
\Gamma_{\ell}x:=\{\gamma x\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}\}
of a point
x=(z,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-4}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies

\#\Gamma_{\ell}x=\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if \ }z\neq 0,\\
1&\text{if \ }z=0.\end{cases}

Furthermore, if
e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
, then
\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}\,:\,\gamma e_{1}=e_{1}\}=\{1\}\subset\ker\phi_{\ell}
. This proves that
(S1)-(S3)
hold true. So, by Theorem
5.1
, there exists a nonradial sign-changing solution
w_{\ell}
of equation (
1.2
) such that

w_{\ell}(gx)=w_{\ell}(x)\quad\text{and }\;\;w_{\ell}(\varrho_{\ell}x)=-w_{\ell}(x)\quad\text{for all }\;g\in G,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

To prove that problem (
1.2
) has infinitely many nonradial sign-changing solutions we show next that

w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{2^{k}},\ldots

are all distinct. To this end if suffices to show that, if
\ell=nm
with
n
even and
u
and
v
are nontrivial functions that satisfy

u(\varrho_{\ell}x)=-u(x)\;\;\;\text{and }\;v(\varrho_{m}x)=-v(x)\;\;\;\text{ for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

then
u\neq v
. Indeed, arguing by contradiction, assume that
u=v
and choose
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})\neq 0
. Since
\varrho_{\ell}^{n}=\varrho_{m}
and
n
is even, we have that

u(\varrho_{m}x_{0})=u(\varrho_{\ell}^{n}x_{0})=u(x_{0})=v(x_{0})=-v(\varrho_{m}x_{0})=-u(\varrho_{m}x_{0}),

which is a contradiction.
∎

For the Yamabe problem (
1.2
) with
p=2
these solutions were recently obtained in
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
