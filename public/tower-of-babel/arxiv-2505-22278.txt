A Moment-Based Shallow Water Model for Coupled Bedload-Suspended Load Transport with Variable Density: Hyperbolicity and Numerical Validation
Afroja Parvin, Giovanni Samaey, Julian Koellermeier

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a Hyperbolic Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (HSWEMED), which extends the recent shallow water moment framework to capture coupled morphodynamics with erosion and deposition. Extending existing moment models, HSWEMED introduces a sediment concentration equation for suspended load, couples a concentration-dependent sediment-water mixture density with the momentum equation and higher-order moments, and adds source terms arising from erosion and deposition. Starting from the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations for a water-sediment mixture, we derive a coupled system that integrates: (i) the shallow water equations, (ii) moment equations for polynomial velocity coefficients, (iii) a depth-averaged suspended-sediment equation, and (iv) an Exner equation for bedload transport with erosion-deposition coupling. The reduced model evolves only the depth-averaged concentration, while the vertical structure is retained for the horizontal velocity through the moment expansion. For the regularized model, we derive the characteristic polynomial and identify a real-root condition for the sediment-coupled part. The analysis shows that strict hyperbolicity can hold for even moment orders, while odd moment orders lead to weak hyperbolicity because of a repeated eigenvalue. We also derive dissipative energy balance relations for the lower-order models. Numerical results are obtained with a second-order path-conservative MUSCL-Rusanov finite-volume scheme with a well-balanced correction for the water-at-rest equilibrium. We verify the well-balanced property, perform a reference-convergence study, and compare dam-break simulations with laboratory experiments from the literature. The results illustrate how the moment approximation and the erosion-deposition coupling affect the free surface, bed evolution, and suspended concentration. Keywords: Shallow water Exner moment model; erosion-deposition; bedload and suspended load sediment transport; hyperbolicity; dam-break, wet/dry fronts.

Introduction

Studying sediment transport in shallow water is an active research topic. It involves the movement of sediment particles influenced by gravity and friction
[
5
]
. This process is essential for evolving riverbeds, estuaries, and coastal areas. Understanding sediment transport dynamics is essential in civil engineering, river and coastal engineering, and flood risk management
[
gonzalez2020robust
]
.

Sediments are usually transported as bedload along the riverbed and suspended loads in the flow. Bedload transport refers to coarser particles that move close to the bed by rolling, sliding, or jumping. In contrast, a suspended load consists of finer particles eroded from the bed, which remain suspended for a time before settling
[
gonzalez2020robust
,
subhasish2014fluvial
]
(see
Figure
1
).

The typical method for modeling bedload transport couples the Shallow Water model (SW)
[
audusse2004fast
,
meng2020localized
]
with the bed evolution equation. The result is known as the Shallow Water Exner model
[
exner1925uber
]
. The Exner equation is based on mass conservation and describes the solid transport flux using a closure relation. In most existing works, this is integrated with the transport equation for suspended sediment, including erosion and deposition processes
[
cantero2019vertically
,
del2023lagrange
,
gonzalez2023numerical
,
zhao2019depth
]
. There are many empirical formulas to quantify solid transport flux, erosion, and deposition at the bed surface (see, e.g.,
[
boittin2019modeling
,
cozzolino2014novel
,
el2011applicability
,
kubo2004experimental
,
morales2009shallow
,
nakajima2002laboratory
,
parker1986self
,
rijn1987mathematical
,
zhao2017comparison
]
, and many others).

In classical shallow water sediment transport models, the near-bed velocity, essential for estimating bed shear stress, sediment flux, and erosion, is commonly approximated using a logarithmic velocity profile. Since these models do not resolve the vertical structure of the velocity, empirical friction laws such as Manning or Chézy are often introduced to parametrize shear stress, with their coefficients typically calibrated by integrating or fitting the logarithmic profile under idealized conditions, such as steady and uniform flow
[
1
,
elgamal2021moment
,
lajeunesse2010bed
,
23
]
. Although tuning friction coefficients may yield reasonable results in steady-state regimes, this approach cannot capture the full dynamics of sediment transport in more general flow scenarios. Consequently, the model lacks the capability to dynamically resolve the vertical velocity structure, limiting its effectiveness in representing near-bed flow in complex or unsteady environments.
Given that velocities near the bed are critical for bedload transport, reliance on depth-averaged quantities and idealized profiles can lead to inaccurate predictions of sediment erosion under non-equilibrium conditions.

Recent research has aimed to improve fluid descriptions in the vertical direction using two-phase flows
[
audusse2021asymptotic
]
or a multi-layer approach
[
bonaventura2018multilayer
,
audusse2011multilayer
,
4
,
16
]
. However, this approach raises challenges, such as the lack of analytical expressions for eigenvalues, the requirement for many layers to capture complex velocity profiles, and increased computational cost.
[
11
]
developed an extended Shallow Water Moment model (SWM) to address these challenges. This model uses a Legendre polynomial expansion to represent horizontal velocity variations in the vertical direction. It applies a Galerkin projection of a transformed Navier-Stokes system for deriving evolution equations for all basis coefficients, called ’moments.’ The model was recently extended for open curved shallow flows
[
steldermann2023shallow
]
, non-hydrostatic pressure
[
scholz2024dispersion
]
, a splitting scheme for dispersive shallow moment equations
[
18
]
, and multi-layer models
[
garres2023general
]
. While the SWM performs well compared to the standard SW
[
11
]
, it lacks hyperbolicity for higher-order moments. Losing hyperbolicity can cause numerical instabilities. In
[
koellermeier2020analysis
]
, the authors resolve this issue for the SWM using a hyperbolic regularization, resulting in the Hyperbolic Shallow Water Moment model (HSWM). Later, the HSWM model was extended by incorporating the Exner equation to simulate bedload transport, resulting in the Hyperbolic Shallow Water Exner Moment model (HSWEM)
[
garres2023general
]
. While this approach includes non-linear friction adapted to the moment framework, it neglects erosion, deposition, and suspension processes, and assumes a constant fluid density. These simplifications limit its ability to capture bottom evolution, since sediment-laden flows should be treated as a water-sediment mixture with variable density. To our best knowledge, there is no hyperbolic shallow water moment model that simultaneously simulates bedload and suspended load transport in a coupled framework.

To address these limitations, this work extends the HSWEM
[
garres2023general
]
to create a coupled framework for simulating bedload and suspended load sediment transport. The proposed framework incorporates vertical velocity structures and accounts for sediment mass changes at the bottom by including erosion and deposition processes. The derivation starts with the standard incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations for a water-sediment mixture with a divergence-free velocity field. Using a moment-based approach
[
11
]
, we derive a shallow water moment model by averaging over the vertical variable, incorporating volumetric sediment concentration for suspended load with erosion and deposition, and the Exner equation for bedload transport, resulting in the Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEMED). In our new SWEMED, incorporating sediment suspension, erosion, and deposition effects introduces extra terms in the mass conservation and Exner equations, which quantify the rate of bed deformation. Additionally, two more terms are added to the momentum conservation and higher-order averaged equations, including friction based on flow velocity at the bottom. These terms account for the influence of sediment concentration and the momentum transfer resulting from sediment exchange between the water column and the erodible bed. The higher-order averages include a sediment discharge term coupled with the Exner equation.

Therefore, the key novelty of this work is the extension of the shallow water moment framework to a coupled sediment-water system with erosion and deposition. Unlike previous moment models that neglect sediment exchange, our proposed model: (i) introduces a depth-averaged sediment concentration equation for suspended load, (ii) couples the variable mixture density with the momentum and higher-order moment equations, and (iii) adds new source terms arising from erosion and deposition, representing the transfer of mass and momentum between the flow and the erodible bed. The reduced model evolves only the depth-averaged concentration
c_{m}
. The near-bed concentration
c_{b}
required in the deposition closure is prescribed algebraically from
c_{m}
through an empirical relation. Accordingly, within the intended dilute to moderately dilute regime, the suspended concentration is not treated as a passive scalar: it feeds back on the hydrodynamics through the mixture density in the hydrostatic pressure and associated buoyancy terms in the depth-averaged momentum and moment equations.

The main contributions of this paper are the following. First, we derive the SWEMED model by coupling the shallow water moment model with a depth-averaged suspended-sediment equation and an Exner equation including erosion and deposition. Second, we analyze the hyperbolicity of the regularized system following the recent work on hyperbolic shallow water moment models
[
koellermeier2020analysis
,
Garres
]
, resulting in the Hyperbolic Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (HSWEMED). Third, we derive dissipative energy balance relations for the lower-order models SWEMED0 and SWEMED1.
Fourth, we develop a finite-volume discretization for HSWEMED based on a second-order path-conservative MUSCL-Rusanov scheme
[
21
]
. We introduce a well-balanced correction for the water-at-rest equilibrium by modifying the Rusanov viscosity term. The corrected scheme preserves the water-at-rest equilibrium over smooth and discontinuous bottoms, and the reference-convergence study gives rates consistent with the expected second-order behaviour. Finally, we use dam-break simulations to compare HSWEMED with SWEED and HSWEM and to assess the model behaviour against laboratory experiments from the literature
[
20
]
. The numerical results show that, in the tested PVC case, HSWEMED predicts less bottom erosion than the depth-averaged SWEED due to the moment-based bottom velocity, while the differences are smaller for the sand case. The comparisons focus on the free surface, sediment-bottom evolution, and depth-averaged suspended concentration.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 derives the coupled Shallow Water Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEMED), incorporating the moment approach for shallow flows together with the sediment concentration equation for suspended load and the Exner equation for bedload transport. Section 3 analyzes the eigenvalue structure and hyperbolicity properties of the regularized HSWEMED transport matrix. Section 4 presents numerical results for dam-break problems and compares them with laboratory experiments from the literature. Finally, Section 5 provides concluding remarks.

2
Shallow water moment model for sediment transport

In this section we present our new model for sediment-laden shallow flows as extension of the HSWEM from
[
Garres
]
by including a sediment concentration equation and erosion deposition effects. The resulting Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEMED) incorporates both hydrodynamic and morphodynamic evolution that interact through additional erosion and deposition source terms. The new system explicitly accounts for sediment concentration, suspension and erosion-deposition processes within the moment framework, leading to a set of coupled equations for the sediment-water mixture. Specifically, the hydrodynamic part is described by the depth-averaged Shallow Water Moment model (SWM) with erosion-deposition, while the morphodynamic part combines suspended load transport through the sediment concentration equation with bed evolution through the Exner equation.

2.1
Hydrodynamic equations

We consider the two-dimensional inhomogeneous Navier-Stokes equations
for simplicity to derive the coupled mathematical model for sediment
transport. However, the derivation can be extended to three spatial
dimensions
[
11
]
. The inhomogeneous Navier-Stokes equations play a central
role in the description of geophysical flows, including rivers and
shallow coastal currents. These flows are incompressible, but the
density varies due to the presence of suspended sediment. In the
present work we consider the following two-dimensional
inhomogeneous Navier-Stokes equations

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u+\partial_{z}w

\displaystyle=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\partial_{x}(\rho u^{2})+\partial_{z}(\rho uw)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}p+\partial_{x}\sigma_{xx}+\partial_{z}\sigma_{xz},

(2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho w)+\partial_{x}(\rho uw)+\partial_{z}(\rho w^{2})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{z}p+\partial_{x}\sigma_{zx}+\partial_{z}\sigma_{zz}-\rho g,

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho u)+\partial_{z}(\rho w)

\displaystyle=0,

(4)

where
u
and

denote the horizontal and vertical velocity
components, respectively,

is the gravitational acceleration, and

\sigma_{xx},\sigma_{xz},\sigma_{zx},\sigma_{zz}

Due to the water-sediment mixture, the density

\rho(t,x,z)=\rho_{w}+c(t,x,z)\,(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w}),

In principle, the density

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u+\partial_{z}w

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho}\,\partial_{x}p+\frac{1}{\rho}\,\partial_{z}\sigma_{xz},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c+\partial_{x}(cu)+\partial_{z}(cw)

\left.\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}(\zeta)\right|_{\zeta=0}=-\rho\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b},

where the bottom velocity is expressed as

u_{b}=\left.\tilde{u}\right|_{\zeta=0}=u_{m}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(t,x)\,\phi_{j}(0)=u_{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(t,x),

(29)

and
\epsilon
is a dimensionless constant defined in
[
Garres
]
.

2.1.4
Higher-order moments of the momentum equation

The evolution equations for the higher-order moments of the momentum equation are derived from the higher-order Galerkin projection
(
25
)
of
(
18
)
.
Thus, the resulting equation for
h\alpha_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}(h\alpha_{i})&+\partial_{x}\left(2hu_{m}\alpha_{i}+h\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}A_{ijk}\right)\\
&=u_{m}\partial_{x}(h\alpha_{i})-\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}B_{ijk}\alpha_{k}\partial_{x}(h\alpha_{j})+F_{b}\left(\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(H_{ij}-G_{ij})\right)\\
&-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}\frac{1}{1-\psi}\partial_{x}Q_{b}+(2i+1)\frac{gh^{2}}{\rho}(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\frac{\partial c_{m}}{\partial x}K_{i}-(2i+1)\left(\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}+\frac{\nu}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}C_{ij}\right),\end{split}

(30)

where
A_{ijk},\,B_{ijk},\,G_{ij},\,H_{ij}
and
K_{i}
introduced here, are constant coefficients and associated with the integrals of the scaled Legendre polynomials.

\displaystyle A_{ijk}=(2i+1)\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\phi_{k}\,d\zeta,\quad B_{ijk}=(2i+1)\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}^{\prime}\left(\phi_{j}d\hat{\zeta}\right)\phi_{k}\,d\zeta,\quad C_{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}^{\prime}\phi_{j}^{\prime}\,d\zeta,

(31)

\displaystyle G_{ij}=(2i+1)\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}^{\prime}\,d\zeta,\quad H_{ij}=(2i+1)\int_{0}^{1}\zeta\phi_{i}\phi_{j}^{\prime}\,d\zeta,\quad K_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}\zeta\phi_{i}\,d\zeta.

We provide the detailed derivation in
Appendix
B.2
.

On the right-hand side of equation
(
30
)
, four additional terms appear compared to the classical SWM
[
11
]
:

1.

The term
F_{b}\left(\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(H_{ij}-G_{ij})\right)
represents a source term associated with erosion and deposition effects at the bottom, governed by the bed exchange rate
F_{b}.

2.

The term
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}\frac{1}{1-\psi}\partial_{x}Q_{b},
which includes the sediment discharge term
Q_{b},
represents the influence of the bedload transport, as defined in
Section
2.2
. This term appears in the equations for the higher-order moments since our model does not assume
\partial_{t}h_{b}=0.

3.

The term
(2i+1)\frac{gh^{2}}{\rho}(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\frac{\partial c_{m}}{\partial x}K_{i}
arises from the higher order Galerkin projection on the spatial variation of sediment concentration. This term signifies the interaction between suspended sediment and momentum balance.

4.

The term
(2i+1)\left(\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}+\frac{\nu}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}C_{ij}\right)
results from the higher-order Galerkin projection of Manning type friction at the bottom i.e.,
\left.\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}(\zeta)\right|_{\zeta=0}=-\rho\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b},
and Newtonian friction within the fluid i.e.,
\left.\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}(\zeta)\right|_{\zeta\in(0,1)}=-\frac{\mu}{h}\partial_{\zeta}\tilde{u}(\zeta)
, and the projection reads

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\partial_{\zeta}\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}\,d\zeta=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\zeta}(\phi_{i}\tilde{\sigma}_{xz})\,d\zeta-\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}\partial_{\zeta}\phi_{i}\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=-\left.\phi_{i}(\zeta)\tilde{\sigma}_{xz}\right|_{\zeta=0}-\frac{\mu}{h}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}^{\prime}\partial_{\zeta}\tilde{u}\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=-\rho\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}-\frac{\mu}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{j}\phi_{i}^{\prime}\phi_{j}^{\prime}\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=-\rho\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}-\frac{\mu}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}C_{ij}.

Remark 2.1
.

We use a classical solid transport discharge laws, the Meyer-Peter
\And
Müller formula, but using the velocity moments and define it in terms of the bottom velocity (
29
) instead of the averaged velocity. A similar procedure was done in the existing literature
[
Garres
]
and for the simpler friction models in
[
11
]
. However, in the classical literature on shallow water equations, the solid transport formula is based on a friction law which is usually calibrated for the average velocity, not for the bottom velocity. By using the bottom velocity for the solid transport discharge law, the model uses the velocity-profile information provided by the moment expansion in the transport terms. The calibration of the corresponding empirical coefficients is not addressed in this work and remains a topic for further study.

2.2
Morphodynamic equations

The morphodynamic part of the model covers the suspended and bedload transport. It consists of the depth-averaged volumetric sediment concentration equation and the Exner equation, as explained below.

2.2.1
Depth-averaged volumetric sediment concentration

In this section, we derive the depth-averaged equation for the suspended sediment concentration. In the mapped
\zeta
-coordinates, the vertically resolved concentration equation
(
19
)
can be written in conservative form as

\partial_{t}(h\tilde{c})+\partial_{x}(h\tilde{c}\tilde{u})+\partial_{\zeta}J=0,

(32)

where the vertical sediment flux
J
is given by

J(t,x,\zeta)=\tilde{c}(t,x,\zeta)\left[\tilde{w}-\partial_{x}(\zeta h+h_{b})\tilde{u}-\partial_{t}(\zeta h+h_{b})\right].

(33)

We define the depth-averaged volumetric sediment concentration
c_{m}
and the depth-averaged concentration flux
h\langle\tilde{c}\tilde{u}\rangle
, respectively, by

c_{m}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{c}(t,x,\zeta)\,d\zeta,\qquad h\langle\tilde{c}\tilde{u}\rangle:=h\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{c}(t,x,\zeta)\tilde{u}(t,x,\zeta)\,d\zeta.

(34)

Integrating
(
32
)
over
\zeta\in[0,1]
and using the kinematic boundary conditions
(
20
)
-
(
21
)
gives no sediment flux through the free surface,
J(1)=0
, while the bed flux is prescribed by
J(0)=E-D
. Here, the erosion and deposition rates
E
and
D
will be defined in
Section
2.2.3
. Therefore,

\partial_{t}(hc_{m})+\partial_{x}\left(h\langle\tilde{c}\tilde{u}\rangle\right)=E-D.

(35)

Since the present model evolves only the depth-averaged concentration
c_{m}
, the depth-averaged concentration flux
h\langle\tilde{c}\tilde{u}\rangle
is not closed. We therefore use the shallow-water closure

\langle\tilde{c}\tilde{u}\rangle\approx c_{m}u_{m}.

(36)

Thus, using
(
36
)
the depth-averaged volumetric sediment concentration equation
(
35
)
reads

\partial_{t}(hc_{m})+\partial_{x}(hc_{m}u_{m})=E-D.

(37)

The closure
(
36
)
is a modelling simplification: it neglects the vertical covariance between concentration and velocity. A more detailed extension would require additional information on the vertical structure of the concentration, for example through separate evolution equations for concentration moments. This extension is not used in the present reduced model and is left for future work.
The near-bed concentration
c_{b}
enters only the deposition closure. Following
[
bradford1999hydrodynamics
]
, we prescribe
c_{b}
algebraically from the depth-averaged concentration through
c_{b}=S_{b}c_{m}
, where
S_{b}
is the empirical near-bed concentration factor defined in
(
47
)
. Thus,
c_{m}
represents the concentration averaged over the water column, whereas
c_{b}
is the corresponding near-bed value used to evaluate deposition. Note that the suspended concentration is not treated as a passive scalar in the coupled SWEMED system. The depth-averaged concentration
c_{m}
enters the mixture density
\rho=\rho(c_{m})
in
(
6
)
, which appears in the hydrostatic pressure
(
10
)
and generates buoyancy contributions in the depth-averaged momentum and moment equations.

2.2.2
Bedload mass balance equation

We use the Exner equation
[
exner1925uber
,
8
]
to model the evolution of bedload transport, incorporating the principle of mass conservation for the sediment layer. This formulation represents sediment transport through a flux term, and the right-hand side of the equation includes a source term that describes erosion and deposition processes, which reads as

\partial_{t}h_{b}+\frac{1}{1-\psi}\partial_{x}Q_{b}=-F_{b},

(38)

where
h_{b}
is the bed elevation,
Q_{b}
is the solid sediment discharge, and
F_{b}
is the bed exchange rate, which accounts for erosion and deposition processes, and defined in
Section
2.2.3
. More explicitly, the Exner equation represents the rate of bed deformation. Active sediment transport and rapid bed deformation will occur if the flow entrains more sediment particles than it deposits.

The result of the Exner equation
(
38
)
is highly dependent on the choice of the sediment discharge formula,
Q_{b}
. We note that there is a plethora of formulas for the sediment discharge term. In this paper, we adopt the following formula
[
Garres
]

Q_{b}=sgn(\tau)\,Q\,\Phi(\theta),

(39)

where
Q=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\rho_{s}}{\rho_{w}}-1\right)g\,d_{s}^{3}}
is the characteristic discharge and
sgn(\cdot)
is the sign function. We chose the expression
(
39
)
for
Q_{b}
since it depends on the bottom shear stress, used in its non-dimensional form
\theta
and
Q_{b}
can be expressed as a function of
\theta
as
\Phi(\theta).
The dimensionless bottom shear stress
\theta
is also called Shields parameter
[
cordier2011bedload
]
and defined by

\theta=\frac{|\tau|}{g\,(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\,d_{s}}=\frac{\rho\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}}{g\,(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\,d_{s}},

(40)

where
\epsilon
is a dimensionless constant
[
garres2023general
]
,
u_{b}
is the velocity at bottom, as defined in
(
29
)
, and
d_{s}
is the diameter of a sediment particle.

There is a framework that includes many different formulas for
\Phi(\theta)
(see
[
camenen2006phase
,
fernandez1976erosion
,
meyer1948formulas
,
14
,
ribberink1994sediment
]
). In this work, we employ the Meyer-Peter
\And
Müller formula
[
meyer1948formulas
]

\Phi(\theta)=8(\theta-\theta_{c})_{+}^{3/2},

(41)

where
(\cdot)_{+}
is the positive part and
\theta_{c}
is the critical shear stress. Notably, sediment transport is initiated only when the Shields parameter
\theta
exceeds the critical threshold
\theta_{c}
.

2.2.3
Morphological conditions

The bed exchange rate
F_{b}
, used in
Section
2.1
and
Section
2.2
, represents a dynamic equilibrium between two effects:
(1) the sediment deposition due to gravitational force and (2) the sediment erosion from the interface due to turbulence. This balance is crucial for understanding sedimentary processes and is defined by

F_{b}=\frac{E-D}{1-\psi},

(42)

Here
E
and
D
denote the erosion and deposition rates, respectively, and
\psi
is the bedload porosity.

To close the system, we refer to
[
bradford1999hydrodynamics
,
del2023lagrange
,
garcia1993experiments
,
gonzalez2020robust
,
22
]
and take the following formulas for erosion and deposition.

The sediment erosion and deposition follow from
[
del2023lagrange
,
gonzalez2020robust
]
, as given by

E=\omega_{o}(1-\psi)E_{s},

(43)

and

D=\omega_{o}c_{b},

(44)

where

-

\omega_{o}
is the settling velocity determined by
[
22
]
,

\omega_{o}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{13.95\nu_{w}}{d_{s}}\right)^{2}+1.09\,\rho_{w}\left(\frac{\rho_{s}}{\rho_{w}}-1\right)gd_{s}}-\frac{13.95\nu_{w}}{d_{s}},

(45)

-

\nu_{w}
is the kinematic viscosity of water and
d_{s}
is the diameter of sediment particle,

-

E_{s}
is the sediment erosion coefficient and computed by
[
garcia1993experiments
]
,

E_{s}=\frac{1.3\times 10^{-7}\mathcal{Z}^{5}}{1+4.3\times 10^{-7}\mathcal{Z}^{5}},

(46)

-

\mathcal{Z}=\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{1}\sqrt{c_{D}}|u_{b}|}{\omega_{o}}\mathcal{R}^{\gamma_{2}}_{p}
with particle Reynolds number
\mathcal{R}_{p}=\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})gd_{s}}d_{s}}{\nu_{w}}
,
u_{b}
the bottom velocity, and
c_{D}
the bed drag coefficient,

-

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are two parameters depending on
\mathcal{R}_{p}

[
gonzalez2020robust
]

\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\left(1,0.6\right),&\text{if $\mathcal{R}_{p}>2.36$}\\
\left(0.586,1.23\right),&\text{if $\mathcal{R}_{p}\leq 2.36$}\end{cases}

-

c_{b}
is the near-bed volumetric sediment concentration used in the deposition law. Since the model transports only the depth-averaged concentration
c_{m}
, we follow
[
bradford1999hydrodynamics
]
and prescribe
c_{b}
algebraically as

c_{b}=c_{m}(t,x)\,S_{b},\qquad S_{b}=0.4\left(\frac{d_{s}}{D_{sg}}\right)^{1.64}+1.64,

(47)

where
S_{b}
is the empirical near-bed concentration factor and
D_{sg}
is the geometric mean size of the suspended sediment mixture. In this work, all sediment particles have the same diameter, so that
D_{sg}=d_{s}
and
S_{b}=2.04
.

2.3
Coupled hydro-morphodynamic model

By incorporating the derivations and definitions outlined in
Section
2.1
and
Section
2.2
, we obtain a closed Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEMED). In contrast to the existing HSWEM in
[
Garres
]
, SWEMED (i) introduces a sediment concentration equation, (ii) couples the variable sediment–water mixture density with the momentum equation and higher-order moments, and (iii) includes additional source terms arising from erosion and deposition. The resulting system consists a total of
N+4
coupled equations (
48
) for the set of convective variables
(h,hu_{m},h\alpha_{1},\ldots,h\alpha_{N},hc_{m},h_{b})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+4}
, where
N
denotes the highest degree of the Legendre polynomial expansions which is also called the order of the moment model.

\underbrace{1}_{\text{mass balance}}+\underbrace{1}_{\text{momentum equation}}+\underbrace{N}_{\text{moment equations}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underbrace{1}_{\text{sediment concentration}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underbrace{1}_{\text{bedload mass balance}}}=\,N+4

Thus the coupled model is formulated as follows

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(hu_{m})&&=\frac{E-D}{1-\psi},\\
&\partial_{t}(hu_{m})+\partial_{x}\left(h\left(u_{m}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{2}}{2j+1}\right)+\frac{gh^{2}}{2}\right)&&=-gh\partial_{x}h_{b}-\frac{gh^{2}}{2\rho}(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\partial_{x}c_{m}\\
&&&\quad+\frac{(E-D)u_{b}}{1-\psi}-\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b},\\
&\partial_{t}(h\alpha_{i})+\partial_{x}\left(2hu_{m}\alpha_{i}+h\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}A_{ijk}\right)&&=u_{m}\partial_{x}(h\alpha_{i})-\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}B_{ijk}\alpha_{k}\partial_{x}(h\alpha_{j})\\
&&&\quad+(2i+1)\frac{gh^{2}}{\rho}(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})\frac{\partial c_{m}}{\partial x}K_{i}\\
&&&\quad+\frac{E-D}{1-\psi}\left(\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(H_{ij}-G_{ij})\right)\\
&&&\quad-(2i+1)\left(\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}+\frac{\nu}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}C_{ij}\right)\\
&&&\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right),\\
&\partial_{t}(hc_{m})+\partial_{x}(hc_{m}u_{m})&&=E-D,\\
&\partial_{t}h_{b}+\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)&&=\frac{D-E}{1-\psi}.\end{aligned}\right.

(48)

We can write the complete system
(
48
)
as a first-order PDE with conservative, non-conservative, and source terms, which reads

\partial_{t}W+\frac{\partial F}{\partial W}\partial_{x}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}W}\,=B\partial_{x}W-A_{s}\partial_{x}W+S_{F}+S_{ED}.

(49)

In the left-hand side of
(
49
)
, the jacobian of the conservative flux is given by

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\partial F}{\partial W}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
gh-u_{m}^{2}-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\alpha_j^2}{2j+1}&2u_{m}&\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2\alpha_1}{2\cdot 1+1}&\dots&\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2\alpha_N}{2\cdot N+1}&0&0\\
-2u_{m}\alpha_{1}-\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}A_{1jk}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}&2\alpha_{1}&2u_{m}+2\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{11k}\alpha_{k}&\dots&2\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{1Nk}\alpha_{k}&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\hskip 85.35826pt\ddots&&&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\hskip-2.84526pt\ddots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
-2u_{m}\alpha_{N}-\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}A_{Njk}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}&2\alpha_{N}&2\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{N1k}\alpha_{k}&\dots&2u_{m}+2\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{NNk}\alpha_{k}&0&0\\
-c_{m}u_{m}&-c_{m}&0&\ldots&0&u_{m}&0\\
\delta_{h}&\delta_{q}&\delta_{q}&\dots&\delta_{q}&\delta_{c}&0\end{pmatrix},$}}

where

\displaystyle\delta_{q}=\frac{\partial}{\partial_{hu_{m}}}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)=\frac{24Q}{1-\psi}sgn(\tau)\frac{\rho\epsilon}{g(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w})d_{s}}\left(\theta-\theta_{c}\right)_{+}^{1/2}\frac{u_{b}}{h},

(50a)

\delta_{h}=\frac{\partial}{\partial_{h}}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)=-u_{b}\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{c_{m}(t,x)\left(\rho_{s}-\rho_{w}\right)}{2\rho}\right)\delta_{q},

(50b)

\delta_{c}=\frac{\partial}{\partial_{hc_{m}}}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)=u_{b}\displaystyle\left(\frac{\rho_{s}-\rho_{w}}{2\rho}\right)\delta_{q},

(50c)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial_{h\alpha_{i}}}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)=\delta_{q}\quad\quad i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\},

(50d)

with bottom velocity
u_{b}
and the Shields parameter
\theta
, defined in
(
29
)
and
(
40
)
, respectively.

On the right-hand side of
(
49
)
, the non-conservative matrix reads

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{gh(\rho-\rho_w)}{2\rho}&0&0&\dots&0&\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{-gh(\rho_s-\rho_w)}{2\rho}&-gh\\
\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{gh(\rho-\rho_w)}{2\rho}&0&u_{m}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{11k}\alpha_{k}&\dots&-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{1Nk}\alpha_{k}&\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{-gh(\rho_s-\rho_w)}{2\rho}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\hskip 85.35826pt\ddots&&&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\hskip-2.84526pt\ddots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{N1k}\alpha_{k}&\dots&u_{m}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}B_{NNk}\alpha_{k}&0&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix},$}}

and the sediment discharge matrix
A_{s}
is resulting from
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{ij}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{Q_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)
and reads

\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle A_{s}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{1j}\delta_{h}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{1j}\delta_{q}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{1j}\delta_{q}&\dots&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{1j}\delta_{q}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{1j\delta_{c}}&0\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{2j}\delta_{h}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{2j}\delta_{q}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{2j}\delta_{q}&\dots&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{2j}\delta_{q}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}G_{2j\delta_{c}}&0\\

0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.$}}

The source terms
S_{F,i}
and
S_{ED,i}
result from the combined effects of friction and the processes of erosion and deposition, respectively, and are given by

S_{F,i}=\begin{cases}0,&\quad\text{if $i=1,N+3,N+4,$}\\
-\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b},&\quad\text{if $i=2,$}\\
-(2i+1)\displaystyle\left(\epsilon|u_{b}|u_{b}+\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{h}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}C_{ij}\right),&\quad\text{if $i\in\left\{3,\ldots,N+2\right\},$}\end{cases}

and

S_{ED,i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{E-D}{1-\psi},&\text{if $i=1,$}\\
\displaystyle\frac{(E-D)u_{b}}{1-\psi},&\text{if $i=2,$}\\
\displaystyle\frac{E-D}{1-\psi}\left(\alpha_{i}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(H_{ij}-G_{ij})\right),&\text{if $i\in\left\{3,\ldots,N+2\right\},$}\\
E-D,&\text{if $i=N+3,$}\\
\displaystyle\frac{D-E}{1-\psi},&\text{if $i=N+4.$}\\
\end{cases}

The resulting system
(
49
)
can be written in the form

\partial_{t}\,W+A_{ED}(W)\partial_{x}\,W=S_{F}(W)+S_{ED}(W),

(51)

where we define the state vector of conservative variables as
W=(h,hu_{m},h\alpha_{1},\ldots,h\alpha_{N},hc_{m},h_{b})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+4}
. We express the transport matrix in explicit form as
A_{ED}(W)=\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial W}-B+A_{s}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+4)\times(N+4)}
. Additionally, the right-hand side of
(
51
)
models the source term, which arises from friction and the considerations of erosion and deposition.

Lower-order moments of SWEMED

, first-order
(N=1)
, second-order
(N=2)
, and third-order

2.3.1.1
SWEMED0

Considering

u(t,x,\zeta)=u_{m}(t,x)
. The zeroth-order Shallow Water Exner Moment model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEMED0) is identical to the standard Shallow Water Exner model with Erosion and Deposition (SWEED). The SWEMED0 model with state vector
W=(h,hu_{m},hc_{m},h_{b})^{T}
can be written in the form of
(
51
)
using the following definitions

\quad F=\begin{pmatrix}h\\

The characteristic polynomial of the first-order

P_{\mathrm{sed}}(\lambda)=0
in
(
71
)
is equivalent to
f_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)=d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
. Hence, the three roots of
P_{\mathrm{sed}}(\lambda)
are the intersection points of the cubic
f_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
and the line
d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
.

The line
d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
has slope
gh\delta_{q}
and vanishes at

\lambda=u_{b}-2\alpha_{1}=u_{m}-\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}.

(73)

For a fixed local state
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N+4}
, the value
u_{b}-2\alpha_{1}
gives the point where
d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
crosses the
\lambda
-axis. In the geometric construction shown in Figure
3
,
h
,
u_{m}
,
\alpha_{1}
, and
\delta_{q}
are fixed, while
u_{b}-2\alpha_{1}
is varied. The higher-order moments enter this quantity through
u_{b}
; in the full model, they may also influence the bedload derivatives.
The boundary of the three-real-root region is reached when two roots merge. In the cubic-line representation, this occurs when the line
d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
is tangent to the cubic
f_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)
. At a tangency point
\xi
,

f^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi)=d^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi)=gh\delta_{q},

(74)

where

f^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)=3\lambda^{2}-4u_{m}\lambda+u_{m}^{2}-gh-\alpha_{1}^{2},\qquad d^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)=gh\delta_{q}.

(75)

Solving
(
74
)
gives

\xi_{\pm}=\frac{2u_{m}\pm\sqrt{u_{m}^{2}+3\left(gh+\alpha_{1}^{2}+gh\delta_{q}\right)}}{3}.

(76)

Let
d_{-}(\lambda)
and
d_{+}(\lambda)
be the tangent lines to
f_{\alpha_{1}}

(
72
)
at
\xi_{-}
and
\xi_{+}
, respectively. They are given by

d_{\pm}(\lambda)=f_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})+gh\delta_{q}(\lambda-\xi_{\pm})=gh\delta_{q}(\lambda-\gamma_{\pm}),

(77)

where

\gamma_{\pm}=\xi_{\pm}-\frac{f_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})}{gh\delta_{q}}.

(78)

The numbers
\gamma_{-}
and
\gamma_{+}
are the zeros of the tangent lines
d_{-}(\lambda)
and
d_{+}(\lambda)
; they are not eigenvalues.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
h>0
and
\delta_{q}>0
. The cubic polynomial
P_{\mathrm{sed}}(\lambda)

(
71
)
has three distinct real roots if and only if

\min(\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+})<u_{b}-2\alpha_{1}<\max(\gamma_{-},\gamma_{+}).

(79)

Proof.

From
(
71
)
and
(
72
)
,

P_{\mathrm{sed}}(\lambda)=d_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda)-f_{\alpha_{1}}(\lambda).

Its critical points are
\xi_{-}
and
\xi_{+}
because

P^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sed}}(\xi_{\pm})=d^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})-f^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})=0.

A cubic polynomial has three distinct real roots if and only if its local maximum and local minimum have opposite signs. Using
(
77
)
and
(
78
)
,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{sed}}(\xi_{\pm})

\displaystyle=d_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})-f_{\alpha_{1}}(\xi_{\pm})

(80)

\displaystyle=gh\delta_{q}\left(\xi_{\pm}-(u_{b}-2\alpha_{1})\right)-gh\delta_{q}(\xi_{\pm}-\gamma_{\pm})

(81)

\displaystyle=gh\delta_{q}\left(\gamma_{\pm}-(u_{b}-2\alpha_{1})\right).

(82)

Since
gh\delta_{q}>0
, these two values have opposite signs exactly when

lies strictly between
\gamma_{-}
and
\gamma_{+}
. This proves
(
79
)
.

.

For

. In this case, hyperbolicity requires the three roots of

\tilde{A}_{ED}(W)
in
(

)

gives a condition under which

4
Numerical simulations

We numerically solve the HSWEMED system
(
65
)
using a second-order path-conservative Monotonic Upstream-Centered Scheme for Conservation Laws (MUSCL)
[
21
]
, combined with a local Rusanov scheme
[
6
,
17
]
. Since
(
65
)
contains non-conservative products, we evaluate the corresponding contributions along straight paths connecting the reconstructed states at each interface
[
7
,
15
]
. We also retain the internal path contribution associated with the linear reconstruction inside each cell
[
7
]
. We use the generalized minmod limiter with parameter
\theta=1.3
for the reconstruction
[
13
]
and a third-order, four-stage Runge-Kutta method for the time integration.

Figure
4
illustrates the MUSCL reconstruction on three neighbouring cells. At the interface
x_{i+1/2}
, the Rusanov artificial viscosity contribution is given by
[
6
,
17
]
.

a_{i+1/2}\Delta W_{i+1/2},\qquad\Delta W_{i+1/2}=W_{i+1/2}^{+}-W_{i+1/2}^{-}

(85)

where

a_{i+1/2}=\max\left\{\max_{k}\left|\lambda_{k}\!\left(A(W_{i+1/2}^{-})\right)\right|,\max_{k}\left|\lambda_{k}\!\left(A(W_{i+1/2}^{+})\right)\right|\right\}.

Here,
W_{i+1/2}^{-}
and
W_{i+1/2}^{+}
denote the reconstructed states at the interface.

:
MUSCL reconstruction on three neighbouring cells. The reconstructed states

and

define the left and right states at the interface
x_{i+1/2}
.

The standard MUSCL-Rusanov scheme does not preserve the water-at-rest equilibrium over a non-flat bottom.
In well-balanced finite-volume methods, stationary equilibria are commonly preserved by separating the equilibrium contribution from the perturbation that is advanced by the numerical scheme
[
audusse2004fast
,
13
,
koellermeier2022steady
]
. Motivated by this principle, we remove only the water-at-rest contribution from the Rusanov viscosity in the present HSWEMED discretization.

At water-at-rest equilibrium,

u_{m}=\alpha_{1}=\cdots=\alpha_{N}=c_{m}=0,\qquad h+h_{b}=\mathrm{constant},

and therefore

\Delta h=-\Delta h_{b}.

Although this jump corresponds to a stationary solution, the Rusanov viscosity
(
85
)
acts on the complete jump of the state vector
W
and perturbs the free surface
h+h_{b}
. To preserve the equilibrium, we remove the corresponding equilibrium contribution

\Delta W_{i+1/2}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\left(-\Delta h_{b,i+1/2},0,\ldots,0,\Delta h_{b,i+1/2}\right)^{T},

from the artificial viscosity term
(
85
)
. The corrected contribution is

a_{i+1/2}\left(\Delta W_{i+1/2}-\Delta W_{i+1/2}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right).

(86)

The correction is applied at every interface. We modify only the Rusanov viscosity, while the reconstructed states, fluxes, path contributions, and source terms remain unchanged. We refer to the resulting scheme as the well-balanced MUSCL-Rusanov scheme for the water-at-rest equilibrium
(\text{MUSCL}_{\mathrm{WB}})
.

We consider four main test settings: first, we verify the well-balanced property for smooth and discontinuous bottom configurations in Section
4.1
; second, we verify the accuracy of the second-order MUSCL-Rusanov scheme in Section
4.2
; third, we apply the scheme to the academic dam-break problem in Section
4.3
; and fourth, we compare the numerical results for different models with laboratory dam-break experiments in Section
4.4
.

5
Conclusion

We developed a one-dimensional coupled hydro-morphodynamic model to simulate sediment transport, incorporating bedload and suspended load dynamics with vertical velocity variations. The derived model is based on the shallow water moment framework, which governs the flow hydrodynamics, volumetric sediment concentration, and morphological evolution through the Exner equation. Using the moment approach allows us to recover the vertical structure of the horizontal velocity of the fluid, thereby approximating the velocity close to the bottom. The bottom velocity is important, as its approximation influences sediment discharge and erosion rates and, consequently, the bedload and suspended load transport. To close the system, we integrated the Meyer-Peter & Müller formula for sediment discharge, empirical laws for erosion and deposition, non-linear Manning type friction for bed resistance, and Newtonian friction to represent fluid shear stresses. The reduced model is called the SWEMED and evolves the depth-averaged concentration
c_{m}
, while the near-bed concentration required in the deposition closure is prescribed algebraically from
c_{m}
.

For the hyperbolicity analysis, we applied the regularization technique of
[
koellermeier2020analysis
]
to the moment block of SWEMED and obtained the HSWEMED. We derived the characteristic polynomial of the regularized transport matrix and obtained a real-root condition for the sediment-coupled cubic factor.
The analysis shows that the moment order affects the eigenvalue structure. For even moment orders, strict hyperbolicity can hold under the stated real-root and separation conditions. For odd moment orders, a repeated eigenvalue appears, so strict hyperbolicity is lost; under the same real-root condition, the regularized HSWEMED transport matrix is weakly hyperbolic.

The numerical results were obtained with a second-order path-conservative MUSCL-Rusanov finite-volume scheme. For the water-at-rest equilibrium, we introduced a well-balancing correction of the Rusanov viscosity. The corrected scheme preserves a constant free surface over both smooth and discontinuous bottoms, while the standard MUSCL-Rusanov scheme perturbs this equilibrium. The reference-convergence test gives rates consistent with the expected second-order behaviour of the scheme. The dam-break simulations then show the effect of the new moment-based erosion-deposition model. Compared with the standard depth-averaged SWEED, which uses only the mean velocity in the sediment transport closures, HSWEMED uses the moment-based bottom velocity. In the tested configurations, this gives a lower bottom velocity and therefore less bottom erosion, especially for the PVC bed. Compared with HSWEM, which includes the moment structure but neglects erosion, deposition, and suspended load, HSWEMED also accounts for the exchange of sediment between the water column and the erodible bed. The comparison with the laboratory experiments of
[
20
]
shows that HSWEMED gives a better description of the PVC bottom evolution in the tested case, while the differences between the models are smaller for sand and discrepancies remain near the hydraulic-jump region.

Ongoing work is directed toward equilibrium stability and steady-state analyses of the HSWEMED model. Future work should also consider improved numerical treatments for wet/dry fronts in higher-order moment models, including positivity-preserving and asymptotic-preserving schemes. Another possible extension is to introduce an independent vertical structure for the suspended concentration through additional concentration moments.

The projection on vertical coupling term reads

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\partial_{\zeta}\left[\tilde{u}\left(\partial_{x}\left(h\int_{0}^{\zeta}(u_{m}-\tilde{u})\,d\hat{\zeta}\right)\right)\right]\,d\zeta-\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\partial_{\zeta}\left(\tilde{u}\frac{\zeta F_{b}}{1-\psi}\right)\,d\zeta-\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\partial_{\zeta}\left(\tilde{u}\partial_{t}h_{b}\right)d\zeta

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{i}\partial_{\zeta}
