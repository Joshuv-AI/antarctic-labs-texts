Equivariant Hypergraph Diffusion for Crystal Structure Prediction
Yang Liu, Chuan Zhou, Shuai Zhang, Peng Zhang, Xixun Lin, Shirui Pan

Abstract
Crystal Structure Prediction (CSP) remains a fundamental challenge with significant implications for the development of new materials and the advancement of various scientific disciplines. Recent developments have shown that generative models, particularly diffusion models, hold great promise for CSP. However, traditional graph-based representations, where atomic bonds are modeled as pairwise graph edges, fail to fully capture the intricate high-order interactions essential for accurately representing crystal structures. In this work, we propose a novel approach that utilizes hypergraphs to represent crystal structures, providing a more expressive abstraction for modeling multi-way atomic interactions. By adopting hypergraphs, we can effectively capture complex high-order relationships and symmetries, such as permutation and periodic translation invariance, which are crucial for characterizing crystal structures. In this work, we propose the E quivariant H ypergraph Diff usion Model ( EH-Diff ), a generative model designed to take advantage of the symmetry-preserving properties of hypergraphs. EH-Diff exploits these features to offer an efficient and accurate method for predicting crystal structures with a strong theoretical justification to preserve invariance properties. Empirically, we conduct extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets, and the results demonstrate that EH-Diff outperforms state-of-the-art CSP methods with only one sample.

Crystal Structure Prediction (CSP), a long-standing challenge in the physical sciences since the early 2000s
(

;

)
, aims to determine the stable 3D structure of a compound based solely on its chemical composition. Given the fundamental role of crystals in a wide range of materials
(
Steiger 2005
;
Cartwright & Mackay 2012
)
, accurately predicting their spatial configuration is crucial
(
Wooster 1953
;
Sato 2016
)
for understanding the physical and chemical properties that govern their applications in various academic and industrial domains, including drug development
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Bannan et al. 2025
)
, battery technologies
(
Xie & Grossman 2018
;
Yin et al. 2024
)
, and catalysis
(
Bao et al. 2023
)
.

Conventional CSP methods mainly use density functional theory (DFT)

. Predicting crystal properties remains challenging because of the inherent complexity of crystalline materials. Crystalline structures, characterized by periodic repetition of a unit cell
(

)
in a 3D lattice, feature highly ordered atomic arrangements. The physical properties of these materials, including electronic, mechanical, and thermal characteristics, depend on their atomic configurations.

Recent advances in AI4Material research have utilized graph-based deep learning models
(
Xie & Grossman 2018
;
Xie et al. 2021
)
, with a particular focus on diffusion models
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Jiao et al. 2024
)
. These models employ graph-based diffusion techniques to generate multiple atoms while preserving the invariant properties within the models. However, traditional graph-based approaches, including graph neural networks and graph transformers, are restricted to pairwise relationships
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
, where atoms are represented as nodes and atomic bonds as edges. This
limitation
restricts their ability to model higher-order interactions, which are crucial to capturing the structural complexity of crystal materials. Crystal structures often exhibit complex interactions, such as shared lattice points and multi-atomic coordination
(
Voet & Rich 1970
;
Grimmer et al. 1974
)
, which are difficult to represent comprehensively using only pairwise edges.

To overcome the limitation above, we employ
hypergraphs
, a robust graph data structure capable of capturing high-order relationships, to model the structure of crystal materials. Hypergraphs offer significant advantages over traditional graph representations by naturally encoding multi-atom interactions through hyperedges that link more than two atoms. In this framework, the model can offer a more expressive means of capturing the intricate coordination and structural dependencies within crystal structures. Hypergraphs represent atomic arrangements, enabling analysis of crystal stability, coordination geometry, and bond distributions. Their ability to incorporate symmetries and periodicity makes them ideal for modeling the fundamental properties and behaviors of crystalline materials.

However, several
challenges
exist in the introduction of hypergraphs into CSP with generative models. First, a major challenge is effectively constructing hypergraph-based material representations. The next challenge involves developing diffusion models that operate on hypergraph data. Unlike traditional graph-based methods, hypergraph representations must capture complex multi-atomic interactions while preserving key structural invariants, such as permutation and periodic translation symmetries inherent in crystalline materials. Moreover, designing a hypergraph-based diffusion model is critical, as it introduces additional complexities. The model must simultaneously ensure that these symmetries are maintained throughout both the forward and reverse diffusion processes. This necessitates the integration of equivariant architectures capable of handling the high-order interactions encoded in hypergraphs, thereby ensuring accurate and symmetry-consistent predictions of crystal structures.

In this work, we address the CSP problem by using a hypergraph-based modeling approach and we propose the Equivariant Hypergraph Diffusion Model, a novel framework designed to capture high-order interactions while preserving intrinsic symmetries and invariance properties of crystal structures (defined in
Section

2.3
). It simultaneously generates the lattice vectors and fractional coordinates of all atoms by employing a proposed denoising hypergraph model with periodic translation (defined in
Section

3
). Through an iterative process of expansion and refinement, our framework captures complex structural dependencies and trains a denoising diffusion model specifically for CSP, while preserving invariance properties (theoretical justification provided in
Section

3.2
). Empirically, we conduct comprehensive experiments across four real-world datasets and results position EH-Diff as a reliable approach for modeling complex crystalline materials, outperforming simple graph-based diffusion models.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We formalize the Crystal Structure Prediction (CSP) problem by introducing a hypergraph modeling framework to describe high-order atomic interactions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to represent crystal structures beyond traditional pairwise relationships, offering a novel perspective on capturing complex atomic coordination.

•

We propose the
E
quivariant
H
ypergraph
Diff
usion Model (
EH-Diff
), a novel framework that integrates hypergraph-based representation learning with a diffusion process. EH-Diff effectively captures high-order atomic interactions while explicitly preserving key symmetries, including permutation and periodic translation invariance, which are fundamental properties of crystal structures.

•

We evaluate the proposed framework through extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets, demonstrating its superior performance compared to state-of-the-art generative models with only one sample.

Problem Setup and Analysis

In this section, we formally define the problem and provide a concise theoretical analysis of the problem. Some basic mathematical definitions are given in

.

2.1
Representation of Crystal Structure

We introduce two frameworks for representing crystal structures and define the CSP task, providing a foundation for modeling atomic configurations in crystal lattices and predicting material behaviors.

Cartesian Coordinate System

A three-dimensional crystal is defined as an infinite periodic arrangement of atoms in a three-dimensional space, characterized by the smallest repeating unit called the unit cell. The unit cell can be represented by a triplet
\mathbf{M}_{C}=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{L})
, where
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times N}
denotes the set of one-hot encoded representations of atom types,
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times N}
represents the Cartesian coordinates of the atoms, and
\mathbf{L}=[\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}]\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the lattice matrix, which contains three basis vectors that describe the periodicity of the crystal. The infinite periodic structure of the crystal can be formally expressed as:

\displaystyle\{(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i})|\mathbf{a}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathbf{a}_{i},\,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{L}\mathbf{k},\,\forall\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\},

(1)

where the
j
-th element of the integer vector
\mathbf{k}
represents the integer translation along the
j
-th basis vector
\ell_{j}
in three-dimensional space for
j\in\{1,2,3\}
.

Fractional Coordinate System

Analogous to the Cartesian coordinate system, a unit cell can also be defined as a triplet
\mathbf{M}_{F}=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{L})
in the fractional coordinate system. In this system, a point represented by the fractional coordinate vector
\mathbf{f}=[f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}]^{\top}\in[0,1)^{3}
corresponds to the Cartesian coordinate vector
\mathbf{x}
, given by
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}\ell_{i}
, where
\ell_{i}
denotes the
i
-th basis vector of the lattice.

Remark 2.1
.

We adopt the fractional coordinate system, representing the crystal as
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}_{C}=(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{F},\mathbf{L})
, where the fractional coordinates of atoms in the unit cell form the matrix
\mathbf{F}\in[0,1)^{3\times N}
. The two frameworks are shown in
Figure

2
.

Remark 2.2
.

The Cartesian coordinate system
\mathbf{X}
uses three standard orthogonal basis vectors as coordinate axes. In crystallography, the fractional coordinate system is commonly employed to capture the periodicity of crystal structures
(
Nouira et al. 2018
;
Ren et al. 2022
)
, using lattice vectors
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
as a basis.

Crystal Structure Prediction (CSP)

CSP predicts the lattice matrix
\mathbf{L}
and the fractional coordinate matrix
\mathbf{F}
from the chemical composition
\mathbf{A}
. The goal is to learn the conditional probability distribution
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
, where
\mathbf{L}
represents the
3\times 3
lattice matrix that defines the unit cell’s periodic structure, and
\mathbf{F}
is the
3\times N
fractional coordinate matrix, where each column represents the fractional coordinates of an atom within the unit cell. Thus, CSP involves learning
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
such that:
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{f}_{i}|\mathbf{A},\mathbf{L})
, where
\mathbf{f}_{i}
denotes the fractional coordinates of the
i
-th atom, and the prediction of
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{F}
minimizes the error between generated and true atomic configurations.

2.2
Hypergraph Modeling of Crystal Structure

Definition 2.3

(Hypergraph)
.

Consider a set of individuals
\mathbf{V}=\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
connected by hyperedges
\mathbf{E}=\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
, forming a hypergraph
\mathbf{H}=\{\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E}\}
consisting of
n
nodes and
m
hyperedges, where each hyperedge can connect any number of nodes.

Let
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represent the node attributes, with
\mathbf{x}_{v}
indicating the attribute of node
v
. The degree of node
v
, denoted as
d_{v}
, is defined by
d_{v}=|\{\mathbf{e}\in\mathbf{E}:\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{e}\}|
. Moreover, let
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{D}_{e}
represent the diagonal degree matrix for nodes in
\mathbf{V}
and the sub-matrix corresponding to nodes within a hyperedge
\mathbf{e}
, respectively. More formal definition of hypergraph can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Building on the previous definition of hypergraph
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
, we extend this concept and its key attributes to the field of crystallography. This extension provides a more detailed framework for understanding the structural and dynamic properties of crystalline materials, thereby introducing new analytical tools and methodologies that enhance the study of crystallography.

Definition 2.4

(Hypergraph-based Crystal)
.

Formally, let the hypergraph
\mathbf{H}_{\text{crystal}}=(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E})
represent a crystal unit cell, where
\mathbf{M}
denotes the unit cell,
\mathbf{V}
represents the set of atoms, and
\mathbf{E}
denotes the set of hyperedges capturing the bonds or interactions between these units, potentially involving more than two atoms simultaneously.

Remark 2.5
.

The hypergraph-based framework models crystal lattices by representing atoms and interactions as nodes and hyperedges, enabling the exploration of complex multi-atom relationships beyond pairwise bonding. This approach facilitates studies in material science, including topological properties, collective atomic behaviors, and novel crystalline structures, advancing our understanding of the structural and dynamic aspects of crystalline materials.

2.3
Symmetries of Crystal Structure

Crystals differ fundamentally from molecules in that they consist of a repeating unit cell organized within a regular three-dimensional lattice. This periodic structure endows crystals with unique quantum symmetries, including invariance under the orthogonal group
O(3)
and periodic translational invariance within the fractional coordinate system. These symmetries play a critical role in defining the physical and quantum mechanical properties of crystalline materials, distinguishing them from discrete molecular entities.

Definition 2.6

(Atom Permutation Invariance)
.

For any permutation
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{S}_{N}
(where
\mathbb{S}_{N}
represents the symmetric group of degree
N
), the distribution
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
remains invariant under the transformation
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}\mathbf{P}|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P})
. This indicates that reordering the atoms does not affect the underlying distribution, implying the inherent permutation symmetry of the atomic configuration.

Definition 2.7

(
O(3)
Invariance))
.

For any orthogonal transformation
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
satisfying
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
, the distribution
p(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
is equivalent to
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
. This invariance indicates that any rotation or reflection of
\mathbf{L}
does not alter the distribution.

Definition 2.8

(Periodic Translation Invariance)
.

For any translation
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
, the distribution
p(\mathbf{L},w(\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{1}^{\top})|\mathbf{A})
is equivalent to
p(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F}|\mathbf{A})
. Here, the function
w(\mathbf{F})=\mathbf{F}-\lfloor\mathbf{F}\rfloor\in[0,1)^{3\times N}
returns the fractional part of each element in
\mathbf{F}
, and
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 1}
is a vector with all elements set to one. This invariance indicates that any periodic translation of
\mathbf{F}
does not alter the distribution
\overline{p}
.

Proposition 2.9
.

\mathbf{H}_{\text{crystal}}
exhibits permutation invariance with respect to the potentials, reflecting the inherent symmetry properties of crystalline structures.

Remark 2.10
.

It should be noted that the defined crystal symmetries are consistent with those described in the Cartesian coordinate system
(
Xie et al. 2021
;
Yan et al. 2022
)
. Specifically, rotations or reflections of the lattice
\mathbf{L}
have no impact on the fractional coordinates of the atoms. Consequently, the invariance
E(3)
under the Cartesian coordinate system can be reduced to the invariance
O(3)
of
\mathbf{L}
alone. Furthermore, since the fractional coordinate system inherently reflects the periodicity of the crystal structure, we can derive a more straightforward formulation of periodic translation invariance without the need to introduce corner points, as previously required under the Cartesian coordinate system.

3
The Proposed Model: EH-Diff

3.1
Diffusion Model on Crystal Structure

Diffusion on
\mathbf{F}
.
To adhere to the bounded domain of
\mathbf{F}
, we utilize the score-based generative model
(
Song & Ermon 2020
)
with the wrapped normal distribution
(
De Bortoli et al. 2022
)
, which has shown effectiveness in the molecular conformer generation task
(
Jing et al. 2022
)
. The forward diffusion process is defined as
q(\mathbf{F}_{t}|\mathbf{F}_{0})\propto\sum_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\times N}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{F}_{t}-\mathbf{F}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}\|_{F}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)
. Here,
\sigma_{t}
is an exponential annealing schedule, and
\mathbf{F}_{t}=w(\mathbf{F}_{0}+\sigma_{t}\epsilon_{\mathbf{F}})
with
\epsilon_{\mathbf{F}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.
q(\mathbf{F}_{t}|\mathbf{F}_{0})
approaches the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{0},1)
when
T
is sufficiently large. For the backward process, we apply the predictor-corrector method
(
Song & Ermon 2020
)
to reconstruct
\mathbf{F}_{0}
from
\mathbf{F}_{T}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{0},1)
. The estimated score
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}
is still provided by the denoising model
\phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{L}_{t},t)
. The training loss for score matching is given as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{F}}&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{F}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{F}_{t}|\mathbf{F}_{0}),t\sim\mathcal{U}(1,T)}\\
&\quad\Big[\lambda_{t}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{F}_{t}}\log q\left(\mathbf{F}_{t}|\mathbf{F}_{0}\right)-\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}\left(C_{t},t\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\Big].\end{split}

(2)

Here,
\lambda_{t}=1/\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{F}_{t}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{F}_{t}}\log q\left(\mathbf{F}_{t}|\mathbf{F}_{0}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]
can be approximated by Monte Carlo sampling methods.

Diffusion on
\mathbf{L}
.
We employ the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM)
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
to diffuse and generate
\mathbf{L}
. The forward diffusion process is defined as a Markov chain
q(\mathbf{L}_{t}|\mathbf{L}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\mathbf{L}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})
,
where the variance schedule
\{\beta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is fixed with
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
. This process progressively transforms
L_{0}
into the normal prior distribution
p(\mathbf{L}_{T})=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
for a sufficiently large
T
, since
q(\mathbf{L}_{t}|\mathbf{L}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}_{t};\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{L}_{0},(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{I})
. For the reverse generation process, we model the reverse dynamics as a conditional Markov chain with learnable transitions
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}|\mathbf{L}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{L}_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{L}_{t},t))
. Here, the mean
\mu_{\theta}
is given by
\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{L}_{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{L}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}}\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},t)\right)
, and the denoising term
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
is provided by the denoising model
\phi_{\theta}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{L}_{t},t)
, which will be detailed later. The training loss is formulated as the expected
\ell_{2}
-distance between the ground truth
\epsilon_{\mathbf{L}}
and the estimated
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_{\mathbf{L}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),t\sim\mathcal{U}(1,T)}\left[\|\epsilon_{\mathbf{L}}-\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(3)

3.2
Denoising Model

Here we introduce our well-designed denoising model
\phi(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{F},\mathbf{A},t)
that produces
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
and
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}
. Let
\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}=\left[\mathbf{h}_{1}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_{2}^{(\ell)},\cdots,\mathbf{h}_{N}^{(\ell)}\right]
denote the node representations of the
\ell
-th layer. The input feature is given by
\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(0)}=\operatorname{MLP}(f_{\text{atom}}(\mathbf{a}_{i}),f_{\text{position}}(t))
, where
f_{\text{atom}}
and
f_{\text{position}}
are the atomic embedding and sinusoidal positional encoding
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
, respectively.

Inspired by the Equivariant Hypergraph Neural Networks (EHNN) framework proposed by
Kim et al. 2022
, we construct our model using an unfolded layer architecture. The
\ell
-th layer of this framework (EHNN-MLP) can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{i}}^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{MLP}\left(\{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell-1)}\}_{i\in\mathbf{i}},\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L},\psi(\sum_{i,j\in\mathbf{i}}(f_{j}-f_{i}))\right),\\
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{i}}\frac{\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{i}}^{(\ell)}}{|\mathbf{i}|},\\
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell-1)}+\operatorname{MLP}\left(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\ell-1)},\{\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{i}}^{(\ell)}\}_{i\in\mathbf{i}}\right),\end{array}\right.

where
\psi:(-1,1)^{3}\to[-1,1]^{3\times K}
is a Fourier Transformation function and
\psi
is periodic translation invariant.

After
L
layers of message passing conducted on the fully connected graph, the lattice noise
\hat{\mathbf{\epsilon}}_{L}
is acquired by a linear combination of
\mathbf{L}
, with the weights given by the final layer, and the fractional coordinate score
\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{F}}
is output by
\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{F}}[:,{i}]=\varphi_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\mathbf{L})})
. We state them as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L}\varphi\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(L)}\right),\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{F}}=\Big[\varphi_{F}(h_{i}^{L})\Big]_{i=1}^{N},

(7)

where
\varphi
is an
\operatorname{MLP}
with output shape as
3\times 3
,
\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{F}}[:,{i}]
defines the
i
-th column of
\hat{{\epsilon}}_{\mathbf{F}}
, and
\varphi_{{F}}
is an MLP on the final representation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Propositions regarding
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
and
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}
:

•

\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
is
O(3)
equivariant;

•

\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}
is periodic translation invariant.

Hence, the generated distribution by EH-Diff is periodic
E(3)
invariant.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
exhibits
O(3)
equivariance, then the corresponding learned marginal distribution
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{L}_{0})
will be invariant under
O(3)
transformations. Similarly, if
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{F}}
is invariant in periodic translations, the learned marginal distribution
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{F}_{0})
will also maintain periodic translation invariance.

3.3
CSP Workflow

Training via Hypergraph Diffusion.

Algorithm

1
outlines the training procedure for the EH-Diff model. The process begins by taking as input the atom features, fractional coordinates, and lattice of the crystal, along with the number of diffusion steps. Initially, noise is sampled for both the lattice and the fractional coordinates from a normal distribution. A random diffusion step is selected from a uniform distribution. The lattice and coordinates are then updated using their respective diffusion equations, incorporating noise to approximate their final states. Next, the model parameters are updated using a denoising function, and the objective is to minimize the combined loss functions for the lattice and fractional coordinates. This iterative training process continues until the model effectively captures the desired crystal properties, thus providing a learned representation capable of predicting complex crystal structures.

Inference.

Algorithm

2
presents the inference procedure for the EH-Diff model, which is used to predict the crystal properties. The process starts with the input of atom features, fractional coordinates, lattice information, an empirical mean, hyperparameters, and the number of diffusion steps. Initially, a sample is drawn from a normal distribution centered on the empirical mean. The diffusion steps are then performed in reverse order, starting from the maximum step to one. For each step, the noise is sampled if the current step is greater than one, and the predicted value is updated using the denoising function derived from the trained model. At each step, the prediction is refined by adjusting the combination of the previous state, mean, and noise components, modulated by hyperparameters. The final output, after all of the diffusion steps have been completed, is the predicted value representing the crystal property of interest.

Experimental Setup

Dataset.
We conducted experiments on four datasets, each with distinct levels of complexity. The first dataset, Perov-5, comprises 18,928 perovskite materials with structurally similar configurations, containing 5 atoms per unit cell
(

;
Castelli et al. 2012b

The third dataset, MP-20, is derived from the Materials Project and contains 45,231 stable inorganic materials, which include most of the experimentally synthesized compounds with up to 20 atoms per unit cell
(
Jain et al. 2013

Lastly, the MPTS-52 dataset represents a more challenging extension of MP-20, consisting of 40,476 structures with up to 52 atoms per unit cell, ordered chronologically by their earliest publication year. Xie et al. 2021

Baseline.
This study distinguishes itself from previous research by focusing on a comparison of two main methodologies. The first methodology follows a sequential predict-and-optimize strategy. In this approach, a model is initially trained to predict material properties, and then optimization algorithms are employed to determine the most favorable structures. We adapt the approach described by

(

Conclusion

We introduce a hypergraph-based framework to model crystal structures and propose the novel Equivariant Hypergraph Diffusion Model (EH-Diff) for Crystal Structure Prediction (CSP). This approach effectively captures the intricate, higher-order interactions inherent in crystalline materials, offering a more comprehensive representation of their structural properties. Using hypergraphs, EH-Diff provides a more intuitive representation of crystal structures, effectively capturing essential symmetries and periodicities. Our experimental results demonstrate that EH-Diff out-
performs existing state-of-the-art CSP methods.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

Let
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots\}
represent a set, and
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
represent an ordered tuple. The notation
[n]
is used to denote the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. The space of order-
k
tensors is denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}\times d}
, where
d
represents the feature dimensionality. For an order-
k
tensor
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}\times d}
, a multi-index
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in[n]^{k}
is used to index the element
\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{i}}=\mathbf{A}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathbb{S}_{n}
denote the set of all permutations of the set
[n]
. A permutation
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
acts on a multi-index
\mathbf{i}
by permuting its components, i.e.,
\pi(\mathbf{i})=(\pi(i_{1}),\ldots,\pi(i_{k}))
, and it acts on the tensor
\mathbf{A}
by permuting the corresponding tensor indices, such that the transformed tensor
(\pi\cdot\mathbf{A})_{\mathbf{i}}=\mathbf{A}_{\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{i})}
.

Consider hypergraph data represented as
\mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{A})
, where
\mathbf{V}
denotes a set of
n
nodes, and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}\times d}
is a tensor that encodes the features of the hyperedges
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
. The order
k
of the tensor
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to the type of the hypergraph. Specifically, a first-order tensor encodes a set of node-specific features (e.g., point clouds), where
\mathbf{A}_{i}
represents the feature associated with node
i
. A second-order tensor encodes pairwise edge features (e.g., adjacency), where
\mathbf{A}_{i_{1},i_{2}}
denotes the feature of the edge
(i_{1},i_{2})
. More generally, an order-
k
tensor encodes the features of hyperedges (e.g., mesh normals), where
\mathbf{A}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
represents the feature of the hyperedge
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
. Ultimately, this framework leads to the well-established concept of hypergraphs with undirected hyperedges of arbitrary order.

Hypergraph as a Sequence of Higher-order Tensors.

The sequence representation of a hypergraph
(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{X})
with a maximum hyperedge order
K
consists of a sequence of
k
-uniform hypergraphs for all
k\leq K
, denoted as

(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)})_{k\leq K}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E}^{(:K)},\mathbf{X}^{(:K)}),

where
\mathbf{E}^{(k)}
represents the set of all order-
k
hyperedges in
E
, and
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}
is a matrix formed by stacking the features
\{\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{e}}\mid\mathbf{e}\in\mathbf{E}^{(k)}\}
for each hyperedge
\mathbf{e}
in
\mathbf{E}^{(k)}
.

In practical scenarios involving undirected hypergraphs, a hypergraph
\mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{X})
is typically defined by a set of
n
nodes
\mathbf{V}
, a set of
m
hyperedges
\mathbf{E}
, and a feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
that encodes the attributes of the hyperedges. Each hyperedge
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbf{E}
is a subset of the node set
\mathbf{V}
, and the order of the hyperedge,
|\mathbf{e}|
, characterizes its type. For example, a first-order edge
\{i\}
corresponds to the
i
-th node, a second-order edge
\{i,j\}
represents a pairwise link between the
i
-th and
j
-th nodes, and in general, an order-
k
edge
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
refers to a hyperedge that connects
k
nodes. The feature associated with each hyperedge
\mathbf{e}
is denoted by
\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that both node and hyperedge features are
d
-dimensional. To accommodate possible variations in dimensionalities, we define
d=d_{\mathbf{v}}+d_{\mathbf{e}}
, where
d_{\mathbf{v}}
and
d_{\mathbf{e}}
represent the dimensionalities of the node and hyperedge features, respectively. The node features are placed in the first
d_{\mathbf{v}}
channels, while the hyperedge features occupy the remaining
d_{\mathbf{e}}
channels. It is important to note that the concept of hypergraphs
(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{X})
presented here does not directly correspond to the higher-order tensors
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}\times d}
discussed earlier. In contrast to these tensors, the hypergraphs we focus on are sparse, have undirected hyperedges, and each hyperedge involves a unique set of node indices.

Permutation Equivariance.
In the context of (hyper)graph learning, the goal is to design a function
f
that takes a higher-order tensor
A
as input and produces an output. Since the tensor representation of a graph undergoes substantial changes when the node labeling is permuted, it is essential that the function
f
exhibit invariance or equivariance with respect to node permutations. Specifically, if the output is a scalar or vector,
f
must be permutation-invariant, meaning that

f(\pi\cdot A)=f(A)

holds for any permutation
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
. Conversely, if the output is a tensor,
f
must be permutation-equivariant, ensuring that

f(\pi\cdot A)=\pi\cdot f(A)

for all
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}

(
Kim et al. 2022
)
. When
f
is implemented as a neural network, typically consisting of a sequence of linear transformations and non-linear activation functions, the task simplifies to the design of linear layers that are both invariant and equivariant under permutations.

Consider the generative Markov process defined as
p(x_{0})=p(x_{T})\int p(x_{0:T-1}\mid x_{t})\,dx_{1:T}
. Suppose that the prior distribution
p(x_{T})
is
G
-invariant and that the Markov transitions
p(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})
, for
0<t\leq T
, are
G
-equivariant. Then, the marginal distribution
p(x_{0})
is guaranteed to be
G
-invariant as well.

Proof.

In the context of the transition probability defined in Eq.
3
, we can express it as follows:

p(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{N}\Big(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mid a_{t}\big(\mathbf{L}_{t}-b_{t}\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A},t)\big),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}\Big),

where the parameters are defined as:

a_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}},\quad b_{t}=\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}},\quad\sigma_{t}^{2}=\beta_{t}\cdot\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}.

Here:

•

\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
represent the time-dependent coefficients associated with the variance schedule.

•

\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
accumulates the product of
\alpha_{s}
values up to time
t
, capturing the cumulative effect of the noise process.

•

\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A},t)
is the predicted noise function conditioned on the current state
\mathbf{L}_{t}
, auxiliary features
\mathbf{F}_{t}
, adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, and time step
t
.

•

\sigma_{t}^{2}
represents the variance of the Gaussian distribution, ensuring consistency with the underlying stochastic process.

As the denoising term
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}
is
O(3)
-equivariant, we have
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{QL}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A},t)=\mathbf{Q}\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A},t)
, for any orthogonal transformation
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3},\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}
.

For the variable
\mathbf{L}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
, we have
\mathbf{QL}\sim\mathcal{N}(Q\bar{\mathbf{L}},\mathbf{Q}(\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})\mathbf{Q}^{\top})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}).
That is,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}\mid\bar{\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{QL}\mid\mathbf{Q}\bar{\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}).

For the transition probability
p(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A})
, we have

p(\mathbf{QL}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{QL}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mid a_{t}(\mathbf{L}_{t}-b_{t}\hat{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{L}}(\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A},t)),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I})=p(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{L}_{t},\mathbf{F}_{t},\mathbf{A}).

Given that the transition function exhibits
O(3)
-equivariance and the prior distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
is
O(3)
-invariant, it follows from
Lemma

D.1
that the marginal distribution
p(\mathbf{L}_{0})
is also
O(3)
-invariant. This conclusion leverages the interplay between the symmetry of the transition function and the invariance of the prior.
∎

Appendix E
Related Works

Crystal Structure Prediction

Traditional computational methodologies, as exemplified by
Pickard & Needs 2011
;
Yamashita et al. 2018
, have combined Density Functional Theory (DFT)
(
Kohn & Sham 1965
)
with optimization algorithms to locate local minima on the potential energy surface. While these methods achieve high accuracy, the computational demands of DFT calculations present a significant challenge, often necessitating a trade-off between computational efficiency and predictive precision. The proliferation of extensive crystal structure databases has enabled the deployment of machine learning techniques as alternative predictors of material energies, serving as a complement or substitute to DFT calculations, typically followed by optimization procedures
(
Jacobsen et al. 2018
;
Podryabinkin et al. 2019
;
Cheng et al. 2022
)
. These data-driven approaches have shown promise in enhancing the speed and scalability of materials discovery and design. Moving beyond the conventional predict-optimize framework, a new class of methods has emerged that utilizes deep generative models to infer stable crystal structures directly from data. These models employ various representations to encapsulate the structural information of crystals, including three-dimensional voxel grids
(
Gebauer et al. 2022
)
, distance matrices
(
Hu et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
, and direct 3D coordinate systems
(
Nouira et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2020
)
. By leveraging these advanced data representations
(
Jiao et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2024
)
, deep generative models contribute to the development of more efficient and accurate methods for predicting and designing novel crystalline materials.

Equivariant Models

Geometrically Equivariant Graph Neural Networks (EGNNs), which maintain
E(3)
symmetry, have emerged as powerful frameworks for the representation of physical entities
(
Schütt et al. 2018
;
Thomas et al. 2018
;
Satorras et al. 2021
)
. These networks have exhibited exceptional performance in the modeling of three-dimensional structures
(
Tran et al. 2023
)
. To adapt these capabilities to the context of periodic materials,
Xie & Grossman 2018
introduced a multi-graph edge construction method designed to capture periodicity through the connection of edges between neighboring lattices. Expanding on this foundation,
Yan et al. 2022
;
Jiao et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2024
incorporated periodic pattern encoding into a Transformer-based architecture, thereby further enhancing the ability to model the complex periodic properties of materials.

Remark E.1
.

CDVAE
(
Xie et al. 2021
)
and DiffCSP
(
Jiao et al. 2024
)
are similar to our work in their use of equivariant generative models to capture the underlying data distribution and generate crystal structures through a score-matching diffusion process. However, their primary focus is on ab initio crystal generation, relying on random sampling of chemical compositions, which differs from the CSP problem we address. The major distinction in our approach lies in how we model the crystal structures. Unlike CDVAE, DiffCSP, and EquiCSP, which use traditional molecular graph representations, we utilize hypergraph models to represent crystal structures. This allows us to capture high-order interactions effectively, providing a richer and more expressive framework for modeling complex crystalline materials, thus enhancing the generation process and achieving better predictive performance.
