Effects of androgen deprivation therapy on cerebral glucose metabolism in prostate cancer
Authors: Song Xue, Honghu Zhang, Yifan Jiang, Lianghua Li, Marcus Hacker, Gang Huang, Jianjun Liu, Xiang Li, Xiang Zhou
Published: 2026

Background

Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) represents a cornerstone of systemic treatment for prostate cancer and is widely applied across multiple disease stages [1, 2]. While its oncologic efficacy is well established, increasing clinical attention has been directed toward the potential adverse effects of long-term androgen suppression, particularly those involving the central nervous system. Androgens play an important role in maintaining neuronal function and cerebral energy homeostasis, and previous studies have reported associations between ADT and changes in cognition, mood, and quality of life in a subset of patients [3, 4].

In clinical practice, assessment of ADT-associated neurocognitive side effects relies largely on neuropsychological testing and patient-reported symptoms. Although informative, these approaches are inherently subjective and may be influenced by baseline cognitive status, comorbidities, and treatment-related fatigue. As a result, subtle or early treatment-related brain changes may go undetected. Objective in-vivo biomarkers capable of capturing cerebral effects of ADT in routine care remain limited. Cerebral glucose metabolism represents a well-established surrogate of neuronal activity. [18F]F-FDG PET has been extensively used to evaluate brain metabolism in neurodegenerative disorders and treatment-related brain changes [3, 5]. Importantly, FDG PET is already routinely performed in patients with prostate cancer for oncologic assessment. This creates a practical opportunity to assess cerebral metabolic effects of ADT using existing imaging data, without additional radiation exposure or changes to standard clinical workflows.

Results

Comparison before and after treatment

Paired comparisons of regional SUVr between pre-ADT and during-ADT [18F]F-FDG PET/MR scans identified six regions with significant pre–post differences after false discovery rate correction (q < 0.05). SUVr decreased in the fusiform gyrus (mean difference = − 0.021, SEM = 0.006, P = 0.0021, q = 0.0022), cerebellar gray matter (mean difference = − 0.022, SEM = 0.011, P = 0.0498, q = 0.0087), and cerebellar white matter (mean difference = − 0.028, SEM = 0.012, P = 0.0246, q = 0.0073). SUVr increased in the pars opercularis (mean difference = 0.025, SEM = 0.011, P = 0.0336, q = 0.0073), postcentral gyrus (mean difference = 0.027, SEM = 0.011, P = 0.0172, q = 0.0073), and precentral gyrus (mean difference = 0.015, SEM = 0.007, P = 0.0347, q = 0.0073). Time-dependent effects

In longitudinal mixed-effects models, fusiform gyrus SUVr showed a significant time-dependent decline with increasing ADT duration (β = −0.00108 per month, 95% CI: −0.00177 to − 0.00039; P = 0.002). No significant time-dependent trends were observed for cerebellar gray matter or cerebellar white matter. Individual longitudinal trajectories and model-derived monthly change estimates are shown in Fig. 1B and D.

Discussion

This paired [18F]F-FDG PET/MR study demonstrates that ADT is associated with selective, region-specific alterations in cerebral glucose metabolism rather than uniform global changes. Among the affected regions, the fusiform gyrus exhibited not only a significant reduction in glucose metabolism between pre- and during-ADT imaging but also a statistically significant, time-dependent decline with increasing duration of ADT exposure, suggesting a regional vulnerability to prolonged androgen suppression. Given the established role of the fusiform gyrus in higher-order visual processing, face recognition, and socio-emotional perception, this progressive metabolic reduction may represent a potential neural substrate underlying previously reported neurocognitive and behavioral changes during long-term ADT [6]. From a mechanistic perspective, androgen receptors are not restricted to classical endocrine-regulatory regions such as the hypothalamus; previous studies have also reported androgen receptor expression or androgen receptor-related signaling in the human temporal cortex, as well as in cortical, hippocampal, amygdalar, and cerebellar regions involved in cognitive and emotional regulation [7, 8]. Therefore, the metabolic decline observed in the fusiform gyrus and related regions may be consistent with a relative sensitivity of these areas to androgen deprivation, although this mechanistic interpretation remains exploratory.

In addition to cortical involvement, significant metabolic reductions were also observed in cerebellar gray and white matter. Although traditionally associated with motor coordination, the cerebellum is increasingly recognized as an integral component of distributed neural networks supporting cognitive, emotional, and attentional functions [9, 10]. The observed reduction in cerebellar glucose metabolism may therefore reflect broader effects of androgen deprivation on cerebral metabolic homeostasis rather than isolated motor-related changes. Notably, cerebellar metabolic alterations did not demonstrate significant time-dependent progression in longitudinal analyses, suggesting that these changes may represent early or relatively stable metabolic effects following the initiation of ADT; however, this interpretation should be confirmed in larger cohorts with more densely sampled longitudinal imaging. Moreover, because hormonal changes may influence cerebral glucose metabolism, and because androgen levels differ between men and women, whether systematic sex-related differences exist in brain [18F]F-FDG PET metabolism remains an interesting question for future investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Second, cognitive or behavioral assessments were not available; therefore, the observed regional metabolic changes could not be directly linked to specific neurocognitive or behavioral outcomes. Third, this study lacked a control group, such as prostate cancer patients who did not receive ADT, making it difficult to fully separate ADT-related effects from disease-related changes or other time-dependent factors. Fourth, because follow-up imaging after discontinuation of ADT was not available, we could not determine whether these cerebral metabolic alterations are reversible after treatment cessation. Finally, although patients with major prior systemic therapies were excluded, potential confounding effects from disease progression or subsequent treatments during follow-up cannot be completely ruled out. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, appropriate control groups, longitudinal cognitive assessments, and post-ADT follow-up imaging are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that ADT is associated with modest, region-specific alterations in cerebral glucose metabolism, as assessed by paired [18F]F-FDG PET/MR imaging. While the observed changes were subtle, they suggest that [18F]F-FDG PET/MR may provide a useful tool for further investigating central nervous system effects of ADT. Future prospective studies integrating standardized neuropsychological assessments will be necessary to clarify the clinical relevance and temporal characteristics of these metabolic alterations.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A single-center retrospective paired [18F]F-FDG PET/MR study included men with histologically confirmed prostate cancer who underwent two whole-brain [18F]F-FDG PET/MR examinations before ADT initiation (pre-ADT) and during treatment (during-ADT) with complete data for quantitative analysis. Patients receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or other systemic anticancer therapies before baseline imaging, those with insufficient PET image quality, or incomplete data were excluded. Forty-three patients were included; the median inter-scan interval was 176 days (range, 75–1874).

Androgen deprivation therapy Regimen

All 43 patients received GnRH agonist-based ADT, as shown in Table 1. GnRH agonists were administered as long-acting subcutaneous or intramuscular injections once monthly or every 3 months. Eight patients received GnRH agonist alone, whereas 35 patients received GnRH agonist plus an additional oral androgen-signaling inhibitor, including first-generation antiandrogens in 8 patients and second-generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitors in 27 patients. Among the latter, 23 patients received novel androgen receptor antagonists and 4 patients received androgen biosynthesis inhibitors targeting CYP17A1.

PET/MR acquisition

Patients fasted for ≥ 6 h and blood glucose was confirmed within an acceptable range prior to tracer administration. Whole-brain PET/MR was acquired approximately 45–60 min after intravenous [18F]F-FDG injection. PET emission data were acquired for 15 min in a dedicated brain acquisition. PET data were reconstructed using the HYPER DPR reconstruction algorithm, with a matrix size of 192 × 192, a field of view of 300 mm, and a voxel size of 1.6 × 1.6 × 2.0 mm. MR-based attenuation correction was performed using a five-tissue segmentation method. Scatter correction and time-of-flight correction were applied during reconstruction. No partial-volume correction was performed. PET reconstruction followed standard clinical procedures and was kept consistent within individuals. Anatomical MR images were used for subsequent brain segmentation.

Image processing and quantification

Automated brain segmentation was performed in individual space using an nnU-Net–based pipeline, with visual quality control by two nuclear medicine physicians. SUV was extracted for 39 predefined brain regions and converted to SUVr using volume-weighted whole-brain mean normalization. Whole-brain mean SUV did not differ significantly between pre-ADT and during-ADT scans (mean difference = − 0.25, P = 0.28), supporting whole-brain normalization for paired and longitudinal analyses.

Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests assessed intra-individual pre–post differences in regional SUVr. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR); nominal P values and FDR-corrected q values were reported. Time-dependent associations with ADT exposure were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models for selected regions, with SUVr as the dependent variable, time since ADT initiation (months) as a fixed effect, and subject-specific random intercepts. All tests were two-sided; P < 0.05 and FDR-corrected q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
