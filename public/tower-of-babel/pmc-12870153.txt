Recombinant Listeria expressing the HPV multivalent E6E7 protein has a broader spectrum of anti-tumor effects against cervical cancer
Authors: Shanping Gan, Jing Tang, Sihan Qin, Qian Ou, Zinan Chen, Zhaobin Chen, Chuan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer in the cervical region of the uterus is a major health issue for women. A live bacterial vector therapeutic vaccine is genetically engineered to stimulate a targeted immune response by integrating the target antigen gene into a live, attenuated vector. Listeria has become a focal point for bacterial vaccine vectors, owing to its distinctive intracellular parasitism and ability to process and present antigens. Antibiotic-resistant plasmids in vaccines present a dual risk, including the promotion of antibiotic resistance and in vivo instability. The implementation of a balanced lethal system can effectively mitigate both challenges.MethodsWe constructed therapeutic vaccinations for cervical cancer with dual-species Listeria vectors—attenuated Listeria monocytogenes (LM) with act A/plc B/dal/dat deletion (LM∆∆dd) and recombinant Listeria ivanovii (LI) whose hemolysin gene (ilo) is substituted with the hly gene of LM and with dal/dat deletion (LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd). Recombinant Listeria-vectored therapeutic vaccines for cervical cancer, expressing HPV6/11/16/18 E6E7 proteins, were constructed based on the balanced lethal system. The basic biological characteristics of the vaccines were described. The vaccinations were evaluated for biosafety, stability, and immunogenicity in mice, and their efficacy was assessed in subcutaneous HPV16/18 E6/E7-expressing tumor models.ResultsThis study demonstrates that recombinant Listeria expressing multivalent HPV E6/E7 proteins exhibit broad-spectrum anti-tumor effects against cervical cancer models. The study showed that the vaccination strains effectively expressed multivalent HPV antigen, exhibited plasmid stability in vitro, and had no significant impact on biochemical reactions and hemolytic activity. In mice, the vaccine strain was biologically safe, and the plasmid remained stable in vivo. A heterologous immunization strategy—LM prime-LI boost-LM final—was employed to immunize the mice, resulting in a robust, antigen-specific cellular immune response. In the subcutaneous HPV16/18 E6/E7-expressing tumor models, this vaccination protocol achieved complete tumor cure rates of 30% and 40%, respectively, and significantly extended the median survival time.ConclusionsOur objective was to evaluate the efficacy of the vaccine against subcutaneous HPV16/18 E6/E7-expressing tumor models, enhance its safety profile, and co-express multivalent HPV antigens to target a broader range of cervical cancer types.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15476-z.

Background

Cervical cancer, a malignancy of the uterine cervix, is among the most common reproductive tract cancers in women. According to the latest global cancer statistics report (excluding basal cell carcinoma) published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 2022 [1], cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer type among women in 25 countries and is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in 37 countries. Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a primary etiological factor in cervical cancer, with persistent infection by high-risk HPV (hrHPV) leading to malignancy [2]. The most common hrHPV types are HPV types 16 and 18 [3]. In contrast, low-risk HPV infections mainly cause wart-like lesions, with HPV types 6 and 11 responsible for 90–95% of such cases [4]. The currently approved HPV vaccines are preventative, designed to protect uninfected individuals against HPV infection [5]. However, there are significant age restrictions on their administration [6], and global vaccination rates remain low due to economic and social factors [7], contributing to the high incidence of cervical cancer. Traditional treatments for cervical cancer, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [8], have notable limitations [9], and the prognosis remains unsatisfactory.

Therapeutic vaccines induce specific anti-tumor immune responses, reduce recurrence, and exhibit fewer adverse effects compared to conventional treatments, making them a viable therapeutic option. Listeria monocytogenes (LM) has been extensively utilized in the development of antiviral and anti-tumor vaccines because of its exceptional ability to process and transport antigens intracellularly in a parasitic manner. Listeriolysin O (LLO), encoded by the hly gene, plays a key role in the escape of Listeria from phagosomes of both phagocytic and non-phagocytic origin [10, 11], as well as in mediating antigen cross-presentation. The HPV oncogenic proteins E6 and E7 are critical contributors to HPV-associated malignancies and are exclusively expressed in tumor cells, making them promising therapeutic targets for cervical cancer vaccines [12]. The ADXS11-001 vaccine, which encodes HPV16 E7 and a truncated fragment of LLO using LM as a vector, has shown promise in clinical studies. However, it has been suspended due to safety concerns associated with the vector, highlighting the urgent need to improve vector safety [13]. Listeria ivanovii (LI) is primarily pathogenic to sheep, with only rare instances of severe human invasive disease [14]. LI shares similar biological characteristics with LM, making it a promising vaccine vector [15]. However, the immunization efficiency of LI as a vaccine vector is lower than that of LM [16]. To enhance immunogenicity, we substituted the hemolysin gene (ilo) of LI with the hly gene of LM.

In the construction of Listeria-vectored vaccines, recombinant strains carrying plasmids that express exogenous antigens are typically screened by antibiotic resistance markers. Although this method helps maintain plasmid stability under antibiotic pressure, it poses the risk of antibiotic resistance gene dissemination, and plasmid loss is likely in the absence of antibiotic selection pressure [17]. To overcome this technological limitation, the balanced lethal system was genetically engineered to achieve no-antibiotic screening. In this system, essential nutrient synthesis genes in the host bacteria are knocked out to construct a nutrient-deficient strain, and the corresponding nutrient genes are integrated into the plasmid. As a result, the recombinant strains depend on nutrient genes carried by the plasmid for survival in the normal culture medium. Studies have shown that Listeria with defective dal and dat genes can effectively restore bacterial cell wall synthesis by reintroducing the dal gene. The resulting balanced lethal system is genetically stable both in vivo and in vitro, maintaining stable antigen presentation and inducing a highly efficient protective immune response [18].

In this study, attenuated LM with act A/plc B/dal/dat deletion (LM∆∆dd) and recombinant LI whose hemolysin gene (ilo) is substituted with the hly gene of LM and with dal/dat deletion (LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd) were used as vaccine vectors. Furthermore, two Listeria-vectored HPV multivalent therapeutic vaccine candidate strains were constructed by electroporating a plasmid carrying the dal gene and the N-terminal and C-terminal shuffled HPV 6, 11, 16, and 18 E6/E7 fusion antigen into the above nutrient-deficient Listeria strains.

The basic biological characteristics of the vaccine candidates were investigated, and the optimal combination of LM prime-LI boost-LM final immunization, established in a previous study, was utilized in a primary-booster-final immunization strategy to evaluate the biosafety, immunogenicity [19], and therapeutic effects of the vaccine candidates in a mouse model. The impact on subcutaneous HPV16/18 E6/E7-expressing tumor models was assessed to enhance vaccine safety and promote multivalent HPV immunization. Co-expression of multivalent HPV antigens overcomes the limitations of single-type vaccines and provides a broad-spectrum solution for the treatment of multiple types of cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

Plasmids, strains, and primers

The strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are shown in Tables S1, S2, and S3.

Mice and cell lines

C57BL/6J female mice (SPF grade, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Chengdu Dashuo Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., and housed at the Laboratory Animal Centre of West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University, following quarantine.

TC-1 cells, co-transformed with the HPV16 E6, E7 gene, and the ras gene in C57BL/6 mouse lung epithelial cells, were obtained from Beijing Silver Bauhinia Company.

U14-E6 cells, a monoclonal cell line of U14 cells overexpressing the HPV18 E6 gene, were sourced from Beijing Prime Biotech Co.

Construction of recombinant Listeria cervical cancer therapeutic vaccine strains expressing HPV multivalent E6E7 protein and vector strain

Construction of LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd

Plasmid pCW619-LI dal was electro-transferred into the LI∆ilo::hly (the LI strain whose ilo gene is substituted with the hly gene) sensory strain, and positive clones were selected using D-BE3 plates (BHI plates containing 400 µg/mL D-alanine and 3 µg/mL erythromycin) and PCR. The positive strains were passaged three times consecutively on D-BE3 plates at 42 °C, followed by six generations of culturing in D-BHI broth (BHI broth containing 400 µg/mL D-alanine) for homologous recombination. Homologous recombination in the LI∆ilo::hly strain was verified through resistance screening, PCR, and sequencing, resulting in the LI∆ilo::hly∆dal strain, in which the dal gene was knocked out. The knockout of the dat gene was performed after electro-transferred into the LI∆ilo::hly∆dal with pCW619-LI dat following the same method, resulting LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd.

Construction of non-resistant plasmids containing the target antigen fragments

Amino acid sequences for the E6 and E7 proteins of HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18 were obtained from the NCBI website. Fusion antigens were designed by alternating the amino-terminal (N-terminal) and carboxy-terminal (C-terminal) structural domains of E6 and E7 proteins from these four HPV types, while retaining a 16-amino acid overlapping region at the junction site [20]. The plasmid carrying the HPV multivalent E6E7 fusion antigen (Fig. S1) was synthesized, sequenced, and verified by Bioengineer Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and is named pCW635. The plasmid is stored in our laboratory.

PCR was used to amplify the vector plasmid pCW631 and the plasmid pCW635 containing the target antigen. The PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by gel recovery to obtain the linearized vector and insert fragments. The linearized vector and insert fragment were then ligated by seamless cloning. The ligated product was transformed into the DH5αΔasd receptor strain, and the plasmid was extracted and verified by PCR and sequencing. The correctly verified plasmid, which contained the target antigenic fragments as well as the asd and dal nutrient genes, was named pCW636.

Construction of vaccine strain and vector strains

The pCW636 plasmid was electro-transferred into the LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd and LMΔΔdd sensory strains, while pCW631 was electro-transferred into the LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd sensory strain. Single colonies grown on the electro-transferred plates were verified by PCR amplification by HPV-seq primers, asd gene primers, dal gene primers, and Listeria-specific primers (LM and LI). The PCR products were sent to Beijing Prime Tech for sequencing verification, and the correctly verified bacteria were named LI-HPVCW-2, LM-HPVCW-2, and LI-CWV-2, respectively.

The construction process of recombinant Listeria cervical cancer therapeutic vaccine strains and vector strains expressing HPV multivalent E6E7 proteins is depicted in the schematic diagram in Fig. S2.

Biological characterization of vaccine strain and vector strains

Protein expression of vaccine and vector strains

Bacterial intracellular and secreted proteins from the vaccine and vector strains were extracted using Lin’s method and quantified using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The proteins were separated by electrophoresis on an 8% separator gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was then blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder and incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibody (anti-HA mouse monoclonal antibody). Afterward, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h with secondary antibody (horseradish peroxidase-labelled goat anti-mouse antibody). The chemiluminescence reaction was developed using HRP chemiluminescence substrate.

Biochemical identification

The biochemical reactions of the vaccine strains LM-HPVCW-2 and LI-HPVCW-2, the vector strains LM-CWV-1 and LI-CWV-2, and the parental strains LMΔΔ (the LM strain with act A/plc B deletion) and LI∆ilo::hly were assessed using a fully automated strain identification and drug sensitivity analyzer (VITEK®2 Compact, Mérieux, France).

Growth curve determination

LM-HPVCW-2, LM-CWV-1, LMΔΔ, LI-HPVCW-2, LI-CWV-2, and LI∆ilo::hly were cultured in BHI broth overnight. A small aliquot of each bacterial solution was inoculated into 60 mL of fresh BHI broth to achieve an initial OD600 value of approximately 0.05. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The OD600 value of each bacterial solution is measured every hour, and the growth curve is plotted.

Hemolysis experiments

LM-HPVCW-2, LM-CWV-1, LMΔΔ, LI-HPVCW-2, LI-CWV-2, and LI∆ilo::hly were cultured to logarithmic growth in BHI broth, then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were diluted in sterile PBS to concentrations ranging from 2− 7, and 100 µL of each dilution was transferred to a 96-well U-shaped plate. PBS control wells were set up simultaneously. A 1% sheep erythrocyte suspension was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to observe hemolysis. Additionally, the strains were inoculated onto blood agar plates by streaking in circles and puncturing under aseptic conditions, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The growth and hemolysis of the strains on the blood agar plates were observed.

In vitro genetic stability experiments

The constructed strains LM-HPVCW-2, LM-CWV-1, LI-HPVCW-2, and LI-CWV-2 were continuously cultured in BHI broth for 25 generations. Bacterial samples from the 1 st, 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th generations were collected, and PCR verification was performed using Listeria-specific primers LM/LI, the asd gene primer, the dal gene primer, and the E6E7-tj primer. Additionally, bacterial samples from the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th generations were streaked on agar plates, and 10 single colonies from each generation were picked for PCR analysis to assess the genetic stability of the strains.

In vivo safety evaluation of vaccine and vector strains

Determination of LD50

The survival of mice in the high, medium, and low-dose groups for each strain was monitored over a period of 10 consecutive days. The LD50 of LM-HPVCW-2, LI-HPVCW-2, and LI-CWV-2 was calculated using the modified Karber method, and the experimental procedure is shown in Fig. S3A. In vivo evaluation of safety

Four vaccine strains were immunized via the tail vein injection to C57BL/6 mice at a dose of 0.1 LD50. Mice were immunized with each of the four strains for 14 consecutive days, and their body weight was monitored on the day of each immunization. Liver, spleen, and lung organ indices, as well as serum ALT and AST levels, were measured on days 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 14th. The liver, spleen, and lung tissues were aseptically extracted from each group on these days and processed for organ load calculation using a milled drop plate. Pathological analysis and scoring of the livers, spleens, and lungs of the mice in each group were performed on days 3 and 14, which were sent to Wuhan BRRF Biological Company for evaluation. The experimental protocol is illustrated in Fig. S3B.

Evaluation of in vivo genetic stability of vaccine and vector strains

Single colonies grown on BHI plates from days 1 st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th of each group were randomly selected for PCR amplification to verify the stability of the plasmids in vivo. The PCR products were then analyzed by electrophoresis. The primers used included Listeria-specific primers LM/LI, asd gene primer, dal gene primer, and E6E7-tj primer, and the experimental protocol is detailed in Fig. S3B.

Immunogenicity evaluation of vaccine and vector strains

C57BL/6 mice were divided into PBS, vector, and vaccine groups. Each group was immunized via the tail vein injection following the LM prime-LI boost-LM final immunization strategy, with each strain administered at a dose of 0.1 LD50. The immunization schedule is shown in Fig. S4, and the specific grouping and immunization strategy are shown in Table S4. Seven days after the third immunization, the spleens were aseptically excised and processed from mice in each group. The isolated splenocytes were stimulated with the peptide mixture (amino acid sequences are shown in Table S5) and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. After incubation, the cells were labeled with immunofluorescence in a light-deprived environment. Flow cytometry was used to quantify the percentages of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells secreting IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-12, IL-1β, and IL-10. Additionally, the proportions of CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and regulatory T (Treg) cells were determined.

Evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of the recombinant Listeria cervical cancer therapeutic vaccine expressing HPV multivalent E6E7 protein

Subcutaneous HPV16/18 E6/E7-expressing tumor models were established by subcutaneously injecting 1 × 105 TC-1 cells or U14-E6 cells into mice. The mice were divided into three groups for immunotherapy: the PBS group, the vector group, and the vaccine group, with 10 mice in each group. The vaccine and vector strains were immunized via the tail vein of C57BL/6 mice at a dose of 0.1 LD50 on the 7th, 14th, and 21 st days, following the LM prime-LI boost-LM final immunization strategy. The specific procedures are shown in Fig. S5 and Table S6. Tumor growth was monitored by measuring the length and width of the tumors with Vernier calipers every day starting from day 7. Tumor volume of the mice was calculated using the formula: (V = 1/2 length×width2). The observation endpoint was reached when a mouse either died, when the tumor volume reached 2000 mm³, or when there was a sustained absence of measurable tumor for ≥ 7 days following the final immunization (defined as “cure” in this study). At the observation endpoint, the mouse was recorded as either deceased or cured. The median survival time for each group was calculated once the last mouse reached the observation endpoint.

Data processing

The experimental data were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance using the chi-squared test. If the data met the assumptions of normal distribution and equal variance, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or a t-test was used. If the data did not meet these assumptions, a non-parametric test was used. For the tumor cure rate, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact binomial method. Graphs were generated using Graphpad Prism 8, and statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS ver. 21.0. A P-value < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Construction of recombinant Listeria cervical cancer therapeutic vaccine strains expressing HPV multivalent E6E7 protein and vector strain

Construction of LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd

PCR was conducted to verify the plasmid pCW619-LI dal after electro-transferring into the LI∆ilo::hly sensory strain. A single colony on the plate showed a clear band at the corresponding position of both the LI-specific primer and the E53-LI dal primer (Fig. 1A). Strains in lanes 3 and 4 without non-specific amplification bands were selected for homologous recombination. After passing the colonies on D-BE3 plates at 42 °C for three consecutive generations, PCR amplification was performed with homologous LI dal primers. The strain with successful gene exchange yielded an amplified fragment of 2051 bp, while the strain without gene exchange yielded a 3158 bp fragment (Fig. 1B). Positive colonies showing gene exchange were passaged for six consecutive generations at 30 °C in non-resistant BHI broth. Subsequently, bacterial culture was serially diluted and plated. Single colony growing on D-BHI plates but not on D-BE3 plates was selected for PCR amplification using LI dal primers. Colonies that did not exhibit the expected bands (Fig. 1C) were considered negative for the dal gene, indicating successful knockout. The knockout results of the dat gene are shown in Fig. 1D–F. The suspicious colonies were amplified using the homologous LI dal/dat primers and sent for sequencing. The sequencing results confirmed the successful knockout of both the dal and the dat genes, validating the construction of the LI∆ilo::hly∆∆dd strain.

Biochemical characterization

In the LI group, the vaccine and vector strains differed from the parental strain in seven biochemical indices—amygdalin, D-xylulose, leucine arylamidase, baculopeptide tolerance, neomycin tolerance, methyl-β-D-glucopyranoside, and O/129 tolerance—while remaining consistent with each other. Similarly, in the LM group, seven biochemical indicators of the vaccine and vector strains differed from the parental strain, and these indicators were consistent between the vaccine and vector strains. Two additional indicators—phosphatidyl phospholipase C and mucopeptide tolerance—showed inconsistencies among the three LM strains. The complete biochemical identification results for all groups are presented in Table S7, while the differential biochemical indices are summarized in Tables S8 and S9.

Growth curve determination

The growth curves of all strains are presented in Fig. 3B. In the LM group, the strains entered the logarithmic growth phase at the 3rd hour, and the stationary phase at the 6th hour. In contrast, the LI group entered the logarithmic phase at the 4th hour and reached the stationary phase at the 6th hour. Within the LI group, the LI-CWV-2 strain exhibited a slightly higher OD600 value than the other two strains during the stationary phase. These results indicate that the LM group entered the logarithmic growth phase earlier and exhibited a faster growth rate and a higher OD600 value at the stationary phase compared to the LI group.

Hemolysis experiments

The hemolysis potencies of the LI vaccine strain, vector strain, and parental strain were 2− 4, 2− 4, and 2− 2, respectively (Fig. 3C(a)), and the hemolysis potency of the vaccine and vector strains was higher than that of the parental strain. In the LM group, all strains showed a hemolytic potency of 20 (Fig. 3C(b)), suggesting comparable hemolytic capacity among them. All six strains—LI∆ilo::hly, LI-CWV-2, LI-HPVCW-2, LM∆∆, LM-CWV-1, and LM-HPVCW-2—were able to grow on BHI plates containing 5% sheep blood (Fig. 3D). The LM group produced narrow, grass-green hemolytic zones, whereas the LI group produced large, clear hemolytic zones, indicating distinct hemolytic patterns between the two Listeria species.

Genetic stability in vitro

The 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th generations of LM-HPVCW-2, LI-HPVCW-2 and LI-CWV-2 were amplified by PCR. Distinct target bands appeared at the expected positions (Fig. 4), indicating that the recombinant strains retained their backfilled plasmid after continuous passage. The the asd, the dal, and the E6E7 genes on the plasmid remained stably maintained across successive generations. Furthermore, ten single colonies from the 5th, 10th, 15th, 20th, and 25th generation plates were individually amplified by PCR with Listeria-specific primers LM/LI, asd gene primer, dal gene primer, and E6E7-tj primer, followed by electrophoresis. In vivo safety evaluation of vaccine and vector strains

Determination of LD50

Different concentrations of each bacterial suspension were intravenously administered to C57BL/6 mice via the tail vein, and the mortality of mice in each group was monitored for 10 consecutive days. The survival curves of the experimental groups are presented in Fig. 5A. Based on the modified Karber method, the LD50 of LI-HPVCW-2, LM-HPVCW-2, and LI-CWV-2 were calculated to be 1.4 × 108 CFU/mouse, 2.3 × 108 CFU/mouse, and 6.4 × 107 CFU/mouse, respectively.

In vivo safety evaluation

Four strains were intravenously inoculated into C57BL/6 mice via the tail vein at a dose equivalent to 0.1 LD50. One day post-injection, the body weights of all four groups significantly decreased but began to recover by day 3 and continued to increase through day 14 (Fig. 5B). Serum ALT and AST levels peaked in all groups on day 1, with LM-CWV-1 exhibiting the highest levels (> 600 U/L). By day 3, ALT and AST levels in all groups had significantly decreased, returning to baseline by day 14 without evidence of regression to toxicity (Fig. 5C). The liver, spleen, and lung indices initially increased, followed by a gradual decline to normal levels. Comparison between groups showed that the lung indices normalized levels, while liver and spleen indices returned to normal at similar time points. No relapse occurred in each group on day 14 (Fig. 6A). Bacterial loads in all groups peaked on days 1–3 and were gradually cleared thereafter. The spleen exhibited the lowest peak bacterial load and the fastest clearance rate, whereas the liver and lung showed comparable bacterial burdens and clearance rates. By day 14, bacterial loads in all groups had fallen below the detection limit, with no evidence of recurrent toxicity (Fig. 6B). Histopathological examination of the liver, spleen, and lungs revealed varying degrees of tissue damage on day 3, which had resolved by day 14 (Fig. 7). Overall, the LD50 values of the LM and LI vaccine and vector strains were markedly higher and exhibited reduced virulence compared with the corresponding parental strains. The organ damage observed in mice was transient, indicating an acceptable safety profile.

Discussion

Persistent infection with high-risk HPV types (e.g., types 16 and 18) is the primary etiological factor in cervical cancer, whereas low-risk HPV types (e.g., types 6 and 11) are non-oncogenic but account for more than 90% of genital warts. Clinically, high-risk and low-risk HPV types often coexist in the same host [21]. Therefore, developing a therapeutic vaccination that targets both high- and low-risk HPV strains is of substantial clinical and public health significance, as it could achieve the dual goals of treating cervical cancer and eliminating related benign lesions.

Listeria, an intracellular parasitic bacterium, possesses distinctive immune-activating properties. Tumor-associated antigens (TAA) secreted by Listeria are processed by the proteasome and presented via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, thereby activating antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and inducing a potent anti-tumor cellular immune response [10].

Because of its potent immune-activating capacity, LM has been extensively investigated as a vaccine vector. The LM-based vaccine ADXS11-001 demonstrated strong immunogenicity in both preclinical mouse models and early-phase clinical trials [22]. Similarly, CRS-207, targeting mesothelin and employing a heterologous prime-boost strategy, exhibited promising results in clinical trials when combined with cyclophosphamide/GVAX [23]. In addition, JNJ-64,041,757, which simultaneously targets EGFRvIII and mesothelin, elicited specific T cell responses and is currently undergoing early-phase combination therapy trials for non-small cell lung cancer [24]. Collectively, these LM-based attenuated vaccines have demonstrated substantial potential as therapeutic cancer vaccines. In contrast to these approaches, our study employed both LI and LM strains as vaccine vectors. Given that LI is pathogenic only to ruminants such as sheep, our vaccine design is superior in safety. Meanwhile, the LM prime-LI boost-LM final regimen elicited an enhanced anti-tumor effect in our mouse models compared to the homologous LM prime-boost-final regimen [19]. Furthermore, our design overcomes the limitation of single antigen targeting by incorporating multivalent antigens for HPV6/11/16/18, thereby broadening immune coverage.

We replaced the antibiotic resistance marker with a nutritional selection marker to promote stable expression of the foreign gene in the host bacterium. The stability of plasmids in the vaccine strains was subsequently verified both in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, after 25 generations of transmission, the plasmids were still stably maintained in the strains. In vivo, the plasmids were stable for up to 7 days post-immunization. A similar approach was reported by Yan Y et al., who constructed an attenuated Edwardsiella tarda vaccine based on the asd balanced lethal system and demonstrated that, in mice, the plasmid persisted for a longer period and mediated more sustained in vivo protein expression than conventional plasmids carrying antibiotic resistance genes [25].

This balanced lethal strategy effectively solved the issue of antibiotic contamination. In addition, biochemical characterization revealed a decrease in drug resistance in the vaccine strains. Although antibiotic resistance is not a direct virulence factor, it plays an important role in the progression of bacterial infection [26]. Consequently, we hypothesized that the reduced drug resistance may attenuate bacterial pathogenicity to some extent. To test this hypothesis, we determined the LD50 of vaccines in the mouse models. The LD50 of the LM and LI vaccine strains was one order of magnitude higher than that of the parental strain, confirming our expectance.

In the safety experiments, the organ index, bacterial load, and pathological conditions showed transient initial changes that recovered to baseline levels afterward. Serum biochemical analyses further supported these findings: ALT/AST levels transiently increased, reaching approximately 4–12-fold above baseline at day 1 post-immunization, but normalized within 3 days. The rapid and complete recovery across all measured parameters indicated that both the vaccine and vector strains induced only mild and reversible physiological effects. The vaccine and vector strains were cleared from the spleen faster than from the liver, possibly because macrophages in the spleen efficiently phagocytose circulating Listeria, which are subsequently degraded within phagolysosomes. In contrast, hepatic Kupffer cells (KCs) can also capture bacteria; however, Listeria secretes hemolysins that disrupt the KC membranes, enabling them to escape into hepatic parenchymal cells [27].

Current therapeutic vaccine development for cervical cancer primarily concentrates on modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) to effectively stimulate and enhance CD8+ T cell-mediated cellular immune responses [28]. This strategy aims to overcome immune suppression within the TME and to direct the immune system toward the specific recognition and elimination of HPV-infected or malignant cells. For instance, the ISA101 peptide vaccine, in combination with PD-1 inhibitors, prompted the infiltration of activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) into the TME of patients with advanced disease [29]. Similarly, the pNGVL4a-CRT/E7 (detox) DNA vaccine markedly enhanced the CD8 + T cell-mediated immune response through conjugating Hsp with the E7 antigen, resulting in histologic regression in approximately 30% of vaccinated CIN 2/3 patients [30]. The success of therapeutic vaccination against cervical cancer depends on robust activation of cellular immunity and effective modulation of the suppressive TME. In our study, we observed a significant increase in the proportion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells co-secreting TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2, cytokines that are essential for CTLs’ activation and effector function. Furthermore, compared with the PBS group, vaccination induced a cytokine profile shift characterized by elevated IL-12—an important driver of anti-tumor Th1 responses—and reduced expression of IL-1β and IL-10, which are associated with pro-inflammatory angiogenesis and immunosuppression, respectively. Collectively, our findings indicated that the recombinant Listeria vaccine strain expressing multivalent HPV E6/E7 antigens effectively elicits a potent anti-tumor immune response.

Furthermore, we observed notable changes in key lymphocyte populations. Compared with the PBS group, the proportion of CTLs was significantly increased, indicating a transition of the immune response toward a robust anti-tumor phenotype characterized by enhanced tumor-specific cytotoxicity. No significant difference was noted in the proportion of immunosuppressive Treg cells, which does not diminish the vaccine’s anti-tumor efficacy, as CTLs’ activation represents the primary effector mechanism driving tumor regression. In addition, we noted a significant decrease in the proportion of NK cells. This unexpected observation warrants further investigation into potential mechanisms, which could be elucidated through future single-cell sequencing analyses.

Therapeutic efficacy analysis demonstrated that in tumor-bearing mice harboring either HPV16- or HPV18-expressing tumors, the median survival time was significantly prolonged compared with the PBS group. These findings indicate that the recombinant Listeria vaccine strain expressing the HPV multivalent E6/E7 antigens exerts potent therapeutic activity against cervical cancer. Based on this tumor model, single-cell sequencing could be employed to characterize dynamic changes in immune cell subpopulations and the degree of immunosuppression within the TME at various time points following vaccination. Furthermore, co-administration with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-1 monoclonal antibodies may yield synergistic therapeutic effects.

While our results in subcutaneous tumor models demonstrated encouraging modulation of the TME and anti-tumor efficacy, we acknowledge that such models do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the native tumor TME or its clinical relevance. To address this translational gap, future studies will employ orthotopic tumor models to more accurately evaluate tissue-specific immune responses. Moreover, advancing the attenuated Listeria-based vaccine toward clinical application requires comprehensive preclinical safety evaluations and process development studies to ensure compliance with regulatory standards. Collectively, these findings support the progression toward a Phase I clinical trial in patients with HPV-associated malignancies, primarily aimed at establishing the vaccine’s safety profile and confirming its capacity to activate HPV-specific T cell responses.
