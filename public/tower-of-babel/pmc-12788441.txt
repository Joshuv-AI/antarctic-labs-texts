Effect of Saline versus Gluconate/Acetate-Buffered Solution versus Lactate-Buffered Solution on Serum Chloride Among Children
Authors: Yunus Nadeemulla Beig Khader, Sana Mehek, Shubhanshu Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Intravenous (IV) fluid administration is routine in pediatric care, but fluid composition can significantly influence serum electrolyte levels. Hyperchloremia, often associated with 0.9% saline, may lead to metabolic acidosis and renal dysfunction. Balanced solutions, including gluconate/acetate-buffered and lactate-buffered fluids, are being explored for safer alternatives.Objective:To compare the effects of saline, gluconate/acetate-buffered, and lactate-buffered IV fluids on serum chloride levels in hospitalized children.Materials and Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted among pediatric in patients receiving one of three IV fluids: saline (n = 30), gluconate/acetate-buffered solution (n = 30), or lactate-buffered solution (n = 30). Serum chloride was measured at baseline and after 24 h of fluid administration. Statistical analysis was performed using ANOVA and paired t-tests.Results:Mean post-infusion chloride levels were significantly higher in the saline group (107.2 ± 3.1 mmol/L) compared to the gluconate/acetate group (103.5 ± 2.4 mmol/L) and lactate-buffered group (102.9 ± 2.8 mmol/L) (P < 0.001). Chloride elevation was most pronounced in the saline group, with associated trends toward mild acidosis.Conclusion:Saline use in children is associated with greater increases in serum chloride compared to balanced solutions. Buffered fluids may be preferable for maintaining electrolyte stability in pediatric fluid therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Intravenous fluid therapy is a cornerstone of pediatric inpatient care for hydration, electrolyte balance, and metabolic support.[1] However, isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl), though commonly used, contains a supraphysiological chloride concentration that may contribute to hyperchloremia and metabolic acidosis in children. Emerging evidence has prompted a shift toward more physiologically balanced fluids, such as gluconate/acetate-buffered and lactate-buffered solutions, which may better preserve acid-base and electrolyte equilibrium.[23] These fluids differ in their buffer content and metabolism, potentially influencing serum chloride levels and related clinical outcomes. While adult data support the use of buffered fluids, pediatric studies remain limited.[45] This study was conducted to compare the effects of saline, gluconate/acetate-buffered, and lactate-buffered IV fluids on serum chloride concentrations among hospitalized children.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study was conducted among pediatric inpatients aged 1 month to 12 years who required intravenous fluid therapy for at least 24 h. Patients were grouped based on the type of IV fluid received: Group A (0.9% saline, n = 30), Group B (gluconate/acetate-buffered solution, n = 30), and Group C (lactate-buffered solution, n = 30). Children with pre-existing electrolyte abnormalities, renal dysfunction, or on diuretics were excluded. Serum chloride was measured at baseline and after 24 h of fluid administration using standardized laboratory protocols. The primary outcome was the change in serum chloride levels within and between groups. Data were analyzed using paired t-tests and One-way ANOVA, with a P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

This study included 90 pediatric patients equally divided into three groups based on the type of IV fluid received. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable across groups. Significant changes in serum chloride levels were observed after 24 h of infusion, particularly in the saline group. Differences in chloride elevation patterns were analyzed across groups, as well as their correlation with fluid type, age group, and acid-base status.

In summary, children receiving 0.9% saline demonstrated a significant rise in serum chloride and a greater incidence of hyperchloremia, often accompanied by a mild decline in bicarbonate. In contrast, buffered fluids, particularly lactate-buffered solutions, better preserved electrolyte balance and acid-base status. These findings support the preferential use of balanced fluids in pediatric IV therapy to minimize iatrogenic disturbances.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that the use of balanced intravenous fluids, specifically gluconate/acetate-buffered and lactate-buffered solutions, significantly mitigates the rise in serum chloride levels among pediatric patients compared to 0.9% saline. The saline group exhibited a notable increase in serum chloride, aligning with concerns about hyperchloremia-induced metabolic acidosis and its potential impact on renal function.[6] Conversely, buffered solutions maintained serum chloride levels closer to baseline, suggesting a more favorable electrolyte balance.[7] These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that balanced fluids reduce the risk of hyperchloremia in critically ill children. While the incidence of new-onset acute kidney injury did not differ significantly among groups, the trend toward better acid-base homeostasis with buffered solutions underscores their potential clinical benefits. The study’s limitations include its single-center design and relatively short follow-up period, which may affect the generalizability and long-term applicability of the results. Future multicenter trials with extended monitoring are recommended to further validate these findings and assess long-term outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Balanced intravenous fluids, such as gluconate/acetate-buffered and lactate-buffered solutions, are effective in minimizing serum chloride elevation in pediatric patients, suggesting their preferential use over saline to maintain electrolyte stability during fluid therapy.
