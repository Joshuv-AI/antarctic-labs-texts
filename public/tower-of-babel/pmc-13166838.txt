Outcomes of intravascular lithotripsy for calcified femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal artery disease: A systematic review with qualitative synthesis
Authors: Yasser Kofiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Heavily calcified peripheral artery disease (PAD) lesions limit the effectiveness of standard endovascular techniques and increase complication risk. This study systematically evaluates the safety and efficacy of intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) as a calcium-modifying therapy.Methods:A systematic literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published between 2020 and July 2025. Studies investigating the use of IVL in calcified femoropopliteal and infrapopliteal arteries were included. Inclusion criteria encompassed observational and interventional designs reporting procedural, safety, or efficacy outcomes. Studies not utilizing IVL as a primary therapy or lacking calcified lesions were excluded.Results:14 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising single-arm cohorts, registries, and randomized controlled trials. IVL consistently demonstrated high procedural success (>95%), low rates of major dissection (≤5.5%), minimal perforation (≤1.1%), and a reduced need for bailout stenting compared to conventional angioplasty. Compared to atherectomy, IVL showed a superior safety profile with lower rates of perforation and distal embolization. Relative to high-pressure balloons, IVL achieved similar luminal gain with significantly fewer flow-limiting dissections.Conclusion:IVL is a safe and effective technique for modifying calcified plaques in peripheral arteries, addressing key limitations of conventional endovascular approaches. By providing a controlled and predictable mechanism for vessel preparation, IVL represents a valuable treatment option in selected patients with heavily calcified peripheral artery disease and may facilitate durable revascularization with a favorable safety profile.

1. Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects over 200 million people globally, and the incidence increases to as high as 20% in people over70.[1] PAD is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity, ranking as the third leading cause after coronary artery disease and stroke,[2] with a significant burden of disease attributable to heavily calcified lesions. These calcific plaques, particularly medial calcium, impair vessel compliance and present a formidable barrier to endovascular therapy[3] Conventional balloon angioplasty often results in inadequate expansion, vessel dissection,[4] and elastic recoil, while atherectomy carries risks of perforation and distal embolization.[5]

Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a disruptive technology designed to safely modify calcified plaque.[6] Adapted from urological lithotripsy, IVL uses localized, high-amplitude sonic pressure waves to fracture intimal and medial calcium selectively, thereby restoring vessel compliance and facilitating low-pressure balloon expansion. This systematic review summarizes the current evidence on the mechanism, efficacy, and safety of IVL for calcified PAD and compares its profile with the established techniques.

1.1. Pathophysiology of calcified PAD

Arterial calcification is an active and organized process common in PAD, particularly in patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease[7,8] It occurs in 2 main forms: intrinsic calcification, associated with atherosclerotic plaque, and intercalcification (Mönckeberg sclerosis), which creates a rigid, inflexible “tube-like” vascular channel.[9] This calcification is the principal problem of endovascular therapy as it prevents adequate lumen gain by resisting balloon expansion, promotes dissection by creating stress points during inflation, and causes acute recoil and restenosis by acting as a mechanical spring.[5] Effective treatment requires a method that can disrupt this calcium ring without causing significant trauma to the soft walls of the surrounding blood vessels.

1.2. The intravascular lithotripsy system

The IVL system (Shockwave Medical, Inc., Santa Clara) consists of a balloon catheter integrated with multiple lithotripsy emulators, that transforms electrical energy into mechanical energy during low-pressure balloon inflation.[10] Upon activation, an electrical discharge within the emitters creates a rapid vaporization bubble in the fluid-filled balloon. The implosion of this bubble generates localized, high-amplitude, pulsatile sonic pressure waves.[11] These pressure waves travel through soft vascular tissue with minimal energy loss but are selectively absorbed by rigid, calcified plaque. This creates discrete, circumferential fractures in the calcium plate.[11] The fracturing of the calcium ring disrupts the “vessel recoil spring,” restoring vessel compliance. This allows subsequent low-pressure (typically 3–4 atm) balloon inflations to achieve full, uniform expansion of the artery with minimal barotrauma.

2. Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published between January 2020 and July 2025. Search terms included “IVL,” “Shockwave,” in combination with “PAD,” femoral,” “femoropopliteal (FP),” and “calcification.” The search strategy adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines Figure 1.

2.1. Inclusion criteria

Studies involving human participants with calcified PAD (FP or infrapopliteal arteries), IVL employed as the primary therapeutic intervention, reporting of procedural, safety, or efficacy outcomes (e.g., technical success, dissection, perforation) and observational (cohort, cross-sectional) or interventional (randomized or non-randomized) study designs.

2.2. Exclusion criteria

Non-original works (reviews, editorials, case reports), Studies where IVL was not the primary therapy, Investigations conducted in coronary or non-peripheral vasculature and Publications not available in English.

2.3. Outcomes of interest

The outcomes of this review were defined a priori to structure data extraction and synthesis. These were categorized into primary, secondary efficacy, and secondary safety endpoints.

2.4. Primary outcome

2.4.1. Procedural success

Defined as the successful delivery and activation of the IVL balloon with a final residual stenosis of <50% after any adjunctive therapy (e.g., low-pressure balloon angioplasty or drug-coated balloon), without the occurrence of a severe flow-limiting dissection (Type C–F) or perforation requiring intervention during the index procedure, (N.B. It is important to note that definitions of procedural success varied across studies. The majority of observational studies defined procedural success as successful IVL delivery and activation with a final residual stenosis of <50% after adjunctive therapy. In contrast, the DISRUPT PAD III randomized controlled trial employed a stricter, core lab-adjudicated endpoint of ≤30% residual stenosis without flow-limiting dissection. For consistency, we report procedural success rates as defined by each study, and the stricter endpoint used in the randomized controlled trial (RCT) is explicitly noted in the results and table footnotes).

2.5. Secondary efficacy outcomes

Acute luminal gain: The immediate post-procedure increase in minimal lumen diameter, measured in millimeters. Freedom from clinically-driven target lesion revascularization: The absence of a repeat intervention (surgical or endovascular) driven by clinical symptoms and/or imaging evidence of restenosis at the target lesion. Patency, defined as a patent target lesion without restenosis (>50% stenosis) and without the need for repeat revascularization, typically assessed at 6- and 12-month intervals via duplex ultrasound or angiography. Bailout stenting rate: The percentage of lesions requiring the implantation of a stent during the index procedure due to suboptimal angioplasty results, such as a flow-limiting dissection or significant recoil.

2.8. Summary of risk of bias

The 2 RCTs (Tepe et al, 2021, 2022)[21,22] were judged to have a low overall risk of bias. Both studies utilized appropriate randomization methods, blinded outcome adjudication via an independent core laboratory, and reported pre-specified endpoints with low attrition, minimizing concerns across RoB 2 domains.

Among the 12 non-randomized studies, the overall risk of bias was more varied. The prospective, multicenter DISRUPT PAD III observational studies (e.g., Adams et al, 2020; Armstrong et al, 2020)[18,19] and the pooled analysis by Madhavan et al (2020)[20] were judged to have a moderate overall risk of bias. The primary concern in these studies arose from the potential for confounding (ROBINS-I Domain 1), as they were single-arm designs without comparator groups. However, their prospective nature, consecutive patient enrollment, and use of core-lab adjudication for angiographic outcomes mitigated bias in other domains.

Similarly, the single-center prospective registry by Aftanski et al[24] was assessed as having a moderate overall risk of bias. Although it was a real-world, all-comers cohort with prospective data collection and quantitative angiographic analysis, the absence of a control group and potential center-specific practice patterns introduced a risk of confounding and limited external validity.

The retrospective, single-center studies (e.g., Baig et al, 2022; Salihi et al, 2025; Gueifão et al, 2025)[25,30,31] were generally assessed as having a serious overall risk of bias. Key limitations included the retrospective design with potential for selection bias (Domain 2), lack of blinding in outcome assessment (Domain 6), and, in some cases, incomplete follow-up data (Domain 5). The potential impact of these biases must be considered when interpreting their reported outcomes.

2.9. Data extraction

Included study characteristics (authors, year, and design), sample size, lesion details, and key outcome measures. Two reviewers (YK and [T.A] performed study selection and data extraction independently. The protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251245163).

2.10. Data synthesis and analysis

Due to the significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity observed across the included studies – specifically in study design (ranging from RCTs to single-arm observational cohorts), patient populations (e.g., varying inclusion of critical limb ischemia, diabetes, renal disease), lesion characteristics (involving FP, infrapopliteal, and iliac segments), and definitions of key outcomes (e.g., patency, procedural success) – a formal quantitative meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate and would likely yield misleading pooled estimates. Therefore, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews without meta-analysis, this review presents a qualitative synthesis of the evidence. Findings are summarized narratively and organized by pre-defined outcome domains (procedural, safety, and efficacy), with data presented descriptively in summary tables.

3.2.3.1. Efficacy patterns

High procedural success rates (95–100%) across all vascular beds, significant acute luminal gain (2.0–3.4 mm) and stenosis reduction (42–55%), durable outcomes with 12-month patency rates of 81 to 92% in FP lesions and superior to PTA in randomized comparison (65.8% vs 50.4% success).

3.2.3.2. Safety consistency

Remarkably low dissection rates (0–3% in most studies), minimal perforation risk (0–1%), low embolization rates (0–1%, except one outlier at 10.5%) and reduced bailout stenting compared to conventional PTA (4.6% vs 18.3%).

3.2.4. Outcomes by anatomical location and treatment strategy

When outcomes were stratified by lesion location, IVL demonstrated consistent efficacy across all arterial beds. In FP lesions, data from 8 studies, including the DISRUPT PAD III RCT and Stavroulakis et al, reported procedural success rates exceeding 95% and 12-month primary patency rates ranging from 81% to 92% when IVL was combined with drug-coated balloons (DCB). In infrapopliteal (below-the-knee) lesions, 3 studies reported procedural success rates of 97% to 99% with no flow-limiting dissections or perforations.

4. Discussion

This systematic review summarizes the results of 14 studies evaluating IVL for the treatment of calcified PAD. Collectively, the data support IVL as a safe, effective, and versatile modality that overcomes key limitations of traditional endovascular therapies. By selectively fracturing vascular calcium using low-pressure sonic waves, IVL enables effective lesion modification while minimizing trauma to surrounding soft tissue, thereby facilitating adequate lumen expansion with a favorable procedural risk profile.

The most consistent finding in the reviewed studies is the exceptional safety of IVL, particularly in reducing the rates of large dissections and perforations compared to historical results of balloon angioplasty and plaque resection. Unlike high-pressure balloons, which exert uncontrolled radial force and often cause dissection at plaque and vascular interfaces,[32] a phenomenon well documented in studies of popliteal femoral interventions[4] or plaque resection, which removes tissue with inherent embolism and perforation risks,[5] IVL relies on pulsed acoustic energy that preferentially propagates through compliant tissue while inducing mechanical stress within rigid calcium. This mechanism explains the reproducible safety profile observed in FP, infrapopliteal, and joint-crossing lesions and aligns with the contemporary paradigm of vascular preparation without vascular injury.[29]

In terms of efficacy, IVL consistently achieves high procedural success and significant lumen gain across various anatomical beds, including heavily calcified and below-knee lesions. These outcomes are clinically significant, as adequate lumen expansion is a well-established indicator of continued patency, particularly when combined with drug- coated balloons.[26] The synergy between IVL and drug- coated balloons is crucial: by disrupting the calcified barrier, IVL may enhance drug permeability and retention, potentially addressing one of the major limitations of drug-coated balloons in highly calcified lesions, namely, heterogeneous drug delivery.[9] Early clinical data support this hypothesis, with studies such as Stavrolakis et al. (2023)[26] demonstrating 12-month patency rates exceeding 80%, comparing favorably with historical outcomes of DCB alone in similar populations.

Furthermore, IVL demonstrates broad applicability to high-risk patient subgroups, including those with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and stent restenosis that groups known for their severe calcification and less effective outcomes with conventional therapies.[7,8] IVL’s ability to modify medial calcium is particularly important in patients of medial calcification (Mönckeberg sclerosis), where balloon angioplasty and atherectomy often fail to restore vessel compliance.[3] By improving arterial elasticity and reducing elastic recoil, a common cause of acute failure in calcified lesions, thereby reducing the need for salvage stents, a consistently observed outcome in the DISRUPT PAD series and consistent with the broader goal of avoiding stenting in peripheral arteries to preserve future options.[22] A key strength of this review is the inclusion of sophisticated evidence from randomized trials and large registries, which enhances the generalizability of the findings following early feasibility studies. Nonetheless, the current evidence base remains weighted toward short- and mid-term outcomes, with limited data extending beyond 2 to 3 years. Long-term durability, particularly with respect of limb salvage in patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), remains an important area for future investigation. The safety profile of IVL for calcified PAD was consistently favorable across the 14 reviewed studies. Rates of major dissection (Type C–F) (Type C–F refers to the specific categories of arterial dissection according to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute classification system for coronary artery dissection, which is also widely applied in peripheral vascular interventions)[33] were very low with reported incidences ranging from 0% to 3.0%, and several studies reported no flow-limiting dissections.[18,23,28,30,31] Additionally, while the economics of IVL appear promising in early analyses,[34] comprehensive cost-effectiveness studies across all healthcare systems are needed to guide adoption and reimbursement.

5. Safety outcomes

The most consistent finding across all studies is the exceptional safety profile of IVL. Major dissection rates ranged from 0% to 3.0% across 12 studies, with the majority reporting 0% to 1.4%. Perforation was rare, occurring in 0% to 1.0% of cases. Distal embolization was minimal (0–1.0%) in most studies, with one outlier reporting 10.5% in a small retrospective series.[30] Thirty-day MALE were low (0–0.8%) in non-CLTI populations, with a single CLTI cohort reporting 5.0% attributed to one major amputation.[31] No procedure-related mortality was reported in any of the 14 studies. These safety advantages are mechanistically explained by IVL’s selective action on calcified plaque rather than the vascular wall, unlike high-pressure balloons, which exert uncontrolled radial force and often cause dissection at plaque-vessel interfaces,[32] or atherectomy, which removes tissue with inherent embolism and perforation risks The recently published FESTIVAL registry (Troisi et al, 2026),[35] a prospective, multicenter observational study of 117 patients undergoing IVL without stenting for de novo common femoral artery disease, provides important supporting evidence that reinforces the findings of this systematic review. Although published after our predefined search period (January 2020–July 2025), the FESTIVAL registry reported a 30-day technical success rate of 97.4% and excellent mid-term outcomes, with primary patency of 96.2% at 1 year and 93.9% at 2 years, and secondary patency of 100% at both intervals. Importantly, the study identified that Azéma Type III lesions (those extending to the femoral bifurcation) were independently associated with worse outcomes, highlighting the importance of lesion morphology in patient selection. These findings corroborate the safety and efficacy of IVL in the common femoral artery and align with the conclusions of our review. Future research should prioritize comparative randomized trials evaluating IVL + DCB compared to other alternative contemporary strategies such as atherectomy with DCB or specialty balloon technologies. Further investigation into the utility of IVL in infrapopliteal arteries and dialysis access circuits is a logical extension of its mechanistic advantages.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, IVL represents a significant advance in the management of calcified peripheral atherosclerosis. Its ability to safely and effectively modulate calcium, along with its compatibility makes it a key component of modern peripheral interventions. By addressing the mechanical challenges of calcification while minimizing vascular injury, IVL may improve procedural safety and contribute to favorable clinical outcomes. These findings support its consideration as a promising therapeutic option in the treatment of this challenging subset of disease, although further high-quality studies are warranted to better define its role in routine clinical practice.
