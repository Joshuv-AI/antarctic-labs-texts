Introduction

We say a random variable
X
is
(s,C)
-Frostman if its distribution measure is
(s,C)
-Frostman, which means that for any
x
and
r>0
we have

P(X\in B(x,r))\leq Cr^{s}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
D_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined by
D_{n}(x)=\frac{k}{2^{n}}
if
k
is the unique integer such that
\frac{k}{2^{n}}\leq x<\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}
. For any random variable
X
,
D_{n}(X)
defines a discrete random variable that takes values in the set of dyadic rationals. We write
H_{n}(X):=H(D_{n}(X))
, where the entropy of a discrete random variable is defined by

H(D_{n}(X))=-\sum_{x}P(D_{n}(X)=x)\log P(D_{n}(X)=x).

In this paper, we establish a general entropy inequality that both sharpens our theoretical understanding and points toward a range of applications. Our study is motivated by recent applications of entropy methods to discretized sum-product estimates
[
11
]
due to Máthé and O’Regan, and to the recent solution of the polynomial Freiman-Ruzsa conjecture
[
1
,
2
]
due to Gowers, Green, Manners, and Tao.

More precisely, we are interested in bounding the following quantity
\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}
from below, where
\phi(x,y)=a_{1}x^{2}+a_{2}xy+a_{3}y^{2}
is a non-degenerate rational quadratic form, i.e.
a_{2}^{2}-4a_{1}a_{3}\neq 0
,
X
and
Y
are independent
(s,C)
-Frostman random variables whose ranges are bounded, and
s\in(0,1)
. We state our main result as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\phi(x,y)
be a non-degenerate rational quadratic form. Let
X
and
Y
be independent
(s,C)
-Frostman random variables whose ranges are contained in
[-c_{1},c_{1}]
, with
0<s<1
, and
C,c_{1}\geq 1
. Then there exist a positive constant
\epsilon=\epsilon(s,\phi)
and an integer
N=N(s,C,c_{1},\phi)
such that for all
n>N,
we have

\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}\geq n(s+\epsilon).

(1)

To show the sharpness of this theorem, we summarize the main statements of several examples in Section
A
:

1.

Any
(s,C)
-Frostman random variable
Z
has the property that
H_{n}(Z)\geq ns-\log C+O(1)
(see Example
A.1
for an example matching this bound). The lower bound in (
1
) says that the discretized entropies of
X+Y
and
\phi(X,Y)
cannot be small simultaneously. We show in Examples
A.1
and
A.2
that at least one of the terms
H_{n}(X+Y)
or
H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))
can be as small as
(1+o(1))ns
.

2.

The positive
\epsilon
might not exist if one of the conditions of the theorem is not satisfied, including
when
s=0
or
1
(see Example
A.1
), the Frostman constant
C
grows with
n
(see Example
A.4
), or the polynomial is degenerate (see Example
A.3
).

3.

In the statement of Theorem
1.1
, the fact that
N
depends on
C
, and the condition that
X
and
Y
are independent are both essential (see Examples
A.4
and
A.5
, respectively).

4.

For any
0<s<1
and any sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an
(s,O(1))
-Frostman random variable
X
with range contained in
[0,1]
, such that

\max\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(X^{2}+Y^{2})\}=(1+o(1))H_{n}(X)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
Y
is i.i.d. with
X
. In other words, the term
ns
can not be replaced by
H_{n}(X)
in the right-hand side of (
1
).

5.

The value of
\epsilon
is not explicit, and
an inspection of the proofs suggests it would be small.

We pause here to add several further remarks. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
presents a connection between
\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}
and the entropy of the “distance” function,
i.e.
H_{n}\big((X_{1}-Y_{1})^{2}+(X_{2}-Y_{2})^{2}\big)
, where
(X_{1},Y_{1})
,
(X_{2},Y_{2})
are i.i.d. copies of
(X,Y)
.

When
a_{1}=a_{3}=0
and
a_{2}=1
, i.e.,
\phi(x,y)=xy
, Máthé and O’Regan
[
11
]
established a different type of connection by replacing the entropy of the “distance” function with that of the “slope” function. By combining this with a generalization of Marstrand’s theorem due to Orponen, Shmerkin, and Wang in
[
19
]
, they were able to prove Theorem
1.1
for the full range of
s
for this specific bilinear form function. We do not see how their method could be adapted to the form of
\phi
with
a_{1},a_{3}\neq 0
. Moreover, in terms of applications, i.e. sum-product type problems over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, the two functions
xy
and
a_{1}x^{2}+a_{2}xy+a_{3}y^{2}
appear to exhibit distinct behaviors. It would be interesting to explore more connections between
\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}
and the entropy of other functions.

The next theorem presents an application of Theorem
1.1
. Here, by
\underline{\dim}_{B}
and
H^{s}
, we mean the lower box dimension and the
s
-Hausdorff measure, respectively.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\phi(x,y)
be a non-degenerate
rational
quadratic form. For
0<s<1
, there exists a positive constant
\epsilon=\epsilon(s{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\phi})
such that for every Borel set
A\subset{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,1]}
with
H^{s}(A)>0
, we have

\max\left\{\underline{\dim}_{B}(A+A),\underline{\dim}_{B}(\phi(A,A))\right\}\geq s+\epsilon.

This theorem is of independent interest, particularly when
\phi(x,y)
is a non-bilinear form, and might be considered as an application of the Falconer distance problem. If
\phi(x,y)=xy
, then stronger results have been obtained by using a variety of techniques, see
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
and references therein for current progress. Given this line of research, it is reasonable to expect different proofs of Theorem
1.2
with other methods. In the prime field setting, such a result has been proved by using tools from incidence geometry, see
[
8
, Theorem 1.5]
and
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
, for example.

For
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
, by
\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A)
we mean the
\delta
-covering number of

A
. The above theorem can be rewritten in the language of covering numbers.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\phi(x,y)
be a non-degenerate rational quadratic form. Given
0<s<1
and a constant
C\geq 1
. Then there exist
\epsilon=\epsilon(s{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\phi})>0
and
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(s,C,\phi)>0
such that the following holds for all
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
. Let
A\subset{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,1]}
be a
\delta
-separated set of cardinality
\delta^{-s}
. Suppose that for all intervals
I
of length at least
\delta
, we have the non-concentration estimate

\#(A\cap I)\leq C|I|^{s}(\#A).

Then we have

\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(A+A)+\mathcal{E}_{\delta}(\phi(A,A))\gg C^{-O(1)}\delta^{-\epsilon}(\#A).

We now sketch the main ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Our base case is corresponding to the diagonal forms
\phi(X,Y)=\alpha X^{2}+\beta Y^{2}
(Lemma
4.1
), with independent
(s,C)
-Frostman random variables
X,Y
. Roughly speaking, by applying the submodularity formula (Lemma
2.18
) on certain algebraic relations between the sum function
X+Y
and the distance function
(X-X^{\prime})^{2}+(Y-Y^{\prime})^{2}
, we are able to reduce the problem to bounding the entropy of the distance function from below. In particular, let
W
be an
(2s,C)
-Frostman random variable with
range(W)\subseteq B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
0<s<1
, the constant
C\geq 1
is bounded and does not depend on
n
, and
R>0
.
Let
W^{\prime}
be a random variable which is i.i.d with
W
. Then there exists a constant
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(s)>0
such that

H_{n}(|W-W^{\prime}|^{2})\geq n\left(s+\epsilon_{0}\right)

(2)

for all
n\geq N_{0}
, where
N_{0}=N_{0}(s,C,R)
.
Note that if the Frostman constant
C
depends on
n
, then the inequality (
2
) might not be true (see Example
A.5
).
To prove (
2
), we make use of results on the Falconer distance problem. While classical estimates taken from
[
12
, Section 4.2]
are sufficient in the case
s>1/2
, we invoke the recent work of Shmerkin and Wang
[
20
]
for
s\leq 1/2
.

For the general form
\phi(X,Y)
, the proof becomes much more complicated. The first step is to transform it to a diagonal form
\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, but the transformed variables
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
are no longer independent. To proceed further, we introduce a novel approach, which can be briefly described as follows. By applying the discretized entropy version of Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem (see Theorem
3.1
), we obtain new random variables
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
that are conditionally independent, and thus reduce the problem accordingly. Generally speaking, there exists a random variable
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
such that

(i)
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\equiv(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
;

(ii)
X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
D_{n}(Y^{\prime})
;

(iii)
Y^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
;

(iv)
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime})
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
.

Then, given that
D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)
with
x,y
being dyadic rationals satisfying
P\big(D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)>0
, in the proof we show that

\begin{split}\big(X^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)\text{ is $\left(s,O\left(C^{2}\cdot 2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)\right)\right)$-Frostman},\\
\big(Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)\text{ is $\left(s,O\left(C^{2}\cdot 2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)\right)\right)$-Frostman}.\end{split}

(3)

The problem now is reduced to the case of random variables

\big(X^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)
and
\big(Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)
, and the corresponding entropy

\max\left\{H_{n}\left(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\right),\,H_{n}\left(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\right)\right\}.

It is clear that we have many pairs
(x,y)
, and for each pair, the corresponding Frostman constants in (
3
) are not uniformly bounded. Besides technical issues including Lipschitz conditions and partitions of measure supports, the main difficulty arises because the Frostman constants may grow with
n
. Note that the idea of scaling the Frostman constants to
1
does not help, since it would change the supports of variables from bounded to unbounded.

We address this challenge by explicitly computing the entropy of the distance function. Instead of (
2
), we have the following for both small and large dimensions:

Large dimension:
Let
W
be an
(2s,C)
-Frostman random variable with
range(W)\subseteq B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
1/2<s<1
,
C\geq 1
and
R>0
. Let
W^{\prime}
be a random variable which is i.i.d with
W
. Then for any number
t
satisfying
t\leq 1
and
s<t<2s-1/2
, we have that for all
n\in\mathbb{N},

H_{n}(|W-W^{\prime}|)\geq nt-\log C+O(|\log R|)+O(|\log(2s-t-1/2)|)+O_{t}(1).

(4)

Small dimension:

Let
X^{\prime\prime}
,
Y^{\prime\prime}
be independent random variables with ranges contained in
[-c,c]
, with
c>0
. Suppose that
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are
(s,C_{1})
-Frostman and
(s,C_{2})
-Frostman, respectively, where
0<s\leq 1/2
and
C_{1},C_{2}\geq 1
. Let
(X^{\prime\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime\prime})
be i.i.d. with
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
. Then for any
t
with
s<t<\frac{s+\varphi(2s)}{2}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

H_{n}\big(|(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})-(X^{\prime\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime\prime})|\big)\geq nt+O_{s}(\log n)+O_{s,t}(\log C_{1}C_{2})+O_{s,c}(1),

(5)

where

\varphi(u)=\frac{u}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{4+u^{2}}-2}{2},\quad(0<u\leq 1).

(6)

By (
4
) and (
5
), the error terms involving the Frostman constants in (
3
) are controlled up to logarithmic level. Then they can be further converted to certain entropy-terms that are feasible to handle. The proof of (
4
) is essentially the same as that of (
2
). To prove (
5
), we adapt the Shmerkin–Wang approach with appropriate modifications, while clarifying, simplifying, and quantifying key steps. Note that our inspection is not verbatim: for instance, in the thin‐tube step we must work with sets having a Cartesian‐product structure. This is why the estimate (
5
) holds for
W=X^{\prime\prime}\times Y^{\prime\prime}
rather than in the general form of (
4
).

Plugging these inequalities in our method, the theorem follows.

Our final comment is that entropy methods have also been used in a range of problems in Additive Combinatorics and Geometric Measure Theory, for example, see
[
3
,
6
,
9
,
15
,

,
20
,
21
,

]
and references therein.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

Let
(\Omega,\mathfrak{B},P)
be a probability space. A random variable
X
is a measurable function from
(\Omega,\mathfrak{B},P)
to another measuable space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathfrak{B}^{\prime})
, on which a pushforward probability measure
\mu
is induced, i.e.,
\mu(B^{\prime})=P(X\in B^{\prime})
for any
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
. We will say that
\mu
is the associated distribution measure of
X
. We denote the range of
X
by
range(X)
, which collects all the possible values that
X
can take. In this paper, all the measurable spaces will be taken to be subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra or its sub-
\sigma
-algebra, and the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. The dimension
m
is always regarded as an absolute constant. The range of any random variable is assumed to be a bounded set. When two random variables
X
and
Y
have the same distribution, we write
X\equiv Y
.

More generally, let
\mu
be a Borel measure. Its support, the smallest closed set whose complement has zero measure, is denoted by
spt(\mu)
. When
\mu
is the distribution measure associated to a random variable
X
, we also write
range_{+}(X)=spt(\mu)
. That is to say,
X
takes value in
range_{+}(X)
with probability
1
.

For a measure
\mu
and a Borel set
E
with
\mu(E)>0
, we define the conditional measure of
\mu
with respect to
E
by
\mu_{E}(B)=\frac{\mu(B\cap E)}{\mu(E)}
for any Borel set
B
. In particular, when
\mu=P
, the conditional probability measure given an event
E
is denoted by
P_{E}
. For a random varaible
X
and such an
E
, we denote by
X_{E}
the random variable satisfying
X_{E}\equiv(X|E)
(see Lemma
B.2
). That is to say,

P_{E}(X_{E}\in B)=\frac{P(\{X\in B\}\cap E)}{P(E)}

for any
B\in\mathfrak{B}
.

For a measurable function
f:\,(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\rightarrow(\Omega^{\prime\prime},\mathfrak{B}^{\prime\prime})
between measurable spaces, the random variable
f(X)
is defined to be the composition of
f
and
X
. The domain and range of
f
are denoted by
domain(f)
and
range(f)
, respectively. Throughout this paper, all the functions are assumed to be measurable. And whenever we write
f(X)
with
X
a random variable and
f
a function, we always mean that
domain(f)\supseteq range_{+}(X)
, where the values taken on a zero-measure set is neglectable.

In this paper, by
f=O_{\kappa}(g)
,
f\ll_{\kappa}g
or
g\gg_{\kappa}f
, we mean that there exists an absolute positive constant
C=C(\kappa)
depending on the parameter(s)
\kappa
such that
|f|\leq Cg
. All logarithms we use here have base
2
.

2.2
Entropy and basic properties

Our primary tools will involve entropy. We begin by recalling some basic definitions and elementary consequences thereof. More discussions can be found in
[
6
,
17
,
7
,
11
,
22
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Entropy of partitions)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a measure space. For a countable partition
\mathcal{A}
of
\Omega
, the entropy of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
H(\mu,\mathcal{A}),
is defined to be

H(\mu,\mathcal{A})=-\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)\log\mu(A).

For two countable partitions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, the conditional entropy of
\mathcal{A}
given
\mathcal{B},
denoted
H(\mu,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B}),
is defined as

H(\mu,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B})=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mu(B)H(\mu_{B},\mathcal{A}).

In above definition, the convention
0\log 0=0
is used for convenience. We also say
\mu(B)H(\mu_{B},\mathcal{A})=0
when
\mu(B)=0
, although
\mu_{B}
is not defined in this situation. Intuitively, the entropy of a random variable will tell us how hard it is to determine a value. Before stating the first lemma, we introduce the following notation:

Z(\mathcal{A})=\{A\in\mathcal{A}\mid\mu(A)>0\},\quad\mathcal{A}\land\mathcal{B}=\{A\cap B\mid A\ \in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}\}.

Moreover, the function
\iota(x)=-x\log x
is non-negative valued, bounded and concave on
[0,1]
. It is increasing on
[0,e^{-1}]
, and decreasing on
[e^{-1},1]
. With this in tow, we record some properties of entropy for partitions, whose proofs are simple exercises.

Lemma 2.2
.

For a probability measure
\mu
and countable partitions
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
, we have the following statements:

(i)
H(\mu,\mathcal{A})\geq 0
with equality holding if and only if
|Z(\mathcal{A})|=1.

(ii)
H(\mu,\mathcal{A})\leq\log|Z(\mathcal{A})|
when
Z(\mathcal{A})
is a finite set, with equality holding if and only if
\mu(A)=1/|Z(\mathcal{A})|
for all
A\in Z(\mathcal{A}).

(iii)
H(\mu,\mathcal{A})+H(\mu,\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{A})=H(\mu,A\land B).

(iv)
H(\mu,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B})\leq H(\mu,\mathcal{A})
, or equivalently
H(\mu,\mathcal{A}\land\mathcal{B})\leq H(\mu,\mathcal{A})+H(\mu,\mathcal{B})
, with equality holding if and only if
\mu(A\cap B)=\mu(A)\mu(B)
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

(v)
H(\mu,\mathcal{A}\land\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{C})\leq H(\mu,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{C})+H(\mu,\mathcal{B}|\mathcal{C})
.

(vi) For any Borel set
Y
,
we have

\mu(Y)H(\mu_{Y},\mathcal{A})\leq H(\mu,\mathcal{A}).

For discrete random variables
X
and
Y
defined on
(\Omega,\mathfrak{B},P)
, the families

\mathcal{A}=\big\{\{X=x\}:\,x\in range(X)\big\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}=\big\{\{Y=y\}:\,y\in range(Y)\big\}

give two partitions of
\Omega
. We have the following definitions.

Definition 2.3

(Entropy of random variables)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be discrete random variables, and
E
be an event. The entropy of
X
is defined as

H(X)=-\sum_{x}P(X=x)\log P(X=x).

The conditional entropy of
X
given
E
is defined as

H(X|E)=H(X_{E})=-\sum\limits_{x}P_{E}(X=x)\log P_{E}(X=x).

And the conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
is defined as

H(X|Y)=\sum_{y}P(Y=y)H(X|Y=y).

For the random variable
Z=(X,Y)
, we denote
H(Z)=H(X,Y)
, instead of
H\big((X,Y)\big)
. We now state some basic properties of entropy for discrete random variables. These properties are well established in the literature, so we omit their proofs.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X,Y
be discrete random variables, and
f
be a function. The following statements hold.

(i)
H(X)\geq 0
with equality holding if and only if
|range_{+}(X)|=1
.

(ii)
H(X)\leq\log|range_{+}(X)|
when
range_{+}(X)
is a finite set, with equality holding if and only if
X
is uniformly distributed on
range_{+}(X)
.

(iii)
H(X)+H(Y|X)=H(X,Y)
, and in particular,
H(X)\leq H(X,Y)
.

(iv)
H(X|Y)\leq H(X)
, or equivalently
H(X,Y)\leq H(X)+H(Y)
, with equality holding when
X
and
Y
are independent.

(v)
H(f(X)|X)=0
, and furthermore
H(f(X))\leq H(X)
.

As a direct consequence, for discrete random variables
X
and
Y
,

H(X)\leq H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X|Y).

(7)

Moreover, Lemma
2.4
(iv) can be generalized to conditional entropy:
H(X_{1},X_{2}|Y)\leq H(X_{1}|Y)+H(X_{2}|Y)
, with equality holding if
X_{1},X_{2}
are conditionally independent given
Y
.

The following lemma shows a concave property of the entropy of a random variable.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
X
be a random variable and
\mathcal{B}
be a finite partition of
\Omega
. Then

H(X)\geq\sum\limits_{B\in\mathcal{B}}P(B)H(X_{B}).

Proof.

Take
\mathcal{A}=\big\{\{X=x\}:\,x\in range(X)\big\}
. Then Lemma
2.2
(iv) shows that

H(X)=H(P,\mathcal{A})\geq H(P,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B})=\sum\limits_{B\in\mathcal{B}}P(B)H(P_{B},\mathcal{A})=\sum\limits_{B\in\mathcal{B}}P(B)H(X_{B}).

∎

In the following, we introduce a statement of the submodular inequality, taken from
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.6

(Submodularity)
.

Let
X,Y,Z,W
be discrete random variables. If
X
is a function of
Z
,
X
is also a function of
W
, and furthermore
Y
is a function of
(Z,W)
, then

H(X)+H(Y)\leq H(Z)+H(W).

We often use the following corollary of submodularity.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
X,Y,Z,W
be discrete random variables. We have

H(X)+H(Y)\leq H(Z)+H(W)+H(X|Z)+H(X|W)+H(Y|Z,W).

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.6
, we have

H(X,Z)+H(X,W)\geq H(X,Z,W)+H(X).

So, by applying Lemma
2.4
(iii) and (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle H(Z)+H(W)+H(X|Z)+H(X|W)+H(Y|Z,W)

\displaystyle\quad=H(X,Z)+H(X,W)+H(Y|Z,W)\geq H(Y|Z,W)+H(X,Z,W)+H(X)

\displaystyle\quad\geq H(Y|Z,W)+H(Z,W)+H(X)\geq H(Y)+H(X).

Hence, the corollary follows.
∎

The next lemma is taken from
[
22
]
, namely, the estimate (22).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a discrete random variable, and
X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}
be conditionally i.i.d. copies of
X
given
Y
. Then

H(X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime},Y)=2H(X,Y)-H(Y).

The following is known as the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality. A proof can be found several places, for example,
[
7
, Theorem 3.11]
.

Lemma 2.9

(Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality)
.

Let
X,Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{m}
be independent discrete random variables. Then

H(X+Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{m})-H(X)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\big(H(X+Y_{i})-H(X)\big),

H(Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{m})-H(X)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\big(H(X+Y_{i})-H(X)\big).

The next lemma is helpful in getting an upper bound for certain entropy-terms.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
0<\lambda\leq 1/2
. Suppose that
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
are non-negative numbers satisfying
\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\leq\lambda
. Then

-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\log p_{i}\leq\lambda\log k+O(1).

Proof.

Define
p_{k+1}=1-\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
, which satisfies
p_{k+1}\geq 1-\lambda\geq 1/2
. Then we assume without loss of generality that

p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{k}\leq p_{k+1}.

Let us consider the the maximum value of the function

f(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k+1})=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{i}\log p_{i},

which is attainable in the feasible region of
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k+1})
. For any
1\leq j<i\leq k+1
, denote

\tilde{f}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{j-1},p_{j+1},\ldots,p_{k+1})=f(p_{1},\ldots,p_{j-1},1-\sum\nolimits_{l\neq j}p_{l},p_{j+1},\ldots,p_{k+1}).

In view of the fact that
p_{i}\geq p_{j}
, one deduces that

\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial p_{i}}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{j-1},p_{j+1},\ldots,p_{k+1})=\log\frac{1-\sum\nolimits_{l\neq j}p_{l}}{p_{i}}=\log\frac{p_{j}}{p_{i}}\leq 0.

That is to say, when the other
k-1
variables are given, the small
p_{i}
is, the larger value
\tilde{f}
and
f
take. Now one can verify that the maximum value of
f
is taken at
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k+1})
with

p_{i}=\frac{\lambda}{k}\,\,(1\leq i\leq k),\qquad p_{k+1}=1-\lambda.

That is to say,

-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{i}\log p_{i}\leq k\cdot\left(-\frac{\lambda}{k}\log\frac{\lambda}{k}\right)-(1-\lambda)\log(1-\lambda)\leq\lambda\log k+1.

It follows that

-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\log p_{i}\leq\lambda\log k+1+p_{k+1}\log p_{k+1}=\lambda\log k+O(1).

∎

2.3
Discretized entropy and properties

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let us define a function
D_{n,m}:\,{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
as the following. For any
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, if
k_{i}
is the unique integer such that
\frac{k_{i}}{2^{n}}\leq x_{i}<\frac{k_{i}+1}{2^{n}}

(i=1,\ldots,m)
, then we define
D_{n,m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=(\frac{k_{1}}{2^{n}},\ldots,\frac{k_{m}}{2^{n}})
. That is to say,
D_{n,m}(x)
locates the left endpoints of the dyadic intervals that the
x_{i}
’s fall in. For convenience, we will simplify
D_{n,m}
as
D_{n}
, where the dimension
m
is determined from the context. For any random variable
X
, the expression
D_{n}(X)
is a discrete random variable that takes values in the set of points whose components are all dyadic rationals. Moreover, if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
are independent (or identically distributed) random variables, then
D_{n}(X_{1}),\ldots,D_{n}(X_{k})
are also independent (or identically distributed, respectively).

For random variables that are not discrete, we can discretize them with the function
D_{n}
. For convenience, for any random variables
X
and
Y
, we denote the discretized entropy by

H_{n}(X):=H(D_{n}(X)),\qquad H_{n}(X|Y):=H(D_{n}(X)\,|\,D_{n}(Y)).

For a probability measure
\mu
, we introduce similar notation. Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
be the collection of all
m
-dimensional cubes
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\,D_{n}(x)=k2^{-n}\}
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. And define
H_{n}(\mu)=H_{n}(\mu,\mathcal{D}_{n})
. When
\mu
is the distribution measure associated to
X
, the above two defintions coincide.

Some properties still hold if we replace
H
by
H_{n}
. For example,

H_{n}(X|Y)=H(D_{n}(X)|D_{n}(Y))=H(D_{n}(X),D_{n}(Y))-H(D_{n}(Y))=H_{n}(X,Y)-H_{n}(Y).

When
X
and
Y
are independent, then
D_{n}(X)
and
D_{n}(Y)
are also independent, which gives

H_{n}(X,Y)=H(D_{n}(X),D_{n}(Y))=H(D_{n}(X))+H(D_{n}(Y))=H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y).

(8)

Generally, one has

H_{n}(X,Y)\leq H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y).

(9)

Combining Lemma
B.2
, one can verify that the statement in Lemma
2.5
also holds for
H_{n}
: If
X
is a random variable and
\mathcal{B}
is a finite partition. Then

H_{n}(X)\geq\sum\limits_{B\in\mathcal{B}}P(B)H_{n}(X_{B}).

(10)

Moreover, the discretized entropy only depends on the discretised distribution. That is to say, if
D_{n}(X)
and
D_{n}(Y)
are identically distributed, then
H_{n}(X)=H_{n}(Y)
.

Furthermore, if
range_{+}(X)\subseteq B(0,R)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for some
R\geq 1
, then
range_{+}(D_{n}(X))
has at most
O_{m}\big(R^{m}2^{mn}\big)
elements. By Lemma
2.4
(ii), it satisfies that

H_{n}(X)\leq mn+m\log R+O_{m}(1).

(11)

The submodular inequality will still hold if we replace the normal entropy with this special kind of entropy, as long as some additional conditions hold, as seen in the following definition.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\kappa
be a positive real number, and
f
be a function.

(i) We say that
f
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz if
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\kappa|x-y|
for any
x,y\in domain(f)
.

(ii) We say that
f
is
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz if
f
is invertible, and both
f
and
f^{-1}
are
\kappa
-Lipschitz.

(iii) If
f
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz, and there exists a decomposition of
domain(f)=\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{R}_{l}
, where
l
is a positive integer viewed as an absolute constant, such that each
f|_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}
has an
\kappa
-Lipschitz inverse
(i=1,\ldots,l)
, then we say that
f
is weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz.

Example 2.12
.

Let
D
be an open convex region in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Let
f:\,D\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a function such that
|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})|\leq M
for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in D
. Then, for any
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
and
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
in
D
, there is some
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})\in D
such that

|f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})-f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})|\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})\right||x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq Mm^{1/2}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore
f
is
O(M)
-Lipschitz, where
m
is viewed as an absolute constant.

Example 2.13
.

Let
\alpha,c_{1},c_{2}
be real numbers with
\alpha\neq 0
and
0<c_{2}<c_{1}
. Let
f(x)=\alpha x^{2}
with domain
[-c_{1},-c_{2}]\cup[c_{2},c_{1}]
. Note that, by the mean value theorem,

2|\alpha|c_{2}\cdot|x-y|\leq|f(x)-f(y)|\leq 2|\alpha|c_{1}\cdot|x-y|

whenever
x,y\in[-c_{1},-c_{2}]
, or
x,y\in[c_{2},c_{1}]
. One asserts that
f
is weakly
\max\left\{(2|\alpha|c_{2})^{-1},\,2|\alpha|c_{1}\right\}
-bi-Lipschitz.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
X
,
Y
be random variables and
\kappa
be a postive real number. We say that
Y
is a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function (or
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz function, or weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz function) of
X
if
Y=f(X)
for some
\kappa
-Lipschitz function (or
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz function, or weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz function, respectively)
f
with
domain(f)\supseteq range_{+}(X)
.

If a function is
\kappa
-Lipschitz with
0<\kappa\leq 2
, then it is also
2
-Lipschitz. Throughout this paper, we always assume without loss of generality that
\kappa\gg 1
, and then
\log\kappa\gg 1
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables. We have the following two statements.

(i) If
f
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz, then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(f(X)|X)\ll\log\kappa.

(ii) If
g
be weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz, then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(Y|g(Y)))\ll\log\kappa.

The above implied constants are independent of
n
, and the concrete expressions of the functions and random variables.

Proof.

We show the proof of (ii) here; (i) follows by similar arguments.

Assume that
domain(g)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
range(g)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m^{\prime}}
, where
m,m^{\prime}
are viewed as absolute constants. When
D_{n}(g(Y))
is determined, one knows that
g(Y)
takes value in a ball with radius
O_{m^{\prime}}(2^{-n})
. By the definition of weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz functions, we can locate
Y
to be lying in the pre-image of this ball, which is a union of at most
O(1)
balls with radius
O_{m^{\prime}}(\kappa\cdot 2^{-n})
. So
D_{n}(Y)
has at most
O_{m,m^{\prime}}(\kappa^{m})
choices. It follows from Lemma
2.4
(ii) that

H_{n}(Y|g(Y))\leq\log(O_{m,m^{\prime}}(\kappa^{m}))\ll\log\kappa.

The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

We have the following two statements.

(i) Let
X
and
Y
be random variables such that
X
is an
\kappa
-Lipschitz function of
Y
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(X)\leq H_{n}(Y)+O(\log\kappa).

(ii) Let
X
and
Y
be random variables such that
X
is a weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz function of
Y
. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(X)=H_{n}(Y)+O(\log\kappa).

Proof.

Regarding (i), by (
7
), it is sufficient to prove that
H_{n}(X|Y)\ll\log\kappa
, which is guaranteed by Lemma
2.15
(i). Regarding (ii), by (
7
) and Lemma
2.15
(ii), we conclude that

H_{n}(Y)\leq H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y|X)=H_{n}(X)+O(\log\kappa).

The conclusion then follows.
∎

Example 2.17
.

Let
X
be a random variable with
range_{+}(X)\subseteq[-c_{1},-c_{2}]\cup[c_{2},c_{1}]
, where
0<c_{2}<c_{1}
. Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ast}
. One can deduce from Example
2.13
and Lemma
2.16
that

H_{n}(\alpha X^{2})=H_{n}(X)+O\big(\max\{1,\log c_{1},\log(1/c_{2})\}\big)+O(\big|\log|\alpha|\big|).

The following is the discretized version of submodularity.

Lemma 2.18
.

If
X
is a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function of
Z
, and also a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function of
W
, and furthermore,
Y
is a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function of
(Z,W)
, then

H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y)\leq H_{n}(Z)+H_{n}(W)+O(\log\kappa)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.7
, we only need to bound
H_{n}(X|Z)+H_{n}(X|W)+H_{n}(Y|Z,W)
by
O(\log\kappa)
. This is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.15
.
∎

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
X
,
Y
be random variables,
and
f
be a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function with
range(f)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, where
m
is viewed as an absolute constant. Suppose that
|X-Y|\leq K
with probability
1
.
Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
2^{-n}\leq K
,

H_{n}(f(X))\leq H_{n}(f(Y))+m(n+\log K)+O(\log\kappa).

And, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
K=O(2^{-n})
,

H_{n}(f(X))=H_{n}(f(Y))+O(\log\kappa).

In particular, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(f(X))=H_{n}(f(D_{n}(X)))+O(\log\kappa).

Proof.

Since
f
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz, we have

|f(X)-f(Y)|\leq\kappa|X-Y|\leq\kappa K

with probability
1
. By (
7
), it follows that

H_{n}(f(X))\leq H_{n}(f(Y))+H_{n}(f(X)|f(Y)).

When
D_{n}(f(Y)))=t
is determined, one has
|f(Y)-t|=O_{m}(2^{-n})
and then
|f(X)-t|\leq\kappa K+O_{m}(2^{-n})
. So
D_{n}(f(X))
has at most
O\big((\kappa K\cdot 2^{n}+O_{m}(1))^{m}\big)
choices. It follows that

H_{n}(f(X))\leq H_{n}(f(Y))+\max\left\{\log\big((\kappa K2^{n})^{m}\big),\,0\right\}+O_{m}(1).

Then the first conclusion follows. When
K=O(2^{-n})
, it satisfies that
H_{n}(f(X))\leq H_{n}(f(Y))+O(\log\kappa)
. And the second conclusion follows by symmetry. The third conclusion follows from the fact that
|X-D_{n}(X)|=O(2^{-n})
, i.e.
K=O(2^{-n})
.
∎

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
l\geq 2
and
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{l}
be random variables. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

H_{n}(X_{1}+X_{2}+\ldots+X_{l})=H(D_{n}(X_{l})+D_{n}(X_{2})+\ldots+D_{n}(X_{l}))+O(\log l).

Proof.

Note that
\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{l}D_{n}(X_{i})
takes values in the dyadic rationals. One has

H\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}D_{n}(X_{i})\right)=H\left(D_{n}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}D_{n}(X_{i})\Big)\right)=H_{n}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}D_{n}(X_{i})\right).

The conclusion then follows from Lemma
2.19
with the
O(l)
-Lipschitz function (see Example
2.12
) given by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})\mapsto x_{1}+\ldots+x_{l}.

∎

Now we show a discretized version of the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality.

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
l\geq 1
and
X,Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{l}
be independent random variables. Then

H_{n}(X+Y_{1}+Y_{2}+\ldots+Y_{l})-H_{n}(X)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\big(H_{n}(X+Y_{i})-H_{n}(X)\big)+O(\log l),

and

H_{n}(Y_{1}+Y_{2}+\ldots+Y_{l})-H_{n}(X)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\big(H_{n}(X+Y_{i})-H_{n}(X)\big)+O(\log l).

Proof.

Note that
D_{n}(X),D_{n}(Y_{1}),\ldots D_{n}(Y_{l})
are also independent. The conclusion follows from Lemmas
2.9
and
2.20
.
∎

Corollary 2.22
.

Let
X,Y,
and
Z
be independent random variables. Then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle H_{n}(X)

\displaystyle\leq H_{n}(X+Y)+O(1),

\displaystyle H_{n}(X+Y+Z)

\displaystyle\leq H_{n}(X+Z)+H_{n}(Y+Z)-H_{n}(Z)+O(1),

\displaystyle H_{n}(X\pm Y)

\displaystyle\leq H_{n}(X+Z)+H_{n}(Y+Z)-H_{n}(Z)+O(1).

Proof.

We only show the proof of the third inequality involving
H_{n}(X-Y)
here. The others are direct consequences of Lemma
2.21
.

By Lemma
2.18
,

H_{n}(X-Y)+H_{n}(X,Y,Z)\leq H_{n}(X+Z,Y+Z)+H_{n}(X,Y)+O(1).

By the independence of
X,Y,
and
Z,
(specifically, by invoking (
8
) and (
9
)) it follows that

H_{n}(X-Y)+H_{n}(X,Y)+H_{n}(Z)\leq H_{n}(X+Z)+H_{n}(Y+Z)+H_{n}(X,Y)+O(1).

The conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent real random variables and
k,l\in\mathbb{Q}
. There exist non-negative integers
d_{1}=d_{1}(k,l)
and
d_{2}=d_{2}(k,l)
such that

H_{n}(kX+lY)\leq(d_{1}+d_{2}+1)H_{n}(X+Y)-d_{1}H_{n}(X)-d_{2}H_{n}(Y)+O_{k,l}(1).

Here by
kX
we mean the product of
k
and the random variable
X
.

Proof.

The proof is trivial when
k=l=0
. When
l=0
and
k\neq 0
, we have

H_{n}(kX)=H_{n}(X)+O_{k}(1)\leq H_{n}(X+Y)+O_{k}(1)

by Corollary
2.22
. Similar conclusion holds when
k=0
. In the following, we assume that
k,l\neq 0
.

First, let us prove the case that
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ast}
. We apply double induction on
|k|
and
|l|
. When
|k|=|l|=1
, we claim that

H_{n}(kX+lY)\leq 3H_{n}(X+Y)-H_{n}(X)-H_{n}(Y)+O(1).

(12)

Indeed, when
k=l=1
, one can deduce (
12
) from the fact that

H_{n}(X+Y)\geq\max\{H_{n}(X),H_{n}(Y)\}+O(1).

When
k=1
and
l=-1
, we take
X,Y,Z
to be independent random variables such that
X
and
Z
are identically distributed. Then Corollary
2.22
gives

H_{n}(X-Y)\leq H_{n}(X+Z)+H_{n}(Y+Z)-H_{n}(Z)+O(1).

By Corollary
2.22
again, we have

H_{n}(X+Z)\leq H_{n}(X+Y)+H_{n}(Z+Y)-H_{n}(Y)+O(1).

(13)

Then (
12
) follows. Since
H_{n}(W)=H_{n}(-W)
for any random variable
W
, the cases that
k=-1
and
l=\pm 1
are also satisfied.

Now let us assume that the conclusion has been proved for some
k
and
l
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
k>0
. We aim to prove the conclusion for
k+1
and
l
. Lemma
2.18
gives us that

H_{n}((k+1)X+lY+Z)+H_{n}(X,Y,Z)\leq H_{n}(Z+lY,X)+H_{n}(X+Z,kX+lY)+O_{k,l}(1),

where the random variable
Z
is taken such that
X,Y,Z
are independent and
X
,
Z
are identically distributed. Combining Corollary
2.22
, we obtain that

\displaystyle H_{n}((k+1)X+lY)\leq H_{n}((k+1)X+lY+Z)+O_{k,l}(1)

\displaystyle\leq H_{n}(Z+lY,X)+H_{n}(X+Z,kX+lY)-H_{n}(X,Y,Z)+O_{k,l}(1)

\displaystyle\leq H_{n}(Z+lY)+H_{n}(X+Z)+H_{n}(kX+lY)-H_{n}(Y)-H_{n}(Z)+O_{k,l}(1).

Combining (
13
) and the inductive hypothesis on
k
, one sees that the conclusion holds with some
d_{1}(k,l)
and
d_{2}(k,l)
satisfying

d_{1}(k+1,l)=d_{1}(1,l)+d_{1}(k,l)+1,\quad d_{2}(k,l)=d_{2}(1,l)+d_{2}(k,l)+2.

By symmetry, we also have the induction on
l
, and the proof is completed for the situation that
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ast}
.

Second, when
k,l\in\mathbb{Q}^{\ast}
, we can write
k=k^{\prime}/t
and
l=l^{\prime}/t
for some
k^{\prime},l^{\prime},t\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ast}
. It follows that

\displaystyle H_{n}(kX+lY)

\displaystyle=H_{n}(k^{\prime}X+l^{\prime}Y)+O_{t}(1)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{1}+d_{2}+1)H_{n}(X+Y)-d_{1}H_{n}(X)-d_{2}H_{n}(Y)+O_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime},t}(1)

for some
d_{1}=d_{1}(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})
and
d_{2}=d_{2}(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})
. The lemma then follows.
∎

Lemma
2.23
implies the following statement: For given

, one has

H_{n}(kX+lY)-H_{n}(X)\ll_{k,l}(H_{n}(X+Y)-H_{n}(X))+O_{k,l}(1)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any i.i.d. random variables
X
,
Y
with bounded range. However, when
k,l
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent, such statement may fail. In Example
A.6
, we would show the following: For any
K>0
, there exists some

such that: for any

, there is a random variable

with range contained in

satisfying

H_{n}(X+\sqrt{2}Y)-H_{n}(X)\geq K(H_{n}(X+Y)-H_{n}(X)),

where

is i.i.d. with

.

2.4
Frostman measures

Now we introduce Frostman measures and their basic properties.

Definition 2.24
.

Let
s,C>0
. We say a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
(s,C)
-Frostman if for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
r>0

\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{s}.

Similarly we also say a random variable
X
is
(s,C)
-Frostman if its distribution measure is
(s,C)
-Frostman, meaning for any
x
and
r>0
we have

P(X\in B(x,r))\leq Cr^{s}.

For a random variable or a probability measure, when
0\leq s_{2}\leq s_{1}\leq 1
and
1\leq C_{1}\leq C_{2}
, the
(s_{1},C_{1})
-Frostman condition can imply the
(s_{2},C_{2})
-Frostman condition.

The next lemma presents the Frostman conditions related to the arithmetic of random variables.

Lemma 2.25
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent real random variables that are both
(s,C)
-Frostman, where
0<s\leq 1
and
C>0
. Then the following statements hold for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i) The random variable
aX+bY
is
(s,O_{a,b}(C))
-Frostman.

(ii) For any invertible linear change of variables
(x_{1},y_{1})=(ax+by,cx+dy)
, the random variable
(X_{1},Y_{1})=(aX+bY,cX+dY)
is
(2s,O_{a,b,c,d}(C^{2}))
-Frostman.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{X}
, and
\mu_{Y}
be the distribution measures associated to
X
and
Y
, respectively.

(i) When
b\neq 0
, for any ball
B(y^{\prime},r)
one has

P(aX+bY\in B(y^{\prime},r))=\int\mu_{Y}\left(B\left(\frac{y^{\prime}-ax}{b},\frac{r}{|b|}\right)\right)d\mu_{X}(x)\leq\int C\left(\frac{r}{|b|}\right)^{s}d\mu_{X}(y)=O_{b}(Cr^{s}).

A similar conclusion follows when
a\neq 0
. When
a=b=0
, the conclusion follows automatically.

(ii) For any ball
B((x,y),r)
,

\displaystyle P((aX+bY,cX+dY)\in B((x,y),r)

\displaystyle\leq P(aX+bY\in B(x,r),cX+dY\in B(y,r)).

If both
aX+bY\in B(x,r)
and
cX+dY\in B(y,r)
occur, we must have

X\in B\left(\frac{dx-by}{ad-bc},\frac{(|d|+|b|)r}{|ad-bc|}\right)
and
Y\in B\left(\frac{-cx+ay}{ad-bc},\frac{(|c|+|a|)r}{|ad-bc|}\right)
.
Therefore,

P(aX+bY\in B(x,r),cX+dY\in B(y,r))\leq C\left(\frac{(|d|+|b|)r}{|ad-bc|}\right)^{s}\cdot C\left(\frac{(|c|+|a|)r}{|ad-bc|}\right)^{s}=O_{a,b,c,d}(C^{2}r^{2s}).

The lemma now follows.
∎

The next property is taken from
[
11
, Lemma 4.8]
.

Lemma 2.26
.

If
X
is
(s,C)
-Frostman for some
s,C>0
, then
H_{n}(X)\geq ns-\log C+O(1)
.

The function
x\mapsto x^{2}
is weakly-bi-Lipschitz in a region that is bounded away from zero. The following lemma shows the relation between a random variable before and after cutting off the values near zero.

Lemma 2.27
.

Let
Z
be a real random variable with bounded range. Assume that there is some
s>0
and
C\geq 1
such that
P(|Z|<r)\leq Cr^{s}
for any
r>0
. For any
0<\eta\leq 2^{-1}(2C)^{-1/s}
, denote by
E
the event that
|Z|\geq\eta
. Then the random variable
Z_{E}
satisfies

H_{n}(Z_{E})=\frac{H_{n}(Z)}{P(E)}+O(C(2\eta)^{s}n)+O(1).

for any all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Recall that
Z_{E}
is the random variable such that
Z_{E}\equiv(Z|E)
, and
P_{E}
is the conditional probability measure given
E
.

Since
P(|Z|<\eta)\leq C\eta^{s}\leq 1/2
, we have
P(E)\geq 1-C\eta^{s}\geq 1/2
. Assume that
k_{0}
is a natural number with
(k_{0}-1)2^{-n}\leq\eta<k_{0}2^{-n}
. Combining the conditions in this lemma, one deduces that

\displaystyle H_{n}(Z_{E})

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{|k|\geq k_{0}+1}P_{E}(D_{n}(Z_{E})=k2^{-n})\log P_{E}(D_{n}(Z_{E})=k2^{-n})

\displaystyle\qquad+O\left(-\sum\limits_{k_{0}-1\leq|k|\leq k_{0}}P_{E}(D_{n}(Z_{E})=k2^{-n})\log P_{E}(D_{n}(Z_{E})=k2^{-n})\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{|k|\geq k_{0}+1}\frac{P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})}{P(E)}\big(\log P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})-\log P(E)\big)+O(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{P(E)}\sum\limits_{|k|\geq k_{0}+1}P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\log P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})+O(1).

First, let us consider the case that
\eta>2^{-n+1}
, which gives
(k_{0}+1)2^{-n}\leq\eta+2^{-n+1}<2\eta
. Applying Lemma
2.10
with the fact

\sum\nolimits_{|k|\leq k_{0}}P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\leq P(|Z|<(k_{0}+1)2^{-n})\leq P(|Z|<2\eta)\leq C(2\eta)^{s}\leq 1/2,

we see that

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{|k|\leq k_{0}}P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\log P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq C(2\eta)^{s}\cdot\log\big(2\eta 2^{n}+3\big)+O(1)\leq C(2\eta)^{s}n+O(1).

Second, we consider the case that
\eta\leq 2^{-n+1}
. Then
k_{0}\leq 3
and

-\sum\nolimits_{|k|\leq k_{0}}P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\log P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})=O(1).

Finally, we conclude that

\displaystyle H(Z_{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{P(E)}\left(H_{n}(Z)+\sum\limits_{|k|\leq k_{0}}P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\log P(D_{n}(Z)=k2^{-n})\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{n}(Z)}{P(E)}+O(C(2\eta)^{s}n)+O(1).

∎

In the following lemma, we show that the probability that the distance function takes small values is related to the Frostman condition.

Lemma 2.28
.

Let
Z
,
Z^{\prime}
be independent random variables. Suppose that
Z
is
(s,C)
-Frostman, where
s,C>0
. Then

P\big(|Z-Z^{\prime}|<r)\leq Cr^{s}.

for any

.

Proof.

Denote by
\mu
the distribution measure associated to
Z^{\prime}
. Since
Z
is
(s,C)
-Frostman, one sees that

P(|Z-Z^{\prime}|<r)=\int P(|Z-z^{\prime}|<r)d\mu(z^{\prime})\leq\int Cr^{s}d\mu(z^{\prime})=Cr^{s}.

∎

The following definition of Riesz energy is related to the distance measure.

Definition 2.29
.

The
s
-Riesz energy of a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

I_{s}(\mu)=\int\int\frac{d\mu(x)d\mu(y)}{|x-y|^{s}}.

For any given measure
\mu
, define the measure
\Delta(\mu)
by

\Delta(\mu)(B)=\int\mu(\{y\in\Omega:|x-y|\in B\})d\mu(x)

for any Borel set
B
. That is to say,
\Delta(\mu)
is the image of
\mu\times\mu
under the distance map
(x,y)\mapsto|x-y|
. We now recall a result from Mattila in
[
12
, Section 4.2, page 61]
.

Lemma 2.30

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with
\spt(\mu)\subset B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for some
R>0
. Then, for any
0<t\leq 1
and for real numbers
0<\eta<r<2R
, we have
\Delta(\mu)([r,r+\eta])\ll_{t}r^{1/2}\eta^{t}I_{t+1/2}(\mu)
.

Lemma 2.31
.

Let
\mu
be a
(2s,C)
-Frostman probability measure with
\spt(\mu)\subset B(0,R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\frac{1}{4}<s\leq 1
,
C\geq 1
and
R>0
. Then for any
r,\eta,t
with
0<\eta<r<2R
,
0<t\leq 1
and
t<2s-\frac{1}{2}
, it satisfies that

\Delta(\mu)([r,r+\eta])=O_{t}\left(\frac{CR^{2s-t}\eta^{t}}{2s-t-1/2}\right)+O_{t}(R^{-t}\eta^{t}).

Proof.

Since
\mu
is
(2s,C)
-Frostman, for
0<t<2s-1/2
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\int|x-y|^{-t-1/2}d\mu(y)=(t+1/2)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(B(x,\ell))}{\ell^{t+3/2}}d\ell

\displaystyle\qquad\leq(t+1/2)\left(\int_{0}^{2R}C\ell^{2s-t-3/2}d\ell+\int_{2R}^{\infty}\ell^{-t-3/2}d\ell\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=O_{t}\left(\frac{CR^{2s-t-1/2}}{2s-t-1/2}\right)+O_{t}(R^{-t-1/2}):=M.

Thus

\displaystyle I_{t+1/2}(\mu)=\int\int|x-y|^{-t-1/2}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\leq\int Md\mu(x)=M.

When
0<\eta<r<2R
and
0<t\leq 1
, one obtains by Lemma
2.30
that

\Delta(\mu)([r,r+\eta])\ll_{t}r^{1/2}\eta^{t}I_{t+1/2}(\mu)=O_{t}\left(\frac{CR^{2s-t}\eta^{t}}{2s-t-1/2}\right)+O_{t}(R^{-t}\eta^{t}).

∎

Proof.

3
Discretized entropy Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem

In this section, we follow the method introduced by Tao in
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
to obtain a generalized discretized entropy version of the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem. We do not know whether such a generalization has been studied in the literature, so we provide a complete proof in this section. The Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem (or one of its many variants) says that if one has a finite subset of the integers whose sums line up a lot, then it must not have many sums. More precisely, given
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z},
whose elements
a_{j}\in A
have
\gamma|A|^{3}
relations of the form
a_{1}+a_{2}=a_{3}+a_{4},
there must be a large subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A,
where
|A^{\prime}|\geq C_{1}(\gamma)|A|
that satisfies
|A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}|\leq C_{2}(\gamma)|A^{\prime}|,
where the constants
C_{j}
above are positive and only depend on
\gamma.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n
be a natural number, and
(X,Y)
be a given random variable. Suppose that

H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y)-H_{n}(X,Y)\leq\log K_{1}.

(16)

Then, there exists a random variable
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
such that

(i)
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\equiv(X,Y)
;

(ii)
X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
D_{n}(Y^{\prime})
;

(iii)
Y^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
;

(iv)
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime})
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
.

In particular, the random variables
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given
D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
.
Moreover,

H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq H_{n}(X)-\log K_{1},

(17)

H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq H_{n}(Y)-\log K_{1}.

(18)

In addition, for any weakly
\kappa
-bi-Lipschitz functions
f
and
g
with

H_{n}(f(X)+g(Y))-\frac{H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y)}{2}\leq\log K_{2},

(19)

we have that

H_{n}(f(X^{\prime\prime})+g(Y^{\prime\prime})|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})-\frac{H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y)}{2}\leq 4\log K_{1}+3\log K_{2}+O(\log\kappa).

(20)

Remark 3.2
.

The original version of entropy Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem in
[
22
]
states for
H
instead of
H_{n}
, does not involve the functions
f
,
g
, and conditions (ii)–(iv) are “given
Y^{\prime}
,
X^{\prime}
,
X^{\prime}
” instead of “given
D_{n}(Y^{\prime})
,
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
,
D_{n}(X^{\prime})
”, respectively. In such a situation, (i)–(iv) is equivalent to the statement that
X^{\prime},Y,X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime}
forms a Markov chain.

The following lemmas are needed in the proof of this theorem.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n
be a natural number, and
(X,Y)
be a given random variable. Then there exists a random variable
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
such that (i)–(iv) in Theorem
3.1
hold, with

X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
being conditionally independent given
D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
B.1
with
A=0
,
B=X
,
C=Y
and
h=D_{n}
, we obtain a random variable
(X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2})
such that:
(X_{1},Y_{1})\equiv(X,Y)
;
X_{2}
and
X_{1}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
D_{n}(Y_{1})
.

Next, let us apply Lemma
B.1
again, with
A=X_{2}
,
B=Y_{1}
,

Lemma 4.1

,

Remark 4.2
.

Compared to Theorem
1.1
, this provides a stronger statement, namely, for all non-zero real-coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Proof.

Write
\phi(x,y)=p(x)+q(y)
, where
p(x)=\alpha x^{2}
and

are functions whose domains are both taken to be
[-c_{2},-c_{1}]\cup[c_{2},c_{1}]
. One asserts by Example

that

(31)

Define
W=(\sqrt{|\alpha|}X,\sqrt{|\beta|}Y),
with
|\alpha|X
and
|\beta|Y
being
(s,O_{\phi}(C_{1}))
-Frostman and
(s,O_{\phi}(C_{2}))
-Frostman, respectively. Let

,

be i.i.d. copies of

. Then
(X_{1},Y_{1})
,
(X_{2},Y_{2})
are i.i.d. copies of

, and

are independent.

Let us first consider the case that
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same sign. Since

, we assume without loss of generality that
\alpha,\beta>0

|W_{1}-W_{2}|^{2}=\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(Y_{1}-Y_{2})^{2}.

Calculation reveals that

\displaystyle\alpha(X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}+\beta(Y_{1}-Y_{2})^{2}=2(\alpha(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})+\beta(Y_{1}^{2}+Y_{2}^{2}))-\alpha(X_{1}+X_{2})^{2}-\beta(Y_{1}+Y_{2})^{2}.

So
|W_{1}-W_{2}|^{2}=f_{1}(X_{1}+X_{2},Y_{1}+Y_{2},p(X_{1})+p(X_{2})+q(Y_{1})+q(Y_{2}))
, where

f_{1}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=2z_{3}-\alpha z_{1}^{2}-\beta z_{2}^{2}.

From Example
2.12
and the fact that
range(X_{1}+X_{2}),range(Y_{1}+Y_{2})\subseteq[-2c_{1},2c_{1}]
, one sees that

is
O_{\phi,c_{1}}(1)
-Lipschitz. We also have that

-Lipschitz function

Moreover,

(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2})=f_{3}(X_{1}+X_{2},Y_{1}+Y_{2},p(X_{1})+p(X_{2})+q(Y_{1})+q(Y_{2}),X_{1}-X_{2},Y_{1}-Y_{2}),

2.18

\displaystyle H_{n}(|W_{1}-W_{2}|^{2})+H_{n}(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2})

\phi
, this means that it depends on the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
occurred in
\phi
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\phi(x,y)=\alpha(x-py)^{2}+\beta(x+y)^{2}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ast}
and
p\in\{0,1\}
. Let
X
and
Y
be independent
(s,C)
-Frostman random variables whose ranges are contained in
[-c_{1},c_{1}]
, where
1/2<s<1
,
C\geq 1
and
c_{1}>0
. Then there exists a positive constant
\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon_{2}(s)
and an

such that

\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}\geq n(s+\epsilon_{2})

for all
n>N
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we write

\Psi:=\max\left\{H_{n}(X+Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\right\}-ns,

(36)

and our aim is to prove that
\Psi\geq\epsilon_{2}n
for some positive
\epsilon_{2}
.

Since
X
,
Y
are independent and both
(s,C)
-Frostman, we deduce by Lemma
2.26
Corollary
2.22
and (
36
) that

\begin{split}&\min\{H_{n}(X),\,H_{n}(Y)\}\geq ns+O_{C}(1),\\
&\max\{H_{n}(X),\,H_{n}(Y)\}\leq H_{n}(X+Y)+O(1)\leq ns+\Psi+O(1).\end{split}

(37)

Let us define the random variable
(X_{0},Y_{0})
as

(X_{0},Y_{0})=(X-pY,X+Y)

with
p\in\{0,1\}
. The ranges of
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are both contained in
[-2c_{1},2c_{1}]
. And
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is an
O(1)
-bi-Lipschitz function of
(X,Y)
, which, because
X
and
Y
are independent, leads to the estimate

H_{n}(X_{0},Y_{0})=H_{n}(X,Y)+O(1)=H_{n}(X)+H_{n}(Y)+O(1)\leq 2ns+2\Psi+O(1).

(38)

By Lemma
2.25
, one deduces that
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are
(s,C_{1}^{\prime})
-Frostman and
(s,C_{2}^{\prime})
-Frostman, respectively, with
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}=O_{C}(1)
. Similarly, one also has that
X_{0}+Y_{0}=(2X+(1-p)Y)
is
(s,O_{C}(1))
-Frostman, and
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is
(2s,O_{C}(1))
-Frostman. We then show below that

H_{n}(X_{0})\leq ns+O(\Psi)+O_{C}(1),\quad H_{n}(X_{0}+Y_{0})\leq ns+O(\Psi)+O_{C}(1).

(39)

Indeed, when
p=0
, one sees by (
36
), (
37
) and Lemma
2.23
that

H_{n}(X_{0})=H_{n}(X)\leq ns+\Psi+O(1),

and

\displaystyle H_{n}(X_{0}+Y_{0})=H_{n}(2X+Y)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq H_{n}(X+Y)+O(H_{n}(X+Y)-H_{n}(X))+O(H(X+Y)-H(Y))+O(1)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq ns+O(\Psi)+O_{C}(1).

When
p=1
, it follows from (
12
), (
36
) and (
37
)
that

\displaystyle H_{n}(X_{0})=H_{n}(X-Y)\leq 3H_{n}(X+Y)-H(X)-H(Y)+O(1)\leq ns+3\Psi+O_{C}(1)

and

H_{n}(X_{0}+Y_{0})=H_{n}(2X)=H_{n}(X)+O(1)\leq ns+\Psi+O(1).

So (
39
) follows.

Next, let
\eta
be a parameter to be chosen later, with
0<\eta<\min\{(1/4C_{1}^{\prime})^{2},\,(1/4C_{2}^{\prime})^{2},\,1/10\}
. Then
C_{i}^{\prime}\eta^{s}<C_{i}^{\prime}\eta^{1/2}<1/4
for

. We denote by
E
the event that
|X_{0}|>\eta,|Y_{0}|>\eta
, and by
P_{E}
the corresponding conditional probability measure. Denote by
(X_{1},Y_{1})
the random variable such that
(X_{1},Y_{1})\equiv(X_{0},Y_{0}|E)
. Since

P(E)=P(|X_{0}|>\eta,|Y_{0}|>\eta)\geq 1-P(|X_{0}|<\eta)-P(|Y_{0}|<\eta)\geq 1-C_{1}^{\prime}\eta^{s}-C_{2}^{\prime}\eta^{s}>\frac{1}{2},

Lemma
B.2
shows that

(X_{1},Y_{1})
is
(2s,O_{C}(1))
-Frostman. Similarly, all of
X_{1}
,
Y_{1}
and
X_{1}+Y_{1}
are
(s,O_{C}(1))
-Frostman.
We can apply Lemmas
B.2
and
2.26
, then combine (
36
), (
38
), (
39
) to get that

\begin{split}&2ns+O_{C}(1)\leq H_{n}(X_{1},Y_{1})\leq\frac{H_{n}(X_{0},Y_{0})}{P(E)}\leq(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))(2ns+2\Psi+O(1)),\\
&ns+O_{C}(1)\leq H_{n}(X_{1})\leq\frac{H_{n}(X_{0})}{P(E)}\leq(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))(ns+O(\Psi)+O_{C}(1)),\\
&ns+O_{C}(1)\leq H_{n}(Y_{1})\leq\frac{H_{n}(X+Y)}{P(E)}\leq(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))(ns+\Psi),\\
&ns+O_{C}(1)\leq H_{n}(X_{1}+Y_{1})\leq\frac{H_{n}(X_{0}+Y_{0})}{P(E)}\leq(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))(ns+O(\Psi)+O_{C}(1)),\end{split}

(40)

and

H_{n}(\alpha X_{1}^{2}+\beta Y_{1}^{2})\leq\frac{H_{n}(\alpha X_{0}^{2}+\beta Y_{0}^{2})}{P(E)}=(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\leq(1+O_{C}(\eta^{s}))(ns+\Psi).

(41)

It follows from (
40
) and (
41
) that

\begin{split}&H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})-H_{n}(X_{1},Y_{1})=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1),\\
&H_{n}(X_{1}+Y_{1})-\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1),\\
&H_{n}(\alpha X_{1}^{2}+\beta Y_{1}^{2})-\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1).\end{split}

Now we write
\log K:=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1)
. Applying Theorem
3.1
with the random variable
(X_{1},Y_{1})
, we obtain the random variable
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
, where
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given

, and

H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq H_{n}(X_{1})-\log K,\quad H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq H_{n}(Y_{1})-\log K.\\

(42)

First, let us take the functions
f
and
g
in Theorem
3.1
to be the identity functions. Then

H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})-\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}\leq 7\log K+O(1).

(43)

Second, we take the functions
f,g
in Theorem
3.1
to be
f(x)=\alpha x^{2}
and
g(y)=\beta y^{2}
. By the construction
(X_{1},Y_{1})\equiv(X_{0},Y_{0}||X_{0}|>\eta,|Y_{0}|>\eta)
, the ranges of
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
are contained in
[-2c_{1},-\eta]\cup[\eta,2c_{1}]
with probability
1
. Now, by Example
2.13
, the random variables
f(X^{\prime\prime})
and
g(Y^{\prime\prime})
are weakly
O_{\phi}(\max\{\eta^{-1},c_{1}\})
-bi-Lipschitz functions of
X^{\prime\prime}
and
Y^{\prime\prime}
, respectively. It follows that

H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})-\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}\leq 7\log K+O_{\phi,c_{1},\eta}(1).

(44)

Next, conditionally given that
D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)
with
x,y
being dyadic rationals satisfying
P\big(D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)>0
, we will prove the following results on induced conditional probability measures:

\begin{split}\big(X^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)\text{ is $\left(s,O\left(C^{2}\cdot 2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)\right)\right)$-Frostman},\\
\big(Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)\big)\text{ is $\left(s,O\left(C^{2}\cdot 2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)\right)\right)$-Frostman},\end{split}

(45)

where the implied constants do not depend on
(x,y)
.
We present the proof for
X^{\prime\prime}
, as the one for
Y^{\prime\prime}
can be done similarly.

For any ball
B(z,r)
, using conditions (i)–(iv) in Theorem
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle P_{E}(X^{\prime\prime}\in B(z,r)|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y))=P_{E}(X^{\prime\prime}\in B(z,r)|D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)

\displaystyle\qquad=P_{E}(X^{\prime}\in B(z,r)|D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)=\frac{P_{E}(X^{\prime}\in B(z,r),D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)}{P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{P_{E}(X_{1}\in B(z,r),D_{n}(Y_{1})=y)}{P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)}\leq\frac{2P(X-pY\in B(z,r),D_{n}(X+Y)=y)}{P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)}.

Denote by
\mu_{X}
,
\mu_{Y}
the measures associated to the independent random variables
X
and
Y
, respectively. Then
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are
(s,C)
-Frostman.
Furthermore, define
I_{n}(y):=[y,y+2^{-n})
. We have that

\displaystyle P(X-pY\in B(z,r),D_{n}(X+Y)=y)=\int\mu_{X}(I_{n}(y-\tilde{y})\cap B(z+p\tilde{y},r))d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y}).

The value inside the integral can be positive only when
I_{n}(y-\tilde{y})\cap B(z+p\tilde{y},r)\not=\emptyset
. Or equivalently,
I_{n}(y)\cap B(z+(p+1)\tilde{y},r)\not=\emptyset
. That is to say,

z+(p+1)\tilde{y}-r\leq y+2^{-n},\quad z+(p+1)\tilde{y}+r\geq y.

We can locate such
\tilde{y}
in an interval
J
of length at most
2r+2^{n}
. We now estimate the above expression in different ways depending on how
r
relates to
2^{-n}
.
First, for
r\geq 2^{-n}
, and recalling that
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Y}
are
(s,C)
-Frostman, we have

\displaystyle\int\mu_{X}(I_{n}(y-\tilde{y})\cap B(z+p\tilde{y},r))d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})\leq\int_{J}\mu_{X}(I_{n}(y-\tilde{y}))d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\int_{J}C\cdot 2^{-ns}d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})=C2^{-ns}\mu_{Y}(J)\leq C2^{-ns}\cdot C(3r)^{s}\ll C^{2}(r2^{-n})^{s}.

Next, for
r<2^{-n}
, we similarly find

\displaystyle\int\mu_{X}(I_{n}(y-\tilde{y})\cap B(z+p\tilde{y},r))d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})\leq\int_{J}\mu_{X}(B(z+p\tilde{y},r))d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\int_{J}Cr^{s}d\mu_{Y}(\tilde{y})=Cr^{s}\mu_{Y}(J)\leq Cr^{s}\cdot C(3\cdot 2^{-n})^{s}\ll C^{2}(r2^{-n})^{s}.

This completes the proof for the claims given in (
45
)

As the expressions to follow get rather lengthy, we now introduce some shorthand. In what follows,
H_{n}(~\cdot~|x,y)
is an abbreviation of
H(D_{n}(\cdot)~|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y)).
By Lemma
B.2
, there exist random variables, depending on
(x,y)
, that have the same distributions with
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y))
, respectively. These random variables take the same value as
(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})
does. By construction, their ranges are contained in
\big([-2c_{1},-\eta]\cup[\eta,2c_{1}]\big)^{2}
. Also note that
(X^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X_{1},Y_{1})=(x,y))
and
(Y^{\prime\prime}|D_{n}(X_{1},Y_{1})=(x,y))
are independent. Recall that the constant
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{1}(s)
in Lemma
4.1
is independent of the concrete random variables. Finally, recall (
45
). Now we can apply Lemma
4.1
with these random variables, with
C_{1}=O_{C}\big(2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)\big)
,
C_{2}=O_{C}\big(2^{-ns}/P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)\big),
and
c_{2}=\eta
,
to deduce that

\displaystyle\max\left\{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y),H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y)\right\}\geq n(s+\epsilon_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad+O\big(-2ns-\log P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)-\log P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)\big)+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).

(46)

By conditional independence, and combining Corollary
2.22
, we also have that

H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)\geq\max\{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|x,y),H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)\}+O(1).

Using a similar argument, Lemma
B.2
, and Example
2.17
, one obtains

\displaystyle H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y),H_{n}(\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y)\}+O(1)

\displaystyle=\max\{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|x,y),H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)\}+O_{\phi,c_{1},\eta}(1).

Then

\begin{split}&\min\{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y),H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y)\}\geq\frac{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|x,y)+H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)}{2}+O_{\phi,c_{1},\eta}(1).\end{split}

(47)

Therefore given the condition that
D_{n}(X_{1},Y_{1})=(x,y)
, it follows from (
46
) and (
47
) that

\displaystyle H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)+H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|x,y)-\frac{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|x,y)+H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\geq n(s+\epsilon_{1})+O\big(-2ns-\log P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)-\log P_{E}(D_{n}(Y^{\prime})=y)\big)+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).

Now we sum all of these expressions respective to each
(x,y)
with the weight
P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y))
. Note that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{x,y}P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y))H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum\limits_{x,y}P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime})=(x,y))H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|x,y)=H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{x,y}P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(x,y))\log P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\sum\limits_{x}P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)\log P_{E}(D_{n}(X^{\prime})=x)=H_{n}(X^{\prime}).

The other terms behave similarly. Therefore,

\displaystyle H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}+Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})+H_{n}(\alpha X^{\prime\prime 2}+\beta Y^{\prime\prime 2}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})-\frac{H_{n}(X^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})+H_{n}(Y^{\prime\prime}|X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq n(s+\epsilon_{1})+O(-2ns+H_{n}(X^{\prime})+H_{n}(Y^{\prime}))+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).

(48)

Substituting from (
42
)–(
44
), and further combining with
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\equiv(X_{1},Y_{1})
we obtain that

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}+7\log K\right)+\left(\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}+7\log K\right)\\
&\qquad-\frac{(H_{n}(X_{1})-\log K)+(H_{n}(Y_{1})-\log K)}{2}-ns+O(H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})-2ns)\\
&\qquad\geq n\epsilon_{1}+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).\end{split}

(49)

Recalling (
40
) and the notation
\log K=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1)
, the left-hand side of (

\leq O\left(\frac{H_{n}(X_{1})+H_{n}(Y_{1})}{2}-ns\right)+15\log K=O(\Psi)+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{C}(1).

So there is some absolute constant
\tilde{C}>0
such that

\tilde{C}\Psi+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)\geq n\epsilon_{1}+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).

Equivalently,

\Psi\gg n\epsilon_{1}+O_{C}(\sqrt{\eta}n)+O_{\phi,s,C,c_{1},\eta}(1).

Recall that we require

, and
\epsilon_{1}
only depends on

. We can take
\eta
to be sufficiently small, which only depends on

and

.
Next we let

be sufficiently large so that the absolute value of the term

does not exceed

when

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For
\phi(x,y)=a_{1}x^{2}+a_{2}xy+a_{3}y^{2}
, the non-degeneracy gives that
a_{2}^{2}\neq 4a_{1}a_{3}
.

When
a_{3}\neq 0
, we have

\phi(x,y)=\left(a_{1}-\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{4a_{3}}\right)x^{2}+a_{3}\left(\frac{a_{2}}{2a_{3}}x+y\right)^{2}.

Take
\tilde{X}=\frac{a_{2}}{2a_{3}}X
and
\tilde{Y}=Y
. Then
\phi(X,Y)=\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, where

\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\left(\frac{4a_{1}a_{3}^{2}}{a_{2}^{2}}-a_{3}\right)\tilde{x}^{2}+a_{3}(\tilde{x}+\tilde{y})^{2}.

When
a_{1}\neq 0
, similar arguments lead to
\phi(X,Y)=\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, with
\tilde{X}=\frac{a_{2}}{2a_{1}}Y
,
\tilde{Y}=X
, and

\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\left(\frac{4a_{1}^{2}a_{3}}{a_{2}^{2}}-a_{1}\right)\tilde{x}^{2}+a_{1}(\tilde{x}+\tilde{y})^{2}.

When
a_{1}=a_{3}=0
, one has
a_{2}\neq 0
and
\phi(X,Y)=\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
, where
\tilde{X}=X
,
\tilde{Y}=Y
, and

\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=-\frac{a_{2}}{4}(\tilde{x}-\tilde{y})^{2}+\frac{a_{2}}{4}(\tilde{x}+\tilde{y})^{2}.

In all cases, the random variables
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
are independent, and both are
(s,O_{\phi,C}(1))
-Frostman. The ranges of
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
are both contained in
[-\widetilde{c_{1}},\widetilde{c_{1}}]
, with
\widetilde{c_{1}}=O_{\phi,c_{1}}(1)
.

Applying Lemma
5.1
with the quadratic form
\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
and the random variables
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
,
we obtain that

\max\{H_{n}(\xi X+\zeta Y),\,H_{n}(\phi(X,Y))\}\geq n(s+\varepsilon_{2})

for some non-zero rational numbers
\xi=\xi(\phi)
and
\zeta=\zeta(\phi)
. Let
\xi=\frac{k}{m}
and
\zeta=\frac{l}{m}
, with
m
being the greatest common divisor of
\xi
and
\zeta
. By Lemmas
2.23
and
2.26
, there are some
d_{1}=d_{1}(\xi,\zeta)
and
d_{2}=d_{2}(\xi,\zeta)
such that

\displaystyle H_{n}(\xi X+\zeta Y)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{1}+d_{2}+1)H_{n}(X+Y)-d_{1}H_{n}(X)-d_{2}H_{n}(Y)+O_{\xi,\zeta}(1)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{1}+d_{2}+1)H_{n}(X+Y)-(d_{1}+d_{2})(ns-\log C)+O_{\phi}(1).

That is to say,

H_{n}(X+Y)\geq\frac{(d_{1}+d_{2})ns+H_{n}(\xi X+\zeta Y)}{d_{1}+d_{2}+1}+O_{\phi,C}(1).

Now the theorem follows by taking
\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{2(d_{1}+d_{2}+1)}
when
n
is sufficiently large.

∎

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X
,

and

(s,C_{1})
-Frostman and

Here we present definitions and results relevant only to this section. Given a set

Lemma 6.4
.

[Part of Lemma 2.3

\displaystyle H_{n}(X^{\prime 2}+Y^{\prime 2})=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{2^{n+1}}\iota\Big(P(D_{n}(X^{\prime 2}+Y^{\prime 2})\quad=i2^{-n})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{2^{2m+1}}\iota\Big(P(X^{\prime 2}+Y^{\prime 2}=k2^{-2m})\Big)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\iota\big(2^{-2m}T(k)\big),

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{2^{-n/2}}{2\sqrt{i}}\left(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big)\right)+O(2^{-n})=\frac{2^{-n/2-1}}{\sqrt{i}}+O\Big(\frac{2^{-n/2}}{n^{3/2}}\Big).

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}+\sum\limits_{i=n}^{2^{n}-1}\right)\iota\big(P(D_{n}(X^{\prime 2})\big)

Appendix B
Appendix - Existence of Certain Random Variable

In this appendix, we prove two lemmas concerning the existence of certain random variables.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
(A,B,C)
be a given random variable. Let
h
be a function such that
h(C)
is a well-defined discrete random variable. Then there exists a random variable
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime})
such that
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\equiv(A,B,C)
,
(B^{\prime\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B,h(C))
, and
(B^{\prime\prime}|A^{\prime},B^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B^{\prime\prime}|h(C^{\prime}))
. Moreover,
B^{\prime\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
h(C^{\prime})
. Furthermore,
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
and
B^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given
h(C^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
A,B,C
are defined as measurable functions from a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},P)
to some other measurable space. Now we construct a new probability space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
. Let

\Omega=\bigcup_{c\in range(h(C))}\Omega_{c},\quad\Omega_{c}:=\{h(C)=c\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:\,h(C(\omega))=c\}.

Construct

\Omega^{\prime}=\bigcup_{c}(\Omega_{c}\times\Omega_{c}).

Then
(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega^{\prime}
if and only if
\omega,\nu\in\Omega_{c}
for some
c
, if and only if
h(C(\omega))=h(C(\nu))
. The
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
can be generated by the related Cartisian products of Borel sets in
\mathcal{B}
, and the probability measure
P^{\prime}
can be induced by the following: if
E\times F
is a Borel subset of
\Omega_{c}\times\Omega_{c}
for some
c
, then

P^{\prime}(E\times F)=\begin{cases}\frac{P(E)P(F)}{P(\Omega_{c})},\quad&\text{if }P(\Omega_{c})>0,\\
0,&\text{if }P(\Omega_{c})=0.\end{cases}

In the following, we assume without loss of generality that
P(\Omega_{c})>0
for all
c\in range(h(C))
. Now for any random variable
D
defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},P)
, let
D^{\prime}
be the following random variable on
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
:

D^{\prime}(\omega,\nu)=D(\omega),\quad(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega^{\prime}.

By this definition, one sees that
\big(f(D))^{\prime}=f(D^{\prime})
for any admissible function
f
. In the following, we verify that
D^{\prime}\equiv D
. For any Borel set
L
,

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(D^{\prime}\in L)

\displaystyle=P^{\prime}(\{(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega^{\prime}:\,D^{\prime}(\omega,\nu)\in L)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{c}P^{\prime}(\{(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega_{c}\times\Omega_{c}:\,D(\omega)\in L\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{c}\frac{P(D\in L,h(C)=c)P(h(C)=c)}{P(h(C)=c)}=P(D\in L).

In particular, we can take
D=(A,B,C)
to get
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\equiv(A,B,C)
. All their corresponding marginal distributions are also the same. One has
h(C)\equiv h(C^{\prime})
as well.

Next, we define
B^{\prime\prime}
as a function on
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{B}^{\prime},P^{\prime})
, given by

B^{\prime\prime}(\omega,\nu)=B(\nu),\quad(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega^{\prime}.

Then, for any Borel sets
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1},D^{\prime}\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime})=c)=P^{\prime}(\{(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega^{\prime}:\,B^{\prime\prime}(\omega,\nu)\in L_{1},D^{\prime}(\omega,\nu)\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime}(\omega,\nu))=c\})

\displaystyle\qquad=P^{\prime}(\{(\omega,\nu)\in\Omega_{c}\times\Omega_{c}:\,B(\nu)\in L_{1},D(\omega)\in L_{2}\})=\frac{P(D\in L_{2},h(C)=c)P(B\in L_{1},h(C)=c)}{P(h(C)=c)}.

When we take
D
to be trivial (a constant function), then
P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1},h(C^{\prime})=c)=P(B\in L_{1},h(C)=c)
and then
(B^{\prime\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B,h(C))
. Moreover, the previous argument implies that
(D^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(D,h(C))
. We can calculate further to obtain

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1},D^{\prime}\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime})=c)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(D\in L_{2},h(C)=c)P(B\in L_{1},h(C)=c)}{P(h(C)=c)}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{\prime}(D^{\prime}\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime})=c)P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1},h(C^{\prime})=c)}{P^{\prime}(h(C^{\prime})=c)}

\displaystyle=P^{\prime}(D^{\prime}\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime})=c)P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1}|h(C^{\prime})=c).

So
P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1}|D^{\prime}\in L_{2},h(C^{\prime})=c)=P^{\prime}(B^{\prime\prime}\in L_{1}|h(C^{\prime})=c)
, i.e.
(B^{\prime\prime}|D^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B^{\prime\prime}|h(C^{\prime}))
. Taking
D=(A,B)
, one gets
(B^{\prime\prime}|A^{\prime},B^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B^{\prime\prime}|h(C^{\prime}))
.

Moreover, if we take
D=B
, then
(B^{\prime\prime}|B^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B^{\prime\prime}|h(C^{\prime}))
. That is to say,
B^{\prime\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are conditionally independent given
h(C^{\prime})
. Since
(B^{\prime\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B,h(C))\equiv(B^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))
, one also deduces that
B^{\prime\prime}
,
B
are conditionally i.i.d. given
h(C^{\prime})
.

Furthermore, it follows from
(B^{\prime\prime}|A^{\prime},B^{\prime},h(C^{\prime}))\equiv(B^{\prime\prime}|h(C^{\prime}))
that
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
and
B^{\prime\prime}
are conditionally independent given
h(C^{\prime})
.

Without confusion, we will simplify this
P^{\prime}
as
P
in applications for convenience.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
X
be a random variable, and
E
be an event with
P(E)>0
. Then there is another random variable
X_{E}
such that
X_{E}\equiv(X|E)
. Moreover, for any function
f
with
domain(f)\supseteq range(X)
, we have

H_{n}(f(X_{E}))=H(D_{n}(f(X))|E),

and

H_{n}(f(X_{E}))\leq\frac{H_{n}(f(X))}{P(E)}.

Furthermore, if
X
is
(s,C)
-Frostman, then
X^{\prime}
is
(s,C/P(E))
-Frostman.

Proof.

Assume that
X
is defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},P)
. The conditional measure given
E
, denoted by
P_{E}
, is given by

P_{E}(L)=\frac{P(L\cap E)}{P(E)},\quad(L\in\mathcal{B}).

Define
X_{E}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},P_{E})
by

X_{E}(\omega)=X(\omega),\quad(\omega\in\Omega).

It is not hard to verify that
X_{E}\equiv(X|E)
, and
f(X^{\prime})=f(X)
. Now

H_{n}(f(X_{E}))=H(D_{n}(f(X_{E})))=-\sum\limits_{x}P_{E}(D_{n}(f(X^{\prime}))=x)\log_{2}P_{E}(D_{n}(f(X_{E}))=x)=H(D_{n}(f(X))|E).

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two partitions of
\Omega
:

\mathcal{A}=\big\{\{D_{n}(f(X))=x\}:\,x\in range(D_{n}(f(X)))\big\},\quad\mathcal{B}=\left\{E,E^{c}\right\}.

With language of entropy of measure, we have

H_{n}(f(X))=H(P,\mathcal{A}),\quad H_{n}(f(X_{E}))=H(P_{E},\mathcal{A}).

By Lemma
2.2
(iv), we have

H(P,\mathcal{A})\geq H(P,\mathcal{A}|\mathcal{B})=P(E)H(P_{E},\mathcal{A})+P(E^{c})H(P_{E^{c}},\mathcal{A})\geq P(E)H(P_{E},\mathcal{A}).

When
X
is
(s,C)
-Frostman, for any ball
B(x,r)
, we have

P_{E}(X_{E}\in B(x,r))\leq\frac{P(X\in B(x,r))}{P(E)}\leq\frac{Cr^{s}}{P(E)}.

Hence
X_{E}
is
(s,C/P(E))
-Frostman.
∎
