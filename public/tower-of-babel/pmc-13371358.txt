Multi-scale remote sensing monitoring of aboveground vegetation carbon storage in long-distance expressways
Authors: Xugang Lian, Shiqi Liang, Yulu Gao, Yucheng Chen, Jiahui Yuan, Junheng Huang, Yinfei Cai, Haifeng Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of expressway roadside vegetation carbon storage is essential for achieving carbon balance and ecological sustainability in the transportation sector. However, this is challenging due to the extensive spatial coverage, marked climatic variation, and pronounced spatial dependence of these vegetation corridors. Focusing on the expressway network of Shanxi Province, this study integrates UAV sample data with Sentinel-2 imagery. A graph convolutional network (GCN) is used to extract road network spatial topology, and estimation models for aboveground vegetation carbon storage are constructed for Shanxi’s northern, central, and southern climatic zones. The performance of XGBoost, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machine models is then compared across these climatic zones, emphasizing the importance of model adaptability and spatial feature integration. The results showed that the incorporation of GCN-derived spatial features significantly enhanced the model’s ability to characterize spatial autocorrelation along linear corridors. Among all models, the XGBoost-GCN model performed best in southern Shanxi, with a validation R² of 0.723. Compared with the overall model, the climate-zoned models improved estimation accuracy by an average of 18.3%, indicating that zoned modeling is more suitable for estimating carbon storage of roadside vegetation under strong climatic gradients. The carbon storage of expressway roadside vegetation in Shanxi Province exhibited an overall spatial pattern of decreasing from south to north. Hydrothermal conditions were identified as the main factors driving its spatial differentiation, while finely managed areas such as interchanges showed relatively high carbon sink potential. These findings indicate that the multi-scale remote sensing approach integrating GCN-derived spatial features with climate-zoned modeling can improve both the accuracy and regional adaptability of carbon storage estimation for roadside vegetation along long-distance expressways, and can provide methodological support for carbon monitoring, carbon sink identification, and differentiated ecological management of transportation infrastructure ecosystems.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of global climate change, controlling greenhouse gas emissions and achieving carbon neutrality have become common goals of the international community [1, 2]. Vegetation carbon storage is an important indicator for evaluating the carbon sink capacity of ecosystems, and its monitoring and assessment are of great significance for studies on the global carbon cycle and climate change.

Expressway roadside vegetation can protect soil, reduce soil erosion, and absorb and fix atmospheric carbon dioxide through photosynthesis. It plays an important role in improving the ecological quality along expressways, alleviating transportation-related carbon emission pressure, and maintaining regional carbon balance [3, 4]. According to the 2024 Statistical Bulletin on the Development of the Transport Industry issued by the Ministry of Transport [5], by the end of 2024, the total mileage of expressways in China had reached 190,700 km, ranking first in the world. With the continued expansion of the expressway network, the area covered by roadside vegetation has also increased accordingly. In this context, systematically assessing the carbon sink capacity of expressway roadside vegetation has become a key step for the transportation sector to achieve carbon neutrality [6, 8].

Expressway roadside vegetation is distributed linearly, and its growth is jointly influenced by road structure, maintenance practices, and traffic activities. Compared with naturally distributed areal ecosystems, carbon storage monitoring in roadside ecosystems is more complex, and conventional monitoring methods are limited in both accuracy and efficiency. Although traditional field measurement methods can provide relatively high accuracy, they are time-consuming and labor-intensive in practice, making them unsuitable for dynamic monitoring of roadside vegetation over long distances and large areas [9, 10]. The development of remote sensing technology has provided a new approach for large-scale vegetation biomass estimation [11, 13]. Satellite remote sensing offers the advantages of broad coverage and high temporal frequency [14], but its spatial resolution remains insufficient for high-resolution monitoring of expressway roadside vegetation [15, 16]. By contrast, UAVs can provide high-spatial-resolution multispectral, hyperspectral, as well as LiDAR data, and have demonstrated clear advantages in applications such as vegetation canopy structure parameter extraction [17, 20] and estimation of aboveground biomass and carbon storage [21-23]. However, due to limitations in flight range and operational cost, UAVs alone cannot support continuous monitoring of long-distance expressway networks at the provincial scale [24, 25]. Therefore, integrating UAV and satellite remote sensing data to build a multi-scale collaborative monitoring framework for roadside vegetation carbon storage has become a practical requirement for achieving efficient and accurate assessment of expressway roadside vegetation carbon storage [26].

Existing studies have shown that integrated UAV and satellite remote sensing approaches for vegetation biomass and carbon storage estimation have been widely applied in ecosystems such as forests and grasslands [27-30]. However, their model construction strategies and feature representation methods remain less suitable for expressway roadside vegetation, which represents a typical linear corridor ecosystem. Under the combined influence of regional environmental differences and along-corridor spatial dependence, current studies still face limitations in regional adaptability, sample support, and spatial feature characterization.

To address these issues, this study conducts a multi-scale, multi-source remote sensing assessment of carbon storage in expressway roadside vegetation by considering both the linear characteristics of expressways and the north–south climatic differences in Shanxi Province. Specifically, climate-zoned carbon storage estimation models were developed according to the climatic differences across Shanxi to improve regional adaptability; a UAV-based high-precision sample database covering the northern, central, and southern climatic zones of Shanxi was used for model training and validation to enhance the representativeness and consistency of sample support; and a graph convolutional network was introduced to extract spatial topological features of the road network, which were further integrated with vegetation indices, texture features, and climatic factors to improve the characterization of spatial dependence along linear corridors. The aim of this study is to improve the accuracy and regional adaptability of carbon storage estimation for roadside vegetation along long-distance expressways, and to provide methodological support for carbon monitoring and differentiated ecological management of transportation infrastructure ecosystems.

Materials and data

Study area

This study takes the 6,300-km expressway network across Shanxi Province as the research object. The study area covers the entire province of Shanxi (110°14′E ~ 114°33′E, 34°34′N ~ 40°44′N), which is characterized by a typical temperate continental monsoon climate, with an annual mean temperature of 3–14 °C and annual precipitation of 400–600 mm. A distinct north–south climatic gradient is observed, with a semi-arid climate to the north of Hengshan Mountain and a transition toward semi-humid conditions to the south. The terrain is dominated by mountains and hills, with an overall topographic pattern of being high in the east and west and low in the center, and the average elevation exceeds 1500 m. The geographical location of the study area is shown in Fig. 1.

To standardize the roadside analysis extent for the entire provincial expressway network, the study area was delineated based on relevant research on expressway ecological impact thresholds and with reference to the technical requirements specified in the Specifications for Environmental Impact Assessment of Expressway Construction Projects (JTG B03-2006) [31]. According to this specification, when a expressway passes through ecologically sensitive areas or urban areas, the maximum extent of ecological environmental impact assessment should extend to 300 m on both sides of the expressway centerline. Given the large spatial extent of the expressway network in Shanxi Province and the fact that it frequently passes through various ecologically sensitive areas and complex landforms, a 300 m buffer on both sides of the expressway centerline was adopted as the monitoring extent of expressway roadside vegetation in order to fully cover the maximum potential ecological impact boundary. Under the 10 m spatial resolution of Sentinel-2 imagery, this 300 m assessment extent can encompass more continuous vegetation patches, thereby reducing the mixed-pixel effect caused by the strip-like distribution of roadside vegetation and improving the extraction of valid pixels.

Overview of the study area

To better reflect the distribution characteristics and growth differences of roadside vegetation under different climatic conditions in Shanxi Province, the study area was divided into three climatic zones based on hydrothermal conditions: the northern Shanxi temperate semi-arid zone, the central Shanxi warm temperate semi-arid zone, and the southern Shanxi warm temperate semi-humid zone [32, 33]. The characteristics of these three climatic zones are listed in Table 1.

The vegetation distribution in the study area exhibits clear regional differences. Northern Shanxi is dominated by xerophytic shrubs such as Hippophae rhamnoides and Caragana korshinskii, as well as ecologically protective poplar plantations. Central Shanxi is characterized by deciduous broad-leaved forests dominated by Quercus liaotungensis and Quercus variabilis, with mixed stands of Robinia pseudoacacia and poplar distributed in valley areas. Southern Shanxi features a vertical vegetation spectrum composed of mixed forests of Pinus tabuliformis, Quercus liaotungensis, and Populus davidiana, together with Vitex negundo shrublands. The construction of roadside ecosystems also shows a functional orientation: low anti-glare shrubs such as juniper and Euonymus hedges are mainly used in central medians; slope systems are stabilized using Amorpha fruticosa and Caragana korshinskii; and interchanges are planted with arbor–shrub–grass mixed communities of Acer mono, Platycladus orientalis, and lilac to create landscape heterogeneity.

Data sources and preprocessing

The data used in this study include UAV-derived biomass samples and Sentinel-2 satellite imagery with a spatial resolution of 10 m. The calculation of vegetation biomass and the construction of the sample database for the UAV sample plots had been completed in our previous work, in which a roadside vegetation biomass model and corresponding sample database for Shanxi Province were established based on UAV multispectral and LiDAR data, using the Random Forest algorithm combined with spatial cross-validation. The present study focuses on the data acquisition schemes, key technical parameters, and preprocessing procedures of the datasets, thereby providing the data foundation for subsequent multi-scale biomass mapping and roadside vegetation carbon storage estimation.

UAV biomass samples

The UAV data and field measurements required for constructing the UAV biomass samples were collected during two periods: from 23 October to 9 November 2024, and from 28 July to 2 September 2025. Shanxi Province lies in the temperate climatic zone, where vegetation enters dormancy in winter and interannual growth changes are relatively slow. Under conditions without major natural or human disturbances, the aboveground biomass of roadside vegetation remains relatively stable at the annual scale. To ensure the spatial representativeness and statistical significance of the samples at the provincial scale, the number of plots was allocated according to the geographic area of each climatic zone, the total expressway mileage, and the distribution of typical vegetation types. A total of 70 plots were established, including 15 plots in the northern Shanxi temperate semi-arid zone, 39 in the central Shanxi warm temperate semi-arid zone, and 16 in the southern Shanxi warm temperate semi-humid zone. The acquisition process is shown in Fig. 2. The dataset corresponding to the sample database has been published in Mendeley Data [34], and is publicly available at:https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/n7b2hrf4ct/1.

During field measurements, steel tapes and flexible measuring tapes were used to obtain basic tree parameters such as diameter at breast height (DBH). DBH was measured at a uniform height of 1.3 m above the ground, with appropriate adjustments made in cases of trunk swelling, branching, or leaning. Each tree was measured two to three times, and the average value was taken as the final result. Information such as tree ID, measurement date, and growth condition was also recorded, and abnormal data were rechecked to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the field measurements.

UAV multispectral data were acquired using a Feima D2000 UAV equipped with a D-MSPC2000 multispectral module. The relative flight altitude was 111 m, the effective sensor resolution was 1.2 million pixels, and the ground sampling resolution was 8 cm. The acquired data covered six bands: 450 nm (Blue), 560 nm (Green), 650 nm (Red), 720 nm (Red Edge 1), 750 nm (Red Edge 2), and 840 nm (NIR). In the data processing stage, the DN values of the original grayscale images were first converted into surface reflectance values through radiometric calibration using calibration panel imagery. Subsequently, multispectral image mosaicking was performed to generate high-precision reflectance imagery for the entire sample area.

UAV LiDAR data were acquired using a Feima D2000 UAV equipped with a D-LIDAR2000 laser scanning module. During flight, the relative altitude was 70 m, the side overlap rate was 35%, and the flight speed was 14 m/s, resulting in an average point cloud density of approximately 94 points/m². During data acquisition, flight altitude was dynamically adjusted based on terrain slope recognition, and adaptive point-cloud-density control was used to improve effective data collection efficiency. The LiDAR data were processed through point cloud computation, denoising and filtering, strip mosaicking, and coordinate transformation to obtain a standardized point cloud dataset.

Satellite imagery

Sentinel-2 satellite imagery from July to September 2025 was selected as the main data source in this study. This period coincides well with the peak growing season of vegetation and can effectively capture the optimal spectral characteristics of roadside vegetation, thus providing high-quality input for biomass inversion. All data were preprocessed in the cloud on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. The Level-2 A products provided by GEE had already undergone orthorectification and atmospheric correction using the Sen2Cor processor, directly yielding surface reflectance data. The QA60 bitmask band containing cloud information was used for cloud masking to remove pixels contaminated by clouds. The spatial resolution of 11 bands was resampled to 10 m using bicubic convolution. A 300 m buffer was generated based on the expressway vector boundary and used as the study area for image clipping. Considering that the 10 m spatial resolution may result in mixed-pixel effects within expressway corridors, an NDVI threshold (NDVI ≥ 0) was applied within the region of interest (ROI) to screen valid vegetation pixels and reduce the influence of non-vegetation background, thereby forming a standardized dataset suitable for roadside analysis.

Methods

As described in Sect. "Data sources and preprocessing", the calculation of vegetation biomass for the UAV sample plots and the construction of the UAV sample database had already been completed in our previous work. This database contains standardized biomass data from 70 sample plots covering the three major climatic zones of northern, central, and southern Shanxi.

This section systematically describes the method used to estimate carbon storage of expressway roadside vegetation based on the integration of multi-source data and machine learning. The overall technical workflow is shown in Fig. 3 and consists of four main steps: (1) extracting spectral, vegetation index, and texture features from Sentinel-2 imagery; (2) modeling the road network topology using a graph convolutional network (GCN) to extract and integrate spatially correlated features; (3) constructing roadside carbon storage estimation models based on climatic zoning; and (4) inverting biomass and calculating carbon storage using the optimal model.

Extraction of satellite-derived features

To estimate carbon storage from satellite imagery, feature variables closely related to vegetation biomass were extracted from the preprocessed Sentinel-2 data. A total of 14 variables were used as the basic input feature set for model development, including 7 vegetation indices and 7 texture features (Table 2). In addition to conventional indices such as NDVI, indices including SAVI and EVI, which are relatively robust to soil background and brightness variation, were incorporated to reduce the interference of background signals from paved road surfaces and bare land on vegetation spectral information. This helped mitigate the mixed-pixel effect in narrow roadside corridor environments.

The GLCM texture features listed in Table 2 were extracted as follows. First, the near-infrared, red, and green bands were combined with weighted synthesis to generate a grayscale image. The grayscale image was then linearly stretched and quantized into 64 discrete gray levels to balance computational efficiency and statistical stability. A 7 × 7 pixel sliding window, corresponding to 70 m × 70 m on the ground, was applied with a step size of 1 pixel. To improve robustness to directional variation, GLCMs were calculated in four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°), and the average of the four directional results was used as the final texture feature value.

Extraction and integration of GCN spatial features

Carbon storage in expressway roadside vegetation exhibits a certain degree of spatial correlation. Adjacent road segments often show similar carbon storage levels when they share comparable ecological environments and management conditions. Traditional machine learning methods usually treat samples as independent from one another and ignore the topological relationships among them, resulting in insufficient characterization of the spatial variation in roadside vegetation carbon storage. Therefore, this study introduced a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) to model the structure of the expressway network and extract informative features that reflect spatial associations among road segments. In this approach, the road network is abstracted as a graph structure. Through message passing and aggregation, the features of each road segment are able to incorporate information from its neighboring segments, thereby encoding spatial context into quantifiable feature vectors. The workflow is shown in Fig. 4, and the specific steps are described below.

Construction of the road network topology graph

Even when two road segments are not directly connected in the transportation network, they may still share similar ecological background conditions as long as they are geographically close. Therefore, adopting an adjacency strategy based on Euclidean distance helps overcome the constraints of the transportation topology itself and more effectively captures ecological similarity and spatial dependence in continuous geographic space.

Previous studies have shown that a 1 km scale is well suited for representing surface spatial heterogeneity and has been widely used in large-scale carbon storage and land-surface system modeling [35]. Compared with coarser resolutions, this scale preserves more local detail; compared with excessively fine resolutions, it can better control data volume and computational complexity. On this basis, to characterize the spatial heterogeneity of roadside vegetation carbon storage while controlling graph size and computational cost, the expressway network across Shanxi Province was divided into 1 km segments, which were used as graph nodes.

Considering that geographically close road segments may still have similar ecological background conditions even if they are not directly connected in the transportation network, this study constructed spatial association edges based on Euclidean distance. Compared with relying solely on transportation topology, this approach is more suitable for capturing ecological similarity and spatial dependence in continuous geographic space. Specifically, an Euclidean distance of less than 5 km between road segments was used as the criterion for defining adjacency. The selection of this threshold was informed by previous studies on spatial modeling of vegetation carbon density. Existing research has shown that, in analyses of spatial autocorrelation for carbon density estimation, a 5 km spatial scale performs better than 2 km and 10 km scales in capturing the spatial autocorrelation of topographic and spectral features, while also yielding higher model validation accuracy [36]. Given that ecological processes in expressway roadside areas are jointly regulated by continuous natural factors such as hydrothermal conditions and topography, a 5 km threshold was ultimately adopted to more reasonably represent the ecological spatial associations of roadside vegetation carbon storage.

GCN spatial feature extraction

The raw node attributes and adjacency matrix are fed into a two-layer GCN. Through graph structure propagation, neighborhood information is aggregated, ultimately outputting an 8-dimensional spatial topology feature vector for each node (abbreviated as GCN_Feat_1 to GCN_Feat_8 in subsequent figures and tables). This vector quantifies the extent to which the road segment is influenced by its topological neighbors. By comparing the outputs with 4, 8, and 16-dimensional embeddings, we selected 8 dimensions as the optimal setting. This configuration is comparable in magnitude to the input satellite-derived features, retaining sufficient road-network topological complexity while reducing the risk of overfitting during the fusion training process. The model was trained under a supervised learning framework using UAV-derived biomass samples as supervision signals. The dataset was divided into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%). The Mean Squared Error (MSE) was adopted as the loss function. Model parameters were optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.01. This learning rate was determined through preliminary experiments to ensure stable convergence while achieving satisfactory performance. The model was trained for 20 epochs. To enhance generalization ability and mitigate overfitting, Dropout (rate = 0.3) and L2 weight decay (coefficient = 1 × 10⁻⁵) were incorporated as regularization strategies during training. Early stopping was not applied, as the training process exhibited stable convergence within the predefined number of epochs.

Considering the potential risk of information leakage in graph neural networks under transductive learning settings, a node masking strategy was adopted. During forward propagation, node features from both the training and validation sets participated in adjacency-based message passing to preserve the structural integrity of the road network. However, only training node labels were involved in loss computation and gradient backpropagation. Validation node labels were completely masked during optimization and did not contribute to parameter updates. Therefore, model parameters were exclusively updated based on training supervision signals, preventing leakage of validation information through graph adjacency propagation.

Spatial feature fusion in GCN

Feature fusion is performed by combining the 8-dimensional spatial features extracted by GCN with the original features, including vegetation indices, texture features, topographic factors, and climatic factors, to form a feature fusion set.

Construction of climate-zoned roadside carbon storage estimation models

Integration of UAV and satellite data

In our previous work, a high-precision UAV sample database covering the three major climatic zones of Shanxi Province was established. This dataset was stored in tabular form and contained the core attributes of 70 sample plots, including plot ID, central geographic coordinates, and biomass. Using the latitude and longitude coordinates of the UAV sample plots as spatial references, the corresponding 10 m Sentinel-2 pixel features, together with resampled topographic and climatic variables, were extracted. The UAV-derived biomass values at the corresponding coordinates were used as the dependent variable, while the satellite remote sensing features served as the independent variables, forming the input dataset for subsequent machine learning models.

Feature variable selection

To address the high dimensionality of multi-source remote sensing data, the potential risk of overfitting, and the need to jointly consider both basic and spatial features in roadside vegetation carbon storage estimation, GCN spatial features were first extracted, and then Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied to perform joint feature selection on both the basic and spatial variables. To fully capture the heterogeneous driving mechanisms of roadside vegetation growth under different climatic conditions, RFE was conducted independently for the overall dataset as well as for the three climate-zoned subsets of northern, central, and southern Shanxi. Based on five-fold cross-validation, variables with relatively low contribution were gradually removed according to feature importance during the iterative training process, and the most representative optimal feature subsets for carbon storage estimation in each climatic zone were finally identified.

Construction of single machine learning models

Considering the complex nonlinear relationships between vegetation carbon storage and remote sensing features, this study first developed single machine learning models without GCN-derived spatial features as baseline models for subsequent comparison with the fusion models. Three representative machine learning algorithms were selected for comparative analysis: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). Among them, XGBoost optimizes the gradient boosting process through efficient parallel computation and regularization techniques; RF enhances model robustness through ensemble learning; and SVM achieves nonlinear mapping in high-dimensional feature space באמצעות kernel functions. These three algorithms each have distinct advantages in handling the high-dimensional and nonlinear nature of multi-source remote sensing data, and can effectively capture the complex coupling relationships between spectral, textural, and other multidimensional features and vegetation carbon storage. The three algorithms were separately applied to each climatic zone as well as to the overall dataset, so as to compare their suitability for vegetation carbon storage estimation under different climatic gradients.

To ensure the robustness of model generalization across the long-distance expressway network at the provincial scale, this study did not perform systematic hyperparameter optimization for the XGBoost, RF, and SVM algorithms. All models were trained and evaluated under the same data partitioning, feature inputs, and evaluation metrics, using commonly adopted empirical parameter settings, with necessary basic tuning conducted on the training set to ensure stable model training. Construction of the GCN spatial feature fusion model

Building upon the single machine learning models, an 8-dimensional GCN spatial feature is introduced and fused with traditional remote sensing features—such as spectral, textural, topographic, and climatic attributes—to enhance the model’s ability to capture vegetation spatial structure and contextual relationships, thereby constructing the GCN spatial feature fusion model. The specific procedure is as follows: the joint feature set is separately input into three machine learning algorithms—XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM)—to build corresponding fusion models (XGBoost‑GCN, RF‑GCN, SVM‑GCN). By integrating spatial correlations and feature diversity, the fusion models provide a more expressive foundation for estimating carbon storage across different climate zones and at the overall scale.

Feature contribution evaluation based on SHAP

Different machine learning algorithms (such as XGBoost, RF, and SVM) have underlying mechanism differences when quantifying feature importance, making it difficult to directly compare their evaluation results. To construct a unified and objective evaluation framework, this study introduces the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method for feature contribution analysis [37, 38]. As a model-agnostic explanation method, SHAP calculates the marginal contribution of each feature, providing a standardized comparison benchmark across different model architectures. Based on this, the study extracts the average absolute SHAP values of each model to quantitatively reveal the relative importance of GCN spatial features, remote sensing indices, and climatic factors in carbon stock estimation.

Accuracy validation method

To comprehensively evaluate model performance, this study used the Coefficient of Determination (R²), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) as core evaluation indicators to test the robustness of the models for each climate zone. By dividing the sample data into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%), the objectivity of the evaluation results was ensured. By comparing the various indicators of single models vs. GCN-fused models, and overall models vs. climate zone models on the validation set, the effectiveness of the GCN spatial feature embedding and climate zoning modeling strategy was systematically validated.

To validate the effectiveness of the GCN model in alleviating the spatial autocorrelation issue of linear corridors, this study uses the global Moran’s I index [39] for spatial autocorrelation quantitative diagnosis. First, the spatial autocorrelation characteristics of the UAV biomass samples are verified. Then, the global Moran’s I of the residuals from the benchmark model and the GCN fusion model are compared to quantify the model’s ability to mitigate spatial autocorrelation. Validation of the effectiveness and independence of GCN spatial feature extraction

The results of the Global Moran’s I test (Table 6) reveal that the measured aboveground biomass across the 70 UAV sample plots exhibits a significant positive spatial autocorrelation (I = 0.412, p < 0.001). This objectively indicates that the spatial distribution of roadside vegetation along the expressway is not random, but rather displays pronounced local clustering characteristics.

In the spatial autocorrelation diagnosis of the model-fitted residuals (Table 6 presents only the comparison of the optimal models for the entire study area and individual climate zones), the residuals of the traditional baseline models without GCN integration still demonstrate significant positive spatial dependence (p < 0.001). This suggests that traditional models have inherent limitations in characterizing spatial correlation effects. Following the introduction of GCN spatial features, the spatial autocorrelation intensity of the residuals in the optimal fusion models for each zone consistently decreased. The above quantitative findings demonstrate that the spatial topological features extracted by the GCN effectively capture the spatial dependence between road segments, thereby mitigating the spatial autocorrelation issue in linear corridor modeling to a certain extent.

Comparison between climate zone and global models

To evaluate the effectiveness of the climate zoning modeling strategy, this section conducts a comparative analysis of the prediction accuracy between the optimal global model and each climate zone-specific model (Table 7). The results indicate that the local modeling strategy based on climate zones effectively improves the estimation accuracy of roadside vegetation carbon storage.

Regarding model explanatory power (R2), the goodness-of-fit for the three climate zone models is substantially higher than that of the global model (R2 = 0.555). Specifically, the Southern Shanxi warm temperate semi-humid zone model performed best (R2 = 0.723), followed by the Northern Shanxi temperate semi-arid zone (R2 = 0.694) and the Central Shanxi warm temperate semi-arid zone (R2 = 0.636). These findings suggest that when processing large-scale data across different climate zones, global models are susceptible to interference from spatial heterogeneity, resulting in a certain “averaging effect”. In contrast, zonal modeling mitigates the negative impacts of heterogeneity by narrowing the spatial scale.

In terms of error metrics, although the absolute error values for the global model (RMSE = 65.6789 g/m2, MAE = 45.4567 g/m2) are relatively small, the NRMSE indicator—which accounts for differences in biomass baselines across regions—shows that the relative errors of all zonal models are maintained at low levels, ranging from 1.62% to 2.93%. Notably, the Southern Shanxi model achieved the lowest relative error (1.62%) across the study area. These comparative results confirm the scientific validity and necessity of conducting climate-zoned estimations at a provincial scale characterized by significant spatial heterogeneity.

This internal variation is closely related to the management intensity of different functional zones along the route: interchange areas, serving as carbon sink hotspots, exhibit significantly higher carbon storage than slope zones (primarily focused on soil stabilization) and central dividers (dominated by low anti-glare shrubs), owing to their composite tree-shrub-grass configurations and refined management.

Discussion

Effectiveness of multi-scale monitoring and GCN fusion model

This study developed a multi-scale remote sensing framework for roadside vegetation carbon storage monitoring by integrating UAV and satellite data, thereby alleviating the scale mismatch between UAV observations and satellite remote sensing. The high-precision biomass data collected from 70 UAV sample plots provided sample support for training the Sentinel-2-based models. This strategy is consistent with existing studies on multi-scale collaborative modeling [30], but in the present study it was further applied to long-distance expressway scenarios, demonstrating its applicability under complex underlying surface conditions.

In addition, the introduction of GCN-derived spatial features improved the model’s ability to characterize spatial associations in roadside vegetation. The results showed that models incorporating GCN spatial features achieved higher overall accuracy than their corresponding single machine learning models, with the improvement being more evident in northern Shanxi, where spatial heterogeneity is relatively strong. In particular, the R² of the SVM-GCN model increased by 0.169. This suggests that carbon storage in expressway roadside vegetation is not determined solely by environmental factors, but is also jointly influenced by corridor effects and human maintenance practices, and therefore exhibits a certain degree of spatial autocorrelation.

Traditional models typically treat pixels or road segments as independent samples and thus fail to capture the spatial dependence determined by road network topology. In contrast, GCN constructs a road network graph to capture similarities among neighboring road segments in terms of microclimate, soil conditions, and management practices, and incorporates these spatial topological features into the modeling process. In this way, it compensates for the limitations of traditional models and improves the accuracy of carbon storage estimation. This finding is also consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of spatial dependence in road-related carbon modeling [41]. Furthermore, the present study suggests that incorporating spatial association as a feature source may be useful for monitoring carbon storage in linear ecosystems.

From the perspective of algorithm performance, the SVM model showed a relatively larger improvement in accuracy, with ΔR² ranging from 0.094 to 0.169, whereas the improvements for XGBoost and RF were comparatively moderate, with ΔR² ranging from 0.005 to 0.062. This is likely because the conventional SVM lacks an explicit mechanism for representing spatial constraints, and the spatial topological features extracted by GCN help compensate for its limitations in modeling spatial dependence. By contrast, XGBoost and RF already possess relatively strong capabilities for learning feature interactions, and therefore the additional gain after incorporating GCN features is smaller.

In terms of overall model performance, the tree-based ensemble learning algorithms, namely XGBoost and RF, outperformed the Support Vector Machine (SVM) in carbon storage estimation accuracy. This difference was particularly evident in the overall model, where the R² of SVM declined noticeably. A possible reason is the difference in the ability of these algorithms to accommodate multi-source heterogeneous data and spatial heterogeneity. By integrating multiple decision trees, XGBoost and RF are better able to handle features with different scales and complex nonlinear interactions. In contrast, SVM relies on kernel functions to search for an optimal separating hyperplane in high-dimensional space. When dealing with overall data characterized by pronounced climatic gradients and spatial heterogeneity, a single hyperplane may be insufficient for effective fitting. Therefore, under the ecological modeling context of linear corridors with strong spatial heterogeneity, tree-based ensemble algorithms appear to show better robustness and applicability.

In summary, for expressway roadside vegetation affected by continuous engineering disturbance and relatively uniform maintenance practices, traditional remote sensing features alone are insufficient to fully characterize the mechanisms shaping carbon storage. Incorporating spatial topological information into the model is an effective way to improve the reliability of carbon storage estimation in linear ecosystems.

Strategic advantages and ecological significance of climate zoning modeling

The climate-zoned models achieved higher estimation accuracy than the overall model. Although the overall model was able to reflect the influence of broad climatic conditions, its unified modeling strategy did not adequately capture differences in ecological processes among regions, and its highest R2 was only 0.555. In contrast, the zoned models for northern, central, and southern Shanxi all achieved higher estimation accuracy, with the highest R2 reaching 0.723 and an average improvement of 18.3%. This result indicates that, in large-scale studies spanning pronounced climatic gradients, climate-zoned modeling can improve the regional adaptability of the model.

The feature importance analysis further showed that the dominant drivers differed among the models for different climatic zones. In the northern temperate semi-arid zone, hydrothermal conditions were the main limiting factors affecting vegetation carbon accumulation. In the central warm temperate semi-arid zone, multi-source remote sensing features showed a stronger synergistic effect. In the southern warm temperate semi-humid zone, the combined influence of hydrothermal factors was more prominent. These differences indicate that the formation of roadside vegetation carbon storage in different regions is jointly influenced by natural conditions, vegetation characteristics, and human management, and that the strength of these influences varies across regions. Climate-zoned modeling is therefore better able to accommodate such regional differences, thereby improving the characterization of spatial variation in roadside vegetation carbon storage and providing a feasible approach to addressing the common problem of insufficient regional adaptability in large-scale carbon storage estimation.

The results of this study further indicate that the climate-zoning strategy improves the regional adaptability of carbon storage estimation models, which is consistent with previous findings that climatic factors regulate vegetation carbon accumulation [42]. In the semi-arid zone of northern Shanxi, precipitation and SAVI were identified as the core driving factors, reflecting the strong response of xerophytic vegetation to water limitation. In this region, annual precipitation is less than 400 mm, vegetation cover is relatively low, shrubs are dominant, and soil background interference is strong; accordingly, SAVI became a key index. In contrast, in the humid zone of southern Shanxi, MTCI and temperature were the dominant factors, indicating that under favorable hydrothermal conditions, vegetation biomass accumulation is positively associated with chlorophyll content. Because the overall model did not sufficiently account for differences in vegetation carbon accumulation processes under different climatic backgrounds, larger estimation errors were more likely to occur in arid areas [43]. This suggests that, when estimating carbon storage of roadside vegetation along long-distance expressways, it is not appropriate to rely simply on a unified model; instead, the influence of ecological process differences across climatic zones on model transferability should be fully considered.

Driving mechanisms of the spatial patterns of roadside vegetation carbon storage

The spatial pattern of carbon storage revealed by the inversion results reflects the combined influence of macroclimatic conditions, human management, and spatial connectivity. Overall, the carbon storage of expressway roadside vegetation in Shanxi Province was highest in southern Shanxi, followed by central Shanxi, and lowest in northern Shanxi, indicating that climatic differences play a major role in shaping the carbon sequestration capacity of roadside vegetation [44]. In the semi-humid zone of southern Shanxi, favorable hydrothermal conditions supported the development of tree-dominated communities and higher carbon accumulation, whereas in the semi-arid zone of northern Shanxi, low temperatures and water limitation led to vegetation being dominated by low-carbon shrub communities.

Under a given climatic background, human intervention is an important factor influencing internal differences in carbon storage within roadside areas. Interchange areas, where arbor–shrub–grass configurations are relatively well developed and maintenance is more intensive, generally showed high carbon storage, exceeding that of slope areas mainly designed for soil stabilization and central medians primarily planted with low anti-glare shrubs. This indicates that the carbon sink level of roadside vegetation is controlled not only by natural hydrothermal conditions, but also by vegetation configuration patterns and management intensity. Even in areas with relatively unfavorable climatic conditions, optimizing vegetation structure and management practices at key nodes may still improve local carbon sink capacity to some extent. These findings provide useful reference for roadside vegetation carbon enhancement management and zone-specific maintenance strategies.

Limitations and uncertainty analysis

The multi-scale collaborative inversion approach developed in this study integrates UAV and satellite-based remote sensing, incorporating information at the UAV scale and thereby extending the conventional research pathway in which roadside vegetation carbon storage estimation has mainly relied on satellite remote sensing. However, as with most large-scale ecological spatial modeling studies, this study involves the integration of multi-source data and a multi-step modeling process, and therefore some degree of uncertainty in the estimation results is unavoidable. Future research should further analyze and improve the method in terms of parameter assumptions, scale effects, data constraints, and algorithmic mechanisms.

Uncertainty arising from parameter assumptions

This study uniformly adopted the default carbon conversion factor recommended by the IPCC (0.5) across the entire region. This factor helps maintain comparability in macro-scale studies and provides a feasible compromise under the constraint of lacking fine-grained (10 m) species-level classification information for expressway corridors. Nonetheless, systematic differences in actual carbon content may exist among vegetation types; the use of a uniform coefficient may introduce baseline uncertainty into the absolute carbon storage estimates. Future studies may integrate hyperspectral remote sensing with field plot measurements to achieve refined identification of dominant species and calibration of carbon content, thereby establishing a regional accounting system based on species-specific carbon fraction coefficients and reducing errors induced by parameter assumptions. In addition, this study did not perform systematic hyperparameter tuning for the GCN, XGBoost, RF, and SVM benchmark models, and the models’ peak performance may not have been fully realized, which could introduce some uncertainty into the model comparison results. Future research will conduct systematic hyperparameter tuning under a cross-validation framework with a larger sample size to further improve model performance and generalization ability.

Uncertainty from multi-scale integration and mixed-pixel effects

Constrained by the high cost of acquiring continuous high-resolution imagery at the provincial scale, this study used Sentinel-2 data at 10 m resolution to invert corridor carbon storage. Although mixed-pixel impacts were partially mitigated by defining a 300 m-wide corridor study zone and introducing indices that are relatively robust to background interference (e.g., SAVI and EVI), mixed pixels may still dilute local spectral signals in areas where paved road boundaries interweave with vegetation ecotones. This can cause information loss and error propagation during scaling procedures. Future work could explore endmember extraction and spectral unmixing approaches, or integrate higher-resolution remote sensing data (e.g., WorldView and Gaofen series) to enhance sub-pixel characterization of fragmented vegetation patches.

Uncertainty due to sample representativeness and incomplete spatiotemporal synchrony

In terms of spatial sampling, although the current 70 UAV sample plots cover the three major climatic zones of Shanxi Province—namely the northern temperate semi-arid zone, the central warm temperate semi-arid zone, and the southern warm temperate semi-humid zone—the sample density and representativeness still have room for improvement relative to the spatial heterogeneity of the province-wide complex expressway network. In addition, the number of samples is unevenly distributed among the three zones, with 15 plots in northern Shanxi, 39 in central Shanxi, and 16 in southern Shanxi. The sample allocation was determined according to the geographic area of each climatic zone and the proportion of expressway mileage, with the aim of improving the representativeness of road network coverage. However, this type of allocation may cause climate zones with larger sample sizes to carry greater weight during model training, making the model more prone to learning the feature patterns of those zones. When applied to zones with relatively sparse samples, differences in climate conditions, vegetation composition, or management practices may lead to reduced model generalization ability and increased prediction bias.

From a temporal perspective, strict temporal synchronization between the UAV data and satellite imagery could not be achieved because of the difficulty of airspace coordination and field campaign organization along operational expressways. The UAV data were collected in two phases, in autumn 2024 and summer 2025, whereas the satellite imagery was uniformly selected from summer 2025. Although woody vegetation biomass is relatively stable at the interannual scale, which may partially reduce the influence of this mismatch, seasonal differences and temporal inconsistency may still introduce uncertainty into the estimation results.

Future research should further increase sample density and regional coverage, and make every effort to obtain synchronized satellite and UAV observations. In addition, strategies such as transfer learning could be explored to support broader-scale dynamic monitoring and model generalization using a small number of high-accuracy samples.

Uncertainty in graph modeling mechanisms and spatial weight specification

At the algorithmic level, the GCN model adopted in this study leaves room for improvement in representing road-network spatial relationships. The adjacency matrix constructed based on Euclidean distance helps capture ecological similarity in continuous geographic space, but it may not fully reflect the barrier effects of local micro-topography on microclimate and vegetation growth, thereby introducing uncertainty in spatial weight allocation. Conclusions

To address the issues of spatial heterogeneity and scale transformation in carbon storage estimation for long-distance linear engineering ecosystems, this study took the expressway network of Shanxi Province as the research object and developed a method for estimating aboveground carbon storage of roadside vegetation by integrating multi-scale remote sensing with Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)-based spatial feature embedding. The main conclusions are as follows:

By constructing a multi-scale collaborative monitoring system based on UAV and satellite remote sensing, and integrating GCN spatial features with climate-zoned modeling, this study achieved carbon storage estimation for roadside vegetation.
