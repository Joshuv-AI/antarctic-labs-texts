Abstract
. We introduce a parameterized family of invariants for ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraphs. To each 𝕂 \mathbb{K} -linear transformation T : 𝕂 ℓ → 𝕂 r T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r} we associate a function Sig ⁡ ( − , T ) \Der(-,T) that maps ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraphs to 𝕂 \mathbb{K} -vector spaces. Given an ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraph ℋ = ( V , E ) \mathcal{H}=(V,E) , we use Sig ⁡ ( ℋ , T ) \Der(\mathcal{H},T) to define an equivalence relation ≡ T \equiv_{T} on V V called T T -fusion , which determines a quotient hypergraph 𝔉 ⁡ ( ℋ , T ) \mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H},T) called the T T -frame of ℋ \mathcal{H} . We show that the map U : 𝕂 ℓ → 𝕂 U:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K} , where U ⁡ ( λ ) = λ ⁡ ( 1 ) + ⋯ + λ ⁡ ( ℓ ) U(\lambda)=\lambda(1)+\cdots+\lambda(\ell) , is universal in that Sig ⁡ ( ℋ , T ) \Der(\mathcal{H},T) embeds in Sig ⁡ ( ℋ , U ) \Der(\mathcal{H},U) , and U U -fusion refines T T -fusion for any T : 𝕂 ℓ → 𝕂 r T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r} . We further show that 𝔉 ⁡ ( 𝔉 ⁡ ( ℋ , U ) , U ) = 𝔉 ⁡ ( ℋ , U ) \mathfrak{F}(\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H},U),U)=\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H},U) for any ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraph ℋ \mathcal{H} , so 𝔉 ⁡ ( − , U ) \mathfrak{F}(-,U) is a closure function on the set of ℓ \ell -uniform hypergraphs. We explore the properties of this one-time simplification of a hypergraph.

1.
Introduction

Invariants of the adjacency matrix of a graph are commonly deployed
to gain insight into the structure of the graph.
Indeed spectral graph theory studies
the properties of graphs through the lens of such invariants
[
6
]
.
Recent works such
as
[
7
]
[
10
]

have sought to generalize spectral methods to hypergraphs.
It has been helpful in such efforts to identify the given hypergraph with a
multilinear product (
tensor
), and
the origins of the current work lie in a
recent spectral theory of tensors
[
8
]
.
New families of linear invariants of tensors were introduced
in
[
5
]
to detect, in a given tensor, hidden patterns
belonging to a broad range of parameterized
families. Our objective is
to adapt those invariants to hypergraphs, and then use them to
explore combinatorial features.

Our main contribution is to define
an equivalence relation called
fusion

on the vertices of a hypergaph. In
that sense our concern is similar to works such
as
[
2
]
[
1
]
that study
hypergraph operators
compatible with equivalence relations. However, our process
works in reverse. Instead of starting with a
prescribed

equivalence on the vertices of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
,
to each linear transformation
T
we compute
operators
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
called
T
-signals

and use them to
define
an equivalence called
T
-fusion
.
We show that the transformation
U:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}
, where

U(\lambda)=\lambda(1)+\cdots+\lambda(\ell)
,
is universal in that, for any
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
, the
\mathbb{K}
-space

\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
embeds in
\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
. As a consequence,

U
-fusion is the most refined of
the fusion relations.

We then study the function that maps

\mathcal{H}
to the quotient
\D(\mathcal{H})
, called the
U
-frame
of
\mathcal{H}
,
defined by that equivalence.
One outcome of this study is that
\D(-)
is a closure function
on the set of finite connected
\ell
-uniform hypergraphs
(Theorem
6.4
).
Another outcome is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
such a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, computes the quotient
\D(\mathcal{H})
.
Thus, our technique offers an efficiently computible, one-time
simplification of a given hypergraph.

We have made a Python implementation of the techniques described
in the paper publicly available on GitHub
[
4
]
.
In Section
7
we report on some experiments carried out
with our programs.
The final section of the paper briefly
discusses two families of hypergraphs whose members are distinguished by
the full tensor invariants in
[
5
]
, but not
by our restricted invariants. This suggests there is much more
to learn from and about this nascent spectral theory of hypergraphs.

2.
Hypergraphs

In this section we summarize just the hypergraph concepts and
notation we need throughout, and refer to
[
3
]
for a thorough
treatment of the subject.

Denote by
Y^{X}
the set of
functions from
X
to
Y
. For
f\in Y^{X}
and
x\in X
, we
denote the image of
x
under
f
by
f(x)
. However, when

Y
is itself a set of functions,
we find it convenient to adopt the notation
x\mapsto f_{x}
, so that

f_{x}(-)
can be used for evaluation of
f_{x}
. Throughout the paper,

\ell\geqslant 3
is a fixed integer, and

[\ell]=\{1,\ldots,\ell\}.

Write
Y^{[\ell]}
as
Y^{\ell}
for short, and
let
\Sym(\ell)
be the group of permutations of
[\ell]
.
For
f\in Y^{\ell}
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
, define

f^{\sigma}\in Y^{\ell}
, where
f^{\sigma}(a)=f(a^{\sigma})

for
a\in[\ell]
. There is an equivalence relation on
Y^{\ell}

where
f
is equivalent to
g
if there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)

such that
g=f^{\sigma}
. The equivalence class

\displaystyle[f]

\displaystyle=\{\,f^{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)\,\}

of
f
is called the
orbit
of
f
under
\Sym(\ell)
.

Definition 2.1

(Hypergraph)
.

An
\ell
-uniform hypergraph
is a pair
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
of finite sets,
where
V
is the
vertex set
, and

E
is a set of orbits of
V^{\ell}
, called
(hyper)edges
,
under the natural action of
\Sym(\ell)
.

Our notational choices merit some explanation.
First, we allow repetitions of vertices in an edge, which is why we consider
tuples
V^{\ell}
rather than
\ell
-subsets
{V\choose\ell}
.
Secondly, we consider
undirected
hypergraphs,
so edges are really
multisets
rather than sequences
since the order of the vertices does not matter.
In contrast to other multiset notations,
we account for symmetry using the action of
\Sym(\ell)

on tuples because this choice aligns better with the invariants upon
which our methods rely. In particular,
by choosing a representative, we regard
e\in E

as an element of
V^{\ell}
and obtain other elements of the
equivalence class as
e^{\sigma}
for

\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
.

Definition 2.2

(Connectedness)
.

A
walk
in an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
is an alternating sequence
x_{1},e_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m-1},e_{m-1},x_{m}

of vertices and edges,
where for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
, there exist
a_{i},b_{i}\in[\ell]
such that

e_{i}(a_{i})=x_{i}
and
e_{i}(b_{i})=x_{i+1}
. The
reachability relation

on
V
, where
y
is related to
x
if there is a walk in
\mathcal{H}

from
x
to
y
, is an equivalence relation;
the
connected components
of
\mathcal{H}
are the equivalence classes.
We say that
\mathcal{H}
is
connected
if it has just one connected component.

The final hypergraph notion we need is that of a quotient. As
in the theory of graphs, the quotient of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
provides
a simplified view of
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.3

(Quotients)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
be an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph,
and let
\approx
be an equivalence relation on
V
.
Define:

•

\overline{V}=\{\,\overline{x}\mid x\in V\,\}
,
the set of
\approx
equivalence classes; and

•

\overline{E}=\{\overline{e}\mid e\in E\}\subseteq\overline{V}^{\ell}
,
where
\overline{e}(a)=\overline{e(a)}
for
a\in[\ell]
.

The
\ell
-uniform hypergraph
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=(\overline{V},\overline{E})

is called the
quotient
of
\mathcal{H}
by
\approx
.

.

Observe that

3.
Signals

Since an edge
e
of an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph

\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
is an orbit
[e]=\{e^{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)\}
,
to each
\ell
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
one can associate an
array
\Gamma:V^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}

where, for
f\in V^{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(f)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if}\;[f]\in E,\\
0&\mbox{else}.\end{array}\right.

Each axis of
\Gamma
(labelled by
a\in[\ell]
) has the same number of
coordinates (indexed by
V
), so
\Gamma
is a cubical array.
It is also symmetric because
\Gamma(f)=\Gamma(f^{\sigma})

for all
f\in V^{\ell}
and all
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
.
The
5\times 5\times 5
symmetric array
\Gamma
for the 5-vertex hypergraph in Example
2.5
is
pictured in Figure
3
.

On the left is a 3-uniform hypergaph

,

.

In this paper we work directly with the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
rather than with
the array
\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
.
The reason for mentioning
\Gamma(\mathcal{H})
at all is that arrays are representations
of tensors, and it is from tensors that we get the invariants
we shall use throughout the paper. Indeed, the following key
definition is an adaption to hypergraphs of the
notion of a
derivation
for tensors
[

, Definition 5.1]
.

Remark 3.1
.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{K}
denotes an arbitrary field.
The specific role of
\mathbb{K}
is inconsequential except that we shall
eventually need it to contain enough distinct elements,
so for convenience we henceforth assume that
\mathbb{K}

is infinite.

Definition 3.2

(
T
-Signal)
.

Given an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
and a linear
transformation
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
, where
r
is a positive integer, a

T
-signal
of
\mathcal{H}

is a function
\delta:[\ell]\to\mathbb{K}^{V}
sending
a\mapsto(\delta_{a}:V\to\mathbb{K})

satisfying

(3)

\displaystyle\forall e\in E,

\displaystyle~\forall\sigma\in\Sym(\ell),

\displaystyle T(\delta*e^{\sigma})=0\in\mathbb{K}^{r},

where
\delta*e^{\sigma}:[\ell]\to\mathbb{K}
maps
a\mapsto\delta_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))
. Put

(4)

\displaystyle\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

\displaystyle:=\{\;\delta\in(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}\mid\delta~\mbox{is a $T$-signal of $\mathcal{H}$}\;\}.

Let
\mathbf{1}:V\to\mathbb{K}
be the constant function
\mathbf{1}(x)=1
for

x\in V
, and let
\mathbf{1}_{a}
denote the function
\mathbf{1}

operating on coordinate
a\in[\ell]
. Similarly, let
\mathbf{0}:V\to\mathbb{K}

be the zero function
\mathbf{0}(x)=0
for
x\in V
.
Each
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
has a kernel

\ker(T)=\{\lambda:[\ell]\to\mathbb{K}\mid T(\lambda)=0\}
.
Observe for
\lambda\in\ker(T)
that the function

(5)

\displaystyle\delta(\lambda):[\ell]\to\mathbb{K}^{V},

\displaystyle\delta(\lambda)_{a}=\lambda(a)\mathbf{1}_{a}

is a
T
-signal of any hypergaph
\mathcal{H}
.
Such signals are constant on every axis, so we call them

constant signals
and put

(6)

\displaystyle\Der_{0}(T)

\displaystyle=\{\;\delta(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\ker(T)\;\}.

Constant signals clearly tell us nothing useful about the structure
of a hypergraph.
For
a\in[\ell]
, define
\hat{a}:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}
where

\hat{a}(b)=1
if
a=b
and
\hat{a}(b)=0
otherwise,
so
\{\,\hat{a}\mid a\in[\ell]\,\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
.
Similarly, let
\{\,\hat{x}\mid x\in V\,\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{K}^{V}
,
where
\hat{x}(y)=1
if
x=y
and
\hat{x}(y)=0
otherwise.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the ‘triangle’ hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
on vertex set
V=\{u,v,w\}

with the single hyperedge
e=(u,v,w)
. Let
T:\mathbb{K}^{3}\to\mathbb{K}
be the linear
transformation defined by
T(\lambda)=\lambda(1)-2\lambda(2)+\lambda(3)
.
Then
\ker(T)
is spanned by the vectors

2\cdot\hat{1}+\hat{2}
and
\hat{1}-\hat{3}
, so

\Der_{0}(T)
contains the (constant) signals

\displaystyle\delta(2\hat{1}+\hat{2})=(2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{1},\mathbf{1}_{2},\mathbf{0}_{3}),

\displaystyle\delta(\hat{1}-\hat{3})=(\mathbf{1}_{1},\mathbf{0}_{2},-\mathbf{1}_{3}).

One can easily verify that

\delta:[3]\to\mathbb{K}^{V}
defined by

\delta_{1}=2\hat{w}
,
\delta_{2}=\hat{u}+\hat{v}
,
\delta_{3}=2\hat{w}

is a (non-constant)
T
-signal.
For example, if
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
is the 3-cycle
(1,2,3)
, then

\displaystyle T(\delta*e^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}(e(1^{\sigma}))-2\delta_{2}(e(2^{\sigma}))+\delta_{3}(e(3^{\sigma}))

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}(e(2))-2\delta_{2}(e(3))+\delta_{3}(e(1))

\displaystyle=2\hat{w}(v)-2(\hat{u}(w)+\hat{v}(w))+2\hat{w}(u)

\displaystyle=0-2(0+0)+0=0.

The next result establishes the linear nature of the invariants
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
, and let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
be an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph. Then

\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
is a subspace of
(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
,
and
\Der_{0}(T)
is a subspace of
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
.

Proof.

Evidently,
(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space under
addition
(\delta+\acute{\delta})_{a}:=\delta_{a}+\acute{\delta}_{a}

and scalar product
(k\delta)_{a}:=k\delta_{a}
.
For
\delta,\acute{\delta}\in(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
,

e\in E
,
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
,
k\in\mathbb{K}
,
a\in[\ell]
,

\displaystyle[(\delta+k\acute{\delta})*e^{\sigma}](a)

\displaystyle=(\delta+k\acute{\delta})_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))

\displaystyle\mbox{(definition of}\;\delta*e^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=(\delta_{a}+k\acute{\delta}_{a})(e^{\sigma}(a))

(operations on
(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
)

\displaystyle=\delta_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))+k\acute{\delta}_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))

(operations on
\mathbb{K}^{V}
)

\displaystyle=(\delta*e^{\sigma})(a)+k(\acute{\delta}*e^{\sigma})(a)

\displaystyle\mbox{(definition of}\;\delta*e^{\sigma}).

Hence,
(\delta+k\acute{\delta})*e^{\sigma}=(\delta*e^{\sigma})+k(\acute{\delta}*e^{\sigma})\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
.
Since
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
is a
\mathbb{K}
-linear transformation,
for
\delta,\acute{\delta}\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

we have

\displaystyle T((\delta+k\acute{\delta})*e^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=T((\delta*e^{\sigma})+k(\acute{\delta}*e^{\sigma}))

\displaystyle=T(\delta*e^{\sigma})+k\,T(\acute{\delta}*e^{\sigma})=0.

It follows that
\delta+k\acute{\delta}\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
, so
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

is a subspace of
(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
.
Finally, let
\lambda\in\ker(T)\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
.
For
e\in E
,
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
,
we have

\displaystyle\forall a\in[\ell],

\displaystyle\delta(\lambda)*e^{\sigma}(a)

\displaystyle=\delta(\lambda)_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))=\lambda(a),

so
\delta(\lambda)*e^{\sigma}=\lambda
. Hence,
T(\delta(\lambda)*e^{\sigma})=T(\lambda)=0
,
so
\delta(\lambda)\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
. The map
\lambda\mapsto\delta(\lambda)

is a
\mathbb{K}
-linear embedding of
\ker(T)
into
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
.

Remark 3.5

(Signals versus derivations)
.

The notion of
T
-derivation in
[
5
, Definition 5.1]
can be
applied directly to the
array

\Gamma
of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
, in
which case each
\delta_{a}
would be a linear operator on the vector
space
\mathbb{K}^{V}
. The notion of signal in Definition
3.2
,
where each
\delta_{a}
is a function
V\to\mathbb{K}
, is
equivalent to requiring that
\delta_{a}
is a
diagonal
operator.
Thus, our derivations only
scale
vertices of
\mathcal{H}
,
whereas the derivations in
[
5
]
involve arbitary linear combinations.
In this sense we consider our hypergraph signals to be
‘combinatorial’ analogues of tensor derivations.

Remark 3.6

(Computing signals)
.

Using signals rather than derivations, we can push the range
of practical applications of our linear invariants of hypergraphs.
The unknown
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
can be written as
linear combinations of a standard basis
(\varepsilon^{(a,x)}\mid a\in[\ell],x\in V)

of
(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
where, if

\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{(a,x)}
, then
\varepsilon_{b}(y)=1
if and
a=b
and
x=y
,
and
\varepsilon_{b}(y)=0
otherwise. Thus, we can represent

\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
as a column vector

(\delta_{a}(x)\mid a\in[\ell],x\in V)
of degree
\ell\cdot|V|
.

Similarly,
T
is represented
as a
r\times\ell
matrix
M=(M_{i,a}\mid i\in[r],\,a\in[\ell])
.
Each
i\in[r]
,
e\in E
,
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)

in condition (
3
)
contributes one linear equation in the
unknowns
\delta_{a}(x)
. Indeed,
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)
is precisely
the nullspace of a matrix with
r\cdot|E|\cdot\ell!
rows
and
\ell\cdot|V|
columns,
whose entry in the row labelled by
(i,e,\sigma)
and
the column labelled by
(a,x)
is
M_{i,a}
.

For many applications the uniformity constant
\ell

of the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
will be quite small (say 3 or 4),
and
r
will typically be smaller than
\ell
. In such cases,
if
\mathcal{H}
has
n
vertices and
m
edges, we can solve for
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

using
O(mn\cdot\min\{m,n\})
operations.
This is much better than the complexity for
derivations in
[
5
]
.

4.
Centroids

We focus now on a particular transformation
C
and explore
the
C
-signals of a hypergraph.
Define
C:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{\ell-1}
to be the map
\lambda\mapsto C(\lambda)
,
where

(7)

\displaystyle C(\lambda)(i)

\displaystyle=\lambda(i)-\lambda(i+1),

\displaystyle\mbox{for}~i\in[\ell-1]

Note that
\lambda\in\ker(C)

if, and only if,
\lambda(1)=\cdots=\lambda(\ell)=k
for some
k\in\mathbb{K}
,
so
\Der_{0}(C)=\{\,k\mathbf{1}_{a}\mid a\in[\ell],\,k\in\mathbb{K}~\}

is 1-dimensional.

For general tensors, the transformation
C
parameterizes null patterns in
which the nonzero entries cluster as blocks down the leading
diagonal
[
5
, Section 7.3]
. Similarly,

C
-signals detect the connected components of a hypergraph.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
be an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph, and

\delta=(\delta_{a}\mid a\in[\ell])
a
C
-signal of
\mathcal{H}
.
If
x,y\in V
are in the same connected component of
\mathcal{H}
, then

(8)

\displaystyle\forall a,b\in[\ell],

\displaystyle\delta_{a}(x)=\delta_{b}(y).

Proof.

Let
\delta
be a
C
-signal of
\mathcal{H}
.
For each
e\in E
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
, we have

\delta*e^{\sigma}\in\ker(C)
,
so
\delta_{1}(e^{\sigma}(1))=\cdots=\delta_{\ell}(e^{\sigma}(\ell))
.

Suppose first that
x
and
y
belong to some edge
e\in E
.
Then, for each distinct pair
a,b\in[\ell]
, there exists

\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
such that
e^{\sigma}(a)=e(a^{\sigma})=x
and

e^{\sigma}(b)=y
, so it follows that
\delta_{a}(x)=\delta_{b}(y)
.
If
\tau
is the transposition of
\Sym(\ell)
interchanging
a

and
b
, then
e^{\tau\sigma}(a)=e^{\sigma}(b)=y
,
e^{\tau\sigma}(b)=e^{\sigma}(a)=x
,
and
e^{\tau\sigma}(c)=e^{\sigma}(c)
for all
c\in[\ell]-\{a,b\}
.
Since
\ell\geqslant 3
, there exists
c\in[\ell]-\{a,b\}
, so we have

\displaystyle\delta_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))=\delta_{c}(e^{\sigma}(c))=\delta_{c}(e^{\tau\sigma}(c))=\delta_{a}(e^{\tau\sigma}(a))=\delta_{a}(y).

The lemma now follows by induction on the length of a
walk in
\mathcal{H}
from
x
to
y
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The bound
\ell\geqslant 3
is important to note. The tensor
invariants upon which our hypergraph invariants are based
are defined only when the
valence
of the tensor
is greater than 2. They are inherently
multi
-linear rather
than linear invariants, and in particular cannot be applied to graphs.
Indeed the
\ell\geqslant 3
assumption was needed at the end of the proof
of Lemma

.

Theorem 4.3
.

The number of connected components of an
\ell
-uniform hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
is equal to the dimension of
\Der(\mathcal{H},C)
.

Proof.

Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{d}
be the connected components
of
\mathcal{H}
. We exhibit a basis of
\Der(\mathcal{H},C)

of size
d
.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define
\mathbf{1}^{[i]}\in(\mathbb{K}^{V})^{\ell}
,
where for
x\in V
,

\displaystyle\forall a\in[\ell],

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{[i]}_{a}(x)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&x\in V_{i}\\
0&\mbox{else}\end{array}\right..

For
e\in E
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
, since
\mathbf{1}^{[i]}_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))=1

for

precisely when

, it follows
that

, and
hence that
\mathbf{1}^{[i]}
is a

-signal.

For scalars
k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}\in\mathbb{K}
and

, we have

\displaystyle\forall a\in[\ell],

\displaystyle(k_{1}\mathbf{1}^{[1]}+\ldots+k_{d}\mathbf{1}^{[d]})_{a}(x)=k_{i}.

Hence,
k_{1}\mathbf{1}^{[1]}+\ldots+k_{d}\mathbf{1}^{[d]}=\mathbf{0}
if, and only if,

k_{1}=\ldots=k_{d}=0
, so the signals
\mathbf{1}^{[i]}

are linearly independent. Finally, if
\delta
is a

. It follows that

is a basis for

\Der(\mathcal{H},C)
.

5.
Universal signals

In view of Theorem
4.3
, we henceforth work
just with connected hypergraphs:

(11)

\displaystyle\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=~\{~\mathcal{H}\;\mid\;\mathcal{H}~\mbox{is a \lx@text@underline{connected} $\ell$-uniform hypergraph}~\}.

The previous section focused on signals of the
transformation
C
. In this section we consider another
transformation
U:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}
, where

(12)

\displaystyle U(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\lambda(1)+\lambda(2)+\cdots+\lambda(\ell).

For each
a\in[\ell-1]
, define
\lambda^{(a)}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
, where

\displaystyle\lambda^{(a)}(b)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\mbox{if}~b=a\\
-1&\mbox{if}~b=a+1\\
0&\mbox{else}\end{array}\right..

Then
\{\,\lambda^{(a)}\mid a\in[\ell-1]\,\}
is a basis for
\ker(U)
, so

\{\,\delta(\lambda^{(a)})\mid a\in[\ell-1]\,\}
is a basis for

\Der_{0}(U)
, which therefore has dimension
\ell-1
.

Remark 5.1
.

If
T(\hat{a})=0\in\mathbb{K}^{r}
for some
a\in[\ell]
, then
condition (
3
) imposes no constraint on

\delta_{a}
. Although such
T
are used to
decompose tensors relative to a fixed
set of axes
[
5
, Section 7.2]
, they provide no
useful information for the symmetric tensors
underlying hypergraphs. We therefore
assume that
T(\hat{a})\neq 0
for all
a\in[\ell]
,
which is evidently the case when
T=U
.

The following result is a special case of
[
5
, Proposition 8.1]
,
but we include a proof tailored to our notation.
It tells us that, for any
T
, the
\mathbb{K}
-space of
T
-signals
of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
embeds into its space of
U
-signals.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\in\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}
, and let
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}

be a linear map.
There are nonzero scalars
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell})\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

\displaystyle\qquad\implies\qquad(k_{1}\delta_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\delta_{\ell})\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U).

Proof.

Let
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
with
T(\hat{a})\neq 0
for all
a\in[\ell]
(cf. Remark
5.1
).
Then
\{v\in\mathbb{K}^{r}\mid v\cdot T(\hat{a})=0\}
,
where
\cdot
is the usual dot product,
is a subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{r}
of codimension 1.
Since
\mathbb{K}
is infinite,
\mathbb{K}^{r}
is not a finite union of hyperplanes, so
there exists
w\in\mathbb{K}^{r}
such that

\displaystyle\forall a\in[\ell],

\displaystyle w\cdot T(\hat{a})\neq 0.

Define
T_{w}:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}
, where
T_{w}(u)=w\cdot T(u)\in\mathbb{K}
.
Since
(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{\ell})
is a
T
-signal,
it is clear from (
3
) it
is also a
T_{w}
-signal. Since
T_{w}(\hat{a})=w\cdot T(\hat{a})\neq 0
,
putting
k_{a}=(w\cdot T(\hat{a}))^{-1}
,
it follows that
(k_{1}\delta_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\delta_{\ell})

is a
U
-signal, as required.
∎

Proposition
5.2
tells us that, under very mild conditions,
the transformation
U
is universal among the
various choices of
T
. We conclude this section with a useful analogue of
Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
\mathcal{H}\in\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}
and

\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
, we have

\displaystyle\forall a,b\in[\ell],

\displaystyle\delta_{a}-\delta_{b}\in\langle\mathbf{1}\rangle.

Proof.

The claim holds if
a=b
, so let
a
and
b
be distinct.
If
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
, then

\displaystyle\forall e\in E,~\forall\sigma\in\Sym(\ell),

\displaystyle U(\delta*e)=\sum_{c=1}^{\ell}\delta_{c}(e^{\sigma}(c))=0.

We must show, for distinct vertices
x,y\in V
,
that
\delta_{a}(x)-\delta_{b}(x)=\delta_{a}(y)-\delta_{b}(y)
.

Suppose first that there is an edge
e\in E

containing
x
and
y
. Then there exists
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
such that

e^{\sigma}(a)=x
and
e^{\sigma}(b)=y
. Since
\delta
is a

U
-signal of
\mathcal{H}
,

(13)

\displaystyle 0~=~\sum_{c\in[\ell]}\delta_{c}(e^{\sigma}(c))~=~\delta_{a}(x)+\delta_{b}(y)+\sum_{c\not\in\{a,b\}}\delta_{c}(e^{\sigma}(c)).

Let
\tau
be the transposition of
\Sym(\ell)
that interchanges

a
and
b
. Then

e^{\tau\sigma}(a)=e^{\sigma}(b)=y,\quad e^{\tau\sigma}(b)=e^{\sigma}(a)=x,\quad e^{\tau\sigma}(c)=e^{\sigma}(c)~\mbox{for}~c\not\in\{a,b\},

so we have

(14)

\displaystyle 0~=~\sum_{c\in[\ell]}\delta_{c}(e^{\tau\sigma}(c))~=~\delta_{a}(y)+\delta_{b}(x)+\sum_{c\not\in\{a,b\}}\delta_{c}(e^{\sigma}(c)).

Subtracting (
14
) from (
13
) we get

\delta_{a}(x)-\delta_{b}(x)=\delta_{a}(y)-\delta_{b}(y)
.

The result now follows by induction on the length of a path in
\mathcal{H}

from
x
to
y
.
∎

6.
Fusion and frames

We return now to a notion
introduced in Section
2
,
namely quotients of hypergaphs. Given a connected
\ell
-uniform
hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
, one can of course form a quotient

\overline{\mathcal{H}}=(\overline{V},\overline{E})
using any
equivalence relation on
V
, but doing so
without reference to
\mathcal{H}
will
tell us nothing useful about its structure. Our strategy is to use the

T
-signals of
\mathcal{H}
to define an equivalence relation on
V
.

Definition 6.1

(Fusion and Frame)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
be a connected
\ell
-uniform hypergraph,
and let
T:\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{K}^{r}
be a linear map. The equivalence relation

(15)

\displaystyle x\equiv_{T}y\qquad\iff\qquad\forall\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},T),\quad\delta(x)=\delta(y)

on
V
is called the
T
-fusion
relation.
The quotient hypergraph
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H},T)
of
\mathcal{H}
under

T
-fusion is called the
T
-frame
of
\mathcal{H}
.

The matter of which
T
to choose
was effectively settled by Proposition
5.2
. For
any
T
and any
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
, the
\mathbb{K}
-space
\Der(\mathcal{H},T)

embeds in
\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
. It follows immediately that
U
-fusion on

V
is always a refinement of
T
-fusion. Thus, we will detect the
most subtle features of
\mathcal{H}
using
U
-fusion and computing
the corresponding frame
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H}):=\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{H},U)
.

Proposition 6.3
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that,
given a connected

, finds

\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
satisfying

(16)

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in V,

\displaystyle x\equiv_{U}y~~\iff~~\delta(x)=\delta(y).

Proof.

We prove that Algorithm
1
behaves as stated.

Given
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)
with
|V|=n

, a basis for

\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
can be computed
using
O(mn\cdot\min\{m,n\})
operations (Remark

).
We suppress the details of this computation and initialize

\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
on Line 1.

Algorithm 1

(Generating Signal)

1:

\mathcal{H}=(V,E)\in\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}
.

2:

\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
such that

for
x,y\in V

\delta(x)=\delta(y)
.

3:

\mathcal{B}\leftarrow
basis for

4:

\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\{~\}

\displaystyle\mathtt{P}(\mathcal{C},\delta)\,:

\displaystyle\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)~\wedge~(~(\forall x)(\forall y)[~\delta(x)=\delta(y)~\iff~\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{C},\,\alpha(x)=\alpha(y)~]~)

Consider a fixed iteration of the loop, where the pair
(\delta,\mathcal{C})
has values

(\delta_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
before the iteration and
(\delta_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1})
at the end,
so we have
\delta_{1}=\delta_{0}+k\beta
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}_{0}\cup\{\beta\}
.

Suppose that
\mathtt{P}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\delta_{0})
holds, and fix

.

If
\delta_{0}(x)=\delta_{0}(y)
and
\beta(x)=\beta(y)
, then

for all

.
But it also follows that

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}(x)+k\beta(x)=\delta_{0}(y)+k\beta(y)=\delta_{1}(y).

Conversely, if
\delta_{1}(x)=\delta_{1}(y)
, then

\displaystyle\delta_{0}(x)+k\beta(x)=\delta_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{1}(y)=\delta_{0}(y)+k\beta(y),

).

The main purpose of a quotient is to simplify a complicated
hypergraph. A well chosen quotient should both simplify and
retain essential features of the original. Given a hypergaph

\mathcal{H}
, the quotient
\D(\mathcal{H})
in a sense captures features of
\mathcal{H}
that are
detectable by linear invariants. It is natural to ask what those
features are, and this is a topic we take up in
Section
8
.
It is also natural to wonder about the dynamics of the function
\D(-)
.
For example, does repetition

\mathcal{H}~\rightsquigarrow~\D(\mathcal{H})~\rightsquigarrow~\D(\D(\mathcal{H}))~\rightsquigarrow~\ldots

yield further simplification?
As it turns out, it does not:
\D(-)
is a closure function.

Theorem 6.4
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a connected
\ell
-uniform hypergraph, then

\D(\D(\mathcal{H}))=\D(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

For
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)\in\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}
, recall that
\D(\mathcal{H})=(\overline{V},\overline{E})
for

\displaystyle\overline{V}=\{\,\overline{x}\mid x\in V\,\},

\displaystyle\overline{E}=\{\overline{e}\mid e\in E\}\subset V^{\ell},

where
\overline{x}=\{y\in V\mid\forall\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U),\,\forall a\in[\ell],\;\delta_{a}(x)=\delta_{a}(y)\}

and
\overline{e}(a)=\overline{e(a)}
for
a\in[\ell]
.
For
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
,
a\in[\ell]
, and
x\in V
,

\delta_{a}
is constant on the class
\overline{x}
.
Hence, each
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
induces a function
\overline{\delta}\in(\mathbb{K}^{\overline{V}})^{\ell}
,
where
\overline{\delta}_{a}(\overline{x}):=\delta_{a}(x)
.
Furthermore, for
\overline{e}\in\overline{E}
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\delta}_{a}(\overline{e}^{\sigma}(a))

\displaystyle=\overline{\delta}_{a}(\overline{e(a^{\sigma})})=\delta_{a}(e(a^{\sigma})),

so
U(\overline{\delta}*\overline{e}^{\sigma})=U(\delta*e^{\sigma})=0
and it follows that

\overline{\delta}\in\Der(\D(\mathcal{H}),U)
.
In particular, if
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
is the element in
Proposition
6.3
and
\overline{\delta}\in\Der(\D(\mathcal{H}),U)
,
then each
\overline{\delta}_{a}
is one-to-one. Hence,
the
U
-fusion classes all have size 1, so

\D(\D(\mathcal{H}))=\D(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

It turns out that this folding mechanism in the frame of a hypergraph
is the consequence of a general phenomenon:

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(V,E)\in\mathtt{HYP}_{\ell}
. For distinct
x,y\in V
,
suppose there are edges
e,f\in E
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\ell)
satisfying

\displaystyle e^{\sigma}(1)=x,\quad f(1)=y,\quad e^{\sigma}(a)=f(a)~\mbox{for}~a\in\{2,\ldots,\ell\}.

Then
x\equiv_{U}y
, so

\overline{x}=\overline{y}
in the frame of
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from the definition of signal.
For
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
,
since
U(\delta*e^{\sigma})=0=U(\delta*f)
,
we have
\sum_{a\in[\ell]}\delta_{a}(e^{\sigma}(a))=\sum_{a\in[\ell]}\delta_{a}(f(a))
.
It follows from our assumptions on
e,f
and
\sigma
that

\delta_{1}(x)=\delta_{1}(y)
and hence that
\delta_{a}(x)=\delta_{a}(y)
for
all
a\in[\ell]
, so
x\equiv_{U}y
.
∎

Proposition 8.2

Let

\hat{E}=E\,\dot{\sqcup}\,\{\hat{e}\}

and
\hat{e}(a)\in W

\hat{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathtt{STAB}_{\ell}(\D)

Since
\mathcal{H}=(V,H)\in\mathtt{STAB}_{\ell}(\D)

6.3

there exists
\delta\in\Der(\mathcal{H},U)
with each
\delta_{a}:V\to\mathbb{K}

it suffices to exhibit
\hat{\delta}\in\Der(\hat{\mathcal{H}},U)

with
\hat{\delta}_{1}

For each
a\in[\ell]
, define
\hat{\delta}_{a}:\hat{V}\to\mathbb{K}
as follows.
For
x\in V
, put
\hat{\delta}_{a}(x)=\delta_{a}(x)
.
This ensures that
\hat{\delta}
satisfies
condition (
3
) for all
e\in E

We must now extend
\hat{\delta}_{a}
to
W

)

\mathtt{C}(\hat{\delta}*\hat{e}^{\sigma})=0

\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\ell)

, for each

\delta_{a}(x)-\delta_{1}(x)=k_{a}

\displaystyle U(\hat{\delta}*\hat{e}^{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{\ell}\hat{\delta}_{a}(\hat{e}^{\sigma}(a))=\sum_{a=1}^{\ell}k_{a}~+~\sum_{a=1}^{\ell}\hat{\delta}_{1}(\hat{e}^{\sigma}(a)).

9.
Closing remarks

In Remark
3.5
we mentioned that signals
are restricted versions of the derivations we could possible
use if we treated the given hypergraph as a tensor. Recall
that there are two reasons for working with these restricted
derivations. The first
is that it is easier to interpret the outcomes from a combinatorial
perspective. The second, as noted in Remark
3.6
,
is that it is much faster to compute
signals than derivations.
Indeed, the specific goal of this paper was to introduce linear
invariants of a hypergraph that can be quickly computed, and which
capture key features of the hypergraph. While there remain questions
to be explored, the main results of the paper achieve the
objectives that motivated the work.

However, the signals used in this paper
do not detect features of the hypergraph that are detectable
by derivations. The space of derivations of a tensor
forms a Lie algebra
[
5
, Theorem 8.2]
and preliminary
experiments with tensors associated to hypergraphs strongly suggest
that in many cases the structure of this Lie algebra can be used
to recover features of the hypergraph that our methods miss
entirely
[
9
]
. We mention two such families of 3-uniform hypergraphs.

First, the hypergaph
\mathcal{H}
in Example
2.5
belongs
to a family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
of “fans” parameterized by the
number
n
of segments in the fan. Thus,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}_{3}
because
\mathcal{H}
has 3 segments.
From Proposition
8.1
we see that
\D(\mathcal{F}_{n})=\mathcal{F}_{1}

for all
n
(we just fold up all of the segments). Thus, our
frame function
\D(-)
does not distinguish between individual members
in the family
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, except insofar as the equivalence
classes of vertices grow in size. On the
other hand, the Lie algebra of derivations of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
grows
in a predictable way as
n
increases. More precisely, the
derivations of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are representations of a family
of simple Lie algebras.

Secondly, the frame

\D(\mathcal{H})
in Example
6
belongs to a family
\mathcal{M}_{n}
of
“mountain ranges”. It follows from Proposition
8.2

that
\mathcal{M}_{n}\in\mathtt{STAB}_{3}(\D)
. In particular,
\D(\mathcal{M}_{n})

does not distinguish the peaks of the mountains from their bases.
On the other hand, these features can (with some effort) be recovered
from the Lie algebra structure of its derivations.

Thus, the application of the
full
invariants introduced
in
[
5
]
to the
tensors
representing hypergraphs
provides a fertile area for future research.
