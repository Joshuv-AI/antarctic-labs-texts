Discovery of azaindole/indole-fused pyrimidine tetracyclic scaffolds as novel potent CDK7 inhibitors
Authors: Fan Pu, Axiao Tao, Pengxiang Qiu, Weishan Deng, Zijian Chen, Zheng Cao, Yunjiao He, Peng George Wang, Yan Zhang, Chengqing Ning, Xuechen Li, Jing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCDK7 has emerged as a highly promising anticancer target, as its dual indispensable roles in cell cycle progression and transcriptional regulation for cancer cell proliferation and survival. Herein, we describe the rational design and identification of novel tetracyclic CDK7 inhibitors with an azaindole/indole-fused pyrimidine scaffold, achieved via a cyclisation-based conformational restriction strategy. Compound 7a emerged as a potent, highly selective CDK7 inhibitor (IC50 = 1.9 nM), outperforming clinical candidate SY-5609 (IC50 = 2.5 nM). It exhibited prominent antiproliferative activity against HCT116 colorectal cancer cells (IC50 = 1.7 nM) and excellent selectivity over other CDK isoforms. In vivo, 7a exerted robust dose-dependent antitumor efficacy in HCT116 xenograft model without obvious toxicity. Collectively, its superior in vitro potency, high CDK7 selectivity, and significant in vivo antitumor activity highlight the potential of 7a as a chemical tool for investigating CDK7 biology and as a lead compound for further pharmacokinetic optimisation.

Introduction

CDK7 is a distinct serine/threonine kinase that belongs to the cyclin-dependent kinase family, embodying critical functions in both cell cycle regulation and transcriptional control1,2. As the catalytic subunit of the ternary CDK-activating kinase (CAK) complex, which consists of CDK7, cyclin H, and MAT1, CDK7 phosphorylates the T-loop of multiple CDKs, including CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK63,4. This phosphorylation activates these kinases, thereby promoting cell cycle progression. In addition to its role in cell cycle regulation, the CAK complex serves as a component of the general transcription factor IIH (TFIIH), where CDK7 phosphorylates the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) at Serine 5 and Serine 7, thus aiding in the initiation of transcription. Furthermore, CDK7 phosphorylates CDK9, which in turn phosphorylates the Pol II CTD at Ser2, thereby driving transcription elongation5–8. Beyond its core roles in cell cycle control and transcriptional regulation, CDK7 has been shown to exert pro-oncogenic effects by directly phosphorylating or modulating the expression of critical oncogenic factors, such as c-Myc9,10. This emphasises the significant and diverse role of CDK7 in the process of oncogenesis.

Indeed, there is increasing evidence indicating that dysregulation of CDK7 is closely linked to malignant progression. The downregulation of CDK7 significantly impairs cancer cell proliferation, while elevated CDK7 expression has been documented in a variety of cancer types (e.g. triple-negative breast cancer, small-cell lung cancer) and correlates with poor clinical prognosis1,11. Notably, cancer cells frequently exhibit “transcriptional addiction”, characterised by a profound dependence on increased transcriptional output to support rapid proliferation and maintain the expression of oncogenic drivers, such as MYC10,12, RUNX113,14, and TAL114, which are associated with super-enhancers. CDK7 plays a crucial role in mediating this vulnerability. Consequently, CDK7 is emerging as a highly promising therapeutic target.

Driven by its significant therapeutic potential, the development of selective CDK7 inhibitors has emerged as a prominent research area, garnering significant interest from both academia and industry. Early covalent inhibitors, such as THZ1, demonstrated potent anti-tumour activity by disrupting super-enhancer-driven transcription; however, they suffered from off-target inhibition of CDK12/13, resulting in broader transcriptional repression and dose-limiting toxicities14,15. Subsequent advancements have produced more selective compounds16–20, which encompass both non-covalent inhibitors (e.g. SY-5609, CT7001/Samuraciclib) and covalent derivatives (e.g. Q901, XL-102/Tacaciclib, YKL-5–124). Currently, several CDK7 inhibitors are undergoing clinical trials (Figure 1), including both covalent inhibitors (SY-1365, LY3405105, Q901 (NCT05394103), XL102) and non-covalent agents (CT7001 (NCT04802759), SY-5609 (NCT04929223), TY-2699a (NCT05866692), EOC237 (NCT05895825), GTAEXS-617 (NCT05985655)). It is noteworthy that earlier covalent inhibitors, such as SY-1365, LY-3405105, and XL102, were discontinued due to inadequate efficacy or intolerable toxicity16,21,22. Among the candidates still in development, Samuraciclib has demonstrated promising clinical benefit rates in HR+/HER2- breast cancer following CDK4/6 inhibitor progression when administered in combination with fulvestrant, presenting manageable toxicity profiles23. Additionally, SY-5609, a thoroughly investigated non-covalent selective CDK7 inhibitor, is currently being evaluated in Phase I/II clinical trials for the treatment of metastatic colorectal cancer (NCT04929223). However, considerable challenges remain, including low plasma exposure and suboptimal oral bioavailability16, which may limit its clinical applicability.

Conformational restriction achieved through macrocyclization or ring closure has been validated as an effective strategy to enhance potency, selectivity, and overall drug-like properties. This approach reduces entropic penalties associated with target binding and stabilises the bioactive conformation24,25. In this study, we present the rational design and biological evaluation of azaindole/indole-fused pyrimidine scaffolds as inhibitors of CDK7. These scaffolds were derived from the SY-5609 framework using a cyclisation strategy to constrain conformational flexibility. This research led to the identification of 7a, which exhibits potent in vitro activity, high selectivity, and significant in vivo anticancer efficacy. Consequently, these findings provide a compelling rationale for the continued development of 7a as a next-generation therapeutic agent targeting CDK7.

Results and discussion

At the outset of this project, no cocrystal structure of human CDK7 in complex with SY-5609 was available. Accordingly, we modelled the binding mode of SY-5609 using the publicly accessible Cryo-EM structure of human CAK in complex with CT7001, which is deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 7B5Q). We then docked SY-5609 into the ATP-binding pocket of CDK7 via Schrödinger’s Glide program. Building on this modelled system, we first characterised the predicted binding mode of SY-5609 within the ATP-binding pocket of CDK7 (Figure 2A) to guide the development of more potent and selective CDK7 inhibitors.

(A) Predicted binding mode of SY-5609 in complex with CDK7 protein (PDB ID: 7B5Q). (B) Superposed binding modes of SY-5609 and indole-fused pyrimidine derivative 7a in complex with CDK7 protein. (C) Rational design of indole-fused pyrimidine tetracyclic scaffolds as CDK7 inhibitors.

Key intermolecular interactions were identified: the 2-aminopyrimidine scaffold anchors to the hinge region via two hydrogen bonds with Met94; the basic nitrogen in the piperidine ring (attached to the amino group) forms a salt-bridge-mediated hydrogen bond with Asp97, thereby reinforcing binding affinity; the 4-position indole substituent projects outward to form a hydrogen bond with Lys41; and the 5-trifluoromethyl group establishes hydrophobic contacts with the gatekeeper residue Phe91, a interaction that may contribute to target activity and selectivity. Drawing on these binding insights, we hypothesised that cyclizing the 5-position of the pyrimidine ring with the indole moiety via a hydrophobic carbon linker would achieve two key objectives (Figure 2C): (1) enhancing interactions with the hydrophobic pocket formed by Phe91, Ala39, and Ile75; (2) restricting the rotational flexibility of the indole ring (via conformational restriction) to improve both binding affinity and target selectivity.

Docking simulations (Figure 2B) confirmed that the cyclized indole-fused pyrimidine derivative adopted a binding mode superimposable with that of SY-5609, validating our design rationale. Accordingly, we designed and synthesised a series of indole-fused pyrimidine tetracyclic derivatives. Subsequent biological evaluations and structure-activity relationship (SAR) analyses identified compound 7a as a highly selective CDK7 inhibitor, which exhibited superior potency to SY-5609 in both CDK7 kinase inhibition assays and antiproliferative assays against the colorectal cancer HCT116 cell line.

Preliminary SAR exploration of indole-fused pyrimidine tetracyclic CDK7 inhibitors

As summarised in Table 1, the initial synthesised tetracyclic derivatives were designed to investigated the impacts of fused ring size and substituent variations (e.g. dimethylpiperidine vs. piperidine moieties), and functional group replacement (e.g. methyl sulphone in 7c instead of the dimethylphosphine oxide (DMPO) group in other compounds) on biological activities. In terms of CDK7 enzymatic inhibition, compound 7a exhibited the most potent activity with an IC50 value of 1.9 nM, which was superior to that of the parent compound SY-5609 (IC50 = 2.5 nM). For antiproliferative activity against human colorectal cancer HCT116 cells, 7a also showed the strongest efficacy (IC50 = 1.7 nM), outperforming SY-5609 (IC50 = 11.4 nM). In contrast, all tested compounds displayed moderate inhibitory activity against K562 cells, with IC50 values in the micromolar range (e.g. 1.921 μM for 7a and 2.348 μM for SY-5609). This profile aligns with the tumour cell selectivity of SY-5609, implying that the target compounds may share a similar target selectivity pattern with the parent molecule.

As shown in Table 2, 7a, 7b, and 7d exhibited exceptional selectivity for CDK7 over these clinically relevant-off target CDKs: specifically, they displayed 277.9-, 243.9-, and 2132.4-fold selectivity for CDK7 versus CDK1, and ∼3919-, ∼2507-, and ∼977-fold selectivity for CDK7 over CDK6, respectively. Against CDK2 and CDK9, these analogues also maintained high selectivity: 7a retained ∼353- and ∼292-fold selectivity, 7b showed ∼733- and ∼383-fold selectivity, and 7d exhibited ∼337- and ∼135-fold selectivity for CDK7.

In contrast, replacing the DMPO moiety (present in 7a, 7b, 7d) with a methyl sulphone group (in 7c) markedly diminished selective CDK7 inhibition: the CDK7/CDK1 selectivity dropped to 114.7-fold, approximately half of 7a’s selectivity. These findings demonstrate that the DMPO group is critical for preserving selective CDK7 inhibition, which is favourable for minimising potential clinical side effects.

Among these analogues, 7a emerges as the most promising lead candidate: it combines potent CDK7 inhibitory activity (IC50 = 1.9 nM) with robust selectivity over a panel of off-target CDKs. This favourable activity-selectivity profile renders 7a an ideal scaffold for optimisation. Accordingly, we selected 7a as the lead compound to conduct subsequent structural modifications (focused on the indole benzene ring and piperidine moiety), to deepen the SAR exploration of this tetracyclic scaffold.

First, replacing the 2-amino-piperidine moiety (the key substituent in lead 7a) with hydroxyl-containing side chains (either linear alkyl or cycloalkyl frameworks) generally resulted in diminished antiproliferative activity against HCT116 cells: most derivatives (e.g. 8a, 8b, 8c, 8e, 8 g) exhibited HCT116 IC50 values in the micromolar range (2.551–8.357 μM), far less potent than the lead. This loss of potency is likely attributed to the disruption of the critical hydrogen bond between the basic nitrogen (of the original piperidine) and Asp97 residue (Figure 1B), as these hydroxyl-bearing substituents alter the electronic property or spatial orientation of the key nitrogen donor.

Notably, two derivatives (8d and 8f), bearing three-carbon linear hydroxyl-containing side chains, retained nanomolar-level HCT116 antiproliferative activity (IC50 = 110 nM and 170 nM, respectively), which correlated with their moderate CDK7 enzymatic inhibitory potency (IC50 = 4.8 nM for 8d; 21.9 nM for 8f). This observation suggests that the three-carbon chain length of these hydroxyl-bearing analogues is favourable for partially preserving the Asp97 hydrogen bond interaction, thereby retaining (albeit reduced) activity. Stereochemistry further modulates potency: derivative 8e (with an R-configured chiral centre in the hydroxyl side chain) exhibited markedly diminished HCT116 activity (IC50 = 2550.7 nM), whereas 8d, possessing a structurally analogous three-carbon side chain with an S-configured chiral centre, retained nanomolar potency, highlighting the importance of stereochemical matching for optimal binding to the CDK7 pocket.

Consistently, compound 8 l with weak HCT116 antiproliferative activity (IC50 = 2835.3 nM) also showed substantially reduced CDK7 inhibitory potency (IC50 = 33.6 nM). These results collectively reinforce a positive correlation between CDK7 enzymatic inhibition and cellular antiproliferative efficacy for this tetracyclic scaffold, while also underscoring the critical roles of side-chain length and stereochemistry in maintaining the key Asp97 hydrogen bond interaction.

Second, modifying the 2-amino-piperidine moiety by replacing it with a pyrrolidine ring (e.g. 8 m) or altering the position of the basic nitrogen in the piperidine framework (e.g. shifting from meta- to para-substitution) led to a dramatic loss of activity: for instance, 8 m displayed a markedly elevated HCT116 IC50 (2179.3 nM). These changes likely disrupt the precise spatial alignment and basicity required for the key hydrogen bond with Asp97 residue, highlighting the critical role of the piperidine ring (including its size and the spatial position of the basic nitrogen) in maintaining this essential interaction and, consequently, target engagement and cellular potency.

Notably, introducing a methyl group on the basic nitrogen of the 2-amino-piperidine moiety exerted only a minor impact on activity: compound 8o retained potent HCT116 antiproliferative activity (IC50 = 35.5 nM), suggesting this subtle alkylation is well-tolerated and does not compromise the scaffold’s biological efficacy.

SAR exploration of the indole benzene ring.

Initially, the DMPO group was replaced with small-molecule functional groups such as bromine (9a and acetyl (9b)). As shown in Table 4, these structural modifications had a negligible effect on CDK7 inhibitory activity. Specifically, 9a exhibited a IC50 of 1.7 nM against CDK7, while 9b showed a CDK7 IC50 of 2.4 nM. Both values were comparable to that of the parent compound 7a (1.9 nM). Notably, 9a displayed potent anti-proliferative activity against K562 cells (IC50 = 556.4 nM), which differed distinctly from 7a and SY-5609 (the latter preferentially inhibits HCT116 cell proliferation over K562). This divergent cellular selectivity implies that 9a may interact with off-target molecular entities. Thus, further target deconvolution studies are warranted to verify its target specificity and eliminate potential off-target effects.

In contrast, replacing the DMPO moiety with bulkier alicyclic substituents (e.g. 9c, 9d) that contain no hydrogen-bond acceptors led to a potency reduction in a volume-dependent manner. For instance, 9c, bearing a moderately sized cycloalkyl group, exhibited an IC50 value of 280.6 nM against HCT116 cells. In comparison, 9d, which carries a more sterically hindered alicyclic substituent, showed a dramatically decreased anti-proliferative potency against HCT116 cells with an IC50 value increased to 833.6 nM, suggesting that excessive steric hindrance may disrupt the optimal ligand-target binding conformation. Moreover, this potency reduction may also be attributed to the loss of H-bonding interactions with Lys41, a residue proximal to the binding pocket of CDK7 (Figure 2A).

Shifting the DMPO group from the 7-position to the 6-position of the indole benzene ring (resulting in 9e) caused a complete loss of anti-proliferative activity, with its IC50 value against HCT116 cells exceeding 10 μM. However, replacing the indole scaffold with an azaindole moiety (yielding 9f) significantly improved the anti-proliferative potency against HCT116 cells (IC50 = 2.095 μM) compared to the position-shifted inactive analogue 9e. This result suggests that the azaindole scaffold can partially restore critical ligand-protein binding interactions, which may be attributed to the adjusted electronic distribution and hydrogen-bonding capacity of the azaindole ring.

Encouraged by this finding, a panel of azaindole derivatives was further synthesised to optimise the activity. Replacing the DMPO group with methyl carbamate (9 g) or sulphonamide substituents (e.g. 9h, 9i, 9j) not only enhanced the anti-proliferative activity against HCT116 cells to the nanomolar range (e.g. 9h: HCT116 IC50 = 57.6 nM) but also preserved or even enhanced CDK7 inhibitory potency. For instance, 9h showed a CDK7 IC50 of 1.0 nM, which was more potent than the parent compound 7a. Nevertheless, these analogues suffered from compromised selectivity towards CDK1: 9 g and 9h displayed CDK1 IC50 values of 38.8 nM and 140.9 nM, respectively.

To elucidate the structural basis underlying the enhanced CDK1 inhibitory activity of these derivatives, we performed molecular docking simulations of 9 g in complex with CDK1 (PDB ID: 6GU3). As depicted in Figure 3A, the predicted binding mode revealed that the aminopyrimidine core of 9 g forms canonical hydrogen bonding interactions with the hinge region residue Leu83. A salt-bridging hydrogen bond was observed between the protonated piperidine nitrogen of 9 g and the carboxylate side chain of Asp86 in CDK1. Furthermore, a crucial hydrogen bond was detected between the carbonyl oxygen of the carbamate moiety in 9 g and the hydroxyl group of Tyr15 in CDK1. This additional hydrogen-bonding interaction is proposed to be the primary structural determinant for the augmented CDK1 inhibitory potency of 9 g. In contrast, structural superimposition of the predicted 7a-CDK7 complex onto CDK1 (Figure 3B) uncovered a steric clash between the dimethyl substituents of the DMPO group in 7a and Tyr15 of CDK1. By comparison, in CDK7, these dimethyl groups are oriented towards the phenyl ring of Phe23, forming a favourable hydrophobic interaction. This distinct binding pattern underscores the critical role of the DMPO group in conferring both binding affinity and selective recognition of CDK7 over CDK1.

Notably, replacing the DMPO group with five-membered heterocyclic moieties yielded derivatives that retained potent CDK7 inhibitory activity and antiproliferative efficacy against HCT116 cells, comparable to those of 7a. Intriguingly, 9 m (CDK7 IC50 = 4.3 nM) bearing a pyrazole group at the C6 position of the indole scaffold exhibited CDK7 inhibitory potency similar to that of 9 l (CDK7 IC50 = 3.6 nM), which incorporates a pyrazole substituent at the C6 position of the azaindole core. To rationalise the potent biological profiles of these heterocycle-substituted derivatives, we conducted molecular docking simulations of 7a and 9 l in complex with CDK7 (PDB ID: 8R9U). As illustrated in the binding model (Figure 3D), 9 l adopted a conformation similar to that of 7a within the CDK7 active site, with three key conserved hydrogen-bonding interactions: (1) the aminopyrimidine motif with Met94 in the hinge region; (2) the basic nitrogen atom of the piperidine side chain with Asp97; and (3) the NH group of the azaindole substituent with Asp155. These hallmark interactions, critical for 7a’s binding affinity, were well-preserved in 9 l, providing a structural rationale for the comparable CDK7 inhibitory potency of these five-membered heterocycle-substituted analogues.

Regrettably, similar to the carbamate/sulphonamide derivatives, these heterocycle-substituted analogues also failed to achieve satisfactory CDK1 selectivity. For example, 9 l exhibited a CDK1 IC50 value of 67.6 nM. Mechanistically, similar to the sulphonamide moiety, the pyrazole group in these analogues was found to form a hydrogen bond with the Tyr15 residue of CDK1 (Figure 3A), an interaction proposed to contribute to their enhanced CDK1 binding and thus compromised selectivity.

Collectively, the systematic SAR exploration delineated the essential pharmacophoric features of this tetracyclic CDK7 inhibitor scaffold (Figure 4): the 2-aminopyrimidine core acts as the conserved hinge-binding anchor, the piperidine side chain is indispensable for high-affinity target engagement via the Asp97 interaction, the fused ring system rigidifies the molecular conformation to optimise hydrophobic pocket occupancy, and the DMPO substituent functions as the critical selectivity switch to achieve specific CDK7 inhibition over other CDK family members. Minor structural modifications to these core motifs can lead to dramatic changes in enzymatic potency, cellular efficacy and isoform selectivity, which provides a clear and rational roadmap for the subsequent structural optimisation of this series of inhibitors.

Cellular effects of 7a

As a core catalytic subunit of the transcription initiation factor TFIIH, CDK7 is well established to mediate phosphorylation of the Ser5 residue within the CTD heptapeptide repeats of RNA Pol II5–8. that 7a exhibits potent inhibitory activity against CDK7 in biochemical kinase assays and exerts robust antiproliferative effects in HCT116 colon cancer cells, we next sought to validate these observations at the cellular level, focusing on the functional consequences of 7a on the CDK7-RNA Pol II CTD phosphorylation axis. SY-5609 was included as a positive control. As shown in Figure 5, treatment with either 7a or SY-5609 induced a concentration-dependent reduction in p-Ser5 RNA Pol II levels relative to the DMSO control. These findings provide direct cellular evidence that 7a targets and inhibits CDK7 kinase activity in intact HCT116 cells. In vitro ADME and in vivo pharmacokinetic PK profile of 7a

Given the promising in vitro activity of 7a, we next characterised its in vitro ADME properties and in vivo pharmacokinetic (PK) profile in mice, as summarised in Table 5. 7a exhibited low Caco-2 permeability and a high efflux ratio (ER), indicating that it is a potential substrate of P-glycoprotein (P-gp), an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter that mediates xenobiotic efflux across biological membranes and frequently linked to drug resistance and impaired oral absorption. The low permeability may stem from its suboptimal lipophilicity (cLogD = 0.36), a key physicochemical attribute governing passive transmembrane diffusion.

For metabolic stability, 7a showed favourable in vitro performance in human liver microsomes, as evidenced by moderate intrinsic clearance (CLint = 14.34 mL/min/kg). Following intravenous administration to mice at 1 mg/kg (n = 3), 7a displayed a moderate systemic clearance (Cl = 65.1 mL/min/kg) and reasonable plasma exposure (AUC(0-t) = 251.6 h*ng/mL). In contrast, oral administration of 7a to mice at 5 mg/kg (n = 3) resulted in substantially diminished PK performance: the Cmax decreased to 27 ng/mL, AUC0-ₜ dropped to 30.2 h·ng/mL, and oral bioavailability (F%) was only 2.4%. This poor oral exposure aligns with 7a’s suboptimal lipophilicity and P-gp substrate property (high ER), two well-recognized determinants of limited oral drug absorption.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of 7a in the HCT116 xenograft model

To further characterise the in vivo antitumor efficacy of compound 7a, we evaluated its therapeutic activity in a HCT116 human colorectal cancer xenograft model established in female BALB/c nude mice. Given the low oral bioavailability of 7a, animals were administered 7a via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at doses of 5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg, once daily (QD) for 21 consecutive days. SY-5609, a clinically validated positive control, was administered orally (p.o.) at 2 mg/kg QD over the same treatment period. Tumour volume and body weight were monitored weekly throughout the study to concurrently assess antitumor efficacy and systemic tolerability. As depicted in Figure 6, the vehicle control group was euthanized early, as tumour volumes in these mice exceeded the maximum allowable tumour volume of 2000 mm³, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. This finding reflects unimpeded tumour growth in the absence of treatment. In contrast, compound 7a exhibited robust, dose-dependent antitumor activity against HCT116 xenografts, with tumour growth significantly inhibited in a dose-responsive manner. Notably, the antitumor efficacy of 7a at 5 mg/kg was comparable to that of the positive control SY-5609, underscoring its potent antitumor activity at this dose level. Importantly, no significant body weight changes were observed across treatment groups, indicating a favourable preliminary safety profile of 7a at the tested doses.

Taken together, we have successfully identified an indole/azaindole-fused pyrimidine tetracyclic scaffold as a novel chemotype of highly selective CDK7 inhibitors. These findings demonstrate that 7a, despite its suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties, exhibits potent in vivo antitumor efficacy and thus merits further structural optimisation to improve its pharmacokinetic profile while retaining its CDK7-specific inhibitory activity and antitumor efficacy.

Chemical synthesis

The synthesis of indole/azaindole-fused tetracyclic derivatives were described via the synthetic routes outlined in Schemes 1–3.

Reagents and conditions: (a) AcOH, 145 °C, 3h, 45–87%, or polysphosphoric acid, 160 °C, 3h, 51%; (b) SEMCl, NaH, THF or DMF, 0 °C to r.t., overnight, 79–100%; (c) DDQ, THF or dioxane, H2O, 3h, 55–67%; (d) t-BuOK, ethyl formate, PhMe, 0 °C to r.t., 3h; (e) morpholine, EtOH, 85 °C, 1h, then 19, NaOMe, overnight, 41–91%; (f) dimethylphosphine oxide, Pd(OAc)2, Xantphos, K3PO4, DMF, 145 °C, MW, 45 min; (g) HCl/dioxane, 60 °C, then EDA, THF, 24–65%; (h) 1H-pyrazole-1-carboximidamide hydrochloride, DIPEA, MeCN, r.t., 16h.

As illustrated in Scheme 1, construction of the tetracyclic core scaffold commenced with commercially available indole/azaindole hydrazines (10) and cyclic ketones (11). These substrates underwent the Fischer indole synthesis to afford the corresponding indole/azaindole-fused tricyclic intermediates (12). Subsequent installation of the N-(trimethylsilyl)ethoxymethyl (SEM) protecting group (13) was accomplished under basic conditions (NaH/THF). Regioselective oxidation of the α-carbon adjacent to the carbonyl moiety was then achieved using 2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-benzoquinone (DDQ), smoothly furnishing the ketone derivative (14).

Reagents and conditions: (a) MeMgBr, ZrCl4, THF, −10 °C to r.t.; (b) TFA, DCM; (c) morpholine, EtOH, 85 °C, 1h, then guanidine hydrochloride, NaOMe, overnight; (d) NaNO2, AcOH, H2O; then TEA, Tf2O, DCM, 0 °C; (e) DIPEA, NMR, 90 °C; (f) dimethylphosphine oxide, Pd(OAc)2, Xantphos, K3PO4, DMF, 145 °C, MW, 45 min; (g) HCl/dioxane, 60 °C, then EDA, THF, 19–63%; (h) NaSO2Me, CuI, NMP, 140 °C.

Next, nucleophilic addition of ethyl formate to the α-position of the ketone (promoted by t-BuOK in toluene) afforded the hydroxymethylene intermediate (15), which was directly engaged in condensation with guanidine derivative (19) to construct the pyrimidine ring, thus delivering the key indole/azaindole-fused tetracyclic scaffold (16). The subsequent functionalization of the aromatic bromide moiety was successfully achieved through three distinct catalytic coupling strategies: palladium-catalysed cross-coupling reactions (compounds 17, 9b, 9c-9d, 9 g-9k, 9n-9q), cuprous(I) iodide-mediated coupling (compound 26), and cuprous(I) oxide/salicylaldoxime system-catalysed coupling (compounds 9 l, 9 m, 9r).

Efficient deprotection of the SEM group was achieved via a two-step sequence: treatment with HCl/dioxane at 60 °C resulted in only partial deprotection (typically cleaving the trimethylsilylethyl segment while retaining the hydroxymethyl moiety). Fortunately, concentration of the reaction mixture followed by stirring with ethylenediamine (EDA) enabled smooth removal of the residual hydroxymethyl group, providing the target compounds. For derivatives bearing piperidine (7a, 7d, 8n, 9a-9r)/pyrrolidine (8 m) or cyclobutanol moieties (8i), a tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc) or tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBS) protecting group was occasionally introduced; notably, this Boc and TBS group could be cleaved concomitantly with SEM deprotection.

The synthesis of compounds 7b-7c, 7e, 8i, and 8 m was depicted in Scheme 2. For these compounds, although the pyrimidine ring formation via condensation of the hydroxymethylene intermediate with guanidine derivatives was feasible, this pathway suffered from unsatisfactorily low yields. To address this, the hydroxymethylene intermediate (15a or 15d) was first converted to a pyrimidin-2-amine derivative by reaction with guanidine. Subsequently, the 2-amino group was transformed into a trifluoromethanesulfonate (OTf) leaving group, which then underwent efficient nucleophilic substitution with primary amine derivatives (22–1, 22–2 or 22–3) to afford corresponding intermediates (24, 25). Final target compounds 7b-7c, 7e, 8i, and 8 m were thereafter furnished via sequential cross-coupling and global deprotection steps.

Experimental section

Chemistry

Unless otherwise specified, starting materials and chromatographic solvents were purchased from commercial suppliers and employed without subsequent purification. 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on Bruker AV400 MHz or AV 600 MHz spectrometer, with DMSO-d6 (δ H = 2.50 ppm, δ C = 39.52 ppm), Methanol-d4 (δ H = 3.31 ppm, δ C = 49.00 ppm), or Chloroform-d (δ H = 7.26 ppm, δ C = 77.16 ppm) as deuterated solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. Chemical shifts (δ) were reported in parts per million (ppm) and coupling constants (J) were given in Hz. Signal multiplicities were designated as s (singlet), d (doublet), dd (double doublet), ddd (double of doublet doublet), dt (double triplet), t (triplet), q (quartet), br s (broad singlet), and m (multiplet). Column chromatography was carried out under pressure using commercially available silica gel (200 − 300 mesh). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were recorded on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer. The purity of all final compounds was determined by ultra‑performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) or high‑performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and all were confirmed to have a purity of > 95%.

General procedures for synthesis of compound 12a, 12b, and 12d

The compound 10 (1.00 equiv.) and cycloheptanone (11a, 1.00 equiv.) or 2,2-dimethyl-cyclohexan-1-one (11b, 1.00 equiv.) were dissolved in acetic acid (0.22 M) and refluxed at 145 °C for 2 h. General procedures for synthesis of compound 9n-9q

To a solution of the intermediate 16o (1.00 equiv.), Pd(dppf)Cl2-DCM (0.05 equiv.), NaHCO3 (2.00 equiv.) in degassed dioxane/H2O (V/V = 2/1, 0.1 M) was added corresponding aromatic boric acid or boronic acid pinacol ester (2.00 equiv.) under an argon atmosphere. The reaction mixture was heated to 100 °C and stirred at 100 °C until completed conversion (typically, overnight), and then cooled to room temperature and diluted with EtOAc and filtered by celite pad. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporator to obtain the crude Suzuki product which was directly used in the next step without further purification.

The crude Suzuki product was added HCl-dioxane solution (4 M) and heated to 60 °C for 30 min. Then the solvent was removed by rotary evaporator and the residue was washed by hexane for 3 times. Then the solid was added ethylene diamine in THF (10%) and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporator and the residue was purified by pre-HPLC (ACN/H2O = 10/90 to 90/10) to obtain the target compound 9n-9q.

Cell culture and cell lines

HCT116 and K562 cell lines were obtained from Procell (catalogue numbers CL-0096 and CL-0130, respectively). HCT116 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with L-glutamine, while K562 cells were cultured in IMDM with L-glutamine (both from Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Both media were supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, A5256701) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and were routinely passaged every 2–3 days.

In vitro anti-proliferation assay

HCT116 and K562 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at densities of 1500 and 8000 cells per well, respectively, and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was subsequently replaced with fresh medium containing serial dilutions of test compounds or vehicle control, and cells were cultured for 72 h. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (TargetMol, C0005) was then added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

Kinase inhibition assay

The inhibitory potencies of the tested compounds against CDKs were evaluated using a fluorescence-based enzymatic assay, with detection performed on a Calliper EZ Reader II platform. IC50 values were determined in the presence of ATP at Km concentration and CDK/cyclin partners (Biortus): 116 μM ATP with 0.02 nM CDK1/cyclin A2, 100 μM ATP with 0.015 nM CDK2/cyclin E1, 164 μM ATP with 20 nM CDK6/cyclin D3, 50 μM ATP with 3 nM CDK7/cyclin H/MAT1, 30 μM ATP with 8 nM CDK9/cyclin T1. Fluorescent peptide substrates (Biortus) were used at a final concentration of 2 μM for all assays: CDK7tide was employed as the substrate for CDK1, CDK2, CDK6, and CDK7, while CDK9tide was specifically used for CDK9 assays. The IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism software.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking studies were performed using Schrödinger Suite 2022–3 (Maestro version 13.5). All calculations were conducted with default parameters unless otherwise specified. The crystal structures of CDK7 (PDB ID: 8RGU) and CDK1 (PDB ID: 6GU3) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Protein structures were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro. Receptor grids were generated using the Receptor Grid Generation panel in Maestro. The centroid of the co-crystallized ligand binding site was used to define the grid box centre. Ligands were built in Maestro and prepared using LigPrep. Ionisation states were generated at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 using Epik, and tautomers were generated with default settings. Energy minimisation was performed using the OPLS4 force field. Ligand docking was performed using Glide in Standard Precision (SP) mode with default parameters. The docking score function was set to SP, and the number of poses per ligand was limited to 5. Post-docking minimisation was performed to refine ligand geometries. The top-ranked pose based on GlideScore was selected for subsequent analysis and figure generation.

Western blot assay

HCT116 cells were treated with 7a or SY-5609 at the indicated concentrations for 24 h. Cells were collected, washed with ice-cold PBS (Corning), and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013E). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Pierce/Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Beyotime, FFP24). After blocking with QuickBlock™ reagent (Beyotime, P0252), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RNA Pol II phosphorylated at Ser5 (Abcam, ab5131) and GAPDH (Cell Signaling, ab4787), both diluted 1:1000 in QuickBlock™ primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime, P0256). The membranes were then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling, 7074P2; Anti-rabbit IgG) diluted 1:2000 in QuickBlock™ secondary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime, P0258) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured with a Tanon Gel Imaging System.

In vivo antitumor efficacy assay

Animal experiments were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern University of Science and Technology. Female BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. and acclimatised for 1 week prior to experimentation. Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled conditions (temperature 20–26 °C, relative humidity 40–70%, 12 h light/dark cycle, and 10–20 air changes per hour). Mice were kept in autoclaved micro-isolator cages (maximum 5 per cage) with environmental enrichment (sterilised tunnels, nesting pads, and chew blocks). Sterilised irradiated rodent diet and autoclaved water were provided ad libitum.

For tumour inoculation, HCT116 cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS. A total of 1 × 107 HCT116 cells suspended in 200 μL of PBS were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of each nude mouse. When tumours reached approximately 120–200 mm³, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): vehicle control, 7a low-dose (5 mg/kg), 7a high-dose (20 mg/kg), and positive control (SY-5609, 2 mg/kg). Compounds were dissolved in 5% DMSO/saline (v/v). 7a was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) once daily for 21 consecutive days, whereas SY-5609 was given orally (p.o.) at the same frequency. The vehicle group received 5% DMSO/saline (i.p.) on the identical schedule. Tumour dimensions were measured using a Vernier calliper, and volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.5 × length × width2. At the end of the study, mice were anaesthetised with carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation followed by humane euthanasia via cervical dislocation, in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the institutional animal welfare regulations, to minimise animal suffering.
