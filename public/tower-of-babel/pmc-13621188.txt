Bombarded? Child’s-eye views of smartphone-based game application advertising: objective insights from Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Marcus Gurtner, Ryan Gage, Moira Smith, Leah Watkins, James Stanley, Louise Signal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren’s engagement with smartphone-based (online) game applications is commonplace. There appears to be no objective evidence base for children’s exposure to advertising within game-apps captured in real-time by children themselves. This study aimed to objectively explore the nature and extent of young adolescents’ exposure to smartphone game-app-based advertising. Sixty-six children aged 11–13 years (mean age = 12.7, SD = 0.79) from 16 schools in the Wellington region of New Zealand used Zoom video teleconferencing software to record real-time, screen-shared internet use for four consecutive days. Forty-one participants recorded game-app use which was analysed for in-game advertising. Participants responded to a single catch-all question inquiring about changes they would make to the online world. Responses mentioning advertising were analysed. On average, children who used game-apps recorded 206 minutes (3.4 hours) of online smartphone use over the four-day study period and spent 39 minutes using game-apps (11.4 minutes/hour, or 19% of their total online time). Per hour spent gaming, on average, participants were exposed to advertising for 9.7 minutes (16.2% of their hourly gaming time) and screen-recorded 22.9 ads. Primarily, ads for other smartphone game applications were advertised within the games (n = 263, 38.2% of all recorded ads). Most other advertising appeared more likely to be of interest, or more suited to adult consumers (e.g. finance and real estate). While ‘playing’ using smartphone game-apps, children were exposed to a high volume of advertising, including advertising for alcohol. Unprompted, one-third (n = 22) of participants mentioned online advertising within their responses and indicated that they wanted to see changes made to online advertising. All responses portrayed online advertising negatively. Children, who are some of the most susceptible to digital advertising, must be protected from advertising online in accordance with the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child.

Introduction

The global video-gaming industry generates more revenue than the movie and music industries combined (Arora 2024). In 2024, the global gaming market was forecast to generate $455 billion USD (Arora 2024) and accommodate 3.42 billion players worldwide (Newzoo 2024). An estimated $98.7 billion USD was generated by the smartphone gaming market, roughly one-fifth (21.7%) of total gaming market revenue (Statista 2024a, 2024b). Global digital advertising expenditure was expected to reach an all-time high of 694 billion USD in 2024, accounting for around 70% of total global ad investments (Statista 2024a, 2024b).

Although advertising has been included within digital games for decades (Baltezarević and Baltezarević 2024), novel hybrid technologies or products have enabled advertisers to target, reach, and communicate with consumers with increasing precision, efficiency and rapidity (Lee and Cho 2020, Baltezarević and Baltezarević 2024). Children constitute a key segment of the online advertising industry and are targeted as primary consumers (Kircher and Foerderer 2023).

Increasing internet and smartphone use have accelerated the monumental growth in digital media consumption and digital advertising globally (Truong et al. 2010, UNICEF 2017). Smartphones are the most commonly used, largest and fastest growing video-gaming platform worldwide (Newzoo 2021), and afford advertisers round-the-clock access to consumers (Lee and Cho 2020). Smartphone-based game-app advertising is commonplace (Baltezarević and Baltezarević 2024, Gurtner et al. 2025), a major constituent of digital advertising, and a key component driving the ever-increasing commercialization of childhood (Linn 2010).

Smartphone-based game-apps are predominantly based on the ‘Freemium’ business model, ‘whereby basic services are provided free of charge while more advanced features must be paid for’ (Oxford Languages 2024a, 2024b). Such game-apps are referred to as ‘F2P’ (free-to-play) games. Most F2P games include ‘in-game’ advertising, referring to ‘the placement of advertisers’ brands in a commercial game to promote their products or services’(Youn and Lee 2012:388). Previous research highlights that children have negative views of F2P in-game advertising given that it interrupts their gaming activities (Martínez 2017).

‘Advergames’ are F2P games that include advertising (Eyice Başev 2024), but, in the current literature, have been distinguished from other F2P games. An advergame typically refers to a F2P game developed to promote one brand or product, and within which, the ‘embedded brand or product is a central part of the game’ (Youn and Lee 2012:388). This differs to F2P games that include in-game advertising for multiple brands or products. In this study, we consider all smartphone-based game-apps that include in-game advertising as advergames. Henceforth, all reference to advergames uses this definition.

Advergames are part and parcel of the largely unregulated internet-based world (Stoilova et al. 2021) and are easily accessible to children. Advergames are highly commercialized digital contexts (Gurtner et al. 2025) combining elements of ‘entertainment, advertising, and gaming’ (Eyice Başev 2024:260), in order to ‘generate positive emotions among consumers and stimulate increased interest in products and services’ (Eyice Başev 2024:260). Within advergame contexts, advertisers endeavour to ‘enlist a new generation of loyal customers’ (Opdyke 2025:4), specifically targeting children and adolescents (Sharma 2020, Christakis and Hale 2025). Recent research found that pre-school-aged (3–5 years) children play advergames using smartphones (Radesky et al. 2022).

While younger children (≤7 years) are most susceptible to advertising owing to ‘immature critical thinking skills and impulse inhibition’ (Radesky et al. 2020:1), 7–11 year-olds still ‘lack the abstract thinking skills that help individuals recognize advertising as a larger commercial concept’ (Radesky et al. 2020:2). Even 15 year-olds have been shown to be unable to accurately identify traditional sole product advergames as ads themselves (Hang and Nairn 2014). In-game ads are included in advergame game-apps by default, to benefit both advertisers and game developers. First, to increase the likelihood that ads reach players (Baltezarević and Baltezarević 2024) (who are predominantly children; Newzoo 2022), and second, to entice players to pay to remove ads (Baltezarević and Baltezarević 2024).

Evidence suggests that exposure to digital food marketing, including via advergames, has similar effects as ‘traditional TV advertising exposure does on young people’s diets, including food choices, preferences, purchase requests, and intake of marketed and other available foods’ (Christakis and Hale 2025:82). This holds true for exposures to marketing for other unhealthy commodities, behaviours and ideologies, such as: use of tobacco products and electronic cigarettes; use of alcohol and other drugs; indoor tanning; and culturally-biased ideals related to body appearance, weight and skin colour (Radesky et al. 2020). There is considerable evidence that exposure to alcohol-related content within internet-based social networking sites (such as TikTok, Facebook, and Instagram) (Cheng et al. 2024) and other (digital) media contexts (de Bruijn et al. 2016, Pettigrew et al. 2020), encourages alcohol consumption, predicts higher use over time (Brunborg et al. 2022), and contributes to harmful drinking behaviours among children and youth (10 to ≤24 years) (Nagata et al. 2023, Cheng et al. 2024).

In highly industrialized countries, considerable time is spent both using smartphones and gaming. For example, American children aged 8–17 years spend an average of 4.5 hours per day using smartphones (with some spending up to 16 hours) (Radesky et al. 2023) and are estimated to spend 1–2 hours per day gaming (Alanko 2023). How much of this gaming time is smartphone-based has largely remained unclear. But previous research by Gurtner et al. (2025), who analysed the same participants’ (n = 41, 11–13 years) screen-recorded data examined by this study, found that on average, nearly one-fifth (18%) of students’ overall online smartphone time involved game-app usage (Gurtner et al. 2025).

While playing (online) games can support children’s relationships with peers and help to alleviate stress and anxiety (Alanko 2023), time spent gaming equates to increased screen time. Children’s high screen time has been linked, unequivocally, to adiposity, depressive symptomatology and unhealthy eating habits (Fang et al. 2019, Stiglic and Viner 2019, Tang et al. 2021). Gaming time is typically passed while sedentary, displacing physical activity (such as outside play) and other health promoting behaviours (such as face-to-face interactions with friends). Physical inactivity is widely acknowledged as a leading risk factor for non-communicable diseases such as obesity, and is detrimental to mental health (Guthold et al. 2018).

The WHO–UNICEF–Lancet Commission highlights exploitative advertising as a major threat to children (Clark et al. 2020). The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) is international law that protects the rights of children (UN General Assembly 1989). In 2021, the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child issued their ‘General comment No. 25 (2021) on children’s rights in relation to the digital environment’ (UN Committee on the Rights of the Child 2021). The general comment emphasizes that children's rights must be respected, protected and fulfilled in the digital environment, and states; ‘advertising in virtual and augmented reality environments to promote products, applications, and services should also be prohibited from engagement directly or indirectly with children’ (UN Committee on the Rights of the Child 2021:7). Moreover, there is mounting concern about the widespread use of commercial ‘dark patterns’ (manipulative design features) within smartphone apps, including children’s apps (Federal Trade Commission 2022).

Dark patterns are design practices employed within online user interfaces to ‘steer, deceive, coerce, or manipulate consumers into making choices that often are not in their best interests’ (OECD 2022:2). Dark patterns are ubiquitous within smartphone game-app environments, where they are deployed in tandem, or tangentially to in-game advertising (Gurtner et al. 2025). For example, some dark patterned ads exist as interactive buttons embedded within game environments. Players are enticed to click these buttons (which then trigger ads to display onscreen) because in-game rewards are offered in exchange. Rewards have in-game value, insofar as they afford in-game opportunities, such as ‘extra chances or extra lives’ (Martínez 2017:857), provide in-game currency or permit player progression to higher levels (Martínez 2017). Alongside game-app advertising, dark patterns encourage users to spend money and/or time within game-app contexts in order to increase business revenue (OECD 2022:8). As a user’s advergame screen time increases, their likelihood of being exposed to harmful commodity advertising increases.

Despite young adolescents—generations ‘Alpha’ (born 2010 and onwards) and ‘Z’ (born 1995–2010)—being most engaged with smartphone advergames globally (Newzoo 2022), there is a dearth of objective evidence on their exposure to advertising therein. Existing research has investigated various aspects of children’s and young adolescents’ exposure to digital advertising, but most evidence originates from self-report data (Fretes et al. 2025), is based on observations made in controlled experimental conditions (McGlinchy et al. 2025), and has been researcher-centred (scanning websites/social media pages/YouTube videos and so on) (Fretes et al. 2025). Experts have argued that there is an ‘urgent need for child-centred research methods’ (Stoilova et al. 2021:11) aimed at exploring all aspects of children’s online lives.

Accordingly, this study uses objective screen-share recording data, captured by young adolescent participants in the ‘Kids Online Aotearoa’ (KOA) study (Gurtner et al. 2022), to explore the nature and extent of smartphone-based advergame advertising. We also report findings from an analysis of participant responses to a catch-all question included in the KOA study (Gurtner et al. 2022), which inquired about action(s) participants would take to improve their online world.

Materials and methods

This paper is a secondary analysis of the dataset used in recent research on children’s smartphone-based gaming content (Gurtner et al. 2025), in which the authors used screen-shared Zoom recordings, self-captured by a sample of New Zealand young adolescents, to objectively investigate smartphone-based gaming content. The screen-shared Zoom recordings originated from the KOA study (2021–24), a cross-sectional observational study of Year 8 students’ real-time experiences of the internet-based world (Gurtner et al. 2022). Both studies are briefly described below. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Otago Human Ethics Committee (Health; 20/006) for both studies and likewise, for this study.

Method for KOA

Year 8 students (aged 11–13 years; n = 154) from 16 schools in New Zealand’s Wellington region screen-recorded their internet-based activity for four consecutive days (Thursday to Sunday) on up to three screen devices (smartphones, laptops or Chromebooks, PCs) using Zoom video teleconferencing software. Students responded to a catch-all question prior to data collection. The sample was stratified by deprivation, ethnicity and sex. Further information on KOA methods have been published elsewhere (Gurtner et al. 2022).

Method for KOA gaming

When this study was conducted (i.e. prior to final recruitment), 66 KOA participants had recorded smartphone-based internet activity. All recordings were screened for ‘gaming content’, defined by the authors as; ‘all instances of clearly displayed textual/visual representations of, or references to gaming’ (Gurtner et al. 2025:3). Forty-one (64.1%) participants used 63 different game-apps, most of which were advergames (85.7%). Each instance of participants’ game-app use was coded for duration. Each ad; ‘a notice or announcement in a public medium promoting a product, service, or event or publicizing a job vacancy’ (Oxford Languages 2024a, 2024b), appearing within periods of game-app use, was coded for duration and assigned one of two labels, either ‘Game’ or ‘Other’, to reflect whether a game-app or something else was being advertised. Labels included descriptions based on what was clearly visible onscreen, such as brand/game names. Further information on the study methodology can be found elsewhere (Gurtner et al. 2025).

Method for KOA smartphone game-app advertising

Cross checking of pre-coded ads and assignment to ad categories

In this study, we analysed all previously coded advertising (coded as part of the aforementioned ‘KOA Gaming’ study) appearing during periods of game-app usage, for each of the 41 participants who had used smartphone-based game-apps. The first author (M.G.) manually verified all pre-coded ‘Game’ and ‘Other’ labels were accurate and classified all ‘Other’ labels using a broad classification schedule (comprising 39 advertising categories) informed by a previous coding protocol (Watkins et al. 2022), for example: ‘Finance’; ‘Online Service Provider’; ‘Food’; or ‘Alcohol’ (see Supplementary Material #S1). All authors reviewed and approved the final data prior to analysis.

KOA catch-all question

All 66 KOA gaming study participants responded to the following catch-all question administered prior to data collection; ‘Imagine that you are the Prime Minister for a day. This is your opportunity to make changes to the online world. What would you change about the online world, and why?’ (Gurtner et al. 2022). Participants were not prompted about advertising before responding. Advertising emerged as a central theme upon reviewing participants’ responses, and as such, was of relevance to this paper, and the responses from the 66 KOA smartphone gaming study participants were subsequently analysed. M.G. manually scanned responses, tallying all those mentioning advertising, and tallying those specifically mentioning game-app-based advertising.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Stata/18 (Statacorp, TX). Durations of exposure to smartphone-based game-app advertising were collated by child for the four-day observation period. Negative binomial regression models were used to calculate children’s average exposure to advertising, and time spent exposed to advertising per hour of gaming time (using game-app usage time as an exposure offset). Results are reported by sex (Salmond et al. 2006).

Results

Sample characteristics

Twenty-five participants (25/66, 37.9%) recorded online smartphone use during the KOA study (Gurtner et al. 2022) but did not record any game-app usage, and so were excluded from analysis. This left 41 participants who recorded game-app use. Of these, 23 were females (56%), 16 males (39%), and two participants identified as non-binary (5%). Mean age was 12.7 years (SD = 0.79). Almost three-quarters (73%) of participants were from low-deprivation backgrounds (n = 30; NZiDep scores of 1 or 2) (Salmond et al. 2006) and of non-Māori non-Pacific (NMNP) ethnicity (n = 31, 76%). Eight participants were Māori (20%) and two were Pacific (5%).

Participants’ screen-recorded smartphone usage and exposure to advergame-based advertising

On average, participants who used game-apps screen-recorded 206 minutes of online smartphone usage across the four-day data collection period. Within this time, an average of 39.2 minutes was spent using game-apps (19% of their total online smartphone time), equating to 11.4 minutes of game-app use per hour spent online. There were no observed differences by sex.

Per hour spent gaming, on average, participants were exposed to in-game advertising for 9.7 minutes (16.2% of their hourly gaming time) and screen-recorded 22.9 ads. For each average, there was no observed difference by sex. Despite male participants recording less in-game advertising than female participants (unadjusted IRR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.18–2.99), this difference was not significant. Overall, 21 (51%) participants screen-recorded in-game advertising within 35/54 (64.8%) distinct advergame game-apps.

Twenty participants (49%) did not screen-record any advertising. Of these 20 participants, 12 screen-recorded use of at least one advergame, whereas 8 solely recorded using non-advergame game-apps. These 20 participants played 21 distinct games (33.3% of total games played), 12 of which (57.1%) were not advergames (those including advertising), such as: ‘Clash of Clans’; ‘Clash Royale’; ‘Minecraft’; and ‘Roblox’. No statistically significant difference was observed for this group by sex.

Participants’ screen-recorded advergame usage

Overall, participants used 63 discrete game-apps, 62 (98.4%) of which were F2P from the Google Play web portal. Fifty-four (85.7%) game-apps were advergames, such as: ‘Tricky Taps’; ‘Fork N Sausage’; ‘Subway Surfers’; and ‘Tomb of the Mask’. In total, 32 (78%) participants used advergames, of which: 19 recorded their use of one sole advergame (59.4%); six recorded using two distinct advergames (18.8%); and 7 recorded their use of 3 or more different advergames (21.9%). Across all participants, nearly one-third (31.7%) screen-recorded their use of multiple advergames. There was no observed difference in terms of advergame use by sex.

Non-advergame game-apps—general insights

Non-advergame game-apps did not include advertising. But comparatively, non-advergames’ in-game contexts appeared to be more heavily monetized, based on our observations of the examples evidenced within participants’ recordings. Non-advergame game-apps seemed to include more microtransactions, which themselves: appeared more frequently within more commercially intended in-game contexts; involved higher purchase prices; and which we perceived to be, linked more intrinsically to users’ in-game progression, status, or competitive advantage. Additionally, the non-advergame game-apps seemed to include higher densities of dark patterns (manipulative design features) within their in-game commercial contexts. Again, this was not analysed independently.

In total, 133 different game-apps were advertised, of which: 114 (85.7%) were advergames (including game genres such as: racing; shooter; turn-based strategy; fantasy; simulation; and player-versus-player); 11 were not (8.3%); and for eight (6%), there was no information on the Google Play web storefront. Participant responses to KOA’s catch-all ‘prime minister for a day’ question

Overall, 22 (33.3%) participants mentioned advertising when responding to the catch-all KOA ‘Prime Minister for a Day’ question. Generally, participants called for online ads to be removed, banned and/or shortened. Comments included: ‘I would get rid of all useless, annoying and scamming ads, from online and offline apps and websites’ (female); ‘I would remove ads and make them illegal, because no one likes ads’ (male); ‘I would make all ads five seconds or shorter. After 5 seconds you should be able to skip, otherwise it takes too long. It's time consuming’ (female); and ‘I would remove pop-up ads, which are sometimes completely inappropriate. When you try to close them, they don't go away’ (female).

Three participants specifically mentioned game-app-based advertising within their responses: ‘Get rid of gambling ads in kids’ games’ (female); ‘No ads in free games’ (male); and ‘I would make games ad-free’ (male).

Discussion

In this objective study exploring smartphone-based game-app advertising, 62% of participants used game-apps and on average within this group, used them for nearly one-fifth (19%) of the total time they spent using smartphones (11.4 minutes per recorded hour). On average, these participants were exposed to advertising for nearly a fifth (16.2%) of their hourly gaming time (9.7 minutes per recorded hour) and screen-recorded 22.9 ads. Overall, there were no observed differences by sex.

Previous research has estimated that 8–17 year-olds in the USA spend between 1 and 2 hours per day playing video games (using multiple devices, including smartphones) (Alanko 2023). Considering our exposure finding of 22.9 ads per hour spent gaming, alongside the upper limit of the estimation of 1–2 hours per day playing video games, as proxies, children in highly industrialized countries may see around 46 ads per day while playing within smartphone game-app environments.

Our study participants predominantly used advergame game-apps. Nearly, one-third of participants who used advergames, screen-recorded use of multiple advergames. Despite using game-apps, nearly half of our game-playing participants did not screen-record advertising. There are several possible explanations for this. These participants included those who had either used non-advergame game-apps (i.e. ad-free game-apps that typically require purchasing); had paid to remove ads from the advergame-app(s) they used; opened advergame-apps for very short durations—too short for ads to display; may have used an ad-blocker or enabled ‘Flight’ mode (which can prevent ads from displaying); or solely navigated ‘non-gameplay’ portions of the advergame, that is, ‘menu screens, settings menus, character customization screens, and commercially intended/shop-like screens’ (Gurtner et al. 2025:3)—game-app contexts where advertising does not typically display.

The non-advergame game-apps appeared to be more detailed in terms of their game design (graphics) and gameplay (game mechanics). These game-apps appeared more tailored towards ‘core’ gamers, defined as ‘those who agree that gaming is an important part of their life’ (Newzoo 2017), as opposed to ‘time filler’ gamers, who play games more casually to pass the time, and who typically use game-apps, which are more rudimentary in terms of their game design and gameplay. While the non-advergames did not expose participants to ads, they appeared to be more heavily monetized and included many dark patterns (manipulative design features) within their application contexts.

Overall, just over half of our study’s game-app users screen-recorded in-game ads. Nearly two-fifths of the total ads advertised other smartphone game-apps, predominantly other advergames. As such, children are likely to be enticed to download and install other advertised advergames, which in turn, exposes them to further advertising. In addition to advertising, advergames frequently expose children to other highly commercialized in-app contexts (such as in-game shops), dark patterns (manipulative design features) and adult themes (such as violence and/or sexual content) (Gurtner et al. 2025).

Smartphone game-apps are an obvious medium for targeting children, as they primarily use smartphones, use them frequently, and often play games as a main activity (Kircher and Foerderer 2023). However, overall, most evidenced ads appeared most likely to be of interest, or more suited to adult consumers (e.g. ‘Finance’ and ‘Home appliances’).

But advertising generic/diverse products and services to children increases their awareness of those products and services. Children are an important consumer segment as direct buyers (current consumers) and future (potential) consumers (Khan et al. 2021, Arora and Diwan 2022). Moreover, increasing children’s awareness of products/services does not solely modulate their own likelihood of purchasing products/engaging services. For example, children (aged 2–14 years) exert tremendous influence over familial buying patterns across diverse product categories (Arora and Diwan 2022), such as travel choices, meal selections and car purchases (Calvert 2008, Khan et al. 2021).

Alarmingly, ads for alcohol-related products were screen-recorded. Youth exposure to alcohol advertising within digital media is widely acknowledged to increase their likelihood of consumption (Nagata et al. 2023, Cheng et al. 2024). One sole ‘Social marketing messages’ ad (‘Unite against COVID-19’—issued by the New Zealand government) was screen-recorded. While protective/healthy advertising may appear within advergame contexts, this was infrequent in this study. But advergames could indeed be harnessed as dissemination channels to communicate healthy messaging to children.

Unprompted, one-third of participants mentioned advertising when responding to the catch-all KOA question: ‘Imagine that you are the Prime Minister for a day. This is your opportunity to make changes to the online world. What would you change about the online world, and why?’ (Gurtner et al. 2022). Those participants all portrayed advertising negatively.

Our participants’ responses echoed sentiments reported by (Martínez 2017), who conducted group interviews with 46 children (9- or 12-years) to explore the umbrella topic of online advertising. In both studies, online advertising was generally emphasized as being ‘annoying’. In relation to smartphone-based game-app advertising specifically, Martínez (2017) highlighted that children found it ‘annoying, tiresome and hard to cope with’ (Martínez 2017:853), and caused them to miss ‘many seconds and minutes’ of gameplay (Martínez 2017:853). A small proportion of this study’s participants specifically mentioned game-app advertising in their responses to the catch-all KOA question. One called for gambling ads to be removed from children’s games, highlighting that these children do encounter harmful advertising within advergame environments.

Despite their annoyance, children still use advergames because they want to ‘play’, but are typically unable to pay to remove ads from advergames, or to purchase pay-to-play game-apps devoid of ads (Martínez 2017). Given that children ‘have little money in their pockets’ (Kircher and Foerderer 2023:1071) and are unlikely to possess the means to purchase online (such as bank cards—typically available to 13+ year olds in New Zealand), they are essentially forced to endure game-app-based advertising.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first globally to use objective screen-share data to explore the nature and extent of the smartphone-based game-app advertising children encounter. Our sample size was small and predominantly comprised NMNP and low-deprivation participants. As such, we were unable to do comparative analysis by ethnicity or socioeconomic status, key to identifying inequities in terms of exposure. Likewise, a larger sample would facilitate determination of any differences by sex.

Smartphones can experience compatibility/functionality issues while operating Zoom and/or may interfere with normal operation of game-apps while Zoom’s screen-recording feature is used simultaneously. Participants may have simply forgotten to screen-record their smartphone use or may have altered their smartphone use while screen-recording. As such, participants may have been exposed to more in-game advertising than evidenced by their recordings. Additionally, given that participants were predominantly from low=deprivation backgrounds, their families may include caregivers who are more likely to play active roles in their children’s use of technology, such as by limiting screen time, which in turn, may have reduced their time spent using advergames—and thus, their likelihood of being exposed to in-advergame advertising.

Given we did not prompt participants about advertising prior to them responding to the KOA catch-all question, their answers are indicative of their attitudes towards online advertising. While a small study from Aotearoa (New Zealand), of predominantly urban-dwelling children, this research reports on a key demographic for which there is a dearth of objective evidence globally. Moreover, it addresses the ‘urgent’ need for participatory, child-centred research methods (Kickbusch et al. 2021, Stoilova et al. 2021). Given the largely homogenized nature of the online world, our findings are likely to be of interest to other nations.

Implications

This study shows that these children frequently encountered advertising while using smartphone-based game-apps, which are themselves, part and parcel of the ever-increasing commercialized and manipulative internet-based world.

Participants spent nearly a fifth of their total recorded online time on smartphones using game-apps. On average, per hour spent ‘playing’ within these virtual environments, they were exposed to 9.7 minutes of advertising and saw 22.9 ads. This finding is in stark contrast to the call by the United Nations to prohibit, by law, the practice of advertising to children within ‘virtual and augmented reality environments to promote products, applications and services’ (UN Committee on the Rights of the Child 2021:7). While most susceptible to advertising, and some of the most vulnerable to the deleterious health effects of digital advertising (Radesky et al. 2020), children are the least likely to be able to pay to avoid it (Martínez 2017).

We found that most screen-recorded advertising was for advergames. This likely entices children to play other advergames, resulting in further exposure to advertising and other content that may be considered harmful (Gurtner et al. 2025). While playing non-advergames may be protective against exposure to in-game advertising, non-advergames appear to be more heavily commercialized and include many dark patterns (manipulative design features). This emphasizes the need for further scrutiny of the relationship between the commercial and digital determinants of health (Kickbusch and Holly 2023) and highlights the importance of ‘enfranchising young people to understand and tackle the digital determinants of health’ (Kickbusch and Holly 2023:2).

Further research

Further investigations could explore the nature of distinct advert types deployed within smartphone game-apps, such as ‘banner’, ‘pop-up’, or ‘playable’ ads, and the extent to which they embed dark patterns (manipulative design features). Objective research exploring dark patterns within smartphone-based game-apps appears to be a research gap. Moreover, studies focused on the ‘behind-the-scenes’ functioning of ad-networks, in terms of how ad-networks disseminate unhealthy commodity advertising to children, are critically urgent. Further research could consider employing bespoke screen-recording software to maximize retrieval of children’s smartphone use. Video or photo elicitation, using data such as that reported in our study, could be used to further encourage children’s engagement and evoke reflection (Thomas et al. 2024). Other researchers could repeat this study using a larger and more diverse sample to permit comparative analyses by socioeconomic status, ethnicity and other socioeconomic factors. Additionally, other areas of the internet-based world could be explored for advertising, particularly advertising for harmful commodities.

Conclusion

Children frequently use smartphone-based game-apps to ‘play’, but much of this playtime involves frequent exposure to advertising. Healthy public policy is urgently needed globally, to ensure that game-apps are supportive environments for all, but particularly for children, who are most vulnerable to harm (Kickbusch and Holly 2023), and who deserve to be protected from such harm in accordance with the UNCRC (UN General Assembly 1989). Digital advertising within game-app contexts encroaches on the right of the child to play, and moreover, to carefree and unadulterated play.
