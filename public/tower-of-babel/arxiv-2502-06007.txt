Transformers versus the EM Algorithm in Multi-class Clustering
Yihan He, Hong-Yu Chen, Yuan Cao, Jianqing Fan, Han Liu

Abstract
LLMs demonstrate significant inference capacities in complicated machine learning tasks, using the Transformer model as its backbone. Motivated by the limited understanding of such models on the unsupervised learning problems, we study the learning guarantees of Transformers in performing multi-class clustering of the Gaussian Mixture Models. We develop a theory drawing strong connections between the Softmax Attention layers and the workflow of the EM algorithm on clustering the mixture of Gaussians. Our theory provides approximation bounds for the Expectation and Maximization steps by proving the universal approximation abilities of multivariate mappings by Softmax functions. In addition to the approximation guarantees, we also show that with a sufficient number of pre-training samples and an initialization, Transformers can achieve the minimax optimal rate for the problem considered. Our extensive simulations empirically verified our theory by revealing the strong learning capacities of Transformers even beyond the assumptions in the theory, shedding light on the powerful inference capacities of LLMs.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated significant success in learning and performing inference on real world high dimensional datasets. Most modern LLMs use the Transformer model
(
Vaswani, 2017
)
as their backbone.

Many existing works have considered the theoretical guarantees of the in-context-learning setup of Transformers
(
Bai et al., 2024
;
Akyürek et al., 2022
)
. However, in practice, LLMs require a significant amount of pretraining data to achieve their empirical advantage. And, little is known about the unsupervised learning guarantees of Transformers, especially after a sufficient number of problem instances are observed by the Transformer model in the pre-training phase. Motivated by the strong empirical performance of Transformers, we provide theoretical analysis of the Transformers on a standard unsupervised learning problem of clustering a mixture of Gaussians in the multi-class setup. Our results suggest that Transformers, like human brains, can benefit from experienced problem instances and learn the way to solve the problem (algorithms). Then, when fed with a new problem instance, Transformers can solve it through the learned algorithms naturally.

The problem of clustering a mixture of multivariate Gaussian is one of the most standard unsupervised learning problems
(
Bishop & Nasrabadi, 2006
)
that can be solved by the EM algorithm or Lloyd’s algorithm
(
Lloyd, 1982
)
. The EM algorithm contains both the
Expectation
and the
Maximization
sub-procedures where the
Expectation Step
creates a function for
the expectation
of the log-likelihood evaluated using the current estimate for the parameters and the
Maximization Step
computes parameters maximizing the expected log-likelihood given by the
Expectation Step
. We draw connections between the Softmax Attention in Transformers and the EM algorithms through the following:

1.

The Expectation Step
involves a normalized sum in
the expectation
whose weight vector is naturally given by the output of the softmax function as

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\exp(z_{1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\exp(z_{i})},\ldots,\frac{\exp(z_{D})}{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\exp(z_{i})}\end{bmatrix}
.

2.

The Maximization Step
involves finding the index with the maximum value in a vector (the Hardmax Function). This is naturally approximated by the Softmax function as its name suggests.

Given the strong connections of the two steps to the Softmax function, we build an approximation theory for Lloyd’s algorithm in a constructive manner. We also note that existing works only build approximation bound for multihead ReLU neural networks
(
Bach, 2017
)
while the Softmax approximation of multivariate to multivariate mapping remains a myth. We resolve this obstacle by proving an approximation bound for multi-head Transformers on a class of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
mappings that might be of independent interests.

Contributions.

We summarize our major contributions as follows:

1.

We rigorously show that a pre-trained Transformer can perform multi-class clustering by drawing its connection to Lloyd’s algorithm, which is used as a proof machine. We provide constructive proof and error bound for the approximation;

2.

We further consider the setup where the Transformer model is trained with independent instances from a class of clustering problems whose labels are used as supervision. We show that Transformers are able to generalize the mapping on new clustering problem instances. We provide upper bounds on the generalization error for the empirical risk minimizer in the pre-training task. Moreover, we show that given a sufficient number of training instances and proper initialization, pre-trained Transformers reach the fundamental limit of the problem;

3.

We systematically evaluate the performance of Transformers through extensive simulations. These empirical results demonstrate that Transformers perform well in the multi-class clustering task even when the assumptions leading to the theoretical results no longer hold.

Related Works

Transformers are algorithm approximators.

Recently, the capacity of Transformers to automatically performing certain algorithms has drawn great attention from researchers. In particular, a rich line of recent works studied the expressive power of Transformers to perform in-context learning (ICL)
(
Akyürek et al., 2022
;
Bai et al., 2024
;
Abernethy et al., 2024

Akyürek et al. (2022)
;
Bai et al. (2024)
;
Abernethy et al. (2024)
studied how Transformers perform gradient descent based training to perform ordinary or sparse linear regression on the context.
Chen et al. (2024b)
further showed how Transformers utilize the multi-head structure to perform in-context sparse linear regression.
Li et al. (2023a)
studied the generalization and stability of transformers in ICL tasks.
Jeon et al. (2024)
studies the information-theoretical lower bound of in-context learning. Another closely related line of works studied how Transformers can be pretrained by gradient descent to perform certain tasks. Specifically,
Zhang et al. (2023)
;
Huang et al. (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2024a)
studied the pretraining optimization dynamics of Transformers to learn in-context linear prediction rules.
Li et al. (2024b)
showed that one-layer Transformers can be trained to perform one-nearest neighbor classification in context.
Ahn et al. (2024)
;
Giannou et al. (2024)
studied the training of Transformers in learning various optimization methods.
Li et al. (2023b)
studied how Transformers can be trained to learn topic models.
Jelassi et al. (2022)
proved that Vision Transformers can learn a class of image-like data whose patches follow certain spatial structures.
Zhang & Cao (2025)
studied how Transformers can learn to perform variable selection in “group-sparse” linear regression.

Other theoretical studies on Transformers.

Various efforts have been made to gain a theoretical understanding of Transformers.
Yun et al. (2019)
analyzed the universal approximation properties of Transformers for sequence-to-sequence functions.
Li et al. (2023b)
studied the mean-filed limit of large-scale Transformers and proved global convergence in regression tasks.

Pérez et al. (2021)
showed that Transformers with hard-attention are Turing complete exclusively based on their capacity to compute and access internal dense representations of the data.
Bhattamishra et al. (2020)

further provided an alternate and simpler proof to show that vanilla Transformers are Turing-complete, and then proved that Transformers with only positional masking and without any positional encoding are also Turing-complete.
Liu et al. (2022)
showed that a low-depth Transformer can represent the computations of any finite-state automaton by hierarchically reparameterizing its recurrent dynamics.
Yao et al. (2021)
demonstrated that Transformers can efficiently process bounded hierarchical languages, offering better space complexity compared to recurrent neural networks.

2.1
The Transformer Architecture

We consider the Softmax Attention Layer, which is defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Softmax Attention)
.

The Softmax Attention layer is defined as a self-attention layer with
M
heads denoted as
Attn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}(\cdot)
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}=\{(\boldsymbol{V}_{m},\boldsymbol{Q}_{m},\boldsymbol{K}_{m})\}_{m\in[M]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. On input sequence
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
,

\displaystyle Attn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{H})=\boldsymbol{H}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{V}_{m}\boldsymbol{H})\text{SoftMax}\Big((\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\boldsymbol{H})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{K}_{m}\boldsymbol{H})\Big),

where
SoftMax
is the activation function defined by

\displaystyle\text{SoftMax}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\exp(x_{1})}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\exp(x_{i})}&\ldots&\frac{\exp(x_{d})}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\exp(x_{d})}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In addition to the Softmax Attention layer, we also consider an un-normalized Attention layer, given by

Definition 2.2

(Un-normalized Attention)
.

The un-normalized Attention layer is defined as a self-attention layer with
M
heads and denoted as
nAttn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}(\cdot)
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}=\{(\boldsymbol{V}_{m},\boldsymbol{Q}_{m},\boldsymbol{K}_{m})\}_{m\in[M]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. On input sequence
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
,

\displaystyle nAttn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{H})=\boldsymbol{H}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\boldsymbol{V}_{m}\boldsymbol{H})(\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}\boldsymbol{H})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{K}_{m}\boldsymbol{H}).

Remark 1
.

The un-normalized Attention layer is the Attention layer without the non-linear activation function. This layer is studied mainly for technical reasons. We also provide results not using the un-normalized Attention layer, despite having weaker rates.

The following defines the classical Fully-Connected (FC) layers with residual connections.

Definition 2.3

(FC Layer)
.

A FC layer with hidden dimension
D^{\prime}
is denoted as
FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)
with parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\in(\boldsymbol{W}_{1},\boldsymbol{W}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}\times D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D\times D^{\prime}}
. On any input sequence
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
, we define

FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}}(\boldsymbol{H}):=\boldsymbol{H}+\boldsymbol{W}_{2}\sigma(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}\boldsymbol{H}).

Then, we use the above definitions on the FC and the Attn/nAttn layers to define the Transformer model and the Transformer+ model.

Definition 2.4

(Transformer)
.

We define the Transformer
TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)
as a composition of the self-attention layers with the FC layers. Consider the output dimension to be
\tilde{D}
, a
L
-layered Transformer is defined by

\displaystyle TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{H}):=

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0}\times FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}^{L}}(Attn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{L}}(\cdots FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}^{1}}(Attn_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{1}}(\boldsymbol{H})))\times\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1},

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times D}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{2}}
.

The two additional matrices
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}
serve for the dimension adjustment purpose such that the output of
TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{H})
or
TF^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{H})
will be of dimension
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
.

Then, we introduce a class of models called the Transformer+, which includes the un-normalized Attention layer.

Definition 2.5

(Transformer+)
.

Under the same notations as definition
2.4
. We define the Transformer+ model
TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{+}(\cdot)
as

\displaystyle TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{+}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{H}):=\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0}\times FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}^{L}}(A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{L}}(\cdots FC_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}^{1}}(A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}^{1}}(\boldsymbol{H})))\times\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1},

where
A\in\{Attn,nAttn\}
is either the Attn layer defined in definition 2.1 or the nAttn layer defined in definition 2.2.

We use
\boldsymbol{\theta}
to denote all the parameters in the Transformer and the super-index
\ell
to denote the parameter matrix corresponding to the
\ell
-th layer.
Under such definition, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\{(\{\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{K}_{m}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{V}_{m}^{(\ell)}\}_{m\in[M]},\boldsymbol{W}_{1}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{W}_{2}^{(\ell)})\}_{\ell\in[L]},\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0},\tilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{1}\}.

Following the notations in
(
Bai et al., 2024
)
, we define the operator norm of the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as follows.

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}:=\max_{\ell\in[L]}\Big\{\max_{m\in[M^{(\ell)}]}\left\{\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{m}^{(\ell)}\|_{2},\|\boldsymbol{K}_{m}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\Theta(B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},B_{M},B_{L})

And for the subspace of

3.1
The Approximation Bound

We first present an approximation bound for Lloyd’s algorithm by both the Transformer and the Transformer+.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
d=o(N)
,
k<d
. There exists a Transformer with number of layers
L=\tau(3+3k)
and norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\lesssim C^{d}(\log N+M)
with the number of heads
M
such that

\displaystyle\Big\|TF_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{H})-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}_{1,1}^{(\tau)}&\boldsymbol{p}_{1,2}^{(\tau)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}_{1,N}^{(\tau)}\end{bmatrix}\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau C^{d}\Big(\sqrt{k}\sup_{j\in[k],\ell\in[\tau]}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{j}^{(\tau)}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log M}{M}}+N^{-1}\Big)

where
\tau
is the number of iterations in the Lloyd’s algorithm, and
p_{1,i}^{(\tau)}
the one-hot coding of the membership
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}^{(\tau)}
there.

Remark 3
.

The two terms in the bound come from the expectation and the maximization steps, respectively. The

The second term related to multi-head attention comes from a few multi-head approximation layers for some general functions. To achieve this bound, we prove a new result on the universal approximation of the Softmax function, which brings in the approximation term discussed in section
3.3
. We also note that the

Theorem 3.2

, there exists a

Given the approximation error provided by Theorems

With probability at least

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\left(A_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{H}),\boldsymbol{P}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)\leq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta_{B_{M},B_{L}}(B_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\mathbb{E}[L\left(A_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{H}),\boldsymbol{P}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})\right)]

\displaystyle+C\sqrt{\frac{D^{2}B_{L}B_{M}\log(NB_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}B_{M}D\sigma m_{0})+\log(2/\delta)}{n}},

Remark 5
.

The above proposition implies that, given the sufficiently large number of samples

Theorem 3.3

(The Matching Upper Bound)

Let

\displaystyle L(

\displaystyle TF_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{H}),\boldsymbol{P}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))\lesssim\exp\left(-(1+o(1))\frac{\Delta^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{k}n^{-1/4}C^{d}\sqrt{Polylog(n)+\log(1/\delta)}+N^{-3/2}.

Our results in the above theorem imply that, given the number of samples

3.3.1
An Approximation Bound for the Softmax Function

We provide a new approximation bound for the sum of Softmax functions to mappings from
\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. We first introduce the class of
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth functions. The
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth function class contains a wide range of functions.

Definition 3.4

(
(
Bach, 2017
)
(
Bai et al., 2024
)
)
.

A function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth if for
s=\lceil(k-1)/2\rceil+2
,
g
is a
C^{s}
function supported on
[-R,R]^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[-R,R]^{k}}\|\nabla^{i}g(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{i},

for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,s\}
, with
\max_{0\leq i\leq s}L_{i}R^{i}\leq C_{\ell}
.

Then, we are ready to present our results on the approximation error of Softmax functions.

Lemma 3.1

(Approximating
d
Dimensional
(R,C_{\ell})
Smooth Mappings by Softmax Neural Networks)
.

Consider an element-wise
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth mapping
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots f_{d_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{\top}
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in[-R,R]^{d}
. There exists a set of points
\{(\boldsymbol{A}_{i},a_{i})\}_{i\in[M]}
with
\sup_{i\in[M]}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{2}\leq C
such that the following holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}\left(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R\right)}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{\ell}}\Big\|\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{Md}a_{i}\text{Softmax}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\right)\Big\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C(f)^{d}\sqrt{\frac{dd_{1}}{M}\log\left(\frac{MR}{dd_{1}}\right)}.

Remark 7
.

The above bound demonstrates the universal approximation of Softmax functions to smooth mappings. Our proof idea utilizes a preliminary result on the sigmoid function and dissects the softmax function into multiple sigmoid functions. For each of the sigmoid functions, we use the probabilistic method to construct
L_{\infty}
approximation bound.

In Lemma
A.2
, our proof applies to the left product of
\boldsymbol{A}
. It is then of general interest to know whether there exists a universal approximation bound for the right product form
\text{SoftMax}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}&1\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}\right)
. Solving this fundamental problem helps us to achieve the rate of the Transformer+ using the Transformer model.

3.3.2
The Proof Sketches of Theorem
3.1

We here provide the proof sketch of the Theorem
3.1
. The proof of Theorem
3.2
is more involved in the Expectation step and is delayed to the appendix. Our proof idea is to manually construct
\boldsymbol{\theta}
for the network and estimate the error caused by each layer constructed.

The Expectation Step.

In the expectation step, we notice the following relationship holds

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\text{SoftMax}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}_{1,1}^{(0)}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{p}_{1,N}^{(0)}\end{bmatrix}\right)\approx\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}&\ldots&\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}\end{bmatrix}.

However, we have
\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{p}_{1,1}^{(0)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}_{1,N}^{(0)}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and the
\boldsymbol{0}
part needs to be cancelled. We then construct another head with
\boldsymbol{0}
matrix in the SoftMax function to cancel out the
\boldsymbol{0}
part in the first head. The two cancellations result in an approximation error of
O(1/N)
.

The Maximization Step

In the maximization step, our proof involves a total of 4 steps. Our initial matrix is given by

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}&\boldsymbol{X}_{2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{k}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N}\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}&\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{2}^{(1)}&\ldots&\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}_{1,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}_{1,k}^{(0)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}^{(0)}_{1,N}\\
\boldsymbol{p}_{2,1}&\boldsymbol{p}_{2,2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}_{2,k}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{p}_{2,N}\\
1&1&\ldots&1&\ldots&1\\
&&\boldsymbol{0}&&&\end{bmatrix}.

Then, in the
Step 1
, we copy in a total of
k
times the first row and move them to the
\boldsymbol{0}
part using two FC layers, with one moving the negative part and one moving the positive part, providing

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&1&\ldots&1&\ldots&1\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{1,1}&\boldsymbol{X}_{2,1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{k,1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N,1}\\
&&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{1,k}&\boldsymbol{X}_{2,k}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{k,k}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N,k}\\
&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, in the
Step 2
, we move
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i\in[M]}
to
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j,i}\}_{j\in[N]}
, yielding

\begin{bmatrix}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&\ldots&1&\ldots&1\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{1,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{k,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\\
&&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{1,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{k,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{X}_{N,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}\\
&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

This step utilizes the approximation bound given by Lemma
A.2
to approximate the function of
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.

Then, in the
Step 3
, we apply the approximation bound again to construct Softmax networks that approximate the mapping from a vector to its norm, providing us with the following matrix.

\begin{bmatrix}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&\ldots&1&\ldots&1\\
\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{k,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{N,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}\\
&&\vdots\\
\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{k,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{N,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}^{(1)}\|_{2}\\
&&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix},

Finally, in the
Step 4
, we obtain approximate vectors to
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{1,i}^{(1)}\}_{i\in[N]}
through applying the softmax function to the submatrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{N,1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}&\ldots&\|\boldsymbol{X}_{N,k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{1}^{(1)}\|_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Using another approximation bound showing the difference between the Softmax and the Hardmax function we accomplish the Maximization step.

Discussions

This section discusses the limitations on the theory part of this work and points to future working directions.

Limitations.

Our limitations in the theoretical results can be summarized as follows:
(1)
From the theoretical perspective, our results guarantee the performance of ERM solutions whereas the true estimator is obtained through stochastic gradient descent method;
(2)
Our theoretical results utilize the context-augmented matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
, which is verified removable from our empirical results.

Future Works.

Beyond resolving the limitations in this work, other future working directions from this work include:
(1)
Taking into consideration of the layer norm in the Transformer architecture.
(2)
Resolving the universal approximation problem raised in section
3.3
;
(3)
Removing the initialization procedure in the theory.

Impact Statement

This paper theoretically analyzes the capability of Transformers in performing EM algorithm. Due to the theoretical nature of this work, there is no negative sociatal impact.

Appendix A
Theoretical Background

This section provides approximation results of the Softmax function in the form of
f(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\text{Sigmoid}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right)+a_{0}
where
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in[M]}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. We consider the class of
(R,C_{\ell})
-smooth functions
(
Bach, 2017
;
Bai et al., 2024
)
defined as follows.

Definition A.1

(
Bai et al. (2024)
)
.

A function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth if for
s=\lceil(k-1)/2\rceil+2
,
g
is a
C^{s}
function supported on
[-R,R]^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[-R,R]^{k}}\|\nabla^{i}g(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{i},

for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,s\}
, with
\max_{0\leq i\leq s}L_{i}R^{i}\leq C_{\ell}
.

We then consider the following class of functions

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}_{d}=\bigg\{f:f(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\pazocal{W}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|=1}\text{Sigmoid}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{v}\right)d\mu(\boldsymbol{v})\bigg\},\text{ with }TV(\mu)=\int_{\pazocal{W}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1}d|\mu(\boldsymbol{v})|<C_{\ell}C(f)^{d},

where
C(f)
is a constant that depends only on
f
.
It is further noted that by
Bach (2017)
, we can write that the class of
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth functions belongs to the above class. Then we prove the following approximation lemma for the sigmoid function, which provides explicit dependence on
B
and
C
.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose
f
is
(R,C_{\ell})
smooth. Then there exists a set of points
(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},a_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{v}_{M},a_{M})\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{2},1)
that makes the following hold

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}\left(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R\right)}\Big|\frac{1}{C_{\ell}}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\text{Sigmoid}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C(f)^{d}\inf_{\epsilon>0}\Big(2\epsilon+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)/\delta)}{M}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim C(f)^{d}\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}\log\Big(\frac{MR}{d}\Big)},

where we also have
\sum_{i=1}^{M}|a_{i}|\leq C(f)^{d}
.

Proof.

The proof goes by the probabilistic method, where we can first use
Pisier (1981)
to show that when we sample from the distribution given by
f(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{|\mu(\boldsymbol{v})|}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}d\mu(\boldsymbol{v})}=\frac{|\mu(\boldsymbol{v})|}{TV(\mu)}
. Then, under this probability measure, we sample independently in a total of
M
samples
(\boldsymbol{V}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{V}_{M})
to obtain that for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{V}
, pointwise

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}

\displaystyle\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{v}\right)d\mu(\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{TV(\mu)}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=TV(\mu){\bigg(}\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{sign}(\mu(\boldsymbol{v}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}\right)\Big]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{sign}\left(\mu(\boldsymbol{v})\right)\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right){\bigg)}.

And we have by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{\bigg(}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[\text{sign}(\mu(\boldsymbol{v}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}\right)\right]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{sign}(\sigma_{f}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right)\Big|\geq\frac{t}{TV(\mu)}{\bigg)}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{CMt^{2}}{TV(\mu)^{2}}\right).

And, by the union bound, we can show that for points in the
\epsilon
-cover of
\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)
, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{\bigg(}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\text{sign}(\sigma_{f}(\boldsymbol{V}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}\right)}_{=:F(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{x})}\Big]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{sign}(\sigma_{f}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right)\Big|\geq\frac{t}{TV(\mu)}{\bigg)}

\displaystyle\leq|\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)|\exp\left(-\frac{CMt^{2}}{TV(\mu)^{2}}\right).

Alternatively, we can show the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[\text{sign}(\mu(\boldsymbol{v}))\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}\right)\right]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\text{sign}(\sigma_{f})\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)/\delta\right)}{M}}.

Then we consider generalizing these results to uniform convergence. For
\boldsymbol{x}
, we denote
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
as the closest point in the
\epsilon
-cover of
\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)
denoted by
\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)
. For function
F
, we can show that for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)}\left|\mathbb{E}[F(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{x})]-\mathbb{E}[F(\boldsymbol{V},\pi(\boldsymbol{x}))]\right|\leq\left|\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{X}_{2})\right|\leq\|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}]\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon.

Then we consider the error given by

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)}\Big|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\boldsymbol{V}_{i},\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\boldsymbol{V}_{i},\pi(\boldsymbol{x}))\Big|\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\epsilon\|\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon.

Then the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[F(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{x})\right]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\boldsymbol{V}_{i},\boldsymbol{x})\Big|

\displaystyle+\sup_{\pi(\boldsymbol{x})\in\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[F(\boldsymbol{V},\pi(\boldsymbol{x}))\right]-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}F(\boldsymbol{V}_{i},\pi(\boldsymbol{x}))\Big|\leq\epsilon+\epsilon+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon)/\delta)}{M}}.

Given these results, we show that there exists a set of parameters
\{(\boldsymbol{V}_{i},a_{i}=TV(\mu)\text{sign}_{f}(\boldsymbol{V}_{i}))\}_{i\in[m]}
where the following holds

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R)}\Big|\frac{1}{C_{\ell}}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\text{Softmax}\left([\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},1]\boldsymbol{V}_{i}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{\epsilon}\bigg\{\epsilon+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\pazocal{N}(\pazocal{B}(\|\cdot\|_{\infty},R),\epsilon))}{M}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\inf_{\epsilon}\bigg\{\epsilon+\sqrt{\frac{d\log\frac{R}{\epsilon}}{M}}\bigg\}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d}{M}\log\left(\frac{MR}{d}\right)},

where we already utilize the estimate given by
Wu (2020)
on the covering number of

∎

Lemma B.1

(Approximating the Hardmax Function by the Softmax Function)
.

Consider a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
x^{*}=\max_{i}\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[d]}
. Define
x^{*}=\max_{i\in[d]}x_{i}
,
\pazocal{N}_{2}=\{i:x_{i}=x^{*}\}
,
\Delta=x^{*}-\max_{i\in\pazocal{N}_{2}}x_{i}
. Define the Hardmax function as
\text{Hardmax}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{x_{1}=\max_{i\in[d]}\{x_{i}\}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}=\max_{i\in[d]}}\{x_{i}\}}&\ldots&\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}=\max_{i\in[d]}\{x_{i}\}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}=\max_{i\in[d]}}\{x_{i}\}}\end{bmatrix}
, we subsequently show that

\displaystyle\Big|\text{SoftMax}(\beta\boldsymbol{v})-\text{Hardmax}(\boldsymbol{v})\Big|\leq\bigg((d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)+\frac{(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)^{2}}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|^{3}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-\beta\Delta).

Proof.

We can show that the difference is given by

\displaystyle\Big\|\text{SoftMax}(

\displaystyle\beta\boldsymbol{v})-\text{Hardmax}(\boldsymbol{v})\Big\|_{2}=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}=\max_{i\in[d]}\{x_{i}\}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{i}=\max_{i\in[d]}\{x_{i}\}}}-\frac{\exp(\beta x_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\exp(\beta x_{i})}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(|\pazocal{N}_{1}|\exp(-2\beta\Delta)+|\pazocal{N}_{2}|\Big(\frac{1}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|+|\pazocal{N}_{1}|\exp(-\beta\Delta)}-\frac{1}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|}\Big)^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg((d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)\exp(-2\beta\Delta)+\frac{(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)^{2}\exp(-2\beta\Delta)}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|\left(|\pazocal{N}_{2}|+(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)\exp(-\beta\Delta)\right)^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigg((d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)+\frac{\left(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|\right)^{2}}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|\left(|\pazocal{N}_{2}|+(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)\exp(-\beta\Delta)\right)^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\beta\Delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg((d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)+\frac{(d-|\pazocal{N}_{2}|)^{2}}{|\pazocal{N}_{2}|^{3}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\exp(-\beta\Delta).

∎
