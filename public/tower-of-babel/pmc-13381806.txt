Introgression of the Glu-A1t allele encoding high molecular weight glutenin subunits A1x21* and A1y21* into winter bread wheat varieties
Authors: Marina Tikhonova, Bulat Islamov, Francisco Andrade, Carlos Guzmán, Anne Ingver, Reine Koppel, Liina Jakobson, Kristiina Laanemets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe applied DNA-based marker-assisted selection to introduce the rare Glu-A1t allele into winter bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) and provided the first validation of the Glu-A1t DNA markers for varieties adapted to Northern European conditions. The allele has earlier only been screened in spring wheat using protein analysis. The Glu-A1t allele encodes a unique pair of high molecular weight glutenin subunits (HMW-GSs), with both A1x21* and A1y21* being expressed. This allele has shown potential for improving bread-making quality and increasing grain protein content in bread wheat. Four molecular markers (the indel marker Xid3, the STS marker Axnull, and two KASP markers Mk-A1-1-12 and Mk-A1-1-13) were validated for their ability to detect Glu-A1t and distinguish it from other GLU-A1 alleles (a, b, c). These markers were used for creating hybrid winter wheat lines, which can be used for evaluating the effects of Glu-A1t on yield and baking quality. Genotyping results were fully consistent with SDS-PAGE analysis of HMW-GS composition, confirming the presence of both A1x21* and A1y21* subunits in F3 hybrids. Overall, this study delivers a novel set of validated molecular tools and genetic resources that can be directly applied in breeding programs aimed at improving wheat baking quality in Northern Europe.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53022-4.Keywords: Glu-A1t allele, HMW-GS, Winter wheat, Marker-assisted selection, Wheat protein SDS-PAGE, Wheat qualitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The bread-making quality of wheat flour depends on the composition and quantity of seed storage proteins, in particular the high molecular weight glutenin subunits (HMW-GSs) encoded by the GLU-1 loci located on the long arms of chromosomes 1A, 1B, and 1D. Each locus consists of two tightly linked genes (for example, GLU-A1-1 and GLU-A1-2) encoding an x-type and a y-type subunit1. In bread wheat (Triticum aestivum), the GLU-A1 locus typically expresses only the x-type subunit, while the y-type gene is usually transcriptionally silent2.

A unique pair of HMW-GSs, A1x21* and A1y21*, was first identified in Swedish spring bread wheat lines3,4. This discovery was notable because, unlike other y-type subunits at the GLU-A1 locus in bread wheat, A1y21* is expressed. Subsequent molecular and phylogenetic analyses suggested that this allele likely originated from Triticum urartu, the A-genome donor of hexaploid wheat5. This rare allele was designated Glu-A1t in the Wheat Gene Catalogue6.

To study its functional potential, the Glu-A1t allele was introgressed into several Australian spring wheat varieties through backcrossing and selection based on HMW-GS composition detected by protein analysis. This led to the development of near-isogenic lines (NILs) carrying the A1y21* subunit, which is encoded by the expressed Glu-A1t allele7–10.

Quality analyses showed that Glu-A1t increased grain protein content and improved dough and baking properties, including loaf volume, dough water absorption, dough strength and stability7–10. Notably, under optimal growth conditions (i.e., stable water availability and absence of drought during grain filling), the NIL carrying the Glu-A1t allele exhibited a significant increase in both grain yield and protein content compared to the recurrent parent. This result suggests that Glu-A1t may help overcome the typical trade-off between yield and grain quality8. Importantly, even under suboptimal field conditions, the presence of Glu-A1t still improved end-use quality without any yield penalty, indicating its good stability across diverse environments. This makes Glu-A1t a promising candidate for breeding programs focused on both quality and productivity. Nevertheless, further data is required to assess the stability and expression of these effects across a wider range of environments and genetic backgrounds.

To date, research on Glu‑A1t has been limited to spring wheat varieties. However, in Northern Europe, characterized by cold winters with consistent snow cover, mild summers, and high relative humidity, winter wheat is the dominant type cultivated11. The strong demand for high-quality, food-grade winter wheat makes this a relevant target region for evaluating the effects of baking quality-related alleles in winter wheat backgrounds12,13. Thus, we chose to introgress the Glu‑A1t allele and validate DNA markers in winter wheat.

Compared to spring wheat, winter wheat has a longer growing period, greater tillering capacity, and more developed root system, which usually contributes to higher yield potential, often at the expense of lower protein content and reduced quality12,14,15. Therefore, it is especially important to focus on improving the end-use winter wheat quality without yield penalty. Both spring and winter wheat have a high nitrogen demand essential for baking quality, and high nitrogen fertilization promotes higher protein content13,16. Despite clear differences in growth habit and environmental adaptation, winter and spring wheat share largely the same underlying controls of glutenin expression and genotype-specific variability15,17. Thus, DNA markers validated in a winter wheat background are expected to be equally applicable for spring wheat breeding programs, facilitating broader use across different wheat types.

Validation of molecular markers is essential, as marker performance may be affected by genetic background, epistatic interactions, and environment-dependent expressional changes. DNA-based molecular markers enable the efficient identification of different HMW-GS alleles in wheat15,17. The Xid3 marker, developed for y-type GLU-A1 genes in both T. urartu and T. aestivum, has shown potential for identifying genotypes derived from wild GLU-A1 locus sources18. Given the phylogenetic origin of Glu-A1t from T. urartu, this marker was selected to detect its introgression into breeding lines.

The development of NILs and heterogeneous inbred families (HIFs) has proven useful for studying the contributions of individual alleles to phenotypic variation in plants19–21. These approaches enable precise genetic analysis and support the creation of improved varieties.

The aim of the present study was to develop pre-breeding methods for introgression of the Glu-A1t allele into winter wheat varieties adapted to Northern European growing conditions using marker-assisted selection. Seed protein analyses were used for verification of DNA markers. As part of this work, F1–F3 hybrids and BC1-BC2 backcross generations were produced as the initial genetic material for subsequent development of NILs and HIFs. This study focuses on the methodological framework for the introgression and molecular identification of the Glu-A1t allele in winter wheat.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The Italian facultative wheat line N11, carrying the allele Glu-A1t that encodes the expressed HMW-GSs A1x21*+A1y21*, was provided by Prof. Domenico Lafiandra and Prof. Stefania Masci from Tuscia University (Italy). This line can be grown either as a winter or spring wheat, as it does not require vernalization to flower. Seven winter wheat varieties (Avenue, Donskaya polukarlikovaya, Fredis, Genius, Julie, Norin, and Tarso) originating from the Centre of Estonian Rural Research and Knowledge (METK) winter wheat breeding collection, were used as recurrent parents. Reciprocal crosses were performed in selected combinations, utilizing N11 as either maternal or paternal parent (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S1). A total of 144 F3 hybrid plants were obtained from 12 crossing combinations through self-pollination. To produce first- and second-generation backcrosses (BC1 and BC2), hybrids carrying the Glu-A1t allele were crossed with the recurrent parental varieties in 10 hybrid combinations (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S2).

Wheat plants were grown under controlled greenhouse conditions and vernalized according to speed vernalization protocol22 with some modifications in a climate chamber for 6 weeks at a temperature of + 8 °C and extended photoperiod (22 h light : 2 h dark). Seeds were sown on the soil surface to promote uniform vernalization.

The spring wheat variety Mireete, although not included in hybridization experiments, served as a reference genotype containing the allele Glu-A1b encoding A1x2* glutenin subunit. This sample was used for both molecular marker amplification and protein analysis.

HMW-GS composition of parental varieties and F3 hybrids was determined using DNA markers and sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) of seed storage proteins. For protein analysis, seeds were cut into halves before planting and the non-embryo parts were used for protein extraction.

DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Genomic DNA was isolated from the leaf tissue of adult wheat plants using extraction buffer with SDS and potassium acetate. Leaf fragments approximately 1.5 cm in length were placed into 1.2 ml tubes containing stainless steel beads and frozen at − 80 °C for 30 min. The frozen samples were then ground at 21 Hz for 1 min using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen).

An extraction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 50 mM EDTA, pH 8.0; 500 mM NaCl; 3% SDS) volume of 160 µl was added to each sample, followed by incubation at 65 °C for 10 min. After this, 53.4 µl of 5 M potassium acetate was added, and the samples were vortexed thoroughly, followed by cooling at 4 °C for 20 min. The tubes were centrifuged at 6200 rpm/ 6963 g for 30 min at room temperature.

For DNA precipitation, 160 µl of the resulting supernatant were transferred to new sterile tubes and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. After incubation at room temperature for 20 min, the samples were centrifuged again at 6200 rpm/ 6963 g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting DNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol. Finally, DNA was dissolved in double-distilled water (ddH₂O) and quantified using a NanoPhotometer NP80 spectrophotometer (IMPLEN).

The HMW-GS GLU-A1 alleles were identified using three types of functional DNA markers: sequence-tagged site (STS), insertion/deletion (indel), and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers. STS and indel markers were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis, while SNPs were detected using competitive allele-specific PCR (KASP assay) (Fig. 1).

DNA markers (Xid3, Axnull, Mk-A1-1-12, Mk-A1-1-13) used in this study for x- and y-type genes of the a, b, c and t alleles of the GLU-A1 locus.

PCR primers designed for DNA markers used in this study (Table 2) were ordered from Microsynth AG.

PCR for STS and indel markers was performed in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 50–150 ng genomic DNA, 1x FIREPol Master Mix Ready to Load, 1.5 mM of MgCl2 (Solis BioDyne), 0.5 µM of each primer (forward and reverse), and ddH2O (up to 20 µL). PCR cycling conditions followed previously published protocols (Table 2). The amplified fragments were separated using a horizontal electrophoresis chamber and the PowerPac Basic Power Supply (Bio-Rad) on a 1.5–2.5% agarose gel with 1x TAE buffer. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under UV light with GelDoc Go Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

PCR with KASP markers were prepared in 384-well plates in 5 µL volumes, each containing 2.5 µL genomic DNA (50–150 ng/µL), 2.5 µL of 2× KASP PACE Genotyping Master Mix (3CR Bioscience), and 0.07 µL assay mix. The allele-specific primers carried the standard FAM (5′-GAAGGTGACCAAGTTCATGCT-3′) and HEX (5′- GAAGGTCGGAGTCAACGGATT-3′) tails with the targeted SNP at the 3′ end. The primer assay mix consisted of 46 µl ddH2O, 30 µl common primer (100 µM), and 12 µl of each tailed primer (100 µM). KASP assays were performed on the CFX384 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad), programmed as recommended by the KASP assay developers (Table 2).

SDS-PAGE analysis of HMW-GSs

Proteins were extracted from crushed halves of individual wheat grains. HMW-GS extraction and SDS-PAGE separation were performed as published earlier23,24. To remove gliadins, 20 mg of whole meal flour was incubated in 0.75 mL of 50% (v/v) propanol for 30 min at 65 °C and 1400 rpm using a Thermomixer (Eppendorf). After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm/ 930 g for 2 min, the supernatant with gliadin fraction was discarded. This extraction step was repeated twice. Then, 100 µL of a freshly made 1.5% (w/v) DTT solution, prepared by mixing propanol at 50% (v/v) and 50 µL of Tris-HCl 0.08 M pH 8.0, was added to the remaining pellet. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at 65 °C and 1400 rpm for 30 min. Following this, the tubes were centrifuged for 2 min at 10,000 rpm/ 930 g and 100 µL of a 1.4% (v/v) vinylpyridine solution (prepared in the same buffer with 50 µL of propanol at 50% (v/v) and 50 µL Tris-HCl 0.08 M pH 8.0 as for DTT) was added to the resulting supernatant, which contained glutenins. The tubes were mixed by vortexing and incubated for 15 min at 1400 rpm and 65 °C, and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm/ 1571 g for 2 min. The supernatant containing the glutenin fraction was collected into a new tube, while the pellet was discarded. The glutenin fraction was mixed with 180 µL of a solution of 625 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 40% glycerol, and 0.02% bromophenol blue (all w/v). The samples were vortexed and heated at 90 °C for 5 min in the Thermomixer, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm/ 1571 g for 2 min.

An aliquot of 8 µL from the supernatant was loaded onto SDS-PAGE gel. Glutenin subunits were separated using vertical SDS-PAGE in a discontinuous Tris-HCl–SDS buffer system (pH 6.8/8.5), with an 8% (w/v) polyacrylamide resolving gel (C: 1.33%). The gels were 1.5 mm thick and had a total height of 15 cm, including 2.5 cm for the stacking layer and 12.5 cm for the separating layer. Electrophoresis was performed at 25 mA per gel at 15 °C for 90 min after the tracking dye left the gel, 5–6 h in total. Gels were stained overnight using 12% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid containing 5% (v/v) ethanol and 0.05% (w/v) Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, followed by overnight destaining in water.

The glutenin subunits were named according to the nomenclature systems of the Wheat Gene Catalogue6.

Results and discussion

In this study, we evaluated DNA markers for marker-assisted selection and introgression of the Glu-A1t into winter wheat varieties. The presence of the A1y21* HMW-GS in the winter wheat genetic background was confirmed by molecular marker analysis and SDS-PAGE of seed storage proteins in F3 hybrids. These hybrids were derived from crosses between the donor line N11, carrying the Glu-A1t allele, and several winter wheat varieties.

Identification of GLU-A1 alleles using DNA markers

We used the Xid3 marker to differentiate the Glu-A1t allele in line N11 from other GLU-A1 alleles present in bread wheat varieties (Glu-A1a, Glu-A1b, and Glu-A1c) (Table 2; Fig. 2a, c and d). This indel marker was developed based on polymorphism within the coding sequences of the GLU-A1 y-type genes from three species, T. aestivum, T. turgidum, and T. urartu18. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the GLU-A1 y-type gene sequence of the line N11 was similar to the y-type genes of T. urartu5.

NTC no-template control, t/c indicates heterozygote with Glu-A1t and Glu-A1c and t/a indicates heterozygote with Glu-A1t and Glu-A1a. c* indicates lines where the presence of Glu-A1c allele was verified with KASP marker Mk-A1-1-13 in a separate analysis as the Xid3 marker does not amplify the Glu-A1c allele. Unedited gel images are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Two PCR fragments of 655 and 718 bp were amplified with marker Xid3. These fragments correspond to t allele (A1x21*+A1y21*) and a (A1x1) or b (A1x2*) alleles of the GLU-A1 locus, respectively. It should be noted that the Xid3 marker does not amplify any specific fragment in homozygous carriers of the null allele Glu-A1c, whereas a 655 bp fragment is consistently amplified in all genotypes containing the Glu-A1t allele, including heterozygotes Glu-A1t/c. Therefore, the presence of this amplicon in Fig. 2a, c and d corresponds exclusively to genotypes carrying Glu-A1t, not to homozygous Glu-A1c individuals. This demonstrates that the marker specificity is consistent and reliable across all analyzed samples.

However, the Xid3 marker does not allow distinguishing the heterozygous genotypes such as Glu-A1t/c (A1x21*+A1y21*/A1xnull), Glu-A1a/c (A1x1/A1xnull), Glu-A1b/c (A1x2*/A1xnull) or Glu-A1a/b (A1x1/A1x2*). In connection with this, we used additional STS and KASP markers for the identification of Glu-A1a and Glu-A1c alleles in our study. The dominant STS marker Axnull is only specific for the null allele c (Table 2; Fig. 2b)25. The KASP markers Mk-A1-1-13 and Mk-A1-1-12 were previously designed based on allele-specific SNPs identified in sequences encoding HMW-GSs A1x1, A1x2* and A1xnull, corresponding to alleles Glu-A1a, Glu-A1b, and Glu-A1c, respectively26.

The KASP marker Mk-A1-1-12 identifies Glu-A1a allele and can distinguish it from other Glu-A1 alleles (Table 2; Fig. 3a). Unfortunately, Glu-A1t/a heterozygotes were indistinguishable from Glu-A1a homozygotes, because they have the same strong HEX fluorescent signal. However, Mk-A1-1-12 could be useful for discerning Glu-A1a and Glu-A1b alleles as this is not possible with Xid3 marker.

The codominant KASP marker Mk-A1-1-13 can distinguish Glu-A1c allele and was used for discerning between homozygous and heterozygous wheat genotypes with Glu-A1c allele and Glu-A1t/c heterozygotes (Fig. 3b). Therefore, it is more preferable than the STS marker Axnull.

Thus, three alleles (a, c and t) at the GLU-A1 locus were identified by using the four abovementioned markers (Figs. 2 and 3). We genotyped seven parental winter wheat varieties, donor line N11, and their 144 F3 hybrids using those validated DNA markers (Supplementary Tables S1, S2).

Marker limitations

Despite their effectiveness, each marker has specific limitations that should be considered when applied in breeding programs. The Xid3 indel marker reliably identifies the presence of Glu-A1t but cannot distinguish heterozygotes carrying null and other functional alleles. The KASP marker Mk-A1-1-12 is unable to differentiate Glu-A1t/a heterozygotes from Glu-A1a homozygotes due to overlapping fluorescence signals. The STS marker Axnull is specific only for the null allele and does not provide information on other GLU-A1 alleles. Awareness of these limitations is essential for accurate genotyping and for the effective use of marker-assisted selection in diverse breeding populations (Fig. 1; Table 2).

HMW-GS composition determined by SDS-PAGE and comparison with genotyping data

To validate the introgression of the Glu-A1t allele and reveal the HMW-GS composition in the parental varieties and hybrid lines, SDS-PAGE analysis was performed. The study focused on identifying homozygous and heterozygous carriers at the GLU-A1 locus, alongside characterization of HMW-GSs encoded by the GLU-B1 and GLU-D1 loci (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables S1, S2). The results of HMW-GS analysis using SDS-PAGE were consistent with genotyping data and confirmed subunit-allele agreement. The donor line N11 carried the following HMW glutenin alleles and subunits: Glu-A1t (A1x21*+A1y21*), Glu-B1b (B1x7 + B1y8) and Glu-D1a (D1x2 + D1y12) (Supplementary Table S1). Among the parental varieties, allele Glu-A1a (A1x1) was present in Donskaya polukarlikovaya, Fredis, and Julie; allele Glu-A1c (A1xnull) was found in Avenue, Genius, Norin, and Tarso. For GLU-B1, Avenue and Julie carried allele d (B1x6 + B1y8), while the others had allele c (B1x7+B1y9). The allele Glu-D1d (D1x5+D1y10) was detected in all varieties. The variety Mireete, used as a reference for the allele Glu-A1b, expressed the A1x2* subunit according to SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Table S1), and the genetic analysis with the Xid3 marker produced a 718 bp amplicon (Fig. 2a). The marker analysis data together with HMW-GS composition of F3 hybrids is presented in Supplementary Table S2.

In some hybrid lines (e.g., 92.1.12 and 92.4.9), the A1y21* subunit appeared faint or nearly undetectable on SDS-PAGE gels due to lower staining intensity; however, this does not indicate its absence. The two subunits A1x21* and A1y21* are encoded by two tightly linked genes at the Glu-A1t locus and are co-inherited3,5. The consistent detection of the A1x21* subunit, together with DNA marker confirmation, indicates that both subunits are present in these genotypes.

SDS-PAGE could not resolve heterozygotes Glu-A1t/c (A1x21*+A1y21*/A1xnull) due to the absence of a detectable subunit band for the null allele c. These heterozygous plants (e.g., hybrids Norin × N11: 92.1.11, 92.1.12, 92.4.9, 92.4.10, 92.5.2, 92.5.4; Avenue × N11: 113.3; Fig. 4) showed a protein banding pattern identical to Glu-A1t homozygote carriers (e.g., line N11; hybrids Norin × N11: 92.5.10; Fredis × N11: 111.6f; Fig. 4). Amplification with marker Xid3 produced only the 655 bp fragment in these heterozygous samples, indicating the presence of the Glu-A1t (A1x21*+A1y21*) allele (Fig. 2c, d). In this regard, SDS-PAGE is similar to Xid3, as neither of the methods allows identification of Glu-A1t/c heterozygotes. In contrast, genotyping with the KASP marker Mk-A1-1-13 revealed that these samples were heterozygous, carrying both the null allele Glu-A1c (A1xnull) and another functional allele (Fig. 3b).

Heterozygous Glu-A1t/a (A1x21*+A1y21*/A1x1) hybrids, such as 112/134.7 (N11 × Fredis), were confirmed by marker Xid3 (Fig. 2d) and SDS-PAGE (Fig. 4). Therefore, identification of homozygous and heterozygous hybrids carrying the Glu-A1t (A1x21*+A1y21*) and Glu-A1a (A1x1) or Glu-A1b (A1x2*) alleles requires the use of the Xid3 marker or SDS-PAGE. In addition, at least one complementary marker specific to the Glu-A1c (A1xnull) allele is required for genotyping of hybrids potentially containing null allele.

The distribution of homozygous and heterozygous genotypes at the GLU-A1 locus within each hybrid combination is summarized in Supplementary Table S3. The proportion of heterozygous carriers of Glu-A1t varied from 0% to 50% (0–18 plants per cross), with an overall average frequency of 25%. No heterozygotes were observed in hybrid combination 129 (Genius × N11). Most hybrid combinations (9 out of 12) contained 1 to 3 heterozygous samples. Hybrid combination 92/131 (Norin × N11) exhibited the highest level of heterozygosity, with 18 heterozygotes, representing 33% of the total number of F3 plants in that cross. The lower frequency of homozygotes with Glu-A1t allele in comparison with that of homozygotes without this allele (with the a or c allele) in F3 (41: 63, chi2 = 4.65, P < 0.05, Supplementary Table S3), indicates the importance and need of validated markers in introgression of this allele in breeding.

Molecular marker-assisted breeding and development of near-isogenic lines

Molecular markers can be used to identify heterozygous and homozygous plants across different generations of hybrids at various stages of the breeding process. The genetically defined lines obtained through such selection can be used both to investigate the phenotypic impact of specific alleles and for practical application in wheat breeding programs.

Marker-assisted selection of heterozygous plants at the target locus (Glu-A1t/c or Glu-A1t/a in our study) across generations enables the development of recombinant inbred lines (RILs) and heterogeneous inbred families (HIFs) segregating at this locus (Supplementary Fig. S1). Near-isogenic lines (NILs) representing a range of recombinant genetic backgrounds can be derived from each HIF. Evaluation of the phenotypic effects of the Glu-A1t allele across these diverse lines helps to identify underlying genotypes that support optimal allele expression. This approach aims to overcome limitations caused by epistatic interactions or reduced penetrance that may confound allele evaluation in a single genetic background19.

Furthermore, the molecular markers Xid3 and Mk-A1-1-13 enabled the identification of Glu-A1t allele homo- and heterozygous carriers used for backcrossing. The NILs derived from these backcrosses enable the assessment of the phenotypic effects of the allele of interest on wheat traits in comparison with the recurrent parent genotype (Supplementary Fig. S2)7–9,21. The most promising lines identified through functional studies may subsequently serve as pre-breeding material for the development of improved wheat varieties.

Conclusions

We have compiled and validated a set of DNA markers for the Glu-A1t allele, which is associated with improved baking quality in bread wheat. Although markers such as the gel-based Xid3 and Axnull, and the KASP assays Mk-A1-1-12 and Mk-A1-1-13 were previously developed for the detection of Glu-A1a, Glu-A1b, and Glu-A1c alleles, they had not been validated for identifying the rare Glu-A1t allele encoding the two expressed A1x21* and A1y21* HMW-GSs. Our study represents the first validation of these markers for Glu-A1t in winter wheat, whereas in spring wheat the presence of Glu-A1t was previously determined solely by protein-based detection of its encoded subunits. The validated markers were successfully applied for the introgression of Glu-A1t into several winter wheat backgrounds. Marker-assisted selection enables identification of heterozygous hybrid plants at the GLU-A1 locus and supports the development of NILs using HIF and backcrossing approaches, providing a robust and reliable methodological framework for breeding programs targeting this allele. Importantly, these markers can also be applied both in spring and winter wheat, extending their utility among different growth habits.

The breeding material developed in this study represents a valuable genetic resource for evaluating the expression and functional effects of Glu-A1t on agronomic and quality traits across diverse genetic backgrounds. This work provides both new germplasm and validated molecular tools for breeding high-quality winter bread wheat adapted to Northern European conditions, highlighting its practical relevance and novelty.
