Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease as a risk factor for cognitive impairment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xia Chen, Zhenjie Yu, Yong Liu, Yang Zhao, Shu Li, Lan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment is affecting plenty of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and it is the main leading cause of quality of life to varying degrees. However, there are still wide discrepancies in these prevalence rates can be attributed to the diversity in study designs, participant age ranges, inclusion criteria and the methodologies used for assessment. Previous studies revealed the association between COPD and cognitive impairment, but the conclusions remain controversial.ObjectivesThe current systematic review aimed to investigate whether COPD is an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment.Study designA systematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, Cochrane Library, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, China Knowledge Resource Integrated Database, Wanfang Database, Chinese Biomedical Database and Weipu Database were searched from inception to 1 December 2022.Eligibility criteriaThe inclusion criteria involved studies that reported cognitive impairment in COPD. We just included cohort designs, published in English or Chinese language.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently extracted and assessed the quality of data using Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale. The outcomes were assessed with random-effects model and reported as the HR with 95% CI using the Review Manager software.Results12 studies from 10 articles reporting on 625 644 people were included. The findings indicated that compared with those without COPD at baseline, patients with COPD were associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. Subgroup analysis showed the association was not significantly different in sex and age, and the subgroup supports that COPD has a higher risk of non-amnestic mild cognitive impairment (na-MCI) than amnestic MCI.ConclusionsPatients with COPD have a higher risk of developing cognitive impairment and are more likely to cause na-MCI compared with those without COPD, and this risk is not affected by gender or age. Therefore, continuous monitoring of cognitive function in COPD is critical.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42021285913.Keywords: Systemic disease and lungs, COPD epidemiology

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a significant public health concern characterised by persistent airflow limitation, which manifests in several debilitating symptoms including respiratory restriction, hypoxaemia and decreased activity tolerance.1 The prevalence of COPD shows considerable variation across different regions, with rates ranging from 2.1% to 26.4%, placing a substantial economic and social burden on societies worldwide. Over a span of 20 years, the global health burden attributed to COPD witnessed a notable increase of 25.7%, escalating from 59.2 million to 74.4 million.2 Furthermore, the complexity of COPD management is exacerbated by the fact that more than two-thirds of those affected suffer from one or more additional chronic comorbidities, negatively impacting the physiological, psychological and social dimensions of patients' lives.3 Cognitive impairment is notably prevalent among patients with COPD, with studies indicating that between 16% and 57% of patients experience this condition.4,6 The wide discrepancies in these prevalence rates can be attributed to the diversity in study designs, participant age ranges, inclusion criteria, and the methodologies and tools used for assessment. Cognitive impairment adversely affects various cognitive functions, impacting patients’ social interaction capabilities and quality of life to varying degrees.7 Notably, a previous investigation8 highlighted that cognitive deficits in patients with COPD impair several cognitive domains including prospective memory, visuospatial memory, short-term digital memory, cognitive processing speed, executive function and non-verbal memory. These impairments further deteriorate self-management skills, medication adherence and personal independence, culminating in a diminished quality of life. Cognitive impairment in patients with COPD is classified into mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia based on disease severity.9 MCI itself is subdivided into amnestic MCI (a-MCI) and non-amnestic MCI (na-MCI) depending on the presence of memory loss.10 While MCI primarily affects memory and attention without severely impacting daily functioning, more severe forms of cognitive impairment can significantly disrupt daily life,11 complicating disease management and prognosis and elevating mortality risk.12 Moreover, cognitive impairment in patients with COPD is often associated with other neurological disorders, such as bipolar disorder, vascular dementia (VD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), indicating a complex interrelationship. The confluence of COPD and cognitive impairment is influenced by a multitude of risk factors, including ageing, comorbidities13 and sleep disturbances,14 underscoring the intricate link between these conditions. Despite this, the relationship between COPD and cognitive impairment remains inadequately understood. Enhancing awareness and understanding of the intricate dynamics between cognitive function and COPD is crucial for the effective evaluation and management of patients with COPD with concurrent cognitive impairment.

Understanding the relationship between COPD and cognitive impairment is paramount for developing early preventive measures. These measures aim to mitigate the overall disease burden on patients. In our pursuit of insights, we recognise that deepening our comprehension of the prevalence and implications of cognitive deficits in individuals with COPD is a critical initial step. This endeavour not only facilitates a comprehensive grasp of the challenges faced by these patients but also aids in crafting targeted interventions. Although previous inquiries have shed light on the potential risk factors associated with cognitive decline,15 16 our literature review reveals a gap in studies definitively categorising COPD as either a precursor to or an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment, based on cohort analysis. A notable study conducted in 2019 highlighted the association between COPD and cognitive impairment through cohort studies; however, its findings were limited to three studies, all based in Taiwan, which poses questions regarding the broader applicability of the results.17 Building on this foundation, Simargi et al18 carried out a systematic review encompassing five studies in 2022, advocating for subsequent investigations to engage in meta-analysis. Wang et al19 performed a meta-analysis incorporating six cohort studies, substantiating the increased susceptibility of patients with COPD to dementia or cognitive decline. Nonetheless, their study placed greater emphasis on descriptive analysis and correlation research, using the relative risk as the primary outcome measure, without taking into account the temporal dynamics influencing the outcomes. Our meta-analysis seeks to fill these gaps by incorporating a wide range of longitudinal studies from both domestic and international sources, thoroughly examining the HR associated with cognitive impairment in patients with COPD. Through this rigorous analysis, our objective is to discern whether COPD indeed serves as a distinct risk factor for cognitive impairment. We intend for our findings to provide a reliable reference for the prompt evaluation and intervention of cognitive impairments in individuals diagnosed with COPD, thereby enhancing patient care and outcomes.

Methods

This meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews.

Search strategy

PubMed, Cochrane Library, MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, China Knowledge Resource Integrated Database, Wanfang Database, Chinese Biomedical Database and Weipu Database were searched from inception to 1 December 2022 to identify articles published in English or Chinese. The following search terms were used: Medical Subject Headings keywords: ‘COPD’, ‘chronic obstructive pulmonary disease’, ‘cognitive impairment’, ‘mental disorders’, ‘relative risk’ and ‘dementia’. All included studies were manually screened to ensure that all eligible studies were captured. Full search strategy from all databases is presented in online supplemental appendix 1.

Study selection

Two investigators worked independently to meticulously extract data from selected studies and assess their eligibility for inclusion. The criteria for inclusion required studies to compare an exposure group, consisting of individuals clinically diagnosed with COPD who were then monitored until a diagnosis of dementia, death or the conclusion of the follow-up period, against a control group of individuals with no prior history of COPD. Additionally, only cohort studies that reported the HR along with its corresponding 95% CI were considered for this systematic review. Conversely, studies were excluded if they presented incomplete data or were published in languages other than English and Chinese, ensuring a comprehensive and reliable analysis of the available evidence.

Quality assessment

The assessment of the quality of cohort studies was meticulously carried out by two researchers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale for Cohort Studies (NOS). This scale evaluates the included studies across three critical dimensions: the selection of the study population, the comparability of the groups being studied and the accuracy of the outcome assessment. To facilitate a semiquantitative evaluation of the studies’ quality, the NOS employs a star system. In this system, the highest quality studies can earn up to one star for each criterion within the selection and outcome categories, with the comparability category being an exception where up to two stars can be awarded. Consequently, the NOS score for a study can range from zero to a maximum of nine stars.20 In instances of disagreement concerning the quality assessment of the studies, a senior investigator was consulted to reach a resolution.

Data extraction

The data were independently extracted by two researchers. The extracted data included the name of the first author, year of publication, study area, study type, sample size, mean age of patients, statistical model, other factors adjusted for in the model, measures, outcomes and other results of the subgroup analysis. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses within this study were meticulously executed using RevMan software, V.5.4. To determine the significance of our findings, we employed two-tailed significance tests with a threshold set at p<0.05. For the purpose of evaluating the association between COPD and the risk of developing dementia or cognitive impairment, we used the adjusted HR and 95% CI obtained from each included study. The inverse variance method played a pivotal role in determining the research weight assigned to each study. To assess the level of heterogeneity among the included studies, we applied Cochran’s Q test in conjunction with Higgins’ I² statistic. Notably, I² values exceeding 50% are indicative of significant heterogeneity, thereby necessitating the application of the random-effects model. In cases where heterogeneity was not substantial, the fixed-effect model was deemed appropriate.21 The potential for publication bias was rigorously evaluated through the examination of funnel plots, specifically in scenarios where 10 or more trials reported on the primary outcome. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure the robustness of our findings, involving the exclusion of studies of lower quality, trials that enrolled participants with specific conditions or studies that exhibited characteristics markedly distinct from the collective. In our approach to subgroup meta-analysis, we systematically categorised the available literature data into subgroups based on several criteria including the type of dysfunction, sex, age and type of MCI. This stratification allowed for the calculation of a consolidated estimate of the 95% CI for each subgroup, offering nuanced insights into the dynamics at play.

Results

Search results

The initial search retrieved 5204 articles, of which 515 were duplicates. After screening the titles and abstracts, 4663 articles were selected based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. After further full-text evaluation, 16 articles were excluded, and 10 articles met the final criteria for systematic review; a total of 13 results from 10 articles(one article has 3 results and another has 2 results) were used for the meta-analysis (figure 1).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram for the study selection process.

Characteristics of all included studies

The compilation of our study draws on the characteristics of 10 articles, details of which are provided in online supplemental table. Each article features both exposure and control groups and was published within the time frame of 2012 to 2020. Geographically, the studies span across four countries: seven were conducted in China, while the USA, the UK and Finland each contributed one study. The health outcomes investigated across these articles include dementia (reported in five studies), MCI (in three studies), bipolar disorder (in two studies) and other mental dysfunctions such as VD, cognitive impairment and PD.

The HR comparing cognitive impairment in individuals with COPD to those without COPD ranged from 0.91 to 2.23, indicating variability in the risk assessment across studies. Notably, the duration and follow-up timelines varied among the included studies, adding to the heterogeneity of the data.

An assessment of the quality of these studies, as determined by the NOS, reveals that all studies were of high quality, with scores ranging from 7 to 9 stars. However, seven studies did not account for confounding factors adequately and merely adjusted these factors to achieve comparability between the groups.622,27 One study relied on self-reported diagnoses of COPD,28 and another failed to report its response rate.28 The methodologies for assessing cognitive impairment varied, with most using International Classification of Diseases Codes, and a minority employing scale-based assessments, as detailed in online supplemental table.

The meta-analysis of HRs, illustrated in figure 2, encompasses these 10 studies, showing HRs between COPD and control groups ranging from 0.91 to 2.23. Employing a random-effects model, the aggregated HR for cognitive impairment in patients with COPD was determined to be 1.39 (95% CI 1.25 to 1.54, I²=89%, p<0.01), highlighting a significant association.

Forest plot and meta-analysis of the risk of cognitive impairment.

Subgroup analysis, delineated in figure 3, explored the impact of COPD on cognitive impairment across different demographics, including sex and age, as well as disease classifications. Despite the adjustments for confounding factors, no statistically significant differences were noted in the risk of cognitive impairment in patients with COPD when analysed by sex or age categories. However, a significant distinction was observed between the risks for a-MCI at 0.97 (0.63–1.51) and na-MCI at 2.36 (1.68–3.31) (p=0.002), suggesting a higher likelihood for patients with COPD to develop na-MCI.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we meticulously reviewed 12 studies from 10 articles, encompassing a total of 625 644 participants, to discern the cognitive implications of COPD as compared with non-COPD individuals. Our findings elucidate several key insights: (1) Individuals within the COPD group exhibited a significantly higher risk of cognitive impairment than their non-COPD counterparts, suggesting that COPD could be an independent risk factor for cognitive decline. (2) The rate of cognitive impairment was notably influenced by the diagnostic criteria employed. Generally, the HR for cognitive impairment hovered around 1.39. Specifically, the HR for na-MCI reached as high as 2.36, indicating a substantial risk, whereas the HR for a-MCI was below 0.97, showing a lower risk. No discernible differences were observed concerning the incidence of cognitive impairment across different sexes and ages. These results underscore the heightened vulnerability of individuals with COPD, particularly to na-MCI. Initial hypotheses posited hypoxaemia as a pivotal factor linking COPD to cognitive impairment.14 Nevertheless, subsequent research has broadened our understanding, highlighting that COPD-induced chronic pulmonary inflammation might precipitate cognitive decline by disrupting the immune system’s equilibrium between damage and repair mechanisms. The infiltration of airway inflammatory mediators into the bloodstream triggers a systemic inflammatory response, undermining the integrity of the blood–brain barrier and fostering cognitive impairment.29 30 Furthermore, studies using resting-state functional MRI have illuminated how hypercapnia, correlating directly with the level of CO2 retention in COPD, can impair cognitive functions, especially memory.31

Compounding these physiological insights, the long-term debilitation of respiratory function, curtailed physical activity and diminished social engagement in patients with COPD are believed to foster negative emotional states, such as anxiety and depression. These emotional disturbances may elevate cortisol levels and diminish brain-derived neurotrophic factors, further impairing cognitive capabilities.32 33

Our research also acknowledges a gap in the literature regarding the exploration of COPD as an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment. To fortify the evidence base, we exclusively included cohort studies while excluding cross-sectional and case–control studies, due to the inherent limitations of these designs in establishing causality. Given the ethical and practical challenges of random assignment in studying COPD’s impact, randomised controlled trials were not feasible. Consequently, our meta-analysis leveraged cohort studies to probe the association between COPD and cognitive decline, with all included studies employing survival analysis to accommodate the lengthy follow-up periods required for observing these outcomes.

In our meta-analysis, the age threshold for subgroup analysis was uniformly set at 65 years, aligning with the majority of the studies we included. This analytical choice led to an intriguing finding: age, as a solitary factor, does not exhibit a statistically significant variance in the risk of developing cognitive impairment, which contrasts with prior research.15 This suggests that factors beyond age may play a more critical role in the progression of cognitive decline, positing COPD as a potential precursor to cognitive impairment.

Moreover, lifestyle factors have been implicated in the onset of cognitive impairment. A review34 highlighted the association between cognitive decline and poor lifestyle choices, such as a diet high in fast food consumed by younger individuals due to their work lifestyle, characterised by elevated levels of salt and sugar. This dietary pattern could exacerbate hyperglycaemia, identified as a significant risk factor for cognitive impairment. Research35 36 supports this, showing a higher incidence of cognitive decline among individuals with impaired glucose tolerance compared with those with normal levels. Yet, the underlying mechanisms linking hyperglycaemia and cognitive impairment remain to be fully elucidated, underscoring the need for further investigation into the effects of hyperglycaemia on cognitive health. Additionally, diets rich in salt have been connected to cognitive decline.37 Our recent study delved into the impact of dietary salt on cerebrovascular function and cognition, revealing that prolonged high-salt diets can lead to cerebral endothelial dysfunction. This dysfunction may stem from a high-salt diet’s influence on the gut’s adaptive immune system, causing an increase in IL-17-producing T-helper lymphocytes (Th17), potentially leading to endothelial dysfunction and cognitive deficits.38

Physical exercise emerges as a protective factor against cognitive decline.33 Its benefits likely derive from enhancing neural plasticity and reducing neural degeneration, alongside improving neurotransmitter functions, cerebrovascular changes and neurotrophin effects, which collectively offer neuroprotection and diminish neurodegeneration.34 However, younger individuals, often encumbered by work commitments, may find fewer opportunities for physical activity compared with their retired older counterparts, possibly raising their risk of cognitive impairment. Furthermore, while age-related declines in memory and thinking skills might suggest a lower diagnosis rate of cognitive impairment among older adults, the underlying risk remains significant. Therefore, it is crucial to prioritise early screening for cognitive function in individuals with COPD to mitigate the risk of exacerbation in later years, highlighting the importance of comprehensive care that extends beyond conventional age and lifestyle risk factors.

Our subgroup analysis revealed no significant statistical difference in cognitive impairment across sexes, a finding that diverges from other studies where men demonstrated higher cognitive impairment rates than women.39 In men, the predominant factors contributing to cognitive impairment include smoking and hyperuricaemia. Extensive research40 has documented that long-term smoking exacerbates the risk of cognitive decline due to its impact on subcortical haemodynamics, memory and executive function. The toxic substances in cigarettes, like nicotine, can damage cerebral arteries, leading to cerebral atherosclerosis, which compromises brain blood supply, eventually causing neurological damage and cognitive impairment. Moreover, hyperuricaemia, identified as a component of metabolic syndrome, has been shown to impair cognitive functions.41 Given that gender significantly influences the prevalence of hyperuricaemia, with men being more susceptible,42 this could account for the higher incidence of cognitive impairment observed in men.

Conversely, for women, the leading causes of cognitive impairment are thought to be anxiety and depression, which are reportedly more prevalent among female patients. Excessive anxiety can elevate cortisol levels and diminish the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factors, adversely affecting cognitive abilities.32 Anxiety and depression also influence hormone levels in women. Notably, many women with COPD are postmenopausal, over the age of 50, experiencing declines in testosterone and oestrogen levels. Given the widespread distribution of hormone receptors in the brain, this hormonal shift could explain the heightened risk of cognitive impairment among female patients.32 43 These gender-specific causal pathways underscore the need for tailored intervention strategies in clinical settings.

Our findings further substantiate that COPD is a risk factor for MCI, particularly na-MCI, which is characterised by impairments mainly in non-memory domains such as executive function, language or visuospatial skills,44 aligning with Feng’s study.45 This may be attributed to higher amyloid-β levels observed in na-MCI patients compared with those with a-MCI.46 Additionally, research by Bu et al47 indicated an increase in amyloid-β levels among patients with COPD. At physiological concentrations, amyloid-beta peptide acts as a memory enhancer, but its accumulation may lead to neurodegeneration and elevate neurotoxicity risk in the brain,48 potentially explaining the elevated incidence of na-MCI.

A strength of our research lies in the comprehensive literature search across eight electronic databases, including studies published in both English and Chinese. Two independent reviewers meticulously classified information from patients with COPD who developed cognitive impairment, allowing for detailed stratification by the degree of cognitive impairment, sex and age. To our knowledge, this is the inaugural meta-analysis focusing on cognitive impairment in patients with COPD across multiple cohort studies. The primary studies included have diligently controlled for confounding variables, significantly enhancing the accuracy of our causal conclusions and minimising potential biases. This methodological rigour ensures the reliability of our findings, providing a solid foundation for academic discourse and clinical application.

Our meta-analysis, while comprehensive, is subject to several limitations that could potentially influence the accuracy and generalisability of our findings. First, the scarcity of relevant cohort studies conducted outside mainland China limits our ability to compare the risk of cognitive impairment in patients with COPD globally. Consequently, the applicability of our results to populations in other regions remains uncertain. Second, we were unable to perform a subgroup analysis considering comorbidities, the severity of COPD or instances of acute exacerbation due to a lack of detailed data in the included studies. This omission narrows the scope of our findings and weakens the evidence of a direct link between COPD and cognitive impairment. Third, the diagnostic criteria and methods for identifying cognitive impairment varied across the studies we analysed. This variability could introduce inconsistencies in the assessment and classification of cognitive impairment, potentially affecting the meta-analysis’s overall conclusions. Fourthly, our analysis is not exempt from publication bias, a common challenge in meta-analyses, where studies with positive findings are more likely to be published than those with negative or inconclusive results. This bias could skew the perceived relationship between COPD and cognitive impairment. Fifthly, we were unable to account for some factors known to be associated with either COPD or cognitive impairment due to limitations in the data provided by the original studies. These omitted factors could play significant roles in the development and progression of cognitive impairment in patients with COPD but remain unexplored in our analysis. In conclusion, these limitations highlight the need for caution in interpreting our study’s results. The identified gaps underscore the importance of further research, particularly studies conducted in diverse geographical locations, with standardised diagnostic criteria, and that explore a broader range of influencing factors. This future research could provide more definitive evidence regarding the relationship between COPD and cognitive impairment, enhancing the study’s credibility and applicability to clinical practice.

Conclusion

Our research conclusively indicates that patients with COPD are at an elevated risk of cognitive impairment or dementia, with this risk appearing to be invariant across gender and age demographics. The results of our subgroup analysis revealed a higher prevalence of na-MCI compared with a-MCI among patients with COPD. This distinction underscores the differential impact of COPD on various cognitive domains, further emphasising the necessity for targeted cognitive evaluations. The implications of these findings for clinical practice are significant. They advocate for the integration of cognitive screenings into the care pathway for patients with COPD from the initial diagnosis. Regular monitoring for cognitive impairment is imperative to ensure timely identification and management of cognitive decline for patients with COPD. Such proactive approaches can substantially aid in optimising the prognosis and enhancing the quality of life for patients with COPD, making cognitive assessment an essential component of comprehensive care for these individuals.

Relevance to clinical practice

The results obtained in this study show that COPD may increase the risk of cognitive impairment. Patients with COPD had higher prevalence in na-MCI than a-MCI, which means more likely to develop into dementia due to a higher amyloid-βlevels deposition. Nurse should not only pay attention to COPD, but also early detection of cognitive impairment through effective screening methods and apply individualised intervention strategies to reduce acute COPD exacerbation and slow the progression of cognitive impairment.
