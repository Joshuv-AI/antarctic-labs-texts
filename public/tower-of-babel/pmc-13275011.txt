Caregiver experiences of healthcare-related administrative burden: a scoping review
Authors: Tess Thompson, Danya K Krueger, Shannon M Delaney, Brianna Connelly, Lauren V Ghazal, Vanessa E Slater, Benjamin Rodriguez, Bridgette Thom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with chronic disease often face administrative burden (eg, dealing with insurance paperwork) that affects their health and their care. Informal caregivers (family and close friends) provide important support to patients, but little is known about how they help patients address administrative burden.MethodsTo address this gap, we conducted a scoping review about caregivers' experiences of healthcare-related administrative burden using Herd and Moynihan's Administrative Burden Framework, which describes learning, compliance, and psychological costs. We reviewed US-based studies about caregivers' experiences of healthcare-related administrative burden when caring for adults with a range of health conditions.ResultsEighteen articles met criteria. More than half were qualitative, and most involved caregivers for cancer patients/survivors. A minority were informed by a theory or conceptual framework. Learning, compliance, and psychological costs tended to overlap. Caregivers often reported that compliance activities such as navigating insurance caused distress and affected their own health.ConclusionThis review—the first, to our knowledge, to characterize the literature about caregivers' experiences of administrative burden—shows caregivers report overlapping burdens in a range of caregiving contexts. As this work matures, theoretically informed research is needed to develop and test interventions to mitigate administrative burden for patients and caregivers.Keywords: caregiving, administrative burden, health systems, healthcare policy, chronic disease

Introduction

The United States has a distinctive healthcare system characterized by complexity, fragmentation, and unequal access to care.1 Care is provided through both public and private financing, and health insurance plays a pivotal role in paying for treatment and services.1 The public and private entities that pay for care often employ utilization management strategies that make it challenging for patients to access care.2 For example, some health plans require patients and/or their providers to obtain prior authorization before accessing certain services or treatments.3 Although utilization management strategies such as prior authorization are intended to control healthcare use and promote evidence-based practices, they are inconsistently implemented across health plans,4 often resulting in delayed or denied care, patient distress, and decreased trust in the healthcare system.3,5-7

Such challenges in receiving care can be conceptualized as administrative burden, defined as “the costs associated with receiving a service or accessing a program”8 or “an individual's experience of a policy implementation as onerous.”9 The Administrative Burden Framework developed by Herd and Moynihan9 describes frictions people encounter when attempting to access state programs, benefits, and services related to healthcare, housing assistance, or unemployment programs. Herd and Moynihan describe 3 components of administrative burden: learning costs (eg, finding out what services are needed and how to obtain them), compliance costs (eg, expending the time and effort needed to use a service, such as completing paperwork), and psychological costs (eg, experiencing stress due to dealing with burdens).10 In the United States, administrative burdens are unevenly distributed across different populations and may exacerbate racial and socioeconomic health disparities.2,6

Terminology to describe administrative burden may differ across disciplines (eg, ordeal mechanisms, sludge, time tax)8,11,12; several related terms apply to healthcare-related challenges in particular. Time burdens and the term time toxicity have been used to describe the time needed to obtain and receive cancer treatment.13,14 Financial burdens refer to the monetary costs of treatment.13 Parsons and colleagues provide a helpful discussion of the distinctions between administrative burden, time burdens, and financial burdens, which the authors contend are interlinked.13

Research on healthcare-related administrative burden to date has focused on physicians and other providers,15,16 but a growing body of work has included patients' perspectives of healthcare-related administrative burden. Two recent scoping reviews have used the Herd and Moynihan framework to examine burden in US patients.8,11 Ilea and Ilea8 reported that most included studies addressed learning and compliance costs, with psychological costs less frequently assessed. Kyle et al.11 combined the Herd and Moynihan framework with the patient treatment trajectory (care-seeking, receiving care, resolving care issues), finding that the literature was most robust in the area of “seeking care” and the least developed in around “resolving care issues” (eg, navigating insurance denials).11

A crucial perspective has been missing from much of this work: that of informal caregivers, who often provide key support to adults with chronic conditions.17 Consistent with broad definitions of informal caregiving,18 we use the term caregiver to describe those who provide a wide range of unpaid assistance to patients, including managing medications, making appointments, and other health-related tasks—acknowledging that some who meet these criteria may not conceptualize themselves as caregivers and may instead see themselves in a social role.19 Caregivers often find themselves thrust into caregiving with little or no training; they often feel they have no choice in accepting a caregiving role, and many are “sandwiched” by caring both for older adults and minor children.20 Focusing on caregivers is consistent with conceptual models such as Burden of Treatment Theory, which illustrates how patients draw on a range of resources—including support from their social networks—to obtain healthcare.21 Herd and Moynihan have written primarily about how administrative burden affects individuals (conceived of as citizens9 or patients10), but they also implicitly acknowledge the role of informal caregivers by advocating for research to systematically examine the role family members may play in handling administrative burden.10 In acknowledgement of this role, the authors of both aforementioned scoping reviews have called for future reviews to detail the administrative burdens experienced by caregivers.8,11

There is a robust literature about informal caregiving that addresses the concept of caregiver burden,22,23 which overlaps with, but is distinct from, the concept of administrative burden. Assessments of caregiving burden vary in the degree to which they specifically address administrative burden. The Oberst Caregiving Burden Scale,24 for example, assesses time spent on caregiving tasks and the perceived difficulty of those tasks; while some scale items relate to administrative burden (eg, “Managing finances, bills, and forms related to the patient's illness”), in most published literature, results are provided in the form of a global burden score or 2 subscale scores (time spent on tasks and perceived task difficulty) rather than describing administrative burden in particular. One of the most common measures of caregiver burden, the Zarit Burden Interview,25,26 does not address administrative burden at all but instead focuses on caregivers' distress due to their caregiving experiences (sample items: “Do you feel that your social life has suffered because you are caring for your relative?” and “Do you feel strained when you are around your relative?”). Although it is clear from the literature that caregivers often report experiencing caregiving and/or psychological burden,22,23 the majority of this work focuses on general distress and does not speak to their experiences of administrative burden.

Guided by the Herd and Moynihan Framework of Administrative Burden9,10 and the Burden of Treatment Theory,21 this scoping review details US-based empirical evidence on informal caregivers' healthcare-related administrative burden, defined here as the unpaid work required to obtain, coordinate, and resolve healthcare and insurance-related tasks for adults with chronic conditions, and the cognitive and emotional load that accompanies this work. We sought to (1) describe study designs, populations, and clinical contexts; (2) characterize how caregiver administrative burden is conceptualized and measured; (3) identify the specific administrative tasks and experiences reported by caregivers and map them onto learning, compliance, and psychological costs; and (4) describe intervention and policy-relevant implications to address caregivers' healthcare-related administrative burdens.

Methods

This scoping review about caregivers' experiences of administrative burden was conducted according to Arksey and O’Malley's methodological framework for scoping reviews27 and follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).28 A detailed protocol was registered on Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to conducting the review (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/XK2JA). As this review was not human subjects research, there was no requirement to obtain informed consent or Institutional Review Board approval.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

Our review questions, developed after an initial assessment of the literature about healthcare-related administrative burden, were as follows: (1) What are the administrative burdens that informal caregivers encounter during their coordination of healthcare and diagnosis-specific treatments for patients? (2) What are the interventions to address healthcare-related administrative burdens caregivers encounter? Preliminary searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, PROSPERO, and OSF Registries to confirm the absence of completed or in-progress reviews on this topic.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

We defined administrative burden as the time and effort required to seek, arrange, receive, and pay for care. Eligible health-related tasks could include performing logistical and administrative tasks associated with coordinating care, managing insurance and medical payments, scheduling and attending medical appointments, communicating with the care team, and handling legal/planning issues related to care. Our inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed observational or experimental empirical studies focused on informal caregivers involved in coordinating healthcare and treatment for patients 18 years of age or older and diagnosed with cancer, diabetes, heart disease, dementia, and other chronic health conditions. Included studies had to assess administrative burden as we defined it. Searches were limited to studies published in English on or after January 1, 2000. Reviews, conference papers and proceedings, dissertations, commentaries, and other gray literature were excluded. Results were also excluded if they focused only on formal (paid) caregivers or caregivers for children, or if they only addressed general distress or caregiving burden without also assessing health-related tasks. Although the initial protocol specified inclusion of studies referencing time toxicity, full-text review revealed substantial heterogeneity in how the construct was defined and measured, resulting in inconsistent alignment with our conceptualization of administrative burden. In several cases, time toxicity was operationalized as the overall time devoted to caregiving or clinical activities, rather than time expended on administrative tasks. Accordingly, studies were excluded when time toxicity or time burdens did not explicitly pertain to administrative burden.

A social sciences librarian (S.M.D.) developed the search strategy to identify relevant peer-reviewed empirical studies in PubMed (National Library of Medicine, 1957-present), Embase (Elsevier, 1947-present), Scopus (Elsevier, 1788-present), Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) with Full Text (EBSCOhost, 1937-present), and ProQuest Healthcare Administration (ProQuest, 1911-present). A systematic search strategy was developed in PubMed in an iterative manner and translated for the remaining databases upon finalization. All database searches were conducted on July 3, 2025. The search strategy was validated against benchmark citations identified prior to searching.13,14,29-32

Search strategies included a combination of controlled vocabulary, when available, and keywords related to (1) caregiving and (2) administrative burdens and time toxicity. Due to the range of words used to describe informal caregivers, keywords included “caregivers” and “care partners” as well as broader terms related to relationships such as “family” and “spouse”. Keywords relating to administrative burdens included various administrative tasks such as prior authorization, scheduling appointments, care documentation, and managing insurance claims. Phrases such as “time spent” or “investing time” were used in conjunction with the task-specific keywords to increase precision of search results.

The complexity and limited searchability of the administrative burden concept presented challenges in developing the search. As a result, the preliminary search in the protocol was revised. Each search strategy (see Online Supplement) was peer-reviewed by a health sciences librarian. Full search strategies for all databases are available in the Online Supplement. Forward and backward citation chaining of included studies supplemented the bibliographic database searches. The team also hand-searched the bibliography of a related scoping review,8 producing 2 additional included studies.

Stage 3: study selection

Results were imported into EndNote 21 for deduplication33 and then imported into Covidence systematic review software for screening. Additional duplicates were detected using Covidence.

Research team members participated in a 2-step screening process and received study-specific training prior to beginning work. Three reviewers (D.K.K., B.T., T.T.) screened titles and abstracts for inclusion. Each abstract was reviewed independently by 2 reviewers, with a third reviewer resolving disagreements as needed. All citations potentially meeting the inclusion criteria were screened in the full text stage by 2 team members independently (D.K.K., B.T., V.E.S., L.V.G., B.R., T.T.), with a third resolving disagreements as needed. The screening process was repeated for all citations acquired through hand searching and forward and backward citation chaining.

Stage 4: charting the data

The team developed a data extraction template and refined it after 3 team members (D.K.K., B.T., T.T.) reviewed and reached consensus on one article. Data extracted included the health condition(s) of care recipients, caregiver sample size, aims, study design, inclusion of a guiding theory or framework (ie, a theory, framework, or conceptual model mentioned in Introduction or Methods as shaping the research), definition of administrative burden (if given), findings related to administrative burden, and type(s) of burden described based on the Herd and Moynihan Framework.9 Information was extracted from articles by 2 team members independently, with either the senior or first author (B.T. or T.T.) reviewing all information and resolving any disagreements.

Stage 5: summarizing and reporting the results

Extracted information for all included studies was exported from Covidence and used to create a table (Table 1) with detailed information about each record. After an initial version of the table was drafted, 3 team members (T.T., B.T., D.K.K.) mapped each study's findings onto the 3 types of costs (learning, compliance, psychological) described by Herd and Moynihan,10 with 2 team members categorizing each study independently and a third team member verifying those categorizations. Information from Table 1 was used to create a narrative synthesis of findings (see below) as mapped onto learning, compliance, and psychological costs.

Results

A total of 4406 abstracts were identified through database searching, and 1295 duplicates were removed. Records were also identified through citation chaining (n = 1257) and hand searching (n = 3). Seventy-three records were retrieved for full-text review. Of these, 18 records met inclusion criteria for this review. The majority (56%) of studies involved caregivers for cancer patients/cancer survivors. Other patient health conditions included dementia (17%), multiple sclerosis (6%), stroke (6%), or a range of chronic conditions (17%). Sample sizes ranged from 11 to 530 caregivers, with most studies (83%) having a sample size smaller than 100. A minority of studies (39%) were informed by a theory or conceptual framework described in the Introduction or Methods. Of these, there was no predominant theory or framework; only social ecological theory51 and Sherwood's theory of caring for patients with brain tumors52 were mentioned in more than one paper (each was cited twice). No authors mentioned the Herd and Moynihan Administrative Burden Framework9 in the Introduction or Methods sections, although one article did cite it in the Discussion.14

Most included studies (56%) used qualitative methods; there were no longitudinal or intervention studies. The most common method for data collection was the use of a semi-structured interview guide in qualitative studies. Three quantitative studies used all or part of the Oberst Caregiving Burden Scale.24

Of the 18 included articles, the majority (89%) addressed compliance costs. In addition, half (50%) addressed learning costs, and half (50%) addressed psychological costs. The majority of included articles (61%) addressed more than one type of burden. Tasks commonly mentioned as burdensome included understanding terminology, communicating with healthcare professionals, dealing with transportation, and handling bills and paperwork.

Types of administrative burden in included studies.

Learning costs

Nine articles showed that learning about resources and obtaining information about benefits was a challenge for caregivers.35,40,47 In a study of caregivers for people with dementia, many caregivers did not apply for Medicaid because they believed they would not qualify; many also did not attempt to determine eligibility.39 Caregivers of patients with a chronic illness reported lacking guidance on insurance and healthcare benefits.40 A majority of caregivers for patients with brain tumors said it was important to understand benefit/medical insurance coverage, but more than half were dissatisfied with how that need was met.42 Rural caregivers for people with age-related health conditions reported not using services because they did not know where to find them or did not know which services were available.43

Compliance costs

Compliance costs were reported in the vast majority of included articles (89%), illustrating how the administrative tasks caregivers often perform are complicated by the fragmentation of the US healthcare system across providers, systems, and payers. Compliance activities involved scheduling appointments,43 communicating with healthcare professionals,30,45 transporting patients to appointments,34,35 and, most commonly, dealing with health insurance, paperwork, and bills.30,38,39,40,43 The included articles suggested that these activities were widespread for caregivers. In one quantitative study of caregivers of adults with cancer, over two-thirds (69%) reported helping with insurance problems and 54% reported helping with medical bills and insurance.38 A survey of caregivers for people with age-related health conditions found 83% reported dealing with medical-related paperwork.43

Psychological costs

In the 9 articles that addressed psychological costs, such costs never occurred in isolation but were linked to learning costs, compliance costs, or both. The fragmented US healthcare system was often a source of frustration to caregivers.14,30,46 In particular, caregivers found learning about resources and complying with system requirements challenging as they helped patients access care. These costs were described by caregivers in emotionally charged qualitative interviews. For example, one family member of a patient who had had life-sustaining care removed said, “the fragmentation of care is scary as hell.”48 In another study, a caregiver for a patient with advanced gastrointestinal cancer described the link between compliance costs and psychological costs: “And it's those things [phone calls with insurance companies] that are the worst, right? They’re not just small sort of regular tasks. They’re like just awful, you know, soul-sucking kind of tasks that can take a long time and you might not have an answer at the end.”46 In another qualitative study, a caregiver of an individual with cancer shared similar experiences: “I would spend anywhere from an hour to four hours a week on the phone [with insurance] trying to get things to happen….. My frustration level it's amazing—I don’t know my blood pressure—because my frustration level was off the charts. I mean, it was absolutely absurd.”30

Interventions

Although no studies formally tested an intervention designed to reduce administrative burden for caregivers, findings from 2 studies suggested potential interventions that could be effective. In one study, caregivers reported they would find services that helped with paperwork beneficial.43 In another study, caregivers reported that telehealth and other “remote interventions” designed to reduce patients' exposure to infection during the COVID-19 pandemic—including prescription delivery, remote monitoring, and streamlined paperwork/bureaucracy from government agencies—had the added benefit of making processes easier and reducing the administrative burden of caregivers as well as patients.49

Discussion

This review provides an important first step in characterizing the literature about informal caregivers' experiences of health-related administrative burden. It offers a caregiver complement to 2 recent reviews examining adult patients' experiences of administrative burden8,11 and shows that, consistent with the Burden of Treatment Theory,21 the challenges of treatment often extend to members of patients' social networks. In the 18 studies included in this review, our team found that the 3 costs of administrative burden (learning, compliance, and psychological) described by Herd and Moynihan9 often overlapped and created challenges for caregivers. Similar to Ilea and Ilea,8 we found that compliance costs (eg, the hassle of completing paperwork) were most commonly assessed, suggesting that these costs may be a core part of what is considered administrative burden in the empirical literature. These findings take on a new urgency in light of recent policy changes that are likely to weaken the care economy, exacerbate burdens on informal caregivers, and increase administrative burden across multiple sectors.53,54

Our findings demonstrate the need for further work to measure and characterize administrative burden. The most common mode of data collection in the included studies was semi-structured interviews, which yielded qualitative data with rich insights, particularly around the psychological costs that arise in conjunction with learning and compliance activities. However, we found no commonly used standardized measures designed specifically to assess caregivers' experiences of administrative burden. This is consistent with previous work noting a similar lack of standardized measures of patient-facing administrative burden.11 Although time use data may capture some activities associated with administrative burden (eg, obtaining medical care for other adults), it does not assess the experience of learning, compliance, or psychological costs.55 Standardized measurement of administrative burden would allow for repeated measurement across time as well comparisons of levels of administrative burden across different conditions or populations. Developing this knowledge base will be necessary to identify particular populations or time points for intervention.

Future research would also benefit from a grounding in established or novel theoretical and conceptual models related to administrative burden. Herd and Moynihan's Administrative Burden Framework9 is a useful starting point, but none of the included articles pointed to that framework as guiding the research. The framework developed by Kyle and colleagues11 may be useful in further understanding how administrative burden changes across the treatment process, including finding information, obtaining care, and navigating challenges that arise. These models could also incorporate caregiver-specific elements by integrating aspects of social networks included in the Burden of Treatment Theory or detailing ways caregivers contribute to patients' administrative capital.21,56

Although we found no studies testing interventions to reduce administrative burden for caregivers, 2 studies had clear implications for intervention. Reblin and colleagues43 found that caregivers were receptive to the idea of interventions to help with paperwork. This suggests the potential for future individual-level or dyadic interventions to help caregivers or caregiver-patient dyads handle issues of compliance and reduce their psychological distress. In addition, Yan and colleagues49 found that policy changes designed to streamline processes during the COVID-19 pandemic reduced administrative burden in caregivers. Their findings point to policy as an important lever in reducing administrative burden, suggesting that improved compliance-related processes may produce benefits throughout a patient's social network. Since types of administrative burden tended to overlap for caregivers, such benefits may occur across multiple domains (eg, interventions to reduce compliance costs might also reduce psychological costs). Interventions, regardless of the target population, must take care not to place additional administrative burden on patients and caregivers and should be one component of broader systemic changes to limit the complexity of the healthcare system. Such efforts can include those highlighted by Yan and colleagues (eg, streamlined paperwork)49 as well as other strategies to improve price transparency and curtail payer interference in the patient-provider relationship.

We focused on informal (unpaid) caregivers in this review because such caregivers provide the bulk of care for adults in the United States with chronic conditions.20 At the same time, we acknowledge recent efforts to compensate family caregivers, as well as the interface between paid and unpaid caregivers.20,57 We also note that authors of some included articles were more specific than others in defining caregiving. We encourage future researchers to explicitly define caregiving and consider which characteristics of caregivers (eg, family role, professional status) are most important to their research questions.

Our review has several strengths. We have responded to prior calls to advance the field by characterizing caregivers' healthcare-related administrative burden.8,11 We worked with a research librarian to conduct our search. The team used several strategies to ensure rigor, including training for research team members, standardized data extraction instruments, and having multiple team members assess records at every stage of the process.

Limitations

Several limitations should also be noted. Due to differences between caregiving for adults vs children, we did not include studies of pediatric populations. Given the uniquely fragmented nature of US healthcare system, we also did not examine studies conducted outside the United States. We acknowledge that the definition and measurement of administrative burden are still a work in progress, and we may have missed studies that addressed the concept but did not match our search terms. Likewise, our team may have excluded records that others would have considered within the scope of the concept.

Conclusion

This work represents a key step in characterizing the overlapping costs of administrative burden in informal caregivers. Others have described ways that both administrative burdens and reliance on informal supports can exacerbate inequality58,59; although our findings about caregivers' experiences of burden suggest that negative consequences may compound among vulnerable dyads, further empirical work is needed to examine these effects. To ensure patients receive equitable and timely care, it is critical that policymakers, healthcare administrators, and health services researchers collaborate to standardize a definition of administrative burden and assess levels of burden systematically in patients and caregivers. Such work will lay the foundation for designing and testing interventions aimed at reducing such barriers to care and ensuring patients and their caregivers are supported as they navigate the US healthcare system.
