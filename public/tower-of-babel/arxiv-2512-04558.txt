On the Limits of Test-Time Compute: Sequential Reward Filtering for Better Inference
Yue Yu and Qiwei Di and Quanquan Gu and Dongruo Zhou

Abstract
Test-time compute (TTC) has become an increasingly prominent paradigm for enhancing large language models (LLMs). Despite the empirical success of methods such as best-of- n n (BoN) sampling and sequential revision, their fundamental limits remain unclear. We address this gap by analyzing a mixture-of-reference policy model and proving that standard BoN is inherently suboptimal. To move closer to the optimal frontier, we study reward-filtered sequential inference, a simple procedure that selectively incorporates only high-reward generations into the context. This mechanism concentrates computation on superior policy candidates and suppresses inferior ones. On the theoretical side, we show that reward-filtered sequential inference yields strictly stronger guarantees than standard TTC paradigms. On the empirical side, we evaluate such an inference strategy across diverse benchmarks and observe consistent improvements over widely used approaches, demonstrating the practical effectiveness of our framework.

Introduction

How to effectively utilize large language models (LLMs) for solving new tasks has become a central research question. Among the many approaches, Test-Time Compute (TTC) has recently attracted significant attention. The key idea of TTC is to allocate additional computation during inference to improve task performance. Unlike post-training approaches such as fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, TTC requires no additional training of the base model. As a result, inference-time alignment methods provide a lightweight yet powerful alternative that greatly simplifies deployment. Well-known TTC methods include Best-of-N (BoN) sampling, chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, and their many variants
(
54
;
43
;
59
;

;
71
;

;

)
.

In this work, we specifically focus on a family of TTC methods that leverage an external reward model, which can assign a score to each generated answer. Perhaps the most basic and widely studied approach in this family is BoN, a representative strategy of
parallel TTC
. Given a problem
x
, the LLM generates
n
answers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, and then selects the one with the highest reward score. BoN is highly intuitive, easy to implement, and has been empirically shown to substantially outperform vanilla decoding across a wide range of tasks. Naturally, this raises a fundamental question:

Is Best-of-N the best one can hope for under a fixed test-time budget?

Recent work
(
52
)
suggests that the answer may be negative. Unlike BoN, which allocates its test-time budget in parallel by generating multiple independent samples, another line of research explores
sequential TTC
. In sequential TTC, generated answers are inserted back into the input window of the LLM, effectively reshaping its conditional distribution as more computation is spent. Empirically, this approach has demonstrated performance gains beyond BoN by gradually biasing the model towards higher-quality outputs. However, despite its practical promise, the theoretical understanding of sequential TTC remains very limited. This motivates our second central question:

What is the best achievable strategy under sequential TTC?

In this work, we formalize TTC with reward models as a decision problem over this mixture-of-reference-policies model and use it to address both questions above. Conceptually, our goals are two-fold: (i) characterize the best-achievable regret under a fixed test-time budget for parallel TTC algorithms such as BoN, and (ii) design a sequential TTC procedure that, using the same reward model, provably attains a strictly better budget–performance trade-off. Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Limits of parallel TTC.
We study the fundamental limit of TTC under a
mixture-of-reference policies
model, where the pretraining data of the LLM is assumed to consist of trajectories generated by multiple underlying policies. Within this setting, we establish a lower bound on the test-time budget required to achieve a near-optimal policy and show that parallel TTC methods, including BoN, fall short of this bound, indicating that despite its popularity, parallel TTC is not optimal under more realistic modeling assumptions.

•

A reward-filtered sequential TTC method.
To go beyond parallel TTC, we study a simple sequential TTC method called
Reward-Filtered Sequential Best-of-n
(RF-SeqBoN), where only high-reward generations are fed back into the LLM’s input window. This procedure progressively refines the generation distribution towards that of the optimal policy induced by the reward function. We prove that, under mild assumptions on the reward model, our method strictly improves upon parallel TTC and achieves significantly larger gains on harder tasks.

•

Experiments.
We conduct extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks and backbone LLMs. Empirical results consistently demonstrate that our approach achieves higher
test-time budget efficiency
than existing baselines, confirming that the theoretical advantages of our method translate into substantial practical improvements.

In the rest of our paper, we formalize the sequential sample-and-evaluate framework for TTC with reward models and specify our mixture-of-reference-policies pretraining assumption in Section

Related Work

Parallel TTC.

Several lines of work have explored parallel TTC. Broadly, two strategies have emerged. The first is based on the self-consistency approach, where multiple answers are generated in parallel from one or more LLMs and the final output is chosen by majority vote

6

7

(
53
;
25
;
46

(

11
)
.
72

within

In our experiments, we carefully control for identical verifiers and token budgets so that improvements cannot be attributed solely to stronger re-rankers, and they have been verified through multiple budget levels
(
61
)
.

Sequential TTC.

Unlike parallel TTC, sequential TTC explicitly decides
when
to spend extra steps and
where
to revise. Classical CoT techniques
(
10
;
70
;
33
;
67
)
have been extensively studied without relying on external reward models. With reward models, several sequential TTC approaches have been explored. For example,
42
propose a few-shot BoN method that leverages a powerful external LLM to generate demonstrations and then selects answers via BoN. Iterative self-refinement and policy-as-verifier approaches provide alternative architectures that can be used as verifiers to trigger revisions
(
38
;
27

(
68

(

29

(
30

Theory of TTC.

A growing theory literature clarifies when extra samples help, when they hurt, and how to apportion TTC. For BoN,
4
correct the folklore
\mathrm{KL}
identity and bound win-rate improvements, while

By contrast, our work focuses on establishing a separation result between parallel and sequential TTC, and on developing an algorithm that uses reward signals to guide sequential TTC—a direction largely absent from prior work.

Preliminaries

Task Description.

We assume access to a large language model (LLM)
\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h)
, which receives an input sequence
h\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
, and then outputs a distribution over subsequent sequences. In our setting, we restrict the output sequences to lie in
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be the prompt set and
p_{\text{ini}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
denote a distribution over initial prompts. In each round, we first draw
x\sim p_{\text{ini}}
. Then an algorithm
Alg
interacts with the language model
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
for multiple times before producing a final action
\widehat{a}\in\mathcal{A}
. We denote the resulting conditional distribution of
\widehat{a}
by
\pi_{\text{Alg}}(\cdot\mid x)
. We assume a reward function
r:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
, about which we make the following assumption:

Assumption 3.1
.

Given any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
a^{\star}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r(a,x)
is unique. Meanwhile,
r(a^{\star}(x),x)=1
.

Because the output space
\mathcal{A}
can be extremely large, it is infeasible to explicitly evaluate either the language model
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
or the reward function
r
over all possible candidates. Instead, we assume that our algorithm operates under the following sequential sample-and-evaluate framework:

Definition 3.2

(
Sequential
sample-and-evaluate framework, generalized from
23
)
.

For a given prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the algorithm may sequentially issue
n
queries
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. For each
i
, it samples
a_{i}\sim\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})
,

and observes the reward value
r(a_{i},x)
. The efficiency (query complexity) of the algorithm is measured by the total number of queries
n
.

Remark 3.3
.

23
studied a more restricted setting of the sample-and-evaluate framework,
where one can only sample actions from a fixed prompt
x
, i.e., from
\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)
.
While conceptually simple, this framework largely ignores the internal structure and sequential
nature of LLMs.

We evaluate performance with respect to a
comparator policy

\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. Given a prompt
x
and comparator policy
\pi^{\star}
, we define the regret of an action
\widehat{a}
as

\displaystyle\text{Regret}(\widehat{a};x,\pi^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x)}\big[r(a,x)-r(\widehat{a},x)\big].

An action
\widehat{a}
is
\epsilon
-optimal with respect to
\pi^{*}
if
\text{Regret}(\widehat{a};x,\pi^{\star})\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in[0,1]
.
When
\widehat{a}
is generated by an algorithm
Alg
, we write
\text{Regret}(\text{Alg};x,\pi^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{a}\sim\pi_{\text{Alg}}(\cdot\mid x)}\text{Regret}(\widehat{a};x,\pi^{\star})
.
Our goal is to design algorithms that, for prompts drawn from
p_{\text{ini}}
, achieve small regret with respect to
\pi^{\star}
while minimizing the
sample complexity
, defined as the total number of times that the algorithm queries
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
.

Pretraining of LLM.

Next, we impose a structural assumption on the pretraining of
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
. Specifically, we assume access to a pretraining dataset
\mathcal{D}
, consisting of trajectories collected from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. Formally, there exists
a finite family of reference policies

\{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x):\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{ref}}\}
,
where
\mathcal{T}_{\text{ref}}
is a finite index set and

p_{\text{ref}}
denotes the prior distribution reflecting the proportion of data contributed by each reference policy.
In practice, different reference policies may correspond to distinct answer-generation styles in the pretraining corpus. For instance, some may produce concise responses while others generate more elaborate or vivid explanations. These stylistic variations naturally form a finite collection of reference policies contributing to the dataset.
The trajectories
h^{t}=(x^{t},a_{1}^{t},\dots,a_{N}^{t})
in the pretraining dataset
\mathcal{D}
are generated as follows:

•

Sample an initial prompt
x^{t}\sim p_{\text{ini}}
and a reference index
\tau^{t}\sim p_{\text{ref}}
. Initialize the history
h_{0}^{t}=x^{t}
.

•

For
i=1,\dots,N
, sequentially draw an action
a_{i}^{t}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{t}}(\cdot\mid x^{t})

and update the history as
h_{i}^{t}=h_{i-1}^{t}\cup(a_{i}^{t})
.

•

Set
h^{t}=h_{N}^{t}
and add it to
\mathcal{D}
.

Remark 3.4
.

In our assumption for pretraining data, all actions within the same trajectory are sampled from the same distribution
\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x)
. Our assumption on the pretraining distribution is aligned with those commonly made for in-context learning
(
62
;
69
)
. In particular, our pretraining dataset does not contain any reward information. This contrasts with the in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) literature
(
36
;
56
;
32
)
, which typically assumes that rewards are included in the pretraining data.

We then interpret the pretrained LLM
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
as being trained on
\mathcal{D}
and we call
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
as a
mixture of reference policy model
since it consists of trajectories drawn from different reference policies. Conceptually, when the pretraining length
T
and the number of data
N
grow to infinity,
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
converges to the conditional distribution induced by the data-generating process
(
32
)
. We formalize this as the following assumption.

Assumption 3.5

(Realizability of
\pi_{\text{LLM}}
)
.

For any trajectory
h\in\mathcal{V}^{*}
, we have
\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid h)
,
where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid h)
denotes the true conditional distribution of the next action
given history
h
under the data-generating process defined by sampling
\tau\sim p_{\text{ref}}
,

x\sim p_{\text{ini}}
, and actions from
\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x)
.

Theorem 4.3

(Lower Bound of Sequential Sample-and Evaluate Algorithms)
.

Suppose the comparator policy
\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x):=a^{\star}(x)
is a deterministic policy. Let
\tau^{\star}(x)\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\text{ref}}

\tau^{\star}(x)\in\arg\max_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\text{ref}}}\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(a^{\star}(x)\mid x)

Meanwhile, let
M^{x,\epsilon}_{\tau^{\star}(x)}:=M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}

•

For any sequential sample-and-evaluate algorithm

such that if

\text{Regret}(A;x,\pi^{\star})>\epsilon

•

\epsilon

Proof Sketch.

First, recall the definition of

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid h)=\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\text{ref}}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\cdot\mid h,\tau)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau\mid h)=\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\text{ref}}}\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau\mid h).

(4.1)

Therefore, for any input sequence

Comparison Against Top-k Algorithm.

In addition to the BoN and PureSeq baselines, we also conducted ablation study to compare RF-SeqBoN against a
Top-k
refinement strategy, which does not use an explicit reward threshold. Both methods can be viewed as instantiations of the SeqBoN meta-algorithm (Algorithm
1
): at iteration
i
, the algorithm samples a new answer from
\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}\left(\cdot|h_{i}\right)
and then updates the history based on the observed reward. For Top-k, we maintain all previously generated answers and their reward scores for a given question and construct
h_{i}
by appending the top-
k
responses ranked by reward, subject to the same sliding-window truncation used for PureSeq and RF-SeqBoN. To ensure a fair comparison, we set
k=3
, matching the
history_budget=3
used for all sequential TTC algorithms, so that the two methods differ only in whether history is defined by a relative ranking (Top-k) or by crossing a fixed reward threshold
\gamma
(RF-SeqBoN). The experiment is conducted on MATH500 dataset with Qwen3–4B–Instruct backbone model and the Llama3.1–8B–PRM–Deepseek–Data reward model, using the same configuration as above.

Figure
3
(c) reports accuracy as a function of generation budget
N
for RF-SeqBoN and Top-k. Across all budgets, RF-SeqBoN consistently outperforms Top-k, with the margin being most pronounced in the low- and mid-budget regimes (small
N
) and gradually shrinking as
N
increases. This pattern aligns with the intuition that, when the generation budget is limited, Top-k is prone to retaining several “locally good” but in fact incorrect trajectories: on difficult problems where genuinely high-reward responses are rare, the top-
k
pool may consist entirely of low-quality yet relatively better generations. Once such spurious but high-ranked solutions are repeatedly recycled into the context, the method effectively degenerates toward PureSeq, reinforcing an erroneous reasoning trace instead of exploring alternative solution paths. By contrast, RF-SeqBoN discards
all
responses whose reward falls below
\gamma
, preventing these degenerate feedback loops and ensuring that only truly high-quality trajectories are used as in-context exemplars. This selective reuse appears particularly beneficial when
N
is small, precisely the setting where every history slot must be used most judiciously.

7
Conclusion

We presented a principled framework for TTC under a mixture-of-policies model and derived fundamental limits on the budget required for near-optimal performance. Our analysis shows that parallel TTC strategies such as BoN are inherently suboptimal. To address this, we proposed
Reward-Filtered Sequential Best-of-
n
(RF-SeqBoN)
, which selectively incorporates high-reward generations and provably refines the distribution toward the optimal policy. Experiments across diverse benchmarks confirm that RF-SeqBoN offers both stronger theoretical guarantees and substantial empirical gains in budget efficiency.

denote the distribution induced by the adaptive rejection sampling algorithm
\texttt{RejectionSampling}_{n}((w_{i},p_{i},M_{i})_{i=1}^{n},x)
(Algorithm
4
) with importance weights
w_{i}(a):=\pi^{\star}(a)/p_{i}(a)
. Then the total variation distance between
\pi^{\star}
and
\pi_{R}
can be upper bounded by:

\mathrm{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi_{R})\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}[\mathcal{E}_{i}]\;+\;2\alpha_{n+1},

where
\mathcal{E}_{i}:=\mathcal{E}_{M_{i}}(\pi^{\star},p_{i})
, and
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n+1}
is a sequence of positive weights satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}=1
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1}\leq\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{M_{i}}\bigg)\bigg].

If we set

M_{i}=M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot,x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}
, we have
\mathcal{E}_{i}\leq\epsilon
due to Definition
4.1
. Then the
TV
distance can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi_{R})\leq\epsilon+\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}}\bigg)\bigg].

(B.1)

Proof.

For simplicity, we fix
x
and ignore all the
x
dependence. We denote
p_{i}(\cdot):=\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})
.
Following the proof of Lemma D.4 in
23
, we consider the truncated pseudo-distribution

\pi_{i}(a):=\min\{\pi^{\star}(a),\,M_{i}p_{i}(a)\},

and
A_{i}:=\sum_{a}\pi_{i}(a)\leq 1
is the total mass of
\pi_{i}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\min\{\pi^{\star}(a),\,M_{i}p_{i}(a)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\notin\mathcal{A}_{i}}\pi^{\star}(a)+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}M_{i}p_{i}(a)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\Big[\pi^{\star}(a)-M_{i}p_{i}(a)\Big].

where
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\pi^{*}(a)>M_{i}p_{i}(a)\}
. Recalling the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{M_{i}}(\pi^{\star},p_{i})
in Definition
4.1
, we have

\quad\mathcal{E}_{i}:=\mathcal{E}_{M_{i}}(\pi^{\star},p_{i})=1-A_{i}.

Following the proof of Lemma D.4 in
23
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{a^{\prime}\sim p_{i}}(a^{\prime}=a\mid\xi_{i}=1)=\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}.

From now on, we begin to prove the original statement.
For
a_{i}\sim p_{i}
, let

q_{i}:=\min\Bigl\{\tfrac{\pi^{\star}(a_{i})}{M_{i}p_{i}(a_{i})},\,1\Bigr\}
be the probability of acceptance at step
i
.
We define

\alpha_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i}}\big[(1-q_{1})\cdots(1-q_{i-1})q_{i}\big],\quad\alpha_{n+1}:=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\big[(1-q_{1})\cdots(1-q_{n})\big],

so that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}=1
.
Let
i^{\star}
denote the index at which Algorithm
4
stops, and let
\widehat{a}
denote the output.

Hence,

\displaystyle\pi_{R}(a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\widehat{a}=a)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(\widehat{a}=a\mid i^{\star}=i)\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=i)}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\widehat{a}=a\mid i^{\star}=n+1)\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=n+1)}_{T_{2}}.

(B.2)

For the first term
T_{1}
, we compute

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}}\mathbb{P}_{a^{\prime}\sim p_{i}}(a^{\prime}=a\mid\xi_{i}=1)\,\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=i\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}}\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{i}}(1-q_{1})\cdots(1-q_{i-1})q_{i}}_{\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\bigg].

(B.3)

For the second term
T_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=n+1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=n+1\mid a_{1},\dots,a_{n})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}(1-q_{1})\cdots(1-q_{n})}_{\alpha_{n+1}}.

(B.4)

Substituting (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) into (
B.2
), we have

\displaystyle|\pi^{\star}(a)-\pi_{R}(a)|

\displaystyle\leq|\pi^{\star}(a)-T_{1}|+T_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}\pi^{\star}(a)-\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\bigg]\bigg|+\alpha_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\pi^{\star}(y)-\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\bigg]\bigg|+2\alpha_{n+1},

where the second inequality uses
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\alpha_{i}=1
.
Now consider the total variation distance:

\displaystyle\text{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi_{R})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a}\big|\pi^{\star}(a)-\pi_{R}(a)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\pi^{\star}(a)-\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}(y)}{A_{i}}\bigg]\bigg|+2\alpha_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}\bigg|\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\Big(\pi^{\star}(a)-\tfrac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\Big)\bigg]\bigg|+2\alpha_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\sum_{a}\Big|\pi^{\star}(a)-\tfrac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\Big|\bigg]+2\alpha_{n+1},

(B.5)

where the last inequality holds due to the triangle inequality.
Finally, for each
i
, we bound

\displaystyle\sum_{a}\Big|\pi^{\star}(a)-\tfrac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}\big|\pi^{\star}(a)-\pi_{i}(a)\big|+\sum_{a}\Big|\pi_{i}(a)-\tfrac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}(\pi^{\star}(a)-M_{i}p_{i}(a))+\sum_{a}\Big(\tfrac{\pi_{i}(a)}{A_{i}}-\pi_{i}(a)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{i}+(1-A_{i})

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{E}_{i},

(B.6)

where
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\pi^{*}(a)>M_{i}p_{i}(a)\}
. The first equation holds due to
\pi_{i}(a):=\min\{\pi^{\star}(a),\,M_{i}p_{i}(a)\}
and
A_{i}\leq 1
. The second equation holds due to
\sum_{a}\pi_{i}(a)=A_{i}
. The last equation holds due to
\mathcal{E}_{i}=1-A_{i}
.
Substituting (
B.6
) into (
B.5
) yields

\displaystyle\text{TV}(\pi^{\star},\pi_{R})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}[\mathcal{E}_{i}]+2\alpha_{n+1}.

(B.7)

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1}=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(1-\frac{A_{i}}{M_{i}}\bigg)\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(1-\frac{1-\mathcal{E}_{i}}{M_{i}}\bigg)\bigg].

(B.8)

Finally, setting
M_{i}=M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}
we have
\mathcal{E}_{i}\leq\epsilon
, and

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(1-\frac{1-\mathcal{E}_{i}}{M_{i}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{M_{i}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}}\bigg)\bigg]

(B.9)

where we use
1-x\leq\exp(-x),\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
and Definition
4.1
. Combining (
B.8
) and (
B.1
), we complete the proof of Theorem
B.1
.
∎

Using this result, now we can prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

let
\pi_{R}
be the auxilliary distribution defined in Theorem

. To begin with, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{abon}}}[r(a,x)]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{R}}[r(a,x)]}_{I_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{R}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{abon}}}[r(a,x)]}_{I_{2}}.

, we have

.

B.2
Proof of Proposition
B.2

Proposition B.2
.

Suppose the following conditions hold for any
x
: there exists some embedding
\phi(a,x)
such that

•

The reward function
r(\cdot,x)
is smooth at
a^{\star}(x)
w.r.t.
\phi(a,x)
.

•

For any
{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\text{ref}}}
, the reference policy
\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x)
is
L
-smooth w.r.t.
\phi(a,x)
.

•

There exists
\Delta(x)>0
such that
\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a^{\star}(x)\mid x)-\sup_{\tau\neq\tau^{\star}(x)}\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(a^{\star}(x)\mid x)>\Delta(x).

Then there exists a threshold
\gamma^{\star}(x)<1
such that Assumption
5.4
holds.

Proposition
B.2
provides a sufficient condition for Assumption
5.4
.
The smoothness of
r
and
\pi_{\text{ref}}
ensures that in a neighborhood of
a^{\star}(x)
, both the reward value and the probability gap between
\tau^{\star}(x)
and other reference policies vary continuously.
Since the margin at
a^{\star}(x)
is strictly positive (
>\Delta(x)
), this gap persists in a small neighborhood around
a^{\star}(x)
.
Consequently, there exists a reward threshold
\gamma^{\star}(x)<1
such that any action with
r(a,x)\geq\gamma^{\star}(x)
lies within this neighborhood and inherits the same probability margin.
Intuitively, if the optimal action is well separated from all others in terms of generation probability, and both the reward and policy distributions change smoothly, this separation extends to all sufficiently high-reward actions.

Proof of Proposition
B.2
.

Since
r
is smooth on the point
a^{\star}(x)
, then there must exists
\gamma^{*}(x)
such that

\displaystyle r(a^{\star}(x),x)-r(a,x)<1-\gamma^{*}(x)\Rightarrow\|\phi(a,x)-\phi(a^{\star}(x),x)\|\leq\Delta/(4L).

(B.10)

Then, due to smoothness assumption on
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)-\sup_{\tau\neq\tau^{\star}(x)}\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(a\mid x)

\displaystyle\geq\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a^{\star}(x)\mid x)-L\|\phi(a,x)-\phi(a^{\star}(x),x)\|

\displaystyle\quad-\sup_{\tau\neq\tau^{\star}(x)}\Big(\log\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(a^{\star}(x)\mid x)\Big)-L\|\phi(a,x)-\phi(a^{\star}(x),x)\|

\displaystyle>\Delta-2L\|\phi(a,x)-\phi(a^{\star}(x),x)\|

\displaystyle\geq\Delta/2.

∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem
5.5

We first need the following lemma modified from Lemma C.3 in
22
:

Lemma B.3
.

For any
i\in[n]
, let
k
denote the length of
h_{i}
, i.e., the number of actions in
h_{i}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\text{LLM}}(a\mid h_{i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)}>\frac{1}{1+e^{-k\Delta}/p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}.

Proof of Lemma
B.3
.

For simplicity, let
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
denote the indices of
a_{i}
that have been put into
h
.
By the product factorization and Assumption
5.4
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau)}{\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau^{\star}(x))}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(a_{i_{j}}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a_{i_{j}}\mid x)}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-\Delta)=-k\Delta,

(B.11)

which yields the claim after exponentiation.

Next, by the assumption of the pretraining distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
, the predictive policy of the LLM is the Bayes mixture

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})=\sum_{\tau}\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau}(\cdot\mid x)\cdot w_{i}(\tau),\qquad w_{i}(\tau):=\frac{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau)\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau)}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\prime})\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau^{\prime})}.

(B.12)

Using the likelihood–ratio bound (
B.11
), for any
\tau\neq\tau^{\star}(x)
,

\displaystyle\frac{w_{i}(\tau)}{w_{i}(\tau^{\star}(x))}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau)}{\mathbb{P}(h_{i}\mid\tau^{\star}(x))}\cdot\frac{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}\leq e^{-k\Delta}\cdot\frac{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}.

(B.13)

Summing over
\{\tau:\tau\neq\tau^{\star}(x)\}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1-w_{m}\big(\tau^{\star}(x)\big)}{w_{m}\big(\tau^{\star}(x)\big)}\leq e^{-m\Delta}\cdot\frac{1-p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}{p_{\text{ref}}(\tau)}.

Then it is easy to obtain that

\displaystyle w_{i}(\tau^{\star}(x))\geq 1-\frac{1-p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}{1+p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))(e^{k\Delta}-1)}>\frac{1}{1+e^{-k\Delta}/p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}

(B.14)

Therefore, for any action
a
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\text{LLM}}(a\mid h_{i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)}\geq w_{i}(\tau^{\star}(x))\geq\frac{1}{1+e^{-k\Delta}/p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}.

(B.15)

∎

Next we have our overall proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Proof of Theorem
5.5
.

Denote
i^{\star}
be the first index
i
satisfying
|h_{i}|=m
. Here we allow
i^{\star}>n
, in which case
|h_{i}|<m
for any
1\leq i\leq n
. By the bound established in Lemma
B.3
and the selection of
m={\log[\epsilon^{-1}p_{\text{ref}}^{-1}(\tau^{\star}(x))]}/{\Delta}
, for any
i>i^{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\text{LLM}}(a\mid h_{i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)}\geq\frac{1}{1+e^{-m\Delta}/p_{\text{ref}}(\tau^{\star}(x))}>1-\epsilon.

(B.16)

Let
M_{0}=M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}
. Using the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{M}
-divergence, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M_{0}}\big(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})\big)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\max\{0,\pi^{\star}(a\mid x)-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\cdot\pi_{\text{LLM}}(a\mid h_{i})\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\max\Big\{0,\pi^{\star}(a\mid x)-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\cdot(1-\epsilon)\cdot\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\epsilon)\cdot\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\max\Big\{0,\pi^{\star}(a\mid x)-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\cdot\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(a\mid x)\Big\}+\epsilon\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{\star}(a\mid x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{M_{0}}\big(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)\big)+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon,

where the first inequality holds due to
B.16
. The second inequality holds due to
\max\{a,b+c\}\leq\max\{a,b\}+c
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
c>0
. The third inequality holds due to
\mathcal{E}_{M_{0}}(\pi^{*},\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)})>0
. The last inequality holds because
M_{0}=M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}
is the smallest
M
such that
\mathcal{E}_{M}(\pi^{*},\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)})\leq\epsilon
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}\leq M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}.

Combined with the result of Theorem
4.3
, we have for any
i>i^{*}

\displaystyle M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid h_{i})}\leq M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\leq M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}.

(B.17)

Note that for each step
i\leq i^{\star}
, the sampled action
a_{i}
is accepted into into
h_{i}
with probability
p_{\text{LLM}}
, defined as:

\displaystyle p_{\text{LLM}}:=\mathbb{P}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}\big(r(a,x)\geq\gamma^{\star}(x)\big).

Therefore,
i^{\star}
satisfies a negative binomial distribution

, and its tail function
\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}>k)
for any

is equal to
\mathbb{P}(j^{\star}<m)
, where
j^{\star}
satisfies a Binomial distribution
B(k,p_{\text{LLM}})
, which is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}>k)=\mathbb{P}\big(B(k,p_{\text{LLM}})<m\big)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{(m-kp_{\text{LLM}})^{2}}{kp_{\text{LLM}}(1-p_{\text{LLM}})}\bigg),

(B.18)

where we use the fact that the Binomial distribution is sub-Gaussian. Therefore, by selecting

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}>\bar{n})<\epsilon.

(B.19)

Next, we analyze the original statement.

By Theorem
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{reward}}}[r(a,x)]

where the last line holds due to (

).

Next, we further decompose it based on the value of

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{reward}}}[r(a,x)]

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(i^{*}=i)\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\min\{i,n\}M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\min\{i,n\})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=2\epsilon+\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}+\sum_{i=\bar{n}+1}^{\infty}\bigg)\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=i)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\min\{i,n\}M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\min\{i,n\})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=i)\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\min\{i,n\}M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\min\{i,n\})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}>\bar{n})

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=i)\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\min\{i,n\}M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\min\{i,n\})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}>\bar{n})

\displaystyle\leq 3\epsilon+\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\bar{n}M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\bar{n})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)

(B.21)

where the second inequality holds because the exponential term is less than 1. The third inequality holds due to
M^{2\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}\leq M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}
. The fourth inequality holds due to (
B.19
) and
\min\{i,n\}\leq\bar{n}
,
M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}\geq M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}
.

When

, as

, we have

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(-\frac{n^{2}}{\bar{n}M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}+(n-\bar{n})\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{n}{M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}}\bigg).

Thus, it suffices to select
n=\text{poly}\log(1/\epsilon)\cdot M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}
.

Otherwise, using basic inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\log^{2}(1/\epsilon)\Big[M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big]^{2}+4\log(1/\epsilon)\bar{n}\Big[M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big]}

\displaystyle\leq\log(1/\epsilon)M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}+2\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}\cdot\sqrt{\bar{n}\Big[M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big]},

where we use the inequality
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
for
a,b>0
.
Therefore, when

\displaystyle n=\log(1/\epsilon)M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}+2\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}\cdot\sqrt{\bar{n}\Big[M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big]},

we have

\displaystyle n^{2}\geq\log(1/\epsilon)n+\log(1/\epsilon)\bar{n}\Big[M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{LLM}}(\cdot\mid x)}-M^{\epsilon}_{\pi^{\star}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\tau^{\star}(x)}(\cdot\mid x)}\Big].

Thus,
\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi^{\star}}[r(a,x)]-\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\text{reward}}}[r(a,x)]\leq 4\epsilon
. It suffices to select

\displaystyle n=\text{poly}\log(1/\epsilon)\Big[M^{x,\epsilon}_{\tau^{\star}(x)}+\sqrt{\bar{n}\big(M^{x,\epsilon}_{\text{LLM}}-M^{x,\epsilon}_{\tau^{\star}(x)}\big)}\Big].

∎

Appendix D
Limitation and Discussion

Reward Hacking.

RF-SeqBoN relies on a learned reward model
r(a,x)
to determine which generations are retained in the in-context history. When the reward model is well aligned with the latent objective, Assumption
5.4
ensures that high-reward actions are attributable to a single near-optimal reference policy, and our analysis shows that reward-based filtering yields improved statistical guarantees. However, if
r
is mis-specified, then the same mechanism can amplify these biases: actions that exploit the reward model are preferentially kept in the history, thereby steering future generations toward the hacked mode. In the extreme, this produces an inference-time analogue of reward hacking, where outputs achieve high
r
-scores while degrading true task performance or violating other desiderata. This effect parallels classical reward hacking and specification gaming in RL
(
2
;
14
)
and recent instances of specification gaming and reward tampering in LLMs trained with preference-based objectives
(
45
;
13
)
. Recent work highlights the importance of addressing these failure modes. Methods such as filtering using lower confidence bounds on the reward or employing question-specific reward thresholds have been proposed to counteract over-optimization
(
18
;
55
;
11
;
17
;
23
;
49
;
15
)
. Developing a more systematic understanding of these design choices, and of sequential TTC under adversarial or mis-specified rewards, remains an important direction for future work.
