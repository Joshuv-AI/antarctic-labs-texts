Molecular typing and phylogenetic analysis of Pasteurella multocida isolates from cattle and buffaloes of West Azerbaijan, Iran
Authors: Safa Farahmand-Azar, Amir Tukmechi, Abdolghaffar Ownagh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPasteurella multocida is a significant veterinary pathogen that infects cattle and buffaloes, causing respiratory and systemic diseases as well as significant financial losses. The genetic diversity of P. multocida must be understood to develop effective control methods. In order to inform disease control strategies, this work employed multilocus sequence typing (MLST) to investigate the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of P. multocida isolates from cattle and buffaloes in Iran’s West Azerbaijan Province.MethodsThree hundred seventy-eight samples were collected from 200 cattle and 178 buffalo, including nasal swabs and lung tissues. The isolates were identified by culture, biochemical testing, and PCR for the kmt1 gene. MLST was performed on seven housekeeping genes (adk, est, pmi, zwf, mdh, gdh, and pgi) to determine genetic diversity. To evaluate the evolutionary relationships between isolates, phylogenetic analysis was done.ResultsOverall, P. multocida was found in 5.62% of buffaloes and 6% of cattle. The bacteria were found to be more common in nasal swabs (5.55%) than in lung tissues (0.26%). All isolates belonged to the ST-122 sequence. Based on a phylogenetic analysis, ST-122 was found to cluster with related types (ST63, ST460, ST473, and ST322).ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate the genetic homogeneity of P. multocida isolates from West Azerbaijan Province, which is most likely associated with one source or mode of transmission. The dominance of the ST-122 type indicates its potential role in the occurrence of cattle and buffalo infections in this region. Background

Pasteurella multocida is a member of the Pasteurellaceae family, which is a significant subgroup within the class Gammaproteobacteria [1]. It is a primary or opportunistic pathogen that colonizes the upper respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts of domestic and wild animals. Under stress conditions such as poor nutrition, co-infections, or environmental challenges, it can become pathogenic. It is of global veterinary importance due to its prevalence, broad host range, and ability to cause major economic losses through treatment costs, mortality, reduced productivity, and stunted growth [2, 3].

P. multocida is serologically variable and historically classified into five capsular serogroups (A, B, D, E, and F) based on the capsular polysaccharides [4, 5] and somatic serovars based on the lipopolysaccharide antigens [6]. In cattle and buffalo, the main diseases caused by P. multocida are hemorrhagic septicemia and pneumonic pasteurellosis with high morbidity and mortality [7]. Hemorrhagic septicemia, which is associated principally with capsular serogroups B and E, is prevalent in Asia and Africa, particularly in areas with high humidity and high population density [8]. However, bovine respiratory disease (BRD) is a universal health problem in young and adult cattle, most often caused by serogroup A, and is widespread in North America and Europe [7]. In addition, P. multocida contributes to mastitis, abortion, and local infections in cattle [1].

Importantly, asymptomatic carriers play a central role in transmission. The capsular serogroups A, B, and E have been isolated from clinically healthy cattle and buffalo, with the nasopharynx and tonsils serving as reservoirs for the bacteria. Carriers shed the organism from the nasal discharge and represent a constant threat to vulnerable herds [9].

However, traditional serological methods have important limitations, including variable sensitivity and specificity of antisera, low discriminatory power, and poor correlation with the genetic or epidemiological population structures of the bacterial population [10].

Several molecular techniques have overcome the limitations of serological techniques, such as restriction endonuclease analysis (REA), ribotyping, pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), and PCR-based techniques such as ERIC-PCR and RAPD-PCR. While these approaches are effective for characterizing P. multocida isolates, widespread use of these methods is limited by the absence of standardized protocols for comparison of results among labs, hindering surveillance epidemiology [11].

Multilocus enzyme electrophoresis (MLEE) is a typing method that detects genetic variation by assessing shifts in the electrophoretic mobility of enzymes responsible for key cellular housekeeping functions [12]. MultiLocus Sequence Typing (MLST) is based on MLEE principles, which examine nucleotide sequence variations in conserved housekeeping genes in the DNA [13].

The PubMLST database hosts two distinct MLST schemes for P. multocida: the multi-host scheme and the RIRDC scheme. The multi-host MLST scheme incorporates isolates originating from a wide range of host species, including birds, pigs, sheep, and cattle. In contrast, the RIRDC MLST scheme was originally developed using isolates collected from specific regional avian populations. The P. multocida RIRDC MLST scheme is based on the sequencing of the internal fragments of seven housekeeping genes (adk, est, gdh, mdh, pgi, pmi, and zwf) [14]. Following sequencing, a nucleotide sequence is given an allelic number, and an allelic profile or sequence type (ST) is defined by a group of alleles at seven loci [15].

Because of its high resolution, reproducibility, and global comparability, MLST is considered the gold standard for phylogenetic and epidemiological analyses of bacterial pathogens [16, 17].

Despite the global importance of P. multocida, limited information exists on its genetic diversity in Iran, particularly in the West Azerbaijan Province, where cattle and buffalo farming are prevalent and respiratory disease outbreaks cause significant economic losses. The present study employed MLST to investigate the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of P. multocida strains from these animals. The resulting data can inform herd management and epidemiological surveillance.

Methods

Sample collection and sampling protocol

This observational, cross-sectional study was conducted in West Azerbaijan Province, Iran. A total of 378 samples, including 192 nasal swabs and 186 lung tissue samples, were collected from cattle and buffalo between November 2022 and January 2024. Both nasal and lung samples were taken from the same animals, although in a few cases only one sample type could be obtained. Cattle were mostly Holstein-Friesian and cross-breeds, and buffalo were of the riverine type. The animals were of an age ranging from 12 to 48 months. Five large municipal slaughterhouses in Urmia, Khoy, Salmas, Mahabad, and Miandoab were used for sampling. These slaughterhouses received animals from approximately 25 farms that were evenly distributed across the same districts. Records of slaughterhouse entries were used to gather data at the farm level. This approach ensured a uniform geographic distribution of sampled animals and provided a representative cross-section of herds throughout the province, thereby minimizing the potential for locality-based sampling bias.

Animals on each of the days of sampling were randomly selected from among those animals offered for slaughter. Systematic farm health records were not available, and no obvious respiratory signs were noted. The lungs were examined for lesions at the time of sampling, and the great preponderance (in excess of 90%) showed no lesions, while there was a small proportion presenting slight localized lesions. Prior to slaughter, each animal was restrained in a cattle chute with the assistance of trained personnel, and the external surface of the nasopharynx was disinfected with 70% ethanol to minimize contamination. A sterile cotton swab was then inserted into the nasal cavity and gently rotated along the mucosal surface to collect bacterial samples. Afterward, the swab was immediately placed in a sterile test tube containing 5 mL of tryptic soy broth (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) as a transport medium. Lung tissue samples were collected aseptically within 30 min post-mortem using sterile scalpels and placed into sterile screw-cap containers without transport medium. All samples were stored in insulated boxes containing ice packs (4 °C) and transported to the Microbiology Laboratory, Urmia University, within 4 h for further analysis [18].

All procedures involving animal handling, restraint, and sampling were performed by trained veterinary professionals in accordance with ethical standards approved by the Veterinary Ethics Committee of Urmia University (Approval No. IR-UU-AEC-3/85). The slaughtering procedures were conducted in accordance with national animal health and welfare laws, which were in line with the World Organization for Animal Health’s (OIE) recommendations on humane slaughter, and under the supervision of official veterinarians from the Iran Veterinary Organization. (IVO).

Cultivation and isolation

Initially, the samples were cultivated on 5% sheep blood agar plates (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in an aerobic environment. The plates were inspected for colony morphologies following incubation. From each plate, one to three representative colonies that varied in size, shape, or appearance were chosen, and they were then subjected to the Gram staining process (Bahar Afshan, Tehran, Iran). Colonies that appeared as Gram-negative coccobacilli were provisionally considered Pasteurella-like and selected for subculture.

To evaluate the growth characteristics, these colonies were subcultured on fresh 5% sheep blood agar and MacConkey agar (Condalab, Madrid, Spain). Isolates showing small, gray, smooth, non-hemolytic colonies on blood agar and no growth on MacConkey agar were considered to be presumptive P. multocida isolates. Using the methods outlined by MacFaddin (2000) and Marru (2013), pure cultures isolated from blood agar were subjected to a battery of biochemical tests [19, 20]. The biochemical tests involved were catalase, oxidase, indole production, motility, nitrate reduction, citrate utilization, urease, methyl red (MR), Voges-Proskauer (VP), and gelatin liquefaction tests. The catalase test was performed by applying a drop of 3% hydrogen peroxide solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to a small part of the fresh bacterial growth on a glass slide, and positivity was indicated by the formation of bubbles immediately upon addition to the fresh bacterial growth. The oxidase test was performed using oxidase reagent discs saturated with 1% tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (HiMedia, Mumbai, India). The deep purple color developed in 10 s indicated positivity.Indole production was confirmed by inoculating the isolates into peptone water (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) and incubating at 37 °C for 48 h, finally adding 0.5 mL of Kovac’s reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The appearance of a red surface layer indicated a positive result. Motility was tested by stab inoculation into 0.4% semi-solid motility agar (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) and incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Diffuse growth radiating from the stab line denoted motility, whereas non-motile P. multocida isolates remained confined to the stab line. Nitrate reduction was assessed by incubating the inoculated nitrate broth (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) at 37 °C for 24 h and then adding 0.5 mL of sulfanilic acid reagent and 0.5 mL of α-naphthylamine reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The appearance of a red color confirmed the reduction of nitrate to nitrite, hence a positive result. Additionally, citrate utilization was evaluated on Simmons’ citrate agar slants incubated at 37 °C for 48 h; whereas growth accompanied by a color change from green to blue would indicate a positive reaction, the isolates in this study showed no color change (remained green), indicating citrate negativity. The activity of urease was surveyed by inoculating a urea agar slant (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) after 24 h of incubation at 37° C No color change occurred, signifying a negative result for the isolates. The tests for MR and VP were conducted by utilizing an MR –VP broth (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) cultured at 37° C for 48 h. For MR, five drops of MR reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). were mixed, the absence of a red color indicated a negative reaction. The VP was conducted by adding 0.6mLα-naphthol and 0.2mL40% KOH (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), resulting, as indicated, in the absence of a red ring formation between 15 and 20 min, signifying a negative response. The gelatin liquefaction test was attempted by utilizing a Nutrient gelatin deep tubes (Condalab, Madrid, Spain), incubating at 37° C temperature for seven days, followed by refrigeration at 30 min. The gelatin failed to liquefy, signifying a negative response.

Isolates having the biochemical characteristics of P. multocida, that is, positive for catalase, oxidase, indole, and nitrate reduction tests, and negative for motility, citrate, urease, MR, VP, and gelatin tests, were found to be P. multocida.

Molecular analysis

Extraction of bacterial DNA

The DNA of the bacterial colonies of P. multocida was extracted via the Blood/Cultured Cells Genomic DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Favorgen, Ping Tung, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A NanoDrop2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used to evaluate DNA quality and quantity by comparing the 260/280 nm absorbances with those of a reference blank of distilled water. The extracted DNA was kept frozen at −20 °C until analysis.

Amplification of the kmt1 gene by PCR

Species identification of P. multocida was confirmed via PCR amplification of the species-specific kmt1 gene [21]. The primers were ordered from the Sina Clon Company (Cinna Gen, Tehran, Iran).

All the 25 µL PCR mixtures contained 3 µL of template DNA at a concentration of 50–100 ng/µL, 1 µL of each primer (0.1 nmol/µL), 12.5 µL of commercial master mix (Ampliqon, Odense, Denmark), and nuclease-free water up to volume. Thermal cycling was completed via a Quanta Biotech thermal cycler (Beverly, USA). Each PCR run included a positive control (DNA from P. multocida PTCC: 1510) and a negative control (nuclease-free water). Only isolates confirmed by both biochemical tests and kmt1-based PCR were included for downstream MLST analysis. All experimental details, including primer sequences, amplicon sizes, and PCR conditions for detecting the kmt1 gene of P. multocida, have been previously described and are fully available in the cited reference [21].

The products of the PCR were separated on 2% agarose using a safe stain (Labnet, Edison, NJ, USA) in 0.5× TBE buffer by gel electrophoresis. The gels were visualized after electrophoresis by ultraviolet exposure using a transilluminator (Syngene Bio-Imaging, Cambridge, UK). The 460 bp products of the kmt1 gene were then purified using ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified amplicons were sequenced by Sanger sequencing on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems).

Multi-locus sequence typing

These isolates were typed for genetic diversity using RIRDC multilocus sequence typing (MLST), following the procedure described by Subaaharan et al. (2010) [22]. The avian P. multocida RIRDC MLST website (http://pubmlst.org/pmultocida/) is hosted by the University of Oxford and funded by the Wellcome Trust, which was established by Jolley et al. (2004) [23].

This technique is based on the nucleotide sequences of the seven housekeeping genes. The seven housekeeping genes are adk (adenylate kinase), est (esterase), pmi (mannose-6-phosphate isomerase), zwf (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase), mdh (malate dehydrogenase), gdh (glutamate dehydrogenase), and pgi (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase) [22].

The target genes were PCR amplified in 25 µL mixtures containing 12.5 µL of 2× PCR master mix (Ampliqon Company, Denmark), 2 µL of DNA template (50–100 ng/µL), 1 µL of forward primer and 1 µL of reverse primer, and nuclease-free water to the required volumes. Each PCR run included a positive control (DNA from P. multocida PTCC: 1510) and a negative control (nuclease-free water). All primers, amplicon sizes, and PCR conditions used for the targeted housekeeping have been previously described and are fully available in the cited references [22].

The PCR products were subjected to gel electrophoresis in 2% agarose with a safe dye (Labnet, USA) and visualized using the Genius Gel Documentation system (Syngene Bio-Image, Germany) and subsequently purified using ExoSAP-IT PCR Product Cleanup Reagent (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purified amplicons were sequenced by Sanger sequencing on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Sequence chromatograms were inspected for quality using Chromas Lite v2.6.6 (Technelysium Pty Ltd), and forward and reverse reads were assembled into consensus sequences.

Capsular typing was not performed in this study, as it was not included in the study design. Future studies may benefit from the use of multiplex PCR for capsular typing, which would provide further characterization of P. multocida strains and their potential association with pathogenicity.

Data analysis

The sequence of each of the seven housekeeping genes were submitted to the P. multocida PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/pmultocida/) via the BLAST-based allele query tool to determine allele numbers. Sequence type (ST) were assigned based on the combination of alleles at the seven loci. eBURST in PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/) is a clustering algorithm that uses MLST data to establish relationships within clonal complexes among similar allelic profile (strains based on a strict group definition of five out of seven alleles) was utilized for the analysis of our data. Phylogenetic analysis, concatenated sequences of the seven MLST loci were aligned and analyzed in MEGA 11.0 software (version 11.0.13) using the neighbor-joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [24]. The maximum composite likelihood model was applied to compute evolutionary distances, and ambiguous positions were removed by pairwise deletion.

Data handling and prevalence calculation

Prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of positive samples by the number of samples examined in each group (species, sample type, or season) and expressed as a percentage. To exclude duplication, one isolate from each animal was used in the analysis. Mixed infections were not observed among the recovered isolates. For MLST, amplicons were reviewed for quality and re-sequenced if sequence reads were of poor quality; only good-quality sequences were sent for allele assignment. Because the number of positive isolates was modest (n = 22), results are presented descriptively as counts and percentages without inferential statistical testing. This approach avoids over-interpretation and allows transparent presentation of the data.

Results

Prevalence of P. multocida in cattle and buffalo

A total of 378 samples were collected. Twelve out of the 200 samples collected from cattle contained P. multocida, representing 6%. Furthermore, 10 out of the 178 (5.62%) buffalo samples contained P. multocida. Cultural and biochemical features

Colonies of P. multocida were small, gray, smooth (mucoid), round, and sweet-smelling. The bacteria were Gram-negative coccobacilli that failed to grow on MacConkey agar and were negative for hemolysis on blood agar. The isolates were biochemically confirmed as immobile, with positive tests for catalase (which causes oxygen bubble formation), oxidase (dark blue), indole (red ring at the junction), and nitrate reduction for all the isolates but negative tests for citrate, urea, methyl red, and Voges-Proskauer, as well as for gelatin liquefaction.

P. multocida molecular confirmation

After the P. multocida isolates were detected via culture and biochemical tests, the species-specific primers KMT1SP6 and KMT1T7, which target the kmt1 gene, resulted in the amplification of a 460 bp fragment, which identified 22 isolates as positive for P. multocida. All kmt1 gene sequences obtained in this study have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) under project accession number CRA033267. A nucleotide sequence comparison of the kmt1 gene of P. multocida revealed high similarity between Iranian and global P. multocida isolates deposited in GenBank. The sequences of the Iranian isolates were 98–99% identical to those of various P. multocida isolates deposited in GenBank® (NCBI).

Multilocus sequence typing and phylogenetic analysis

P. multocida isolates were tested for genetic characteristics and diversity via the RIRDC scheme on the basis of partial sequencing of seven housekeeping genes [22]. Seven housekeeping genes yielded PCR amplicons of approximately 570, 620, 641, 702, 739, 784, and 808 bp for the adk, mdh, est, gdh, pmi, pgi, and zwf genes, respectively, as observed via 2% gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1).

Discussion

P. multocida is an important pathogen associated with respiratory diseases in various animal groups, such as cattle and buffalo [25]. This bacterium is the etiologic agent for several diseases, such as fowl cholera in chicken, atrophic rhinitis in pigs, and hemorrhagic septicemia in cattle and buffalo [26]. This microorganism is a significant economic threat to the animal industry. It causes significant economic losses through decreased ruminant productivity, increased culling rates, and increased mortality rates. In addition, the development of antibiotic resistance in some strains of P. multocida is another concern [27] Traditional methods for identifying this bacterium, which are primarily based on biochemical characteristics, face significant challenges. Inconsistent outcomes for specific isolates during biochemical testing make the final identification cumbersome [28]. P. multocida strains were isolated in the present study via conventional methods accompanied by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

In the present study, P. multocida was confirmed in nasal swabs and lung tissue samples from buffaloes and cattle. Among the 378 samples, 22 (5.82%) were positive for bacteria. The findings are congruent with others. For example, Cengiz reported a 4.8% infection rate in lung samples with and without lesions [29]. Similarly, Gharibi et al. reported a 5.48% frequency from nasopharyngeal and nasal swabs of buffaloes and cattle in Iran [30] Furthermore, according to Shayegh et al., 6.02% of tracheal and nasal swabs from healthy and diseased bovines were positive for the causative agent [31].

Past studies have reported different rates of occurrence of P. multocida in ruminants. The rate of occurrence of P. multocida in cattle ranges between 1.67% and 42.9%, according to different studies. The highest rate of occurrence of 42.9% was reported by Allam et al. in a study that tested 70 bovine nasal swab samples [32]. Akalu et al. tested 400 nasopharyngeal swabs and lung tissue samples and reported a 20.25% frequency [18]. Ashrafi et al. reported an 11.6% frequency in a study of 120 bovine lung specimens in Iran [33]. The lowest rate of 1.67% was reported in Egypt for 60 bovine lung samples by El Nadi [34].

In Egypt, El-Seedy et al., in a comprehensive study, reported an 18.2% infection rate of P. multocida from 406 deep nasal swabs of calves with respiratory signs [35]. On the other hand, Hajikolaei et al. reported that nasopharyngeal infection with P. multocida was observed in only 2.4% of the cattle studied [36].

Studies have yielded different outcomes regarding P. multocida in other ruminants. In a report on 349 goat and sheep lungs, Mombeni et al. reported a 22.6% prevalence [37], whereas Khalili et al. reported a mere 1.87% prevalence in a study of 320 suspected sheep lung tissues [38].

A study in which sheep and cows were tested in combination had a varied outcome. Tabatabaei et al. reported a 19.1% prevalence among 120 cow and sheep lung samples [39]. Similarly, Khamesipour et al. had a 9% frequency from 333 pneumonia-infected and normal lung samples [40].

There are several reasons for these inconsistencies. Animal population density, sampling protocols, population under examination, local Pasteurellosis prevalence, history of disease, and control measures are among them. Prophylactic vaccination programs, breeding programs (indigenous or intensive), and stress reduction can also minimize the incidence. Notably, the peak in the number of carriers typically occurs soon after disease onset and then declines gradually. There may be stress factors involved in precipitating the disease state in carrier animals. Seasonal isolates of P. multocida had the highest infection rate in winter in cattle and buffalo, as was reported in earlier research by Karimkhani [41]. The observed increase in winter infection is most likely due to a decrease in temperature, immunosuppression of the animal through decreased immune cell activity, heightened vulnerability to infection, and increased concentration of livestock during winter, conditions that lead to heightened contact between animals and ease of disease transmission. The findings of the present work show that efficient herd management in winter, such as providing appropriate environmental and nutritional conditions, lowers the transmission of disease and illness in animals.

The MLST scheme of RIRDC was adopted for the present study to analyze genetic diversity and describe the population structure of isolated bacterial strains. MLST checks the sequence of seven housekeeping genes, where mutations are known to accumulate slowly [42]. The principal strength of MLST is the portability of the results. Given that the results are portable, comparisons with database entries of several contributors are possible, and very closely related bacterial strains may be distinguished. Since the initial MLST scheme for Neisseria meningitidis, MLST has been extensively utilized to examine genetic diversity, population structure, and the molecular epidemiology of various bacterial and fungal diseases [42, 43].

All 22 P. multocida isolates in this study were found to be genetically related on the basis of the seven allelic loci. The isolates belonged to ST122 after the MLST database was queried (http://pubmlst.org/pmultocida/).

Up to March 14, 2025, the P. multocida MLST database included 2,968 isolates originating in 41 host species across 55 countries. Although the database had begun by focusing on bird isolates, ruminant isolates actually constitute the majority today, 599/2,968 (20.2%) having originated in cattle and buffalo. More precisely, ST-122 comprised 250/2,968 (8.5%) of the entire set of isolates, 141/250 (56.4%) having originated in cattle and buffalo. Additionally, 160 isolates were listed as having originated in haemorrhagic septicaemia cases, including 107 originating from cattle and buffalo; critically, 94 belonged to ST-122. Collectively, the findings establish that ST-122 constitutes a common kind of P. multocida isolate in ruminants and the most common sequence type identified in connection to haemorrhagic septicaemia among bovines worldwide.

Multilocus sequence typing has been applied to examine genomic diversity in the disease-causing bacterium P. multocida. Different studies have utilized this approach to determine the genomic diversity of the pathogen and its phylogeny in different hosts. For the first time, Subaaharan et al. applied the RIRDC MLST method for large-scale screening of 66 poultry isolates of P. multocida. This analysis revealed 29 alleles of 63 Australian isolates and three reference strains. The MLST findings were concordant with those of other methods, such as multilocus enzyme electrophoresis, ribotyping, restriction endonuclease analysis, pulsed field gel electrophoresis and REP-PCR [22]. In their thorough research, Hotchkiss et al. tested a large set of isolates of different host species and resolved 62 sequence types (STs) to 195 P. multocida strains. The most notable observation of their paper was that they isolated ST122 from a wide range of mammalian species, such as cattle, buffalo, bison, and elephant, demonstrating that the sequence adapts to a wide variety of mammalian host [44]. Moustafa et al. used PFGE and MLST on 23 isolates from Pakistan and Thailand, 20 of which matched with ST122. This observation verifies the widespread of this type of sequence on the Asian continent [45]. ST122 was also isolated from cattle, goats, mutton, and pigs of India, confirming the presence of the sequence in a variety of hosts [46]. Mistry et al. isolated the sequence from sheep and the P52 vaccine strain, demonstrating the viability of the sequence for vaccine development [47].

In addition to being identified from greater ruminants, the P. multocida ST122 strain has also been isolated from other host species, such as sheep, pigs, and birds. The host range represents the high adaptability of ST122 in a spectrum of animal species that hold ecological and epidemiological importance.

In this study, the similarity of all the isolates at seven loci likely reflects shared ancestry between strains with a shared P. multocida ancestor. In addition, the results revealed that ST122 is endemic in West Azerbaijan, providing insight into the regional epidemiology of the pathogen. Phylogenetic comparison indicated that ST122 is quite close to ST63, ST460, ST473, and ST322. Interestingly, these bacteria were found to be present in cattle from different countries, i.e., from Europe to Asia. The geographical distribution indicates a global distribution for this group of sequences, with a specific concentration being recorded for the Asian continent.

Conclusions

This study in West Azerbaijan, Iran, found a low but stable prevalence of P. multocida in cattle (6%) and buffaloes (5.62%). The higher detection in nasal swabs compared to lung tissue suggests that the upper respiratory tract is the main site of colonization. Seasonal analysis showed that although bacteria were detectable throughout the warmer months, their isolation was comparatively higher in the winter. Environmental variables, host-related traits, and management strategies that promote bacterial survival may all contribute to this persistent presence.

Molecular typing identified all isolates as ST-122, demonstrating genetic homogeneity and confirming ST-122 as the predominant strain in bovines and buffaloes in this region. ST-122, a lineage responsible for most bovine HS cases worldwide. Although sampled animals were healthy, this finding highlights subclinical circulation and supports the value of MLST epidemiological surveillance and may inform vaccine development in Iran.

Although MLST was utilized to determine the genetic diversity of P. multocida isolates, capsular typing was not performed in this study. We suggest that future studies on P. multocida could benefit from multiplex PCR-based capsular typing to provide additional insights into strain-specific pathogenicity and epidemiology.
