Proper pair potential for mutually interacting point dipoles Preprint: APS/123-QED
Lucas H. P. Cunha

Abstract
Dipolar interactions play fundamental role in particle dynamics and structuring in the molecular to colloidal scales, thus being crucial to describing a variety of chemical, physical, and biological phenomena. Here, we derive the pair potential for mutually interacting point dipoles under the action of external fields. The derived relations account for the spatial dependence of the dipole moments thus far missed in the literature. Missing such terms leads to considerable errors as the dipoles are closer and more susceptible.

Here, we derive the proper pair potential relation for point dipoles with mutual induced magnetization under the action of external magnetic fields. The derived relations account for the spatial dependence of the dipolar moments, which has not been previously done in the literature

As here demonstrated, neglecting the potential term related to the spatial dependence of the magnetization might lead to considerable errors as closer and more susceptible are the particles.

The physical construction of point dipoles relies on the laws of electromagnetism. Classically, it consists of a pair of point monopoles of opposite charges separated by an infinitesimal distance from the perspective of the observer. In the magnetostatic context, dipoles can also be modeled by an infinitesimal electrical current loop. Although the two models might lead to discrepancies in certain contexts (see Boyer
Boyer 1988
for further details) for the current description both models recover the same relations.

The dipolar moment might be an intrinsic property of the particle, here referred to as fixed dipole moment, or induced by interactions with other field sources, e.g., nearby dipoles and externally imposed fields. For instance, when certain particles are subject to an electric field, they develop a heterogeneous distribution of their internal charges leading to their polarization
Sherman et al. 2018
;
Thole 1981
. In the magnetic context, the initially uncorrelated magnetic moments constituting the particles align to an applied magnetic field, resulting in the net magnetization of the particle, e.g., paramagnetic particles
Cunha et al. 2024
;
Spatafora-Salazar et al. 2021
. In both contexts, we end up with an induced dipole moment which can be taken as a linear response to the imposed field for relatively weak field strengths, as will be considered throughout this work.

We focus on systems in which magnetizable, or polarizable, particles are subjected to external fields while interacting with neighboring particles. As the individual particles magnetize, they induce fields that disturb the magnetization of the neighbors. This process results in a complex multi-body interaction response which is highly dependent on the particles’ arrangements. This phenomenon is referred to in the literature as the mutual dipole magnetization/polarization
Spatafora-Salazar et al. 2021
;
Sherman et al. 2019
, and plays a crucial role in the context of molecular polarization
Thole 1981
and phase separation
Sherman et al. 2018
;
Spatafora-Salazar et al. 2021
. The computation of mutually interacting point dipoles, although computationally expensive as it consists of a full-filed
3N\times 3N
linear system (for
N
particles in a 3-dimensional arrangement), is fairly straightforward. However, it has been a common practice in the literature to calculate the energetic pair potentials using the standard relation for fixed dipoles, i.e., no spatial dependence of the dipole moments. As shown here, this practice can lead to considerable errors.

First, to introduce some relevant concepts and mathematical derivations, we elaborate on the potential for a single magnetic dipole subjected to a non-uniform magnetic field. We model the magnetization of the point dipole as an isotropic linear response to the local field,
\bm{m}=\bm{m}_{p}+\chi\bm{H}
, where
\bm{m}_{p}
is the permanent dipole component, and
\chi
is the magnetic susceptibility assumed as constant, i.e., linear regime of magnetization. Notably, for non-uniform fields, there is a spatial dependence of the particle’s magnetization. The force on a dipole subjected to a non-uniform magnetic field reads
Boyer 1988
;
Rosensweig 2013

\bm{f}=\mu_{0}\bm{m}\cdot\nabla_{x}\bm{H}

(1)

where
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of the vacuum and
\nabla_{x}
regards the spatial derivative evaluated at the particle’s position. In case the particle’s magnetization does not depend on space (
\chi=0
), we can write the potential
U=-\mu_{0}\bm{H}\cdot\bm{m}
such that
\bm{f}=-\nabla_{r}U
, given that
\nabla_{x}\times\bm{H}=\bm{0}
considering the absence of electrical currents in the system
Rosensweig 2013
. Here, the notation
\nabla_{r}
refers to the derivative with respect to the particle displacement.

We aim now to find a potential
U
that properly accounts for the particle’s magnetization spacial dependency. Using the irrotationality of
\bm{H}
, the fact that the particle’s magnetization is not influenced by its own induced field, and the linearity of the induced dipole with
\bm{H}
, we get to

We make the use of the vectorial idendities
\bm{a}\cdot\nabla\bm{b}=\bm{a}\times\nabla\times\bm{b}+(\nabla\bm{b})\cdot\bm{a}
,
\nabla(\bm{b}\cdot\bm{a})=(\nabla\bm{b})\cdot\bm{a}+(\nabla\bm{a})\cdot\bm{b}
, and
\nabla(\bm{a}\cdot\bm{a})=2\bm{a}\cdot\nabla\bm{a}+2\bm{a}\times(\nabla\times\bm{a})

\bm{m}\cdot\nabla_{x}\bm{H}=\nabla_{r}\left(\bm{H}\cdot\bm{m}-\frac{\chi}{2}|\bm{H}|^{2}\right).

(2)

Finally, the potential accounting for the magnetic dipole spatial dependence is written as

U=-\mu_{0}\left(\bm{H}\cdot\bm{m}-\frac{\chi}{2}|\bm{H}|^{2}\right).

(3)

\mathcal{G}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[3\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})}{|(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})|^{5}}-\frac{\bm{I}}{|(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})|^{3}}\right].

(4)

In this scenario, the mutual interaction between the particles’ magnetization leads to

\bm{m}_{1}=\bm{m}_{1,p}+\chi_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2,1}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{m}_{2}+\chi_{1}\bm{H}_{0},

(5)

where
\bm{r}
is the distance vector between the two particles, as sketched in Fig.
1
. Note that
\mathcal{G}_{1,2}=\mathcal{G}_{2,1}
. Similar expression is valid for the magnetization
\bm{m}_{2}
, so that one can solve for the couple magnetization of the dipoles
Spatafora-Salazar et al. 2021
. The force acting on the dipole
\bm{m}_{1}
due to the field generated by the dipole
\bm{m}_{2}
for a uniform field
\bm{H}_{0}
is then given by
\bm{f}_{1}=\mu_{0}\bm{m}_{1}\cdot\nabla_{x}(\mathcal{G}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{m}_{2})
. As
\nabla_{x}
does not account for the particles relative displacement, we have that
\nabla_{x}\bm{m}_{1,2}=\bm{0}
and
\nabla_{x}\times(\mathcal{G}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{m}_{2})=\bm{0}
. Then, using Eq.
5
, and the facts that
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{G}=\nabla_{r}\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{T}=\mathcal{G}
, we get to

\bm{f}_{1}=\mu_{0}\nabla_{r}\left[\bm{m}_{2}\cdot\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{1}-\frac{\chi_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{1})^{2}-\frac{\chi_{1}}{2}(\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{2})^{2}\right].

(6)

Finally, the complete pair potential reads

U=-\mu_{0}\left[\bm{m}_{2}\cdot\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{1}-\frac{\chi_{2}}{2}(\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{1})^{2}-\frac{\chi_{1}}{2}(\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}_{2})^{2}\right].

(7)

\bm{H}_{0}
without permanent dipole components. Hence,
\bm{m}_{1}=\chi(\bm{H}_{0}+\mathcal{G}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{m}_{2})

\bm{m_{1}}=\bm{m_{2}}=\bm{m}

\bm{m}

Keaveny and Maxey 2008

Cunha et al. 2024

\bm{m}=\left(\mathcal{I}-\chi\mathcal{G}\right)^{-1}\cdot\chi\bm{H}_{0},

7

U=-\mu_{0}\left[\bm{m}\cdot\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m}-\chi(\mathcal{G}\cdot\bm{m})^{2}\right].

In conclusion, we derived the pair potential for mutually interacting point dipoles under the action of external fields. The derived equation contains a term accounting for the dipoles’ moment spatial dependence. All calculations were developed in the magnetostatic context, but they also hold to polarizable electric dipoles subjected to electric fields. We show that ignoring the dipoles’ moment spatial dependence, as commonly done in the literature, leads to considerable errors in both the potential and forces for close dipoles with high susceptibility. It weakens both the attractive and repulsive interactions between pair dipoles, although the effect is stronger than the first. Important to state the derived relations cannot be directly used for problems with more than two interacting dipoles, and the more elaborated calculations for many-body problems are yet to be derived.
