On the rate of convergence of estimating the Hurst parameter of rough stochastic volatility models
Xiyue Han *, Alexander Schied

Abstract
In [Han & Schied, 2023, arXiv 2307.02582 ], an easily computable scale-invariant estimator ℛ n s {\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n} was constructed to estimate the Hurst parameter of the drifted fractional Brownian motion X X from its antiderivative. This paper extends this result by proving that ℛ n s {\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n} also consistently estimates the Hurst parameter when applied to the antiderivative of g ∘ X g\circ X for a general nonlinear function g g . We also establish an almost sure rate of convergence in this general setting. Our result applies, in particular, to the estimation of the Hurst parameter of a wide class of rough stochastic volatility models from discrete observations of the integrated variance, including the rough fractional stochastic volatility model.



1
Introduction and statement of the main result

We consider a stochastic volatility model, where the price process is driven by a stochastic differential equation with respect to a standard Brownian motion
B
,

dS_{t}=\sigma_{t}S_{t}\,dB_{t}.

Here, the process
\sigma
is continuous and adapted and referred to as the volatility process. It was discovered empirically by Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum
[
13
]
that the volatility process
\sigma
does not exhibit diffusive behavior but instead is much rougher. This discovery led to the development of rough stochastic volatility models, in which the smooth diffusive dynamics of classical models are replaced by rougher counterparts, such as fractional Brownian motion or Gaussian Volterra processes. A specific example here is the
rough fractional stochastic volatility model
proposed in
[
13
, Section 3]
, where logarithmic volatility is modeled by a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
X^{H}
. To be more precise, we have

(1.1)

\sigma_{t}=\exp(X^{H}_{t}),

where
X^{H}
solves the following integral equation

(1.2)

X^{H}_{t}=x_{0}+\rho\int_{0}^{t}(\mu-X^{H}_{s})\,ds+W^{H}_{t},\qquad t\geq 0,

for a fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1/2)
. In particular, it was shown in
[
12
]
that a value
H\approx 0.1
appears to be most adequate for capturing the stylized facts of empirical volatility time series. Following the advent of the rough fractional stochastic volatility, many rough volatility models have been proposed. Notable examples include the rough Heston model
[
8
,
9
]
and the rough Bergomi model
[
2
,
10
]
in which the volatility process is based on Gaussian Volterra processes. For an overview of recent developments in rough volatility, we refer to the book
[
3
]
.

In the model (
1.1
), the degree of roughness of the volatility process is governed by the Hurst parameter
H
. When trying to estimate the Hurst parameter
H
, the major difficulty is that the volatility process
\sigma
cannot be observed directly from given data; only the asset prices
S
can be observed. Thus, one typically computes the quadratic variation of the log prices,

(1.3)

\langle\log S\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{2}\,ds,

which is also known as the integrated variance, and then obtains proxy values
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
by numerical differentiation. The roughness estimation is then based on those proxy values. However, it was shown in
[
7
]
that the error that arises in the numerical differentiation might distort the final roughness estimation.

Various approaches have been proposed to tackle this issue. Assuming that the volatility process is driven by a fractional Brownian motion, Bolko et al.
[
4
]
employ the generalized method of moments to estimate the Hurst parameter. In addition, Fukasawa et al.
[
11
]
develop a Whittle-type estimator under a similar parametric setting. Chong et al.
[
5
,
6
]
substantially extend the previous results by considering a semi-parametric setup, in which, with the exception of the Hurst parameter of the underlying fractional Brownian motion, all components are fully non-parametric.

To address the same issue, in
[
14
]
we constructed an estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
that estimates the Hurst parameter
H
in (
1.1
) based on discrete observations of the integrated variance (
1.3
). In contrast to other estimation schemes
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
11
]
, our estimator is constructed in a strictly pathwise setting and, in fact, estimates the so-called roughness exponent
R
, which coincides with the Hurst parameter for fractional Brownian motion
[
17
]
. The fact that our estimator is built on a strictly pathwise approach makes it very versatile and applicable also in situations where trajectories are not based on fractional Brownian motion; see, e.g.,
[
14
, Examples 3.5]
for further discussions.

In our pathwise setting, we consider a given but unknown function
x\in C[0,1]
and denote
y(t):=\int_{0}^{t}g(x(s))\,ds
for a function
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Based on the observations of function
y
over the dyadic partition
\mathbb{T}_{n+2}
, i.e.,
\{y(k2^{-n-2}):k=0,\cdots,2^{n+2}\}
, we introduce the coefficients

(1.4)

\vartheta_{n,k}:=2^{3n/2+3}\left(y\left(\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}\right)-2y\left(\frac{4k+1}{2^{n+2}}\right)+2y\left(\frac{4k+3}{2^{n+2}}\right)-y\left(\frac{4k+4}{2^{n+2}}\right)\right),

for
0\leq k\leq 2^{n}-1
. Our estimator for the roughness exponent of the trajectory
x
is now given by

(1.5)

\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y):=1-\frac{1}{n}\log_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\vartheta_{n,k}^{2}}.

In contrast to many other estimators proposed in the literature,
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(y)
can be computed in a straightforward manner. For instance, to estimate the Hurst parameter of the rough fractional stochastic volatility model (
1.1
)—(
1.2
), we take
x
as a sample trajectory of the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
X^{H}
and
g(t)=e^{2t}
so that

(1.6)

y(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\big(\exp(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

replicates the integrated variance corresponding to the sample trajectory
x
.

In particular, the fact that our estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is derived from a purely pathwise consideration makes it applicable to an even wider class of processes. For instance, let
X
be given by

(1.7)

X_{t}:=x_{0}+W^{H}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{s}\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

where
\xi
is progressively measurable with respect to the natural filtration of
W^{H}
and satisfies the following additional assumption.

•

If
H<1/2
, we assume that the function
t\mapsto\xi_{t}
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. bounded in the sense that there exists a finite random variable
C
such that
|\xi_{t}(\omega)|\leq C(\omega)
for a.e.
t
and
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
\omega
.

•

If
H>1/2
, we assume that for
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.
\omega
the function
t\mapsto\xi_{t}(\omega)
is Hölder continuous with some exponent
\alpha(\omega)>2H-1
.

We emphasize that the class of processes defined by (
1.7
) is sufficiently general. In particular, for
H<1/2
, any
\mathbb{P}
-a.s continuous drift
\xi
clearly satisfies the condition. One can also specify conditions on the drift term of a stochastic differential equation driven by fractional Brownian motion under which the assumption is satisfied; see Theorem 1.6 in
[
15
]
. Now, suppose that
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is strictly monotone and let
Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g(X_{s})\,ds
. It is shown in
[
14
, Corollary 2.3]
that
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(Y)\rightarrow H
as
n\uparrow\infty
with probability one. This consistency result applies in particular to the rough fractional stochastic volatility model defined in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), where we take
\xi_{s}=\rho(\mu-X^{H}_{s})
and
g(t)=e^{2t}
.

However, the estimator
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is not scale-invariant, and the performance of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is highly sensitive to the underlying scale of the function
y
. To solve this issue, a scale-invariant modification of
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
was constructed in
[
14
]
as in the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
with
\alpha_{0}>0
.
For
n>m
, the
sequential scaling factor

\eta_{n}^{s}
and the
sequential scale estimate

{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y)
are defined as follows,

(1.8)

\begin{split}\eta^{s}_{n}&:=\mathop{\hbox{\rm arg\,min}}_{\eta>0}\sum_{k=n-m}^{n}\alpha_{n-k}\Big(\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k}(\eta y)-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k-1}(\eta y)\Big)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(y):=\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}(\eta^{s}_{n}y).\end{split}

The corresponding mapping
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}:C[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called the
sequential scale estimator
.

There is no rule of thumb for choosing the parameters
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}
. As a matter of fact, the performance of
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
is dependent on the actual Hurst parameter
H
. Nevertheless, as will be shown in (
1.9
), regardless of the choice of
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}
, the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
shares the same asymptotic rate of convergence; see also
[
17
]
for further approaches to construct scale-invariant estimators. For given
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{m}
, the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
can be represented as a linear combination of the estimators
\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{k}
as follows,

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}^{s}=\beta_{n,n}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}+\beta_{n,n-1}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n-1}+\cdots+\beta_{n,n-m-1}\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n-m-1},

where the coefficients
\beta_{n,k}
are explicitly given in
[
14
, Proposition 2.6]
. To be more specific, we have

\beta_{n,k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}n^{2}(n-1)}&\text{if $k=n$,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}nk}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{n-k}}{k-1}-\frac{\alpha_{n-k-1}}{k+1}\Big)&\text{if $n-m\leq k\leq n-1$,}\\
\displaystyle\frac{-\alpha_{m}}{c^{\textrm{\rm s}}_{n}n(n-m)(n-m-1)}&\text{if $k=n-m-1$,}\end{cases}\quad\text{for}\quad c^{s}_{n}:=\sum_{k=n-m}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{n-k}}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}.

Therefore, the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}^{s}
can be computed in a fast and straightforward manner. In addition, the rate of convergence of the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
was primarily studied in
[
14
, Theorem 2.7]
, which is quoted here for the convenience of the reader. Let
X
be as in (
1.7
) and
Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\,ds
. Then the following almost sure rate of convergence holds for the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
,

(1.9)

|{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(Y)-H|=\mathcal{O}\big(2^{-n/2}\sqrt{\log n}\big).

Here, the rate of convergence was only established under the assumption that
g
is the identity function. Hence, this result cannot be applied directly to establish the consistency or the convergence rate of
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
for rough stochastic volatility models. In these models, we typically make discrete observations of the integrated variance of the form

\langle\log S\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{s}\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}g(X_{s})\,ds,\qquad t\in[0,1].

for some strictly increasing nonlinear function
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Such a choice leads to non-Gaussian dynamics and thus lies beyond the scope of
[
14
, Theorem 2.7]
. Our following theorem extends the convergence result (
1.9
) to the case in which
g
twice continuously differentiable function satisfying a very mild regularity condition.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

(1.10)

\int_{0}^{1}\big(g’(X(s))\big)^{2}\,ds>0\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\geq 0
. Let
X
be as in (
1.7
) and

Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g(X_{s})\,ds,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then the following almost sure rate of convergence holds for the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
,

(1.11)

|{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(Y)-H|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\cdot 2^{-(\frac{H}{2}\wedge\frac{1}{4})n}\right).

Remark 1.3
.

Note that the condition (
1.10
) is automatically satisfied if
g
is strictly monotone, such as the function
g(t)=e^{2t}
used in (
1.6
). It is also satisfied for the choice
g(t)=t^{2}
. Indeed, we have

\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(W^{H}_{s})\big)^{2}\,ds=4\int_{0}^{1}(W^{H}_{s})^{2}\,ds>0

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
because
\{(s,\omega):W^{H}_{s}(\omega)=0\}
is a
\rm{Leb}[0,1]\otimes\mathbb{P}
-null set, and so (
1.10
) follows by way of the absolute continuity of the law of
X
established in
[
15
]
.

It is also worthwhile to point out that the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 2.7]
relies essentially on the Gaussianity of the antiderivative
Y
. Hence, its approach does not extend to the setting of
Theorem

1.2
, where the process
g\circ X
is no longer Gaussian, and so neither is the process
Y
. Instead,
Theorem

1.2
is established by a pathwise approach, which is, in fact, robust and applicable to a wide range of rough volatility models. As will become clear in the proof, the convergence rate in
Theorem

1.2
depends only on the convergence rate of
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}(Y)
(see (
1.9
)) and the Hölder continuity of the process
X
, or equivalently, of the fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
. In many rough volatility models, such as the rough Bergomi model
[
12
]
, the process
X
is modeled by a Gaussian Volterra process, wheares the function
g
remains to be exponential. In this setting, the convergence rate in (
1.9
) can be deduced by exploring the covariance structure of
Y
; see, e.g.,
[
14
, Proposition 2.7]
.

A potential concern is that the convergence rate of our estimator depends on the Hurst parameter
H
itself. In particular, for very small values of
H
, such as
H\approx 0.1
, commonly considered in the rough volatility literature, large sample sizes might be required for an accurate estimation. In contrast, the following numerical study shows that the finite-sample performance at
H=0.1
is in fact better then the asymptotic rate (
1.11
) would suggest. Even at
n=10
, or equivalently, with
2^{12}
observations, the sequential scale estimator
{\mathscr{R}}^{s}_{n}
already gives very reliable estimates.

x_{0}=2

.

Proof of Theorem 1.2

To prove
Theorem

1.2
, we adopt the notation used in
[
14
]
. For any given deterministic function or random process
f
, we write

(2.1)

\begin{split}\theta^{f}_{n,k}&=2^{n/2}\left(2f\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{k}{2^{n}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{k+1}{2^{n}}\Big)\right),\\
\vartheta^{f}_{n,k}&=2^{3n/2+3}\left(f\Big(\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-2f\Big(\frac{4k+1}{2^{n+2}}\Big)+2f\Big(\frac{4k+3}{2^{n+2}}\Big)-f\Big(\frac{4k+4}{2^{n+2}}\Big)\right).\end{split}

Here, the coefficients
(\theta^{f}_{n,k})
are also referred to as the Faber–Schauder coefficients of
f
, and coefficients
(\vartheta^{f}_{n,k})
are the approximated Faber–Schauder coefficients (
1.4
) with respect to
f
as in
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
. Furthermore, for a given function or process
f
, we write
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{f}_{n}:=\big(\vartheta^{f}_{n,0},\vartheta^{f}_{n,1},\dots,\vartheta^{f}_{n,2^{n}-1}\big)^{\top}
. In particular, if
f
is a Gaussian process,
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{f}_{n}
then defines a Gaussian vector. In this section, we let

Y_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}W^{H}_{s}\,ds\quad\text{and}\quad V_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g\big(W^{H}_{s}\big)\,ds

be the antiderivative of
W^{H}
and
g\circ W^{H}
respectively. We denote the covariance matrix of the Gaussian vector
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{n}
by
\Psi_{n}
, and we fix
\tau_{H}:=\text{trace}\,\Psi_{0}
. It was shown in
[
14
, Proposition 4.9]
that there exists a positive constant
c_{H}>0
such that

(2.2)

\limsup_{n\uparrow\infty}\delta_{n}^{-1}\left|2^{n(H-1)}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{n}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\leq 1\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-}a.s.

for
\delta_{n}=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\sqrt{\log n}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we now denote

\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}:=\left(\vartheta_{2n,2^{n}i},\vartheta_{2n,2^{n}i+1},\cdots,\vartheta_{2n,2^{n}(i+1)-1}\right)^{\top},\qquad 0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1.

In other words, the vectors
\left(\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}\right)
divide the Gaussian vector
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n}
into
2^{n}
equally partitioned subvectors.

The proof of
Theorem

1.2
results from a sequence of intermediate lemmas, which we summarize below to outline the roadmap of this proof.

•

First,
Lemma

2.1
obtains the uniform almost sure rate of convergence of the Gaussian vectors
(\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i})
. Furthermore,
Remark

2.2
transfers this convergence result to two-sided bounds for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}
in a strictly pathwise sense.

•

Second,
Lemma

2.3
shows that the two-sided bounds in
Remark

2.2
would carry over from the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}
to that of
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{V}_{2n,i}
.

•

Next,
Lemma

2.4
derives the two-sided bounds for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{V}_{2n}
based on the bounds in
Lemma

2.3
.

•

Finally,
Lemma

2.5
applies the two-sided bounds in
Lemma

2.4
to obtain the almost sure convergence rate of
{\mathscr{R}}_{2n}^{s}(V)
.

Let us begin by considering the uniform convergence rate of the Gaussian vectors
\left(\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}\right)
similar to (
2.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For
H\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
c_{H}>0
such that

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\delta_{n}^{-1}\sup_{0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1}\left|2^{n(2H-3/2)}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\leq 1\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-}a.s.

for
\delta_{n}=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\sqrt{n\log 2+2\log n}
.

Proof.

Let us denote the covariance matrix of the Gaussian vector
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}
by
\Phi_{2n,i}
. By definition, the matrix
\Phi_{2n,i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
diagonal partitioned matrix of
\Psi_{2n}
. As the fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
is self-similar and admits stationary increments, we have

(2.3)

\Phi_{2n,i}=2^{(1-2H)n}\Psi_{n}.

This then gives

\text{trace}\,\Phi_{2n,i}=2^{(1-2H)n}\text{trace}\,\Psi_{n}=2^{(3-4H)n}\tau_{H}.

Furthermore, applying
[
14
, Proposition 4.9]
to (
2.3
) yields

\left\lVert{\Phi_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{2}=2^{(1-2H)n}\left\lVert{\Psi_{n}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\kappa_{H}2^{n(2-4H)},\qquad 0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1,

for some
\kappa_{H}>0
. For any given
\delta>0
, it follows from the concentration inequality
[
1
, Lemma 3.1]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1}\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1}\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right|\geq\delta\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left\{\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right|\geq\delta\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right|\geq\delta\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\sqrt{\text{trace}\,\Phi_{2n,i}}\right|\geq 2^{n(\frac{3}{2}-2H)}\delta\sqrt{\tau_{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\phi\exp\left(-\frac{2^{n(3-4H)}\delta^{2}\tau_{H}}{4\left\lVert{\Phi_{2n,i}}\right\rVert_{2}}\right)=2^{n}\left(\phi\exp\left(-\frac{2^{n(3-4H)}\delta^{2}\tau_{H}}{4\left\lVert{\Phi_{2n,0}}\right\rVert_{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\phi\exp\left(-\frac{2^{n(3-4H)}\delta^{2}\tau_{H}}{4\left\lVert{\Phi_{2n,0}}\right\rVert_{2}}+n\log 2\right)\leq\phi\exp\left(\frac{2^{-n}\delta^{2}\tau_{H}}{4\kappa_{H}}+n\log 2\right),

for some constant
\phi>0
. We now take
c_{H}:=\sqrt{4\kappa_{H}/\tau_{H}}
and
\delta_{n}:=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\big(2\log n+n\log 2\big)^{-1/2}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, plugging
\delta=\delta_{n}
into the above inequality yields that

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1}\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\geq\delta_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\phi}{n^{2}}.

As the expression on the right-hand side of (
2.4
) is absolutely summable, the Borel–Cantelli lemma yields that

(2.5)

\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}\delta_{n}^{-1}\sup_{0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1}\left|2^{n(2H-\frac{3}{2})}\left\lVert{\frac{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}_{2n,i}}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}}}}\right\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-1\right|\leq 1\qquad\mathbb{P}\text{-}a.s.

This completes the proof.
∎

We now start our pathwise analysis to obtain the bounds for the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{Y}_{2n,i}
. To this end, let us first of all clarify the notation we are going to use in the following proofs. We fix
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and let
x
be a typical sample path of
W^{H}
. Here, we refer to the sample paths that do not belong to the null set as typical sample paths. We let

y(t):=\int_{0}^{t}x(s)\,ds,\qquad u(t)=g(x(t))\quad\text{and}\quad v(t)=\int_{0}^{t}u(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}g(x(s))\,ds.

Using these notations, we can rephrase
Lemma

2.1
in the following strictly pathwise manner.

Remark 2.2
.

It follows from
Lemma

2.1
that for a typical sample path
x
of fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
, there exist
n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
and a positive constant
c_{H}>0
such that for
n\geq n_{x}
, we have

(2.6)

(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\tau_{H}\leq 2^{n(4H-3)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{y}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\leq(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\tau_{H},

for
\delta_{n}=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\sqrt{n\log 2+2\log n}
and all
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
. Here, the collection of typical sample paths consists of sample paths that are continuous and satisfy the convergence rate (
2.5
).

The following lemma shows that the two-sided bounds (
2.6
) can be carried over from the sample path
y
to the sample path
v
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
x
be a typical sample path of
W^{H}
. Then, there exist
n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{H}>0
and intermediate times
\tau^{a}_{n,i},\tau^{b}_{n,i}\in[2^{-n}i,2^{-n}(i+1)]
such that for
n\geq n_{x}
, we have

\begin{split}&\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{a}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}-2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big|\right)\tau_{H}\leq 2^{n(4H-3)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n,i}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}+2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big|\right)\tau_{H},\end{split}

for
\delta_{n}=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\sqrt{n\log 2+2\log n}
and all
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
.

Proof.

To prove this lemma, we let
\theta_{m,k}^{f}(s):=\theta_{m,k}^{f(s+\cdot)}
. That is,
\theta_{m,k}^{f}(s)
are the Faber–Schauder coefficients (
2.1
) of the function
t\mapsto f(s+t)
for given
s\geq 0
. One can avoid undefined arguments of functions in case
s+t>1
by setting
f(t):=f(t\wedge 1)
for
t\geq 1
. Furthermore, we let

\zeta^{x}_{n+1,2k}(s):=2^{(n+1)/2}\left(x\Big(\frac{4k+2}{2^{n+2}}+s\Big)-x\Big(\frac{4k}{2^{n+2}}+s\Big)\right)\big(x(\tau_{n+2,4k}(s))-x(\tau_{n+2,4k+1}(s))\big)

and

\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{n+1,2k}:=2^{n+5/2}\int_{0}^{2^{-n-1}}\zeta^{x}_{n+1,2k}(s)\,ds,

where
\tau_{n+2,k}(s)\in[2^{-n-2}k+s,2^{-n-2}(k+1)+s]
are certain intermediate times such that for
s\in[0,2^{-n-1}]
. It follows from
[
14
, Equation (4.5)]
that for
0\leq k\leq 2^{2n}-1
, we have

(2.7)

\begin{split}\big(\vartheta^{v}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}&=\big(g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\big)^{2}\big(\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}+\left(g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\right)^{2}\big(\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{2n+1,2k}\big)^{2}\\
&\qquad+2g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{2n+1,2k}.\end{split}

for intermediate times
\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,k},\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,k}\in[2^{-2n-1}{k},2^{-2n-1}(k+1)]
. It remains to compute the contribution of each term in (
2.7
). For simplicity, we will consider the special case
i=0
, and analogous computations can be done for
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
. First, as
x
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for
\alpha<H
, then there exists
c_{x}>0
such that

|x(\tau_{n+2,4k}(s))-x(\tau_{n+2,4k+1}(s))|\leq c_{x}|\tau_{n+2,4k}(s)-\tau_{n+2,4k+1}(s)|^{\alpha}\leq c_{x}2^{-\alpha n}.

The same argument also leads to

\left|x\Big(\frac{2k+1}{2^{n+1}}+s\Big)-x\Big(\frac{2k}{2^{n+1}}+s\Big)\right|\leq c_{x}2^{-\alpha n}

for
s\in[0,1]
. The above inequalities then lead to

|\zeta^{x}_{n+1,2k}(s)|\leq c_{x}^{2}2^{(\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha)n+\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad|\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{n+1,2k}|\leq c_{x}^{2}2^{(\frac{1}{2}-2\alpha)n+2}.

Furthermore, as
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists
\kappa_{x}>0
such that
32(g^{\prime\prime}(x(s)))^{2}\leq\kappa_{x}
for all
s\in[0,1]
. Then,

(2.8)

2^{(4H-3)n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\right)^{2}\left(\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{2n+1,2k}\right)^{2}\leq\kappa_{x}c_{x}^{2}2^{(4H-8\alpha)n}.

In addition, as
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
x
is continuous, the intermediate value theorem yields the existence of intermediate times
\tau^{a}_{n,0},\tau^{b}_{n,0}\in[0,2^{-n}]
such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{a}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\big)^{2}\big(\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}\leq\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}.

Applying (
2.6
) then yields the existence of
n_{1,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{1,x}
,

\begin{split}\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{a}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\tau_{H}&\leq 2^{n(4H-3)}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\big)^{2}\big(\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\big)^{2}\\
&\leq\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\tau_{H}.\end{split}

Finally, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, for
n\geq n_{1,x}
, we have

(2.10)

\begin{split}&2^{(4H-3)n}\left|\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{2n+1,2k}\right|\\
\leq&\,c_{x}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}\tau_{H}}(1+\delta_{n})2^{(2H-4\alpha)n}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|.\end{split}

Taking
\alpha=\frac{7}{8}H
in (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad 2^{(4H-3)n}\left(2\left|\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}g^{\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\sharp}_{2n+1,2k})\big)g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\vartheta^{y}_{2n,k}\widetilde{\zeta}^{x}_{2n+1,2k}\right|+\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(g^{\prime\prime}\big(x(\tau^{\flat}_{2n+1,2k})\big)\right)^{2}\left(\widetilde{\zeta}_{2n+1,2k}\right)^{2}\right)\\
&\leq\kappa_{x}c_{x}^{2}2^{-3Hn}+c_{x}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}\tau_{H}}(1+\delta_{n})2^{-3Hn/2+1}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|.\end{split}

As
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
as
n\uparrow\infty
and
g^{\prime}\circ x
is continuous, there exists
n_{2,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{2,x}
,

\kappa_{x}c_{x}^{2}2^{-3Hn}+c_{x}\sqrt{\kappa_{x}\tau_{H}}(1+\delta_{n})2^{-3Hn/2+1}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|\leq 2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|.

Take
n_{x}:=n_{1,x}\vee n_{2,x}
. Together with (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), the above inequality yields that for
n\geq n_{x}
, we get

\begin{split}&\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{a}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}-2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|\right)\tau_{H}\leq 2^{n(4H-3)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n,0}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big)^{2}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}+2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,0}))\big|\right)\tau_{H}.\end{split}

In particular, note that the value of
n_{x}
depends only on the trajectory
x
but not on the index
i
, thus, the above inequality carries over to all
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is strictly monotone, and let
x
be a sample path of
W^{H}
. Then, there exist
n_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{H}>0
and
\lambda_{x}>0
such that for
n\geq n_{x}
,

(2.11)

\begin{split}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}(1-\varepsilon_{n})\tau_{H}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds\right)&\leq 2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq(1+\delta_{n})^{2}(1+\varepsilon_{n})\tau_{H}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds\right),\end{split}

where
\delta_{n}=c_{H}\cdot 2^{-n/2}\sqrt{n\log 2+2\log n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}=\lambda_{x}\cdot 2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}
.

Proof.

We begin by proving the upper bound in (
2.11
). It follows from
Lemma

2.3
that there exists
n_{3,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{3,x}
, we have

(2.12)

\begin{split}2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}&=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}2^{n(4H-3)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n,k}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq 2^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}+2^{-5Hn/4}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big|\right)\tau_{H}.\end{split}

Furthermore, it follows from
[
18
, Theorem 7.2.14]
that the fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
admits an exact uniform modulus of continuity
\omega(u)=u^{H}\sqrt{\log(1/u)}
. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,1]\\
|t-s|<h\end{subarray}}\frac{\left|W^{H}_{t}-W^{H}_{s}\right|}{\omega(|t-s|)}=\sqrt{2}\right)=1.

Hence, there exists
n_{4,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{4,x}
and
0\leq i\leq 2^{n}-1
, we have

(2.13)

|x(s)-x(\tau^{b}_{n,i})|\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot\omega\left(|s-\tau^{b}_{n,i}|\right)\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot\omega(2^{-n})=2^{-Hn}\sqrt{2n\log 2},

for
s\in[2^{-n}i,2^{-n}(i+1)]
. Since
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
x\in C[0,1]
, then there exist positive constants
\kappa_{x},\widetilde{\kappa}_{x}>0
such that
|g^{\prime}(x(s))|\leq\kappa_{x}
and
|g^{\prime\prime}(x(s))|\leq\widetilde{\kappa}_{x}
for
s\in[0,1]
. Together with (
2.13
), it then yields that for
n\geq n_{4,x}
,

(2.14)

\begin{split}&\quad\,\left|\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds-2^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}\right|\\
&=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\int_{2^{-n}i}^{2^{-n}(i+1)}\left(\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}-\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}\right)ds\right|\\
&=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\int_{2^{-n}i}^{2^{-n}(i+1)}\left(g^{\prime}(x(s))+g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\right)g^{\prime\prime}(x(\widetilde{\tau}^{b}_{n,i}))\big(x(s)-x(\tau^{b}_{n,i})\big)ds\right|\\
&\leq\kappa_{x}\widetilde{\kappa}_{x}2^{-nH}\sqrt{8n\log 2},\end{split}

where
\widetilde{\tau}^{b}_{n,i}\in[2^{-n}i,2^{-n}(i+1)]
are intermediate times. Take

\lambda_{x}:=\sqrt{32\log 2}\cdot\kappa_{x}\widetilde{\kappa}_{x}\big(\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds\big)^{-1},

and it then follows that

(2.15)

2^{-n}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}\cdot 2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds.

As
g^{\prime}\circ x
is continuous, we have
\sup_{s\in[0,1]}|g^{\prime}(x(s))|<\infty
and
\sup_{n}2^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))|<\infty
. Moreover, by assumption, we have
\int_{0}^{1}(g^{\prime}(x(s)))^{2}\,ds>0
. Finally, we have
(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\uparrow 1
as
n\uparrow\infty
. Thus, there exists
n_{5,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{5,x}
, we get

(2.16)

\displaystyle 2^{-(1+5H/4)n}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big|g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}\cdot 2^{-Hn}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds

(2.17)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}\cdot 2^{-Hn}(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds.

Thus, for
n\geq n_{3,x}\vee n_{4,x}\vee n_{5,x}
, it follows from (
2.12
), (
2.15
) and (
2.17
) that

\begin{split}2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}&=2^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}2^{n(4H-3)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n,k}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\leq(1+\delta_{n})^{2}\left(1+\lambda_{x}2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}\right)\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds,\end{split}

which yields the upper bound in (
2.11
).

For the lower bound, note that
\tau^{a}_{n,i}
are also intermediate times within
[2^{-n}i,2^{-n}(i+1)]
. Following the arguments in (
2.14
) yields the existence of
n_{6,x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{6,x}
,

\left|\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds-2^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{b}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}\right|\leq\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds,

which then implies

2^{-n}(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(\tau^{a}_{n,i}))\big)^{2}\geq(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}\cdot 2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds.

This, together with (
2.16
), shows that for
n\geq n_{3,x}\vee n_{5,x}\vee n_{6,x}
,

2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\geq(1-\delta_{n})^{2}\left(1-\lambda_{x}2^{-nH}\sqrt{n}\right)\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds.

Now, taking
n_{x}:=n_{3,x}\vee n_{4,x}\vee n_{5,x}\vee n_{6,x}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is strictly monotone, and let
x
be a typical sample path of
W^{H}
. Then, we have

\left|\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{2n}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}}\right)-H\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot 2^{-(H\wedge\frac{1}{2})n}\right).

Proof.

Note that

\begin{split}H-\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{2n}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}}\right)&=(H-1)+\frac{1}{4n}\log_{2}\frac{\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}\\
&=\frac{1}{4n}\log_{2}\frac{2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}\\
&=\frac{1}{4n}\log_{2}\left(1+\left(1-\frac{2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}\right)\right)\\
&\sim\frac{1}{4n}\left(1-\frac{2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad n\uparrow\infty.\end{split}

Applying
Lemma

2.4
gives

\frac{1}{4n}\left(1-\frac{2^{n(4H-4)}\left\lVert{\bar{\bm{\vartheta}}^{v}_{2n}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\ell_{2}}}{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(x(s))\big)^{2}\,ds}\right)\sim\frac{1}{n}\left((1+\delta_{n})^{2}(1+\varepsilon_{n})-1\right)\sim\frac{\delta_{n}\vee\varepsilon_{n}}{n}\quad\text{as}\quad n\uparrow\infty,

where
\delta_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{n}
are as in
Lemma

2.4
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

It was shown in
[
15
, Theorem 1.4]
that the law of
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
(x_{0}+W^{H}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
. Hence, it suffices to prove this assertion for fractional Brownian motion
W^{H}
and
V_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}g(W^{H}_{s})\,ds
.

Now, suppose that
n=2m
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. It then follows from
Lemma

2.5
that with probability one,

\left|\widehat{\mathscr{R}}_{n}\left(\frac{V}{\sqrt{\tau_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\big(g^{\prime}(W^{H}_{s})\big)^{2}\,ds}}\right)-H\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot 2^{-(\frac{H}{2}\wedge\frac{1}{4})n}\right).

Thus, it follows from
[
14
, Proposition 2.6(d)]
that the assertion in
Theorem

1.2
holds for the case
n=2m
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For the case
n=2m+1
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the assertion can be proved analogously. This completes the proof.
∎
