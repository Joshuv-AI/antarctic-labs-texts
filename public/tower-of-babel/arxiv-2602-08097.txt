Prune, Don’t Rebuild: Efficiently Tuning α \alpha -Reachable Graphs for Nearest Neighbor Search
Tian Zhang, Ashwin Padaki, Jiaming Liang, Zack Ives, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
Vector similarity search is an essential primitive in modern AI and ML applications. Most vector databases adopt graph-based approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search algorithms, such as DiskANN [ 10 ] , which have demonstrated state-of-the-art empirical performance. DiskANN’s graph construction is governed by a reachability parameter α \alpha , which gives a trade-off between construction time, query time, and accuracy. However, adaptively tuning this trade-off typically requires rebuilding the index for different α \alpha values, which is prohibitive at scale. In this work, we propose RP-Tuning , an efficient post-hoc routine, based on DiskANN’s pruning step, to adjust the α \alpha parameter without reconstructing the full index. Within the α \alpha -reachability framework of prior theoretical works [ 8 , 7 ] , we prove that pruning an initially α \alpha -reachable graph with RP-Tuning preserves worst-case reachability guarantees in general metrics and improved guarantees in Euclidean metrics. Empirically, we show that RP-Tuning accelerates DiskANN tuning on four public datasets by up to 43 × \times with negligible overhead.

Introduction

Vector similarity search is an essential primitive in modern AI and ML applications, such as retrieval augmented generation
[
5
]
, linking and matching for knowledge graph construction
[
18
]
, and curating datasets for model training and fine-tuning.
Most vector database implementations adopt graph-based approximate-nearest-neighbor (ANN) search algorithms. At scale, DiskANN
[
10
]
is widely used, e.g., in Milvus
[
17
]
and Microsoft’s CosmosDB
[

[
12

).
A challenge with indices like DiskANN is that the tradeoffs between accuracy, latency, and space are not well understood — requiring users to empirically evaluate the indices under different hyperparameter settings, until the right tradeoffs are found.

To address the challenge, we propose
RP-Tuning
, an efficient post-hoc pruning algorithm derived from the
RobustPrune
subroutine used in the DiskANN index construction.
RP-Tuning
enables dynamic adjustment of DiskANN’s reachability parameter, giving a tunable trade-off between accuracy, latency, and index size. By starting from a high-quality base graph, it avoids the cost of repeated index reconstruction. Leveraging recent theoretical frameworks for
\alpha
-reachable graphs
[
8
,
7
]
, we provide a rigorous analysis of
RP-Tuning
. Namely, we prove that a graph pruned via
RP-Tuning
preserves specific worst-case reachability guarantees in general metrics while achieving superior worst-case bounds in Euclidean metrics.

Our empirical evaluation across four public datasets demonstrates that
RP-Tuning
accelerates the DiskANN tuning process by up to 43
\times
. Moreover, indices tuned with
RP-Tuning
exhibit performance gains over those rebuilt with the same reachability parameters. Similarly to model distillation, our techniques allow for the “distillation” of an index into different configurations, suitable for different hardware capabilities, with predictable accuracy.

2.1
DiskANN Algorithm Review

There are multiple variants of DiskANN
[
6
,
9
,
1
]
. We review two variants that are relevant to this paper: the original Vamana heuristics proposed in
[
10
]
and the “slow preprocessing” variant formalized in
[
8
]
.

The search algorithm is the same for both variants (see
Algorithm

2
). Given the index graph, the search is initialized by seeding a candidate set
\mathcal{L}
with a designated start vertex
s
(typically the medoid) and maintaining an empty set of visited vertices
\mathcal{V}
. In each iteration, the algorithm explores the graph by selecting the point
p^{*}
from the unexplored candidates in
\mathcal{L}
that is most proximal to the query
q
. By immediately adding the out-neighbors
N_{\text{out}}(p^{*})
to the candidate set
\mathcal{L}
and marking
p^{*}
as visited, the algorithm effectively “walks” through the graph’s edges toward the query. To ensure the search remains computationally tractable, the candidate set
\mathcal{L}
is restricted to a maximum size
L
; if the addition of neighbors causes the set to exceed this bound,
\mathcal{L}
is pruned to retain only the
L
points closest to
q
. This iterative process of greedy selection and neighborhood expansion continues until no unvisited points remain in the search beam, at which point the
k
best-found candidates are returned.

The index construction of both variants relies on the subroutine
RobustPrune
(see
Algorithm

1
).
RobustPrune
prunes out-neighbor candidates for the input vertex
p
and utilizes a parameter
\alpha\geq 1
to favor “diverse” neighbors that provide long-range shortcuts across the dataset. The algorithm proceeds by iteratively selecting the point
p^{*}
in the candidate set
\mathcal{V}
that is closest to the target point
p
. Once a candidate is added to
N_{out}(p)
, a pruning condition is applied to all remaining candidates: any point
p^{\prime}
that is closer to the newly added out-neighbor
p^{*}
than it is to the target point
p
by a factor of
\alpha
(i.e.,
D(p^{*},p^{\prime})<D(p,p^{\prime})/\alpha
) is removed from the candidate set
\mathcal{V}
. The intuition is that the edge
(p,p^{\prime})
is potentially not important if moving from p to p* decreases the distance to p’ by a factor of
\alpha
.

The “slow preprocessing” construction executes
\textsc{RobustPrune}(p,P\setminus\{p\},\alpha,n-1)
for every vertex
p
in the graph. Notice the candidate set
\mathcal{V}=P\setminus\{p\}
, and the degree limit
R=n-1
, which results in an
O(n^{3})
time complexity that is impractical at scale. However, it provides a rigorous structural foundation,
\alpha
-reachability, for analyzing the algorithm’s worst-case performance on construction time, query time, and approximation factor.

Definition 2.1

(
\alpha
-reachable and
\alpha
-reachability

Then, the following result on the “slow preprocessing” construction can be proved. Recall

\displaystyle D(b_{j},q)

From the above, we can see the parameter

•

Storage:
O(n\cdot(4\alpha)^{\lambda}\log\Delta)

•

Query Time:
O\left(\left(L+\log_{\alpha}\frac{\Delta}{(\alpha-1)\epsilon}\right)\cdot(4\alpha)^{\lambda}\log\Delta\right)

•

Approximation:
\left(\epsilon+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)

As

The original Vamana index construction, on the other hand, caps the maximum out-degree of the graph for fast construction in practice. Besides
RobustPrune

[
10

for details. Despite no clear worst-case guarantee, Vamana can achieve state-of-the-art empirical performance, and the parameter

RP-Tuning
and Worst-Case Guarantees

In this section, we propose
RP-Tuning
, a simple yet disciplined pruning algorithm based on
RobustPrune
, which efficiently prunes an
\alpha
-reachable graph
G
into a sparser graph with a theoretical guarantee of worst-case performance trade-off.

RP-Tuning

RP-Tuning
takes an initially
\alpha_{1}
-reachable graph
G=(P,E)
, and a new reachability target
\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{1}
as inputs. It simply runs
\textsc{RobustPrune}(p,N_{out}(p),\alpha_{2},\infty)
for every
p\in P
and outputs the modified graph
G^{\prime}=(P,E^{\prime})
.

We study the worst-case reachability of the output
G^{\prime}
of
\textsc{RP-Tuning}(G,\alpha_{2})
where
G
is an
\alpha_{1}
-reachable graph. It turns out that the worst-case reachability of
G^{\prime}
is a function of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and depends on the metric space and whether the pruning subroutine
RobustPrune
sorts the out-neighbors. To distinguish the unsorted version from
RobustPrune
, we use
UnsortedRobustPrune
to denote the procedure that is the same as
RobustPrune
but only replaces the step “
p^{*}\leftarrow\arg\min_{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}D(p,p^{\prime})
” in
Algorithm

1
with “
p^{*}
being an arbitrary out-neighbor of
p
”. Our results on the reachability of
G^{\prime}
in different settings are summarized in

3.1
and

3.2

3.1
Proof of
Theorem

3.1

Let
G=(P,E)
be an
\alpha_{1}
-reachable graph and let
G^{\prime}=(P,E^{\prime})
be obtained by applying
\textsc{UnsortedRobustPrune}(p,\emptyset,\alpha_{2},\infty)
at every
p\in P
.

Let
\alpha^{*}
denote the reachability of
G^{\prime}
. Fix an ordered pair
p,z
that is tight for
\alpha^{*}
-reachability and satisfies
(p,z)\notin E^{\prime}
(such a pair must exist since
\alpha^{*}
is finite). Then, the directed edge
(p,z)
was either (a) absent in
G
, or (b) pruned from

(p,y)\in E
for which
D(y,z)\leq D(p,z)/\alpha_{2}
, which gives
\alpha^{\ast}\geq\alpha_{2}
.

In case (a),
\alpha_{1}
-reachability of

with parameter

either (a1) prunes

from

D(x,y)\leq D(p,y)/\alpha_{2}

is at least the value of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{p,x,y,z\in X}{\min}

\displaystyle{D(p,z)}/{D(x,z)}

\displaystyle D(y,z)\leq{D(p,z)}/{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle D(x,y)\leq{D(p,y)}/{\alpha_{2}}

By invariance under scaling, we may take
D(p,z)=1
and flip the objective to get the following crucial lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,D)
be a metric space. Given parameters
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq 1
, the worst-case reachability of the graph
G^{\prime}
obtained by taking an
\alpha_{1}
-reachable graph

\min\{\alpha_{2},1/\mathrm{OPT}\}

\displaystyle\underset{p,x,y,z\in X}{\max}

\displaystyle D(x,z)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle D(y,z)\leq{1}/{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle D(x,y)\leq{D(p,y)}/{\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle D(p,z)=1

We solve the optimization problem in a straightforward geometric way. We first apply the triangle inequality and the constraints in the above problem to get an upper bound of

\displaystyle D(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq D(x,y)+D(y,z)

\displaystyle\leq D(p,y)/\alpha_{2}+1/\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(D(p,z)+D(y,z))/\alpha_{2}+1/\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+1/\alpha_{1})/\alpha_{2}+1/\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1)/(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}),

where the first and third lines use the triangle inequality and the second and fourth lines use the constraints of the optimization problem. We also know

.

In a general metric space or a Euclidean space

Proof Overview of
Theorem

3.2

Now we use

where neighbors in the graph are considered for pruning in ascending order of their distances to the pivot point. The proof follows the same template as the unsorted case. The key difference is that, because

processes candidates in increasing distance from

any point

Moreover, since

serves as the
\alpha_{1}
-reachability witness for

, we also have
D(p,y)\leq D(p,z)
. This yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(X,D(\cdot,\cdot))
be a metric space, and
p,x,y,z\in X
be four points. Given parameters satisfying
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq 1
, the worst-case reachability of the graph
G^{\prime}
obtained by taking an
\alpha_{1}
-reachable graph and applying
\textsc{RobustPrune}(p,\emptyset,\alpha_{2},\infty)
at every

is
\min\{\alpha_{2},1/\mathrm{OPT}\}
, where
\mathrm{OPT}
is the value of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\underset{p,x,y,z\in X}{\max}

\displaystyle D(x,z)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle D(y,z)\leq{1}/{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle D(x,y)\leq{D(p,y)}/{\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle D(x,y)\leq D(p,y)\leq D(p,z)=1

Notice that the only difference in the optimization problem is that there is an ordered condition in the last constraint, which rules out the pattern seen in the previous subsection. However, we can obtain an upper bound on the optimal objective of the problem in the same way using the triangle inequality and problem constraints:
D(x,z)\leq D(x,y)+D(y,z)\leq D(p,y)/\alpha_{2}+1/\alpha_{1}\leq 1/\alpha_{2}+1/\alpha_{1}=(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})/(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})
. Note that the upper bound is smaller than the one in unsorted case. In the general metric space, the upper bound is actually tight.

Lemma 3.7
.

In a general metric space
(X,D)
, there exists a setting of four points

Related Work

Approximate nearest-neighbor index structures are core to every vector database system, and their importance has motivated recent NeurIPS challenges
[
14
,
13
]
. Early real-world systems leveraged the FAISS library
[
4
]
, often using its implementation of the Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds
[
12
]
(HNSW) algorithm. However, few theoretical properties are known about HNSW, and it targets scenarios where the entire index fits into RAM. Other techniques more appropriate to disk-based storage, such as ANNOY
[
15
]
(based on random hyperplane splits into memory-mapped files), and inverted-list-based methods, such as SPANN
[
3
]
, are not well understood theoretically. DiskANN
[
6
,
10
,
1
]
remains a commonly used, state-of-the-art method for disk-based indexing. Its graph-based approach has been studied theoretically
[
8
,
7
]
. Our work builds upon the formal understanding of DiskANN, but focuses on leveraging the theoretical insights to obtain practical index tuning.
