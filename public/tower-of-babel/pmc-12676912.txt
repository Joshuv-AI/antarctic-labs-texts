Cementless Versus Cemented Fixation in Total Knee Arthroplasty: Analysis of Regional Tibial Bone Density and Clinical Outcome
Authors: Daniel Wai-yip Wong, Qunn-jid Lee, Chi-kin Lo, Kenneth Wing-kin Law, Esther Wai-yee Chang, Yiu-Chung Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCementless fixation in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has theoretical advantages of being biological and bone preserving, but some surgeons are less confident in using it given previous reports of high failure rates in some implant designs. This study aimed to investigate the effect of fixation method on tibial bone density, clinical outcome, and survivorship. The main research question was whether fixation method would affect the postoperative change in tibial bone density in TKA.MethodsThis study analyzed 53 cementless TKAs and 53 cemented TKAs of the same brand (Triathlon, Stryker). Digital radiological densitometry (DRD) was used to quantify the changes in regional tibial bone density (RTBD) within the first 2 years. Clinical outcome scores and survivorship were recorded.ResultsSignificant decreases in RTBD were observed in both groups in all tibial regions (p < 0.001 at all time points). RTBD was significantly higher in 7 of 8 tibial regions in cementless TKA at 6 months. The significance persisted in 2 regions of the lateral tibial condyle until the second year (p = 0.014 and p = 0.029). Clinical outcome scores (Knee Society Score, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, and Forgotten Joint Score) were similar. No case of aseptic loosening was reported.ConclusionsProximal tibial bone resorption was common in both cementless and cemented TKAs. Cementless fixation preserved more tibial metaphyseal bone globally at 6 months and at the lateral tibial condyle at 24 months. Its early clinical outcomes and survivorship were comparable to those of cemented fixation.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Robotic-assisted surgery, Bone density, Bone resorption

Cemented fixation has been the popular mode of fixation in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) due to its promising long-term results.1) Cementless fixation, on the other hand, has cast doubt on some surgeons due to previous reports of early failure and aseptic loosening.2,3) However, in the past decade, there has been a renewed surge of interest in cementless fixation following the popularization of robotic arm-assisted surgery to improve the precision of bone cuts.4) In the United States, the use of cementless fixation in TKA increased from 1.9% in 2012 to 18.8% in 2021.5) Besides, new implant designs, materials, and 3-dimensional (3D) printing technology have been available to achieve better osseointegration than previous generations.6,7) With all these technological advancements, the use of cementless fixation may potentially result in comparable long-term survivorship as cemented fixation.6,8)

“Are there any additional benefits of using cementless fixation in TKA, when compared to cemented fixation?” This is an important question to answer before one can justify the shift from the reliable cemented fixation to a less commonly used method. Theoretically, cementless fixation is more biological. As more and more young patients are having their knees replaced,9) it could be advantageous to choose a biological fixation method that preserves the bone remodeling effect at the implant-bone interface, which could potentially prolong implant longevity.10) Cementless fixation also preserves more bone stock, whose effect is most significant in the case of revision, where bone loss can be minimized without the need to remove the cement mantle overlying the bone surface.11,12) In the absence of cementation, intraoperative cement embolization can be avoided and operative time may be shortened. The absence of cement debris also reduces third body wear.12)

Few previous studies have compared the change in tibial bone density following cementless and cemented fixation and they have reported non-uniform results.13,14,15,16,17) The current study aimed to quantitatively compare the postoperative tibial bone density between the 2 fixation modes in 2 modern implants of the same brand, using the method of digital radiological densitometry (DRD).13,18,19,20,21) It was hypothesized that cementless fixation would result in a similar decrease in tibial bone density when compared to cemented fixation. A second hypothesis was that cementless fixation would achieve non-inferior early clinical outcomes and survivorship compared to cemented fixation.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Kowloon West Cluster, Hospital Authority, Hong Kong (No. EX-23-052(186-09)), and written informed consent was waived by the Committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study Design

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted in a total joint replacement centre. Patients’ demographic data, body mass index (BMI), operative details, perioperative knee plain radiographs, outcome scores, and survivorship were reviewed and recorded.

The first cohort involved 53 consecutive cementless TKAs (in 45 patients) performed during the period of August 2020 to January 2021, using the MAKO robotic arm-assisted system (Stryker). A preoperative computed tomography scan of the lower limb of the operation side was obtained for accurate intraoperative surface mapping, soft-tissue balancing, bone cutting, and implant positioning. All 53 knees were implanted with the cementless 3D-printed Triathlon Tritanium model (Stryker). The second cohort involved 53 consecutive cemented TKAs (in 47 patients) performed during the period of May 2018 to January 2019, using conventional instruments. All 53 knees were implanted with the cemented Triathlon model (Stryker). All implant components were cemented with Simplex P (Stryker).

Exclusion Criteria

Five knees (2 cementless and 3 cemented) were excluded for having a preoperative valgus mechanical alignment. Four knees (3 cementless and 1 cemented) were excluded for having less than 18 months of follow-up.

Outcome Measurements

Primary outcome: regional tibial bone density

The regional tibial bone density (RTBD) was quantified using the method of DRD.13,18,19,20,21) Standard anteroposterior (AP) plain radiographs were taken early postoperatively within 3 days, at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively. AP radiographs were considered standard if the posterior femoral condyles were not visible and there was 45%–55% overlapping of fibular head with tibia. The tibia was divided into 8 regions of interest (ROI) (Fig. 1).

Description of regions of interest (ROI). The tibia was divided into 8 ROI: medial (M1 to M4), lateral (L1 to L3), and distal (D1). The height of each region occupied one-third of the height of the tibial stem. M1 and M2 regions were divided by the midpoint of the medial tibial baseplate. An ROI was drawn as a best-fit polygon while avoiding the bone cortex, cement, implant, and fibular head.

The RTBD was analyzed by importing the electronic plain radiograph into the eUnity software (version 7.1.1.204, Mach7 Technologies Canada Inc.). A ROI was defined by drawing a best-fit polygon while excluding the bone cortex, cement, implant, and fibular head (Fig. 1), after which a program-generated mean pixel value of the ROI (range, 0–4,096) was recorded. Secondary outcomes

Clinical outcome scores including Knee Society Score (KSS), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score and Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) were measured by our specialist nurses at 4 weeks preoperatively, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Postoperative mechanical alignment was measured from the lower limb scanogram taken at postoperative 6 months. Postoperative range of motion (ROM) was measured by our specialist nurses at postoperative 1 year. Survivorship was recorded with aseptic loosening as the endpoint.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was calculated with reference to the data from Small et al.13) Taking the bone densities at the M1 region at 3 years as the benchmark (decrease in bone densities: 14.7% in the cementless group and 10.4% in the cemented group), to attain a 95% confidence level and 0.9 power, the sample size required was 41 in each arm (1 : 1 ratio).22)

Data Analyses

All data analyses were performed by IBM SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used as parametric tests, while the Mann-Whitney U-test and Fischer’s exact test were used as non-parametric tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Continuous variables were expressed in mean ± standard deviation, corrected to 1 decimal place, unless otherwise specified.

Demographics and Preoperative Assessments

Fifty-three knees in 45 patients (age, 68.5 ± 5.6 years) were in the cementless group, while 53 knees in 47 patients (age, 68.7 ± 7.1 years) were in the cemented group. The 2 groups had similar female sex ratios and BMI (Table 1). All except 1 knee in the cemented group were operated for primary osteoarthritis. Both groups had comparable mean preoperative varus mechanical alignments. Mean preoperative ROM between the 2 groups was similar (Table 2).

RESULTS

Radiographic Analysis

Radiographically, the cementless group was followed up for 25.0 ± 3.6 months, while the cemented group was followed up for 23.6 ± 4.3 months (p = 0.058).

Intra-group change in RTBD

In either group, a significant decrease in RTBD was observed in all 8 tibial regions at all time points (cementless group: 9.5%–41.7%, cemented group: 14.1%–41.9%; p < 0.001, compared to early postoperative RTBD) (Fig. 2). The most significant drop in RTBD was observed in the M1 region (cementless group: 41.7%, cemented group: 41.9%, at 24 months), which corresponded to the superomedial portion of the medial tibial condyle (Fig. 2).

Change in postoperative regional tibial bone density over 2 years. RTBD: regional tibial bone density, M: medial, L: lateral, D: distal. *p < 0.05.

Between-group comparison of RTBD

Early postoperatively, the RTBD values in all 8 tibial regions were similar in both groups (Fig. 2). At postoperative 6 months, the cementless group demonstrated a smaller decrease in the RTBD in the M1-M4 and L1-L3 regions (global). Such significant differences persisted in the L2 and L3 regions (lateral tibial condyle) until the second year (Fig. 2).

Clinical Outcomes

Outcome scores

There was significant improvement in KSS and WOMAC scores at postoperative 6 months and 1 year in both groups (p < 0.001). When comparing between the 2 groups, all clinical outcome scores, in terms of KSS, WOMAC, and FJS were similar at postoperative 6 months and 1 year, except that there was a slightly better WOMAC score in the cemented group at 1 year (cementless group: 13.6 ± 2.7, cemented group: 10.9 ± 2.1; p = 0.023) (Table 3).

Survivorship

The survivorship was 100% for both groups at 24 months, with aseptic loosening defined as the endpoint.

DISCUSSION

The current study compared the postoperative changes in tibial bone density between cementless and cemented TKA. Both cementless and cemented groups showed significant reductions in RTBD across all tibial regions postoperatively, most notably in the superomedial region (M1). The cementless group had a smaller decline in M1-M4 and L1-L3 regions at 6 months, with differences persisting in L2-L3 up to 24 months. Clinically, both groups improved in KSS and WOMAC scores. Postoperative alignment, ROM, and early survivorship (100% at 24 months) were comparable.

The effects of cementless fixation on postoperative tibial bone density in TKA remain an important research focus, with studies reporting both comparable and potentially favorable outcomes relative to cemented fixation. Two comparative studies by Small et al.13) and Abu-Rajab et al.14) found no significant differences in bone density decline between cemented and cementless groups at 2 and 10 years, respectively, indicating comparable stress shielding effects.

In contrast, some studies have demonstrated better bone density preservation with cementless fixation. Linde et al.15) conducted an randomized controlled trial (RCT) and reported improved bone mineral density (BMD) in 2 tibial regions in the cementless group at 24 months (medial: +1.8% vs. −8.6%, p < 0.05; anterior: +7.5% vs. −4.2%, p < 0.05). Similarly, another RCT by Nivbrant et al.16) also showed significantly less BMD loss in cementless TKAs at 24 months (medial: −5.5% vs. −12.7%, p = 0.02; lateral: +3.1% vs. −7.4%, p = 0.02), though they questioned its clinical relevance due to a higher early revision rate (4.3% vs. 2.0%) and possible BMD compaction due to subsidence. Minoda et al.17) observed reduced lateral tibial BMD loss (−6.7% vs. −36.8%, p = 0.002) with cementless porous tantalum components at 24 months, but they highlighted that the finding could be multifactorial, e.g., implant material and design, rather than due to fixation method alone. The current study aligns with these findings, showing less bone resorption in the lateral tibial condyle (L2 and L3 regions) at 24 months with cementless fixation (Fig. 2).

On top of the above, the current study had investigated the change in RTBD at smaller intervals within the first 2 years (6 months, first year, and second year) and found that the rate of bone resorption was dynamic and non-linear (Fig. 2). At 6 months, cementless fixation demonstrated significantly less bone resorption in 7 of the 8 tibial regions. The significant difference remained in 5 regions at the first year and only in 2 regions at the second year. The authors postulate that this was due to the gradually increasing effect of stress shielding in cementless TKA in the post-implantation period. At early post-implantation, no bony ingrowth was present at the implant-bone interface.23) Less implant-bone anchorage correlates to less stress shielding.24,25) As bony ingrowth increased with time, the effect of stress shielding intensified, leading to more bone resorption.

Previous studies raised concerns over early tibial implant migration and potential higher early revision risk in cementless TKA.15,16) However, these studies only reported results within 2 years. A more recent RCT by van der Lelij et al.7) studied their patients beyond 5 years and found no further implant migration between the second and fifth years (change in mean maximum total point motion, cementless: 0.02 mm vs. cemented: 0.07 mm). They concluded that cementless implants remained stable after initial early migration.

In the current study, no case of aseptic loosening was reported in both groups within the first 2 years. The authors believe that the better tibial metaphyseal bone preservation in cementless TKA has contributed to the prevention of excessive tibial tray subsidence at the early stage, thus preventing early loosening. Early clinical outcome scores in the first year were similar (Table 3). With such non-inferior early survivorship and clinical outcome, the use of cementless fixation shall be justified in selected young patients with reasonable bone quality, in the pursuit of biological fixation and potentially better longevity.

There were several limitations for this study. First, its retrospective nature resulted in inevitable biases. Second, DRD, instead of the gold standard dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), was used to quantify tibial bone density. We were limited in that we could not measure the true BMD. First described by Hernandez-Vaquero et al.,18) DRD was used by subsequent studies as a tool for retrospective analysis of bone density using historic radiographs.13,19,20,21) Given the previous validation against DEXA scan with good intraobserver and interobserver agreements (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.72–0.88, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient: 0.84–0.93),18) it can serve as a reliable alternative to DEXA scan. Its strength over DEXA scan is that it allows finer division of tibial regions for more precise comparisons of ROI. Third, the difference in surgical techniques (robotic arm-assisted for cementless group and conventional for cemented group) may be a potential source of bias, though their direct effects on the change in tibial bone density over 2 years were questionable. Fourth, as the 2 cohorts included 2 similar implants from 1 brand only, the results of the current study were inadequate to be generalized to all implant designs in the market. Finally, a follow-up period of 2 years was short. Future research shall include subjects with longer follow-up, to look for midterm survivorship and to observe whether the bone preserving property at the lateral tibial condyle in cementless fixation could persist beyond 2 years.

Proximal tibial bone resorption was common in both cementless and cemented TKAs. Cementless fixation preserved more tibial metaphyseal bone globally at 6 months and at the lateral tibial condyle at 24 months. Its early clinical outcome and survivorship were comparable to those of cemented fixation.
