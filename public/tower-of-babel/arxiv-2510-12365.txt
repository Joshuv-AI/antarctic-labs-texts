Planted clique recovery in random geometric graphs
Konstantin Avrachenkov, Andrei Bobu

Abstract
We investigate the problem of identifying planted cliques in random geometric graphs, focusing on two distinct algorithmic approaches: the first based on vertex degrees (VD) and the other on common neighbors (CN). We analyze the performance of these methods under varying regimes of key parameters, namely the average degree of the graph and the size of the planted clique. We demonstrate that exact recovery is achieved with high probability as the graph size increases, in a specific set of parameters. Notably, our results reveal that the CN-algorithm significantly outperforms the VD-algorithm. In particular, in the connectivity regime, tiny planted cliques (even edges) are correctly identified by the CN-algorithm, yielding a significant impact on anomaly detection. Finally, our results are confirmed by a series of numerical experiments, showing that the devised algorithms are effective in practice. Keywords: planted clique, random geometric graph, vertex degrees, common neighbors, network anomalies

Introduction

Graphs give a natural way to model real-world networks. One of the most natural questions that arises in such modeling is the identification of nodes or groups of nodes with certain properties. For instance, many researchers are interested in finding large cliques in graphs that correspond to tightly connected communities in a real network. One possible variant of such a task is to reveal ‘suspicious’ structures or anomalies in the graph that cannot naturally appear in the corresponding network.

The problem of identification of artificial large cliques in Erdős–Rényi graphs is a classical problem in graph theory. It is called the
planted
(or
hidden
)
clique problem
and can be formalized as follows. Consider a realization of the Erdős–Rényi graph
G^{ER}(n,1/2)
with
n
nodes and the probability of an edge 1/2. Choose randomly its
k
nodes. These nodes are declared a
planted clique
, and we add all missing edges between them. The task is to identify the planted clique with high probability using a polynomial-time algorithm.

The planted clique problem appeared in 1995 with the work of Kučera
[
14
]
. He found that if
k=\Omega(\sqrt{n\log n})
, then the problem can be solved by a simple algorithm based on comparing the degrees of vertices. Let us notice that the largest clique in an Erdős–Rényi graph
G^{ER}(n,1/2)
is of size
\log_{2}n(1+o(1))
a. s. Therefore, such an algorithm may be far from optimal. In 1998, it was proved in
[
1
]
that for any
c>0
there is a polynomial-time algorithm that recovers a planted clique with high probability for
k>c\sqrt{n}
. Certain classes of polynomial algorithms have been proved to fail for
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, but a general barrier has not yet been found (see discussion in
[
8
]
–
[
10
]
).

It is surprising and curious that such an interesting and natural problem has not been stated for other models of random graphs. In the current paper, we propose a similar question for random geometric graphs, which are a fairly popular research object lately. Random geometric graphs resemble real social, technological, and biological networks in many aspects due to their tendency to cluster
[
11
]
–
[
20
]
. Also, they can be useful in statistics and machine learning tasks: correlations between observations in a data set can be represented by links between corresponding close vertices (see e.g.,
[
2
]
). Artificial cliques can signal a malicious intervention in the network or some problems with the data.

Another curious example of a possible application is steganography (see
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
). The idea is that a hidden message (
stego
) is put inside a big piece of information (
cover
, which can be, say, an image). The adjacency matrices can represent such pieces, while a planted clique put in them corresponds to a stego (the Ising model from
[
9
, Ex. 2.1]
looks close to that setting). One of the problems of steganography is to determine how large a stego can be that cannot be detected by any algorithm. This is perfectly consistent with the planted clique recovery problem. In steganography, it is usually assumed that the pixels for the stego are chosen independently, whereas in the case of the planted clique problem, they are dependent (as elements of the adjacency matrix). Nevertheless, we think that a formulation with dependencies might be even more realistic and interesting for steganography.

One of the key properties of random geometric graphs is the dependency between edges caused by their geometrical structure. In these graphs, the nodes have corresponding spatial positions and the probability of edge appearance depends on the distance between these positions (in contrast to Erdős–Rényi graphs that are more ‘homogeneous’). As a rule, random geometric graphs have larger clique numbers than Erdős–Rényi graphs with the same average node degree. Indeed, in a geometric graph, a cluster of vertices that are sufficiently close to each other will automatically form a clique. This gives an intuition that the planted clique problem in this case can be richer and will depend on the parameters of a graph.

In the current paper, we consider hard random geometric graphs (RGG)
[
16
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
18
]
. In this model, the vertices’ positions are chosen uniformly at random on a
d
-dimensional torus, where
d\geq 1
is fixed, and a threshold distance
r:=r_{n}
is set such that nodes are connected if and only if their distance (induced by the
L_{2}
norm on the torus) is less than
r
.

The main point of this paper is to show that the geometric structure of a graph allows for the detection of artificial structures (such as planted cliques) much more effectively than in Erdős–Rényi graphs. It is useful to recall that the asymptotics of the clique number of the hard geometric graph on
n
vertices is (see e.g.,
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
19
, Theorem 6.16]
):

\omega_{n}\sim\begin{cases}O(1)\quad a.s.,&\text{if $\mu\leq n^{-\varepsilon}$ for some $\varepsilon>0$},\\
\frac{\log n}{\log\left(\frac{\log n}{\mu}\right)}\quad a.s.,&\text{if $n^{-\varepsilon}\ll\mu\ll\log(n)$ for all $\varepsilon>0$},\\
\mu f(t)\quad a.s.,&\text{if $\mu/\log(n)\to t\in(0,\infty)$},\\
\mu/2^{d}\quad a.s.,&\text{if $\mu\gg\log(n)$},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\mu
is the expected degree of
G(n,r)
, and
f(t):=\frac{H_{+}^{-1}(2^{d}/t)}{2^{d}}
, where
H^{-1}_{+}
is the inverse of the entropy
H(x)=1-x+x\log(x)
on the half-line
(1,\infty)
. Equation (
1
) shows a major difference in the clique number among different regimes of RGG. In the dense and connectivity settings, the largest clique in the graph has size proportional to
\mu
; in sparse settings, it is super-linear in
\mu
. It might be tempting to conjecture that any artificially planted clique needs a size larger than the
natural
clique number in RGG, to detect it. Although for the Erdős–Rényi models this might be the case, in RGGs it is possible to go far below the thresholds in (
1
), as we will show later.

Our main contributions are as follows. First, we study an algorithm based on vertex degrees (shortly,
VD-algorithm
) that was introduced in
[
14
]
for the Erdős–Rényi model. We determine a recovery threshold for the VD-algorithm to recover a planted clique. Second, we introduce a simple polynomial algorithm based on common neighbors (we denote this algorithm by
CN-algorithm
). We prove that the CN-algorithm solves the problem for hard random geometric graphs in a much richer regime. Surprisingly, the algorithm recovers even extremely small cliques, such as planted edges. Finally, we present practical experiments showing that CN- and VD-algorithms work well in practice.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce geometric random graphs, formulate the planted clique problem, and give a detailed description of VD- and CN-algorithms. Main results concerning the theoretical performance guarantees of the algorithms are collected in Section
3
. Next, we provide in Section
4
numerical experiments as evidence supporting the theoretical results. Proof for the VD-algorithm and the CN-algorithm can be found respectively in Sections
5
and
6
. Lastly, we collect some thoughts on further research directions in Section
7
.

2
Problem statement

We will now state the problem more precisely. We use the common notation
G=(V,E)
to indicate a simple graph, where
V
is a set of vertices and
E
is the set of edges.
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent random variables uniformly distributed in the unit
d
-dimensional cube
U=[0,1]^{d}
. To exclude boundary effects, we suppose that
U
is equipped with the toroidal metric. Formally, for any position vectors
x=(x_{1},...,x_{d}),y=(y_{1},...,y_{d})
, the distance on
U
is given by

d_{T}(x,y)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min\left(|x_{i}-y_{i}|,\ 1-|x_{i}-y_{i}|\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The random geometric graph model is parameterized by the expected number of vertices
n>0
and a connection probability function
p_{n}
. Let us denote by
N_{n}
a Poisson random variable with mean
n
. We consider the vertex set
V=\{1,\ldots,N_{n}\}
, where each vertex
i
is associated with its position
X_{i}
. Any two vertices at distance
x
are connected independently with the connection probability
p_{n}(x)
. In other words,

p_{n}(x)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\{i,j\}\in E\,|\,d_{T}(X_{i},X_{j})=x\bigr).

The model defined in this way is called a random geometric graph model, and will be denoted by
G(n,p_{n})
. In what follows, we will focus on
hard random geometric graphs
(RGG), where the connection probability
p_{n}
has threshold form:

p_{n}(x)=1(x\leq r_{n})

(2)

for some
r_{n}>0
, and for technical reasons we will assume
r_{n}<1/4
. In other words, in RGGs, each vertex has its sphere of influence of radius
r_{n}
, within which it forms connections. The resulting graph is denoted by
G(n,r_{n})
.

The average expected degree in
G(n,r_{n})
is denoted by
\mu_{n}
. Average degree and radius of the connectivity are linked through the equation
\mu=n\phi_{d}r_{n}^{d}
, where
\phi_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional ball of radius 1.

Now we are ready to describe the
planted clique problem
, which is the object of the current investigation. Specify a positive integer
k_{n}\geq 2
, that can vary with the network size
n
. Consider the graph
G(n,r_{n})
and select randomly
k_{n}
vertices
K_{n}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k_{n}}\}
from
V
. Next, add all the missing edges between all pairs of vertices in
K_{n}
, forcing
K_{n}
to form a clique. We denote the resulting graph by
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
. Our goal is to recover the planted clique
K_{n}
. We focus on the conditions for the exact recovery, that is, we would like to build an algorithm that outputs a set
\hat{K}_{n}
of size
k_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(K_{n}=\hat{K}_{n}\right)=1.

(3)

Well-posedness of the problem

In the process of planting a clique on the vertex set
K
in
G(n,r_{n})
, some edges between elements of
K
may already be present. Therefore, only the missing edges are added. However, for certain parameter choices, the clique
K
may already be complete in the original graph
G(n,r_{n})
. In this case,
G(n,r_{n})=G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
, making the problem ill-posed because it is impossible to distinguish an instance of the random geometric graph from the graph augmented by a planted clique.
Consequently, we will exclude the ill-posed parameter settings defined by
\{k=O(1)\}\wedge\{\mu_{n}\propto n\}
from our analysis because for this parameter regime,
\mathbb{P}\left(G(n,r_{n})=G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})\right)>0
, and we might otherwise encounter recovery issues.

Notation

We now introduce some notations used throughout the paper. The degree of a vertex
i\in V
in a graph is denoted by
Z_{i}=|N(i)|
, where
N(i)
indicates the set of neighbors of
i
. The number of neighbors of
i
in
U\subset V
is instead denoted by
Z_{i}^{U}=|N(i)\cap U|
(note that
i
does not necessarily belong to
U
). Moreover, the number of common neighbors of vertices
i
and

is denoted by
Z_{ij}=|N(i)\cap N(j)|
.
The maximum and minimum degrees in
G(n,r_{n})
are denoted by
\Delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n}
respectively.

The comparisons
f(n)\ll g(n)
,
f(n)\gg g(n)
and
f(n)\sim g(n)
are understood standardly:
f(n)=o(g(n))
,
g(n)=o(f(n))
and
f(n)=g(n)(1+o(1))
respectively, as
n\to\infty
. For a graph
G
, the notation
\omega(G)
denotes its clique number (the size of the largest clique). We say that a sequence of events
A_{n}
happens almost surely (a.s.) if there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=1
for all
n>n_{0}
. We say that a sequence of events
A_{n}
occurs with high probability (or asymptotically almost surely) if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=1
.

2.1
Vertex Degrees (VD) algorithm

Since the addition of planted edges increases the degree of a vertex, the most natural and simplest way to detect a hidden clique is based on vertex degrees. This algorithm, although naive, gives a basic result for Erdős–Rényi graphs and can be described as follows. First, we calculate the degrees of all the vertices. Then, we construct
\hat{K}^{(\text{VD})}
by selecting the
k
vertices with the largest degrees. We call this VD-algorithm, and its formal description is given in Algorithm
1
.

Input:

Graph
(V,E)
; size
k
of the planted clique

Output:

A set of vertices

for

i\in V

do

Count
Z_{i}=\deg(i)

Select the largest
Z_{i_{1}},\ldots,Z_{i_{k}}

return

\hat{K}^{(\text{VD})}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}

Algorithm 1

VD-algorithm

The computational complexity of the VD-algorithm in
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
is
O_{p}(n(\log(k_{n})+\mu_{n}))
for computing the degrees of all vertices and finding top-
k_{n}
degrees by the min-heap method
[
5
]
. The main advantages of this algorithm are its rather low complexity and simple implementation.

The idea is that if
k_{n}
is sufficiently large, then the algorithm returns exactly the set
K_{n}
with high probability. This algorithm was first used for planted clique recovery in Erdős–Rényi graphs (see
[
14
, section 6, Theorem 6.1]
).

Theorem 1

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
G^{\text{ER}}_{k_{n}}(n,1/2)
be an Erdős–Rényi graph on
n
vertices with a planted clique of size
k_{n}>C\sqrt{n\log n}
with
C>0
large enough. Then, the VD-algorithm recovers the planted clique in
G^{\text{ER}}_{k_{n}}(n,1/2)
with high probability.

2.2
Common Neighbors (CN) algorithm

Next, we introduce an algorithm based on the common neighbors of two adjacent vertices, Algorithm
2
. It constructs the estimator
\hat{K}^{\text{(CN)}}
as follows: we check all edges
(i,j)
; if
i
and

have in common exactly

neighbors, we check whether
i,j
, and their common neighbors form a clique; if this is the case, the algorithm returns this
k

is not found, it returns the empty set.

Graph

A set of vertices

(i,j)\in E

Common neighbours check:

Let

be the set of common neighbors of

|Z_{ij}|=k-2

then

Clique check:

if

Z_{ij}

then

\{i\}\cup\{j\}\cup Z_{ij}
;

return

\hat{K}^{\text{(CN)}}=\varnothing

Algorithm 2

CN-algorithm

In
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
, the worst case complexity of this algorithm is
O_{p}(\mu_{n}n(n+k_{n}^{2}))
, since for any fixed edge, finding common neighbors requires at most
n
operations, and testing for a clique of size
k_{n}
requires not more than
\binom{k_{n}}{2}
operations.

The intuition behind this algorithm is illustrated in Figure
1
, which shows why the CN-algorithm does not confuse the planted clique with natural cliques in RGG. If two vertices
i,j\notin K_{n}
form a natural edge, then they might have common neighbors in both regions
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, which are contained in the intersection of the two balls centered in
X_{i},X_{j}
. Pairs of vertices located in opposite regions have a distance larger than
r_{n}
; thus, they are not naturally adjacent. In other words, if
i
and
j
have common neighbors in both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, then their common neighbors cannot form a clique. Therefore, the algorithm is likely to skip the pair
(i,j)
.

The common neighbors approach has been widely used for link prediction in networks, through the definition of score-based methods that typically depend on the number of common neighbors of two vertices
[
21
,
15
]
. In this paper, we show that common neighbors in geometric networks are informative for other tasks, such as anomaly detection.

3
Analysis of the algorithms

In this section, we summarize our results on VD- and CN-algorithms. Specifically, we retrieve the regimes of RGG under which exact recovery of the clique
K_{n}
is possible, utilizing the estimators
\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(VD)}}
and
\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(CN)}}
.

3.1
VD-algorithm

Recall that
\mu_{n}=n\phi_{d}r_{n}^{d}
denotes the expected degree in
G(n,r_{n})
, whereas
\Delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n}
stand for the maximum and minimum degrees, respectively. Assume that
\mu_{n}/\log(n)\to\alpha\in[0,\infty]
when
n
goes to infinity. From
[
19
]
and
[
16
]
we know that in the hard geometric graph conditioned on the number of vertices
N_{n}=n
,

\Delta_{n}/T(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}1,\quad\text{ with }T(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{\log(n)}{\log(\log(n)/\mu)}&\text{if $\alpha=0$ },\\
\mu H^{-1}_{+}(\alpha^{-1})&\text{if $\alpha\in(0,\infty]$ },\end{cases}

(4)

and

\delta_{n}/t(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}1,\quad\text{ with }t(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\alpha\in[0,1)$},\\
\mu H^{-1}_{-}(\alpha^{-1})&\text{if $\alpha\in[1,\infty]$}.\end{cases}

(5)

The functions
H^{-1}_{+}
and
H^{-1}_{-}
are the two branches of the inverse entropy function,
H(x)=1-x+x\log(x)
. Using the Lambert
W
function, for any
y>0
we can write explicitly
H_{+}^{-1}(y)=\exp\left\{{W_{0}\left(\frac{y-1}{e}\right)+1}\right\}>1
and
H_{-}^{-1}(y)=\exp\left\{{W_{-1}\left(\frac{y-1}{e}\right)+1}\right\}<1
. When
\alpha=\infty
, we use the convention
1/\infty=0
; thus, in this case
H_{+}^{-1}(0)=H_{-}^{-1}(0)=1
.

The results in Equations (
4
)-(
5
) extend to the random graph
G(n,r)
defined in Section
2
, where
N_{n}
is Poisson with mean
n
. Indeed, with high probability
n-n^{2/3}<N_{n}<n+n^{2/3}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle T(n-n^{2/3})<\Delta_{n}<T(n+n^{2/3})\quad\text{w.h.p.},

\displaystyle t(n-n^{2/3})<\delta_{n}<t(n+n^{2/3})\quad\text{w.h.p.},

hence the maximum and minimum degrees squeeze onto the sequences
T(n)
and
t(n)
respectively.

We now state the main result for the VD-algorithm.

Theorem 2

(VD-algorithm – positive result)
.

Given
\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}/\log(n)
,

•

if
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
(sparse or connectivity regime), then the VD-algorithm exactly recovers the planted clique in
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
as soon as
k_{n}>(1+\varepsilon)(T(n)-t(n))
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
T(n),t(n)
defined in (
4
) and (
5
);

•

if
\alpha=\infty
(dense regime), then the VD-algorithm exactly recovers the planted clique in
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
as soon as
k_{n}>\varepsilon\mu_{n}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.

The idea of the proof is quite simple. The output
\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(VD)}}
of Algorithm
1
is the set of
k_{n}
vertices with largest degree in
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
. Therefore, we can quantify the probability for the algorithm to succeed by:

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(VD)}}=K\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{i\in K_{n}}Z_{i}>\max_{i\in V\setminus K_{n}}Z_{i}\right).

(6)

In other words, for the VD-algorithm to correctly identify the clique, every clique vertex must have a degree strictly greater than any non-clique vertex. This occurs when the minimum degree within the clique, approximately
k_{n}+\delta_{n}
, exceeds the maximum degree outside it,
\Delta_{n}
. The resulting success condition,
k_{n}>\Delta_{n}-\delta_{n}
, follows directly from the asymptotic behavior of
\Delta_{n}
and
\delta_{n}
in (
4
) and (
5
).

We do not have a complete result for the VD-algorithm, in that we cannot conclude that the algorithm fails or achieves only partial recovery in the entire region of the
\mu
–
k
plane. Nonetheless, we can provide some negative results.

Theorem 3

(VD-algorithm – negative result)
.

Suppose either:

(i)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}/\log(n)=\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
k_{n}\leq(1-\varepsilon)(T(n)-\mu_{n})
, for
0<\varepsilon<1
;

(ii)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}/\log(n)=\infty
, with
\mu_{n}=o(n)
and
k_{n}=o(\sqrt{\mu_{n}})
.

Then the VD-algorithm fails to recover the planted clique in
G_{k_{n}}(n,r_{n})
asymptotically almost surely.

Theorem
3
explains that small planted cliques cannot be recovered from solely ordering vertex degrees in the connectivity and super-connectivity regime. In the next section, we show that the CN-algorithm is more powerful in this sense.

The proofs of Theorems
2
and
3
are given in Section
5
.

3.2
CN-algorithm

The output
\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(CN)}}
of Algorithm
2
exactly matches the planted clique
K_{n}
when: (a) there exists at least one edge
(i,j)
of the planted clique such that
|Z_{ij}|=k_{n}-2
and
Z_{ij}
forms a clique;
and
(b) for all other edges
(i,j)
, either
|Z_{ij}|\not=k_{n}-2
or
Z_{ij}
does not form a clique. Thus, it is possible to explicitly compute the success probability of the CN-algorithm by analyzing these two conditions separately.
The next theorem summarizes under which regimes for the graph density
\mu_{n}
and the clique size
k_{n}
the CN-algorithm works with high probability.

Theorem 4

(CN algorithm – positive result)
.

The CN-algorithm succeeds asymptotically almost surely if

k_{n}\leq\alpha n,\alpha\in(0,1),\quad\mu_{n}ne^{-c_{1,d}\mu_{n}}=o(1)

(7)

or

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{n}n}{2}\frac{(c_{2,d}\mu_{n})^{k_{n}-2}e^{-c_{2,d}\mu_{n}}}{(k-2)!}=o(1)&\text{if $k_{n}-2\leq c_{2,d}\mu_{n}$},\\
\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\frac{\mu_{n}^{k_{n}-1}e^{-\mu_{n}}}{(k_{n}-2)!}=o(1)&\text{if $k_{n}-2\geq\mu_{n}$ and $k_{n}=o(n/\mu_{n})$},\end{cases}

for some constants

.

To get an explicit description of the region where
\hat{K}_{n}^{\text{(CN)}}
exactly matches

, the inequalities in (

\mu_{n}ne^{-c_{1,d}\mu_{n}}=o(1)
if and only if
\mu_{n}\gg-W_{-1}(-c_{1,d}/n)/c_{1,d}=(\log n+\log\log n)/c_{1,d}+O(1)

[
4
]
). In particular, the CN-algorithm correctly identifies a planted clique of any size if

We observe that the CN-algorithm is effective in certain special cases. Importantly, it recovers small cliques. Indeed, take

\mu_{n}\geq(1+\epsilon)\log(n)

k_{n}\log k_{n}\gg\log(n)

5
Proofs for the VD-algorithm

For simplicity of notation, from now on, we will write
\mu=\mu_{n}
,
k=k_{n}
, and
r=r_{n}
since it is clear that all these quantities scale with the size
n
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Our aim is to find under which regimes for
\mu
and
k
in
G_{k}(n,r)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}>\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}\right)=1.

(9)

First, observe that

\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}\leq\Delta_{n}.

Moreover, for each clique vertex
i\in K
, we have
Z_{i}=(k-1)+Z_{i}^{V\setminus K}
, thus

\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\geq k-1+\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}^{V\setminus K}\geq k-1+\delta_{n-k}.

Then, condition (
9
) is verified if
k>\Delta_{n}-\delta_{n-k}+1
holds with high probability, in the large
n
limit.
At this point, we can study separately the cases for finite and infinite
\alpha
. If
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
(connectivity or sub-connectivity regimes), observe that

\frac{\Delta_{n}-\delta_{n-k}}{T(n)-t(n-k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}1.

In particular: if
k=o(n)
, then
t(n)=(1+o(1))t(n-k)
and condition (
9
) is verified as long as
k>(1+\varepsilon)(T(n)-t(n))
, for some
\varepsilon>0
; if
k=\Theta(n)
, condition (
9
) is trivially satisfied.
Instead, when
\alpha=\infty
we have
H_{-}^{-1}(\alpha^{-1})=H_{+}^{-1}(\alpha^{-1})=1
, thus

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Delta_{n}-\delta_{n-k}}{\mu}=0,\quad\text{ in probability}.

Therefore, (
9
) is achieved by choosing
k=\varepsilon\mu
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Consequently, as soon as
k>(1+\varepsilon)T(n)
,
\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}>\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}
with high probability, and the VD-algorithm correctly recovers the planted clique.
∎

Interestingly, despite a simple proof, it is not easy to relax the conditions of Theorem
2
. Indeed, consider
k=o(n)
. Then the following lemma holds.

Lemma 1
.

Consider
G_{k}(n,r)
, with
k=o(n)
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}=\Delta_{n}\right)=1.

(10)

where
\Delta_{n}
denotes the maximum degree in
G(n,r)
.

Proof.

Let
G(n,r)
be a random geometric graph and
G_{k}(n,r)
its modified version where a clique is planted on
k
randomly chosen vertices. Without loss of generality, assume that vertex
1
has maximal degree in
G(n,r)
. We have
1\in\{\text{argmax}_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}\}
if and only if
1\not\in K
. This latter event is independent of
Z_{1}
and occurs with probability
1-k/n
. Since
k=o(n)
, this probability goes to one as
n\to\infty
.
∎

From Lemma
1
we infer that if for all clique vertices
i\in K
the relation
Z_{i}^{V\setminus K}+k>\Delta_{n}
holds asymptotically almost surely, then the VD-algorithm works. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that
\min_{i\in K}|\{j\in N(i),j\notin K\}|+k>\Delta_{n}
. However, it is not immediate to compute this minimum as the degree of the clique vertices may be dependent. We can partially overcome these difficulties in the next Lemma
2
, showing that for sufficiently small
k
the dependency vanishes.

Lemma 2
.

Let
k=o\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{\mu}}\right)
. Then the degrees of the clique vertices are asymptotically independent.

Proof.

The degrees of the clique vertices are independent if and only if there is no overlap between the balls of radius
r
centered at the vertices. The logarithm of the probability of this event can be computed in the same fashion as in the analysis of the birthday paradox:

\displaystyle\log\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mbox{balls centered at clique vertices do no overlap}\right)=\log\left(\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\left(1-l\phi_{d}r^{d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\log\left(1-l\phi_{d}r^{d}\right)\geq-\phi_{d}r^{d}\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+O(r^{2d}k^{3}).

(11)

If
k^{2}r^{d}\to 0
, then the probability of no overlap converges to one. Hence, in this case, the degrees of the clique vertices are asymptotically independent. The condition
k^{2}r^{d}\to 0
is equivalent to the condition
k=o\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{\mu}}\right)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

(i) Since the balls of radius
r
centered at the clique vertices do not overlap a.a.s., it is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\leq\max_{i\in V\backslash K}Z_{i}\right)\to 1.

From (
4
) and Lemma
1
we know that
\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}=T(n)(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))
.
Write
T(n):=\mu h
, with
h=H_{+}^{-1}(\alpha^{-1})>1
as in Equation (
4
). Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in K\colon Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}\leq T(n)(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))-k\right)\to 1.

(12)

At this point, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in K\colon Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}\leq T(n)(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))-k\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i\in K\colon Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}>T(n)-k\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=1-\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}>T(n)(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))-k\right)^{k}(1+o(1)),

where in the last step we used Lemma
2
.

Applying a Chernoff bound to the Poisson random variable
Z_{i}
for
n
large, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}>T(n)(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))-k\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i}^{V\backslash K}>h^{\prime}\mu\right)^{k}+\mathbb{P}\left(o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)<-\delta(h-h^{\prime})\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu k(h^{\prime}\log h^{\prime}-h^{\prime}+1)}+o(1)=o(1)

for some
1<h^{\prime}<1+\delta(h-1)
(recall that
k\leq(1-\delta)(T(n)-\mu)=(1-\delta)(h-1)\mu
; therefore,
T(n)-k=h\mu-k\geq\mu+\delta(h-1)\mu
). Thus, condition (
12
) is satisfied.

(ii) We will prove that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}>\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Let
M>0
. We can tessellate the torus with
M^{d}
cubes of side length
1/M
each. To get the desired result, we will proceed in two steps: first, we show that every cube has at least one node from
V\setminus K
such that the ball of radius centered in this node is completely contained in the cube; second, for small
k
, we show that some of the balls previously constructed may naturally contain an
excess-
k
amount of nodes.

Formally, partition the cube
[0,1]^{d}
into cubes
C_{1},C_{2},...
of side length
1/M
, indexed in the set
\mathcal{M}
with cardinality
M^{d}
. Each cube
C_{i}
completely contains a ball
B_{i}
centered in
v_{i}
if and only if the node
v_{i}
is contained in the
core

Q_{i}
of the cube. In particular, the core
Q_{i}
is a smaller
d
-dimensional cube of side length
(1/M-2r)
. Denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
the set of vertices in
V\setminus K
whose positions are contained in
Q_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\not=\varnothing\}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\not=\varnothing\}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle\geq 1-M^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathcal{Q}_{1}\not=\varnothing\}\right).

(15)

Since
N_{n}-k>\alpha n
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, with high probability, we obtain
\mathbb{P}\left(\{\mathcal{Q}_{1}\not=\varnothing\}\right)\leq e^{-\alpha n(1/M-2r)^{d}}+o(1)
. Then, for any fixed
M
, the probability that all cores contain at least one vertex that is not part of the planted clique converges to 1, as
n
grows. In other words, with high probability, each of the cubes
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
contains a vertex
v_{i}\in V\setminus K
such that its ball of radius
r
is completely contained in
C_{i}
and these balls are all disjoint.

Since the balls around vertices
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
do not overlap, the degrees of these vertices are i.i.d.
\text{Poisson}(\mu)
. Moreover, the minimum degree among the planted clique vertices is stochastically dominated by the number of nodes contained in a ball of radius
r
augmented by
k
, that is,
\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\prec Y+k
, where
Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu)
.

The VD-algorithm fails to output the correct planted clique whenever
\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}>\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}
. Computing the probability of the complementary event yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}\leq\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}\leq Y+k\right)

(16)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i}\leq Y+k,\forall i\in\mathcal{M}\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y=y\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Z\leq y+k\right)^{M^{d}}

(18)

where
Y,Z\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu)
. Since
\mu\to\infty
, in the large
n
limit the random variable
\text{Poisson}(\mu)
is approximated by
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mu)
. Then, substituting
y=\mu+s\sqrt{\mu}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}<\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\Phi\left(s+\frac{k}{\sqrt{\mu}}\right)^{M^{d}}ds

(19)

where
\varphi(\cdot)
and
\Phi(\cdot)
are the probability and cumulative distribution functions of the standard normal distribution. Lastly, set
\delta:=k/\sqrt{\mu}=o(1)
. A first-order approximation yields
\Phi(s+\delta)=\Phi(s)+\delta\varphi(s)+O(\delta^{2})
. Therefore,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\Phi\left(s+\delta\right)^{M^{d}}ds=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(s)\Phi(s)^{M^{d}}ds+\delta M^{d}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(s)^{2}\Phi(s)^{M^{d}-1}ds+O(\delta^{2}).

Since
\varphi(s)=\Phi(s)^{\prime}
, the first integral in the latter equation equals
\frac{1}{M^{d}+1}
; then, bounding the second integral with 1 and putting the pieces together, we obtain:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in V\setminus K}Z_{i}<\min_{i\in K}Z_{i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{M^{d}+1}.

(20)

Finally, by setting
M^{d}=1/\varepsilon-1
, the desired result is achieved.
∎

6
Proofs for CN-algorithm

The CN algorithm iterates over all edges and stops whenever it encounters an edge with
k-2
common neighbors forming a clique. There are two scenarios where the algorithm fails to identify the
k
-clique:

(a)

All pairs
(i,j)\in K^{2}
have at least one common neighbor outside
K
.

(b)

There exists an edge outside the clique,
(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}
, such that the number of common neighbors is
|Z_{ij}|=k-2
and they form a clique.

Therefore, we can express the failure of the CN-algorithm in terms of the following events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{n}=\left\{\forall(i,j)\in K^{2},|Z_{ij}|>k-2\right\},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{n,1}=\left\{\exists(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}:N_{i}\cap N_{j}\textit{ form a clique}\right\},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{n,2}=\left\{\exists(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}:|Z_{ij}|=k-2\right\}.

(23)

The probability that the algorithm fails is upper bounded by the probability of the event
\mathcal{A}\cup(\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2})
, and

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}\cup(\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}))\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}))\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})+\min\{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{1}),\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{2})\}.

(24)

We compute the probability of
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
separately, and after that conclude under which conditions on
k=k(n)
and
\mu=\mu(n)
the algorithm succeeds, with high probability. We remind you that in our assumptions, the connection radius is set to be
r\in(0,1/4)
for RGG, and the planted clique problem is assumed to be well-posed, as stated in Section
2
.

Lemma 3
.

In
G_{k}(n,r)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(25)

Proof.

Observe that for any edge
(i,j)\in K^{2}
the number of common neighbors is
|Z_{ij}|\geq k-2
, as all the other vertices in the clique are common neighbors of
i
and
j
. However,
|Z_{ij}|>k-2
if and only if
i,j
do share at least one common neighbor from the vertex set
V\setminus K
. A necessary condition for such an event is
d(X_{i},X_{j})<2r
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i,j\in K:|Z_{ij}|>k-2\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i,j\in K:N(i)\cap N(j)\cap(V\setminus K)\not=\varnothing\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i,j\in K:d(X_{i},X_{j})<2r\right),

(26)

We now distinguish two cases: when
k=O(1)
, or it grows with
n
.

Case
k=O(1)
: Pivoting around a selected vertex
i\in K
, a necessary condition for the event in (
26
) to happen is that all other vertices
j\in K
have position inside a ball centered in
X_{i}
of radius
2r
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall i,j\in K:d(X_{i},X_{j})<2r\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall j\in K:X_{j}\in B(X_{i},2r)\right)

\displaystyle=(2^{d}\phi_{d}r^{d})^{k-1}=(2^{d}\mu/n)^{k-1},

and the latter quantity converges to 0 as
n
goes to infinity unless
\mu\propto n
, that is, unless the problem is ill-posed.

Case
k\gg 1
: From the technical assumption
0<r<1/4
on the model
G_{k}(n,r)
, it holds
2r<\sqrt{d}/2
for all
d\geq 1
. In other words,
2r
is smaller than the diameter of the
d
-dimensional torus. Therefore, we can consider two points
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
that are at a distance larger than
2r
. Let
U
and
V
be two open balls centered in
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
of small enough radius such that their distance is still larger than
2r
. Since
k\gg 1
, and vertices in
K
are distributed uniformly at random, it follows that both
U
and
V
contain at least one vertex of the set
K
with high probability. Therefore, there exists at least one pair of vertices in
K
at a distance larger than
2r
, and the probability in (
26
) converges to 0.

In particular, if
\mu\ll n
or
k\gg 1
and
2^{d}\mu/n<1-\varepsilon
, for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=0
.
∎

Next, we bound the probabilities of events
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

Lemma 4
.

In
G_{k}(n,r)
:

(i) If
\mu\ll n
then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)\leq(2+\varepsilon)\mu ne^{-c_{1,d}\phi_{d}r^{d}\,(n-k+k\,O(r^{2}))}+o(1),

for some

.

(ii) If

, there exists some constant

Proof.

First, we prove (i). We bound the probability:

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{i}\cap N_{j}\text{ form a clique}\right)

(29)

When
(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}
, the distance between

The common neighbors of

and

must have their positions inside the intersection of two balls of radius

drawn around

and

(as shown in Figure

inside the regions

, respectively (see Figure

, but

.

:

and

At any fixed dimension

, the volume of the two regions

,
R_{2}
has value

,
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{i}\cap N_{j}\text{ forms a clique}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\{\forall v\in V\setminus K,X_{v}\not\in R_{1}\}\cup\{\forall v\in V\setminus K,X_{v}\not\in\triangle R_{2}\}\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\exists\;\text{square of size $1/M^{d}$ that contains no vertices of $V\backslash K$})

\displaystyle\leq M^{d}e^{-M^{-d}n-k\log(1-M^{-d})}.

This completes the proof.
∎

It remains to bound the probability of

Lemma 5
.

In

, if

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}\right)\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\mu n}{2}\cdot\frac{(c_{2,d}\mu)^{k-2}e^{-c_{2,d}\mu}}{(k-2)!}+o(1)&\text{if $k-2\leq c_{2,d}\mu$},\\
\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\mu n}{2}\cdot\frac{((k-2)/e)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}+o(1)&\text{if $c_{2,d}\mu<k-2<\mu$},\\
\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\frac{\mu n}{2}\cdot\frac{\mu^{k-2}e^{-\mu}}{(k-2)!}+o(1)&\text{if $k-2\geq\mu$},\end{cases}

(31)

for some
c_{2,d}>0
.

Proof.

We bound the probability:

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}\right)\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{ij}|=k-2\right).

(32)

First, observe that for any edge
(i,j)\in E\setminus K^{2}
the distance between the two endpoints is
x:=d(X_{i},X_{j})\leq r
. For ease of notation, we denote by
A(x,r)=\mathcal{V}(B(X_{i},r)\cap B(X_{j},r))
the volume of the intersection of the two balls, which only depends on
x
and
r
. Let us now distinguish two cases:

Case 1.

When
i,j\in V\setminus K
, their common neighbors can only be found in the intersection
B(X_{i},r)\cap B(X_{j},r)
. In this case,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{ij}|=k-2\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right).

(33)

Case 2.

When
i\not\in K
and
j\in K
(or vice versa using a symmetric argument), the common neighbors can be found in the intersection
B(X_{i},r)\cap B(X_{j},r)
if they are not part of the planted clique, or they can be found in the entire ball
B(X_{i},r)
if they are part of the planted clique. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{ij}|=k-2\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))+W=k-2\right),

(34)

where
W
is the number of clique vertices in
B(X_{i},r)\setminus B(X_{j},r)
.

In
Case 1
, we will bound
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}\right)
by taking the supremum over
0\leq x\leq r
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right)\leq\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right).

Observe that, when
x
increases from
0
to
r
, the Poisson parameter
nA(x,r)
decreases from
\mu
to
c_{2,d}\mu
, with
c_{2,d}>0
. Then, we have the following:

(i)

If
k-2\leq c_{2,d}\mu
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(c_{2,d}\mu)=k-2\right)=\frac{e^{-c_{2,d}\mu}(c_{2,d}\mu)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}.

(ii)

If
k-2\geq\mu
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(\mu)=k-2\right)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}.

(iii)

If
c_{2,d}\mu<k-2<\mu
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(k-2)=k-2\right)=\frac{((k-2)/e)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}.

In
Case 2
, we need some extra steps.

(i)

If
k-2\leq c_{2,d}\mu
, we use the fact that the Poisson density is monotone on
[0,nA(x,r)]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))+W=k-2\right)\leq\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\max_{0\leq l\leq k-2}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=l\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\max_{0\leq l\leq k-2}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(c_{2,d}\mu)=l\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(c_{2,d}\mu)=k-2\right)=\frac{e^{-c_{2,d}\mu}(c_{2,d}\mu)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}.

(ii)

If
k-2\geq\mu
, then we use the Markov inequality and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\left[W\right]\leq k\mu/n
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))+W=k-2\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))=k-2\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(\mu)=k-2\right)+\frac{k\mu}{n}=\frac{e^{-\mu}(\mu)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}+\frac{k\mu}{n}=\frac{e^{-\mu}(\mu)^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}+o(1).

(iii)

If
c_{2,d}\mu<k-2<\mu
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,r)}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(nA(x,r))+W=k-2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Poi}(k-2)=k-2\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-2)^{k-2}e^{-(k-2)}}{(k-2)!}.

The result follows by applying the Stein-Chen method and the union bound, verbatim with Lemma
4
.
∎

Before proving the main result for the CN-algorithm, we want to highlight that the bound in (
31
) is not always informative. Indeed, when
c_{2,d}\mu<k-2<\mu
, we can consider separately the two cases:

•

If
\mu=\omega(1)
, then Stirling’s approximation yields

\frac{\mu n}{2(k-2)!}\left(\frac{k-2}{e}\right)^{k-2}=(1+o(1))\frac{\mu n}{\sqrt{2\pi(k-2)}}

(35)

which diverges for
n\to\infty
.

•

If
\mu=O(1)
, then
\frac{\mu n}{2(k-2)!}\left(\frac{k-2}{e}\right)^{k-2}=nO(1)
, which again diverges.

The conclusion here is that event
\mathcal{B}_{2}
may not vanish if
k
is in the interval
(c_{2,d}\mu,\mu)
, thus we cannot guarantee the success of the CN-algorithm in this region. This is further confirmed by the numerical experiments in Section
4
. Indeed, Figure
3(b)
and
3(c)
show a sudden drop in the CN-algorithm performance, exactly when the parameters lie inside the orange band in Figure
2(b)
that delineates the region
k\in(c_{2,d}\mu,\mu)
.

Combining the previous lemmas, we can now demonstrate the main result for the CN algorithm.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

By (
24
), we need that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=o(1)
and either
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)=o(1)
or
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}\right)=o(1)
. In particular,

•

Lemma
3
implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)=o(1)
in not ill-imposed parameter range.

•

From Lemma
4
, we conclude that Equation (
7
) is the necessary condition for
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\right)=o(1)
. Moreover, notice that the upper bound in Lemma
4
(ii) goes to zero for large enough
M
because
k<\alpha n
, and that
\mu\sim\gamma n
automatically satisfies (
7
).

•

Lastly, from Lemma
5
we see that condition (
8
) implies
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}_{2}\right)=o(1)
.

∎

7
Conclusions and future research

In the present paper, we have studied a variation of the classical planted clique problem that was first formulated for Erdős–Rényi graphs. We showed that the same task for geometric graphs provides a more diverse setting. Moreover, it turned out that the geometrical properties of a graph create the possibility of recovering very small planted cliques (even planted edges), which is rather remarkable.

Two algorithms were studied in the paper from both theoretical and practical perspectives. The first one, the VD-algorithm, is rather simple and was introduced in
[
14
]
. We have conducted a comprehensive theoretical analysis of this algorithm for random geometric graphs, revealing asymptotic thresholds for exact recovery. The second one, the CN-algorithm, was shown to recover the planted clique for a wider range of parameters of random geometric graphs. Some numerical experiments were conducted on random geometric graphs. They confirm the obtained theoretical results and demonstrate that the devised algorithms can be applied in practice to real-life tasks.

Several open questions can be further investigated. Firstly, the planted clique problem can be easily reformulated for the case of soft random geometric graphs, such as Waxman graphs. Another direction would be to consider RGG with no fixed dimension. Indeed, when
d
grows to infinity, geometric random graphs converge to Erdős–Rényi graphs
[
3
,
7
]
; hence, it would be interesting to understand the impact of high-dimensionality in the algorithmic performance.

Another possible direction of research is to consider that a planted clique can be defined in a slightly different manner. For instance, one natural way to build a planted clique is geometrical: take a circle in the unit square and connect all the vertices with positions inside it. It is clear that the CN-algorithm, for example, is useless in this case. We do not know if there is another algorithm that solves this problem completely, but all ideas in this direction will be of great interest.

Ultimately, one can focus on refining the existing algorithms in terms of complexity and practical implementation.
