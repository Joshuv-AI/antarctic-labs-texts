Correspondence on “Open-access clinic for arthralgia: patient flow and diagnostic pathways—a retrospective cohort study” by “Nielsen et al.”
Authors: Elise van Mulligen, Annette van der Helm-van Mil
Published: 2026

Early identification of inflammatory arthritis is essential, and it is encouraging to see that more centres across Europe are investing in strategies to detect inflammatory arthritis as early as possible. In this context, we read the article by Nielsen et al [1] with great interest. Their initiative reflects the growing commitment within rheumatology to improve timely access to a rheumatologist, and we welcome the attention given to optimising early referral pathways.

Because our department at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), the Netherlands, has operated an early arthritis recognition clinic (EARC) since 2010, this topic is very familiar to us [2]. Although the work by Nielsen et al [1] is highly valuable, their terminology prompted reflection. The way ‘EARC’ and ‘open-access arthralgia clinic’ (OAC) are defined in their article appears to differ from how these terms are used in our setting. In our setting, the EARC serves as an intermediate setting between primary and secondary care. If general practitioners (GPs) are unsure about the presence or absence of clinical arthritis, patients can be referred to this EARC screening clinic. At this clinic, a rheumatologist performs a full joint examination in a short (5-minute) visit. Patients with clinical arthritis are seen back at the general outpatient clinic, and patients without clinical arthritis remain at the GPs. The design of this EARC screening clinic differs from that of the Leiden Early Arthritis Clinic (EAC), which has existed since 1993. This clinic is located in secondary care and includes all new patients with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of arthritis of recent onset, who are then followed long-term. Comparing this with the data of Nielsen et al [1], it seems their OAC resembles our EARC, whereas their EARC resembles our standard outpatient clinic and EAC. Such terminology differences are understandable since each centre develops its own workflow, but this may complicate comparability and could cause confusion.

We also noted with interest that patients in the OAC underwent ultrasound examination. As more centres explore how to detect inflammatory arthritis at the earliest possible stage [[3], [4], [5]], it becomes increasingly important to understand the optimal, efficient combination of clinical assessment and imaging in a setting between primary and secondary care. Ultrasound may offer added value in certain scenarios, but its exact role in a triage setting as an addition to physical examination remains an open and fascinating question. Clarifying when imaging truly enhances early arthritis detection and when it may not be necessary will be essential for designing screening pathways for early arthritis detection that are both effective and sustainable.

Overall, we highly value the work of Nielsen et al [1] and emphasise that initiatives similar to theirs are essential for improving early inflammatory arthritis detection. We believe that adopting uniform terminology for early arthritis recognition clinics would support clarity and comparability across centres. As more institutions develop similar clinics, a shared vocabulary will help the field move forward collectively and coherently.
