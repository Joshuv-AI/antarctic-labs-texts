Postoperative outcomes among Northern versus Southern Ontario patients undergoing common intermediate- to high-risk elective surgeries: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Max Chen, Kaitlin Duncan, Robert Talarico, Sarah McIsaac, Daniel I. McIsaac
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeNorthern Ontario residents experience multiple health disparities compared with those in Southern Ontario. It is unknown whether this leads to differences in surgical outcomes. We sought to compare postoperative outcomes of patients from Northern and Southern Ontario.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study using linked administrative health care data to identify all adult patients undergoing selected elective intermediate- to high-risk noncardiac surgeries in Ontario, Canada between 2009 and 2022. The primary outcome was 30-day mortality following surgery. The secondary outcomes were number of days alive at home, hospital length of stay, total health care system costs, discharge disposition, and readmissions. We used regression models to estimate the adjusted association between the exposure and outcomes.ResultsThis study identified 562,115 patients, including 41,191 (7.3%) from Northern Ontario. We did not find strong evidence that mortality rates were higher for Northern vs Southern Ontario residents (adjusted odds ratio, 1.04; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.85 to 1.27). Health system costs were lower for Northern Ontario residents at 30 days [adjusted ratio of mean (RoM), 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89 to 0.96] and at 365 days (adjusted RoM, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90 to 0.96). Hospital length of stay was longer for Northern Ontario residents (adjusted RoM, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.11). The number of days alive at home and rate of readmission were not statistically different between the two groups.ConclusionNorthern Ontario residency was not associated with increased odds of mortality after intermediate- to high-risk elective noncardiac surgery. Overall, we found no clinically meaningful differences in postoperative outcomes between patients from Northern and Southern Ontario.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-024-02860-9.

Each year, more than 300,000 major surgical procedures are performed in the province of Ontario, Canada, mostly on a planned (also called elective) basis.1 Unfortunately, even after planned surgery, moderate to severe complication rates approach 20%.2 Surgical outcomes are influenced by patient-specific and procedural factors. Current risk indices for perioperative outcomes endorsed by major societies such as the Canadian Cardiovascular Society and the American Heart Association focus predominantly on patient characteristics such as underlying cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, functional status, and frailty.3,4 Concurrently, procedural urgency and invasiveness are recognized as some of the strongest risk factors for adverse outcomes after surgery.5

Recently, social determinants of health have also emerged as important modifiers of surgical outcomes for Canadian patients. For example, a systematic review found that surgical patients with Indigenous identity experienced a 30% greater likelihood of postoperative mortality and at least a 1.5-fold increase in postoperative complications.6 These findings were corroborated in a further study that found an association between Nunavut Inuit status and a 25% relative increase in postoperative morbidity and mortality.7 While the mechanisms underlying these crucial associations are complex and reflect effects of colonialism and government policies, direct impacts of social determinants of health are likely implicated.

Northern Ontario is a region where geographic and socioeconomic disparities are well-defined contributors to population health outcomes. At baseline, Northern Ontario has higher rates of poverty, food insecurity, and smoking than Southern Ontario. The proportion of the population identifying as Indigenous is four to five times greater than the rest of the province.8 Residents of Northern Ontario are more likely to die prematurely because of suicide and circulatory and respiratory diseases than residents of Southern Ontario, resulting in an average life expectancy that is two years shorter.8 Importantly, previous research has shown higher rates of ambulatory care sensitive conditions, such as cardiovascular adverse events, in Northern Ontario residents, and this may reflect a lack of access to preventive care services.9 More residents of Northern Ontario live rurally, requiring longer travel distances to access tertiary care,10 which may contribute to more postoperative complications including death.11,12 In the setting of acute cardiovascular disease, Northern Ontario patients have reduced access to consultant physicians and key diagnostic tests independent of rurality suggesting systemic gaps in health care resource allocation and availability.13 Despite these disparities, it remains unknown whether there are differences in postoperative outcomes between patients from Northern vs Southern Ontario. To bridge this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective population-based study to estimate and explore the outcomes of Northern vs Southern Ontario patients undergoing planned, intermediate- to high-risk inpatient noncardiac surgeries.

Methods

Design and setting

We conducted a population-based retrospective cohort study in Ontario, Canada using a linked administrative health care data set. All data used for this project were routinely collected and anonymized, exempting institutional review under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act. The study data set was assembled at ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences) via standardized and validated processes, allowing deterministic linkage using encrypted identifiers. Specific data sets used in this study were the Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), which reflects all hospitalizations; the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, reflecting emergency department visits; the Continuing Care Reporting System, reflecting long-term and respite care; the Home Care Database, indicating records of home health services; the Ontario Cancer Registry, capturing cancer diagnoses; the Ontario Drug Benefits Database, identifying prescription drug claims for residents aged 65 yr or older; the Client Agency Program Enrollment, capturing family physician rostering; and the Registered Persons Database, reflecting residency and vital status.

Cohort

We identified all Ontario patients aged 18 yr or older on the day of planned, intermediate- to high-risk, inpatient noncardiac surgery using standardized methods to capture sex-neutral surgeries that occurred during an elective hospital admission. We used an established set of noncardiac surgical procedure codes that represent surgeries of intermediate to high physiologic stress.3 These procedure codes have been used together extensively in previous studies.14,15 The included surgeries are listed in Table 1 (with codes in Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] eAppendix 1). Exposure

The primary exposure was receipt of index surgery at a Northern Ontario hospital (captured from the DAD based on each institution’s postal code), identifying those undergoing surgery in the Northeast and Northwest Local Health Integration Networks (LHINs). The remaining twelve LHINs constituted the comparator exposure of Southern Ontario. To support sensitivity analyses, we also tested alternative exposure definitions, including a variable that captured residential vs care location concordance (this categorical variable had four levels: Northern Ontario residents receiving care in Northern Ontario LHINs; Northern Ontario residents receiving care in Southern Ontario LHINs; Southern Ontario residents receiving care in Northern Ontario LHINs; and Southern Ontario residents receiving care in Southern Ontario LHINs [reference category]), as well as the cardinal distance from the centre of each participant’s postal code to their surgical hospital as a proxy for travel distance.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. We identified deaths that occurred in-hospital with the DAD; deaths after discharge were identified with the Registered Persons Database. Mortality is a core perioperative outcome measure validly captured at ICES.16 Secondary outcomes included 30-day and one-year measures of days alive at home (DAH) and total health system costs, as well as length of stay (LoS), discharge disposition, and readmissions. Days alive at home is a validated patient-centred outcome with a minimally important difference of three days.17,18 The remaining perioperative outcomes are validly captured at ICES and are important to both patients and the health care system.19,20

Covariates

We identified patient demographics (age, sex, neighbourhood income quintile, and rural vs urban residency) from the DAD and the Canadian Census. We identified comorbidities using the International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes in the three years preceding surgery.21 Frailty status was determined with the preoperative frailty index.22,23 Number of hospitalizations, emergency department visits in the year before surgery, and total health system costs were captured by the DAD, the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, and using standardized costing algorithms, respectively.19 We calculated all costs in 2022 Canadian dollars (CAD). Preoperative residence in a long-term care facility was identified from the Continuing Care Reporting System. Patient enrolment with a family physician was identified with the Client Agency Program Enrollment. We recorded the full procedure code for each surgery.

Sample size and missing data

We included all eligible members of the Ontario population. Using a projected 1.5% 30-day mortality rate, we estimated having over 99% power to detect a 0.2% absolute risk difference and could support at least 30 parameters in a multivariable logistic regression model with explained variance as low as 2%.24 We planned to use multiple imputation if more than 1% of outcome or exposure data were missing, but with only 77 (0.1%) of exposure data missing and all outcomes complete, we conducted a complete case analysis.25

Analysis

We conducted all analyses using SAS version 9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). We described baseline participant characteristics stratified by Northern vs Southern Ontario residence and compared strata using absolute standardized differences.26

We estimated the associations between Northern vs Southern Ontario surgery (which was a binary exposure variable with Southern Ontario as the reference category) and outcomes on an unadjusted and then adjusted basis. Unadjusted estimates were derived from regression models that had only the binary exposure variable as a covariate. Adjusted estimates were derived from regression models that included the binary exposure term along with terms for sex, age, neighbourhood income quintile, rurality, year of surgery, surgical procedure, each Elixhauser comorbidity, frailty index score, count of prior hospitalizations and emergency department visits, and presence of a family physician (parameterization is described in ESM eAppendix 2). The ESM eTable provides model parameters for the primary outcome as an example. Clustering by hospital was accounted for using Generalized Estimating Equations (exchangeable covariance matrix) and regression models were appropriate for each outcome distribution (logistic for binary outcomes, negative binomial for DAH, and log-gamma for costs and LoS).

Sensitivity and effect modifier analyses

We then re-estimated the primary analyses using propensity score overlap weights to adjust for confounders27 (see ESM eAppendix 3). We also tested the association of a four-level residential vs care location concordance with outcomes. We conducted tests for effect modification using prespecified variables based on a multiplicative interaction term between the exposure and surgical procedure, sex, cancer status, neighbourhood income quintile, and cardinal home-to-hospital distance (as a continuous restricted cubic spline with three knots).

Results

We identified 562,115 individuals having their first elective noncardiac surgery between 1 April 2009 and 31 March 2022, including 520,924 (92.7%) patients from Southern Ontario and 41,191 (7.3%) from Northern Ontario (Fig. 1). Patients from Northern Ontario were more likely to have rural residence, belong to the lowest income quintile, and have travelled longer distances to their index hospital. In addition, a greater proportion of Northern Ontario patients were not rostered with a family physician and had a higher number of emergency department visits in the year preceding surgery. Total health system costs were significantly lower for Northern Ontario residents at 30 days (adjusted ratio of mean [RoM], 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89 to 0.96) and 365 days (adjusted RoM, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90 to 0.96), while LoS was longer (adjusted RoM, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01 to 1.11) for Northern Ontario residents. Most other secondary outcome rates were not significantly different between exposure groups including DAH at 30 (adjusted RoM, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.97 to 1.03) and 365 days (adjusted RoM, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.99 to 1.00), and readmission rates (adjusted OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.88 to 1.16). While most patients were discharged home, point estimates for nonhome discharge rates were lower for Northern Ontario residents, although not significantly so (adjusted OR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.20 to 1.32). Event rates and unadjusted estimates are presented in Table 2.

Associations between residential versus care location concordance and outcomes

Full results of concordance analyses are presented in ESM eAppendix 4. In summary, we found no association between concordance and 30-day mortality, and fully concordant comparisons (i.e., Northern Ontario resident at a Northern Ontario hospital compared with Southern Ontario resident at a Southern Ontario hospital) were consistent with primary analyses. Northern and Southern Ontario residents cared for in Northern Ontario hospitals tended to have lower costs of care, and Northern Ontario residents tended to have a longer LoS at Northern and Southern Ontario hospitals. Southern residents cared for at Northern Ontario hospitals had significantly lower rates of nonhome discharge, while Northern Ontario residents cared for at Southern Ontario hospitals had significantly higher rates of nonhome discharge.

Effect modifier analyses

The odds of mortality varied across different surgical procedures (Fig. 2). Mortality at 30 days was lower for Northern Ontario residents undergoing total hip replacements. Nevertheless, 30-day mortality was higher for Northern Ontario residents undergoing total knee replacements, endovascular abdominal aortic aneurysm repairs, and pneumonectomies or lobectomies. There was no effect modification with income except at the highest quintile where Northern Ontario patients experienced increased mortality. Additionally, there was no effect modification with sex or cancer status (ESM eAppendix 5). Discussion

In a population-based cohort study comparing postoperative outcomes after intermediate- to high-risk elective surgery between residents from Northern and Southern Ontario, we did not find an association between Northern Ontario residence and postoperative mortality. While small but statistically significant differences were identified for LoS and health system costs, our findings overall suggest that, after case-mix adjustment that included measures of neighbourhood income and rurality, along with baseline demographics, comorbidity, frailty and procedural risk, there do not appear to be substantive geographic differences in routinely collected outcomes after major elective surgery in Ontario. Whether differences exist in patients’ qualitative experiences after emergency or cardiac surgeries remains to be evaluated.

While a variety of data suggest that individuals living in Northern Ontario face meaningful health and health care disparities compared with those in Southern Ontario, our data do not suggest that substantive differences in postoperative outcomes exist between the two populations. In fact, along with a lack of statistical evidence of any difference in postoperative mortality rates, our estimated 95% CI for an absolute risk difference was not consistent with a difference of even 0.2%, which our sample size estimates suggested being potentially meaningful. Northern Ontario patients did have longer hospital lengths of stay, which might be influenced by a greater proportion of Northern Ontario patients residing rurally, which could influence discharge readiness assessment as such patients may have reduced access to care upon discharge home. Nevertheless, as both groups had a median LoS of 4 days, these statistical differences do not appear to be clinically meaningful at the individual level.

Despite statistically longer LoS, total health system costs were lower for Northern Ontario residents (unadjusted median difference of ~ CAD 1,000, ~ 8% relative adjusted decrease). This is somewhat counterintuitive as LoS is a major driver of perioperative costs. These cost differences could reflect more efficient hospital practices, but the potential for reduced access to postdischarge care (precluding further accrual of costs), also requires consideration. As the costs captured in this study include only those incurred by the health care system and do not account for patient out-of-pocket expenses (e.g., fees for travel and accommodation associated with a surgical admission may not be fully covered by provincial grants), lower health system costs do not preclude a greater overall financial impact on Northern Ontario patients.29 Considering that Northern Ontario residents were more likely to belong to the lowest income quintile and travel longer distances for care, they may be disproportionately impacted by the financial consequences of surgery. Additional research is required to quantify costs accumulated by Northern Ontario patients undergoing surgery, impacts on quality of life and patient-reported recovery, as well as qualitative analyses of patient experiences.

Mortality in our cohort was relatively low for a major noncardiac surgery cohort,30 and likely reflects a predominance of total knee and hip arthroplasty cases, and inclusion criteria enrolling all adult patients (as opposed to only including patients 45 yr or older) limited to planned surgeries. Nevertheless, even within our cohort, effect modification by procedure was present, with intermediate- to high-risk procedures like endovascular aortic aneurysm repair and pneumonectomy carrying a significantly greater mortality risk in Northern Ontario residents, and other major vascular and intraperitoneal surgeries like open aortic aneurysm repair, peripheral vascular repair, liver resection and pancreaticoduodenectomy each directionally suggestive of greater mortality for Northern Ontario residents. Further investigation into institution- or system-level factors associated with potential differences in mortality and other core outcomes is warranted. While most complex vascular and oncologic surgeries are already regionalized in high-volume centres in Ontario, these data may suggest a need to reinforce systems of care for Northern residents who require particularly invasive and high-risk surgery, or could suggest greater surgical disease severity at presentation, which was not measured in our data.

Strengths and limitations

As an observational study, our effect estimates are limited to measures of association, and use of health care administrative data introduces risk of misclassification bias. While our primary exposure and outcomes should be validly and accurately captured, outcomes like health system costs may provide an incomplete picture as out-of-pocket costs were not included. We also lacked measures of surgical disease severity, which could bias results to the null if Northern Ontario residents had greater severity. As a population-based study, our results have direct relevance to the Ontario health care system but may not fully generalize to other large Canadian provinces with different organization of surgical care.

Conclusions

While Northern Ontario residents encounter numerous health care challenges, our study did not find a higher rate of postoperative mortality among patients from this region undergoing selected elective intermediate- to high-risk noncardiac surgeries. Furthermore, there were likely no clinically meaningful differences in secondary outcomes. The observed trends toward differences in nonhome discharge dispositions and health system costs may suggest variations in access to health care resources across the province. Further investigation is required to better understand these trends and their implications for health care delivery and patient outcomes in different regions.
