Investigation and application of data balancing and combined discriminant model in rock burst severity prediction
Authors: Shaohong Yan, Runze Liu, Yanbo Zhang, Xulong Yao, Yueqi Yang, Qi Wang, Bin Guo, Shuai Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the development of intelligent rock burst prediction models, issues such as incomplete data coverage and data imbalance are frequently encountered. These issues may lead to risks of overfitting in predictive models, poor generalization capabilities, and increased bias, which in turn may result in misjudgments and unpredictable losses. To accurately predict rock burst disasters and mitigate or eliminate related threats, this paper proposes a composite prediction model that integrates Density-Based Nonlinear Resampling (DBNR)-Tomek Link data balancing algorithms with Bayesian Optimization (BO)-Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)-Random Forest (RF). Initially, this study collected and organized a total of 301 recorded rock burst disaster field observation data, covering various tectonic plates, engineering types, rock origins, rock textures, and rock burst types. Subsequently, from a data analysis perspective, we employed the PCA-SSA-K-means unsupervised clustering algorithm to delve into the underlying information contained within the data, thereby validating the rationality of categorizing rock bursts into four grades. Then, using the L2 norm to optimize the dimensionality of the indicators and supplementing with indicator importance ranking and hypothesis testing, we selected the maximum tangential stress of the surrounding rock, the ratio of the maximum tangential stress of the surrounding rock to the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock (stress coefficient), and the elastic energy index as the criteria for rock burst intensity grading. Following that, the DBNR-Tomek Link sampling method was applied to balance the sample data, optimizing the data sample ratio and ultimately expanding the sample size to 396, improving the proportion of data samples from 2:3:4:1 to 1:1:2:1, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization performance. Ultimately, a BO-MLP-RF composite prediction model was constructed based on Bayesian Optimization (BO), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Random Forest (RF) algorithms, with the Bayesian Optimization method ensuring that the model fits the training data well and generalizes to the test data. The results of tenfold cross-validation demonstrated that the model’s accuracy is consistently around 92.5%, combining the training results of rock burst models with imbalanced datasets, proving that the MLP model, adept at modeling nonlinear data, and the RF model, skilled in modeling large-scale data, serve as basic classifiers. This demonstrates that the application of data balancing and combined discriminative model schemes have enhanced the model’s predictive performance and stability. The model is capable of providing high-accuracy, high-efficiency early warning monitoring services for rock burst phenomena in rock engineering, thereby ensuring engineering safety.Keywords: Rock burst intensity classification prediction, DBNR-Tomek Link sampling method, Bayesian optimization, Multi-layer perceptron, Random forest, Combination judgment prediction modelSubject terms: Applied mathematics, Petrology

Introduction

Efficient prediction and classification of rock burst intensity are crucial for the effective prevention and control of rock burst disasters, theoretically and practically. By accurately forecasting and differentiating between various levels of rock burst intensity, engineers can enhance their risk assessment of rock burst disasters and adopt suitable preventive strategies. This approach significantly improves the safety and reliability of geotechnical engineering, ultimately safeguarding personnel and equipment.

Establishing a rock burst intensity classification prediction model involves two primary stages: gathering and processing rock burst data and developing and assessing the prediction model. This paper will focus on a detailed examination of these two critical components.

During the collection and preprocessing phase of rock burst data, various factors—such as the differing frequencies and severities of rock burst incidents, human errors in data recording during transmission, and challenges in data collection that prevent comprehensive global coverage—result in several issues within the dataset. These issues include inadequate representation of geological conditions, incomplete feature indicators for various types of rock burst data, and insufficient samples for rare categories (e.g., regions with no rock bursts and those experiencing severe rock bursts). Consequently, traditional early warning models encounter challenges, including abnormal behavior with new or unseen data, training data that fails to cover all geological scenarios, and warning outputs that lean toward more common rock burst situations (e.g., mild and moderate rock bursts). To mitigate these challenges, Tan et al.1 utilized the Local Outlier Factor (LOF) algorithm for detecting, replacing, and removing outliers; however, this method can distort the feature distribution of the original dataset. Sun et al.2 implemented multi-class augmentation sampling techniques to address data imbalance, including Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE), Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN), and SMOTE-ENN (Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique–Edited Nearest Neighbors). Although these methods significantly enhanced classification performance by addressing the class imbalance, they can also inadvertently introduce noise and disrupt the original feature distribution of the dataset when generating numerous samples. These limitations affect the model training process and decrease prediction accuracy. Thus, it is crucial to consider both the balance and the reasonable distribution of the data to improve the accuracy and reliability of the rock burst intensity classification prediction model.

In the model development and validation stage, selecting feature indicators is critical to the success of predictive models. An appropriate combination of predictive indicators can significantly improve the accuracy of the rock burst prediction model, decrease computational complexity, and enhance the model’s generalization capability3. Typically, selecting indicators involves calculating the correlations among them4. Man et al.5 employed grey relational analysis to select predictive indicators, which is advantageous due to its lower sensitivity to the nonlinear distribution of rock burst data and outliers. Nevertheless, grey relational analysis often assumes linear relationships among samples, thereby overlooking potential nonlinear interactions. Sun et al.3 utilized a correlation-based feature selection algorithm for the selection of predictive indicators, which is beneficial as it does not necessitate complex calculations or parameter configurations, thus maximizing data retention while reducing feature dimensionality. However, this method can lead to the incorrect exclusion or selection of features, as it fails to account for the interrelationships among them. Song et al.6 approached predictive indicator selection by integrating rock mechanics theory, rock burst datasets, and predictive algorithms, utilizing gradient-boosting decision trees to assess the importance of each indicator. This method is robust against outliers and missing values, requiring no additional preprocessing; however, excessive tree size and depth during training can result in increased time complexity and the risk of overfitting. In conclusion, the algorithm used for indicator selection should meet the following criteria: effectively represent the nonlinear relationships in rock burst data, exhibit high robustness to outliers and missing values, and maintain a reasonable level of model complexity.

In the section on establishing and validating prediction models, the integration of big data analytics and machine learning techniques has led to the development of nonlinear rock burst prediction methods that apply mathematical approaches while considering multiple influencing factors7. This methodology has yielded promising results in rock burst intensity classification. The primary idea involves training a specific number of rock burst instance data samples to build a predictive model, employing techniques such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Neural Networks, and Random Forest (RF) models8. Despite their differing underlying principles, these methods share a common goal and possess distinct advantages and disadvantages, necessitating parameter optimization for all models. SVM is typically utilized for regression or prediction tasks involving small sample datasets. However, its performance declines when addressing the nonlinear, imbalanced, and large-scale datasets characteristic of rock bursts, rendering it inadequate for fulfilling prediction needs. Neural network models can automatically learn complex patterns and relationships from datasets; however, their sensitivity to noise and outliers can adversely affect model generalization9. Chen et al.10 optimized the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm using grid search and cross-validation methods, effectively avoiding overfitting and enhancing model efficiency. This indicates that hyperparameter optimization can significantly improve model performance during the establishment process. Nonetheless, XGBoost’s large operational scale and sensitivity to outliers can impact its generalization ability. After analyzing seismic data from practical engineering projects, Ma et al.11 proposed a predictive model incorporating data balancing and model prediction techniques. They found that the Borderline-SMOTE1-Adaboost algorithm performed best in prediction accuracy. They concluded that balancing data before constructing predictive models can enhance model versatility, though relying solely on enhanced sampling algorithms during the balancing phase inevitably introduces noise, affecting prediction outcomes. Liu et al.12 addressed the challenge of incomplete observational data in rock engineering by proposing an ensemble tree model optimized with the Hunger Game Search (HGS) algorithm. This model showed promising results in rock burst classification predictions with incomplete datasets, though the effectiveness of ensemble learning is often limited when trained on large sample datasets. Sanei et al.13 employed machine learning techniques, including Multilayer Perceptrons and Radial Basis Functions, to estimate pore pressure and minimum horizontal stress from logging data, demonstrating the generalization capability of the MLP model in big data and unknown datasets. Zhou et al.14 applied various intelligent optimization algorithms to SVM and found that these algorithms significantly enhanced machine learning model capabilities. They indicated that intelligent optimization can effectively improve a model’s learning ability; however, the selected SVM models are primarily suited for binary classification, which poses challenges for multiclass prediction tasks. Xue et al.15 highlighted that imbalanced rock burst datasets could limit the accuracy and reliability of machine learning-based predictions. They proposed an enhanced sampling algorithm based on Copula theory and Monte Carlo simulation to balance datasets. Their comparative analysis showed that the accuracy of the balanced model surpassed that of the unprocessed model, underscoring the importance of data balancing in enhancing rock burst prediction accuracy. However, their approach relied solely on enhanced sampling algorithms for data balancing, which may also introduce interference data, compromising model generalization and accuracy. Sanei et al.16 used machine learning algorithms optimized by genetic algorithms for predictions, achieving excellent results, further supporting the notion that optimization algorithms can effectively improve predictive performance. Aghakhani Emamqeysi et al.17 employed adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems, artificial neural networks, and support vector methods for predicting rock elastic parameters, discussing the efficacy of single versus ensemble models and finding that ensemble models yield better prediction results than single models.

In summary, the establishment and refinement of a highly accurate and efficient rock burst prediction model must focus on three key aspects: dataset preprocessing, hyperparameter optimization, and the selection of appropriate base models.

In summary, due to the sudden and complex nature of rock burst disasters, coupled with issues such as imbalanced sample proportions and significant nonlinearity in existing datasets, there is a need to develop a highly efficient, accurate, and adaptable rock burst classification prediction model. This paper focuses on balancing sample proportions in big data and establishing a high-precision predictive model. The technical approach taken in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Methods

The construction process of the rock burst intensity grading prediction model involves several key stages: data preprocessing, feature selection, model development, and model optimization and validation.

The 301 sets of rock burst data collected from the actual project are classified according to the four levels of no rock burst activity, mild rock burst activity, moderate rock burst activity, and severe rock burst activity. The proportion of samples at each level is about 2:3:4:1. The imbalance of the number of samples at all levels will affect the accuracy and operation efficiency of the prediction model. Therefore, in the early stage of model establishment, it is necessary to implement appropriate processing methods to balance the data set.

DBNR-Tomek link sampling method for data balance processing

Given the challenges posed by class imbalance, pronounced nonlinearity in data features, and the substantial scale of the rock burst dataset, we adopt a Density-Based Nonlinear Resampling (DBNR) approach to address these issues. This method focuses on enhancing minority class samples, supplemented by Tomek Link for data cleansing, thereby improving the clarity of classification boundaries.

The Density-Based Nonlinear Resampling (DBNR) method37, primarily employed for data balancing, shows exceptional efficacy in managing complex data distributions. DBNR’s central strategy enhances data representation by injecting additional samples into lower-density areas and excising noisy data, thereby boosting the model’s ability to learn from minority class samples while curtailing noise interference. In contrast, traditional resampling techniques, like random undersampling and oversampling, risk losing critical minority class data or incorporating noise and redundancy38. By accounting for the density distribution of varying sample categories within the dataset, DBNR more accurately targets samples for resampling, enhancing post-resampling data balance. To ensure data quality and mitigate the risks associated with dataset over-balancing and the introduction of noise, the Tomek Link method is employed for data cleaning. This technique, described in Tomek Link39, tackles issues related to class imbalances and the selection of boundary samples in datasets. The principal strategy of Tomek Link is the identification and elimination of boundary sample pairs, which are nearest neighbors lying between distinct classes and form a boundary within the dataset. By analyzing and removing these pairs, Tomek Link sharpens the distinctions at class boundaries. During the preprocessing phase, the application of Tomek Link facilitates the removal of majority class outliers, thus equalizing the distribution between the minority and majority classes40.

Bayesian hyperparameter optimization

During the training of machine learning models such as Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and Random Forest (RF), selecting appropriate parameters remains a significant challenge46. There is no clear performance function to assess whether adjustments have led to a local or global optimum.

Bayesian hyperparameter optimization addresses this issue effectively. Utilizing a probabilistic surrogate model or objective function based on Bayesian principles, it aims to identify the global optimum of an undefined function, thereby determining the most effective hyperparameters for enhanced performance. This optimization is especially pertinent for the complex hyperparameter spaces of MLP and RF models in rock burst warning systems, adept at managing various types of hyperparameters in scenarios where resource limitations and efficiency are paramount.

Another advantage of Bayesian hyperparameter optimization is its capacity to promptly adjust the direction of optimization and tailor the objective function, thereby optimizing the overall time spent on these processes47. The utilization of probabilistic surrogate models and acquisition functions48, particularly Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPE) and Expected Improvement (EI), significantly enhances both the efficiency and accuracy of the optimization49.

Data processing of rock burst data set

Data analysis and screening

At present, there are three main types of criteria for rock burst intensity classification: strength stress ratio method, energy method and lithology method. According to the above three criteria, the determination of rock burst grade is divided into two schemes. According to the single factor rock burst identification method, Chen et al.50 divided the rock burst into three grades: N (no rock burst activity, labelled as I), M (medium and low rock burst activity, labelled as II), H (severe rock burst activity, labelled as III). Based on the rock strength and the overall failure criterion, Guo et al.51 divided the rock burst into four grades by hypothesis and derivation: N (no rock burst activity, numerical label is 1), L (mild rock burst activity, numerical label is 2), M (moderate rock burst activity, numerical label is 3), H (severe rock burst activity, numerical label is 4). The better to understand the characteristics and distribution of rock burst data and improve the accuracy of the subsequent rock burst intensity classification prediction model, the unsupervised data clustering method is used to cluster large sample rock burst data sets. From the perspective of data relationship, dividing rock burst into four levels has more proved helpful to find potential patterns and structures in the data.

Furthermore, a multitude of scholars52–55 have developed and expanded multidimensional evaluative metrics based on the intrinsic properties of rocks, achieving notable advancements in engineering research. Previous studies have established two crucial parameters of rock performance: compressive strength and tensile strength. The brittleness index, the ratio between these strengths, serves as a vital gauge of rock brittleness. Additionally, the elastic energy index, calculated from a single cycle of loading and unloading under uniaxial compression, quantifies the proportion of strain energy retained versus that dissipated. A higher brittleness and elastic energy index increase the probability of rock bursts, underscoring their significance as key determinants. Tangential stress also plays a critical role in forecasting the angle of rock fractures, significantly affecting rock burst occurrences.

Many of these indicators involve on-site calculations, and some engineering data encounter issues such as missing data in certain indicators. To ensure the absence of missing data in the large dataset selected for this study and to prevent substantial data errors during manual calculations, six fundamental indicators were chosen for this study. These indicators not only represent the selections made by the majority of researchers in their studies, but are also easily obtainable and can be used to derive other multidimensional rock burst evaluation indicators. They include the maximum tangential stress in the opening’s boundary, the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock, the tensile strength of the rock, the ratio of the maximum tangential stress to the uniaxial compressive strength of rock. (Stress coefficient), the ratio of the uniaxial compressive to the tensile strength of rock. (Brittleness coefficient), and the elastic strain energy index of rock, defined as the ratio of the elastic energy stored to the dissipated energy in one cycle of cycling compression tests. Model index screening

The mechanism of rock burst is complex, and influenced by numerous factors. Typically, most researchers combine previous experiences to determine rock burst classification prediction indicators, leading to subjective and less convincing indicator selection. This paper adopts the criteria of reflecting the primary influencing factors during the occurrence of rock bursts as comprehensively as possible and being easily accessible in practical engineering for indicator selection. It identifies six indicators from different dimensions: the maximum tangential stress in the opening’s boundary, the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock, the tensile strength of the rock, the ratio of the maximum tangential stress to the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock (Stress coefficient), the ratio of the uniaxial compressive to the tensile strength of rock (Brittleness coefficient), and the elastic strain energy index of rock, defined as the ratio of the elastic energy stored to the dissipated energy in one cycle of cycling compression tests.

When there are too many input indicators with strong correlations among them, it can increase the complexity of model construction and training time, potentially affecting the predictive performance of the model. Conversely, if there are too few input indicators, they may not fully reflect the influencing factors related to rock burst occurrences. This paper selects indicators based on the L2 norm to control model complexity, reduce overfitting, and improve the training performance of machine learning models. Ultimately, the paper chooses three indicators—maximum tangential stress of surrounding rock, the ratio of maximum tangential stress of surrounding rock to uniaxial compressive strength of rock (stress coefficient), and elastic energy index—as indicators for rock burst severity classification prediction. Additionally, the study employs decision tree models, Pearson correlation coefficient, Spearman correlation coefficient, and Kendall algorithm for ranking the importance of indicators. Balanced expansion of rock burst data sets

At present, most of the classification prediction models for the rock burst intensity are based on small samples, and the rock burst data sets used in training are unbalanced among different levels of samples. In this paper, a total of 301 global engineering rock burst data sets are collected, which constitute a large data sample, but the proportion of four rock burst grade samples is about 2:3:4:1. The imbalance of rock burst sample data at all levels directly attenuates the training performance of the classification model and reduces the universality of the model. The DBNR-Tomek Link sampling method is used to balance the data (Fig. 12). Firstly, the DBNR algorithm calculates the density value of each sample point, divides the samples into sparse regions and dense regions, increases the number of samples in sparse regions, and completes the oversampling process. The data set containing the newly generated samples is processed by the Tomek Link algorithm, and each sample point is paired with the nearest neighbour to form Tomek Link for label comparison. If the same, the data are retained: if different, the data are removed, and the data cleansing process is completed to obtain a new data set after data balance.

The low misjudgment rate of 7.5% indicates that employing data balancing and combined discriminative models effectively boosts the accuracy of rock burst warnings. Continuous enhancements in model training and increasing the number of learning samples through online learning are recommended to progressively eliminate errors.

The results indicate that in algorithm design, the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) prediction model excels at nonlinear modeling but is susceptible to outliers, leading to unstable predictions. The Random Forest (RF) prediction model is adept at handling large datasets and demonstrates robustness against noise and outliers; however, it offers limited interpretability for nonlinear models and is vulnerable to the nonlinear datasets typical in rock bursts. By employing a combination discrimination approach, the strengths of both models are integrated and their weaknesses mitigated, establishing the algorithmic advantages of the Bayesian optimized Multilayer Perceptron and Random Forest complementary prediction model (BO-MLP-RF). In terms of data balancing, the dataset obtained using the DBNR-Tomek Link sampling method significantly enhances model accuracy during training, underscoring the importance of data balancing before model training as a key to achieving high accuracy and efficiency in predictive models.

During the model validation process, a tenfold cross-validation method was employed. This involved repeatedly dividing a test dataset into distinct complementary subsets, performing cross-validation multiple times, and using the average of these validations as the final result. This approach ensured both the accuracy and stability of the outcomes.

Discussion

This study is mainly conducted from three aspects, including the determination and verification of the rationality of rock burst classification, the balance processing of rock burst data samples and the establishment of rock burst prediction model.

Study of the determination and verification of the rationality of rock burst classification

This research examines the validity of rock burst classification by analyzing rock burst data to uncover deeper insights within the dataset. We apply an unsupervised clustering method to assess which rock burst classification strategy proves to be the most rational. Throughout this investigation, we implement a controlled variable approach, using the silhouette coefficient of the clusters as the evaluation criterion. This process involves two iterative experiments to identify the optimal clustering strategy. Ultimately, we perform principal component analysis (PCA) on the extensive rock burst dataset for dimensionality reduction, subsequently optimizing K-means clustering using the Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA). Through self-optimization of the model, we establish that the optimal number of clusters is four. Our comparison shows that the clusters derived from unsupervised methods align closely with the original labels of the sample data, supporting the appropriateness of dividing the rock burst intensity dataset into four categories. Furthermore, when comparing our findings with previous studies51, it is noteworthy that the classification strategy based on rock data aligns well with those developed from rock strength and overall failure criteria, further validating the four-tier classification of rock burst severity. However, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations of this approach, as the effectiveness of the classification is significantly influenced by the underlying rock burst dataset.

Rock burst disasters occur unpredictably across the globe, and this study could not fully gather data covering all regions worldwide. As a result, this limitation is an unavoidable drawback of the proposed methodology. Thus, an important research avenue for future rock burst disaster warning systems is to compile or simulate the creation of a more extensive dataset that includes a broader spectrum of geographical locations, engineering projects, rock types, and the various causes of rock bursts.

Study on the balanced processing of rock burst data samples

In the study of balancing rock burst data samples, an analysis of the rock burst dataset revealed a significant imbalance in the quantity of samples across various levels. Training with an imbalanced dataset can degrade the classification performance of the decay model and reduce the generalizability of the classification model. Currently, there is limited research on balancing rock burst datasets. Some researchers have employed augmentation sampling techniques to enhance rock data samples2, achieving a balanced dataset for rock bursts, which has yielded positive results in the model development phase.

Although these studies have unveiled important discoveries, they also possess certain limitations. First, augmentation sampling techniques increase the dataset’s sample size, leading to higher computational costs and longer training times, which can negatively impact the efficiency of model training. Second, the significant duplication and creation of new data during the augmentation process may result in the loss of essential information contained in the original dataset. Lastly, augmentation methods unavoidably introduce noise points, which can interfere with the training process and decrease the model’s predictive accuracy. Therefore, there is a need for a combined strategy that effectively addresses both overfitting and underfitting in classification models, alleviates the interference caused by uneven sample distribution, and significantly improves the efficiency and accuracy of predictive models.

Despite revealing significant findings, these studies also have their limitations. First, augmentation sampling methods increase the number of samples in the dataset, leading to higher computational costs and longer training times, which can negatively impact training efficiency. Second, the extensive duplication and synthesis of new data during the augmentation process may result in the loss of critical information from the original dataset. Finally, these augmentation methods inevitably introduce noise points that disrupt the training process and reduce the predictive accuracy of the model. Overall, a combined approach is necessary to effectively prevent both overfitting and underfitting in classification models, alleviate the interference caused by uneven sample distributions, and significantly enhance the efficiency and accuracy of predictive models.

Study on the establishment process and advantages of BO-MLP-RF rock burst prediction model

In the research on establishing rock burst prediction models, earlier studies predominantly focused on calculating predictions based on the mechanisms of rocks18. In recent years, methods using machine learning for rock burst prediction have gradually emerged7,8 and have shown promising results11,12,14,15. This study selects two fundamental models: the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) prediction model, which consists of multiple neurons arranged in a multilayer structure. This model excels in nonlinear modeling but is sensitive to outliers and prone to overfitting when dealing with complex data. The Random Forest (RF) prediction model, on the other hand, integrates multiple decision trees, making it adept at handling large-scale datasets and robust against noise and outliers, although it offers limited interpretability for nonlinear models.

In conclusion, the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model effectively captures intricate patterns and features in data, thereby improving its ability to learn from nonlinear datasets. Conversely, the Random Forest (RF) model decreases the risk of overfitting and enhances generalization when trained on extensive datasets. These models complement one another, allowing the combined predictive model to effectively adapt to diverse data types and application contexts. Bayesian optimization iteratively refines hyperparameter combinations, aligning with the requirements of both the MLP and RF models while adeptly handling complex parameter spaces. Throughout each iteration, Bayesian optimization updates its probabilistic model, facilitating real-time adjustments in decision-making processes based on newly acquired data. One of the notable strengths of Bayesian optimization relative to other optimization algorithms is that it does not necessitate a closed-form expression of the objective function or its gradient information, making it particularly effective for models characterized by difficult-to-assess black-box functions. Additionally, Bayesian optimization is capable of uncertainty estimation, which enhances its robustness in dealing with noisy datasets. This paper presents the development of a combined predictive model, BO-MLP-RF, integrating Bayesian hyperparameter optimization with MLP and RF to capitalize on their complementary strengths. Through rigorous iterative testing and validation, the combined discriminative model has been shown to effectively manage the nonlinear relationships present in rock burst datasets, thereby reducing the risk of poor performance associated with individual models in certain contexts. The predictions from this combined model support and enhance each other, contributing to improved robustness overall.

While these studies provide valuable insights, they also have certain limitations. The prediction accuracy of the combined discriminant model remains stable at approximately 92.5%, which is not ideal for early warning of rock burst disasters. As such, a possible avenue for future research could be to adopt different perspectives, focusing on data characteristics or engineering projects with rock burst risks. By incorporating more sophisticated optimization techniques and combining them with machine learning models, it may be possible to achieve superior predictive performance.

Study on the application of BO-MLP-RF model to engineering practice

In this study, the application of the rock burst early warning model involved analyzing 301 recorded observational datasets from rock burst incidents spanning Asia, Europe, and North America, and encompassing various engineering projects like diversion tunnels and coal mines18–36. The data included different rock origins such as metamorphic, sedimentary, and igneous rocks, and textures ranging from marble to copper ore, addressing multiple rock burst types like strain and shear bursts. To analyze the data, an enhanced sampling and cleaning technique was used to balance the sample sizes for different rock burst grades. Furthermore, data augmentation was carried out to simulate potential rock burst scenarios in underrepresented regions, using the extensive dataset as a learning base. The MLP and RF models were chosen for their suitability with large, non-linear datasets, with Bayesian optimization applied to fine-tune hyperparameters during training. The resulting BO-MLP-RF model demonstrated high accuracy, sensitivity, and generalizability in early rock burst warning.

The BO-MLP-RF model plays a critical role in enhancing the safety and efficiency of geotechnical engineering projects. It supports real-time data monitoring and rapid dissemination of warnings, enabling swift protective measures that mitigate potential human and financial losses. Moreover, this model contributes to the reliability of engineering operations, elevating standards across geotechnical projects. Efficiently utilizing the predictive capabilities of the model allows for strategic planning and resource optimization, boosting overall construction productivity while maintaining safety.

To sum up, the contributions of this paper extend beyond the development of an effective, highly accurate, and widely applicable rock burst predictive model for practical engineering use. It also advances a fresh perspective for researchers, advocating for the creation of rock burst early warning models that incorporate strategies of data balancing and holistic predictive analysis.

Conclusion

This paper presents an analysis of rock burst big data in the context of safety engineering. It explores the rationality of rock burst classification through instance data analysis, improves dataset balance to ensure better alignment with future engineering needs, and develops a novel combined discriminative warning model based on rock burst datasets. Consequently, a BO-MLP-RF rock burst warning model has been created for practical engineering applications. During this study, analyses and discussions were carried out, leading to the following conclusions.

Moreover, this model indirectly boosts the reliability of engineering projects and enhances the quality and safety of geotechnical engineering. Based on the alerts, construction personnel are able to promptly revise plans and optimize resource distribution, which not only ensures safety but also increases construction efficiency.
