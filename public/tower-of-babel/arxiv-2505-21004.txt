ClearSphere: Multi-Earphone Synergy for Enhanced Conversational Clarity
Lixing He, Yunqi Guo, Zhenyu Yan, Guoliang Xing

Abstract
. In crowded places such as conferences, background noise, overlapping voices, and lively interactions make it difficult to have clear conversations. This situation often worsens the phenomenon known as "cocktail party deafness." We present ClearSphere, the collaborative system that enhances speech at the conversation level with multi-earphones. Real-time conversation enhancement requires a holistic modeling of all the members in the conversation, and an effective way to extract the speech from the mixture. ClearSphere bridges the acoustic sensor system and state-of-the-art deep learning for target speech extraction by making two key contributions: 1) a conversation-driven network protocol, and 2) a robust target conversation extraction model. Our networking protocol enables mobile, infrastructure-free coordination among earphone devices. Our conversation extraction model can leverage the relay audio in a bandwidth-efficient way. ClearSphere is evaluated in both real-world experiments and simulations. Results show that our conversation network obtains more than 90% accuracy in group formation, improves the speech quality by up to 8.8 dB over state-of-the-art baselines, and demonstrates real-time performance on a mobile device. In a user study with 20 participants, ClearSphere has a much higher score than baseline with good usability.



1.
Introduction

Figure 1
.
ClearSphere is a collaborative conversation enhancement solution that models the interactions among users. The system is driven by the user’s intention to talk, automatically find a pal, and deliver clear speech within the same conversation.

The rising popularity of smart earphones, with approximately 455 million units shipped globally in 2024, marking an 11.2% year-on-year growth
(
Patently Apple 2025
)
, frames these devices as a new popular category after smartphones.
In addition to the original usage of playing music, current earphones offer unprecedented convenience and rich functionalities. For example, the microphone array on earphones can capture clear audio through beamforming
(
Chatterjee et al. 2022
)
. Besides, the head-tracking technology works well to enable spatial audio, which is made possible by motion sensors in earphones
(
Han et al. 2023b
;
Han et al. 2023a
)
.
Combining the above advantages, we observe that earphones are a promising platform for monitoring human conversation, as they consider both the verbal and non-verbal (head orientation) features.

Conversation is an interactive form of communication between two or more people and plays a crucial role in socialization. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, people naturally form separate conversation groups, and ideally, each person wants to focus solely on the members of their own group—regardless of distance or competing voices. The “cocktail party effect”
(
Pollack and Pickett 1957
)
refers to the human ability to selectively concentrate on a particular voice in noisy environments. However, this perceptual mechanism has limitations. Many individuals experience what is known as “cocktail party deafness,” which hinders their ability to follow conversations in crowded settings due to hearing impairments, cognitive overload, or overwhelming background noise. As a result, clear communication in environments such as conferences, networking events, or guided tours becomes increasingly challenging.

To improve hearing during conversations, using earphones as assistive tools presents a promising direction. However, existing works on speech enhancement fall short of our needs, as they do not specifically target conversational contexts. For example, some methods enhance hearing based on sound categories
(
Veluri et al. 2023
)
, speaker recognition
(
Veluri et al. 2024
)
, or listener distance
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. Yet, these factors may not accurately capture the dynamics of natural conversation. We cannot assume that all conversations occur within a defined area, which contradicts the assumptions made by SoundBubble
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. In contrast, the method proposed in
(
Chen et al. 2024b
)
is the only one that effectively extracts conversations based on turn-taking dynamics and previously recorded audio. However, it suffers from a major limitation: the reliance on turn-taking events introduces significant delays, rendering it impractical for real-time processing.

To address the limitations outlined above, we have developed ClearSphere, a system that leverages multi-device collaboration from a holistic perspective. In contrast to most existing research on earphones, which focuses on single-device capabilities
(
Dong et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2023
;
Duan et al. 2024
)
, ClearSphere acknowledges that conversation is inherently a multi-user activity. By transforming individual earphones into a networked system, our approach integrates the advantages of wearable technology with the strengths of distributed sensor networks.

Our earphone network departs from the conventional assumptions of wireless acoustic sensor networks in two fundamental ways
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
. Traditionally, such networks assume that (1) sound sources appear randomly and independently of sensor node placement, and (2) the sensor nodes themselves remain static. In contrast, our conversation-driven system operates under a reversed premise: the sound sources—human speakers—are co-located with mobile sensor nodes (earphones), and these nodes dynamically organize themselves into conversation groups, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The network is both triggered and guided by user speech, with the network map continuously updated through analysis of acoustic and spectral cues.

As the network map evolves, it enables the identification of conversation groups, detection of new participants, and recognition of members who have exited the interaction. This allows the system to maintain group coherence and ensure clear intra-group communication. Ultimately, by replaying the enhanced speech through noise-canceling headphones, we can augment human auditory perception and outperform the natural cocktail party effect.

We propose a conversation network protocol, which connects earphone users through both verbal and non-verbal signals. Under the protocol, we construct a system called ClearSphere on commercial headphones. However, there are some challenges associated with its implementation.

•

Dynamic peer discovery and network formation.
Since conversations occur spontaneously and participants are mobile, the system must continuously identify nearby speakers, localize their positions, and group devices into socially coherent clusters without any fixed infrastructure.

•

Topology estimation and coordination.
Given noisy, distributed observations across heterogeneous devices, it is necessary to align local acoustic views into a consistent global topology to support multi-party interactions and spatial reasoning.

•

Low-latency conversation enhancement.
To augment user perception, the system must extract the target conversation in real-time from overlapping speech mixtures, using incomplete, lossy, or delayed relay signals from peers, while respecting bandwidth and hardware constraints.

To address these challenges, we introduce three key contributions of ClearSphere. Real-world and simulated datasets were collected to validate the system’s core components, with volunteers participating in realistic conversational scenarios. We will open-source our project after publication. Our key contributions include:

(1)

A conversation-driven networking protocol.

We design a novel network protocol that leverages turn-taking dynamics, speaker orientation, and acoustic signatures to dynamically form and update conversation groups. This protocol enables mobile, infrastructure-free coordination among earphone devices. It includes (i) a node discovery module that jointly performs speaker localization and identification in real time, and (ii) a geometric calibration pipeline that estimates global network topology using only on-device audio and motion observations.

(2)

A robust target conversation extraction model.

Building on the network structure, we develop a latency-aware, bandwidth-efficient conversation enhancement module that leverages peer relay audio as conditioning signals. We propose an adaptive multi-source speech filter with attention-based fusion and dynamic audio compression to ensure clarity and robustness against network delays, packet loss, and compression artifacts.

(3)

A practical implementation with real-world validation.

We implement ClearSphere on commercial earphones equipped with microphones and IMUs, and evaluate its performance through real-world experiments and user studies. Our system achieves over 90% accuracy in conversation group formation, improves speech clarity by up to 8.8 dB SNR over state-of-the-art baselines, and demonstrates real-time performance (RTF > 1) on mobile devices. An adaptive bandwidth controller further improves performance under constrained conditions.

Related Work

2.1.
Conversation Modeling

Conversation involves the exchange of thoughts, feelings, and ideas between two or more people. A natural conversation is not simply a combination of multiple speeches; it also includes both verbal and non-verbal elements. Modeling conversations has significant technical and social implications.

Non-verbal feature

Non-verbal features, also known as body language
(
D’mello and Graesser 2010
)
, play a crucial role in conversations. For example, the orientation of a speaker’s head can indicate their level of interest. According to
(
Grauman et al. 2022
)
, the two main objectives for modeling social interaction are "look at me" and "talk to me." While using smart glasses equipped with both visual and audio inputs makes this task easier
(
Donley et al. 2021
)
, it remains a challenge when relying solely on audio. Most sound source localization techniques primarily focus on the direction of arrival
(
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Yang and Zheng 2022
)
, which does not accurately reflect the speaker’s orientation since it ignores sound directivity.

(
Gedik and Hung 2018
)
presents a method that leverages dynamics of social interactions inferred from acceleration data, employing transfer learning to detect social activity.
Previous research
(
Ahuja et al. 2020
)
has suggested estimating both the location and orientation of the speaker, but these methods typically operate under strict assumptions, such as using a static microphone array.

Verbal feature

In addition to non-verbal cues, the content of speech is also a vital aspect of conversation, as highlighted in
(
Sacks et al. 1974
)
. A closely related task is speaker diarization
(
Bredin 2023
)
, which identifies "who spoke when" and can further transcribe the corresponding text. By analyzing the conversation content
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, we can generate meeting summaries
(
Ramprasad et al. 2024
)
or improve readability
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Beyond semantic analysis, the dynamics of turn-taking in conversations are noteworthy, as they exhibit clear temporal patterns and are essential for understanding social interactions
(
Levinson and Torreira 2015
)
.

Previous studies have attempted to model conversation, such as Flower-Pop
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
, which incorporates smartphones to monitor and proactively mediate the conversation. Or,
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Chang et al. 2022
)
that predict turn-taking events by examining natural overlaps and backchannels. However, it is important to note that turn-taking detection is only effective once it has occurred, which means that waiting for enough data can pose a significant challenge. On the other hand, SocialMind
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
uses a large language model to help people who are anxious in conversation by providing suggestions, capturing both the non-verbal and verbal features.
In contrast, ClearSphere is the first collaborative system designed to model conversations using commercially available earphones.

2.3.
Audio Enhancement

Prior research on audio augmentation in wearable devices provides a foundation for enhancing conversations in noisy environments, as targeted by ClearSphere. We categorize relevant work into three areas: location-based audio augmentation, sound-based speech enhancement, and multi-user audio systems.

Location-based audio enhancement

Location-based audio enhancement delivers context-specific audio based on a user’s position or orientation. For instance, museum audio guides, such as those described in
(
Härmä et al. 2004
)
, play narrations triggered by a user’s proximity to exhibits. Ear-AR
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
enhances immersion by aligning stereo audio with the user’s head-related transfer function, using head-tracking to create spatial soundscapes. While effective for static, single-user experiences, these approaches assume predefined audio triggers and do not address dynamic, multi-user interactions like conversations, where relative user positions and intentions (e.g., head orientation toward a speaker) are critical.

Sound-based audio enhancement

Instead of location-based enhancement, sound-based approach enables users to hear manipulated sounds like superhuman hearing. Combined with active noise cancellation, we can control the user’s listening seamlessly if the latency is as low as 20-30 ms
(
Stone and Moore 1999
;
Gupta et al. 2020
)
.

Fixed-target methods, such as noise suppression
(
Wiener 1949
)
, assume stationary noise spectra, while dynamic target sound extraction adapts to conditions like speaker embeddings
(
Zmolikova et al. 2023
;
Veluri et al. 2024
)
, lip vibrations
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, or spatial bubbles
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. Other approaches filter non-speech sounds by type
(
Veluri et al. 2023
)
, embedding of the sound
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
, or use prior ambient audio for noise cancellation
(
Shen et al. 2018
)
.
However, these methods often focus on single-user scenarios or specific sound categories, which means they do not effectively capture the complex dynamics of group conversations, such as turn-taking in verbal communication and head orientation in non-verbal communication.

In ClearSphere, we propose a multi-device audio system that extends its capabilities to collaborative settings, instead of the current research mainly on vision-based approaches, such as map sharing
(
Dhakal et al. 2022
)
or secure state sharing
(
Slocum et al. 2024
)
.

3.
Background

This section lays the technical foundation for ClearSphere, focusing on acoustic sensing techniques vital for enhancing conversations in mobile, multi-user environments.
First, we discuss sound localization, which accurately identifies speaker positions using earphone microphone arrays, providing essential information to create a conversation map.
Next, we review the Wireless Acoustic Sensor Network (WASN), our reference design for the conversation network, which facilitates collaborative sensing across multiple devices.

3.1.
Sound Localization

Consider a microphone array consisting of
N
microphones located at known positions
\mathbf{p}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. Let the position of the sound source be denoted by
\mathbf{s}=(x_{s},y_{s})
.
The time it takes for the sound to reach the microphone
i
can be expressed as:

t_{s,i}=\frac{d_{s,i}}{c},d_{s,i}=\sqrt{(x_{s}-x_{i})^{2}+(y_{s}-y_{i})^{2}}

where
d_{s,i}
is the distance from the sound source
s
to microphone
i
, and
c
is the speed of sound in air. Since the sound source is unknown to the receiver, we can not directly estimate
t_{s,i}
but calculate the time difference of arrival (TDoA)
t_{s,i}-t_{s,j}
where
i,j\in(1,N)
instead. In practice, any representations that construct the cross-channel relation can be used for sound source localization, such as GCC
(
Knapp and Carter 1976
)
or SALSA-Lite
(
Nguyen et al. 2022
)
.

Compared to the conventional way of sound source localization, the deep learning-based approach performs better with fine-grained time resolution and binaural recording (modeling the head-related transfer function). Specifically, deep learning-based sound localization can be divided into two stages: feature extraction and location estimation. We convert it into a channel-time-frequency representation by applying a log-mel spectrogram transformation for each channel. Next, we calculate inter-channel features such as the GCC between every pair of channels. Lastly, we concatenate every pair of channels with the original log-Mel spectrograms as the final representations. For example, the feature has a shape of
3\times T\times F
for two-channel audio recordings, which can be considered as the input to the neural network. Alternatively, an end-to-end neural network is also possible for sound localization.

Sound localization is often simplified to a scenario where only one sound source is present at a time
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
;
Yang and Zheng 2022
)
.
To extend the compatibility, alternative methods such as multi-track
(
Cao et al. 2021
)
and multi-ACCDOA
(
Shimada et al. 2022
)
formats have been proposed to localize overlapping sound.

3.2.
Wireless Acoustic Sensor Network

We are studying a wireless acoustic sensor network where a group of sensor nodes is randomly distributed in a reverberant environment
(
Bertrand 2011
)
. We assume that the internal geometric configuration of each node’s microphone array is known and that all microphones within the array are sampled at the same time, which we believe is a reasonable assumption following
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
. Additionally, we assume that there is coarse time synchronization among the clocks of the sensor nodes, enabling us to aggregate the data globally. By default, we assume a two-dimensional space, but it can be easily extended to a three-dimensional space.

Since a sensor node cannot determine its own position or orientation within the global coordinate system, all observations are made in the local coordinate system of each node.
Each sensor node
l
(where
l=1,\ldots,L
) computes acoustic features (e.g., DoA estimates
\phi_{k}^{l}
and distance
d_{k}^{l}
) to the acoustic source
k
, all reference to the node’s local coordinate system. Consequently, we gather a total of
K\times L
estimates as the inputs.

From the global view of the WASN, it is composed of
L
sensor nodes each equipped with a microphone array located at positions
n_{l}=[n_{l,x},n_{l,y}]^{T}
and oriented at angles
\theta_{l}
(where
l=1,2,\ldots,L
) relative to a global coordinate system defined by the x and y axes. There are
K
acoustic sources located at positions
s_{k}=[s_{k,x},s_{k,y}]^{T}
(where
k=1,2,\ldots,K
). Note that all the above global notations are unknown and will be estimated through a geometry calibration process based on the observed acoustic signals from sensor nodes.

4.1.
Design Overview

ClearSphere is a novel network system specifically designed to establish connections that focus solely on conversational features. To do so, we define a conversation network protocol, which is the rule of how to convert the acoustic sensing into basic factors in the network system.

It is important to recognize that there are existing platforms, like Livekit
(
Livekit [n. d.]
)
, which offer real-time audio-visual sharing capabilities through the WebRTC
(
Blum et al. 2021
)
Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU).
In contrast, ClearSphere leverages the existing functionalities of these platforms and enhances them with advanced conversation modeling.

Conversational network protocol

To implement our conversation network, the first step is answering the question: How can we technically model the nature conversation?”
We break down a conversation into individual actions (voice) performed by each participant. For example, when user A speaks to user B, we can define a sender (user A) and a receiver (user B).
The direction of the head (direction of voice) indicates their willingness to speak and hear, which is a non-verbal feature of conversation. As shown in Fig.
2
, the “Group1” refers to this case, which is the simplest group where each of the people in the group looks at the others. Suppose we use acoustic sensing that can estimate the direction of arrival; it only reflects the direction you hear the sound, rather than the direction it is coming from.
Using this feature alone can not represent the whole conversation, but only the willingness to hear.
As a result, it is necessary to estimate both the position and orientation of all the users, motivate the use of sensor network and geometric calibration as discussed in
3
.
However, the above paradigm is still problematic because we may not preserve a strict eye contact for any pairs of people in conversation, such as “Group2” in Fig.
2
. As a solution, we extend the definition so that only having a connection with one of the people within the group is enough for enrollment.

Until now, only non-verbal features (head orientation) have been considered since it is more stable and highly correlated to conversation. Instead, the dynamics of turn-taking, as described by
(
Sacks et al. 1974
)
, can play an important role in confirming the conversation. During conversation, random motion of the head can introduce errors in acoustic sensing, making it difficult to find the conversation. We consider it an indicator to show the conversation state, where we can cancel a conversation if no turn-taking has happened. Note that the detection of turn-taking can normally take a few seconds, so there can be errors during the shifting of the group.
We acknowledge the fact that many people may rarely speak out during the conversation, but act as a listener. As a result, the turn-taking event is only considered when the distance between users is larger than a threshold, corresponding to the case “Group3” in Fig.
2
.

Component specifications

Following the protocol we defined earlier, we propose a comprehensive system design of ClearSphere, as shown in Fig.
3
. This system consists of a two-step framework: the network setup and the downstream task.
In the network setup, there are two components: node discovery and geometric calibration. For the downstream task, there is one component: conversation enhancement. This structure allows for an organized approach to establishing and improving conversational interactions within the system.

The first component, node discovery, acts as the front end of our system. In order to access both the speaking and hearing attention, we analyze the spatial and acoustic features of received audio and automatically discover the nearby nodes. This process is explained in detail in Section
4.2
.

The second component, geometric calibration, is performed in a centralized way by aggregating the output of node discovery. Although each node can only observe others with the local coordinate, by optimizing the global geometry, we can obtain a network topology after a cold start. Only after the topology is obtained, we can construct the conversation as discussed in Sec.
4.3
.

The last component, conversation enhancement, is the downstream task enabled by conversation network protocols. Specifically, we can download the compressed audio (relay) from other users in the same conversation, treat it as a condition to extract the clear conversation speech, as discussed in Section
4.4
.

4.2.
Node Discovery for Conversation Setup

Conversation can be depicted through the interaction between speakers; the speaker presents the interest according to the direction of the voice (also the direction of the head). In ClearSphere, each user is considered as one node, where we reformulate speaker localization as a dynamic node discovery problem in a mobile acoustic network, as shown in the upper part of Fig.
4
.

Discover in two-stages

To establish a functional network topology, each device must discover both the spatial distribution and identity signatures of neighboring nodes. While joint estimation of these properties (analogous to sound event localization and detection) is theoretically possible, prior work shows such approaches suffer from computational complexity and poor scalability
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
. Instead, we adopt a decoupled two-stage discovery process that separately handles node localization and identity association and then fuses the results for network coordination.

As for the sound localization, we extract the cross-channel features as Sec.
3
and utilize a convolutional recurrent neural network (CRNN) to process them, which consists of multiple convolutional layers, a GRU layer, and the final linear layer to predict the output. We select multi-track
(
Cao et al. 2021
)
as our output format since we are only interested in one sound class. Specifically, we represent the sound source location in a Cartesian coordinate system with a normalized range, leaving the distance estimation for a later section. Compared to the signal processing-based method, neural sound localization can work well with overlapping sounds and a variable number of sound sources.
Besides, we consider using neural speaker diarization
(
Bredin 2023
)
to extract the identities, which supports overlapping speech and multiple speakers as well.

Joint node discovery

However, there is no free lunch to split the joint task into two relatively easy tasks; the corresponding location of the speaker is still unclear. To solve this problem, we can perform beamforming for each detected sound source and check which speaker’s sound is amplified, which brings two subsequent problems: 1) how to perform beamforming with a limited microphone array, and 2) how to determine the identity of the beamformed speech. Although there are existing solutions for them respectively, directly combining them can lead to degradation since they are not co-optimized. Specifically, the beamformed voice can still contain noises, while the speaker identity extraction may not handle it well.

Instead, we propose a joint model to solve both the beamforming and embedding extraction at the same time. Specifically, we keep the beamformer (e.g., delay-and-sum beamformer) the same and reshape the output from
B\times C\times T\times F
to
B\times T\times(F\times C)
and use a linear projector with layer normalization to predict the d-vector. In addition, the final d-vector is the average over time. Since the output is not audio, we can not use the common loss function for neural beamformer (i.e., scale-invariant signal-noise ratio), but the cosine similarity loss as follows:

\text{L}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=1-\frac{\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|\|\mathbf{B}\|}

,
where
A)
is estimated d-vector, and
B
refers to the d-vector from clean speech with the speaker verification model
1
1

1

https://github.com/resemble-ai/Resemblyzer
.

In summary, the discovered node can be classified into three groups: self-voice, interested voice, and other voices. Note that the self-voice and interested voices come from a similar direction (i.e., the front of the user) with different distances, so it is hard to differentiate them with direction information only. Instead, we assume that the self-identity is given as prior knowledge, which is practical through offline or online enrollment. As a result, the self-voice can be identified by the similarity with the stored speaker embedding, so that we will not discover it as a new node.

4.3.
Network Geometric Calibration

As shown in the design of the conversation network protocol, localization is crucial. By aggregating observations from multiple participants, we can estimate the network’s geometry through analysis of the observations. Each node has information about the locations of nearby nodes in local coordinates. By projecting each node’s position into a global coordinate system, we can estimate the locations of all nodes accurately. With precise geometric data, especially regarding the orientation of each node, we can identify the conversation group using the conversation protocol.

This issue can also be framed as a multi-sensor localization problem, commonly referred to as geometric calibration. Since the physical medium used for localization is acoustic, this falls under the category of WASN, as discussed in Section
3
. In the following, we first discuss two features that are important in calibration: observation sharing and distance estimation, then discuss the key notation difference to the conventional WASN and present our algorithm in the last subsection.

Observation sharing

The principle of geometric calibration is one source can be observed by multiple node, where the relative position is related to the nodes’ positions. Thus, it is important to align the source across nodes.
It is a trivial task when there is only one sound source, but it becomes complicated with 1) overlapping sound, 2) the sound isn’t observed by some nodes. Since we have already extracted the corresponding speaker embedding for each source, we can assign the same identity to the sources with similar embeddings.
Specifically, for the speakers observed by one user (including itself), we pair all of them with the observations from other users and only keep the ones that have high similarity. The similarity is calculated by the cosine similarity of two embeddings.
A group of sources with more than 0.8 similarity is considered the same source. We don’t make the assumption that one source can be observed by all users.
In practice, every device uploads its observed embeddings from Sec.
4.2
to a central server and performs the above matching. Only the source that is observed by at least two nodes are considered as valid ones in the following.

Distance estimation

According to the output of Sec.
4.2
, we can only obtain the direction of arrival, while the distance to the sound source is skipped.
However, the distance information is important in geometric calibration since the optimization can be under-determined without it. Ideally, audio recordings already embed distance information in a reverberant environment, as illustrated in the following:

y(t)=h(t)*x(t)+v(t)=h_{e}(t)*x(t)+h_{l}(t)*x(t)+v(t)

, where
v(t)
corresponds to white sensor noise and
h(t)
represents the room impulse response, the symbol
*
denotes convolution. However, the performance highly relies on a known environment, so the scalability is not satisfying
(
Krause et al. 2021
;
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
.

Instead, we propose to estimate the distance by comparing the sound level between the transmitter and receiver. Ideally, the attenuation of the audio represents the propagation path so that it is related to the distance. Specifically, we can get the initial distance estimation by referring to the sound propagation formula, supposing all the devices share the same amplification. Although the estimated distance is still noisy, we can mitigate the problem in the later optimization.

Mobile WASN

According to Sec.
3
, our network can be considered as mobile WASN, an extension of WASN where the acoustic node can move freely. Similarly, localization of sensor nodes can be achieved by geometric calibration, given sufficient observed sound sources. Compared to the conventional notation, mobile WASN has different assumptions as follows.

(1)

First, the assumption that only one sound source is present during a measurement is unrealistic due to the possibility of overlapping speech and other background sounds. Specifically, if we denote the maximum number of simultaneous speech sources as
M
, we can estimate a total of
M\times K\times L
instances, which is also compatible with fewer than
M
number of sources by adding a blank estimate.

(2)

Second, while all the estimates are considered valid in the previous context, the arbitrary movement of users can create a much greater distance between two sensor nodes, which means that some sound sources may not be observed by all sensor nodes.

(3)

Third, the acoustic source
S_{k}
is defined as the
k^{th}
observed source during a specific time period. In this context,
k
(where
k=1,2,\ldots,K
) can also serve as a time identifier. The notation is important because we can align the sound source with the location of the sensor nodes, which are also time-dependent and can be represented as
n_{k,l}
. Since the sensor nodes can move freely, we use
T_{k,l}
and
R_{k,l}
to indicate the translation and rotation between
n_{k,l}
and
n_{0,l}
for the
l^{th}
node.

(4)

Lastly, it is expected that
n_{k,l}
will match at least one of the
S_{k}
since the sensor node is one of the sound sources. Note that it is not valid conversely since there are sound sources that come from people without earphones.

Geometric calibration

We perform mobile geometric calibration with the networked earphones, which share a similar principle as other geometric calibrations that minimize a cost that is defined to evaluate how well these converted observations align with the assumed geometry. Among the various algorithms, data set matching is an efficient algorithm for geometric calibration. Our algorithm is based on that of
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
; the details of it are out of the scope of this paper..

Different from the original algorithm, we have a rotation matrix
R_{k,l}
and a translation vector
n_{k,l}
that are dependent on both the source and the node. As a result, we rewrite the relative locations in the global coordinates:

G_{k,l}=R_{k,l}d_{k,l}[cos(\theta_{k,l}),sin(\theta_{k,l})]+n_{k,l}

Multiple nodes observe each source at the same time; if all the estimates are perfectly right, all
S_{k,l}
would map to a unique position
G_{k,l}
. Hence, the geometry can be inferred by minimizing the deviation of the projected source positions by the cost function below:

\arg\min\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|G_{k,l}-S_{k,l}\right\|_{2}^{2}

, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denoting the Euclidean norm. There exists no closed-form solution for the above nonlinear optimization problem, so it has to be solved using an iterative optimization algorithm.

Compared to our setting, previous methods estimate

R_{l}
and translation
n_{l}
for L sensor nodes, while ours estimate
K\times L
nodes equivalently. The naive approach is performing geometric calibration for each source, downgrading to K times one source geometric calibration, and merging the results afterward.
According to the analysis in
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
, the performance of geometric calibration depends on the number of sources. Consequently, the one-source geometric calibration will give unreliable results and harm the final performance.

Instead, we can further compose the translation into initial and movement:

R_{k,l}=\overline{R}_{k,l}R_{0,l}
and
n_{k,l}=\overline{n}_{k,l}+n_{0,l}
, where
\overline{R}_{k,l}
refers the rotation from the first source to the k source for sensor l and
\overline{n}_{k,l}
refers the corresponding translation. We can see that if the
\overline{R}_{k,l}
is an identity matrix and
\overline{n}_{k,l}
is zero, the problem downgrades to the same as
(
Gburrek et al. 2021a
)
. As a result, we rewrite the above equation:

G_{k,l}=R_{0,l}d_{k,l}\overline{R}_{k,l}[cos(\theta_{k,l}),sin(\theta_{k,l})]+\overline{n}_{k,l}+n_{0,l}

Fortunately, we can estimate
\overline{R}_{k,l}
and
\overline{n}_{k,l}
by a motion sensor on the node, which means our estimation targets become
R_{0,l}
and
n_{0,l}
so that the problem becomes estimating one locations instead of track. In other words, we can apply the data set match algorithm in the appendix.

In addition, the above processing can be generalized to multiple sources. Suppose
M
is the maximum number of simultaneous sources, the
\overline{K}
refers to the real number of sources within K times (
K\leq\overline{K}\leq M\times K
). We can set the
R
and
n
accordingly to fit in the original setting.

Dynamic calibration

Based on our design, we conduct geometric calibration for every
\overline{K}
observation, which is not ideal for real-time inference. As a solution, we implement a sliding-window approach that allows us to repeat geometric calibration each time we receive new observations. Given that the runtime latency for this process is minimal, we plan to address the optimization in future work.

Meanwhile, selecting an optimal
\overline{K}
becomes critical, as it seems to be related to the latency and performance. Although the correlation between latency is obvious, the correlation with performance can be complicated. Specifically, the motion estimation (
\overline{R}_{k,l}
and
\overline{n}_{k,l}
) by IMU is also poisoned by accumulated errors with increasing
\overline{K}
, which means the calibration may not be accurate with a large
\overline{K}
. A small sliding window can be a direct solution to mitigate the degradation caused by noisy estimates. However, since the optimization needs sufficient observations to converge, the small window can conversely degrade the usability. Instead, we propose a weight decay algorithm where the calibration results focus more on the nearest observations. Specifically, we set the weight as
1/(T-t)
, where
T-t
refers to the difference between the current time and the time of observations.

Problem formulation

Our design goal is to extract the desired conversation from a mixture of speech under specific conditions. Compared to speaker embedding, the raw audio provides fine-grained information but consumes more bandwidth. Suppose we can obtain the relatively clean audio as input (or called relay) as shown in the bottom part of Fig.
4
, a naive solution is to directly use it, just like MUTE
(
Shen et al. 2018
)
did. However, the above assumption is not practical since we can not make sure the transmitted audio matches the target speech even if we perform noise suppression on it. At the same time, transmitting full-spectrum audio without compression is impractical because the typical sample rate can be as high as 48,000 Hz. Although there are plenty of compression tools, there will always be a difference between raw audio and compressed audio.

On the other hand, we cannot assume that the received audio will be the same as the audio before transmission, even after accounting for compression loss. The instability of the network can cause latency, packet loss, and bit distortion.
Fortunately, we can simulate these perturbations during training to help us adapt effectively.

In conclusion, our problem is how to design a conversation extraction model that can better utilize noisy conditional audio.

Adaptive audio control

Ideally, we are supposed to transmit the relay audio with high quality as much as possible. However, the required bandwidth may not be always available, especially when there are multiple users at the same time. To tackle this issue, we suggest an adaptive compression controller that adjusts compression levels to reduce bandwidth usage and latency while preserving audio quality. We believe that as interference increases (which can be estimated by the network topology and voice activity), finer details in the relay audio become necessary. For example, when the distance between the transmitter and receiver is large, the valid speech has much less volume than the noise.

We measure the correlation between performance and compression ratios at different interference levels in Fig.
7(a)
. For each line that refers to one input SNR, we observe that the relationship between bandwidth and performance does not follow a straightforward linear pattern. When we have a fixed bandwidth, the controller can make a simple decision when there’s only one participant, utilizing all available bandwidth. However, the situation becomes more complex with multiple participants, as we must distribute the bandwidth among them. To address this, we use the data obtained in Fig.
7(a)
and conduct a grid search to determine the optimal bandwidth allocation based on the number of participants and the total available bandwidth.

In summary, the controller described above can be implemented in a centralized manner, allowing it to collect observations and make corresponding decisions for more than two participants. Specifically, we have
N^{2}
pairs of nodes, each of which will be assigned an associated optimal bandwidth to transmit the audio accordingly.

Target conversation extraction

Recall that the existing target speech extraction mainly uses a vector as the condition, either speaker embedding
(
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Veluri et al. 2024
)
or a one-hot vector
(
Veluri et al. 2023
)
. In other words, they compress the audio into a time-invariant feature with an ultra-high compression rate. Apparently, higher compression will lose fine-grained information; ClearSphere is designed to balance the compression and performance.

We take the previous work of target speaker extraction
(
Veluri et al. 2024
)
as a starting point.
Specifically, the noisy speech is first converted to the time-frequency dimension by an encoder (invertible like short-time-fourier-transform) and then processed by TFGridNet
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. To enable target speaker extraction, the speaker embedding is projected to the same feature length and repeated to align with the time dimension.
Thus, we can extend it by providing a time-dependent feature instead of using the same feature all the time. A direct solution is to apply the same encoder above to the relay audio, which will result in the same dimension in both time and frequency. Similarly, we still use a linear projector to map it into feature space. However, the above design is vulnerable to the latency of relay audio if the delay is larger than the length of one frame. Applying an inconsistent relay audio and noisy speech can lead to huge performance degradation.

We propose a latency-aware framework that compensates for the complicated audio delay due to system mismatch, propagation, and communication. Our idea to mitigate is adjusting the time resolution of the feature to match the latency.
Specifically, we modify the speaker embedding model that is a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network
(
Wan et al. 2018
)
with a projection layer, considering all the hidden states instead of only the last one.
Suppose the latency equals
N
times the frame length, we perform padding on the last two frames using the time-invariant speaker embedding.
As for the estimation of padding length (the same as latency), we can use the Generalized Cross-Correlation (GCC) feature between the transmitted audio and noisy speech and identify the first peak as the offset. While this peak may not be perfectly accurate, it can still significantly improve the performance empirically. Besides, ClearSphere is expected to work well with multiple conditions (participants). A naive solution is to apply the speech filter multiple times, which is computation-intensive and may conflict. Instead, we propose an attention mechanism that combines multiple conditions into the intermediate feature as follows:

X=Attention([X_{0},X_{1},...])

, where
X_{i}
refers to the
i^{th}
conditions.

On the other hand, because we pad our features with the time-invariant speaker embedding, we can expect that the minimum performance will be equivalent to that of using speaker embedding alone. To further reduce bandwidth usage, we can rely solely on the speaker embedding when performance is adequate.
We conducted an additional measurement to establish when it is appropriate to activate neural compression, as shown in Fig.
7(b)
. If the output at 10 dB is considered satisfactory, our observations indicate that enabling the speaker embedding when the input signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) exceeds 5 dB is sufficient.

We conduct a user study evaluation through an online form. We first recruit 20 volunteers to give a subjective evaluation of the enhanced performance of our system. The second session of the user study evaluates the performance of ClearSphere under different compression levels (bandwidths). Runtime latency

ClearSphere is designed to provide a real-time experience for users, meaning that it is good to have less latency. Since we can process data at the frame level, it is crucial to keep the frame length under 50 ms and ensure that the real-time factor is greater than 1.
We tested the latency on both a smartphone (Pixel 7) and a desktop computer, using either a CPU (i7-11700k) or a GPU (RTX 3090). The results show that the real-time factor (the time to process one frame divided by the duration of the frame) is 2 on the smartphone, 10 on the desktop CPU, and 1250 on the desktop GPU. Compared to baselines such as
(
Veluri et al. 2024
)
, our system exhibits similar overhead since the majority of the neural network architecture is the same, with only additional computation required for the encoder.

It is also important to note that we do not include the latency of geometric calibration in our real-time inference measurements, as this process can be performed on a server in parallel and takes only 0.2 seconds for 100 observations.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
Advanced Network for Large-scale experiment

Due to our limited resources, we are unable to conduct real-world experiments with a large group of people. However, with a suitable dataset, we can thoroughly evaluate ClearSphere and stimulate research in other areas, such as: 1) modeling social interactions, including body language, similar to the Ego4D project
(
Grauman et al. 2022
)
, and 2) improving speech recognition in contexts with background noise.

The currently available networks, including the default Wi-Fi option, Bluetooth, and Bluetooth mesh, may not be suitable for large-scale deployment. Wi-Fi Direct
(
Alliance [n. d.]
)
could be a promising alternative to explore due to its high bandwidth capabilities. In contrast, Bluetooth has advantages over Wi-Fi, such as lower power consumption and greater accessibility, but it struggles with supporting multiple users. Considering Bluetooth mesh, which is designed for multiple users, the bandwidth is not sufficient for audio but only for sending speaker-embedding. In conclusion, it would be advantageous to explore new hardware designs for sharing audio and images, particularly in networks like MUTE
(
Shen et al. 2018
)
, and to consider approaches that integrate both hardware and software development.

6.2.
Single Device Solution

ClearSphere is a collaborative system that operates with multiple devices. However, its effectiveness can be limited when there are only a few devices or just one. A key advantage of collaboration in this system is its ability to model conversations bidirectionally, which may still be achievable with a single device.

Earphone

Currently, the localization of the sound source does not represent bidirectional interest in conversation. However,
(
Ahuja et al. 2020
)
shows that it is possible to estimate the orientation of others using multiple microphones, taking advantage of the directivity of human speech. Although the system’s performance in the presence of interference and movement has not been thoroughly studied, it shows promise for future research.

Smart glasses

Smart glasses offer more powerful computational resources compared to headphones and include cameras that can gather detailed information. However, the typical design of smart glasses does not support noise-canceling features, making it impractical to manage audio in the same way as ClearSphere.
Nonetheless, smart glasses can easily model the conversation thanks to the camera on them. Specifically, we can estimate others’ head orientation to detect their interest. Besides, smart glasses can enable other interesting applications, including immersive tours in museums or libraries, or provide feedback on the screen according to the transcribed speech
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
.

6.3.
Virtual Meeting

ClearSphere is designed to fit the natural conversation, or face-to-face meeting. However, it may not be restricted to face-to-face conversation. Instead, it can be extended to virtual meetings, where participants are not present in person. Recalled that there are three important features in ClearSphere: head direction, location, and voice, which are possible to obtain remotely with a camera and microphone-equipped device, such as a laptop.
Although the location can not be accessed explicitly, we can determine it by allocation or virtual movement. In conclusion, all functionality of ClearSphere can be reproduced online as a remote version of it, which can extract conversation easily so that the research highlight is shifted to maintain an immersive experience, both in visual
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
and audio
(
Nowak et al. 2023
)
.

7.
Conclusion

We introduced ClearSphere, a system that enhances voice clarity in noisy environments through multi-device collaboration. By combining advanced acoustic processing, geometric calibration, and conversation enhancement, ClearSphere effectively isolates target conversations in crowded settings.
Our implementation on commercial headphones shows substantial performance improvements over existing solutions. This framework addresses conversation tracking and audio isolation challenges while ensuring scalability and flexibility.
Overall, ClearSphere advances assistive auditory technologies, paving the way for improved communication in complex acoustic environments.
