Fertility-related psychological health and social pressures among women of average and delayed childbearing age: a multicenter survey
Authors: Qinqin Yuan, Mi Su, Yanhong Li, Min Hu, Yaling Zhou, Xin Li, Aijie Xie, Yanmei Xia, Yu Lu, Xixi Wu, Tenglan Wu, Biao Huang, Dan Luo, Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis multicenter study aimed to assess the psychological health and social pressures experienced by women delaying childbearing, compared to those of average childbearing age age. The study further explored the socio-demographic factors contributing to the emotional well-being of these women during early pregnancy.MethodsA total of 1232 women, aged 18–45 years, were recruited between January and December 2023. Participants were categorized into two groups: average childbearing age (< 30 years) and delayed childbearing (≥ 30 years). Psychological health was evaluated using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Socio-demographic data, including educational background, employment status, income level, and stress sources, were collected and analyzed. Binary logistic regression and multiple linear regression were employed to identify factors associated with negative emotional states and perceived stress.ResultsThe delayed childbearing group exhibited significantly higher levels of anxiety and stress compared to the average childbearing age group. Factors such as career development stress, reproductive health issues, and social expectations related to delayed childbirth were identified as independent contributors to negative emotional states. Women in the delayed childbearing group reported higher levels of age-related anxiety (65.1%) and societal pressure (54.6%) than their counterparts. Multiple regression analyses indicated that delayed childbearing, career stress, financial stress, and reproductive health issues were key factors affecting perceived stress.ConclusionsWomen delaying childbearing experience significant psychological distress due to both societal pressures and self-imposed expectations. Understanding these stressors is crucial for improving mental health support for women in this demographic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04150-y.Keywords: Delayed childbearing, Multi-center study, Anxiety, Stress

Background

In recent decades, there has been a significant shift in reproductive patterns worldwide, with an increasing number of women choosing to delay childbearing [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, the global average age at first birth has increased from approximately 26–28 years to around 29–31 years [3, 4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that women’s fertility potential begins to decline after 30 years [5–7], which may heighten psychological concerns regarding reproductive health and pregnancy outcomes among women in this age range.

Women who delay childbirth often face unique psychological stressors that may differ from those encountered by younger mothers. Social pressure is a significant factor, as societal expectations of the “ideal” age for motherhood often conflict with individual life choices [8, 9]. In many cultures, delayed childbearing can be perceived as a deviation from traditional norms, leading to feelings of stigmatization, anxiety, and self-doubt [10, 11]. Women may internalize these societal pressures, resulting in emotional distress that can manifest as anxiety, depression, or feelings of inadequacy. In addition to societal pressures, women delaying childbirth may experience internal conflicts related to fertility concerns, body image, and perceived loss of reproductive opportunities [12, 13].

Despite the growing body of research on the physiological aspects of delayed childbearing, the psychological implications remain underexplored. Existing literature on the subject tends to focus on specific subsets of women, such as those undergoing assisted reproductive technologies or those experiencing infertility [14], leaving a gap in our understanding of the broader population of women who choose to delay motherhood for personal, social, or economic reasons.

This multicenter study seeks to fill this gap by exploring the psychological health and social pressures faced by women who delay childbearing. Specifically, it aims to examine the prevalence of mental health issues, including depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as the societal factors contributing to these psychological challenges. By utilizing a large and diverse sample of 1232 women across multiple centers, this study will provide a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between delayed childbearing, mental health, and societal pressures.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter, cross-sectional study aimed to assess the psychological health and social pressures experienced by women at different stages of childbearing. This multicenter study was conducted across six tertiary maternal and child health hospitals located in both urban and suburban regions of Sichuan Province. These centers were selected based on their comprehensive obstetric services, patient volume, and representativeness of the regional population. A total of 1232 pregnant women, with gestational ages between 10 and 12 weeks, were recruited between January and December 2023. Participants were categorized into two groups based on maternal age: those aged 30 years or older were classified as the delayed childbearing group, whereas those younger than 30 years were categorized as the average childbearing age group. Inclusion criteria encompassed singleton pregnancies confirmed via ultrasound, a gestational age between 10 and 12 weeks, and no history of severe psychiatric disorders. Women with multiple pregnancies or those unable to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

The study was conducted across multiple centers to ensure a diverse sample representing various socio-economic and cultural backgrounds. To ensure consistency across sites, standardized procedures for recruitment, consent acquisition, and questionnaire administration were implemented (Supplementary File 1). This study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400094710) on December 26, 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Chengdu Women’s and Children’s Central Hospital (Approval No. 2023097), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. And this study was conducted and completed strictly in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Demographic and clinical data, including age, educational background, marital status, income, employment status, and fertility history, were collected and analyzed for potential associations with psychological outcomes.

Data collection

Comprehensive socio-demographic and clinical data were collected through structured questionnaires and medical record review to ensure a holistic assessment of factors associated with psychological health. The following variables were included: maternal age (years), gravidity, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), smoking status, alcohol consumption history, reproductive health issues, and use of assisted reproductive technology (ART). Socio-economic indicators encompassed marital status, educational level (no formal education/primary/secondary, tertiary, or postgraduate), employment status (full-time or not), and household income level (categorized as low, medium, or high according to local economic standards).

Psychosocial factors assessment

Psychological health was assessed using three standardized and validated instruments: the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [15], the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [16], and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [17]. All psychometric instruments, including the EPDS, PSS, and STAI, were administered using validated Chinese versions that had previously undergone translation and back-translation procedures to ensure cultural and linguistic equivalence. The reliability of these instruments in our study population was confirmed through pilot testing, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding 0.80 for all scales.

Although the EPDS was originally developed to screen for postpartum depression, it has been widely validated and applied for assessing depressive symptoms during pregnancy in both clinical and research settings. Previous studies have demonstrated its reliability and sensitivity for detecting antenatal emotional distress. The EPDS evaluates depressive symptoms through 10 items concerning mood, anxiety, and sleep disturbances, with scores ≥ 10 indicating the need for further evaluation. The PSS measures perceived stress, with scores > 13 signifying significant stress. The STAI assesses both state (temporary) and trait (persistent) anxiety, with scores < 40 for state anxiety and < 35 for trait anxiety considered within normal limits. These tools are widely recognized in maternal and reproductive health research, offering reliable and valid assessments of psychological health. Negative emotional states were defined as the presence of anxiety, depression, or high-stress state among participants.

In addition to the standardized psychological instruments, a self-designed questionnaire was developed to evaluate psychosocial factors relevant to delayed childbearing. This questionnaire comprised six major domains: career advancement, fear of parenting, financial insecurity, age-related anxiety, partner factors, and social and cultural expectations. Each domain included two representative items, with responses recorded in a dichotomous format (Yes/No). Example items included: “I prefer to establish my career before having children” (career advancement), “I am concerned I may not be able to raise a child well” (fear of parenting), “I want to be financially stable before becoming a parent” (financial insecurity), “I am anxious about the risks of pregnancy as I get older” (age-related anxiety), “My partner prefers to delay having children due to career or financial concerns” (partner factors), and “I feel social pressure to follow traditional timelines for marriage and childbearing” (social and cultural expectations). These items were designed to capture perceived stressors commonly reported by women during early pregnancy, particularly those delaying childbearing. Participants were also encouraged to provide open-ended responses describing additional sources of stress or psychological concerns not covered by the structured items. The content and structure of the full questionnaire are provided in Supplementary file 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and psychological data. Continuous variables such as age and psychological scale scores were expressed as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables like education level, marital status, and employment status were presented as frequencies and percentages. Independent t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between the average childbearing age and delayed childbearing groups, while chi-square tests were employed for categorical variables.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing PSS scores, while binary logistic regression was used to examine factors contributing to negative emotional states. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the influence of socio-economic factors on mental health outcomes. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.

Results

The selection process for study participants is illustrated in Fig. 1. During the screening phase, individuals with a history of psychiatric conditions were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 1232 women. Of these, 553 (44.9%) were allocated to the average childbearing age group, while 679 (55.1%) were assigned to the delayed childbearing group. The average age of the participants at enrollment was 29.75 ± 4.42 years, and the mean pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was 21.99 ± 2.78 kg/m². Additionally, 78 participants (6.3%) had conceived through assisted reproductive technologies, and 183 (14.9%) were classified as having advanced maternal age. Interestingly, only 736 participants (59.7%) reported feeling adequately prepared for childbirth (Table 1).

Furthermore, notable differences were observed in the sources of stress between the two groups. In the delayed childbearing group, 542 participants (79.8%) cited career-related stress, 442 (65.1%) reported anxiety related to age, and 371 (54.6%) mentioned stress from social and cultural expectations. These proportions were significantly higher than the 59.9%, 17.2%, and 23.5%, respectively, reported by participants in the average childbearing age group. In contrast, only 239 participants (35.2%) in the delayed childbearing group reported financial stress, which was significantly lower than the 52.8% observed in the average childbearing age group (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Participants exhibiting symptoms of anxiety, depression, or high stress were categorized as experiencing negative emotional states. To further investigate the factors influencing negative emotional states in women, binary logistic regression analysis was performed. After adjusting for pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, education level, employment status, income, marital status, age-related anxiety, and stress from social and cultural expectations, the results revealed several independent factors associated with negative emotional states. Specifically, delayed childbearing (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.15, 2.99, p = 0.010), reproductive health issues (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.34, 2.42, p < 0.001), career advancement stress (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.25, 2.39, p = 0.001), and financial stress (OR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.42, 3.54, p < 0.001) were all significantly associated with negative emotional states. The likelihood of experiencing a negative emotional state was 87% higher among women who delayed childbearing. Additionally, reproductive health issues, career advancement stress, and financial stress increased the risk of negative emotional states by 0.99, 0.72, and 1.58 times, respectively (Fig. 2A).

As logistic regression analysis indicated that economic pressure had a significant impact on negative emotional states among women in early pregnancy, a further subgroup analysis was conducted to examine the influence of different income levels on these emotional states. Among participants with low income, the prevalence of negative emotional states was notably higher compared to those with medium income (42.1% vs. 33.2%, p = 0.030). Interestingly, within the low, medium, and high-income categories, the medium-income group showed the lowest proportion of participants experiencing negative emotional states, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2B).

Binary logistic regression was employed to further investigate the factors influencing anxiety. After adjusting for pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, educational level, employment status, income level, marital status, career advancement stress, and financial stress, the analysis revealed that delayed childbearing, reproductive health issues, and age-related anxiety were independent factors significantly associated with anxiety in women. Delayed childbearing, reproductive health issues, and age-related anxiety increased the risk of anxiety in women by 0.85, 1.01, and 0.77 times, respectively (Table 2).

Discussion

This multicenter study provides valuable insights into the psychological health and social pressures experienced by women delaying childbearing. Our findings underscore the complex interplay between socio-demographic factors, reproductive health, and psychological well-being, highlighting the unique challenges faced by women in this demographic.

Psychological health in women delaying childbearing

The study revealed that women in the delayed childbearing group exhibited significantly higher levels of anxiety and perceived stress compared to their peers in the average childbearing age group. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which suggests that delayed childbearing often coincides with elevated psychological distress [12]. Previous studies have demonstrated that women who delay childbirth tend to experience increased levels of anxiety and stress, largely due to societal expectations, career demands, and concerns related to fertility and reproductive health [18, 19]. Our study further corroborates these findings by identifying delayed childbearing as a significant predictor of negative emotional states, with women in this group having an 87% higher risk of experiencing anxiety and stress.

Social and cultural pressures

The social pressures associated with delayed childbearing emerged as a dominant theme in our study. Overwhelmingly, participants in the delayed childbearing group reported career-related stress, age anxiety, and pressure stemming from societal and cultural expectations. In line with prior research, these stressors were significantly more prevalent among delayed childbearing women compared to those with average childbearing age age [20, 21]. Notably, career advancement stress was cited by 79.7% of women in the delayed childbearing group, reflecting the growing tension between professional goals and the desire to start a family. This aligns with the broader societal context, where women often face the dual challenge of achieving career success while also conforming to traditional expectations of motherhood [22, 23].

Age-related anxiety was another major contributor to stress in our cohort. Women in the delayed childbearing group frequently expressed concerns about the impact of aging on fertility and the potential risks associated with advanced maternal age. These concerns are supported by medical evidence indicating that reproductive potential gradually declines with age, and that advanced maternal age is associated with increased risks of pregnancy complications, such as gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and chromosomal abnormalities [24, 25]. Despite advancements in assisted reproductive technologies, persistent societal expectations and stigma related to age and fertility continue to impose considerable psychological strain on women who delay childbirth.

In traditional Chinese society, there is a strong emphasis on early marriage and childbearing, which places significant pressure on women to conform to these expectations, especially as they approach their thirties. Before the age of 30, the anxiety surrounding age-related fertility and societal expectations is relatively low, but after 30, both the societal and familial pressures, along with the internalized fear of declining fertility, intensify. This increase in stress is primarily driven by the expectation from families and society for women to fulfill traditional roles of motherhood at a “proper” age. Additionally, women themselves often impose pressure on their own reproductive choices, fearing the consequences of delayed childbirth, which compounds the psychological strain [26, 27]. This dual burden—externally from society and family, and internally from self-imposed expectations—creates significant emotional distress for women who delay childbearing in their thirties.

Socio-economic factors and mental health

Our study also identified socio-economic factors, such as income and education, as significant contributors to psychological health. Women with lower income levels were more likely to experience negative emotional states, a finding that resonates with the broader body of research on the relationship between socio-economic status and mental health. Previous studies have highlighted the impact of financial stress on women’s well-being, particularly in the context of reproductive decisions [28, 29]. In our study, financial stress was found to significantly contribute to negative emotional states, with women in the low-income group reporting a higher prevalence of negative emotional states compared to those with medium income. Interestingly, the medium-income group, despite facing other pressures, exhibited the lowest rates of negative emotional states, suggesting that a certain level of financial security may buffer against the mental health challenges associated with delayed childbearing.

Education level and employment status also emerged as important factors influencing psychological outcomes. Highly educated women and those employed full-time were more likely to experience career-related stress, which contributed to higher levels of perceived stress and anxiety. This could reflect the pressure to balance career aspirations with reproductive expectations, which is particularly pronounced among highly educated, career-driven women who delay childbearing for professional or personal reasons [30, 31].

Reproductive health and psychological well-being

Reproductive health issues, such as concerns about fertility, pregnancy complications, and the potential for infertility, were found to be strong predictors of anxiety in the delayed childbearing group. Previous studies have demonstrated that reproductive health problems are a major source of psychological distress, as they are often perceived as barriers to achieving desired family outcomes [32, 33]. Women in our study who experienced reproductive health issues had a significantly higher risk of developing anxiety, which further emphasizes the need for comprehensive reproductive health counseling and psychological support for women considering delayed childbearing.

While this study provides valuable insights into the psychological health of women delaying childbearing, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, and although data were collected from multiple tertiary centers, selection bias may exist, as participants attending early prenatal care might not represent the general population. The reliance on self-reported data also introduces the potential for recall or social desirability bias. In addition, the findings may not fully capture the experiences of women from diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds, particularly those in rural or marginalized settings. Lastly, the study did not include detailed assessments of partner, family, or workplace support, which may influence psychological well-being. Future longitudinal and mixed-methods studies are warranted to address these limitations and provide a more comprehensive understanding of reproductive mental health among women who delay childbearing.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that women who delay childbearing may experience elevated levels of anxiety and stress, potentially influenced by social pressures, reproductive health concerns, and socio-economic factors. While these associations suggest important psychological and social implications of delayed childbearing, the cross-sectional nature of the study precludes causal inference. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and viewed as preliminary evidence supporting the need for further longitudinal research. Nonetheless, the results indicate that tailored mental health support and socio-educational strategies may be beneficial for women considering or experiencing delayed childbearing.
