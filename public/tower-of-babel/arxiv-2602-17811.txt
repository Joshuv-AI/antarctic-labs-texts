Faster Parallel Batch-Dynamic Algorithms for Low Out-Degree Orientation
Guy Blelloch, Andrew Brady, Laxman Dhulipala, Jeremy Fineman, Kishen Gowda, Chase Hutton

Abstract
A low out-degree orientation directs each edge of an undirected graph with the goal of minimizing the maximum out-degree of a vertex. In the parallel batch-dynamic setting, one can insert or delete batches of edges, and the goal is to process the entire batch in parallel with work per edge similar to that of a single sequential update and with span (or depth) for the entire batch that is polylogarithmic. In this paper we present faster parallel batch-dynamic algorithms for maintaining a low out-degree orientation of an undirected graph. All results herein achieve polylogarithmic 0pt, with high probability (whp); the focus of this paper is on minimizing the work, which varies across results. Our first result is the first parallel batch-dynamic algorithm to maintain an asymptotically optimal orientation with asymptotically optimal expected work bounds, in an amortized sense, improving over the prior best work bounds of Liu et al. [SPAA ’22] by a logarithmic factor. Our second result is a O ⁡ ( c ​ log ⁡ n ) O(c\log n) orientation algorithm with expected worst-case O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\sqrt{\log n}) work per edge update, where c c is a known upper-bound on the arboricity of the graph. This matches the best-known sequential worst-case O ⁡ ( c ​ log ⁡ n ) O(c\log n) orientation algorithm given by Berglin and Brodal [Algorithmica ’18], albeit in expectation. Our final result is a O ⁡ ( c + log ⁡ n ) O(c+\log n) -orientation algorithm with O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{2}n) expected worst-case work per edge update. This algorithm significantly improves upon the recent result of Ghaffari and Koo [SPAA ’25], which maintains a O ⁡ ( c ) O(c) -orientation with O ⁡ ( log 9 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{9}n) worst-case work per edge whp.

Technical Overview

In this paper, we revisit two of the fastest sequential algorithms, one classic and one recent, for maintaining
low out-degree orientations and study how to parallelize them
[
13
,
6
]
. In the rest of this section, we
describe the challenges of parallelizing these algorithms and our solutions.

2.1
Amortized Low Out-Degree Orientation

The Brodal-Fagerberg Algorithm.

In their seminal work, Brodal and Fagerberg
[
13
]
proposed a simple dynamic algorithm for maintaining an
O(c)
-orientation with optimal bounds, where
c
is an upper-bound on the maximum arboricity of the graph over the sequence of updates. Their algorithm works as follows:
For edge deletions, the algorithm simply deletes the edge. For edge insertions, it orients the new edge arbitrarily out of one of its endpoints. If this causes the vertex’s out-degree to exceed a
cutoff threshold
which is
O(c)
, the algorithm flips all of its outgoing edges and repeats this process for any other vertex, one by one, whose out-degree becomes excessive.

They analyze their algorithm by comparing the number of edge flips performed over a sequence of updates to the number of edge flips performed by an optimal offline algorithm that sees the entire sequence of operations in advance. Following their terminology, we call an edge
good
if it agrees with the offline orientation and
bad
otherwise.
They define a potential function equal to the number of bad edges in the graph. Edge insertions and edge flips performed by the offline strategy can increase the potential. However, if a vertex has many out-edges, most of them must be bad (since there can be at most
c
good edges incident to that vertex). Thus, by flipping all of them, the algorithm can only decrease the overall potential.
Hence, by a potential-based analysis, the number of flips performed by the algorithm over the entire sequence of updates is bounded by the number of flips performed by the offline strategy.
Finally, they show that there exists an offline strategy that maintains an
O(c)
-orientation with
O(\log n)
flips.
Intriguingly, they show that the amortized number of flips performed by this algorithm is
O(1)
-competitive with the number of flips made by any other sequence of flips that maintains a bounded out-degree, and thus the algorithm achieves optimal work up to constant factors. Note that in the sequential setting, a single flip takes constant work.

Parallelizing the Brodal-Fagerberg Algorithm: Challenges and Solution.

Unfortunately, parallelizing this algorithm appears difficult due to the chains of sequential dependencies inherent in its repeated-flipping approach.
For instance, consider Figure
1
. In each step, we flip one vertex below
4c
, but these flips cause another vertex’s out-degree to rise to
4c
. Even if we flipped all vertices with high out-degree in each step, operating on Figure
1
still has high 0pt, because a vertex does not become high out-degree until its neighbor flips.

Figure 1:
Example graph with high 0pt for Brodal-Fagerberg’s Algorithm. Let the cutoff for flipping be
4c
. Note that the black edges can all be inserted in any order without the algorithm performing any flips because the out-degree bound is not violated. When the green edge
(v_{1},a)
is inserted,
v_{1}
flips, then
v_{2}
in the next step (now having
4c
), so on and so forth. Thus there will be
\Omega(t)
iterations of the algorithm, and
t
can be as high as
\Theta(\frac{n}{c})
. Note that similar examples arise even if the edges are not inserted in an arbitrary orientation direction.

Our solution is the following simple strategy: collect all of the outgoing edges of the vertices that currently exceed the
cutoff threshold
(same as before), and run a parallel static orientation algorithm on the local subgraph induced by these edges to orient them.
This guarantees that the out-degrees of these vertices are now bounded by the threshold of the static algorithm (i.e.,
static threshold
), whereas the rest of the vertices, in the worst case, have their out-degrees bounded by the sum of the cutoff threshold and the static threshold of
O(c)
.

Our parallel algorithm is arguably simpler than the original algorithm in the sense that it requires a
single round
of static orientation on a local affected subgraph to fix the orientation.
The static orientation step effectively reduces the dependencies, but at the cost of a slightly worsened bound (by constant factors) on the out-degree. At the same time, our approach of using static orientation yields the same asymptotic potential reduction as Brodal and Fagerberg’s strategy. If a high-degree vertex enters the static orientation, it will necessarily leave with far fewer out-edges than it started with, meaning that many flips occurred off of this vertex—enough for a constant amount of potential to be lost per flip.

Our amortized algorithm result is stated in Theorem
2.1
and proved in Section
4
. We also show that the algorithm is asymptotically optimal in terms of the number of flips performed, as stated in Theorem
2.2
, since it satisfies the same property as the sequential algorithm; i.e., it performs asymptotically the same number of flips as any algorithm that maintains an
O(c)
orientation.

Given an arboricity
c

1

O(b)

O(b\log n)

•

supports batch insertions in amortized expected
O(b)

O(\log^{2}n)

O(b\log n)
work and
O(\log n)

•

supports batch insertions in deterministic amortized
O(b\log n)

O(\log^{2}n)

2.2
Orientation with Worst-Case Guarantees

The Berglin-Brodal Algorithm (
k
-Flips).

Although the algorithm of Brodal-Fagerberg
[
13
]
is optimal, it only provides amortized guarantees, and obtaining worst-case bounds is of both strong theoretical and practical interest.
In recent work, Berglin and Brodal
[
6
]
gave an extremely simple algorithm for orientation with worst-case guarantees that we call the
k
-Flips Algorithm
.
The algorithm has a single parameter,
k
. On each insert or delete operation, it repeats the following
k
times: flip an edge incident on a (current) max out-degree vertex. Depending on the choice of parameters,
k
will be between constant and
O(\log n)
.

To determine which edge is flipped, they store the out-edges in a queue, flipping edges from the front of the queue, and adding them to the back.
By using a queue, they avoid repeatedly flipping the same edge back-and-forth between two max-degree vertices.

Brodal and Fagerberg’s approach
[
13
]
to analyzing their optimal amortized algorithm does not apply to the
k
-Flips algorithm. In a single invocation of the
k
-Flips algorithm, the total number of bad edges (potential) can increase. However, Brodal and Fagerberg’s analysis relies on the potential decreasing greatly every invocation. Instead, Berglin and Brodal’s analysis
[
6
]
relies on a careful accounting scheme.

First, potentials are introduced on edges so that the out-degree of a vertex is closely approximated by its potential (the sum of its out-edges’ potential). These potentials are chosen so that flipping an edge from a vertex with sufficiently high out-degree will lower the potential of the graph overall.
However, even as the overall potential of the graph decreases, it is still possible that the potential (and hence out-degree) could accumulate on a single vertex over time.
To bound this accumulation, they develop an abstract
counter game
.
In this counter game, played by a single adversarial player, there is a set of counters with weights.
On each turn, the player can move weight from one counter to another, but with certain restrictions. For example, weights can only be moved from counters with close to the maximum weight. They say that a counter game
dominates
a potential state if each counter has weight greater than its corresponding vertex’s potential value.
Their analysis shows that this counter game is general enough to simulate the changes of vertex potentials over time, but restricted enough to have bounded growth on the maximum weight of any counter.

Why is Adapting the
k
-Flips Algorithm to the Batch Setting Hard?

The sequential worst-case algorithm immediately gives a parallel dynamic algorithm (parallelizing a single update), because it only performs
k=O(\log n)
flips, and thus runs in
O(\log n)
0pt.
However, in the parallel
batch-dynamic
setting, applying the sequential algorithm on a batch of size
b
would yield
\Omega(b\log n)
0pt.
Therefore, we would like to find a way to perform many edge flips at once to lower the 0pt for batch updates.
However, we run into four difficulties when flipping many edges simultaneously:

(1)

We must decide which edges to flip. We cannot choose arbitrary edges to flip, because we need a net reduction in potential.

(2)

We must demonstrate that potentials do not accumulate significantly. In Berglin-Brodal’s counter game
[
6
]
, a single move consists of shifting weight from a single counter that is close to the maximum weight to other counters. In a batch update, we have many vertices change and transfer potential.
We must either demonstrate that there exists a sequence of moves of their counter game that dominates a batch update, or find another method to bound potential movements.

(3)

Data structures that are trivial in the sequential setting, if naively adapted, become expensive in the parallel batch-dynamic setting, contributing an additional
O(\log n)
factor to the work. For the potential argument, we need a container for the out edges with FIFO-like behavior. Furthermore, we must maintain which vertices have high out-degree, so that we know from which vertices to flip edges.

(4)

In the
k
-flips algorithm, the choice of later edge flips strongly depends on the earlier flips, since the maximum out-degree vertex changes with each flip. This dependence is important in the analysis, as it implies that the maximum out-degree can worsen by at most
1
in a single call of
k
-flips, which is critical for the counter game.
However, because we must pick many edges to flip at once, we seemingly lose this precision and the ability to bound the amount by which the maximum out-degree can increase in a single time step.

We adapt the Berglin and Brodal
k
-flips algorithm
[
6
]
to the parallel batch-dynamic setting while ensuring good worst-case bounds.
We address these four issues as follows. Among them, Issue 4—i.e., bounding the worsening of the maximum out-degree—is the most challenging.

Resolving Issue 1: Choosing Which Edges to Flip.

An analog of flipping an edge from a max-degree vertex is flipping a set of edges from high-degree vertices. We capture this notion using a skyline.

Informally, we define a
skyline
to be a subset of edges of high out-degree vertices, where the number of edges contributed by each vertex increases with its out-degree.
A skyline is so named because, examining the bar graph of vertex out-degrees and highlighting a portion of the bar equal to the number of edges selected, the highlighted portion looks like the skyline of a city, as shown in Figure
2
.
In Sections
5
and
11
, we formally define skylines and show how to compute a skyline with good work and 0pt bounds.

We choose to flip skylines of edges and show that flipping an entire skyline of size
K
reduces the potential by
K\epsilon
, provided that the skyline is sufficiently high (Lemma
13.5
). We also show that statically orienting a skyline (which is flipping a subset of a skyline) does not harm the potential, in Lemma
13.6
.

Resolving Issue 2: Accumulation of Potential.

We bound the accumulation of potential over time by adapting Berglin and Brodal’s counter game
[
6
]
to the batch setting. We call this adaptation the
batch counter game
. This game involves
n
counters, where each counter
x
holds a non-negative real weight
u(x)
. The game is parameterized by a starting weight
Y
(initially
u(x)=Y
for all
x
) and a cap
H
. Given a current counter state
u
, a move in the game is defined by a threshold
T
and a new state
u^{\prime}
, denoted as
u\to_{T}u^{\prime}
, subject to the following rules:

(R1)

Counters cannot lose weight if their weight is below the threshold, and counters above the threshold cannot drop below it. Formally:
\forall x,u^{\prime}(x)\geq\min(u(x),T).

(R2)

The total weight of the counters cannot increase:
\sum_{x}u^{\prime}(x)\leq\sum_{x}u(x).

(R3)

No counter may exceed the threshold by more than the cap:
\forall x,u^{\prime}(x)\leq T+H.

We illustrate a valid move in Figure
3
. We say that
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{f})
is a valid game sequence if for each step
t\in[f-1]
, there exists some
T_{t}
such that
u_{t}\to_{T_{t}}u_{t+1}
. In Section
12
, we prove that during any valid game sequence, the maximum counter weight is bounded. Formally, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that all counters start at weight
Y>H
. The max counter weight during any valid game sequence is
O(Y+H\log n)
.

Figure 3:
Example of counter movements. Here,
T=10
and
H=3
. Note that the total amount taken from counters above
T
, which is 11, is less than the amount added to counters, which is 10. Note that all counters end up with weight under
T+H=13
, and that no counter below
T
loses weight.

A single move of our game offers far more possibilities than a single move in Berglin and Brodal’s game, leading to a more involved proof of the bounds of the counter game. However, giving a single move more power greatly simplifies our domination arguments, where we use the counter game to simulate the batch orientation algorithms. We give a more detailed comparison of the games in Section
12
.

Resolving Issue 3: Finding Efficient Data Structures.

We develop the first deterministic parallel batch-dynamic bag data structure that supports batch operations (append, delete from pointers, peek/pop, size) with constant work per item.
Note that this bag is unsorted, cannot efficiently determine if an element is present, and can only efficiently delete an element given a pointer to it.
This bag is trivial to maintain in the sequential setting (a singly linked-list would do), but nontrivial in the parallel batch-dynamic setting, and surprisingly has never been rigorously designed.
In Section
10
, we prove its bounds.

Lemma 2.4
.

There exists a deterministic bag that for a batch of
b
operations requires
O(b)
work and
O(\log b)
0pt.

Resolving Issue 4: Bounding the Maximum Out-Degree.

Because
k
-flips always flips away from a max-out degree vertex, their algorithm has a max out-degree increase of at most 1 per edge update. The analysis of the
k
-flips algorithm relies on this fact.
However, a batch insert, when edges are arbitrarily oriented, can dramatically increase the out-degree of a vertex.
For example, inserting a star can worsen the out-degree of the “center” of the star by up to
b
.
Furthermore, flipping the skyline can cause certain vertices, such as hubs with many in-edges, to have their out-degrees increase dramatically.

Because the batch counter game is the best tool we have to bound our out-degree, we are restricted in our choice of algorithm. We design an algorithm to match the batch counter game, rather than picking a seemingly efficient algorithm and then finding an analysis method that accompanies it.
We note that our algorithms are relatively simple, but proving the max out-degree bounds is involved because we must connect the algorithm to the counter game. We give two solutions to how we can limit the increase to the max out-degree. The first solution leads to a worse overall out-degree bound but is work-efficient. The second leads to the same
O(c+\log n)
orientation bound that Berglin and Brodal
[
6
]
achieves, but costs an additional
O(\log n)
factor in the work. With this intuition, we can now discuss our algorithms.

After the batch update, we first perform multiple skyline flips to reduce potential.

Technical Note: Dominating with the Counter Game.
To complete our proofs, we must argue that the batch counter game is powerful enough to simulate our batch-dynamic orientation algorithms. We say that a counter state
dominates
a graph if the weight of each counter is at least the potential of its corresponding vertex.
This analysis becomes significantly more complicated in the batch setting compared to Berglin and Brodal’s analysis because many vertices change out-degrees and because our algorithms, though still simple, are more complicated than

-Flips.

At a high level, this analysis proceeds as follows. We define a property of an algorithm subcall,
(H,T)

dominate the potentials of the graph orientations (Lemma
7.5
). We show that being dominated by the counter game actually leads to the max out-degree bound across an update sequence (Theorem
7.11
).
Finally, we show in Lemma

and Lemma

that all calls of the two-stage and reinsertion algorithms are either trivial or

We use the work-span (or work-depth) model for binary fork-join parallelism with atomics to analyze parallel algorithms
[

The model assumes a set of threads that share memory.
A thread can

two child threads that run in parallel.
When all children complete, the parent thread continues.
The

the

Computations can be executed using a randomized work-stealing

[
12
,
4
,
27
]
. The atomic operations Test and Set (TS) and Compare and Swap (CAS) are permitted.

There are several variants of work-span models, including the CRCW PRAM
[

]
. Importantly, however, most of
these models are robust with respect with each other in terms of work. In particular, it is possible cross simulate
the models while asymptotically preserving work, at least assuming randomization

26

.
The 0pt among models, however, can differ by a logarithmic factor (e.g., simulating n-way forking of a PRAM with binary forking requires a logarithmic increase in 0pt). We therefore state precise asymptotic bounds
for work, but focus just on polylogarithmic 0pt.

Parallel Batch-Dynamic Algorithms.

Our algorithms operate in the parallel batch-dynamic setting. In this setting, we have a sequence of
batch
updates
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{i}\}
(edge insertions/deletions) and a sequence of

undirected graphs

is empty, and each

We let

Similar to design goals for efficient
parallel algorithms, the gold standard for parallel batch-dynamic algorithms is
to design algorithms that are

the same work as the best sequential dynamic algorithms that processes the updates
in a batch one-at-a-time) and have low (ideally poly-logarithmic) 0pt in the
worst case.

Our adaptive adversary is able to see the out-degree orientation we have chosen after each time step, and may pick the next batch updates based on this knowledge. However, our adaptive adversary may not see random bits that we have not yet requested. This permits us to use a randomized semisort as a subroutine.

We use the notation

to mean
\{1,2,\ldots,x\}
.

6.1.2

The counter game concerns a

H

where each counter holds a non-negative real-valued weight. The rules of the game dictate how weight can be redistributed in relation to the cap. A

is given by a weight function

where

is the weight of counter
x_{i}
.

For a fixed threshold

-move to be a pair of states

that satisfies:

(R1)

u^{\prime}(i)\geq\min\{u(i),T\}

.

(R2)

\sum_{i}u(i)\geq\sum_{i}u^{\prime}(i)
.

(R3)

u^{\prime}(i)\leq T+H
for all
i\in[n]
.

If
(u,u^{\prime})
form a legal

-move, we denote this by
u\to_{T}u^{\prime}
. Moreover, we say that
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{f})
is a valid game sequence if for each step

, there exists some
T_{t}
such that

.

The following result, whose proof is deferred to Section

Theorem 6.4

Suppose that all counters start at weight

.

6.2
Technical Overview

In this section, we outline our strategy for bounding the maximum out-degree. We effectively reduce the problem of maintaining a low-degree orientation to the problem of constructing a corresponding valid game sequence in the counter game.

6.2.1
From domination to out-degree bounds

Fix any moment during the update sequence and consider the current orientation

\overline{G}
with potential function

and starting weight

\max_{x}d^{+}(x)=O\bigl(Y+H^{*}\log n+\beta\bigr).

Consequently, our main task is to maintain a valid dominating counter state while processing an online sequence of update batches.

6.2.2
Effect of edge updates

Let

. The application of

To restore the domination property while adhering to the counter game rules, our update algorithms must transform the orientation from potential

-Bounded Framework

The transition from

1.

Potential release:
The algorithm must perform flips to ensure that the total potential decreases by at least

2.

The algorithm must ensure that for any vertex

3.

Degree cap:
The final maximum out-degree must be bounded by

For an update procedure whose calls are

(Lemma

).

6.2.4
General Algorithm Structure.

Our primary tool for releasing potential is the

, as defined in Section
5

) and size

, we release at least
x\epsilon

). Both of our algorithms proceed in two logical stages: a

, where skylines are flipped to satisfy the potential release condition, and a

, where the graph is modified to satisfy the degree cap

We present two algorithms that trade off work for the tightness of the parameter

, which directly dictates the quality of the worst-case degree bound.

Algorithm 1: Two-Stage.

As indicated by the name, this algorithm procedures in two stages which naturally correspond to a potential release stage and degree reduction stage. In the first stage, we identify and flip a skyline of size
|B|\eta
. This releases sufficient potential to offset the batch update. However, flipping a skyline can drastically increase the maximum out-degree of the graph.

To enforce the degree cap, we perform a second stage which collects a second skyline of the same size. Instead of flipping the second skyline, we statically
3c
-orient it. In this way, we can ensures that the maximum out-degree is roughly bounded by
T_{2}+O(c)
. By setting

and taking

, this procedure satisfies the
(H,T)
-bounded definition.

Algorithm 2: Reinsertion.

To achieve a tighter bound, we must reduce the gap
H
between the skyline threshold
T
and the maximum out-degree. The
Two-Stage
algorithm is limited by the
3c

.

After performing skyline flips to release potential, we collect a final skyline and

-orient it. To strictly enforce the tighter cap

, we identify a “high subset” of edges
F

(H,T)
-bounded condition with

.

The removed edges

|F|\leq|B|(1-1/\log n)
), ensuring the recursion 0pt is polylogarithmic. This allows us to construct a chain of valid counter moves with a smaller cap, leading to an improved worst-case bound.

7
Domination Framework

The goal of this section is to establish a general framework for bounding the maximum out-degree of our dynamic orientation algorithms. We proceed as follows. First, in Section
7.1
, we formally define the notion of an
(H,T)
-bounded call. Then in Section
7.2
, we demonstrate that any
(H,T)
-bounded call corresponds to a legal move in the counter game while preserving a domination invariant. Building on this correspondence, Section
7.3
proves our main structural result: for any update procedure whose calls are either
(H,T)
-bounded or trivially produce orientations of maximum degree
O(\delta)
, the global out-degree is bounded by

\max_{x}d^{+}_{\overline{G}}(x)=O\left(\delta+\bigl(H+\delta\epsilon+1\bigr)\log n\right).

Finally, we apply this theorem to the Two-Stage Algorithm in Section
7.4
and the Reinsertion Algorithm in Section
7.5
.

Throughout this section, we fix an online update algorithm
A
and a sequence of update batches
\mathcal{G}=\{B_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
. Let
\overline{G}_{0}
be the empty orientation on
n
vertices and define
\overline{G}_{i}:=A(\overline{G}_{i-1},B_{i})
. Let
p_{i}
denote the vertex-potential function associated with
\overline{G}_{i}
. Recall
\beta:=6\delta\epsilon
and

\eta:=1+1/\epsilon+2\sigma
.

7.1
Preliminaries

We associate one counter with each vertex. For an orientation
\overline{G}
with potential function
p
, we say a counter state
u

dominates

p
if
u(v)\geq p(v)
for every vertex
v
. For a counter state
u
and a potential function
p
, define the vertexwise gaps

g^{+}(u,p)(v):=\max\{p(v)-u(v),0\},\qquad g^{-}(u,p)(v):=\max\{u(v)-p(v),0\},

and their totals

\operatorname{Gap}^{+}(u,p):=\sum_{v}g^{+}(u,p)(v),\qquad\operatorname{Gap}^{-}(u,p):=\sum_{v}g^{-}(u,p)(v).

For two potential functions
p,q
, define the vertexwise change and increase

\delta(p,q)(v):=q(v)-p(v),\qquad\delta^{+}(p,q)(v):=\max\{\delta(p,q)(v),0\},

and their totals

\Delta(p,q):=\sum_{v}\delta(p,q)(v),\qquad\Delta^{+}(p,q):=\sum_{v}\delta^{+}(p,q)(v).

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
u
dominate
p
. Then for any potential function
r
,

\operatorname{Gap}^{+}(u,r)\leq\Delta^{+}(p,r).

Proof.

Let
P:=\{v\in V:\ r(v)>u(v)\}
. Then

\operatorname{Gap}^{+}(u,r)=\sum_{v\in P}(r(v)-u(v))=\sum_{v\in P}\bigl(p(v)-u(v)\bigr)+\sum_{v\in P}\bigl(r(v)-p(v)\bigr).

Since
u
dominates
p
, the first sum is nonpositive. Therefore,

\operatorname{Gap}^{+}(u,r)\leq\sum_{v\in P}\delta^{+}(p,r)(v)\leq\Delta^{+}(p,r).

∎

8
Conclusion

In this paper we have presented three different algorithms for parallel batch-dynamic low out-degree orientation. Our first algorithm has optimal work bounds (at the cost of amortization and randomness). We provide two worst-case solutions, which either give a slightly worse out-degree bound or a slightly worse work bound. One interesting future work would be to explore low out-degree orientations in cases with adaptive arboricity (where the bounds are with respect to the current arboricity, not the overall maximum), as done in the sequential setting by Chekuri et al.
[
15
]
. It would also be interesting to explore different points on the tradeoff curve, for example if worst-case work-efficiency can be achieved for
O(c)
or
O(c+\log n)
orientation for sparse graphs.

We note that the counter game does not take into account the density constraint given by the arboricity of the graph. We believe it may be possible to improve the counter game by incorporating this constraint.

to a complete binary tree and back in
O(x)
work and
O(\log x)
0pt
[
33
]
.

To each node of the linked list representation of the skew number, we add a pointer to a complete binary tree and to the leftmost child of this tree.

Peek.
To peek from a skew bag, we sequential prefix sum to see the appropriate number of vertices. For all of the vertices except the largest one, we write out the elements into an array with the appropriate offset. For the largest vertex, it is possible that the associated tree is much larger than the peek value. Thus, we walk up the left spine of this final tree until finding a subtree sufficiently large. Then, we write out this subtree to an array.

Note that walking up the spine will take at most
O(\log x)
time for a peek of size
x
because we stop once we exceed the peek value, so this step is sufficiently inexpensive.

Append, Initial.
To insert to a skew bag (or during skewInit), to increment, one grabs an element from the batch and makes it the parent of two existing binary trees in the linked list. The leftmost pointer is set to be the leftmost pointer of the left child of the root. To insert a value
2^{i}-1
, this many elements are taken from the input array and formed into an independent complete binary tree, then inserted with the new bit. A flag is kept during the recursion to determine which node is the leftmost leaf.

Append, Optimized.
The above version of append adds a
O(\log x)
factor to the 0pt, because in each of
O(\log x)
while loop iterations,
O(\log x)
depth of creating a binary tree is spent. To reduce the 0pt down to
O(\log x)
, the creation of the binary tree is deferred until the end, where all binary trees are created at once. This leads to
O(\log x)+O(\log x)=O(\log x)
0pt.

Delete.
We keep a list of trees that we remove from the bag. Whenever we remove a single element (a root), we store this as a tree with one vertex. Thus, after running delete, we have a list of length
O(\log x)
of removed elements. Some of the elements we wish to delete (and some elements we did not want to delete) will have been removed from the bag, and some are still within the bag. We want to place the elements we accidentally removed into the holes created by the deleted elements still in the bag.

To do this, each deleted element marks that it is being deleted, and each removed element marks itself as having been removed. Then, filter the deleted pointers list to only include those not removed, and filter the removed elements list by those which should not have been deleted. Now we can map the elements we want to return into pointers of deleted elements still in the bag.

Our total work is
O(x)
, because we are removing and replacing
x
elements.

Note that when we split a tree, we do not know the leftmost child of the right subtree. Thus, we mark trees where we do not know the leftmost child. After the function run, we (in parallel) traverse the left spine (from the root, downward) to find the leftmost child. Since split calls occur on trees of halving size, the total traversal distance is
O(\log x+\log\frac{x}{2}+\ldots+1)=O(\sum_{i=1}^{\log{x}}\log{\frac{x}{2^{i}}})\leq O(\log^{2}x)
, which is much less than our base cost of
O(x)
. The depth of each tree is
O(\log x)
so the traversals can occur in
O(\log x)
0pt.

Thus we conclude Lemma
2.4
.

Technical Note: The RSL Special Bag.
Let
Z
be the max out-degree bound of an orientation algorithm. One may be concerned that taking a skyline when there are many vertices with max out-degree higher than
Z
would lead to

collecting an edge set that is not formally a skyline, because the special high bag of the RSL can have vertices with out-degree in range
[Z,n]
. However, this is not a concern, because every skyline we take will include all vertices in this special bag, and the maximum possible threshold is
Z
. Therefore we do not need to special case the skyline fetching function
GetDemands
.

In particular, we note that a single invocation of our algorithm only takes skylines of the same size through the call. Note that before the invocation, there are no vertices in the RSL special bag, bag
M+1
, which contains vertices with out-degree higher than
Z
. When a batch inserts
b
edges, this leads to vertices with collectively up to
b
weight crossing into this bag. Since all skylines taken have at least
b
weight, all of this weight is collected. Therefore, the threshold of this skyline flip is no more than the max degree. By Lemma
11.1
and Lemma

, we have that the weight after the flip above
Z
is no more than the size of the skyline. Therefore, all skylines taken will access all of the weight in the special bag.

14.1
Maximal Matching

Here we apply our batch-dynamic orientation algorithms to the problem of
maintaining a maximal matching. A
maximal matching

M
of a graph

G=(V,E)
is a set of edges such that no two edges in
M
share an endpoint,
and every edge in
E\setminus M
shares an endpoint with some edge in
M
.
The connection between low out-degree orientations and dynamic maximal
matching appears in many papers in the low-outdegree orientation
literature
[
35
,
40
]
. The key
idea is that a
c
-orientation allows each unmatched vertex to
efficiently propose matchings to a bounded number of neighbors,
enabling fast searches for “replacement” edges for the matching when
a matched edge is deleted.

Amortized Batch-Dynamic Maximal Matching.

Liu et al.
[
37
]
gave a parallel batch-dynamic
maximal matching algorithm based on their
(4+\epsilon)c
-orientation
algorithm. Their approach maintains, for each vertex
v
, a hash table

I_{v}
of unmatched in-neighbors (vertices
u
with an edge oriented
from
u\to v
where
u
is unmatched). On edge insertions, they run a
parallel static maximal matching
[
10
,
21
]
on the
subgraph induced by inserted edges between unmatched vertices. On edge
deletions that remove matched edges, they first attempt to rematch the
newly unmatched vertices with their out-neighbors, and then use a
doubling scheme to progressively query more in-neighbors until all
vertices are either matched or have no unmatched neighbors. The work
analysis requires a simple charging argument to amortize the cost of
unsuccessfully checking an in-neighbor that gets matched to another
vertex in the same batch.

Our improved orientation algorithm (Algorithm
1
) can
be directly plugged into this framework. The only change is replacing
their
(4+\epsilon)c
-orientation with our
7c
-orientation, which has
better work bounds. The matching algorithm itself and the analysis
remains unchanged.

Theorem 14.1
.

Given a graph
G
with arboricity at most
c
, there exists a parallel
batch-dynamic algorithm that maintains a maximal matching
M
of

G
. For a batch

of
b
edge updates, the algorithm runs in
amortized expected
O(b(c+\log n))
work and
O(\log^{2}n)

0pt whp.

Proof.

The proof follows the analysis of Liu et al.
[
37
]
,
substituting our orientation bounds. By Theorem
2.1
, our
orientation algorithm performs amortized
O(b\log n)
expected work per
batch. The matching maintenance costs
O(bc)
work per batch: processing
out-neighbors costs
O(c)
per newly unmatched vertex, and the cost of
scanning in-neighbors amortizes to
O(c)
per vertex that becomes matched
(since each matched in-neighbor has out-degree
O(c)
and is queried at most

O(c)
times before becoming matched). The static maximal matching subroutine

O(\log^{2}n)

Explicit Coloring via Palettes.

Ghaffari and Koo
[
23
]
give a simple palette-based approach for
maintaining an explicit
O(c\log n)
-coloring of an input graph with arboricity
c
.
At initialization, each vertex

v
is assigned a random
palette

\mathcal{C}(v)
: each color from a pool
of
C=O(c\log n)
colors is included in
\mathcal{C}(v)
independently with
probability
\frac{1}{2c}
, giving each vertex
\Theta(\log n)
colors whp. Given a low out-degree orientation where each vertex has at most

O(c)
out-neighbors, each vertex
v
selects any color from its palette that
does not appear in any out-neighbor’s palette. Since each color appears in

\mathcal{C}(v)
with probability
\frac{1}{2c}
and is absent from each
out-neighbor’s palette with probability
1-\frac{1}{2c}
, the probability that
a given color is “good” (present in
\mathcal{C}(v)
and absent from all
O(c)

out-neighbors’ palettes) is
\Omega(1/c)
. With
\Theta(c\log n)
colors in the
pool, at least one good color exists whp. This algorithm assumes an oblivious adversary
since the palettes are fixed at initialization and do not change throughout the algorithm’s run.

When edges are inserted or deleted, only vertices whose out-neighbors change
must recompute their colors. To recolor a vertex
v
, we check each color in

\mathcal{C}(v)
against the palettes of
v
’s
O(c)
out-neighbors; checking a
single color takes
O(c)
work and
O(\log n)
0pt (each palette is stored in a hash table),
and checking all
O(\log n)
colors in
\mathcal{C}(v)
takes
O(c\log n)
work
and
O(\log n)
0pt in parallel.
