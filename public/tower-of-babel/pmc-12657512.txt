Advanced oxidation of ethylene and propylene glycol in wastewater using hydrodynamic cavitation-activated persulfate: a kinetic and techno-economic study
Authors: N Santosh Srinivas, K Kishore Ramanan, John Bosco Balaguru Rayappan, Gautham B Jegadeesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, the activation of persulfate (PS) using a vortex diode-based hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) system for the degradation of ethylene glycol (EG) and propylene glycol (PG) in aqueous media was evaluated. Glycol degradation in a PS activated system was determined under varying glycol concentrations (100–1000 mg/L), PS-to-glycol molar ratios (0.06–3.04), and operating temperatures (25–55 °C). Degradation kinetics followed a first-order model, with the per-pass rate constant for PG at temperatures below 30 °C calculated at 2.7 × 10−4 per pass, while EG showed negligible degradation under similar conditions. At elevated temperatures (> 55 °C), the rate constants increased to 6.02 × 10−4 per pass for EG and 3.9 × 10−4 per pass for PG, indicating the strong thermal enhancement synergistic with cavitational effects. HC/PS system demonstrated almost twofold higher cavitation yield and faster treatment time for 90% degradation compared to electrical heating/PS system. Complete degradation (> 90%) required a PS: glycol ratio greater than 4. The degradation intermediates identified included formic acid, acetic acid, propanoic acid, and hydroxy-acetaldehyde, suggesting sulfate and hydroxyl radical-driven pathways. Techno-economic analysis revealed that HC/PS used significantly less energy (233.4–645.9 kWh/m3) than electrical heating/PS (718.9–1043 kWh/m3), indicating its superior energy efficiency. The operational cost including oxidant costs for glycol degradation using HC/PS system was found to be 3.55 times more economical than conventional thermal activation, with a threefold reduction in treatment time (6 h vs. 18 h for 90% degradation). These findings establish HC as a scalable, energy-efficient, and cost-effective method for the oxidative treatment of glycol-rich wastewater streams.Keywords: Hydrodynamic cavitation, Ethylene glycol, Propylene glycol, Persulfate, Vortex diodeSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Natural and anthropogenic impacts have depleted usable water sources, both in quality and quantity1,2. Among the various organic pollutants detected in water, propylene glycol (PG) and ethylene glycol (EG) have emerged as significant environmental concerns3–6. These glycols are extensively used in industrial applications, including the production of antifreeze, aviation de-icing fluids, and as chemical solvents. Glycols are classified as high production volume (HPV) chemicals, with projected compound annual growth rates (CAGR) of 6.1% for ethylene glycol and 5.3% for propylene glycol in the U.S. market between 2023 and 2030. Due to their widespread production and use, these compounds can enter aquatic environments through industrial effluents, accidental spills, and improper disposal4,5. The concentration of PG and EG in industrial wastewater can vary significantly, depending on the specific source and the nature of the industrial process involved5,7. EG concentrations in untreated wastewater from production plants can average between 1300 and 1800 mg/L, while airport run-off where glycols are used extensively for de-icing has shown levels as high as 19,000 mg/L8,9. In streams near airports, ethylene glycol concentrations have ranged from as low as 2 mg/L up to 3100 mg/L, with occasional peaks observed to be much higher10. Propylene glycol-based deicing fluids in wastewater have been reported at about 7% (70,000 mg/L) in rainwater. Overall, EG and PG releases can be substantial, with an estimated 0.5–1 million pounds of glycols discharged annually to surface waters in the United States10. These high concentrations can exceed the thresholds for effective biological treatment, which is generally limited to 1000 mg/L for EG and PG in activated sludge systems. If not properly managed, this level of contamination can lead to treatment inefficiencies, oxygen depletion in aquatic ecosystems, and inhibition of microbial activity.

Both PG and EG are water-soluble and can be cause severe environmental and health effects4. Ethylene glycol, in particular, poses a severe threat due to its high toxicity and persistence11. Once in aquatic systems, it breaks down into glycolic acid and oxalic acid, which has been linked to fish kills and disruption of aquatic ecosystems12,13. Propylene glycol, although less harmful, can contribute to reduction in biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and eutrophication in water bodies. Acute exposure of both chemicals can lead to symptoms like nausea, vomiting, kidney damage and central nervous system depression14. Their presence in aquatic systems for long durations can disrupt the physiological behavior of humans and animals. Unfortunately, conventional activated sludge process fails to effectively degrade glycols due to elevated glycol and salt concentrations, necessitating the development of advanced and sustainable treatment technologies3,7,13.

In recent years, more effective technologies such as TiO2-assisted photocatalysis, UV, UV/Fenton’s reaction, UV/H2O2 and cavitation-based methods are being used for degrading organic contaminants such as dyes, pharmaceutical compounds and glycols7,15–21. Using a persulfate/UV combination, ethylene glycol (EG) at a concentration of 1500 mg/L achieved a degradation efficiency of 85.7%, which was nearly twice as effective as the degradation observed with the H2O2/UV combination16. Photocatalytic degradation of EG and PG using P-25 TiO2 under acidic pH conditions has shown efficiencies exceeding 90%7. Wang et al.22 reported 99.1% conversion of PG and 88.4% COD removal using Fenton’s reagent in a semi-continuous reactor. Near-complete degradation of EG was also observed using photo-Fenton processes18. However, these methods often require high doses of oxidants such as H2O2, persulfate (PS), or permanganates, as well as activators like UV light or metal catalysts, which can increase operational costs and complexity23,24.

One method to activate oxidant radicals for advanced oxidation processes (AOP) is the use of hydrodynamic cavitation (HC). The efficacy of HC in enabling the degradation of recalcitrant compounds has been well established23,25–29. When an organic pollutant contaminated water stream passes through a hydrodynamic cavitation device, the intense local conditions (high temperatures (up to 5000 K) and pressures (up to 1000 atm)) are created by bubble collapse, facilitating the oxidation of organics into smaller intermediates, which can further be degraded into carbon dioxide and water26,30,31. The scalability of hydrodynamic cavitation systems also allows for their integration into existing wastewater treatment infrastructure, making them a practical solution for industries seeking to comply with stringent environmental regulations26,30,32,33. The addition of oxidizing agents such as hydrogen peroxide and persulfate (PS) amplifies this process by generating additional radicals, ensuring complete mineralization25. Thermal activation of persulfate is known to be the driving force for the generation of sulphate (SO•−) and hydroxyl radicals (OH•)421,34–36. Research has shown that thermal activation of persulfate occurred at temperature > 30 °C, which breaks the O–O bond required for the generation of SO4•− and OH• radicals21,35,36. Because of this activation process, the oxidant requirement is reduced significantly. A comparative analysis of Bisphenol-A degradation using thermal activation of persulfate (PS) showed that HC-assisted activation required the lowest amount of the oxidant (44 µM of PS), when compared to catalyst-based, electrical heating based, UV light-based and ultrasonic based activation (> 100 µM of PS)36. HC assisted activation of PS resulted in the doubling of PAH degradation, when compared to other activation methods19,37. Furthermore, hydrodynamic cavitation enhances mass transfer and mixing, improving the contact between oxidizing radicals and organic moieties25,26,38,39. This synergistic approach leverages the oxidative power of radicals generated by both cavitation and the selected AOP, enabling the effective treatment of complex wastewater streams.

In our previous study30, we utilized a vortex-diode based HC unit to determine the optimal conditions for maximizing the radical yield with high per pass rate. We reported maximum sulphate (SO4•−) radical generation at a pressure drop of 250 kPa, and use of persulfate ions at 10 mM concentration30. Similar results were obtained using aniline, acetone and other hydrocarbons. Building upon this foundation, the current study investigates the application of HC-assisted persulfate activation for the degradation of ethylene glycol (EG) and propylene glycol (PG) present in industrial wastewater streams. Simulated solution of EG and PG at concentrations commonly observed in industrial wastewater was used for the study. The effect of process parameters such as oxidant: glycol ratio, temperature and total glycol load on degradation rates was evaluated. In addition to the parametric evaluation, a comprehensive techno-economic analysis is performed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness and scalability of the HC-activated persulfate process for industrial implementation. Finally, degradation pathway is postulated by identifying intermediate by-products, enabling a mechanistic understanding of glycol degradation under HC-assisted persulfate oxidation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Propylene glycol AR (CAS No: 57-55-6, 99.5% purity, SRL Chem, India), ethylene glycol AR (CAS No: 107-21-1; > 99% purity, Avra Synthesis, India), sodium persulfate (Na2S2O8, CAS No: 7775-27-1, 98% reagent grade, Spectrum, India), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98% purity, CAS No: 1310-73-2), potassium peroxy monosulfate (CAS No: 70693-62-8, > 98% purity, Spectrum, India), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, ACS reagent, CAS No: 7647-01-0; SRL Chem, India) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, CAS No: 7722-84-1, 30% w/w, Avra Synthesis, India) were used as-received, without any additional purification. Double distilled water was used throughout the study.

Experimental work

For the hydrodynamic cavitation, a vortex diode-based HC (Vivira Process Technologies, India) equipped with 5 L reaction vessel was used27,30,33,40. The schematic of the HC equipment is shown in Fig. 1. The fluid flow across the vortex diode—based HC unit has been well reported in our previous study, as well as other studies30,33,40. The fluid was pumped across the cavitating device using 0.75 HP CNP make CHL 2–40 with 30 m pressure head at a volumetric flow rate of 2 m3/hr. The pressure across the cavitation device was maintained at 250 kPa, and most reactions were performed for a duration of 6 h. The reaction temperature was monitored using a temperature probe (Baumer, 1 °C resolution) and pH was monitored Systronics MKVI pH electrode.

Schematic of the vortex—diode hydrodynamic cavitation unit26,29.

Experiments were first performed to evaluate the efficacy of 3 oxidants, namely hydrogen peroxide, sodium persulfate (PS) and potassium peroxy monosulfate (PMS) on EG and PG degradation. In our previous study, we had noted that sulfate radicals generated using PS provided the maximum cavitation yield30. Solution containing 1000 mg/L EG and PG was passed through the HC device using 10 mM oxidant. The initial pH of the solution was 7.4 ± 0.5, and the pH of the solution was monitored during the experiment. The data revealed that PS was the best among those investigated. PMS and hydrogen peroxide gave negligible EG and PG degradation, and hence were not considered for further studies. Experiments were then performed to evaluate effect of PS: glycol ratio using glycol concentration of 250–1000 mg/L and PS concentration (0–60 mM) on percent degradation and degradation rates. Additionally, the effect of total glycol load (EG and PG combined) on percent degradation was also determined.

There has been a lot of discussion on the thermal activation of PS using various methods such as electrical, ultrasound, hydrodynamic cavitation, microwave, and UV light, the fundamental principle being breaking of the O–O bond in the structure of PS thermally to form SO4•− radicals35,36. Activation of persulfate to form persulfate radicals has been observed to occur at temperatures > 30 °C, even with electrical or UV-based heating20,36. However, the efficiency of the various thermal activation methods depends on the contaminant type, oxidant used, and the process parameters20. Unfortunately, there is very limited information on the comparison of thermal activation methods with respect to a specific contaminant. It is our postulation that the thermal activation of PS occurring due to increase in fluid temperature in a HC unit is a result of either: (1) the collapse of cavities which creates extreme conditions at localized areas with high temperature; or (2) transfer of heat due to the friction between the pump’s moving parts and the fluid. Hence, we evaluated the extent of persulfate activation in two different modes: (1) fluid flowing through the HC unit under non-isothermal conditions wherein fluid temperature was allowed to increase without external cooling; (2) fluid flowing through the HC unit under isothermal conditions wherein a chiller was used to cool the liquid in the reactor (temperature of flowing fluid maintained at 23 ± 4 °C). Additionally, we evaluated PS activation via electrical heating of the EG/PG contaminated solution in a non-HC set-up (absence of cavities). In the non-isothermal set-up, since the fluid was not cooled, the temperature of the fluid was increased to almost 55 ± 2 °C. The increase was observed to occur within 3 h of the experiment. Post—3 h, the temperature of the fluid stabilized at 55 ± 2 °C. For electrical heating based thermal activation, a solution containing EG or PG at a pre-determined concentration was mixed with PS of a pre-determined concentration in a shaker-flask apparatus, and heated electrically till the solution reached 55 ± 5 °C. Here again, the temperature increased gradually to 55 °C in about 3 h.

Analysis

The determination of PG and EG was performed using Shimadzu HPLC equipped with LC 20AD pump, SIL-20A autosampler and RID-20A refractive index detector. The column oven was maintained at 30 °C. Deionized water (~ 18.3 MΩ) was used as mobile phase and 1 mL/min flow rate was passed through Shimpack solar C18 column. The samples were not subjected to any derivatization protocols prior to analysis. The solutions were prepared prior to the priming of the pump to maintain homogeneity of all the constituents. The experimental samples were filtered with 0.22 µ syringe filter prior to HPLC analysis. For identification of the intermediates and by-products, samples were analyzed using Shimadzu GC–MS QP2010 SE with Single Quade Mass Spectrometer. The sample was diluted in equivalent volumes of Acetonitrile and GC–MS was performed using TG-WaxMS (30 m × 0.22 mm X 0.25 µm). The column flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min and the oven temperature was maintained at 100 ºC for 1 min, and ramped to 250 °C (10 °C/min) and held for 4 min. The mass spectrometer scan parameters were monitored from 25–500 amu41. The reaction products were identified through NIST mass spectral library.

Results and discussion

We first evaluated EG and PG degradation in the absence of any oxidant under cavitating conditions. The pH was varied from 3 to 11, and the pressure drop across the vortex diode was 250 kPa. There was no reportable change in the EG and PG concentration across various pH (3–11) over 6 h. This suggested that in the absence of the oxidant, EG and PG does not undergo thermally—induced or cavitation—induced degradation. We even evaluated the option of external aeration to the system via an aerator, which pumped in air at the pre-determined flow rate. The results were the same, and no appreciable degradation was observed within the given reaction time.

Effect of oxidant: glycol ratio on degradation

Figure 2a shows the kinetic plot for EG degradation over time using PS as the oxidant (1–10 mM), and a pressure drop of 250 kPa across the HC device. The EG concentration ranged from 250 to 1000 mg/L, which converted to PS: PG molar ratio of 0.06–2.48 (Table 1). The temperature increased from an initial value of 30 ± 2 to 55 ± 2 °C at the end of the process. The percent degradation and first—order degradation rate constant (per-pass constant) is tabulated in Table 1. Increasing PS: EG ratio increased degradation rates. When the PS: EG ratio is 0.06, degradation was only 4%. When the ratio was increased to 0.62 (EG = 1000 mg/L (16.12 mM) and PS = 10 mM), percent degradation increased to 25%. Upon decreasing the initial EG concentration to 250 mg/L (4.03 mM) while keeping the same PS concentration (10 mM), percent degradation increased further to 84%. It was also observed that the degradation curves did not reach a plateau, indicating that extending the reaction time could further enhance the overall percentage of glycol degradation. Figure 2b shows the kinetic plot for PG degradation over time using PS as the oxidant and a pressure drop of 250 kPa across the HC device. The PG concentration ranged from 250 to 1000 mg/L and the concentration of PS was varied from 1 to 10 mM. This converted to a PS: PG molar ratio varying from 0.08 to 3.04 (Table 1). Expectedly, increasing the oxidant amount (i.e., PS: PG molar ratio) increased percent degradation and degradation rate. When 1000 mg/L (13.14 mM) PG solution was treated using 1 mM PS using the HC unit, the degradation observed was only 2%, and lower than the 4% degradation observed for EG. Upon increasing the PS concentration to 10 mM, PG degradation increased to 19%. For lower initial PG concentration (500 & 250 mg/L) and 10 mM PS (PS: glycol ratio of 1.52–3.05), the % degradation increased to 39% and 83%, respectively. Similar to EG kinetics, degradation curves did not plateau, and hence longer reaction time is required to achieve higher PG degradation. It was also observed that EG percent degradation was higher PG percent degradation. EG has more accessible and less sterically hindered C–H and O–H bonds, which are more susceptible to attack by hydroxyl (•OH) and sulfate (SO4•−) radicals. In contrast, PG’s secondary alcohol structure is slightly more resistant to radical attack due to steric hindrance from the methyl group.

The kinetic data fitted well to the first—order equation (dashed line in Fig. 2a, b, R2 > 0.90) and the per—pass rate constants were determined. As seen in Table 1, per—pass rate constant for EG degradation ranged from 0.80 × 10–4 to 40.3 × 10–4 per pass−1. The per—pass rate constant for PG ranged from 0.88 × 10–4 to 32.9 × 10–4 per pass−1 over the entire range of PS: PG molar ratios. Increasing the oxidant amount 10 times increased the per—pass rate constant by over 40–50 times, clearly suggesting that more oxidant amounts are required for faster and complete degradation. Equations 3–6 show the possible chemical reactions occurring in the process that would result in the mineralization of the glycols via the formation of the intermediates21,35. Persulfate ion (S2O8−) converts to sulphate radicals (SO4•−), during the cavitation process19–21,26,30,35,42. In addition to the persulfate radicals, hydroxyl radicals (OH•) will also be formed. When the sulphate (SO4•−) and hydroxyl (OH•) radicals attacks the organic molecule, the glycols can decompose to its by-products (Eqs. 5, 6).

Ideally, 1 mol of PG or EG would require 1 mol of the persulfate for complete mineralization according to Eqs. 5, 6. However, for most organic pollutants, the ratio of PS to contaminant tested was 4–700, with enhanced degradation (> 80%) observed only when PS: contaminant ratio was > 5021,35,36,43,44. Nie et al.34 reported that upon increasing the PS to chloramphenicol ratio from 1:1 to 80:1, percent degradation increased from 10.7 to 90.1%. Similar observations were made for Congo red and methylene blue dyes21, BTEX36, and other pharmaceutical compounds36. In comparison, this study showed that if PS: glycol ratio of > 2.5, more than 80% degradation can be achieved for a given PG/EG concentration within the given reaction time.

The extent of glycol degradation is expected to be dependent on the total glycol load in the wastewater. In most industrial wastewater streams, both EG and PG, along with acetaldehyde and formaldehyde can be present12,45. We investigated the percent degradation of EG and PG in a solution containing both organic moieties to see if their combined presence affected overall degradation. Figure 3 and Table 1 presents the data for these experiments. When both species are present in equal amounts (250 mg/L) totaling to a glycol load of 500 mg/L, and 10 mM PS was used (PS: glycol ratio of 1.36), the percent degradation was 37% for EG and 45% for PG. At about the similar PS: glycol ratio of 1.52–1.54, the percent degradation was 56% for EG and 39% for PG (Table 1). The data clearly showed that while PG degradation decreased by 6%, percent EG degradation reduced significantly (by 19%), suggesting that the absence of enough amount of SO4•− radicals impairs degradation rates and efficiencies. Upon increasing the oxidant load to 60 mM and using a mixed solution 1000 mg/L EG and 1000 mg/L PG (PS: glycol ratio = 2.05), percent degradation was still only 23% for EG and 39% for PG (Table 1). The data here strongly suggests that for enhanced degradation of the glycols (> 90%), the PS: glycol ratio has to be 4 or higher. In the combined glycol system, the more pronounced decrease in EG degradation compared to PG can be attributed to competitive interactions for SO4•− radicals. In isolated system, wherein EG is the only organic moiety, the EG molecule appeared more reactive with SO4•− radical. However, in the presence of PG in the system, PG appeared to act as a stronger initial scavenger or react faster with SO4•−, thereby decreasing the available of sulphate radical for EG degradation. This competition disproportionately affected EG, as its degradation appears to rely more heavily on radical availability. Additionally, structural differences between EG and PG such as EG’s two primary hydroxyl groups versus PG’s combination of primary and secondary hydroxyls may also influence their respective reaction pathways and sensitivity to radical concentration changes.

Effect of temperature on glycol degradation

As temperature increases, the vapor pressure of the liquid rises, reducing the pressure differential required for bubble formation and thus lowering the cavitation inception threshold46. This means cavitation is more likely to occur at higher temperatures, which accelerates the generation of SO4•− radicals. However, elevated temperatures also reduce the density and viscosity of the liquid, which can affect bubble dynamics, growth, and collapse26,27. The energy released during bubble collapse may decrease at very high temperatures due to reduced thermal conductivity and damping effects, potentially diminishing cavitation intensity27. The degradation rate can reduce with increase in temperature beyond a critical value. The general consensus is that increasing the temperature beyond 30 °C is essential for the thermal activation of persulfate and generation of SO4•− radicals35,36. However, in the studies conducted using vortex diode—based HC systems without the use of PS as oxidant, the fluid temperature was mostly maintained at 18 ± 4 °C27,40,47,48. In most of these studies, degradation of the organic pollutants was observed even in the absence of any additional oxidant27,47. Therefore, we wanted to evaluate whether operating the vortex diode–based HC unit below 30 °C could still effectively degrade glycols in the presence of PS as the oxidant. This approach represents a process intensification strategy, wherein the use of persulfate as a sulfate radical source enhances oxidative degradation under low-temperature, energy-efficient conditions.

Figure 4 presents the degradation kinetics of PG and EG under isothermal conditions. Since a chiller was used to cool the flowing fluid through the cavitation device, the temperature did not change much, suggesting isothermal conditions (23 ± 4 °C). When operated under non-isothermal conditions, the temperature increased to about 50 °C till about 80–100 passes. Beyond the region of 100 passes, the system exhibited more or less the same temperature (Fig. 4), similar to the observation made in other studies27,40,49. Under non-isothermal conditions, EG reduced from 1000 mg/L (16.12 mM) to 737 mg/L (11.87 mM), a 25% degradation (Table 1). Ethylene glycol degradation under isothermal conditions was negligible (< 1%), hence not shown in Fig. 4. PG degraded 19% using 10 mM PS under non-isothermal conditions (Fig. 2). However, under isothermal conditions where temperature of the flowing fluid never exceeded 30 °C, PG degradation was significantly lower (11%), clearly demonstrating the temperature effect on PS activation (Fig. 4, Table 2). The initial rate of degradation was low. However, beyond 100 passes, as the temperature increased to > 40 °C, increased degradation rates was observed. The rate constant for PG degradation under isothermal conditions was considerably lower (2.7 × 10–4 per pass−1, Table 2), when compared to that observed at 55 °C (3.92 × 10–4 per pass−1). The degradation rate of EG under non-isothermal conditions was determined to be 6.02 × 10–4 per pass−1 (Table 2). The increase in degradation rates with temperature observed here were in agreement with studies reported earlier34,44,50,51. Upon increase in temperature from 50 to 80 °C, chloramphenicol degradation rate increased almost 24 times50. Ciprofloxacin degradation rate increases almost 5-times when temperature of PS-activation increased from 35 to 60 °C51. Degradation rates of 59 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) using thermally activated persulfate increased between 7 and 20 times when temperature increased from 20 to 40 °C44. This goes to show that for effective glycol degradation using thermally activated PS, temperature of 40 °C or higher is required.

Effect of persulfate activation mode on glycol degradation

The mode of thermal activation of PS not only determines the efficacy of SO4•− radical generation, but also the overall economics of the process. The efficiency of the various thermal activation methods depends on the contaminant type, oxidant used, and the process parameters20,35,36,52. Unfortunately, there is very limited information on the comparison of thermal activation methods with respect to a specific contaminant. Hence, we evaluated the thermal activation of PS using an electrical heating (non-HC) method (Fig. 5), and compared it to results from the HC-assisted PS activation method (Fig. 2). 250 mg/L of EG and PG was treated with 10 mM PS in an electrically heated flask (non-HC) for a duration of 6 h. In this non-HC thermal method, the initial degradation was slow as the temperature of the fluid took about 100 min to reach 50 °C. Upon attaining the required temperature of > 50 °C, the degradation was observed to be rapid (Fig. 5). The first-order per pass rate constant was found to be 13.0 × 10–4 min−1 for PG and 6.68 × 10–4 min−1 for EG (Table 2). The percent degradation for PG and EG was also lower, 46% and 49%, respectively (Table 2). In comparison, the percent degradation of PG and EG in the HC-assisted thermal activation of PS was 83% and 84%, respectively, almost twice as high. This clearly indicates the higher efficacy of HC over electrical heating in the thermal activation of PS and generation of SO4•− radicals. The main concern over the thermal activation of PS has been its capital and operating costs. This comparative result here would suggest that use of HC in activating persulfate might be more cost-effective compared to other methods.

Reaction pathway and mechanism

Identification of intermediates and by-products of the degradation reaction is essential in understanding the mechanism of the process. We analyzed a HC/PS activated EG/PG treated sample and a non-HC (thermal)/PS activated EG/PG treated sample via GC–MS. This was done to determine the possibility of different reaction pathways. The GC–MS analysis of HC/PS treated EG/PG samples were found to majorly contain residual EG and PG, formic acid, acetic acid, hydroxy-acetaldehyde (also known as glycolaldehyde), and propionic acid in minor amounts (Table 3). The intermediates identified are low in content and considered mostly non-toxic22. The reaction intermediates from the electric heating (non-HC)/PS treated EG/PG process were acetic acid, hydroxy-acetaldehyde, propionic acid, 1-hydroxypropan-2-one, and unreacted EG and PG. Lactic acid was also detected in minor quantities (Table 3).

Irrespective of the activation mode, persulfate dissociates to form SO4•−, which then reacts with water to form OH• radicals (Eqs. 5–6). As a result, both sulfate (SO4•−) and hydroxyl (•OH) radicals actively contribute to oxidative transformations such as C–C, C–O, and C–H bond cleavage, as well as hydrogen abstraction16,36,53. Wang et al.22 reported that the intermediates formed during Fenton’s reaction with PG were aldehyde, acetone, methanol, formic acid, and acetic acid. It was also noted that formaldehyde was not observed in the GC–MS analysis, as it may have oxidized to oxalic acid22. Fazliev et al.54 noted that the main products of EG H2O2/UV-C photolysis were glycolaldehyde, acetaldehyde, formaldehyde, and formic acid. The C–C bond cleavage results in forming formaldehyde and formic acid. The presence of acetic, formic and oxalic acid as the major intermediates in a EG degraded sample was also reported in other studies16,55,56. Glycolaldehyde is formed through the deprotonation of ethylene glycol (EG) by reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydroxyl (HO˙) or sulfate (SO4•−) radicals16,57. Certain intermediates such as acetaldehyde hydroxy (25.7%), acetic acid (18.82%), and formic acid (10.82%) are detected in significant amounts in the HC/PS system. Given that these intermediates are significantly lower or not detected at all in the electrical heating/PS system suggests enhanced oxidation and breakdown of intermediates under cavitation. Additionally, the presence of compounds like 2-propanone-1-hydroxy (5.56%) and 2-buten-1-ol (2.56%) in the non-HC system indicates incomplete or alternative degradation routes. Furthermore, the higher percent of PG and EG in the electrical heating/PS system (33.9% and 35.71%, respectively) compared to the HC/PS system (21.02% and 22.26%) implies that HC facilitates higher oxidant yield, resulting in the oxidation of the diols into lower molecular weight acids and aldehydes. Overall, the HC-assisted system appears to favor more extensive degradation and oxidation, yielding a broader range of oxidized intermediates, consistent with higher radical generation through cavitation.

Figure 6a, b show the possible reaction pathways for EG and PG degradation. The degradation of EG is due to oxidative attack-initiated C–C cleavage resulting in the formation of formaldehyde and acetaldehyde (Fig. 6a). Acetaldehyde upon secondary hydroxyl radical mediated oxidation either forms 2-hydroxy acetaldehyde or undergoes direct oxidation to acetic acid, commonly known as the aldehyde-water shift reaction58. Since, formaldehyde was not detected in the GC–MS analysis, it is hypothesized that either all of the formaldehyde was oxidized to non-volatile formic acid59,60 or evaporation of formaldehyde vapors occurred during the process (as the boiling point of formaldehyde is − 19.1 °C). Other side products like propionic acid were also detected in the samples, which could be attributed to methyl substitution and cross reaction of unreacted ethylene glycol with oxidative products16. The initial C–C cleavage due to OH• radical attack on PG results in the conversion of the parent molecule to acetaldehyde and 2-hydroxy propanone (Fig. 6b), similar to those reported in literature22. Further conversion of acetaldehyde to 2-hydroxy acetaldehyde and acetic acid was due to hydroxylation and oxidative conversion, most likely due to OH• radical attack. The presence of propionic and lactic acid in minor amounts is most likely due to the sequential hydrogen abstraction and C–O cleavage of PG61. It is to note that aldehyde and hemi-acetal intermediates were present comparatively lower to its acid compatriots, suggesting the overall safety and reduced environmental risk of the process23. The detection of intermediates like hydroxy-acetaldehyde strongly indicates •OH-mediated oxidation pathway, rather than a SO4•− mediated pathway.

Figure 7 compares oxidant radical yields during glycol degradation under varying oxidant: glycol ratios operated under hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) and also for a specific oxidant: glycol ratio in an electric (thermal) non-HC/PS system. As the oxidant: glycol ratio increases, there is a rise in radical yield expectedly, indicating enhanced sulfate radical generation due to greater persulfate availability. However, the HC/PS system demonstrates a markedly higher yield at lower O:G ratios, particularly peaking at intermediate loadings, suggesting that cavitation more efficiently activates persulfate into reactive radicals. Notably, the optimal oxidant radical yield was observed at a PS:EG ratio of 1.24 and at 3.04 for PG (Fig. 7). Interestingly, a further increase in oxidant loading to a 2.48 ratio for EG resulted in reduced radical yield, suggesting possible attenuation or recombination of SO4•− radicals at higher concentrations. This trend indicates the importance of carefully balancing oxidant dose with organic load to avoid diminishing returns in radical generation. The oxidant radical yield for PG was consistently higher at optimal conditions, likely due to the more complex oxidation pathways involving hydrogen abstraction from both the methyl group and the α-position of the hydroxyl moiety. The thermally activated PS system showed lower oxidant yield at the same oxidant: glycol ratio when compared to HC, implying a higher activation energy requirement and potential inefficiencies in radical formation. Overall, the HC method outperforms thermal activation by achieving higher radical yields at lower oxidant doses, improving process efficiency.

The cost analysis was done using the oxidant: glycol ratio at the most optimal conditions. In the hydrodynamic cavitation (HC) system, the primary energy demand arises from the single-stage centrifugal pump, with a power dissipation of 21.76 W L−1, determined based on the temperature gradient (ΔT/Δt) at a pressure drop of 250 kPa. The average cost of persulphate and electricity cost (Rs. 7.65/kW) was used to calculate the overall treatment costs. The estimated treatment time to achieve 90% degradation was used to evaluate the operational costs. HC/PS achieved significantly higher cavitation yields and reduced treatment times, with EG reaching 90% degradation in just 6 h compared to 17.94 h under thermal activation. The analysis revealed that the operational cost of HC-assisted degradation was approximately 3.55 times lower than that of thermal activation (Table 4). This significant reduction in treatment time is attributed to the enhanced conversion of SO4•− radicals to OH• radicals and an optimized oxidant-to-pollutant ratio. While the additive cost (persulfate) remained constant across all scenarios, the overall process and total treatment costs were considerably lower for HC/PS. For EG treatment, the total cost dropped from INR 8,627.87/m3 (thermal) to INR 2,434.63/m3 (HC/PS), demonstrating a 3.5-fold reduction. Comparable trends were also observed for propylene glycol degradation. These results clearly illustrate the economic and operational superiority of HC-assisted activation, making it a more viable and sustainable option for industrial wastewater treatment involving recalcitrant glycols.

The environmental impact of the process was determined using an ECOSAR analysis which helps ascertain the toxicity (LC50) of compounds before and after degradation62. The degradation compounds identified via GC–MS analysis for Ethylene and propylene glycol were used to perform ECOSAR analysis. LC50 value of the compounds were tested against the representative aquatic and earthworm species, indicators for assessing the environmental impact of the process. The percentage change in LC50 with respect to the parent compound has been represented in Fig. 8. The percent change in LC50 values for EG degradation products indicates a significant increase in toxicity compared to the parent compound. Notably, oxidative byproducts such as formic acid, acetic acid, and propanoic acid exhibit higher toxicity toward aquatic organisms, posing greater environmental risks than EG itself. In contrast, the degradation products of PG show a comparatively lesser increase in toxicity, with LC50 values for fish, Daphnids, Mysids, and earthworms indicating relatively lower ecological harm. This suggests that oxidative metabolites of PG are more environmentally benign upon release into aquatic ecosystems. Given these observations, it is essential to optimize the degradation process to minimize the formation of toxic intermediates, especially in the case of EG. Tailoring advanced oxidation processes to favor the formation of non-toxic or readily biodegradable compounds is crucial before the treated effluents are discharged into the environment or proximal water bodies. Such an approach ensures compliance with environmental safety standards and enhances the overall sustainability of the wastewater treatment strategy.

Conclusions

This work demonstrated that a combination of hydrodynamic cavitation and an electron acceptor, persulfate, can effectively degrade ethylene and propylene glycol in water. HC-alone did not provide any glycol degradation, but HC-PS system degraded the glycols with percent degradation ranging from 19 to 84%. Among the key process variables identified were PS: oxidant ratio and temperature. To achieve > 90% decomposition of the glycols, a PS: glycol ratio greater than 3–4 is required. Furthermore, the process is ineffective if the fluid temperature is < 30 °C. This study showed that PS gets thermally activated by HC at a temperature > 50 °C. It was also found that HC-PS method for SO4•− radical generation was better than electric-PS method. GC–MS analysis of the reaction products revealed the presence of formic acid, acetic acid, propanoic acid, and glycolaldehyde as the major products. Even though complete mineralization of the glycols was not seen in this method, their conversion of low—toxicity intermediates such as acetic and formic acid would be beneficial in obtaining non-toxic treated water. Furthermore, operating vortex diode-based cavitation process at higher pressure drops could minimize the overall time to achieve optimal degradation. Future work based on the leads obtained from this study will focus on sequential and parallel configuration of vortex diodes for which can be retrofitted in industrial waste glycol effluents. The design and operating parameters obtained during the course of this research will be economically beneficial and sustainable degradation process especially during high volume effluents compared to other combinatorial advanced oxidation processes.
