TLR4-paxillin-Rac1 signaling mediates LPS-induced dysregulation of keratinocyte function
Authors: Rahul Patil, Peter Lialios, Micaila Curtis, Eleni Hughes, Sana Surrency, Alison Grafton, Yoontae Kim, Lily Sabol, Argyris K. Lazaris, Stella Alimperti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryKeratinocyte migration is fundamental to re-epithelialization and the restoration of epithelial barrier integrity in the skin and oral mucosa, though it is highly susceptible to impairment by the bacterial inflammatory mediator lipopolysaccharide (LPS). We demonstrate that LPS-activated toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) interacts directly with the focal adhesion scaffold paxillin to induce site-specific phosphorylation at Ser273. This signaling event promotes persistent Rac1 activation, which disrupts the adhesion turnover essential for coordinated migration. Functional validation using Ser273 paxillin mutants demonstrated that this site-specific phosphorylation is necessary and sufficient to drive migratory dysfunction. Furthermore, pharmacologic Rac1 inhibition suppresses paxillin phosphorylation and restores epithelial organization, revealing a bidirectional relationship between paxillin and Rac1 signaling. These findings define a TLR4-paxillin-Rac1 signaling axis that links inflammatory activation to focal adhesion remodeling, identifying Rac1-dependent adhesion dysregulation as a mechanistic driver of impaired epithelial repair.

Introduction

Acute and chronic wounds represent growing global clinical burden, placing substantial strain on healthcare systems and significantly reducing patient quality of life.1,2,3,4 While acute wounds typically follow a highly orchestrated repair cascade, chronic wounds, such as diabetic, pressure, and venous ulcers, are in a persistent inflammatory state.5,6,7 This failure to progress is often driven by bacterial contamination and the sustained activation of innate immune pathways,8,9,10,11,12 which markedly increases the risk of local and systemic infection, potentially progressing to sepsis or necessitating surgical intervention.13,14,15,16,17 Thus, there is a critical need to identify novel molecular targets for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to restore epithelial repair and improve healing outcomes under inflammatory and infectious conditions.

A central consequence of the inflammatory wound microenvironment is the disruption of epithelial repair, particularly keratinocyte migration, which is essential for restoring barrier integrity.18,19,20,21,22 Among the factors contributing to this dysfunction, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a bacterial virulence factor and major component of Gram-negative bacterial outer membranes,23,24,25,26 has been shown to impair epithelial migration and tissue remodeling.27,28 Mechanistically, LPS engages toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a key upstream regulator that links inflammatory stimuli to intracellular signaling pathways controlling epithelial repair and cellular migratory responses.27,28,29,30 Specifically, studies have demonstrated that LPS binding to the TLR4 activates MyD88-and TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β) (TRIF)-dependent signaling cascades, leading to activation of p38 and JNK MAPK pathways, induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, and modulation of keratinocyte proliferation and migration.28,31,32,33,34,35 Despite this, its role in regulating the mechanosensitive pathways that govern focal adhesion dynamics and cytoskeletal organization remains poorly understood.

Focal adhesion dynamics and cytoskeletal organization are fundamental mechanosensitive processes that coordinate keratinocyte migration by integrating extracellular matrix (ECM) cues with intracellular signaling.36,37,38 Central to this integration is paxillin, a focal adhesion adaptor that functions as a signaling hub linking integrin39,40 engagement to the activity of Rho family GTPases, particularly Rac1, thereby coordinating the actin remodeling required for cell polarization, lamellipodia formation, and directional motility.41,42 Paxillin both regulates and is regulated by Rac1 through phosphorylation-dependent signaling complexes that spatially control adhesion turnover and protrusive activity. Specifically, phosphorylation of paxillin at Y31 and Y118 creates docking sites for CrkII, which recruits the Rac1 guanine nucleotide exchange factor DOCK180 to drive localized Rac1 activation and lamellipodia formation.43,44 Similarly, phosphorylation at S273 promotes assembly of the GIT1-βPIX-PAK complex at focal adhesions, further enhancing localized Rac1 activation and rapid adhesion turnover at the leading edge.45,46,47 Additional regulatory inputs, including cyclin D1-Cdk4- and Brk-mediated phosphorylation of paxillin, amplify Rac1-dependent protrusive signaling.48,49 Conversely, Rac1 reinforces paxillin function through downstream effectors such as PAK and JNK, which phosphorylate paxillin to stabilize focal adhesion remodeling and sustain migratory signaling.45,47 Through this bidirectional feedback architecture, paxillin integrates Rac1 activation with adhesion dynamics while coordinating Rac-driven protrusion at the leading edge and RhoA-mediated contractility at the trailing edge, ensuring efficient cytoskeletal reorganization and directional keratinocyte migration during epithelial repair.44,46

While the paxillin-Rac1 axis is fundamental to mediating keratinocyte motility, the precise mechanism by which inflammatory cues disrupt this mechanotransduction pathway to impair epithelial repair has remained elusive. Herein, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which LPS disrupts keratinocyte migration through TLR4-dependent dysregulation of paxillin and Rac1. We demonstrate that LPS promotes direct physical interaction between TLR4 and the focal adhesion scaffold paxillin, inducing sustained, site-specific phosphorylation at Ser273. This modification sustained aberrant Rac1 activation, resulting in defective adhesion turnover and loss of coordinated migratory behavior. Interestingly, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of Rac1 restored epithelial organization and rescued wound closure in vitro. Moreover, phosphomimetic phosphorylation of paxillin at Ser273 was sufficient to increase Rac1 activity in the absence of LPS, whereas the non-phosphorylatable mutant prevented Rac1 activation in response to LPS, demonstrating a bidirectional signaling relationship between paxillin phosphorylation and Rac1 activation. These results provide new insight into how inflammatory microenvironments compromise epithelial repair and identify adhesion-associated signaling pathways as potential therapeutic targets to restore epithelial function under inflammatory conditions.

Results

LPS and F. nucleatum disrupt keratinocyte migration and scratch wound closure in vitro

Cell migration is a fundamental process in wound closure, during which keratinocytes move from the wound edge toward the center to restore the epithelial barrier. To investigate mechanisms of migration impairment under infection-mimicking conditions using an exclusively in vitro model, keratinocytes were exposed to Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum at multiple of infection [MOI] = 6) and subjected to a scratch-wound assay. F. nucleatum-exposed cells exhibited markedly reduced scratch-gap closure (∼40%) after 40 h compared to untreated controls (p < 0.0001) (Figures 1A and 1B). Live-cell tracking (Video S1) confirmed diminished cell displacement and restricted migration (Figure 1C), indicating impaired scratch-gap closure. Given that the bacterial virulence factor LPS is a major component of Gram-negative bacterial outer membranes and a potent inducer of inflammation,50,51 we further evaluated its effects on keratinocyte migration. Exposure to LPS (100 ng/mL) for 40 h significantly impaired scratch-gap closure, achieving only ∼25% closure relative to controls (p < 0.0001) (Figures 1D and 1E). Live-cell tracking (Video S2) further demonstrated reduced cell displacement and confined migration patterns in LPS-treated cells, confirming compromised migratory capacity (Figure 1F). TLR4 mediates LPS-induced inhibition of keratinocyte migration and scratch-wound closure

To elucidate the mechanisms by which LPS impairs cell migration, we investigated TLR4, which is the primary receptor responsible for recognizing LPS and initiating downstream inflammatory signaling.52,53,54 Our results showed that LPS treatment at concentration of 100 ng/mL significantly increased TLR4 expression by approximately 2.5-fold compared to control (p < 0.0001) (Figures 2A and 2B). Silencing TLR4 using small interfering RNA (siRNA, siTLR4) effectively reduced TLR4 expression even in the presence of LPS, confirming that LPS increases TLR4 expression and activates TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling. To determine whether TLR4 mediates the effects of LPS on keratinocyte motility, we assessed functional outcomes using a scratch-wound recovery assay (Figure S1). Control cells achieved robust scratch-gap closure (∼80%), which was markedly reduced by LPS exposure (∼25%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2C). Similarly, non-targeting negative control siRNA transfected cells (scramble) treated with LPS showed only ∼35% closure. In contrast, TLR4-silenced cells maintained high scratch-gap closure efficiency (∼80%), and this remained largely preserved (∼70%) even after LPS treatment. The significant difference between LPS-treated control and TLR4-knockdown cells (p < 0.0001) highlights TLR4 as a key mediator of LPS-induced inhibition of keratinocyte migration in vitro. Consistent with this, TLR4 knockdown rescued LPS-induced inhibition of cell migration. While scrambled siRNA controls showed limited recovery (∼25%), TLR4-deficient cells demonstrated significantly improved migration (∼75%) in the presence of LPS. Live-cell tracking (Video S3) further confirmed restored directional movement and displacement in TLR4-silenced cells, comparable to untreated controls (Figure 2D). TLR4 regulates focal adhesion signaling through site-specific phosphorylation of paxillin

Next, we investigated downstream effectors involved in TLR4-mediated regulation of cell migration. We focused on paxillin, a key focal adhesion adaptor protein that coordinates cell-ECM interactions and plays a critical role in cell motility.55 Initially, we performed immunoblot analysis by targeting total paxillin and its phosphorylation at multiple sites (Y118, Y188, Ser126, Ser178, and Ser273) under LPS stimulation at concentration of 100 ng/mL for 15 min. Our results showed that LPS selectively enhanced phosphorylation at Ser273, with an approximately 3-fold increase compared to untreated controls (p < 0.05), while total paxillin levels remained unchanged (Figures 3A and 3B). Phosphorylation at other sites (Y118, Y188, Ser126, and Ser178) did not change significantly, suggesting a site-specific effect of LPS on Ser 273. To further characterize the dynamics of this phosphorylation, we examined time dependence in Ser273 phosphorylation following LPS exposure (15, 30, and 60 min). Phospho-paxillin (Ser273) levels were significantly increased (∼3-fold) at 15 min (∗∗∗p < 0.001), decreased at 30 min (∼2-fold; ∗∗p < 0.01), and remained elevated above control at 60 min (∼1.5-fold; ∗p < 0.01) (Figures 3C–3F), indicating a rapid activation followed by a gradual decline over time. Consistent with these findings, LPS treatment also upregulated paxillin phosphorylation in oral and epidermal keratinocytes as early as 15 min post-treatment (Figures S3A and S3B), suggesting that this signaling response is not cell-line specific. To test whether this effect is mediated by TLR4, we examined paxillin-TLR4 interactions and Ser273 phosphorylation in cells after blockage of TLR4. Co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed direct binding between TLR4 and paxillin upon LPS stimulation (Figures 3G and 3H), supporting a physical interaction. Moreover, TLR4 silencing abrogated LPS-induced Ser273 phosphorylation (p-paxillins273), while control and scramble cells showed significant increases in phosphorylation (p < 0.001 and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Figures 3I and 3J). Paxillin Ser273 phosphorylation drives LPS-induced impairment of keratinocyte migration

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that LPS stimulation induces significant changes in total paxillin localization in keratinocytes. In control cells, paxillin (green) displayed a diffused cytoplasmic pattern with minimal focal adhesion-like structures (Figure 4A). After 15 min of LPS (100 ng/mL) exposure, total paxillin redistributed prominently to the cell periphery and membrane protrusions, as indicated by red arrows (Figure 4A), suggesting active focal adhesion reorganization. To explore the functional relevance of paxillin Ser273 phosphorylation in LPS-induced migration impairment, we employed site-specific phosphorylation mutants of paxillin.47 Keratinocyte cells were transfected with wild-type paxillin (Pax WT), a dephosphomimetic mutant (Pax A; S273A), and a phosphomimetic mutant (Pax D; S273D). The S273A mutation prevents phosphorylation at this residue, thereby modeling a constitutively non-phosphorylated state, whereas the S273D substitution introduces a negatively charged residue that mimics constitutive phosphorylation. Immunoblot analysis using a phospho-specific antibody against Ser273 demonstrated robust and selective signal enrichment in Pax D expressing cells, with minimal signal detected in Pax A cells, confirming the expected functional behavior of each mutant. Quantitative analysis revealed phospho-Ser273 levels in transfected Pax D cells were approximately 2.5-fold higher than Pax WT and 2.9-fold higher than Pax A cells (p < 0.0001) (Figures 4B and 4C). Furthermore, to validate the functional significance of these observations, we performed quantitative scratch-wound assays (Figure S2). Control cells exhibited efficient scratch-gap closure (∼80%), while LPS treatment significantly reduced closure (∼35%, p < 0.01). Cells expressing the phosphomimetic Pax D mutant displayed similarly impaired migration (∼45% closure), as cells exposed to LPS. Conversely, cells expressing the non-phosphoryl Pax A mutant maintained high closure efficiency (∼70%) in presence of LPS (Figure 4D). To visualize the subcellular distribution of each paxillin variant in living cells, keratinocytes expressing PaxWT-EGFP, PaxD-EGFP, or PaxA-EGFP were monitored by live confocal microscopy before and following LPS stimulation (Video S4 and Figure 4E). The GFP fluorescence signal from each construct revealed distinct subcellular localization patterns that were consistent with their respective phosphorylation states and functional roles. Upon LPS stimulation (t = 15 min), Pax WT-EGFP showed increased accumulation at focal adhesion-like structures at the cell periphery, consistent with LPS-driven Ser273 phosphorylation (Figure 4A) promoting focal adhesion engagement and adhesion remodeling in response to inflammatory stimulation. PaxD-EGFP, the phosphomimetic mutant exhibited prominent accumulation at focal adhesion-like structures even under basal, unstimulated conditions. In contrast, PaxA-EGFP, the dephosphomimetic mutant displayed a more diffuse GFP fluorescence distribution with reduced accumulation at focal adhesion structures following LPS stimulation. This distinct localization pattern suggests that constitutive mimicry of Ser273 phosphorylation alters paxillin’s interaction with focal adhesion complexes, potentially preventing the aberrant adhesion engagement driven by LPS and thereby maintaining a more dynamic adhesion turnover state that is permissive for cell migration (Figure 4E). This interpretation is consistent with our functional observation that PaxA expression rescues LPS-induced defects in keratinocyte migration. Based on live-cell tracking (Video S5) revealed that LPS-treated Pax-WT-expressing cells showed restricted movement. Similarly, cells expressing the phosphomimetic Pax-D mutant also exhibited limited migration, mimicking the LPS effect without exogenous stimulation. In contrast, the non-phosphorylatable Pax-A mutant maintained normal migration even after LPS exposure. This demonstrates that alterations in paxillin phosphorylation at Ser273 are sufficient to control cell motility (Figure 4F). TLR4-Rac1 signaling mediates LPS-induced impairment of keratinocyte migration

To further investigate how the paxillin-TLR4 axis regulates keratinocyte migration, we examined the involvement of Rac1, a small GTPase known to drive lamellipodia formation and membrane ruffling, processes dependent on focal adhesion dynamics and essential for directed cell movement during tissue repair.47,56 LPS treatment (100 ng/mL) significantly increased active Rac1-GTPase levels by approximately 1.5-fold compared to untreated controls (∗∗p < 0.01; Figures 5A and 5B), while total Rac1 protein levels remained unchanged. Consistent with these findings, LPS also significantly elevated active Rac1-GTPase levels in oral epidermal keratinocytes (Figures S3C and S3D), indicating that LPS-driven Rac1 activation represents a broader keratinocyte response to bacterial-derived stimuli. This indicates that LPS activates Rac1 without affecting its overall expression, implicating Rac1 as a key downstream effector of LPS-induced migratory impairment. To confirm the specificity of this activation, we used NSC23766, a selective Rac1 inhibitor that blocks activation by interfering with guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) binding.57 Pretreatment with NSC23766 (50 μM) for 30 min completely suppressed LPS-induced Rac1 activation, restoring Rac1-GTPase levels to baseline values comparable to untreated cells (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; Figures 5C and 5D). Finally, to determine whether TLR4 mediates LPS-induced Rac1 activation, cells transfected with non-targeting negative control siRNA transfected cells (scramble) induced a robust increase in Rac1-GTPase levels by 120% (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001), similar to non-transfected control. However, TLR4 knockdown reduced Rac1 activation to similar levels as the control groups (Figures 5E and 5F). LPS impairs keratinocyte migration through the Rac1-paxillin signaling axis

To determine whether Rac1 acts upstream of paxillin phosphorylation as a downstream effector of TLR4 signaling, we assessed whether Rac1 activity is required for LPS-induced paxillin Ser273 phosphorylation. Protein analysis revealed that LPS stimulation significantly increased phospho-paxillin (Ser273) levels, whereas co-treatment with NSC23766 diminished this effect (Figures 6A and 6B). These results demonstrate that Rac1 activity is essential for the LPS-mediated phosphorylation of paxillin at Ser273. To further validate the link between Rac1 activation and paxillin Ser273 phosphorylation, keratinocytes were treated with a Rac/Cdc42 activator (0.25 units/mL) and analyzed by western blot over a time course. Phosphorylation of paxillin at Ser273 showed a transient increase, peaking at 15–30 min post-stimulation (∼2-fold increase, ∗∗p < 0.01), before returning to baseline by 60 min (Figures 6C and 6D). This kinetic profile suggests that Ser273 is a rapid, early target of Rac1 signaling. We next investigated whether paxillin phosphorylation status could, in turn, modulate Rac1 activation, potentially forming a feedback loop. To this end, we compared Rac1 activation across cells expressing different paxillin mutants. Specifically, in Pax WT-expressing cells, LPS treatment increased Rac1-GTPase levels by approximately 1.5-fold compared to untreated controls (∗p < 0.05). Notably, cells expressing the phosphomimetic Pax D (S273D) mutant exhibited elevated basal Rac1-GTPase levels (∼3-fold), comparable to those observed in Pax WT cells following LPS stimulation. In contrast, Pax A (S273A)-expressing cells did not show significant Rac1 activation in response to LPS (Figures 6E and 6F). Discussion

Keratinocyte migration is essential for re-epithelialization during wound healing, facilitating restoration of epithelial barrier integrity and tissue homeostasis in the oral mucosa and skin.21,58,59,60,61 Disruption of this process under inflammatory conditions is a feature of delayed epithelial repair.27,62,63,64,65 While migration has been linked to focal adhesion scaffolds such as paxillin,66,67,68,69 the contribution of inflammatory cues directly perturbs adhesion-dependent signaling to impair migration remains incompletely understood. Specifically, most prior studies have focused on receptor-mediated inflammatory signaling, particularly through TLR4,28,50,70,71,72 without resolving how these pathways mechanistically alter the adhesion machinery that governs migratory behavior. Herein, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which LPS, a potent inflammatory mediator derived from Gram-negative bacteria,23,24,25,26,50,51 disrupts keratinocyte migration through TLR4-dependent dysregulation of paxillin and Rac1. Our findings establish a mechanistic link between inflammatory receptor activation and focal adhesion dysfunction, revealing a TLR4-paxillin-Rac1 signaling axis that drives impaired epithelial migration. These results provide new insight into how inflammatory microenvironments compromise epithelial repair and identify adhesion-associated signaling pathways as potential targets to restore epithelial regeneration under inflammatory conditions.

To investigate these mechanisms, we performed in vitro experiments demonstrating that both LPS and F. nucleatum significantly impair keratinocyte migration. This impairment was evidenced by delayed wound closure in scratch assays and reduced migration and displacement in live-cell tracking analyses. To understand these findings, it is important to consider the biological framework of the migration assay employed. The scratch assay models mechanically coordinated collective migration driven by redistribution of monolayer tension, release of contact inhibition, and force transmission across intercellular junctions.73,74,75 Within this framework, focal adhesion remodeling and cytoskeletal organization are regulated predominantly by mechanical cues and cell-cell coupling rather than by exogenous chemotactic gradients.75,76,77,78 By intentionally avoiding growth factor-induced chemotaxis, we were able to isolate the contribution of the TLR4-paxillin-Rac1 axis to adhesion-dependent mechanotransduction without introducing additional receptor-mediated signaling pathways that could confound interpretation. Furthermore, the scratch assay provides a reproducible and physiologically relevant model of epithelial gap closure, allowing controlled interrogation of collective mechanics within a defined experimental context.73,75,78,79,80,81,82 Future studies incorporating defined chemotactic gradients or growth factor stimulation will help determine how this adhesion-regulatory pathway integrates with receptor-driven directional migration in more complex microenvironments. Importantly, our live-cell tracking analyses focused on early post-scratch intervals during which gap closure is driven predominantly by lateral migration rather than cell division.79,81,82,83 Under these conditions, we did not observe differences in cell density indicative of a dominant proliferative contribution to closure. Within this mechanically defined setting, our results demonstrate that bacterial components, such as virulence factors, disrupt cytoskeletal organization and coordinated epithelial motility, providing mechanistic insight into how microbial stimuli impair keratinocyte migration in vitro.84,85

At the cellular level, our findings reveal that the adverse effects of LPS on keratinocyte migration are mediated through the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin signaling axis, which serves as a critical interface linking inflammatory signaling via TLR428,50,70,71,72,86,87 to cytoskeletal remodeling and focal adhesion dynamics via paxillin.55,66,67,68,69,87,88 LPS upregulates TLR4 expression in keratinocytes,89 initiating downstream signaling cascades that impair epithelial migration. Specifically, siRNA-mediated knockdown of TLR4 in our model not only blocked LPS-induced upregulation but also restored scratch gap closure, highlighting the central role of TLR4 in regulating inflammation-driven defects in keratinocyte motility. These findings align with previous in vivo and in vitro research demonstrating that genetic deletion, pharmacological inhibition, or inherent deficiency of TLR4 can partially or completely rescue inflammation-associated repair defects in epithelium, thereby accelerating the resolution of tissue damage.30,53,54,90 However, TLR4 is not exclusively activated by bacterially derived LPS. During wound repair, a diverse array of endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), fibronectin fragments, heat shock proteins, hyaluronan degradation products, and oxidized phospholipids, are released from damaged or necrotic tissue and can potently engage TLR4.20,91,92,93,94,95 Elevated TLR4 expression and signaling have been reported in non-healing and diabetic wounds, where sustained TLR4 activation perpetuates a pro-inflammatory state that is incompatible with productive tissue repair.96,97,98 The engagement of TLR4 by these endogenous ligands suggests that the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin signaling cascade identified here may be relevant not only in the context of bacterial infection but also under conditions of sterile inflammation, as commonly observed in diabetic wounds and other chronic wound etiologies.96,97,98,99 Furthermore, TLR4 activation can engage parallel downstream signaling branches, including NF-κB, MAPK, and interferon regulatory factor pathways, that independently suppress keratinocyte migration, proliferation, and ECM remodeling.100,101,102,103 The coactivation of these pathways in the wound microenvironment suggests that healing failure results from the cumulative disruption of multiple interconnected processes, and that the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis identified here represents one critical node within this broader signaling network.

To further elucidate the downstream TLR4 mediators involved in cell migration, we focused on a key focal adhesion protein, paxillin, which has been implicated in cell migration and wound healing.66,67,68,69,104 Interestingly, our results demonstrated the direct binding of TLR4 to paxillin upon LPS exposure indicating that TLR4/paxillin complex modulates focal adhesion dynamics in vitro. Our data further revealed that LPS specifically increases paxillin phosphorylation at Ser273, whereas other established phosphorylation sites, including Y118, Y188, Ser126, and Ser178, remain unaffected.105,106,107 This site-specific modification underscores the precision of LPS-TLR4 signaling in modulating focal adhesion dynamics. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that Ser273 phosphorylation is mediated through the TLR4 signaling axis, as upon TLR4 knockdown completely abolished this phosphorylation event.

We expanded our mechanistic analysis to the Rho GTPase Rac1, which is a central regulator of cell motility, specifically examining its interaction with paxillin-TLR4. While Rac1 is classically associated with promoting front-rear polarity and lamellipodia formation necessary for directed cell migration,41,108,109,110,111,112 its role in wound healing is highly context-dependent.112,113,114 Under physiological conditions, tightly regulated Rac1 activity is essential for coordinated keratinocyte migration and epithelial repair.114,115 However, in inflammatory, pathogen-driven contexts, chronic or aberrant Rac1 activation can disrupt this balance by uncoupling lamellipodial protrusion from the properly timed turnover of focal adhesions, ultimately leading to disordered motility and impaired wound closure.116,117,118,119,120 Consistent with this framework, our findings demonstrate that LPS-induced Rac1 activation impairs keratinocyte migration through dysregulation of paxillin-dependent focal adhesion signaling, and that pharmacologic Rac1 inhibition is sufficient to restore migratory function in vitro. Mechanistically, LPS-driven TLR4 activation promotes sustained Rac1 activation,117 which recruits and activates p21-activated kinase 1 (PAK1), a canonical Rac1 effector kinase.89 Activated PAK1 directly phosphorylates paxillin at serine 273 (Ser273), a key regulatory site that modulates focal adhesion turnover.

Under physiological conditions, phosphorylation of paxillin at Ser273 is tightly regulated and transient, enabling the dynamic assembly and disassembly of focal adhesions required for coordinated cell migration.45,47 This reversible phosphorylation facilitates the spatially and temporally controlled recruitment of signaling complexes, including the GIT1-βPIX-PAK module,47 which promotes localized Rac1 activation, lamellipodial extension, and efficient adhesion turnover at the leading edge. Such regulated cycling allows keratinocytes to establish front-rear polarity, form new adhesions at the leading edge, and release mature adhesions at the trailing edge, thereby supporting effective forward translocation. In contrast, sustained Ser273 phosphorylation under prolonged TLR4 activation in the presence of LPS disrupts this dynamic equilibrium by stabilizing paxillin at focal adhesions and preventing normal adhesion turnover. This persistent adhesion engagement interferes with coordinated cytoskeletal remodeling and impairs the detachment phase of migration, ultimately restricting forward cell movement. Consistent with this mechanism, expression of the phosphomimetic PaxD mutant resulted in constitutive focal adhesion localization and impaired migration47 even in the absence of LPS, phenocopying the effects of inflammatory stimulation and demonstrating that Ser273 phosphorylation is sufficient to drive the migratory defect. Conversely, expression of the non-phosphorylatable PaxA (Ser273A) mutant prevented LPS-induced adhesion stabilization and restored directional migration and scratch-gap closure in vitro. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that disruption of Ser273 phosphorylation alters focal adhesion dynamics and migration behavior45,46 and collectively establish Ser273 phosphorylation as a critical molecular switch that governs focal adhesion turnover and keratinocyte motility under inflammatory conditions.

Pharmacological inhibition of Rac1 using NSC23766 effectively reversed LPS-induced defects in keratinocyte migration in vitro, restoring motility and scratch-gap closure to levels comparable to untreated controls. These results align with the study by Takaya et al.,121 which showed that Rac1 inhibition promotes wound regeneration in mouse fetuses by altering actin cytoskeletal dynamics. Together, these observations underscore the critical role of Rac1 in the regulation of epithelial migration. However, the therapeutic implications of targeting Rac1 must be interpreted within the appropriate biological context. In bacterially infected or LPS-rich wound environments, where TLR4-driven Rac1 activation disrupts cytoskeletal dynamics and impairs focal adhesion turnover, targeted Rac1 inhibition may represent a rational strategy to normalize keratinocyte migration and restore wound closure. In contrast, diabetic wounds, as demonstrated by Fan et al.,108 are characterized by intrinsically reduced Rac1 activity, and exogenous Rac1 activation in that setting promotes keratinocyte proliferation and improves healing. Thus, inhibiting Rac1 in diabetic wounds could potentially exacerbate impaired repair rather than ameliorate it, underscoring the critical importance of wound etiology in designing Rac1-targeted interventions. Importantly, our experimental framework differs from the diabetic context since we examine LPS-induced Rac1 hyperactivation in non-diabetic keratinocytes, focusing on its effects on focal adhesion dynamics and migration rather than on cell-cycle progression. Collectively, these findings underscore that Rac1 does not function as a simple pro- or anti-migratory signal in wound repair but rather that the directionality and magnitude of its effect are tightly coupled to the upstream pathological stimulus and the broader wound microenvironment.113,114 Future studies examining Rac1 activity in wound contexts that combine diabetic conditions with bacterial infection will be particularly important in determining how these pathological states interact and whether Rac1-targeted therapies can be meaningfully tailored to specific wound etiologies.

To assess the generalizability of our findings beyond the oral epithelial setting, we examined whether LPS-induced TLR4-Rac1-paxillin activation and its migratory consequences could be reproduced in primary epidermal keratinocytes. In general, oral and skin keratinocytes arise from distinct anatomical compartments and are adapted to fundamentally different microenvironments.122,123,124,125 Epidermal keratinocytes form a stratified, keratinized epithelium optimized for barrier function against desiccation and mechanical stress, whereas gingival keratinocytes maintain a partially keratinized or non-keratinized epithelium that is continuously exposed to the oral microbiome and its associated microbial products, including LPS from Gram-negative bacteria.122,123 This chronic microbial exposure has shaped gingival keratinocytes to exhibit a distinct immunological phenotype, innate immune tolerance mechanisms, oxidative stress, and cytokine secretion profiles compared to their epidermal counterparts.126,127,128,129 Despite these substantial differences, LPS similarly induced paxillin phosphorylation and Rac1 activation in primary epidermal keratinocytes, and pharmacological inhibition of Rac1 reversed LPS-induced defects in scratch-gap closure in these cells. The convergent activation of the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis across both keratinocyte populations therefore suggests that this signaling mechanism reflects a conserved, cell-intrinsic response to innate immune stimulation rather than a tissue-specific phenomenon, while also reinforcing the broader relevance of our findings to cutaneous wound healing contexts.

Collectively, our results identify the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin signaling axis as a key mechanism by which LPS disrupts keratinocyte migration, directional persistence, and in vitro scratch-wound closure. LPS-mediated TLR4 activation triggers aberrant Rac1 signaling and paxillin Ser273 phosphorylation, impairing focal adhesion turnover and directional motility. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of Rac1 restores adhesion dynamics and migratory coordination, suggesting that selective modulation of this pathway can alleviate inflammation-induced epithelial migration defects without broadly suppressing immune function.130,131 While the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis was the primary focus of this study, we acknowledge that additional mechanisms likely act in parallel or converge on this signaling network during impaired wound closure. These include cytokine-driven inflammatory cascades, such as those mediated by TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6,132,133,134 mechanical and matrix-dependent cues135,136,137 and downstream MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways.138,139 Future studies will be needed to fully delineate the relative contributions of these parallel signaling pathways and their crosstalk with the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis in the context of inflammation-impaired epithelial repair.

Overall, our in vitro mechanistic model demonstrates LPS-induced impairment of keratinocyte migration and delayed epithelial gap closure regulated via TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis. While our data do not directly model chronic wound pathology, it is well-established that delayed wound closure is a central and defining feature of chronic wounds, and that persistent bacterial burden and sustained innate immune activation are key drivers of this delay.5,13,14,140,141,142 In this context, the TLR4-Rac1-paxillin axis identified here provides a plausible molecular mechanism through which bacterially derived LPS could prolong delayed epithelial repair, potentially contributing to the failure of wound closure that characterizes chronic wound environments. Furthermore, in vivo studies will be essential to determine whether targeting this pathway can mediate wound closure under pathophysiologically relevant conditions. By elucidating this regulatory axis at both molecular and cellular levels, our study provides mechanistic insight into how LPS-driven inflammatory signaling disrupts keratinocyte migration and cytoskeletal organization, and identifies a specific, targetable pathway with the potential to support epithelial repair in wound environments characterized by persistent microbial challenge.

Limitations of the study

Our data establish a robust mechanistic framework using in vitro models, providing a foundation for future studies to integrate these findings with in vivo complexities, such as vascular support, immune responses, matrix remodeling, and systemic signaling. To this end, the scratch-wound assay reflects mechanically driven collective migration but does not include the chemotactic cues or growth factor gradients that are present during physiological wound healing. Finally, while Rac1 inhibitor (NSC23766) improved the migration defect, its in vivo translational potential remains to be fully characterized.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

Human immortalized gingival keratinocyte cells (HIGKs)143,144 were cultured in serum-free keratinocyte growth medium (GibcoTM, Thermofisher) supplemented with bovine pituitary extract and human epidermal growth factor. Primary human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs; #PCS-200-010, ATCC) were cultured in serum-free keratinocyte growth medium (GibcoTM, Thermofisher) enriched with KGM-GoldTM SingleQuots® supplements (#00192152, Lonza), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and were used between passage of HIGKs from 6-10 and NHEKs from 2-4. The influence of sex or gender on the results reported in this study was not directly investigated, as this work focused on molecular signaling mechanisms in established keratinocyte cell line models. HIGKs and NHEKs were tested for mycoplasma contamination prior to use and confirmed negative. Cell line identity was verified by supplier certification. LPS from Escherichia coli O26:B6 (#00-4976-93, Thermofisher) were reconstituted in endotoxin-free water, and cells were treated with 100 ng/ml of LPS for the indicated time periods. For the Rac activation assay, the cells were treated with Rac/CDC42 Activator (0.25 units/mL;# CN02, Cytoskeleton) for 15, 30, and 60 min. For Rac inhibition experiments, cells were pretreated with the Rac inhibitor NSC23766 (50 μM; #21-611-0R, Tocris Bioscience™) for 30 min prior to downstream analyses.

Bacteria culture

F. nucleatum (F.n.; ATCC 25586) was cultured on Trypticase Soy Agar II plates with 5% w/v sheep blood (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 4 d to allow colony growth. The plates were inverted and placed in a 2.5 L anaerobiosis jar (Sigma) with an anaerobic gas generator (AnaeroGen) to create an oxygen-depleted environment, then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Anaerotest strips (Sigma) were included in the jar to confirm the presence of an anaerobic atmosphere. Once colonies had formed, individual colonies were picked with a disposable inoculating loop and cultured in microcentrifuge tubes containing Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) media supplemented with hemin (1 mg/mL) and menadione (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA). The tubes were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 20-24 h until they reached an optical density (OD) of 0.1-0.3. OD was measured using an ND-1000 spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 600 nm.144 To achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 6, the calculations assumed that at OD600 = 0.1, liquid cultures contained approximately 1.82×108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL for F.n.. MOI of 6 was used to coculture bacteria with cells for scratch and live migration assay.

Method details

Plasmid transfection

Paxillin-S273A-EGFP, Paxillin-S273D-EGFP, and Paxillin-WT-EGFP constructs were generously provided by Dr. Miguel Vicente Manzanares (Instituto de Biología Celular y Molecular del Cancer (IBMCC-CSIC). TLR4-specific siRNA (Silencer™ siRNA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #AM16708, Assay ID 110005; target sequence: 5′-GGACTTTGCCAACTGGAAA-3′) and a non-targeting negative control siRNA (Silencer™ Select Negative Control No. 1 siRNA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #4390843; target sequence: 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′) were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching ∼75% confluence prior to transfection. For each well, 3.75 μL Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11668-027) was diluted in 125 μL Opti-MEM™, and 2.5 μg plasmid DNA or 100 nmoles of siRNA was mixed with 10 μL P3000™ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11058021) in 125 μL Opti-MEM™. After 5 min incubation at room temperature, the diluted DNA and Lipofectamine solutions were combined and incubated for 15 min to allow complex formation. The transfection mixture was added dropwise to the cells and incubated for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium. Cells were incubated for an additional 48 h before being used for downstream assays.

Scratch closure assay

HIGKs and NHEKs were seeded 3000 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates and cultured until they reached confluence. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch in the cell monolayer. Detached cells and debris were removed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fresh serum-free medium containing LPS (100 ng/ml) treatments were added. Scratch gap closure was monitored at 40 h post-scratch using an Echo Rebel inverted Microscope equipped with a digital camera. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH) to quantify the percentage of scratch closure.145

Immunofluorescence and live imaging

The cells were fixed with 4%v/v paraformaldehyde (PFA) (#XJ357386, Thermofisher) in PBS (pH 7.4) for 30 min at room temperature (RT), followed by washing twice with PBS. Next, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (#SLBT4524, Sigma) in PBS for 30 min and blocked with a solution containing 0.01% Triton X-100, 5% goat serum (Sigma) in PBS overnight at 4°C. Next, samples were incubated with primary antibody against total paxillin (1:100 dilution, #50195, Cell signaling technology) was applied overnight at 4°C. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor™ 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100 dilution, #3118727, Thermofisher) for overnight at room temperature. F-actin was stained with Alexa Fluor™ 555 Phalloidin (1:500, #A34055, Thermofisher), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, #096M4014V, Thermofisher) for overnight. For live-cell imaging experiments, 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (Mattek) were coated with 5 μg/mL fibronectin (#CB-40008A, Thermofisher) for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cells were seeded at 30,000 cells/well subjected to time-lapse imaging for 1 h under controlled temperature (37 °C) and CO2 (5%) conditions. Confocal images were acquired using a Nikon CSU-W1 SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope to monitor cell migration and GFP-paxillin localization in transfected cells. In this experiment, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (1:10,000, #62249, Thermofisher). Z-stack projections, channel merging, and cross-sectional analyses were performed using ImageJ.145 Cell migration was analyzed using the LIMTracker plugin in Fiji,146 with migration parameters quantified by tracking at least 20 cells per condition across three independent experiments. The plugin was used for nucleus detection and tracking. Migration track plots and related figures were generated using MATLAB 2022b.

Immunoblotting

The cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Cell Signaling Technology). Lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (PierceTM, #23225). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were resolved at 4% to 12% gradient SDS-PAGE gels (Thermo Fisher). Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Thermo Fisher) and blocked with 5% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% v/v Tween 20 Detergent (TBST) (Thermo Fisher) for 1h at room temperature. Membranes were probed overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-human paxillin (1:1000 dilution; #50195, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-human phospho-paxillin Ser273 (1:1000 dilution, #PAP-85720, Thermo fisher), rabbit anti-human Rac1 antibody (1:1000 dilution, #2465, Cell signaling technology) rabbit anti-human TLR4 (1:1000 dilution, #14358, Cell Signaling Technology) and rabbit anti-human β-actin (1:5000 dilution; #4970, Cell Signaling Technology). The antibodies were diluted and incubated in 5 % w/v BSA in TBST (Thermo Fisher) overnight in 4 °C. Proteins of interest were detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated (HRP-linked) anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:1000 dilution; #7074, Cell Signaling Technology) and protein bands were visualized with chemiluminescent substrate Pico PLUS (Thermo Fisher, # 34580) and Azure 300 Imaging System (Azure Biosystems), according to manufacturer instructions. All western blots were quantified by ImageJ according to standard procedures.145

Rac1 activity assay

Rac1 activity was assessed using a Rac1 Activation Assay Kit (#BK035, Cytoskeleton) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the cells were lysed, and 500 μg of total protein was incubated with PAK-PBD beads for 4 h at 4°C. Beads were washed and bound proteins were eluted in 2× Laemmli sample buffer. Samples were analyzed by Western blotting using a rabbit anti-human Rac1 antibody (1:1000 dilution, #2465, Cell Signaling Technology). Active Rac1 levels were quantified and normalized to total Rac1 expression in corresponding whole-cell lysates by ImageJ according to standard procedures.145

Immunoprecipitation

The cells were lysed in ice-cold IP Lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) and lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™). Next, 500 μg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 10 μg mouse anti-human TLR4 antibody (ProteinTech, #66350-1-Ig) or mouse IgG isotype control (Cell Signaling Technology, #5451S) with gentle rotation. Protein G agarose beads (Cell Signaling Technology, #37478S) were then added and incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed, and bound proteins were eluted in 2× Laemmli sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #2547329), resolved by SDS–PAGE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #25040810), and transferred to membranes for immunoblot analysis. Membranes were probed with rabbit anti-paxillin antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #50195), followed by HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #7074). Protein bands were detected using SuperSignal™ Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized with an Azure 300 imaging system (Azure Biosystems) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software following standard procedures.145

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. Sample sizes for each experimental group are provided in the figure legends (N). All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times. Data were assessed for normality, and no significant variation between groups was detected. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. For experiments involving multiple time points or treatment conditions, one-way or two-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test where appropriate. All experiments were conducted and analyzed in a blind manner to ensure unbiased interpretation. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical details, including sample size (n), statistical tests used, and significance values, are provided in the figure legends and corresponding results sections.
