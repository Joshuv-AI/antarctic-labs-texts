Quantitative analysis of patient motion in walk-through PET scanner and standard axial field of view pet scanner using infrared-based tracking
Authors: Rabia Aziz, Jens Maebe, Florence Marie Muller, Yves D’Asseler, Stefaan Vandenberghe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-axial field-of-view (LAFOV) Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scanners provide high sensitivity, but throughput is limited because of time-consuming patient positioning. To enhance throughput, a novel Walk-Through PET (WT-PET) scanner has been developed, allowing patients to stand upright, supported by an adjustable headrest and hand supports. This study evaluates the degree of motion in the WT-PET system and compares it with the standard PET-CT.MethodsThree studies were conducted with healthy volunteers to estimate motion. The first two studies assessed motion in the WT-PET’s Design I (Study 1) and Design II (Study 2), while the third study compared motion on a standard PET-CT scanner bed (Study 3). Infrared markers placed on the head, shoulders, chest, and abdomen were tracked and processed using image-processing techniques involving thresholding and connected component analysis. Videos were recorded for normal breathing and breath-holding conditions, and 2D centroids were transformed into 3D coordinates using depth information.ResultsThe results shows a significant reduction in motion during breath-holding, especially for the abdomen. Mean motion distances decreased from 2.63 mm to 2.18 mm in Study 1 and from 2.42 mm to 1.67 mm in Study 2. Statistical analysis revealed notable differences in motion between the WT-PET and mCT scanners. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated non-normal motion distributions in the head, right shoulder, and abdomen for both systems, leading to the use of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for all markers. Significant differences were found in the right shoulder (p = 0.0266), left shoulder (p = 0.0004) and chest (p < 0.0001) but no significant differences were observed in the head (p = 0.1327) and abdomen (p = 0.8404).ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of patient motion in a WT-PET scanner with respect to the standard PET. The findings highlight a significant increase in shoulder and chest motion, while the head and abdomen regions showed more stability.Keywords: Walk-Through PET, Long axial field of view, Infrared localization, Rigid motion analysis, Patient throughput, Motion artifacts

Background

The development of long-axial field-of-view (LAFOV) positron emission tomography (PET) instruments has greatly broadened the scope of clinical and research PET studies. With an axial field of view (AFOV) over 60 cm, these systems provide high sensitivity compared to standard AFOV systems (16–30 cm) and enable dynamic imaging by simultaneously capturing the radiopharmaceutical distributions across the body’s major organs [1]. The increased sensitivity can be used to improve the scan quality, reduce the injected activity, and/or shorten the scan duration. However, there are several practical challenges associated with LAFOV PET. The development, acquisition and service costs of existing LAFOV PET systems are very high, with a typical system costing between 8 and 12 million euros (i.e., 3–4 times higher than the standard PET-CT), hindering many hospitals from buying such systems. Although the scan time of LAFOV can be reduced significantly (down to about 2 min) compared to standard AFOV systems (10–15 min), patient positioning on the bed remains slow, increasing the total scan duration to 5–10 min and limiting high patient throughput [2].

To tackle the aforementioned challenges of LAFOV PET, a novel dual flat panel Walk-Through PET (WT-PET) system design has recently been proposed [3]. The design of the WT-PET system is similar to the recent flat-panel airport scanners, in which a person walks into the scanner and stands between two flat detector panels. These panels, measuring 106 cm in height and 71 cm in width, are spaced 50 cm apart (front to back of patient), as can be seen in Fig. 1, effectively covering the torso from the head to the upper thigh. Figure 1 represents the monolithic Lutetium–yttrium oxyorthosilicate (LYSO) detector configuration in the system along with the dimensions of the single detector block. Thanks to the monolithic LYSO detectors, the WT-PET scanner can achieve a spatial resolution of sub-2 mm. The inclusion of depth of interaction (DOI) technology in the detector design allows the panels to be positioned closer to the patient. This configuration significantly reduces the number of required detectors, lowering the cost by nearly half compared to a cylindrical system with equivalent sensitivity and AFOV [4–6]. Moreover, the WT-PET design minimizes the time required for patient positioning and, combined with a short scan time of 30 s, reduces the total throughput to approximately 3–5 min. This shorter scan time is justified by the high sensitivity of the WT-PET system. Furthermore, time-of-flight (TOF) technology enhances effective sensitivity, requiring only half the counts when TOF resolution improves from 400 to 200 ps to maintain the same image quality [3]. The mock-up scanner, as shown in Fig. 1, measures 2.3 m in height, 2 m in width, and 1.5 m in depth, resulting in a smaller footprint compared to standard PET scanners.

One concern with such a system is the issue of patient motion, which may be higher for standing patients compared to lying patients, leading to deterioration of image quality.This arises from two types of movement, i.e., physiological motion and non-physiological motion. Physiological motion includes processes such as respiration, heartbeat, peristalsis, and bowel activity, all of which result in the deformation of internal organs. Non-physiological motion, on the other hand, refers to random movements of different body parts such as arms, shoulders or shifts in head position [7, 8]. Motion-induced blurring can cause inaccuracies in defining regions of interest (ROIs) and measurement of absolute changes in standardized uptake values (SUVs) [9–11]. Developing high-resolution PET scanners such as the WT-PET with a spatial resolution of 2 mm further intensifies this challenge, as these devices are highly sensitive to even the slightest of patient movements. Therefore, motion reduction and correction are important considerations in PET to ensure optimal image quality and accurate diagnostic outcomes [12].

Recently, several motion correction techniques have been proposed to address the head motion in brain studies [13–15], as well as to compensate for physiological motion, particularly cardiac [16] and respiratory motion [17–19], in PET imaging. Despite advancements in motion correction techniques, several limitations remain in addressing motion in LAFOV PET imaging. First, the complexity of accurately modelling and compensating for motion across the entire body is significant, given the diverse and concurrent movements of different anatomical regions [20]. Second, current motion correction algorithms often require extensive computational resources and processing time, which can be a barrier in clinical settings where rapid image reconstruction is necessary [12]. Third, integrating motion correction with existing imaging protocols can be challenging, requiring modifications to acquisition procedures and potentially complicating workflow [21].

Therefore, it is important to minimize the patient’s motion as much as possible, which can be achieved by providing proper patient support during acquisition. Furthermore, breath-hold scans become feasible for some patients with a 30-second scan duration, significantly reducing respiratory motion. Previous studies with an initial mock-up of the Walk-Through PET that was equipped with only hand supports showed that there is increased motion in the head and shoulders [22, 23]. The findings revealed a substantial increase in movement when individuals stood with minimal support. To address this issue, a new prototype design of the scanner mock-up was developed, which included headrests and hand supports to reduce motion [24, 25]. This study presents two WT-PET mock-up designs, referred to as WT-PET Design I and WT-PET Design II, with the objective of evaluating rigid body motion under both normal breathing and breath-hold conditions. Additionally, the study compares the extent of motion observed with these designs to the motion experienced by subjects lying on a standard PET-CT scanner bed.

Materials and methods

Three separate studies have been conducted to evaluate motion. The first two studies involve motion estimation on Design I of WT-PET and Design II of the WT-PET scanner mock-up. In contrast, the third study involves motion estimation on the standard PET-CT scanner bed to compare the degree of motion between standing in WT-PET and lying down on the standard PET-CT scanner bed. Prior to the motion studies, the accuracy of the measurements using a depth camera was assessed.

Accuracy of Orbbec Femto Mega

To evaluate the accuracy of the Orbbec Femto Mega depth camera, a series of experiments have been performed using a Vexta two-phase stepping motor controlled through a VXM controller to precisely move two infrared markers placed at distinct positions on the controller. In these experiments, one marker remained stationary while the other marker was moved 30 mm with precise steps of 1 mm sequentially in the X, Y, and Z directions. Figure 2 shows both the stationary marker and the moving marker and also indicates the direction of movement. This setup allowed for precise measurement of the depth camera’s accuracy in different spatial directions. The experiments were performed with the depth camera positioned directly in front of the setup (along the Z-axis) at three varying distances: 55 cm, 70 cm, and 100 cm. Four videos were recorded for each camera position, and the respective step sizes in the X, Y, and Z directions were calculated.

Setup for accuracy measurements, illustrating both stationary and moving markers. The camera was positioned at different distances (55, 70 and 100 cm) facing the moving marker

Participants

Healthy volunteers, all employees of the Ghent University, participated in three studies to evaluate motion in the WT-PET scanner and the standard Siemens Biograph mCT scanner. Each study had 20 participants who were free of known respiratory or cardiovascular conditions to ensure a homogeneous study population. Participants were thoroughly briefed on the study’s objectives, procedures, and the importance of minimizing movement during the scanning process. Before beginning the studies, informed consent was obtained from all participants to ensure they understood the scope, data privacy policies, and potential risks.

Standard PET Scanner Bed (Study 3)

This study aims to conduct a comprehensive comparative analysis of the degree of motion between two distinct scenarios: when a participant is lying down on a cylindrical PET scanner for 8 min and when standing upright in the WT-PET Design II (Study 2A) for 30 s. This study was carried out using the Siemens Biograph mCT scanner at the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Ghent University Hospital.

The camera was mounted on a tripod stand and positioned at the back of the scanner, as can be seen in Fig. 3D. Due to the specific study protocols, which required participants to stretch their arms over their heads, the placement of the shoulder markers was adjusted accordingly. The markers were positioned slightly closer to the arms to be fully visible during the video recording, as represented by Fig. 3E. This adjustment was essential to ensure accurate motion tracking under the given constraints.

Participants received instructions from the PET technician on how to properly lie down on the scanner bed using the headrest visible in Fig. 3E after the infrared markers were placed on significant anatomical points. The technician provided precise guidance to ensure that each participant was positioned correctly and comfortably on the headrest of a standard PET-CT scanner represented by Fig. 3E, which is critical for obtaining reliable motion data. Once the participant was in position, an eight-minute video recording session commenced, which is a typical acquisition time for a standard PET-CT [26].

Data acquisition and processing

For studies 1 and 2, motion data was collected under both normal breathing and breath-hold conditions using a depth camera (Orbbec FemtoMega) with a resolution of 640 x 576 pixels (for the narrow field of view unbinned mode) to capture 3D coordinates of infrared markers over time. Each video lasted approximately 30 s, corresponding to the scan time of WT-PET. For study 3, motion data was acquired equivalent to the scan time of 8 min on standard PET-CT. All recorded videos were submitted to an image processing pipeline to extract RGB, depth, and infrared channels. Figure 4A represents the extracted RGB, infrared and depth images for Study 3. The infrared (IR) images, in particular, were subjected to further pre-processing to isolate the markers from the background noise and other artifacts.

Infrared image pre-processing

The pre-processing of IR images involved following traditional image processing techniques:

Thresholding: This step helped to distinguish infrared markers from the rest of the image by converting the image into a binary format.

Connected component analysis: This technique was used to identify and label individual markers within the binary image. From the detected components (markers), 2D centroids were extracted using image moments (i.e., a weighted average of the particular pixel intensities present in the image), which are statistical properties of the image region. The calculation of these centroids is crucial for determining the precise location of each marker.

2D to 3D transformation

These 2D centroids are then converted into 3D centroids by using depth information obtained from the depth camera. A neighborhood around the specified 2D point was examined. This neighborhood was defined as a square area extending horizontally from ’x - radius’ to ’x + radius’ and vertically from ’y - radius’ to ’y + radius’. The squared Euclidean distance from the center point was computed for each pixel within this area. Pixels were included in the analysis based on two criteria: their squared distance from the center point had to be within the specified radius, and they had to have valid depth values (i.e., positive depth values and within the image bounds [576, 640]). This ensured that the depth values used for calculations were reliable and relevant. Valid depth values from the selected neighbourhood were collected into a list. Simultaneously, corresponding pixels in the depth image were highlighted to visualise the analysed region. This marking helped in tracking the region being processed and facilitated further analysis. The mean depth of the valid depth values was calculated. This average depth value served as a representative depth for the specified 2D point, providing a reliable measure for 3D transformation. The 2D coordinates were transformed into 3D spatial coordinates using the mean depth and intrinsic camera parameters. The transformations involved adjusting the 2D coordinates based on the camera’s focal length and principal point, scaled by the mean depth value. This process accurately mapped the 2D point into the 3D space, yielding coordinates in millimeters for all three dimensions. Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test revealed non-normal distributions for the head (p = 0.0088) and right shoulder (p = 0.0026) in the WT-PET data, while the left shoulder (p = 0.2437), chest (p = 0.0810), and abdomen (p = 0.1072) showed normal distributions. For the standard PET-CT scanner, non-normal distributions were found in the head (p = 0.0031), right shoulder (p = 0.0313), and abdomen (p = 0.0076), whereas the left shoulder (p = 0.2348) and chest (p = 0.2766) exhibited normal distributions.

Since the motion data for all markers does not follow a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test serves as a suitable choice for the evaluation of significant differences of the motion among markers between WT-PET Design II (Study 2A) and standard PET scanner (Study 3). The analysis revealed no significant differences in motion for the head (p = 0.1327) and abdomen (p = 0.8408) between the WT-PET Design II and standard PET scanners. However, significant differences were observed for the left shoulder (p = 0.0004), right shoulder (p = 0.0266), and chest (p<0.0001) markers, indicating increased motion in the WT-PET compared to the standard PET scanners. Table 3 summarizes these results and illustrates that motion in the left shoulder, chest, and right shoulder was significantly higher in the WT-PET compared to the standard PET scanner, while motion in the head and abdomen showed no significant differences. Discussion

The accuracy measurements indicate that the Orbbec Femto Mega demonstrates varying levels of accuracy across different directions and distances. In the x-direction, the camera maintains a high level of precision, with minimal deviations from the expected step size, as evidenced by the narrow IQR and low percentage error in Fig. 6. This suggests that the camera’s performance in detecting movements along the x-axis is reliable. However, in the y-direction, the accuracy is less consistent, particularly at closer distances (55 cm and 70 cm), where the camera tends to overestimate the step size. This may be due to limitations in the camera’s depth perception or calibration issues at these specific distances. The z-direction shows the most considerable discrepancies, especially at the 55 cm and 100 cm positions, where the measured step sizes are consistently lower than expected. The box plot analysis shown in Fig. 6 highlights these trends, with the broader IQRs and outliers in the y- and z-directions indicating variability in the measurements. These findings suggest that while the Orbbec Femto Mega depth camera performs well in certain conditions, its accuracy may be affected by specific factors such as directionality and distance from the target. These discrepancies are less than 0.2 mms, which is only one-tenth of the spatial resolution of the WT-PET scanner. Therefore, the Orbbec Femto Mega is well-suited for estimating motion in this application.

The analysis of motion profiles as represented by Fig. 7 revealed significant variability in the movement of different anatomical markers, with the abdomen exhibiting the most pronounced motion. This finding aligns with the natural movement patterns of the abdominal region during respiration, where diaphragmatic breathing induces considerable displacement. In contrast, the head and shoulder markers displayed less motion, which is consistent with the expected stability of these areas, particularly when participants are in a standing position. In addition, the abdomen’s higher AAD values indicate more motion than the head and shoulders, as evident from Fig. 8A and Fig. 8B, emphasizing the need for specialized attention in scanner design and operation. These findings suggest that implementing motion correction algorithms or gating techniques could be particularly advantageous for the abdominal region in enhancing PET image quality. Such techniques could effectively mitigate motion artifacts, which are critical for achieving high-resolution and accurate diagnostic images [27].

Moreover, the Euclidean distance analysis reinforced these observations by showing that breath-hold conditions generally resulted in reduced movement compared to normal breathing, as can be seen in Fig. 9A. This suggests that breath-holding could be an effective method for minimizing motion artifacts during imaging. However, it is important to acknowledge that breath-holding may not be feasible for all patients, particularly those with respiratory conditions or difficulties. Therefore, alternative strategies or adaptive techniques might be necessary for such populations. A comprehensive understanding of the extent and nature of motion across different anatomical regions is essential for guiding the design improvements of the WT-PET scanner.

The statistical analysis highlights that the WT-PET system leads to more motion in specific body regions, particularly the shoulders and chest, compared to the standard PET scanner. This increased motion is likely due to the more open and less restrictive design of the WT-PET, which gives subjects greater freedom to move, especially in the upper body. Both shoulders and the chest exhibited significantly more movement in the WT-PET system, suggesting that the less confined environment may lead to more relaxed breathing and involuntary or voluntary movement. This poses challenges for image quality, as motion artifacts could reduce the clarity of PET images and impact diagnostic accuracy. To address this, patient positioning and support in the WT-PET system may need to be adjusted, potentially with more targeted restraints or breathing control protocols [28] to minimize upper body movement. On the other hand, the head and abdomen showed no significant differences in motion between the two systems, indicating that both scanners manage head motion effectively, which is crucial for brain imaging. The stability of abdominal motion across systems may be due to similar lower body positioning, where breathing patterns play a key role. Moreover, from Table 2, it can be observed that the discrepancy of motion change for each marker between the WT-PET and mCT scanner is not more than 1 mm, which indicates the reliability of the use of the WT-PET scanner mock-up for future studies. Subsequently, the box plot (Fig 10) visually supports these findings, highlighting the median, quartiles, and potential outliers in motion data for each marker position. The box plot shows greater spread and variability in motion for the shoulders and chest in the WT-PET scanner compared to the standard scanner, while the head and abdomen regions show comparable distributions.

In addition, the paired differences plot represented by Fig 11 highlights individualized motion changes across body markers when comparing the WT-PET and mCT systems. Notably, the chest marker shows an overall upward trend, suggesting that the WT-PET scanner induces greater motion in the chest region. This could be due to the scanner’s open design, allowing for more movement compared to the confined space of the standard PET. For the head and right shoulder, there is a downward trend, indicating reduced motion in the standard PET scanner. The left shoulder and abdomen markers show more variability. The mixed trends for the left shoulder may reflect differences in individual comfort or positioning, while the minimal motion change in the abdomen indicates similar behaviour between the two scanners in this region.

The increased motion observed in the WT-PET mockup scanner, especially in the chest and shoulders, underscores the need for more sophisticated motion correction technologies in next-generation PET scanners. Current PET systems rely on post-scan corrections or patient restraints to mitigate motion artifacts, but these methods may not be fully effective in open systems like WT-PET. To address this, future scanner design will integrate real-time motion tracking and correction technologies. [29] These could involve external markers, infrared cameras, or even electromagnetic sensors to continuously monitor patient movement during the scan. This data could be fed into advanced algorithms that adjust the imaging parameters in real time, ensuring that motion artifacts are minimized without requiring repeated scans [30]. The use of machine learning models to predict and compensate for motion could also be explored, allowing for more robust image reconstruction despite significant patient movement. [31] Similarly, adaptive imaging protocols that adjust based on the specific body region being scanned or the individual patient’s likelihood of movement could improve both image quality and scanning efficiency. For instance, in regions where motion is more pronounced, such as the shoulders or chest, faster acquisition protocols could be implemented to reduce the impact of movement on image resolution. By synchronizing image acquisition with the patient’s natural motion (e.g., respiratory gating), it could be possible to get clearer, more precise images in these challenging areas.

Overall, the WT-PET scanner shows promise in significantly improving patient throughput and reducing scan times; further refinements are needed to address the motion issues in specific anatomical regions. Future studies will focus on optimizing patient positioning and exploring advanced motion correction algorithms to enhance the scanner’s overall performance.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of patient motion using a WT-PET scanner mock-up, focusing on the movement of different anatomical markers under normal breathing and breath-hold conditions. The findings highlight the substantial variability in motion across different body parts, with the shoulders and chest showing the most pronounced movement in the WT-PET scanner as compared to the standard PET scanner due to the open and less restrictive design of the WT-PET. In contrast, the head and abdomen regions demonstrated relatively stable motion in both scanners.
