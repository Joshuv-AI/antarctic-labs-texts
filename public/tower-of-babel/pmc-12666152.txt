Second-event endpoints (EFS2, PRFS2 and PFS2) after anti-PD-(L)1-based RCTs: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Pablo Jimenez-Labaig, Oriol Mirallas, Ana Isabel Martin-Quesada, Antonio Rullan, Dario Trapani, Teresa Amaral, Enriqueta Felip, Josep Tabernero, Kevin J Harrington
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly anti-PD-(L)1s, have transformed cancer care by their extended efficacy, even receiving next-line. Event-free survival 2 (EFS2), progression/recurrence-free survival 2 (PRFS2) and progression-free survival 2 (PFS2) capture the time from randomization to objective recurrence/progression or death on the first subsequent therapy, potentially offering a more accurate measure of durable benefit than first-event endpoints. Our aim is to review the magnitude of this benefit and evaluate long-term endpoints as surrogates for overall survival (OS) in immunotherapy for solid malignancies.MethodsA Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis searched Web of Science, National Institutes of Health Clinical Trials Registry, American Society of Clinical Oncology and European Society of Medical Oncology conference databases from inception until June 12, 2025, for anti-PD-(L)1-based randomized controlled phase II/III clinical trials (RCTs) in patients with solid malignancies that reported EFS2, PRFS2 or PFS2. HRs comparing ICI and non-ICI arms were pooled using a random-effects meta-analytic approach. Sensitivity analyses and meta-regressions assessed effect modifiers. A linear regression compared EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 and EFS/disease-free survival (DFS)/PFS versus OS HRs.Results47 RCTs met inclusion criteria from 2,078 citations, comprising 58 intervention comparisons (ICs) and 34,973 patients. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) was the most investigated tumor (17 RCTs). Anti-PD-(L)1s performed generally worse when administered as a subsequent therapy in non-ICI-experienced patients (p<0.05). Early anti-PD-(L)1 use was associated with significantly improved EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.68 to 0.76), with consistent findings across curative and recurrent/metastatic settings, and particularly strong effects in RCTs involving patients with deficient mismatch repair or high microsatellite instability tumors and those with more radiological responses. In 54 ICs reporting both OS and EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 HRs, a strong correlation (R²=0.74) was observed across all RCTs, and an even stronger 0.86 specifically among patients with NSCLC (22 ICs). Conversely, EFS/DFS/PFS presented R² 0.39 across all RCTs (51 ICs), and 0.65 in NSCLC (21 ICs; p<0.01).ConclusionsEarly use of anti-PD-(L)1 therapy is associated with prolonged disease control and may be linked to enhanced responsiveness to subsequent therapies, mostly based on NSCLC data. Additionally, EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 are solid candidate surrogates for OS and could be considered for routine inclusion and prespecified into immunotherapy RCTs to better capture long-term benefit and inform regulatory, clinical, and reimbursement decision-making.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024585378.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Solid tumor

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized oncology by unblocking physiological immune downregulation through pathways such as the programmed cell death (ligand) 1 axis (PD-(L)1), which cancer cells exploit to survive.1 When a response is achieved, clinically and/or radiologically, it is commonly sustained longer than responses to chemotherapy, sometimes lasting years.2,4 A key driver of this long-term antitumor activity is the development and maintenance of memory T cells.5 6

Several factors may influence the probability of achieving durable disease control. These include patient age, performance status, comorbidities, concomitant medications, and disease-related factors (disease burden and location, intrinsic molecular biology, immune gene signatures and previous treatments).7 The timing of ICI administration may also be crucial.8 9 As with most new agents, ICIs were initially investigated in refractory palliative settings,3 and have since been increasingly incorporated into earlier lines and curative settings across multiple histologies.4

A beneficial impact from prior ICI therapy on subsequent outcomes, compared with not having received prior ICIs, was first reported in studies of ipilimumab for melanoma,10 11 where fewer patients needed further treatment lines. Similarly, in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), pembrolizumab showed improved progression-free survival (PFS) from randomization to the second progression.2 These findings support early ICI use, potentially prolonging control by targeting residual clones or conditioning the tumor microenvironment.12 13

EFS2 (event-free survival 2), PRFS2 (progression/recurrence-free survival 2), and PFS2 generally define the time from randomization to objective disease progression or death on the first subsequent therapy, in the curative and recurrent/metastatic (R/M) settings, respectively, which are emerging as valuable endpoints. They may better reflect long-term immunotherapy effects and correlate more closely with overall survival (OS) than EFS, disease-free survival (DFS) and PFS, supporting their potential to enhance the speed and accuracy of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) evaluations.14 15 To date, no study has compiled and assessed the post-ICI EFS2, PRFS2 and PFS2 benefits in their corresponding treatment settings across RCTs in oncology.

To address these gaps, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the benefit of prior anti-PD-(L)1 therapy on EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 in RCTs involving patients with solid tumors, compared with those who did not receive anti-PD-(L)1 drugs; examine modifying demographic, clinical, treatment settings, or methodological factors; and assess correlation strength with OS compared with EFS/DFS/PFS.

Methods

This study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024585378) and conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA),16 Meta-analyses Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE)17 (online supplemental table S1), and Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of Health Research (EQUATOR) guidelines.18

Search methodology, data extraction and quality assessment

Web of Science was searched from inception to October 7, 2024. Databases from the American Society of Clinical Oncology, the European Society of Medical Oncology, and the National Institutes of Health Registry of Clinical Trials were also consulted. The search was updated through June 12, 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: RCTs involving patients with solid tumors, with at least one arm receiving anti-PD-(L)1 either as monotherapy or in combination with other anticancer treatments, in any treatment setting (curative or palliative); at least one arm not receiving anti-PD-(L)1-based therapy; data reporting on EFS2, PRFS2, or PFS2, and their HR for both ICI-experienced and non-ICI-experienced patients; efficacy assessed based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 or iRECIST, with no language restrictions. Crossover trials were eligible if randomization occurred before subsequent therapy. Exclusion criteria were as follows: publications reporting <30 patients treated in each arm; studies assessing ICIs other than anti-PD-(L)1s.

Search terms, data extraction and synthesis strategy are presented in online supplemental table S2. The risk of bias was assessed using the RoB2 tool.19

Statistical analyses

Meta-analyses were conducted when at least three different intervention comparisons (ICs) with distinct control arms were available per each tumor type and treatment setting. The primary outcome was the pooled HR for EFS2, PRFS2, or PFS2 comparing ICI versus non-ICI treatments, using the natural logarithm of the HR and its SE. Random-effects models were used due to expected heterogeneity. Trials with shared control arms were adjusted to avoid double-counting by dividing the control population between ICs.

Sensitivity analyses and meta-regressions were applied to assess the impact of qualitative and quantitative variables when data were available from at least 10 studies. A negative β estimator indicated a greater increase in the difference in second-event endpoint between patients who previously received ICI and those who did not. In contrast, a positive β estimator suggested a smaller difference relative to the variable being assessed.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² index (0–25% interpreted as low, >25–50% moderate, >50–75% substantial, >75% considerable). Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plots and Egger’s test (if ≥10 studies),20 and trim-and-fill correction was applied when bias was suspected.21 Linear regressions were performed, comparing coefficient of determination (R²) values based on log-transformed HRs of EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 and EFS/DFS/EFS versus OS, adjusting for their 95% CIs. All analyses were performed using R V.4.3.1,22 considering statistically significant a two-sided p value<0.05.

Results

From 2,078 citations, 47 RCTs were included, comprising 58 ICs (PRISMA flowchart in online supplemental table S2). In total, 34,973 patients were analyzed (19,300 in the experimental arm receiving immunotherapy alone or in combination), with median follow-up ranging 9.1–73.7 months23,69 (figure 1).

Most RCTs were phase III (trials, k=44, 94%), with the remainder being phase II. 11 RCTs treated 8,148 patients in the curative (6 adjuvant, 4 postchemoradiation, 1 neoadjuvant), while 36 focused on R/M setting (n=26,825) of which 33 were first-line (online supplemental table S3 and S4). Overall, PFS2 data were reported in 36 RCTs with 47 ICs, while EFS2 and PRFS2 data were available in eight and three trials, respectively. Median age ranged from 54 to 68. Overall, 37% were female (k=47), 80% were current/people who have formerly smoked (k=25), 25% had squamous histology (k=47), 48% had recurrent tumors (k=20), and 50% had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 1 (k=40). NSCLC was the most investigated tumor type (k=17, 36%), followed by melanoma (k=5, 11%) and renal cell (k=4, 9%). Depending on the trial, what was considered as “high” (in trials with multiple PD-L1-positive subgroups) or at least “positive” tumor PD-L1 expression was observed in 49% of patients (k=34), with five trials (n=1583; 5% of total sample) enrolling only PD-L1-positive cases. Two studies (n=610, 2% of the sample) included only patients with somatic deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-high). Additional histology and race data are in online supplemental table S2.

Overall, 70% of patients received anti-PD-1, mainly pembrolizumab (k=16, 34%). Across the 58 experimental arms, 25 included chemotherapy, mostly platinum-based, 13 anti-CTLA-4, 8 antiangiogenic agents, 2 BRAF and PARP inhibitors, and one androgen receptor targeted agent.

39 RCTs reported subsequent therapies. Among patients naïve to anti-PD-(L)1, 48% received next-line systemic therapy (45% chemotherapy, 54% immunotherapy). In contrast, 32% of ICI-experienced patients received further treatment (63% chemotherapy, 19% immunotherapy).

Second-event endpoints were assessed post hoc in 31 studies and as a secondary endpoint in 16, reported across 22 abstracts, 17 articles, and 8 ClinicalTrials.gov entries. Concerning censoring rules for second-event endpoints, 27 RCTs did not specify them for second-event endpoints, whereas the rest of trials censored patients alive without progression at their last known alive date; three trials also censored patients not initiating, discontinuing, or not starting subsequent therapy (online supplemental table S3 and S4). In regard to risk of bias, two trials showed a high risk28 61 due to the lack of blinded independent central review (BICR) for imaging assessments during both the first and the subsequent treatment. The rest raised some concerns mainly due to absent BICR in next-line (k=45), and unblinded prior-line treatments (k=28). A domain-by-domain breakdown of the RoB 2 assessments is presented in online supplemental table S5.

All tumor types and treatment settings

Across the 58 ICs, prior anti-PD-(L)1 was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of second disease progression/relapse, with a pooled HR of 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.76; I²=64%) (figure 2) (online supplemental table S6).

Meta-regressions showed that studies with a higher overall response rate (ORR) in the initial ICI arm were linked to a lower risk of subsequent progression (β=−0.33, 95% CI −0.62 to 0.05, p=0.02). Regarding subsequent treatments, 54% of patients in the non-ICI arms received subsequent immunotherapy, compared with only 19% in the ICI arms. This variable was assessed in a meta-regression, which showed that studies with higher proportions of non-ICI-experienced individuals receiving subsequent immunotherapy presented worse outcomes (β=−0.26, 95% CI −0.47 to −0.05, p=0.01) increasing the pooled EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 difference between ICI and non-ICI arms. Finally, trials with older patient groups exhibited a decreased benefit from immunotherapy overall (β=0.02, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.03, p=0.02) (online supplemental table S7).

When data were stratified by tumor type, significant differences were observed (p<0.01). The greatest risk reduction was seen in colorectal cancer (including patients with dMMR/MSI-high tumors), NSCLC and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, while the smallest benefit was observed in prostate and ovarian cancers. Similar pooled HRs were observed when data were stratified by treatment setting: curative (EFS2/PRFS2; 11 ICs) versus R/M (PFS2; 47 ICs), with HRs of 0.72 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.80) and 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.76), respectively (online supplemental table S8).

A higher proportion of patients with dMMR/MSI-high was linked to greater benefit (β=−0.48, 95% CI −0.80 to −0.17, p<0.01). Excluding these studies, anti-PD-(L)1s remained protective across 43 RCTs and 53 ICs (pooled HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.77; I²=60%) (online supplemental figure S1A).

Recurrent/metastatic setting

Through 36 RCTs and 47 ICs, the pooled HR for PFS2 was 0.72 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.76; I²=68%). Omitting trials that enrolled patients with dMMR/MSI-high (k=29), HR remained significant at 0.73 (95% CI 0.69 to 0.77; I²=65%) (online supplemental figure S1 and table S6).

As previously shown, higher ORR (β=−0.39, 95% CI −0.68 to −0.10) and dMMR/MSI-high status (β=−0.49, 95% CI −0.82 to −0.16) were associated with greater PFS2 benefit from early ICI (p<0.01), while age reduced it. In this setting, the proportion of patients receiving subsequent immunotherapy ranged from 17% in the ICI-based arms to 53% in the non-ICI-based arms, with trials having higher subsequent immunotherapy use in control arms showing worse PFS2 outcomes (β=−0.32, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.07; p=0.01). Tumor-specific results matched previous analyses (online supplemental table S7 and S8).

Curative setting

In the 11 curative-intent RCTs, the pooled HR for EFS2/PRFS2 was 0.72 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.80; I²=27%), with a funnel plot that did not indicate publication bias. No statistically significant meta-regressions were found (online supplemental figure S1, table S6 and S7).

Non-small cell lung cancer

Across 17 RCTs and 22 ICs compiling curative and R/M settings, anti-PD-(L)1-based arms showed a pooled significant risk reduction of subsequent progression of 0.73 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.79; I²=69%) (figure 3). Regarding the specifically curative setting, 4 RCTs and ICs showed a pooled HR of 0.75 (95% CI 0.53 to 1.05; I²=59%); while in the R/M setting, 13 RCTs reported 18 ICs, with a pooled HR of 0.73 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.80; I²=72%) (online supplemental table S6).

As previously reported, higher ORR in ICI arms was linked to greater PFS2 differences (β=−0.82, 95% CI −1.39 to −0.26, p<0.01). In this setting, 16% vs 56% of patients received subsequent immunotherapy in the ICI and non-ICI arms, respectively. Similarly to prior analyses, more patients receiving subsequent ICI in control arms increased PFS2 differences (β=−0.66, 95% CI −1.23 to −0.09, p=0.03), reflecting reduced benefit compared with early ICI (online supplemental table S7 and S8).

Cutaneous malignant melanoma

Across five RCTs with six ICs, prior ICI significantly increased the EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 (HR=0.67, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.74). In the adjuvant melanoma subgroup (k=3), the pooled EFS2/PRFS2 HR was 0.69 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.83), while in R/M melanoma (k=2, 3 ICs), PFS2 HR was 0.63 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.89). Heterogeneity was low, although the small number of studies limits certainty. Visual analysis of the funnel plot did not suggest publication bias (online supplemental figure S1, S2 and S6).

As a general insight across all these meta-analyses, even after adjustment using the trim-and-fill method in the presence of publication bias, a statistically significant effect favoring anti-PD-(L)1-based arms persisted (online supplemental figure S2 and table S6). Second, anti-PD-1 agents consistently showed more favorable pooled HRs than anti-PD-L1 agents, especially in R/M NSCLC (HR=0.67 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.73) vs 0.83 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.92); p<0.01). Second-event endpoints estimates varied by data source (p<0.01), with peer-reviewed journals showing lower and more favorable HRs than ClinicalTrials.gov across all analysis groups. The largest difference was observed in the R/M NSCLC analysis, with a pooled HR of 0.67 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.73) vs 0.87 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.97; p<0.01). In addition, post hoc analyses in NSCLC also showed stronger effects than predefined secondary analyses (p<0.05) (online supplemental table S7).

Lastly, no significant associations were found between pooled HRs and risk of bias, sample size, patient or tumor characteristics, follow-up, trial phase, and adding anti-CTLA-4 or chemotherapy across analyses (online supplemental table S7 and S8).

EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 correlation with overall survival

A total of 54 ICs were found, presenting both OS and EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2, observing an R² of 0.74 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.86; p<0.01), markedly higher than the R² of 0.39 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.61; p<0.01) for OS versus EFS/DFS/PFS in 51 ICs. This trend was even more pronounced in trials involving patients with NSCLC, where the correlation between OS and EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 endpoints was exceptionally strong (R²=0.86; 95% CI 0.75 to 0.98; p<0.01) across 22 ICs, as compared with an R² of 0.65 (95% CI 0.39 to 0.91; p<0.01) for EFS/DFS/PFS in 21 ICs (figure 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis examining the effect of prior anti-PD-(L)1 on outcomes of next-line therapy after relapse or progression in patients with solid tumors. It includes extensive data from 47 RCTs, enrolling 34,973 patients with different tumor types, treatment settings and geographic regions. Several findings emerge.

First, the study provides further evidence of the durable benefit of immunotherapy.70 The pooled HR for second-event endpoints, across comparisons by tumor type and treatment setting, was consistently associated with better outcomes when patients received prior ICI, despite study heterogeneity. While this does not imply that anti-PD-(L)1 therapy should be given to all patients with cancer regardless of clinical context or treatment setting; it highlights their potential to exert treatment effects beyond their administration period.71

Second, while sensitivity analyses showed no significant difference between curative and R/M RCTs, likely due to fewer number of curative RCTs (11 vs 36), a higher proportion of patients in non-anti-PD-(L)1 arms later receiving immunotherapy was associated with worse EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 in meta-regressions. A global 15% reduction in the use of subsequent systemic therapies was also observed in patients who received early ICI compared with those in the control arms. This suggests that delayed anti-PD-(L)1 use might be associated with reduced effectiveness. Promoting an initial adaptive immune response may help control metastatic disease, including microscopic deposits, or reshape the tumor microenvironment to enhance responsiveness to later therapies, prolonging disease control.8 72 73 However, this interpretation should be viewed in light of the postprogression imbalance in the proportion of patients receiving subsequent immunotherapy (54% in non-anti-PD-(L)1 arms vs 19% in anti-PD-(L)1 arms). One of the strongest factors correlated with improved second-event endpoints was the dMMR/MSI-high disease. High neoantigen loads from defective DNA repair are strong predictors of ICI response.12 74 75 Studies including these patients showed the greatest delays to second progression, supporting that early immunotherapy may have lasting antitumor effects. Beyond the relative rarity of dMMR/MSI-high disease, those studies that appeared to derive greatest overall benefit trials from ICIs were Keynote-024 assessing immunotherapy in patients with ≥50% PD-L1 NSCLC tumors, and Keynote-189 and RATIONALE-309 which evaluated chemoimmunotherapy combinations in NSCLC and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (single study).47 56 76 Conversely, prostate and ovarian carcinomas were the cancer types with the lowest apparent benefit. As might be expected, the meta-analysis of five RCTs in malignant melanoma showed one of the lowest pooled HRs, reaffirming anti-PD-(L)1 as a cornerstone of its treatment.5

Somewhat unexpectedly, PD-L1 expression did not significantly modify outcomes across studies. However, trials with the greatest HR benefits were those that selected patients based on this predictive biomarker, such as Keynote-024.54 This apparent contradiction may be explicable in terms of the fact that the aggregate analyses merged different PD-L1 thresholds, assessment methodologies and included large number of trials that did not select patients based on PD-L1 expression.77

This work also underscores a positive influence of ORR when ICI rather than non-ICI based therapy is administered first. While radiological response in control arms showed no significant correlation with PFS2, tumor shrinkage after ICI was consistently associated with longer time to second progression, especially observed in R/M NSCLC. A strong initial response may indicate effective immune activation and clone elimination, leading to extended disease control across subsequent lines of therapy. These effects were not mirrored in chemotherapy arms, which could reflect differences in mechanisms of action of ICIs and their ability to modify tumor microenvironment.78 79

Our analysis revealed interesting differences in outcomes according to type of immunotherapy administered. Sensitivity analysis revealed statistically significant differences among ICI drugs, with anti-PD-1 being associated with better outcomes than anti-PD-L1. This finding highlights, but does not conclude the still open debate on differences among ICIs.80 As 71% of patients in the included RCTs received anti-PD-1 agents, this may also reflect sample imbalance. Additionally, patient characteristics also had an impact, with older patient having worse outcomes, in line with prior literature reports.81

Although the addition of CTLA-4 inhibitors, chemotherapy and/or other targeted agents to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy has shown favorable outcomes in certain scenarios,47 67 72 our sensitivity analyses found no significant added impact on EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2, even in more homogeneous subgroups like R/M NSCLC. This might be explained by trial heterogeneity, non-protocolized sequencing, or cumulative toxicities reducing tolerance and survival in subsequent therapies.79

Finally, although OS remains the gold standard for evaluating antineoplastic treatments, our findings underscore the potential of EFS2, PRFS2, and PFS2 as valuable surrogates. This is supported by the presented linear regressions showing solid model performance explaining up to 74% of OS variability in all tumor types, and 86% within NSCLC, though they still do not fully account for all variability in OS. Unlike OS, they are not less affected by per-protocol crossovers and subsequent therapies, making them valuable intermediate endpoints.14 In addition, OS requires a long follow-up period to reach maturity, and results are often confounded by postprogression therapies. However, these endpoints are not frequently investigated or predefined as secondary endpoints, even in positive trials, despite their growing relevance in regulatory and reimbursement approvals as osimertinib, olaparib, ribociclib, and durvalumab.82

This study has limitations to consider. First, while prior ICI therapy appears to be associated with better second-event endpoints, causality remains uncertain due to heterogenous censoring rules from non-protocolized subsequent treatments, post hoc radiological monitoring without BICR, and the use of shared control arms across ICs. Meta-analyses using individual patient-level data or platform trials with predefined subsequent treatments could yield more accurate efficacy results for later therapy lines and address this limitation, while also improving the ability of EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2 endpoints to capture OS.83 Regarding censoring rules, their heterogeneity in second-event endpoints likely introduced informative censoring and drove the marked between-arm imbalance in postprogression immunotherapy, with implications for statistical analyses adding potential overestimation of immunotherapy effects.

Next, moderate to substantial heterogeneity was generally observed. Variability existed even within the same tumor groups, with variations in initial treatment strategies, PD-L1 expression, MMR/MSI status and follow-up durations (with potential attrition among studies). The limited number of studies reporting EFS2 and PRFS2 hampers direct comparison with PFS2 data, introducing heterogeneity in the combined endpoint analysis of EFS2/PRFS2/PFS2. Nonetheless, our broad inclusion criteria improved the external validity and representativeness of our findings. We tried to mitigate these issues by studying the effect of these variables through meta-regressions and sensitivity analyses. Finally, significant publication bias was found for several of the studies’ outcomes. In this context, data from ClinicalTrials.gov showed significant differences compared with those in peer-reviewed publications or conference proceedings, suggesting that negative trials often remain unpublished.84 However, after trim-and-fill statistics were applied, assuming symmetric metrics, no statistically significant differences were found in the pooled HR for those outcomes.

In conclusion, our study highlights that anti-PD-(L)1 agents are associated with prolonged disease control beyond initial treatment failure as a general insight in solid tumor oncology. These findings contribute to the ongoing discussion on treatment sequencing and suggest that earlier incorporation of ICIs may be linked to longer-term benefits, particularly in patients with recurrent or metastatic NSCLC, where the evidence was strongest. As the field evolves, EFS2, PRFS2 and PFS2 are meaningful surrogates for OS, and should be considered as additional prespecified endpoints in RCTs to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term benefits of immunotherapy in cancer care.
