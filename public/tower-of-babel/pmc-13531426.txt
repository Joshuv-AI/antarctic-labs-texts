1,2-Diketones as in situ persistent radical precursors: symmetric and unsymmetric diacylation of alkenes under visible light
Authors: Jiayuan Wang, Jiacong Guo, Chao-Jun Li, Huiying Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radicals are key reactive intermediates in organic synthesis; however, their high reactivity often undermines selectivity in conventional systems. The persistent radical effect (PRE) has emerged as a powerful strategy to mitigate this limitation by enabling sustained, selective radical transformations. Herein, we report a catalyst-free, visible-light-enabled alkene diacylation in which 1,2-diketones serve as in situ precursors to persistent α-ketyl radicals, enabling access to both symmetric and unsymmetric 1,4-diketones. For symmetric products, 1,2-diketone generates the persistent α-ketyl radical, while photoinduced homolysis of a second diketone provides a transient acyl radical; regioselective addition affords an α-hydroxyketone that undergoes Norrish type I fragmentation with aldehyde extrusion. For unsymmetric products, SET redox exchange between 1,2-diketones and α-keto acids generates the persistent radical and an acyl radical, and sequential radical addition gives unsymmetric 1,4-diketones. This strategy obviates the need for transition-metal or N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) catalysis and enables direct access to persistent α-ketyl radicals under mild, catalyst-free photochemical conditions. Collectively, these results expand the transformation chemistry of 1,2-diketones and establish them as readily available, inexpensive persistent acyl radical equivalents for selective radical synthesis.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, rapid advances in radical chemistry have established radical–radical cross-coupling (RRC) as a powerful strategy for constructing carbon–carbon and carbon–heteroatom bonds.1–7 Despite its step economy and broad functional-group compatibility, the high reactivity and short lifetime of many radical intermediates often lead to poor regio- and chemoselectivity in conventional reaction systems, significantly limiting their utility in precision synthesis.8 To address these challenges, the persistent radical strategy has emerged as an enabling concept, leveraging enhanced radical stability and improved selectivity to achieve controllable radical transformations.9–13

Most approaches to persistent radicals rely on intramolecular electron delocalization to stabilize the radical center. A landmark example dates back to Gomberg's observation of the triphenylmethyl radical under metal- and catalyst-free conditions in 1900.14 Since then, various other persistent radicals have been reported, including nitroxide radicals,15,16 TEMPO,17–20 cyanopyridinyl radical anions,21–26 and α-ketoester radical anions,27–32 among others33–35 (Scheme 1a). These precedents have inspired the design of new persistent-radical manifolds. For example, N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) catalysis can generate Breslow intermediates that undergo single-electron transfer to afford persistent acyl radical equivalents (Scheme 1b).36–45 More recently, Aggarwal and co-workers reported the use of persistent boryl radicals as highly reductive photoredox catalysts, further highlighting the potential of persistent radicals in organic catalysis.46 Despite these advances, the structural diversity of known persistent radicals remains limited relative to the vast array of transient radicals, and their use as readily accessible radical precursors or catalytic mediators is still underdeveloped. Accordingly, the development of structurally tunable, readily available persistent radical precursors remains a fundamental objective and is expected to broaden the scope of selective radical transformations.

Design of 1,2-diketone persistent radicals for alkene dicarbonylation.

Carbonyl compounds are among the most extensively studied substrates in photochemistry.47–53 The photochemistry of ketones can be traced to the seminal Norrish type I and type II photoreactions discovered by Norrish and his contemporaries in the mid-20th century.54–59 These classic processes established that excited-state ketones can undergo either α-cleavage to generate acyl–alkyl radical pairs or intramolecular γ-hydrogen abstraction to form 1,4-biradical intermediates, thereby laying the mechanistic foundation for carbonyl radical chemistry. In this context, acyl radicals have long been recognized as versatile intermediates for C–C bond formation. However, despite the broad range of available precursors—including aldehydes, carboxylic acids, anhydrides, acyl chlorides, and α-keto acids—acyl radicals generated by conventional methods typically exhibit high reactivity and short lifetimes.60–62 These intrinsic properties often result in poor regio- and chemoselectivity, as well as inefficient radical–radical cross-coupling under catalyst-free conditions, thereby limiting their broader application in synthetic transformations. 1,2-Diketones represent a unique class of carbonyl compounds; by virtue of their distinctive dicarbonyl framework and extended conjugation, they are valuable building blocks in organic synthesis and materials science. They have been widely used in heterocycle synthesis and as photoinitiators, among other applications.63,64 Despite these advances, current studies of 1,2-diketones remain comparatively limited and have largely focused on reductive coupling65–68 and deoxygenative functionalization reactions69,70 whereas their potential in synergistic dicarbonyl activation is still unexplored. Recently, Zhuo and co-workers reported a molybdenum-catalyzed C(O)–C(O) bond cleavage, offering a new entry to 1,2-diketone activation.71 Notably, radical pathways for such bond cleavage have not been reported, nor has the use of 1,2-diketones as precursors to persistent acyl-radical equivalents. Building on our studies of photochemical reactions,72–79 we envisioned a persistent-radical-enabled radical-relay strategy in which persistent α-carbonyl radicals are generated from 1,2-diketones via hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) or single electron transfer (SET), wherein acyl radicals are produced either by SET oxidation/decarboxylation of α-keto acids or by photolytic homolysis of 1,2-diketones. In this design, the acyl radical addition to an alkene generates a transient alkyl radical that is subsequently intercepted by the persistent α-carbonyl radical to form an α-hydroxyketone intermediate; a Norrish type-I fragmentation then extrudes an aldehyde to deliver the corresponding 1,4-dicarbonyl products. We reasoned that this persistent-radical-enabled relay, employing readily available 1,2-diketones as persistent acyl radical precursors, could enable both symmetric and unsymmetric diacylation of alkenes under mild conditions without transition-metal catalysts or NHC catalysis.80–89

Results and discussion

To evaluate the feasibility of this hypothesis, 1,2-di-p-tolylethane-1,2-dione (1a) and styrene (1b) were selected as model substrates to optimize the diacylation conditions (Table 1). Under 405 nm irradiation in MeCN, 1,4-diketone 1c was obtained in 52% yield. In contrast, shorter-wavelength (380 or 365 nm) irradiation was less effective, and no product was detected with longer-wavelength (425 or 465 nm) light or in the dark, underscoring the wavelength dependence of the process (entries 1–6). Solvent investigation revealed MeCN to be optimal; CHCl3, acetone, ethyl acetate, and DMSO each diminished the yield (entries 7–10). Addition of sodium formate markedly improved the yield (entries 11–14); when 0.4 equivalents of sodium formate were used, the highest yield was obtained (entry 12), consistent with formation of a persistent radical intermediate from 1a with sodium formate. Notably, the addition of a small amount of water further increased the yield to 86% (entry 15). This improvement may be attributed to the enhanced solubility of sodium formate in the presence of water.

Following the establishment of optimal reaction conditions, we examined the scope of alkenes (Table 2). A broad range of styrenes was compatible, and diverse functional groups were well tolerated. Styrenes bearing electron-donating substituents (–Me, –Et, –tBu, –Ph, and –OMe) on the aryl ring afforded the desired products (1c–5c) in good to excellent yields. Halogen- and CF3-substituted styrenes also performed well, delivering 6c–9c without erosion of the C–X bonds. Notably, sensitive functional groups such as TMS (10c) and hydroxyl (11c) were tolerated. Furthermore, multisubstituted (12c) and meta-substituted (13c) alkenes underwent smooth transformation, and even the sterically hindered ortho-methyl substituted styrene furnished 14c efficiently. Heteroaromatic alkenes (dibenzofuran and pyridine-derived substrates) as well as α-methylstyrenes were viable, providing products 15c–17c in good yields. Unactivated alkenes provided the desired adducts (18c and 19c), albeit with diminished efficiency. We next examined the scope of aryl 1,2-diketones. Both electron-donating groups (–Me, –tBu, and –OMe) and halogen-containing (–F, –Cl, and –Br) diketones furnished the corresponding products (20c–25c) in good to high yields. The ortho-methoxy substituted diketone was also competent, providing product 26c in good yield. However, photolysis of unsymmetrical diketone 9a generates two acyl radicals of comparable reactivity through homolytic cleavage, resulting in poor regioselectivity. To characterize the resulting product distribution, the four regioisomers 20c, 22c, 27c, and 28c were successfully separated by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (prep-HPLC) and isolated in satisfactory yields.

To overcome the challenge of selectively accessing unsymmetrical diacylation products, we designed a dual-radical strategy that combines a transient acyl radical with a persistent radical derived from 1,2-diketones. Because these two radical classes differ markedly in reactivity and lifetime, we anticipated their reactivities could enable regioselective addition to an alkene. In an initial proof-of-concept experiment conducted under 405 nm light irradiation, pyruvic acid (transient acyl precursor), diketone 4a (persistent radical source), and styrene in MeCN in the presence of Cs2CO3 afforded the unsymmetrical diacylation product 29c in high yield, with only minor formation of the symmetrical byproduct 22c. This result confirmed the feasibility of the dual-radical cooperative control process. Guided by this outcome, we systematically optimized the reaction conditions (please see details in the SI) to establish an efficient and selective protocol for unsymmetrical diacylation.

Under the optimized reaction conditions, we investigated the scope of the unsymmetrical diacylation (Table 3). Initial studies focused on alkyl α-keto acids, revealing that pyruvic acid and its derivatives performed efficiently, generating the desired products (29c–31c) in high yields. Introducing bulkier substituents at the β-position had no appreciable effect on the reaction outcome, affording products 32c and 33c in high yields. In contrast, when the substituent is located at the α-position, the reaction pathway diverges. Instead of affording the desired bisacyl products, the process undergoes sequential decarboxylation and decarbonylation to generate the corresponding alkyl radical intermediates for further transformations (see the SI for details). We then extended the protocol to aryl glyoxylic acids; the parent substrate delivered 27c in high yield. Aryl glyoxylic acids bearing electron-donating substituents (–Me and –OMe) reacted smoothly to give corresponding products (34c–35c and 28c) in good to high yields. Halogen-containing (p-F, p-Cl, and p-Br) substrates were likewise compatible, providing products 36c–38c in good to high yields.

Building upon these results, we next probed the scope of 1,2-diketones. Aryl 1,2-diketones bearing electron-donating alkyl groups (–Me and –tBu) as well as weakly electron-withdrawing halogens (–F, –Cl, and –Br) were readily converted to the desired products (39c–44c) in good yields. Notably, an electron-withdrawing group (formylmethyl ester) was also compatible, furnishing product 45c efficiently.

We then examined the scope of alkenes and observed broad generality and good functional-group tolerance. Methyl-substituted styrenes reacted smoothly irrespective of substitution position (ortho-, meta-, or para-), affording products 46c–48c in good to high yields. Styrenes bearing electron-donating groups (–tBu, –Ph, and –OMe) provided corresponding products 49c–51c in 65–68% yields, while those containing electron-withdrawing substituents (–F, –Cl, –Br, and –CF3) were likewise competent, generating products 52c–55c. The protocol tolerated a range of functional groups, including trimethylsilyl (56c), hydroxyl derivatives (57c), free alcohol (58c), ester (59c), cyano (60c), and alkyne (61c). Heteroaryl olefins also participated effectively to furnish products (62c and 63c) in good yields. In addition, α-methylstyrene afforded product 64c, featuring an all-carbon quaternary center, further highlighting the breadth of this method.

To gain deeper insight into the mechanism of the 1,2-diacylation reaction, we first investigated whether the α-carbonyl radical anion generated upon reduction of benzil exhibits persistent-radical character. The ethyl benzoylformate radical anion,27,28 a structurally related persistent radical, was selected as a reference. Both species possess an α-dicarbonyl framework but differ in the substituent attached to the second carbonyl group: a phenyl group in the benzil-derived radical anion and an ethoxy group in the ethyl benzoylformate radical anion. In the benzil-derived radical anion, the additional phenyl ring extends the π-conjugated system, facilitating delocalization of the unpaired electron and thereby enhancing the thermodynamic stability of the radical. To quantitatively evaluate this structural rationale, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to compare the radical stabilization energies (RSEs) of the benzil radical anion, the ethyl benzoylformate radical anion, and an NHC-stabilized acyl radical anion (Scheme 2a).90 The computational results indicate that the RSE of the benzil radical anion is higher than that of the ethyl benzoylformate radical anion and lies within the range associated with established persistent radicals. Because a higher RSE generally indicates greater radical stabilization, these computational results are consistent with the structural analysis and support the classification of the benzil radical anion as a persistent radical.

Mechanistic studies.

Having established the persistent radical nature of the benzil radical anion, we next conducted a series of control experiments to further investigate the mechanism of the 1,2-diacylation reaction. Radical inhibition studies were performed by adding 2.0 equivalents of the radical scavenger TEMPO under the standard conditions for symmetrical diacylation (condition A) and unsymmetrical diacylation (condition B). In both cases, the reactions were completely inhibited, and neither expected product 1c nor 39c was detected. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) unambiguously identified the corresponding TEMPO-trapped adducts e–h (Scheme 2b and c). Notably, the persistent radical g could also be trapped, providing evidence for the involvement of these radical intermediates. We next carried out radical clock experiments, which afforded the ring-opened products 1j and 1k (Scheme 2d and e), further corroborating radical addition to alkenes. Furthermore, trace amounts of compound 1l were detected under the standard reaction conditions, suggesting that it might be a key intermediate in this transformation. To verify this hypothesis, 1l was independently synthesized and subjected to the photochemical conditions (405 nm irradiation in acetonitrile for 24 hours), delivering the desired product 20c in 92% yield; GC-MS analysis confirmed the concomitant formation of benzaldehyde (Scheme 2f). This result is similar to the transformation that Mayo observed in 1964.91 Therefore, based on the above experiments, 1l is regarded as the key intermediate for the formation of the diacylated product. For the unsymmetrical diacylation system, intermediate studies revealed that switching the light source from 405 to 425 nm under otherwise identical conditions selectively afforded 1m in 89% yield (Scheme 2g). Subsequent irradiation of isolated 1m at 405 nm for 24 h produced the target product 39c in 95% yield, again accompanied by benzaldehyde, as detected by GC-MS (Scheme 2h). Together, these experiments establish 1m as a competent and likely on-pathway intermediate in the unsymmetrical diacylation process.

Based on the aforementioned experimental results, a plausible mechanism is proposed in Scheme 3. Upon visible-light irradiation, 1,2-diketone (a) is excited to triplet diradical state A, which undergoes Norrish type-I cleavage to generate benzoyl radical B. Radical B adds to the alkene to afford the benzyl carbon-centered radical C. In parallel, another molecule of the A intermediate undergoes either hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) with sodium formate to generate CO2˙− or single-electron transfer (SET) reduction by CO2˙− to generate the crucial persistent radical intermediate D. The transient radical C preferentially cross-couples with the more stable, persistent radical D, rather than undergoing nonselective coupling with another highly reactive acyl radical B, thereby affording the key intermediate E, which may equilibrate with the corresponding five-membered hemiketal E′. This pathway is consistent with the persistent radical effect, although alternative pathways involving addition of C to the neutral 1,2-diketone followed by electron-transfer and proton-transfer steps cannot be completely excluded. Continued irradiation induces a second Norrish type-I cleavage of E or E′, generating an acyl-alkyl radical pair, which ultimately eliminates one equivalent of aldehyde to furnish the symmetrical diacylation product F. For the unsymmetrical diacylation, the data are consistent with a sequential radical pathway. The 1,2-diketone again forms triplet diradical A under irradiation. Meanwhile, base-mediated deprotonation of the α-keto acid followed by single-electron transfer to the diradical A produces the anion radical J and the corresponding oxyl radical G. The anion radical J abstracts a proton from α-keto acids to generate the persistent radical D, whereas radical G rapidly decarboxylates to form acyl radical H. Addition of acyl radical H to the alkene generates the transient radical I, which preferentially cross-couples with the more stable, persistent radical D, rather than with another highly reactive radical H to form the symmetric diacylation product. This selective radical–radical coupling is crucial for controlling the reaction selectivity and leads to intermediates K or K′. Finally, Norrish type-I cleavage of K, accompanied by aldehyde extrusion, furnishes the unsymmetrical diacylation product F.

Proposed mechanism.

Conclusions

This study reveals the use of 1,2-diketones as a readily available, inexpensive, and catalyst-free class of precursors to persistent radicals. By precisely modulating the synergistic interplay between their intrinsic dicarbonyl structure and Norrish type-I cleavage, we achieved highly efficient 1,2-diacylation of alkenes. Notably, the platform can be extended to the more demanding unsymmetrical diacylation by integrating transient acyl radicals. The protocol features operational simplicity (no exogenous catalysts), mild conditions, broad substrate scope, and high functional group compatibility. Beyond expanding the reactivity profile of 1,2-diketones in radical chemistry, this work establishes a general strategy for accessing persistent acyl-radical equivalents. We anticipate that these species will find broad utility in radical-mediated bond constructions and enable the development of efficient synthetic methodologies.
