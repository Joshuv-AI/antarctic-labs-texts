On Boolean PCSPs with Polynomial Threshold Polymorphisms
Katzper Michno

Abstract
In pursuit of a deeper understanding of Boolean Promise Constraint Satisfaction Problems ( 𝖯𝖢𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{PCSP} s), we identify a class of problems with restricted structural complexity, which could serve as a promising candidate for complete characterization. Specifically, we investigate the class of 𝖯𝖢𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{PCSP} s whose polymorphisms are Polynomial Threshold Functions ( 𝖯𝖳𝖥 \mathsf{PTF} s) of bounded degree. We obtain two complexity characterization results: (1) with a hardness condition introduced in [ BWŽ21 ] , we establish a complete complexity dichotomy in the case where coefficients of 𝖯𝖳𝖥 \mathsf{PTF} representations are non-negative; (2) dropping the non-negativity assumption, we show a hardness result for 𝖯𝖳𝖥 \mathsf{PTF} s admitting coordinates with significant influence, conditioned on the Rich 2-to-1 Conjecture proposed in [ BKM21 ] . In order to prove the latter, we show that a random 2-to-1 minor map retains significant coordinate influence over the p p -biased hypercube with constant probability.

Introduction

The class of
Promise Constraint Satisfaction Problems
(
\mathsf{PCSP}
) can be seen as
qualitative
approximation problems within
\mathsf{NP}
, originally introduced in
[
AGH17
]
. Perhaps the most famous
\mathsf{PCSP}
problem is the
Approximate Graph Coloring
, which for a pair of parameters
s<t
asks to find a
t
-coloring of the input graph, which is promised to be
s
-colorable. The question of classifying the complexity of this problem is known for its notorious difficulty. Although polynomial-time algorithms that solve the case where
t
is a sublinear function of the number of vertices have been known for decades and are still improving, currently achieving
t\approx n^{0.2}
for
3
-colorable graphs
[
Wig83
,
Blu94
,
KT17
,
KTY24
]
, not much is known about the case where
t
is a constant. We know that if
s=3
and
t=5
, then the problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
GK00
,
KLS00
]
. For general
s
, the strongest known result states that
Approximate Coloring
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for
t=2s-1
. In particular, the case for
s=3,t=6
remains open. However, we know that the problem is hard for all constants
s,t
under the assumption of a certain variant of Khot’s
Unique Games Conjecture

[
DMR09
,
GS20
]
.

The
Approximate Coloring
problem is only a single representative of the extremely rich class of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. We generally define
\mathsf{PCSP}
s using the language of
relational structures
, which we now briefly introduce. A
relational signature
consists of a sequence of
relational symbols

R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
with fixed corresponding arities
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
. A
relational structure
over
universe

A
of such a signature is a tuple
\mathbb{A}=(A;R_{1}^{\mathbb{A}},\dots,R_{k}^{\mathbb{A}})
, where for every
i\in[k]
,
R_{i}^{\mathbb{A}}\subseteq A^{r_{i}}
is a relation of arity corresponding to the arity of the symbol
R_{i}
. We say that two relational structures are
similar
if they have the same signature. Given two similar structures
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
over universes
A
and
B
respectively, by
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
we denote the fact that there exists a
homomorphic map
from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
, i.e. a function
\varphi:A\to B
that preserves relations. Formally, for every symbol
R
of arity
n
in the signature,
\varphi
must satisfy that

\forall\,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A:(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in R^{\mathbb{A}}\implies(\varphi(a_{1}),\varphi(a_{2}),\dots,\varphi(a_{n}))\in R^{\mathbb{B}}.

To define a
\mathsf{PCSP}
problem, we need two similar structures
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
such that
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
. We say that such a pair of structures
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is a
\mathsf{PCSP}

template
. Now, the problem
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
can be stated as follows: Given an input structure
\mathbb{X}
such that
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
, find and output a
homomorphism
from
\mathbb{X}
to
\mathbb{B}
. The assumption that
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
is the
promise
, which is not part of the input, but guarantees that a solution exists, because
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
and homomorphisms are composable. Observe that in this framework, the
Approximate Coloring
problem is exactly
\mathsf{PCSP}(K_{s},K_{t})
1
1

1

Here
K_{t}
is a complete undirected graph on
t
vertices, i.e. a relational structure with universe of size
t
and one binary relation consisting of all pairs of distinct elements.
.

The above definition yields the
search
variant of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. We also consider the
decision
version: In this variant, the problem
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
asks us to distinguish between the cases where
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{X}\not\to\mathbb{B}
. Here, the requirement
\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
ensures that these two scenarios are disjoint and that the problem is well defined. It is clear that every
\mathsf{PCSP}
is in
\mathsf{NP}
(as long as the universes of the underlying structures are finite) and that the decision variant trivially reduces to the search version. However, it is unknown whether the opposite statement is true.

We can see that the framework of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s is highly expressive and only a small portion of
\mathsf{PCSP}
problems have simple combinatorial interpretations like
Approximate Coloring
. It is therefore imperative to search for abstract tools in order to successfully classify larger chunks of
\mathsf{PCSP}
problems. The general, state-of-the-art machinery for this purpose is the
algebraic approach
. To properly introduce the algebraic approach, we have to take a step back and talk about the class of
Constraint Satisfaction Problems
— the predecessors of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s.

1.1
Constraint Satisfaction Problems and the algebraic approach

The class of
Constraint Satisfaction Problems
(
\mathsf{CSP}
) has been present in the theory of Computational Complexity for decades. The historically original formulation of
\mathsf{CSP}
problems is as follows: Every
\mathsf{CSP}
consists of a domain, variables, and constraints, and the goal is to find an evaluation of the variables that satisfies all given constraints. The classic example of a
\mathsf{CSP}
is
\mathsf{3}
-
\mathsf{SAT}
— given a set of variables and clauses, we want to find an evaluation of variables that satisfies all clauses, which play the role of constraints.

Although the above definition explains the name of the
\mathsf{CSP}
class, we will use another, equivalent formulation of
\mathsf{CSP}
s in the language of
relational structures
. Given a structure
\mathbb{A}
, the problem
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
in the search variant asks us to find a
homomorphism
of the input structure
\mathbb{X}
to
\mathbb{A}
. Similarly to
\mathsf{PCSP}
s, the decision version of a
\mathsf{CSP}
only requires to decide whether
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
. Interestingly, it is known that every search
\mathsf{CSP}
reduces to its decision version
[
BKW17
]
.

Example 1.1

(
\mathsf{3}
-
\mathsf{SAT}
)
.

Consider a relational structure
\mathbb{A}=(\{0,1\};\{0,1\}^{3}-\{(0,0,0)\},\neq)
, which consists of a Boolean universe and two relations. The first relation is ternary and consists of all Boolean triples with at least one
1
. The second relation is the binary inequality
\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
. Every instance of
\mathsf{3}
-
\mathsf{SAT}
with a set of literals
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},\neg x_{1},\dots,\neg x_{n}\}
and clauses
C\subseteq X^{3}
can be encoded as a relational structure
\mathbb{X}=(X;C,\{(x_{i},\neg x_{i}):i\in[n]\})
. We can see that the formula is satisfiable if and only if
\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{A}
, and thus
\mathsf{3}
-
\mathsf{SAT}
is exactly the problem
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
.

Example 1.2

(
\mathsf{3}
-
\mathsf{COLORING}
)
.

The problem of coloring a given graph with 3 colors can be equivalently stated as the problem of finding a homomorphism of the input graph into a triangle
K_{3}
. The vertices of
K_{3}
serve as colors, and since
K_{3}
has no loops, any pair of vertices connected by an edge must be assigned to different vertices in
K_{3}
. Therefore, the problem is equivalent to
\mathsf{CSP}(K_{3})
.

The systematic study of
\mathsf{CSP}
s has already begun in the previous century. One of the first works in this area is due to Thomas Schaefer
[
Sch78
]
and his
dichotomy
result, which states that every
\mathsf{CSP}
defined by a structure with Boolean universe
\{0,1\}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard or solvable in polynomial time. The next milestone was established by Hell and Nešetřil
[
HN90
]
, who showed that an analogous dichotomy is true for graph
\mathsf{CSP}
s, i.e. such that the underlying structure has only one relation, which is binary and symmetric. These two results motivated a conjecture proposed in 1998 by Feder and Vardi
[
FV99
]
, which stated that the entire class of
\mathsf{CSP}
s with finite domain admits such dichotomy. This problem is today known by the name of
CSP Dichotomy Conjecture
and was the main motivation of numerous major breakthroughs in the study of
\mathsf{CSP}
s for almost three decades, until its positive resolution by Zhuk and Bulatov in 2017
[
Zhu17
,
Bul17
,
Zhu20
]
.

To understand the methodology of studying
\mathsf{CSP}
s that led to this celebrated conclusion, we first have to introduce relevant notions. Typically, we write tuples with a bar above, e.g.
\overline{x},\overline{y}
. If a tuple
\overline{x}
is indexed by
[n]
, then
x_{i}
is the value of
i
-th element of
\overline{x}
for every
i\in[n]
. Perhaps the most crucial concept in the whole of
\mathsf{CSP}
research are
polymorphisms
of relational structures.

Definition 1.3

(
Polymorphism
)
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}
are two
similar
structures of signature
\tau
over universes
A
and
B
, respectively. We say that a function
\varphi:A^{n}\to B
is an
n
-ary
polymorphism
of
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
, if given any symbol
R
in
\tau
of arity
m
and tuples
\overline{a}^{(1)},\dots,\overline{a}^{(n)}\in R^{\mathbb{A}}
, we have

\big(\varphi(a_{1}^{(1)},\dots,a_{1}^{(n)}),\dots,\varphi(a_{m}^{(1)},\dots,a_{m}^{(n)})\big)\in R^{\mathbb{B}}.

A convenient way to think about polymorphisms is as follows: Suppose that we have a
m\times n
matrix consisting of columns
\overline{a}^{(1)},\dots,\overline{a}^{(n)}\in R^{\mathbb{A}}
. If we apply a polymorphism
\varphi
to rows of the matrix and obtain values
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
, then it must hold that
(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})\in R^{\mathbb{B}}

Alternatively, polymorphisms can be defined as
homomorphisms
of the form
\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{B}
, where the power of relational structure is defined in a standard way. We denote the set of polymorphisms of
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
by
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
. In the special case where
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{B}
, we write
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
and simply call it the set of structure polymorphisms. Arguably, the most important polymorphisms are
projections
, i.e. functions of the form
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto x_{i}
. Indeed, it is an easy exercise to show that all projections of all arities over the universe of
\mathbb{A}
belong to
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
(see
Fig.

1
for an example of a projection as a polymorphism).

Figure 1:

K_{3}^{2}
is a structure consisting of universe
[3]^{2}
and a single symmetric, binary relation
\leftrightarrow
defined as:
(a,b)\leftrightarrow(c,d)
if and only if
a\leftrightarrow b
and
c\leftrightarrow d
. It is simple to verify that the function
f:(x,y)\mapsto y
is a
homomorphism
of
K_{3}^{2}
to
K_{3}
. As a consequence,
f
is a
polymorphism
of
K_{3}
.

The first significant progress in general understanding of
\mathsf{CSP}
s was the invention of the
algebraic approach

[
BJK05
,
JCG97
,
Jea98
]
. It established a tight connection between the complexity of
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
and the set
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
. What was quickly observed is that the richer the set of polymorphisms is, the easier the
\mathsf{CSP}
gets, in the sense that if
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A})\subseteq\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{B})
, then
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{B})
reduces to
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
in logarithmic space. Therefore, if
CSP Dichotomy Conjecture
was supposed to be true, the question remained: How
exactly
poor must the set
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A})
be for
\mathsf{CSP}(\mathbb{A})
to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard? To answer this question, we have to understand
minors
, which are the next central notion in the algebraic approach to
\mathsf{CSP}
s.

Definition 1.4

(
Minor
)
.

Suppose that
f:A^{n}\to B
is a function and
\pi:[n]\to[m]
is a map. By
f\xrightarrow{\pi}g
or
g=f^{\pi}
, we denote that
g
is an
m
-ary
minor
of
f
with respect to
minor map

\pi
, which means that for every choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in A
, we have

g(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m})=f\big(a_{\pi(1)},a_{\pi(2)},\dots,a_{\pi(n)}\big).

In other words, a minor is a function that can be obtained from the original function by permuting, adding, and identifying variables. We are particularly interested in sets of functions that are closed under the operation of taking minors — such sets are called
minions
. Crucially,
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})

1.2
Adding the promise to CSPs

The class of
\mathsf{PCSP}

Are analogues of Schaefer’s and Hell-Nešetřil dichotomies true for Boolean and graph PCSPs?

Unfortunately, it seems that we are still far from resolving this problem. As it turns out,
\mathsf{PCSP}

One of the most apparent manifestations of

Definition 1.8

(

1.3
Fourier Analysis and Polynomial Threshold Functions

Fourier analysis of Boolean functions
is a field of research which developed a plethora of powerful tools to study the nature and behavior of various classes of Boolean functions, i.e. functions of the form
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
. The heart of Fourier analysis is
Fourier decomposition
. Briefly speaking, it is a representation of a given function as an element of a vector space spanned by an orthonormal basis consisting of functions indexed by subsets of coordinates. We defer the formalization of these notions to
Section

4
, but the important thing is that we can deduce a lot about the nature of the function from the coefficients of this decomposition. We point to
[
KKL88
,
Fri98
,
MOO05
,
Lif18
,
Kee+21
]
for some examples of prominent results in the field of
Fourier analysis
.

In this paper, we attempt to apply the theory of Fourier analysis to polymorphisms of Boolean
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. As we have seen, the bulk of hardness results for
\mathsf{PCSP}
s consists of finding an appropriate choice function that selects some distinguished coordinates in every polymorphism. The difficulty in finding such a function lies in the fact that the selected coordinates must be compatible with the operation of taking minors. This motivates the search for measures of coordinate significance, which would be transported through minor maps. A natural fit for such a measure seems to be one of the central notions of Fourier analysis: the
influence
. If
p\in(0,1)
, by
\mu_{p}
we denote the (product)
p
-
biased distribution
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, where each bit is independently set to
1
with probability
p
and to
0
with probability
1-p
. For a tuple
\overline{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
s\in\{0,1\}
, by
\overline{x}^{i\to s}
we denote the tuple equal to
\overline{x}
with the value of
x_{i}
set to
s
.

Definition 1.17

(
Influence
)
.

Suppose that
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
i\in[n]
. The
influence
of coordinate
i
in
f
over the
p

\mathbf{Inf}^{(p)}_{i}[f]=\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{\overline{x}\sim\mu_{p}}\bigg[\Big(f(\overline{x})-\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{s\sim\mu_{p}}\big[f(\overline{x}^{\,i\to s})\big]\Big)^{2}\bigg].

The
total influence
of

is the sum of individual influences of all coordinates, i.e.
\mathbf{I}^{(p)}[f]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Inf}^{(p)}_{i}[f]
.

Simply put, the coordinate influence measures the probability that changing the input value on this coordinate changes the function output. Sadly, it is not difficult to construct an example of a
minor map
that does not preserve significant coordinate influence in the original function.

Example 1.18

(Influence collapse)
.

Let
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
be a function defined as follows:

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x_{2}=x_{3}=x_{4}$},\\
x_{1}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We can observe that
\mathbf{Inf}^{(1/2)}_{1}[f]=3/4
, while all other coordinates have influence
1/4
. A naive way of defining a choice function
C
based on this fact would be to set

, since
1
has a significantly larger influence than all other coordinates. However, identifying coordinates
2,3,4
yields a minor in which
1
has influence equal to
0
.

However, we are able to show that in a certain class of Boolean functions, significant coordinate influence is preserved with constant probability over a uniformly random
2-to-1
minor. This result is strongly inspired by
[
BKM21

in
[

]

Proposition

1.22
). We are able to apply our tools to
\mathsf{PCSP}

2
Hardness from random 2-to-1 condition

In this section, we give an overview of how hardness conditions for
\mathsf{PCSP}
s are typically obtained. Our goal is to show that
Rich 2-to-1 Gap Label Cover
can be reduced to any
\mathsf{PCSP}
satisfying
random 2-to-1 condition
, which will immediately imply
Theorem

1.14
. We note that this result has already been shown in
[
BGS21
, Section 4.2]
in the special case of Boolean
\mathsf{PCSP}
s with monotone polymorphisms — we describe it in a more general setting. The reduction follows a standard framework and is split into two parts; this approach was originally established in
[
Bar+21
]
.

Before we proceed to the construction, we introduce the necessary notions. A
minor condition
is a finite set of
identities
of the form

f(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)})\approx g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),

where
f
and
g
are functional symbols and
\pi:[n]\to[m]
. In addition, we require that the sets of symbols on left and right-hand sides are disjoint. Given a minion
\mathcal{M}
on
(A,B)
, we say that an identity is
satisfied
in
\mathcal{M}
if there exists a
symbol interpretation

\zeta
that assigns a function in
\mathcal{M}
to every symbol, such that

\forall x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in A:\zeta(f)(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(n)})=\zeta(g)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}).

Furthermore, a minor condition is satisfied in
\mathcal{M}
if there exists an interpretation that satisfies all identities simultaneously.

A minor condition is
trivial
if it is satisfied in every
minion
. We note that if a minor condition
\Sigma
is satisfied in a minion of
projections
on a set of at least two elements, then it is automatically trivial. To see why, observe that if such a satisfying interpretation assigns projections
\zeta(f)(\overline{x})=x_{i}
and
\zeta(g)(\overline{y})=y_{j}
, then it must hold that
x_{i}=x_{\pi(j)}
for every choice of
\overline{x}
, and since the underlying set has a size of at least two, this implies
i=\pi(j)
. Now, to find a satisfactory interpretation of
\Sigma
in another minion on
(A,B)
, we use a small trick: take a unary function
h:A\to B
in the minion and generate all minors of
h
of the form
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto h(x_{i})
. The functions obtained are not exactly projections, but behave similarly. In particular, the interpretation
\zeta^{\prime}
defined as
\zeta^{\prime}(f)(\overline{x})=h(x_{i})
if and only if
\zeta(f)(\overline{x})=x_{i}
, satisfies
\Sigma
.

We now define the intermediate problem in our reduction, called
Promise Minor Condition
.

Definition 2.1

(
Promise Minor Condition
)
.

Given
n\geq 1
and a minion
\mathcal{M}
, the
Promise Minor Condition
problem
\mathsf{PMC}_{n}(\mathcal{M})
is the problem defined as follows: for an input
minor condition

\Sigma
with
identities
of arity at most
n
, answer
\mathsf{YES}
if
\Sigma
is
trivial
, and answer
\mathsf{NO}
if
\Sigma
is not
satisfied
in
\mathcal{M}
.

We emphasize that the parameter
n
is treated as a constant and the size of an instance is the number of identities. As it turns out, if
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is a
\mathsf{PCSP}

template
and
\mathcal{M}=\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
, then
\mathsf{PMC}_{n}(\mathcal{M})
is log-space reducible to
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
. This fact follows from a construction that resembles
long code tests
, and is one of the most fundamental results in algebraic approach to
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. We refer to
[
Bar+21
, Section 3.3]
for more details.

The remaining part is to show how
Rich 2-to-1 Gap Label Cover
can be reduced to
Promise Minor Condition
. Suppose that
\Psi=(L\cup R,E,2n,n,\Pi)
is an instance of
Rich 2-to-1 Label Cover
. We construct a minor condition from
\Psi
in the following way. We identify every vertex
u\in L
with a symbol
f_{u}
of arity
2n
and
v\in R
with a symbol
g_{v}
of arity
n
. Moreover, for every edge
e=(u,v)
and its corresponding constraint
\pi_{e}\in\Pi
, we write the following identity:

f_{u}\big(x_{\pi_{e}(1)},\dots,x_{\pi_{e}(2n)}\big)\approx g_{v}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}).\immediate\immediate

Denote the resulting minor condition by
\Sigma
. Observe that
\Sigma
is essentially an equivalent presentation of
\Psi
: the
labelings
of
\Psi
are in 1-to-1 correspondence with the interpretations of
\Sigma
in
projections
. This automatically yields the
completeness
of our reduction, i.e. preservation of
\mathsf{YES}
-instances — if all
constraints
of
\Psi
can be simultaneously satisfied, then
\Sigma
is
trivial
.

It is the other side that is challenging: the preservation of
\mathsf{NO}
-instances, which is called
soundness
. For this part to work, we need additional assumptions about
\mathcal{M}
. In our case, this assumption is the
random 2-to-1 condition
. We show how to utilize this condition in the proof of the following proposition, which, together with the fact that
\mathsf{PMC}_{n}(\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}))
reduces to
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
, finishes the reduction and implies
Theorem

1.14
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a minion. If
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
random 2-to-1 condition
, then there is a positive constant
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\mathcal{M})
such that for every
n\geq 1
,
\mathsf{Gap\text{-}Rich\text{-}2\text{-}to\text{-}1}_{n}[1,\varepsilon]
poly-time reduces to
\mathsf{PMC}_{2n}(\mathcal{M})
.

Proof.

Fix a minion
\mathcal{M}
, which satisfies the
random 2-to-1 condition
with constants
M,\tau>0
and a choice function
C
. Let
\varepsilon=\tau/(2M^{2})
and fix any
n\geq 1
. We want to show that
\mathsf{Gap\text{-}Rich\text{-}2\text{-}to\text{-}1}_{n}[1,\varepsilon]
reduces to
\mathsf{PMC}_{2n}(\mathcal{M})
. We start with a
Rich 2-to-1 Label Cover
instance
\Psi=(L\cup R,E,2n,n,\Pi)
. Next, we construct a minor condition
\Sigma
as in (
2
). It is obvious that
\Sigma
can be constructed in polynomial time. Therefore, it remains to show that this is a proper reduction.

Completeness.
Suppose that
\Psi
is a
\mathsf{YES}
-instance of
\mathsf{Gap\text{-}Rich\text{-}2\text{-}to\text{-}1}_{n}[1,\varepsilon]
and all constraints in
\Pi
are satisfied with a
labeling

\sigma:L\cup R\to[2n]
. As discussed above,
\sigma
induces an
interpretation
that assigns the
projection

\overline{x}\mapsto x_{\sigma(w)}
to the symbol corresponding to vertex
w
. This interpretation satisfies all identities, and thus
\Sigma
is
trivial
.

Soundness.
By contraposition, suppose that
\Sigma
is satisfied in
\mathcal{M}
, which is witnessed by an interpretation
\zeta
. Our goal is to show there is a labeling
\sigma:L\cup R\to[2n]
, which satisfies more than
\varepsilon
-fraction of constraints in
\Pi
. Consider a random labeling
\sigma
, such that for every
u\in L
, the value
\sigma(u)
is chosen uniformly at random from
C(\zeta(f_{u}))
. Similarly,
\sigma(v)
is chosen uniformly at random from
C(\zeta(g_{v}))
for every
v\in R
.

Observe that for every identity as in (
2
), the function
\zeta(g_{v})
is a 2-to-1 minor of
\zeta(f_{u})
with respect to the map
\pi_{e}
. Fix any
u\in L
and let
\Sigma(u)
be the set of identities in
\Sigma
involving the symbol
f_{u}
.
Richness
of
\Psi
gives that if we choose, uniformly at random, an identity in
\Sigma(u)
, take the symbol
g_{v}
on the right-hand side and return
\zeta(g_{v})
, we obtain a uniform distribution over all 2-to-1 minors of
\zeta(f_{u})
. From the
random 2-to-1 condition
, we have that at least a
\tau
-fraction of identities in
\Sigma(u)
satisfies
\pi_{e}(C(\zeta(f_{u})))\cap C(\zeta(g_{v}))\neq\emptyset
. For every such identity, since both sets
C(\zeta(f_{u}))
and
C(\zeta(g_{v}))
have sizes at most
M
, we have
\pi_{e}(\sigma(u))=\sigma(v)
with probability at least
1/M^{2}
over the choice of
\sigma
. Hence, the expected fraction of satisfied constraints in
\Pi
adjacent to
u
is at least
\tau/M^{2}
. By summing over
u\in L
, we obtain that the expected number of constraints satisfied by
\sigma
is at least
\tau/M^{2}>\varepsilon
.
∎

Dichotomy for Positive Polynomial Thresholds of bounded degree

Our main goal in this section is to prove
Theorem

1.20
, but first we introduce
Positive Polynomial Threshold Functions
and relevant notation. If
\overline{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, then by
\overline{x}\oplus i
we denote the tuple
\overline{x}
with entry on
i
-th position flipped. Suppose that
Q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a multilinear polynomial. For every
S\subseteq[n]
, we let
\widehat{Q}(S)
be the coefficient of
Q
corresponding to term
\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
. We say that
Q
is

if the constant term of

is equal to

and that it is

if all non-constant coefficients of

are non-negative.

Definition 3.1

(
Positive Polynomial Threshold Function
)
.

A function

is a
Positive Polynomial Threshold Function
(
\mathsf{PTF}^{+})
if there exists an
unbiased
, and
positive
polynomial
Q:\{0,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
such that
f=\langle Q|^{t}
, where

\langle Q|^{t}(\overline{x})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }Q(\overline{x})<t,\\
1&\text{ if }Q(\overline{x})\geq t.\end{cases}

If
Q
has degree
k
, we say that
\langle Q|^{t}
is a
representation
of
f
of degree
k
and that
f
is a
\mathsf{PTF}^{+}
of degree
k
. For every
k\geq 0
, by
\mathsf{PTF}_{k}^{+}
we denote the class of
Positive Polynomial Thresholds
of degree
k
.

For the remainder of this section, we will assume that all representations are
normalized
, i.e. that the sum of coefficients of the underlying polynomial is
1
. Observe that every
\mathsf{PTF}^{+}
must be
monotone
. Furthermore, it is clear that every
\mathsf{PTF}^{+}
is a
\mathsf{PTF}
of the same degree. In other words,
\mathsf{PTF}_{k}^{+}\subseteq\mathsf{PTF}_{k}
for every
k\geq 0
.

We now introduce the notion of coordinate significance that we utilize in our dichotomy result — the
weight
which is simply the sum of coefficients corresponding to terms including the coordinate.

Definition 3.2

(
Weight of coordinate
)
.

Suppose that
Q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a multilinear polynomial. We define the
weight
of coordinate
i
in
Q
as

\omega_{i}[Q]=\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{Q}(S).

We are in a position to reveal the boundary between tractable and
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problems in
Theorem

1.20
. The condition that captures the dichotomy boundary is the following
regularity condition
.

Condition 3.3

(
Regularity condition
)
.

Suppose that
k\geq 1
and
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathsf{PTF}_{k}^{+}
is a minion. We say that
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
k
-regularity condition if

\forall\,\varepsilon>0:\exists\,t\geq 0,Q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ of degree $k$}:\Big(\langle Q|^{t}\in\mathcal{M}\,\text{ and }\,\max_{i\in[n]}\,\omega_{i}[Q]<\varepsilon\Big).

Theorem 1.20

(Extended version)
.

Suppose that
k\geq 0
and
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is a Boolean
\mathsf{PCSP}
template such that
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})\subseteq\mathsf{PTF}_{k}^{+}
. Then

(1)

either
\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
satisfies the
k
-
regularity condition
and
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is tractable, or

(2)

\mathsf{Pol}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
does not satisy the
k
-
regularity condition
and
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete,

where in both cases
\mathsf{PCSP}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
is the decision version of the problem.

We divide the proof of
Theorem

1.20
into two parts. For tractability, we show that if a minion
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
regularity condition
, then it contains
threshold functions
of arbitrarily large arities — we achieve this with the help of results of
[
BGS21
]
, which establish a connection between threshold functions and so-called
Shapley values
. This is enough to imply tractability due to
Theorem

1.16
.

On the other hand, we can show that if
\mathcal{M}
does not satisfy the
regularity condition
, then it must satisfy the
layered choice condition
, which implies hardness by
Theorem

1.11
. These two results are encapsulated in
Proposition

3.4
and
Proposition

3.9
, which we prove in the following two subsections.

3.1
Tractability from regularity condition

The main focus of this subsection is to prove the following result, which implies the tractability part of
Theorem

1.20
by
Theorem

1.16
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
k\geq 1
is an integer and
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathsf{PTF}_{k}^{+}
is a minion satisfying the
k
-
regularity condition
. Then
\mathcal{M}
contains threshold functions of arbitrarily large degree.

Intuitively, the
regularity condition
asserts that there are arbitrarily “regular” functions in
\mathcal{M}
, i.e. such that no coordinate is visibly more significant than others. This intuition of regularity in general functions can be captured by various notions of coordinate significance, which are not always equivalent, or even comparable. In this section, we focus on one of such notions, called
Shapley values
. We provide the following combinatorial definition of Shapley values from
[
BGS21
, Definition 2.7]
, but note that there are also equivalent analytical definitions
[
ODo14
, Exercise 8.31]
.

Definition 3.5

(
Shapley values
)
.

Suppose
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
monotone
Boolean function and
i\in[n]
. Consider the following probabilistic experiment. Draw uniformly a permutation
\sigma
of elements of
[n]
. Let
\overline{x}_{0}=(0,\dots,0)
be of length
n
and
\overline{x}_{j}=\overline{x}_{j-1}\oplus\sigma(j)
for every
j\in[n]
. The
Shapley value
of coordinate
i
in
f
is defined as

\Phi_{i}[f]=\Pr\Big[f\big(\overline{x}_{\sigma^{-1}(i)-1}\big)=0\text{ and }f\big(\overline{x}_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}\big)=1\Big].

In other words, the
Shapley value
of coordinate
i
is the probability that when flipping bits from
0
to
1
in order according to a uniformly drawn permutation of
[n]
, flipping the value on
i
-th coordinate is a moment when value of
f
changes from
0
to
1
. Observe that if
f
is not constant, then there is exactly one such moment for every permutation — and therefore the sum of
Shapley values
of coordinates in
f
must be
1
.

The importance of the notion of Shapley values lies in its connection with the so-called
sharp threshold phenomenon
. Suppose that
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a Boolean function and
p\in[0,1]
. We consider the function
\zeta:p\mapsto\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[f]
, where
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[f]
is the expected value of
f
in the product
p
-biased distribution. It is known that if
f
is non-constant and monotone, then
\zeta
is a continuous, strictly increasing function. We say that
f
admits a
sharp threshold
if
\zeta(p)
grows from values close to
0
to values close to
1
in a short interval around the
critical probability
, i.e. the value
q\in(0,1)
for which
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{q}[f]=1/2
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
[s,t]
is the
\varepsilon
-threshold interval if
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{s}[f]=\varepsilon
and
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{t}[f]=1-\varepsilon
. As long as the critical probability is bounded away from
0
and
1
by a constant, it is well understood what classes of monotone functions admit arbitrarily small threshold intervals for all
\varepsilon>0
. It turns out that they are exactly the classes of functions with arbitrarily small
Shapley values

[
Kal04
, Theorems 3.3 and 3.5]
.

The phenomenon of sharp thresholds has also found its usage in the context of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. The simplest class of Boolean monotone functions exhibiting arbitrarily sharp thresholds are the
majority functions
, or more generally,
threshold functions
(this fact follows from classic concentration inequalities for binomial distribution). Therefore, it seems plausible to conjecture that as long as a Boolean minion
\mathcal{M}
contains functions of arbitrarily small threshold intervals (or equivalently, arbitrarily small
Shapley values
), then
\mathcal{M}
must contain threshold functions of arbitrary large arities. This turns out to be true, as has been proven with a probabilistic argument in
[
BGS21
]
.

Lemma 3.6

(Lemma 3.4 in
[
BGS21
]
)
.

For every
m\geq 2
, there exists a constant
\delta=\delta(m)>0
such that the following holds. Suppose
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a
monotone
Boolean function such that

\max_{i\in[n]}\,\Phi_{i}[f]\leq\delta.

Then
f
has a
threshold function
of arity at least
m
as a
minor
.

To prove
Proposition

3.4
, we will show that
regularity condition
implies that the minion contains functions with arbitrarily small
Shapley values
. To this end, we will use probabilitic method to find minors in
\mathcal{M}
that satisfy even stronger regularity assumptions than
regularity condition
: that every two coefficients of their representations corresponding to sets of the same size are almost equal. This turns out to be a sufficient condition for small
Shapley values
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For every
k\geq 1
and
\delta>0
, there exist constants
m=m(k,\delta)\geq 1

\tau=\tau(k,\delta)>0

\bigg(\forall\,S,T\subseteq[m]\text{ with }|S|=|T|:\Big|\widehat{Q}(S)-\widehat{Q}(T)\Big|\leq\tau\bigg)\implies\max_{i\in[m]}\,\Phi_{i}[f]\leq\delta.

Proof.

Fix values of
k\geq 1
and
\delta>0

m^{-\frac{1}{2k}}\leq\delta/3,\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}>\frac{2k^{2}}{m},\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}m^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}\geq k,\text{\hskip 28.45274pt}\tau=\frac{k}{m\cdot 2^{m+1}}.\immediate\immediate

(
*
)

Observe that the first three conditions of (

∗

‣
3.1
) can be satisfied with sufficiently large

. Suppose that
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
is a monotone Boolean function as in the lemma statement. We will show that
Shapley values
of

are at most
\delta
.

We start with the observation that we can bound
weights
of coordinates. First, we bound the difference of weights of any two coordinates

\displaystyle\Big|\omega_{i}[Q]-\omega_{j}[Q]\Big|

3.2
Irregularity implies hardness

This section is devoted to proving the following proposition, which implies the hardness part of

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose

is an integer and

-

satisfies the

.

The fact that a minion

fails to satisfy the

implies that there exists a constant

, such that every

of every function in

has a coordinate with

\varepsilon

) representations of bounded degree. Therefore, the fact that a coordinate has significant weight in one representation does not necessarily mean that there is no other representation in which its weight is negligible. This issue motivates the following definition of

Definition 3.10

(

\mathcal{M}
fails to satisfy the

\chi_{i}\in L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
defined as

\chi_{i}(\overline{x})=\frac{x_{i}-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}.

In general, the
Fourier character
corresponding to set
S
is
\chi_{S}=\prod_{i\in S}\chi_{i}
, for every
S\subseteq[n]
.

It is well known that
Fourier characters
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
. Therefore, every function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, seen as an element of
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
, has a unique representation
f=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\chi_{S}
, where
\hat{f}(S)=\langle f,\chi_{S}\rangle
. The representation is called the
Fourier decomposition
, while the collection
\{\hat{f}(S):S\subseteq[n]\}
is referred as
Fourier coefficients
. Observe that
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[\chi_{S}(\overline{x})]=0
for every non-empty
S
, which implies that
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[f]=\hat{f}(\emptyset)
. Orthonormality of
Fourier characters
gives the classic
Parseval-Plancherel identity
:

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\hat{g}(S)

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)^{2}

We will also encounter the low or high
degree truncations
. Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
and an integer
d
, by
f^{\leq d}=\sum_{|S|\leq d}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\chi_{S}
we denote its
low-degree truncation
to degree
d
. Similarly by
f^{>d}
we denote its
high-degree truncation
, defined analogously. Moving forward, we recall the notion of
influence
introduced in
Section

1
:

See
1.17

We can see that the influence of a coordinate is a measure of coordinate
significance
of given function. In section
Section

3
we utilized another measure of coordinate significance: the
Shapley values
. It turns out that if
f
is monotone, the
Shapley value
of a coordinate is the average of influence over all
p
-biased distributions (see
[
ODo14
, Exercise 8.31]
).

If the function
f
is Boolean valued, the influence of coordinate
i
can be interpreted as the probability that flipping the value of
i
-th coordinate changes the function value (multiplied by a term depending on
p
). Additionally, the influence of coordinates of function
f
has the nice property of being tightly correlated with the
Fourier decomposition
of
f
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
p\in(0,1)
and
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is a function. For every
i\in[n]
we have

\mathbf{Inf}^{(p)}_{i}[f]{\mathrel{\to}^{\!(1)\!}}\sum_{S\ni i}\hat{f}(S)^{2}{\mathrel{\to}^{\!(2)\!}}p(1-p)\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}\Big[\big(f(\overline{x})-f(\overline{x}\oplus i)\big)^{2}\Big]{\mathrel{\to}^{\!(3)\!}}p(1-p)\cdot\Pr_{p}\Big[f(\overline{x})\neq f(\overline{x}\oplus i)\Big].

Proof.

Let
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as
g(\overline{x})=f(\overline{x})-\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{s\sim\mu_{p}}[f(\overline{x}^{\,i\to s})]
. Clearly, we have
\mathbf{Inf}^{(p)}_{i}[f]~=~\left\lVert g\right\rVert^{2}
. We want to find the
Fourier decomposition
of
g
. From the fact that
\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[\chi_{i}]=0
we obtain that

\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{s\sim\mu_{p}}\Big[f(\overline{x}^{\,i\to s})\Big]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{s\sim\mu_{p}}\Big[\chi_{S}(\overline{x}^{\,i\to s})\Big]=\sum_{S\not\ni i}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\chi_{S}(\overline{x}).

Therefore we have
g=\sum_{S\ni i}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\chi_{S}
and the
Parseval-Plancherel
identity implies (1). To obtain (2), consider
\Delta(\overline{x})=f(\overline{x}^{\,i\to 1})-f(\overline{x}^{\,i\to 0})
. Observe that
\Delta(\overline{x})
does not depend on
x_{i}
. It follows that

g(\overline{x})=\begin{cases}-p\cdot\Delta(\overline{x})&\text{ if }x_{i}=0,\\
(1-p)\cdot\Delta(\overline{x})&\text{ if }x_{i}=1.\end{cases}

We can now rewrite norm of
g
in the following way:

\displaystyle\left\lVert g\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}\Big[p^{2}\cdot\Delta(\overline{x})^{2}\,\Big|\,x_{i}=0\Big]+p\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}\Big[(1-p)^{2}\cdot\Delta(\overline{x})^{2}\,\Big|\,x_{i}=1\Big]

\displaystyle=\big[(1-p)p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}p\big]\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}\Big[\Delta(\overline{x})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}\Big[\big(f(\overline{x})-f(\overline{x}\oplus i)\big)^{2}\Big],

which gives (2). The equality (3) follows simply from the fact that
f
is Boolean valued.
∎

In particular, the equality (1) in
Proposition

4.2
implies the following connection between
total influence
and
Fourier decomposition
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that
p\in(0,1)
and
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function. Then the
total influence
of
f
can be expressed as

\mathbf{I}^{(p)}[f]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}|S|\cdot\hat{f}(S)^{2}.

4.1
Random minors and the pull-back distribution

Our ultimate goal in this section is to understand the relation between coordinate
influence
in a function and its random minor in attempt to construct hardness reductions from
Label Cover
problems to
\mathsf{PCSP}
s. Clearly, this relation heavily depends on how we draw the random minor map. There are several possibilities; we could draw from a uniform distribution over the space of all minors. This is the most general approach and therefore difficult to analyze. On the other hand, in
Section

3
we were looking for minors satisfying certain properties by drawing a random minor uniformly from all possibilities of a fixed arity. Despite how this setting proved useful in the construction of a specific minor, the fixed arity renders it useless in the context of reductions.

The middle ground between these two is drawing from a uniform distribution over all
d
-to-
1
minors. This approach imposes structural limitations over the result, while retaining enough strength for
Label Cover
reductions to be feasible, as we have seen in
Section

2
in example of the
random 2-to-1 condition
. Analysis of influence in random
2
-to-
1
minor was pioneered in
[
BKM21
]
in order to prove the equivalence of
Unique Games Conjecture
and
Rich 2-to-1 Conjecture
(with imperfect completeness). They showed that if a function over unbiased cube has its Fourier coefficients concentrated on
low-degree
coefficients and its random
2
-to-
1
minor has a coordinate with significant
influence
, then it almost certainly must have been obtained by identification of a coordinate with significant influence in the original function:

Lemma 4.4

(Lemma 23 in
[
BKM21
]
for Boolean functions)
.

Suppose
\delta,\zeta>0
and
d\geq 1
is an integer. There are positive constants
\gamma=\gamma(\delta,\zeta)
and
\tau=\tau(d,\delta,\zeta)
such that the following holds. Suppose
f:\{0,1\}^{2n}\to[0,1]
is a function such that
||f^{>d}||^{2}\leq\gamma
. Then

\Pr_{\pi}\bigg[\exists\,j\in[n]:\mathbf{Inf}^{(1/2)}_{j}[f^{\pi}]\geq\delta\text{ and }\max_{i\in\pi^{-1}(j)}\mathbf{Inf}^{(1/2)}_{i}[f^{\leq d}]\leq\tau\bigg]\leq\zeta,

where
\pi:[2n]\to[n]
is a uniformly random
2
-to-
1
minor map.

The proof of
Lemma

4.4
depends on deep analytical results for functions on symmetric groups. The brief idea behind it is that the space of
2
-to-
1
minor maps can be embedded into the symmetric group of
2n
elements, and therefore the function of influence in the minor can be viewed as a function on symmetric group. Using hypercontractive results of
[
Fil+24
]
, it is then possible to bound higher moments of this function in order to show a tight concentration around the expectation. After that, the result follows from a union-bound argument.

We propose a different result, which states that significant coordinate influence over the
p
-
biased distribution
(with
p
bounded away from
0
and
1
) is preserved with constant probability, as long as the total influence of the original function is not too large.

See
1.22

Compared to
Lemma

4.4
, our result has two drawbacks. First, and most notably,
Lemma

4.4
states that, with high probability, all influential coordinates of the minor must have been obtained from influential coordinates in the original function, but our result allows us to focus only on one coordinate with no control over the rest at all. Secondly,
Proposition

1.22
only asserts a constant (but possibly very small) probability that the desirable event will occur, while
Lemma

4.4
states that the event is almost certain. However, these two issues do not pose any obstacle in our hardness reduction applications, as we will see later in the proof of
Theorem

1.21
.

Furthermore, the proof of
Proposition

1.22
is elementary and avoids any hypercontractivity or analysis of functions on symmetric groups, and generalizes the assumptions of
Lemma

4.4
in two ways: (1) the setting of unbiased distribution is generalized to
p
-biased distributions, and (2) the assumption of bounded total influence is a direct weakening of the assumption of
low-degree concentration
, as the
total influence
of a function
f:\{0,1\}^{2n}\to[0,1]
satisfying
||f^{>d}||^{2}\leq\gamma
is at most
2n\gamma+d
due to
Corollary

4.3
.

In order to analyze the influence of a random minor, we utilize the notion of
pull-back distribution
(originally introduced in
[
BKM21
]
), which is the distribution over
\{0,1\}^{2n}
obtained by drawing a random
2
-to-
1
map
\pi
, an element
\overline{x}
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
over a
p
-biased distribution and then “pulling”
\overline{x}
back through
\pi^{-1}
.

Definition 4.5

(
Pull-back distribution
)
.

Suppose
p\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. The
pull-back distribution

\nu_{p}^{(2n)}
over
\{0,1\}^{2n}
is defined by the following process: draw a uniformly random map
\pi:[2n]\to[n]
, a tuple
\overline{x}\sim\mu_{p}^{(n)}
and return
\overline{z}=\pi^{-1}(\overline{x})
.

Similarly to biased distributions, we sometimes write
\nu_{p}
instead of
\nu_{p}^{(2n)}
, if the dimension is clear from the context. The usefulness of
pull-back distribution
arises from its clear connection with the process of taking a random minor, and then evaluating it in
p
-biased distribution. We want to analyze how similar
\nu_{p}^{(2n)}
and
\mu_{p}^{(2n)}
are. It is clear that they are not the same: the support of the former consists only of elements with an even number of ones (and an even number of zeros). However, it turns out that this is the only case where an element
\overline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
has a smaller measure in the pull-back distribution than in the classic biased distribution.

Lemma 4.6
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
p\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\,\overline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
with an even number of ones, we have

\nu_{p}(\overline{z})\geq C\cdot\mu_{p}(\overline{z}).

Proof.

Fix numbers
p\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and a tuple
\overline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
with
2k
ones for some
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. First, we calculate the probability that for a uniformly chosen 2-to-1 map
\pi:[2n]\to[n]
, the tuple
\overline{z}
is consistent with
\pi
, i.e. that
\overline{z}
is in the image of
\overline{x}\mapsto\pi^{-1}(\overline{x})
(in other words, that
\pi
pairs ones with ones and zeros with zeros in
\overline{z}
). Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all 2-to-1 maps. Every map is consistent with
{n\choose k}
tuples, and hence there are
|\mathcal{S}|\cdot{n\choose k}
pairs
(\pi,\overline{z})
such that
\overline{z}
is consistent with
\pi
. By symmetry, every tuple is consistent with the same number of maps, equal to
|\mathcal{S}|\cdot{n\choose k}/{2n\choose 2k}
. Dividing by the number of all maps, we obtain:

\forall\,\overline{z}\in\{0,1\}^{2n}:\Pr_{\pi}\big[\overline{z}\,\text{ is consistent with }\pi\big]=\frac{{n\choose k}}{{2n\choose 2k}}.

We obtain the equality
\nu_{p}(\overline{z})=p^{k}\cdot(1-p)^{n-k}\cdot{n\choose k}/{2n\choose 2k}
. If
k=0
or
k=n
, then the lemma statement clearly holds for
C=1
. Otherwise, we use Stirling’s approximation
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
to bound the ratio of binomial coefficients:

\displaystyle\frac{{n\choose k}}{{2n\choose 2k}}=\frac{n!\cdot(2k)!\cdot(2n-2k)!}{k!\cdot(n-k)!\cdot(2n)!}\sim\frac{\sqrt{4nk(n-k)}\cdot n^{n}\cdot(2k)^{2k}\cdot(2n-2k)^{2n-2k}}{\sqrt{2nk(n-k)}\cdot k^{k}\cdot(n-k)^{n-k}\cdot(2n)^{2n}}\geq C\cdot\frac{k^{k}\cdot(n-k)^{n-k}}{n^{n}},

where
C
is a universal positive constant. Moving forward, we want to show that

\frac{k^{k}\cdot(n-k)^{n-k}}{n^{n}}\geq p^{k}\cdot(1-p)^{n-k}.\immediate\immediate

To this end, we consider the derivative of
\zeta:p\mapsto p^{k}\cdot(1-p)^{n-k}
for
p\in(0,1)
. Simple calculations show that
\zeta^{\prime}
is positive in the range
(0,k/n)
, negative in the range
(k/n,1)
, and equal to
0
in
k/n
. Therefore,
\zeta
attains its maximum in the range
(0,1)
for
p=k/n
, in which case (
4.1
) becomes an equality. Finally, we obtain the desired inequality:

\nu_{p}(\overline{z})=p^{k}\cdot(1-p)^{n-k}\cdot\frac{{n\choose k}}{{2n\choose 2k}}\geq C\cdot p^{2k}\cdot(1-p)^{2n-2k}=C\cdot\mu_{p}(\overline{z}).\qed

We note that the inverse of
Lemma

4.6
(with a different constant) is known to be true if
p=1/2
and
\overline{x}
is
roughly balanced
(see
[
BKM21

). In fact, we believe that it can be generalized to any
p\in(0,1)
with an appropriate definition of roughly balanced inputs; we leave it as an exercise for the curious reader.

In this section, we describe our application of

To properly introduce this notion, we first have to define the
noise operator
. All of the following definitions are standard; we refer to
[
ODo14

for a more involved introduction of these concepts in the setting of unbiased distribution. The generalization to
p

Definition 4.11

(
Noise operator
)
.

Given
\delta,p\in[0,1]
and
\overline{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the
(p,\delta)
-
noisy distribution
of
\overline{x}
denotes the distribution over elements
\overline{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
defined as follows: for every
i\in[n]
independently, let

y_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{ with probability $\delta$},\\
\text{drawn from }\mu_{p}&\text{ with probability }1-\delta.\end{cases}

The
(p,\delta)
-noisy distribution of
\overline{x}
is denoted by
N_{\delta,p}(\overline{x})
.

The
noise operator

\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}
on space
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
is the operator that assings to every function
f
a corresponding function defined as

\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[f](\overline{x})=\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{\overline{y}\sim N_{\delta,p}(\overline{x})}\big[f(\overline{y})\big].

One can think of the parameter
\delta
as the level of
correlation
between the original point and its perturbation — the higher the correlation (the closer
\delta
is to 1), the more similar to
\overline{x}
we expect
\overline{y}\sim N_{\delta,p}(\overline{x})
to be. This is further reflected in the following fact, which says that the
Fourier decomposition
of
\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[f]
resembles the decomposition of
f
as long as the noise parameter is not far from
1
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Suppose
f\in L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{n},\mu_{p})
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. The
Fourier decomposition
of
\,\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[f]
is

\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[f]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\delta^{|S|}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\chi_{S}.

Proof.

Observe that
\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}
is a linear operator. Therefore, it suffices to show that
\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[\chi_{S}]=\delta^{|S|}\cdot~\chi_{S}
. This holds trivially if
S=\emptyset
. If
S
is not empty, we obtain:

\mathbf{T}_{\delta,p}[\chi_{S}](\overline{x})=\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{\overline{y}\sim N_{\delta,p}(\overline{x})}\big[\chi_{S}(\overline{x})\big]=\prod_{i\in S}\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{y\sim N_{\delta,p}(x_{i})}\big[\chi_{i}(y)\big]=\prod_{i\in S}\delta\cdot\chi_{S}(x_{i})+(1-\delta)\cdot\mathop{\mathds{E}}_{p}[\chi_{S}]=\delta^{|S|}\cdot\chi_{S}.\qed

In pair with the
noise operator
is the notion of

\mathbf{NS}_{\delta,p}[f]=\Pr\big[f(\overline{x})\neq f(\overline{y})].

Proposition 4.15

Proof.

Fix

. Let

1.22

5
Conclusions and future research

As we have seen, we were able to identify several conditions that guarantee complexity characterizations of
\mathsf{PCSP}
s with
polymorphism

minions
consisting of
Polynomial Threshold Functions
of bounded degree. In
Section

Theorem

1.20
by allowing negative coefficients, while keeping the assumption of monotonicity.
