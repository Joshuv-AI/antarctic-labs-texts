Identification of driving factors and water quality evaluation of groundwater in Quanzhou City, a typical coastal area in Southeast China
Authors: Guowei Shi, Jianfang Dong, Huijun Dong, Yi Li, Yuanjing Zhang, Yaci Liu, Yasong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Identifying the drivers and threats to coastal groundwater quality has emerged as one of the critical environmental and resource management issues. In this study, 140 shallow groundwater samples were collected from the coastal plain of Quanzhou City in China. Methods applied included mathematical statistics, correlation analysis, ionic ratio, isotope techniques, hydrochemical facies evolution diagrams (HFE-D), entropy weight water quality index (EWQI), health risk evaluation model and Monte Carlo simulation. The results demonstrated that the groundwater in the study area was neutral to weakly acidic, mainly soft water and fresh water. The groundwater chemical types were mainly composed of Cl-Na (37.86%), HCO3-Ca-Na (32.14%) and HCO3-Ca (27.86%). The natural sources of groundwater chemical composition were mainly controlled by the rock weathering, evaporation and cation exchange action. Nitrate with relatively high content in groundwater was found to originate mainly from anthropogenic inputs including domestic sewage and agricultural activities. The results of the stable isotope analysis showed the contributions of potential nitrate sources were 66.6%, 21.5%, 15.0%, and 2.5% for sewage and manure, soil nitrogen, synthetic fertilizer, and atmospheric deposition, respectively. The results of EWQI indicated that the overall groundwater quality of this study area was relatively good and suitable for domestic use. However, health risk evaluation based on Monte Carlo simulation showed that the probabilities of non-carcinogenic risk from groundwater nitrate were 4.31% for males, 5.71% for females, 13.93% for children and 25.80% for infants, respectively. The intake of high nitrate content groundwater via drinking pathway would pose a threat to the health of the local residents and require attention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23651-2.

Introduction

Coastal regions are the most economically dynamic and fastest growing areas in the world. In China, the coastal cities occupy less than 15% of the country’s land area, however, the population accounts for nearly 50% of the national total, and GDP nearly 60% of the national total1. Water plays a crucial role in the economic development and the survival of the living beings in coastal cities. However, with rapid development of economic construction and the increase in population, the water environment has changed dramatically and the contradiction between water supply and demand has become increasingly evident2. The coastal areas are facing ecological and environmental problems such as eutrophication of water bodies, seawater intrusion and steep increase in the content of various pollutants3. Groundwater is vital for domestic and agricultural purposes in semi-arid and arid regions, where it may be the only freshwater source1. It is estimated that approximately 44% of the world’s population are reside in coastal areas, which are dependent on groundwater supplies for industrial utilization, food and energy security, agricultural irrigation and ecosystems4.

Due to the acceleration of urbanization, the high intensity of human activities will bring large-scale pollutants to the groundwater of the coastal areas5. Hydrochemistry can reveal the mechanism of interaction between groundwater and the surrounding environment6–8. The chemical composition of regional groundwater is affected by natural factors such as climate, geology and geographical conditions, but it is also increasingly driven by human activities9,10. Groundwater resources in coastal areas that are significantly influenced by anthropogenic inputs generally display higher nitrate content, resulting in deterioration of water quality11,12. The continued rise in nitrate concentration has become one of the major water environment problems of global concern and is widespread throughout the world13. For example, the shallow groundwater in Jinjiang City has a large area of excessive nitrate, and nitrate water in the coastal plain of Xiamen City has become one of the major water chemistry types14. Therefore, it is important to identify the main influencing factors of the groundwater evolution and to carry out groundwater quality assessment in the coastal plain.

Several integrated geochemical methods were used to explore the hydrochemistry characteristics and evolution of coastal groundwater, including statistical analysis, ion ratio diagrams, isotope techniques, Gibbs diagrams, Piper diagrams, and hydrochemical facies evolution diagram (HFE-D)15–18. The source of nitrate contamination in coastal groundwater is usually determined by isotope technique19, multivariate statistical methods20 and ion ratios21. Furthermore, groundwater quality assessment is also an important way to understand the current quality status of groundwater22–24. At present, common groundwater quality evaluation methods include gray clustering, fuzzy comprehensive evaluation, artificial neural network and water quality comprehensive index method25–27. Compared with these methods, the entropy weighted water quality index (EWQI) utilizes the information entropy to assign weights for groundwater quality parameters, effectively avoiding the anthropogenic influence in the calculation of the index weights, and more realistically reflecting the current status of groundwater quality28,29. In addition, human health risk assessment is an effective approach for evaluating the level of the health risk posed by various contaminants30,31, which is an important requirement for supporting groundwater quality evaluation and water resource management32. By incorporating the Monte Carlo simulation method, the health risk assessment results are brought closer to reality. In recent years, the Monte Carlo simulation has been widely used in health risks caused by toxic and harmful elements in groundwater, surface water and soil33.

Less attention to groundwater in coastal cities in Southeast China since these cities mainly dependent on surface water for their water supply. In contrast, groundwater serves as an important emergency strategic water resource for Quanzhou City, as well as water sources for industry, agriculture and some drinking purposes. It plays a necessary supporting role in the socioeconomic development of Quanzhou City. At present, few studies have attempted to clarify the current status of groundwater quality and its main controlling factors, as well as the risk to human health in typical coastal plain, Southeast China. Therefore, this paper is first to systematically clarify the drivers and threats to coastal groundwater quality of Quanzhou City. The main research objectives of this study were: (1) to characterize the hydrogeochemical characteristics of groundwater; (2) to identify the driving factors including natural and human factors of groundwater chemistry components; (3) to evaluate the groundwater quality by using the EWQI method, and (4) to conduct health risk evaluation of nitrate in groundwater by using the health risk assessment model and Monte Carlo simulation. This study can provide a reference for groundwater quality monitoring and rational development and utilization in the coastal plain area of the world.

Study area

The study area is located in the southeastern coastal area of Quanzhou City, in the lower reaches of the Jinjiang River Basin, adjacent to Quanzhou Bay. The landscape is dominated by plains, with low mountains and hills in the west and north, and the terrain gradually decreases from the northwest to the southeast direction. This region falls within a subtropical maritime monsoon climate, with an annual average temperature of 17–21 °C. The mean annual precipitation ranges from 1000 to 1756 mm, and most of them occur during wet season from June to September, leading to uneven annual runoff distribution in this area. The area is covered with loose Quaternary sediments, mainly clay and sandy clay, with granite and tuff dominating the bedrock stratigraphy. The main aquifer types are loose pore-bearing rock aquifers and fissure-bearing aquifers of intrusive rocks, and the degree of water enrichment is relatively weak. Jin River is the largest river in Quanzhou City, which originates from Anxi County and consists of two major prongs (East River and West River). The two prongs merge into the main stream of Jin River, and eventually flow into Quanzhou Bay and Taiwan Strait34. Although rainfall is abundant, the freshwater resources in this area are relatively scarce. As the main freshwater resource, groundwater recharge mainly depends on the atmospheric precipitation and surface water. The domestic, agricultural and industrial wastewater discharges also became sources of groundwater recharge during the past decades due to the high-intensity human activities. It has brought great pressure to the groundwater environment of the coastal plain. At the same time, it is close to the ocean, and will also face some problems such as seawater intrusion.

Materials and methods

Groundwater sampling and analysis

In this study, 140 groundwater samples were collected across the coastal plain area of Quanzhou City in June 2020 (Fig. 1). The groundwater samples were mostly collected from civil wells within 30 m, with the buried depth of the groundwater level varied from 0.5 to 16 m. Each well was pumped for more than 10 min to eliminate the negative impact of stagnant water in the borehole. Moreover, the sample bottles needed to be rinsed for three times with the target groundwater before the sampling. All groundwater samples were sealed and stored in a refrigerator (4 ℃) and sent to the laboratory as quickly as possible. All samples were tested by the Mineral Water and Environmental Monitoring Center of the Ministry of Natural Resources. The analyses for groundwater indicators included pH, Potassium(K+), Sodium (Na+), Calcium (Ca2+), Magnesium (Mg2+), Ammonium ion (NH4+), Chloride (Cl−), Sulfate (SO42-), Bicarbonate (HCO3−), Nitrate (NO3−), Fluoride (F−), Iodide (I−), Nitrite ion (NO2−), Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), total dissolved solids (TDS) and total hardness (TH).

Location map of the study area and distribution of groundwater samples.

The value of pH was measured in the field using a multiparameter instrument (WTW Multi340i/SET, Germany). Total dissolved solids (TDS) were measured by the gravimetric method. Metal cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+ and Mg2+) and trace elements (Fe and Mn) were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (Agilent 7500ce ICP-MS, Tokyo, Japan). Ions of Cl−, SO42−, NO3−, F−, I−, NO2− and NH4+ were determined with the aid of ion chromatography (Shimadzu LC-10ADvp, Japan). Bicarbonate concentrations were measured by acid-base titration method. In order to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the test results, anion and cation balance analyses were carried out in accordance with the principle of charge equilibrium, and the analytical errors were all less than 3%. The test results were statistically analyzed by means of mathematical statistics, Piper diagrams and correlation analysis to identify the hydrochemical characteristics of shallow groundwater. The natural and anthropogenic control factors of groundwater solutes were then analyzed by means of Gibbs’ diagrams and ionic ratios28.

Moreover, 32 of groundwater samples were selected to analysis the ratios of stable isotope in typical nitrate-contaminated area. The isotope analyses of δ2H-H2O and δ18O-H2O were performed employing a stable isotopic ratio mass spectrometer (MAT 253, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at the Key Laboratory of Groundwater Sciences and Engineering, Ministry of Natural Resources, China. Briefly, NO3− was converted to nitrous oxide (N2O) by chemical reduction, and then δ15N and δ18O of N2O was determined. The produced N2O was concentrated and purified on a trace gas system, and the isotopic composition was determined using an isotope ratio mass spectrometer. During the measurement of δ15N, USGS34 potassium nitrate (KNO3, δ15N=-1.8 ± 0.2‰) and USGS32 KNO3 (δ15N = + 180 ± 0.1‰), standards were used to correct the values obtained. For δ18O measurement, the samples were referenced using USGS34 (δ18O = + 27.9 ± 0.6‰) and USGS35 sodium nitrate (NaNO3, δ18O = + 57.5 ± 0.6‰) standards. The precision for δ15N and δ18O was ± 0.3‰.

Human health risk assessment

The excessive toxic substance in coastal groundwater would potentially threaten the health of water consumers. Health risk assessment connects the levels of pollutants in coastal groundwater with a probability of toxic effects for human population39, including non-carcinogenic risk and carcinogenic risk assessment. These health risk could be posed via some pathways, such as inhalation, dermal contact and oral intake40. Furthermore, the populations with different age and sex should be taken into consideration separately due to their different tolerances to toxic substances in water bodies. Four different groups were considered in this study, e.i. infants (mean age 1 year), children (mean age 12 years), adult males (mean age 30 years), and adult females (mean age 30 years). Recommended value of health risk assessment model.

The risk evaluation model developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) can quantitatively reveal the potential risk of hazardous elements in coastal groundwater to human health through the dermal and oral pathways. Referring to the health risk assessment guidelines recommended by USEPA, the threshold of non-carcinogenic risk is 1. When the non-carcinogenic risk index (HI) is < 1, the non-carcinogenic risk of toxic substance is at an acceptable level. In contrast, HI >1 indicates that toxic substance has certain non-carcinogenic risk to human health, and as HI increases, the non-carcinogenic health risks tend to increase. The cancer risk (CR) indicates the carcinogenic risk caused by oral intake, and is at an acceptable level when it is between 1 × 10− 6-1*10− 4. While CR is greater than 1*10− 4, it could pose certain carcinogenic risk to human41. Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation is a common probability simulation model in risk assessment, which can solve the uncertainty of pollutants concentration and exposure parameter42. Hence this was used to determine the uncertainty in the risk analysis data with Excel software using Oracle Crystal Ball ® (version 11.1.50720) using 10,000 repetitions. Based on literature data, USEPA IRIS database and field survey results, probability distribution assumptions were defined for each parameter in the model (Table S1). To reduce the uncertainty in the risk estimation, the histograms showing the probability distribution of risks were plotted and its 95th percentile values were analysed. Moreover, sensitivity analysis is an effective way to determine the relative influence of various input variables on risk estimates. It aids in the identification of parameters that have a greater impact on the results obtained in a model43. Accordingly, the sensitivity was analyzed to determine the most influencing parameters responsible for health risk.

Results and discussion

General characteristics of groundwater chemistry

The statistics of the major components in groundwater were shown in Table 2. The pH value ranged from 5.71 to 7.81, with average value of 6.92, indicating that the groundwater was neutral or weakly acidic in general. The order of main cations in groundwater was Na+ >Ca2+ >K+ >Mg2+, and the cations were dominated by Na+ and Ca2+. The concentration of Na+ and Ca2+ in groundwater varied in the range of 10.12–968 and 5.39–608.4 mg/L, with average values of 71.8 and 48.33 mg/L, respectively. The major anions followed the trend: HCO3− > Cl− >NO3− > SO42-. The concentration of HCO3− and Cl− ranged from 5.92 to 586.4 and 10.5 to 1667 mg/L, with average values of 138 and 95.45 mg/L, respectively. The NO- 3 concentration of groundwater varied from ND (not detected) to 193.1.1 mg/L, and the average value was 59.12 mg/L. It has been calculated that the milligram equivalent percentage of NO3− in groundwater is greater than that of SO42-, with an average percentage of 17.80%, indicating nitrate has become one of the dominant anions in groundwater. The NO3− concentration of most groundwater samples in this study were much larger than the national standard for Class III water in groundwater (GB/T 14848 − 2017) (20 mg/L), with the exceedance rate of 87.9% and the maximum exceedance multiplier of 9.65.

Piper diagram can better reflect the different ionic compositions and hydro-chemistry types in coastal groundwater44. As shown in the Fig. 2a, most of the groundwater samples were located in the Cl-Na type, mixed HCO3-Ca-Na type and HCO3-Ca type regions, with the proportions of 37.86%, 32.14% and 27.86%, respectively. The cations were mainly concentrated in the Na-K type, Ca type and their transition region, and the anions were mainly concentrated in the HCO3 type, Cl type and their transition region according to two lower triangles. There was no groundwater points distributed in Mg type and SO4 type regions. Correlations between different hydrochemical variables were discussed using Pearson correlation coefficients to qualitatively assess the relationships between the groundwater components (Fig. 2b). It can be seen that TDS was significantly positively correlated with the conventional components of groundwater (except for the three nitrogen (NO3−, NH4+, NO2−), Fe and Mn) with high covariance. The correlation coefficients between TDS and Na+, Mg2+ and Cl− were more than 0.9, which indicated that these ions account for a large portion of the contribution to the TDS. Furthermore, the correlation coefficients of Na+, Mg2+ and Cl− were all above 0.8, indicating the existence of a certain symbiotic relationship, and also revealing that dissolution have a certain contribution to the formation of hydrochemical conditions. NO3− has poor correlation with other components, and only has a negative correlation with HCO3− (P < 0.01), with a correlation coefficient of -0.22. It indicates that NO3− has a different source compared to other conventional components, and are likely from anthropogenic emissions.

Driving mechanisms of hydrochemistry evolution

Natural factors

The natural factors controlling groundwater chemistry can be roughly classified into three categories including evaporation, precipitation and rock weathering45. Gibbs diagrams, constructed from the relationship between Na+/(Na++Ca2+) or Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) and TDS, can be used to analyze the natural evolution process of groundwater chemistry46. Figure 3 showed that the Na+/(Na++Ca2+) and Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) ratios of most groundwater samples varied from 0.17 to 0.92 and 0.05–0.98. The large range of variations indicated that the sources of Na and Cl in groundwater were affected by a variety of effects47. Most of the samples were distributed in the rock weathering control area, suggesting the groundwater chemistry components were mainly controlled by water-rock interactions in nature. Furthermore, a small portion of the samples in the Gibbs diagram showed a distribution trend from the rock dominance to the evaporation dominance. It indicated that evaporation was also a controlling factor for the hydrochemical characteristics in some sporadic locations, but this impact was not apparent. Study has showed that almost all water-rock interactions in rivers or lakes around the world fall within the dashed box of the Gibbs diagram, while the samples located on the outside of the dashed line indicates that the water body has been affected by human activities to some extent3.

In order to further explore the source of water chemistry, the end-element diagrams constructed by Ca2+/Na+ with Mg2+/Na+ or HCO3−/Na+ can be used to discriminate the effects of different rock weathering types on natural water quality48. As shown in Fig. 4a-b, most of the sample points were concentrated in the silicates control area, indicating that silicate weathering played a dominant role in groundwater chemistry. Some water samples fell within the transition zone between the silicate and evaporite dominance, suggesting that the chemical components of groundwater were also subjected to the influence of evaporite mineral dissolution. The groundwater samples controlled by evaporite dissolution may be due to the shallow depth of groundwater in the coastal plain, resulting in the occurrence of the evaporation and concentration. Also, it may be related to the seawater intrusion that leads to the increase of Na concentration. As shown in Fig. 4c, the main source of Na and Cl was rock salt dissolution when Na+/Cl−=1. While the majority of the groundwater samples, varied in the range of 0.30–2.89, were distributed above the Na/Cl = 1 isochrone, indicating that there were other sources besides rock salt dissolution, which may come from the dissolution of silica-aluminate rock minerals. The average value of Na/Cl coefficient of standard seawater was 0.86, and the samples near the 0.86 seawater line indicated that may be groundwater mixed with seawater due to seawater intrusion1.

To verify the seawater influence analysis from the end-element diagrams and achieve a better understanding of the status of the shallow aquifer, a hydrochemical facies evolution diagram (HFE-D) was also adopted for analysis49. The HFE-D provides an easy way to identify the state of a coastal aquifer with respect to intrusion/freshening phases and which are identified by the distribution of anion and cation percentages in the square diagram50. Figure 5 suggested that the shallow aquifer was freshening extensively, with most samples located in f1-f4 areas. Our samples were mainly distributed within the freshening phase (86.5%) above the mixing line, indicating an early stage of freshening where mingling of fresh water with seawater occurred (Na+-Cl− facies). The aquifer was being recharged to a significant degree, which was rinsing the residual salinity from prior salinization. During the freshening phase, the initial Na–Cl water gradually became Ca–HCO3, but this occurred via an intermediate Na–HCO3 facies or one of the other isopic facies. This characteristic of the freshening phase was explained by the direct exchange reactions that drove the increase of Na+ in the water. However, around 13.5% of samples were confined within the intrusion phase, showing different stages of intrusion. These proximal facies dominated over sub-stages i2 and i1, which represented the distal facies or the initial stages of intrusion, and reflected a period of intrusion with seawater-freshwater mixing and reverse ion exchange49. Samples near Quanzhou Bay with elevated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ concentrations fell within the intrusion zone of the HFE diagram, indicating seawater intrusion.

Human factors

In coastal plains with high human concentration, human activities are an important driving factor for the evolution of groundwater chemistry. Groundwater in coastal areas affected by intensive anthropogenic activities usually tends to contain increased amounts of nitrate, chloride, and sulfate48. NO3− is one of the widely distributed anthropogenic contaminants in coastal water bodies, and its levels in nature are relatively low, usually below 10 mg/L. Any water sample exceeding this limit can be considered to be due to anthropogenic inputs of external substances52. As shown in Fig. 5a, NO3− concentration in most groundwater exceeded the geologic limit of 10 mg/L, which implied that anthropogenic inputs had a significant impact on composition of water chemistry. The range of NO3− concentration in groundwater in Jinjiang City in 2014 was reported to be 0.2–57.8 mg/L53, while the highest concentration in this study reached 193.1 mg/L, indicating that the nitrate pollution had been seriously intensified in recent years. In the groundwater samples with NO3− concentration more than 10 mg/L, TDS showed a positive correlation with nitrate (Fig. 6a). It indicated that human activities brought dissolved substances other than NO3− to the shallow groundwater, and changed the hydrochemcial characteristics and increased the salinity of the groundwater.

Fig. 6Plots of NO3− vs. TDS (a), (SO42-/Ca2+) vs. (NO3−/Ca2+) (b) and (NO3−/Na+) vs. (Cl−/Na+) (c).

Plots of NO3− vs. TDS (a), (SO42-/Ca2+) vs. (NO3−/Ca2+) (b) and (NO3−/Na+) vs. (Cl−/Na+) (c).

The ratio relationship between SO42−/Ca2+ and NO3−/Ca2+ can reveal the impact and degree of the influence of human activities on the water chemistry phases54. As shown in Fig. 6b, the ratio of NO3−/Ca2+ was greater than SO42−/Ca2+ in most of the groundwater samples, suggesting that agricultural activities or domestic sewage discharges had a certain impact on the groundwater quality. In addition, the relationship between the ratio of NO3−/Na+ and Cl− /Na+ implies various sources, such as domestic sewage, agricultural activities, carbonates and silicates, evaporates, etc4. Similarly, Fig. 6c showed that most of the sample points were distributed between agricultural activities and domestic sewage. Therefore, this suggested that under the interference of human productive life and agriculture, the solutes input from the outside infiltrated into the shallow groundwater with rainfall, which changed the natural water chemistry phase in groundwater.

Moreover, the common kriging method was used to determine the spatial distribution characteristics of groundwater nitrogen (Fig. S1). Overall, the NO3− content of groundwater in the study area was low in the western areas and high in the eastern areas and estuaries. The nitrate content in the western region was mostly concentrated between 40 and 60 mg/L, while the nitrate concentration in the eastern areas bordering Quanzhou Bay was usually greater than 60 mg/L. Nitrate located at the coastal zone of Huian country and Jinjiang City was generally more than 60 mg/L. Owing to the high economic concentration and intensive human activities in coastal areas, nitrate pollution has gradually increased from inland to coast. Similarly, study found that the proportion of high-nitrate groundwater in the urbanized areas of the Pearl River Delta was more than twice that in the non-urbanized areas55.

Nitrate source apportionment using SIAR

As discussed above, NO3− played a significant role in groundwater pollution. The stable isotope results (δ15N-NO3− and δ18O-NO3−) of 32 groundwater samples were presented in Fig. 7a. The δ15N-NO3− values of groundwater samples ranged from 3.59‰ to 34.79‰ with a mean value of 14.60‰, and the δ18O- NO3− values ranged from 0.19‰ to 17.27‰ with a mean value of 5.09‰. The cross-plot of δ15N-NO3− and δ18O-NO3− showed that most of the groundwater samples were within the isotopic ranges of Manure and Sewage, Soil N and their overlapping areas, demonstrating the mixing effects of different sources. Only one sample was located in the overlapping zone of synthetic fertilizer and soil N. It indicated that sewage and manure were the main sources of nitrate in groundwater in the study area. Moreover, the MixSIAR model was applied to estimate the proportional contributions of different potential NO3− sources to the groundwater NO3− pollution. This model exhibits high sensitivity to the fractionation factors of nitrogen and oxygen isotopes. However, the denitrification process influences the fractionation of these isotopes. Therefore, it is essential to first analyze whether significant denitrification occurs in the groundwater of study area to ensure the validity of using this model for source identification.

Fig. 7Isotopic compositions of δ15N and δ18O in potential NO3− sources (a) and proportional contribution of NO3− sources estimated by SIAR (b) in groundwater samples in coastal plain of Quanzhou City.

Isotopic compositions of δ15N and δ18O in potential NO3− sources (a) and proportional contribution of NO3− sources estimated by SIAR (b) in groundwater samples in coastal plain of Quanzhou City.

Anaerobic environment is conducive to microbial denitrification, and the ideal environment for groundwater denitrification is DO < 0.2 mg/L. While at DO levels above 2 mg/L, the aerobic environment will suppress denitrification, and oxygen will be the first electron donor instead of nitrate56. The studied aquifer in this study was unconfined with shallow groundwater levels. The DO concentrations measured were all above 2 mg/L, indicating an aerobic condition restricted the presence of denitrification. Also, during denitrification, as NO3− concentrations decrease, the δ15N- NO3− and δ18O-NO3− values of the residual nitrate increase and exhibit a linear positive correlation, with a δ15N/δ18O enrichment ratio typically between 1.3:1 and 2.1:157. As shown in Fig. 7a, 93.7% of the groundwater samples collected in this study fell outside the zone indicative of significant denitrification, further confirming that no significant denitrification occurred in the groundwater. Furthermore, although a negative correlation between δ15N-NO3− (or δ18O-NO3−) and NO3− relationship was observed, the weak correlation coefficient of 0.08 (or 0.24) suggested that denitrification did not have a great impact on the isotope composition of NO3− (Fig. S2). Therefore, the fractionation coefficient Ckj was set to 0.

As shown in Fig. 7b, the results of SIAR model indicated that M&S contributed the maximal NO3− to aquifer system, accounting for 66.6%, and then followed by SN (21.5%), CF (15%) and AP (2.5%). The coastal area exhibited a relatively high urbanization rate and population density, resulting in significant sewage from human activities, which provided considerable nitrogen for groundwater. In addition, agricultural application of manure in the study area released nitrate into groundwater through soil decomposition of this organic fertilizer. This contribution of CF arose from the nitrogen in fertilizers, which underwent nitrification and was converted to nitrate after entering the soil-water system. During the sampling period in June, heavy precipitation led to the infiltration of N into the groundwater, and thus explaining the contribution of SN and CF to the groundwater NO3− pollution in study area. The proportion of nitrate input by atmospheric deposition was small and negligible. In general, M&S was the principal nitrate source for study area, with the lowest standard deviation among four potential sources. Therefore, reducing the output of M&S and the improvement of infrastructure in rural area are the key factors of nitrate contamination control in coastal area in Quanzhou City.

Groundwater quality evaluation based on EWQI

A total of 16 hydrochemical parameters (TDS, Th, K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, NH4+, Cl−, SO42-, HCO3−, NO3−, F−, I−, NO2−, Fe and Mn) were used as evaluation indexes. The thresholds for each hydrochemical parameter used for drinking purposes were referenced to the Chinese Groundwater Quality Standards (GAQS, 2017) and the World Health Organization guidelines (WHO, 2017).

The EWQI values, ranged from 16.20 to 492.55, indicated that the water quality of the shallow groundwater in the study area was highly variable, varied from excellent (Rank 1) to very poor (Rank 5). The mean value of the EWQI was 63.96, suggesting that the overall quality of groundwater was good. Most groundwater samples (91%) had EWQI values below 100, indicating excellent-to-good quality and suitability for direct drinking. Only three groundwater samples had EMQI values between 100 and 150, which belonged to the medium water quality type. Only one sample point plotted in the poor (Rank 4) water quality category. In addition, there were 7 groundwater samples (5%) with EMQI values greater than 200 and the water quality was very poor. These very poor groundwater samples may be due to the occurrence of seawater intrusion in the coastal area, resulting in high concentrations of indicators such as Cl, Na, TDS (Fig. 8b). Overall, most of the shallow groundwater was suitable for domestic usage and even for direct drinking in this study. Notably, the EWQI values in the excellence-good water quality types gradually became larger with the increase of the NO3− concentration (Fig. 8a). There was a positive correlation between them, which indicated that the NO3− played a significant contribution to the EWQI values. Although groundwater quality assessment can indicate the suitability of groundwater for drinking in the study area, it does not reflect the potential health risk posed by local groundwater pollution, and therefore the issue of local groundwater nitrate health risk needs further discussion.

Human health risk evaluation

Health risk assessment based on concentration

As discussed before, some toxic elements including NO2−, NH 4+, F, I, Fe and Mn have low detection rates. Since this study only considers the health risks posed by nitrate in groundwater to humans. Nitrate pollution in high concentration not only deteriorates the quality of groundwater, but also poses a threat to the safety of drinking water and health of local residents11,47. Long-term exposure to groundwater with high nitrate content through drinking water or skin can induce methemoglobinemia, hypoxia, blurred consciousness and other symptoms, and even endanger the lives of patients58. The inhalation pathway is limited and negligible due to the poor volatility of nitrate in groundwater buried below the surface. Washing and drinking are the main forms of human utilization of groundwater resources, therefore dermal contact and oral intake are the main pathways of human exposure to nitrate in groundwater.

The calculated HICDI ranged from 0.069 to 6.034, 0.045–4.023, 0.021–1.857 and 0.024–2.194 for infants, children, men and women, respectively, with corresponding mean values of 1.847, 1.232, 0.568, and 0.679 (Fig. 9a). The mean HICDI for infants and children via drinking water intake was greater than 1, which exceeded the USEPA recommended risk threshold. The maximum values reaching 6.032 and 4.023, respectively, which are significantly higher than the acceptable level and require attention. Statistically, infants, children, males and females with HICDI values more than 1 via oral pathway accounted for 73.57%, 52.86%,10.71% and 21.43%, respectively, indicating the people of different ages had varying degrees of non- carcinogenic risk. The HICDD for infants, children, men and women ranged from 0 to 0.060, 0-0.016, 0-0.005 and 0-0.004, with mean values of 0.018, 0.005, 0.001 and 0.002 respectively, suggesting that the non-carcinogenic risk of dermal exposure was much less than 1 (Fig. 9b). The HQ values of nitrate ingestion via drinking water was significantly greater than that of dermal exposure, with a difference of two orders of magnitude. Therefore, the threat of nitrate contamination of groundwater in the study area to human health risk was mainly dominated by the drinking water route, and the non-carcinogenic risk through skin contact routes such as bathing was negligible. The CR values of infants, children, men and women through oral intake pathway were 1.09*10−6-9.65*10−5, 1.09*10−6-9.64*10−5, 3.96*10−7-3.51*10−5, and 7.27*10−7-6.44*10−5 (Fig. 9c), which were all less than the threshold of 1*10−4 and at an acceptable level. It indicated that nitrate contamination in groundwater poses no obvious cancer risk to human beings. Nitrate in groundwater poses a higher risk to infants and children may be due to the strong sensitivity to environmental pollution during the critical window period of early development and the relatively poor metabolism of human beings9.

Uncertainty analysis using Monte Carlo simulation

As only deterministic health risk assessments were conducted in the above study, it is necessary to incorporate Monte Carlo simulations to quantify the uncertainties in risk evaluation probabilistically. By applying Monte Carlo simulation, the health risks associated with groundwater nitrate in the coastal plain can be expressed as approximate probabilities, accounting for uncertainties arising from variations in pollutant concentrations, toxicological responses, and individual susceptibility59. The results were presented in Fig. 10 and Table S2. The probabilities of non-carcinogenic risk from groundwater nitrate were 4.31% for males, 5.71% for females, 13.93% for children and 25.80% for infants with average risk indices of 0.26, 0.31, 0.53 and 0.86, respectively. The 95th percentile values for infants and children were 1.16 and 1.87, which exceeded the acceptable thresholds for non-carcinogenic risks. The mean and 95th percentile carcinogenicity risk values of nitrate for different groups were all below 1*10− 4, indicating no carcinogenic risk associated with exposure to groundwater nitrate. Moreover, the probabilistic values of risk were slightly lower than those determined from the deterministic technique, which may be attributed to the overestimation of risk caused by the health risk based on concentration. It did not consider the distribution characteristics and uncertainty of each model parameter. Nevertheless, estimating the risk for maximum concentration was also required to determine the severity or greatest risk for a given location33. The results of sensitivity analysis were similar for males, females, children and infants. It indicated that nitrate concentration accounts for 53% and EF values accounts for 44% of the influence on risk in both groups, highlighting that nitrate concentration and exposure frequency are the primary factor affecting human health risks.

Groundwater plays a crucial role in securing residential water supply and maintaining ecological balance. This study indicated the hydrogeochemical fingerprint, driving factors and health risk of groundwater in a typical coastal area in Quanzhou City. Recommendations for scientific development and rational management of groundwater resource in typical coastal areas are raised based on the present research as follows:

Conclusions

The groundwater quality is a decisive factor in maximizing its economic and social benefits in coastal areas. This study aimed to systematically clarify the drivers and threats to coastal groundwater quality of Quanzhou City in China. The pH value of groundwater in the study area ranged from 5.71 to 7.81, with Na+ and Ca2+ as the main cations and HCO3−and Cl− as the main anions. The values of TDS and TH indicated that groundwater was mainly soft and fresh water. The mean concentration of NO3− in groundwater was 59.12 mg/L, which became one of the dominant anions. Weathering and dissolution of minerals (silicates, carbonates and evaporites) and cation exchange were the main natural factors affecting the chemical composition of groundwater, and individual groundwater points may be affected by seawater intrusion. Anthropogenic inputs such as agricultural activities and municipal wastewater discharge changed the natural water chemistry phases of groundwater, and resulted in the deterioration of water quality. The Bayesian model (stable isotope analysis in R, SIAR) suggested that sewage and manure were the dominant sources of nitrate, followed by soil nitrogen. EWQI analysis revealed that most of the samples were at the excellent-medium quality level, which indicated that the overall groundwater quality was relatively good. The results of the health risk evaluation and Monte Carlo simulation indicated that long-term intake of high-nitrate groundwater in the region could pose a potential health risk to humans, especially infants and children, and therefore control by the drinking water route could be considered to reduce the risk.
