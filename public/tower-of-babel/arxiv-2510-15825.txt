On the topology of complex map-germs and a general Lê-Greuel formula
Lê Dũng Tráng, Juan J. Nuño-Ballesteros, José Seade

Abstract
Consider a singular holomorphic map-germ f : ( X , 0 ¯ ) → ( ℂ , 0 ) f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0) where X X is a singular complex analytic variety in ℂ N {\mathbb{C}}^{N} , and another holomorphic map-germ g : ( X , 0 ¯ ) → ( ℂ , 0 ) g:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0) which is “sufficiently good” relatively to f f . We use stratified Morse theory to determine up to homeomorphism, the topology of the Milnor fiber F f F_{f} out from the slice F g , f F_{g,f} and the Morse data of a Morsification of the restriction of g g to F f F_{f} . This generalizes classical results for the case where X X is non-singular, and it provides a general formula comparing the Euler characteristics of F f F_{f} and F g , f F_{g,f} . Restricting to the case where the singularity of X X at 0 ¯ {\underline{0}} is isolated, the formula for the difference of the Euler characteristics becomes algebraic and easily computable, generalizing in two directions the classical Lê-Greuel formula for the Milnor number of isolated complete intersection germs (ICIS): Firstly, X X can have an isolated singularity, and secondly f f can have arbitrary critical set. This unifies several known formulae in this vein: i) Lê-Greuel for ICIS of arbitrary codimension; ii) the formula relating the Milnor number of a curve with that of a function on it, and an extension of it for surfaces; iii) the formula for determinantal singularities; iv) and the one for the image Milnor number. All of these are special cases of our general formula.



Introduction

In his seminal work L’Analysis situs et la géométrie algébrique, Solomon Lefschetz viewed algebraic manifolds as being swept out by their intersections with planes varying within a pencil, deriving from this perspective profound topological consequences. René Thom introduced ideas along these lines that Bernard Teissier, Lê Dũng Tráng, and others later developed into major advances in singularity theory. This line of thought led to Lê’s article [31], where he described the Milnor fiber of a holomorphic map-germ by considering non-singular slices defined by a linear form. That work also inspired the classical Lê–Greuel formula for the Milnor number of isolated complete intersection singularities (ICIS).

In this work, we study the topology of Milnor fibers of functions defined on arbitrary singular varieties, continuing in that vein.

Consider a complex analytic singular variety
(X,0)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and a non-constant holomorphic germ

f:(X,0)\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
with arbitrary critical locus. By definition, its
Milnor fiber
is the local geneal fiber,
i.e.
the intersection
F_{f}:=f^{-1}(t)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}
where
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}
is a local Milnor ball for
X
and for
f
and
t
is sufficiently close to
{\underline{0}}
with respect to
\varepsilon
.
The homeomorphism type of the local Milnor fiber of
f
can be determined from the topology of the slice

(\ell,f):(X,0)\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
associated with a general linear form
\ell
, together with the stratified Morse data of the restriction
\ell|_{F_{f}}
.

In fact, there exists a Zariski-dense open set
\Omega
of linear forms on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
such that, for every
\ell\in\Omega
, the polar variety of
(\ell,f)
is a curve
\Gamma
. Setting
\Phi=(\ell,f)
, one observes that
\Gamma\cap\Phi^{-1}(0,0)=\{{\underline{0}}\}
; by Serre’s results (cf.
[
28
]
),
\Phi|_{\Gamma}
is therefore a finite morphism. Moreover,
\ell
can be chosen so that
\Phi|_{\Gamma}
is injective onto its image
\Delta
, the
Cerf diagram
, which is analytic by Remmert’s proper mapping theorem
[
16
, 1.18]
. Finally,
\Phi
satisfies the Thom condition at the origin, and consequently admits a local Milnor–Lê fibration.

Recall that a stratified mapping
X\to V
between Whitney stratified spaces satisfies
the
Thom condition
if for any pair of strata
S_{\alpha}
and
S_{\beta}
in
X
such that

S_{\alpha}\subset{\overline{S}_{\beta}}
,
and for any sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset S_{\beta}
converging to
x\in S_{\alpha}
such that the sequence
of kernels
\ker d_{x_{n}}(f|_{S_{\beta}})
converges to some subspace
T

(in the corresponding Grassmannian), we have
\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\subset T
.

We refer to the introduction in
[
20
]
and to the original text of R. Thom

[
54
]
for further discussions on the Thom condition. In the literature this
is also called the Thom
a_{f}
-condition or the Thom property. Once we know that
a given map
X\to V
between Whitney stratified complex analytic spaces has
the Thom condition, then we can argue as in
[
32
]
and conclude that the map
has a local Milnor-Lê fibration as stated above for the map
\Phi
.

We use these facts to
study and determine the topology of the Milnor fiber
F_{f}
out from a slice by a
general linear form. Then we extend this to slices by holomorphic functions on
X

which have an isolated critical point with respect to
f
, a concept that we make precise below, in Definition
5.6
.
As a consequence we get a formula that determines the difference between the Euler
characteristics of the Milnor fibers
F_{f}
of the germ
f
and that of the pair
(g,f)
.
In the particular case where the ambient variety
X
has an isolated singularity, this difference
\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})

can be determined algebraically. This leads to a generalization of the classical Lê-Greuel
formula for the Milnor number of ICIS that does not require
f
to have an isolated singularity,
and the ambient space can be singular. This also unifies several known Lê-Greuel type formulas.

We first show that for a general linear form
\ell
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, the restriction of
|\ell|

to the fiber
F_{f}
is a Morse function with ordinary quadratic singularities in the sense
of Section
1
. We then
use stratified Morse theory as described by Goresky and MacPherson in
[
22

21
]
,
to build up the Milnor fiber
F_{f}
out from the slice
F_{\ell,f}
and the Morse data of
|\ell|
on
F_{f}
(Theorem
4.5
).

Recall that in classical Morse theory (see for instance
[
39
]
) one considers a smooth manifold
M
and a Morse
function
h
on it; one defines the Morse data for
h
at a critical point
p\in M
to
be a pair of topological spaces
A,B
with
B\subset A
, determined by the corresponding Morse index.
As we cross the level
a=h(p)
, the local change in the topology of
M_{\leq a}

to the level
M_{\leq a+\varepsilon}
is given by attaching
(A,B)
to
M_{\leq a}
along
B
.
Given a Whitney stratified space
Z
, say in a manifold
M
, and
h:Z\to{\mathbb{R}}
, restriction of a smooth function on
M
,
a stratified Morse point of
h
means a point
p
which is critical for
h_{|S\alpha}
,
the stratum that contains
p
, and such that its differential does not annihilate any
limit of tangent spaces at points in other strata having
p
in their closure. Now,
as described by M. Goresky and R. MacPherson in
[
22
]
the Morse data one needs to
build up the topology of
Z
is the product of the tangent Morse data on
{S_{\alpha}}
,
defined as in the smooth case, and the normal Morse data. The latter is a pair
(A’,B’)
where
A’
is homeomorphic to
a normal slice, or to the cone over the link
L_{S_{\alpha}}
of the stratum, and
B’

is the
lower half-link
(we refer to
[
22
,
21
]
for more on the subject; see
p. 66 in
[
22
]
for the definition of the half links and the normal Morse data).

Coming back to our setting, we notice too that the
critical points of
|\ell|
in each Whitney stratum
S_{\alpha}\subset F_{f}
are the intersection
points of
S_{\alpha}
with the polar curve
\Gamma
. At each such point the Morse data is

(D_{\alpha},\partial D_{\alpha})\times D_{\alpha}\times(\rm{Cone}\,(L_{\alpha}^{+}\cup_{\partial}L_{\alpha}^{-}),L_{\alpha}^{-})
,
where
L^{\pm}_{a}
are the half links and

L_{\alpha}^{+}\cup_{\partial}L_{\alpha}^{-}
is homeomorphic to the link of the stratum
(see
[
22
, p. 66]
); so the cone over
L_{\alpha}^{+}\cup_{\partial}L_{\alpha}^{-}
is homeomorphic to a normal slice.

It follows (Theorem
4.5
) that the Milnor fiber
F_{f}
is homeomorphic to

\,F_{\ell,f}\times{\mathbb{D}}^{2}\,

to which one attaches, at each stratum,

\delta_{\alpha}
disjoint copies of the local Morse data

(D_{\alpha},\partial D_{\alpha})\times D_{\alpha}\times(\hbox{Cone}\,(L_{\alpha}^{+}\cup_{\partial}L_{\alpha}^{-}),L_{\alpha}^{-})\,,

where
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of critical points of
|\ell|
on
the stratum
S_{\alpha}
of complex dimension
\alpha
,
and
D_{\alpha}
is a closed disc in
S_{\alpha}
of real dimension
\alpha
, which equals
the Morse index of each Whitney
stratified Morse critical point in
S_{\alpha}
.
Moreover, we may replace each Morse data by

(D_{\alpha},\partial D_{\alpha})\times D_{\alpha}\times(\hbox{Cone}\,(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}),\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is the complex link, and we get

F_{f}
up to homotopy.

We remark that each stratum
S_{\alpha}
where
\ell|_{F_{f}}
has a critical point
contributes with one or more branches
\Delta_{\alpha}
to the Cerf diagram
\Delta
, and
the number
\delta_{\alpha}
above can be computed as
(\Delta_{\alpha}\cdot\{t=0\})_{0}
, the intersection number of the curves

\Delta_{\alpha}
and
\{t=0\}
at the origin
0\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

This theorem is a generalization of Theorem
(2.3) in
[
30
]
obtained in the smooth case.

More generally, we may replace the linear form
\ell
with a holomorphic germ
g
having an isolated critical point relative to
f
. Then the polar set of
\Phi=(g,f)
is a one-dimensional curve intersecting
\Phi^{-1}(0)
only at
{\underline{0}}
, and
\Phi
satisfies the Thom condition. Hence
\Phi
admits a local Milnor fibration and, by
[
6
]
, a Morsification of the restriction
g|{F_{f}}
to the Milnor fiber
F_{f}
of
f
, which may itself be singular if
X
has a non-isolated singularity at
{\underline{0}}
. Applying stratified Morse theory, we reconstruct
F_{f}=f^{-1}(t_{0})\cap{\mathbb{B}}\varepsilon
from the slice
F_{g,f}=g^{-1}(u_{0})\cap f^{-1}(t_{0})\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\varepsilon}
, a Milnor fiber for
g|{F_{f}}
, together with the normal Morse data of a Morsification of
g|{F_{f}}
. We then establish Theorem
5.13
for
(g,f)
in the same spirit as Theorem
4.5
. As before, up to homotopy each local normal Morse datum may be replaced by
(\mathrm{Cone}(\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}),\mathcal{L}_{\alpha})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is the complex link of the stratum.

This allows us to compare the Euler characteristic of the Milnor fibers of
f
and
(g,f)
. In the case where
F_{f}

and
F_{(g,f)}
are equidimensional, we get:

\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=\sum_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}m_{\alpha},

(0.1)

with

n=\dim(X,{\underline{0}})-1
,
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of
critical points of the Morsification of
g
on each stratum
S_{\alpha}\cap F_{f}

and

m_{\alpha}=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}\chi(\hbox{Cone}({\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}),{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha})

where
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is the complex link.

In the particular case where
X
is non-singular away from
{\underline{0}}
, this formula becomes Theorem
6.1
:

\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=(-1)^{n}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}}{(f)+J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}}\;.

(0.2)

where
J_{X}(f,g)
is the ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}
given by

J_{X}(f)=\frac{I_{X,{\underline{0}}}+J_{N-d+r}(\phi,\bar{f})}{I_{X,{\underline{0}}}}\;.

Here,
\bar{f}
is a holomorphic extension of
f
to a neighborhood of
{\underline{0}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
,

J_{N-d+r}(\phi,\bar{f})
is the ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{N}
generated by the
(N-d+r)
-minors
of the Jacobian matrix of
(\phi,\bar{f})=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k},\bar{f}_{1},\dots,\bar{f}_{r})
,
and
J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
is the saturation of
J_{X}(f,g)
with respect to
(f)
(see Section
6

When
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smooth, the zero locus
V(J_{X}(f))
is precisely the set of critical points of
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{r},0)
.

Lê-Greuel type formulas have been considered previously in various setting,
as for instance in
[
14

10

49

50

This also
unifies other known Lê-Greuel type formulas for the Milnor number:

The formula relating the Bassein and Buchweitz-Greuel Milnor number

and the Goryunov and Mond-Van Straten Milnor number of a function on

We also get an extension of this for normal and smoothable Gorenstein surface singularities;

The Lê-Greuel formula in

[

for the image Milnor number in the corank 1 case.

1
Preliminaries on singular points of complex analytic functions

Throughout this work we denote by
{\underline{0}}
the origin in the source

X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
of a map and by
0
the origin in the target. Similarly, we use
{\mathbb{B}}
for
balls in the (ambient space of the) source and
{\mathbb{D}}
for those in the target, unless it is stated
otherwise.

1.1
The smooth case

Let
(X,{\underline{0}})
be the germ of an analytic space contained in an open neighborhood
U
of the origin
{\underline{0}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, and let

f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
be a germ of complex analytic function. We shall denote also by
f:X\to V
a representative of the germ
f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
.

In the case where
X
is smooth, J. Milnor in his book
[
40
]
studied properties of complex analytic functions
with isolated critical points. In particular, if
f:X\to{\mathbb{C}}
has an isolated critical point at
x\in X
then, there is an open neighborhood

U_{x}
such that the differential of
f
is
\neq 0
in
U_{x}-\{x\}
. Then:

•

there is a sufficiently small positive real number
\varepsilon

such that for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}
such that
0\,<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
, the sphere in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
centered at
x
and with radius
\varepsilon^{\prime}
intersects
X
transversally; any such sphere is called a Milnor sphere for
f
at
x
. And,

•

Given such an
\varepsilon’
,
there exists a positive real number
\eta

so that if
{\mathbb{B}}_{\varepsilon}
is the ball bounded by the Milnor sphere
{\mathbb{S}}_{\varepsilon}
, then
the restriction of
f
to
{\mathbb{B}}_{\varepsilon}\cap f^{-1}({\mathbb{D}}_{\eta}\setminus\{0\})
is a localy trivial fibration
with a fiber which has the bouquet of
\mu(f,x)
real spheres of dimension
\dim X-1
.

The number
\mu(f,x)
of spheres is called the Milnor number of
f
at
x
. There is another way to understand the Milnor number.
First notice that if we choose local coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

of
X
in a neighborhood
of
x
, and corresponding coordinates
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
in the cotangent bundle of
T^{*}(X)
over this neighborhood, then we have a map of

X\to T^{*}(X)
given in this neighborhood by:

\xi_{1}=\frac{\partial f}{x_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{n}=\frac{\partial f}{x_{n}}.

Thus one has the well-known:

Lemma 1.1
.

The image of
df
in the cotangent bundle
T^{*}(X)
of
X
is non singular.

It is clear that
a critical point is isolated if and only if locally the image
{\rm Im}\,df
intersects the zero section
of the cotangent bundle of
X
at an isolated point. Using the Milnor number we have:

Lemma 1.2
.

The Milnor number of
f
at
x
equals the intersection number of the zero section of the cotangent bundle
T^{*}(X)

of
X
and the image of
df
in
T^{*}(X)
.

We recall that a critical point
x
of
f
is called
non-degenerate
or
ordinary quadratic
when the Hessian matrix of
f
at
x
in some local coordinates is non-degenerate (i.e., has non-zero determinant). By the Morse lemma, this is equivalent to the fact that
f
can be written as
\sum_{1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}
in local coordinates
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
. In terms of the Milnor number, it is well known that
x
is an ordinary quadratic point of
f
if and only if
\mu(f,x)=1
. In particular, any ordinary quadratic point is an isolated critical point. Thus, we have from Lemma
1.2
:

Corollary 1.3
.

The critical point of
f
at
x
is ordinary quadratic if and if the image
{\rm Im}\,df
of the differential
and the zero section of the cotangent bundle
T^{*}(X)
of
X
intersect transversally at
(x,0)
.

Complex analytic functions on a singular complex analytic set

We now extend this discussion to complex analytic functions on a singular complex analytic set following

]

(see also

]

In this situation one has to consider a Whitney complex stratification

.
Then we say that:

Definition 1.4
.

A point

Definition 1.5

A point

We recall the construction of conormal spaces, as in

Let

Now suppose that

T_{Z}^{*}U:=\overline{T_{Z_{reg}}^{*}U},

Finally, let

1.3
Morsification

As above, let
X\subset U
be a closed analytic subset with a Whitney complex stratification
{\mathcal{S}}=(S_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
. Let
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a holomorphic function and assume that
\tilde{f}\colon U\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic extension of
f
. Our definition of Morse function is taken from
[
6
, Definition 4.1]
(see also
[
22
, page 52]
in the real case).

Definition 1.7
.

We say that
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a (complex) Morse function, in the stratified sense, if the two following conditions hold:

1.

For any critical point
x\in S_{\alpha}
, such that
\dim S_{\alpha}\geq 1
, the restriction
f|_{S_{\alpha}}
has an ordinary quadratic point at
x
.

2.

For any critical point
x\in S_{\alpha}
and for any sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset S_{\beta}
such that
x_{n}\to x
,
\lim_{n}T_{x_{n}}S_{\beta}=Q
,
we have
d\tilde{f}(x)(Q)\neq 0
.

Remark 1.8
.

1.

The condition (1) in Definition
1.7
is equivalent to saying that
{\rm Im}\,d\tilde{f}
intersects transversally all the strata of the conormal space
T_{X}^{*}U
in the cotangent bundle
T^{*}U
. In particular, all critical points of a Morse fuinction are isolated.

2.

If
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a Morse function, then the absolute value
|f|\colon X\setminus f^{-1}(t)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is also a real Morse function in the sense of Goresky-McPherson
[
22
, page 52]
. This will be necessary later in order to use stratified Morse theory.

3.

The condition (2) in Definition
1.7
implies that if

Definition 1.9
.

Given a holomorphic function

The proof of the following proposition can be found in
[

Proposition 1.10
.

If

is the germ of a holomorphic function with an isolated critical point, then there exists a Morsification

of

, for some representative

Remark 1.11
.

1.

A careful revision of the proof in
[
6
, Proposition 4.3]
shows that the Morsification

can be taken as

The same arguments in the proof of

can be used to see that given a stratified subset

.

3.

When

is a Morsification of

, then the number of critical points of

near a critical point

is equal to the Milnor number of the restriction
\mu(f|_{S_{\alpha}},x)
.

Example 1.12
.

The condition 2 in Definition

is necessary. For instance, take

as the subset given by

We see that

2
Relative Polar Curves

This section concerns well-known results about polar curves relative to
stratifications and linear forms. The main properties we need in the sequel are summarized in propositions
2.3
and
2.9

2.1
Settings

Let
X
be an equidimensional analytic subset of an open neighbourhood

of the point
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and
let:

f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)

be the germ of a complex analytic function.We still call

a representative of this germ. Let
\mathcal{S}=(S_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in A}
be a Whitney stratification adapted to

of a sufficiently
small representative

of

.
By a Whitney stratification we mean a regular complex
analytic stratification as defined by Whitney in
[
57
, Section 19, p. 540]
. In particular the strata

and
their closures
\overline{S}_{\alpha}
are complex analytic spaces. We can assume that
{\underline{0}}
is in the closure
\overline{S}_{\alpha}
of
all the strata. So, the set

of indices is finite. We say that the stratification is adapted to
a complex analytic subspace if this is a union of strata.

One can prove (see for instance
[
35
]
or
[
36
, Theorem 6.7.1]
) that there is a non-empty Zariski subset

\Omega_{\alpha}
of the space of linear functions such that for every

in
\Omega_{\alpha}
we have that

\ell({\underline{0}})=0

and the critical locus

of

(\ell,f)
to the stratum, is either
empty or a non-singular curve. Then, the closure

\Gamma_{\alpha}
of

in

is either empty or a reduced
curve. Furthermore, one can choose the

\Omega_{\alpha}

so that the restriction

(\ell,f)|_{C_{\alpha}\setminus f^{-1}(0)}

is finite for all strata. Since the number of strata is locally finite, the intersection
\cap_{\alpha\in A}\Omega_{\alpha}
is a
Zariski dense open
set. For any linear form
\ell\in\cap_{\alpha}\Omega_{\alpha}
, we have that the union

\Gamma_{\ell}=\cup_{\alpha\in A}\overline{\Gamma}_{\alpha}
of the closures of the
\Gamma_{\alpha}
is either empty or a reduced curve.
We define (see for instance
[
33
, p. 310]
):

Definition 2.1

For every
\ell\in\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}:=\cap_{\alpha\in A}\Omega_{\alpha}

the polar curve of

at
{\underline{0}}
relative to
\ell
and to the stratification
\mathcal{S}
.

For
\ell\in\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}
, the contribution of each stratum
S_{\alpha}

to
\Gamma_{\alpha}
is either empty or a union of branches of the polar curve
\Gamma
.

Now let

be the image of
\Gamma_{\ell}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
by
\Phi:=(\ell,f)
.

Definition 2.2
.

When

is empty or a curve, it is called
the
Cerf diagram, of

at
{\underline{0}}
relative to
\ell
and to the stratification
\mathcal{S}

For simplicity, when there is no ambiguity we call

the polar curve of

at
{\underline{0}}

write

at
{\underline{0}}

when the stratification is fixed,

and the Cerf diagram

In
[

one proves that for any

, and

In the above discussion we may ask what happens if we replace the linear function

We consider a germ

{\mathbb{C}}^{N}

(S_{\alpha})
of

{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}

for

{\underline{0}}
, which means that for all
\epsilon^{\prime}

the sphere

is transverse to all the strata

we have a Whitney stratification induced on

5.1
The Thom condition

Suppose for now that we have a holomorphic map germ
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},0)
.
We fix a representative
f\colon X\to V
, where
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
is an open neighbourhood of the origin.
A
stratification
of
f\colon X\to V
is a pair of analytic Whiney stratifications

(S_{\alpha})
and
(T_{\beta})
of
X
and
V
, respectively, such that
for each
S_{\alpha}
there exists
T_{\beta}
with
f(S_{\alpha})\subset T_{\beta}
and
the restriction
f\colon S_{\alpha}\to T_{\beta}
is a submersion. It is known that every
map between (real or complex) analytic spaces can be made a stratified map taking
appropriate stratifications (see
[
13
]
in the semi-algebraic case, the analytic one being similar).

The
discriminant
of
f
with respect to the stratification is defined as

\widehat{\Delta}:=\bigcup_{\dim T_{\beta}<k}\overline{T_{\beta}}\;.

By construction,
\widehat{\Delta}
is a closed analytic subset of
V
of dimension
<k
. Moreover,
the restriction

f\colon X\cap f^{-1}(V\setminus\widehat{\Delta})\longmapsto V\setminus\widehat{\Delta}

is a stratified submersion and for any
u\in V\setminus\widehat{\Delta}
, the fibre
f^{-1}(u)
is a stratified subspace of codimension
k
in
X
with the induced stratification. Moreover, if
V
is connected, then so is
V\setminus\widehat{\Delta}
. A problem is that this mapping is not proper in general, so we cannot use the Thom-Mather First Isotopy Lemma to conclude that it is a locally
C^{0}
-trivial fibration.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},0)
be a holomorphic map germ. We say
that
f
admits a
Milnor-Lê fibration
if there exists a stratification of a representative

f\colon X\to V
such that for any
\epsilon,\delta
, with
0<\delta\ll\epsilon\ll 1
, the restriction

f\colon{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}\cap X\cap f^{-1}(\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\delta}\setminus\widehat{\Delta})\longmapsto\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\delta}\setminus\widehat{\Delta},

(5.1)

is a locally trivial stratified fibration with the induced stratification.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume that
f\colon X\to V
admits a stratification which satisfies the Thom condition. Then
f
admits a Milnor-Lê fibration.

Proof.

Observe that by the Thom-Mather First Isotopy Lemma
[
42

, the
condition that a proper stratified map be a
locally trivial stratified fibration is equivalent to saying that it is a stratified submersion.

Notice that for any
\epsilon,\delta>0
and for any stratum

S_{\alpha}\subset X
, the restriction of
f

to
S_{\alpha}\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}\cap f^{-1}(\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\delta}\setminus\widehat{\Delta})
is
a submersion. Hence, we must prove that we can choose
\epsilon,\delta>0
conveniently,
so that when we consider the restriction of
f
to
strata of the form
S_{\alpha}\cap{\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}}\cap f^{-1}(\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\delta}\setminus\widehat{\Delta})
,

f
is a submersion. This is equivalent to proving that choosing
\epsilon,\delta>0
conveniently,
the fibers of the map
5.1
in Definition
5.1
are stratified transversal to the sphere
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}
.

We first choose
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}
is transverse to all the strata
S_{\alpha}

such that
S_{\alpha}\subset f^{-1}(0)
. We claim that there exists
\delta>0
such that
for any

,
\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}

is transverse to

, where

is the stratum which contains

.
In particular, this implies that

is a submersion on

S_{\beta}\cap{\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}}\cap f^{-1}(\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\delta}\setminus\widehat{\Delta})

If the claim were false we could find a sequence

in

{\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon}}\cap f^{-1}(V\setminus\widehat{\Delta})
such that

and

\ker d_{x_{n}}(f|_{S_{\beta}})\subset T_{x_{n}}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon},

where

. By taking subsequences if necessary,
we can assume that all terms

and that

This implies that

T_{x}S_{\alpha}=\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\subset T\subset T_{x}\mathbb{S}_{\epsilon},

is the stratum such that

, in contradiction with the choice of

.

Any holomorphic mapping
f\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{n+k},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},0)

which defines an ICIS admits a Milnor-Lê fibration where
\widehat{\Delta}=f(C)
and
C
i
s the subset of critical points of
f
.

However, when
k>1
, there are holomorphic mappings
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},0)

which do not admit a Milnor-Lê fibration, even if
X
is smooth and
f

a complete intersection (with non-isolated singularity). The following example is due to Lê and it can
be found in
[
52
]
.

Example 5.5
.

Consider
\Phi\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{3},0)\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
given by

\Phi(x,y,z)=(y,x^{2}-y^{2}z)
. Then
f
does not admit a Milnor-Lê fibration and hence,
it does not admit a stratification which satisfies Thom’s condition.

5.2
Functions with isolated singularities relatively to
f

We start with a holomorphic function
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
. We already know that this always satisfies the Thom condition, by
[
5
]
,
and therefore it admits a Milnor-Lê fibration
[
32
]
. We look
at functions
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
such that we have a well-defined polar curve and

\Phi=(g,f)\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
also admits a Milnor-Lê fibration.
We fix the following objects:

1.

A representative
\Phi=(g,f)\colon X\to V
, for some open neighbourhood
V
of
0
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

2.

A Whitney stratification of
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
with strata

\mathcal{S}=(S_{\alpha})
in
X
and with only two strata
\left\{{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\},\{0\}\right\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}

We denote by
C(\Phi)
the set of critical points of
\Phi
respect to
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 5.6
.

We say that
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
has an
isolated
critical point (or singularity) with respect to
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
if
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
has an isolated critical point at the origin
x={\underline{0}}
with respect to
\mathcal{S}
(see Definition
1.5
).

The main theorem of this section says that if
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
has an isolated critical point respect to
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
then
\Phi=(g,f)\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
satisfies the Thom condition and hence, admits a Milnor-Lê fibration.

Theorem 5.7
.

Suppose that
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
has isolated critical point respect to

f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
. Then:

1.

\Gamma:=\overline{C(\Phi)\setminus f^{-1}(0)}
is empty or a curve in X (i.e., closed analytic of dimension one);

2.

the restriction
\Phi|_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma\to V
is finite, in particular,
\Delta:=\Phi(\Gamma)
is an analytic curve in
V
(by Remmert’s finite mapping theorem);

3.

\Phi\colon X\to V
admits a stratification
\{\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{T}\}
, such that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{S}
, it has discriminant
\widehat{\Delta}=\Delta\cup({\mathbb{C}}\times\{0\})
and it has the Thom condition.

In particular,
\Phi=(g,f)\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
admits a Milnor-Lê fibration.

Proof.

We assume that
U\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
is an open neighbourhood of
{\underline{0}}
such that
X
is closed analytic in
U
and
\tilde{g}\colon U\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a holomorphic extension of
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
.
Since
{\underline{0}}
is an isolated critical point, we can also assume that
{\underline{0}}
is the only critical point of
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

We consider the relative conormal bundle of
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
, defined as

T_{X,f}^{*}U:=\bigcup T_{S_{\alpha},f}^{*}U,

where

T_{S_{\alpha},f}^{*}U:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}U\ |\ x\in S_{\alpha}\text{ and }\xi\left(\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\right)=0\}.

By
[
5
]
, the Thom condition holds for
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
and this implies that
T_{X,f}^{*}U
is closed analytic in
T^{*}U
. Let
d\tilde{g}\colon U\to T^{*}U
be the differential mapping given by
x\mapsto(x,d_{x}\tilde{g})
. It follows that the set

C:=(d\tilde{g})^{-1}(T_{X,f}^{*}U)

is also closed analytic in
X
.

Observe that given a point
x\in S_{\alpha}
,
x\in C
if and only if
x
is a critical point of the restriction of
g
to the fibre
S_{\alpha}\cap f^{-1}(f(x))
. In particular, when
f(x)=0
,
S_{\alpha}\subset f^{-1}(0)
and hence
x\in C
if and only if
x
is a critical point of the restriction of
g
to
S_{\alpha}
. Since
{\underline{0}}
is the only critical point of
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we have
C\cap f^{-1}(0)=\{{\underline{0}}\}
and thus
\dim C\leq 1
.

Otherwise, if
f(x)\neq 0
, then
f|_{S_{\alpha}}
is a submersion and hence,
x\in C
if and only if
x
is a critical point of
\Phi|_{S_{\alpha}}
. We deduce that
\Gamma=\overline{C\setminus f^{-1}(0)}=\overline{C\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}}=C
and also
\dim\Gamma\leq 1
. Moreover,
T_{S_{\alpha},f}^{*}U
has codimension
N-1
in
T^{*}U
, so
(d\tilde{g})^{-1}(T_{S_{\alpha},f}^{*}U)
has also codimension
\leq N-1
in
U
. Thus,
\Gamma
has dimension 1 (if not empty). This shows item 1. But item 2 also follows, for
\Phi^{-1}(0)\cap\Gamma\subset C\cap f^{-1}(0)=\{{\underline{0}}\}
, hence
\Phi|_{\Gamma}\colon\Gamma\to V
is finite after shrinking the representatives, if necessary.

This proof item 3 is just an adaptation of the proof given in
[
20
, Theorem 2.6]
(see also
[
19
, Theorem 5.6.3]
) in the particular case that
g
is the restriction of a submersion. In the target
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
we consider the stratification
\mathcal{T}
with four strata:
V\setminus\widehat{\Delta}
,
\Delta\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
,
{\mathbb{C}}\times\{0\}\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
and
\{{\underline{0}}\}
. In the source
X
, we consider the stratification
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with strata of the form
S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\setminus\Gamma
and
S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\cap\Gamma
, for each
S_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{S}
and
T\in\mathcal{T}
.

The strata
T\in\mathcal{T}
are smooth, after shrinking the representatives, if necessary.
We prove that all the strata
A\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
are also smooth.
Consider first a stratum of the form
A=S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\setminus\Gamma
, with
S_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{S}
and
T\in\mathcal{T}
. We have three subcases: (a)
A=\{{\underline{0}}\}
, which is smooth trivially, (b)
A\subset f^{-1}(0)\setminus\{0\}
, with
T={\mathbb{C}}\times\{0\}\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
, so
A=S_{\alpha}
is also smooth or (c)
A\subset X\setminus f^{-1}(0)
, in this case
T=V\setminus\widehat{\Delta}
,
A=S_{\alpha}\setminus\Gamma
is smooth again.
Otherwise, we consider a stratum
A=S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\cap\Gamma
, which is either the point
\{{\underline{0}}\}
or has dimension 1 and its closure is analyitic. By the curve selection lemma, we can take a smaller representative such that
A
is smooth.

By construction,
\Phi
maps strata of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
onto strata of
\mathcal{T}
. We have to show that
\Phi
maps these strata submersively. If
A=S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\setminus\Gamma
, the only non-trivial case is when
S_{\alpha}\subset f^{-1}(0)\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
and
T={\mathbb{C}}\times\{0\}\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
. We see that
A=S_{\alpha}
and
\Phi\colon S_{\alpha}\to T
is a submersion, because
g|_{S_{\alpha}}
has no critical points. Otherwise,
A=S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\cap\Gamma
is mapped by
\Phi
onto
T
. Now, the only non-trivial case is when
T=\Delta\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
. But
A
and
T
have dimension 1 and
\Phi
is finite on
\Gamma
, so we can assume that
\Phi\colon A\to T
is a local diffeomorphism.

It is clear that
\mathcal{T}
is a Whitney stratification of
V
, but we have to show that
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
satisfies the Whitney conditions. The case of a pair strata contained in
X\setminus\Gamma
follows from
[
20
, Lemma 2.5]
. The case of a pair of strata in
\Gamma
is trivial, since one of them must be the stratum
\{{\underline{0}}\}
. Hence, it only remains to consider the case of a pair of strata
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
such that
A\subseteq\overline{B}
,
B\subset X\setminus\Gamma
and
A\subset\Gamma
has dimension 1. However, in this case, the set of points

in
A
such that

is not Whitney regular over
A
at

is analytic and has dimension 0 (see e.g.
[
18
, Proposition 2.6]
). After shrinking the representatives if necessary, we can assume that
B
is Whitney regular over
A
.

Finally, it only remains to check the Thom property for
\{\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{T}\}
. Let
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
strata such that
A\subseteq\overline{B}
. If
A\subset\Gamma
, the restriction
\Phi:A\to T
is a local diffeomorphism and the Thom condition is satisfied automatically. Otherwise, suppose that
A\subset X\setminus\Gamma
, we must have also
B\subset X\setminus\Gamma
and

A=S_{\alpha}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T)\setminus\Gamma,\quad B=S_{\beta}\cap\Phi^{-1}(T^{\prime})\setminus\Gamma,

(5.2)

for some
S_{\alpha},S_{\beta}\in\mathcal{S}
and
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
.

We take a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
B
which converges to a point
x\in A
such that
\ker d_{x_{n}}(\Phi|_{B})
converges to some space
P
. We must show
\ker d_{x}(\Phi|_{A})\subset P
. It follows from (
5.2
) that

\ker d_{x}(\Phi|_{A})=\ker d_{x}(\Phi|_{S_{\alpha}})=\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\cap\ker d_{x}\tilde{g}

and analogously,

\ker d_{x_{n}}(\Phi|_{B})=\ker d_{x_{n}}(\Phi|_{S_{\beta}})=\ker d_{x_{n}}(f|_{S_{\beta}})\cap\ker d_{x_{n}}\tilde{g}.

After taking a subsequence if necessary, we can assume that
\ker d_{x_{n}}(f|_{S_{\beta}})
converges to some space

. Since
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfies the Thom condition for
\mathcal{S}
,
\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\subset Q
. Moreover,
x\notin\Gamma=C
, so
d_{x}\tilde{g}(\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}}))\neq 0
and hence,
d_{x}\tilde{g}(Q)\neq 0
. But this implies that

P=\lim_{n}\left(\ker d_{x_{n}}(f|_{S_{\beta}})\cap\ker d_{x_{n}}\tilde{g}\right)=Q\cap\ker d_{x}(\tilde{g})\supset\ker d_{x}(f|_{S_{\alpha}})\cap\ker d_{x}\tilde{g},

as required.

5.3
Morsifications and the theorem

Given a Whitney stratified complex analytic space
Z\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and a
holomorphic function
h\colon Z\to{\mathbb{C}}
, a Morsification of
h
is a real analytic 1-parameter deformation

G\colon(Z\times{\mathbb{R}},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
such that if we set
G(x,t)=g_{t}(x)
, then each
g_{t}
is complex analytic,

g_{0}=g
and if
t\neq 0
is small enough, then
g_{t}
is a stratified Morse function on
X
.

The existence of Morsifications of a function
h\colon Z\to{\mathbb{C}}
at an isolated stratified critical
point is proved in
[
6
, Proposition 4.2]
. Therefore we have:

Lemma 5.12
.

Suppose that
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
has an isolated critical point with respect
to
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
. Then the restriction of
g
to each Milnor fiber
F_{f}
of

admits a Morsification.

Let
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
have an isolated critical point with respect to
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)

and denote by
F_{f}
and
F_{g,f}
the fibers of
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
and
\Phi=(g,f)\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
, respectively.
Let
g_{t}=g+t\ell
be a Morsification of
g
with respect to
f
as in
5.12
.
For each stratum
S_{\alpha}
, we denote by
\delta_{\alpha}
the number of critical points of
the Morsification
g_{t}
on the induced stratum
S_{\alpha}\cap F_{f}
.

We now determine the topology of the Milnor fibre
F_{f}
using the Milnor fiber
F_{g,f}

and the restriction to
F_{f}
of the Morsification of
g
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
X
is a closed analytic subset of an

and denote by

(g,f)\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)

is homeomorphic to the space
F_{g,f}\times{\mathbb{D}}^{2}

\delta_{\alpha}
Morse data

(D_{\alpha},\partial D_{\alpha})\times D_{\alpha}\times(\rm{Cone}\,(L_{\alpha}^{+}\cup_{\partial}L_{\alpha}^{-}),L_{\alpha}^{-})\,,

where

\delta_{\alpha}

Proof.

We take a representative
\Phi=(g,f)\colon X\to V

By Theorem

{\mathbb{C}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
:

(u,v,w)\mapsto(u,v,w^{2},w^{5}+uvw^{3}+(u^{3}-5uv-v)w).

A simple computation shows that
X
has the following reduced equation:

y^{2}-x(x^{2}+uvx+u^{3}-5uv-v)^{2}=0,

where
(u,v,x,y)
are the coordinates in
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
. Now we take
f\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}

and
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}
as the functions
f(u,v,x,y)=v
and
g(u,v,x,y)=u
, so
\Phi=(g,f)\colon X\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

is the projection onto the
(u,v)
-plane.

For
v\neq 0
, the fibre
F_{f}=f^{-1}(v)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}
is the disentanglement
of the singularity
F_{4}
in Mond’s list
[
44
]

(see Figure
4
). Hence
F_{f}
has the homotopy type of a bouquet of four
2
-spheres, so that
\chi(F_{f})=1+4=5
.

Figure 4:

On the other hand, for generic
(u,v)
,
F_{g,f}=g^{-1}(u)\cap f^{-1}(v)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}

is the Morsification ot the plane curve singularity
A_{4}
, so it has the homotopy type of a bouquet
of two
1
-spheres and hence,
\chi(F_{g,f})=1-2=-1
. We get
\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=6
.

Finally, we see from the computations in
[
50
, Example 4.2]
that there are exactly 6 critical
points of
g
restricted to each stratum in the interior of the fibre
F_{f}
. Namely, three of them
correspond to the 0-dimensional stratum and are the three Whitney umbrellas that appear in the stable
perturbation of the
F_{4}
singularity. The complex link
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
of the Whitney umbrella
has the homotopy type of
S^{1}
, hence

m_{\alpha}=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}\chi(\hbox{Cone}({\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}),{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha})=(-1)^{0}(1-0)=1.

The other three critical points correspond to critical points of
g
on the 1-dimensional stratum,
which is the double point curve. At these points the complex link is
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\simeq S^{0}
, hence

m_{\alpha}=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}\chi(\hbox{Cone}({\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha}),{\mathcal{L}}_{\alpha})=(-1)^{1}(1-2)=1.

Moreover, there are no critical points of
g
on the 2-dimensional stratum of regular points.
This gives
\sum_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}=6
.

6
An algebraic formula

We now give an algebraic formula for the difference
\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})
in
the case that
(X,{\underline{0}})
has isolated singularity, but we still allow
f
to have non
isolated singularities. In fact, our formula is quite simple and it has several applications that we discuss in the next section.

Suppose that
(X,{\underline{0}})
has pure dimension
d
, with
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.
Given a map germ
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{r},0)
, we denote by
J_{X}(f)
the Jacobian ideal
of
f
with respect to
X
. This is defined as follows: take generators
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k}

of the ideal
I_{X,0}
in
\mathscr{O}_{N}
and take
\bar{f}\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{N},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{r},0)

a holomorphic extension of
f
. Then
J_{X}(f)
is the ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}
given by

J_{X}(f)=\frac{I_{X,{\underline{0}}}+J_{N-d+r}(\phi,\bar{f})}{I_{X,{\underline{0}}}},

where
J_{N-d+r}(\phi,\bar{f})
is the ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{N}
generated by the
(N-d+r)
-
minors of the Jacobian matrix of
(\phi,\bar{f})=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k},\bar{f}_{1},\dots,\bar{f}_{r})
.
When
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smooth, the zero locus
V(J_{X}(f))
is precisely the set of
critical points of
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{r},0)
.

Recall that given ideals
I,J
in a ring
R
, the saturation if
I
with respect to
J
is the ideal

I\colon J^{\infty}=\left\{a\in R\ |\ aJ^{k}\subseteq I,\text{ for some $k\geq 1$}\right\}.

When
R=\mathscr{O}_{X,0}
we have a nice geometrical interpretation:

V(I\colon J^{\infty})=\overline{V(I)\setminus V(J)}\,,

where the bar means the Zariski closure (see e.g.
[
25
]
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that
(X,{\underline{0}})
has pure dimension
n+1
and it has an isolated singularity.
If
g\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
has an isolated critical point with respect to
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
. Then,

\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=(-1)^{n}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}}{(f)+J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}}\;.

Proof.

Since
X
has an isolated singularity, we have only two strata

X\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
and
\{{\underline{0}}\}
. The fibre
F_{f}
is in this case a smooth manifold with boundary,
hence it follows from Theorem
5.13
(see also
[
10
]
) that

\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=(-1)^{n}\delta_{\alpha},

where
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of critical points of a Morsification of
g
on
the interior of
F_{f}
. By the conservation of the Milnor number,
\delta_{\alpha}

is equal to the sum of the Milnor numbers of the critical points of
g
on the interior
of
F_{f}
. That is, for
0<\delta\ll\epsilon\ll 1
and for
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
0<|u|<\delta
,
the fibre
F_{f}=f^{-1}(u)\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}
is smooth at
x\in\mathaccent 28695{F_{f}}=f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}
, thus:

\delta_{\alpha}=\sum_{x\in\mathaccent 28695{F_{f}}}\mu(g|_{F_{f}};x)=\sum_{x\in\mathaccent 28695{F_{f}}}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{F_{f},x}}{J_{F_{f}}(g)}.

Moreover,
X
is also smooth at any point
x\in\mathaccent 28695{F_{f}}
, which implies that

\frac{\mathscr{O}_{F_{f},x}}{J_{F_{f}}(g)}\cong\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X,x}}{(f-u)+J_{X}(f,g)}

Suppose now that at
x={\underline{0}}
the ideal
J_{X}(f,g)
has an irredundant primary decomposition in
\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}
given by

J_{X}(f,g)=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathfrak{q}_{t}.

On the one hand, the saturation with respect to
(f)
can be written as

J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}=\bigcap_{i}(\mathfrak{q}_{i}\colon f^{\infty}).

A simple computation shows that
\mathfrak{q}_{i}\colon f^{\infty}=\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}
when
f\in\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_{i}}
and

\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_{i}\colon f^{\infty}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_{i}}
otherwise.
In particular,
J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
does not have any
\mathfrak{m}
-primary
component, that is, all its primary components have dimension
\geq 1
.

On the other hand, we have

V(J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty})=\overline{V(J_{X}(f,g))\setminus V(f))}=\Gamma,

the relative polar curve. We consider in
\Gamma
the analytic structure given
by the ideal
J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
, so that
\Gamma
has local ring

\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma}=\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}/J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
.

By condition 1 in Definition
5.8
,
\dim\Gamma=1
, therefore all
the primary components of
J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
must have dimension
\leq 1
.
This shows that in fact,
\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma}
is unmixed of dimension
1
, that is,
all the primary components have dimension 1. Also, by construction,
\Gamma\cap V(f)=\{{\underline{0}}\}
,
which implies that
f
defines a regular element in
\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma}
and thus,

\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma}
is Cohen-Macaulay (see
[
47
, Exercise C.3.1]
).

By
[
17
, Proposition 7.1]
, we compute the local intersection number at
{\underline{0}}

of the hypersurface
V(f)
and the relative polar curve
\Gamma
as the length:

i(V(f),\Gamma;{\underline{0}})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma}}{(f)}.

Finally, we use the conservation of the multiplicity (see
[
47
, Corollary E.6]
):
for
0<\delta\ll\epsilon\ll 1
and for
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
0<|u|<\delta
,

\displaystyle i(V(f),\Gamma;{\underline{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}}i(V(f-u),\Gamma;x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\Gamma,x}}{(f-u)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X,x}}{(f-u)+J_{X}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X,x}}{(f-u)+J_{X}(f,g)}=\delta_{\alpha},

since
f
is a unit in the ring
\mathscr{O}_{X,x}
when
x\in f^{-1}(u)\cap\mathaccent 28695{{\mathbb{B}}}_{\epsilon}
.
∎

Example 6.2
.

Let
(g,f)\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{3},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{2},0)
be the mapping
g(x,y,z)=x+y+z
and
f(x,y,z)=xyz
.
In this example,
f
has a non-isolated critical point, but
g
has an isolated critical point
with respect to
f
. The fibre
F_{f}
has the homotopy type of
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, so
\chi(F_{f})=0
.
The fibre
F_{g,f}
is diffeomorphic to the Milnor fibre of the plane curve
xy(x+y)=0
at the origin,
so it has Milnor number 4 and we have
\chi(F_{g,f})=1-4=-3
. Hence
\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{g,f})=3
.

On the other hand,
J_{2}(f,g)
is generated by the
2\times 2
-minors of the Jacobian matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}yz&xz&xy\\
1&1&1\end{array}\right)

which are
yz-xz,yz-xy,xz-xy
. We get that
J_{2}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}
is generated by
y-z,y-x
and hence

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{3}}{(f)+J_{2}(f,g)\colon f^{\infty}}=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{3}}{(xyz,y-z,y-x)}=3\,.

Suppose now that
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
has pure dimension
n+1
and it has isolated
singularity at
0
. Consider a holomorphic function
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
and
choose generic linear functions
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. We define
X_{n+1}=X
,
f_{n+1}=f
and for
i=1,\dots,n
:

X_{i}=X\cap\ell_{n}^{-1}(0)\cap\dots\cap\ell_{i}^{-1}(0),\quad f_{i}=f|_{X_{i}}\colon(X_{i},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0).

Corollary 6.3
.

With the hypotheses of Theorem
6.1
, one has:

\chi(F_{f})=\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}(-1)^{i-1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X_{i},0}}{(f_{i})+J_{X_{i}}(f_{i},\ell_{i-1})\colon f_{i}^{\infty}}+\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X_{1},0}}{(f_{1})}.

Proof.

The genericity of the linear forms implies that for each
i=2,\dots,n+1
,
(X_{i},{\underline{0}})

has pure dimension
i
and an isolated singularity, and
\ell_{i-1}\colon(X_{i},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)

has an isolated critical with respect to
f_{i}\colon(X_{i},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
. Moreover,
we have
F_{f_{i},\ell_{i-1}}=F_{f_{i-1}}
. By Theorem
6.1
,

\chi(F_{f_{i}})-\chi(F_{f_{i-1}})=(-1)^{i-1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X_{i},{\underline{0}}}}{(f_{i})+J_{X_{i}}(f_{i},\ell_{i-1})\colon f_{i}^{\infty}}.

Finally, for
i=1
,
(X_{1},{\underline{0}})
has dimension 1 and
\chi(F_{f_{1}})
is precisely the local
degree of
f_{1}\colon(X_{1},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
, that is,

\chi(F_{f_{1}})=\deg(f_{1})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X_{1},{\underline{0}}}}{(f_{1})}.

∎

7
Applications

In this section we discuss various consequences of Theorem
6.1
.

7.1
The classical Lê-Greuel formula

We follow the basic construction, as explained in Looijenga’s book
[
41
, Section 5.A]
.
We start with a holomorphic map germ
f=(f_{1},\dots,k_{k})\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{n+k},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},{\underline{0}})

which defines an ICIS
(X,{\underline{0}})=V(f_{1},\dots,k_{k})
of dimension
n
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+k}
.
After a linear change of coordinates in
{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
, we can assume that the
u_{k}
-axis in
{\mathbb{C}}^{k}

only meets the discriminant
\widehat{\Delta}
of
f
at
{\underline{0}}
. This generic choice of coordinates
implies that
f^{\prime}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1})
also defines an ICIS
(X^{\prime},{\underline{0}})=V(f_{1},\dots,k_{k-1})
of dimension
n+1
.
The classical Lê-Greuel formula says:

Theorem 7.1

(
[
24
,
31
]
)
.

With the above notation,

\mu(X,{\underline{0}})+\mu(X^{\prime},{\underline{0}})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{n+k}}{(f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1})+J_{k}(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})}.

This formula follows easily from Theorem
6.1
as we show below.

Proof.

We can perform a generic linear change of coordinates in
{\mathbb{C}}^{k-1}
and
assume that the
u_{k-1}
-axis only meets the discriminant
\Delta^{\prime}_{0}
of
f^{\prime}
at
{\underline{0}}
.
Hence,
(X^{\prime\prime},{\underline{0}})=V(f_{1},\dots,f_{k-2})
is an ICIS of dimension
n+2
. But this choice of
coordinates also forces that
F_{f}=F_{f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k}}
and
F_{f^{\prime}}=F_{f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}}
,
where
f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k}\colon(X^{\prime\prime},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
are the restrictions of
f_{k-1},f_{k}
to
(X^{\prime\prime},{\underline{0}})
, respectively.

By Theorem
6.1
,

\chi(F_{f^{\prime}})-\chi(F_{f})=(-1)^{n+1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X^{\prime\prime},{\underline{0}}}}{(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1})+J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})\colon(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1})^{\infty}}.

Since
f
defines an ICIS, we have
C(f)\cap f^{-1}({\underline{0}})=\{{\underline{0}}\}
, where
C(f)
is
the critical locus of
f
. Hence,
C(f)
is Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
k-1

(see e.g.
[
41
, Proposition 4.7]
). Thus, the ideal
J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})

defines a
1
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay variety in
(X^{\prime\prime},0)
. In particular,
it is unmixed, that is, we have a primary decomposition

J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})=\mathfrak{q}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathfrak{q}_{r},

where all its primary components
\mathfrak{q}_{i}
have dimension 1.
Also,
V(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1})\cap V(J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k}))=\{{\underline{0}}\}
, therefore
f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}\notin\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_{i}}
. We get:

J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})\colon(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1})^{\infty}=J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k}),

and hence

\displaystyle\chi(F_{f^{\prime}})-\chi(F_{f})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{X^{\prime\prime},{\underline{0}}}}{(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1})+J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{n+k}}{(f_{1},\dots,f_{k-1})+J_{k}(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})},

where the second equality follows easily from the definition of
J_{X^{\prime\prime}}(f^{\prime\prime}_{k-1},f^{\prime\prime}_{k})
.
In this case, the fibres
F_{f^{\prime}}
and
F_{f}
have the homotopy type of bouquet of spheres
of dimensions
n
and
n-1
, respectively, and the numbers of spheres in each case are
the Milnor numbers
\mu(X^{\prime},{\underline{0}})
and
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
, respectively. Thus,

F_{f^{\prime}}=1+(-1)^{n}\mu(X^{\prime},{\underline{0}})
,
F_{f}=1+(-1)^{n-1}\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
and

\chi(F_{f^{\prime}})-\chi(F_{f})=(-1)^{n+1}(\mu(X,{\underline{0}})+\mu(X^{\prime},{\underline{0}})).

∎

7.2
Lê-Greuel formula for smoothable space curves

In this subsection we assume that
(X,{\underline{0}})
is a space curve, that is,
\dim(X,{\underline{0}})=1

and
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
for some
N
. The Milnor number
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
was introduced
by Bassein
[
1
]
when
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable and by Buchweitz-Greuel
[
8
]
in general:

\mu(X,{\underline{0}})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\omega_{X,{\underline{0}}}}{d\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}},

where
\omega_{X,{\underline{0}}}
is the dualizing module of Grothendick and

d\colon\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}\to\omega_{X,{\underline{0}}}
is induced by the exterior derivation.
When
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable, the smoothing has the homotopy type of a bouquet of
1-spheres and the number of such spheres is
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
.

Suppose we have a holomorphic function
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
which is finite,
that is
f^{-1}(0)=\{{\underline{0}}\}
. The Milnor number
\mu(f)
of a function on a space
curve was introduced by Goryunov
[
23
]
for space curves in
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}

and by Mond-Van Straten
[
46
]
in the general case. This is:

\mu(f)=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\omega_{X,{\underline{0}}}}{df(\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}})},

where now
df\colon\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}\to\omega_{X,{\underline{0}}}
is induced by the exterior
product with
df
. When
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable,
\mu(f)
is equal to the sum of the
Milnor numbers of the critical points of
f
on the smoothing. The main relationship between
\mu(f)
and
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
is:

\mu(f)=\mu(X,{\underline{0}})+\deg(f)-1,

(7.1)

where
\deg(f)
is the local degree, that is, the number of points of the fibre
F_{f}

of
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
(see
[
29
]
when
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable and
[
48
]
for the general case).

We assume that
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable and take a smoothing, that is, a flat
morphism
\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
such that
X_{t}:=\pi^{-1}(t)
is
smooth when
t\neq 0
and
X_{0}=X
. We denote by
\bar{f}:(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)

any holomorphic extension of
f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
.
We give another proof of (
7.1
) based on our Theorems
5.13
and
6.1
.

Corollary 7.2
.

With the above notation, we have:

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\bar{f})\colon\pi^{\infty}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\mu(X,{\underline{0}})+\deg(f)-1\,,

\displaystyle\mu(X,{\underline{0}})

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\ell)\colon\pi^{\infty}}-m_{0}(X,{\underline{0}})+1\,,

where
\ell\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a generic linear form and
m_{0}(X,{\underline{0}})
is the multiplicity of
(X,{\underline{0}})
.

Proof.

The flatness of
\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
implies that

\dim(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})=2
and that
\pi
is regular in
\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}
.
But
f
is finite, so it is also regular in
\mathscr{O}_{X,{\underline{0}}}=\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}/(\pi)
.
Thus
\pi,\bar{f}
form a regular sequence and hence,
\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}
is
Cohen-Macaulay. In particular,
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})
has pure dimension 2. Moreover, the facts
that
X_{t}
is smooth for
t\neq 0
and
(X,{\underline{0}})
has isolated singularity give that
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})
has also isolated singularity.

We use Theorems
5.13
and
6.1
with the functions
\pi,\bar{f}\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
:

\chi(F_{\pi})-\chi(F_{\bar{f},\pi})=-\delta_{\alpha}=-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\bar{f})\colon\pi^{\infty}}

where
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of critical points of a Morsification
of
\bar{f}
on the interior of
F_{\pi}
. But since
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable,
the number
\delta_{\alpha}
is equal to
\mu(f)
and we get the first formula.

For the second formula, just observe that
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})=1-\chi(F_{\pi})
and
\deg(f)=\chi(F_{\bar{f},\pi})
.

Finally, the last formula follows from the other two when
f=\ell
,
taking into account that the local degree of
\ell:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
is the multiplicity
m_{0}(X,{\underline{0}})
.
∎

7.3
Lê-Greuel formula for isolated determinantal singularities

We recall some basic definitions and properties of isolated determinantal
singularities (IDS) from
[
49
]
. Let
0<s\leq m\leq n
be integers. We
denote by
M_{m,n}=M_{m,n}({\mathbb{C}})
the space of complex matrices of size

m\times n
and by
M_{m,s}^{s}
the subset of matrices with rank
<s
.
We say that
(X,{\underline{0}})
is an IDS of type
(m,n;s)
if
(X,{\underline{0}})=F^{-1}(M_{m,n}^{s})

for some holomorphic map germ
F\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{N},{\underline{0}})\to M_{m,n}
such that:

1.

\operatorname{codim}(X,{\underline{0}})=(m-s+1)(n-s+1)
,

2.

X
is smooth at
x
and
\operatorname{rank}F(x)=s-1
, for all
x\neq{\underline{0}}
in a neighbourhood of the origin.

For technical reasons we restrict ourselves to the cases where either
s=1

or
N<(m-s+2)(n-s+2)
. Then, we have that
(X,{\underline{0}})
is smoothable. In fact,
a determinantal smoothing is constructed as follows: choose a generic matrix
A\in M_{m,n}

and consider the map germ
F_{A}\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{C}},{\underline{0}})\to M_{m,n}
given by
F_{A}(x,t)=F(x)+tA
.
Then,
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})=F_{A}^{-1}(M_{m,n}^{s})
is a determinantal variety in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{C}}

and
\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
is the restriction of the projection
\pi(x,t)=t
.
If
A
is generic, then for all
t\neq 0
,
X_{t}:=\pi^{-1}(t)
is smooth and
F_{A}
has rank
s-1
at any point in
X_{t}
.

It follows that the topology of the Milnor fibre
F_{\pi}=X_{t}\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}
of
the determinantal smoothing is independent of the choice of
A
. In general,

F_{\pi}
does not have the homotopy type of a bouquet of spheres (see
[
11
]
),
so we cannot speak of a Milnor number. We consider instead the vanishing Euler characteristic
defined in
[
49
]
as
\nu(X,{\underline{0}})=(-1)^{d}(\chi(F_{\pi})-1)
, where
d=\dim(X,{\underline{0}})
.

Assume now that
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
is a holomorphic function with isolated critical point
defined on the IDS
(X,{\underline{0}})
. We associate a pair of invariants with this function. As above,
we denote by
\bar{f}\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
any holomorphic extension of
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
.
On the one hand, the Milnor number
\mu(f)
is the number of critical points of
f_{a}|_{X_{t}}
,
where
f_{a}
is a Morsification of
\bar{f}
on the determinantal smoothing
X_{t}
.
On the other hand, the vanishing Euler characteristic of the fiber is also defined as

\nu(X\cap f^{-1}(0),{\underline{0}})=(-1)^{d-1}(\chi(F_{\pi}\cap f_{a}^{-1}(c))-1),

where
c
is a regular value of
f_{a}
. It is shown in
[
49
]
that both numbers
are well defined and depend only on the germs of
X
and
f
at
{\underline{0}}
.

In this setting we have again a Lê-Greuel type formula for IDS. The following corollary
can be proved by using the same arguments as in Corollary
7.2
:

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
be a holomorphic function
with isolated critical point on an IDS
(X,{\underline{0}})
of dimension
d
. Then,

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}}}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\bar{f})\colon\pi^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\nu(X,{\underline{0}})+\nu(X\cap f^{-1}(0),{\underline{0}}),

\displaystyle\nu(X,{\underline{0}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{d+1}(-1)^{i+1}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{i},{\underline{0}}}}{(\pi_{i})+J_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}(\pi_{i},\ell_{i-1})\colon\pi_{i}^{\infty}}+m_{0}(X,{\underline{0}})-1\right),

where
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{d}\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
are generic linear forms,

\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathcal{X}\cap\ell_{d}^{-1}(0)\cap\dots\cap\ell_{i}^{-1}(0)
and
\pi_{i}=\pi|_{\mathcal{X}_{i}}
.

The second equation in Corollary
7.3
was proved originally in
[
49
]
.

7.4
Lê-Greuel formula for image Milnor number

The image Milnor number was introduced by Mond
[
45
]
for a
hypersurface
(X,{\underline{0}})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}
which is defined as the image of
a holomorphic map germ
f\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{n},S)\to({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1},{\underline{0}})
with isolated instability.
Here
S\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is a finite subset whose cardinality is the number of irreducible components of
(X,{\underline{0}})

and the restriction
({\mathbb{C}}^{n},S)\to(X,{\underline{0}})
is the normalisation, since
f
must be finite
and generically one-to-one. The condition that
f
has isolated instability ensures
that
f
admits a stabilisation, provided that either
f
has corank one or
(n,n+1)

are nice dimensions in the sense of Mather
[
43
]
. By definition, a stabilisation
is a 1-parameter unfolding
F\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{C}},S)\to({\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}\times{\mathbb{C}},{\underline{0}})
given by

F(x,s)=(f_{s}(x),s)
with the property that
f_{0}=f
and
f_{s}
has only stable singularities when
s\neq 0
.

Mond proved in
[
45
]
that
X_{s}\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}
has the homotopy type
of a bouquet of
n
-spheres, where
X_{s}
is the image of
f_{s}
and
0<|s|<\delta\ll\epsilon
.
The number of such spheres is called the image Milnor number. This is denoted by
\mu_{I}(f)

and it is independent of the choice of
\delta,\epsilon
and the stabilisation. Since
\mu_{I}(f)

is invariant under coordinate changes in the source and target of
f
, and the normalisation
is unique up to coordinate changes in the source, it is also natural to consider it
as an invariant of
(X,{\underline{0}})
: the image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(X,{\underline{0}})
of
(X,{\underline{0}})
.

There is a long standing conjecture by Mond
[
45
]
which claims that

\operatorname{codim}_{\mathscr{A}_{e}}(f)\leq\mu_{I}(f),

with equality when
f
is weighted homogeneous. Here,
\operatorname{codim}_{\mathscr{A}_{e}}(f)

is the extended
\mathscr{A}
-codimension and can be interpreted geometrically as
the number of parameters in a miniversal unfolding of
f
. Hence, the conjecture is
an inequality of type
\tau\leq\mu
, where
\tau
is the usual Tjurina number of
(X,{\underline{0}})
,
but for the image Milnor number instead of the classical Milnor number.
The main difference is that, instead of deforming the implicit equation of
X

to get a smooth object, we deform the parametrisation of
X
to get a stable object.
We refer to the survey
[
50
]
for a recent account on the Mond conjecture.

The image
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})
of the stabilisation
F
, together with the projection

\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
,
\pi(y,s)=s
, defines a flat deformation of

(X,{\underline{0}})
whose Milnor fibre is
F_{\pi}=X_{s}\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\epsilon}
. We also have a natural
stratification of
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})
given by the stable types on
\mathcal{X}\setminus\{{\underline{0}}\}
.
We denote such stratification by
(S_{\alpha})
.

Assume now that
f
has corank one and let
\ell\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a generic
linear function, which defines a hyperplane
H=\ell^{-1}(0)
. Then

f^{-1}(H)
is smooth at
S
and the restriction
f\colon(f^{-1}(H),S)\to(H,{\underline{0}})
has
isolated instability. As a consequence, its image
(X\cap H,{\underline{0}})
has also a well defined
image Milnor number
\mu_{I}(X\cap H,{\underline{0}})
. The following Lê-Greuel type formula in this context was proved in
[
50
]
:

Theorem 7.4
.

With the above notation we have:

\mu_{I}(X,{\underline{0}})+\mu_{I}(X\cap H,{\underline{0}})=\sum_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha},

where
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of critical points of
\ell
on the stratum
S_{\alpha}\cap X_{s}
.

The corank one assumption on
f
also implies that the multiplicity is
m_{\alpha}=1

for all stratum
S_{\alpha}\cap X_{s}
(see
[
50
]
). Hence, this formula can be seen
as a particular case of our Theorem
5.13
. Unfortunately, we do not have yet
an algebraic version of this formula, as in the previous subsections. We presume
that such an algebraic version of the formula could help for proving the Mond conjecture,
at least in the corank one case.

7.5
Lê-Greuel formula for smoothable Gorenstein surfaces

Let
(X,{\underline{0}})
be a normal surface singularity which is also smoothable and Gorenstein. Take a smoothing
\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
with Milnor fibre
F_{\pi}
. It follows
from the works of Wahl
[
56
]
and Greuel and Steenbrink
[
26
]

that
\beta_{1}(F_{\pi})=0
and that
\beta_{2}(F_{\pi})
is independent of the choice of the smoothing,
where
\beta_{i}
is the
i
th-Betti number. Thus, it makes sense to call Milnor number to
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})=\beta_{2}(F_{\pi})
.

Suppose now that
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
is a holomorphic function with
isolated critical point. Then the especial fibre
(X\cap f^{-1}(0),{\underline{0}})
is
a space curve and has a well defined Milnor number
\mu(X\cap f^{-1}(0),{\underline{0}})
, as in Subsection
7.2
.
We choose any holomorphic extension
\bar{f}\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
of
f
. We define
the Milnor number
\mu(f)
as the sum of the Milnor numbers of the critical points of
\bar{f}
restricted
to the interior of
F_{\pi}
.

The next corollary follows again easily from Theorems
5.13
and
6.1

and contains the Lê-Greuel type formulas for smoothable Gorenstein surfaces:

Corollary 7.5
.

With the above notation we have

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},0}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\bar{f})\colon\pi^{\infty}},

\displaystyle\mu(f)

\displaystyle=\mu(X,{\underline{0}})+\mu(X\cap f^{-1}(0),{\underline{0}}),

\displaystyle\mu(X,{\underline{0}})

\displaystyle=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X},0}}{(\pi)+J_{\mathcal{X}}(\pi,\ell_{2})\colon\pi^{\infty}}-\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathscr{O}_{\mathcal{X}_{2},0}}{(\pi_{2})+J_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}(\pi_{2},\ell_{1})\colon\pi_{2}^{\infty}}+m_{0}(X,{\underline{0}})-1,

where
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}:{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{C}}
are generic linear forms,

\mathcal{X}_{2}=\mathcal{X}\cap\ell_{2}^{-1}(0)
and
\pi_{2}=\pi|_{\mathcal{X}_{2}}
.

An immediate consequence of the corollary is that
\mu(f)
is well defined and depends only on
f\colon(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
.

Example 7.6
.

We consider a surface
(X,{\underline{0}})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{5}
which is defined by the Pfaffians

P_{1},\dots,P_{2r+1}
of a skew-symmetric matrix
F=(f_{ij})
of size
(2r+1)\times(2r+1)

and with entries
f_{ij}\in\mathscr{O}_{5}
such that
f_{ji}=-f_{ij}
. Recall that
the Pfaffian
P_{i}
is the square root of the determinant of the submatrix of
F
obtained
after removing the
i
th-row and
i
th-column (see e.g.
[
7
]
).

If
(X,{\underline{0}})
has dimension 2, then it is Gorenstein, by a theorem due to
Buchsbaum and Eisenbud
[
7
]
. In fact, the same theorem says that
any Gorenstein surface
(X,{\underline{0}})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{5}
can be obtained in this way. When
r=1
,
(X,{\underline{0}})
is
a complete intersection, but if
r>1
and all the entries
f_{ij}
are in the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{5}
,
then
(X,{\underline{0}})
is not a complete intersection. As far as we know, this is the simplest example of
a Gorenstein surface which is not a complete intersection.

If we choose the functions
f_{ij}
in such a way that
(X,{\underline{0}})
has isolated singularity,
then
(X,{\underline{0}})
is normal. Moreover,
(X,{\underline{0}})
is also smoothable since a smoothing is
constructed as follows: take a skew-symmetric matrix
A=(a_{ij})
of the same size
whose entries are generic constants
a_{ij}\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
a_{ji}=-a_{ij}
.
We consider the 3-fold
(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{5}\times{\mathbb{C}}
defined by the
Pfaffians of
F+tA
and the projection
\pi\colon(\mathcal{X},{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
,
\pi(x,t)=t
.
It follows that
\pi^{-1}(0)=X
and
\pi^{-1}(t)
is smooth if
t\neq 0
and the matrix
A
is generic enough.

We compute the Milnor number
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})
in the case that we take a
skew-symmetric matrix
F=(f_{ij})
of size
5\times 5
and whose entries
f_{ij}

are linear forms, i.e., homogenous polynomials of degree one. If the coefficients of
f_{ij}

are generic enough then
(X,{\underline{0}})
has isolated singularity and we can proceed with this example.
We use Corollary
7.5
to compute the Milnor number of
(X,{\underline{0}})
with
the aid of
Singular
, which returns
\mu(X,{\underline{0}})=4
.

7.6
A remark on indices of vector fields

Let
(X,{\underline{0}})
be a complex analytic germ of dimension
n+k
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and
f:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}}^{k},0)

a holomorphic map-germ, stratified with respect to some complex analytic Whitney stratifications
adapted to
V=V(f)
. Assume further that

{\underline{0}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
is a stratum and
f
satisfies the Thom condition
with respect to the above stratification. One has a local Milnor-Lê fibration.
We let
{\mathbb{B}}_{\varepsilon}
be a Milnor ball for
X
, small enough so that each stratum in
X\cap{\mathbb{B}}_{\varepsilon}
has
{\underline{0}}
in its closure. We let

F_{f}=f^{-1}(t)\cap B_{\varepsilon}
be a Milnor fiber of
f
.

Let
g:(X,{\underline{0}})\to({\mathbb{C}},0)
be a germ with an isolated critical point relatively to
V(f)

in the stratified sense,
and that
(f,g)
satisfies the Thom condition with respect to the given stratification. We denote by

F_{f,g}
the corresponding Milnor fiber. We equip the fibers
F_{f}
and
F_{f,g}
with the stratification
obtained by intersecting these fibers with the Whitney strata of
X
.

Just as in
[
3
, Section 2]
, at each point
x
near the boundary
\partial F_{f}
we can project
the gradient
\overline{\nabla}g(x)
to the stratum that contains
x
, and glue all these vector fields
together by a partition of unity in order to get a stratified vector field on
U
that we denote by
\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}}
.
Now we extend
\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}}
over the interior of
F_{f}
using radial extension
as defined by M. H. Schwartz (see
[
2
, Section 7]
or
[
4
, Section 2.3]
), and let

{\rm Ind}(\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}})
be the sum of all the local Schwartz (or radial) indices of this
extension. Then the Lê-Greuel formula in
[
10
]
says:

\chi(F_{f})-\chi(F_{f,g})={\rm Ind}(\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}})\;.

(7.2)

The problem now is computing the index
{\rm Ind}(\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}})

when
F_{f}
is singular.

If
k=1
,
V
is equidimensional and
g
has an isolated critical point relative to
f
, then Theorem
5.13
implies:

{\rm Ind}(\overline{\nabla}g|_{F_{f}})=\sum_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha}m_{\alpha}\,,

where
\delta_{\alpha}
is the number of critical points in
each stratum in
F_{f}
of a Morsification of
g
, and

m_{\alpha}=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}\chi({\rm Cone}(L_{\alpha}),L_{\alpha})\,,

where
L_{\alpha}
is the complex link of the stratum.
Is this a particular case of a general theorem?
