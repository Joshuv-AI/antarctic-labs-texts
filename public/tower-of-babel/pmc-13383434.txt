hUCMSC-exosomes attenuate acute lung injury by inhibiting ferroptosis in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells through ribosomal protein RPS11 upregulation
Authors: Anyun Ding, Mingou Yan, Yunong Li, Zhongyun Bi, Jingchun Song, Lin Zhao, Renyu Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hUCMSC-Exos) are a promising treatment for acute lung injury (ALI)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), but traditional delivery methods have limitations. Therefore, this study presents a noninvasive therapeutic approach for ALI/ARDS, offering new mechanistic insights and identifying potential therapeutic targets.ResultsWe established a nebulized LPS-induced ALI model that was characterized by diffuse lung injury and high homogeneity. Following inhalation, hUCMSC-Exos were observed to be internalized by pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells. Analysis revealed that hUCMSC-Exos alleviated ALI by reducing the severity of histological damage, pulmonary oedema, lung inflammation and ferroptosis. Additionally, hUCMSC-Exos improved the mitochondrial function of human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (HPMECs) via the transfer of mitochondrial components. Subsequent proteomic sequencing of mitochondria isolated from HPMECs receiving different treatments revealed the significant differential expression of ribosomal proteins among the groups. The most significantly upregulated protein, RPS11, was identified as a key mediator; its knockdown blocked the ability of hUCMSC-Exos to suppress ferroptosis and restore mitochondrial function in HPMECs. Mechanistically, hUCMSC-Exos exert their effects by enhancing mitochondria-encoded protein translation.ConclusionsWe report a mechanism whereby hUCMSC-Exos upregulate RPS11 to promote mitochondria-encoded protein translation, rescuing mitochondrial function, inhibiting ferroptosis in HPMECs, and ultimately alleviating ALI. Validated across multiple models and supported by multi-omics analyses, our findings collectively establish nebulized hUCMSC-Exos as a promising cell-free therapy targeting mitochondrial homeostasis in HPMECs for the treatment of ALI.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04565-1.Keywords: ALI/ARDS, Endothelial cell, hUCMSC exosomes, Ferroptosis, Mitochondrial function, Mitochondrial translation, RPS11

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) constitutes a life-threatening syndrome manifesting refractory hypoxemia secondary to the dysfunction of alveolar‒capillary barrier permeability and bilateral alveolar infiltrative lesions. ALI/ARDS treatment remains a major challenge in critical care medicine because of the unchanged mortality burden and the lack of targeted drug therapies [1]. Sepsis is the primary aetiology of ARDS, with a case fatality rate of 37% [2]. Human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (HPMECs), the core structural components of the alveolar‒capillary barrier, act as central mediators in the pathological process of lung injury caused by different aetiologies [3–5]. The high mortality rate of ALI/ARDS is closely related to HPMEC damage; thus, exploring therapeutic mechanisms to protect the function of HPMECs is crucial for advancing novel therapeutic strategies for ALI/ARDS [6].

Emerging cell-based therapeutic strategies, particularly those involving mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos), are garnering increasing attention in regenerative medicine for the treatment of ALI/ARDS [7]. MSCs, which exhibit anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory and restorative effects, have potential applications in ALI/ARDS [8]. However, cell therapies carry a risk of pulmonary embolism and carcinogenicity [9, 10]. The therapeutic effects of MSCs are due primarily to paracrine effects, with exosomes identified as the key mediators. In addition, exosomes are more stable and less immunogenic than MSCs [11]. Therefore, exosomes, as cell-free alternatives, may be more advantageous and MSC-Exos are an important area for future research on ALI/ARDS [8]. Exosomes, a class of extracellular vesicles measuring 40–160 nm in diameter, serve as critical mediators of intercellular communication. These membrane-bound structures encapsulate diverse biomolecular cargoes, including proteins, nucleic acids (DNA/RNA), lipids, and metabolic byproducts that collectively orchestrate cell signalling processes [12]. MSC-Exos can alleviate or even cure ALI/ARDS through a variety of mechanisms that attenuate inflammatory dysregulation and cell death responses mediated or influenced by different pulmonary cell types [13]. Many randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials have shown that intervention with MSC-Exos is safe and effective, significantly reducing mortality in patients with COVID-19-related ARDS [14, 15]. To date, most of the published studies on MSCs and MSC-Exos have involved intravenous injections, whereas only a few studies have explored the treatment of ALI/ARDS via inhalation of nebulized MSC-Exos. However, nebulization appears to be a more promising option than injection because exosome loss may occur after intravenous administration, and nebulization can improve lung bioavailability with fewer systemic side effects [10, 16]. Recent studies have shown that nebulized MSC-Exos can reduce lung injury and inflammation in LPS-induced mouse models [17]. Moreover, their inhalation has been shown to improve symptoms in COVID-19 patients [18]. Following inhalation, nebulized MSC-Exos exhibited rapid and extensive distribution throughout lung tissues within one hour, while demonstrating the capability to penetrate the air‒blood barrier and enter the blood circulation. These observations confirm their potential to traverse this barrier and exert their effects on HPMECs [19].

Unlike classical apoptosis, ferroptosis is a regulated cell death modality driven by iron-mediated oxidative stress, where compromised antioxidant defences lead to peroxidation of membrane phospholipids [20]. Ferroptosis serves as a critical driver of the pathogenesis and progression of many diseases, such as cancer, degenerative diseases, and ALI/ARDS [21, 22]. Ferroptosis is modulated by a network of interconnected metabolic pathways, including redox homeostasis, iron metabolism, lipid metabolism and mitochondrial activity. Mitochondrial metabolism constitutes a fundamental mechanism underlying ferroptosis [22]. Furthermore, alterations in mitochondrial morphology and function, particularly metabolic reprogramming and reduced ATP production, may directly contribute to ferroptosis initiation [23]. Mitochondria can trigger and amplify ferroptosis induced by cysteine deficiency and glutathione depletion [21]. Exosomes have been shown to be involved in disease progression by regulating ferroptosis [24]; however, the specific mechanism involved remains incompletely understood.

Mitochondria are the central organelles of cellular energy metabolism and play key roles in anabolic and catabolic processes, bioenergetics, cell signalling, and programmed cell death [25]. Accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with a range of respiratory diseases, including ALI/ARDS, bronchial asthma and pulmonary fibrosis [26]. However, the specific mechanisms of mitochondrial function regulation in different cells remain unclear although effective therapies to alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction are urgently needed [27].

Numerous studies have revealed that certain subsets of extracellular vesicles are enriched with mitochondrial components (mtDNA, mtRNA, and mitochondrial proteins) and that these mitochondrial extracellular vesicles (mitoEVs) effectively deliver functional mitochondrial cargo to modulate recipient cell functionality under diverse pathophysiological conditions [27, 28]. Notably, emerging evidence has demonstrated that mitochondria isolated from autologous or heterologous sources exhibit robust biocompatibility, positioning targeted mitochondrial transfer as an emerging organelle-targeted treatment modality for human diseases [29].

This study was designed to elucidate the specific mechanism by which the inhalation of nebulized human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hUCMSC-Exos) attenuates LPS- or CLP-induced ALI by inhibiting ferroptosis in HPMECs. Our study shows that hUCMSC-Exos improve the mitochondrial function of HPMECs upon the transfer of mitochondrial components. Using proteomics, single-cell sequencing, and further experimental validation, ribosomal protein S11 (RPS11) was identified as a key molecule in this process. Mechanistically, hUCMSC-Exos promote mitochondrial translation by upregulating RPS11 expression, thereby restoring mitochondrial function and inhibiting ferroptosis in HPMECs.

Materials and methods

Preparation of hUCMSCs

hUCMSCs were from ZQXZbio (DF-GMP-ZB09BA, Shanghai, China). The hUCMSCs were cultured in serum-free medium for mesenchymal stem cells (SC2013-G, TBD, China) within a thermoregulated humidity-controlled chamber at 37 °C under atmospheric pressure with 5% CO2. The cellular population presented full compliance with the ISCT identification criteria for MSCs [30]. The morphology of the cells was assessed with inverted microscopy and passaged every 48 to 72 h. For hUCMSC characterization, the expression of surface molecules CD73, CD90, CD105, CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (BD, USA) was detected by flow cytometry.

Isolation and characterization of hUCMSC-Exos

High-quality hUCMSCs (3rd–5th generation) were selected for medium replacement with fresh serum-free medium when they reached approximately 80–90% confluence, and the supernatant was collected after 48 h of culture. Exosome isolation was performed by differential centrifugation of the conditioned medium under refrigerated conditions as follows: clarification at 300 × g, followed by successive centrifugation steps under refrigerated conditions at 2,000 × g and 10,000 × g to eliminate cellular debris. The processed supernatant was sterile-filtered (0.22 μm) before ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g to harvest the exosomes from hUCMSC cultures. The extracted hUCMSC-Exos were reconstituted in PBS and cryopreserved at -80 °C for subsequent experimental applications. An ultrastructural analysis via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the exosomal cup-shaped ultrastructure. Quantitative profiling utilizing nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed hydrodynamic diameter profiles and concentrations. Western blotting was employed to detect the exosomal markers CD9, CD63 and CD81 (Proteintech, China) in hUCMSC-Exos. hUCMSC-Exos were labeled with PKH26 (Sigma, USA), added to HPMECs, cultured for 24 h, and then observed with CLSM.

Animal experiments

All experiments received approval from the Animal Experiment Center Committee of China Medical University, and all animal procedures adhered to institutional guidelines (Approval number: KT2021170). Healthy SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from HFK Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The mice were acclimated to standard conditions with unrestricted provision of food and water. After preliminary experimental mapping, we applied a mouse nebulizer drug delivery device (KW-DM-YWH, Nanjing, China), which allowed inhalation of the 1.0 mg/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) solution through a mask to construct the mouse ALI model. 2 h later, each mouse inhaled a suspension of hUCMSC-Exos containing 2 × 10⁹ particles under the nebulization conditions described in Table S1, whereas control mice inhaled the same volume of nebulized PBS for an equivalent duration. 24 h later, the mice were euthanized, with lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) subsequently collected. The degree of pathological damage to the lung tissue was assessed by haematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining. Pulmonary oedema was quantified by calculating the wet/dry weight (W/D) ratio. Liver, heart, spleen and kidney samples were also obtained for histological evaluation to assess the biosafety of the hUCMSC-Exos. The remaining tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experiments.

HE staining

For histology, lung specimens were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm, and they were subjected to HE, sealed, and observed under a microscope. The severity of injury was scored on the basis of five independent variables: pulmonary edema, alveolar and interstitial inflammation, haemorrhage, atelectasis, and hyaline membrane formation. Histopathological injury was scored on a scale from 0 to 4, where a score of 0 represented no detectable injury, 1 indicated involvement of up to 25% of the assessed area, 2 corresponded to 25–50% involvement, 3 to 50–75%, and 4 denoted injury affecting more than 75% of the tissue. In each section, each injury was scored in ten randomly selected areas (200 ×). Inflammation scores were calculated by averaging the scores determined independently by two individuals.

Lung injury evaluation

The wet weights of the lungs were recorded for each group. Following 48 h of dehydration at 60 °C, the samples were reweighed to measure dry weight. Lung water content was determined by W/D ratio. BALF was obtained by tracheal instillation of 1 ml PBS, and lavage was repeated 3 times. Then, the BALF was centrifuged (600 × g), and the supernatant was collected and cryopreserved at -20 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using protein assay kit, and levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) were analysed with ELISA kits (RUIXIN, China).

Cell culture and treatment

HPMECs were from TongPai Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). We used 1 µg/ml LPS (Sigma, USA) stimulation for 24 h as a model of cell injury. hUCMSC-Exos (50 µg/ml) and 1 µg/ml LPS solution were added to HPMECs, which were then cultured for 24 h to assess the effect of hUCMSC-Exos. We collected plasma samples from 20 ARDS patients to stimulate inflammation in HPMECs in vitro. The severity of these samples was classified into three levels, mild, moderate, and severe, based on the Berlin definition. Combined representative plasma samples of the different severities were adjusted to 10% in 1% complete culture medium, with healthy volunteer plasma as the control [31]. HPMECs were also cocultured with hUCMSC-Exos (50 µg/ml) for 24 h. The cells and culture supernatants were collected for protein extraction and ELISA. All procedures involving human samples in this study conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Institutional Research Committee of the First Hospital of China Medical University (approval number: 2024-986‐2).

Iron, GSH, and lipid peroxidation assays

Iron and GSH levels were assessed using respective assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China). All kits were used according to the instructions. Iron and GSH levels were measured in lung tissues, and iron concentrations were measured in plasma samples. For the cellular experiments, lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels of HPMECs were detected by C11 BODIPY (1:1000, Thermo, USA) staining, and the results were imaged using a CLSM and quantified by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence staining

ALI mice were subjected to inhalation of nebulized PKH26-labeled hUCMSC-Exos, and 24 h later, the lung tissues were collected, dehydrated, embedded, and frozen in O.C.T. compound. The cryosections were blocked and incubated with a primary anti-CD31 antibody overnight, after which the immunofluorescence signal detection was facilitated after incubation with a secondary antibody. As previously described, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining, deparaffinised, antigenically repaired, and incubated with primary antibodies against glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4; 67763-1-AP, Proteintech), hydroxynonenal (4HNE; bs-6313R, Bioss), and CD31 (11265-1-AP, Proteintech), followed by a subsequent incubation with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies (Elabscience, China) at a dilution of 1:200. Tissue sections underwent confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM); fluorescence intensity and colocalization were calculated using ImageJ.

After PKH26-labeled hUCMSC-Exos were incubated with HPMECs for 24 h, the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and the cells were detected by CLSM. HPMECs were plated in 24-well cell culture with LPS and hUCMSC-Exos for 24 h. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4; 22401-1-AP, Proteintech), GPX4 (67763-1-AP, Proteintech), and RPS11 (ab175213, Abcam). Finally, the cells were observed using CLSM, and the fluorescence intensity and colocalization coefficient were calculated using ImageJ.

Western blotting

RIPA buffer was added to the tissue samples, after which the samples were pulverized using an ultrasonic cell disruptor to extract lung tissue and whole-cell proteins. Following SDS–PAGE separation (100 V), proteins were electroblotted onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; 18985-1-AP, Proteintech), ACSL4, GPX4 (Proteintech, China), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1; #4393, CST), VDAC (81538-1-RR, Proteintech), RPS11 (ab175213, Abcam) and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequent to binding the corresponding HRP secondary antibody, the signals were developed, detected and observed using ECL.

Mitochondrial labeling

hUCMSCs were incubated with MitoTracker Red fluorescent dye staining solution (1:1000, Thermo, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, and then fresh medium was added for further culture. After 48 h, the medium was obtained to isolate the exosomes, and the exosome precipitates were stored at -80 °C. MitoTracker Green fluorescent dye (1:1000, Thermo, USA) was incubated with the HPMECs for 20 min, and MitoTracker Red-labeled hUCMSC-Exos were added for 24 h of culture. The cells were visualized using CLSM, and the degree of colocalization was calculated using ImageJ.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS levels

MitoSOX Red Superoxide Indicator (Thermo, USA) is a mitochondrial-selective fluorophore for living cells. The oxidation products emit intense fluorescence. Before use, a stock solution was prepared by adding an amount of DMSO. After pretreatment, HPMECs were loaded with MitoSOX (1:1000) and were then incubated for 30 min. The data were collected using flow cytometry, and the fluorescence intensity was analysed using FlowJo.

Analysis of the oxygen consumption rate

An analysis of the mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was performed using an Oroboros analyser (Innsbruck, Austria), and respiration was reported as the OCR. After quantifying basal oxygen consumption, 2 µl of oligomycin (Huawei, Beijing, China) was introduced to inhibit mitochondrial complex V. The maximum respiration rate was gradually reached by the addition of 1 µl of FCCP (Huawei, Beijing, China). Finally, 1 µl of antimycin A and 1 µl of rotenone (Huawei, Beijing, China) were administered to differentiate mitochondrial and residual (nonmitochondrial respiration) oxygen consumption. The subsequent data were processed using DatLab software.

Mitochondrial extraction

The cells were first digested and collected, after which they were resuspended in ice-cold mitochondrial extraction buffer. After initial centrifugation (500 × g) to sediment the nuclei and unlysed cells, sequential centrifugations at 1,000 × g (10 min, 4 °C) and 11,000 × g (15 min, 4 °C) yielded mitochondrial pellets and cytosolic fractions, respectively, which were solubilized in PMSF-supplemented RIPA buffer for subsequent experiments.

Mitochondrial proteomic profiling

To identify differentially expressed proteins in the mitochondria of HPMECs from different groups, mitochondria were obtained as described above and subjected to proteomic profiling using 4D label‑free quantitative mass spectrometry.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE292681 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The dataset includes control data and data from mice with LPS-induced ALI. Quality control was performed using the following criteria: the detected gene counts per cell ranged from 300 to 7000, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were larger than 1000 (top 3% removed), the proportion of mitochondrial gene expression was below 10%, and the proportion of erythrocyte-related gene expression was less than 3%. Subsequently, we applied LogNormalize for normalization and used principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. Cell clustering was performed with the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cell type annotation was performed using the mouse-specific dataset in CellMarker 2.0, and candidate cell types were ranked according to matching scores. Final annotation results were verified by literature review and comparison with canonical marker gene signatures reported in previous single-cell sequencing studies.

Statistical analysis

All the data represent the mean ± SD. One-way or two-way ANOVA (Graph Pad Prism 10.0) was used for statistical comparisons between groups (≥ 3), with additional Tukey’s post hoc correction. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of hUCMSCs and exosomes

The hUCMSCs appeared spindle shaped under an inverted microscope (Fig. S1A). The stem cell surface marker proteins CD73, CD90, and CD105 were positively expressed in primary hUCMSCs, while CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR exhibited low expression, which proved their identity (Fig. S1B). Characterization of hUCMSC-Exos and nebulized hUCMSC‑Exos was performed by TEM, NTA, and Western blotting. Vesicles with cup-shaped double-membrane structures, which are characteristic of exosome morphology, were visible under TEM (Fig. S1C). NTA analysis showed that the size distribution of hUCMSC-Exos remained within 30–150 nm after nebulization (Fig. S1D), and this result was further validated by dynamic light scattering (DLS), which revealed no significant difference in average particle size between the nebulized and non-nebulized groups (Fig. S1E). Western blot results suggested that the exosome marker proteins were highly expressed in the isolated vesicles (Fig. S1F). These results indicated that we effectively isolated exosomes secreted by hUCMSCs. Moreover, nebulization did not compromise the structural integrity of hUCMSC-Exos. As shown in Fig. S1G, PKH26-labeled exosomes were successfully taken up by HPMECs.

hUCMSC-Exos inhalation alleviates LPS- or CLP-induced ALI in mice

We constructed a mouse model of ALI via inhalation of nebulized LPS to investigate the protective effects of hUCMSC-Exos. The Cecal Ligation and Puncture (CLP) model is complicated to establish and exhibits high heterogeneity, and lung injury caused by the intratracheal instillation of LPS is often localized and inhomogeneous [32]; therefore, we constructed this inhalation model of ALI (Fig. S2A–B). This model ensures diffuse lung injury, the procedure is simple and the model is uniform. Next, we optimized the dosing regimen. Mice received increasing doses of nebulized hUCMSC-Exos following LPS inhalation. HE staining of lung tissue sections at 24 h post-treatment identified the medium dose as the optimal therapeutic dose (Fig. 1B). The W/D ratio was significantly increased in the LPS group but markedly decreased by hUCMSC-Exos administration (Fig. 1C), indicating that hUCMSC-Exos alleviated pulmonary edema in mice. Quantification of BALF components revealed elevated total protein levels in LPS-treated mice compared with controls, which were significantly restored by the inhalation of hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 1D). LPS induced inflammation in the lungs, whereas hUCMSC-Exo treatment significantly attenuated the increases in the proinflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 1E–G). The histological examination demonstrated that the alveolar septa of lung tissues in the LPS group were markedly thickened, and inflammatory exudation and haemorrhage were observed in the alveolar lumen, whereas lung injury was significantly ameliorated upon inhalation of nebulized hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 1H). Consequently, LPS-treated mice presented substantial increases in pulmonary damage scores, which were effectively attenuated by hUCMSC-Exos administration (Fig. 1I). To test whether the therapeutic effects of hUCMSC-Exos could be applied to other models of ALI, we used the well-established CLP model to induce lung injury (Fig. S2C–D).

The inhalation of nebulized substances, a traditional strategy of delivering drugs to the lungs, is widely used in critical care because of its safety and convenience. We further investigated the pulmonary retention and biodistribution of hUCMSC-Exos. hUCMSC-Exos were labeled with DiR, a lipophilic fluorescent tracer, and then administered to mice via nebulization. Images of major organs were acquired at indicated time points after inhalation of DiR-labeled hUCMSC-Exos. As shown in Fig. 1J, hUCMSC-Exos were significantly enriched in the lungs at 2 h after nebulized inhalation, and a high level of retention was still observed at 24 h. Subsequently, the biodistribution of hUCMSC-Exos in ex vivo lung tissues were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed using the IVIS Lumina Series III Living Image System. Notably, hUCMSC-Exos entered the circulation as early as 2 h after administration, as evidenced by the strong fluorescent signal detected in the blood, suggesting their potential to target endothelial cells. To further identify the cellular targets of hUCMSC-Exos, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Pulmonary endothelial cells were labeled with anti-CD31 antibodies, and colocalization of PKH26-labeled hUCMSC-Exos with CD31-positive endothelial cells (Fig. 1L) demonstrated that hUCMSC-Exos were internalized by pulmonary endothelial cells.

Furthermore, in addition to the efficacy of hUCMSC-Exo treatment, we also evaluated its safety. Histological analyses of the heart, kidney, liver, and spleen of the treated animals did not reveal any significant injury or toxicity (Fig. S3A). Moreover, all haematological parameters and biochemical indices remained within the normal reference ranges and showed no significant differences compared with those in the control group (Fig. S3 B–C). Collectively, these results indicate that nebulized hUCMSC-Exos effectively ameliorate LPS- or CLP-induced ALI in mice.

hUCMSC-Exos attenuate inflammatory response and ferroptosis in HPMECs treated with LPS or ARDS patient plasma

We detected ferroptosis-related indicators in an in vitro model to determine the specific mechanism by which hUCMSC-Exos regulate LPS-induced ferroptosis in HPMECs. First, ELISA showed that the levels of the inflammatory cytokines were increased in LPS-treated HPMECs, whereas hUCMSC-Exos decreased the levels of these cytokines (Fig. 2A–C). The western blot results showed a significant increase in expression of the iNOS and ACSL4 proteins in the LPS-treated HPMECs, whereas the administration of hUCMSC-Exos decreased their expression. In contrast, the expression of the GPX4 and FTH1 proteins was decreased in LPS-treated HPMECs, while hUCMSC-Exos restored their expression (Fig. 2D). The results of flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining revealed that hUCMSC-Exos reversed the LPS-induced increase in lipid peroxidation in HPMECs (Fig. 2E–F). Consistent with the Western blot results, immunofluorescence staining confirmed the modulatory effect of hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 2G).

As an approach to mimic the inflammatory environment of patients with ARDS in vitro, we stimulated HPMECs with ARDS patient plasma followed by administration of hUCMSC-Exos to evaluate the regulatory effect on ferroptosis. Quantitative analysis demonstrated higher plasma iron concentrations in ARDS patients compared to healthy volunteers (Fig. 2I). In addition, the secretion of the inflammatory cytokines was substantially increased in HPMECs exposed to plasma from ARDS patients, and their levels were significantly reduced after the addition of hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 2J). Moreover, the plasma of ARDS patients could lead to ferroptosis in HPMECs, and ferroptosis was inhibited after hUCMSC-Exo treatment. The Western blot results were consistent with the previous findings, and hUCMSC-Exos reduced the ARDS plasma-induced increase in the expression of the iNOS and ACSL4 proteins. In addition, hUCMSC-Exos reversed the decrease in GPX4 and FTH1 protein expression induced by plasma from ARDS patients (Fig. 2K). In summary, hUCMSC-Exos inhibit the inflammatory response and ferroptosis in HPMECs induced by LPS or plasma from ARDS patients.

hUCMSC-Exos mediate the transfer of mitochondrial components to HPMECs

MitoEVs derived from healthy cells (primarily stem cells) play a therapeutic role in lung injury models by restoring mitochondrial function in recipient cells [27]. Donor cells (hUCMSCs) were first labeled with the mitochondrial probe MitoTracker Red, and then hUCMSC-Exos were isolated from the labeled hUCMSC culture supernatant to further explore whether hUCMSC-derived mitochondrial components can be transferred into HPMECs by hUCMSC-Exos. Next, the recipient HPMECs were labeled with MitoTracker Green, and the prelabeled hUCMSC-Exos were incubated with HPMECs for 24 h. CLSM showed that MitoTracker Red successfully labeled the mitochondrial components in hUCMSCs (Fig. 3A), and the labeled mitochondrial components in hUCMSC-Exos were internalized by HPMECs and colocalized with recipient mitochondria (Fig. 3B). Consistently, treatment with hUCMSC-Exos significantly increased the proportion of MitoTracker Deep Red-positive HPMECs to 28.6% (Fig. S4D). These results further support the hypothesis that hUCMSC-Exos can transfer mitochondrial components into HPMECs and colocalize with their mitochondria.

hUCMSC-Exos improve LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in HPMECs

Subsequently, we examined the effects of hUCMSC-Exos on mitochondrial function post-transplantation into HPMECs. For this purpose, we examined the morphology and function of mitochondrial HPMECs from different treatment groups. As expected, TEM revealed smaller, rounded mitochondria with diminished or absent cristae after LPS stimulation, whereas the morphology of the mitochondria improved after the addition of hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 3C–D). Super-resolution confocal microscopy imaging showed the same regulatory effect (Fig. 3E–F). We also detected the levels of proteins related to mitochondrial fusion and fission in lung tissues and HPMECs using Western blotting. The LPS group exhibited reduced levels of the mitochondrial fusion proteins MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 but an increased level of the fission protein DRP1. Treatment with hUCMSC-Exos reversed this imbalance, markedly upregulating MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 expression while downregulating DRP1 expression (Fig. S4B–C). In addition, LPS treatment resulted in marked MMP reduction and increased mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production in HPMECs, whereas hUCMSC-Exos restored these parameters (Fig. 3G–H). We further assessed mitochondrial respiration by measuring the cellular OCR using an Oroboros analyser and found that LPS significantly reduced basal and maximal respiration as well as ATP production in HPMECs, and these effects were reversed by hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 3I). Thus, hUCMSC-Exos improve mitochondrial function in HPMECs by transferring the mitochondrial components.

We extracted the mitochondria from HPMECs in the Control, LPS, and LPS+hUCMSC-Exo groups and analysed the differences in the mitochondrial protein composition of each group using proteomic sequencing technology to better understand the underlying molecular mechanism. The data were obtained from three independent replicates of the samples, and quality control of the data was performed to obtain reliable sequencing results. The volcano plot shows that 54 proteins were differentially expressed between the LPS and Control groups and that 95 proteins were differentially expressed between the LPS+hUCMSC-Exo and LPS groups (Fig. 4B, F). Next, we subjected the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) to a GO functional enrichment analysis; notably, ribosome biogenesis (RPL24, RPL7, RPS24, RPL7, RPS23, ABCE1, RPS15A, RPS25, and RPS11), ribosomal small subunit biogenesis (RPS23, RPS15A, RPS25, and RPS11), and tRNA aminoacylation for protein translation (EPRS1, MARS1, and LRRC47) were enriched in the DEPs (Fig. 4E, G). To delineate the underlying biological mechanisms, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DEPs were enriched in the ribosome and ferroptosis (Fig. 4C, H). In addition, we performed a Reactome enrichment analysis, and the most highly enriched pathways were ribosome and translation-related pathways (Fig. 4D, I). Next, we established a protein‒protein interaction network (PPI) consisting of core DEPs and found that ribosomal proteins, including RPS11, RPS3, RPS19, RPS20, RPS23, RPS25, and RPS15A (Fig. S5A) accounted for a large proportion of the core DEPs comparing LPS to the control, and LPS+hUCMSC-Exo to LPS. In addition, Venn diagram analysis revealed the overlap of seven DEPs (Fig. S5B).

To explore the potential targets of gene therapy for ALI, we obtained and analysed the gene expression profile in relevant dataset from the GEO database. Based on PCA, cells were clustered into 34 distinct populations using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). These clusters were further identified as specific cell types by evaluating the expression of their marker genes using the FindAllMarkers of Seurat. UMAP analysis revealed 14 distinct cell populations in the mouse lungs: alveolar macrophages, B cells, dendritic cells, epithelial cells, epithelial cell and macrophage_mix, fibroblasts, lymphatic endothelial cells, monocyte-derived macrophages, neutrophils, NK cells, pericytes, progenitor cells, pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, and T cells (Fig. 4J). Notably, the proportion of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells was lower in LPS-treated mice than in the control group. Therefore, we analysed the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of the two groups of mice and conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. S5C–D).

Integrated analysis using a Venn diagram revealed that RPS11 was the only factor significantly upregulated at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels (Fig. 4K–L). To determine whether hUCMSC-Exos restored the LPS-induced downregulation of RPS11 in mitochondria, we isolated mitochondria from HPMECs in different treatment groups and performed Western blot analysis. Additionally, RPS11 expression was examined at the whole-cell level (Fig. 4M). Furthermore, we also confirmed the expression of RPS11 in hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. S4F). These data suggest that hUCMSC-Exos may improve HPMEC mitochondrial function by upregulating the expression of RPS11.

hUCMSC-Exos alleviate ferroptosis and improve mitochondrial function via RPS11 upregulation in HPMECs

To further validate the regulatory role of RPS11 in mitochondrial function and ferroptosis, we transfected HPMECs with siRNA and subsequently assessed the subsequent protein levels (Fig. S4E). Western blot analysis revealed that the knockdown of RPS11 further increased ACSL4 expression while decreasing GPX4 and FTH1 expression, effects that were rescued by hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 5A). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that hUCMSC-Exos abrogated the increase in lipid peroxidation and the loss of MMP caused by RPS11 knockdown (Fig. 5C–D). Furthermore, flow cytometry confirmed that RPS11 knockdown further increased the LPS-induced increase in mtROS, which was again significantly attenuated by hUCMSC-Exos treatment (Fig. 5B). Oroboros analysis demonstrated that si-RPS11 significantly reduced basal and maximal respiration as well as ATP production in HPMECs, and these effects were reversed by hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 5E).

Furthermore, based on a direct statistical comparison between the LPS + hUCMSC-Exos and si-RPS11 + LPS + hUCMSC-Exos groups, we found that hUCMSC-Exos treatment could still rescue mitochondrial function and inhibit ferroptosis in HPMECs despite RPS11 knockdown. This finding suggested that the hUCMSC-Exos-mediated increase in RPS11 was more likely attributable to direct transfer than to induction of endogenous expression. Consistently, RT–qPCR analysis showed that hUCMSC-Exos treatment did not significantly alter RPS11 mRNA levels in HPMECs (Fig. S4G). In summary, these results identify RPS11 as a key mediator of LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in HPMECs and demonstrate that hUCMSC-Exos may exert therapeutic effects by upregulating RPS11.

hUCMSC-derived exosomal RPS11 enhances mitochondrial protein translation in HPMECs

RPS11, a mitochondrial ribosomal protein [33], may play a role in finely regulating mitochondrial ribosome translation [34]. Immunofluorescence staining showed the increased colocalization of RPS11 with MitoTracker Red, indicating that after hUCMSC-Exo treatment, RPS11 preferentially accumulated in the mitochondria of HPMECs (Fig. 6A). To further investigate the molecular function of RPS11 in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis in HPMECs, we tracked the subcellular localization of exosomes derived from hUCMSCs stably expressing RPS11-GFP after their uptake by HPMECs. The spatial association between RPS11-GFP and mitochondria in HPMECs was then examined by structured illumination microscopy (SIM). Notably, substantial colocalization of RPS11-GFP with PK Mito Red-labeled mitochondria was observed (Fig. 6B). Next, we explored the effect of RPS11 knockdown on the translation of OXPHOS system protein components encoded by the mitochondrial genome. The expression of mitochondrial genome-encoded proteins decreased after RPS11 knockdown. Further analysis showed that the expression levels of MTND1, MTND3, CYTB, MTCO1, and ATP6 in the si-RPS11 LPS group were lower than those in the LPS group, but their activity was restored after treatment with hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 6C).

To investigate the potential role of mitochondrial translation in mediating the anti-ferroptotic effects of hUCMSC-Exos, we inhibited mitochondrial translation with doxycycline and then assessed its impact on ferroptosis. The results demonstrated that doxycycline pretreatment markedly weakened the anti-ferroptotic activity of hUCMSC-Exos (Fig. 6D–E). These results suggest that hUCMSC-Exos may increase the translation of mitochondria-encoded mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes by delivering RPS11, thereby restoring mitochondrial function and inhibiting ferroptosis.

hUCMSC-Exos inhalation alleviates LPS- or CLP-induced ferroptosis in lung tissue via RPS11 upregulation

We further validated the regulatory effects of hUCMSC-Exos in the treatment of ALI through in vivo experiments. GSH reduces lipid hydroperoxides to lipid alcohols and reflects the cellular antioxidant capacity; GSH levels were significantly decreased in the LPS group but increased in the hUCMSC-Exo group (Fig. 7B). In addition, iron deposition in lung tissues was significantly increased in the LPS group, but decreased in the hUCMSC-Exo group (Fig. 7C). The expression of the ferroptosis markers ACSL4, GPX4, FTH1, and iNOS were detected by Western blotting. ACSL4 and iNOS proteins were significantly upregulated in lung tissues of the LPS group but downregulated by hUCMSC-Exos treatment. In contrast, GPX4 and FTH1 protein expression were significantly suppressed in the LPS group, whereas hUCMSC-Exos treatment elevated both proteins (Fig. 7A). The Western blot result from CLP-induced ALI model were consistent with those from LPS model (Fig. 7H). Further immunofluorescence staining revealed that the fluorescence intensity of GPX4 was diminished in LPS-treated lung tissues, contrasting with enhanced expression of GPX4 in the hUCMSC-Exo group. The lipid peroxidation level was detected using 4HNE, and results showed that the fluorescence intensity of 4HNE was increased in lung tissues of the LPS group, whereas its intensity was decreased in the hUCMSC-Exo group (Fig. 7D). Next, we evaluated the role of pulmonary endothelial cells in the inhibition of ferroptosis in lung tissue following the inhalation of nebulized hUCMSC-Exos. The expression of GPX4 in the CD31-positive cells of the hUCMSC-Exo group was markedly increased, as shown by double immunofluorescence staining for GPX4 and CD31 (Fig. 7E), indicating that the inhalation of nebulized hUCMSC-Exos ameliorated ALI in mice by inhibiting ferroptosis in pulmonary endothelial cells.

Discussion

Acute lung injury (ALI)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a frequent aetiology of critical respiratory failure, characterized by diffuse inflammatory damage in the lung caused by various factors. Pathologically, it features alveolar epithelial–endothelial barrier disruption, leading to intra-alveolar accumulation of proteinaceous inflammatory exudate [3, 7], for which effective therapeutic agents are still lacking. We constructed a nebulized hUCMSC-Exos inhalation model for ALI treatment, delivering hUCMSC-Exos via a mask to avoid heterogeneity associated with the use of a nebulization chamber. After the inhalation of hUCMSC-Exos, pulmonary oedema was significantly improved, and inflammatory exudation and bleeding in the alveolar space were significantly decreased. We also observed that nebulized hUCMSC-Exos were substantially deposited in the lung and readily internalized by mouse pulmonary endothelial cells. Further exploration is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic potential in ALI.

MSC-Exos are emerging as promising therapeutics for ALI/ARDS. However, MSCs can be isolated from different tissue sources (e.g., the umbilical cord, adipose tissue). hUCMSCs are particularly promising because of their proliferative ability, high immunomodulatory capacity, and easy accessibility [35, 36]. This evidence indicates that MSC-Exos have broad prospects and potential for the treatment of ALI/ARDS. MSC-Exos have shown potential anti-inflammatory activity against multiple diseases, such as by reducing inflammation in lung diseases and improving organ function [37]. Additionally, miR-125b-5p in MSC-Exos can ameliorate ALI by inhibiting ferroptosis [38]. In this study, hUCMSC-Exos attenuated LPS-induced ferroptosis in HPMECs, restoring GPX4 and FTH1 downregulation, reversing 4HNE and ACSL4 upregulation, increasing antioxidant levels and decreasing lipid peroxidation. Additionally, we constructed an in vitro inflammation model to mimic the pathological characteristics of ARDS using plasma from ARDS patients to stimulate HPMECs. Assays of plasma iron levels in ARDS patients and healthy volunteers showed that the plasma iron concentrations were significantly higher in ARDS patients, and inflammatory response and ferroptosis marker assays revealed that hUCMSC-Exos inhibited HPMEC ferroptosis induced by the plasma of ARDS patients. In vivo experiments further revealed that hUCMSC-Exos alleviated ferroptosis in the lung tissue of mice with ALI. Moreover, the inhalation of hUCMSC-Exos may have exerted a protective effect by inhibiting ferroptosis in pulmonary endothelial cells.

Ferroptosis is strongly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [39, 40]. Mitochondria centrally regulate the development of diverse diseases, and their dysfunction can lead to abnormal cell and organ functions in various disease states, such as sepsis and ALI [41]. The study of how extracellular vesicles affect function of recipient cells by transferring mitochondrial components has received attention [29]. Moreover, the selection of appropriate cellular sources of mitoEVs is a key factor in achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes, and the extracellular vesicles produced by umbilical cord-derived MSCs contain almost all the components of mitochondria [26]. MSC extracellular vesicles restore mitochondrial function by transferring mitochondria, thereby improving alveolar capillary barrier properties [31]. AdMSC-Exos reduce lung inflammation by transferring mitochondrial components, improving macrophage mitochondrial function and restoring macrophage metabolism and immune homeostasis [42]. Our data showed that hUCMSC-Exos transferred mitochondrial components into HPMECs, colocalizing with their endogenous mitochondria and enhancing mitochondrial function, improving mitochondrial morphology and increasing the MMP and OCR while reducing mtROS levels. Next, to identify the mitochondrial components that were transferred into HPMECs by hUCMSC-Exos, we isolated mitochondria from HPMECs in different treatment groups and subjected them to proteomic sequencing.

Notably, proteomic analysis identified numerous ribosomal proteins among the DEPs. Subsequent integration with single-cell RNA sequencing data further revealed that RPS11 exhibited the most significant differential expression. Furthermore, ribosome-related signalling pathways were also significantly enriched. Mitochondria utilise a translational mechanism distinct from that of the cytosol. Nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins are synthesized by cytoplasmic ribosomes [43]and then selectively imported into mitochondria with the aid of molecular chaperones (e.g., HSP70/HSP90), typically in an matrix-targeting sequences (MTS)-dependent manner [44]. In contrast, mitochondria-encoded polypeptides are translated by mitochondrial ribosomes and constitute the structural core of electron transport chain (ETC) complexes [45]. Multiple regulatory factors participate in the modulation of mitochondrial translation, which is coordinated with cytosolic translation through stress responses. The inhibition of mitochondrial translation leads to the suppression of cytosolic translation and metabolic disruption, ultimately impairing cellular function [46]. Moreover, studies have shown that under stress, cells tend to take up exogenous mitochondria, followed by the upregulation of mitochondrial translation to promote endogenous mitochondrial biogenesis [47]. Additionally, emerging evidence has revealed the presence of abundant mRNA and ribosomes on the mitochondrial surface, with active translation observed on the outer mitochondrial membrane, suggesting localized translation of mitochondrial proteins at the mitochondrial surface [48–50]. Our results confirmed that the knockdown of RPS11 exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis, accompanied by increased levels of lipid peroxidation and mtROS, as well as the downregulation of mitochondrial-encoded proteins of the OXPHOS system. These effects were effectively reversed by hUCMSC-Exos. Furthermore, pretreatment with the mitochondrial translation inhibitor doxycycline significantly suppressed the anti-ferroptotic effect of hUCMSC-Exos. Notably, hUCMSC-Exo treatment did not alter RPS11 mRNA levels in HPMECs, whereas exosomal RPS11-GFP showed prominent colocalization with mitochondria in recipient HPMECs, suggesting that hUCMSC-Exos may exert their therapeutic effect through exogenous delivery of RPS11. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hUCMSC-Exos enhance mitochondrial translation through the upregulation of RPS11, thereby improving mitochondrial function and inhibiting ferroptosis in HPMECs. The specific mechanism by which the ribosomal protein RPS11 regulates mitochondrial translation and its precise role in the development of ALI should be further investigated.

Ribosomal damage has been reported to lead to mitochondrial fragmentation and increase the MMP and levels of mtROS, which may be associated with ribosomal stress [51]. Upon glucose deficiency, the ribosomal small subunit protein Cpc2/RACK1 mediates the binding of ribosomes to the outer mitochondrial membrane and the cessation of protein synthesis, leading to mitochondrial fragmentation and cytoplasmic ribosome-mediated stress on mitochondria [52]. Furthermore, mitochondrial translation can directly initiate mitochondrial stress responses [46]. Accordingly, we propose a novel concept—mitochondrial ribosome stress—defined as the cellular stress response triggered by the dysfunction of mitochondrial ribosomes under various internal and external influences.

To date, studies on MSC-Exo treatment have predominantly used intravenous injections. However, the accumulation of exosomes in the liver, kidneys, and spleen following intravenous infusions limits their concentration in lung tissue and may affect the efficacy of ALI treatment [53]. As a noninvasive route of administration, the inhalation of nebulized drugs is widely used to treat respiratory diseases [54]. The lungs have a large surface area and extensive vascularization and are highly permeable to many peptides and proteins; thus, small molecules are quickly absorbed through the lungs, have relatively high bioavailability, and have reduced first-pass metabolism. In addition, the drug is retained in the lung tissue, resulting in a prolonged duration of drug action in the lungs [55, 56]. Inhaled particles should be < 5 μm to allow them to cross the alveolar–capillary barrier, with < 2 μm particles determined to be the best for deposition in the alveolar region [55]. On the other hand, nebulized drug delivery devices can spray an ultrafine spray of the drug with a particle size of < 5 μm along with compressed air, which meets the conditions for reaching the site of lung injury [10]. Furthermore, our results demonstrated that nebulization treatment preserved both the structural integrity and biological activity of hUCMSC-Exos. In addition, hUCMSC-Exos were substantially deposited in the lungs as early as 2 h after nebulized inhalation, remained at a high level at 24 h, and showed minimal distribution in major organs, supporting their favourable biocompatibility and pulmonary targeting potential. Therefore, studying the mechanism of inhaled hUCMSC-Exos for the treatment of ALI is crucial for promoting their future clinical application.

We acknowledge some limitations of this work. First, all of our treatments were delivered by inhalation through a nebulizer. Using different routes of administration to determine the optimal method and frequency of administration will help maximize its benefits. Second, while this study focused specifically on the mechanism by which hUCMSC-Exos target pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, we are simultaneously investigating their therapeutic effects in ALI through the modulation of alveolar macrophages and epithelial cell functions. Finally, we performed a proteomic analysis of mitochondria isolated from HPMECs across different treatment groups to identify proteins that may contribute to the therapeutic efficacy from both clinical and pharmacological perspectives. Multi-omics analysis subsequently identified RPS11 as a key target. The ribosomal protein reported here, RPS11, provides preliminary insights into the therapeutic potential of hUCMSC-Exos. However, the precise mechanism by which hUCMSC-Exo-derived RPS11 is incorporated into the mitochondrial network of recipient cells and modulates mitochondrial translation remains to be further elucidated. Furthermore, further investigation of mitochondrial ribosome stress and how mitochondrial translation affects downstream signalling pathways such as mitochondrial ribosome stress may represent a promising direction for future research.

In conclusion, we report that the inhalation of nebulized hUCMSC-Exos has a favourable safety profile and therapeutic efficacy in treating ALI in mice. The evidence presented here suggests that hUCMSC-Exos alleviate ALI by upregulating RPS11, which promotes mitochondrial translation, thereby improving mitochondrial function and suppressing ferroptosis.
