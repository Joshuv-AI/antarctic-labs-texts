A new look on large deviations and concentration inequalities for the Ewens-Pitman model
Bernard Bercu, Stefano Favaro

Abstract
The Ewens-Pitman model is a probability distribution for random partitions of the set [ n ] = { 1 , … , n } [n]=\{1,\ldots,n\} , parameterized by α ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in[0,1) and θ > − α \theta>-\alpha , with α = 0 \alpha=0 corresponding to the Ewens model in population genetics. The goal of this paper is to provide an alternative and concise proof of the Feng-Hoppe large deviation principle for the number K n K_{n} of partition sets in the Ewens-Pitman model with α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) and θ > − α \theta>-\alpha . Our approach leverages an integral representation of the moment-generating function of K n K_{n} in terms of the (one-parameter) Mittag-Leffler function, along with a sharp asymptotic expansion of it. This approach significantly simplifies the original proof of Feng-Hoppe large deviation principle, as it avoids all the technical difficulties arising from a continuity argument with respect to rational and non-rational values of α \alpha . Beyond large deviations for K n K_{n} , our approach allows to establish a sharp concentration inequality for K n K_{n} involving the rate function of the large deviation principle.



Keywords:
Concentration inequality; Ewens-Pitman model; large deviation principle; Mittag-Leffler function

1
Introduction

The Ewens-Pitman model was introduced in
Pitman (1995)
as a generalization of the Ewens model in population genetics
Ewens (1972)
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider a random partition of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
K_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
partition sets of which

K_{r,n}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
appear
r
times,

n=\sum_{r=1}^{n}rK_{r,n}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and}\hskip 18.49988ptK_{n}=\sum_{r=1}^{n}K_{r,n}.

For
\alpha\in[0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, the Ewens-Pitman model assigns to
\mathbf{K}_{n}=(K_{1,n},\ldots,K_{n,n})
the probability

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\theta}(\mathbf{K}_{n}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}))=n!\frac{\left(\frac{\theta}{\alpha}\right)^{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i})}}{(\theta)^{(n)}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)^{(i-1)}}{i!}\right)^{k_{i}}\frac{1}{k_{i}!},

(1)

where, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
(a)^{(n)}
stands for the rising factorial of
a
of order
n
, that is

(a)^{(n)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
. The Ewens model is recovered from (
1
) by setting
\alpha=0
. The distribution (
1
) admits a sequential construction through the Chinese restaurant process
Pitman (1995)
;
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
, a Poisson process construction by random sampling from the Pitman-Yor random measure
Perman et al. (1992)
;
Pitman (1997)
, and a construction through compound Poisson random partitions
Dolera and Favaro (2021)
. We refer to
(
Pitman, 2006
, Chapter 3 and Chapter 4)
for an overview of the Ewens-Pitman model, including applications in a variety of research fields, e.g., population genetics, excursion theory, Bayesian statistics, combinatorics and statistical physics.

1.1
Background

There exists a rich body of literature on the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the number
K_{n}
of partition sets in the Ewens-Pitman model, e.g., almost-sure and Gaussian fluctuations, large and moderate deviations, Berry-Esseen inequalities, laws of iterated logarithm, central limit theorems and laws of large numbers
Korwar and Hollander (1973)
;
Pitman (1995)
;
Pitman (1997)
;
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
;
Pitman (2006)
;
Favaro and Feng (2015)
;
Favaro et al. (2018)
;
Dolera and Favaro (2020)
;
Dolera and Favaro (2021)
;
Bercu and Favaro (2024)
;
Contardi et al. (2024)
. Feng-Hoppe large deviation principle is one of the earliest results in the literature
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
. For
\alpha=0
,
K_{n}
is a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables
Korwar and Hollander (1973)
, from which it follows easily that (
K_{n}/n)
satisfies a large deviation principle with good rate function

I_{\theta}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle x\log\Big(\frac{x}{\theta}\Big)-x+\theta}&\text{ if }\hskip 9.24994ptx>0\\[5.69046pt]
\theta&\text{ if }\hskip 9.24994ptx=0\\[5.69046pt]
+\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.\vskip 3.87498pt

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
K_{n}
is no longer a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables. However, by a careful analysis of the moment-generating function of
K_{n}
,
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
proved the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Feng-Hoppe large deviation principle)
.

Under the Ewens-Pitman model with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, the sequence (
K_{n}/n)
satisfies a large deviation principle with good rate function

I_{\alpha}(x)=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{xt-L_{\alpha}(t)\right\},

(2)

where

L_{\alpha}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\log\bigl(1-(1-e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}\bigr)&\text{ if }\hskip 9.24994ptt>0\\[5.69046pt]
0&\text{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is lengthy and technically involved. Its core consists in showing that

\vskip 3.87498pt\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathds{E}_{\alpha,\theta}[\exp\{tK_{n}\}]=L_{\alpha}(t),

(3)

from which Theorem
1.1
follows using Gärtner–Ellis theorem
(
Dembo and Zeitouni, 1998
, Theorem 2.3.6)
. Assuming
\theta=0
, the proof of (
3
) starts from the calculation of the moment-generating function
m_{n}(t)
of
K_{n}
, and proceeds along three steps: i) for
\alpha
rational, the large
n
behaviour of
m_{n}(t)
is derived through tedious algebra and combinatorics; ii) for non-rational
\alpha
, upper and lower large
n
approximations of
m_{n}(t)
are constructed by leveraging monotonicity and considering nearby rational
\alpha
; iii) a continuity argument is applied to bridge the gap between rational and non-rational values of
\alpha
. The proof of (
3
) is completed by reducing the case
\theta\neq 0
to
\theta=0
, though the argument could benefit from a greater clarity.

1.2
Our contribution

In this paper, we present a new approach to large deviations for
K_{n}
, offering an alternative and concise proof of Theorem
1.1
. Assuming
\theta=0
, the novelty of our approach lies in the use of an integral representation of
m_{n}(t)
in terms of the (one-parameter) Mittag-Leffler function
(
Gorenflo et al., 2014
, Chapter 3)
. Then, by leveraging a well-known integral representation of the Mittag-Leffler function
Gorenflo et al. (2002)
, we obtain a sharp expansion of
m_{n}(t)
, which leads to (
3
) through straightforward algebra. In doing this, we also clarify and make rigorous how the case
\theta\neq 0
reduces to the case
\theta=0
. This approach significantly simplifies the original proof Theorem
1.1
, as it allows to avoid the technical difficulties arising from the continuity argument with respect to rational and non-rational values of
\alpha
.

Beyond large deviations for
K_{n}
, our approach allows to establish a sharp concentration inequality for
K_{n}
involving the rate function
I_{\alpha}
in (
2
) as stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the Ewens-Pitman model with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, for any
x\in(0,1)
and all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\theta}\left(\frac{K_{n}}{n}\geq x\right)\leq\frac{P_{n}(\alpha,\theta)c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)}{\alpha}\exp\left(\!-nI_{\alpha}(x)\right),

(4)

where

P_{n}(\alpha,\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+n\big)^{\theta_{\alpha}}}&\text{ if }\hskip 9.24994pt\theta>0\\[5.69046pt]
1&\text{ if }\hskip 9.24994pt\theta\leq 0\end{array}\right.

and

c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)=\frac{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(\theta+1)}{\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+1\right)\Gamma(n+\theta)},

(5)

with
\theta_{\alpha}=\theta/\alpha
and
\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor
the integer part of
\theta_{\alpha}
. In particular, if
\theta=0
then
P_{n}(\alpha,\theta)c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)=1
.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we present an alternative derivation of the moment-generating function of
K_{n}
, through the sequential construction of the Ewens-Pitman model, as well as a sharp expansion of it. Such an expansion is the key ingredient to obtain an alternative and concise proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
, and to prove Theorem
1.2
in Section
4
. In Section
5
we conclude by discussing some directions for future work.

2
On the moment-generating function of
K_{n}

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, let
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\theta}
be the law of the Pitman-Yor random measure, which induces the Ewens-Pitman model (
1
), and let
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\theta}
be the corresponding expectation. For an integer
n\geqslant 1
, denote by
m_{n}
the moment-generating function of
K_{n}
defined, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, by

m_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\theta}[\exp(tK_{n})].

From a change of measure formula that links
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\theta}
, with
\theta>-\alpha
, and
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,0}

(
Pitman, 1997
, Section 3)
, denote

M_{n}(\alpha,\theta)=\frac{a_{n}\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+1\right)\Gamma(K_{n})},

(6)

where
\theta_{\alpha}=\theta/\alpha
and

a_{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{k}{k+\theta}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(\theta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\theta)}.

It is not difficult to see that the sequence
(M_{n}(\alpha,\theta))
is a positive
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,0}
-martingale with mean value
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[M_{n}(\alpha,\theta)]=1
. Consequently, we deduce directly from (
6
) that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

m_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\theta}[\exp(tK_{n})]=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})M_{n}(\alpha,\theta)]=c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)I_{n}(t),

(7)

where
c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)
is given in (
5
) and

I_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\exp(tK_{n})\frac{\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)}{\Gamma(K_{n})}\right].

One can observe that the moment-generating
m_{n}(t)
is finite for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for all
n\geq 1
, since
K_{n}\leq n
.

2.1
The case
\theta=0

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta=0
,
(
Feng and Hoppe, 1998
, Equation (3.5))
provides an expression for
m_{n}(t)
in terms of the rising factorial moments of
K_{n}

(
Pitman, 2006
, Chapter 3)
. In particular for all
n\geq 1
and all
t>0
,

m_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha\ell)}{\Gamma(\alpha\ell+1)}.

(8)

We propose an alternative proof of (
8
), which is of independent interest. Let
F
be the generating function of the sequence
(m_{n}(t))
, which is given, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}

and for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, by

F(t,z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}m_{n}(t)z^{n-1}.

(9)

The radius of convergence of
F
, denoted
R^{F}(t)
, should depend on
t
and it is positive since, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
|m_{n}(t)|\leq e^{n|t|}
. The next lemma provides an expression for
F
by relying on the definition of
K_{n}
in terms of the sequential construction of the Ewens-Pitman model
Pitman (1995)
;
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta=0
, we have for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

R^{F}(t)=1+\left|\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right|^{1/\alpha}.

(10)

Moreover, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|<R^{F}(t)
,

F(t,z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\left(1-\frac{(e^{t}-1)}{(e^{t}-1)-e^{t}(1-z)^{\alpha}}\right).

(11)

Proof.

It follows from
(
Pitman, 1995
, Proposition 8)
, for all
n\geq 1
,
K_{n+1}=K_{n}+\xi_{n+1}
where
the conditional distribution
\mathcal{L}(\xi_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n})
is the Bernoulli
\mathcal{B}(p_{n})
distribution with
p_{n}=\alpha K_{n}/n
. Consequently, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle m_{n+1}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\exp(tK_{n+1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\exp(tK_{n})\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(t\xi_{n+1})|\mathcal{F}_{n}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[\exp(tK_{n})p_{n}e^{t}+\exp(tK_{n})(1-p_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=m_{n}(t)+(e^{t}-1)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[p_{n}\exp(tK_{n})\right].

As
p_{n}=\alpha K_{n}/n
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}\left[p_{n}\exp(tK_{n})\right]=\alpha m_{n}^{\prime}(t)/n
,
which implies that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for all
n\geq 1
,

m_{n+1}(t)=m_{n}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{n}(e^{t}-1)m_{n}^{\prime}(t).

(12)

From (
9
) and (
12
), we obtain for all
|z|<R^{F}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F(t,z)}{\partial z}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}(n-1)m_{n}(t)z^{n-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nm_{n+1}(t)z^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nm_{n}(t)z^{n-1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha(e^{t}-1)m_{n}^{\prime}(t)z^{n-1}

\displaystyle=F(t,z)+z\frac{\partial F(t,z)}{\partial z}+\alpha(e^{t}-1)\frac{\partial F(t,z)}{\partial t},

which leads to

(1-z)\frac{\partial F(t,z)}{\partial z}+\alpha(1-e^{t})\frac{\partial F(t,z)}{\partial t}=F(t,z)

(13)

with two initial values

F(t,0)=e^{t}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274ptF(0,z)=\frac{1}{1-z}.

(14)

Now, the classical method of characteristics allows to solve the first-order partial differential equation (
13
). We can associate (
13
) to
the ordinary differential system given by

\frac{dz}{1-z}=\frac{dt}{\alpha(1-e^{t})}=\frac{dw}{w},

where
w
stands for
F
. We assume in the sequel that
t>0
, inasmuch as the proof for
t<0
follows exactly the same lines. On the one hand, the equation binding
w
and
t
is such that

\frac{dw}{w}=\frac{dt}{\alpha(1-e^{t})}.

(15)

From (
15
), the solution is

w=C_{1}\left(\frac{e^{t}}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

(16)

On the other hand, the equation binding
z
and
t
is similar but inhomogeneous, that is we write

\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{1}{\alpha(e^{t}-1)}z-\frac{1}{\alpha(e^{t}-1)}.

(17)

We obtain from (
17
), by means of the variation of constant method, that the solution satisfies

z=1+C_{2}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

(18)

By coupling (
16
) and (
18
),

C_{1}=f(-C_{2}),

(19)

where
f
remains to be explicitly calculated using the boundary values (
14
).
We get from (
16
) that

C_{1}=F(t,z)\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

Hence, we deduce
from (
16
), (
18
) and (
19
) that
for all
t>0
and for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|<R^{F}(t)
,

F(t,z)=\left(\frac{e^{t}}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1/\alpha}f\left(\left(\frac{e^{t}}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1/\alpha}(1-z)\right)=c(t)f(c(t)(1-z)),

(20)

where

c(t)=\left(\frac{e^{t}}{e^{t}-1}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

We already saw from (
14
) that for
z=0
,
F(t,0)=e^{t}
, which implies from (
20
) with the special value
z=0
that
e^{t}=c(t)f(c(t))
. Hence, we find from
the change of variable
s=c(t)
that for all
s>1
,

f(s)=\frac{s^{\alpha}}{s(s^{\alpha}-1)}.

(21)

Finally, it follows from (
20
) and (
21
) that
for all
t>0
and for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|<R^{F}(t)
,

F(t,z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\left(\frac{\left(c(t)(1-z)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(c(t)(1-z)\right)^{\alpha}-1}\right)=\frac{1}{1-z}\left(1-\frac{1}{1-\left(c(t)(1-z)\right)^{\alpha}}\right),

leading to (
11
).
The radius of convergence (

) comes from (

), which completes the proof.

From now on, the calculation of (

) follows from a direct application of Lemma

. In particular, consider the function
\mathcal{F}
defined, for all
t>0
and for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|z|<R^{F}(t)

\mathcal{F}(t,z)=(1-z)F(t,z)=\varphi(\xi(t,z)),

(22)

where

\xi(t,z)=c(t)(1-z)

and

\varphi(z)=\frac{z^{\alpha}}{z^{\alpha}-1}=\frac{1}{1-z^{-\alpha}}.

By choosing

such that
|z|<1<|\xi(t,z)|
, we obtain from Newton’s generalized Binomial theorem

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\xi(t,z)\right)^{-\alpha k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{k}(1-z)^{-\alpha k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{k}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha k)^{(n)}}{n!}z^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{k}(\alpha k)^{(n)}z^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}m_{n}^{\mathcal{F}}(t)z^{n},

,

m_{n}^{\mathcal{F}}(t)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{k}(\alpha k)^{(n)}.

(23)

Moreover, we have from (
9
) together with the application of (
22
) that
m_{1}(t)=e^{t}
and for all
n\geq 1
,
m_{n+1}(t)=m_{n}(t)+m_{n}^{\mathcal{F}}(t)
. Accordingly, we deduce from (
23
), for all
n\geq 1
, that

\displaystyle m_{n+1}(t)=e^{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}^{\mathcal{F}}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{t}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}(\alpha\ell)^{(k)}

\displaystyle=e^{t}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(\alpha\ell)^{(k)}}{k!}

\displaystyle=e^{t}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1}{n!}(\alpha\ell+1)^{(n)}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}(\alpha\ell+1)^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{\ell}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha\ell+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha\ell+1)},

m_{n}(t)
in (

Lemma 2.2
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta=0
, we have for all
t>0
and for all
n\geq 2
,

m_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(1-\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{-n}-R_{n}(t),

(24)

where

R_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{n-1}e^{-y}\int_{0}^{+\infty}G_{\alpha}(x,(1-e^{-t})y^{\alpha})dxdy

(25)

with

G_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}\exp(-x^{1/\alpha})\left(\frac{y\sin(\pi\alpha)}{x^{2}-2xy\cos(\pi\alpha)+y^{2}}\right).

(26)

Proof.

It follows from (
8
), by applying the integral representation of
\Gamma(n+\alpha\ell)
, that for all
n\geq 1
and all
t>0
,

\displaystyle m_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-e^{-t})^{\ell}}{\Gamma(\alpha\ell+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{n+\alpha\ell-1}e^{-y}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{n-1}e^{-y}E_{\alpha}\left((1-e^{-t})y^{\alpha}\right)dy,

(27)

where
E_{\alpha}
stands for the Mittag-Leffler function
(
Gorenflo et al., 2014
, Chapter 3)
, which is defined, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, by

E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{\ell}}{\Gamma(\alpha\ell+1)}.

We deduce from the integral representation of the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}
given by
(
Gorenflo et al., 2002
, Theorem 2.3)
that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|\arg(z)|<\pi\alpha
,

E_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\exp(z^{1/\alpha})-\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{\alpha}(x,z)dx,

(28)

where the function
G_{\alpha}
is defined in (
26
). Consequently,
we obtain from (
27
) and (
28
) that

m_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{n-1}e^{-y}\exp((1-e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}y)dy-R_{n}(t),

(29)

where the remainder
R_{n}(t)
is given by (
25
). In particular, the first term in the right-hand side of (
29
) can be easily calculated. As a matter of fact, by a simple change of variables

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!y^{n-1}e^{-y}\exp((1-e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!y^{n-1}\exp(-y(1-(1-e^{-t})^{1/\alpha}))dy

\displaystyle=\left(1-\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{-n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!x^{n-1}\exp(-x)dx

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\left(1-\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{-n}.

(30)

Finally, the compact expression (
24
) follows from (
29
) and (
30
), which completes the proof.

2.2
The case
\theta\neq 0

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, an extension of Lemma

is possible by exploiting the change of measure formula (

). In particular, it allows us to prove that for all
n\geq 1

t>0
,

m_{n}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(\theta+1)e^{-t\theta_{\alpha}}}{\Gamma(n+\theta)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}\big(\theta_{\alpha}+1\big)^{(\ell)}\Big(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\Big)^{\ell}\frac{\Gamma(n+\theta+\alpha\ell)}{\Gamma(\theta+\alpha\ell+1)}.

(31)

The expression (
31
) is new, and we recover (
8
) from (
31
) by setting
\theta=\theta_{\alpha}=0
. From (
31
), by applying the integral representations of
\Gamma(n+\theta+\alpha\ell)
, we obtain that for all
n\geq 1
and all
t>0

\displaystyle m_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\theta+1)\text{e}^{-t\theta_{\alpha}}}{\Gamma(n+\theta)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1-e^{-t})^{\ell}}{\ell!\Gamma(\theta+\alpha\ell+1)}(\theta_{\alpha}+1)^{(\ell)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{n+\theta+\alpha\ell-1}e^{-y}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\theta+1)\text{e}^{-t\theta_{\alpha}}}{\Gamma(n+\theta)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}y^{n+\theta-1}e^{-y}E^{\theta_{\alpha}+1}_{\alpha,\theta+1}((1-e^{-t})y^{\alpha})\mathrm{d}y,

(32)

where
E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}
stands for the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function
(
Gorenflo et al., 2014
, Chapter 5)
defined, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, by

E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma)^{(l)}z^{\ell}}{\ell!\Gamma(\alpha\ell+\beta)}.

This is a generalization of the Mittag-Leffler function, which is recovered by setting
\gamma=\beta=1
. The expression (
32
) is new, and we find again (
27
) from (
32
) by setting
\theta=\theta_{\alpha}=0
.

To the best of our knowledge, no integral representation of the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function, analogous of that in (
28
) for the Mittag-Leffler function, is available in the literature. In particular, the proof of
(
Gorenflo et al., 2002
, Theorem 2.3)
, in combination with
(
Mainardi and Garrappa, 2015
, Equation 3)
, suggests that such an integral representation may not exists for all values of
\theta_{\alpha}
.

3
A concise proof of Theorem
1.1

First of all, we focus our attention on the special case
\theta=0
. In particular, it follows from the elementary fact that
K_{n}\geq 1
, together with the use of Jensen’s inequality, that for all
t\leq 0
,

t\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[K_{n}]\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})]\leq t.

(33)

However, we know from
(
Pitman, 2006
, Section 3.3.)
that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[K_{n}]=\frac{1}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}.

Hence, we immediately obtain from (
33
) that
for all
t\leq 0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log m_{n}(t)=0.

(34)

Hereafter, we assume that
t>0
. For all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
x^{2}-2xy\cos(\pi\alpha)+y^{2}\geq\sin^{2}(\pi\alpha)y^{2}
.
Consequently, the function
G_{\alpha}
given by (
26
) satisfies for all
x>0
and
y>0
,

G_{\alpha}(x,y)\leq\frac{\exp(-x^{1/\alpha})}{\pi\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)y},

which implies that for all
y>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{\alpha}(x,y)dx\leq\frac{1}{\pi\alpha\sin(\pi\alpha)y}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-x^{1/\alpha})dx\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\pi\sin(\pi\alpha)y}.

(35)

Hence, we deduce from the expression of
R_{n}(t)
in (
25
) together with (
35
) that

0<R_{n}(t)\leq\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(n-\alpha)}{\Gamma(n)\pi\sin(\pi\alpha)(1-e^{-t})}\leq\frac{n^{1-\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}{(n-\alpha)\pi\sin(\pi\alpha)(1-e^{-t})}

(36)

where the right-hand side of (
36
) is
due to Wendel’s inequality,
\Gamma(n+1-\alpha)\leq n^{1-\alpha}\Gamma(n)
.
Hereafter, denote

d(t)=1-\left(\frac{e^{t}-1}{e^{t}}\right)^{1/\alpha}.

It follows from (
24
) and (
36
) that

\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(d(t)\big)^{-n}\Big(1-\frac{n^{1-\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)\big(d(t)\big)^{n}}{(n-\alpha)\pi\sin(\pi\alpha)(1-e^{-t})}\Big)<m_{n}(t)<\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(d(t)\big)^{-n}.

(37)

Therefore, as
0<d(t)<1
, we obtain from the upper and the lower bounds in (
37
) that
for all
t>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log m_{n}(t)=-\log d(t)=L_{\alpha}(t).

(38)

The function
L_{\alpha}
is finite and differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
, with
L_{\alpha}(t)=0
for all
t\leq 0
. Then, Gärtner-Ellis theorem
(
Dembo and Zeitouni, 1998
, Theorem 2.3.6)
completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the special case
\theta=0
.
It only remains to carry out the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case

\theta\neq 0
. In particular, one can observe that the convergence (
34
) also holds for all
t\leq 0
as
soon as
\theta>-\alpha
. On the one hand, assume that
\theta>0
. Then, we obtain once again from Wendel’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)}{\Gamma(K_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Gamma\left(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+K_{n}\right)}{\Gamma(K_{n})}\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+K_{n}\big)^{\{\theta_{\alpha}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+K_{n}\big)^{\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor}\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+K_{n}\big)^{\{\theta_{\alpha}\}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+K_{n}\big)^{\theta_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+n\big)^{\theta_{\alpha}},

where
\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor
and
\{\theta_{\alpha}\}
stand for the integer and fractional parts of
\theta_{\alpha}
. It follows from
the fact that the Euler Gamma function is strictly increasing on
[2,\infty[
that, as soon as
K_{n}\geq 2
,
\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)>\Gamma\left(K_{n}\right)
. Consequently, as soon as
K_{n}\geq 2
, we deduce from (
7
) that for all
t>0
,

c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})]\leq m_{n}(t)\leq c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)\big(\lfloor\theta_{\alpha}\rfloor+n\big)^{\theta_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})].

(39)

Furthermore, it is easy to see that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)=0.

Hence, by taking the logarithm on both sides of (
39
), we obtain from the squeeze theorem that for all
t>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log m_{n}(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})]=L_{\alpha}(t).

(40)

On the other hand, assume that
-\alpha<\theta<0
. Since
-1<\theta_{\alpha}<0
,
\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\geq 2
as soon as
K_{n}\geq 3
, which implies that

\Gamma\left(K_{n}\right)>\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)
. Moreover, we get from Gautschi’s inequality that

\frac{\Gamma\left(\theta_{\alpha}+K_{n}\right)}{\Gamma(K_{n})}>K_{n}^{\theta_{\alpha}}>n^{\theta_{\alpha}}.

Therefore, as soon as
K_{n}\geq 3
, we obtain from (
7
) that for all
t>0
,

c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)n^{\theta_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})]\leq m_{n}(t)\leq c_{n}(\alpha,\theta)\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,0}[\exp(tK_{n})].

(41)

Finally, we deduce from (
41
), together with the application of squeeze theorem, that the convergence (
40
) also holds in the case
-\alpha<\theta<0
, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4
Proof of Theorem
1.2

As for the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we start by considering the special case
\theta=0
. In particular, for all
x\in(0,1)
and for any
t>0
, it follows directly from Markov’s inequality
Bercu et al. (2015)
that

\mathbb{P}(K_{n}\geq nx)=\mathbb{P}(\exp(tK_{n})\geq\exp(nxt))\leq\exp(-nxt)m_{n}(t).

(42)

Hence, we immediately obtain from (
37
) and (
42
) that for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(K_{n}\geq nx)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\exp\big(\!\!-n(xt-L_{\alpha}(t)\big).

(43)

Then, by taking the optimal unique value
t_{x}>0
such that
L_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t_{x})=x
, we find from (
43
) that

\mathbb{P}(K_{n}\geq nx)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\exp\big(\!-nI_{\alpha}(x)\big)

as
I_{\alpha}(x)=xt_{x}-L_{\alpha}(t_{x})
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the case
\theta=0
. Finally, the case
\theta>0
and the case
-\alpha<\theta<0
follow exactly along the same lines using (
42
) together with (
39
) and (
41
) respectively, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

5
Concluding remarks

We proposed a new look on large deviations for the number
K_{n}
of partition sets in the Ewens-Pitman model with
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\theta>-\alpha
, offering an alternative and concise proof of Feng-Hoppe large deviation principle. In addition, our approach allowed to establish a sharp concentration inequality for
K_{n}
involving the rate function of the large deviation principle.

An interesting direction for future research is to refine Theorem
1.1
in terms of sharp large deviations, in the spirit of the pioneering work of Bahadur-Rao on the sample mean
Bahadur and Rao (1960)
; see also
Bercu et al. (2024)
for sharp large deviations in the context of the number of descents in a random permutation, and the references therein. Along this direction, one may improve Theorem
1.2
by obtaining a more precise estimate of the pre-factor
P_{n}(\alpha,\theta)
. We argue that the moment-generating function (
32
) provides a promising starting point for developing both sharp large deviations and refined concentration inequalities for
K_{n}
. However, a lot of work remains to be done, particularly in the derivation of a sharp expansion of the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function, which plays a crucial role in this analysis.

A large deviation principle for the number
K_{r,n}
of partition subsets of size
r
in the Ewens-Pitman model was first established in
Favaro and Feng (2015)
, building on the original approach of
Feng and Hoppe (1998)
. A natural question is whether our approach can be adapted to provide an alternative proof of this result. If successful, this could lead to the derivation of a concentration inequality for
K_{r,n}
, which, to the best of our knowledge, remains an open problem in the literature. Furthermore, studying large deviations and concentration inequalities for functionals that involve both
K_{n}
and
K_{r,n}
is also a promising direction for future research.
