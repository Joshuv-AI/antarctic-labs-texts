Post-Carrollian Mechanics, Ideal Gas, and Gravity
Mojtaba

Abstract




Energy and momentum in Newtonian mechanics have the familiar relations, (
\mathrm{E}=mv^{2}/2
) and (
\mathbf{P}=m\mathbf{v}
), derived from the non-relativistic limit of special relativity. In this study, we find the corresponding relations to formulate the so-called “post-Carrollian mechanics” by applying the ultra-relativistic limit to tachyon theory, resulting in (
\mathrm{E}={m\,c^{\,3}}/{v}
) and (
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{\hat{v}}\,{m\,c^{\,3}}/{2\,v^{\,2}}
). Using these, we determine the energy-momentum relation and investigate the thermodynamics of an ideal gas composed of post-Carroll particles. Moreover, by applying the ultra-relativistic limit to Einstein’s equations coupled to tachyon dust, we find the post-Carrollian gravitational potential. Finally, utilizing the geodesic equation, we determine the post-Carrollian gravitational field, which unlike the Newtonian case is found to be radially outward.

Keywords
: Post-Carroll particles, Post-Carrollian mechanics, Post-Carrollian Ideal gas, Post-Carrollian Gravitational field

2.1
Energy

By expanding the tachyon Lorentz factor (
A.16

\gamma_{{}_{{}_{t}}}=\pm\,\frac{c}{v}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})
. As a result, the tachyon energy (
A.14

1.1
), where we consider only positive values. As discussed earlier, by assigning the vanishing leading term to the zero energy of magnetic Carroll particles, the subleading term yields

\displaystyle{\boxed{\mathrm{E}_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\frac{m\,c^{\,3}}{v}\,.}}

(2.1)

We refer to this as the “post-Carrollian energy”. We observe that while the Newtonian energy (
A.10

\mathrm{E}_{{}_{N}}=mv^{2}/2

v

2.2
Momentum

To derive the momentum formula in the post-Carrollian framework, we express the tachyon’s total momentum (
A.15
) as a series expansion in powers of

(considering only positive values, since
\gamma_{{}_{{}_{t}}}=\pm\,\frac{c}{v}+\mathcal{O}(c^{3})
). This yields the total momentum of tachyon (

1.2
))

\mathbf{P}=mc\left(1\,+\,\frac{c^{2}}{2v^{2}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}\left(\,(c/v)^{4}\,\right)\right)\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}\,,

(2.2)

where
v=|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{v_{i}v_{i}}
denotes the magnitude of the velocity vector
\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{\hat{v}}=\mathbf{v}/v
is the velocity unit vector. As discussed earlier, the leading term in the expansion (
1.2
) or (
2.2
) yields the magnetic Carroll particle momentum and the subleading term corresponds to what we identify as the “post-Carrollian momentum”

\displaystyle{\boxed{\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m\,c^{\,3}}{v^{\,2}}\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}\,.}}

(2.3)

As we observe, the post-Carrollian momentum (
2.3
), which aligns with the direction of velocity, demonstrates an inverse relationship with the square of the velocity magnitude. This is in contrast to the Newtonian momentum (
A.10
),
\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{N}}=m\,\mathbf{v}
, which is directly proportional to the velocity magnitude.

An alternative perspective on the post-Carrollian momentum is as follows. By analogy with the Newtonian energy defined in (
A.9
) as
\mathrm{E}_{{}_{N}}=\mathrm{E}-\mathrm{E}_{0}
, we introduce the post-Carrollian momentum through the relation

\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}_{0}\,.

(2.4)

This is the total momentum of a tachyon
\mathbf{P}
subtracted by the so-called “rest momentum” of the tachyon
\mathbf{P}_{0}=mc\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}
. Here, we use the term “rest momentum” in analogy with “rest energy” in the Newtonian case. The concept of rest momentum is further explained in Appendix
A.3
. As we observe, the rest momentum
\mathbf{P}_{0}
is nothing but the momentum of a magnetic Carroll particle, which has zero energy. Therefore, by omitting terms of order
\mathcal{O}\left((c/v)^{4}\right)
in (
2.2
) and substituting the remaining terms into (
2.4
), we obtain the post-Carrollian momentum (
2.3
), offering an alternative approach analogous to the Newtonian case. We note that when
v\to\infty
(
\frac{c}{v}\to 0
), the post-Carrollian momentum (
2.3
) vanishes,
\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=0
, and the total momentum in (
2.4
) reduces to the rest momentum—the momentum of a magnetic Carroll particle.

2.3.1
Carroll–Schrödinger equation

The Carroll–Schrödinger equation, which describes an equation in two spacetime dimensions, was initially discovered using a technique detailed in
[
32
]
. Here, our goal is to reproduce this equation by applying the quantum mechanical prescription to the post-Carrollian energy-momentum relation. This derivation is similar to the traditional method of obtaining the Schrödinger equation (
A.12
) from the Newtonian energy-momentum relation (
A.11
). To this end, let us consider the relation (
2.5
) in one dimension

\mathrm{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\frac{\left(\mathrm{E}_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}\right)^{2}}{2\,m\,c^{3}}\,.

(2.6)

In order to formulate a quantum wave equation in the post-Carrollian framework, we apply the following quantum mechanical prescription

\mathrm{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}\to i\hbar\,\partial_{x}~,\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt\mathrm{E}_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}\to-\,i\hbar\,\partial_{t}\,,

(2.7)

to the equation (
2.6
). This leads us to

\displaystyle{\left(i\hbar\,c\,\partial_{x}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2mc^{2}}\,\partial_{t}^{2}\right)\psi=0\,,}

(2.8)

which, by interpreting
\psi
as a field, corresponds precisely to the obtained equation in
[
32
]
, called the Carroll–Schrödinger equation. This naming is motivated by the observation that under the substitution
x\leftrightarrow ct
, the equation (
2.8
) transforms into the two-dimensional Schrödinger equation (
A.12
). In higher spacetime dimensions, the equation corresponding to (
2.8
) was derived in
[
32
]
and called the “generalized Carroll–Schrödinger equation”. The derivation of this generalized equation through the quantum mechanical prescription requires additional details, which are provided in Appendix
B
. Below, we examine the symmetry algebra associated with the Carroll–Schrödinger equation (
2.8
). We note that the symmetry algebra corresponding to the generalized Carroll–Schrödinger equation (
B.5
) has yet to be determined.

2.3.2
Carroll–Schrödinger algebra

In
1+1
spacetime dimensions, the Carroll algebra
[
1
]
, involving time translation
H
(Hamiltonian), space translation
P
, and Carroll boost
B
, is given by
[\,P\,,\,B\,]=H
. Here, the Hamiltonian is a central charge and commutes with all generators. In particular, the relation
[\,H\,,\,B\,]=0
has a physical interpretation: Carroll boosts leave the energy of any state invariant. This algebra can admit a central charge
M
, leading to the centrally extended Carroll algebra

\displaystyle[\,P\,,\,B\,]=H\,,\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt[\,H\,,\,B\,]=M\,,

(2.9)

which is satisfied by the representations

H=\partial_{t}\,,\hskip 18.49988ptB=x\,\partial_{t}-im\,t\,,\hskip 18.49988ptP=\partial_{x}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{M}=-\,im\,.

(2.10)

This structure was called the “Carroll-Bargmann algebra” in
[
32
]
, drawing an analogy to the Bargmann algebra, which is a central extension of the Galilei algebra. More recently, the reference
[
46
]
has been referred to (
2.9
) as the “post-Carrollian algebra”, where the Hamiltonian
H
is no longer a central charge. Physically, this implies that a boost applied to a post-Carrollian state alters its energy. This can be understood as incorporating corrections to the magnetic Carroll theory
[
46
]
.

Accordingly, while the leading terms in (
1.1
), (
1.2
) (magnetic Carroll theory) correspond to the Carroll algebra, the subleading terms (post-Carrollian theory) are linked to the post-Carrollian algebra (
2.9
). To see this connection, one should demonstrate that the equation (
2.8
), derived from the post-Carrollian energy-momentum relation (
2.6
), remains invariant under the transformation

\delta\psi=\big(~\lambda_{{}_{H}}\,H~+~\lambda_{{}_{P}}\,P~+~\lambda_{{}_{B}}\,B~+~\lambda_{{}_{M}}\,M~\big)\,\psi

(2.11)

where
H
,
P
,
B
, and
M
are the generators of the post-Carrollian algebra (
2.9
), and
\lambda_{{}_{H}}
,
\lambda_{{}_{P}}
,
\lambda_{{}_{B}}
,
\lambda_{{}_{M}}
denote the associated transformation parameters. This demonstration can be easily verified by rewriting the equation (
2.8
) (assuming
c=1=\hbar
) in terms of generators, which yields
(iP+\frac{1}{2m}\,H^{2})\,\psi=0
, where the representations in (
2.10
) are utilized. It is then straightforward to see that this equation remains invariant under the transformations of
H
and
P
. The invariance under the transformations
B
and
M
, i.e.
\delta\psi=(\lambda_{{}_{B}}\,B+\lambda_{{}_{M}}\,M)\,\psi
, leads to the condition:
H\lambda_{{}_{B}}\big(i+\frac{M}{m}\big)\,\psi=0
. This condition is satisfied by setting
M=-\,im
, as specified in (
2.10
). As a result, while the Carroll algebra does not describe the symmetry of the equation (
2.8
), we have demonstrated that the post-Carrollian algebra (
2.9
) does indeed constitute its symmetry algebra.

It is worth noting that the post-Carrollian algebra (Carroll-Bargmann algebra) (
2.9
) can be conformally extended by including the generators of dilatation

and the spatial special conformal transformation
\mathbb{K}
. The resulting algebra, characterized by the following nonzero commutation relations

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &[\,P\,,\,B\,]=H\,,\hskip 9.24994pt&&[\,H\,,\,B\,]={M}\,,\\[2.0pt]
&[\,P\,,\,D\,]=2\,P\,,\hskip 9.24994pt&&[\,D\,,\,\mathbb{K}\,]=2\,\mathbb{K}\,,\\[2.0pt]
&[\,H\,,\,D\,]=H\,,\hskip 9.24994pt&&[\,P\,,\,\mathbb{K}\,]=D\,,\\[2.0pt]
&[\,D\,,\,B\,]=B\,,\hskip 9.24994pt&&[\,H\,,\,\mathbb{K}\,]=B\,,\end{aligned}

(2.12)

is referred to as the “Carroll–Schrödinger algebra”
[

]
(see also
[
33
]
), which has a dynamical critical exponent of

[
32
]
that the Carroll–Schrödinger algebra (
2.12
) is the symmetry algebra of the Carroll–Schrödinger equation (
2.8
), analogous to how the Schrödinger algebra governs the symmetry of the Schrödinger equation.

Up to this point, we have focused on symmetries in
1+1
dimensions. In higher dimensions, where rotation generators emerge, the post-Carrollian algebra (
2.9

[

We note that extending the Carroll algebra by including a central charge—specifically, the generator

[\,P_{i},\,P_{j}\,]=\epsilon_{ij}A_{1}
and boosts

[

2.4
Newton’s second law

Newton’s second law of motion, which is a fundamental postulate and a law of nature, states that the time derivative of the momentum is equal to the force

\mathbf{F}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{dt}}\,.

(2.13)

Utilizing this definition, and employing the post-Carrollian momentum vector
\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}
derived in (
2.3
), we find (assuming the direction of the velocity vector remains constant over time; i.e.
d\mathbf{\hat{v}}/dt=0
)

{\boxed{~~\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}}{\mathrm{dt}}\,=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{dt}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m\,c^{\,3}}{v^{\,2}}\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}\right)&=-\,m\,\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)^{3}\mathbf{a}\,,\end{aligned}~~}}

(2.14)

where
\mathbf{a}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{v}=a\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}
is the acceleration vector, with
a=dv/dt
being the magnitude of the acceleration vector. We refer to (
2.14
) as “post-Carrollian Newton’s second law of motion”.

This formula, which includes a negative sign, suggests the possibility of an outward gravitational field produced by post-Carrollian matter, as will be demonstrated later using the geodesic equation. The presence of velocity in the denominator of the momentum relation (
2.3
) is responsible for the emergence of the negative sign in the above expression. We note that this result depends on the magnitude of the particle’s velocity
v
, distinguishing it from the Newtonian case, where
\mathbf{F}_{{}_{N}}=m\,\mathbf{a}
(
A.13
).

Another approach to derive (
2.14
) is as follows. By expanding the tachyon version of Newton’s second law (
A.20
) in terms of powers of
c/v
, we get

\mathbf{F}=m\,\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)\Big(~\mathbf{a}-(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}})\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}~\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)^{3}\Big(\,\mathbf{a}-\,3\,(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}})\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}\,\Big)\,+\,\mathcal{O}\left((c/v)^{5}\right)\,.

(2.15)

Assuming that the acceleration vector and velocity vector are aligned
\mathbf{a}=a\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}
, we have
(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}})\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}=\mathbf{a}
. This eliminates the leading term
(c/v)^{1}
in (
2.15
) while simultaneously simplifying the subleading term
(c/v)^{3}
. Consequently, by dropping the higher order terms
\mathcal{O}\left((c/v)^{5}\right)
, the relation (
2.15
) reduces to
\mathbf{F}=-\,m\,\mathbf{a}\left({c}/{v}\right)^{3}
. This provides another method for obtaining (
2.14
).

To close this section, let us consider the general case where the velocity direction varies with time
d\mathbf{\hat{v}}/dt\neq 0
. In this case, we find

\mathbf{F}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)^{3}\Big(\,\mathbf{a}-\,3\,(\mathbf{\hat{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}})\,\mathbf{\hat{v}}\,\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\left(\frac{c}{v}\right)^{3}\Big(\,\mathbf{a}_{\!{}_{\perp}}-\,2\,\mathbf{a}_{\,\shortparallel}\Big)\,,

(2.16)

where
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}_{\,\shortparallel}+\mathbf{a}_{\!{}_{\perp}}

Post-Carrollian ideal gas

This section presents a significant application of the post-Carrollian energy-momentum relation (

In the canonical ensemble, we consider a system consisting of

4.1
Gravitational potential

It is shown that the Einstein’s field equations admit solutions for the energy-momentum tensor of tachyon dust (see e.g.

According to the general relativity, the curvature of spacetime is related to the distribution of matter through the Einstein’s equations

4.2
Gravitational field

The Newtonian gravitational field can be derived from the geodesic equation under some assumptions. For the reader’s convenience and to maintain a consistent approach throughout this section, we have provided a review of this derivation in Appendix

The trajectory of test particles within the gravitational field of a curved spacetime is determined by the geodesic equation which applies equally to both timelike particles (relativistic particles) and spacelike particles (tachyons). The geodesic equation is given by

Special relativity

In the context of special relativity, the total energy

(B.3)

Taking this into consideration, one can derive the following expression

\sqrt{\nabla^{\,i}\nabla_{i}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}}}\left(x\cdot\partial+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\equiv\nabla_{x}\,.

(B.4)

As a result, by applying the prescription (
B.2
) into (
B.1
) and utilizing (
B.4
), we can reproduce the generalized Carroll–Schrödinger equation
[
32
]
, which is

\displaystyle\left(i\hbar c\,\nabla_{x}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2mc^{2}}\,\partial_{t}^{2}\right)\psi=0\,,

(B.5)

\displaystyle\text{with}\hskip 9.24994pt\nabla_{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}}}\left(x\cdot\partial+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\,.

We note that in
1+1
dimensions, where
d=1
, the operator
\nabla_{x}
simplifies to the usual derivative
\partial_{x}
, thereby reducing the equation (
B.5
) to the Carroll–Schrödinger equation (
2.8
).

In the post-Carrollian framework, we notice that the momentum prescription indicated by (
B.2
) represents a deviation from the standard momentum prescription (
\mathrm{P}_{i}\to i\hbar\,\partial_{i}
). One possible explanation for this deviation can be explained as follows. When transitioning from the relativistic framework to the Newtonian framework, the momentum
\mathbf{P}
experiences no shift, while the energy undergoes a shift
\mathrm{E}_{{}_{N}}=\mathrm{E}-mc^{2}
(
A.9
), which is a scalar shift. However, when transitioning from the tachyon framework to the post-Carrollian framework, energy experiences no shift, but momentum undergoes a shift given by
\mathbf{P}\!_{{}_{\mathrm{PC}}}=\mathbf{P}-mc\,\mathbf{v}/v
(
2.4
), which can be regarded as a vector shift. This distinction in the nature of the shifts could account for the modified form of the momentum prescription as given by (
B.2
).

Finally, as demonstrated in (
2.6
), we note that the post-Carrollian momentum is always positive, leading to the selection of the prescription outlined in (
2.7
) or (
B.2
). However, in the Newtonian case, we recall that the energy is always positive (
A.11
), necessitating a reversal of the sign in the prescription, i.e.
\mathrm{E}_{{}_{N}}\to i\hbar\,\partial_{t}
and
\mathrm{P}\!_{{}_{N}}\to-\,i\hbar\,\partial_{x}
.

Appendix C
Newtonian gravity

In this section, we will review the derivation of the Newtonian gravitational potential from Einstein’s field equations in Section
C.1
and the gravitational field from the geodesic equation in Section
C.2
. Our goal is to use a similar approach in Section
4
to derive the post-Carrollian counterpart.

C.1
Gravitational potential

As discussed in Section
4.1
, we can proceed from the weak and static field equations (
4.6
) by using an appropriate energy-momentum tensor. As well established in the literature, the weak and static field equations (
4.6
) can be coupled to the relativistic dust

T^{\mu\nu}=\rho\,U^{\mu}U^{\nu}\,,

(C.1)

where
\rho>0
represents the rest mass density and
U^{\mu}
is the timelike four-velocity vector, given by

U^{\mu}=(c\,\gamma,v^{i}\,\gamma)\,,

(C.2)

with

\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}}\,,

(C.3)

where
v^{2}=v(t)^{2}=v^{i}(t)v_{i}(t)
can, in principle, be time-dependent. We note that the four-velocity vector
U^{\mu}
is the tangent vector at any point of world line
x^{\mu}(\tau)
defined as
U^{\mu}={dx^{\mu}}/{d\tau}
, where
x^{\mu}=(ct,x^{i})
is the four-position vector and
\tau
is the proper time, defined by
dt/d\tau=\gamma
. More precisely, the components of (
C.2
) are
U^{0}=\frac{dx^{0}}{d\tau}=c\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}=c\,\gamma
and
U^{i}=\frac{dx^{i}}{d\tau}=\frac{dx^{i}}{dt}\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}=v^{i}\gamma
, where
v^{i}={dx^{i}}/{dt}
.

By substituting (
C.2
) into (
C.1
), the components of the energy-momentum tensor, and its trace
T=g_{\mu\nu}\,T^{\mu\nu}\approx(\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu})T^{\mu\nu}
up to zeroth order in
h
, become

\displaystyle T_{00}=\rho\,c^{2}\,\gamma^{2}\,\approx\,\rho\,c^{2}\,,

(C.4)

\displaystyle T_{i0}=\rho\,c\,v^{i}\gamma^{2}\,\approx\,\rho\,c\,v^{i}\,,

(C.5)

\displaystyle T_{ij}=\rho\,v^{i}v^{j}\,\gamma^{2}\,\approx\,\rho\,v^{i}v^{j}\,,

(C.6)

\displaystyle T=\eta_{\mu\nu}\,T^{\mu\nu}=\eta_{00}\,T^{00}+\eta_{ij}\,T^{ij}=\rho\,\gamma^{2}\left(-\,c^{2}+\delta_{ij}v^{i}v^{j}\right)=-\,\rho\,c^{2}\,.

(C.7)

The approximate symbols (
\approx
) in (
C.4
)-(
C.6
) indicate that the non-relativistic limit (
v\ll c
) or the Newtonian limit has been taken.

By employing (
C.4
) and (
C.7
), the
00
-component of the weak and static field equations (
4.6
), up to the order of
\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}/c^{2}
, reduces to

\nabla^{2}h_{00}=-\,\frac{8\pi\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}}{c^{2}}\,.

(C.8)

Substituting

h_{00}=-\,\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}\,,

(C.9)

into the equation (
C.8
), we recover Poisson’s equation for Newtonian gravity, along with the Newtonian gravitational potential as its solution:

\nabla^{2}\phi={4\pi\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\phi=-\,\frac{MG_{\!{}_{N}}}{r}\,.

(C.10)

The gravitational energy potential
U
and the gravitational force
\mathbf{F}
then become

U=m\,\phi=-\,\frac{m\,M\,G_{\!{}_{N}}}{r}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{F}=-\,\nabla U=-\,\frac{m\,M\,G_{\!{}_{N}}}{r^{2}}\,\mathbf{\hat{r}}\,,

(C.11)

demonstrating an attractive gravitational force between two Newtonian particles with masses
m
and
M
.

By applying (
C.5
) and (
C.7
), the
i0
-component of the weak and static field equations (
4.6
) contains no terms of the order
\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}/c^{2}
. Therefore
\nabla^{2}h_{i0}=0
, demonstrating that
h_{i0}=0
or
g_{i0}=\eta_{i0}+h_{i0}=0
.

In addition, using (
C.6
) and (
C.7
), the
ij
-component of the weak and static field equations (
4.6
), up to the order of
\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}/c^{2}
, simplifies to

\nabla^{2}h_{ij}=-\,\frac{8\pi\rho\,G_{\!{}_{N}}}{c^{2}}\,\delta_{ij}\,,

(C.12)

which implies that
h_{ij}=-\,\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}\,\delta_{ij}
. Accordingly, recalling
h_{00}=-\,\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}
, we get

\displaystyle g_{00}

\displaystyle=-\,1+h_{00}\,,

\displaystyle g_{i0}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(C.13)

\displaystyle g_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(1+h_{00}\right)\delta_{ij}\,.

As a results, the full line-element that solves the linearized Einstein’s equations and describes the geometry generated by a weak and static field is given by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-\left(1+\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}\right)\,c^{2}dt^{2}+\left(1-\,\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}\right)\,dx^{i}dx_{i}\,,

(C.14)

where
\phi
is a solution of the Poisson’s equation (
C.10
).

C.2
Gravitational field

Our approach to deriving the Newtonian gravitational field from the geodesic equation differs slightly from traditional approaches in the literature, as we take the non-relativistic limit at the final stage. As illustrated below, it is structured to be comparable to the derivation of the post-Carrollian gravitational field discussed in Section
4.2
. The geodesic equation is given by

\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\nu\rho}\frac{dx^{\nu}}{d\tau}\frac{dx^{\rho}}{d\tau}=0\,,

(C.15)

or (when separating the summing indices into
0
and
i
)

\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{ij}~\frac{dx^{i}}{d\tau}\frac{dx^{j}}{d\tau}+2\,c\,\Gamma^{\mu}_{0i}~\frac{dt}{d\tau}\frac{dx^{i}}{d\tau}+c^{2}\,\Gamma^{\mu}_{00}~\frac{dt}{d\tau}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=0\,,

(C.16)

where
x^{\mu}=(ct,x^{i})
,
\tau
is the proper time, and
\Gamma^{\mu}_{\nu\rho}
are Christoffel symbols

\Gamma^{\mu}_{\nu\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\,g^{\mu\lambda}\Big({\partial_{\nu}}g_{\rho\lambda}+\partial_{\rho}g_{\nu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}g_{\nu\rho}\Big)\,.

(C.17)

By employing
dt/d\tau=\gamma
, where
\gamma
defined in (
C.3
), and applying the chain rule, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,\frac{dx^{\mu}}{dt}\,,

(C.18)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{d\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\,\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{dt^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{4}}{c^{2}}\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})\,\frac{dx^{\mu}}{dt}\,,

(C.19)

where

\frac{d\gamma}{dt}=\frac{d\gamma}{dv^{i}}\,\frac{dv^{i}}{dt}=\frac{\gamma^{3}}{c^{2}}\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})\,,

(C.20)

is utilized, with
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}
representing the inner product of the velocity vector
v^{i}=dx^{i}/dt
and acceleration vector
a^{i}=dv^{i}/dt
. Accordingly, using (
C.18
) and (
C.19
), the geodesic equation (
C.16
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{dt^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{c^{2}}\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})\,\frac{dx^{\mu}}{dt}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{ij}~v^{i}\,v^{j}+2\,c\,\Gamma^{\mu}_{0i}~v^{i}+c^{2}\,\Gamma^{\mu}_{00}=0\,.

(C.21)

The Newtonian limit of the geodesic equation (
C.15
)/(
C.21
) is acquired by three assumptions: (
i
) the gravitational field is weak, so it can be treated as a small perturbation to the flat spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}
, (
ii
) the gravitational field is static, meaning it does not vary with time
\partial_{0}\,g_{\mu\nu}=0
, (
iii
) the particles are moving slowly, with velocities much less than the speed of light
v\ll c
.

The first assumption simplifies the Christoffel symbols to
\Gamma^{\mu}_{\nu\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\,\eta^{\mu\lambda}({\partial_{\nu}}h_{\rho\lambda}+\partial_{\rho}h_{\nu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}h_{\nu\rho})
. Then, applying the requirements
h_{i0}=0
and
h_{ij}=\delta_{ij}h_{00}
derived in the previous subsection, along with the second assumption
\partial_{0}\,h_{\mu\nu}=0
, the components of the Christoffel symbols become:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{0}_{ij}=\Gamma^{i}_{0j}=\Gamma^{0}_{00}=0\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{0}_{0i}=\Gamma^{i}_{00}=-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{i}\,h_{00}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta_{ik}\,\partial_{j}+\delta_{jk}\,\partial_{i}-\delta_{ij}\,\partial_{k}\right)h_{00}\,.

(C.22)

Using these components, the geodesic equation (
C.21
) for
\mu=0
leads to the condition

\frac{\gamma^{2}}{c^{2}}\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})=v^{i}\partial_{i}h_{00}\,.

(C.23)

For
\mu=k
, the geodesic equation (
C.21
) gives

\frac{d^{2}x^{k}}{dt^{2}}+2\,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{c^{2}}\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a})\,v^{k}=-\,\partial_{k}\phi-\,\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\,\partial_{k}\phi\,,

(C.24)

where the condition (
C.23
) and
h_{00}=-\,\frac{2\phi}{c^{2}}
have been applied. At this stage, we take the non-relativistic limit (
v\ll c
), which eliminates the second terms on both sides of (
C.24
), ultimately leading to

\frac{d^{2}x^{k}}{dt^{2}}=-\,\partial_{k}\phi\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\text{or}\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{x}}{dt^{2}}=-\,\mathbf{\nabla}\phi\,.

(C.25)

By defining the Newtonian gravitational field as
{g}_{\!{}_{N}}^{k}:={d^{2}{x^{k}}}/{dt^{2}}
and substituting the Newtonian gravitational potential (
C.10
),
\phi=-\,{MG_{\!{}_{N}}}/{r}
, into (
C.25
), we obtain the Newtonian gravitational field

\mathbf{g}_{{}_{N}}:=\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{x}}{dt^{2}}=-\,\mathbf{\nabla}\phi=-\,\frac{MG_{\!{}_{N}}}{r^{2}}~\mathbf{\hat{r}}\,,

(C.26)

indicating that the Newtonian gravitational field is radially inward, see Figure
3
.

An important result here is that the term containing
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{a}
in (
C.24
) ignores in the non-relativistic limit. Consequently, the gravitational field (
C.26
) remains independent of the direction of the test particle’s velocity and acceleration, a property that plays a crucial role in the post-Carrollian gravitational field.
