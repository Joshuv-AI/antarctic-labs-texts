Interplay between epigenetics, senescence and cellular redox metabolism in cancer and its therapeutic implications
Authors: Geoffrey Balamurli, Angeline Qiu Xia Liew, Wee Wei Tee, Shazib Pervaiz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
There is accumulating evidence indicating a close crosstalk between key molecular events regulating cell growth and proliferation, which could profoundly impact carcinogenesis and its progression. Here we focus on reviewing observations highlighting the interplay between epigenetic modifications, irreversible cell cycle arrest or senescence, and cellular redox metabolism. Epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation and histone modifications, dynamically influence tumour transcriptome, thereby impacting tumour phenotype, survival, growth and spread. Interestingly, the acquisition of senescent phenotype can be triggered by epigenetic changes, acting as a double-edged sword via its ability to suppress tumorigenesis or by facilitating an inflammatory milieu conducive for cancer progression. Concurrently, an aberrant redox metabolism, which is a function of the balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and intracellular anti-oxidant defences, influences signalling cascades and genomic stability in cancer cells by serving as a critical link between epigenetics and senescence. Recognizing this intricate interconnection offers a nuanced perspective for therapeutic intervention by simultaneously targeting specific epigenetic modifications, modulating senescence dynamics, and restoring redox homeostasis.

Introduction

Despite significant strides in the therapeutic management of cancer, therapy resistance remains a formidable challenge, leading to more aggressive and difficult-to-treat disease. Therapy resistance can arise via genetic or non-genetic mechanisms and unravelling these pathways has potential implications in the development of effective and targeted therapies. Notably, epigenetic regulation of gene expression, cellular senescence, and aberrant redox milieu have been directly or indirectly associated with the acquisition of chemoresistance, however, how these disparate molecular processes synergize to drive phenotypic switch is not well understood. Here we attempt to provide a critical review of published observations. This review is organized into three mains sections. The first section introduces epigenetics and its role in therapy resistance, emphasizing its involvement in redox regulation and cellular senescence. The second section involves a deeper understanding of cellular senescence and the paradoxical role it plays as both a tumour suppressor and a tumour initiator. We also discuss the interplay between cellular senescence and aberrant redox state in therapeutic resistance. Lastly, we discuss potential therapeutic strategies targeting epigenetic modifications, cellular senescence and redox homeostasis to eradicate residual diseases that could potentiate the formation of metastatic and aggressive drug-resistant tumours.

Epigenetics

The term “epigenetics” was introduced in 1942 b y Conrad Hal Waddington, who defined it as the study of the interplay between genotypes and phenotypes [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Over time, the field has evolved to encompass the study of heritable or stable changes in phenotypes or gene expressions that occur without alterations in the Watson-Crick DNA base pairing [1,6]. Epigenetic modifications include DNA methylation, modification(s) of histones as well as non-coding RNA (ncRNA) [1,2,[5], [6], [7]]. These modifications, in turn, can influence chromatin accessibility, impacting the binding of proteins and subsequently regulating gene transcription [8]. DNA methylation

DNA methylation is one of the earliest described epigenetic mechanisms implicated in key biological processes including development and cellular differentiation, X-chromosome inactivation in female mammals and transcriptional regulation [1,[8], [9], [10], [11]]. It involves methyl group addition to DNA strand, which alters the activity of the DNA segment. In the human genome, all cytosines have the potential to become methylated, and in particular, on CpG sites—regions on the DNA composed of a cytosine followed by a guanine nucleotide in 5′ to 3’ direction. Methylation of these CpG dinucleotides represses gene transcription as well as noncoding genomic regions [12].

DNA methylation is mediated by DNA methyl transferases (DNMTs), namely DNMT1, DNMT3a, DNMT3b and DNMT3L [8,10,11,13,14]. DNMT1 is a maintenance methyl transferase that preferentially methylates hemi-methylated DNA. It plays a crucial role during S phase where it ensures that methylation patterns are maintained and inherited in daughter cells [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Unlike DNMT1, DNMT3a and DNMT3b are de novo methyl transferases that preferentially target unmethylated CpG dinucleotides [8,20,21]. DNMT3L is homologous to the de novo methyl transferases; however, it lacks the catalytic domain found in the other DNMTs [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. DNMT3L aids DNMT3a and DNMT3b by increasing enzyme activity and their ability to bind to the methyl group donor, S-adenosyl-l-methionine [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Notably, studies of DNA methylation in non-CpG sites, termed CpH sites (where H is an A, C or T nucleotide), have also gained attention after its identification, first in pluripotent cells, and subsequently in other tissues and organs [[8], [9], [10]]. In embryonic stem cells, CpH methylation can be found in actively transcribed gene bodies, while in neural tissues enriched levels of CpH methylation can be found in transcriptionally silent genes [28]. Similar to CpG sites, DNMT3a and DNMT3b aid in the de novo methylation of CpH dinucleotides; however, CpH methylation is not conserved upon DNA replication by DNMT1 [28].

Histone modifications

Chromatin is composed of DNA wrapped around histones, forming repeating units called nucleosomes, which are the basic structural and functional elements of chromatin. Each nucleosome comprises of an octamer made of two copies of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 histone proteins [[29], [30], [31]]. These octamers are wrapped by approximately 146 base pairs of the DNA, giving rise to a nucleosome. Modifications of the histones can influence chromatin structure which eventually alters the regulation of transcription [30]. Chromatin structure can be altered based on the type of histone modification and this leads to the formation of euchromatin, which is transcriptionally more active and heterochromatin which is transcriptionally inactive. Euchromatin referring to the higher order structure of the chromatin, involves histones being loosely wrapped by the DNA. In contrast, heterochromatin refers to condensed chromatins which are tightly bound by the DNA. The most common histone modifications include methylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation [30,[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. However, there are many other modifications including sumoylation, deimination, ADP ribosylation, β-N-acetylglucosamine, histone tail clipping and proline isomerization [[38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]].

One of the earliest histone modifications is acetylation, which is associated with activation of gene transcription [45]. However, in recent studies, histone acetylation was shown responsible for mediating processes such as gene silencing, cell cycle progression and DNA repair [30,46,47]. Histone acetylation is a reversible process and is catalyzed by discrete set of enzymes called histone acetyltransferases (HATs). HATs acetylate and remove the positive charge on lysine ε-amino groups of histone N-terminal tails by catalyzing the transfer of acetyl groups from acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl Co-A) [48]. Some examples of HATs that promote histone acetylation include KAT2A, KAT2B and KAT3B [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. In contrast, there are enzymes which act to reverse this acetylation of the histones termed as histone deacetylases (HDACs). HDACs remove acetyl groups on the lysine residues of histones, thereby reestablishing the positive charge on lysine. This consequently allows the DNA to be tightly wrapped around the histone cores which leads to gene silencing due to transcriptional suppression. Examples of HDACs that mediate histone deacetylation include HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3 and HDAC6 [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]].

Histone methylation has also been shown to influence chromatin compaction as well as the signalling of various protein complexes [55]. The most extensively studied histone methylation involves the mono, di or tri-methylation of lysine residues at the ε-amino groups; however, histone methylation can also occur on other basic residues such as arginines and histidines [55]. Similar to acetylation, histone methylation is a reversible process regulated by two classes of enzymes: histone methyltransferases (HMTs) and histone demethylases (HDMs). Some examples of HATs and HMTs include DOTL1 and LSD1 [[56], [57], [58]]. These myriad methylation events at various sites of the histones are recognized by chromatin effector molecules termed as readers which recruit other molecules to the methylation site. Consequently, the recruitment of other molecules allows for the change of chromatin state to bring about transcriptional regulation [59]. For instance, it has been reported that lysine methylation of histone H3 by DOT1 impairs the interaction between telomeric regions and binding proteins Sir2 and Sir3 [60]. In addition, the trimethylation of histone 3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) is associated with active transcription while the trimethylation at lysine 27 (H3K27me3) functions as silencers to repress gene expression [55,61,62].

Histone phosphorylation occurs on serine, threonine and tyrosine residues at the N terminal tails of the histones [63]. As the DNA is negatively charged, the addition of phosphates, which brings a negative charge to the residues of the histone, prevents the DNA from being tightly wrapped around histones, thereby allowing for a more open-chromatin state [64]. Like histone acetylation and methylation, histone phosphorylation is also a reversible process involving the transfer of a phosphate group from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to target residues of histones, leading to the conversion of ATP to adenosine diphosphate (ADP). Histone phosphorylation is regulated by kinases and phosphatases, such as ATM, ATR and PP1γ, which are responsible for the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of histones, respectively [[65], [66], [67]]. Due to the permissive chromatin landscape as a consequence of histone phosphorylation, this histone modification is often associated with transcriptional activation. Interestingly, recent studies suggest an interplay of histone phosphorylation with histone acetylation whereby select HATs can preferentially bind to histone tails that have been phosphorylated at the serine 10 residue [64,68].

Noncoding RNAs

ncRNAs are transcripts that do not encode for proteins yet play a significant role in many biological processes including the epigenetic control of gene expression [[69], [70], [71], [72]]. The two types of ncRNAs include housekeeping ncRNA and regulatory ncRNA. Regulatory ncRNAs are further classified into two subcategories: small ncRNA and long ncRNAs (lncRNAs) [73,74].

Three most common small ncRNAs include small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs) and PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) [[72], [73], [74]]. siRNAs have been reported to induce transcriptional gene silencing though DNA methylation and histone modification [75,76]. For example, siRNAs have been shown to promote epigenetic based transcriptional gene silencing through the RNA interference (RNAi) pathways [77]. Studies have also shown that miRNAs have implications for epigenetic modifications that are crucial in humans. For example, miR-N5 has been shown to play an inhibitory role in acetylation of histone H3 protein at lysine 56 (H3K56) in the promoter regions of genes including β-catenin and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) [78]. Additionally, piRNAs have been reported to play a vital role in DNA methylation and histone modification at the promoter region of genes regulating cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis in U937 leukemia cell line [79].

Several studies have highlighted the involvement of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) in epigenetic processes. For instance, the deacetylase SIRT6, responsible for reversing acetylation marks on histone H3 at lysine residues 9 and 56 (H3K9 and H3K56), is regulated by the lncRNA lncPRESS1. This interaction prevents SIRT6 from deacetylating H3K9 and H3K56, thereby influencing the pluripotent fate of human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) [80]. Furthermore, the lncRNA HOXA-AS2 has been documented to influence the activity of chromatin-modifying enzymes, including the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which results in changes in H3K27 methylations [81].

RNA modifications

Epigenetic modifications can also occur on RNA such as ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), messenger RNA (mRNA) and small nuclear RNA (snRNA), which could have structural and functional implications in the cell [[82], [83], [84]]. The most common RNA modification in eucaryotic cells would be N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, which is involved in processes such as stress response, tumorigenesis and embryonic development [85,86].

m6A sites are most frequently found in conserved motifs proximal to mRNA stop codons and 3′-untranslated regions [87,88]. Several writers, erasers and readers regulate m6A levels which can be found not just in mRNAs but in lncRNAs, miRNAs, snRNAs, small nucleolar RNAs as well as ribosomal RNAs [89]. Amongst the writers, several genes are crucial for the installation of m6A by implicating the m6A methyl transferase complex (MTC). These genes include METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, ZC3H13, VIRMA and many others. The key writers for the deposition of m6A would be METTL3 and METTL14 which forms a stable heterodimer that transforms METTL3 to a catalytically active state and this is the only catalytic subunit of the MTC reported thus far [89]. Beyond the mRNA, m6A modifications have been observed in rRNAs and these modifications are catalyzed by a complex formed upon dimerization of METTL5 and TRMT112 [90]. m6A modifications have also been reported in snRNAs mediated by METTL16 [91]. Similar to the diverse m6A modifications mentioned above, myriad m6A methyltransferases serve unique purposes to modify the RNA in a site- and species-specific manner [89]. Apart from m6A modifications, changes in methyl transferases can also impact RNA modifications. For example, the depletion of methyltransferase METTL1 and WDR4 genes impairs the levels of methylated guanosine of tRNAs, thereby negatively impacting the proliferative and invasive capacities of cancer cells [92]. Likewise, methylation of rRNA on CpG islands of the promoter regions implicates rRNA promoter activity and gene expression in hepatocellular carcinoma [93]. As such, methylation-mediated silencing of rRNAs could be a potential therapeutic strategy for regulating gene expressions as well as for effective treatment outcomes [93].

RNA modifications are also mediated by demethylases termed as erasers. One such eraser is the fat mass and obesity associated protein (FTO) [94]. To govern mRNA translation and regulate the expression of lipid related genes, m6A demethylation is catalyzed by FTO resulting in preadipocyte differentiation, adipogenesis, fat deposition and the development of obesity [94]. Similarly, m6A demethylase ALKBH5 was discovered to play a crucial role in DNA damage response during ROS-mediated stress. In a recent study by Yu et al., in 2021, redox stress resulted in the modulation of several genes including genes associated with DNA damage repair through the regulation of ALKBH5 [95]. In addition, DNA dioxygenases ten-eleven translocations (TETs) have also been reported to function as RNA demethylases modulating RNA modifications whereby TET1 was observed to promote RNA oxidation of f5C to ca5C while TET2 mediated m5C oxidation in mRNA [[96], [97], [98], [99]].

Just like the writers and erasers, readers also play a crucial role in RNA modifications which can drastically affect RNA metabolism and function. For example, an mRNA transport adaptor Aly/Ref was identified to regulate mRNA export by emulating the role of a m5C site-specific mRNA binding protein [100]. Similarly, YBX1 was reported to play a pivotal role as an RNA-binding protein that governed the stability of CD31, EMCN and BMP4 through a site specific m5C dependent manner. The disruption of YBX1 was shown to prevent the release of BMP4 which consequently blocked osteogenic differentiation in mesenchymal stromal cells of the bone [88,101]. Taken together, RNA modification is a prominent branch of epigenetic modifications that plays a crucial role in homeostatic growth and survival of cells, and dysregulation of this key process is associated with disease pathology.

Epigenetics in cancer and therapy resistance

Epigenetic changes play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of cancer. Since the discovery of various epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation, research has been directed towards understanding the involvement and/or the role of these modifications in the processes of cancer initiation and progression [102,103]. For example, DNA hypomethylation was found to increase genomic instability due to chromosomal rearrangement, contributing to cancer progression [104,105]. Additionally, it facilitates oncogenesis by allowing increased expression of several oncogenes in the absence of methylation [106,107]. For example, the loss of DNMT1 results in the loss of methylation on enhancers of HES1 and OLFM4, which are genes vital for intestinal stem cell proliferation. Additionally the DNA hypomethylation of LINE1 repetitive elements has been shown to dysregulate APC gene which could drive the initiation of colorectal carcinomas [107].

Similarly, aberrant histone modifications can also promote or repress cancer progression in a context specific manner. In 2005, Fraga et al. [108] demonstrated that loss of H4K16 acetylation as well as H4K20 trimethylation were crucial for the initiation of human cancer [92]. This investigation was one of the first studies to demonstrate the role of histone modifications in cancer progression. In other studies, high levels of HATs were shown associated with tumour aggressiveness and recurrence in breast cancer, while trimethylation of H3K27 suppressed transcription of genes that fuel aggressiveness in drug refractory cancers [51,[109], [110], [111]].

Apart from inhibiting tumour suppressors and promoting oncogenes, epigenetic modifications also impact other hallmarks of cancer. For example, the increase in activity of HDAC1 has been reported to enable angiogenic invasion by regulating the expression of MMP14, which is necessary for angiogenic sprouting [112]. Furthermore, histone modifications such as H4K16 acetylation maintains the gene activity of TMS1, a tumour suppressor gene that regulates epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) [[113], [114], [115], [116]].

Aberrant signaling by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, a major driver of oncogenesis and its progression, is critical in disrupting the tight balance between cell proliferation and cell loss. Interestingly, m6A methyltransferase METTL14 was shown to be responsible for methylation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis, thus inhibiting proliferation, migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells [117]. Similarly, METTL3 activates m6A-GLUT1-mTORC1 network to facilitate progression of colorectal cancer [118]. Apart from RNA modifications, aberrant activation of mTOR signaling influences DNA methylation levels which promotes tumour progression and survival in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and combination therapy including mTOR and DNMT inhibitors synergistically inhibits HCC progression [119].

Recent investigations on ncRNAs have also implicated their role in cancer progression and metastasis. For example, Kim et al. [120] described the role of the miR-34 family in processes such as oncogene activation, tumour progression and regulation of apoptosis. Within the miR-34 family, miR-34a exhibits anti-cancer effects by targeting genes linked to the cell cycle and those that regulate apoptosis. Reports indicate that miR-34a suppresses the expression of Met and BCL2, thereby resulting in the deceleration of cancer proliferation and enhanced survival, particularly in human KRAS-positive and p53-negative patients [121,122]. The other members of the miR-34 family, miR-34b and miR-34c, play a crucial role in metastasis by impacting anchorage-independent growth and EMT in lung adenocarcinoma [120,122,123]. In non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC), higher levels of miR-34b and miR-34c methylation have been identified and reported to promote cancer proliferation and metastasis [124].

In addition to their crucial role in tumour progression and development, epigenetic alterations also play a significant role in conferring therapy resistance in cancer [[125], [126], [127]]. In a recent study, Debruyne et al. [127] reported that resistance to ALK inhibitors in neuroblastoma cells was a function of transcriptional reprogramming and a shift of gene dependency from MYCN to BORIS when MYCN was epigenetically repressed due to increased H3K9Me3 levels [127]. BORIS, a paralogue of CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), facilitates the interaction between genes and their regulatory elements. In their study, Debruyne et al. illustrated how BORIS led to an increase in chromatin occupancy and promoted new chromatin interactions, thereby supporting the expression of several genes associated with ALK inhibitor resistance [127]. In ovarian cancer, Taxol resistance was mediated by chromatin modifications on the ABCB1 gene with elevated levels of H3K9 and H3K14 acetylation [128]. Interestingly, the upregulation of HATs, p300 and GCN5, played a vital role in chromatin remodelling to increase ABCB1 expression and silencing of these HATs sensitized resistant cells to Taxol [128]. In addition, epigenetic modifications can govern redox imbalance which plays a crucial role in carcinogenesis and therapeutic resistance in cancer [129,130]. For example, epigenetic modification of TXNIP, an inhibitor of the thioredoxin system, which alters cellular redox milieu and compromises the function of TXNIP in suppressing growth and metastasis and promoting apoptosis [131]. To that end, DNA methylation has been reported to silence TXNIP expression in leukemia, while in breast cancer miR-373 inhibits the translation of TXNIP [132,133]. In addition, Hagiwara et al. [134] demonstrated that the oncogenic protein MUC1-C induces the polybromo-associated BAF (PBAF) chromatin remodelling complexes in prostate cancer. This study revealed how MUC1-C enhances the expression of SLC7A11 by increasing chromatin accessibility through the formation of a complex with NRF2 and PBRM1 genes. The MUC1-C and PBRM1 complex also activates other NRF2 target genes, including G6PD and PGD, which regulate the pentose phosphate pathway. Consequently, MUC1-C enables cells to efficiently undergo DNA damage repair and regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [134]. With an efficient control of intracellular ROS, cancer cells gain a survival advantage against ROS-mediated therapies, thus promoting the acquisition of therapy resistance. Collectively, these investigations highlight the key role of epigenetic modifications in cancer initiation and therapy resistance through the dysregulation of several genes as well as redox homeostasis. Epigenetic modifications may be used as biomarkers of disease progression or treatment failure, and as such, modulating these epigenetic modifications may be a critical strategy to deter cancer progression or therapeutic resistance.

Epigenetics and cellular senescence

Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence has been theoretically defined as a permanent state of growth arrest. In 1961, it was first described by Leonard Hayflick that human lung fibroblasts stopped proliferating and exited the cell cycle after multiple rounds of cell division [135]. This phenomenon is therefore termed as “Hayflick limit” which refers to the number of times a cell can divide before it ceases to proliferate and enter the stage of senescence [135]. Senescent cells are characterized by phenotypic, cellular, biochemical and secretory changes. Some of the hallmarks of senescence include an enlarged and flattened cell morphology, cell cycle arrest, accumulation of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity and acquisition of a secretory phenotype (senescence associate secretory phenotype; SASP). SASP encompasses a group of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, proteases, growth factors and metalloproteinases which can serve as a double-edged sword [136]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-8 (IL-8) are specifically highly expressed in SASP to promote inflammation during senescence [137]. The growth arrest phenotype can be promoted by the inflammatory mediators found in SASP which act in both an autocrine or paracrine manner to affect neighbouring cells [138,139].

Physiologically, cellular senescence plays important roles in tissue remodelling and homeostasis by promoting wound repair [140,141]. During mammalian embryonic development, senescent cells have been found to ensure proper development of the inner ear and mesonephros excretory organ [142]. However, aberrant accumulation of senescent cells in aging tissues can eventually trigger the development of various age-related pathologies such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease [143].

While replicative senescence (RS) is primarily driven by telomere shortening due to cellular replication [144], senescence can also be triggered by multiple intrinsic and extrinsic stress signals such as chemotherapy- and radiation-induced DNA damage, oxidative stress, expression of activated oncogenes (oncogene induced senescence; OIS) and other stress stimuli [145]. During DNA damage repair (DDR), tumour suppressor p53 triggers a series of pathways to promote senescence and DNA repair. However, if the DNA damage is too extensive, p53 will promote the transcription of apoptosis-inducing target genes to eliminate these cells [[146], [147], [148]]. Both activation of senescence and apoptosis serve a tumour suppressor function by limiting aberrant proliferation and maintaining genome stability [149]. On the flip side of it, tumor cells can evade chemotherapy-induced apoptosis by entering senescence (therapy induced senescence; TIS), as senescent cells acquire resistant phenotype. Studies also provide evidence that cancer cells during TIS can regain proliferative capacity, a phenomenon termed as “pseudo-senescence” [[150], [151], [152], [153]], which could be due to the selective knockdown of proteins involved in the induction and maintenance of senescence [[154], [155], [156]]. This phenomenon can lead to the development of malignant tumours that are resistant to therapeutic agents [157,158]. Due to this property of evading apoptotic execution, accumulation of senescent cells has broad implications in numerous disease states [159,160].

Crosstalk between epigenetic modifiers and cellular senescence

Many studies have shown that dysregulation of epigenetics plays an important role in promoting senescence and stem cell aging through transcriptional regulation of numerous genes [[161], [162], [163]]. This part of the review will highlight the interplay between epigenetics and cellular senescence (Fig. 2). Changes in chromatin structure and heterochromatin remodelling typically occur in aging thus resulting in the derepression of silenced genes in numerous eukaryotic organisms [[164], [165], [166]]. Specifically in senescence, loss of heterochromatin is commonly observed, partly due to the perpetual DNA damage which lowers the expression of histone chaperone and histone biosynthesis [167]. During the induction of senescence, there is a widespread change in the compaction of chromatin. For example, there is a global de-condensation of the chromatin at the site of DNA damage which will then condense again to activate ATM/ATR signalling as part of the DDR [168]. One unique feature of senescent cells is the formation of senescence-associated heterochromatin foci (SAHF), which is prevalent in OIS and RS [169,170]. SAHF are regions of specialized nuclear heterochromatin structures that are dense and methylated, thereby repressing genes that drive proliferation [169]. These heterochromatin foci are enriched with H3K9me3 and H4K20me3 in the core while H3K27me3 forms a ring at the periphery [171]. SAHF play a critical role in the induction and maintenance of senescence by promoting the induction of SASP [172]. However, it is important to note that SAHF formation is not a general characteristic of senescent cells, as it depends largely on the cell type and method of senescence induction [173]. Exposure to chemotherapy drugs, senescence-inducing bacterial toxins and serial passaging can induce SAHF formation in some fibroblast cell lines but not in keratinocytes [174]. Also, SAHF have a role in tumour suppression since the heterochromatic foci inhibit the expression of proliferating genes. As such, any impairment of SAHF integrity is likely to promote cellular transformation [175]. Polycomb group proteins (PcG proteins) are chromatin modifiers which govern cell cycle, developmental and differentiation processes [172,176]. PcG proteins are a family of evolutionarily conserved proteins that serve as epigenetic repressors of transcription [177]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that PcG proteins play an important role in regulating cell cycle and carcinogenesis, specifically in cancer development and progression [178,179]. In proliferating cells, PRC, which are multi-protein complexes, can suppress transcription of senescence-associated genes [[180], [181], [182]]. Scaffold-attachment-factor A (SAFA), which belongs to the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family, can interact with PRC1 and 2 to either inhibit or promote senescence [183]. p21-associated DNA damage-activated (PANDA), a lncRNA, serves similar function as SAFA to suppress senescence through its interaction with PRC; depletion in proliferating fibroblasts induces senescence [183]. In senescence, PANDA acts as a decoy by binding to nuclear transcription factor Y subunit alpha (NF-YA), a transcription factor that promotes proliferation, thus preventing it from binding to and promoting the transcription of its target genes in senescence. As such, downregulation of PANDA during senescence allows senescent cells to regain proliferative capacity since NF-YA is free to promote the transcription of proliferation-associated genes [183]. Another PcG, EZH2, has diverse roles in cellular senescence. EZH2 knockdown induces the onset of senescence and SASP acquisition along with p53/p21 activation via DNA damage that is dependent on DNA replication [172]. Mechanistically, EZH2 promotes trimethylation of H3K27 and loss of this trimethylation by pharmacological inhibition of EZH2 triggers senescence without a change in EZH2 expression [172]. Such mechanism of induction of senescence was surprisingly achievable in the absence of DNA damage or p21 induction; however, associated with the activation of the p16 pathway [172]. In the case of OIS, Jumonji domain-containing protein-3 (JMJD3), a H3K27 demethylase, was shown to induce p16INK4a-dependent senescence via the activation of INK4a but not ARF [184]. Collectively, these findings provide evidence to implicate PcG proteins and PRC in tumour suppression via promoting senescence.

HDACs also play an important role in cellular senescence. The inhibition and/or downregulation of HDAC2 or HDAC7 promotes the expression of p16 and p21, which in turn induces cell cycle arrest. Conversely, the re-expression of HDAC7 can delay senescence and allow proliferation [185]. Similarly, HDAC4, also plays a role in regulating senescence [186] and is downregulated in OIS and replicative senescence [187]. HDAC4 specifically binds and monitors H3K27 acetylation levels at enhancers and super-enhancers that govern the senescent transcriptome profile [188]. Di Giorgio et al. [187] reported that knocking down HDAC4 induces senescence by increasing the expression of senescence-related genes and SA-β-Gal activity. In leiomyosarcomas which has high expression of HDAC4, knocking down HDAC4 results in cell cycle arrest, senescence and SASP production [187]. Furthermore, specific interaction between HDAC4 and H3K27ac at senescence super-enhancer regions was reported, thus indicating the importance of HDAC4 in modulating senescence [188]. Notably, studies have also indicated that changes in the H3K27ac/H3K27me3 ratio are drivers of senescence and cancer [[189], [190], [191]]. Similarly, shRNA knockdown of histone acetylase (HAT), p300, can delay the establishment of RS in fibroblasts as these cells are still slowly proliferating [189]. However, overexpression of p300 was not seen to induce premature senescence [189]. In this regard, direct interaction between p300 and senescence-associated super-enhancer regions has been demonstrated, which further supports evidence that p300 serves as the main HAT in these regions [189]. In addition, modifications on histone H4, such as H4K20, have also been implicated in cellular senescence where H4K20me3 is especially enriched in DNA sequences found within the domains of SAHF [192]. While the increase in H4K20me3 alone does not induce senescence in primary human cells, it strengthens OIS. One could conjecture that H4K20me3 exerts a tumour suppressive effect by slowing down the process of tumorigenesis. These findings provide evidence that histone-modifying enzymes play a crucial role in regulating cellular senescence.

Senescent cells and SASP have also been shown to promote cellular reprogramming of cancer cells into a stem-like state, which provides a conducive environment for the acquisition of malignant and drug resistant phenoptypes [193,194]. High-mobility-group protein B2 (HMGB2), is a DNA binding protein that modulates chromatin structure and promotes higher order chromosomal organization which in turn regulates DNA replication, DNA repair and transcription [195]. HMGB2 prevents 10.13039/501100003548SASP genes’ incorporation into SAHF and preferentially binds to 10.13039/501100003548SASP gene loci during senescence to promote the expression of proteins such as IL-1α, IL-8 and IL-6 [196]. Supporting this, gene knockdown of HMGB2 results in a decrease in the amount of heterochromatic foci and increases the spread of repressive heterochromatin marks into 10.13039/501100003548SASP gene loci thus preventing the expression of 10.13039/501100003548SASP factors [196]. However, HMGB2 can inhibit the formation of SAHF during senescence. As such, it has been found that HMGB2 serves as a regulator to promote SASP expression by preventing its gene loci from being incorporated into SAHF [196]. Therefore, it is desirable to target this pathway to control cancer cell proliferation via senescence induction, while at the same time preventing the pro-inflammatory milieu created by the SASP factors.

As mentioned in the earlier section, there is a dynamic change in the chromatin structure during senescence which drives the formation of super enhancers at SASP gene loci to promote the transcription of SASP factors. Increasing H3K27ac at senescence super-enhancer regions along with the recruitment of Bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4), which acts as a transcriptional and epigenetic regulator, promotes the upregulation of SASP [190]. Even though BRD4 was shown to be essential for SASP expression in OIS or DNA damage-induced senescence, it is interesting to note that Dong et al. [197] demonstrated tumour promoting activity of BRD4 via inhibiting cellular senescence through the E2F/miR-106b-5p/p21 pathway. As such, the dichotomous effect of BRD4 on cellular senescence could be cell type and/or context (stimulus) dependent.

Mixed-lineage leukemia 1 (MLL1), a H3K4 methyltransferase and oncoprotein, has also been linked to SASP expression [198]. MLL1 inhibition can significantly downregulate the expression of SASP factors such as IL-6, IL-8, IL-1α, MMP1 and MMP3 in both OIS transformed fibroblasts and primary human melanocytes, hence demonstrating MLL1 epigenetic control over these genes [198]. Interestingly, despite inhibiting the inflammatory genes within SASP, blocking MLL1 did not affect OIS [198]. Another histone methyltransferase, disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L), was reported to methylate H3K79 at the IL-1α locus to promote gene transcription [199]. Similar to MLL1, DOT1L epigenetic regulation of SASP is independent of the senescence cell cycle arrest phenotype [199]. Therefore, MLL1 and DOTL1 inhibition are powerful therapeutic strategies to prevent tumour cell proliferation by inducing senescence through its regulation of proliferation-associated genes while preventing the detrimental effects of SASP in promoting drug resistance and tumour progression.

G9a and G9a-like protein (GLP) are nuclear histone lysine methyltransferases that repress transcription, and are degraded by the proteasome during senescence as a result of DDR [200]. As a result, there is an increase in expression of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 [200]. JMJD3, as mentioned earlier, has been shown to induce cellular senescence and upregulated in numerous malignant transformed glioma cell lines [201]. Ectopic expression of JMJD3 can increase the expression of SASP factors and induce senescence in glioma cells suggesting that it can induce SASP activation [201].

Other than the epigenetic upregulation of SASP by methyltransferases, demethylases and histone binding proteins, NAD-dependent deacetylase sirtuin-1 (SIRT-1) can negatively regulate the expression of SASP at the transcriptional level [202]. As SIRT-1 is a deacetylase, decreased expression of SIRT-1 during senescence can increase the acetylation of H3K9 and H4K16 which drives the transcription of IL-6 and IL-8 [202]. This phenomenon is also observed in SIRT-1 depleted non-senescent cells [202]. Even though DNA damage and senescence are commonly found hand in hand, notably, HDAC inhibition with sodium butyrate induces senescence and SASP in the presence of DNA damage [203,204]. Together, the aforementioned studies demonstrate that chromatin remodelling as a result of DDR may supersede DNA breakage to be the significant regulator of SASP.

Interplay between cellular redox status, epigenetics and senescence

ROS is a group of reactive oxygen intermediates, which includes radical and non-radical entities such as superoxide anion (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical (OH•). Superoxide radical, generated upon one electron reduction of molecular oxygen, is the source for the generation of other species such as H2O2 via the action of superoxide dismutase (SOD1, 2 and 3) or peroxynitrite (OONO−) generated upon reaction with nitric oxide (NO) [205,206]. The main sources of endogenous ROS are the membrane bound NADPH oxidase complexes (NOXs) and the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), while exogenous ROS stimuli include metals, redox cycling compounds, exposure to radiation, chemotherapeutic drugs and carcinogens [207]. During homeostatic growth, the levels of intracellular ROS are kept within physiological range by the action of the anti-oxidant defence systems, which comprise of enzymatic and non-enzymatic factors [208]. An imbalance between the generation (too much) and disposal (too little) of ROS results in aberrant accumulation of ROS, described as oxidative stress [209]. Depending upon the severity of oxidative stress, the biological outcome spans a spectrum ranging from cell survival and proliferation to cellular senescence, tissue damage and cell death (Fig. 3). To that end, it is imperative that a clear distinction be made with respect to the different molecular ROS agents rather than attributing a particular phenomenon or cellular response to an increase in overall ROS levels [210]. The physiological signalling function as well as the pathological effects of the various ROS agents have been demonstrated in a host of recent reports. For example, our work over the years provides a clear distinction between the signalling outcome(s) of O2•− vs H2O2-mediated effects and the intracellular ratio of the two ROS species [[211], [212], [213], [214]]. While a significant tilt in the ratio in favor of H2O2 creates a permissive environment for the execution of cell death [[215], [216], [217], [218]], the opposite, i.e., a significant increase in intracellular O2•−, endows cells with a survival advantage by blunting death execution [214,215,[219], [220], [221], [222]]. Indeed, the intermediary role of O2•− was clearly demonstrated in the settings of pro-oncogenic signalling such as the sustained activation of c-Myc and NF-κB as well as the increase in the expression of the death receptor inhibitory protein cFLIP and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 [221,[223], [224], [225], [226], [227], [228], [229]]. Interestingly, some of these effects are mediated through an increase in gene transcription [221], while others appear to involve post-translational modification of proteins, brought about for example by inhibiting the putative tumour suppressor protein phosphatase, PP2A [[226], [227], [228]]. Notably, an intricate crosstalk between intracellular redox milieu and epigenetic regulation of gene transcription has also been demonstrated, such as in the settings of cardiac fibrosis [230]. To that end, aberrant redox signalling promotes fibrotic process via increased fibroblast proliferation, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, endothelial injury and cellular senescence. The crosstalk between epigenetics and redox homeostasis have been brought to light over the past decade ever since the shift of focus toward the two phenomena begun. In a recent study by ElBeck et al. (2024), the researchers demonstrated how oxidative stress downregulates IDH2 which is an enzyme that is responsible for the production of mitochondrial NADPH [232]. Surprisingly, L-2-hydroxyglutarate (L2HG), the reduced form of 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) that generates NADH upon the redox reaction was observed to be elevated. The authors also drew an association between elevated levels of IDH2 and L2HG. With their data, it seems to suggest that there is a cooperation between oxidative and antioxidative events where IDH2 is increased from the antioxidant role of L2HG but decreased due to oxidative stress [232]. Interestingly, in Mlp−/− mice (LIM protein deficient mice that is comparable to dilated cardiomyopathy), lower levels of 5hmC in an intron was detected compared to Wild Type (WT) mice and this correlated with the lower expression of ldh2 [232]. Further findings of their experiments revealed that 5hmC plays a role in modulating energy metabolism and redox regulated ldh2 expression. Collectively, their work reveals the complex interplay between redox homeostasis and epigenetic modifications where fluctuations of TET enzymes can epigenetically alter 5hmC levels resulting in changes in redox balance.

Based on experimental evidence, one might conjecture that a majority of physiological signalling attributed to ROS could be due to SOD-mediated dismutation of O2•− to H2O2, while pathological outcomes in general could be a function of NOS-stimulated NO reactivity with O2•− to generate OONO− [233]. How this plays out in the context of the interplay between intracellular Oxidative/Nitrative stress and epigenetic regulation of gene expression remains to be elucidated and could have potential therapeutic implications from the standpoint of developing species-specific strategies rather than targeting ROS globally.

Interestingly, ROS and RNS (reactive nitrogen species) have been shown to play a critical role in the induction and maintenance of senescence [234,235]. As a matter of fact, exposure to non-lethal concentrations of H2O2 is an established method for senescence induction [236]. Also, a striking feature of senescent cells is an elevated level of ROS as observed in RS, stress-induced senescence or OIS [234,237,238]. ROS play a role in initiating and advancing cellular senescence by directly damaging mitochondrial DNA and working in conjunction with modifications in telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), as well as the activity of the p53 and Ras pathways [143,239]. Additionally, ROS production through sequential signaling involving GADD45-MAPK14 (p38MAPK)-GRB2-TGFBR2-TGFβ is essential for maintaining growth arrest stability during the establishment of the senescent phenotype [240]. Notably, while ROS-induced senescence has been shown to be a function of DDR activation [241], recent evidence also points to a senescence-associated redox state downstream of the activation of DDR and other checkpoints of senescence [242]. Furthermore, ROS can induce non-coding miRNAs such as miR-210 and miR-494 which contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and autophagy inhibition, thereby leading to the acquisition of the senescent phenotype [243].

It is plausible that fine tuning the relationship between cellular redox state and the senescent phenotype as well as identifying effector mechanism(s) could provide novel targets for intervention to block the damaging effects of senescence such as in age-related pathologies. To that end, redox regulation of epigenetics has been shown to play a significant role in senescence. ROS-induced changes in DNA such as oxidative modifications can directly impact the epigenetic landscape by altering DNA methylation patterns, histone modifications, and chromatin structure [231]. ROS exert modifications on histones and DNA by engaging in interconnected phases of epigenetic regulation of mitochondrial and nuclear DNA [244,245]. In experimental settings, treatment with H2O2 increases the frequency of transition mutations in methylated CpG [246]. In addition, oxidation of guanine to 8-oxo-7,8 dihydroguanine (8-oxoG) in the gene promoter region may serve as an epigenetic mark and provide a platform for then assembly of the transcription machinery [247].

Apart from DNA replication that leads to telomere shortening, ROS can contribute to the shrinkage of telomeres, thereby leading to the induction of senescence. Telomere shortening and chronic inflammation are closely intertwined. Multiple studies have demonstrated that chronic inflammation induces oxidative stress and worsens telomere dysfunction, ultimately triggering cellular senescence [248,249]. Additionally, SIRT-1 plays a role in regulating ROS-dependent FOXO factors, which are involved in cell growth, proliferation, and longevity [250]. Similarly, pharmacological activation of SIRT1 was shown to prevent oxidative stress-induced endothelial dysfunction and senescence acquisition [251]. Based on these observations, it is plausible that age-related increase in ROS contribute to reduced SIRT-1 activity, consequently promoting a senescent-like phenotype. It is important to note that by modulating acetylation/deacetylation SIRT-1 can also elicit antioxidant effects [252,253]. Finally, recent evidence also suggests a novel mechanism of ROS-mediated regulation of epigenetics in which ROS act as nucleophilic compounds to hasten or delay esterification or hydrolysis reactions in cells [252]. Taken together, these observations highlight the intricate crosstalk between cellular redox status, epigenetics and senescence. Clinical implications and therapeutic strategies

Chemotherapeutic drugs such as alkylating agents, anthracyclines, and platinum-based compounds are heavily employed in the management of diverse cancer types. However, due to the lack of selectivity and the occurrence of drug resistance, these agents are often insufficient to eradicate cancers nor result in a progression free survival [254]. Emerging studies have demonstrated a phenomenon of redox resetting amongst cancers which allow cancer cells to acquire redox balance as well as stronger antioxidant mechanisms upon drug treatment [255]. For example, drug-induced ROS can consequentially result in increased glutathione levels which enhances the antioxidant system in cancer [256]. This elevation of glutathione levels supports tumour progression, metastasis and drug resistance [257]. In addition, the expression and activity of drug efflux pumps, as a result of redox homeostasis, allows tumour cells to survive after drug treatment and rendering the inefficacy of several drugs [258,259]. It is evident that redox resetting is a bane to cancer treatment and newer strategies should be imposed to prevent redox resetting from interfering with successful treatment outcomes. On this note, current FDA approved chemotherapeutic agents may still be effective if used correctly in conjunction with agents that prevent a boost in the antioxidant system. Through this approach, cancers may not be able to fall back on the available antioxidant system for survival and the likelihood of successful treatment outcomes would be higher.

Interestingly, accumulation of senescent cells following drug treatment has been shown as a factor in therapy resistance and associated with a significantly lower intracellular ROS levels [260]. Therefore, there has been a heightened interest in designing strategies to selectively target this population to prevent disease relapse. Such class of compounds are termed as senolytics [235,261] such as the Bcl-2/Bcl-xL BH3 mimetic navitoclax (ABT-263) and the combination of TKI inhibitor dasatinib and the flavanol quercetin (DQ) [262,263]. A second class of compounds, termed as senomorphics, target the SASP inflammatory mediators rather than inducing cell death in senescent cells [264]. The use of senolytics in combination with chemotherapy as a “one-two punch’ approach has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy, which leverages on the ability of chemotherapeutic agents to induce senescence followed by their selective elimination with senolytics [265,266]. Notably, epigenetic regulation has been reported as an essential mechanism of the senolytic combination DQ; DQ induced notable alteration in epigenetic markers in the hippocampus, consequently enhancing the cognitive abilities of aged male Wistar rats [267]. In addition, 3 days of DQ treatment reversed hypomethylation status of senescent adipose precursor cells by increasing DNA methylation of ZMAT2, thereby decreasing its expression; ZMAT2 expression increases during senescence [268]. Although these observations do not pertain to cancer, they intriguingly suggest that DQ could potentially influence epigenetic processes to carry out its senolytic activity. While ABT-263 and HSP-90 inhibitors primarily exert their senolytic effects through apoptosis induction and mitochondrial dysfunction, their connection to epigenetic mechanisms requires additional investigation [262,263]. Furthermore, dasatinib and fisetin have also been shown to modulate ROS levels in other context, which eventually leads to cell death [269,270]. Along similar lines, quercetin can increase intracellular ROS in cervical cancer cells to induce apoptosis [271]. Whether the redox modulating property and senolytic activity work in a linear relationship or synergistically to target senescent population remains to be determined.

The redox sensitive transcription factor, NRF2, modulates ROS levels and plays a crucial role in maintaining a permissive redox environment for cell survival and growth in cancer cells [272,273]. NRF2 is negatively regulated by KEAP1 and demethylation of its promoter region by drugs such as decitabine result in reduced levels of NRF2, which consequently impacts oxidative stress response in cancer cells [274,275]. Even at very low doses, the combination therapy of decitabine and cisplatin or gemcitabine showed synergistic killing effects in NSCLCs, thereby suggesting that a combination approach that involves promoting cytotoxicity and epigenetically modulating ROS could be a promising strategy for better treatment outcomes [276]. Similarly, epigenetic therapy using HDAC inhibitors have also shown to sensitize cancers to chemotherapy though the accumulation of ROS. In a recent study domatinostat, a HDAC class I inhibitor, sensitized pancreatic cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as fluoropyrimidines, irinotecan, oxaliplatin, gemcitabine and taxol [277]. The study revealed that domatinostat potentiated the effect of chemotherapy through the accumulation of ROS and stress-induced apoptosis. Additionally, further investigations elucidate the use of domatinostat to alter redox homeostasis in cancer stem cells by downregulating FOXM1, a redox modulating transcription factor. Together, these findings provide ample evidence that targeting the crosstalk between these three critical drivers of carcinogenesis and its progression could have tremendous potential in the therapeutic management of inherent or acquired drug refractory cancers.

Concluding remarks

There is now a preponderance of evidence to support an intricate crosstalk between cellular redox state, epigenetic landscape and senescence phenotype in various pathological states, in particular cancer and age-related disorders. Therefore, several methods have recently emerged to illustrate how epigenetic therapies, redox modulation and senescence regulation have the potential to prevent treatment failure. While these were mostly discussed from the standpoint of post-therapy response, it is plausible that tumours that are inherently resistant due to deregulation of one or more of the three processes might also benefit from the novel therapeutic approaches. For example, targeting the aberrant redox environment or the senescent population within the bulk tumor(s). To that end, altered redox homeostasis is an inherent feature of many human cancers, which could blunt execution signals, specifically those that predominantly rely on targeting the cellular redox milieu. Notably, a recent study provides credence to this by demonstrating that a change in cellular redox homeostasis through targeting iron complexes induces mitochondrial dysfunction and efficient execution of human cancer cells from diverse cancer types [278]. Similarly, approaches to target deregulation of methylation events, such as gene silencing or overexpression are already under clinical evaluation. Undoubtedly, a better understanding of the underlying mechanism(s) of intrinsic therapy resistance will inform why certain cancers are non-responsive to drug treatment, and if the natural factors or genetic/epigenetic alterations that promote intrinsic resistance have a role to play in redox homeostasis, strategies such as utilizing epigenetic and/or redox modifiers, senomorphics and/or senolytics in combination with chemotherapy could open a window of opportunity to eradicate minimal residual diseases and prevent treatment failure/relapse.
