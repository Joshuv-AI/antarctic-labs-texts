Essential Thrombocythemia–Associated Distal Radial Pseudoaneurysm Treated With Thrombin Injection
Authors: Hiroki Saito, Kaito Yamada, Yuta Kagaya, Masanori Kanazawa, Masanobu Miura, Masateru Kondo, Hideaki Endo, Akihiro Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistal radial artery access is associated with a low complication rate and infrequently leads to pseudoaneurysm formation. Consequently, the clinical characteristics of pseudoaneurysms in essential thrombocythemia (ET) are not well characterized.Case SummaryA 79-year-old woman with ET developed a pulsatile wrist mass 2 weeks after coronary angiography via distal radial artery access. Imaging revealed a 17-mm pseudoaneurysm. Conservative compression was unsuccessful. The initial thrombin injection resulted in partial thrombosis with distal escape and re-entry flow. A subsequent injection with manual proximal radial compression achieved complete thrombosis without complications.DiscussionIn ET, qualitative platelet dysfunction may compromise thrombus stability after thrombin injection. Proximal radial compression is crucial in distal radial pseudoaneurysms to prevent distal escape and achieve durable thrombosis.Take-Home MessageBecause ET can compromise thrombus stability, sufficient inflow reduction is essential for successful thrombin injection in distal radial pseudoaneurysms.Key words: complication, coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention

Treatment of dRA Pseudoaneurysm Using Proximal Compression in ET

This illustration summarizes the clinical course of a dRA pseudoaneurysm in a patient with ET. The initial thrombin injection produced transient thrombosis, but the clot lacked sufficient stability and was disrupted by persistent arterial inflow. A second injection, performed with manual proximal radial compression, eliminated inflow and resulted in durable thrombosis with preserved distal perfusion. dRA = distal radial artery; ET = essential thrombocythemia.

History of Presentation

A 79-year-old woman presented with a pulsatile mass at the left wrist. She reported no pain or neurologic symptoms. Two weeks earlier, she had undergone coronary angiography via the left distal transradial artery using a 4-F sheath and was discharged the following day without complications. Physical examination revealed a soft, pulsatile swelling at the puncture site. No signs of infection or distal ischemia were noted.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of essential thrombocythemia (ET), which was treated with hydroxyurea and aspirin. There is no prior history of thrombotic or hemorrhagic events.

Investigations

Ultrasound examination revealed a 17-mm pseudoaneurysm at the distal radial artery (dRA) puncture site, with active inflow visualized on color Doppler imaging (Figure 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography substantiated the presence of a well-defined pseudoaneurysm cavity with arterial communication (Figure 2). Laboratory tests showed an elevated platelet count consistent with ET, but coagulation parameters were within normal limits.

Management

After diagnosing a pseudoaneurysm of the dRA, the patient was admitted for conservative management. After approximately 2 days of compression therapy, this approach failed to resolve the pseudoaneurysm, which remained active on follow-up imaging. Despite ongoing pulsatility of the mass with compression therapy, a heart team discussion was held to evaluate management approaches, including surgical ligation and balloon occlusion to reduce distal outflow. However, surgical access was deemed technically challenging due to the lesion's distal location, and balloon occlusion would have required repuncture of the brachial artery, raising concerns about pseudoaneurysm formation at a second access site. As a result, treatment with ultrasound-guided thrombin injection was planned.

Under ultrasound guidance, 500 U of thrombin were injected in 100-U increments into the pseudoaneurysm cavity, targeting a site distant from the neck. During the injection, some hyperechoic material was observed moving away from the injection site, suggesting incomplete containment of intraluminal contents (Video 1). Distal perfusion remained intact, and no ischemic symptoms developed. Thrombosis was successfully initiated, and the transient cessation of blood flow was confirmed using color Doppler imaging (Video 2). Follow-up imaging conducted 10 minutes later showed that blood flow had re-entered the cavity (Video 3). Although the hyperechoic material migrated distally, dRA flow and digital perfusion remained normal without ischemic symptoms. Because embolic migration would typically result in at least transient flow reduction or clinical ischemia, this finding was interpreted as a washout phenomenon rather than true embolization. Although true embolization could not be completely excluded, adequate inflow reduction was necessary to promote stable thrombus formation within the pseudoaneurysm and to minimize the risk of further distal migration of thrombin or intraluminal contents. Based on this assessment, manual proximal radial artery compression was applied under real-time ultrasound guidance during the second thrombin injection (Video 4). Compression was increased until inflow into the pseudoaneurysm cavity was no longer detectable, ensuring adequate inflow reduction before thrombin administration.

No distal escape was observed during the injection. After the injection, compression was maintained for an additional 30 minutes, resulting in complete cessation of inflow. After the additional 30 minutes of proximal radial compression, Doppler ultrasonography confirmed preserved dRA perfusion, and no digital ischemic symptoms were observed. No recurrence was noted during follow-up.

Discussion

The use of the dRA has become more common in recent years because it has a lower rate of vascular complications than conventional radial artery (cRA) access.1 Notably, the incidence of pseudoaneurysm is reported to be lower with dRA, making it an exceedingly rare complication.2 Although thrombin injection has been increasingly recognized as an effective treatment for pseudoaneurysms after cRA access,3 the optimal management strategy for pseudoaneurysms that result from dRA access remains uncertain. Although there have been isolated reports of mechanical compression4 and thrombin injection5 used for treating dRA pseudoaneurysm, their effectiveness and application in this context, especially in patients with hematologic disorders, have not been clearly defined. To our knowledge, pseudoaneurysm formation in the setting of ET has not been previously described after dRA, cRA, or femoral access. A delayed dRA, such as that observed in this case, appears distinctly rare.

In this case, the patient had ET, a myeloproliferative neoplasm associated with both thrombotic and hemorrhagic tendencies. Patients with ET have an increased risk of arterial thrombosis (11%), venous thrombosis (7%), and bleeding complications (8%).6 These bleeding tendencies reflect qualitative platelet dysfunction that may impair thrombus consolidation at vascular injury sites, even when routine coagulation parameters are normal. In this case, thrombin resistance was unlikely because an initial thrombus formed appropriately. Platelet-fibrin dysfunction in ET can lead to unstable clots, and the recurrence was primarily due to an unstable thrombus that could not withstand the relatively strong arterial inflow. Additionally, ET-related endothelial activation, inflammatory cytokine signaling, and occasional acquired von Willebrand factor abnormalities may compromise access site hemostasis. These abnormalities may have contributed to impaired hemostasis in this individual case; however, a causal relationship cannot be established from a single observation.

This experience suggests that flow-control strategies—such as proximal radial artery compression or balloon occlusion—may improve the effectiveness of thrombin injection for treating dRA pseudoaneurysms, particularly in patients with hematologic conditions. In this case, balloon occlusion was avoided due to concerns about potentially causing a second pseudoaneurysm at the brachial access site.

Follow-up

The patient remained asymptomatic during outpatient follow-up. No recurrence of pseudoaneurysm or local complications was observed in serial ultrasound examinations over 3 months. Her ET remained stable under continued treatment with hydroxyurea and aspirin.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that qualitative platelet dysfunction in ET may compromise thrombus stability after thrombin injection, allowing persistent inflow to disrupt initially formed clots. When treating pseudoaneurysms via the dRA, achieving adequate inflow control is essential for durable hemostasis.
