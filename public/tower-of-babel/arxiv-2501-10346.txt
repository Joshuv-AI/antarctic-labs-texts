Normal forms and geometric structures on Hopf manifolds
Paul Boureau

Abstract
. We prove that every Hopf manifold of dimension n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , primary or secondary, admits a holomorphic ( G , X ) (G,X) -structure compatible with its complex structure, where X = 𝐂 n X=\mathbf{C}^{n} and G G is generated by the translations and the Guysinsky–Katok group of invertible sub-resonant polynomials. This extends to any dimension a result of B. McKay and A. Pokrovskiy, and rests on a self-contained treatment of Berteloot’s approach to the Poincaré–Dulac normal form. We then study, in the body of the paper, to what extent the structure is unique: marked uniqueness fails, since diagonal Hopf manifolds already carry n ! n! pairwise inequivalent structures, but any two compatible structures differ by a global automorphism of 𝐂 n \mathbf{C}^{n} , exactly one structure is aligned, and the compatible structures are classified by a coset space of the sub-resonant group.



1.
Introduction

Hopf manifolds and the sub-resonant group

Hopf manifolds are among the most studied compact complex manifolds carrying no Kähler metric. Following Kodaira
[
Ko
]
, a Hopf manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
is a compact complex manifold whose universal cover is biholomorphic to
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
; it is primary when its fundamental group is infinite cyclic, and secondary otherwise. A primary Hopf manifold is a quotient
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle\gamma\rangle
by a contraction
\gamma
, an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
whose iterates converge to
0
uniformly on compact sets; by Hartogs’ theorem
\gamma
extends to an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing
0
, and it is a contraction exactly when the eigenvalues of
d_{0}\gamma
lie in the punctured unit disc. These manifolds go back to H. Hopf
[
Ho
]
, who introduced the first examples
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle z\mapsto 2z\rangle
; a secondary Hopf manifold is a finite quotient of a primary one, the possible finite groups being classified for surfaces by Kato
[
Ka
]
. These manifolds have been intensively studied, notably as model spaces for locally conformally Kähler and Vaisman metrics; we refer to the recent survey of Istrati and Otiman
[
IO
]
and to the monograph
[
OV
]
for an account and further references.

By the Poincaré–Dulac theorem, revisited in Sections
3
–
4
,
\gamma
is holomorphically conjugate to a polynomial map
h
. Guysinsky and Katok
[
GuKa
]
singled out the relevant class of polynomials: fixing an upper triangular representative
L
of
d_{0}\gamma
, the sub-resonant polynomials relative to
L
are those whose monomials satisfy prescribed inequalities among the moduli of the eigenvalues, and the invertible ones form a finite-dimensional algebraic group
SR^{*}(L)
under composition, into which the normal form
h
falls. We set

X=\mathbf{C}^{n},\qquad G:=\big\langle\,SR^{*}(L),\ \mathbf{C}^{n}\,\big\rangle\subset\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n}),

the group generated by
SR^{*}(L)
and the translations of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

The main theorem

Given a holomorphic homogeneous
G
-space
X
, a
(G,X)
-structure on a manifold is an atlas of
X
-valued charts whose transition maps belong to
G
. McKay and Pokrovskiy
[
McPo
]
constructed such structures on Hopf surfaces; Ornea and Verbitsky
[
OrVe
]
obtained affine structures in the non-resonant case; and Madera
[
Ma
]
recently built flat holomorphic Cartan geometries on every complex Hopf manifold by a different route, compared with ours at the end of this introduction. Our main result extends the existence to all dimensions and to all Hopf manifolds at once.

Theorem 1
.

Every Hopf manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
admits a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure compatible with its complex structure.

The construction is uniform, but the two cases are handled separately in the body. For a primary Hopf manifold, the Poincaré–Dulac normalization identifies
M
with
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
(Section
6
), and the inclusion
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
is a developing map with holonomy
h\in SR^{*}(L)\subset G
; we call the resulting structure the canonical structure. For a secondary manifold
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\Gamma
, the deck group
\Gamma
is a finite extension of a cyclic contraction group
\langle h\rangle
, and we show in Section
11
that
\Gamma
lies entirely in
SR^{*}(L)
— because
h
turns out to be central in
\Gamma
— so that the same inclusion is again a developing map.

Non-uniqueness

The canonical structure is far from unique, and the failure is already visible in the simplest case. Let
M=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle L\rangle
with
L=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
,
0<|\lambda_{1}|<\dots<|\lambda_{n}|<1
. For each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
the permutation matrix
P_{\sigma}
is a developing map, with holonomy
P_{\sigma}LP_{\sigma}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})
; this is again a diagonal matrix, hence lies in
SR^{*}(L)
, since the sub-resonance condition constrains the positions of the nonzero coefficients and not their values. The
n!
resulting structures are pairwise inequivalent (Example
7.1
and Section
10
).

The body of the paper analyses this non-uniqueness for
n\geq 2
. Section
7
shows that every developing map extends to a global automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
, so that two compatible structures differ by a global automorphism of the model. Sections
8
–
9
single out a distinguished aligned structure and prove it to be the unique one of its kind, and Section
10
classifies all compatible structures by a coset space
SR^{*}(L)\backslash\mathcal{N}(h)
. In dimension one the situation is altogether different: a one-dimensional Hopf manifold is an elliptic curve, and it carries a one-parameter family of affine
(G,X)
-structures, so that no uniqueness holds (this is classical, see Gunning
[
Gu
]
); we therefore restrict to
n\geq 2
throughout.

A detailed comparison with the recent work of Madera
[
Ma
]
, which reaches closely related results by a converse route, is given in Section
12
at the end of the paper.

Section
3
presents sub-resonance type polynomials in a block formulation valid for an arbitrary invertible contracting linear part, and establishes the structural results used throughout (block triangular form, characterization of linear sub-resonant maps, degree bound). Section
4
contains the revisited proof of the Poincaré–Dulac theorem. Section
5
constructs the model group
G
. Section
6
establishes the global dynamics of contracting sub-resonant maps, identifies the normalized model, and proves the existence theorem for primary manifolds. Section
7
proves the rigidity of developing maps (Proposition
7.2
). Section
8
introduces the holonomy flag and the alignment condition. Section
9
proves the uniqueness of the aligned structure (Proposition
9.4
). Section
10
classifies compatible structures (Proposition
10.1
) and shows that non-uniqueness persists modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
. Section
11
treats secondary Hopf manifolds and completes the proof of Theorem
1
. Finally, Section
12
compares our results and methods with the recent work of Madera.

Throughout, we have sought to keep the prerequisites to a minimum and to give the arguments in full detail: apart from the normalization procedure of Berteloot, the text is essentially self-contained, and we have favoured completeness of the proofs over brevity.

I sincerely thank Bertrand Deroin, who introduced me to this problem and was of great help through his exceptional availability. I also extend my heartfelt thanks to Matthieu Madera.

2.
Holomorphic
(G,X)-
structures

We briefly review the notion of
(G,X)-
structure introduced by W. Thurston
[
Th
]
. Curious readers may refer to
[
Go
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a complex Lie group acting transitively on a manifold
X
. A
(G,X)
-structure on a manifold
M
is an atlas
\{(U_{i},\phi_{i})\}_{i\in I}
with values in
X
such that for every
i,j\in I
, the transition map
\phi_{i}\circ\phi_{j}^{-1}
is the restriction of an element of
G
.

Remark 2.2
.

If
G
is a complex Lie group and the action of
G
on
X
is holomorphic, then any
(G,X)
-structure on a manifold
M
naturally inherits a complex structure, as the transition maps are holomorphic. Such a
(G,X)
-structure will be called a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure. If
M
is already a complex manifold, we say that a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure on
M
is compatible if the complex structure it induces coincides with the given one; equivalently, if its charts are biholomorphisms onto their images.

We now recall a general recipe that provides a canonical way to construct
(G,X)
-structures on quotient spaces under proper and free actions. It encompasses many classical examples, including the structures that will be constructed in this article, which are all of this type.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group acting transitively on a manifold
X
. Let
U\subseteq X
be an open subset and
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
G
such that the action of
\Gamma
on
U
is free and properly discontinuous. Then the quotient manifold
U/\Gamma
inherits a
(G,X)
-structure.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the
1
-dimensional Hopf manifold defined by
M=\mathbf{C}^{*}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma=\langle\gamma\rangle
is the cyclic group generated by the dilation
\gamma(z)=2z
.

In this case, we set
X=\mathbf{C}
and
G=\text{Aff}(\mathbf{C})
, the group of complex affine transformations
z\mapsto az+b
. Let
U=\mathbf{C}^{*}\subset X
. The action of
\Gamma
on
U
is free and properly discontinuous. Since
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
G
and the action is holomorphic, the quotient
M
inherits a complex
(G,X)
-structure from
X
and is diffeomorphic to a real
2
-torus.

Proposition
2.3
is the mechanism behind all the structures constructed in this paper: for a primary Hopf manifold we shall take
\Gamma=\langle h\rangle
(Section
6
), and for a secondary one the full deck group
\Gamma
of the primary cover (Section
11
); in each case
\Gamma
will be shown to sit inside
G
.

2.1.
Developing map, holonomy and equivalence

Let
M
be a manifold equipped with a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure, and let
\pi:\widetilde{M}\to M
be its universal cover. It is a classical fact (see
[
Go
,
Th
]
) that the structure is encoded by a developing pair
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
: a local biholomorphism

\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits:\widetilde{M}\longrightarrow X

and a morphism
\rho:\pi_{1}(M)\to G
satisfying the equivariance relation
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\circ\gamma=\rho(\gamma)\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits
for every deck transformation
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(M)
. Such a pair is not canonically attached to the structure: it is well defined only up to the action of
G
by

(2.1)

g\cdot(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho):=(g\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\ g\,\rho\,g^{-1}),\qquad g\in G,

and conversely every equivariant pair arises from a
(G,X)
-structure. A
(G,X)
-structure is thus the same thing as a
G
-orbit of developing pairs; we call the pairs representatives of the structure, and we say a structure is compatible (with a given complex structure on
M
) when its developing map is a local biholomorphism for that complex structure, a property independent of the representative.

Definition 2.5

(marked equivalence)
.

Two developing pairs
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
and
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
on the same complex manifold
M
(with the same marking of
\pi_{1}(M)
) are equivalent if there exists
g_{0}\in G
with

\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime}=g_{0}\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho^{\prime}=g_{0}\,\rho\,g_{0}^{-1},

that is, if they lie in the same
G
-orbit under (
2.1
). A
(G,X)
-structure is an equivalence class for this relation. Throughout, we denote a structure by
\mathcal{S}
and write
\mathcal{S}=[\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho]
when
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
is a representative.

Remark 2.6
.

In our situation the elements of
G
are polynomial maps of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
(Section
5
); by the identity principle, an element of
G
is determined by its restriction to any nonempty open subset of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
. The second condition in Definition
2.5
then follows from the first, since
\rho(\gamma)=\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\circ\gamma\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{-1}
on a nonempty open set; a representative is therefore determined by its developing map alone.

3.
Sub-resonance type polynomials

In this section, we present the sub-resonance type polynomials introduced by Guysinsky and Katok in
[
GuKa
]
, in a block formulation valid for an arbitrary invertible contracting linear part.

Let
L\in M_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be an upper triangular invertible matrix whose eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, counted with multiplicity, satisfy

0<|\lambda_{1}|\leq\dots\leq|\lambda_{n}|<1.

Let
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\dots<\mu_{l}
denote the distinct moduli of the eigenvalues, and for
1\leq i\leq l
set

E_{i}:=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|=\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}(L),

where
E^{\lambda}(L)
is the characteristic space of
L
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
, so that

\mathbf{C}^{n}=E_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{l}.

We also introduce the modulus flag of
L
,

V_{\bullet}:\qquad 0=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\dots\subset V_{l}=\mathbf{C}^{n},\qquad V_{i}:=E_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus E_{i}=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}(L),

of signature
d(L):=(\dim V_{1},\dots,\dim V_{l})
. More generally, for any invertible matrix
B
whose eigenvalues have moduli among
\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{l}\}
, we write
\mathcal{F}(B)
for its modulus flag,
\mathcal{F}(B)_{i}:=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}(B)
.

We fix once and for all an adapted basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
: a Jordan basis of
L
ordered by non-decreasing moduli. In this basis
L
is upper triangular with diagonal entries
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, and the basis is adapted to the decomposition
\mathbf{C}^{n}=E_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus E_{l}
. We write vectors
z=(t_{1},\dots,t_{l})
in blocks according to this decomposition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
be a homogeneous polynomial map fixing
0
. We say that it is of type
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})
if for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{l}\in\mathbf{C}
and
(t_{1},\dots,t_{l})\in E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{l}
, we have:

P(a_{1}t_{1}+\cdots+a_{l}t_{l})=a_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{l}}P(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{l}).

Since a polynomial map is a sum of homogeneous terms, we can introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

A polynomial map
P:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing
0
is said to be of sub-resonance type with respect to
L
if each component
P_{i}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to E_{i}
consists only of homogeneous terms of type
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})
satisfying

(3.1)

\ln\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}.

The notions of homogeneous term and type are invariant under linear coordinate changes preserving the decomposition
\mathbf{C}^{n}=E_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus E_{l}
; it follows that the space of sub-resonance type polynomials does not depend on a coordinate system, but only on this decomposition and on the moduli
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{l}
. We denote by
SR(L)
the set of sub-resonance type polynomials with respect to
L
, and by
SR_{k}(L)
the subspace of those that are homogeneous of degree
k
(all types satisfying
\sum_{j}s_{j}=k
). When the moduli are pairwise distinct,
l=n
and one recovers the definition of
[
GuKa
]
.

Remark 3.3

(monomial criterion)
.

In the adapted coordinates, write
z^{I}:=z_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{i_{n}}
for a multi-index
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
, and
H_{I,j}:=z^{I}e_{j}
. For
1\leq k\leq n
let
b(k)\in\{1,\dots,l\}
be the block of the coordinate
k
, so that
|\lambda_{k}|=\mu_{b(k)}
. The monomial map
H_{I,j}
is homogeneous of type
s(I)
, where
s_{b}(I):=\sum_{k:\,b(k)=b}i_{k}
, and it is sub-resonant if and only if

\ln\mu_{b(j)}\ \leq\ \sum_{b}s_{b}(I)\ln\mu_{b}\ =\ \sum_{k}i_{k}\ln|\lambda_{k}|.

The following structural lemma will be used constantly.

Lemma 3.4

(block triangular form)
.

Let
g\in SR(L)
, written
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{l})
in blocks. Then for every
i
,

g_{i}(t_{1},\dots,t_{l})\ =\ C_{i}\,t_{i}\ +\ Q_{i}(t_{i+1},\dots,t_{l}),

where
C_{i}\in\mathrm{End}(E_{i})
is linear and
Q_{i}
is a polynomial map without constant term. Moreover every type
s
occurring in
g
satisfies

\sum_{j}s_{j}\ \leq\ \frac{\ln\mu_{1}}{\ln\mu_{l}}\ =\ \frac{\ln|\lambda_{1}|}{\ln|\lambda_{n}|}.

Proof.

Let
\beta
be a term of
g_{i}
of type
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{l})\neq 0
. Since
\ln\mu_{j}<0
for all
j
:

(a)
s_{j}=0
for
j<i
. Otherwise
\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}\leq s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}\leq\ln\mu_{j}<\ln\mu_{i}
, contradicting (
3.1
).

(b) if
s_{i}\geq 1
then
s=e_{i}
. If
s_{i}\geq 2
then
\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}\leq 2\ln\mu_{i}<\ln\mu_{i}
; if
s_{i}=1
and
s_{j}\geq 1
for some
j>i
(indices
j<i
being excluded by (a)), then
\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}\leq\ln\mu_{i}+\ln\mu_{j}<\ln\mu_{i}
. In both cases (
3.1
) is violated.

Thus
g_{i}
is the sum of a term of type
e_{i}
, linear in
t_{i}
and independent of the other variables, and of terms of types
s
with
s_{j}=0
for
j\leq i
, i.e. depending only on
(t_{i+1},\dots,t_{l})
.

For the degree bound: since
\ln\mu_{j}\leq\ln\mu_{l}<0
,

\ln\mu_{1}\leq\ln\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}\leq\Big(\sum_{j}s_{j}\Big)\ln\mu_{l},

whence
\sum_{j}s_{j}\leq\ln\mu_{1}/\ln\mu_{l}
.
∎

We set

c_{0}(L):=\left\lceil\frac{\ln|\lambda_{1}|}{\ln|\lambda_{n}|}\right\rceil.

Corollary 3.5
.

Every element of
SR(L)
has degree at most
c_{0}(L)
, and
SR_{k}(L)=\{0\}
for
k>c_{0}(L)
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
g\in SR(L)
. Then:

(1)

for every
z\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
and every
i
,
Dg(z)(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i}
;

(2)

a linear map
A
is sub-resonant with respect to
L
if and only if
A(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i}
for every
i
. Consequently, the set of linear parts
\{Dg(0):g\in SR^{*}(L)\}
is exactly the parabolic subgroup

P(V_{\bullet}):=\{A\in GL_{n}(\mathbf{C})\ :\ A(V_{i})=V_{i}\ \ \forall i\},

where
SR^{*}(L)
denotes the set of elements of
SR(L)
with invertible derivative at the origin.

Proof.

(1) By Lemma
3.4
, the Jacobian matrix of
g
at any point is block upper triangular with respect to the decomposition
\mathbf{C}^{n}=\bigoplus_{i}E_{i}
:
\partial g_{i}/\partial t_{j}=0
for
j<i
. Such a matrix maps
V_{k}=E_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus E_{k}
into itself.

(2) The direct implication is (1) applied to
A
(or Lemma
3.4
directly). Conversely, if
A(V_{j})\subseteq V_{j}
for all
j
, the component
A_{i}
of
A
only involves the variables
t_{j}
with
j\geq i
; the corresponding types
e_{j}
,
j\geq i
, satisfy
\ln\mu_{i}\leq\ln\mu_{j}
, hence (
3.1
). Finally, any
A\in P(V_{\bullet})
is itself a (linear) element of
SR^{*}(L)
, and any linear part of an element of
SR^{*}(L)
lies in
P(V_{\bullet})
by (1) together with invertibility and a dimension count.
∎

Guysinsky and Katok proved that
SR^{*}(L)
is a finite-dimensional algebraic group under composition.

Theorem 3.7

(M. Guysinsky and A. Katok)
.

SR^{*}(L)
forms a finite-dimensional algebraic group under composition.

Proof.

Let us first show that this set is closed under composition. Let
F=(F_{1},...,F_{l})
and
G=(G_{1},...,G_{l})
be two sub-resonant polynomials. We have
F\circ G=(F_{1}\circ G,...,F_{l}\circ G)
, and for
1\leq i\leq l
, we must show that the types of the homogeneous terms of
F_{i}\circ G
are sub-resonant. Let
Q_{i}
be a homogeneous term of
F_{i}
. There exists
(s_{1},...,s_{l})
satisfying
\ln\mu_{i}\leq\displaystyle\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}
such that for all
a_{1},...,a_{l}\in\mathbf{C}
and
(t_{1},...,t_{l})\in E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{l}
,

Q_{i}(a_{1}t_{1}+...+a_{l}t_{l})=a_{1}^{s_{1}}...a_{l}^{s_{l}}Q_{i}(t_{1}+...+t_{l}).

Now let
R_{j}
be a homogeneous term of
G_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq l
. Then there exists
(s_{1}^{j},...,s_{l}^{j})
satisfying
\ln\mu_{j}\leq\displaystyle\sum_{k}s_{k}^{j}\ln\mu_{k}
such that for all
a_{1},...,a_{l}\in\mathbf{C}
and
(t_{1},...,t_{l})\in E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{l}
,

R_{j}(a_{1}t_{1}+...+a_{l}t_{l})=a_{1}^{s_{1}^{j}}...a_{l}^{s_{l}^{j}}R_{j}(t_{1}+...+t_{l}).

Thus, for
a_{1},...,a_{l}\in\mathbf{C}
and
(t_{1},...,t_{l})\in E_{1}\times\cdots\times E_{l}
, we have:

\displaystyle Q_{i}\left(\sum_{j}R_{j}(a_{1}t_{1}+...+a_{l}t_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=Q_{i}\left(\sum_{j}a_{1}^{s_{1}^{j}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{l}^{j}}R_{j}(t_{1}+...+t_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}^{s_{1}^{1}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{l}^{1}}\right)^{s_{1}}\cdots\left(a_{1}^{s_{1}^{l}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{l}^{l}}\right)^{s_{l}}Q_{i}\left(\sum_{j}R_{j}(t_{1}+...+t_{l})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}^{s_{1}s_{1}^{1}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{1}s_{l}^{1}}\right)\cdots\left(a_{1}^{s_{l}s_{1}^{l}}\cdots a_{l}^{s_{l}s_{l}^{l}}\right)Q_{i}\left(\sum_{j}R_{j}(t_{1}+...+t_{l})\right).

It remains to verify that

\ln\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{k}\sum_{j}s_{k}^{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{k}.

We have
\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{j}s_{k}^{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{k}=\sum_{j}\sum_{k}s_{k}^{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{k}
, and
\ln\mu_{j}\leq\displaystyle\sum_{k}s_{k}^{j}\ln\mu_{k}
for all
j
because
R_{j}
is sub-resonant. Thus:

\sum_{k}\sum_{j}s_{k}^{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{k}\geq\sum_{j}s_{j}\ln\mu_{j}.

The result follows from the sub-resonant nature of
Q_{i}
. Therefore,
SR^{*}(L)
is closed under composition. Since this operation is polynomial in the coefficients, it is algebraic.

By Corollary
3.6
, the linear part of an element
F\in SR^{*}(L)
preserves the flag
V_{\bullet}
, hence so does its inverse, which is therefore also a sub-resonant polynomial.

Now let us show that sub-resonant polynomials relative to
L
with invertible derivative at
0
are invertible under composition and that their inverse is also sub-resonant. Let
F
be such a map. By composing on the right with the inverse
P_{1}
of the derivative of
F
at
0
, we obtain a map
F_{1}=Id+S_{2}+S_{>2}
, where
S_{2}
is a sum of homogeneous polynomials of degree
2
and
S_{>2}
is a sum of terms of strictly higher degrees. By construction, the sum of two sub-resonant polynomials relative to
L
is a sub-resonant polynomial relative to
L
. Thus, the polynomial
P_{2}:=Id-S_{2}\in SR^{*}(L)
. By composing
F_{1}
with
P_{2}
on the right, we obtain the polynomial
F_{2}:=F_{1}\circ P_{2}=Id-S_{2}+S_{2}+S_{>2}=Id+S_{3}+S_{>3}
. According to Corollary
3.5
, the degree of the maps involved is bounded by
c_{0}(L)
. It follows that there exists an integer
m
such that, continuing the previous construction, we have
F_{m}=Id
.

Thus, we have shown that
F
is invertible and

F^{-1}=P_{1}\circ\cdots\circ P_{m},

where each
P_{i}\in SR^{*}(L)
. It follows that
F^{-1}\in SR^{*}(L)
. The operation associating a polynomial in
SR^{*}(L)
to its inverse is polynomial in the coefficients, hence it is algebraic.

Finally,
SR^{*}(L)
is indeed a finite-dimensional algebraic group, the dimension being finite by the bound on the degree of sub-resonant polynomials.

∎

Remark 3.8
.

The computation at the beginning of the proof of Theorem
3.7
does not use the invertibility of the derivatives: the space
SR(L)
itself is stable under sums, under extraction of homogeneous components, and under composition. This will be used repeatedly in Sections
6
–
10
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Every element of
SR^{*}(L)
is a polynomial automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing the origin, whose inverse is again a sub-resonant polynomial. The identities
F\circ F^{-1}=F^{-1}\circ F=\mathrm{id}
, being polynomial, therefore hold on all of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

4.
A theorem of Poincaré and Dulac

In this section, we provide a revisited proof of the well-known Poincaré–Dulac normal form theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
be a holomorphic map whose linear part at the origin
L
is invertible and contracting. There exists a polynomial
P
and a local biholomorphism
\phi
such that
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=\text{Id}
, and
\phi^{-1}\circ F\circ\phi=P
.

We base our work on the proof by Berteloot in
[
Be
]
. Given a holomorphic transformation
F:B_{r}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
, where
B_{r}
denotes the ball of radius
r
centered at the origin in
\mathbf{C}^{n}
, it can be expanded in the form
F=\sum_{p\geq 1}H_{p}
, where
H_{p}
is a homogeneous polynomial map of degree
p
. We then formally construct a local biholomorphism by composing transformations designed to eliminate the term of a certain degree in this decomposition. This method requires studying the convergence of the formally constructed coordinate change.

In this section, all these convergence issues are resolved by the following proposition, derived from
[
Be
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
N
be an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing the origin, and let its linear part
L:=N^{\prime}(0)
satisfy
a\|z\|\leq\|L(z)\|\leq A\|z\|
for some
0<a\leq A<1
.

Let
F:B_{r}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
be a holomorphic map such that

F=N+\sum_{p\geq k}H_{p},

where
H_{p}
is a homogeneous polynomial map of degree
p
.

Then, if
k>\frac{\ln a}{\ln A}
, the sequence
(N^{-p}\circ F^{p})_{p}
converges, and its limit defines a local biholomorphism
\phi
such that
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=\text{Id}
, and
\phi^{-1}\circ F\circ\phi=N
.

Consider a holomorphic map
F=L+\sum_{p\geq 2}H_{p}
, where
L
is as in Section
3
. We work in the adapted coordinate system fixed there, in which
L
is upper triangular.

Definition 4.3
.

We denote by
\mathcal{H}^{p}
the
\mathbf{C}
-vector space of
p
-homogeneous polynomial maps from
\mathbf{C}^{n}
to
\mathbf{C}^{n}
. We equip
\mathcal{H}^{p}
with the canonical basis
\mathcal{B}^{p}:=\{H_{I,j}=z^{I}e_{j},\ |I|=p,\ 1\leq j\leq n\}
of Remark
3.3
.

Definition 4.4
.

Any holomorphic transformation of the form
\sum_{p\geq k+1}h_{p}
where
h_{p}\in\mathcal{H}^{p}
will also be denoted by
o(k)
.

To apply Proposition
4.2
, we aim to reduce the problem to a map of the form
N+o(k)
, where
k
satisfies the hypotheses of the proposition; any integer strictly greater than
c_{0}(L)
will do, for a suitable choice of norm.

The proof then consists of cancelling the non-sub-resonant terms of degree greater than or equal to
2
by successively conjugating
F
with local biholomorphisms of the form
\psi_{p}:=I+h_{p}
, where
h_{p}\in\mathcal{H}^{p}
for
p=2,\dots,c_{0}(L)+1
. Let us first specify the effect of such a conjugation:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\psi:=I+h
, where
h\in\mathcal{H}^{q}
. Assume
F=L+S_{q-1}+H_{q}+o(q)
, where
S_{q-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{H}^{q-1}
and
H_{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{q}
. Then

\psi^{-1}\circ F\circ\psi=L+S_{q-1}+\big[H_{q}+L\circ h-h\circ L\big]+o(q).

The following family of operators appears:

M_{L}^{q}:\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{H}^{q}&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{H}^{q}\\[3.0pt]
h&\longmapsto&h\circ L-L\circ h.\end{array}\right.

The invertibility of
M_{L}^{q}
in the previous proposition would allow us to conjugate
F
into a holomorphic transformation without terms of degree
q
; in general only the non-sub-resonant part of degree
q
can be cancelled, as we now explain.

The central idea for studying these operators is the following.

Proposition 4.6
.

M_{L}^{q}
is triangular on a reordering of
\mathcal{B}^{q}
.

Proof.

Indeed, for
|I|=q
and
1\leq j\leq n
, writing
L=(l_{t,k})
(upper triangular), we have:

\displaystyle M_{L}^{q}(H_{I,j})(z_{1},...,z_{n})

\displaystyle=(H_{I,j}\circ L)(z_{1},...,z_{n})-(L\circ H_{I,j})(z_{1},...,z_{n})

\displaystyle=H_{I,j}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=t}^{n}l_{t,k}z_{k}\right)e_{t}\right)-z^{I}Le_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}l_{1,k}z_{k}\right)^{i_{1}}\cdots\left(l_{n,n}z_{n}\right)^{i_{n}}e_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}l_{i,j}H_{I,i}(z_{1},...,z_{n})

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{I}-\lambda_{j})H_{I,j}(z_{1},...,z_{n})+\left(\sum_{I^{\prime}}\alpha_{I^{\prime}}z^{I^{\prime}}\right)e_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}l_{i,j}H_{I,i}(z_{1},...,z_{n})

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{I}-\lambda_{j})H_{I,j}(z_{1},...,z_{n})+\sum_{I^{\prime}}\alpha_{I^{\prime}}H_{I^{\prime},j}(z_{1},...,z_{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}l_{i,j}H_{I,i}(z_{1},...,z_{n})

We define an order on the multi-indices such that
I^{\prime}\ll I
if
i_{n}^{\prime}>i_{n}
, or if
i_{n}^{\prime}=i_{n}
and
i_{n-1}^{\prime}>i_{n-1}
, and so on. This order ensures that all multi-indices appearing in the sum
\sum_{I^{\prime}}\alpha_{I^{\prime}}H_{I^{\prime},j}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
are strictly less than
I
. We naturally extend this order to the pairs
(I,j)
by
(I^{\prime},j^{\prime})\ll(I,j)
if
I^{\prime}\ll I
, or
I^{\prime}=I
and
j^{\prime}<j
.

We have thus shown that
M_{L}^{q}
is upper triangular in this reordering of
\mathcal{B}^{q}
.
∎

These operators behave very well with respect to the space of sub-resonant polynomials relative to
L
. Let us first establish the following:

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
r
:

(1)

SR_{r}(L)
is stable under
M_{L}^{r}
.

(2)

The pre-images of a polynomial in
SR_{r}(L)
under
M_{L}^{r}
are all sub-resonant.

Proof.

(1) Fix
r
. Let
h
be a polynomial of degree
r
that is sub-resonant relative to
L
. It suffices to note that
M_{L}^{r}(h)=h\circ L-L\circ h
is a sum of compositions of sub-resonant maps, hence sub-resonant by Theorem
3.7
and Remark
3.8
(stability of
SR(L)
under composition and sums). We used the fact that
L
is sub-resonant relative to itself, which follows from Corollary
3.6
since
L
preserves
V_{\bullet}
.

(2) To show the second point, consider
h=\sum_{(I,j)}c_{I,j}H_{I,j}
, a polynomial that is not sub-resonant relative to
L
. Then, there necessarily exists
(I,j)
such that
c_{I,j}\neq 0
and
H_{I,j}
is not sub-resonant relative to
L
. Let
(I_{0},j_{0})
be maximal for the order of Proposition
4.6
satisfying the previous condition. We have:

M_{L}^{r}(h)=c_{I_{0},j_{0}}(\lambda^{I_{0}}-\lambda_{j_{0}})H_{I_{0},j_{0}}+\sum_{(I,j)\neq(I_{0},j_{0})}c_{I,j}M_{L}^{r}(H_{I,j})+R,

where

R=c_{I_{0},j_{0}}M_{L}^{r}(H_{I_{0},j_{0}})-c_{I_{0},j_{0}}(\lambda^{I_{0}}-\lambda_{j_{0}})H_{I_{0},j_{0}}.

We claim that
\lambda^{I_{0}}\neq\lambda_{j_{0}}
: otherwise, taking moduli,
\ln\mu_{b(j_{0})}=\ln|\lambda_{j_{0}}|=\sum_{k}i_{k}\ln|\lambda_{k}|
, so that
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
would be sub-resonant by the monomial criterion of Remark
3.3
. Thus, the term
c_{I_{0},j_{0}}(\lambda^{I_{0}}-\lambda_{j_{0}})H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
is not sub-resonant relative to
L
. According to Proposition
4.6
, the remainder
R
does not contain any component in
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
.

By Proposition
4.6
again, if
H_{I,j}
is not sub-resonant, then
M_{L}^{r}(H_{I,j})
does not have a component in
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
, due to the maximality of
(I_{0},j_{0})
. Finally, from the first point of the lemma, if
H_{I,j}
is sub-resonant, then
M_{L}^{r}(H_{I,j})
is also sub-resonant and hence has no component in
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
. Thus,
\sum_{(I,j)\neq(I_{0},j_{0})}c_{I,j}M_{L}^{r}(H_{I,j})
has no component in
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
.

As a result,
M_{L}^{r}(h)
has a non-zero component in
H_{I_{0},j_{0}}
, and therefore
M_{L}^{r}(h)
is not sub-resonant relative to
L
, which concludes the second point.

∎

It follows the

Proposition 4.8
.

For any
r
, we have

SR_{r}(L)+\mathrm{Im}(M_{L}^{r})=\mathcal{H}^{r}.

Proof.

According to the previous lemma, since the zero polynomial is clearly sub-resonant,
\ker(M_{L}^{r})
is included in
SR_{r}(L)
. By iterating the lemma, we have that
E^{0}(M_{L}^{r})
, the characteristic subspace of
M_{L}^{r}
associated with the eigenvalue
0
, is included in
SR_{r}(L)
. It is clear that a characteristic subspace of an endomorphism associated with a non-zero eigenvalue is included in its image. Thus, using

\mathcal{H}^{r}=E^{0}(M_{L}^{r})\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\neq 0}E^{\lambda}(M_{L}^{r}),

we obtain the proposition.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the following:

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
F:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
be a holomorphic map whose linear part at the origin
L
is invertible and contracting. There exists a polynomial
h\in SR^{*}(L)
with
h^{\prime}(0)=L
and a local biholomorphism
\phi
such that
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi^{\prime}(0)=\mathrm{Id}
, and
\phi^{-1}\circ F\circ\phi=h
.

Proof.

We write
F=L+H_{2}+o(2)
where
H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
. According to Proposition
4.8
, there exist
h_{2}\in SR_{2}(L)
and
f_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
such that
h_{2}+M_{L}^{2}(f_{2})=H_{2}
. Applying Proposition
4.5
to the local biholomorphism
\psi_{2}=\mathrm{Id}+f_{2}
, we obtain:

F_{2}=\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ F\circ\psi_{2}=L+h_{2}+H_{3}+o(3),

where
H_{3}\in\mathcal{H}^{3}
. Iterating this process, for every
3\leq r\leq c_{0}(L)+1
, there exists a polynomial
h_{r}\in SR_{r}(L)
such that conjugation by the local biholomorphism
\psi_{r}=\mathrm{Id}+f_{r}
given by Propositions
4.8
and
4.5
yields:

F_{r}=\psi_{r}^{-1}\circ F_{r-1}\circ\psi_{r}=L+h_{2}+\dots+h_{r}+H_{r+1}+o(r+1).

Note that
h_{c_{0}(L)+1}=0
automatically, since
SR_{c_{0}(L)+1}(L)=\{0\}
by Corollary
3.5
.

Set
h:=L+h_{2}+\dots+h_{c_{0}(L)}
. By construction
h
is a sub-resonant polynomial relative to
L
whose derivative at the origin is
L
, which is invertible; hence
h\in SR^{*}(L)
, and
h
is an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
by Corollary
3.9
. Moreover
F_{c_{0}(L)+1}=h+o(c_{0}(L)+1)
. Since
c_{0}(L)+2>\ln|\lambda_{1}|/\ln|\lambda_{n}|
, we may choose a norm on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
and constants
0<a\leq A<1
with
a\|z\|\leq\|Lz\|\leq A\|z\|
and
\ln a/\ln A<c_{0}(L)+2
. Proposition
4.2
, applied to
N=h
and
k=c_{0}(L)+2
, provides a local biholomorphism tangent to the identity conjugating
F_{c_{0}(L)+1}
to
h
; composing all the conjugations gives the desired
\phi
.
∎

5.
The model group

Throughout the rest of the paper,
M=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle\gamma\rangle
is a primary Hopf manifold:
\gamma
is a contraction, that is, an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing
0
whose iterates converge to
0
uniformly on compact sets. Let
L
denote an upper triangular representative of
d_{0}\gamma
, with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
as in Section
3
, and let
h\in SR^{*}(L)
be the Poincaré–Dulac normal form of
\gamma
provided by Theorem
4.9
. By Corollary
3.9
,
h
is a polynomial automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
; Section
6
identifies
M
with the normalized model
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
.

Let us prove the following:

Proposition 5.1
.

G:=\langle SR^{*}(L),\mathbf{C}^{n}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{C}^{n}
acts by translation, is an algebraic group of finite dimension.

Proof.

For
\tau\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
, let
t_{\tau}
denote the map
z\mapsto z+\tau
. We begin by proving

Lemma 5.2
.

G=\{t_{\tau}\circ h\mid\tau\in\mathbf{C}^{n},h\in SR^{*}(L)\}.

Proof.

We need to verify that for every
(\tau,h)\in\mathbf{C}^{n}\times SR^{*}(L)
,

t_{-h(\tau)}\circ h\circ t_{\tau}\in SR^{*}(L).

The group structure of
SR^{*}(L)
ensures the invertibility of the derivative at
0
. Let us demonstrate the sub-resonant nature of the result. To this end, note that for a fixed
\tau
, the map
h\mapsto t_{-h(\tau)}\circ h\circ t_{\tau}
, that is
h\mapsto h(\cdot+\tau)-h(\tau)
, is linear in
h
. Since in the adapted coordinates every element of
SR(L)
is a linear combination of sub-resonant monomials
H_{I,j}
(Remark
3.3
), it suffices to verify the condition for such monomials.

We have:

t_{-H_{I,j}(\tau)}\circ H_{I,j}\circ t_{\tau}(z)=\big((z_{1}+\tau_{1})^{i_{1}}\cdots(z_{n}+\tau_{n})^{i_{n}}-\tau_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\tau_{n}^{i_{n}}\big)\,e_{j}.

Expanding, the homogeneous terms of
t_{-H_{I,j}(\tau)}\circ H_{I,j}\circ t_{\tau}
are scalar multiples of the monomials
z^{J}e_{j}
with
J\leq I
componentwise and
J\neq 0
. Each of them is sub-resonant: by Remark
3.3
and the sub-resonance of
H_{I,j}
,

\ln\mu_{b(j)}\leq\sum_{k}i_{k}\ln|\lambda_{k}|\leq\sum_{k}j_{k}\ln|\lambda_{k}|,

where the second inequality holds because
j_{k}\leq i_{k}
and
\ln|\lambda_{k}|<0
for every
k
.
∎

We can now explicitly describe the group law of
G
. Let
(\tau_{1},h_{1})
and
(\tau_{2},h_{2})
be two elements of
G
. We have:

(\tau_{1},h_{1})\cdot(\tau_{2},h_{2})=t_{\tau_{1}}\circ h_{1}\circ t_{\tau_{2}}\circ h_{2},

=t_{\tau_{1}}\circ t_{-\alpha}\circ t_{\alpha}\circ h_{1}\circ t_{\tau_{2}}\circ h_{2},

=t_{\tau_{1}-\alpha}\circ h_{3},

=(\tau_{1}-\alpha,h_{3}),

where
\alpha=-h_{1}(\tau_{2})
and
h_{3}=t_{\alpha}\circ h_{1}\circ t_{\tau_{2}}\circ h_{2}
. By the lemma and the group structure of
SR^{*}(L)
,
h_{3}\in SR^{*}(L)
.

From the previous calculation, and since
(t_{\tau}\circ h)^{-1}=h^{-1}\circ t_{-\tau}
, we obtain:

(\tau,h)^{-1}=\big(h^{-1}(-\tau),\ t_{-h^{-1}(-\tau)}\circ h^{-1}\circ t_{-\tau}\big).

We observe that these relations are algebraic in
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
,
h_{1}
, and
h_{2}
.
∎

The following complement to Lemma
5.2
will be used constantly in the study of uniqueness.

Lemma 5.3
.

Every
g_{0}\in G
can be written in a unique way as
g_{0}=t_{\tau}\circ s
with
\tau\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
and
s\in SR^{*}(L)
; moreover
\tau=g_{0}(0)
. Consequently:

(1)

\mathop{\rm Stab}_{G}(0)=SR^{*}(L)
;

(2)

for every
g_{0}=t_{\tau}\circ s\in G
and every
x\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
,
Dg_{0}(x)=Ds(x)
preserves the flag
V_{\bullet}
.

Proof.

Existence is Lemma
5.2
. For uniqueness:
s(0)=0
, so
(t_{\tau}\circ s)(0)=\tau
; hence
\tau=g_{0}(0)
is determined, and then
s=t_{-\tau}\circ g_{0}
. If
g_{0}(0)=0
then
\tau=0
and
g_{0}=s\in SR^{*}(L)
, which proves (1). Point (2) follows from
Dt_{\tau}=\mathrm{id}
and Corollary
3.6
, together with Corollary
3.9
for the invertibility of
Ds(x)
at every point.
∎

6.
Global dynamics, the normalized model, and existence

In this section we establish the global dynamics of contracting sub-resonant maps, deduce that
\langle h\rangle
acts freely and properly discontinuously on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, identify
M
with the normalized model
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
, and prove the existence theorem (Theorem
1
) for primary Hopf manifolds.

Lemma 6.1

(global dynamics)
.

Let
g\in SR(L)
be such that the spectral radius of
L_{g}:=Dg(0)
is
<1
. Then:

(1)

g^{p}\to 0
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
;

(2)

\mathop{\rm Fix}(g)=\{0\}
.

In particular these conclusions hold for every
g\in SR^{*}(L)
whose linear part is similar to
L
(for instance
g=h
), as well as for all its powers
g^{m}
,
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

Write
g
in the block triangular form of Lemma
3.4
:
g_{i}=C_{i}t_{i}+Q_{i}(t_{i+1},\dots,t_{l})
(with
Q_{l}=0
). The linear part
L_{g}
is block upper triangular with diagonal blocks
C_{1},\dots,C_{l}
(the linear part of
Q_{i}
only contributes to strictly upper blocks), so
\mathop{\rm Sp}(C_{i})\subseteq\mathop{\rm Sp}(L_{g})
and the spectral radius of each
C_{i}
is
<1
. Fix
\rho\in(\max_{i}\rho_{\rm sp}(C_{i}),1)
; by the spectral radius formula there is
C\geq 1
with
\|C_{i}^{m}\|\leq C\rho^{m}
for all
i,m
.

Let
K\subset\mathbf{C}^{n}
be compact; write
z^{(p)}:=g^{p}(z)
and let us prove by descending induction on
i
that
\delta_{i}(p):=\sup_{z\in K}\|t_{i}^{(p)}(z)\|\to 0
.

Case
i=l
. Lemma
3.4
gives
t_{l}^{(p)}=C_{l}^{p}\,t_{l}^{(0)}
, and
\|C_{l}^{p}\|\to 0
.

Induction step. Assume
\delta_{j}(p)\to 0
for all
j>i
. Since
Q_{i}
is a polynomial map without constant term, there is
C^{\prime}>0
such that
\|Q_{i}(w)\|\leq C^{\prime}\|w\|
for
\|w\|\leq 1
; the sequences
\delta_{j}(p)
,
j>i
, being bounded and tending to
0
, the quantity

\varepsilon_{p}:=\sup_{z\in K}\big\|Q_{i}\big(t_{i+1}^{(p)}(z),\dots,t_{l}^{(p)}(z)\big)\big\|

is bounded and tends to
0
. The recursion
t_{i}^{(p+1)}=C_{i}\,t_{i}^{(p)}+Q_{i}\big(t^{(p)}_{>i}\big)
solves to

t_{i}^{(p)}=C_{i}^{p}\,t_{i}^{(0)}+\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}C_{i}^{\,p-1-k}\,Q_{i}\big(t^{(k)}_{>i}\big),\qquad\text{whence}\qquad\delta_{i}(p)\ \leq\ C\rho^{p}\sup_{K}\|t_{i}\|\ +\ C\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\rho^{\,p-1-k}\varepsilon_{k}.

It remains to check that the second term, the discrete convolution

a_{p}:=C\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\rho^{\,p-1-k}\varepsilon_{k}

of the geometric sequence
(\rho^{k})
with the bounded null sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})
, tends to
0
. Let
E:=\sup_{k}\varepsilon_{k}<\infty
and fix
\delta>0
; choose
k_{0}
with
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\delta
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Splitting the sum at
k_{0}
, for
p>k_{0}
we have

a_{p}\ =\ C\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\rho^{\,p-1-k}\varepsilon_{k}\ +\ C\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{p-1}\rho^{\,p-1-k}\varepsilon_{k}\ \leq\ C\,E\,k_{0}\,\rho^{\,p-k_{0}}\ +\ C\,\delta\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,j}\ =\ C\,E\,k_{0}\,\rho^{\,p-k_{0}}\ +\ \frac{C\,\delta}{1-\rho},

where in the first sum we bounded each
\varepsilon_{k}\leq E
and
\rho^{\,p-1-k}\leq\rho^{\,p-k_{0}}
(as
k\leq k_{0}-1
), and in the second we bounded each
\varepsilon_{k}\leq\delta
and extended the geometric sum to infinity. The first term tends to
0
as
p\to\infty
(since
\rho<1
), so
\limsup_{p}a_{p}\leq C\delta/(1-\rho)
; as
\delta>0
was arbitrary,
a_{p}\to 0
. Therefore
\delta_{i}(p)\to 0
, and by descending induction this proves (1).

(2) If
g(q)=q
then
q=g^{p}(q)\to 0
, so
q=0
.

Finally, if
g\in SR^{*}(L)
has linear part similar to
L
, then
\rho_{\rm sp}(L_{g})=\rho_{\rm sp}(L)<1
; and
g^{m}\in SR^{*}(L)
(Theorem
3.7
) has linear part
L_{g}^{m}
, of spectral radius
<1
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
g\in SR^{*}(L)
have linear part similar to
L
(for instance
g=h
). Then
\langle g\rangle\simeq\mathbf{Z}
acts freely and properly discontinuously on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Freeness. If
g^{m}(z)=z
with
m\geq 1
and
z\neq 0
, then
z\in\mathop{\rm Fix}(g^{m})=\{0\}
by Lemma
6.1
, a contradiction; the case
m\leq-1
reduces to this one by applying
g^{-m}
. In particular,
g
has infinite order.

Proper discontinuity. Let
K\subset\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
be compact and
d:={\rm dist}(0,K)>0
. By Lemma
6.1
,
g^{m}(K)\subset B(0,d/2)
for

, so
g^{m}(K)\cap K=\emptyset
for

; and for

,

is equivalent to

∎

Proposition 6.3

(normalized model)
.

There is a biholomorphism

\Phi:\ (\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle\gamma\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\ (\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle.

In particular the generator of
\pi_{1}(M)\simeq\mathbf{Z}
acts, in the normalized model, by
h
.

Proof.

By Corollary
6.2
,
M^{\prime}:=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
is a complex manifold. Let
\varphi
be the local conjugation of Theorem
4.9
, so that
\gamma\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ h
near
0
. Equip
\mathbf{C}^{n}
with an adapted norm in which
\|L\|<1
; since
Dh(0)=L
, we have
\|h(z)\|\leq c\|z\|
(with
c<1
) on a ball
B=B(0,r)
small enough, so that
h(B)\subseteq B
, and we may choose
r
so small that
\varphi
is defined and injective on a neighborhood of
\overline{B}
. By induction,
\gamma^{k}\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ h^{k}
on
B
for every
k\geq 0
(the identity at step

together with
h^{k}(B)\subseteq B
yields step
k+1
).

Construction. Let

. Since
\gamma
is a contraction,
\gamma^{p}(u)\to 0
; as
\varphi(B)
is a neighborhood of
0
, there is an admissible

, i.e. with
\gamma^{p}(u)\in\varphi(B)
. Set

\widetilde{\Psi}(u):=h^{-p}\big(\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p}(u))\big)\in\mathbf{C}^{n}

(

is a global automorphism by Corollary
3.9
, so

is everywhere defined).

Independence of

. If

is admissible, set

; then
\gamma^{p+1}u=\gamma(\varphi(x))=\varphi(h(x))
with
h(x)\in B
:
p+1

h^{-(p+1)}\big(\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p+1}u)\big)=h^{-(p+1)}(h(x))=h^{-p}(x).

Any two admissible integers are compared through the chain of intermediate ones (every integer larger than an admissible one is admissible), so
\widetilde{\Psi}
is well defined.

Properties. Locally, one and the same

is admissible on a neighborhood of

(continuity, openness of
\varphi(B)
), so
\widetilde{\Psi}
is holomorphic and, as a composition of local biholomorphisms, is a local biholomorphism. If
\widetilde{\Psi}(u)=0
then
\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p}u)=h^{p}(0)=0
, hence
\gamma^{p}(u)=0
and

(injectivity of
\gamma
), a contradiction:
\widetilde{\Psi}
takes values in
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Finally, if
p
is admissible for
\gamma(u)
then

\widetilde{\Psi}(\gamma(u))=h^{-p}\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p+1}u)=h\cdot h^{-(p+1)}\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p+1}u)=h(\widetilde{\Psi}(u)):

\widetilde{\Psi}
is equivariant and descends to a holomorphic map

, a local biholomorphism.

Injectivity. Suppose
\widetilde{\Psi}(u)=h^{m}\,\widetilde{\Psi}(u^{\prime})
. Choose
p
admissible for
u
and
p^{\prime}
for
u^{\prime}
, and set
x=\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p}u)
,
x^{\prime}=\varphi^{-1}(\gamma^{p^{\prime}}u^{\prime})\in B
; the relation reads
x=h^{a}(x^{\prime})
with

. Up to exchanging
(u,p)
and
(u^{\prime},p^{\prime})
, assume

; then

and

\gamma^{p}(u)=\varphi(h^{a}(x^{\prime}))=\gamma^{a}(\varphi(x^{\prime}))=\gamma^{a+p^{\prime}}(u^{\prime}).

The iterates of
\gamma
being injective, we deduce that
u
and
u^{\prime}
lie on the same
\gamma
-orbit, so
[u]=[u^{\prime}]
in
M
. Hence
\Phi
is injective.

Surjectivity. Let
z\in\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. By Lemma
6.1
,
h^{p}(z)\in B\setminus\{0\}
for some
p\geq 0
; set
u:=\varphi(h^{p}(z))\in\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. The integer
0
is admissible for
u
and
\widetilde{\Psi}(u)=\varphi^{-1}(u)=h^{p}(z)
, so
\Phi([u])=[z]
.

\Phi
is therefore a biholomorphism. For

,
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is simply connected, hence it is the universal cover of

, with deck group
\langle h\rangle\simeq\mathbf{Z}
(Corollary
6.2
gives the infinite order).
∎

From now on, we identify
M=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
via Proposition
6.3
, and for

we let
\gamma
denote the generator of
\pi_{1}(M)
acting by
h
. The canonical structure
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{can}}
is given by
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{\mathrm{can}}=\mathrm{incl}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{can}}(\gamma)=h
.

Proof of Theorem
1
, primary case.

By Proposition
6.3
,
M
is biholomorphic to
(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
. By Corollary
6.2
, the group
\langle h\rangle\subseteq SR^{*}(L)\subseteq G
acts freely and properly discontinuously on the open subset
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
of

, and

acts transitively and holomorphically on

(it contains the translations). By Proposition

, the quotient inherits a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure; its charts are local inverses of the covering map composed with the inclusion into

.
∎

7.
Analytic rigidity of developing maps

We assume
n\geq 2
throughout the remainder of the paper. The non-uniqueness announced in the introduction takes its simplest form in the following example, which we will use as a running illustration.

Example 7.1

(the permutation family)
.

Let
h=L=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with
0<|\lambda_{1}|<\dots<|\lambda_{n}|<1
, and
M=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle L\rangle
. For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, let
P_{\sigma}
denote the associated permutation matrix. Then

\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\nolimits_{\sigma}:=P_{\sigma}|_{\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}},\qquad\rho_{\sigma}(\gamma):=P_{\sigma}LP_{\sigma}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})

is a representative of a compatible
(G,X)
-structure on
M
: indeed
\rho_{\sigma}(\gamma)
is a diagonal matrix, hence belongs to
SR^{*}(L)
, the linear sub-resonance condition constraining only the positions of the nonzero coefficients and never their values. We will see (Proposition
10.1
) that these
n!
structures are pairwise inequivalent, and even pairwise inequivalent modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
when
L
is non-resonant.

The first structural result asserts that every developing map is the restriction of a global automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\mathcal{S}
be a compatible
(G,X)
-structure on
M
, with representative
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
. Then:

(1)

\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits
extends uniquely to an entire map
\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
, which is a local biholomorphism at every point;

(2)

p:=\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}(0)
is the unique fixed point of
\rho(\gamma)
, and
g:=t_{-p}\circ\rho(\gamma)\circ t_{p}\in SR^{*}(L)
;

(3)

\Psi:=t_{-p}\circ\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}
is a holomorphic automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
, with
\Psi(0)=0
and
\Psi\circ h\circ\Psi^{-1}=g
.

In particular
(
coarse uniqueness
)
, any two compatible structures on
M
have developing maps differing by post-composition with a global holomorphic automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
, which conjugates their holonomies.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

Let
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
be a compatible structure:
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits:\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
is a local biholomorphism and
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\circ h=\rho(\gamma)\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits
.

(1) Extension. The components of
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits
are holomorphic on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with
n\geq 2
; by Hartogs’ extension theorem they extend (uniquely) to
\mathbf{C}^{n}
. Both sides of the identity
\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}\circ h=\rho(\gamma)\circ\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}
are holomorphic on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
and coincide on the dense open set
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
; they therefore coincide everywhere.

Jacobian. Let
J:=\det D\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{C}^{n})
. Since
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits
is a local biholomorphism,
J
does not vanish on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose
J(0)=0
: then
1/J
is holomorphic on
B(0,1)\setminus\{0\}
and extends holomorphically to
B(0,1)
by Hartogs (
n\geq 2
); the identity
J\cdot(1/J)=1
, valid on
B(0,1)\setminus\{0\}
, extends by continuity and contradicts
J(0)=0
. Hence
J(0)\neq 0
and
\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}
is a local biholomorphism at every point of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

(2) Fixed point and recentering. Let
p:=\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}(0)
. Evaluating the equivariance at
0
gives

\rho(\gamma)(p)=\rho(\gamma)\big(\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}(0)\big)=\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}(h(0))=\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}(0)=p.

By Lemma
5.3
,
g:=t_{-p}\,\rho(\gamma)\,t_{p}\in G
fixes
0
, so
g\in SR^{*}(L)
. Set
\Psi:=t_{-p}\circ\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}
; then
\Psi(0)=0
and

(7.1)

\Psi\circ h=g\circ\Psi\qquad\text{on }\mathbf{C}^{n}.

Differentiating (
7.1
) at
0
:
A\,L=L_{g}\,A
, where
A:=D\Psi(0)
is invertible by the Jacobian step and
L_{g}:=Dg(0)
; hence
L_{g}=A\,L\,A^{-1}
is similar to
L
and
\rho_{\rm sp}(L_{g})<1
. Lemma
6.1
applied to
g
gives
\mathop{\rm Fix}(g)=\{0\}
, whence
\mathop{\rm Fix}(\rho(\gamma))=t_{p}(\mathop{\rm Fix}(g))=\{p\}
: the fixed point is unique.

(3) Bijectivity of
\Psi
. Note that
h
and
g
are automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
(Corollary
3.9
), and that (
7.1
) gives, for every
k\in\mathbf{Z}
,

(7.2)

\Psi\circ h^{k}=g^{k}\circ\Psi.

Let
U_{0}
be an open neighborhood of
0
on which
\Psi
is injective (local biholomorphism at
0
).

Injectivity. If
\Psi(x)=\Psi(y)
, then by (
7.2
),
\Psi(h^{p}x)=g^{p}\Psi(x)=g^{p}\Psi(y)=\Psi(h^{p}y)
for every
p\geq 0
. By Lemma
6.1
applied to
h
, we have
h^{p}x,h^{p}y\in U_{0}
for
p
large; the injectivity of
\Psi
on
U_{0}
gives
h^{p}x=h^{p}y
, hence
x=y
.

Surjectivity.
\Psi(U_{0})
is an open neighborhood of
0
. Let
w\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
; by Lemma
6.1
applied to
g
,
g^{p}(w)\in\Psi(U_{0})
for some
p\geq 0
:
g^{p}(w)=\Psi(x)
,
x\in U_{0}
. Then, by (
7.2
) with
k=-p
,

w=g^{-p}(\Psi(x))=\Psi\big(h^{-p}(x)\big).

\Psi
is therefore a biholomorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
onto
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

For the last assertion (coarse uniqueness), let
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{2},\rho_{2})
be two compatible structures and
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{i}=t_{p_{i}}\circ\Psi_{i}
on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
the corresponding decompositions; set
\Theta:=t_{p_{2}}\circ\Psi_{2}\circ\Psi_{1}^{-1}\circ t_{-p_{1}}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n})
. Then
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{2}=\Theta\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{1}
, and the relation between holonomies follows from
\rho_{i}(\gamma)=\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{i}}\circ h\circ\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits_{i}}^{\,-1}
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

The coarse uniqueness statement is already a strong rigidity result: a priori, two developing maps could have differed by a wild, non-extendable local biholomorphism; in fact they differ by a global automorphism of the model. But this statement forgets the group
G
: the group
\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n})
is huge and does not preserve
G
, so this notion of equivalence identifies structures that the geometry
(G,X)
really distinguishes, such as all the structures of Example
7.1
. It says that the geometry of
M
is unique up to isomorphism of geometries. The right notion of uniqueness of the structure must be finer; this is the object of the next section.

8.
The holonomy flag and aligned structures

8.1.
The model flag is intrinsic

The following linear algebra lemma shows that the flag
V_{\bullet}
is an intrinsic invariant of the geometry
(G,X)
: by Lemma
5.3
and Corollary
3.6
, the differentials at
0
of the elements of the isotropy group
\mathop{\rm Stab}_{G}(0)=SR^{*}(L)
form exactly the parabolic subgroup
P(V_{\bullet})
, and:

Lemma 8.1

(parabolic lemma)
.

The subspaces of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
invariant under all elements of
P(V_{\bullet})
are exactly
0,V_{1},\dots,V_{l}
. Consequently,
V_{\bullet}
is the unique flag of signature
d(L)
invariant under
P(V_{\bullet})
.

Proof.

The
V_{i}
are invariant by definition. Conversely, let
W\neq 0
be a subspace invariant under every element of
P(V_{\bullet})
.

Step 1:
W
is invariant under every endomorphism
\varphi
preserving the flag. Let
\varphi
satisfy
\varphi(V_{i})\subseteq V_{i}
for all
i
. The polynomial
t\mapsto\det(\mathrm{id}+t\varphi)
equals
1
at
t=0
, so
\mathrm{id}+t\varphi
is invertible for small
t\neq 0
, and preserves each
V_{i}
(with equality by dimension):
\mathrm{id}+t\varphi\in P(V_{\bullet})
. Then
(\mathrm{id}+t\varphi)(W)\subseteq W
and
\mathrm{id}(W)\subseteq W
give
t\varphi(W)\subseteq W
, hence
\varphi(W)\subseteq W
.

Step 2. Let
w\in W\setminus\{0\}
and let
i
be minimal with
w\in V_{i}
(so
w\notin V_{i-1}
). Let
v\in V_{i}
be arbitrary. Since
w\notin V_{i-1}
, there is a linear form
\xi
with
\xi(w)=1
and
\xi|_{V_{i-1}}=0
. The endomorphism of rank
\leq 1

\varphi:=\xi(\cdot)\,v

preserves the flag: for
k<i
,
\xi(V_{k})\subseteq\xi(V_{i-1})=0
; for
k\geq i
,
\varphi(V_{k})\subseteq\mathbf{C}v\subseteq V_{i}\subseteq V_{k}
. By Step 1,
v=\varphi(w)\in W
. Hence
V_{i}\subseteq W
.

Step 3. Let
i^{*}
be the largest index
i
such that there exists
w\in W
with
w\in V_{i}\setminus V_{i-1}
. By Step 2,
V_{i^{*}}\subseteq W
; and by maximality
W\subseteq V_{i^{*}}
. So
W=V_{i^{*}}
.

Finally, an invariant flag consists of invariant subspaces, hence of some of the
V_{i}
’s; if it has signature
d(L)
, it is
V_{\bullet}
.
∎

8.2.
The holonomy flag and the alignment condition

By Proposition
7.2
, any representative
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
of a compatible structure
\mathcal{S}
determines the following data: the unique fixed point
p
of
\rho(\gamma)
, and the differential
D\rho(\gamma)(p)
, which is similar to
L
(it equals
L_{g}=ALA^{-1}
in the notation of the proof, since
\rho(\gamma)=t_{p}\,g\,t_{-p}
and
D\rho(\gamma)(p)=Dg(0)=L_{g}
). Its eigenvalues therefore have moduli
\mu_{1}<\dots<\mu_{l}
with the same multiplicities as those of
L
, and its modulus flag has signature
d(L)
. These data depend on the chosen representative, but the invariant we extract from them will not (Lemma
8.3
).

Definition 8.2

(holonomy flag; alignment)
.

Given a representative
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
of a compatible structure
\mathcal{S}
, with
p
the unique fixed point of
\rho(\gamma)
, its holonomy flag is the modulus flag of
D\rho(\gamma)(p)
,

\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)\ :=\ \mathcal{F}\big(D\rho(\gamma)(p)\big),\qquad\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)_{i}=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}\big(D\rho(\gamma)(p)\big)\ \subset\ \mathbf{C}^{n}\simeq T_{p}\mathbf{C}^{n}.

We say the structure
\mathcal{S}
is aligned if some (equivalently, by Lemma
8.3
, any) representative has holonomy flag equal to
V_{\bullet}
.

Lemma 8.3

(invariance)
.

For representatives
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
and
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
in the same
G
-orbit,
\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)=V_{\bullet}
if and only if
\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})=V_{\bullet}
. Hence alignment is a well-defined property of the structure
\mathcal{S}
. Moreover, the canonical structure is aligned.

Proof.

It suffices to treat
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})=(g_{0}\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\,g_{0}\rho g_{0}^{-1})
for
g_{0}\in G
. The fixed point of
\rho^{\prime}(\gamma)=g_{0}\rho(\gamma)g_{0}^{-1}
is
p^{\prime}=g_{0}(p)
and, by the chain rule,

D\rho^{\prime}(\gamma)(p^{\prime})=Dg_{0}(p)\cdot D\rho(\gamma)(p)\cdot Dg_{0}(p)^{-1}.

Conjugation by an invertible matrix
B
transports characteristic spaces (
E^{\lambda}(BTB^{-1})=B\,E^{\lambda}(T)
), hence modulus flags:
\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})=Dg_{0}(p)\cdot\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
. Now
Dg_{0}(p)
preserves
V_{\bullet}
by Lemma
5.3
; thus
\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)=V_{\bullet}
if and only if
\mathcal{F}(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})=Dg_{0}(p)(V_{\bullet})=V_{\bullet}
.

For the canonical structure, the representative
(\mathrm{incl},\rho_{\mathrm{can}})
has
p=0
,
\rho_{\mathrm{can}}(\gamma)=h
,
Dh(0)=L
and
\mathcal{F}(L)=V_{\bullet}
by definition.
∎

9.
Uniqueness of the aligned structure

The technical core is the following rigidity lemma. It corrects the naive rigidity statement, which is falsewithout a hypothesis on
L_{g}
(as the linear counterexample
\psi=P_{\sigma}
already shows), by identifying the hypothesis that makes it true, namely the position of the flag.

We first record two elementary jet extraction identities.

Lemma 9.1

(jet extraction)
.

Let
u=\sum_{j\geq 1}u_{j}
and
v=\sum_{m\geq 1}v_{m}
be holomorphic germs at
0
fixing
0
, written in homogeneous components, and let
k\geq 2
. Write
v^{(k-1)}:=\sum_{m\leq k-1}v_{m}
and
u^{(k-1)}:=\sum_{j\leq k-1}u_{j}
for the truncations, and
[\,\cdot\,]_{k}
for the degree-
k
homogeneous component. Then

(9.1)

\displaystyle[u\circ v]_{k}

\displaystyle=u_{1}\circ v_{k}\ +\ \big[(u-u_{1})\circ v^{(k-1)}\big]_{k},

(9.2)

\displaystyle[u\circ v]_{k}

\displaystyle=u_{k}\circ v_{1}\ +\ \big[u^{(k-1)}\circ v\big]_{k}.

Proof.

By polarization,
[u\circ v]_{k}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\ \sum_{m_{1}+\dots+m_{j}=k}\widetilde{u}_{j}(v_{m_{1}},\dots,v_{m_{j}})
, where
\widetilde{u}_{j}
is the symmetric
j
-multilinear form of
u_{j}
and
m_{i}\geq 1
. For (
9.1
): the
j=1
term contributes
u_{1}\circ v_{k}
in degree
k
; and for
j\geq 2
, no
m_{i}
can be
\geq k
(otherwise the sum of the
j\geq 2
degrees would be
\geq k+1
), so
v
may be replaced by
v^{(k-1)}
. For (
9.2
): the term
j=k
only contributes through
\widetilde{u}_{k}(v_{1},\dots,v_{1})=u_{k}\circ v_{1}
(as soon as one argument is some
v_{m}
with
m\geq 2
, the degree is
\geq k+1
); the terms
j>k
have degree
>k
; the terms
j\leq k-1
constitute
[u^{(k-1)}\circ v]_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 9.2

(aligned rigidity)
.

Let
h,g\in SR^{*}(L)
with
Dh(0)=L
and
\mathcal{F}(L_{g})=V_{\bullet}
, where
L_{g}:=Dg(0)
. Let
\psi
be a local biholomorphism at
0
such that
\psi(0)=0
and

\psi\circ h=g\circ\psi\qquad\text{near }0.

Then
\psi\in SR^{*}(L)
; it is therefore polynomial of degree
\leq c_{0}(L)
and extends to an automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

Write
\psi=\sum_{k\geq 1}\psi_{k}
for the Taylor expansion in homogeneous components (convergent near
0
),
A:=\psi_{1}=D\psi(0)
, and similarly
h=L+\sum_{j\geq 2}h_{j}
,
g=L_{g}+\sum_{j\geq 2}g_{j}
, where
h_{j},g_{j}\in SR_{j}(L)
(the homogeneous components of an element of
SR(L)
are sub-resonant, by definition).

Step 1: linear part. In degree
1
, the equivariance gives
A\,L=L_{g}\,A
with
A
invertible. Conjugation transports characteristic spaces:
A(E^{\lambda}(L))=E^{\lambda}(L_{g})
for every eigenvalue
\lambda
. Summing over
|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}
:

A(V_{i})=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}(L_{g})=\mathcal{F}(L_{g})_{i}=V_{i},

by the alignment hypothesis. Hence
A\in P(V_{\bullet})
, and by Corollary
3.6
,

and
A^{-1}
are linear sub-resonant maps:
\psi_{1}=A\in SR_{1}(L)
. Note also that

is equivalent to

(9.3)

A^{-1}\,L_{g}\,A=L.

Step 2: induction. We show by strong induction that
\psi_{k}\in SR_{k}(L)
for every

; the case

is Step 1. Let

and suppose
\psi_{m}\in SR_{m}(L)
for

; in particular the truncation

belongs to
SR(L)
.

Let us extract the degree-
k
component of
\psi\circ h=g\circ\psi
. By (
9.1
) applied to
u=g
,
v=\psi
:

[g\circ\psi]_{k}=L_{g}\circ\psi_{k}+A_{k},\qquad A_{k}:=\big[(g-L_{g})\circ\psi^{(k-1)}\big]_{k}.

By (
9.2
) applied to
u=\psi
,
v=h
:

[\psi\circ h]_{k}=\psi_{k}\circ L+B_{k},\qquad B_{k}:=\big[\psi^{(k-1)}\circ h\big]_{k}.

Now
g-L_{g}\in SR(L)
,
h\in SR(L)
and
\psi^{(k-1)}\in SR(L)
; by stability of

under composition and extraction of homogeneous components (Remark

),
A_{k},B_{k}\in SR_{k}(L)
. Equating the two extractions gives

(9.4)

\psi_{k}\circ L\ -\ L_{g}\circ\psi_{k}\ =\ A_{k}-B_{k}\ \in\ SR_{k}(L).

Set

. Using (

):

\psi_{k}\circ L-L_{g}\circ\psi_{k}=A\circ\chi_{k}\circ L-(ALA^{-1})\circ A\circ\chi_{k}=A\circ\big(\chi_{k}\circ L-L\circ\chi_{k}\big)=A\circ M^{k}_{L}(\chi_{k}).

Since

and Lemma

(2) implies

. Finally
\psi_{k}=A\circ\chi_{k}\in SR_{k}(L)
by stability, which closes the induction.

Step 3: conclusion. By Corollary
3.5
,
SR_{k}(L)=0
for

, so
\psi_{k}=0
for
k>c_{0}(L)
: the Taylor series of
\psi
is a polynomial, and the germ
\psi
coincides with this polynomial. All its homogeneous components are sub-resonant and

is invertible:
\psi\in SR^{*}(L)
. The last assertions follow from Corollaries
3.5
and
3.9
.
∎

Remark 9.3
.

The hypothesis

Proposition 9.4
.

Let

. There is exactly one compatible aligned structure on

, the canonical structure. Any of its representatives has developing map equal, up to translation, to the restriction of an element of

; in particular it is polynomial of degree at most

.

Proof of Proposition
9.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an aligned structure, and choose a representative

:
p
,

and
\Psi=t_{-p}\circ\widehat{\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n})

. Since

, alignment means

. The germ of

at

: there exists

Set

∎

10.
Classification and optimality

10.1.
Classification of compatible structures

Set

\mathcal{N}(h)=\big\{\Psi\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n})\ :\ \Psi(0)=0,\ \ \Psi\,h\,\Psi^{-1}\in SR^{*}(L)\big\}.

The group
SR^{*}(L)
acts on
\mathcal{N}(h)
by left composition: if
s\in SR^{*}(L)
and
\Psi\in\mathcal{N}(h)
, then
(s\Psi)h(s\Psi)^{-1}=s(\Psi h\Psi^{-1})s^{-1}\in SR^{*}(L)
. For
\Psi\in\mathcal{N}(h)
, we write
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
for the structure represented by the pair
\big(\Psi|_{\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}},\ \rho_{\Psi}\big)
, where
\rho_{\Psi}(\gamma):=\Psi h\Psi^{-1}
.

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
.

(1)

The assignment
\Psi\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
induces a bijection

SR^{*}(L)\backslash\mathcal{N}(h)\ \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\ \big\{\text{compatible $(G,X)$-structures on }M\big\}.

(2)

The aligned structure corresponds to the class of the identity in
SR^{*}(L)\backslash\mathcal{N}(h)
.

(3)

If
h=L=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
with pairwise distinct moduli, then
P_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{N}(h)
for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and the classes
[P_{\sigma}]
are pairwise distinct; exactly one of them,
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
, is aligned. If moreover
L
is non-resonant, these
n!
structures are pairwise inequivalent modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)

(
Proposition
10.2
)
.

Proof of Proposition
10.1
.

(1) The map is well defined. For
\Psi\in\mathcal{N}(h)
,
\Psi|_{\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}
is a local biholomorphism and
\rho_{\Psi}(\gamma)\in SR^{*}(L)\subset G
, with the equivariance
\Psi\circ h=\rho_{\Psi}(\gamma)\circ\Psi
:
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
is a compatible structure.

Surjectivity. This is Proposition
7.2
: any structure has a representative
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
with
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits=t_{p}\circ\Psi|_{\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}
for some
\Psi\in\mathcal{N}(h)
, and this structure equals
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
, since
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
and the defining representative of
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
are related by
g_{0}:=t_{p}\in G
(the relation between the holonomies follows as in the proof of Proposition
9.4
).

Injectivity / compatibility with the classes. Let
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in\mathcal{N}(h)
. Suppose
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi_{1}}=\mathcal{S}_{\Psi_{2}}
, i.e. their defining representatives are
G
-equivalent: there exists
g_{0}\in G
with
\Psi_{2}=g_{0}\circ\Psi_{1}
on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, hence on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
by continuity. Evaluating at
0
:
0=\Psi_{2}(0)=g_{0}(\Psi_{1}(0))=g_{0}(0)
, so
g_{0}\in\mathop{\rm Stab}_{G}(0)=SR^{*}(L)
(Lemma
5.3
) and
[\Psi_{1}]=[\Psi_{2}]
in
SR^{*}(L)\backslash\mathcal{N}(h)
. Conversely, if
\Psi_{2}=s\circ\Psi_{1}
with
s\in SR^{*}(L)\subset G
, then
g_{0}:=s
shows the defining representatives are
G
-equivalent, since
\rho_{\Psi_{2}}(\gamma)=s\,\rho_{\Psi_{1}}(\gamma)\,s^{-1}
by direct computation; hence
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi_{1}}=\mathcal{S}_{\Psi_{2}}
.

(2) If
\Psi=s\in SR^{*}(L)
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{can}}
(their representatives are related by
g_{0}=s
), hence
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
is aligned. Conversely, if
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}
is aligned, Proposition
9.4
gives
\mathcal{S}_{\Psi}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{can}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{id}}
, so
[\Psi]=[\mathrm{id}]
by (1).

(3)
g_{\sigma}:=P_{\sigma}LP_{\sigma}^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})
is a diagonal matrix, hence preserves the coordinate flag
V_{\bullet}
(here
V_{i}=\mathrm{Vect}(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})
, the moduli being pairwise distinct); by Corollary
3.6
,
g_{\sigma}\in SR^{*}(L)
and
P_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{N}(h)
. If
[P_{\sigma}]=[P_{\sigma^{\prime}}]
, then
s:=P_{\sigma^{\prime}}P_{\sigma}^{-1}=P_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}}\in SR^{*}(L)
; being linear,
s
preserves each
V_{i}=\mathrm{Vect}(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})
(Corollary
3.6
), which for a permutation matrix forces
\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}(\{1,\dots,i\})=\{1,\dots,i\}
for every
i
, hence
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma
. Finally, the defining representative of
\mathcal{S}_{P_{\sigma}}
has holonomy flag
\mathcal{F}(g_{\sigma})=P_{\sigma}(V_{\bullet})
, which equals
V_{\bullet}
if and only if
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
; so
\mathcal{S}_{P_{\sigma}}
is aligned exactly when
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
. The statement modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
is Proposition
10.2
below.
∎

10.2.
Persistence of non-uniqueness modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)

The following statement shows that the defect of uniqueness cannot be absorbed by the automorphisms of the manifold: two distinct compatible structures may remain distinct even after precomposition by an automorphism of
M
.

We say that two compatible structures
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
are equivalent modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
if there exists
f\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
with
f^{*}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
as structures; in terms of representatives, if there exist a lift
\widetilde{f}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
of
f
and
g_{0}\in G
with
\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime}=g_{0}\circ\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits\circ\widetilde{f}
, for some representatives
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits,\rho)
of
\mathcal{S}
and
(\mathop{\rm dev}\nolimits^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
h=L=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
,
0<|\lambda_{1}|<\dots<|\lambda_{n}|<1
, be non-resonant:
\lambda^{I}\neq\lambda_{j}
for every multi-index
I
with
|I|\geq 2
and every
j
. Then:

(1)

every lift to
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
of an automorphism of
M
is the restriction of an invertible diagonal linear map;

(2)

the structures
\mathcal{S}_{P_{\sigma}}
,
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, are pairwise inequivalent modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
.

Proof.

(1) Let
f\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
and let
\widetilde{f}
be a lift to the universal cover
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Compatibility with the deck group reads
\widetilde{f}\circ L\circ\widetilde{f}^{-1}=L^{\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
(conjugation by
\widetilde{f}
is an automorphism of
\langle L\rangle\simeq\mathbf{Z}
, hence sends the generator to
L^{\pm 1}
). By Hartogs (
n\geq 2
),
\widetilde{f}
extends to a holomorphic map
\widehat{f}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
, and the relation
\widehat{f}\circ L=L^{\varepsilon}\circ\widehat{f}
extends by analytic continuation. Then
\widehat{f}(0)
is a fixed point of
L^{\varepsilon}
; since
\mathop{\rm Fix}(L)=\mathop{\rm Fix}(L^{-1})=\{0\}
, we get
\widehat{f}(0)=0
. The Jacobian argument of the proof of Proposition
7.2
(applied verbatim to
\widehat{f}
, which is a local biholomorphism on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
) shows that
B:=D\widehat{f}(0)
is invertible; differentiating at
0
:
B\,L=L^{\varepsilon}B
, so
L
and
L^{\varepsilon}
are similar. If
\varepsilon=-1
, the eigenvalues of
L^{-1}
have modulus
>1
: impossible. So
\varepsilon=1
and
\widehat{f}
commutes with
L
.

Expand
\widehat{f}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\widehat{f}_{k}
(no constant term since
\widehat{f}(0)=0
); commutation gives, in degree
k
,
\widehat{f}_{k}\circ L=L\circ\widehat{f}_{k}
. In the monomial basis
H_{I,j}
, this equation reads
(\lambda^{I}-\lambda_{j})\,c_{I,j}=0
for each coefficient
c_{I,j}
of
\widehat{f}_{k}
. For
k=|I|\geq 2
, non-resonance gives
c_{I,j}=0
:
\widehat{f}=B
is linear. Finally
BL=LB
with
L
diagonal with pairwise distinct eigenvalues (their moduli are distinct) forces
B
diagonal.

(2) Suppose
\mathcal{S}_{P_{\sigma^{\prime}}}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{S}_{P_{\sigma}}
modulo
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
: there exist a lift
\widetilde{f}
and
g_{0}\in G
with
P_{\sigma^{\prime}}=g_{0}\circ P_{\sigma}\circ\widetilde{f}
on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, hence on
\mathbf{C}^{n}
. By (1),
\widetilde{f}=D|_{\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}
with
D
diagonal invertible. Evaluating at
0
:
0=g_{0}(P_{\sigma}D\,(0))=g_{0}(0)
, so
g_{0}\in SR^{*}(L)
(Lemma
5.3
), and

g_{0}=P_{\sigma^{\prime}}\,(P_{\sigma}D)^{-1}=P_{\sigma^{\prime}}\,D^{-1}P_{\sigma}^{-1}=P_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}}\cdot\big(P_{\sigma}D^{-1}P_{\sigma}^{-1}\big),

the product of a permutation matrix and a diagonal matrix: it is a monomial matrix with underlying permutation
\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}
. Being linear and in
SR^{*}(L)
,
g_{0}
preserves each
V_{i}=\mathrm{Vect}(e_{1},\dots,e_{i})
(Corollary
3.6
), which forces
\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{-1}=\mathrm{id}
, i.e.
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
.
∎

11.
Secondary Hopf manifolds

So far
M=W_{h}=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\langle h\rangle
has been a primary Hopf manifold. We now show that the construction extends, without modification of the model
(G,X)
, to every secondary Hopf manifold, and we isolate the algebraic reason why.

Let
X
be a secondary Hopf manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
. By definition its universal cover is
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, and
X=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\Gamma
with
\Gamma=\pi_{1}(X)\subset\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
acting freely and properly discontinuously; by the theorem of Kodaira in dimension two and Hasegawa
[
Has
]
in general,
\Gamma
contains a normal infinite cyclic subgroup
\langle\gamma_{0}\rangle
of finite index generated by a contraction. Applying the Poincaré–Dulac normalization (Theorem
4.9
) to
\gamma_{0}
, that is, replacing
\Gamma
by a conjugate under a biholomorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
fixing the puncture, we may assume that
\gamma_{0}=h\in SR^{*}(L)
is in normal form, with
Dh(0)=L
. Thus
\langle h\rangle\lhd\Gamma
is normal of finite index, and
F:=\Gamma/\langle h\rangle
is the finite group of the covering
W_{h}\to X
.

We first record the rigidity of the elements of
\Gamma
, extending to non-contracting elements the arguments already used for
h
and for
\mathop{\rm Aut}(M)
(Proposition
10.2
). The following lemma, and more generally the structure of
\Gamma
as a central extension of a finite group by
\langle h\rangle
, is presumably well known; we include a proof for completeness and to keep the paper self-contained.

Lemma 11.1
.

Every
g\in\Gamma
extends to a polynomial automorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing
0
, and satisfies
g\circ h=h\circ g
. In other words,
h
is central in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
g\in\Gamma
. Its components are holomorphic on
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with
n\geq 2
, so by Hartogs’ theorem
g
extends to a holomorphic map
\widehat{g}:\mathbf{C}^{n}\to\mathbf{C}^{n}
; the same applies to
g^{-1}
, and the relations
\widehat{g}\circ\widehat{g^{-1}}=\widehat{g^{-1}}\circ\widehat{g}=\mathrm{id}
, valid on the dense open set
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, extend by continuity, so
\widehat{g}\in\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n})
.

Since
\langle h\rangle
is normal in
\Gamma
, there is
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
with
g\,h\,g^{-1}=h^{\varepsilon}
(the only automorphisms of
\mathbf{Z}
being
\pm 1
); this identity of automorphisms of
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
extends to
\mathbf{C}^{n}
. Evaluating at
0
gives
\widehat{g}(0)=\widehat{g}(h(0))=h^{\varepsilon}(\widehat{g}(0))
, so
\widehat{g}(0)
is a fixed point of
h^{\varepsilon}
. As in the proof of Proposition
7.2
, the Jacobian of
\widehat{g}
cannot vanish at
0
(otherwise
1/\det D\widehat{g}
would extend across
0
by Hartogs, contradicting
\det D\widehat{g}\not\equiv 0
), so
\widehat{g}
is a local biholomorphism at
0
; since
\mathop{\rm Fix}(h)=\mathop{\rm Fix}(h^{-1})=\{0\}
by Lemma
6.1
, we get
\widehat{g}(0)=0
. Differentiating
g\,h\,g^{-1}=h^{\varepsilon}
at
0
yields
B\,L\,B^{-1}=L^{\varepsilon}
with
B:=D\widehat{g}(0)
invertible; hence
L
and
L^{\varepsilon}
are similar and have the same spectrum. If
\varepsilon=-1
, the eigenvalues of
L^{-1}
have modulus
>1
while those of
L
have modulus
<1
: impossible. Therefore
\varepsilon=+1
, that is
g\,h\,g^{-1}=h
.
∎

Proposition 11.2
.

Let
X
be a secondary Hopf manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
, written
X=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\Gamma
with
\Gamma\subset\mathop{\rm Aut}(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
containing a normal cyclic contraction subgroup
\langle h\rangle
of finite index,
h
a Poincaré–Dulac normal form. Then
\Gamma\subset SR^{*}(L)\subset G
; consequently the inclusion
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
is a developing map for a compatible
(G,X)
-structure on
X
, with holonomy the inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow G
. Together with the primary case, this completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof of Proposition
11.2
.

Let
g\in\Gamma
. By Lemma
11.1
,
g
is a biholomorphism of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
fixing
0
with
g\circ h=h\circ g
. Apply the aligned rigidity lemma (Lemma
9.2
) with the following choice of data: the rôle of “
h
” is played by
h
, the rôle of “
g
” is also played by
h
, and the rôle of “
\psi
” by
g
. The hypotheses hold:
h\in SR^{*}(L)
with
Dh(0)=L
; the flag condition
\mathcal{F}(L_{g})=V_{\bullet}
becomes
\mathcal{F}(L)=V_{\bullet}
, which is true by definition of
V_{\bullet}
;
g
is a local biholomorphism at
0
fixing
0
; and the intertwining relation “
\psi\circ h=g\circ\psi
” becomes
g\circ h=h\circ g
, which holds. The lemma yields
g\in SR^{*}(L)
.

Since
g\in\Gamma
was arbitrary,
\Gamma\subset SR^{*}(L)\subset G
. The group
\Gamma
acts freely and properly discontinuously on the open subset
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
of
X_{\mathrm{model}}=\mathbf{C}^{n}
, on which
G
acts transitively and holomorphically; by Proposition
2.3
, the quotient
X=(\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})/\Gamma
inherits a holomorphic
(G,X)
-structure. Its charts are local inverses of the covering map followed by the inclusion
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
, hence biholomorphisms for the complex structure of
X
: the structure is compatible, with developing map the inclusion
\mathbf{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}^{n}
and holonomy the inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow SR^{*}(L)\subset G
. This settles the secondary case, and with the primary case of Section
6
completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Remark 11.3
.

The proof shows that the decisive fact is the centrality of
h
in
\Gamma
, not merely its normality: it is centrality that turns the normalization relation into the commutation
g\circ h=h\circ g
, to which aligned rigidity applies with the flag condition automatically satisfied. In concrete terms,
D\widehat{g}(0)
commutes with
L
, hence preserves every characteristic subspace
E^{\lambda}(L)
and therefore every
V_{i}=\bigoplus_{|\lambda|\leq\mu_{i}}E^{\lambda}(L)
: the modulus flag
V_{\bullet}
is preserved by the whole deck group, with no genericity or non-resonance assumption. This is why no secondary quotient can obstruct the descent of the structure, and why, in contrast with the failure of marked uniqueness on primary manifolds, there is here no combinatorial room for a counterexample: the permutation phenomenon of Example
7.1
arises from developing maps that do not commute with
h
, whereas deck transformations necessarily do.

Remark 11.4
.

The uniqueness results of Sections
7
–
10
also descend, in the following sense. A compatible structure on
X
pulls back to a
\Gamma
-invariant, hence a fortiori
\langle h\rangle
-invariant, compatible structure on the primary cover
W_{h}
, i.e. to a compatible structure on
W_{h}
whose holonomy representation extends from
\langle h\rangle
to
\Gamma
. Thus the compatible structures on
X
correspond to the
F
-equivariant compatible structures on
W_{h}
, a subfamily of the classification of Proposition
10.1
; the aligned structure, being canonical, is
F
-equivariant and descends to the distinguished compatible structure on
X
produced by Proposition
11.2
.

12.
Comparison with the work of Madera

The recent work of Madera
[
Ma
]
addresses a closely related question by a converse route, and it is worth spelling out where the two approaches meet and where they diverge. Madera constructs, on every primary Hopf manifold, holomorphic
G
-structures of higher order in the sense of Kobayashi (principal subbundles of the frame bundle
R^{k}(M)
) and shows that they induce flat holomorphic Cartan geometries. His structure group
G^{r}_{\beta}
, the group of sub-resonant polynomial transformations of maximal degree
r
attached to the eigenvalue data
\beta
, coincides with the group
SR^{*}(L)
used here, and the Lie group
\widetilde{G^{r}_{\beta}}
generated by
G^{r}_{\beta}
together with the translations of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
is exactly our model group
G
. His Lemma 7.1.1, stating that
\theta_{-P(z)}\circ P\circ\theta_{z}\in G^{r}_{\beta}
, is the same computation as the one underlying our Lemma
