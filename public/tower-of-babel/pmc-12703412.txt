Avian Influenza annual report 2024
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), José Cortiñas Abrahantes, Inma Aznar, Michiel Boom, Iancu Catalin, Fernanda Dórea, Malin Grant, Kenneth Fergus Mulligan, Gabriele Zancanaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report summarises the outcomes of avian influenza (AI) surveillance activities conducted in 2024 in the context of the Avian Influenza Data Collection (AIDC), the One Health (OH) initiatives of the EU4Health Programme (EU4H, 2022 co‐funded grant agreements on emerging and re‐emerging zoonotic pathogens) and the SENTINEL project. Surveillance encompassed poultry, captive and wild birds, and mammals across EU Member States (MSs) and selected non‐EU countries. A summary of the outbreaks reported in the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS) is also provided. A total of 276,621 records were submitted under the AIDC by 31 reporting countries, 16,755 under OH and 9804 under SENTINEL. Additionally, 204,851 poultry population records were voluntarily provided. Surveillance was risk‐based, aiming to detect highly pathogenic (HPAIV) and low pathogenic influenza A viruses (LPAIV), enhance early warning capacity and improve understanding of zoonotic potential. In poultry, in the context of the AIDC, 27,739 establishments were sampled, yielding 218,667 samples. HPAIV was detected and reported in 107 visits, mainly subtype H5N1 and LPAIV in 53. The highest HPAIV activity occurred in the first quarter. In total, 394 poultry outbreaks were reported in the EU and 66 in non‐EU countries through the ADIS. In captive birds, 296 visits were reported via the AIDC, resulting in seven HPAIV and one LPAIV detections. Ninety outbreaks were reported in 12 EU countries and 53 in five non‐EU countries through ADIS. In relation to wild birds, samples from 36,668 animals were submitted via the AIDC and 6375 via OH and SENTINEL, yielding a total of 1317 HPAIV detections, primarily in waterfowl. Twenty‐three EU countries and eight non‐EU countries reported a total of 910 outbreaks through ADIS. Mammal surveillance included 89 AIDC and 8960 OH samples. Four HPAIV detections were reported via AIDC, while additional virological and serological positives, mainly in carnivores, were identified in OH programme.Keywords: 2024, avian influenza virus, highly pathogenic, HPAI, low pathogenic, LPAI, poultry population, serology, virology, wild birds

SUMMARY

All EU Member States (MSs) are required to implement surveillance programmes for avian influenza virus (AIV) in both poultry and wild birds, collectively referred to as the Union Surveillance Programme (USP), in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/429 (‘Animal Health Law’). The implementing provisions are detailed in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/690 and Annex II of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/689.

This report is primarily based on data collected under the USP, which, since 2023, may also include surveillance data on mammals. The data are submitted to the European Commission (EC) within the Avian Influenza Data Collection (AIDC). In addition to EU MSs, data are also provided by European Free Trade Association countries (Iceland, Norway, Switzerland), EU candidate countries (Georgia, North Macedonia) and other countries in Europe or at the Europe–Asia interface, including the United Kingdom (Northern Ireland), 1 Ukraine, Moldova and Türkiye.

Data coverage varies across countries: regarding poultry, some reported exclusively on active surveillance, while others also included results from passive surveillance, in a few cases limited to positive or negative findings.

Additional data sources include the One Health (OH) surveillance programmes and the SENTINEL project. The OH surveillance programmes are grant‐funded initiatives aimed at monitoring emerging and re‐emerging zoonotic pathogens. In 2024, the SENTINEL project involved active surveillance of wild birds at seven key migration sites within and beyond Europe, coordinated by 14 participating countries.

The USP applies risk‐based surveillance, aiming to: (i) enable early detection of highly pathogenic influenza A viruses (HPAIV) in poultry and wild birds; (ii) detect HPAIV in poultry species showing limited or no clinical signs; (iii) identify circulating low pathogenic influenza A viruses (LPAIV) with potential for inter‐flock transmission; and (iv) enhance understanding of the role of wild birds in AIV spread and the zoonotic potential of both HPAIV and LPAIV.

Risk‐based sampling strategies differ across countries, resulting in variations in the proportion of positive cases among species or production types. Such data are not directly comparable between regions and cannot be used to estimate prevalence or incidence without considering the specific design of each national surveillance plan. Observed differences between species, countries or years therefore reflect methodological diversity rather than disparities in quality or effort.

Since the adoption of the SIGMA data model for reporting, poultry establishments (PEs) can be consistently tracked using unique establishment identifiers. This enables the identification of multiple visits to the same PE within a given year. A visit is defined as one or more sampling events carried out within a 30‐day period; sampling events separated by more than 30 days are treated as independent visits. Consequently, analyses focus on visits rather than individual establishments, providing a more accurate representation of surveillance intensity and outcomes.

In total, 276,621 records were submitted via AIDC, 16,755 under the OH surveillance programmes and 9804 through the SENTINEL project. In addition, 204,851 poultry population records were voluntarily provided by several countries to support data interpretation and contextual analysis.

POULTRY

Surveillance overview

Under the AIDC, 27,739 PEs were visited across 31 reporting countries (RCs), yielding 40,555 sampling sessions and 218,667 samples. The number of PEs visited varied widely, from 16 in Belgium to 15,204 in Romania. On average, 13.9 birds were sampled per session (range: 1.5–38.6), reflecting heterogeneous national sampling strategies.

Findings reported under the AIDC

Serological testing identified influenza A(H5) antibodies in 48 visits across six countries (Bulgaria, France, Netherlands, Poland, Spain and Sweden) and A(H7) antibodies in one visit (Spain). Influenza A antibodies were detected in 115 visits reported by six countries, with laying hens and growers most frequently affected.

Of 5157 visits involving virological testing, 235 yielded positive results. Out of these virological positive results pathogenicity results were reported for 107 detections of highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (HPAIV) and 53 of low pathogenic avian influenza virus (LPAIV; 38 confirmed, 15 inferred by subtype). In 33 cases, the test detected H5 or H7 antigens, but the results on the pathogenicity were not reported. In 42 cases results were reported on the detection of generic Influenza A viruses, but no additional information was reported. HPAIV detections were reported by seven countries (Germany, Iceland, Italy, Norway, Poland, Slovakia and Sweden) and LPAIV by another seven (Bulgaria, Finland, France, Italy, Lithuania, Netherlands and Romania); Italy was the only country reporting both. All but one HPAIV case (an H5N5 detection in Iceland) were subtype H5N1.

HPAIV detections peaked during the first 10 weeks of the year, while LPAIV detections were more evenly distributed, showing minor peaks in early, mid and late 2024. Most positive cases were reported under official EU surveillance, with additional contributions from private industry schemes and Iceland's national surveillance programme.

Outbreaks in and outside the EU as reported to ADIS?

A total of 394 outbreaks were reported within the EU and 66 in non‐EU countries. Fifteen EU countries (Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Sweden, Slovakia) and six non‐EU countries (Albania, Iceland, Moldova, North Macedonia, Norway, Türkiye) were affected.

An HPAI H7N5 outbreak in German laying hens was notable, likely originating from an initially low‐pathogenic strain circulating on the same farm. Outbreaks followed the typical seasonal pattern of avian influenza, with increased incidence during winter and an inter‐epidemic period over summer.

CAPTIVE BIRDS

Surveillance overview

Under the AIDC, nine RCs conducted surveillance in captive bird establishments, comprising 296 sampling sessions across 263 establishments, with a total of 1820 samples collected. On average, 9.5 birds were sampled per session.

Findings reported under the AIDC

Serological testing was carried out by four countries (Romania, Spain, Ireland and Norway), accounting for 143 visits. Only one seropositive result was reported – by Ireland – for antibodies against H5 antigens.

Virological testing was conducted by nine countries during 133 visits. Eight positive detections were reported: seven HPAIV and one LPAIV. HPAIV detections occurred in Slovenia (1), Norway (1), Slovakia (2) and Poland (3), while Spain reported the single LPAIV case. Of the HPAIV detections, one sample from Norway was subtyped as H5N5; all others were H5N1. The category most frequently affected was ‘own consumption’ (five cases).

HPAIV‐positive establishments were detected mainly at the beginning and end of the year, consistent with the seasonal pattern of avian influenza.

Outbreaks in and outside the EU as reported to ADIS

A total of 90 outbreaks were reported in captive bird establishments across 12 EU countries (Austria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia and Spain).

An additional 53 outbreaks were reported by five non‐EU countries (Moldova, North Macedonia, Norway, Türkiye and Ukraine).

As observed in poultry, outbreaks followed the expected influenza seasonality, with peaks during the colder months and a marked inter‐epidemic phase in summer.

WILD BIRDS

AIDC

Across RCs, 36,668 wild birds were sampled, about one‐third belonging to the waterfowl group. Sampling effort varied widely, from 22 birds in Malta to 20,354 in Italy.

Serological testing was limited to Romania (60 birds) and Switzerland (1 bird), with all samples testing negative.

Virological testing involved 36,607 samples, over half from Italy. In total, 1977 birds tested positive: 1286 for HPAIV, 638 for LPAIV (225 confirmed, 413 inferred by subtype) and 53 subtyped as H5 or H7 with unknown pathogenicity.

One Health Surveillance

Under OH surveillance, 4480 wild birds were sampled.

Belgium and Norway conducted serology (94 and 50 birds, respectively): seven Norwegian shorebirds (14%) tested positive for H5 antibodies, while Belgium detected AIV antibodies in 13 raptors, five shorebirds and two songbirds.

Virological testing of 4335 birds (Germany 4119; Belgium 120; Norway 96) detected 16 HPAI‐positive birds – 15 H5N1 in Germany and one H5N5 in Norway.

SENTINEL project

The SENTINEL project tested 1946 wild birds across 12 countries, mostly waterfowl (80%). Serological tests (Belgium, Norway) were all negative.

Virological testing of 8880 samples identified 15 HPAIV detections, including two H5N1 and one H5. In 12 cases the subtype was not reported.

Combined data

When combining AIDC, OH and SENTINEL data, only Greece, Northern Ireland, Luxembourg and Malta reported no positive wild bird detections.

Most positives were found in waterfowl (1109), followed by unspecified species (691). Woodpeckers were least affected (three detections). Waterfowl also accounted for the highest number of HPAIV positives (460).

Positive detections followed a seasonal pattern, with LPAIV increases preceding winter HPAIV peaks, while overall sampling remained stable throughout the year.

Outbreaks in and outside the EU as reported in ADIS

A total of 863 outbreaks were reported across 23 EU countries, and 47 outbreaks by eight non‐EU countries. As observed in poultry and captive birds, cases showed the typical winter seasonality of influenza.

MAMMALS

AIDC

Three countries collected a total of 89 samples from 41 animals, representing two Orders. No serological investigations were conducted in mammals. Four mammals in Norway tested positive for HPAIV, all of which were identified as the H5N5 subtype.

OH surveillance

A total of 8960 mammals were sampled under the OH surveillance programmes, yielding 11,079 samples in total. The majority of sampled mammals (62%, n = 5548) belonged to the order Carnivora.

Serological testing was conducted by seven countries – Belgium, Denmark, Germany, Ireland, Netherlands, Slovenia and Sweden – on 4199 animals/samples. Slovenia, Sweden, Ireland and Denmark reported a combined total of 11 positive results for antibodies against H5 antigens, while Sweden additionally detected antibodies against H7 in one animal.

Virological testing was performed by nine countries – Belgium, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Latvia, Slovenia, Netherlands and Sweden – on 5411 animals/samples. Positive virological results were reported by Germany, Sweden, Slovenia and Netherlands. The HPAIV detected in Slovenia was subtyped as H5N1.

INTRODUCTION

The pathogen

Avian influenza (AI) is a contagious viral disease caused by a virus from the Orthomyxoviridae family, primarily affecting poultry and wild water birds. Based on the surface glycoproteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), influenza A viruses (AIVs) are classified into 16 HA subtypes (H1–H16) and 9 NA subtypes (N1–N9). In contrast, the internal genes, such as the matrix (M) and nucleoprotein (NP) genes, are much more conserved across different influenza A viruses and therefore show little antigenic variation.

AIVs are classified as either highly pathogenic (HPAIV) or low pathogenic (LPAIV) based on their molecular characteristics and their ability to cause disease and mortality in chickens. In poultry, LPAIV infections often cause mild respiratory signs or may remain asymptomatic, whereas HPAIV, particularly in chickens and turkeys, typically results in severe disease and high mortality. Poultry with LPAI may show mild or no symptoms, while HPAI causes severe illness and death. Both spread quickly through flocks, so strict biosecurity measures are crucial. LPAI can mutate into highly pathogenic strains, making surveillance on LPAIV strains vital.

In poultry production systems, this potential for mutation is of great concern, as it can lead to sudden outbreaks of HPAI. So far, only subtypes H5 and H7 have mutated to become HPAIV.

Transmission

Recently, HPAIVs have increasingly affected wild bird species, and now also appear to be adapting to mammals. Nonetheless, they remain primarily adapted to avian hosts. Animal‐to‐human transmission has occurred occasionally. Humans can become infected after exposure to infected animals or contaminated environments.

HPAI overview in Europe between 2016 and 2023

HPAI has shown a dynamic pattern in Europe and beyond over the past decade, with changing subtypes, host ranges and epidemiological features. The epidemic of 2016–2017 began in October 2016, when H5N8 was first detected in a wild bird in Hungary, later spreading across 19 EU Member States (MSs), particularly affecting duck and goose farms. By spring 2017 the outbreaks had declined, though sporadic detections continued in Italy and Bulgaria until the end of the year, when H5N6 appeared in a few holdings.

In 2018, H5N6 was again confirmed in wild birds across northern and western Europe, persisting at low levels until September, while outbreaks of H5N8 in Bulgarian poultry extended into December.

In 2019, only a few poultry cases were reported in Bulgaria, while H5N6 remained confined to wild birds. The following year saw two epidemic waves: an early one caused by H5N8 in poultry across central and eastern Europe from December 2019 to March 2020, and a second beginning in October 2020 with large numbers of infected migratory wild birds and outbreaks in poultry across western and northern Europe. This epidemic continued into 2021, but from the second quarter the situation improved, although wild bird infections persisted through the summer.

The 2021–2022 epidemic proved the most severe ever recorded in the EU, dominated by H5N1, with additional detections of H5N8 and H5N5. During the 2022 summer, seabird colonies suffered massive mortality and poultry outbreaks were difficult to control in high‐density farming areas until the situation gradually improved later in the year.

The 2022–2023 season started in September 2022 and extended through 2023, affecting 26 MSs. While less severe for poultry than the previous season, wild bird cases increased and from October 2022 H5N1 was increasingly observed in mammals, including wild carnivores, fur farm animals, marine mammals and pets, though still infrequently. In May/June 2023, black‐headed gulls (Chroicocephalus ridibundus), and end of October 2023, common cranes (Grus grus) were particularly affected. It was not clearly defined if the reason for these peaks of infection was due to viral introduction by migratory wild birds or the result of the over‐summering of the pathogen.

Expansion in the range of host species

In late 2021, a new reassortant of clade 2.3.4.4b H5 HPAI A/goose/Guangdong/1/96 (GsGd) lineage, emerged in Euroasia. Between 2021 and 2024, it spread to North America and, for the first time, South America and Antarctica. Besides infecting birds, this reassortant has infected numerous mammalian species since its emergence, including some species for the first time, such as cattle. In March 2024, H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b genotype B3.13 started spreading among dairy cattle in the USA. It has also been found in other ruminants, such as alpacas and goats. The US Department of Agriculture (USDA) confirmed the first detection of a second genotype, namely D1.1, until then found in poultry, wild birds and sporadically in other mammals, on January 31, 2025.

Between March 2024 and 6 June 2025, the HPAI H5N1 B3.13 virus was confirmed in 1074 dairy herds across 17 USA states, the majority (917) having been affected in 2024. Hence, the infection rate has been gradually slowing. In 2024, there was also a case in swine in a further state. 2 In March 2025, HPAI H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b was detected for the first time in a sheep in the United Kingdom.

The EU surveillance

Directive 2005/94/EC on the control of AI was the first to require all EU MSs to implement surveillance programmes in both poultry and wild birds (collectively referred to as the Union Surveillance Programme, USP). This directive was later repealed and replaced by Regulation (EU) 2016/429 (‘Animal Health Law, AHL’), 3 with its implementing rules set out in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/690 4 and in Annex II of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/689. 5

Under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/2002, 6 MSs shall submit to the European Commission (EC) every year data on the results of the implementation of their surveillance programmes. MSs shall submit the data electronically via the Animal Disease Information System (ADIS).

The objectives of the USP are to detect HPAI early in poultry and wild birds, detect HPAI in poultry species that generally do not show significant clinical signs, and to detect circulating LPAI that may easily spread between poultry flocks. Monitoring of LPAI focuses on areas with a high density of PEs, given the associated risk on mutations of LPAIV into HPAIV. USP data should also contribute to the knowledge on the role of wild birds in the spread of any AIV, and on HPAIV and LPAIV posing a potential zoonotic risk.

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/1798 7 states that the competent authorities within MSs ‘shall include in the targeted animal population [for surveillance] kept or wild animals of species that are not listed for the purpose of the relevant listed disease when the competent authority considers that they constitute a risk for animal and human health’. Hence, this regulation also sets the legal framework for AI surveillance in mammals.

Surveillance activities required after confirmation of a category A disease outbreak (such as HPAI) is regulated in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/687. 8

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

In 2017, EFSA received a mandate with Terms of Reference to “collect, collate, validate, analyse and summarise in an annual report the results from avian influenza surveillance carried out by Member States in poultry and wild birds.” In the context of Article 31 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, 9 from 2018 onwards, EFSA was requested to provide technical and scientific assistance to the EC to deliver on this mandate. This implies that EFSA has been responsible for the annual surveillance report on AI since 2018. In addition, the collection and harmonisation of all data related to the surveillance activities taking place in MSs has been conducted by EFSA since January 2019.

Additional information

The main novelty of this report, compared to reports in previous years, is the inclusion of data originating from sources other than the surveillance activities performed under the USP. In this report EFSA summarises laboratory results originating from other types of surveillance activities reported to EFSA by EU MSs. European Food Trade Association (EFTA) countries, Northern Ireland (under the Agreement on the Withdrawal of the UK from the EU), and EU candidate countries also submitted data to EFSA on a voluntary basis.

Methodologies

Data manipulation

This year, with the adoption of the SIGMA data model for reporting, it was possible to track PEs using establishment IDs. This allowed the adoption of decision rules to extract conclusions at test level, animal level and establishment level, as follows:

Each test result is reported as a set of information (i.e. whether the test was serological or virological, the antigen targeted by the test, the outcome). The first algorithm combines all these variables to extract the information for that specific test (e.g. detection of antibodies against AIV, detection of Ag‐H5 antibodies, HPAIV, etc.)

Each test result is reported as a set of information (i.e. whether the test was serological or virological, the antigen targeted by the test, the outcome). The first algorithm combines all these variables to extract the information for that specific test (e.g. detection of antibodies against AIV, detection of Ag‐H5 antibodies, HPAIV, etc.)

More than one sample can be taken from each individual animal, and each sample can undergo more than one test. The second algorithm combines all test conclusions related to each individual animal (outcome of the first algorithm) to assess individually its status (e.g. H5 N1 HP, negative, etc.)

More than one sample can be taken from each individual animal, and each sample can undergo more than one test. The second algorithm combines all test conclusions related to each individual animal (outcome of the first algorithm) to assess individually its status (e.g. H5 N1 HP, negative, etc.)

In each establishment, at a certain moment in time, more than one bird can be sampled. The third algorithm combines all the conclusions on all birds (outcome of the second algorithm) sampled in each specific establishment on a given moment to assess its status. This last algorithm is relevant only for poultry and captive birds.

In each establishment, at a certain moment in time, more than one bird can be sampled. The third algorithm combines all the conclusions on all birds (outcome of the second algorithm) sampled in each specific establishment on a given moment to assess its status. This last algorithm is relevant only for poultry and captive birds.

In each step, a single positive result was sufficient to classify the element (animal or establishment) as positive. More clearly, an animal is positive to the parameter of interest (e.g. H5 antigens) if at least one test was positive. Similarly, an establishment is classified as positive to the parameter of interest if at least one bird was classified as positive for that parameter. In this way, each establishment is classified based on the full diagnostic pattern, including the serological tests and the virological tests conducted on all birds sampled.

To account for the possibility of multiple visits (i.e. an investigation by means of one or more sampling events on different dates, but epidemiologically related) to the same establishment throughout the year, the following time dimension was introduced: all samples collected within a window of 30 days 14 are considered to belong to the same visit (sampling events are grouped as the same visit if they happen less than 30 days apart). Sampling events conducted more than 30 days apart were treated as belonging to independent visits.

As a result, the focus shifts from counting positive establishments to counting visits . If, for example, an establishment underwent two visits and tested positive in both during the same surveillance year (e.g. positive to HPAI H5 in February and again in November), it contributes two cases to the total count.

The results of this report are divided in two groups: descriptive statistics describing each data collection individually (Chapter 3) and analyses performed combining all available data (Chapter 4). In Chapter 3, results are summarised by the source of data received by EFSA, the animal species and production types and by the type of results (serological and virological results).

For the combined analysis reported in Chapter 4, please note that the classification of the activity as ‘active’ or ‘passive’ is based solely on the values reported under the variable ‘Target Group’. The terminology used is not related in any way to the type of surveillance, commonly known as ‘active surveillance in poultry’ (when referring to planned activities) and ‘passive surveillance in poultry’ (when referring to sampling in connection to clinical suspicions). Laboratory results related to birds belonging to the category alive without clinical signs, alive, slaughtered, hunted, hunted without clinical signs, sentinel or trapped were classified as ACTIVE. When the birds belonged to the category hunted with clinical signs, alive with clinical signs, found dead, dead symptomatic, culled, RK (road/predator killed) or dead non‐symptomatic, the laboratory results were classified as PASSIVE (Table 2).

CONCLUSIONS

Poultry

In 2024, a total of 27,739 poultry establishments were sampled. Some establishments were sampled in several occasions during the year, resulting in 40,555 sampling events. A total of 218,667 samples were collected.

Overall, in poultry the sampling effort was broadly comparable to that of the previous year in most countries. Nonetheless, a substantial increase in sampling by a few reporting countries resulted in a higher overall number of samples compared with previous years. This apparent increase may also be partly attributable to the inclusion of data from surveillance components that were not reported in earlier years. As an example, nine MSs and three EFTA countries submitted data on both active and passive surveillance in poultry, including positive and negative results.

Different sampling strategies appear to have been implemented across countries. Nine reporting countries relied predominantly – or exclusively – on serological testing, while three focused mainly – or exclusively – on virological testing. The observed variability may also be related to differences in the poultry population structure among countries.

Regarding serological results in poultry, from the total 30,794 visits reported, 48 had seropositive results for influenza A(H5) viruses across six countries (Bulgaria, France, Netherlands, Poland, Spain and Sweden). One country (Spain) also reported a positive result for influenza A(H7) virus. Six countries reported 115 visits yielding a positive result for AIV antibodies. (Bulgaria, France, Italy, Norway, Spain and Sweden).

Among the total 5157 visits reported, 235 resulted in positive virological results – 107 for HPAIV and 53 for LPAIV (38 known to be LP and 15 assumed to be LP based on the subtype). Seven countries reported HPAIV detection (Germany, Iceland, Italy, Norway, Poland, Slovakia, Sweden) and seven reported LPAIV detection (Bulgaria, Finland, France, Italy, Lithuania, Netherlands, Romania). Only Italy reported both. Of the 107 HPAIVs positive results, only one – reported by Iceland – was subtyped as H5N5. All other HPAI cases were H5N1. Five RCs did not report results from virological testing: Austria, Belgium, Switzerland, Ireland and Malta. It is important to note that none of these RCs had seropositive results. Some RCs only reported virological positive results: Germany, Finland, Iceland, Italy, Lithuania, Norway, Romania, Slovakia.

Risk‐based sampling strategies differ across countries, resulting in variations in the proportion of positive cases among species or production types. Such data are not directly comparable between regions and cannot be used to estimate prevalence or incidence without considering the specific design of each national surveillance plan. Observed differences between species, countries or years therefore reflect methodological diversity rather than disparities in quality or effort.

The total number of outbreaks reported in ADIS in 2024 by EU and non‐EU countries was 394 and 66, respectively. Fifteen EU countries and six non‐EU countries were affected.

Of particular note is an outbreak of HPAI H7N5 in an establishment keeping laying hens in Germany, which likely emerged from a LPAI virus initially affecting the farm.

HPAIV detections peaked during the first 10 weeks of the year, while LPAIV detections were more evenly distributed, showing minor peaks in early, mid and late 2024.

Captive birds

Nine RCs reported sampling in establishments with captive birds. A total of 296 sampling events were conducted in 263 unique establishments, resulting in the collection of 1820 samples. On average, 9.51 birds were sampled per sampling event.

For the first time in this report, data related to the captive birds sector is presented separately from poultry.

Four countries conducted serological testing in captive bird establishments: Ireland, Norway, Romania and Spain. Samples were collected in 143 visits. Of those, only one, reported by Ireland, yielded a positive result for antibodies against H5 antigens. Nine countries conducted 133 visits with virological testing in 263 captive bird establishments. Seven virological positive results were confirmed to be HPAIV (1 in SI, 1 in NO, 2 in SK and 3 in PL), and one was confirmed to be LP (Spain). Of the seven HPAI cases, one, reported by Norway, was subtyped as H5N5. All other HPAI cases were H5N1.

HPAIV‐positive establishments were detected mainly at the beginning and end of the year, consistent with the seasonal pattern of avian influenza.

Wild birds

Wild birds were sampled under different frameworks, i.e. the AIDC, One Health surveillance and the SENTINEL project, totalling 43,043 animals.

The amount of active surveillance data on wild birds increased compared with previous years, particularly during the second half of 2024. The SENTINEL project contributed significantly to this increase.

Four countries conducted serological testing yielding the detection of H5 antibodies in seven birds. Virological testing was performed in 38,986 birds, covering the entire European territory, when considering the contribution of the One Health surveillance and the SENTINEL project. A total of 1317 detections of highly pathogenic strains were reported by 24 countries. Most positives were found in waterfowl. Waterfowl also accounted for the highest number of HPAIV positives.

A total of 863 outbreaks were reported in ADIS across 23 EU countries, and 47 outbreaks by eight non‐EU countries.

As observed in poultry and captive birds, cases showed the typical winter seasonality of influenza, with LPAIV increases preceding winter HPAIV peaks.

Mammals

Regarding the surveillance sampling in mammals, three countries tested 41 animals in the context of the AIDC and 10 countries tested 8960 animals under the One Health surveillance, for a total of 9001 animals and 11,168 samples. Serological investigations in 4199 animals yielded 11 detections of H5 antibodies in 4 countries (Denmark, Ireland, Sweden and Slovenia) and 1 detection of H7 antibodies (Sweden). Virological testing was conducted on 5452 mammals yielding four detections of highly pathogenic H5N5 strains in Norway and one highly pathogenic H5N1 in Slovenia.

Surveillance activities targeting mammals were reported for the first time, reflecting both the evolving epidemiological situation and the implementation of the One Health Surveillance Direct Grant initiative. These data are essential to support further studies on the potential zoonotic behaviour of the virus.

Overall, passive surveillance continued to demonstrate its effectiveness in detecting highly pathogenic viruses across all bird categories (poultry, captive and wild). In contrast, active surveillance appeared to be more effective in detecting low pathogenic strains.
