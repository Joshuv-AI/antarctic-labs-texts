Mlan : Language-Based Instruction Tuning Improves Zero-Shot Generalization of Multimodal Large Language Models
Jianhong Tu, Zhuohao Ni, Nicholas Crispino, Zihao Yu, Michael Bendersky, Beliz Gunel, Ruoxi Jia, Xin Liu, Lingjuan Lyu, Dawn Song, Chenguang Wang

Abstract
We present a novel instruction tuning recipe to improve the zero-shot task generalization of multimodal large language models. In contrast to existing instruction tuning mechanisms that heavily rely on visual instructions, our approach focuses on language-based instruction tuning, offering a distinct and more training efficient path for multimodal instruction tuning. We evaluate the performance of the proposed approach on 9 unseen datasets across both language and vision modalities. Our results show that our language-only instruction tuning is able to significantly improve the performance of two pretrained multimodal models based on Llama 2 and Vicuna on those unseen datasets. Interestingly, the language instruction following ability also helps unlock the models to follow vision instructions without explicit training. Compared to the state of the art multimodal instruction tuning approaches that are mainly based on visual instructions, our language-based method not only achieves superior performance but also significantly enhances training efficiency. For instance, the language-only instruction tuning produces competitive average performance across the evaluated datasets (with even better performance on language datasets) with significant training efficiency improvements (on average 4x), thanks to the striking reduction in the need for vision data. With a small number of visual instructions, this emerging language instruction following ability transfers well to the unseen vision datasets, outperforming the state of the art with greater training efficiency.

1
Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLM) have advanced and enabled a wide range of vision-language tasks such as visual question answering and image captioning
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Alayrac et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023b
;
Lin et al. 2023
.
Their zero-shot generalization ability to unseen tasks (tasks not included in the training) has the potential to further improve broader real world applications
Driess et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023a
.
However, these MLLMs’ zero-shot generalization ability on unseen tasks (including language or vision tasks) as well as the efficiency (e.g., training efficiency) of such generalization, has been underexplored.

In this work, we introduce a novel language-based instruction tuning method,
Mlan
, to improve the zero-shot task generalization of MLLMs (Figure
1
). By equipping multimodal models with strong language instruction following ability, we can significantly improve their generalization ability to language tasks. Furthermore, since pretrained MLLMs lack strong instruction following skills, this emerging language instruction following ability will also transfer to the vision modality. In essence, our approach is grounded in the principle of viewing language as the foundation, not only in the design of MLLMs but also throughout the instruction tuning process.
Specifically, we devise a new instruction tuning recipe that enables models to learn the foundation instruction following ability through language instructions. This then triggers the models to also follow visual instructions without or with only a small amount of vision instruction data, which in return improves the training efficiency of our method.

This idea is motivated by two lines of work. (1) Language instruction tuning
Wei et al. 2022
;
Sanh et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022
. Unlike the existing instruction tuning which only focused on the instruction following ability of large language models (LLM), we explore the language instruction following ability’s impact on both the language and vision modalities. Existing instruction tuning often involves three steps: reformat existing datasets with instructions, finetune LLMs on the collection of formatted datasets, and test the finetuned models on unseen datasets. Due to the efficient nature of language, this line of instruction tuning work was able to scale to include a relatively large amount of tasks during the training. We follow this general design to reformat language datasets with instructions, which enables us to scale our instruction tuning to a wide range of language datasets for MLLMs. (2) Visual instruction tuning
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
. The existing instruction tuning for multimodal models is vision based. Such work heavily relies on visual instruction data or pretraining multimodal models with a language focused training phase to enable vision instruction following. In comparison, we explore bringing vision instruction following ability based on the foundation language instruction following ability. A notable downside of vision-based instruction tuning is its cost due to the inclusion of visual data, resulting in significantly lower training efficiency (e.g., 4x less than language instruction tuning based on our investigation). To add stronger vision instruction following ability to the models, we follow the general pipeline of visual instruction tuning work but involve significantly less vision data to mitigate the training efficiency issue. Roughly, our approach is a combination of these two lines of instruction tuning studies, where the language instruction tuning enables the method to generalize to more tasks and the visual instruction tuning helps the method to transfer across different modalities.
The design of our language-based instruction tuning is important: we use language to learn the foundation instruction following ability for MLLMs, then transfer this ability to vision using optional visual instructions. By instruction tuning with language data, our method unlocks language instruction following ability and allows enhancing vision instruction following ability of MLLMs in a training efficient manner.

We demonstrate that our method is both effective and training efficient. We evaluate the performance on 9 unseen tasks across language and vision modalities. We pretrain two MLLMs based on base LLMs including Llama 2
Touvron et al. 2023
and Vicuna
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chiang et al. 2023
following the state of the art multimodal training mechanisms
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Liu et al. 2023a
. We then apply our instruction tuning method to the MLLMs and observe the following key insights. (1) Our foundation language-based instruction tuning (with language-only instruction data) significantly outperforms the pretrained models by an average of 15.4% across both unseen language and vision tasks. Surprisingly, this language instruction following ability transfers effectively to the vision modality. It outperforms the pretrained models by a large margin (10.4%) on the vision tasks, and its improvements on the language tasks, as expected, are significant (19.1%). (2) When compared to the state of the art multimodal instruction tuning, our base method (with language-only instruction tuning) performs on par with the multimodal instruction tuning recipe of LLaVA
Liu et al. 2023a
and Cambrian-1
Tong et al. 2024
on average when all are trained with our dataset and evaluated. In particular, we improve the performance on the language datasets by 3.8% and 1.4% compared to LLaVA and Cambrian-1 recipes, respectively. Importantly, our method improves their training efficiency by 4.6x and 3.9x by reducing the training tokens by 78% and 74%, respectively. (3) To achieve optimal performance in both worlds (language and vision tasks), our method only needs to trade a small portion (e.g., approximately 10% for Llama 2) of the language instruction data for vision instructions. This setting outperforms state of the art LLaVA
Liu et al. 2023a
(3.7% and 0.8% on language and vision tasks, respectively) and Cambrian-1’s instruction tuning recipe (1.4% and 1.4% on language and vision tasks, respectively) with a significant improvement in training efficiency (3.1x and 2.6x respectively).
Overall, our results show that language is the key ingredient for enabling stronger zero-shot generalization in MLLMs. As a more efficient representation compared to images, language allows models to generalize across a wider range of tasks and modalities. This aligns with the findings behind the success of LLMs, but we extend this insight to the instruction tuning of MLLMs.
We hope our findings will foster future research on language centered training and instruction tuning, paving the way for fundamental advancements in large foundation models.

2
Approach

The goal of our language-based instruction tuning method,
Mlan
, is to improve the zero-shot generalization of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Our approach enables MLLMs to generalize to more unseen datasets by leveraging language instructions and then transferring this generalization ability to the vision modality with optional vision-language instructions. We follow the standard design of existing instruction tuning methods
Wei et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023b
consisting of three steps: selecting training data and formatting the data with instructions (Sec.
2.1
), finetuning a pretrained MLLM on the training set (Sec.
2.2
), and evaluating the instruction tuned model on unseen (held-out) datasets (Sec.
2.3
).
The key difference of
Mlan
lies in its improved zero-shot performance and training efficiency.

2.1
Training Task Selection

Though MLLMs can still process language, such language understanding is grounded in images. This suggests current approaches to multimodal instruction tuning could be underutilizing the full potential of language.
While using images during instruction tuning is different from language-based approaches, there still exist situations where images and textual contexts are incorporated in a similar manner.
For example, the general idea of answering questions from paragraphs as in SQuAD
(
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
)
, or asking about direct facts about an image as in VQAv2
(
Goyal et al. 2017
)
is the same.
Both need to understand the context, and then extract the relevant answer, regardless of whether the context is language or originally an image.
Figure
2
displays two similar examples, one from SQuAD and one from VQAv2. For SQuAD, the answer is obtained by extracting the last line of the long paragraph context referencing “fireside chats”. For VQAv2, the answer is obtained by understanding the central part of the image with the man throwing a baseball and wearing a baseball glove.
In both cases, the MLLM is required to consider what exists in a certain part of the context and formulate a succinct answer.

Figure 3:

Training datasets included for instruction tuning. These datasets are first placed in instruction following format, then are sampled to instruction tune the pretrained models. In
Mlan
, we start by sampling from all language datasets and optionally sample from a small amount of the vision tasks.
White denotes that a dataset belongs to the commonsense reasoning task, while yellow denotes the question answering task.

Motivated by such tasks shared across modalities, we curate a training dataset such that each collection of vision datasets corresponding to a specific task is similar in nature to some collection of language datasets, which we say correspond to that same task.
We consider two types of tasks: question answering and commonsense reasoning.
In total, we select 5 language datasets and 2 vision-language datasets for training (Figure
3
).
Inspired by InstructBLIP
Dai et al. 2023
and FLAN
Wei et al. 2022
, we manually craft 20 natural language instructions and a template for each task to convert every dataset into instruction format. Then, we use this format for both model training and evaluation.
With this carefully constructed training setup, we expect the transfer of the language instruction following ability to the vision modality, while improving the training efficiency as the high fixed token count of images is no longer required.

2.2
Training Details

Models and Multimodal Pretraining

We follow the architecture design of LLaVA
Liu et al. 2023a
that connects a visual encoder with a projector that enables the LLM to use the outputs of the visual encoder to process image inputs in addition to texts.
We choose CLIP-ViT-L/14@336
Radford et al. 2021
and a two-layer MLP with GELU activation as the visual encoder and the projector, respectively. We select the base LLMs as Llama 2 7B
Touvron et al. 2023
and Vicuna 7B
Zheng et al. 2023
.
We conduct multimodal pretraining for both models on LLaVA-Pretrain-595K using the same hyper-parameters as in
Liu et al. 2023a
. These models are then finetuned on our language-heavy training dataset for one epoch using a global batch size of 64, a cosine learning schedule, a learning rate of 2e-5, a warm-up ratio of 0.03, and no weight decay.
Both the visual encoder and LLM are frozen throughout the pretraining session while the parameters in the MLP projector are updated. After pretraining, the visual encoder and the projector function as a visual tokenizer that turns an image into tokens compatible with the LLM.

Instruction Tuning

To test our instruction tuning methodology, we finetune two MLLM checkpoints with data samples entirely from language datasets.
The key reason for this is that language is still central to interacting with MLLMs. Language, rather than vision, remains the primary medium for users to interact with models when they specify their needs.
Thus, with
Mlan
we propose to create a strong language base through language-only finetuning, and we show that it unlocks zero-shot generalization across modalities.
Though language data is the most important aspect of instruction tuning, exposure to vision-language data is still beneficial to overcome distributional shifts and improve robustness.
This encourages us to define a separate version of our method called
\textsc{Mlan}_{v}
, which shifts the sampling regime such that vision-language data now has a nonzero probability of being sampled for training.
However, a key difference between our method and the traditional vision-language instruction tuning framework is that, with the help of the foundation instruction following ability from language, we only need a small amount of vision-language training data compared to all instances the model is trained on. This further adapts our MLLM to handle multimodal instances effectively. Importantly, it maintains a strong language base and a low training cost while benefiting from the interaction between training data across modalities.
Most existing approaches work the other way around
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Tong et al. 2024
)
, including language data to supplement vision-based multimodal instruction tuning.
However, the conventional approach requires many more training tokens and relies on a greater number of vision-language datasets. Our method anchors in a shift in perspective, treating
language as the foundation
not only in constructing the MLLMs but also during its instruction tuning.
In practice, the base LLM is always updated, while the projector is only updated if there are vision-language instances in the training set.
More information about our instruction tuning method and the recipes chosen for each model are in Appendix
B
.

3
Experiments

In this section, we show that
Mlan
is both more effective and training efficient compared to the pretrained MLLMs as well as state of the art multimodal instruction tuning recipes across all the tasks we evaluate. With visual instruction tuning,
Mlan
v
generalizes better between language and vision modalities.

3.1
Main Results

We compare various instruction tuning methods built upon the multimodal pretrained Llama 2 and Vicuna.
We include the following settings:
(1) Pretrain: The MLLM after multimodal pretraining with no instruction tuning.
(2) LLaVA and Cambrian-1: We use the state-of-the-art multimodal instruction tuning recipes of LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
and Cambrian-1
(
Tong et al. 2024
)
on our training dataset, with 6% and 25% language instruction data respectively.
(3)
Mlan
and
Mlan
v
: Our language-based instruction tuning method, the former having language-only datasets and the latter with a small amount of vision-language data substituted in, roughly 12.5% of the
total
instruction tuning data used by the model.

Zero-shot Task Generalization

We report the scores of pretrained MLLM and instruction tuned models on 9 benchmarks in Table
1
, with average scores visualized in Figure
5
. Compared to Pretrain models,
Mlan
successfully boosts the language performance by up to 27.6% and 10.5% for Llama 2 and Vicuna, respectively. Expectedly, our models obtain the best average language scores among all instruction tuned models. More interestingly, although they are not explicitly trained on vision data,
Mlan
unlocks zero-shot abilities in vision datasets, where our models not only beat the comparison methods but also perform competitively to models heavily trained on vision data. For example, Llama 2 with
Mlan
achieves 52.7% accuracy on AI2D, surpassing our LLaVA by 4.2%. Our results align with the intuition that stronger language instruction following abilities often generalize to vision-language tasks
Liu et al. 2023a
. Another piece of evidence is the elevated scores established by the pretrained Vicuna model compared to the pretrained Llama 2, the former of which’s unimodal language instruction following abilities can be favorably transferred to multimodal tasks without cost.

Zero-shot Modality Generalization

The efficacy of language-to-vision transfer can be further enhanced by mixing a relatively small portion of vision data, as evidenced by our
Mlan
v
models. While achieving similar language performance, they see a significant boost in vision scores. Both models trained in this approach perform the same as their best vision-heavy comparison methods. Particularly, the Vicuna-based
Mlan
v
model slightly outperforms our LLaVA by 0.3% on average on vision datasets. Such performance gain is achieved while maintaining similar performance on language datasets, and thus, the
Mlan
v
models yield the best overall average scores.

3.2
Training Efficiency

A major advantage of our method is that it significantly reduces the computational cost measured by the number of training tokens processed by the base LLM compared to vision-based instruction tuning. Table
2
details the number of training tokens, including those in the visual prefix. Including visual inputs dramatically increases the training burden, with the heaviest vision-based instruction tuning setting, LLaVA, requiring the LLM to process nearly 4 times more tokens than
Mlan
. While
Mlan
v
demands vision data, it still saves more than about 10 million tokens and achieves better performance. Therefore, the vision instruction following abilities can be efficiently learned by enabling the transfer of strong language instruction following abilities.

Figure 6:
Average scores on Llama 2 based MLLMs with respect to the percentage of language data mixed in. For a given percentage, we allocate that much of the training budget to language-only data, while vision-language data fill in the rest of the training dataset.

To uncover the fundamental mechanism behind the improved vision instruction following without much explicit training, we vary the proportion of language instruction tuning data, incrementing 12.5% at a time. We show the performance of Llama 2 based models with different amounts of language instruction data in Figure
6
. While the average scores on language datasets remain high after including 37.5% language data, the average scores on vision datasets are the best from 25% to 75%. Notably, the fact that using all vision data does not yield the best vision performance underscores the significance of foundational language capabilities in addressing visual tasks. Moreover, we notice that, from using 0% to 12.5% vision data, the vision performance trend shows the greatest increase compared to other intervals. This trend suggests that transferring language abilities is very efficient and takes only a small portion of complementary vision data, and also that the return diminishes as we add in more visual data. 3.3
Data Budget Scaling

Figure 7:
Average scores with respect to the percentage of language training data instances, scaling from 0% to 100% of our budget.

We then conduct scaling experiments by decreasing the number of instances in the training dataset to study the performance of language-only instruction tuning under different training budgets.
The new datasets contain random samples from the original language datasets.
Figure
7
shows the scaling results.
The performance on all datasets increases by the largest margin from no instruction tuning to using 25% of the data and gradually saturates afterward. Therefore, using 80k instances is sufficient for our evaluation suite. It further shows that language-based instruction tuning is sample-efficient. In principle, we may further reduce the training budget without significantly hurting the performance, a similar finding to
Liu et al. 2023a
.

3.4
Dataset Diversity Analysis

Figure 8:
Distribution of the cosine similarity of random question pairs sampled in the language and vision-language settings. The stars (***) indicate significant differences
(p<0.001)
between the mean similarity supported by the z-test.

Previously, in Llama 2 based models, while our
Mlan
v
model achieves the same high performance on vision datasets, the vision-based comparison methods fall behind on language datasets by up to 4.2%. This observation suggests that language favorably transfers to the vision domain, but the converse is not true.
We hypothesize this is due to the varied diversity of the datasets.
Therefore, we employ a SentenceTransformer model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
, to encode the question into high-dimensional embeddings and compute the cosine similarity between 10k question pairs sampled in three settings: both from vision-language data, both from language data, one from language data and the other one from vision-language data.
Figure
8
reports the mean cosine similarities. The vision data appears more homogeneous, with a higher mean, while the language data is more diverse.
Additionally, the mean across language and vision-language datasets is on par with the mean on language datasets.
This could imply that language data, at least in our training data, better generalizes to vision datasets thanks to greater heterogeneity.

Related Work

Multimodal large language models (MLLM) are language models endowed with the ability to use multiple modalities, such as images, videos, and audio
(
OpenAI 2024
;
MetaAI 2024
;
Team et al. 2023
;
OpenAI et al. 2024
;
Rubenstein et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Ataallah et al. 2024
)
.
The most widely adopted are vision enhanced LLMs, where many design choices are already extensively studied
Liu et al. 2023a
;
McKinzie et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Laurençon et al. 2024
;
Tong et al. 2024
;
Karamcheti et al. 2024
.
A prevalent approach to building such MLLMs links pretrained visual encoders
(
Radford et al. 2021
;
Oquab et al. 2023
)
to LLMs
(
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chiang et al. 2023
)
via an adapter, thus transforming deep image features into soft prompts for the base LLM.
In our work, we focus on one of the simplest yet high-performing and widely adopted MLLMs, using only a multi-layer perceptron as the adapter
(
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Driess et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Zeng et al. 2024
)
.
Compared to existing MLLM studies, our focus is to further finetune these models for better zero-shot task generalization across modalities.

Inspired by the success of instruction tuning in LLMs in zero-shot generalization
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023c
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, following a pretraining step for vision-language feature alignment, there is a multimodal instruction tuning step to improve zero-shot performance on multimodal tasks
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024c
)
.
Notably, InstructBLIP
(
Dai et al. 2023
)
and LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023b
)
transform existing datasets into data with multimodal instructions using manual templates and synthetic data, a practice adopted by or expanded upon in subsequent work
(
Tong et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
.
Further work investigates how instruction tuning varies under different settings, e.g., how different components of the MLLM should learn differently during instruction tuning
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
and how instruction tuning works in a continual learning setting with many new tasks
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
.

Though the primary goal of multimodal instruction tuning is to improve vision-language performance with vision-based data, language data is often included in both pretraining
(
McKinzie et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
and finetuning
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Huang et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
;
Dai et al. 2024
)
to prevent catastrophic forgetting and improve language performance.
Many such papers disregard the impact of finetuning with language data on vision performance, focusing solely on language performance when ablating language data away, though there are notable exceptions
(
Huang et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2023
;
Ye et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2024
)
.
In these cases, there is modest evidence of transfer ability between modalities, where finetuning on both language and vision data exhibits about equal or better performance than training on one modality alone.
However, in each of these works that finetune with language data alongside vision data, this performance boost is achieved by increasing the dataset size without consideration of how such data will increase the training cost (except
Tong et al. 2024
, who experiment with different mixtures but do not greatly vary the language data or hold the composition of the vision data constant while varying the language data).
Hence even though better performance is obtained when increasing the dataset size to train on language data, the instruction tuning step is more costly.
Due to the general cost of instruction tuning a MLLM, there have been many approaches aiming to decrease the cost of instruction tuning in the multimodal setting. These primarily include using lightweight adapters to decrease the number of parameters
(
Luo et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
and choosing a subset of the training data using the MLLM itself or other models
(
Chen et al. 2024c
;
Wei et al. 2023
;
Lee et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
.

A simpler way to decrease the cost is to instruction tune exclusively on language data.
This is unexplored in the simple MLLM adapter paradigm, with
Huang et al. 2023
being the only model that uses language alone, though it has an extensive pretraining step that differs from most common approaches.
Even then, there are no experiments combining the language data with any amount of vision instruction data, so the performance and cost savings of a language-based instruction tuning setup are both unknown.
Since training on language instruction data is cheaper than training on the same number of vision instances, and language is foundational to the functioning of MLLMs, we propose a novel method that anchors instruction tuning on language to greatly decrease training cost while increasing zero-shot generalizability for both language and vision tasks, especially with the addition of a small amount of vision data.

5
Conclusion

We present
Mlan
, a language-based multimodal instruction tuning strategy for MLLMs to enhance zero-shot abilities and enable transfer to multimodal tasks.
We empirically demonstrate that language instruction following abilities are foundational to MLLMs, even when solving vision tasks.
Crucially, language-based instruction tuning improves performance in both modalities while reducing the need for expensive image inputs.
We find that our method improves upon both the pretrained MLLMs and the instruction tuning recipe of state of the art models
(
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Tong et al. 2024
)
, which utilize a majority of vision data.
Language-only instruction tuning with
Mlan
performs well, but when we replace a small amount (around 10%) of our language-only training data with vision-language data, we improve on vision-language tasks at improved training efficiency.
With our method, we hope to bring attention to the importance of language in MLLMs, specifically in instruction tuning.
With this intuition, we believe future work can curate more language-based data for multimodal instruction tuning that further improves not only language, but also vision performance.

6
Limitations

Our experiments are performed on models with the same multimodal architecture and pretraining procedure, not accounting for more advanced architecture or large-scale multimodal pretraining. A complicating factor is that for most MLLMs, only the final model is released, which has already been finetuned, not the pretrained checkpoint.
Our analysis could also use experiments testing how instruction tuning varies when different tasks are trained on versus held-out, or on sequential finetuning versus sampling language-only and vision-language data together.
Furthermore, due to limited computation resources, the finetuning experiments have the same data budget of 80k instances, while existing fine-tuning data may contain hundreds of thousands or even multi-million instances.
Finally, though our experiments show the power of language-only instruction on a small scale, testing large-scale training and more diverse testing sets to see how
Mlan
scales is among future investigations.

Appendix A
Additional Implementation Details

For language-based instruction tuning, we use our carefully crafted dataset with tasks across modalities. To avoid data contamination, only the train split of each dataset is used for finetuning, and the test split, or the validation split if the test split is not publicly available, is reserved for evaluation.
Similar to various multimodal instruction tuning work
Xu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
, we select unseen datasets of both modalities for evaluation.
Though all datasets belong to our fixed task categories of question answering and commonsense reasoning, we focus on testing generalization across modalities.
Particularly, since the base LLMs are frozen during multimodal pretraining, the models finetuned with our method are not explicitly instruction tuned on image inputs or are only tuned using a small amount of images, making generalization to vision tasks more challenging.
Additionally, two datasets of note that were seen only during the evaluation, POPE and ScienceQA, evaluate hallucination and science knowledge, respectively.
These datasets belong to our tasks but are notably different due to their out of distribution focus relative to the other datasets within each respective task, allowing us to further test generalization abilities.

We maintain a fixed data budget of 80k instances throughout the training sessions. The question answering and commonsense reasoning tasks each contribute an equal number of instances. When the group of datasets from either task contains less than 40k instances, we fill them in with bootstrapped samples.
At the dataset level of each task, we uniformly sample from each dataset to prevent model over-fitting to a specific dataset. See Appendix
C.1
for more on how instances are sampled.
To extract answers more easily, we append an additional line reading "the direct answer is <answer>" to every training instance. The complete direct answering instruction sets are in Table
10
. After finetuning, the models can provide brief responses to be extracted as the predicted answer. The instructions are in Appendix
C.2
.

Appendix B
Additional Training Details

We finetune two pretrained MLLMs on the language-only data and denote them as
Mlan
, which is evaluated on the unseen datasets across modalities.
We further improve the generalization efficacy to vision tasks by substituting a small portion of language data with randomly sampled vision data, denoted as
Mlan
v
. For Llama 2, we substitute 12.5% of the language-only data for vision-language data in the sampling regime. Since Vicuna (we are using Vicuna-1.5) is already instruction tuned on an additional 257k ShareGPT data on top of Llama 2, we increase the proportion of vision data when working with Vicuna so that vision data roughly makes up 12.5% of the
total
instruction tuning data, including ShareGPT. To accomplish this, we fill 50% of our training data with samples from vision-language data.
Acknowledging the recent trend of including a small portion of language-only data into vision instruction tuning data, we establish two additional baselines by finetuning on two separate versions of our training dataset that contain only 6% and 25% language instruction data, similar to the ratios in
Liu et al. 2023a
and
Tong et al. 2024
. For a fair comparison, we limit the total number of training samples in all settings to 80k from our training data.

Appendix D
Additional Related Work

Our work focuses on choosing a simple multi-layer perception as the adapter in LLaVA
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Liu et al. 2023a
.
In contrast, BLIP-2
Li et al. 2023b
and Flamingo
Alayrac et al. 2022
design attention-based modules to attentively pool visual features, among a variety of other choices that combine existing methods or create new ones
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Laurençon et al. 2024
)
.
To train the model, most often there is a pretraining step focusing on aligning the multimodal features with a modality connector
(
Yin et al. 2023
)
, though some models are trained from scratch
(
Huang et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2024
)
.
A main design choice in MLLMs is whether to freeze or unfreeze the LLM during finetuning.
Unfreezing the LLM effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting by maintaining language-only performance
(
MetaAI 2024
;
Driess et al. 2023
;
Alayrac et al. 2022
)
, but results in worse vision-language performance
(
Lin et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2024
)
. In our work, we show that with an unfrozen LLM, training on a strong language-based dataset on a fixed data budget improves performance across modalities.
To evaluate MLLMs, there is a wide variety of vision-language tasks
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
)
. However, Cambrian-1
Tong et al. 2024
demonstrate that certain vision-language datasets, including some we used (AI2D and RealWorldQA), exhibit only a minor drop in performance of around 5% if vision is disabled, suggesting that current vision-language evaluations may be more language-focused. Though there is a need for more vision-centric analysis, this emphasizes how important language is in many vision tasks, a fact central to our work.
