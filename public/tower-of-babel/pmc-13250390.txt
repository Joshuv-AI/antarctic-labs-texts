Biomarkers for Early Detection of Diabetic Kidney Disease in Pediatric and Adolescent Populations: A Narrative Review
Authors: Iran Malekzadeh, Narges Falsafi, Mohammad Nazemi, Hanie Taherkhani, Mahboube Bahroudi, Asim Abbas, Mastaneh Moghtaderi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDiabetic nephropathy is among the most critical long‐term complications linked to diabetes, substantially influencing both morbidity and mortality. Although it was once largely associated with adults, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) has now become a leading source of kidney complications among children worldwide. Acting as both an indicator and predictor of cardiovascular complications and end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD), DKD in pediatric diabetic patients is of increasing concern.MethodsThis study was conducted as a narrative review of the literature focusing on emerging biomarkers for the early detection of diabetic kidney disease in pediatric and adolescent populations.ResultsTraditionally, microalbuminuria assessed through spot urine samples remains the standard method for identifying early kidney involvement in patients with diabetes. However, growing evidence suggests that renal tubular injury often precedes overt glomerular damage, particularly in pediatric and adolescent populations. This highlights the potential value of tubular biomarkers, such as neutrophil gelatinase‑associated lipocalin (NGAL) and kidney injury molecule‑1 (KIM‑1), which may detect subclinical kidney injury before the onset of persistent albuminuria. Emerging biomarkers, including cystatin C, liver‑type fatty acid‑binding protein (L‑FABP), inflammatory mediators, and metabolic markers, may further improve the early identification and risk stratification of diabetic kidney disease, enabling timely intervention and personalized monitoring strategies in children and adolescents with diabetes.ConclusionIdentifying new biomarkers for early‐stage kidney involvement in diabetic youth could significantly enhance timely diagnosis and treatment strategies.Keywords: adolescent, diabetes, kidney disease, pediatric, renal biomarkers

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by complete or partial insulin deficiency, typically manifesting during childhood or adolescence [1]. Recent trends show an increasing prevalence of Type 2 diabetes among younger populations, largely attributed to the global surge in obesity rates [2, 3]. Diabetic nephropathy (DN) remains one of the most severe long‐term complications associated with both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. Research suggests that within 5 to 10 years following the onset of diabetes, DN may develop in 10% to 50% of affected individuals [4, 5]. The primary manifestation of DN is microvascular damage, specifically targeting the renal glomeruli and leading to microalbuminuria. The earliest detectable abnormalities include elevated renal plasma flow and hyperfiltration, resulting in kidney enlargement [6, 7, 8]. As the disease progresses, reduced glomerular filtration culminates in hypertension, an often‐irreversible state that may advance to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) despite conventional therapeutic interventions [9]. Persistent hyperglycemia precipitates a cascade of microvascular complications such as nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy, as well as macrovascular conditions like myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease [10]. Histologically, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) affecting the glomeruli is marked by basement membrane thickening, podocyte foot process effacement, mesangial matrix expansion, and endothelial fenestration loss [11]. Sustained high blood glucose levels also induce significant tubular alterations, including early tubular hypertrophy and thickening of the tubular basement membrane, changes that may precede glomerular pathology. These findings underscore the potential utility of tubular biomarkers as more sensitive indicators of early DKD compared to glomerular changes [11, 12]. Although DKD was initially considered a non‐inflammatory condition, emerging evidence emphasizes the critical role of inflammatory processes affecting both the glomeruli and tubules. Inflammation now appears central to the onset and progression of DKD, especially in pediatric and adolescent populations [13, 14, 15].

Conventionally, microalbuminuria (30–300 mg/24 h) serves as the earliest detectable clinical sign of nephropathy in Type 1 diabetes, usually manifesting when the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is still within or above the normal range. Overt proteinuria (> 300 mg/24 h) typically follows after 5–10 years of disease duration and is often accompanied by hypertension and a decline in GFR [16, 17, 18, 19]. Hyperfiltration, defined as a GFR of 120–150 mL/min/1.73 m², has been observed in 25%–40% of adolescents with diabetes [5, 20]. This phenomenon initially increases kidney plasma flow and intraglomerular pressure, contributing to early albuminuria and eventually leading to GFR decline and progressive DKD [21].

Most studies investigating novel biomarkers for DKD have been conducted in adult populations, raising concerns about their generalizability to pediatric and adolescent patients. Children and adolescents differ physiologically from adults in several important ways, including renal maturation, hormonal milieu, growth rate, and metabolic status, all of which may influence biomarker expression, clearance, and baseline reference ranges. Consequently, diagnostic thresholds and interpretive cutoffs derived from adult cohorts may not accurately reflect early kidney injury in youth.

For instance, Cystatin C levels may vary with growth and puberty despite being relatively independent of muscle mass, while NGAL and KIM‐1 concentrations can be higher in children due to heightened tubular metabolic activity. Similarly, inflammatory markers such as TNF‐α may reflect developmental immune differences rather than true renal injury. These biological variations emphasize the need for age‐ and sex‐adjusted reference intervals and pediatric‐specific validation studies. Without such data, extrapolation from adult‐derived thresholds risks under‐ or overestimating early DKD in younger populations. Emerging biomarkers for early detection of diabetic kidney disease in children and adolescents.

Given the limitations of traditional diagnostic markers, particularly in normoalbuminuric patients, there is a growing imperative to identify and validate novel biomarkers for early DKD detection. In this review, we explore emerging biomarkers based on their diagnostic utility in children and adolescents with diabetes.

Methods

This study was conducted as a narrative review in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). A comprehensive literature search was performed using the PubMed and Google Scholar databases to identify relevant studies addressing biomarkers for the early detection of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in pediatric and adolescent populations. Articles published in English were included. Studies focusing exclusively on adult populations without pediatric applicability, non‑diabetic kidney diseases, or publications lacking sufficient methodological detail were excluded.

The keywords used included “diabetic kidney disease,” “diabetic nephropathy,” “DKD,” “biomarkers,” “renal biomarkers,” “tubular biomarkers,” “children,” “pediatric,” “paediatric,” “adolescent,” “cystatin C,” “neutrophil gelatinase‑associated lipocalin (NGAL),” “kidney injury molecule‑1 (KIM‑1),” “liver‑type fatty acid‑binding protein (L‑FABP),” “tumor necrosis factor‑alpha (TNF‑α),” “TNF receptor 1,” “TNF receptor 2,” and “pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2).” Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of retrieved records for relevance to the review topic.

Cystatin C

Cystatin C (Cys‐C) is a naturally occurring inhibitor of cysteine proteases, produced by all nucleated cells. It passes freely through the glomeruli, is fully reabsorbed in the proximal tubules, and is not secreted, making it a reliable endogenous indicator of kidney dysfunction. This protein is pivotal in the breakdown of intracellular proteins and peptides [22, 23]. Cysteine cathepsins, the enzymes regulated by Cys‐C, are involved in various physiological processes such as protein degradation, precursor protein activation, bone remodeling, immune responses, and cell death [24]. Cystatin C is the most prominent and potent member of this inhibitor family, playing a role both inside and outside of cells in regulating enzymatic activity. Its involvement in pathological conditions like cardiovascular diseases and inflammation has also been documented [25]. Over the last decade, extensive research has validated serum Cys‐C as a sensitive biomarker for estimating glomerular filtration rate (GFR), showing better predictive value than serum creatinine for assessing mortality and cardiovascular risks in elderly individuals. Importantly, Cys‐C levels are unaffected by sex, age, or muscle mass [26]. A longitudinal study on 237 patients with type 2 diabetes assessed both serum and urinary Cys‐C over 29 months, demonstrating a link between increased Cys‐C and reduced eGFR, as well as the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [27]. The results indicated that Cys‐C might signal declining kidney function earlier than creatinine. Supporting this, another study by Jeon and colleagues found rising levels of Cys‐C as chronic kidney disease (CKD) advanced from stage I to III and from normal albumin levels to microalbuminuria, showing a strong correlation with the albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio [28].

Neutrophil Gelatinase‐Associated Lipocalin

Neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin (NGAL) is a protein released by neutrophils and damaged epithelial cells of the nephron. In response to kidney injury, it appears simultaneously in both blood and urine. NGAL plays a crucial role in the innate immune system, particularly in defense against bacterial infections, and is also produced by immune cells, liver cells, and kidney tubular cells during various pathological conditions [29, 30]. Primarily synthesized by proximal tubular cells, NGAL becomes detectable in urine during the early stages of acute kidney injury (AKI). Its release is predominantly from proximal tubules, and elevated levels are indicative of damage to these structures. Studies show that NGAL has greater predictive accuracy for AKI in children than in adults. Baseline plasma NGAL levels can be elevated in conditions such as cancer or systemic infections, which may reduce its specificity. Urinary NGAL can also rise during urinary tract infections. Additionally, NGAL has proven effective in the early detection of contrast‐induced nephropathy (CIN) in pediatric patients [31, 32]. Since tubular injury often precedes glomerular damage in diabetic individuals, NGAL is considered a useful early biomarker for diabetic nephropathy (DN). It can reveal kidney involvement before proteinuria becomes apparent. A study involving 144 patients with type 2 diabetes demonstrated that both serum and urinary NGAL levels were predictive of upcoming albuminuria, reinforcing its utility for early DN diagnosis [33].

Kidney Injury Molecule‐1

Kidney Injury Molecule‐1 (KIM‐1) is an epithelial adhesion molecule markedly upregulated following ischemic renal injury. KIM‐1 is located on the apical surface of proximal renal tubular cells, and its presence in serum rises substantially in cases of tubular damage [34, 35]. In instances of ischemic and toxic acute kidney injury (AKI) in humans, KIM‐1 can be detected across all three segments of the proximal tubule. Beyond AKI, KIM‐1 has been identified as a highly sensitive biomarker for tubular injury across a spectrum of renal diseases, including focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, IgA nephropathy, membranoproliferative and membranous glomerulonephritis, acute rejection episodes, chronic allograft nephropathy, systemic lupus erythematosus, diabetic nephropathy, hypertension, and Wegener granulomatosis [36]. Evidence from a nested case‐control study involving 190 patients with incident diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and a prospective cohort study of 1156 individuals indicates that KIM‐1 is a robust predictor of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) decline in both early and advanced stages of DKD [37].

Pyruvate Kinase M2

Among the isoforms of pyruvate kinase enzymes (L, R, M1, and M2), Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2) is notably elevated in podocytes and plays a vital role in the development of acute kidney injury (AKI). PKM2 has emerged as a key mediator connecting glycolytic processes to inflammatory pathways during kidney injury. Additionally, PKM2 is implicated in diabetes‐associated microvascular complications, performing crucial regulatory functions and undergoing significant post‐translational modifications [38, 39, 40]. Heightened expression of proinflammatory cytokines, driven in part by PKM2 activity, contributes to pancreatic beta‐cell destruction and subsequent hyperglycemia in diabetic patients. Moreover, inflammation of glomerular endothelial cells stimulates macrophage activation, facilitating the development of diabetic nephropathy (DN) [41, 42]. Thus, PKM2 may foster renal inflammation by enhancing NF‐kB pathway activation in type 2 diabetes‐related nephropathy, positioning it as a potential target and biomarker for monitoring disease progression [43, 44, 45].

Tumor Necrosis Factor‐Alpha and Its Receptors

Tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) is a multifunctional cytokine that mediates inflammation, apoptosis, and immune regulation. Elevated levels of TNF‐α and its receptors (TNFR1 and TNFR2) have been observed in individuals with diabetic nephropathy (DN), correlating with renal deterioration and increased risk of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) [46, 47]. Although the precise mechanisms driving tubular injury in DN are still under investigation, inflammation plays a significant role. TNF‐α, released by both infiltrating and resident kidney cells, binds to its receptors, initiating pro‐inflammatory signaling cascades that can lead to cell death and fibrosis [48]. Histological studies have shown that higher circulating levels of TNFR1 and TNFR2 are closely linked with early structural alterations in the glomeruli, such as reduced endothelial fenestrations, glomerular sclerosis, and podocyte damage. A study by Dongsheng Cheng and colleagues demonstrated that TNF‐α inhibition in diabetic rats mitigated renal damage by reducing albuminuria and structural injury to both glomeruli and tubules. This protective effect was attributed to the suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome and a reduction in oxidative stress [48, 49].

Liver‐Type Fatty Acid‐Binding Protein

Liver‐type fatty acid‐binding protein (l‐FABP) is primarily expressed in the proximal tubular cells of the kidney and is released into urine under hypoxic conditions due to impaired peritubular capillary perfusion. Among the nine known fatty acid‐binding proteins (FABPs), the liver‐type variant is most prevalent in hepatocytes and enterocytes and constitutes a significant portion of cytosolic proteins. Due to its rapid release from damaged cells, l‐FABP has emerged as a promising biomarker for detecting early renal injury [50, 51]. Elevated urinary l‐FABP levels indicate tubular stress and are associated with the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly in conditions involving tubular ischemia [52, 53, 54]. Findings by Nakamura and colleagues suggest its potential utility for monitoring CKD progression in normotensive individuals, reinforcing its role in early detection strategies [53]. While most biomarker research in DKD has been conducted in adult populations, emerging pediatric studies showed that these biomarkers are biologically and clinically relevant in children and adolescents [1, 4, 55]. The underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms of early DKD including glomerular hyperfiltration, tubular stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction are conserved across age groups, supporting the biological plausibility of these markers in youth. Moreover, pediatric and adolescent patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes exhibit early structural and functional renal changes that precede albuminuria, similar to adults.

Limitations of Emerging Biomarkers for Early DKD Detection

Although these biomarkers offer promise for early identification of DKD, several limitations currently restrict their routine clinical application, particularly in pediatric settings.

Cystatin C: Although more accurate than creatinine for estimating GFR, Cys‐C assays are more expensive and not universally available in routine clinical laboratories. Results may also be influenced by thyroid dysfunction, corticosteroid use, and inflammatory states, reducing specificity for renal function alone.

Overall, while these biomarkers enhance pathophysiological understanding and show potential for early DKD detection, widespread clinical implementation will require assay standardization, cost reduction, establishment of pediatric reference values, and validation of clinical utility through large, prospective multicenter studies.

Although most novel biomarkers for DKD remain in the research phase, a pragmatic, stepwise approach can facilitate their gradual integration into pediatric and adolescent diabetes care:

1.Continue standard screening using annual urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (ACR) and estimated GFR (eGFR) as recommended by the ISPAD and KDIGO guidelines. These remain the cornerstone of DKD surveillance.

In summary, these biomarkers should currently be viewed as complementary tools to traditional screening rather than replacements. With further validation, they have the potential to transform pediatric DKD management by enabling earlier detection, personalized risk assessment, and timely intervention.

Conclusion

To summarize, emerging biomarkers for diabetic nephropathy (DN) are proving to be invaluable for early diagnosis and improved disease management. Each biomarker—NGAL, KIM‐1, PKM2, TNF‐α, and l‐FABP—provides distinct insights into the pathophysiology of DN. NGAL is particularly effective in detecting early tubular damage, especially in pediatric populations, while KIM‐1 serves as a reliable indicator of tubular injury due to its specific expression in proximal tubular cells. PKM2 highlights the link between altered glucose metabolism and inflammatory responses in diabetic nephropathy. TNF‐α, as a central inflammatory mediator, represents a potential therapeutic target through its inhibition, and l‐FABP is a promising marker for identifying tubular ischemia and the progression of chronic kidney disease.

Several biomarkers have already demonstrated measurable changes in pediatric cohorts. Urinary NGAL and KIM‐1 have been shown to rise in adolescents with diabetes even before the onset of persistent albuminuria, indicating early tubular stress. Cystatin C provides a more accurate estimation of glomerular filtration rate in children because it is independent of muscle mass and pubertal stage. l‐FABP and inflammatory markers such as TNF‐α have also correlated with early renal dysfunction in youth‐onset diabetes cohorts. Together, these findings support the translational relevance of these biomarkers in pediatric practice.

Importantly, applying these markers to children may offer even greater clinical benefit than in adults, since early identification of kidney injury provides a longer window for intervention and prevention of irreversible nephron loss. Despite their significant diagnostic potential, the adoption of these biomarkers in routine clinical practice necessitates standardized evaluation protocols and careful consideration of patient‐specific clinical contexts. Larger, prospective pediatric studies are required to validate cutoff values, establish age‐‐ and sex‐specific reference ranges, and confirm their prognostic value. Integrating these biomarkers into clinical care has the potential to transform pediatric DN management by enabling earlier detection, personalized monitoring, and more effective intervention strategies.
