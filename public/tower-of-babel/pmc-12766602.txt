Quantitative myocardial blood flow and perfusion reserve with exercise cardiovascular magnetic resonance
Authors: Alexander Schulz, Tess E Wallace, Kelvin Chow, Xiaoming Bi, Amine Amyar, Jennifer Rodriguez, Fahime Ghanbari, Martin S Maron, Ethan J Rowin, Peter Kellmann, Warren J Manning, Reza Nezafat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial blood flow (MBF) and myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) can be quantified using vasodilator stress cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR). Exercise stress CMR (Ex-CMR) offers a more physiological assessment of cardiac functional reserve. While visual interpretation of Ex-CMR perfusion has been successfully applied, the feasibility of quantitative Ex-CMR perfusion remains unproven.We aimed to assess the feasibility of quantitative Ex-CMR perfusion imaging for characterizing exercise-induced perfusion responses and to perform a pilot study comparing MBF and MPR among patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and non-cardiac dyspnea (NCD).MethodsIn this prospective study, patients with HCM, HFpEF, or NCD underwent Ex-CMR at 3T using a supine ergometer. Exercise was performed outside the scanner bore, followed by stress perfusion imaging 45-60 s post-exercise and rest perfusion 5-7 min later. A dual-sequence protocol with inline pixel-wise quantification was used to calculate MBF and MPR. Image quality and feasibility were visually assessed. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance and t-tests; linear regression was used to explore clinical associations.ResultsOf 108 patients enrolled, 9 were excluded due to obstructive coronary artery disease or reduced ejection fraction. Quantitative Ex-CMR was successful (at least one analyzable paired rest and post-exercise slice) in 90% (10/99) of cases. Most frequent quality issues were inadequate gating or arrhythmias and slice misalignment. The final cohort included 89 patients: 34 HCM, 34 HFpEF, and 21 NCD. Patients with HCM showed significantly lower MBF and MPR than HFpEF and NCD (MBF: 1.03 ± 0.27 vs 1.25 ± 0.40 and 1.13 ± 0.25 mL/min/g; MPR: 1.27 ± 0.21 vs 1.41 ± 0.29 and 1.44 ± 0.22; all p < 0.05). Peak exercise heart rate was the strongest independent predictor of MBF (β = 0.009, p < 0.001) and MPR (β = 0.004, p = 0.022).ConclusionEx-CMR quantitative MBF and MPR assessment is feasible in most patients after image quality control. While the increase in MBF was limited during low-to-moderate exercise intensity in this pilot study, Ex-CMR revealed distinct perfusion response patterns among studied cohorts.Keywords: Quantitative myocardial perfusion, Myocardial blood flow, Exercise CMR, Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction

Background

Non-invasive assessment of myocardial blood supply has become a cornerstone in guiding both diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making [1], [2]. Among available modalities, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) has emerged as a leading technique due to its high diagnostic accuracy, prognostic value, and pivotal role in informing both invasive and non-invasive treatment strategies [3], [4], [5]. While pharmacologic vasodilator stress using agents such as adenosine or regadenoson is considered the reference standard for assessing myocardial blood flow (MBF) and myocardial perfusion reserve (MPR) [6], [7], it does not reflect the complex hemodynamic responses encountered during daily life physiological exercise, when most patients actually experience symptoms [8]. Unlike vasodilator stress, exercise stress accounts for systemic physiology, including exercise capacity and hemodynamic interplay, and may provide insights into myocardial perfusion.

With recent advances in acquisition techniques, the use of exercise during CMR (Ex-CMR) has gained growing attention. Ex-CMR has been explored as a high-resolution, pharmacologic agent–free alternative for assessing myocardial perfusion and ischemia. The ability to acquire cine images with full ventricular coverage during physiologic exercise without the need for stress agents offers a unique advantage for simultaneously evaluating perfusion and wall motion abnormalities. Early studies demonstrated the feasibility of visually detecting ischemia and predicting adverse outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease [9], [10]. Subsequent work confirmed the feasibility of visual first-pass perfusion imaging during exercise [9], [11], [12], with some studies reporting diagnostic superiority over conventional modalities such as exercise stress single-photon emission computed tomography [11] and additional diagnostic value in patients with congenital coronary anomalies [13].

While visual assessment remains a commonly used method for detecting myocardial ischemia, quantitative perfusion imaging for calculating MBF at rest and during hyperemia has gained prominence. This technique provides observer-independent, highly accurate evaluation of reduced myocardial perfusion [14], [15], [16], [17], enhances diagnostic performance [18], [19], and prognostication [20], [21]. Quantitative perfusion measurements are derived from myocardial and left ventricular (LV) blood pool signal intensity time curves, converted to units of gadolinium concentration, using pharmacokinetic modeling methods. Recent advances in inline reconstruction and motion correction now allow pixel-wise perfusion maps to be generated efficiently, facilitating post-processing and accelerating clinical translation of quantitative perfusion imaging [19], [22]. However, the feasibility of quantitative myocardial perfusion in the setting of Ex-CMR has not been demonstrated.

Therefore, in this study, we sought to evaluate the feasibility of quantitative perfusion imaging using Ex-CMR for MBF and MPR assessment. A rigorous image quality analysis was conducted to assess diagnostic image quality and interpretive confidence in quantitative stress perfusion scans in a convenience cohort that included patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and non-cardiac dyspnea (NCD).

Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the local institutional review board. All participants provided written informed consent. All patients were prospectively recruited for a research Ex-CMR scan between March 2022 and October 2024. As a feasibility study, the quantitative perfusion sequence was added to an ongoing Ex-CMR imaging study in our center that includes three patient cohorts of HCM, HFpEF, and NCD, where symptom burden and dyspnea are tightly linked to exercise capacity [23], [24], [25]. As microvascular dysfunction may represent a key pathophysiological mechanism in HCM and HFpEF [26], [27], [28], [29], [30] and contribute to symptom development, the ability to directly and non-invasively assess MBF during physiological exercise would be of particular value and serve as a platform to evaluate the feasibility of quantitative Ex-CMR perfusion. Recruited patients were diagnosed with HCM according to 2024 American Heart Association (AHA) criteria (≥15 mm hypertrophy without evidence of other etiologies causing a similar magnitude of hypertrophy, or ≥13 mm cases with positive genetic testing or family history of HCM) [31]. HFpEF was diagnosed according to the 2022 AHA/American College of Cardiology guidelines with patients having signs or symptoms of heart failure, preserved LV ejection fraction ≥50%, and evidence of elevated LV filling pressure [32]. Dyspneic patients (New York Heart Association class ≥ II) without any known or suspected cardiac disease, defined by normal biatrial and biventricular dimensions, preserved systolic and diastolic function, and absence of pulmonary arterial hypertension, were classified as having NCD. Patients were excluded from this study if they 1) were younger than 18 years 2) had atrial fibrillation at the time of CMR, 3) had an estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, 4) were unable to perform supine bike exercise due to orthopedic limitations, 5) had non-magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) compatible devices/implants, 6) were pregnant or lactating, and 7) had a gadolinium allergy.

CMR imaging protocol

All images were acquired using a 3T CMR system (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers AG, Forchheim, Germany). Biventricular systolic function was obtained from long-axis and short-axis cine images, acquired using a retrospectively electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated, balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence, accelerated using a resolution enhancement generative adversarial inline neural network research prototype [33]. Cine images were acquired with generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisition (GRAPPA) rate 2% and 25% phase resolution, readout matrix 192, field of view 360 × 360 mm2, slice thickness 8 mm, slice gap 2 mm, flip angle 43°, echo time (TE) 1.7 ms, repetition time (TR) 3.3 ms, and reconstructed with in-plane resolution of 1.9 × 1.9 mm2 and temporal resolution of 37 ms.

Perfusion imaging was planned in three short-axis slices (basal, mid, and apical) based on end-systolic long-axis cines using a dual-sequence inline perfusion research prototype with sequence parameters described below. Following physiological exercise stress on the gantry table outside the magnet bore, patients were repositioned in the magnet bore and received a contrast bolus of 0.05 mmol/kg gadobutrol (Gadavist, Bayer HealthCare, Leverkusen, Germany) via a power injector (MEDRAD® Spectris Solaris EP MR, Bayer HealthCare) at 4 mL/s, followed by a 30 mL saline flush at the same rate.

To accommodate both functional and perfusion imaging post-exercise, first-pass post-exercise perfusion images were acquired approximately 45-60 s after completion of exercise. Although this delay is suboptimal for capturing peak hyperemia, it enables evaluation of both LV function and myocardial perfusion while minimizing motion artifacts due to high heart rates. After a 5- to 7-minute interval, a second contrast bolus (0.05 mmol/kg) was administered for rest perfusion imaging, using the same slice locations as the post-exercise acquisition.

Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was performed 10 min after the second bolus of contrast agent using an ECG-gated, segmented two-dimensional phase-sensitive inversion-recovery sequence with spectral fat saturation during end-diastole. Imaging parameters included a bSSFP readout, GRAPPA acceleration (rate 2), two shots per slice, flip angle of 60°, receiver bandwidth of 450 Hz, field of view 340 mm, in-plane resolution 1.5 × 1.5 mm², slice thickness 3 mm, slice spacing 10 mm, TE 2.5 ms, and TR 5.3 ms, with full short-axis coverage. Inversion times (TIs) were optimized to null normal myocardium using an in-house developed Look-Locker sequence.

Exercise protocol

Fig. 1 illustrates the exercise protocol in relation to the imaging protocol. Patients were advised to refrain from caffeine intake for 24 h before the scan. Beta-blockers were not withheld in this study, as the aim was to evaluate cardiac responses during exercise under conditions reflecting patients’ everyday life, including their regular medication use. This approach is consistent with clinical practice, where exercise testing is performed to assess the adequacy of therapy and to investigate symptoms under the patient’s usual treatment conditions. Physiological exercise was performed using a CMR-compatible ergometer (Lode, Groningen, The Netherlands) mounted on the scanner table. To optimize patient monitoring and safety, exercise was conducted outside the bore. It began at an initial resistance level (Ω), which was increased every 2 min by Ω while maintaining a constant pedaling cadence of 75 rpm, with a maximum of five stages. Based on the individual’s daily activity level and symptom severity, resistance Ω ranged from 10-15 W.

Immediately following either the completion of 10 min of exercise or patient exhaustion, subjects were repositioned inside the scanner bore for post-exercise imaging. At each exercise stage, subjective exertion was assessed using a 4-point scale (“easy,” “moderate,” “hard,” “very hard”). In addition, vital signs, including heart rate and blood pressure, were recorded during each exercise stage and throughout the recovery phase.

Quantitative perfusion imaging

Quantitative myocardial perfusion images were acquired during free breathing using a dual-sequence approach that uses separately optimized protocols to acquire the low-resolution images for arterial input function (AIF) calculation, and for multi-slice myocardial imaging [34]. Both sequences use an optimized saturation recovery preparation pulse train [35] with a saturation recovery delay and an accelerated single-shot readout. The following acquisition parameters were used for myocardial perfusion images: bSSFP readout, spectral fat suppression, field of view = 360 × 270 mm2, acquired in-plane resolution 1.9 × 2.4 mm2, slice thickness 8 mm, slice spacing 16 mm, temporal GRAPPA (TPAT) R = 3, TE 1.04 ms, TR 2.1 ms, receiver bandwidth 1080 Hz/px, flip angle 50°, TI 50 ms. Low-resolution AIF images were acquired using a gradient echo readout with two monopolar echoes to correct for T2* losses during the first pass, and the following image parameters: matrix size 64 × 34, slice thickness 10 mm, GRAPPA R = 2, TR 2.52 ms, TE1/TE2 0.78/1.72, flip angle 5°, and TI 28.3 ms. The acquisition also includes three proton-density weighted images used for surface coil intensity correction and normalization of signal values, acquired using a low-flip angle gradient echo readout without saturation recovery preparation. Contrast injection was timed at 8 heartbeats after the start of data acquisition. A low-resolution AIF image at the basal slice position was acquired and followed by three short-axis images at each cardiac cycle for 80 cycles. Images were reconstructed inline on the scanner using the Gadgetron software framework as described previously [36]. Briefly, all images are respiratory motion corrected using a non-rigid image registration. The AIF signal is corrected for saturation non-linearities at higher gadolinium concentration using Bloch simulations and a look-up table correction. AIF and myocardial images are converted into units of gadolinium concentration (mmol/L), which are then used for calculating a pixel-wise MBF (mL/min/g) map using a blood tissue exchange model for modeling the contrast kinetics [37]. Outputs included qualitative T1-weighted perfusion images with and without motion correction, a representative image of the AIF acquisition, a final quantitative perfusion flow map for pixel-wise quantification of MBF, and quality plots of gating performance and visualization of the AIF curve.

Qualitative evaluation of perfusion acquisitions

Before quantitative measurements, individual steps of the acquisition and reconstruction were evaluated in all perfusion scans, individually for each slice in resting and post-exercise acquisitions. As shown in Fig. 2, the first step included an inspection of the AIF curve and performance of cardiac gating using the inline provided quality plots. If the AIF curve showed multiple contrast peaks or late arrival of the contrast agent, the perfusion acquisition was excluded from analyses. Performance of cardiac gating and occurrence of premature ventricular contractions were assessed within the critical zone, defined by the upslope of the first-pass perfusion on the AIF curve, where errors are most likely to affect the accuracy of MBF quantification. If individual slices showed premature ventricular contractions or acquisitions were misgated within the critical zone, they were excluded from analyses.

Further assessment of acquisition success included evaluation of proper planning, effectiveness of motion correction, and the presence of significant artifacts or noise (Fig. 3). For image planning, slices that did not capture the full myocardial circumference throughout all cardiac phases were excluded. Motion-correction performance was assessed by reviewing the image frames across the cardiac cycle and evaluating the alignment of the LV myocardium. Slices with inadequate alignment showing relevant shifting of the LV myocardium were excluded. Lastly, artifact presence was evaluated, and myocardial segments or slices exhibiting artifacts—such as those caused by respiratory motion, off-resonance effects, or suboptimal shimming—were excluded from the analysis. If an imaging slice or an individual myocardial segment was found to be unsuitable for quantitative analyses, the final reason for that decision was recorded (e.g., suboptimal AIF, gating/premature ventricular contractions, planning, motion correction, and artifacts). If multiple issues had justified the exclusion of that slice or segment, it was recorded as “multiple.” After excluding low-quality slices and segments, we evaluated the availability of paired rest and post-exercise CMR perfusion acquisitions at the matching slice location. Final quantification of MPR was performed for patients with at least one complete rest–post-exercise slice pair available at the same anatomical location.

Image Analyses

Measurements of biventricular cardiac volumes and systolic function were obtained from the short-axis stack using CVI42 (version 6.0.2, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Alberta, Canada) using automated contouring of endo- and epicardial borders at end-systolic and end-diastolic cardiac phases with manual corrections if needed. Presence of LGE was manually assessed and recorded for each myocardial segment according to the 17-segment American Heart Association (AHA) model, including transmural extent and transmural location of LGE (subendocardial, midwall, epicardial). MBF was quantified by manually delineating endocardial and epicardial borders on all slices of the inline-generated perfusion maps for rest and post-exercise acquisitions, in which each pixel represents MBF in mL/min/g of myocardial tissue, using Medis QMass (version 4.0.70.4, Medis Suite, Leiden, The Netherlands). The right ventricular (RV) inferior insertion point was defined to segment the myocardium according to the 17-segment AHA model, excluding the apical segment (segment 17). Segments showing subendocardial, transmural, or prominent mid-myocardial LGE—excluding the RV insertion points—were excluded from MBF and MPR analysis. MBF was calculated as the global average of all remaining segments for rest and post-exercise scans and MPR was determined as MPR=MBF post exerciseMBF rest for segments having paired data of MBF available. All post-processing analyses were performed by an experienced CMR reader (A.S., Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance Level III, 9 years of experience).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Depending on data distribution, continuous variables were compared using either Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons across multiple patient groups were performed using analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate, followed by post hoc testing with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test. Correlation was assessed using Pearson’s method, and results are reported as correlation coefficients. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to assess associations between potential influencing factors and MPR. Variables with a p-value <0.1 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Regression coefficients (β) and correlation coefficients (r) are reported as unadjusted for univariable and adjusted for multivariable analyses. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (Version 29, IBM Corp., Armonk, New York) and GraphPad Prism (Version 10, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California).

Results

Study cohort

A total of 108 patients (59 ± 13 years, 43 males) underwent quantitative Ex-CMR myocardial perfusion imaging. After excluding five with flow-limiting (obstructive) CAD and four patients with resting LV ejection fraction <50% at the time of the study, perfusion images of the remaining 99 participants were further evaluated. Fig. 4 provides an overview of the usability of acquired quantitative perfusion slices at rest and post-exercise, along with the reasons for excluding individual myocardial segments or entire slices. A total of 594 quantitative perfusion slices were assessed, comprising 297 slices acquired at rest and 297 post-exercise. Among the resting scans, 262 slices (88%) were deemed fully suitable for MBF quantification. In two slices (1%), individual myocardial segments had to be excluded, while 33 slices (11%) were excluded entirely due to quality concerns. The most common reasons for exclusion were planning errors (10/35, 28%) and gating issues or premature ventricular contractions (16/35, 48%). Artifacts, AIF errors, and multiple combined issues (each 3/35, 8%) contributed less frequently. For post-exercise acquisitions, 230 slices (77%) were fully analyzable. In 14 slices (5%), individual segments were excluded, and 53 slices (18%) were excluded entirely. The primary reasons for exclusion mirrored those at rest: planning errors (17/67, 24%) and gating issues/premature ventricular contractions (36/67, 48%). Inadequate motion correction accounted for 7/67 (10%) exclusions, artifacts or noise for 3/67 (4%), and multiple issues were observed in 10/67 (14%) cases. For final MBF and MPR analyses, 48 patients (48%) had all three slices available, 41 patients (42%) had one or two paired slices available, and 10 patients (10%) had to be excluded due to a lack of any analyzable paired slices.

The final study cohort comprised 89 patients, including 34 patients with HCM, 34 patients with HFpEF, and 21 with NCD.

Patient demographics and CMR characteristics

Table 1 summarizes participant demographics and CMR characteristics across the three groups. Patients with HFpEF and HCM were significantly older (p < 0.001) and had a higher body mass index (BMI) (p = 0.016). There was a trend toward a higher proportion of male patients in the HFpEF and HCM groups (p = 0.070). Hyperlipoproteinemia and arterial hypertension were more common in HFpEF and HCM. Non-obstructive CAD was present only in the HFpEF and HCM groups, with no significant difference between HFpEF and HCM in terms of the number of affected vessels or history of coronary interventions. Beta-blocker therapy was significantly more frequent in patients with HCM (p < 0.001). Patients with HCM also exhibited higher LV mass index and stroke volume index compared to the other groups (p < 0.001 for both), along with a significantly greater prevalence of LGE (p < 0.001).

MBF post-exercise and MPR showed weak to moderate correlations with maximum heart rate during exercise (MBF post-exercise: r = 0.540, p < 0.001; MPR: r = 0.396, p < 0.001) and heart rate during the post-exercise perfusion acquisition (MBF post-exercise: r = 0.616, p < 0.001; MPR: 0.353, p < 0.001). Additionally, neither maximum resistance nor total exercise duration significantly correlated with MBF post-exercise, while maximum workload exhibited a weak correlation with MPR (r = 0.267, p = 0.011). No significant differences in MBF post-exercise or MPR were observed between patients reporting higher subjective exercise intensity or symptoms and those who did not (Fig. 6).

Rest MBF did not differ significantly among the three cohorts (p = 0.266). In contrast, post-exercise MBF differed significantly between groups (p = 0.020), with the highest values observed in patients with HFpEF, followed by NCD, and the lowest in patients with HCM (Supplemental Table 1 and Fig. 7). Discussion

This prospective study evaluated the feasibility of quantitative perfusion imaging using Ex-CMR. We demonstrated that 1) quantitative perfusion imaging using Ex-CMR is feasible with a high success rate, 2) Ex-CMR quantitative perfusion shows distinct post-exercise MBF and MPR patterns across different patient groups, and 3) post-exercise MBF and MPR are significantly associated with exercise performance as measured by maximum exercise heart rate.

Across prior Ex-CMR studies, in-scanner supine bicycle exercise has gained prominence due to its practicality and the ability to maintain controlled workloads directly on the scanner table, allowing real-time cine imaging during peak exercise for assessment of wall motion abnormalities [38]. However, myocardial perfusion imaging is typically performed not during, but immediately after exercise to balance physiological fidelity with image quality [12], [39]. The resulting delay in perfusion acquisitions after peak exercise is often unavoidable due to transfer times; however, even in protocols with in-scanner exercise, functional acquisitions are typically prioritized immediately post-exercise [38]. This may come at the cost of some accuracy in capturing MBF at peak exertion, but it potentially improves overall acquisition robustness and data usability. Quantitative perfusion is particularly susceptible to artifacts and depends heavily on high-quality data reconstruction—especially under conditions of elevated heart rate, intense respiration, and patient repositioning following exercise. A previous study on quantitative myocardial perfusion using adenosine stress CMR reported severe artifacts in over 20% of scans, though none were deemed non-diagnostic [40]. We observed that limitations in quantitative perfusion imaging using Ex-CMR primarily stemmed from errors in image planning, ECG gating, and motion correction. Planning issues primarily affected basal or apical slices, likely due to patient repositioning in the bore after pre-exercise prescription, resulting in occasional inclusion of the LVOT or overly apical coverage. Such errors occur more frequently than in pharmacologic stress studies, where the patient remains stationary. Future implementation of accelerated sequences enabling the acquisition of additional slices could mitigate these issues by improving ventricular coverage. ECG gating errors mainly resulted from the combined effects of strong gradients of perfusion acquisitions [41], increased respiratory motion, and elevated heart rates during exercise. The latter two factors also challenge current motion-correction algorithms. Similar to recent advances in cine imaging [42], real-time non-gated quantitative perfusion techniques [43] or respiratory navigated real-time slice tracking [44] may offer promising solutions to overcome these challenges in the future.

After rigorous quality control, MBF and MPR could be reliably quantified post-exercise. Participants showed appropriate hemodynamic responses (heart rate, blood pressure, and subjective effort) and a significant increase in MBF compared to rest. However, physiological exercise, particularly using a low-to-moderate exercise protocol, did not induce the same degree of hyperemia as reported under pharmacological stress [29], [30], [45], [46] and therefore does not achieve flow levels typically considered representative of “stress” MBF. Nonetheless, limited exercise performance with subsequent limited heart rate response is also a marker of general exercise intolerance (e.g., muscular deconditioning), secondarily constraining MBF as patients may be unable to fully utilize their coronary reserve. While standardizing the protocol to achieve comparable predicted maximum heart rates across all participants and including studies in healthy volunteers would facilitate direct comparisons of MBF, such an approach might neglect disease-specific limitations and differences in exercise capacity. Therefore, finding a balance between a physiologically realistic exercise paradigm and a highly standardized, heart rate–driven protocol for comparative assessment of coronary physiology remains challenging. Since exercise testing has limited accuracy for detecting obstructive CAD in patients who fail to achieve target heart rates [47], our proposed low-to-moderate, symptom-adapted exercise protocol may not be optimal for detecting epicardial coronary artery disease. However, it may be valuable for evaluation of the complex pathophysiology of microvascular perfusion and future studies should optimize exercise protocols for the specific clinical question being addressed.

The physiological cascade of exercise involves increasing myocardial work, which raises oxygen consumption. This demand is met primarily through increased MBF, as myocardial oxygen extraction is already near maximum (∼70%) at rest. The chronotropic response contributes significantly to this increase in oxygen consumption [48], [49], and prior studies in healthy subjects have demonstrated a direct relationship between heart rate and MBF [50], [51], [52]. Impaired MBF and MPR, as previously observed in patients with HFpEF and HCM [29], [45], [46], may not only contribute to chronotropic incompetence but also result from it. Elevated LV filling pressures during exercise may further aggravate impaired myocardial perfusion in these patients by reducing aorto-ventricular—and therefore coronary artery—pressure gradients.

In this pilot study, patients with HFpEF showed post-exercise MBF and MPR values comparable to those of patients with NCD, despite lower absolute and relative increases in heart rate. In contrast, patients with HCM exhibited both reduced myocardial perfusion and lower peak heart rate, despite similar workloads and relative heart rate increases compared to HFpEF. Beta-blocker and calcium channel blocker therapy could have contributed to this attenuated heart rate and perfusion response; it was not independently associated with perfusion metrics in multivariable models. However, the observation of higher workload but lower heart rates in HCM suggests a possible medication effect. Further research is warranted to delineate the relative contribution of disease-specific pathophysiological mechanisms versus inadequate exercise performance or contribution from rate-limiting medication during the scan. Additionally, defining specific threshold values for Ex-CMR–derived MBF and MPR, potentially normalized to exercise performance, will be essential to better reflect the functional limitations observed in real-world clinical settings.

Limitations

This was a prospective single-center study conducted in a highly experienced CMR environment, which may limit the generalizability of the results to less specialized settings. No invasive or pharmacologic reference standards were used to validate MBF or MPR measurements, and thus, the observed perfusion abnormalities cannot be definitively attributed to microvascular dysfunction. While most patients experienced adequate resistance during exercise, patients only reached approximately 80% of the age–predicted maximal heart rate. Nevertheless, the exercise protocol was standardized across participants to enhance comparability. The supine position used during Ex-CMR may impose distinct hemodynamic conditions compared to upright exercise, such as increased LV end-diastolic pressures and reduced coronary artery perfusion pressure, which could potentially exaggerate ischemic responses. Alternative forms of exercise may yield different MBF results. Patients were not matched for comorbidities or potential confounders, which may have influenced perfusion outcomes. Although patients with NCD lack a diagnosis of overt cardiac disease, subclinical abnormalities may nonetheless impair myocardial perfusion. The absence of a control group limits the ability to define pathological thresholds for Ex-CMR–derived perfusion measurements. Beta-blocker use may have further confounded the relationship between heart rate and perfusion response. Perfusion acquisitions were not performed during exercise, which may have provided higher absolute values for MBF and more closely mimic physiologic stress, but may also compromise image quality, monitoring capabilities, and patient safety.

Conclusion

This prospective study demonstrates that MBF and MPR can be assessed using quantitative perfusion imaging with supine bicycle Ex-CMR, with high success rates. Despite a limited increase in MBF during low-to-moderate exercise intensity, Ex-CMR–based quantitative perfusion imaging captures distinct MBF and MPR responses across different patient populations with a strong association between perfusion parameters and heart rate response to exercise, underscoring the physiological interplay between exercise performance, chronotropic competence, and myocardial perfusion. Further studies are warranted to establish exercise-specific thresholds for CMR–based myocardial perfusion imaging and to clarify the relative contribution of impaired exercise capacity to reduced perfusion.
