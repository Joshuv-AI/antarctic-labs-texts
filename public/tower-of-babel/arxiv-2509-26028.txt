Optimally building spanning graphs in semirandom graph processes
Michael Anastos 1, Maurício Collares 2, Joshua Erde 3, Mihyun Kang 4, Dominik Schmid 4, Gregory B. Sorkin 5

Abstract
. The semirandom graph process constructs a graph G G in a series of rounds, starting with the empty graph on n n vertices. In each round, a player is offered a vertex v v chosen uniformly at random, and chooses an edge on v v to add to G G . The player’s aim is to make G G satisfy some property as quickly as possible. Our interest is in the property that G G contain a given n n -vertex graph H H with maximum degree Δ \Delta . In 2020, Ben-Eliezer, Gishboliner, Hefetz and Krivelevich showed that there is a semirandom strategy that achieves this, with probability tending to one as n n tends to infinity, in ( 1 + o Δ ​ ( 1 ) ) ​ 3 ​ Δ ​ n 2 \left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{3\Delta n}{2} rounds, where o Δ ​ ( 1 ) o_{\Delta}(1) is a function that tends to 0 0 as Δ \Delta tends to infinity. We improve this to ( 1 + o Δ ​ ( 1 ) ) ​ Δ ​ n 2 \left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2} , which can be seen to be asymptotically optimal in Δ \Delta . We show the same result for a variant of the semirandom graph process, namely the semirandom tree process introduced by Burova and Lichev, where in each round the player is offered the edge set of a uniformly chosen tree on n n vertices, and chooses one edge to keep.



1.
Introduction

The
random graph process
was introduced by Erdős and Rényi in their groundbreaking papers on random graphs
[
20
,
21
]
. In this process, starting with the empty graph with vertex set
[n]\coloneqq\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
, a sequence of edges
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{\binom{n}{2}}
is chosen, where in the
t
th step the edge
e_{t}
is chosen uniformly at random from the set
\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{t-1}\}

of edges which have not already been chosen. One of the motivations for studying this process is that it gives a natural coupling for the set of
uniform random graphs

G(n,m)
, where
G(n,m)
is a graph chosen uniformly at random from all graphs with vertex set
[n]
and with
m
edges. Indeed, it is easy to see that for each
0\leq m\leq\binom{n}{2}
the graph
G_{m}\colon=([n],\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\})
has the same distribution as
G(n,m)
, and hence the random graph process leads to a natural coupling of the random graphs
G(n,0),G(n,1),\ldots,G\left(n,\binom{n}{2}\right)
such that

G(n,0)\subseteq G(n,1)\subseteq\ldots\subseteq G\big(n,{\textstyle\binom{n}{2}}\big).

This allows us to think of the structure of the random graph
G(n,m)
as
evolving
as we increase the parameter
m
. The random graph process has been extensively studied, and there has been much research into how this process is affected by restricting or changing the distribution of the edges chosen in each step (see monographs on random graphs
[
15
,
25
,
34
]
).

For example, given some decreasing graph property
\mathcal{P}
, we could restrict our choice of edges by only allowing an edge
e_{t}
in the
t
th step if the resulting graph
G_{t}
has the property
\mathcal{P}
(see e.g.,
[
22
]
). In this way, the process would stop not with a complete graph, but with a maximal graph with the property
\mathcal{P}
. This gives a simple and explicit way to sample a random extremal graph with property
\mathcal{P}
, although the distribution on the set of such graphs may not be uniform. These models have been useful for the study of
H
-free graphs
[
13
,
16
,
46
]
, which has important applications in Ramsey theory
[
10
,
13
,
38
]
, as well as in the study of random planar graphs
[
31
,
37
]
and in other contexts
[
12
,
36
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

Other results focus on how the process is affected if we introduce some bias, either random or via some strategic agency, to the choice of the edge in each step. In particular, perhaps motivated by a paradigm in computer science known as the
power of two random choices

[
4
,
43
]
, Achlioptas suggested a variation of the random graph process, now known as the
Achlioptas process
(see
[
11
]
). Starting with the empty graph on
n
vertices, in each step
two
(or several) edges are sampled uniformly at random and one of those is selected according to some rule and added to the graph. We are then interested in how the choice of this rule can affect the evolution of the random graph process. Particular focus has been given to how it can affect the
phase transition
this model undergoes, where the size of the largest component transitions from logarithmic to linear, specifically, how this phase transition can be delayed or accelerated
[
11
,
14
,
23
,
35
,
51
]
and how “smoothly” it occurs
[
1
,
9
,
49
,
50
]
.

Similar questions about the effect of strategic choice on random processes were considered by Ben-Eliezer, Hefetz, Kronenberg, Parczyk, Shikhelman and Stojaković
[
8
]
, who studied the so-called
semirandom graph process
suggested initially by Michaeli.
Starting with the empty graph
G_{0}
on the vertex set
[n]
, in each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
a player
we refer to as “Builder”
is
offered
a vertex
v_{t}

chosen from
[n]
uniformly at random and independently of all previous rounds. Builder then
chooses
an edge
e_{t}
incident to
v_{t}
and adds it to the graph
G_{t-1}
, i.e.,
E(G_{t}):=E(G_{t-1})\cup\{e_{t}\}
. Equivalently, Builder is offered the edge set of a random
star
on vertex set
[n]
centred at
v_{t}
, and chooses an edge
e_{t}
from this star. Builder’s goal is to build a graph satisfying a given graph property
\mathcal{P}
(typically monotone increasing) as quickly as possible. For convenience, we also allow Builder to claim no edge in a round, which clearly does not give Builder any strategic advantage.
Although recent, the semirandom graph process has received extensive attention,
with results on graph properties including containment of a perfect matching
[
8
,
30
]
, Hamiltonicity
[
24
,
26
,
28
,
29
]
, containment of bounded-size subgraphs
[
6
,
8
]
, and the chromatic and independence numbers
[
27
]
.

Before proceeding, let us mention that we write “whp” to mean “with high probability”, that is, with probability tending to
1
as
n
tends to infinity.
We use the usual Landau notation, such as
\omega(\ln n)
,
also with the assumption that
n\to\infty
.
By contrast, we write
o_{\Delta}(1)
to denote an arbitrary function
that approaches 0 as
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
.

In this paper we concern ourselves with the property of constructing a copy of a given
n
-vertex graph
H
of maximum degree
\Delta
. Alon
[
8
, Question 6.2]
asked whether every graph of bounded maximum degree can be constructed in
O(n)
rounds. This was resolved affirmatively by Ben-Eliezer, Gishboliner, Hefetz and Krivelevich
[
7
]
,
who showed the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Let
H
be an
n
-vertex graph with maximum degree
\Delta=\Delta(n)
. Then, in the semirandom graph process, Builder has a strategy guaranteeing that whp,
after

\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad}l}\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}\text{ rounds}&\text{if }\Delta=\omega\mathopen{}\left(\ln n\right)\mathclose{},and\\[4.30554pt]
\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{3\Delta n}{2}\text{ rounds}&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}

the constructed graph will contain a copy of
H
.

Because
H
may contain
\frac{\Delta n}{2}
edges, Builder needs at least that many rounds.
Thus,
Theorem

1.1
is asymptotically optimal for
\Delta=\omega\mathopen{}\left(\ln n\right)\mathclose{}
.
On the other hand, for smaller
\Delta
,
it is a factor of 3 away from the trivial lower bound.

Ben-Eliezer, Gishboliner, Hefetz and Krivelevich asked if the first bound in
Theorem

1.1
can be shown to hold for all
\Delta

[
7
, Problem 1.4]
.
For constant
\Delta
,
Theorem

1.1
implies that
O(n)
rounds suffice, and to say more would mean characterising the leading constant.
(As noted earlier, there is ongoing exploration of the constants for
\Delta=1
, namely for perfect matchings, and for
\Delta=2
, namely for Hamilton cycles.)
So, retaining the factor
1+o_{\Delta}(1)
, the question posed is really about the behaviour for
\Delta=\omega\mathopen{}\left(1\right)\mathclose{}
.

Our main result answers this question affirmatively: Builder can build any
n
-vertex graph with maximum degree
\Delta
in time asymptotically matching the trivial lower bound.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H
be an
n
-vertex graph with maximum degree
\Delta=\Delta(n)
. Then, in the semirandom graph process, Builder has a strategy guaranteeing that whp after

(1)

\displaystyle\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}

rounds, the constructed graph will contain a copy of
H
.
Furthermore, the same statement holds after
\Poisson\left(\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}\right)
rounds.

As we will show (
Remark

2.4
), the two statements in
Theorem

1.2
are equivalent.

As with
Theorem

1.1
, explicit quantitative bounds for fixed
\Delta
can be extracted from our proofs, but they will be far from optimal.
As in
[
7
]
, without loss of generality we will assume in our proofs that
\Delta
is at least a sufficiently large constant.

Let us explain the main ideas allowing us to improve upon the bounds given in
Theorem

1.1
.
Here, as for many properties in semirandom processes, it is easy to construct an “almost solution” efficiently, and the hard part lies in “completing” this solution.
Indeed, if we arbitrarily identify the vertex set of the target graph
H
with the vertex set
[n]
of Builder’s graph, then in the first
(1+o_{\Delta}(1))\frac{\Delta n}{2}
rounds,
Builder can greedily obtain nearly all the required edges.
Calling a vertex incident to any missing edge
failed
,
Lemma

3.5
shows that with high probability, at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.95}\right)n
vertices fail.
(And
Remark

3.10
shows that no greedy strategy can increase that
0.95
exponent above 1,
decreasing this “subexponential” fraction to “superexponential”.)

From this point on, we aim to
repair
failed vertices.
More precisely, if
v
is a failed vertex and there exists a vertex
v^{\prime}
such that
v^{\prime}
is adjacent to all vertices in
N_{H}(v)
and
v
is adjacent to all vertices in
N_{H}(v^{\prime})
, then swapping the labels of
v
and
v^{\prime}
reduces the number of failed vertices.
In the time available, the probability of constructing any particular such substitution is constant (bounded away from 1) and thus to go beyond what can be done greedily requires a large set of
swapping candidates

C(v)
dedicated to a single failed vertex
v
.
Intuitively (but read on), in the time remaining we cannot expect a vertex in
N_{H}(v)
to be offered enough times to form edges to every swapping candidate
v^{\prime}
,
so instead it is natural to use offers of
v^{\prime}
to form the edges to
N_{H}(v)
.
For there to be a reasonable chance that
v^{\prime}
is offered
|N_{H}(v)|
times requires spending
\Delta n
rounds.
At a high level, this is the strategy employed in
Theorem

1.1
,
and the source of its extraneous additive
\Delta n
.

To match the lower bound, after the greedy phase Builder can only afford to play for another
\Delta n\cdot o_{\Delta}(1)
rounds.
In this case, a swapping candidate will succeed with low probability, so
the strategy above will work only if we can
dedicate superexponentially many swapping candidates to each failed vertex.
This is possible if
there are fewer than, say,
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.04}\right)n
failed vertices,
but as said,
Remark

3.10
shows that no greedy algorithm can produce that outcome.

Enabled by a technical improvement in the greedy phase (see
Lemma

3.8
), our main contribution is a phase (see
Section

5
) that uses
\Delta n\cdot o_{\Delta}(1)
rounds and repairs all but a superexponentially small number of failed vertices
(leaving at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.04}\right)n
failed vertices, per
Lemma

5.2
).
We call it a
bridging phase
, since it bridges the gap between the greedy phase and a regime where the swapping strategy above works in
\Delta n\cdot o_{\Delta}(1)
rounds.
To put it another way, with the number of failed vertices bounded by
\exp\left(-\Delta^{c}\right)n
,
it brings the exponent from
c<1
to
c>1
.

Our bridging phase consists of two subphases.
In the first subphase — contrary to the “intuitively” above — for each failed vertex
v
,
we claim edges
wv^{\prime}
between neighbours
w\in N_{H}(v)
and candidates
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
,
in rounds in which
w
is offered, rather than
v^{\prime}
.
This works because here we ensure (by excluding a tiny number of failed vertices, “hubs” and hub neighbours, per
Definition

3.6
) that not too many failed vertices have
w
as a neighbour,
and we deliberately choose
C(v)
to be small,
with the result that
w
does not have too many required edges,
so it is likely to be offered enough times to claim all of them.
Thus,
almost every
neighbour
w\in N_{H}(v)
of
v
will be joined to every
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
after this first subphase.

In the second subphase, we return to the original idea of claiming such edges
wv^{\prime}
in rounds in which
v^{\prime}
is offered.
The key difference is that now a typical
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
is already connected to almost every
w\in N_{H}(v)
by the first subphase, so it is likely that in the second subphase
v^{\prime}
is offered enough times to claim the remaining edges.
It is then very likely that
some

v^{\prime}\in C(v)
can successfully be swapped with
v
.
(Edges between
v
and
N_{H}(v^{\prime})
are claimed in rounds where the corresponding vertex in
N_{H}(v^{\prime})
is offered, and are not problematic.)
Only a superexponentially small number of failures will remain after this subphase
(
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.04}\right)n
failed vertices, per
Lemma

5.2
), and
the original swapping strategy is then efficient enough to finish the construction.

This result was discovered while studying the same problem for a modified building process, the semirandom
tree
process introduced by Burova and Lichev in 2023
[
18
]
.
In this process, in the
t
th round Builder is offered the edge set of a spanning tree
T_{t}
chosen uniformly at random from all spanning trees on the vertex set
[n]
and independently of the previous rounds.
To maintain a clear distinction, we will refer to the usual semirandom graph process as the semirandom
star
process for the rest of this paper.
Burova and Lichev
[
18
]
showed, amongst other results, that in the semirandom tree process, perfect matchings and Hamiltonian cycles can be built in time asymptotically equal to their size.
(By contrast, strictly larger time is required in the semirandom star model.)
However, for clique factors, they could not establish even that time
O(n)
suffices, remarking that they were unable to extend the methods of
[
7
]
and asking if the analogue of
Theorem

1.2
might fail to hold in this model
[
18
, Question 1.5]
.
We obtain the following theorem, analogous to
Theorem

1.2
, in particular answering the question of Burova and Lichev in the negative.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
H
be an
n
-vertex graph with maximum degree
\Delta=\Delta(n)
. Then, in the semirandom
tree
process, Builder has a strategy guaranteeing that whp after

(2)

\displaystyle\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}

rounds, the constructed graph will contain a copy of
H
. Furthermore, the same statement holds after
\Poisson\left(\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}\right)
rounds.

As with
Theorem

1.2
, the two statements in
Theorem

1.3
are equivalent (see
Remark

2.4
).

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In
Section

2
we introduce all necessary notation and probabilistic tools. In
Sections

3
,
4
,
5
and
6
we describe Builder’s strategy in detail. In

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we state the graph theoretical and probabilistic results that we will use in our argument.

2.1.
Hajnal-Szemerédi Theorem

We will make use of the Hajnal-Szemerédi Theorem on equitable colourings (
[
33
, Theorem 1]
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
H
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta
. For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
and every
k\geq\Delta^{i}+1
, there exists a
k
-partition of
V(H)
such that

(1)

vertices in the same part are at pairwise distance at least
i+1

(2)

the part of largest cardinality contains at most one more vertex than the part of smallest cardinality.

Note that Hajnal and Szemerédi originally stated this theorem only for the case
i=1
. However, for larger
i
the result is implied by considering the auxiliary graph
H^{i}\supseteq H
that is obtained by joining all vertices of
H
that are at pairwise distance at most
i
in
H
.

2.2.
Chernoff bounds

We use the following Chernoff bound for Poisson random variables (see, e.g.,
[
44
, Theorem 5.4]
) and binomial random variables (see, e.g.,
[
34
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a random variable with mean
\mu>0
and either Poisson or binomial distribution. Then, for
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq\mu+t\right]\leq e^{-\mu}\left(\frac{e\mu}{\mu+t}\right)^{\mu+t}\leq\left(\frac{e\mu}{t}\right)^{t},

the weaker last form a convenience here.
Moreover, for
0\leq t\leq\mu
, it also holds that

\mathbb{P}\left[X\leq\mu-t\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\mu}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq\mu+t\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{3\mu}\right).

2.3.
Concentration of a number of events

We will repeatedly employ the following lemma, that if events for vertices some distance apart are independent, then the number of events is concentrated.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
0<p^{\prime}<p<1
. Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph with
maximum degree at most
\Delta=\Delta(n)
. For every
x\in V(G)
, let
A_{x}
be an
event which holds with probability at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)
and suppose that for any subset
X\subseteq V(G)
of vertices at pairwise distance at least
d
, the events
\{A_{x}\colon x\in X\}
are mutually independent. If
\Delta
is sufficiently large,
then whp the number of
A_{x}
which hold is at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{p^{\prime}}\right)n
.

It is clear from Markov’s inequality (and detailed below) that the lemma’s conclusion holds with probability tending to 1 as
\Delta\to\infty
, but the lemma’s “whp” means with probability tending to 1 as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let us define
q=\frac{1}{2p}
and let
X:=|\{x\in V(G)\colon A_{x}\text{ holds}\}|
. We consider two cases,
\Delta\geq\ln\ln n
and
\Delta\leq\ln\ln n
.

In the first case, since
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\leq\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)n
, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[X>\exp\left(-\Delta^{p^{\prime}}\right)n\right]\leq\frac{\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)n}{\exp\left(-\Delta^{p^{\prime}}\right)n}

\displaystyle=o_{\Delta}(1)=o(1),

using that (within this case) as
n\to\infty
, necessarily
\Delta\to\infty
.

In the second case, by the Hajnal-Szemerédi Theorem (
Theorem

2.1
) we may partition
V(G)
into
t:=\Delta^{d-1}+1
parts
V_{1},\ldots,V_{t}
such that for each
i\in[t]
we have
n_{i}:=|V_{i}|\geq\frac{n}{\Delta^{d-1}+1}-1\geq\frac{n}{2\Delta^{d-1}}
for sufficiently large
n
and vertices in the same part are at pairwise distance at least
d
. In particular, the events
\{A_{x}\colon x\in V_{i}\}
are mutually independent for each
i
.

Hence, if we let
X_{i}:=|\{x\in V_{i}\colon A_{x}\text{ holds}\}|
, then each

X_{i}
is binomially distributed, with
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}\right]\leq m_{i}\coloneqq\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)n_{i}
and
X=\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i}
. Note that our choice of
q
implies that
m
is almost linear in
n
. Indeed, since
\Delta^{d-1}
and
\Delta^{p}
are both
o(\ln n)
,

\displaystyle\ln m_{i}

\displaystyle\geq-\Delta^{p}+\ln n-\ln(2\Delta^{d-1})\geq(1-o(1))\ln n.

Then, by the Chernoff bound (
Lemma

2.2
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[X_{i}\geq 10\,m_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e\cdot m_{i}}{10\,m_{i}}\right)^{10m_{i}}<e^{-10m_{i}}\leq\exp\left(-10\>(1-o(1))\>\ln n\right)<n^{-9}.

Hence, since
t=\Delta^{d-1}+1
and
\Delta\leq\ln\ln n
, by a union bound the probability that there is some
i\in[t]
such that
X_{i}\geq 10m_{i}
is at most
n^{-8}=o(1)
. Hence, with probability
1-o(1)
,

X=\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i}\leq 10\sum_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}=10\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}=10\exp\left(-\Delta^{p}\right)n\leq\exp\left(-\Delta^{p^{\prime}}\right)n,

where the last inequality uses that
\Delta
is sufficiently large.
∎

2.4.
Poissonisation

A useful technique that we will employ is
Poissonisation
(see, e.g.,
[
44
, Section 5.4]
).
As in the second part of
Theorem

1.2
, we run the semirandom process not for a fixed number of rounds
\tau n
but a Poisson-distributed number of rounds
\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)
.
Equivalently, we may think of running the semirandom process for a fixed time
\tau n
, with offers occurring at times given by a Poisson process of rate
1
.
(In either case, each offer, independently, is of a uniformly random vertex.)

Analysing the Poissonised process is simpler because,
by the subdivision property of the Poisson process, the process described is equivalent to each vertex being offered at times given by a Poisson process of rate
1/n
, the processes all independent.
Hence, the following holds:

(3)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline:Poissonisation}\begin{split}&\text{In $\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)$ rounds, each vertex is offered $\Poisson\left(\tau\right)$ times, and the number of offers}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\text{for different vertices are mutually independent.}\end{split}

Remark 2.4
.

The two statements in
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
are equivalent.

Proof.

For either theorem,
the Poisson case would establish that, for some strategy and some positive
f(\Delta)=o_{\Delta}(1)
,
taking
\mu=(1+f(\Delta))\frac{\Delta n}{2}
,
with
\Poisson\left(\mu\right)
offers the strategy succeeds whp.
By the Chernoff bound (
Lemma

2.2
), this number of offers
\Poisson\left(\mu\right)
is whp at most

(1+\Delta^{-1/3})\mu
:
the failure probability is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Poisson\left(\mu\right)>(1+\Delta^{-1/3})\mu\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\Delta^{-1/3}\mu)^{2}}{3\mu}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^{1/3}n}{6}\right)=o(1).

This implies that the strategy succeeds whp in a fixed
(1+\Delta^{-1/3})\mu=(1+o_{\Delta}(1))\frac{\Delta n}{2}
rounds.

A similar argument shows the converse.
∎

2.5.
Balanced orientations

We will also use a lemma, shown in
[
7
, Lemma 2.4]
, on “balanced” orientations of a graph.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
H
be a graph. Then there exists an orientation
\vec{H}
of the edges of
H
such that
d^{+}_{\vec{H}}(v)\leq\left\lceil d(v)/2\right\rceil
for all
v\in V(H)
.

Lemma

2.5
can be proved by adding an extra vertex adjacent to vertices of odd degree, finding an Eulerian circuit in each connected component of the resulting graph, and deleting the extra vertex.

3.
The Greedy phase

By
Remark

2.4
, it suffices to prove the Poisson case of
Theorem

1.2
.
In the following sections we formally describe and analyse the different phases of Builder’s strategy. In each phase we assume Builder is given a (potentially empty) graph
G

When following the

Remark 3.2

Run the

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\Poisson\left(\tau_{\textsc{Out}}\right)<\left\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\right\rceil\right]

By the union bound, the probability of any failure is

3.2.
Second subphase: InGreedy

For reasons explained in

Definition 3.6

A

Remark 3.9
.

If we used only the

Remark 3.10
.

Although the

such that after swapping the labels of

and
v^{\prime}
in

(in

to neighbours of

(See

Denoting this set of required edges by the

if there exist a failed vertex

such that all edges in

(using

is repairable using
v^{\prime}
and we swap the labels of

and
v^{\prime}
, then neither vertex will be failed. We refer to this process as

v

For efficiency, we will attempt to repair a set

We will avoid trying to repair two adjacent vertices

and

at once, since repairing

means that

has an edge to

, but repairing

means replacing it with some

So we will restrict

For the same reason, no replacement candidate

To avoid confusion, and since anyway

from

We will also make the natural restriction of choosing distinct replacement candidates for each

That is, for each

of swapping candidates,
with these sets disjoint from

and from one another.
As discussed in the introduction, with

So furthermore, to avoid “overburdening”

To sum up, our constraints for swapping candidates are captured in the definition below.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a non-empty independent set of failed vertices. A

for

(C(v))_{v\in I}
of subsets of

of equal cardinality such that:

(1)

The sets
(C(v))_{v\in I}
are pairwise disjoint and disjoint from

(2)

The set
I\cup\bigcup_{v\in I}C(v)
has minimum distance at least 2.

(3)

The set
\bigcup_{v\in I}C(v)
has minimum distance at least 3.

The

of
\mathcal{C}
is the cardinality of

for any

\bigcup_{v\in I}\bigcup_{v^{\prime}\in C(v)}R(v,v^{\prime})
is called

-relevant

In all cases, we will construct the candidate list
\mathcal{C}
greedily.
That is, each vertex in

\Delta+1
vertices from consideration,
each candidate
v^{\prime}
blocks at most
\Delta^{2}+1
vertices,
and any non-blocked vertex can be added to a smallest set

.

In general, Builder’s aim is to claim
\mathcal{C}
-relevant edges whenever possible. However, to simplify analysis, we will generally define a set
A
of
active
vertices and only consider rounds in which Builder is offered a vertex of this set, where the definition of

depends on the phase of the building process.

In Sections

The strategy in both of these phases follows the same “metarule”.

(The

-rule)
.

Let

After a certain number of rounds of applying the MetaRule,

For every

and swap the labels of

4.1.
Batches and limitations

Let us sketch a naive strategy to repair vertices, and give some crude estimates of the parameters involved and the limitations of this strategy.

By the Hajnal-Szemerédi Theorem (
Theorem

2.1
), we can divide the set
F
of failed vertices into
\Delta+1
independent sets, which we call
batches
, of roughly equal size. Given a batch
I
, we can greedily construct a candidate list
\mathcal{C}
for
I
from the vertices in
V(G)\setminus F
satisfying
Definition

4.1
,
with coverage
s
. Naively it would seem that to maximise the chances of success, we would like to maximise
s
.

Now, up to unimportant factors polynomial in
\Delta
,
s
can be at most
n/\left|{F}\right|
. However,

Remark

3.10
means that the greedy algorithm must leave at least
\exp\left(-2\Delta\right)n
failed edges, and (up to
\Delta
factors) the same number of failed vertices, implying that in the best case
s\lessapprox\exp\left(2\Delta\right)
. Note that, in reality,
Lemma

3.5
would give us a slightly smaller
s\approx\exp\left(\Delta^{0.95}\right)
.

For
v\in I
,
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
and
w\in N_{H}(v)
, how will we claim
\mathcal{C}
-relevant edges
(v^{\prime},w)

(the thick edges in
Figure

1
)?
For each such
w
, there are precisely
s
such edges, one for each
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
.
On the other hand, for each
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
,
v^{\prime}
is a replacement candidate only for
v
,
and thus incident to at most
\Delta
such edges.
Since
s
is much larger than
\Delta
, we will be much more successful claiming such edges on offers of
v^{\prime}
.

Builder will spend
\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)
rounds per batch.
The chance that a given
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
receives
\Delta
offers, to make edges to all of
N(v)
, is crudely estimated by
\mathbb{P}\left[\Poisson\left(\tau\right)\geq\Delta\right]\approx\left(\frac{\tau}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}
.
So, the probability that a given
v
is repairable is, again estimating crudely with a union bound,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\text{ is repairable}\right]\lessapprox|C(v)|\left(\frac{\tau}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}\lessapprox s\left(\frac{\tau}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}.

However, after the Greedy stage we only have time for
o_{\Delta}(1)\Delta n
further rounds, so in order to spend
\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)
for each of
\Delta
batches requires that
\tau=o_{\Delta}(1)<1
.
So, estimating crudely, the probability that a fixed vertex gets repaired is

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\text{ is repairable}\right]\lessapprox s\left(\frac{\tau}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}\lessapprox e^{2\Delta}\Delta^{-\Delta}=o_{\Delta}(1),

and only a tiny proportion of the vertices would be repaired by this strategy.

4.2.
Workaround

To make this work, we employ a “bridging” phase in our strategy, which uses two key ideas. First, by excluding hubs, we are able to divide
F
into fewer independent batches and hence spend more time on each batch (increasing
\tau
).

Second, and more importantly, we make the unintuitive choice to artificially reduce the coverage
s
.
In itself, this would make each vertex less likely to be swapped.
However, consider a thick edge in
Figure

1
: a
\mathcal{C}
-relevant edge
(v^{\prime},w)
with
v\in I
and
w\in N_{H}(v)
.
With
s\leq\Delta
it becomes more efficient to claim such edges with offers of
w
.
Doing so, we will be able to claim most of the
\mathcal{C}
-relevant edges incident to most
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
.
At this point, a typical
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
only needs to be offered a small number of times to claim the remaining
\mathcal{C}
-relevant edges
(v^{\prime},w)
, and so we switch and claim such edges with offers of
v^{\prime}
.

We will show that the following the above strategy results in just
\exp\left(-\Delta^{{1.04}}\right)n
failed vertices.

Then, in the “final” phase, the method works as presented in
Section

4.1
: we can take
s
about
\exp\left(\Delta^{1.04}\right)
(and larger in subsequent iterations),
at which point the quantity
s(\frac{1}{\Delta})^{\Delta}
in (

) becomes large, and repair becomes overwhelmingly likely.
That is, in
Figure

1
, thick edges like
(w,v_{1})
are made on offer of
v_{1}
: it is unlikely that
v_{1}
receives
\Delta

Remark 6.2
.

This procedure differs structurally a little from that in the bridging phase.
There,
one time

Here, we construct just

In the bridging phase, the partitioning was essential: if we repeatedly chose new independent sets, a failed vertex would be in none of them with substantial probability, so we could reduce the number of failures only by a constant factor.
In this final phase, the iteration is essential to resolve every single failed vertex.
Within an iteration, we choose to work with a single independent set, for simplicity. (Alternatively, we could have worked in turn through
\Delta+1
independent sets partitioning

Our choice means that there is no gain in resolving more than a constant fraction of the failures in an independent set: even if we resolved absolutely every failure in one independent set, that would resolve only a

fraction of all failures, and resolving (for example) half this many leads to running time of the same order of magnitude.
So, reminiscent of the greedy phase, extra iterations here decrease the number of failures “only” exponentially; the difference is that now the iterations get faster and faster.

\Poisson\left(\tau_{F}\cdot n\right)

\tau_{F}\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\Delta^{0.01}\right)
.

Hence, altogether, whp the number of rounds Builder uses is at most

\Poisson\left(\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}+2\Delta^{0.99}+3\Delta^{0.9}+\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{4}\Delta^{0.01}\right)\right)\cdot n\right)=\Poisson\left(\left(1+o_{\Delta}(1)\right)\frac{\Delta n}{2}\right).

The above together with
Remark

2.4
complete the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
∎

8.
The semirandom tree process

In this section we describe how Builder’s strategy can be adjusted to work in the semirandom
tree
process. While the main ideas used to derive
Theorem

1.2
are also applicable here, there are some technical difficulties we highlight in this section.

8.1.
General probabilistic tools and results

In this subsection, we introduce some probabilistic tools necessary to analyse the edge distribution of a random spanning tree and use them to derive some key properties about uniform spanning trees. To sample a spanning tree uniformly at random, we rely on the
Aldous-Broder algorithm

[
3
,
17
]
.
Roughly, this process performs a random walk on
G
(in our case,
G=K_{n}
) and only records the edges which lead to vertices not previously visited.
Specifically, begin with an arbitrary vertex
v_{1}\in V(G)
and perform a simple random walk
(X_{m})_{m\geq 1}
on the graph
G
with initial vertex
X_{1}=v_{1}
. For each vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus\{v_{1}\}
let
t(w)=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{N}\colon X_{m}=v\}
be the hitting time for
v
. Note that
t(v)
is almost surely finite for each
v
. We construct a random tree
T\subseteq G
by letting the parent of each
v\in V(G)\setminus\{v_{1}\}
be the vertex
X_{t(v)-1}
immediately preceding the first time we visit
v
.

Lemma 8.1

(
[
3
,
17
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. If
T
is constructed according to the Aldous-Broder algorithm on
G
, then
T
is uniformly distributed over all spanning trees of
G
.

We also rely on a result due to Moon
[
45
]
, appearing for example in
[
2
, p. 205]
. A
rooted forest
is a forest together with a choice of a root in each component. We can imagine a rooted forest as a directed graph, by orienting edges away from the root in each component. This defines a subgraph relation on the set of rooted forests on
n
vertices.

Lemma 8.2

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
F_{n,m}
be a rooted forest with vertex set
[n]
having
m
components. Then, the number of rooted trees with vertex set
[n]
containing
F_{n,m}
is exactly
n^{m-1}
.

Using the Aldous-Broder algorithm and
Lemma

8.2
we can derive the following two key lemmas about the edge distribution of a random spanning tree.

The first governs the probability of a
wasted
round, in which Builder is offered no edges from a predefined set of
relevant
edges.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
S\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
be a set of edges and
T
a tree on vertex set
[n]
sampled uniformly at random from all spanning trees of
K_{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E(T)=\emptyset\right]\leq\exp\left(-\,\frac{|S|}{n}\right).

Proof.

We may assume by
Lemma

8.1

that
T
is sampled according to the Aldous-Broder algorithm run on
K_{n}
starting from
v_{1}:=1
.
Suppose the vertices are added to the tree
T
in the order
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
.

Direct each edge in
\left\{v_{i},v_{j}\right\}\in S
backwards according to this order, i.e.,
directed from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, as
(v_{i},v_{j})
, if
j<i
.
Denote by
N_{S}^{+}(v_{i})
the set of edges out of
v_{i}
,
with
d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})=\left|{N_{S}^{+}(v_{i})}\right|
its outdegree;
note that
\sum_{i}d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})=\left|{S}\right|
since each edge gets directed.

We claim that, conditioned on the order, independently for each pair
0<j<i\leq n
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\operatorname{parent}{(v_{i})}=v_{j}\right]=f(i,j)\geq 1/n
, for some fixed formula
f
.
Consider the step after
v_{i-1}
has been added to the tree.
This step walks to a random vertex
w
.
It is convenient to imagine that the
K_{n}
contains self-loops, so that there are
n
possible outcomes for
w
including
w=v_{i-1}
; this makes no difference to the Aldous-Broder algorithm’s outcome.
There are two cases.
The first is that
w
is a new vertex, which thus becomes
v_{i}
, so
\operatorname{parent}(v_{i})=v_{i-1}
;
this occurs with probability
(n-({i-1}))/n
.
The second is that
w
is a revisit, equally likely to be any of
v_{1}
through
v_{i-1}
; this occurs with probability
({i-1})/n
.
In the second case case, by symmetry, any of
v_{1}
through
v_{i-1}
is equally likely to become
\operatorname{parent}(v_{i})
, giving probability mass
\frac{{i-1}}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{{i-1}}=\frac{1}{n}
to each
v_{j}
with
j<i
.
Vertex
v_{i-1}
has additional probability mass from the first case (and it is easy to see that the probabilities add to 1 as they must).
This defines
f(i,j)
and establishes the claim.

Any directed edge in
N_{S}^{+}(v_{i})
goes to a lower-index endpoint
v_{j}
,
and by the claim,
\mathbb{P}\left[\operatorname{parent}(v_{i})=v_{j}\right]\geq 1/n
.
For a given
v_{i}
these events are disjoint,
so
\mathbb{P}\left[(v_{i},\operatorname{parent}(v_{i}))\in S\right]\geq d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})/n
.
By the claim, these events are independent for distinct
v_{i}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E(T)=\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i}\left(1-\frac{d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})}{n}\right)\leq\prod_{i}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})}{n}\right)=\exp\left(-\sum_{i}\frac{d_{S}^{+}(v_{i})}{n}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\left|{S}\right|}{n}\right).

∎

In one round, by symmetry (see (
19
)),
the probability that Builder is
offered
any given edge is
\frac{2}{n}
,
but this does not mean that Builder will
claim
this edge, since several edges of interest may have been offered.
The next lemma shows that whenever Builder is interested in a set
S
of “not too many” edges,
each of those edges is claimed
with probability at least
\frac{1}{n}
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
S\subseteq\binom{[n]}{2}
be a set of edges satisfying
\left|{S}\right|\leq\frac{n}{4}
, let
e\in S
and let
T
be a tree with vertex set
[n]
sampled uniformly at random from all spanning trees of
K_{n}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E(T)=\{e\}\right]\geq\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

Since each spanning tree of
K_{n}
contains
n-1
edges, by symmetry it is clear that for each
e\in S
,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[e\in E(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{\binom{n}{2}}=\frac{2}{n}.

Then it suffices to show that the probability that
T
contains any fixed pair of edges is at most
\frac{4}{n^{2}}
, for if this holds, then for any
e\in S
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[S\cap E(T)=\{e\}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[e\in E(T)\right]-\sum_{f\in S\setminus\{e\}}\mathbb{P}\left[\{e,f\}\subseteq E(T)\right]\geq\frac{2}{n}-(|S|-1)\frac{4}{n^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{n}.

Now, let
e,f\in S
be an arbitrary pair of edges and assume first that they are adjacent. If we view this pair of edges as a forest (with
n-3
further isolated vertices) on
[n]
, then there are precisely
3
different rooted forests
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}
on the edge set
\left\{e,f\right\}
, since the roots of isolated vertices are fixed (recall that a rooted forest is a forest with a choice of a root in each component). By
Lemma

8.2
, for each
F_{i}
there are
n^{n-5}
rooted trees which contain
F_{i}
. Since each tree can be rooted in
n
different ways, it follows that the total number of spanning trees containing
\{e,f\}
is
3n^{n-4}
.
If
e
and
f
are not adjacent, then there are four possible rooted forests with edge set
\{e,f\}
, and hence by the same argument there are
4n^{n-4}
spanning trees containing
\{e,f\}
.

In both cases, by Cayley’s formula, the probability that
\left\{e,f\right\}
is contained in a uniformly chosen spanning tree is at most
\frac{4n^{n-4}}{n^{n-2}}=\frac{4}{n^{2}}
, as claimed.
∎

8.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We follow a strategy with the same three phases as that for the semirandom star.
In the
Greedy
phase, there is now no need to consider directed edges, and the two subphases described in
Section

3
can be combined into one, in which in each round Builder attempts to claim a failed edge.

Lemma 8.5
.

After
\Poisson\left((\frac{1}{2}\Delta+3\Delta^{0.9})n\right)
rounds of a greedy strategy, whp the number of failed vertices is at most
2\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.9}\right)n
and the number of failure hubs at most
\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.07}\right)n
.

Proof.

The first
\Poisson\left((\frac{1}{2}\Delta+2\Delta^{0.99})n\right)
rounds whp include

(\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\Delta^{0.99})n
rounds,
and we analyse the greedy strategy in three subphases, of durations

{\frac{1}{2}\Delta n}
,
{\Delta^{0.99}n}
, and
\Poisson\left(\Delta^{0.99}n\right)
.

We first claim that, after the first subphase, whp the number of failed edges is less than
\ell_{1}:=\Delta^{1/2}n
.
If not, by
Lemma

8.3
,
the number
X
of wasted rounds (and thus edges failed at the end) is at most

\Binomial\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta n,\exp\left(-\Delta^{1/2}\right)\right)
.
By the Chernoff bounds (
Lemma

2.2
),

\mathbb{P}\left[X\geq\ell_{!}\right]\leq\left(\frac{e\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Delta\exp\left(-\Delta^{1/2}\right)n}{\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{1/2}n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{1/2}n}<\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{1/2}n}=o(1).

So, whp
X<\ell_{1}
, contradicting the hypothesis that the number of failed edges remains at least
\ell_{1}
.

Similarly, suppose at the start of the second subphase there are fewer than
\Delta^{1/2}n
failed edges, but after the second subphase there are more than
\ell_{2}:=n/6
failed edges.
Then by
Lemma

8.3
, in each round Builder is offered a failed edge with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\ell_{2}/n\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-1/6\right)>1/10
, and hence the total number of rounds in this subphase in which Builder claims a failed edge is at least
\Binomial\left(\Delta^{0.99}n,1/10\right)
.
Whp this exceeds
\Delta^{1/2}n
, contradicting the assumption that there were fewer failed edges at the start of this subphase.

Then the third subphase starts with a set
S
of failed edges, with
\left|{S}\right|\leq\ell_{2}=n/6
.
By
Lemma

8.4
, in each round, for any
e\in S
, with probability at least
1/n
, the tree
T
offered intersects
S
uniquely in
e
. Only accept an edge in such rounds and do not update
S
. Should an accepted edge be the unique one again later, “accept” it again.

Since at most one edge can be accepted in each round, by a similar argument as in
Section

2.4
, if we let
X_{e}
be the number of times that an edge
e\in S
is accepted during this third subphase, we have that the random vector
(X_{e}\colon e\in S)
is distributed as a mutually independent collection of Poisson random variables, each of whose mean is at least
\Delta^{0.99}
, and so each edge fails to be accepted with probability at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)
.

In particular, the number of edge failures at the end of this subphase is dominated by
\Binomial\left(n/6,\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)\right)
, and by Chernoff is whp at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)n
.

For a vertex
v
to be a hub after the third subphase means that
\Delta^{0.1}
vertices in
N(v)
are failed, and thus at least
\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{0.1}
edges in
G[N^{2}(v)]
are failed.
As just argued, any such edge failed at the start of the subphase remains failed with probability at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)
, and edge failures are mutually independent, so the probability that a given
v
is a hub is at most

\mathbb{P}\left[\Binomial\left(\Delta^{2},\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)\right)\geq\Delta^{0.1}\right]\leq\left(\frac{e\Delta^{2}\exp\left(-\Delta^{0.99}\right)}{\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{0.1}}\right)^{\Delta^{0.1}}\leq\left(e^{-\Delta^{0.98}}\right)^{\Delta^{0.1}}=\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.08}\right).

Hence, the expected number of hubs is at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.08}\right)n
.
Furhermore, since edge failures are mutually independent, and “hub-ness” of vertices at distance five or more depends on failures in disjoint edge sets, by
Lemma

2.3
the number of hubs is whp at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.07}\right)n
.
∎

In the
Bridging
phase here,
the preprocessing stage needed for the semirandom star process is not necessary, due to the independence of offered edges in the tree model. As we will see below, it is sufficient that (as for the star process) we exclude the hubs and so can reduce the number of partition classes and increase the number of rounds spent on each class.

In both the bridging and final phases, the set of relevant edges is given as
S:=\bigcup_{v\in I}\bigcup_{v^{\prime}\in C(v)}R(v,v^{\prime})
, where
I\subseteq F
is an independent set,
\mathcal{C}
is a candidate list, and the set of swapping candidates
C(v)\in\mathcal{C}
and the edges
R(v,v^{\prime})
necessary for a swap are defined as in
Section

4
. We observe that for all
v\in I
and for all
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
it holds that
|R(v,v^{\prime})|\leq 2\Delta
and
\mathcal{C}
can be greedily chosen such that
|C(v)|=\frac{n}{(\Delta^{2}+1)|I|}
,
implying that

(20)

\displaystyle\left|{S}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in I}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in C(v)}|R(v,v^{\prime})|\leq|I|\cdot\frac{n}{(\Delta^{2}+1)|I|}\cdot 2\Delta\leq\frac{n}{4}.

In these subphases, as in the third subphase of the tree-process greedy phase, in a round where a tree
T
is offered, we will accept an edge
e
if and only if
S\cap E(T)=\left\{e\right\}
, and we do not update
S
(except of course when we switch to the next independent set
I
).
By (
20
) and
Lemma

8.4
, in each round, Builder claims a given
e\in S
with probability at least
\frac{1}{n}
and so, by a similar argument as before the probability that an edge
e\in S
is claimed after
\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)
is at least
1-\exp\left(-\tau\right)
and these events are mutually independent for different edges. Therefore, given a failed vertex
v
and a swapping candidate
v^{\prime}\in C(v)
, the probability that
v^{\prime}
can repair
v
after
\tau
rounds is at least

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[R(v,v^{\prime})\subseteq G\right]\geq(1-\exp\left(-\tau\right))^{2\Delta},

and by extension,

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\text{ is not repairable}\right]\leq(1-(1-\exp\left(-\tau\right))^{2\Delta})^{|C(v)|}.

In the
Bridging
phase, the strategy is this.
Partition the non-hub failed vertices into
\Delta^{0.1}
independent sets of equal size,
for each independent set create a candidate list of coverage
\Delta^{0.6}
,
and spend time
\Poisson\left(\tau n\right)
, with
\tau=\Delta^{0.8}
, on repairing each independent set, for time
\Poisson\left(\Delta^{0.9}n\right)
in all.
(For the star process we also avoided repairing hub-adjacent vertices, to avoid overloading their hub neighbours with relevant edges; consider vertex
w
in
Figure

1
.
In the tree process all that matters is that the gadgets’ edges are distinct; it is irrelevant if many such edges have a common vertex.)

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
G
be a graph containing at most
2\exp\left(-\Delta^{{0.9}}\right)n
failures and at most
\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.07}\right)n
hubs.
If Builder follows the strategy described, then whp afterwards there are at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{{1.06}}\right)n
failures (including hubs).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle(1-\exp\left(-\tau\right))^{2\Delta}\geq 1-2\Delta\exp\left(-\Delta^{{0.8}}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-\Delta^{{0.7}}\right).

Hence, (
22
) gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[v\text{ is not repairable}\right]\leq\left(\exp\left(-\Delta^{{0.7}}\right)\right)^{\Delta^{0.6}}=\exp\left(-\Delta^{{1.3}}\right).

By
Lemma

2.3
, whp the total number of such failures is at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.2}\right)n
.
Adding to this the number of hubs, the total number of failed vertices is at most
\exp\left(-\Delta^{1.06}\right)n
.
∎

In the
Final
phase,
\tau=o_{\Delta}(1)
, and hence by (
21
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[R(v,v^{\prime})\subseteq G\right]\geq(1-\exp\left(-\tau\right))^{2\Delta}\geq(\tau/2)^{2\Delta},

which is larger than in the semirandom star process (compare with (
17
)).
Choosing the coverage of the candidate lists here to match what was done for the star process, the equivalent of
Lemma

6.3
therefore holds here too,
and so do the equivalents for the rest of the final phase,

Lemmas

6.4
and
6.5
.

Finally, the equivalent of
Section

7
assembles the results from these three phases to establish
Theorem

1.3
.

9.
Discussion

In
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
we showed that in both the semirandom star process and the semirandom tree process, Builder can construct a copy of any
n
-vertex graph
H
with maximum degree
\Delta=\Delta(n)
in time asymptotically
\frac{1}{2}\Delta n
(the number of edges potentially required),

In contrast, for other graph properties it is known that the two models behave differently.
In the semirandom tree model, properties including
k
-connectedness and Hamiltonicity can be achieved in time
asymptotically equal to the number of edges
[
18
]
, whereas in the semirandom star process this is impossible
[
8
]
.
Specifically, in the semirandom star process, whp constructing a perfect matching requires at least
0.932n
rounds
[
30
]
and constructing a Hamilton cycle at least
1.26575n
rounds,
whereas in the semirandom tree process, whp a perfect matching can be constructed in
n/2+o(n)
rounds and a Hamiltonian cycle in
n+o(n)
rounds
[
18
]
.

It would be interesting to know if, contrary to the above examples, there is any graph property which is
easier
to achieve in the semirandom star process than in the semirandom tree process.

Question 9.1
.

Is there a graph property
\mathcal{P}

that can be achieved whp in the semirandom star process
asymptotically faster than it can in the semirandom tree process?

What about semirandom processes in which Builder is offered the edge set of a different spanning structure, such as a random Hamiltonian path
or a random perfect matching?

Viewed in a more general context, as a semirandomised version of a “picker-chooser” type game (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
), there is a natural generalisation of the semirandom tree process to arbitrary
matroids
.
(See, e.g.,
[
47
]
for an introduction to the theory of matroids.)
For example, one could consider the following semirandom process, which we call the
semirandom basis process
, defined over the
finite field model
.
For
q
a prime power, in each round Builder is
offered
a uniformly random basis
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
of the field
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
,
from which Builder may
choose
one vector.

As with random graphs, threshold phenomena have been studied in the finite field model
[
19
,
39
]
, which has been suggested as an interesting “toy model” for problems in additive number theory
[
32
,
48
,
52
]
. It would be interesting to know if there are strategies in the semirandom basis process which whp can build certain structures, for example, arithmetic progressions or Schur triples, much quicker than the thresholds for their appearance in the corresponding “classical” random finite field process, where a sequence of elements
\bm{v_{1}},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
is generated by choosing each
\bm{v}_{i}
uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0},\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{i-1}\}
.

Question 9.2
.

Let
q
be a prime power. Does Builder have a strategy in the semirandom basis process over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
to construct

(1)

a
3
-term arithmetic progression; or

(2)

a set
\{\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z}\}
such that
\bm{x}+\bm{y}=\bm{z}
,

in significantly fewer than
q^{\frac{n}{3}}
rounds?

The question is of interest because it follows from work of Chen and Greenhill
[
19
]
that in the classical random finite field process, whp both of these structures first appear when
\Theta\left(q^{\frac{n}{3}}\right)
elements have been chosen.
