Developmental pathways from authoritative mothering to early school-age externalizing problems: a longitudinal chain mediation model from preschool involving effortful control and negative emotion regulation
Authors: Ni Zhang, Jinyun Chen, Xin Guo, Guziyi Xu, Jia Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExternalizing problem behaviors (EPB) in children have an impact on their future tendencies towards criminal and violent behaviors. Maternal attitudes and behaviors are of great significance in the development of children’s temperament and emotion regulation strategies, which in turn affect children’s behavioral manifestations. This study aims to analyze the relationship between authoritative maternal parenting style (AMPS) in preschool children and children’s EPB in early school-age, and to analyze the mediating roles of temperament effortful control (EC) and negative emotion regulation strategies (NERS) as well as their sub-dimensions (passive coping, emotional venting, aggressive behaviors).MethodsIn this two-year longitudinal study, a cohort of 305 preschool-aged children (Mage = 4.23 years, SD = 0.84) at baseline (T1) was recruited from Chinese kindergartens. Data were collected at three time points: AMPS at T1, EC and NERS at T2, and EPB at T3.ResultsAMPS (T1) was significantly positively associated with EC (T2), and significantly negatively associated with NERS (T2) and their sub-dimensions. EC (T2) showed significantly negative correlations with both NERS (T2) and their sub-dimensions, as well as EPB (T3). Moreover, NERS (T2) and their sub-dimensions showed a significant positive correlation with EPB (T3). AMPS does not directly influence children’s EPB in early school-age; however, it can indirectly affect these behaviors through the parallel and sequential mediating roles of EC and NERS (including all their sub-dimensions).ConclusionsThe presence of AMPS in preschool children is associated with a reduced risk of EPB in early school-age, mediated through its influence on children’s EC and NERS.

Introduction

Problem behaviors in early childhood refer to behavioral manifestations that occur during children’s growth and development. These behaviors are influenced by multiple factors such as physiological, familial, and social determinants. Due to the immaturity of developmental abilities such as emotion regulation and social skills, young children may exhibit behaviors that deviate from age-appropriate normative standards [1, 2]. Researchers have further categorized these problem behaviors into two primary dimensions: internalizing and externalizing behaviors [3]. Among them, externalizing problem behaviors (EPB) are characterized by individuals releasing internal dissatisfaction and impulses outward, primarily manifesting as deficient self-control, hyperactivity, and aggressive behaviors. These behaviors violate social norms and ethical standards, specifically including excessive motor activity, disciplinary noncompliance, conduct disorders, and antisocial behaviors [4]. From the perspective of individual development in early childhood, children exhibiting EPB often demonstrate deficits in self-regulatory capacity, which not only impedes the cultivation of pro-social behaviors but also exerts detrimental effects on their psychological well-being. At the societal level, the persistence of such externalizing behaviors into adulthood substantially elevates the risk of criminal engagement, thereby posing potential threats to public security and social stability [5]. Therefore, investigating the developmental pathways and mechanisms of EPB in early school-age children is of critical importance for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Authoritative maternal parenting style and children’s externalizing problem behaviors

Social learning theory [6] highlights the importance of observational learning, imitation, and modeling. Children, in their early years, exhibit a pronounced capacity for imitation, making them highly susceptible to environmental influences and the behavior of others. During this developmental period, caregivers, especially parents, emerge as primary role models, given the extensive time children spend with them. Aligned with this perspective, the family system theory emphasizes the family’s role as a major influence on children’s development [7, 8], particularly through parenting practices [9]. From the perspective of the development of children’s brains and nervous systems, the brains and nervous systems of young children develop rapidly and are particularly sensitive to the upbringing environment [10]. Early adversity can lead to a reduction in the volume of children’s amygdala, hippocampus, etc., thereby causing more EPB later in development [11]. This is powerfully explained by the parental acceptance-rejection theory, which posits that the failure to fulfill a child’s fundamental need for parental acceptance—manifested as warmth, care, and support—is a form of early psychosocial adversity that elicits a profound sense of rejection. This perceived rejection, in turn, is a cross-culturally validated predictor of maladaptive outcomes, including heightened hostility, aggression, and a significantly increased risk of EPB [12]. Therefore, during the critical stages of a child’s nervous system and social development, it is particularly important for the primary caregivers to provide warm and accepting parenting behaviors for the child’s current and future adaptive development outcomes [13]. Within this framework, parenting styles function as proximal mechanisms that significantly shape children’s cognitive schemas, emotional regulation abilities, and behavioral development. Empirical evidence substantiates that these parenting modalities serve critical functions in both preventing maladaptive behaviors and implementing corrective interventions for existing behavioral challenges through neurocognitive and socioemotional pathways [14].

Parenting style encompasses the constellation of attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors parents exhibit in the process of raising and socializing their children [15, 16]. Based on dimensions of emotional support and control, researchers have commonly categorized parenting into three types: permissive (characterized by high support and low control, with minimal rules and a tendency to indulge children’s preferences); the authoritative style (combining high support with high control, encouraging autonomy within clear boundaries); and the authoritarian style (featuring low support and high control, demanding unquestioning obedience) [17, 18]. Among these, the authoritative style stands out as a significant protective factor against behavioral problems in preschoolers and is strongly associated with positive developmental outcomes [19]. It fosters children’s independence through cooperative decision-making and mutual respect—for instance, by involving children in family discussions and jointly setting goals. Children raised authoritatively tend to exhibit greater self-confidence, independence, and proactive social exploration, alongside superior problem-solving and emotion regulation skills. Furthermore, this parenting approach enhances intrinsic motivation and self-regulation [20], and helps to establish a positive family environment [21, 22]. By being warm, responsive, and reinforcing—attentively meeting children’s needs, providing timely comfort, and offering consistent positive feedback—authoritative parenting effectively helps prevent or reduce the occurrence of problem behaviors [14, 23, 24].

Within the family system, mothers often assume a central caregiving role, and their parenting approaches exert a particularly strong influence on children’s behavioral and emotional outcomes. Previous research has identified a significant negative association between authoritative maternal parenting style (AMPS) and early childhood externalizing behaviors [22, 25]. Building on this foundation, the present study aims to further examine the effect of AMPS in young children on EPB in early school age, with a focus on uncovering the underlying psychological mechanisms that underlie this relationship.

Temperamental effortful control as a mediating mechanism

Temperament refers to stable individual differences exhibited by individuals in terms of emotional expressivity, activity level, attentional focus, reactive characteristics, and self-regulatory capacity. These differences have a biological basis and begin to manifest in the early stage of development. According to different classification criteria, Rothbart categorized children’s temperament into negative emotionality, extraversion, and effortful control [26, 27]. Effortful control (EC) as the core trait of the self-regulatory system in children’s temperament, refers to an individual’s capacity to inhibit dominant responses and execute subdominant responses according to situational demands. This construct serves as a significant predictor of children’s emotional-social development and academic achievement [28–30].

Although EC has a temperamental basis, developmental science widely acknowledges that its specific trajectory and ultimate level are significantly influenced by early social experiences, particularly through parent-child interactions [31, 32]. AMPS, characterized by warm responsiveness and clear, reasonable demands, provides a crucial “scaffolding” environment in which children can practice and internalize self-regulatory skills. Within such supportive interactions, children gradually effectively develop the capacity to manage their attention, behavior, and emotions [33]. The preschool period represents a window of rapid development in self-regulatory capacities, during which AMPS exerts a particularly stable and meaningful influence on the maturation of EC [34]. As EC strengthens, it enables children to concentrate on problem-solving, flexibly shift their thinking patterns, filter out distractions, and approach challenges from multiple perspectives, leading to more efficient and adaptive outcomes. Given its relatively high stability across childhood, EC also serves as a robust predictor of future behavioral outcomes [30, 35]. For example, in adverse or challenging situations, children with higher levels of EC are better able to restrain impulsive emotional reactions, thereby reducing the likelihood of EPB [35–37]. Longitudinal evidence further underscores the formative role of parenting in this process. Eisenberg et al. found that maternal warmth and positive expressivity during mid-elementary school predicted higher EC two years later, which subsequently predicted lower levels of EPB in adolescence [31]. Notably, the primary causal direction flowed from parenting to child EC, with no empirical support for the reverse pathway. In light of this evidence, the present study conceptualizes AMPS as a core environmental factor that fosters the development of EC, which in turn is hypothesized to function as a key mediator for subsequent fewer EPB.

Negative emotion regulation strategies as a mediating mechanism

Emotion regulation, a crucial component of early socialization and a key mechanism in children’s psychological development [25], undergoes rapid growth and maturation during the preschool years [38]. This period thus presents an ideal window for investigating the various factors that influence the development of emotional regulation and its subsequent relationship with later adjustment problems.

Emotion regulation is defined as the process by which individuals influence the occurrence time, type, experience and expression of emotions [39]. It primarily involves the selection and application of specific strategies [40]—coordinated efforts, both conscious and unconscious, employed to achieve emotional goals [41]. These strategies are commonly categorized as either positive (or adaptive) or negative (or maladaptive) [42]. Previous studies have generally revealed that the use of negative emotional regulation strategies (NERS) is a stronger predictor of subsequent EPB in children [25, 42]. NERS, such as passive coping strategy, emotional venting strategy, aggressive behaviors strategy, often result in the suppression of children’s negative emotions and their direct transformation into EPB [42].

Although the overall NERS exhibits a general consistency with its three sub-dimensions, passive coping, emotional venting, and aggressive behavior, distinct essential differences exist in both the strength of their associations and the operational pathways through which they relate to children’s EPB. Specifically, while emotional venting strategies (e.g., crying, complaining) do not directly constitute EPB, yet frequent and dysregulated venting can indirectly increase the risk of such behaviors. Aggressive behavior strategies (e.g., pushing, snatching) are positively associated with EPB and directly elevate the likelihood of children exhibiting these behaviors. In contrast, passive coping strategies (e.g., avoidance, procrastination) exhibit only a weak association with children’s EPB and primarily exert indirect impacts [39, 43–45]. These findings further suggest that NERS are closely linked to later externalizing problems in childhood. The heterogeneity in strategy preferences implies that the core manifestations of emotional dysregulation—as well as the underlying psychological mechanisms involved—differ across strategies, which may in turn lead to varying predictive strengths for EPB.

The development of children’s emotion regulation strategies unfolds through early social interactions, with maternal parenting styles providing a critical developmental context that shapes emerging regulatory tendencies. Within supportive and responsive caregiving environments, such as those characterized by authoritative parenting, young children are progressively guided toward more adaptive regulatory patterns and exhibit a marked reduction in their reliance on negative emotion regulation strategies (NERS) [46, 47]. For example, when parents respond to children’s negative emotions with calmness and reasoned explanations, they not only model constructive regulatory behaviors for the child to observe and internalize but also actively scaffold the child’s growing capacity to understand and manage emotional experiences. Further studies indicate that an authoritative parenting style is particularly effective in guiding children to reduce the use of maladaptive emotion regulation strategies and adopt adaptive ones, thereby significantly lowering the likelihood of early-onset behavioral problems [48, 49]. Taken together, these findings suggest a developmental pathway in which NERS may function as a key mediating mechanism through which authoritative maternal parenting influences the emergence of externalizing problems over time.

The chain mediation of temperamental EC and NERS

As a key foundation of emotion regulation [31], EC is essential for the development of children’s ability to regulate their emotional responses [50]. Research shows that the ability to inhibit and control behavior and emotions contributes to the development of emotional expression and regulatory skills, serving as a reliable predictor of children’s emotion regulation [51]. Furthermore, cognitive flexibility, which emphasizes the capacity to flexibly switch between emotional and behavioral responses, is also closely related to the emotional regulation ability of preschool children [52]. EC develops rapid throughout early childhood [53], enabling children to flexibly regulate their thoughts, emotions and behaviors.

Children with high levels of EC show superior performance in emotional expression and comprehension, exhibiting a greater propensity to employ adaptive strategies for regulating persistent negative emotions. In contrast, children with low EC have difficulties in shifting their attention, suppressing inappropriate reactions, or activating positive coping mechanisms, which may prevent them from effectively regulating their own emotions [54, 55]. Previous studies have supported that the higher the EC level in children at a younger age, the fewer problem behaviors may occur during subsequent development [25, 56]. Extant research has consistently demonstrated that children’s EC exerts a significant impact on the selection and implementation of their emotion regulation strategies. Nevertheless, current scholarly inquiries predominantly focus on the overarching association between EC and NERS, and have yet to conduct an in-depth exploration of the differential associations between EC and the subdimensions of NERS—specifically, passive coping strategies, emotional venting strategies, and aggressive behavior strategies. For example, there remains a degree of ambiguity regarding two critical questions: first, whether EC, at a global level, exerts differential effects across the various subdimensions of NERS; and second, whether such differences are associated with the global functional characteristics of EC.

Guided by this evidence, we posit that AMPS in the preschool years shapes the development of later children’s EPB through a sequential mediation process involving early temperamental EC and subsequent NERS. Despite separate acknowledgments of the roles of EC and NERS, research exploring their interplay as a chain of mediators is limited. The accumulated findings thus lay the necessary groundwork for testing this integrated model.

The objective of this study is to adopt a two-year longitudinal research method, aiming to systematically elucidate the internal mechanism by which the AMPS at preschool-age, children’s temperamental EC, and NERS act on children’s EPB in early school-age. Furthermore, this study will also focus in depth on the subdimensions of NERS, analyzing their differential impacts on young children’s externalizing problems as well as the chain mediating role between them and EC. Through this in-depth exploration, the research results are expected to provide scientific evidence for educators and parents, helping them adjust parenting strategies in a targeted manner, accurately cultivate children’s emotion regulation abilities, and thus create a more suitable growth environment for children to promote their physical and mental health. Method

Participants

This study employed a longitudinal design with three waves of data collection (T1, T2, T3) at 12-month intervals. A total of 322 preschool children (aged 3–6 years) were initially recruited at T1 (December 2022). Follow-up assessments were conducted at T2 (December 2023, ages 4–7) and T3 (December 2024, ages 5–8), yielding 320 and 311 valid responses, respectively. Only participants who provided data at two consecutive time points were retained in the final sample. By the end of the third survey, a total of 17 participants had been absent at least once due to illness, transfer or other reasons (effective rate of 94.7%). The independent-samples t-tests revealed no significant differences between participants who completed all surveys and those who dropped out after T1 on any of the key study variables at T1 (all p-values > 0.05, ranging from 0.20 to 0.61). This indicates that the attrition was random and did not introduce systematic bias into the final sample.

The final analytical sample at T1 consisted of 305 children (Mage = 4.23, SD = 0.84), including 160 boys (52.5%) and 145 girls (47.5%). Among them, 64.9% were only children, and 53.4% had participated in early education programs. In terms of maternal education, 6.6% completed secondary or high school, 23.9% held an associate degree, 52.1% a bachelor’s degree, 14.1% a master’s degree, and 3.3% reported other educational backgrounds.

Measures

Authoritative maternal parenting style

In this study, the subscale of the Chinese revised version of the Parental Authority Questionnaire (PAQ) developed by Buri was used to measure mothers’ democratic parenting style (e.g., “Joint discussion of family affairs”) [18, 57]. Responses were collected on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Uncertain, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree). There are a total of 10 items. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the authoritative parenting style of mothers was 0.73.

Effortful control

The temperament of children was assessed using the Effortful Control subscale of the Chinese revised version of the Children’s Behavior Questionnaire (CBQ) developed by Putnam and Rothbart [27]. This Chinese revised version of the CBQ, as a reliable and valid assessment tool, has been widely used in the field of child temperament research [13]. This subscale comprises 30 items across five dimensions: attention focusing (e.g., “When drawing or coloring in a book, shows strong concentration.“), inhibitory control (e.g., “Is good at following instructions.“), perceptual sensitivity (e.g., “Seems to listen to even quiet sounds.“), falling reactivity (e.g., “If upset, cheers up quickly when s/he thinks about something else.“) and low intensity pleasure (e.g., “Enjoys gentle rhythmic activities.“), with responses recorded on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Extremely untrue, 2 = Quite untrue, 3 = Slightly untrue, 4 = Neither true nor untrue, 5 = Slightly true,6 = Quite true, 7 = Extremely true). In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the Effortful Control subscale was 0.79.

Negative emotion regulation strategies

This study utilized the Negative Emotion Regulation Strategies subscale from the Preschool Children Emotion Regulation Strategies Questionnaire developed by Lu [45]. The subscale comprises 17 items across three dimensions: passive coping strategy (e.g., “Immediately retreat to another place to avoid the child who hit him.“), emotional venting strategy (e.g., “Cry right away.“), and aggressive behavior strategy (e.g., “Immediately go push him too.“). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 2 = Seldom, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Always), with higher scores indicating more frequent use of specific strategies. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the negative emotion regulation strategies subscale in this study was 0.84.

Child problem behavior scale (CBCL-CR)

This study employed the Chinese revised version of the Achenbach Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL-CR) [58], which was originally developed by Achenbach (1991) [59]. Completed by the children’s parents, this scale was used to measure children’s externalizing problem behaviors. The original version of the questionnaire encompasses nine dimensions, with a total of 70 items. The subscale of externalizing problem behaviors consists of 34 items from three dimensions: hyperactivity (e.g., “Unable to sit still.“), aggression (e.g., “children often fight.“), and discipline violation (e.g., “Skipping school and classes.“). A 3-point Likert scale (0 = Does not match, 1 = Partially matches, 2 = Very much matches) was used for scoring. Higher total scores on the scale are positively correlated with the severity of externalizing problem behaviors in children. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.94.

Procedure

This study used a descriptive research design with two-year follow-up data and parent-report measures as the main approach. Prior to the initiation of the study in October 2022, the research team collaborated with partner kindergartens to reach out to target families. One week later, the first direct communication with parents was done to explain the study’s significance and participation details. In the subsequent phase, paper-based informed consent forms were distributed to the parents or legal guardians of the child participants, detailing the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and participants’ rights. Signed forms were collected to finalize enrollment, and consent was therefore obtained from the parents or legal guardians for all children in the study.

Upon completion of ethical approvals, a multi-wave longitudinal tracking approach was implemented, with natural classes serving as the sampling unit. Data collection proceeded at three time points: at T1, the Authoritative Maternal Parenting Style (AMPS) was assessed; at T2, children’s EC and NERS were measured; and at T3, children’s EPB were evaluated. All questionnaires were administered in paper format following standardized procedures for distribution, collection, and verification of completeness.

Data analysis plan

During the data processing stage, the collected questionnaires were initially screened, and invalid ones were excluded. Subsequently, Excel and SPSS 25.0 software were employed to conduct in - depth analysis and processing of the collected questionnaire data. The specific procedures were as follows: sequentially performing a common method bias test, descriptive statistical analysis, and correlation analysis. In this research, AMPS (T1) was designated as the independent variable, children’s EPB (T3) as the dependent variable, EC (T2) and NERS (T2) as mediating variables, and age (T1) as the control variable. The chain mediation model was verified using Model 6 in the SPSS macro PROCESS [60]. During the verification procedure, the Bootstrap resampling approach was utilized, with the number of resamples set at 5000 and the confidence interval set at 95%. To mitigate the risk of multicollinearity, all key variables were standardized prior to the test. Moreover, to further explore the mechanism of action of NERS in specific contexts, this study separately incorporated each sub - dimension of NERS (T2), including the passive coping strategy (T2), emotional venting strategy (T2), and aggressive behavior strategy (T2), into the aforementioned chain mediation model for in-depth analysis. The objective was to comprehensively disclose the role of NERS between the authoritative parenting style and children’s EPB at early school-age.

Common method biases test

This study collected data from the same research participants using multiple questionnaires. However, the primary data source relied on parent-reported measures, which might introduce potential response bias. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of findings, Harman’s single-factor test was employed to assess common method variance during data analysis for all research variables. The results revealed 25 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, among which the first factor accounted for 14.7% of the total variance significantly below the 40% threshold. These analytical outcomes suggest that common method bias was not a substantial concern in the current study.

The chain mediating effects analyses

As shown in the path analysis results of Fig. 2, the AMPS at T1, while controlling for child age (T1), has a significant positive predictive effect on children’s EC (T2) (β = 0.154, p < 0.01) and a significant negative predictive effect on NERS (T2) (β = -0.174, p < 0.01). In these regressions, child age (T1) was a significant predictor of NERS (β = 0.150, p < 0.05) but not a significant predictor of EC (β = -0.089, p = 0.164). In addition, EC (T2) has a direct and significant negative predictive effect on NERS (T2) (β = -0.247, p < 0.001). When the AMPS (T1), EC (T2), and NERS (T2), simultaneously predict EPB (T3), EC (T2) has a significant negative predictive effect on EPB (T3) (β = -0.148, p < 0.05), while NERS (T2) has a significant positive predictive effect on EPB (T3) (β = 0.262, p < 0.001). However, child age (T1) was not a significant predictor of EPB in this final model (β = 0.051, p = 0.428). The direct predictive effect of this model is not significant (β = -0.038, p = 0.497). The model shows a complete mediating effect [61].

Discussion

While previous evidence for the relationship between AMPS and children’s EPB has often been derived from cross-sectional studies, this study employed a longitudinal design to trace the developmental impact of parenting on EPB in early school-age children. Our findings indicate that AMPS, children’s EC, and NERS were all significantly associated with later EPB. Furthermore, AMPS exerted an indirect influence on EPB through the separate mediating pathways of EC and NERS, consistent with existing literature [36, 62].

Critically, a chain mediation analysis revealed that EC and NERS sequentially mediate the link between AMPS and subsequent EPB. The inhibitory effect of AMPS on EPB was fully mediated through these internal regulatory pathways, illustrating a developmental mechanism in which parenting shapes behavior via child-level self-regulatory processes, which supports the views of previous researchers [31]. Although we statistically controlled for the child’s exact age in months in all analyses to partial out the influence of normative maturation, the developmental period spanned by our sample encompasses substantial growth in self-regulation and marked declines in externalizing behaviors. This inherent variability, while representative of the preschool-to-school transition, may nevertheless influence the precision and generalizability of the estimated pathways. Therefore, we explicitly emphasize that the present findings should be interpreted as capturing processes operative across a wide developmental window. Future research would valuably refine these insights by examining whether and how these mechanisms differ within narrower, more homogenous age bands—particularly during pivotal transitions such as the shift from preschool to formal schooling.

The mediating role of EC of temperament

This study revealed that maternal authoritative parenting style exerted a significant positive predictive effect on temperamental EC, which in turn emerged as a significant negative predictive effect on EPB in early childhood. These findings are consistent with previous research outcomes [36, 63]. Children raised in an authoritative parenting style demonstrate enhanced capacities in behavioral self-regulation and impulse control, thereby promoting the development of EC temperament [64, 65]. Regarding externalizing problems, children with higher EC show a greater capacity to inhibit or activate their behaviors, thereby reducing the occurrence of externalizing problems. In contrast, those with weaker EC tend to exhibit significant challenges in shifting attention from immediate impulsive gratification to subsequent adverse consequences, i.e., exhibiting greater difficulty in controlling aggressive behaviors [66–68]. Taking building block activities as example, authoritative mothers cultivate children’s EC by guiding them to contemplate block arrangement and geometric selection, thereby facilitating the development of persistence and attentional focus through scaffolded trial-and-error exploration. Children with higher levels of EC develop enhanced capacity for behavioral and emotional regulation in daily contexts, which significantly reduces the occurrence of EPB. Among children, those with high EC aren more likely to comply with collective activity rules in kindergarten settings, effectively inhibiting inappropriate impulses such as random physical movements or vocal outbursts, while sustaining focused attention on task content [55].

The mediating role of NERS

This study found that the AMPS indirectly influences externalizing problems through NERS. Specifically, AMPS showed a significant negative association with NERS, while NERS in turn were positively associated with externalizing problems. These findings align with previous research [62, 69].

Under normal circumstances, mothers shape their children’s emotion regulation mainly through the following ways: Children directly imitating their mother’s emotional response or emotion regulation strategies and use them as a basis to construct their own emotion regulation strategies. The authoritative parenting style emphasizes a balance between high responsiveness and reasonable demands, characterized by respect for children’s autonomy and encouragement of emotional expression [70]. In daily family interactions, mothers with an authoritative style are more likely to adopt problem-oriented communication strategies rather than power-assertive approaches [71]. They engage effectively in emotional communication with children, guiding them to accurately identify and understand their own emotions. During the parent-child interaction process, mothers’ positive responses to their children’s emotional reactions contribute to enhancing the effectiveness of children’s emotion regulation. In addition, mothers do not set restrictions on children’s emotional expression, but create an inclusive atmosphere for preschool children so that children can avoid inhibiting the expression of their negative emotions, thereby preventing children from hindering the use of appropriate emotion regulation strategies, and avoiding the adverse effects of such inhibition behavior on children’s learning of emotional regulation strategies. A noteworthy finding worthy of in-depth exploration is that although children’s age does not directly predict EPB, it can significantly and positively predict the use of NERS. This indicates that for older children, the social situations they face are more complex. If they fail to establish adaptive emotion regulation patterns during this critical period, they tend to rely more on existing negative strategies, which indirectly increases the risk of developing EPB [72, 73]. This further highlights emotion regulation as a core mediating mechanism connecting individual maturity factors and behavioral outcomes. In conclusion, when children exhibit negative emotional reactions, mothers typically adopt the approach of patient communication to assist the children in identifying their emotional states, such as anger, sadness, disappointment, etc., and guide them to analyze the root causes of these emotions. Children who can effectively employ emotion regulation strategies and manage their emotions in challenging situations tend to show more positive social behavioral development [74, 75]. In contrast, those who frequently use NERS such as aggression, venting, or avoidance are more likely to experience impaired peer communication and exhibit more problem behaviors [76, 77].

In addition, through an in-depth exploration of NERS, it has been found that emotional venting strategy, as a negative emotion coping mechanism, has the most significant impact in the influence of AMPS on children’s EPB. Generally, when children encounter setbacks or conflicts, they tend to release their inner feelings of dissatisfaction through behaviors such as crying, screaming and throwing objects [78]. While this mechanism may provide temporary emotional relief for children in the short term, it does not facilitate the development of healthy emotion regulation capacities over extended periods. Conversely, it may foster excessive reliance on externalized behavioral responses to internal discomfort, thereby neglecting effective internal emotional management and regulation. This behavioral pattern could consequently increase the incidence frequency of EPB [79]. It is manifested in that when children face setbacks or feelings of dissatisfaction, they may choose to attack others to vent their negative emotions. Such behavior not only causes harm to others but also further intensifies the children’s own difficulties in emotion regulation. It ignores the long-term effects of emotion management and the impact on others. In this situation, children are likely to increase the occurrence of EPB [80]. Passive coping strategy refers to a behavioral pattern in which children refrain from initiating proactive measures to resolve encountered adversities. The emotion regulation strategies of passively coping children exhibit a developmental transition characterized by shifting from other-oriented social dependency toward self-directed individual approaches during early ontogeny [81]. The indirect effect of passive coping strategy is the smallest. This may indicate that under the AMPS, children are more inclined to actively confront problems rather than adopt avoidance or evasion strategies [82]. This reflects the effectiveness of authoritative mothers in cultivating children’s ability to actively deal with challenges and difficulties.

The chain mediating role of EC of temperament and NERS

Analysis of the chain mediation model indicated a graded pattern in the association between effortful control (EC) and the subdimensions of negative emotion regulation strategies (NERS), with the strongest link observed for emotional venting, followed by aggressive behavior, and then passive coping. This graded pattern, while observed through concurrent measurement of EC and NERS at T2, aligns closely with theoretical expectations regarding the core functions of EC. It is important to note that the proposed sequential mediation from EC to NERS, though strongly grounded in developmental theory [31], should be interpreted as consistent with the hypothesized developmental sequence rather than as definitive evidence of temporal precedence. Future longitudinal research employing staggered measurements is needed to robustly confirm the directionality of this relationship. Nevertheless, the clear differentiation in the strength of these associations lends robust empirical support to the overall serial mediation model proposed in this study.

The core components of EC, such as inhibitory control, attention control and activation control, exert a direct effect on the early stages of emotion generation [83]. When individuals encounter negative emotional stimuli, those with higher EC levels can utilize executive functions more efficiently. They inhibit impulsive urges to express primitive negative emotions, redirect attention away from emotion-eliciting triggers, or modify their cognition of emotional events through cognitive reappraisal, thereby fundamentally reducing the need for emotional venting [84].

From the perspective of the process theory of emotion regulation, emotional venting strategies are generally regarded as response-focused and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies. Their essence is a direct behavioral manifestation of poor impulse control. These strategies rarely involve cognitive regulation and may most directly reflect immediate emotional dysregulation caused by underdeveloped executive functions in individuals, particularly a lack of inhibitory control ability. When children are confronted with intense negative emotions, emotional venting becomes the most direct and automatic way of release if they lack effective cognitive reappraisal abilities or problem-solving skills [73, 85–88]. Therefore, the strength of EC has the most direct and strongest association with emotional venting strategies.

In contrast, aggressive behavior strategies are not merely expressions of emotional impulsivity. Although they contain significant impulsive components, they typically involve more external direction and interpersonal interaction processes, and may also include a certain degree of intentionality. While emotional impulsivity is an important precursor to an individual’s choice of aggressive behavior strategies, their eventual implementation is often multiply influenced by other cognitive processes such as hostile attribution bias, deficient social problem-solving skills, and moral desensitization [86, 89]. EC reduces the likelihood of children choosing aggressive behavior strategies primarily by inhibiting emotional impulses triggered by anger and frustration, as well as enhancing individuals’ problem-solving abilities and consequence-consideration capacities in conflict situations. However, given the multiplicity of motivations behind aggressive behavior strategies, the influence of EC on them is relatively weaker than its influence on purely emotional venting strategies.

Compared to emotional venting strategies and aggressive behavior strategies, passive coping strategies is found to have the weakest association with EC. This may be because passive coping is characterized more by behavioral “inaction” or cognitive “rumination” than by an active and overt loss of behavioral control. Children who frequently adopt passive coping methods may not lack the ability to inhibit impulses, but rather lack the activation control ability to initiate positive behaviors, develop effective plans, and organize actions. Given that this ability represents only one facet of the multifaceted construct of EC [90], the association between passive coping and the broader EC trait is necessarily weaker compared to strategies such as aggressive behavior or emotional venting, which may reflect deficits across multiple EC components (e.g., inhibitory control). Furthermore, passive coping strategies are closely linked to factors as low motivation, learned helplessness, and depressive tendencies [91, 92], which largely fall outside the conventional conceptual scope of EC. Thus, although EC may help reduce children’s reliance on passive coping, its capacity to promote proactive and adaptive behaviors is considerably weaker than its role in curbing purely emotional venting or aggressive behaviors. Taken together, because passive coping is influenced by a range of factors beyond the domain of EC, the predictive power of EC on such strategies is inherently limited.

In addition, the association between temperamental EC and NERS is also significantly influenced by parenting styles. This study further reveals that AMPS exert a chain effect on young children’s selection of NERS through EC, thereby clarifying the potential mechanism for predicting EPB. In other words, maternal adoption of an authoritative parenting style facilitates the cultivation of children’s temperamental EC. This temperamental EC has a significant negative effect on young children’s choice of NERS, thereby effectively reducing the probability of young children exhibiting EPB. This chain-like connection also indicates that the behavioral development of young children stems from the interaction of multiple factors, presenting a complex mechanism of action. Children growing up under an authoritative parenting style can typically effectively promote the positive development of the EC temperament [33]. However, there are significant differences among young children with different levels of EC in their application of emotion regulation strategies. A high level of EC ability reflects young children’s advantages in attention and self-control, while also demonstrating their excellent abilities in emotional expression and emotional understanding. When facing challenges and difficulties, young children with high EC ability are often able to quickly focus their attention, eliminate external distractions, and try various strategies to solve problems, continuously engaging in trial and error. In addition, these children can reduce the frequency of using NERS to cope with the adverse effects triggered by negative emotions [55]. Young children with low levels of EC exhibit weaker capacity to sustain focused attention when facing challenges and are highly susceptible to attentional disengagement due to external distractions; concurrently, they show significant impairments in behavioral self-regulation and a pronounced tendency to frequently adopt NERS [82]. Moreover, children who frequently employ NERS are more likely to exhibit more EPB [77].

Research significance and limitations​

This study has refined the theoretical framework of the relationships among AMPS, temperament, emotion regulation strategies, and problem behaviors in the field of early childhood development. It emphasizes the chain mediating role of temperament and emotion regulation strategies in the process where AMPS influence children’s problem behaviors. The research shows that the AMPS indirectly affects children’s EPB through the temperament of EC and NERS. For parents, the study suggests that adopting an authoritative parenting style is conducive to cultivating children’s temperament of EC, reducing the use of NERS, and lowering the risk of problem behaviors. For educators, this research provides a new perspective, highlighting that by improving the educational environment and cultivating children’s cultivating EC and adaptive emotional regulation strategies, a synergistic effect can be achieved to reduce children’s EPB and promote their healthy growth.

This study has certain limitations. First, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the reliance on a sample drawn only from China and primarily on maternal reports. Future studies would benefit from recruiting more diverse samples and incorporating multiple informants (e.g., fathers, teachers) to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of various parenting styles. Second, internalizing and EPB in early childhood often exhibit non-specificity and an overlapping nature, which introduces methodological complexity when clearly distinguishing between these behavioral dimensions. Future research needs to control this factor as a key variable to enhance the accuracy of research findings. Third, the conceptual model was tested as unidirectional, focusing on the pathway from AMPS to child EC and subsequent outcomes. It did not account for the potential bidirectional dynamics wherein a child’s EC may also influence the subsequent quality of maternal parenting. Future longitudinal studies with cross-lagged designs would be valuable to clarify these potential reciprocal influences. Finally, this study did not control for children’s baseline levels of EPB (T1) in the analytical models. This omission is primarily attributable to the research design’s focus on investigating the developmental pathway from parenting styles to behavioral outcomes, which led to the decision not to employ the identical EPB measurement instrument at T1 as was used at T3. This methodological limitation means we cannot entirely rule out the potential influence of children’s initial behavioral characteristics on the various paths within the model. Future longitudinal studies should include baseline measurements of the outcome variable.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study expands the theoretical system of the relationships among parenting styles, temperament, emotion regulation strategies, and problem behaviors in the field of childhood development. The results show that the AMPS during preschool-age indirectly affects children’s EPB in early school-age through their temperament of EC and NERS. Here, the temperament of EC and NERS play a complete mediating role. The study also finds that among NERS, emotional venting strategy has the greatest impact on children’s behaviors, followed by aggressive behaviors strategy, and passive coping strategy has the least impact. These findings contribute to understanding the influence of mothers’ parenting styles on children’s behaviors and provide theoretical support for formulating intervention measures, helping educators and parents design effective intervention measures to promote the healthy development of children.
