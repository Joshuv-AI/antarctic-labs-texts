Methylome profile of medaka eggs and sperm
Authors: Xuegeng Wang, Ramji K Bhandari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEggs and sperm are responsible for the continuation of generations. Following the epigenetic reprogramming of the embryo, core epigenetic information present in the sperm and eggs is transmitted to offspring somatic cells prior to the blastula stage, which specifically influences gene expression in the cells. Differences in the patterns of DNA methylation between the paternal and maternal genomes are critical to regulating allele-specific gene expression in the developing embryo, constituting the basis of genomic imprinting in mammals. While the information on allele-specific epigenetic information has been limited to mammals, it is not clearly understood whether non-mammalian vertebrate gametes possess any sex-specific allelic epigenetic information and whether somatic cells maintain the allele-specific epigenetic information, particularly DNA methylation. To determine the landscape of DNA methylation in paternal and maternal alleles in a non-mammalian vertebrate, we profiled the methylome of egg in medaka fish and compared it with our previously published medaka sperm methylome. We identified a set of gamete-specific differentially methylated regions (DMRs) in the genome- medaka eggs maintained a significantly lower global methylation profile than the sperm. Based on our sequencing depth and data, 10 DMRs were hypermethylated, and 237 DMRs were hypomethylated in the eggs compared to the sperm methylome. Somatic cells in blastula maintained some of those parental gamete-specific DNA methylation profiles. Those DMRs are associated with 70 genes, suggesting that they may have imprinted-like functions and warrant further investigation.KEYWORDS: Epigenetic reprogramming, Medaka, DNA methylation, imprinted gene, gametes

Introduction

The sperm and oocyte are highly specialized cells and can form an entire embryo upon fertilization. They carry both genetic and epigenetic information that is important for the development of the embryo. Genomic imprinting occurs in germ cells with epigenetic modifications unique to paternal or maternal alleles and controls the subsequent expression of genes in a parent-of-origin manner. DNA methylation is one of the epigenetic modifications associated with genomic imprinting [1,2]. The allele-specific epigenetic marks are established in parental germ cells and maintained in the somatic cells of the offspring. Errors in epigenetic imprinting in somatic cells may result in birth defects in the derived offspring [3].

Genomic imprinting is widely found in mammals but is considered absent in fish, such as zebrafish (Danio rerio), which lack genomic DNA methylation reprogramming during embryogenesis [4,5]. In goldfish, a previous study has reported that Igf2 CpG island was a parental differentially methylated region (DMR) that was hypermethylated in sperm, indicating that the genomic imprinting might exist in lower vertebrates [6]. However, the methylation pattern of goldfish Igf2 DMR was not maintained during embryogenesis, suggesting the function of the imprinted gene differs from mammals [6]. However, recent studies suggest this statement does not apply to all fish of all genera. For instance, it is reported that allelic hypermethylation of the sex determination region on the X chromosome silenced the expression of the X-borne hydin-1 gene, leading to sex determination to males in the channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) [7]. Whereas the Y-borne hydin-1 gene was undermethylated and expressed, which leads to the sex determination of females [7]. In the Siamese fighting fish (Betta splendens), a transposon-induced DNA hypermethylation on the X chromosome reduced the expression of the X-linked dmrt1 allele, leading to female development [8]. These results indicated the existence of genomic imprinting in the channel catfish and fighting fish [7,8].

Gonochoristic fish process their genome methylation in developing germ cells and post-fertilization embryos differently from non-gonochoristic fish [9–12]. Unlike zebrafish [4,13,14], the Hd-rR strain of medaka (Oryzias latipes), a gonochoristic fish with a distinct set of sex chromosomes, maintains a similar DNA methylation reprogramming mechanism occurring in mammals during the embryogenesis [15]. Although the methylome profile was determined by the non-sequencing method (5mC-ELISA), paternal methylation profiles in sperm are erased quickly during the first few stages of cleavage following fertilization of the egg, whereas females undergo erasure at a much slower rate. The genome is in the ground state of reprogramming during the morula stage. The de novo methylation proceeds in the embryo, proceeding to the blastula stage, and a high level of methylation is maintained thereafter. Despite similarities in epigenetic reprogramming mechanisms of the embryo and primordial germ cells (PGCs) between gonochoristic fish, including medaka, and mice, it is unknown if the medaka fish possess genomic imprinting mechanisms, particularly differential DNA methylation profiles between gametes and their maintenance in somatic cells of the embryo, comparable to mammals. Medaka has been extensively used to understand mechanisms underlying epigenetic inheritance of parental epigenetic traits [9]. To further understand how epigenetic inheritance of parental memory takes place, it is essential to compare the methylome profile of gametes to understand allele-specific epigenetic differences. Here, we determined the whole genome methylome profiles of eggs of medaka fish by whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and compared them with our publicly available methylome profiles of medaka sperm and blastula and bioinformatically screened the sequence data to identify potential imprinted-like genomic regions in the gametes (eggs and sperm) showing similar inherited patterns in somatic cells of the blastula stage embryos.

Materials and methods

Fish care and sample collection

The medaka (Hd-rR) strains were maintained under standard conditions at the University of North Carolina Greensboro according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #20-006). The embryos were staged by developmental time and morphology under a brightfield microscope [16]. Sperm epigenome was previously sequenced [15], eggs for epigenome analysis were collected in the present study using the same strategy, and genome DNA was isolated as previously described [15]. Briefly, eggs were collected 30 min after spawning and sorted to separate fertilized eggs from unfertilized eggs. After mitochondria depletion, genomic DNA was extracted from the egg samples using ZR-Duet™ DNA/RNA MiniPrep Plus kit (Zymo Research, D7003) according to the manufacturer’s manual and stored at −20 degree until analysis. A flowchart of the experimental design is presented in Figure S1.

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) libraries were prepared with NEBNext® Ultra™ II FS DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, E7805S) according to the manufacturer’s user manual as previously described [15,17]. The premade MethylC-Seq libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten instrument with pair-end 150 bp sequencing strategy at Novogene Inc. Sperm methylome data of the same colony males was obtained from the publicly available database [15], and details of the sequencing runs are provided in Table S1.

Bioinformatic data analysis

Bismark (Version: v0.23.0) was used to map bisulfite sequencing reads onto medaka reference genome (Ensembl release-95) with default parameters [18]. The reads only mapped to chromosomes were considered for further analysis. The utility ‘bismark_methylation_extractor’ wrapped in Bismark was used to extract methylation levels in each site. The mapped reads in both strands were considered to calculate the methylation level. If the CpG site was covered by at least five reads, it would be filtered for calculation. The software tool metilene (Version: 0.2–8) was used to identify differentially methylated regions (DMR) [19]. We chose the stringiest strategy, which is that the differential methylation of the locus should be present in all three biological replicate samples (n = 3, 3×Me), which limited the total number of DMRs present in eggs and sperm. The sequences are available on NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE160094.

Results

To gain insights into maternal genomic DNA methylation in medaka, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing on medaka unfertilized eggs, and compared it with published WGBS data obtained from the sperm and blastula [15,20]. WGBS analysis results showed that the global methylation level in CpG sites in the egg was significantly lower than that in the sperm (Figure 1a, 41.84% vs 56.27%, p < 0.001), which is consistent with the previous study [15]. Next, we classified CpG sites into three classes, hypermethylated (methylation level >0.75), hypomethylated (methylation level <0.25), and intermediate methylated (0.25 ≤ methylation level ≤0.75), according to their methylation levels. Consistent with the global genomic methylation, the proportion of hypomethylation CpG sites is higher in the egg than in sperm (Figure 1b and S1A, 35.22% vs. 11.19%). The proportion of hypermethylation CpG sites is lower in eggs than in sperm (Figure 1b and S1A, 15.45% vs. 19.40%). In contrast, methylation levels in non-CpG sites did not differ significantly (Figure S1B). To further examine the detailed DNA methylation across the genome, we employed a 5 kb genomic probe strategy [21]. Beanplot showed the distribution of CpG methylation levels of 5 kb genomic probe of medaka sperm and egg showed a consistent global methylation pattern (Figure 1c and S1C).

Asterisk indicates statistical significance (***p < 0.001). (b) Distribution of CpG methylation levels across the genome. (c) Beanplots showing the distribution of CpG methylation levels of the 5 kb genomic probes. (d) Promoters filtered with methylation levels in egg and sperm. (e) Gene ontology enrichment of hypo-promoters.

To investigate the genes involved in gametic DNA methylation in medaka, we filtered promoters both hypomethylated in the egg and sperm (Hypo-promoter) or both hypermethylated in the egg and sperm (Hyper-promoter). In total, 9,101 promoters were identified as hypo-promoter, and 32 promoters were identified as hyper-promoters (Figure 1d). Nearly half of the hypo-promoters overlapped with CpG islands, and a quarter of hypo-promoters were located on the CpG shores and shelves regions (Figure S1D). Gene ontology analysis showed these genes with hypo-promoters majorly enriched in organ development and cell fate specification-related items in the biological process; nucleosome, kinase, chromatin, and transcription regulator complex-related items in cellular component, and kinase receptor activity, DNA-binding transcription regulator activity and nucleic acid-binding activity-related items in molecular function (Figure 1e). We did not see significant enrichment with hyper-promoters. Intriguingly, most Hypo-promoters (8,592 out of 9,101) were also hypomethylated at the blastula stage (Figure S2), indicating their possible intergenerational inheritance and essential role in embryogenesis.

To determine parental allele-specific DMRs (PADs) in medaka gametes, we first identified differentially methylated regions (DMR) between sperm and egg with a threshold: methylation difference >0.1 and q < 0.05. In total, 247 DMRs were identified (Table S2). Within them, 10 DMRs were hypermethylated, and 237 DMRs were hypomethylated in eggs (Figure 2a and S3-S4). Approximately half of DMRs (117 out of 247) were overlapped with CpG islands (CGI), and 59 DMRs were located on CGI shores, 12 DMRs were located on CGI shelves (Figure 2b), suggesting a potential relationship between CGIs and PADs (Figure S4).

DNA methylation in medaka unfertilized egg and sperm reveals potential imprinted genes. (a). Gametic differentially methylated regions (gDMRs) identified between egg and sperm. (b) Relationship between gDMRs and CpG islands. (c) Genes overlapped with gDmrs. (d) A sample of gDMR hypermethylated in egg. (e) A sample of gDMR hypomethylated in egg.

Next, the DMRs were mapped to genic regions to predict PADs. As shown in Figure 2c, 103 DMRs are located in genic regions (exon or intron), 10 DMRs were located within the upstream 5kb regions, and 25 DMRs were located within the downstream 5kb regions. While 109 DMRs did not relate to any genes. In total, 70 genes were identified as associated with DMRs and defined as PADs (Table S3). Within them, 19 genes were not annotated, and 51 genes were annotated.

Among the annotated PADs, 9 were hypermethylated in the egg. A representative PAD with a DMR hypermethylated in the egg is shown in Figure 2d. There is an DMR located in the first exon of uqcrfs1. On the contrast, 42 were hypomethylated in the egg among the annotated PADs. The mctp1a gene with multiple DMRs hypomethylated in the egg is shown in Figure 2e.

Discussion

The present study identified a set of potentially imprinted-like genomic regions with distinct gamete-specific (parent of origin) DNA methylation patterns and their association with genes, which provide information on genomic imprinting-like features in lower vertebrates. Ross et al. compared the methylome of gametes in medaka and identified 54,579 DMRs hypermethylated in the sperm and 1,906 DMRs hypomethylated in the sperm [14]. Some DMRs, hypermethylated, or hypomethylated in the sperm, were preserved in embryos at later embryonic stages and shared some characteristics of genomic imprinting [14]. In the present study, the DNA methylation profile of the genomic region associated with the mctp1a gene was of paternal origin and established as differentially methylated in blastula (somatic cells), suggesting that intergenerational inheritance of paternal epigenetic marks by offspring. If such parental allele-specific DMRs (PADs) are imprinted allele-specifically or not need experimentation as tools are not available to check allele-specific methylation patterns in non-mammalian vertebrate species. They might be DMRs, or they might be associated with parental alleles. The Mctp1a protein enables calcium ion binding and is involved in the negative regulation of cell migration, endocytosis, and response to oxidative stress [22]. In esophageal cancer cells, the expression of mctp1a was down-regulated due to the hypermethylation at the promoter region of mctp1a [23,24]. Intriguingly, the lrrtm4, a homologous gene of the human imprinted gene LRRTM1 [25], was identified among the PADs. The LRRTM1 is a maternally suppressed gene in humans [25], whereas the medaka lrrtm4 is hypomethylated in the egg, indicating it may be paternally suppressed. The imprinted-like functional role of these genes, however, requires further investigation. Among the parental allele-specific DMRs identified in the present study, the uqcrfs1, involved in mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III assembly and respiratory electron transport chain [26,27] was hypomethylated in sperm and inherited as is by blastula via paternal inheritance. In somatic cells (renal carcinoma cells), the expression of uqcrfs1 was inversely correlated with the methylation levels of the uqcrfs1 promoter [28]. Another PAD was atp1b1, an integral membrane protein responsible for establishing and maintaining the electrochemical gradients of Na and K ions [29,30], which was silenced by promoter methylation in renal carcinoma cells [31]. The present results indicate that DMRs in sperm and eggs may cause differences in gene expression in embryos.

The DNA methylation profile of gametes is reprogrammed in embryos soon after fertilization. Although whether all fish reprogram their genome DNA methylation similarly or differently is debated [11,12,14], the results in medaka fish seem to be different from developmental or whole-life hermaphrodite fish, suggesting that fish with genetic control of sex have somewhat similar patterns of DNA methylation reprogramming of embryos after fertilization. Capturing a high-resolution DNA methylation profile at earlier stages of cleavages (1-cell stage through blastula) may clarify the process [12]. In the Hd-rR strain of medaka, DNA methylation dynamics of cleavage stage embryos from the 1-cell stage through blastula revealed a distinct pattern of reprogramming different from other strains of medaka and species [12,15]. The medaka sperm genome is hypermethylated, and the egg genome is comparatively hypomethylated [14,15]. Upon fertilization, the genome of medaka sperm undergoes rapid demethylation in the zygote at 1-cell through 16-cell stages and continues until the blastula stage, accompanied by oocyte genome demethylation [15] similar to mice. No studies, except for medaka, have examined DNA methylation reprogramming in cleavage stage embryos from fertilization to the 32-cell stage in fish, during which the significant erasure and re-establishment of DNA methylome seem to occur [12,14,15]. Not only the gamete and embryo reprogramming but similar patterns of genome methylation have also been found in primordial germ cells (PGCs) of medaka and mice [17], which broadens the advantage of medaka to be a suitable animal model to study epigenetic inheritance and gene imprinting [9]. In previous studies, we and peer researchers have characterized transgenerational defects induced by chemical and non-chemical stressors in medaka [32–35]. As genomic imprinting processes are epigenetically controlled and epigenetic processes are sensitive to environmental stressors [36–38], the present findings of gamete-specific differentially methylated genomic regions that resemble imprinted gene regions in mammals raise the necessity of further studies to illustrate the essential role of those regions in embryo development [39], environment–gene interactions [40], epigenetic inheritance [41], epigenetic assimilation of phenotypic traits [42], and disease [43]. The present results provide a list of parental allele-specific DMRs, their association with genes in medaka gametes, and their persistence in offspring blastula, opening avenues to the community for further research utilizing fish to study epigenetic inheritance.
