Pearce’s Characterisation in an Epistemic Domain
Ezgi Iraz Su

Abstract
Answer-set programming ( 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} ) is a successful problem-solving approach in logic-based 𝖠𝖨 \mathsf{AI} . In 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} , problems are represented as declarative logic programs, and solutions are identified through their answer sets. Equilibrium logic ( 𝖤𝖫 \mathsf{EL} ) is a general-purpose nonmonotonic reasoning formalism, based on a monotonic logic called here-and-there logic. 𝖤𝖫 \mathsf{EL} was basically proposed by Pearce as a foundational framework of 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} . Epistemic specifications ( 𝖤𝖲 \mathsf{ES} ) are extensions of 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} -programs with subjective literals. These new modal constructs in the 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} -language make it possible to check whether a regular literal of 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} is true in every (or some) answer-set of a program. 𝖤𝖲 \mathsf{ES} -programs are interpreted by world-views, which are essentially collections of answer-sets. (Reflexive) autoepistemic logic is a nonmonotonic formalism, modeling self-belief (knowledge) of ideally rational agents. A relatively new semantics for 𝖤𝖲 \mathsf{ES} is based on a combination of 𝖤𝖫 \mathsf{EL} and (reflexive) autoepistemic logic. In this paper, we first propose an overarching framework in the epistemic 𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{ASP} domain. We then establish a correspondence between existing (reflexive) (auto)epistemic equilibrium logics and our easily-adaptable comprehensive framework, building on Pearce’s characterisation of answer-sets as equilibrium models. We achieve this by extending Ferraris’ work on answer sets for propositional theories to the epistemic case and reveal the relationship between some 𝖤𝖲 \mathsf{ES} -semantic proposals.



1
Introduction

Answer-set programming
(
\mathsf{ASP}
), introduced by Gelfond&Lifschitz

[
8
,
9
]
, is an approach to declarative logic programming. Its reduct-based semantics is defined by
stable models
(alias,
answer-sets
),
essentially the supported classical models of a logic program.

\mathsf{ASP}
has demonstrated success
in solving problems within logic-based
\mathsf{AI}
: a problem is first encoded
as a logic program, and then efficient
\mathsf{ASP}
-solvers are
employed to compute its stable models corresponding to the solutions.
However, as Gelfond pointed out in his seminal work
[
5
]
,

\mathsf{ASP}
encounters challenges in accurately representing and
reasoning about incomplete information. The difficulty
arises when a program involves multiple stable models, and
a proposition holds in one stable model but contradicts another.
The main reason for this drawback lies in the local performance of
\mathsf{ASP}
’s negation as failure (
NAF
) operator,
which handles incomplete information within individual stable models.
To address this issue and
enable collective reasoning about incomplete information,
we need additional tools in the language of
\mathsf{ASP}
.
Epistemic modal operators provide one potential solution to
\mathsf{ASP}
’s limitation
with incomplete information. By integrating such operators
into the
\mathsf{ASP}
-language, the new modal constructs in the extended language allow us
to quantify over a collection of stable models and check whether a proposition
holds in every (some) stable model.

The initial approach to this problem is by Gelfond’s

epistemic specifications

[
5
,
6
]
, referred to as
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
here:
Gelfond extended
\mathsf{ASP}
with epistemic constructs
known as
subjective literals
. Indeed,
with the incorporation of epistemic modalities
\mathsf{K}
and
\mathsf{M}
,
he could represent incomplete information within stable-model collections.
While a subjective literal
\mathsf{K}\,l
(
\mathsf{M}\,l
) makes it possible to check
whether a literal
l
is true
in every (some) stable model of a collection, in particular,
the epistemic negation
\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}
accurately captures collective reasoning of incomplete information.
The extended language is interpreted in terms of

world-views
, which are, in essence, stable-model collections.
However, researchers have soon realised that
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
allows unsupported world-views.
Thus, Gelfond himself
[
7
]
, along with many other researchers,
have proposed various semantic revisions for
\mathsf{ES}
; each aiming to eliminate
newly-appearing unintended results.
The first counter-example that undermines the soundness
of
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics is the model
\{\{p\}\}
resulting from
the epistemic rule
p\leftarrow\mathsf{K}p
. This problem with recursion through
\mathsf{K}

arises due to epistemic circular justification;
yet efforts to resolve this problem do not focus on the core
reasons for the emergence of unsupported models in
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
.
This situation leads to incrementally more complex reduct definitions. Although
we refrain from calling these solutions ad hoc, as they can be based on reasonable grounds,
we find it crucial to reveal the underlying reasons behind
the existence of such models under
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics. Moreover, we introduce a conventional and straightforward generalisation of ASP’s
reduct definition to epistemic logic programs,
which constitutes our first contribution here.

One line of world-view computing methods in the literature
depends on the reduct-based
fixed-point techniques within the logic programming domain,
with
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
serving
as the prototype and most subsequent formalisms being its follow-ups.
In a parallel, purely logical context, world-views are computed as (reflexive) (auto)epistemic
extensions of equilibrium models. The initial attempt in this direction
was made by Wang&Zhang
[
20
]
, whose semantics
has captured the world-views of
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
.
Sequentially, stronger formalisms followed
[
19
,
2
,
18
]
.
These epistemic equilibrium logics (
\mathsf{EEL}
s) share a common approach: a twofold
world-view computation process. First, they determine stable models of an

\mathsf{ES}
-program
\Pi
in terms of truth (
t
) by applying the

t
-minimality criterion of the formalism.
This involves generalising the usual
t
-minimality method which is used to
compute stable models (equilibrium models)
to
\mathsf{ES}
-programs, resulting in the epistemic equilibrium models (
EEMs
)
of
\Pi
.
The inclusion of epistemic constructs into the
\mathsf{ASP}
-language requires the minimisation of these concepts as well, which is fundamental in nonmonotonic epistemic logics. Thus, once
t
-stable models are determined, a
knowledge-minimality technique should also be applied to guarantee stability
in terms of knowledge (
k
). As a result, world-views are stable-models w.r.t. both truth and knowledge.
One formally strong
k
-minimality approach applied to
\mathsf{ES}
is Schwarz’s

[
13
]
minimal model reasoning for nonmonotonic modal logics. Cabalar et al.
[
2
]
pioneered the introduction of this technique to
\mathsf{ES}
,
proposing a new semantics based on a combination of Pearce’s
equilibrium logic (
\mathsf{EL}
)
[
12
]

and Schwarz’s nonmonotonic
\mathsf{KD45}

[
13
,
17
]

(equivalently, Moore’s autoepistemic logic). Their
formalism so represents a nonmonotonic epistemic logic of belief where

\mathsf{K}
is interpreted as
the self-belief of a rational agent
.
It also captures
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics under a foundedness restriction.
Su
[
18
]
then suggested employing
the reflexive closure of
\mathsf{KD45}
-models, namely
\mathsf{SW5}
-models,
in the search for
k
-minimal models and proposed reflexive autoepistemic
\mathsf{EL}
. This formalism alternatively applies Schwarz’s minimal model technique for nonmonotonic
\mathsf{SW5}
as a
k
-minimality criterion,
aligning it more closely with other
\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms where
the
\mathsf{K}
operator formalises
knowledge
.

The existence of many
\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms without a common agreement makes it difficult
to understand the current state of the art. Thus, as a natural continuation, we explore the relationship between them.
Our reference point will be classifying
\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms
under a twofold world-view computation method. We then generalise Ferraris’ lemma, enabling

the capture of equilibrium models of a theory as its stable models
, to the epistemic case. Using our new result, we transform
EEMs
to truth-stable (
t
-stable) models of epistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
and vice versa. This work will then help
\mathsf{ASP}
programmers
better understand existing
\mathsf{EEL}
s, being reflected in the
logic programming context and also give rise to a versatile and solid framework
in epistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
, an approach not studied before, which will be
our main contribution here.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows:
Sect.
2

provides preliminary information about
\mathsf{ASP}
and Gelfond’s primary

\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics.
Sect.
3
presents epistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
(
\mathsf{EASP}
) as a unifying
framework for several
\mathsf{ES}
-semantics.
Sect.
4
makes a short overview of the existing

\mathsf{EEL}
s in the literature, focusing on their
t
-minimality methods.
Sect.
5
establishes a correspondence
between these
\mathsf{EEL}
s and
\mathsf{EASP}
by generalising Ferraris’ lemma to
\mathsf{EASP}
.
Sect.
6
concludes the paper with
future work plan.

2
Background:
\mathsf{ASP}

and epistemic specifications (
\mathsf{ES}
) in a nutshell

In this context,
\mathsf{ASP}
-formulas are built from an infinite set
\mathbb{P}
of atoms
using the connectives, viz. reversed implication (
\leftarrow
), disjunction (
\lor
),
conjunction (
\land
),
NAF
(
\mathtt{not}
), strong negation (
{\sim}
), true (
\top
) and false (
\bot
). In
\mathsf{ASP}
,
a
literal

l
is an atom
p
or a strongly-negated atom
{\sim}p

for
p\in\mathbb{P}
. An
\mathsf{ASP}
-program consists of a finite set of rules
\mathtt{r}:\mathtt{head(r)}\leftarrow\mathtt{body(r)}
s.t.
\mathtt{body(r)}
is formed by a conjunction of literals possibly preceded by
NAF
,
and
\mathtt{head(r)}
is formed
by a disjunction of literals: for
0\leq m\leq n\leq k
,

\displaystyle l_{1}\lor\ldots\lor l_{m}~\leftarrow~l_{m+1}\,\land\,\ldots\,\land\,l_{n}\,\land\,\mathtt{not}\,l_{n+1}\,\land\,\ldots\,\land\,\mathtt{not}\,l_{k}\,.

(1)

Alternatively, we call
\mathtt{body}

goal
and its conjuncts
subgoals
.
When
m=0
, we suppose
\mathtt{head(r)}
to be
\bot
and call the rule
\mathtt{r}

constraint
. When
k=m
, we call
\mathtt{r}

fact
and omit both

\mathtt{body(r)}
and
\leftarrow
. When
k=n
, we call
\mathtt{r}
a positive rule.
A program composed of only positive rules is positive.
Finally, as strong negation can be removed from a logic program via auxiliary atoms, this paper mostly ignores
{\sim}
for simplicity.

A
valuation
is a consistent (possibly empty)
set
T
of literals, i.e.,
p\not\in T
or
{\sim}p\not\in T
for any
p\in\mathbb{P}
.
A valuation
T
satisfying an
\mathsf{ASP}
-program
\Pi
(which means
T\models\Pi
) is a
classical
model of
\Pi
.
Then,
stable-models of
\Pi
are
its reduct-based minimal classical models.
Stable-model semantics is based on a program transformation
that aims to eliminate ‘
\mathtt{not}
’
from
\Pi
w.r.t.
\Pi
’s
classical model
T
(a candidate model),
resulting in a positive program
\Pi^{T}
referred to as

reduct
of
\Pi
w.r.t.
T
:
(
reduct-taking
) replace

\mathtt{not}p\text{ with }\top
if
T\models\mathtt{not}\,p

(equivalently, if
T\not\models p
, i.e.,
p\not\in T
);
otherwise, with
\bot
.
This approach also requires that the valuation
T
be a smallest (minimal)
model of this reduct
\Pi^{T}
w.r.t. subset relation. Eventually,
the successful models of this process are called
stable models

(alias,
answer-sets
) of
\Pi
.

2.1
Gelfond’s epistemic specifications:

\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}

Epistemic specifications (
\mathsf{ES}
) extends
\mathsf{ASP}
-programs with
the epistemic modal operators

\mathsf{K}
(‘known’) and
\mathsf{M}
(‘may be true’).
The language
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{ES}}}
contains four kinds of literals:

objective literals (
l
)
,

extended objective literals (
L
)
,

subjective literals (
g
)
, and

extended subjective literals (
G
)
, viz. for
p\in\mathbb{P}
,

\begin{array}[]{c c c c}\boldsymbol{l}&\boldsymbol{L}&~~~~~~\boldsymbol{g}&\boldsymbol{G}\\
\hline\cr p\mid{\sim}p&~~~~~~l\mid\mathtt{not}\,l&~~~~~~\mathsf{K}\,l\mid\mathsf{M}\,l&~~~~~~g\mid\mathtt{not}\,g\end{array}

Note that
\mathsf{ASP}
’s regular literals are called objective literals in
\mathsf{ES}
.
By convention, the belief operator
\mathsf{M}
can be defined in terms of the knowledge operator
\mathsf{K}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}\,\mathtt{not}\,
, meaning that they are dual.

An
\mathsf{ES}
-rule
‘
\mathtt{r}:l_{1}\lor\ldots\lor l_{m}\leftarrow e_{1}~\land~\ldots\,\land\,e_{n}
’ is an extension of an
\mathsf{ASP}
-rule (
1
) with extended subjective literals
that can appear exclusively in
\mathtt{body(r)}
as subgoals. Thus,

\mathtt{body(r)}=e_{1}\land\ldots\land e_{n}
is a conjunction of arbitrary

\mathsf{ES}
-literals.
Then, an
\mathsf{ES}
-program
is a finite collection of
\mathsf{ES}
-rules.

Truth conditions:

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a non-empty collection of valuations.
Let
I
be a valuation, which is not necessarily included in
\mathcal{T}
. Then,
for an objective literal
l
and a subjective literal
g
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{lcllcl}\mathcal{T},I\models l&\text{ if }&l\in I;\hskip 104.0pt\mathcal{T},I\models\mathtt{not}\,l&\text{ if }&\!\!l\notin I.\\
\mathcal{T},I\models\mathsf{K}\,l&\text{ if }&l\in T\text{ for every }T\in\mathcal{T};\hskip 30.0pt\mathcal{T},I\models\mathtt{not}\,g&\text{ if }&\mathcal{T},I\not\models g.\\
\mathcal{T},I\models\mathsf{M}\,l&\text{ if }&l\in T\text{ for some }T\in\mathcal{T};&&\end{array}

Note that the satisfaction of an objective literal
l
is independent of
\mathcal{T}
,
while the satisfaction of a subjective literal
g
is independent of
I
.
Thus, we simply write
\mathcal{T}\models g
or
I\models l
.
Then, we define the satisfaction of an
\mathsf{ES}
-program
\Pi
as follows:

\mathcal{T},I\models\Pi
if for every rule
\mathtt{r}\in\Pi
,

\mathcal{T},I\models\mathtt{r}
, i.e., explicitly

\displaystyle\mathcal{T},I\models\mathtt{body(r)}\text{~~ implies~~ }\mathcal{T},I\models\mathtt{head(r)}.

An
\mathsf{S5}
-model is a nonempty collection of possible worlds, each with assigned truth values, where the connection between these worlds is defined by an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, and transitive).
In this context, we assume an
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\mathcal{T}

to be in the form of a nonempty set of valuations s.t. any two valuations are related.
When
\mathcal{T},T\models\Pi

for every
T\in\mathcal{T}
,
we say that
\mathcal{T}
is a
classical

\mathsf{S5}
-
model

of
\Pi
.
In particular, when we designate a valuation
T
s.t.
\mathcal{T},T\models\Pi
, we call
(\mathcal{T},T)
a
pointed

\mathsf{S5}
-model
of
\Pi
. Extending this to a set
\mathcal{T}_{0}
of designated valuations,

\langle\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}_{0}\rangle
is said to be a
multi-pointed

\mathsf{S5}
-model of
\Pi
.
To facilitate reading, we symbolise a multi-pointed

\mathsf{S5}
-model
\langle\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}_{0}\rangle

by underlying its designated valuations
T\in\mathcal{T}_{0}

in an explicit representation of
\mathcal{T}
.
Given
\mathcal{T}=\{\{a\},\{b\},\emptyset\}
, the (multi)pointed
\mathsf{S5}
-models

\langle\mathcal{T},\{a\}\rangle
and
\langle\mathcal{T},\{\{a\},\{b\}\}\rangle
correspond to

\{\underline{\{a\}},\{b\},\emptyset\}
and

\{\underline{\{a\}},\underline{\{b\}},\emptyset\}
respectively.
When no valuation is underlined or specified, by default this means that any valuation of
\mathcal{T}
behaves as designated.
The rest of the paper uses the terms “point”, “valuation” and
“world” interchangeably. Finally,
given a syntactic
\mathsf{ES}
-construct (head, rule, program, etc.)

\varphi
, when
\mathcal{T},T\models\varphi
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}
, we simply
write
\mathcal{T}\models\varphi
.

Semantics:

An
\mathsf{ES}
-program
\Pi
is interpreted by means of its world-views, which are
selected from among its
\mathsf{S5}
-models. Thus,
given a candidate
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\mathcal{T}
of
\Pi
,
we first compute the (epistemic) reduct
\Pi^{\mathcal{T}}=\{\mathtt{r}^{\mathcal{T}}:\mathtt{r}\in\Pi\}

of
\Pi
w.r.t.
\mathcal{T}
by
replacing every subjective literal
\mathsf{K}\,l
(
\mathsf{M}\,l
), possibly preceded by
NAF
,
with
\top
if
\mathcal{T}\models\mathsf{K}\,l\,(\mathsf{M}\,l)
; otherwise, with
\bot
.
Then,
\mathcal{T}
is a
world view
of
\Pi
if

\mathcal{T}=\texttt{AS}(\Pi^{\mathcal{T}})
where

\texttt{AS}(\Pi)
denotes the set of all stable models of
\Pi
.
The reduct definition of
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}

is so oriented to remove extended subjective literals.
The resulting program
\Pi^{\mathcal{T}}

is then a nonepistemic, but not necessarily positive

\mathsf{ASP}
-program potentially containing
NAF
. In fact,
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}

offers a twofold reduct definition; first removing epistemic operators
w.rt.
\mathcal{T}
and then eliminating
NAF
w.r.t.
T\in\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{X}
akin to
\mathsf{ASP}
’s methodology.

Motivation

Example 1

The one-rule program

Example 2

Another recursive program
\Sigma=\{a\leftarrow\mathsf{M}\,a\}
yields the same world-views in
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
. Researchers have widely varying perspectives on the intended models of
\Sigma
.
While some find both models reasonable, the others argue that
\Sigma
should
have one model; yet they also differ on which model should be preferred. We will not engage in
this debate, as different approaches may prove useful depending on the specific problem
at hand. Our stance on the topic is distinct. In alignment with Su et al.’s
approach
[
19
]
, and following the tradition of intuitionistic modal logics,
we will adopt a positive belief operator here, namely

,
which is not definable in terms of
\mathsf{K}
and

. As
\mathsf{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{def}}}{{=}}\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}\,\mathtt{not}\,
, in our opinion,

\mathsf{M}
cannot be regarded as purely positive like
\mathtt{not}\,\mathtt{not}\,a
in
\mathsf{ASP}
. Remember that Su et al. handle
\mathsf{M}
as a syntactic sugar, giving a concise representation for the equivalent formulas

\mathtt{not}\,\mathtt{not}\,\hat{\mathsf{K}}
,
\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,\mathtt{not}\,\mathtt{not}
, and
\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}\,\mathtt{not}\,
.
Also recall that in epistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
, aligning with

\mathsf{ASP}
, double
NAF

should not vanish regardless of where it occurs. On the other hand,
similarly to
\Gamma
in Ex.
1
,
we claim that the intended model of
\Sigma^{\prime}=\{a\leftarrow\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,a\}
should
be
\{\emptyset\}
.

3
Epistemic Answer Set Programming (
\mathsf{EASP}
)

This section introduces a direct generalisation of logic programs under stable-model
semantics (aka,
\mathsf{ASP}
-programs) to epistemic logic programs
under stable
\mathsf{S5}
-model semantics. This new concept has been partially explored
by
[
16
]
.
The shift from the general term
world-view
to

stable
\mathsf{S5}
-model
in
\mathsf{EASP}
, and
equilibrium
\mathsf{S5}
-model

in the following section is intended to emphasise the purpose of this work.
Our main motivation for this study arises from the unsupported models that emerge
due to circular justifications under
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics
(see Ex.
1
-
2
).

\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
’s reduct definition deviates somewhat from the traditional approach.
We here propose a new reduct definition for
\mathsf{ES}
-programs,
oriented to eliminate exclusively
NAF
. Thus, our reduct is a positive program,
similar to the method in search for stable models.

The new approach exploits a two-step computation process,
focusing on stability in terms of truth (
t
) and knowledge (
k
).
The method involves finding the minimal models in terms of truth first, and then
refining them further w.r.t. a
k
-minimality criterion
to select stable
\mathsf{S5}
-models. Such models then capture truth and knowledge minimality concepts that is central in (nonmonotonic) epistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
.
In broader terms,
what we refer to as
t
-minimality in
\mathsf{ES}
is essentially an extension
of the familiar minimisation criterion of
\mathsf{ASP}
from classical models to classical

\mathsf{S5}
-models. However,
k
-minimality is a relatively new concern
within the
\mathsf{ASP}
field compared to the well-established method of
t
-minimality.
The necessity for such a technique has become evident
with the incorporation of epistemic
concepts into
\mathsf{ASP}
and the need to
maximise epistemic possibilities (i.e., ignorance).

A stable
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\mathcal{T}
of an epistemic logic program
\Pi

is its
\mathsf{S5}
-model
s.t. each valuation
T\in\mathcal{T}
forms
\Pi
’s
pointed
\mathsf{S5}
-model
(\mathcal{T},T)
where
T
is minimal
w.r.t. truth and
\mathcal{T}
is minimal w.r.t. knowledge.
For a nonepistemic
\mathsf{ASP}
-program
\Pi
, such valuations are
\Pi
’s
stable-models in
\mathsf{ASP}
, and the (unique) stable
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\mathcal{T}
is the set of all such models.
Similar to stable-models of
\mathsf{ASP}
, the intuition underlying stable
\mathsf{S5}
-models
is to capture the
rationality

of an agent associated with an epistemic logic program

\Pi
:
“
an agent is not supposed to believe
anything that it is not forced to believe.
”
The aim, in principle, is to determine which propositions can be
nonmonotonically
inferred from
\Pi
by considering all its stable-models.
These inferences are then used to deduce new information
about the knowledge of
\Pi
.

3.1
The Language of
\mathsf{EASP}

(
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{EASP}}}
)

The language
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{EASP}}}

extends that of
\mathsf{ASP}
by epistemic modalities
\mathsf{K}
and
\hat{\mathsf{K}}
.
Literals (
\lambda
) of
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{EASP}}}
are of two types;

objective (
l
)
and

subjective (
g
)
literals, viz.

l\coloneqq p~\mid~{\sim}p
and

g\coloneqq\mathsf{K}\,l~\mid~\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l

for
p\in\mathbb{P}
. Then,
\mathtt{not}\,\lambda

means
failing to derive
\lambda
, the query
\lambda?
is undetermined and
assumed to be false
; yet we do not offer literal interpretations
of the modalities for the sake of flexibility.

Replacing literals of
\mathsf{ASP}
with those of
\mathsf{EASP}
in (
1
), we obtain
an
\mathsf{EASP}
-rule

\mathtt{r}
, viz.

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\lor\ldots\lor\lambda_{m}\,\leftarrow\,\lambda_{m+1}~\land~\ldots~\land~\lambda_{n}~\land~\mathtt{not}\,\lambda_{n+1}~\land~\ldots~\land~\mathtt{not}\,\lambda_{k}\,.~~~~~~(\text{ for }0\leq m\leq n\leq k)

(2)

in which
\lambda_{i}
’s are objective or subjective literals for every
i=1,\ldots,k
.
When we restrict
\lambda_{i}
’s to objective literals, the resulting program
is a disjunctive logic program
[
9
]
.
Hence,
\mathsf{EASP}
-rules are conservative extensions of
\mathsf{ASP}
’s disjunctive rules (
1
).
Different from
\mathsf{ES}
, we allow
\mathsf{K}\,l
and
\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l
to appear in
\mathtt{head(r)}
.
While the use of subjective literals in the head has not yet been fully explored, we still
find it useful to provide the same syntax structure with
\mathsf{ASP}

for easier understanding of the approach. This way, extensions to richer
languages are straightforward via the main
\mathsf{ASP}
track.
An
epistemic logic program
(
\mathsf{ELP}
), also known as
\mathsf{EASP}
-program, is a finite collection of
\mathsf{EASP}
-rules (
2
).

3.2
Semantics of
\mathsf{EASP}
in terms of stable

\mathsf{S5}
-models

We first introduce
t
-minimality concept in
\mathsf{EASP}
. Based on the existing

\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms in the literature, we provide two slightly different approach.
For example, the program
\Phi=\{\mathtt{r}_{1},\mathtt{r}_{2},\mathtt{r}_{3}\}

\displaystyle\mathtt{r}_{1}=a\lor b.\hskip 50.00008pt\mathtt{r}_{2}=a\leftarrow\mathsf{K}\,b.\hskip 50.00008pt\mathtt{r}_{3}=b\leftarrow\mathsf{K}\,a.

(3)

may produce
t
-minimal models

\mathcal{T}_{1}=\{\{a\},\{b\}\}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\{\{a,b\}\}
; yet
it may also yield
\mathcal{T}_{1}
only, depending on how restrictive we want to be.
In
\mathsf{EASP}
, this subtle distinction originates from differing approaches
of
t
-minimality techniques,
emphasising functional vs. relational perspective.

Definition 1
(weakening of a point in an
\mathsf{S5}
-model
in terms of truth: functional approach)

Given a nonempty collection
\mathcal{T}

of valuations, let
\texttt{s}{\ :\ }\mathcal{T}\rightarrow 2^{\mathbb{P}}

be a
subset

function s.t.
\texttt{s}(T)\subseteq T
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
Let
id
refer to the identity function, and let

\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}]=\big\{\texttt{s}(T)\big\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{T\in\mathcal{T}}}

denote the image of
\mathcal{T}
under
s
.
A
functional (
f
) weakening
of
\mathcal{T}

at a point
T\in\mathcal{T}

by means of
s
is identified with
\langle\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}(T)\rangle

s.t.
\texttt{s}\neq id
on
\mathcal{T}
and

\left.\texttt{s}\right|_{\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\}}=id
, by which we take a strict subset of

T\in\mathcal{T}
and keep the elements of
\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\}
unchanged.
We say that
\langle\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}(T)\rangle
is

f
-weaker

than
\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle

on
T\in\mathcal{T}
and denote it by
\langle\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}(T)\rangle\lhd_{\texttt{f}}\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle
.

Def.
1
has already been introduced by
[
16
]
; yet the following more cautious approach is novel.

Definition 2
(weakening of a point in an
\mathsf{S5}
-model
in terms of truth: relational approach)

Let
\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}\ :\ \mathcal{T}\Rightarrow 2^{\mathbb{P}}

be a multi-valued
subset
function s.t.
\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\subseteq 2^{T}
and

\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\neq\emptyset
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
For ease of understanding,
we also design
\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}
as a serial subset relation,
relating each
T\in\mathcal{T}
to at least one element from
2^{T}

and form the collection

\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}=\big\{(T,H):H\in\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\big\}_{\scriptscriptstyle{T\in\mathcal{T}}}
.
Then, a
relational (
r
) weakening
of
\mathcal{T}

at a point
T\in\mathcal{T}
by means of
\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}
is identified
with
\langle\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\rangle

s.t.
\texttt{s}\neq id
on
\mathcal{T}
and

\left.\texttt{s}\right|_{\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\}}=id
, by which we replace only

T
in
\mathcal{T}
by a set of its subsets including
at least one strict subset
H\subset T
.
We say that
\langle\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\rangle

is

r
-weaker
than
\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle

on
T\in\mathcal{T}
and denote it by
\langle\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}(T)\rangle\lhd_{\texttt{r}}\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle
.

We now define a nonmonotonic satisfaction relation

\models^{*}
for
\mathsf{S5}
-models, involving
a
t
-minimality criterion based on set inclusion
over each set
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
Intuitively, this condition says that
none of the weakenings of
\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle
is an
\mathsf{S5}
-model
of an epistemic logic program (
\mathsf{ELP}
)
\Pi
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 3
(generalisation of the truth-minimality (
t
-minimality) criterion of
\mathsf{ASP}
to
\mathsf{EASP}
)

For a positive
\mathsf{EASP}
-program
\Pi
,
let
\mathcal{T}
be a nonempty collection of valuations, and
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T},T\models^{*}_{\!\texttt{f}}\Pi\text{ \ \ iff \ \ }\mathcal{T},T\models\Pi\text{ ~and~ }\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}(T)\not\models\Pi\text{ for every }\texttt{s}\text{ s.t.\ }\langle\texttt{s}[\mathcal{T}],\texttt{s}(T)\rangle\lhd_{\texttt{f}}\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle.

(4)

Thus,
\mathcal{T}
is a
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
-minimal
model of
\Pi
if

\mathcal{T},T\models^{*}_{\!\texttt{f}}\Pi
for every
T\in\mathcal{T}

[
16
]
. In this paper, we also define

\models^{*}_{\!\texttt{r}}
by replacing
s
with
\texttt{s}_{\!\texttt{r}}
, and

\lhd_{\texttt{f}}
with
\lhd_{\texttt{r}}

in (
4
) and produce

\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}
-minimal
models of
\Pi
accordingly.

Although the above definitions seem to be technically complex and daunting,
they are easily applied:

Example 3

Reconsider first the program
\Phi
,
identified by (
3
),
and its
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\{\{a,b\}\}
. Then
construct
2^{\{a,b\}}=\{\{a,b\},\{a\},\{b\},\emptyset\}
. Since the

f
-weaker models
\{\{a\}\}
,
\{\{b\}\}
, and
\{\emptyset\}
of
\mathcal{T}_{2}

do not satisfy
\mathtt{r_{3}}
,
\mathtt{r_{2}}
, and
\mathtt{r_{1}}
respectively,
\Phi
does not hold in them either. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is
a
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
-minimal model of
\Phi
.

What eliminates
\mathcal{T}_{2}
in the second approach is the relational nature
of the weakening methodology because now we have to consider all possible subsets
of

different from

and

2^{2^{\{a,b\}}}\setminus\{\mathcal{T}_{2},\emptyset\}
.
The element
\{\{a\},\{b\}\}

from this set, namely an

-weakening of

at the point

. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
fails to be a

-minimal model of

.

for every pointed
\mathsf{S5}
-model of

, viz.

and

.
Also note that

Thus, our reduct is not interested in

and

in

.

Fact 1

Functional minimality implies relational minimality
because any function can be defined as a relation.
Thus, a
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}
-minimal model of an
\mathsf{ELP}

\Pi
is a
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
-minimal model of
\Pi
,
but not vice versa.

Example 4

Consider the
\mathsf{EASP}
-program

\Sigma=\{\mathtt{r_{1}},\mathtt{r_{2}},\mathtt{r_{3}},\mathtt{r_{4}}\}

with its rules explicitly represented below:

\displaystyle\mathtt{r_{1}}=a\lor b.\hskip 30.0pt\mathtt{r_{2}}=c\leftarrow b.\hskip 30.0pt\mathtt{r_{3}}=d\leftarrow\mathsf{K}\,a.\hskip 30.0pt\mathtt{r_{4}}=\bot\leftarrow\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,d.

Note that
\Sigma
is a positive program.
We compute that
\big\{\{a\},\{b,c\}\big\}
is a

t
-minimal model of
\Sigma
:

\big\{\underline{\{a\}},\{b,c\}\big\}\models\Sigma

while its only

-weakening

\big\{\underline{\emptyset},\{b,c\}\big\}
refutes it. Likewise,

\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{b,c\}}\big\}\models\Sigma

while all its
f
-weakenings, i.e.,
\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{b\}}\big\}
,

\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{c\}}\big\}
,
and
\big\{\{a\},\underline{\emptyset}\big\}
do not satisfy it.
We leave it to the reader to show that

\mathcal{T}
is also
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}
-minimal; yet
we give a hint that while computing the
r
-weakenings of, for example,

\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{b,c\}}\big\}
, we consider
all possible models
including
\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{b\}},\underline{\{c\}}\big\}
,
\big\{\{a\},\underline{\{b,c\}},\underline{\emptyset},\underline{\{b\}}\big\}
, etc. There are 14 of such models.
Clearly,
\{\{b,c\}\}
is
\Sigma
’s other

that is unintended and to be eliminated under

-minimality conditions.
Note that like

is not

Remark 1

The need for relational minimality arises from the fact that under singleton
\mathsf{S5}
-models like
\{\{p\}\}
, the literals
\mathsf{K}\,p
,
\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,p
, and
p
are of no difference since quantification is trivially performed over just one valuation
\{p\}
.
For instance, notice that when we replace
\mathsf{K}\,l
by
l
in
\Phi

(
3
),
the resulting
\mathsf{ASP}
-program has the stable model
\{a,b\}
.
Using relational weakening, we increase epistemic possibilities
(points) while reducing truth. Quantifying over these points then reveals the nontrivial functionality of subjective literals.
In a sense, the relational
t
-minimality approach simultaneously embeds in itself a kind of
k
-minimality strategy by increasing ignorance with epistemic possibilities.
The difference between two minimality methods strikingly appears for
\Phi

under the
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\{\{a,b\}\}
(see Ex.
3
).
Adding the constraint

\mathtt{r_{c}}=\bot\leftarrow\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}\,a
into
\Phi
, the new program

\Phi^{\prime}=\Phi\cup\{\mathtt{r_{c}}\}
has a world-view
\{\{a,b\}\}
under several
\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms.
Some researchers find this result unsupported;
yet the existing
k
-minimality techniques is unable to eliminate this model.
Thus, a more restrictive
t
-minimality tool has been designed
to remove models like
\{\{a,b\}\}
while computing
t
-minimal models.
We do not discuss this issue here, as our aim is just to establish a correspondence between existing
\mathsf{ES}
-formalisms; to put it better, to demonstrate the reader how current epistemic equilibrium logics are manifested
in the logic programming domain.

We will now see how to compute stable w.r.t. truth (
t
-stable)
models of an arbitrary
\mathsf{EASP}
program potentially including
NAF
.
Satisfaction of the subjective literal
\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l
is the same as
\mathsf{M}\,l
in
\mathsf{ES}
.
What makes the
difference is primarily how the reduct definition handles them.

Definition 4
(generalisation of the conventional reduct
definition of
\mathsf{ASP}
to
\mathsf{EASP}
)

For an arbitrary
\mathsf{EASP}
-program
\Pi
, let
\mathcal{T}

be a nonempty collection of
valuations, and let
T\in\mathcal{T}
.
Then, the reduct
\Pi^{\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle}
of
\Pi

w.r.t. the pointed
\mathsf{S5}
-model
\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle
is defined by
replacing every occurrence of
NAF
-negated (i.e., preceded by
NAF
)
literals
\mathtt{not}\,\lambda
in
\Pi
with the truth-constants

\displaystyle\bot\text{ \ if \ }\mathcal{T},T

\displaystyle\models\lambda~~~~~~~(\text{ for }\lambda=l\text{ \ if \ }T\models l;~~~\text{ for }\lambda=\mathsf{K}\,l\ (\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l)\text{ \ if \ }\mathcal{T}\models\mathsf{K}\,l\ (\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l)\ );

\displaystyle\top\text{ \ if \ }\mathcal{T},T

\displaystyle\not\models\lambda~~~~~~~(\text{ for }\lambda=l\text{ \ if \ }T\not\models l;~~~\text{ for }\lambda=\mathsf{K}\,l\ (\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l)\text{ \ if \ }\mathcal{T}\not\models\mathsf{K}\,l\ (\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,l)\ ).

Thus,
\mathcal{T}
is a
t
-minimal model
of

\Pi
if
\mathcal{T},T\models^{*}\Pi^{\langle\mathcal{T},T\rangle}

for every
T\in\mathcal{T}

[
16
]
.

While Def.
4
provides a general definition,
its specialisation to
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
and
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}

).

Example 5

Consider the
\mathsf{EASP}
-program

\Gamma=\{\mathtt{r_{1}},\mathtt{r_{2}},\mathtt{r_{3}},\mathtt{r_{4}}\}
where its rules are explicitly shown below:

\displaystyle\mathtt{r_{1}}=a\lor b.\hskip 20.0pt\mathtt{r_{2}}=c\leftarrow\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,a\land\mathtt{not}\,b.\hskip 20.0pt\mathtt{r_{3}}=d\leftarrow\mathtt{not}\,\mathsf{K}\,a\land b.\hskip 20.0pt\mathtt{r_{4}}=\bot\leftarrow\mathtt{not}\,\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,c.

We claim that
\big\{\{a,c\},\big\{b,d\big\}\big\}
is a
t
-minimal model of
\Gamma
.
We first compute the following reducts:

\left.\begin{matrix}\hskip 26.00009pta\lor b.&\\
\hskip 26.49997ptc\leftarrow\hat{\mathsf{K}}\,a\land\mathtt{not}\,\bot.&\\
\hskip 16.49995ptd\leftarrow\mathtt{not}\,\bot\land b.&\\
\bot\leftarrow\mathtt{not}\,\top.\end{matrix}\right\}\Gamma^{\{\underline{\{a,c\}},\{b,d\}\}}\\
\hskip 15.0pt\text{ and }\hskip 8.0pt\left.\begin{matrix}\hskip 26.00009pta\lor b.&\\

The above reducts

and

(

)

means that the effect of the entire rule

is negligible,
and
\mathtt{r}
can be safely omitted. When

(

) appears as a subgoal,

,

\big\{\{a,c\},\underline{\{b,d\}}\big\}\models\Gamma^{\{\{a,c\},\underline{\{b,d\}}\}}
, all its

\big\{\{a,c\},\underline{\{b\}}\big\}

-model

-minimal model of

, and

-minimality tools

.

4
Epistemic Extensions of Equilibrium Logic

Equilibrium logic
(
\mathsf{EL}
) is a nonmonotonic formalism, basically proposed by Pearce
[
12
]
as a logical and mathematical framework of
\mathsf{ASP}
.

\mathsf{EL}
is based on
here-and-there logic
(
\mathsf{HT}
),
a three-valued monotonic logic which is intermediate between classical logic
and intuitionistic logic.
An
\mathsf{HT}
-model is an ordered pair
(H,T)
of valuations

H,T\subseteq\mathbb{P}
satisfying
H\subseteq T
.
The semantics of
\mathsf{EL}
, via
equilibrium models
, is obtained through a

t
-minimality criterion over
\mathsf{HT}
-models:
T
is an equilibrium model of

\varphi
iff
T,T\models_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{HT}}}\varphi
(i.e.,
T\models\varphi
)
and (
t
-minimality condition
)

H,T\not\models_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{HT}}}\varphi
for any
H
strictly included in
T

(
H\subset T
). In summary,
Pearce has generalised
\mathsf{ASP}
by characterising
its stable-models as equilibrium models in
\mathsf{EL}
.

(

)

Inspired by
\mathsf{EL}
’s success as a foundational framework for

,

[

,

,
19
]

an epistemic extension of
\mathsf{EL}
as an alternative semantics for
\mathsf{ES}
.
We here name their approach
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{20a}}}
and recall
how
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{20a}}}
produces its

-minimal models,

).
For our purposes, we do not include their

-minimality method,

-world-views among its

.

4.1.1
Epistemic here-and-there logic (
\mathsf{EHT}
)
and its equilibrium
\mathsf{S5}
-models
w.r.t. truth

\mathsf{EHT}
extends
\mathsf{HT}
with nondual epistemic modalities

and
\hat{\mathsf{K}}
, both of which are primitive and structurally identical to the modalities in

.
Depending on knowledge-minimality conditions,
these modalities may characterise different epistemic concepts, so

The language of
\mathsf{EHT}
(
{\cal L}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{EHT}}}
) is given by the
grammar below, where the formulas outside

are in bold.

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\mid\bot\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\varphi\rightarrow\varphi\mid\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\varphi\mid\boldsymbol{\hat{\mathsf{K}}}\varphi\,.~~~~~~~~(\text{for }p\in\mathbb{P})

As usual, the derived formulas
\neg\varphi
,
\top

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we first discuss the problems that arise under Gelfond’s original
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics, aiming to
shed light on the underlying reasons for these issues.
We also briefly overview the follow-up semantics, that were
primarily proposed to address the limitations of
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
.
Next, we introduce a flexible and robust
framework for epistemic logic programs called
\mathsf{EASP}
, which
already accommodates Su’s traditional
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
-minimal
\mathsf{S5}
-models,
as studied in
[
16
]
, and
their novel variations known as
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}
-minimal
\mathsf{S5}
-models.

We recognise that all existing epistemic equilibrium logics (
\mathsf{EEL}
s) in the literature
share a two-step world-view computation process. This motivates us to explore their similarities and beyond
within the
\mathsf{EASP}
context. To this end,
we generalise Ferraris’ lemma (see
[
4
]
, p. 3), which establishes
a correlation between stable models and equilibrium models, to the epistemic case.
We then examine how these
\mathsf{EEL}
s are reflected within the
\mathsf{EASP}
framework.
This approach also allows us to investigate whether
different
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{f}}
(
\texttt{t}_{\!\texttt{r}}
) minimality methods, such as
those presented in
[
16
]
and
[
19
]
, produce the same results
when considered at least within
the current
\mathsf{EASP}
language fragment. It is worth noting that the technique in

[
16
]
is
slightly easier than that in
[
19
]
, which
raises an immediate research question for future studies. Furthermore,
Ferraris’ generalised lemmas lead to the strong equivalence
characterisations of

\mathsf{EASP}
-programs through the logical equivalences of their translations in
\mathsf{EHT}
,
akin to Lifschitz et al.’s finding
[
11
]

in regular
\mathsf{ASP}
.
Finally, future work will also involve a more detailed investigation of how
Gelfond’s
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
-semantics can be reflected into the
\mathsf{EEL}
domain,
following a similar approach as discussed in this paper. This study will help us better identify
the problems of
\mathsf{ES_{\scriptscriptstyle{94}}}
, as well as its
possible similarities with other
\mathsf{ES}
-semantic approaches originally proposed
in the
\mathsf{EEL}
domain.
