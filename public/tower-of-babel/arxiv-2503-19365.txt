Improved Approximation Algorithms for Three-Dimensional Knapsack
Klaus Jansen Debajyoti Kar Arindam Khan

Abstract
We study the three-dimensional Knapsack (3DK) problem, in which we are given a set of axis-aligned cuboids with associated profits and an axis-aligned cube knapsack. The objective is to find a non-overlapping axis-aligned packing (by translation) of the maximum profit subset of cuboids into the cube. The previous best approximation algorithm is due to Diedrich, Harren, Jansen, Thöle, and Thomas (2008), who gave a ( 7 + ε ) (7+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for 3DK and a ( 5 + ε ) (5+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for the variant when the items can be rotated by 90 degrees around any axis, for any constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . Chlebík and Chlebíková (2009) showed that the problem does not admit an asymptotic polynomial-time approximation scheme. We provide an improved polynomial-time ( 139 / 29 + ε ) ≈ 4.794 (139/29+\varepsilon)\approx 4.794 -approximation algorithm for 3DK and ( 30 / 7 + ε ) ≈ 4.286 (30/7+\varepsilon)\approx 4.286 -approximation when rotations by 90 degrees are allowed. We also provide improved approximation algorithms for several variants such as the cardinality case (when all items have the same profit) and uniform profit-density case (when the profit of an item is equal to its volume). Our key technical contribution is container packing – a structured packing in 3D such that all items are assigned into a constant number of containers, and each container is packed using a specific strategy based on its type. We first show the existence of highly profitable container packings. Thereafter, we show that one can find near-optimal container packing efficiently using a variant of the Generalized Assignment Problem (GAP).

Introduction

Three-dimensional Knapsack (
3DK
) is a fundamental problem in computational geometry, operations research, and approximation algorithms.
In 3DK, we are given a set of items which are axis-aligned cuboids (defined by their width, depth, and height) with associated profits, and a three-dimensional (3D) knapsack which is an axis-aligned unit cube. The goal is to find a nonoverlapping axis-aligned packing (by translation) of a subset of items into the knapsack such that the profit of the packed items is maximized.

In 1965, Gilmore and Gomory
[
28
]
introduced the 3D Knapsack problem – motivated by cutting stock problems (e.g., cutting up of graphite blocks for anodes). They provided heuristics for the special case when the items need to be cut along axis-aligned planes.
Since then, the problem and its several variants (under practical constraints) have been well-studied in operations research – typically from the viewpoints of meta-heuristics, MILP, tree search, machine learning methods, and greedy algorithms
[
13
,
46
]
.
In the last decade,
3DK
has become a central problem in logistics to increase operational efficiency by efficiently utilizing the space in various domains, such as airline cargo management, warehouse management, and robotic container loading
[
7
]
.
The prominence of this problem in the industry is evidenced by the comprehensive survey on 3D packing by Ali, Ramos, Carravilla, and Oliveira
[
7
]
, who have mentioned hundreds of papers in recent years.
There have been multiple programming challenges and competitions focusing on variants of
3DK
, e.g., OPTIL.io
[
3
]
, ICRA palletization competition
[
2
]
, etc.
Several companies like Boxify
[
1
]
, Symbotic
[
4
]
, etc. are specializing in 3D packing for their operations.

Packing problems are fundamental and popular among mathematicians (e.g., see Kepler’s sphere packing conjecture
[
41
]
and
Soma cube
puzzle
[
29
]
).
Surprisingly,
3DK
is relatively less studied in computational geometry and approximation algorithms.
Only in 2008, Diedrich, Harren, Jansen, Thöle, and Thomas
[
20

provided the first provable approximation guarantees for the problem. They first gave a simple polynomial-time
(9+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms for any constant
\varepsilon>0
. Thereafter, they gave a more sophisticated
(7+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for
3DK
. For the case when the items can be rotated by 90 degrees around any axis, they provided a
(5+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm.
Afterward, Chlebík and Chlebíková
[
17
]
showed that the problem admits no asymptotic polynomial-time approximation schemes (APTAS) even for the cardinality case (when each item has the same profit).

Compared to the classical (1D) Knapsack, multidimensional Knapsack is much harder as there are different types of items and they can interact in a complicated way.
For example, 2D items can be big (large in both dimensions), small (tiny in both dimensions), vertical (large in height, small in width), or horizontal (large in width, small in height).
Most approximation algorithms for 2DK show the existence of a highly profitable structured packing where the knapsack is partitioned into a constant number of boxes such that each box contains only items of one type and can be packed easily by simple greedy algorithms.
Large boxes contain a single large item, vertical (resp. horizontal) boxes contain only vertical (resp. horizontal) items (thus one can essentially ignore the long dimension of the item and the box becomes a 1D knapsack), and small boxes contain small items (one can consider the area of an item as a proxy and use 1D knapsack solution).
However, this is difficult for 3D packing.
As pointed out by Bansal, Correa, Kenyon, and Sviridenko
[
10
]
, one of the obstacles to generalize methods from rectangle packing to cuboid packing is that “
the interaction of say 3-dimensional rectangles which are long in one direction but short in the other two seems much more complicated than in the two-dimensional case.
”

In 2008, the authors in
[
20
]
mentioned that “
it is of interest whether here (for
3DK
) an algorithm with ratio
(6+\varepsilon)
or less exists.
”
However, despite significant progress in the 2D case, there has been no improved approximation algorithm for
3DK
in almost two decades.

1.1
Our results

In this paper, we introduce
container packing
for 3D Knapsack. We show that we can divide the 3D knapsack into
O(1)
number of regions (called
containers
) such that the dimensions of these containers come from a polynomial-sized set.
For each container
C
we define its capacity
\mathtt{cap}(C)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and for each item
i
we define its size in
C
to be
f_{C}(i)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We also associate an algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
for the container.
We require
\mathcal{A}_{C},f_{C}
such that for any
C
and any set of items
T
satisfying the packing constraint
\sum_{i\in T}f_{C}(i)\leq\mathtt{cap}(C)
, almost all items in
T
(barring a small profit subset) can be packed into
C
by algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
.
The assignment of items into containers thus becomes a variant of the Multiple Knapsack problem (however, the knapsacks can have different capacities and items can have sizes specific to the knapsacks) — also a special case of Generalized Assignment Problem (GAP).
For
O(1)
knapsacks, there exists a PTAS for GAP
[
23
]
.
After the assignment of items to the container
C
, the corresponding algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
ensures that the items admit a feasible profitable packing.

Container packing is a powerful and general technique. For example, by choosing appropriate functions
f_{C}
, our containers can handle complicated packings, beyond simple greedy algorithms.
For example, we use six different types of containers in this work (see Figures
3
and
14
).
Apart from the Stack containers and Volume containers (similar to 2D packing), we introduce Area containers (packed using a variant of Next-Fit-Decreasing-Height (
NFDH
) algorithm
[
19
]
), Steinberg containers (where different types of items are packed together using our new volume-based packing algorithm
3D-Vol-Pack

– it packs items in layers where each layer is packed using either a greedy algorithm or a 2D packing algorithm called Steinberg’s algorithm
[
47
]
), and
\mathsf{L}
-Containers (where two different types of stacks are packed together when rotations are allowed).
Note that Steinberg container or
\mathsf{L}
-Container goes beyond simple stack or shelf-based algorithms and equips us to handle more complicated packings of different types of items.
Finally, we show that given an optimal packing, we can modify it to some
container packing
having significant profit. The functions
f_{C}
can be considered analogous to the
weighting functions
in bin packing
[
19
]
, and we expect that more complicated packing algorithms can be incorporated into this framework by suitably defining
f_{C}
.
We also believe that our techniques might find usage in other multidimensional packing and scheduling problems.

Using this, we obtain improved approximation guarantees for 3DK (see Table
1
).
In particular, we obtain
(139/29+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
3DK
and
(30/7+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
3DKR
(when rotations by 90 degrees are allowed around all axes).
For the cardinality case (when all items have the same profit), we obtain
17/4+\varepsilon
and
24/7+\varepsilon
, respectively, for the cases without and with rotations. Finally, in the case when the profit of an item is equal to its volume (also called uniform profit-density), we obtain approximation guarantees of

and
3+\varepsilon

(3-\varepsilon)
-approximation for

\varepsilon>0
, using the containers mentioned in this paper (see
Section

1.2
Related work

Two other related problems are 3D Bin Packing (BP) and Strip Packing (SP).
In 3D BP, given a set of cuboids and unit cubes as bins, the goal is to find a nonoverlapping axis-aligned packing of all items into the minimum number of bins.
In 3D SP, given a set of cuboids, the goal is to pack them into a single 3D rectangular box of unlimited height such that the height of the packing is minimized.
For 3D BP and 3D SP, the best-known (asymptotic) approximation ratios are
T_{\infty}^{2}\approx 2.86

[

]
and
3/2+\varepsilon

[

]

There has also been a series of work in 2D BP

and

[

37
]
.
Another related problem is the maximum independent set of rrectangles, where given a set of possibly overlapping hyperrectangles the goal is to find the maximum number of disjoint hyperrectangles

[

,
25
,
45
,
26
,
27
]
.
In 2D, many variants of the Knapsack problem have been studied where the knapsack is a unit square and objects are polygons
[

or circles
[

,
15
]
.
For more details on related problems, we refer the readers to surveys

Notation and Preliminaries

We are given a set of
n
items
I=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
Each item
i\in I
is a cuboid with width
w_{i}\in(0,1]
, depth
d_{i}\in(0,1]
, height
h_{i}\in(0,1]
, and an associated profit
p_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The volume of an item
i
is
v(i)=w_{i}\times d_{i}\times h_{i}
,
and its base area is defined as
w_{i}d_{i}
.
We are also given a knapsack
K:=[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1]
.
If an item
i
is placed (by translation) in an axis-aligned way at
(i_{x},i_{y},i_{z})
then it occupies the region:
[i_{x},i_{x}+w_{i}]\times[i_{y},i_{y}+d_{i}]\times[i_{z},i_{z}+h_{i}]
.
For a feasible packing we need
i_{x}\in[0,1-w_{i}],i_{y}\in[0,1-d_{i}],i_{z}\in[0,1-h_{i}]
.
In a packing, two items are nonoverlapping if their interiors are disjoint.
Our goal is to find a nonoverlapping packing of a subset of these items that maximizes the total profit.
In
3DK
, we allow only translation, whereas
3DKR
allows translation and rotation by 90 degrees around all axes.
For any set of items
T
, we shall let
p(T),h(T)
and
v(T)
denote the total profit, sum of heights, and volume of
T
, respectively. Let OPT denote an optimal packing and
\mathtt{opt}:=p(\text{OPT})
.

3
Container Packing

In this section, we first define container packing and describe the containers used in our work.
Then, we state our main structural result which shows that there exist container packings with high profit that can be searched for in polynomial time.

Definition 3.1

(Container Packing)
.

A packing of a set of items
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
is said to be a Container Packing if

•

the knapsack can be partitioned into a collection
\mathcal{C}
of non-overlapping regions called containers and some empty spaces. Each container
C\in\mathcal{C}
has an associated capacity
\mathtt{cap}(C)
, a function
f_{C}\colon I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and a polynomial-time algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
,

•

there is a function
g\colon I^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, such that for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, items in
g^{-1}(C)
are packed into
C
using algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
,

•

for any
C\in\mathcal{C}
and
T\subseteq I
such that
\sum_{i\in T}f_{C}(i)\leq\mathtt{cap}(C)
, for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a polynomial-time computable set
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
with
p(T^{\prime})\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))p(T)
such that
T^{\prime}
can be packed into
C
by the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
.

A Container Packing is said to be
guessable
if

•

the number of containers inside the knapsack is bounded by a constant
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
,
2
2

2

The notation
O_{\varepsilon}(f(n))
means that the implicit constant hidden in big-
O
notation can depend on
\varepsilon
.

•

the number of distinct possible types of containers (defined by their sizes along each dimension) is bounded by
n^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
, a polynomial in
n
,

•

whether a given collection of
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
containers can be placed non-overlappingly inside the knapsack, can be checked in
n^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
(polynomial) time.

We shall call a container
guessable
if it comes from some polynomially-sized set of distinct container types. Though, in this paper we only use cuboids as containers, the idea of container packing can be generalized to other shapes in 3D or higher dimensions.

We show that we can first use a PTAS for GAP (see
Appendix

B
for details) to assign items into containers and
then use the corresponding algorithms to pack items inside the containers – this gives us a near-optimal container packing.

Theorem 3.1
.

There is a PTAS for maximum profit guessable container packing.

Proof.

Since the number of distinct dimensions of containers is polynomially bounded, and a guessable container packing only has
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
containers, it is possible to enumerate all feasible choices of containers that fit into the knapsack in polynomial-time. For each such choice, we create an instance of GAP with one (1D) knapsack per container, where the (1D) knapsack corresponding to a container
C
has capacity
\mathtt{cap}(C)
and the size of an item
i
for the (1D) knapsack is given by
f_{C}(i)
. The profit of an item for any (1D) knapsack is set to be the same as the actual profit of the item. We now use the PTAS for GAP with a constant number of knapsacks
(
Lemma

B.1
)
to obtain an assignment of the input items into the containers that do not violate the container capacities. Finally, for each container
C
, we use the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{C}
to pack a subset of the items assigned to it, by losing only an
\varepsilon
-fraction of profit. Overall, this gives a PTAS to compute the maximum profitable guessable container packing.
∎

T_{2}
can be packed inside a Steinberg container of height
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We begin by discretizing the depths of the slots inside the wide compartments.

Claim 4.5
.

By losing a profit of at most
O(\delta)\mathtt{opt}_{1}
, the depths of all the slots for wide and small items inside a wide compartment can be assumed to be a multiple of
\delta^{9}
.

Proof.

We partition the compartment into strips each of depth
\delta^{5}
and discard the minimum profitable strip, see
Fig.

10
for an illustration. Note that the depth of the compartment is a multiple of
\delta^{4}
, and hence the number of strips is at least
1/\delta
. Since each item has depth at most
\delta^{10}
, any item intersects at most two strips, and hence the total profit of the deleted items is at most an
O(\delta)
-fraction of the profit of the compartment. For any slot that is intersected by the deleted strip, we push the remaining items towards the front face of the slot, and notice that
in the resulting packing, the wide and small items are packed using
NFDH
(ignoring widths) and
3D-NFDH
, respectively.

Figure 10
:
Rounding the depths of slots in a wide compartment.
The gray regions contain the small items.
All items intersecting the deleted strip are discarded.

The resulting free space of depth
\delta^{5}
inside the compartment will now be used to increase the depth of each arrangement. Specifically, we round up the depth of each arrangement (and hence each slot) to the next integral multiple of
\delta^{9}
. Since the total number of arrangements is
O(1/\delta^{2})
, the total increase in depth is bounded by
O(\frac{1}{\delta^{2}})\cdot\delta^{9}<\delta^{5}
.
∎

In an analogous way, the widths of the slots inside long compartments can be made integral multiples of
\delta^{9}
. We next round up the heights of each of the configurations to integer multiples of
\mu/\varepsilon
. Finally, we create the containers out of the configurations.

Recall that each configuration comprises
1/\delta^{2}
compartments for big items, and
O(1/\delta^{3})
compartments for packing the wide, long, and small items. We create a Stack container corresponding to each of the compartments for the big items. The width (resp. depth) of this container is the same as the width (resp. depth) of some big item. Each wide compartment was further partitioned into
O(1/\delta^{3})
slots for packing the wide and small items. Note that due to
Claim

4.5
, the depth of a wide/small item is at most a
\delta
-fraction of the depth of the corresponding slot. Also, since we put the small items packed inside slots of width smaller than
\delta^{9}
in the set
T_{2}
, the width of any small item in the set
T_{1}
is at most a
\delta
-fraction of the width of the slot in which it is packed. Finally, the height of any configuration is at least
\mu/\varepsilon
, which is at least an
1/\varepsilon
-factor larger than the height of any item in
I_{1}
. We create an Area container corresponding to each slot for the wide items and a Volume container for each slot for the small items. The width of an Area container is the same as the width of some item in
I_{1}
. Also since the width of a wide compartment can have
O(1/\delta^{2})
distinct values, and the width of a Volume container is either the same as the width of the wide compartment or equals the difference of the width of the compartment and the width of some slot (Area container), the width of a Volume container also lies in a polynomial-sized set. An analogous thing is done for the slots inside the long compartments. Finally, the heights of each of the Stack, Area, and Volume containers are set to be the height of the configuration. Overall, we obtain
O(1/\delta^{6})
guessable containers corresponding to each configuration.

Since
T_{2}
does not contain any big item and
v(T_{2})\leq O(\delta^{4})
by
Lemma

4.2
, the conditions of
Lemma

2.4
are trivially satisfied, and thus the whole set
T_{2}
can be packed into a Steinberg container of height
\varepsilon
.
∎

After applying the above lemma, the rounded heights of all the configurations for
T_{1}
sum up to at most
(1-2\varepsilon)+\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}\cdot O(\frac{1}{\delta^{6}})\leq 1-\varepsilon
, for sufficiently small
\mu
. Since items of
T_{2}
are packed inside a Steinberg container of height
\varepsilon
, all the containers fit inside the knapsack and we obtain the following theorem overall.

Theorem 4.6
.

We can repack a subset of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1}
with profit at least
\left(\frac{2}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt}_{1}
into
O(1/{\delta^{12}})
guessable Stack, Area, and Volume containers.

An equivalent restructuring of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{2}
and
\text{OPT}\cap I_{3}
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 4.7
.

\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\left(\frac{2}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\max\{\mathtt{opt}_{1},\mathtt{opt}_{2},\mathtt{opt}_{3}\}
.

4.2
A simple
(5+\varepsilon)
-approximation

We now present a simple
(5+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
3DK
, which already improves upon the
(7+\varepsilon)
-approximation
[
20
]
. For this, first, we provide the following packing guarantee that will also be useful later.

Lemma 4.8
.

Given a box
B
whose base has dimensions
w_{B}\times d_{B}
and height 1. Let
T
be a set of items with
v(T)\leq v(B)/4
such that each
i\in T
has
w_{i}\leq w_{B}
,
d_{i}\leq d_{B}
and
h_{i}\leq\varepsilon^{4}
. Let
T_{\ell}:=\{i\in T\mid w_{i}>w_{B}/2\text{ and }d_{i}>d_{B}/2\}
and
T_{s}:=T\setminus T_{\ell}
. Then it is possible to find a subset
T^{\prime}_{\ell}\subseteq T_{\ell}
with
p(T^{\prime}_{\ell})\geq(1-\varepsilon)p(T_{\ell})
and partition
B
into a Stack and a Steinberg container by a horizontal plane parallel to the base, such that
T^{\prime}_{\ell}
and
T_{s}
can be fully packed into the Stack and Steinberg containers, respectively. Furthermore, the heights of the two containers are integer multiples of
\varepsilon^{2}
.

Proof.

To begin with, we create a Steinberg container having height
3(1+7\varepsilon^{2})\frac{v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}+\varepsilon^{2}
. Since
h_{i}\leq\varepsilon^{4}
, we have that the height of any item is at most an
\varepsilon^{2}
-fraction of the height of the container. Clearly, the volume of the container is at least
3(1+7\varepsilon^{2})v(T_{s})
. This implies that the whole set
T_{s}
can be packed into the container since

\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\varepsilon^{2}\right)\cdot 3(1+7\varepsilon^{2})v(T_{s})\geq v(T_{s})

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. The height of the Steinberg container is then rounded up to the nearest integral multiple of
\varepsilon^{2}
- the resulting height is bounded by
\frac{3v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
. In the remaining height of the box, we create a Stack container. Next, we sort the items of
T_{\ell}
in non-increasing order of their profit to height ratio and pick a maximal prefix
T^{\prime}_{\ell}
that fits inside the Stack container. If
T^{\prime}_{\ell}=T_{\ell}
, we already obtain a profit of
p(T_{\ell})
and are done. Else,
h(T_{\ell})
must be at least
1-\frac{3v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}-O(\varepsilon^{2})\geq\frac{1}{5}
, since
v(T_{s})\leq v(B)/4
. Also, since the height of each item is bounded by
\varepsilon^{4}
, we have

h(T^{\prime}_{\ell})\geq 1-\frac{3v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}-O(\varepsilon^{2})-\varepsilon^{4}=1-\frac{3v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}-O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(2)

Now since
v(T)\leq v(B)/4
, we have
\frac{1}{4}w_{B}d_{B}h(T_{\ell})+v(T_{s})\leq\frac{1}{4}w_{B}d_{B}
, and thus

h(T_{\ell})\leq 1-\frac{4v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}\leq 1-\frac{3v(T_{s})}{w_{B}d_{B}}.

(3)

From (
2
) and
(
3
)
, we get
h(T^{\prime}_{\ell})\geq h(T_{\ell})-O(\varepsilon^{2})\geq(1-\varepsilon)h(T_{\ell})
, since
h(T_{\ell})\geq 1/5
. Hence
p(T^{\prime}_{\ell})\geq(1-\varepsilon)p(T_{\ell})
and we are done.

∎

Then the following lemma, together with
Theorem

3.1
, gives a
(5+\varepsilon)
-approximation.

Lemma 4.9
.

\mathtt{opt}\leq(5+\varepsilon)\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
.

Proof.

Trivially we have
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\mathtt{opt}_{L}
, since we can create a
Stack
container for every item in
\text{OPT}\cap L
(at most
1/\mu^{3}
such items). Hence if
\mathtt{opt}_{L}>\mathtt{opt}/5
, we are already done. Otherwise we have
\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3}\geq\frac{4}{5}\mathtt{opt}
. Now since
v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}\leq 1
, there must exist an
i\in[3]
such that
\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{i}}{v_{i}}\geq\frac{4}{5}\mathtt{opt}
– w.l.o.g. assume
i=1
. We have two cases.

Case 1:
v_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}
.
In this case,
Lemma

4.8
(with the box
B
corresponding to the whole knapsack) implies that
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathtt{opt}_{1}
(since each item in
I_{1}
has height at most
\mu<\varepsilon^{4}
). Hence if
\mathtt{opt}_{1}>\mathtt{opt}/5
, we are done. Otherwise we have
\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3}\geq\frac{3}{5}\mathtt{opt}
. W.l.o.g. assume that
\mathtt{opt}_{2}\geq\frac{3}{10}\mathtt{opt}
,
Corollary

4.7
gives us
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\left(\frac{2}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\cdot\frac{3}{10}\mathtt{opt}=\left(\frac{1}{5}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt}
.

Case 2:
v_{1}>\frac{1}{4}
.
In this case, we sort the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1}
in non-increasing order of their profit to volume ratio, and pick the maximal prefix
T
whose volume does not exceed
\frac{1}{4}
. Then we have
v(T)\geq\frac{1}{4}-\mu
. Applying
Lemma

4.8
to
T
, we obtain

\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1/4-\mu}{v_{1}}\right)\mathtt{opt}_{1}\geq\left(\frac{1}{5}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt}
,
thus completing the proof.
∎

The ratio of
(5+\varepsilon)
is tight. One such instance is given when
v_{1}=\frac{1}{8},v_{2}=v_{3}=\frac{3}{8}
and
\mathtt{opt}_{1}=\mathtt{opt}_{L}=\frac{1}{5}\mathtt{opt}
and
\mathtt{opt}_{2}=\mathtt{opt}_{3}=\frac{3}{10}\mathtt{opt}.

We will obtain further improvements by using the lower bounds in the following sections.

4.3
Second lower bound on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}

In this subsection, we consider the packing of
\mathrm{OPT}\cap(I_{1\ell}\cup L)
, and restructure it to obtain a guessable container packing, by losing only an
O(\varepsilon)
-fraction of the profit.
Let
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}:=\mathrm{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
and
\text{OPT}_{L}:=\mathrm{OPT}\cap L
. Since each item in
L
has all side lengths at least
\mu
, the number of such items in the packing is
|\text{OPT}_{L}|\leq 1/\mu^{3}
.
Also, since the width and depth
of each item in
I_{1\ell}
is at least 1/2, the items of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
must be packed in layers, i.e., the top and bottom faces of any item in
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
when extended parallel to the bottom face of the knapsack will not intersect another item from
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
. We now discretize the positions of the items in the packing.

Lemma 4.10
.

The distance of the left (resp. front) face of any item from the left (resp. front) face of the knapsack can be assumed to lie in a polynomial-sized set.

Proof.

We push each item as much to the left as possible. Then for each item in
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
, the distance of its left face (from the left face of the knapsack) can be written as the sum of the widths of at most
1/\mu
items from
L
. Similarly, the distance of the left face of any item in
\text{OPT}_{L}
is the sum of the widths of at most
1/\mu
items of
L
and at most one item from
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
. Analogous claims hold by pushing the items as much to the front as possible.
∎

Figure 11
:
Packing of items in

\text{OPT}_{L}
(resp.
T

\text{OPT}_{1\ell}\setminus T

Let
T\subseteq\text{OPT}_{1\ell}

\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
, for some
k
to be chosen later. Our goal is to obtain a container packing of all items in
\text{OPT}_{L}
, together with a subset of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
having a profit of at least
(1-\varepsilon)\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}
. To this end, we shall pack all items in
T
and ensure that the number of discarded items of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
is bounded by
ck
, for some constant
c
. By a result of
[

]
, the profit of the discarded items can be bounded by
\varepsilon\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}
, for appropriately chosen
k
. We now outline the details of our restructuring procedure. See

We draw horizontal planes passing through the top and bottom faces of the items in

\text{OPT}_{1\ell}\setminus T

Fig.

11
). The number of discarded items is bounded by
2|\text{OPT}_{L}|=O(1/\mu^{3})
. The remaining items of

are now completely packed inside at most
k+2|\text{OPT}_{L}|+1
horizontal strips demarcated by the planes. Each such strip is a cuboidal box that is pierced from top to bottom by at most
1/\mu^{2}
Large
items
. Further, there are only polynomially-many positions of these Large
items
inside the strip owing to
Lemma

4.10
. We focus on one such strip
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

The items of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}\setminus T
present inside the strip
\mathcal{S}
can be repacked into at most
O(1/\mu^{8})
Stack containers, whose widths and depths lie in a polynomial-sized set.

Proof.

Consider the top face of the strip
\mathcal{S}
. We extend the boundary edges of the at most
1/\mu^{2}
Large
items
, till they hit the boundary of another Large
item
or the boundary of the knapsack. Consider all the rectangular regions formed by these lines. We call a region to be

if it does not contain any Large

, and is not completely contained inside a larger region (see

Then note that the top face of each item of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
that is packed inside
\mathcal{S}
must lie completely inside at least one of these maximal regions, and we assign the item to one of these regions arbitrarily. Afterward, we permute the items of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
so that all items assigned to a particular maximal region are stacked consecutively one above the other. Hence, for each maximal region, we obtain a Stack container containing all items of
\text{OPT}_{1\ell}
assigned to it, whose width and depth are the same as those of the region. Since there were at most
1/\mu^{2}

For each item in

, we create a Stack container containing the single item itself. Together with

4.11

Lemma 4.12
.

By discarding
O(1/\mu^{11})k

\text{OPT}_{1\ell}\setminus T

Proof.

If a Stack container contains a single item, we make the height of the container equal to the height of that item. Consider now a Stack container having at least two items of

. Let
\bar{h}
be the height of an item with the second largest height. We discard the item with the largest height and use the resulting empty space to round down the height of the container to the nearest integer multiple of
\bar{h}
. Since the number of items packed inside the container is bounded by
n
, the rounded height of the container cannot exceed
n\bar{h}
. Finally, since the number of Stack containers inside any strip is

.
∎

Together with the
O(1/\mu^{3})

4.12

O(1/\mu^{11})k

Lemma 4.13

(

Let

Theorem 4.14

Consider the packing of

4.4
Third lower bound on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}

In this subsection, we restructure the packing of items in
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1}
. Recall that
\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}=p(\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell})
and
\mathtt{opt}_{1s}=p(\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s})
, so that
\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{1s}=\mathtt{opt}_{1}
. Also,
v_{1}:=v(\text{OPT}\cap I_{1})
and
v_{1s}=v(\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s})
.

Theorem 4.15
.

Consider the packing of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1}
. It is possible to repack subsets of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
and
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
into a guessable Stack and Steinberg container, respectively, whose profit, say
P
, satisfies the following properties.

•

\displaystyle P\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{1s}}{\max\{3v_{1s},1\}}
.

•

If
v_{1}\leq 1/3
, then
\displaystyle P\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{3}{4}\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{1s}\right)
.

•

If
v_{1}\leq 1/4
, then
P\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\mathtt{opt}_{1}
.

In order to prove the above, we first establish the following lemma that will also be used in the next subsection.

Lemma 4.16
.

The following statements hold.

1.

If
v_{1}\in(2\varepsilon,1/3]
, it is possible to obtain a container packing of a subset
T\subseteq\text{OPT}\cap I_{1}
with
p(T)\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{3}{4}\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{1s}\right)
, such that the items of
T\cap I_{1\ell}
and
T\cap I_{1s}
are packed into a Stack and a Steinberg container, respectively. Both the containers have an
1\times 1
base and further, their heights are integer multiples of
\varepsilon^{2}
and they sum to at most
3v_{1}
.

2.

If
v_{1}\leq 2\varepsilon
, the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
and
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
can be packed into a Stack and a Steinberg container respectively, both of whose bases have dimensions
1\times 1
, and whose heights are
8\varepsilon
each.

Proof.

We first give a proof of the second case, which is much simpler. Since
v_{1}\leq 2\varepsilon
, we must have
h_{1\ell}\leq 8\varepsilon
, and therefore the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
can be packed into a Stack container with
1\times 1
base and height
8\varepsilon
. Similarly, the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
can be packed into a Steinberg container with
1\times 1
base and height
8\varepsilon
using
Lemma

2.4
, since
\mu\leq\varepsilon\cdot 8\varepsilon
and
\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\varepsilon\right)8\varepsilon>2\varepsilon\geq v_{1s}
.

For the first case, we further subdivide the proof into two cases depending on the relative values of
v_{1}
and
v_{1s}
.

Case 1 :
v_{1}-v_{1s}>\varepsilon
.
In this case, we first create a Steinberg container
C
with
1\times 1
base and height
3(1+7\varepsilon^{2})v_{1s}+\varepsilon^{2}
. Note that the height of any item
i\in\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
satisfies
h_{i}\leq\mu<\varepsilon^{4}\leq\varepsilon^{2}h_{C}
. We show that
v_{1s}\leq\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\varepsilon^{2}\right)v(C)
, which implies that the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
can be fully packed into the container
C
by
Lemma

2.4
. We have

\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\varepsilon^{2}\right)v(C)\geq(1-6\varepsilon^{2})(1+7\varepsilon^{2})v_{1s}>v_{1s},

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. We now round up the height of
C
to the next multiple of
\varepsilon^{2}
. The resulting value of
h_{C}
is at most
3v_{1s}+9\varepsilon^{2}
. Note that since all items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
are packed, we obtain the full profit of
\mathtt{opt}_{1s}
from them. Next, we sort items in
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
in non-increasing order of profit to height ratio, and pick a maximal prefix whose height does not exceed
\varepsilon^{2}\lfloor 3v_{1}/\varepsilon^{2}\rfloor-h_{C}
and pack them into a Stack container of the same height and with an
1\times 1
base. Note that the height of the Stack container is also an integer multiple of
\varepsilon^{2}
. If we are able to pack all items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
, we obtain a profit of
\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}
, and are done. Otherwise, the height of the packed items is at least
3v_{1}-\varepsilon^{2}-h_{C}-\mu\geq 3v_{1}-3v_{1s}-11\varepsilon^{2}\geq(1-4\varepsilon)(3v_{1}-3v_{1s})
. The profit obtained from
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
is then at least

\frac{(1-4\varepsilon)(3v_{1}-3v_{1s})}{h_{1\ell}}\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}\geq\left(\frac{3}{4}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
v_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4}h_{1\ell}+v_{1s}
.

Case 2 :
v_{1}-v_{1s}\leq\varepsilon
.
Since
v_{1}>2\varepsilon
, it follows that
v_{1s}>\varepsilon
in this case. We group the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}
into maximal groups of volume at most
8\varepsilon^{2}
each. Since
v_{1s}>\varepsilon
, the number of groups is at least
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
and each group (except possibly one) has a volume of at least
8\varepsilon^{2}-\mu>7\varepsilon^{2}\geq 7\varepsilon^{2}v_{1s}
. We delete the group having minimum profit. The remaining items have a volume of at most
(1-7\varepsilon^{2})v_{1s}
and a profit of at least
(1-O(\varepsilon))\mathtt{opt}_{1s}
. We claim that all these items can be packed into a Steinberg container

having an
1\times 1
base and height

. First note that since
v_{1s}>\varepsilon

and therefore, for any

, we have
h_{i}\leq\mu<\varepsilon^{3}\leq\varepsilon^{2}h_{C}
. Also

\left(\frac{1}{3}-2\varepsilon^{2}\right)v(C)\geq(1-6\varepsilon^{2})(1-\varepsilon^{2})v_{1s}>(1-7\varepsilon^{2})v_{1s}

and thus the claim follows using
Lemma

. We round up the height of the Steinberg container

to the nearest integer multiple of
\varepsilon^{2}
. Next, we create a Stack container of
1\times 1
base, and having a height such that the sum of the heights of the two containers is exactly
\varepsilon^{2}\lfloor 3v_{1}/\varepsilon^{2}\rfloor
. This directly ensures that the height of the Stack container is a multiple of
\varepsilon^{2}
. Also note that the height is at least
3v_{1}-3v_{1s}+\varepsilon^{2}
. We sort the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
in non-increasing order of their profit to height ratio and pick a maximal prefix that has height at most
3v_{1}-3v_{1s}+\varepsilon^{2}
, which we pack in layers inside the Stack container. If, by doing this, we are able to pack all items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
, we already obtain a profit of
\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}
, and are done. Else the total height of the packed items is at least
3v_{1}-3v_{1s}+\varepsilon^{2}-\mu>3v_{1}-3v_{1s}
. Hence, the profit obtained from items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1\ell}
is at least
\frac{3v_{1}-3v_{1s}}{h_{1\ell}}\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}\geq\frac{3}{4}\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}
, and we are done.
∎

.

Proof of
Theorem

4.15
.

For the first bound, we sort the items of
\text{OPT}\cap I_{1s}

The proof of the second and third cases directly follows from

Proof.

We do away with the case when

From now on, we assume that

\displaystyle x(b-y)+y(y-2\delta b)+y(\ell-x-\delta b)=bx+\ell y+y^{2}-2xy-3y\delta b.

For

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(s,t)}{\partial t}=\ell+2t-2s\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial^{2}f(s,t)}{\partial t^{2}}=2.

Thus, using the above claim and

\times

The set
L
contains items that have all three dimensions more than
\mu

\mathrm{OPT}

Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{L}\coloneqq\mathrm{OPT}\cap L
.
Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{1}
denote the set of items of
\mathrm{OPT}\setminus\mathrm{OPT}_{L}
which are oriented in
\mathrm{OPT}

such that their height is at most
\mu
. Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{2}
denote the set of items of
\mathrm{OPT}\setminus(\mathrm{OPT}_{L}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{1})

\mathrm{OPT}

\mu
in
\mathrm{OPT}

\mathrm{OPT}_{3}\coloneqq\mathrm{OPT}\setminus(\mathrm{OPT}_{L}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{1}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{2})

Let
\mathtt{opt}_{L}=p(\mathrm{OPT}_{L})
and
\mathtt{opt}_{i}\coloneqq p(\mathrm{OPT}_{i})

Next, we discuss the lower bound on

6.4.1
Lower bound on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
based on
\mathsf{L}
-Containers

Lemma 6.6
.

We have that
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\left(\frac{7}{24}-O(\varepsilon)\right)(\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3})
, when rotations of items are allowed.

Proof.

Recall that any item in
\mathrm{OPT}_{1}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{2}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{3}=I\setminus L
has at least one of the dimensions not exceeding
\mu
.
However, since rotations are allowed, we can assume that the items of
I\setminus L
have height at most
\mu
.
We sort the items of
I\setminus L
in non-increasing order of their profit densities (the profit/volume ratio), and
pick the largest prefix
P
whose total volume does not exceed
7/24-5\sqrt{\mu}
. Since the volume of each item
in
I\setminus L
is at most
\mu
, we obtain that
v(P)\geq\min\{7/24-6\sqrt{\mu},v(I\setminus L)\}
.
Since
P
denotes the subset of
I\setminus L
of highest profit density whose volume is at least
v(P)
, it implies that

\displaystyle p(P)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3},\frac{v(P)}{v(\mathrm{OPT}_{1}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{2}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{3})}(\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq(7/24-6\sqrt{\mu})(\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3}).

The last inequality follows since
v(\mathrm{OPT}_{1}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{2}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{3})\leq 1
and
v(P)\geq\min\{v(I\setminus L),7/24-6\sqrt{\mu}\}
.
To conclude,
Lemma

2.6
ensures that the set
P
can be packed into a knapsack in two containers (one Steinberg container and one Stack container or
\mathsf{L}
-Container),
both with unit width and unit depth, and heights as integral multiples of
\mu
.
∎

6.4.2
Other lower bounds on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}

We can obtain more lower bounds on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
in the case of rotations by performing similar restructurings as the ones in the case of non-rotations.
We divide
\mathrm{OPT}_{1}
into two sets
\mathrm{OPT}_{1\ell}

The profits
\mathtt{opt}_{2\ell},\mathtt{opt}_{2s}

We perform two different restructurings of the optimal packing into guessable container packings.

Claim 6.7
.

We have
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))(\mathtt{opt}_{L}+\max\{\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell},\mathtt{opt}_{2\ell},\mathtt{opt}_{3\ell}\})
.

Proof.

We focus on the packing restricted to
\mathrm{OPT}_{1\ell}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{L}
in the optimal packing
\mathrm{OPT}
.
We can perform the same restructuring procedure outlined in
Section

4.3
. Thus, by
Corollary

4.20
, we obtain the claim.
∎

Claim 6.8
.

We have
\displaystyle\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)(\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{OPT}_{s}\coloneqq\mathrm{OPT}_{1s}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{2s}\cup\mathrm{OPT}_{3s}
. In other words,
\mathrm{OPT}_{s}
is the set of items in the optimal packing that have one dimension at most

\mu
, and at least one of the other two dimensions not exceeding
1/2
. Thus
p(\mathrm{OPT}_{s})=\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s}
. We sort items of
\mathrm{OPT}_{s}
in non-increasing order
of profit density (profit/volume ratio) and select the maximum prefix whose volume is at most
\frac{1}{3}-2\mu
. Using
Lemma

2.4
, these selected items can be packed into a
Steinberg container of height
1
. If by doing this, we are able to pack all items of
T
, we trivially obtain a profit of

\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s}
. Otherwise, the total volume of packed items is at least
\frac{1}{3}-3\mu
, and the profit obtained is at least

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{3}-3\mu}{v(\mathrm{OPT}_{s})}(\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s})\geq\left(\frac{1}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)(\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s}),

where the last inequality follows since
\mu
is at most
\varepsilon
.
∎

We are ready to state the final lower bound on
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
.

Lemma 6.9
.

We have that
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))({\mathtt{opt}}/{6}+{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}/{3})
.

Proof.

We combine
Claim

6.7
and
Claim

6.8
. By
Claim

6.7
, we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))(\mathtt{opt}_{L}+\max\{\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell},\mathtt{opt}_{2\ell},\mathtt{opt}_{3\ell}\})

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\mathtt{opt}_{L}+\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{2\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{3\ell}}{3}\right).

By
Claim

6.8
, we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)(\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s}).

Adding both the above equations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}{2}+\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{1\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{1s}+\mathtt{opt}_{2\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{2s}+\mathtt{opt}_{3\ell}+\mathtt{opt}_{3s}}{6}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}{2}+\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{1}+\mathtt{opt}_{2}+\mathtt{opt}_{3}}{6}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}{2}+\frac{\mathtt{opt}-\mathtt{opt}_{L}}{6}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}{3}+\frac{\mathtt{opt}}{6}\right).

That ends the proof.
∎

Final approximation ratio

The following lemma, combined with

6.4

6.5
.

Lemma 6.10
.

There exists a guessable container packing of profit at least
\left(\frac{7}{30}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt}

•

All
Stack
containers.

•

One Stack container and one Steinberg container.

•

One
\mathsf{L}
-Container and one Steinberg container.

Proof.

By

6.6
Uniform profit-density case

In this section, we show the following result.

Theorem 6.13
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a polynomial-time
(3+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for
3DKR
when the profit of an item is equal to its volume.

As before, we let
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
denote the maximum profit of a guessable container packing. We show the following lemma, which coupled with
Lemma

6.4
gives us the above theorem.

Lemma 6.14
.

\mathtt{opt}\leq(3+O(\varepsilon))\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}
.

Proof.

Trivially, we have
\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\mathtt{opt}_{L}
, since we can create a
Stack
container for every item in
L
. For brevity of notation, let
\text{OPT}_{s}=\text{OPT}\cap(I\setminus L)
. W.l.o.g. assume that the items of
\text{OPT}_{s}
are oriented such that
w_{i}\geq d_{i}\geq h_{i}
holds for each
i\in\text{OPT}_{s}
. We now classify the items in
\text{OPT}_{s}
as follows: let
I^{\prime}=\{i\in\text{OPT}_{s}\mid w_{i}>1/2,d_{i}>1/2,h_{i}\leq\mu\}
and let
I^{\prime\prime}=\text{OPT}_{s}\setminus I^{\prime}
. Also, we define
\mathtt{opt}^{\prime}=p(I^{\prime})
and
\mathtt{opt}^{\prime\prime}=p(I^{\prime\prime})
. We can obtain (guessable) container packings of (subsets of)
I^{\prime}
and
I^{\prime\prime}
as described below.

Ignoring the depths of the items in
I^{\prime}
, we obtain a set of rectangles with one dimension at most
\mu
and the other exceeding
1/2
. By
Lemma

6.2
, these rectangles can be packed either exhaustively or to an extent such that the total (front) area packed is at least
\frac{3}{4}-3\mu
. Since the depth of each item is more than
1/2
, the total volume (and therefore profit) packed is at least
\min\left\{\frac{3}{8}-\frac{3\mu}{2},\mathtt{opt}^{\prime}\right\}\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{3\mu}{2},{\mathtt{opt}^{\prime}}\right\}
. Further, this is either a Stack or an
\mathsf{L}
-Container whose dimensions are same as those of the knapsack.

Next, note that since the items were oriented such that
w_{i}\geq d_{i}\geq h_{i}
, it follows that each item in
I^{\prime\prime}
has depth at most
1/2
. Hence by
Lemma

2.4
, we can pack a subset of
I^{\prime\prime}
with volume (profit) at least
\min\left\{\frac{1}{3}-2\mu,\mathtt{opt}^{\prime\prime}\right\}
into a Steinberg container with dimensions identical to that of the knapsack.

From the above discussion, we get that

\mathtt{opt}_{\mathrm{gs}}\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{3}-2\mu,\frac{{\mathtt{opt}_{L}}+{\mathtt{opt}^{\prime}}+{\mathtt{opt}^{\prime\prime}}}{3}\right\}\geq\left(\frac{1}{3}-O(\varepsilon)\right)\mathtt{opt},

since
\mathtt{opt}\leq 1
, and thus the lemma follows.
∎

Finally, this approximation ratio is also tight by our current analysis. An instance to show this is given by
\mathtt{opt}^{\prime}=\mathtt{opt}^{\prime\prime}=\mathtt{opt}_{L}=\frac{1}{3}\mathtt{opt}.

7
Hardness for Guessable Container Packings

In this section, we show that, when rotations are not allowed, there exist input instances for which any guessable container packing can contain at most
1/3
factor of the optimal profit.
This construction is in the same spirit as
[
23
]
, where they showed for 2D packing one cannot obtain
(2-\varepsilon)
-approximation using rectangular regions that are either packed using
NFDH
or stacks.
However, we use more types of containers and show we can not still beat factor 3 by
O(1)
number of containers.

For ease of presentation, we assume that the knapsack has side length
N=2^{m+1}
, where
m
is an integer.
We define an input instance consisting of
3m
items, which can be partitioned into three sets
\{G_{i}\}_{i\in[m]},\{H_{i}\}_{i\in[m]},\{I_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
as follows.

•

For each
i\in[m]
, the item
G_{i}
has both width and depth
N+1-2^{i-1}
and height
2^{i-1}
.

•

For each
i\in[m]
, the item
H_{i}
has width
2^{i-1}
and depth
N+1-2^{i-1}
and height
N+1-2^{i}
.

•

For each
i\in[m]
, the item
I_{i}
has width and height
N+1-2^{i}
and depth
2^{i-1}
.

Each item has a profit exactly equal to
1
.

Remark 7.1
.

Each item
G_{i}
has both width and depth more than
N/2
. Each item
H_{i}
has both depth and height more than
N/2
. Each item
I_{i}
has both width and height more than
N/2
.

The next lemma shows that the above-defined input instance can be entirely packed in a knapsack, implying that the optimal profit is
3m
.

Lemma 7.2
.

The above-defined input instance has an optimal profit of
3m
.

Proof.

Consider

. All three items can be configured as shown in

16
. This leaves a space of

in the remaining knapsack.

in the same configuration, leaving a space of dimensions

Theorem 7.3
.

For any integer

, for any container packing of the above input instance with profit at least
(1/3+\varepsilon)(3m)

\Omega(\varepsilon\log N)

To show the above theorem, intuitively, we need to show that as we try to pack more profit, an average container can not hold many items.
To do this, we make use of the fact that items
G_{i},H_{i},I_{i}
are skewed relative to each other. More formally, we show the following lemma.

Lemma 7.4
.

Consider a container packing
\mathcal{C}
of the above input instance. Let
s_{1}
denote the number of indices

such that the pair

s_{2}

Claim 7.5
.

Every container in

be a Stack container.

Proof.

Due to

7.1

.
∎

Now, we show three claims that will give us

7.4

Claim 7.6

Consider any
i\in[m]
. Suppose both
G_{i}
and
H_{i}

7.1

is greater than
N

G_{i}

in the previous paragraph,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{GI}=\{(G_{i},I_{i}):i\in[m]\text{ and }G_{i},I_{i}\text{ are packed in $\mathcal{C}$}\}

\displaystyle\pi(H_{i},I_{i})

\displaystyle=\text{the container in which }H_{i}\text{ is packed,}

\displaystyle\pi(G_{i},I_{i})

\displaystyle=\text{the container in which }G_{i}\text{ is packed.}
