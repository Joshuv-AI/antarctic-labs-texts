Simulation and optimization of heavy fuel oil (HFO) refining platform to low sulfur marine fuel (LS-FO) by oxidative desulfurization
Authors: Rasoul Karimi, Behrang Golmohammadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has implemented new sulfur content regulations for marine fuels in response to growing environmental concerns, including global warming. These regulations severely and costly restrict refinery operations. Oxidative desulfurization (ODS) is an attractive desulfurization method that has advantages such as mild operating conditions and a hydrogen-free process over traditional processes such as hydrodesulfurization (HDS). One can employ oxidative desulfurization in addition to or instead of hydrodesulfurization. Organic sulfur molecules undergo oxidative desulfurization, which results in the formation of polar sulfones. The refined fuel oil is then separated from the oxidized sulfones using methods such liquid-liquid extraction, distillation, and absorption. This work examines and simulates the process of separating sulfur-containing oxidized chemicals from fuel oil utilizing oxidative desulfurization technology. Acetic acid served as the catalyst and hydrogen peroxide as the chosen oxidant. This effort involved simulating the oxidative desulfurization of fuel oil and then using absorption methods to separate the oxidized sulfones. Subsequent procedures including absorption and distillation were used to separate and recover the resultant effluent, solvent, and oxidant from the remaining components. According to the simulation results, the procedure was run at 80 °C and a modest pressure of less than 5 bar. Sulfur content in the original hydrocarbon fuel was 3.5% by weight; at the output, it was less than 0.5% by weight. The findings of the sensitivity analysis of a few key factors demonstrated that the conversion percentage increased from 84 to 98% when the reactor’s diameter was changed from 0.5 to 1.2 m and its length was extended to the desired amount. The reaction conversion percentage has increased significantly when the mass flow rate of hydrogen peroxide is increased from 0.5 to 1.5 kg/h, the pressure is increased from 1.5 to 5 bar, and the temperature is raised to 30 °C. According to the process optimization data, the ideal state had an exergy efficiency of 55.57%, a solvent waste rate of 1.75%, and a sulfur content of 0.22% in the fuel.

Introduction

The impact of greenhouse gas emissions from marine diesel engines on human health and climate change has garnered attention on a global and regional scale. The International Maritime Organization has set a sulfur limit for marine fuels in an effort to lower ship emissions. Low-sulfur fuels or exhaust gas cleaning equipment are therefore required to lower sulfur dioxide (SO2) emissions1–4. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has set global sulfur emission limitations for marine fuels in response to growing attention over the past few years over the shipping industry’s participation in the generation of numerous environmental pollutants5,6 For instance, ships transporting big oil tankers accelerated by about 4%, while vessels carrying containers accelerated by more than 11%. These ships go farther over shorter periods of time as their speed rises. Additionally, they use more gasoline, which results in greater pollutants7. The scale of the change and the related worldwide costs make this law extremely significant. a bill that will alter supply chain economics and the price of raw materials. Increased freight rates for very big crude oil carriers and increased overhead costs for producers due to container ships may force them to increase output rates8. According to a recent study, the inner lining of marine combustion engines is negatively impacted by high-sulfur fuels9,10. Different types of marine fuels are obtained from oil refining technology and its units, including marine gas oil (MGO/DMA/DMZ/DFA/DFZ), marine distillate gas oil (MDO/DMB/DFB), intermediate fuel oil (IFO), Marine heavy fuel oil etc11. Because heavy fuel oil is inexpensive and readily available, it has been used extensively as a maritime fuel for nearly fifty years12,13. The global application of legislation restricting the amount of sulfur permitted in gasoline emissions is one example of the harsh steps that have been taken in response to evidence of environmental harm caused by oxidized sulfur species and efforts to create a cleaner environment. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) published a new law that went into effect on January 1, 2020, and states that the sulfur content cannot be more than 0.5%. This announcement is likely to increase demand for cleaner transportation fuels globally grow at an exponential rate14.

Ford and associates first introduced the oxidative desulfurization technique in 1967 to desulfurize an oil cut with a boiling temperature of more than 250 °C15. Since the start of the new millennium, there has been an increased desire to research alternative methods, particularly oxidative desulfurization, due to the increasingly stringent environmental restrictions to reduce the level of sulfur in fuels and the shortcomings of the conventional process (hydrogen desulfurization) in meeting these strict requirements16,17. While heavy sulfur compounds are difficult to remove due to steric hindrance in the hydrogen desulfurization method18, oxidative desulfurization has advantages such as mild operating conditions, high selectivity, no need for expensive hydrogen, and the potential to desulfurize sulfur compounds with high steric barriers, such as alkylbenzothiophenes19. Oxidative desulfurization can be accomplished by a variety of oxidants, homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysts, and various separation techniques, which will be covered in more detail20,21. Sobati and colleagues22 conducted a study utilizing the response surface method to examine the effects of various parameters on the efficiency of ultrasonically assisted desulfurization of diesel fuel. The system used formic acid as the catalyst and hydrogen peroxide as the oxidizer for oxidation. A reduction of 84.38 % in sulfur was achieved when the ratios of oxidant to sulfur and formic acid to sulfur were 3.58 and 23.64, respectively. Comparing these findings to comparable reports from earlier investigations in the same system, they are noticeably lower23. According to a report, Dehkordi and associates desulfurized kerosene with a sulfur concentration of 0.16% by mass using the oxidation system of hydrogen peroxide and acetic acid. In this investigation, the highest percentage of sulfur removed was 83.3% 24.

Another study on the oxidative desulfurization of kerosene with ultrasonically assisted acetonitrile liquid-liquid extraction was carried out by Karamzadeh et al.25. The hydrogen peroxide oxidation system was studied in this study. There was formic acid utilized. The elemental sulfur level (2490 ppm) had 95.4% of its sulfur removed, according to the data26. A few papers contrasted the effects of acetic and formic acid. By using formic acid to oxidize heavy fuel oil, Farshi et al. discovered that viscous polymeric materials were formed as byproducts27.

Additionally, Otaibi et al. observed that partial oxidation resulted from a decrease in the desulfurization rate due to the higher acidity of formic acid while examining the desulfurization rate for crude oil using formic acid as opposed to acetic acid28. Collins et al. employed the first polyoxometalates (POM) catalyst for the oxidative desulfurization process. In a two-phase system of water and toluene at 50 °C, phosphotungstic acid was used for the oxidative desulfurization (ODS) of a model oil containing dibenzothiophene in toluene, as well as for gas oil, using hydrogen peroxide as the oxidant and tetraoctylammonium bromide (TOAB) as a phase transfer agent. The reaction was carried out at 60 °C29.

There have been numerous reports of in-depth study in the areas of software and numerical simulations of various components of an oil refining unit and its derivatives thus far. Rahmanian et al. simulated a crude oil unit in 2018. The outcomes demonstrated a strong correlation between the data and real-world information, particularly when it came to heavier hydrocarbons. The variables such as three-phase separator operating pressure, temperature and operating pressure, input feed characteristics are important parameters30. Elugbenga et al. (2021) verified the molar velocities of oil and gas flow in three-phase separators. This study compared the various impacts of varied input operating conditions on the flows of gas and oil. The findings demonstrated that the output gas flow dropped as the separator’s input pressure increased. The output gas flow increases as the feed stream’s temperature rises. Additionally, it was discovered that, under constant temperature and pressure, altering the flow rate had no effect on the output methane concentration31.

The study and analysis of the research that has been done indicates that there are still issues, such as fuel hydrocarbon waste at certain phases of desulfurization32. Excessive extraction solvent consumption (volume nearly equal to fuel in each extraction step), as well as the creation of useless byproducts during the oxidation stage. Furthermore, partial oxidation and a shift in crude oil’s quality are occasionally seen.

The best-case scenario for this work would involve using low-toxicity materials with the right composition and mild processing conditions. To that end, acetic acid was selected as a catalyst and hydrogen peroxide as an oxidant, which are both appropriate and have good overlap for this work. Acetic acid is a cheap, non-toxic compound that can be used, and its secondary and return flows from the existing columns have been used to design the process and remove sulfur compounds without the need for costly and toxic solvents. The problem states that switching ships’ fuel from high-sulfur petroleum products to low-sulfur petroleum products is both imperative and crucial. Establishing a strategic pre-refining platform is the goal of this project in order to produce fuel that is higher grade, less polluting, and of higher quality.

Despite these advances, major challenges remain. Existing ODS processes typically involve high levels of solvent and can lead to partial oxidation, which affects the quality of the final fuel product. These drawbacks reveal a need for more efficient, sustainable ODS methods that can reduce byproduct formation and minimize solvent usage, making them more applicable for large-scale use in the shipping industry. Addressing these limitations is crucial to meet regulatory demands while maintaining an economically viable approach to fuel desulfurization.

This study aims to address these challenges by using acetic acid as a sustainable and cost-effective catalyst, coupled with hydrogen peroxide as the oxidant. This combination seeks to optimize desulfurization conditions, focusing on minimizing byproduct formation and reducing solvent waste, which would enhance the practicality and environmental sustainability of ODS for marine fuel applications. By refining the ODS approach with these specific catalysts, this study proposes a solution that could meet the stringent sulfur emission standards while offering a feasible pathway toward cleaner marine fuel. Materials

Heavy fuel oil high sulfur

Marine oil, also known as heavy fuel oil (HFO), is highly toxic and viscous. Its spillage poses a serious threat to marine ecosystems and the communities that depend on marine resources for economic, nutritional, and cultural needs, as it degrades slowly in the marine environment. This dependence on the sea heightens the risk of adverse impacts from oil spills. Fuel oil is one of the petroleum hydrocarbons produced during the refining of crude oil, after naphtha, gasoline, and kerosene. Due to its dark color, it is often referred to as black oil. This fuel is commonly used in certain power plants, marine diesel engines, and furnaces because it is among the least expensive options. Generally, fuel oil is defined as any liquid fuel used in engines to generate power or in boilers and furnaces for heating, except for fuels with a flash point above 42 °C. The fuel oil studied here is Iran’s light fuel oil, which contains 3.5% sulfur by weight.

Hydrogen peroxide

As an oxidant, hydrogen peroxide (Dr. Mojallali, Iran) was employed. H2O2, or hydrogen peroxide is a weak acid and a pale blue liquid with a viscosity somewhat higher than that of water. This material is corrosive, unpleasant, and has high oxidizing qualities. Hydrogen peroxide oxidizes the sulfur compounds in the fuel to the matching sulfones during the oxidative desulfurization process. Since hydrogen peroxide may ignite fuels and is caustic and reactive, it is typically diluted with water. Apart from its other attributes like affordability, corrosive strength, and minimal pollution, the primary application of this oxidant can be attributed to its capacity to produce water, which is the only byproduct.

Acetic acid

While it is possible to perform the oxidative desulfurization process without the need for catalysis, doing so would negate one of the main benefits that ODS has over HDS due to the harsh working conditions involved. Among the carboxylic acids, acetic acid (Dr. Mojallali, Iran) was chosen as catalyst. Due to the fact that very little acid is isolated in the aqueous solution, this acid is weak. At 16.5 °C pure anhydrous acetic acid, also known as glacial acetic acid, freezes into a colorless crystalline solid after absorbing water from its surroundings. Both concentrated acid solutions and pure acid are highly caustic. One of the most basic carboxylic acids is acetic acid. This acid is a crucial chemical reagent and industrial chemical that is used in the manufacturing of polyvinyl acetate, cellulose acetate, and polyethylene terephthalate.

Methods

Method for simulating process units

The characteristics of the output flows can be computed by knowing the model guiding the process, the process and mechanical characteristics of the operational devices, and the input flows’ process characteristics. Even with the brief explanation given above, there are a wide range of simulation applications. You can guarantee the proper working of the gadgets and swiftly enhance the complex’s operation. It can be used, for instance, to identify device flaws like distillation column floods and widespread converter blockage. For instance, you may create a distillation column model and outline its parameters, inputs, and outputs before simulating the actual distillation column. These programs are used by all of the major oil firms to optimize their current units and create new ones. A cheap and efficient technique to precisely comprehend the process, enhance its functionality, and examine the dynamic behavior of the system before process issues arise is to model and simulate the process.

Process unit sensitivity analysis method

Sensitivity analysis is the initial stage in determining the ideal operating conditions. Sensitivity analysis ascertains the potential effects of various independent variable values on a certain dependent variable, often known as the target variable, given a set of assumptions. Stated differently, sensitivity analysis looks at how the uncertainty from various sources affects the model’s total uncertainty. This analysis is a technique for forecasting outcomes when variables in a certain area change. We can list experiment design, parameter estimation, uncertainty analysis, parameter scaling, and stability analysis as applications of sensitivity analysis.

The impact of every reactor operational parameter has been examined in this study utilizing sensitivity analysis. There are six primary reactions in the reactor employed for this study. The primary parameters of reactor length, reactor diameter, temperature, and mass flow rate have all been used to monitor the rate of advancement of each reaction. The target parameter is the conversion % parameter.

Process unit optimization method

In general, an optimization process always moves in a direction that brings one or more parameters to their minimum or maximum. Since the mentioned parameters are under the influence of process variables; Therefore, they are known as objective functions. Parameters such as production capacity, income, operating costs, energy consumption, etc., are among the most common objective functions of process industries optimization. In carrying out optimization studies, the correct determination of process variables and their change interval is very important. Usually, the process variables should be selected in such a way that they have the most effect on the target functions.

Stated differently, their modifications result in appreciable modifications of the objective functions. Conversely, what restricts the range of process variables is the conditions that were initially established as design considerations; these kinds of limitations in optimization computations are referred to as constraints. Optimization aims to choose the best option among a range of efficient options, but the only option that is closest to the optimal economic performance and performance.

A system may employ single- or multi-objective optimization, depending on the requirements and preferences. The objective is to enhance sulfur-free fuel oil production by the use of H2O2 solvent. This problem is examined in two distinct scenarios:

Hydrogen peroxide is added to the incoming feed, which is labeled with the word (FEED). The oxidation reactor now contains the resultant chemical. The feed entered the reactor at 25 °C and 3.4 bar, exiting at 54 °C and 2.4 bar, meeting the process requirement of a 1-bar pressure drop in the target oxidation reactor. The reactor in use has a volume of 5.750 m³ and a length of 5 m. Under these optimized conditions (from 25 °C to 54 °C and from 3.4 to 2.4 bar), the reactor achieved the specified 1-bar pressure reduction with the given volume and length.

The primary goal of the T-100 column is to recover H₂O₂ at the start of the process. The sulfur absorber stream, composed of sulfur-free secondary hydrocarbons recycled from the T-101 column, enters from the top, while the reactor feed is in a gas-liquid phase. The column contains 10 trays, with a pressure drop of about 0.06 bar as the flow descends from higher to lower trays a relatively low value for absorption columns, indicating minimal resistance.

The V flow, or the outflow from the top of the column, contains neither sulfur compounds nor heavy hydrocarbons. The only sulfur compound present is gaseous SO₂, at a concentration of approximately 0.08%. The primary components of this stream are water and light hydrocarbons, such as propane, butane, and pentane, which entered from the T-103 column.

The T-101 column is primarily responsible for separating heavy components, like heavy fuel oil, from hydrogen peroxide. Steam enters from the bottom, aiding in the separation of water and H₂O₂, while lighter hydrocarbons and fuel oil compounds exit from the top. The column has a total of twenty stages, and similar to the T-100 absorption column, it experiences a low pressure drop as the flow moves from bottom to top. The main objective of the T-100 column is to recycle H₂O₂, employing a 10-tray setup to enable efficient gas-liquid separation with a pressure drop of 0.06 bar per tray. Temperatures remained mostly stable across the trays, with a notable increase from tray 9 to tray 8 as steam ascended. Flow rate variations indicated a high liquid flow rate at the column’s base, where significant cooling also occurred.

The process includes a T-102 distillation column, where light fuel oil, the desired product, is a major component of the output. The upper flow of the column contains sulfur and lighter components, while the lower flow removes much heavier, sulfur-free compounds.

Figure 4a illustrates the temperature gradient along the T-102 column, which ranges from 100 °C to 600 °C. The temperature is highest near the reboiler (tray 10) at the base, while it is lowest at the top due to the condenser, which condenses the vapor into liquid, creating a return flow at the column’s top. The T-103 column is an additional absorption tower in the process, primarily designed to extract sulfur components from light fuel oil. Before entering the column, the inflow is cooled from 90 °C to 25 °C. Both this tower and the T-102 column aim to reduce the sulfur content of the light fuel oil while absorbing sulfurous pollutants. The LL output stream from the T-103 column, with a flow rate of 134.6 kg/h, represents the final waste that must be removed from the system due to its significant sulfur content.

The T-101 column separates heavy components and hydrogen peroxide, while the T-102 column completes the distillation process, retaining the desired light fuel oil and removing heavier, sulfur-free components. The temperature in T-102 ranges from 100 °C to 600 °C across the trays, with higher temperatures near the reboiler and maximum pressure at the top of the column. The T-103 column finalizes sulfur removal from light fuel oil by cooling the input streams from 90 °C to 25 °C. The output (LL stream) demonstrates a significant sulfur concentration of 134.6 kg/h, which is disposed of as waste. The optimized output from T-102 and T-103 contains less than 0.5% sulfur.

In the simulation, the final result of the complete process is derived from two streams. Both streams contain less than 0.5% sulfur compounds, originating from the lower output product of the T-102 column and the upper product of the T-103 column, respectively. Similar to the reactor’s length, the reactor’s diameter positively impacted the reaction conversion rate. Naturally, this rise was only made up to a certain limit; as a result, no change in the conversion percentage was seen as the diameter increased from 1.4 m to higher levels because the reactions’ maximum conversion percentage had already been reached. Conversely, the majority of reactions saw an increase in conversion from 84 to 98% when the reactor’s diameter was increased from 0.5 to 1.2 m.

As the statistics make evident, raising the temperature of the feed often raises the percentage of conversion in all reactions since the aforementioned processes are endothermic. When the feed temperature is raised from 10 to 15, the conversion % increases linearly; when the feed temperature is raised to 15 or higher, the conversion percentage increases exponentially. Until the temperature prevents the feed from decomposing, this increase is continued. In the second and third reactions, due to the high temperature sensitivity of the reactants, as the temperature increases more than 30, due to the decomposition of part of the reactants inside the reactor, the percentage of material conversion, which is actually the conversion of reactants into products, also decreases. has been discovered This value has reached 50 °C in the fourth reaction, and at this temperature the reactants are disintegrated while entering the reactor.

Conclusion

The usage of low-quality fossil raw materials, such as sour and heavy oil, is unavoidable due to the rise in energy demand and the decline in fossil fuel sources. As of 2018, diesel and gasoline with a very minimal quantity of 10–15 ppm are required to be used, according the environmental legislation set in nations where 50% of the land area is devoted to agriculture. The oil refining sector faces a significant operational and financial difficulty in meeting the necessary criteria. Sulfur removal from fossil fuels is therefore regarded by the refinery industries as one of the most crucial aspects of their operations. Different oxidants, homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysts, and different separation techniques can all be used in the oxidative desulfurization process. The project’s work can be categorized into three sections: process optimization, process simulation, and sensitivity analysis of key process parameters. According to the results of the process simulation, the procedure was run at 80 °C and a modest pressure of less than 5 bar. Sulfur content in the original hydrocarbon fuel was 3.5% by weight; at the output, it was less than 0.5% by weight.
