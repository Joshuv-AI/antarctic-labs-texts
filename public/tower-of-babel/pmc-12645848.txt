Coverage and influencing factors of influenza vaccination among community healthcare workers in Shanghai, 2023–2024
Authors: Yanhong Ding, Jing Xu, Wenxin Ying, Yue Lu, Hongmei Xu, Anran Zhang, Li Zhang, Chuchu Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeasonal influenza vaccination is critical for community healthcare workers (HCWs) to protect themselves, patients, and families, yet uptake remains low in China. This study investigates the influenza vaccination status among community HCWs in Shanghai and analyses the factors driving vaccination. A cross-sectional survey was conducted in 47 Community Health Service Centers (CHSC) in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, China. Vaccination status in the 2023–2024 influenza season and reasons for or against vaccination were surveyed. The potential factors influencing vaccination uptake were determined using bivariate logistic regression. Among 1,781 surveyed HCWs, 335 (18.81%, 95% CI: 17.04–20.72) received influenza vaccination in 2023–2024. Nurses had the highest coverage (32.08%, 95% CI: 20.30–46.45), followed by public health workers (26.78%, 95% CI: 23.40–30.45), with clinicians the lowest (11.64%, 95% CI: 9.21–14.59) (P < .001). Significant correlates of vaccination included age, education, occupation, and monthly household income (P < .05). Unvaccinated HCWs cited main reasons as follows: concerns about side effects (clinicians 32.60%, technicians 35.60%); self-rated good health (public health workers 32.30%, support staff 37.70%); and for nurses, perceived ineffectiveness/not worth the cost alongside side effect concerns (30.00% each). The coverage rate of influenza vaccination among community HCWs in the Pudong New Area of Shanghai is low. Improving influenza vaccination uptake among community HCWs requires a multifaceted approach combining education and policy support.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza is a highly contagious acute respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.1 Globally, about one billion cases occur annually, including 3–5 million severe cases and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths.1 Between the 2010–2011 and 2014–2015 epidemic seasons, China experienced an average annual excess mortality of 88,000 influenza-associated respiratory deaths, predominantly (80%) in persons over 60 years.2,3 Vaccination is the most effective measure against influenza-related illness and death.1 The Chinese Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC) has recommended community healthcare workers (HCWs) – a key and high-risk group, including clinical staff, public health workers, and health quarantine staff.4 Influenza vaccination for HCWs serves dual critical purposes: it prevents workforce inefficiency and absenteeism caused by influenza infections, thereby safeguarding healthcare organizational operations while also effectively curbing viral transmission to colleagues and vulnerable patients. This protective measure ensures sustained operational capacity for patient care delivery,4 aligning with the recommendations regarding HCW immunization in the World Health Organization (WHO) position paper.1 A study’s results also suggest the value of incorporating community HCWs’ recommendations for seasonal influenza vaccination into existing primary public health programs to increase vaccination coverage among high-risk groups in China.5

Despite severe influenza threats and available vaccines, China’s influenza vaccination rate remains extremely low – a situation exacerbated as its national Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) does not include the vaccine, mirroring the global struggle to maintain coverage without sustained programs.6–8According to one survey, the overall rates of influenza vaccination across China during the 2020–2021 and 2021–2022 epidemic seasons stood at 3.2% and 2.5%, respectively.9 Another Chinese study reported that among HCWs, influenza vaccination coverage was 43.7% (741/1,697) in the 2020–2021 season and 35.4% (600/1,697) in the 2021–2022 season, a pattern reflecting a declining trend.10 In Pudong New Area, Shanghai, China, the overall vaccination coverage during the 2021–2022 influenza season reached 17.7% (95% CI: 17.0%, 18.3%).11

There are several barriers to influenza vaccine uptake among HCWs in China, including vaccine hesitancy12 among HCWs, out-of-pocket costs for the influenza vaccine, and other factors.13 Shanghai, a major metropolis in eastern China and among the world’s most developed cities, boasts a high standard of medical care. Nevertheless, the influenza vaccine coverage rates and related influencing factors among community HCWs in Shanghai remain insufficiently explored and discussed. Additional research is warranted to enhance the vaccination coverage rate (VCR) for influenza prevention.

Accordingly, the present study sought to evaluate the VCR among community HCWs. in Shanghai during the 2023–2024 influenza season, as well as to identify the factors underlying vaccination or non-vaccination. The results of this research are expected to offer scientific evidence for formulating targeted strategies and interventions aimed at increasing influenza vaccination rates among community HCWs.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at Pudong CDC (PDCDC). Written informed consent was acquired from all participants prior to data collection. All personally identifiable information was anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality.

Study design

The present study adopted a retrospective cross-sectional survey design to investigate influenza vaccination coverage, its influencing factors, and the specific reasons for vaccination or non-vaccination among community HCWs in Pudong New Area, Shanghai. The sample size, determined to be 702 participants, was calculated using the formula for estimating the minimum sample size in cross-sectional studies, with parameters including an estimated influenza vaccination coverage of 13.19%,14 a margin of error (d) of approximately d ≈ 0.012π, a 90% response rate, a 5% alpha risk, 80% statistical power, and a clustering effect of 2:n=Zα/22×π×1−πd2

Community Health Service Centers (CHSCs) are established in each sub-district/town of Pudong New Area, Shanghai, with a total of 47 CHSCs. The staff size of each CHSC varies, typically ranging between 100 and 200 employees. Their primary responsibilities include providing primary healthcare services, public health services, health education and promotion, as well as health supervision and coordination to residents living in the surrounding areas. We conducted our study across all 47 CHSCs from September 20th to 26th, 2024. A pilot study was conducted by the Pudong New Area Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and relevant data were included in the research. In our study, staff from the CDC first conducted face-to-face training with randomly selected investigators (public health workers) from 47 Community Health Service Centers (CHSCs). These community investigators, after being trained by the professional team, then performed a simple random sampling of 20–60-year-old medical staff at their respective CHSCs (30–40 individuals per center), carried out face-to-face interviews, and collected questionnaires.

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews. The validated questionnaire was used for the interviews, and the researchers recorded the responses in real time using the ‘Questionnaire Star’ platform. The data were then automatically exported to Excel for analysis. The instrument comprised three distinct sections: (i) sociodemographic parameters (age, sex, professional category, education level, and household monthly income); (ii) self-documented influenza immunization history, with subsequent probing of motivating factors among vaccine recipients and barriers among non-recipients; and (iii) willingness to be vaccinated, evaluated specifically in the unvaccinated group. Before the interviews, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To ensure data validity, all study interviews were administered exclusively in Mandarin Chinese. Trained PDCDC personnel systematically examined each questionnaire during the investigation period to assess response completeness, internal consistency, and eliminate redundancies. Questionnaires failing to meet the inclusion criteria were flagged for interviewer reevaluation and potential re-collection.

Statistical analyses

All of the analyses were conducted in R, version 4.3.3 (R Core Team, R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to determine the distributions of demographic characteristics. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to compare the adjusted VCRs among the different subgroups. Binary logistic regression models were used to investigate the predictors of influenza vaccination.

By age group, coverage was highest in the 35 ~ 44 years age group (21.92%, 95% CI: 18.78–25.41), followed by the 45 ~ 54 years (18.95%, 95% CI: 15.58–22.83), and 55 ~ 59 years (17.92%, 95% CI: 11.40–26.83) and 20 ~ 34 years (15.48%, 95% CI: 12.67–18.76) age groups. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed several independent factors associated with influenza vaccination uptake among healthcare workers after adjustment for potential confounders. Occupational designation emerged as a significant predictor, with public health workers (OR = 2.90, 95%CI: 2.11–4.02, p < .001) and nurses (adjusted OR = 3.10, 95%CI: 1.60–5.85, p < .001) demonstrating substantially higher vaccination rates compared to clinicians. Age stratification showed workers aged 35 ~ 44 years had significantly greater odds of vaccination than their younger counterparts aged 20–34 years (adjusted OR = 1.50, 95%CI: 1.10–2.06, p = .010). Reasons for receiving or not receiving the influenza vaccine

Among the 335 community HCWs who received an influenza vaccination during the 2023–2024 influenza season, 158 public health workers (51.80%), 15 nurses (4.90%), 29 technicians (9.50%), 61 clinicians (20.0%), and 42 support staff (13.80%) responded to the vaccination reasons survey, whereas 30 (9.00%) refused. The top 3 reasons for receiving the influenza vaccine among all occupation groups were that they believed the vaccination could prevent disease due to influenza (95.60%, 100%, 82.80%, 96.70%, and 95.20%, respectively), and they believed the vaccination could reduce complications due to influenza (70.90%, 60.00%, 65.50%, 63.90%, and 52.40%, respectively), and the affordable vaccination price (38.60%, 40.00%, 34.50%, 29.50%, and 21.40%, respectively). Additionally, the top 4 and top 5 motivating factors varied by occupational group, as detailed in Figure 2(a).

Among the 1446 community HCWs who were unvaccinated during the 2023–2024 influenza season, 257 public health workers (30.00%), 20 nurses (2.4%), 146 technicians (17.30%), 291 clinicians (43.50%), and 130 support staff (15.40%) responded to the vaccination reasons survey, whereas 602 (33.80%) refused.

All five occupational groups reported the same top five reasons for non-vaccination, though with varying order of importance: (1) they were healthy and did not need to be vaccinated, (2) suspicions about side effects, (3) concerns about ineffective vaccines not be worth the cost, (4) vaccines being unnecessary because the flu was not serious, and (5) distrust in vaccine quality.

Key occupational differences emerged in the primary reasons. Public health workers were most likely to believe that they were healthy and did not need to be vaccinated (32.30%). Nurses were equally likely to prioritize the belief that the vaccine was ineffective and thus not worth the cost (30.00%), as well as suspicions about side effects (30.00%). Technicians were most concerned about side effects (35.60%), while clinicians were predominantly concerned about side effects (32.60%). Support staff were most likely to believe that they were healthy and did not need to be vaccinated (37.70%). Willingness to vaccinate

We additionally analyzed motivation patterns among unvaccinated community HCWs expressing a willingness to receive future vaccination. The choices also differed among the occupation groups. Notably, when vaccination willingness was assessed under general conditions, the predominant response across all occupational groups was refusal to select any positive vaccination motivators. Specifically, 24.50% of public health workers, 50% of nurses, 28.30% of technicians, 26.90% of clinicians, and 39.20% of support staff selected “unwilling under any circumstances” options, effectively indicating that they were unlikely to accept vaccination regardless of potential incentives.

Vaccination motivators varied significantly across occupational groups. Occupation-Specific Motivational Patterns: Public health workers were most influenced by high flu activity that year (18.9%), followed by confirmed good vaccine efficacy with high strain match (17.60%) and workplace programs (paid) (16.30%). Nurses prioritized high flu activity that year (15.40%) and workplace programs (paid) (11.50%), good vaccine efficacy with a high strain match (7.70%), and new-borns in the family (7.70%). Technicians were primarily motivated by workplace program (paid) (19.70%), followed closely by confirmed good vaccine efficacy with high strain match (18.20%) and high flu activity that year (17.70%). Clinicians placed the highest importance on good vaccine efficacy with a high strain match (19.9%) and high flu activity that year (19.70%), with a workplace program (paid) (14.30%) as a secondary factor. Support staff cited high flu activity that year (13.60%), workplace program (paid) (13.10%), and good vaccine efficacy with a high strain match (12.50%) as key motivators. Discussion

Pudong New Area accounts for approximately one-fifth of Shanghai’s total area and population. This survey was carried out across all 47 CHCS in the district, encompassing urban, urban-rural fringe, and rural locations. As such, the sample can be regarded as representative of Shanghai’s Pudong New Area. The present study offers comprehensive insights into influenza vaccination coverage and its determinants among community HCWs in Shanghai. For the 2023–2024 season, we recorded a VCR of 18.81% (95% CI: 17.04–20.72), alongside key sociodemographic predictors (age, education, occupation, and income) and behavioral factors influencing vaccine uptake. The research yielded three particularly valuable findings for public health practice: (1) occupation-specific patterns in vaccination motivators and barriers, (2) concerning levels of vaccine hesitancy even among HCWs willing to consider vaccination, and (3) distinct misconceptions driving vaccine refusal across professional groups. These results offer critical insights for designing targeted interventions to improve HCW vaccination rates – a crucial component of pandemic preparedness and routine influenza control strategies.

The VCR among community HCWs in the 2023–2024 season was 18.81% (95% CI: 17.04–20.72). This rate was comparable to earlier reports from China, including: 11.60% in the 2018/2019 season,15 22.3% across seven regions in 2020–2021,16 13.19% in Shanghai’s Songjiang District (2018),14 and19.37% in Nanchang (2022–2023),17 However, the VCR in our study was significantly lower than rates observed in other Chinese regions (e.g., Shandong, Lanzhou, Sichuan),18–20 and high-income countries (e.g., Finland, Italy, USA).21–27 Subgroup analysis indicated that community HCWs consistently exhibited lower VCRs regardless of local vaccination subsidy policies, suggesting additional barriers beyond financial support.

VCRs among occupations such as clinicians, nurses, technicians, and support staff were different. In Sichuan, VCRs among occupations (clinicians 52.5%, nurses 58.2%, technicians67.7%, and support staff 55.3%) were higher than those in our survey. Additionally, in Nanchang, VCR among occupations (clinicians 14.04%, technicians 18.45%, and public health workers 56.25%) was higher than that reported in our survey. Conversely, studies from Shanghai’s Songjiang District reported lower occupation-specific VCRs (clinicians 7.35%, nurses 20.18% and technicians 5.56%) than our survey. An interesting finding was made. The seasonal influenza VCR differs between clinicians practising traditional Chinese medicine and modern Western medicine,17,28 and clinicians’ VCR was lower than that of modern Western medicine. Differences in VCR across roles indicate that interventions should be tailored to the specific occupations of community HCWs.

To address this persistently low VCR, international experiences with mandatory vaccination policies offer valuable insights. For instance, Finland was the first European country to introduce a nationwide mandatory seasonal influenza vaccination policy for HCWs by mandating that administrators of health care institutions employ only vaccinated HCWs.23 Similarly, in the U.S., many hospitals mandate influenza vaccinations for HCWs.29 Notably, we observed that while community HCWs showed a higher likelihood of recommending influenza vaccination compared to hospital-based HCWs.5 The same study has also shown that 31% of pregnant women and 78% of older adults would follow HCWs’ vaccination advice,5 and guardians were more likely to vaccinate children when receiving HCWs’ recommendations. In contrast to nations with compulsory vaccination policies, China’s EPI does not presently incorporate influenza vaccines. Our study reveals important considerations for potential policy development. The optimal strategy would combine institutional mandates with free vaccination policies. This dual approach could increase HCW vaccination rates while preserving their advocacy role. Future policies should address both the determinants of HCW vaccination identified in our study and the distinct factors influencing their recommendations.

The influenza vaccine is widely recognized, but people remain unaware of its benefits. In our multivariate analysis, sociodemographic factors influencing awareness status – including age group, educational level, monthly household income, and occupation – were consistent with previous analyses.11,30,31 Across populations, higher educational attainment and income are nearly universally linked to increased access to health information and greater health literacy. Among community HCWs, those aged 35–44 years show a higher likelihood of influenza vaccination. This finding suggests that onboarding and continuous education programs for younger and older HCWs should be strengthened. This discrepancy may stem from increased health awareness with age, as accumulated medical knowledge and experience enhance their recognition of vaccines’ role in disease prevention. Notably, HCWs include a higher proportion of individuals with advanced degrees compared to the general population, and such groups typically possess knowledge of vaccines. However, the present study found that higher educational levels correlate with lower vaccination uptake, indicating vaccine hesitancy in this subgroup. This result is consistent with the observations of Yuwei Liu et al.18 The significant “know-do” gap among highly educated HCWs requires interventions that go beyond disseminating information to address skepticism. These interventions could include peer-led discussions and leadership endorsements.

Clinicians exhibit lower vaccination rates compared to other healthcare technicians. Despite their comprehensive knowledge of diseases and treatments, clinical HCWs may have insufficient understanding of preventive healthcare; additionally, their higher exposure to adverse vaccination reactions could negatively shape their attitudes toward vaccination. This finding underscores the importance of integrating vaccination into clinical professional responsibility and patient safety protocols. Public health workers, by contrast, demonstrate higher vaccination rates. Nurses, due to their frequent patient interactions and elevated infection risk, may exhibit greater willingness to be vaccinated. These groups could be enlisted to help raise awareness of vaccinations. In conclusion, our study is consistent with that of Yuwei Liu et al.18

Our multivariate analysis provides a stratified framework for policy-making. By moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach to target specific subgroups (e.g., the highly educated, clinicians, and younger staff), we can design more precise and effective interventions to improve overall VCR.

An analysis of vaccination motivations revealed three predominant reasons consistently cited across occupational groups. The top 3 reasons for the occupation groups were that public health workers, nurses, technicians, clinicians, and support staff all believed that vaccination could prevent disease due to influenza, that vaccination could reduce complications due to influenza, and that the vaccination was affordable, similar to that reported in those studies.15,18,20,32 These universally acknowledged benefits should form the cornerstone of generalized promotional campaigns aimed at all healthcare workers.

The vaccination declination reasons varied significantly by occupational category. For public health workers, 32.30% cited “believing they were healthy and did not need to be vaccinated” as the primary reason. Among nurses, two equally prevalent reasons were reported (30.00% each): “believing the vaccine ineffective, not worth the cost” and “worrying about the side effects.” Technicians showed the highest percentage (35.60%) for “worrying about the side effects,” closely followed by clinicians at 32.60% with the same concern. Support staff most commonly reported “believing they were healthy and not needing vaccination” (37.70%). These patterns are consistent with previous research findings.15,18,20,21,32 This pattern of occupation-specific barriers necessitates equally tailored intervention strategies to effectively address each group’s unique concerns.

A systematic analysis of HCWs’ influenza vaccination identifies consistent behavioral patterns: positive motivators are self-, family, and community protection,33 while key barriers include fear of vaccine-associated influenza, low perceived risk, overconfidence in natural immunity, disease trivialization, low motivation, and misconceptions.33

Occupational disparities in vaccination motivators were clearly observed. Public health workers and clinicians predominantly relied on evidence-based rationales, with 17.60–19.90% citing good vaccine efficacy with high strain match and 18.90–19.70% responding to high flu activity as key determinants. Conversely, nurses and support staff exhibited stronger influence from practical factors: 11.50–13.10% identified workplace programs (paid) as decisive, while 7.70% and 6.80% respectively cited newborn care responsibilities and known cases nearby. Technicians uniquely prioritized workplace programs (paid) (19.70%), followed by high strain match considerations (18.20%). These variations probably stem from differential clinical training exposure, occupational settings, and individual risk assessment patterns.

The investigation demonstrated three consistent predictors of vaccination willingness among community HCWs: current seasonal flu prevalence, verified vaccine effectiveness with strain match, and compensated workplace initiatives – aligning with prior research.15,16,18 Financial accessibility emerges as the fundamental enabler, suggesting local governments should implement subsidized vaccination schemes with employer cost-sharing. When integrated with regional outbreak containment strategies, such approaches could optimize community HCW immunization policies. Current research confirms that isolated measures prove inadequate; comprehensive systematic reviews emphasize the necessity of coupling educational interventions with no-cost vaccine access.34

Notably, when evaluating baseline vaccination willingness, all occupational groups demonstrated substantial resistance. Selection of “unwilling under any circumstances” options occurred at these rates: public health workers 24.50%, nurses 50.00%, technicians 28.30%, clinicians 26.90%, and support staff 39.20%, indicating rejection of standard motivators. Comparable Australian data35 confirmed that recent immunization education participants showed higher vaccination rates.

These findings reveal significant deficiencies in community HCWs’ influenza vaccine knowledge. Healthcare institutions should consequently implement comprehensive, position-specific education programs addressing vaccine science and preventive strategies, aiming to enhance both personal vaccination rates and patient recommendation practices.30

Building directly upon the motivators and barriers identified above, we propose that enhancing influenza immunization rates among community HCWs requires integrated educational and policy approaches. Scientifically-grounded awareness campaigns should systematically address prevalent misconceptions while clearly articulating disease risks and vaccine benefits. Policy initiatives must prioritize fully subsidized vaccination programs, optimally implemented through workplace-based delivery systems. Within Shanghai’s context, synergistic application of these strategies – combining validated educational content with enhanced public health messaging – could significantly improve vaccine receptivity. An optimized implementation framework would incorporate: (1) evidence-based misconception correction and risk communication, (2) universal no-cost vaccine access as a policy cornerstone, and (3) institutional immunization requirements. This multilayered approach shows particular promise for overcoming vaccination barriers specific to Shanghai’s community HCWs.

This study shares methodological constraints common to retrospective cross-sectional surveys, particularly potential recall inaccuracies and non-representative sampling. In our survey, the pilot study is also part of our research content. Since the PDCDC provided relevant training to public health workers, their health literacy was clearly higher than that of other community health workers who had not received such training. Public health workers likely obtained more influenza vaccine information, possibly leading to their over-sampling in this investigation. It may inevitably lead to some bias; however, in this study, the influenza VCR among community HCWs remained low (26.78%). Finally, though our findings are specific to Shanghai, they point to a potential universal implication: effective public health action may benefit from precise local diagnosis. Our study also offers a transferable analytical framework, which could help policymakers in other regions identify local barriers and design tailored interventions.

Conclusion

This study is the first to quantitatively assess influenza VCR among HCWs in Pudong New Area, Shanghai, China. Despite global recommendations for HCW vaccination, the influenza VCR among HCWs in Pudong remains alarmingly low. To bridge this gap, an integrated approach – combining evidence-based educational campaigns (e.g., addressing vaccine misconceptions and emphasizing influenza risks) with structural policy interventions (e.g., fully subsidized vaccination coupled with workplace initiatives) – is essential. The proposed framework, tailored to Pudong’s community HCWs, holds significant potential to improve immunization uptake and reinforce seasonal influenza prevention capacity in primary care settings.
