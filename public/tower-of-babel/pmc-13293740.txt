Characteristics of cannabis warnings in the United States: A content analysis
Authors: Sarah D. Kowitt, Rachel Callanan, Luke I. Haqq, Kristen L. Jarman, Sonia A. Clark, Gillian L. Schauer, Julia A. Dilley, Leah M. Ranney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe goal of this study was to describe characteristics of warnings from state cannabis programs in the United States.MethodsFrom September 2024 to February 2025, we compiled regulations related to cannabis warnings on product packages in states with legalized cannabis sales (medical and adult-use), verifying regulations with regulators from each state. Two coders independently coded warning regulations for different characteristics.ResultsWe identified 60 state cannabis programs across the United States with legalized cannabis sales, of which 56 (93%) required cannabis warnings. Of the programs requiring warnings, 55% included warning content about a specific health risk, most often addiction (41%). Few programs had warnings about cannabis' effects on mental health (16%), respiratory health (5%), or cardiovascular health (4%). Few programs required warnings to contrast with backgrounds (23%), rotating warnings (7%), or front-of-package warnings (4%). The mean number of words in warnings was 57 (Standard Deviation: 30), and the mean reading level was 9th grade.ConclusionsAlthough 93% of cannabis programs with legalized sales require warnings, only half mention specific health risks; less than 10% require rotating or front-of-package warnings; and warnings may be difficult to read and understand. Further research is vital to strengthen cannabis warning labels.

Introduction

Cannabis policies legalizing cannabis use have increased substantially in the United States (US) in recent years. As of April 2025, 23 states have legalized state-regulated adult-use cannabis (sometimes called “recreational use”), and 42 states plus Washington, District of Columbia (DC) have legalized state-regulated medical-use cannabis (Cannabis Regulators Association, 2026). Corresponding with increased legalization, in 2024, half of all US adults (51%, 133 million adults) reported ever using cannabis, 16% reported past 30-day use, and 7% were classified as having cannabis use disorder (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). In addition, for the first time in 2022, the number of people ages 12 and older reporting daily or near-daily cannabis use surpassed the number of people reporting daily or near-daily alcohol use (Caulkins, 2024).

While cannabis consumption is associated with known health benefits, including alleviating nausea associated with chemotherapy, relieving chronic pain, and improving multiple sclerosis spasticity (National Academies of Sciences, 2017), it is also associated with risks that many consumers may not know about (Goodman and Hammond, 2022). For instance, cannabis use is associated with worsened respiratory health, overdose injuries (especially among children), cognitive impairment, development of schizophrenia and other psychoses, cannabis use disorder, development of depressive disorders, suicidal ideation and suicide attempts, impaired driving, and lower birth weight of offspring if used during pregnancy (National Academies of Sciences, 2017). Despite these risks, overall perceptions of cannabis risk have declined over time (Levy et al., 2021).

Legalization of cannabis has created opportunities for communication about the health risks. Specifically, most states that have legalized cannabis require warnings on product packages (Meek et al., 2024). Warnings are an evidence-based policy that can inform consumers about potential risks associated with product use, but are only effective if they are well-designed (Popova et al., 2024). Research suggests best practices for warnings on substance use products, including that 1) warnings should articulate specific, rather than vague health risks, 2) warnings should use short warning statements with a reading grade level as low as possible (Wogalter, 2006), 3) warnings should be large and placed on the front of packages to attract and hold attention (Hammond, 2011), 4) rotating warnings can help prevent warning fatigue and communicate a broad range of risks (Noar et al., 2016a), and 5) images can help capture and hold attention, as well as assist with understanding of health risks (Noar et al., 2016b). While evidence for warning best practices draws heavily from tobacco warnings, the principles of educating consumers about potential harms of these consumer products are similar despite the significant differences between cannabis and tobacco. Indeed, a 2024 review of cannabis warnings found that mandated cannabis warnings in Canada—which include many warning best practices, such as short, rotating warning statements about specific cannabis risks that appear on approximately half of the front of plain packages—improved noticing and recall of warnings (Massey et al., 2024).

The fragmented legalization of cannabis in the US has created a landscape with inconsistent communication about the health risks, and states have taken different approaches to communication, including through warnings (Schauer, 2024). Two previous content analyses of cannabis warning regulations have been conducted, finding varied alignment with warning best practices (Meek et al., 2024; Malouff and Schutte-Malouff, 2020). However, both focused on regulations from states with legalized adult-use cannabis, covering 10 states (conducted in 2019) (Malouff and Schutte-Malouff, 2020) and 20 states (conducted in 2024) (Meek et al., 2024). Up-to-date information is needed on cannabis warning characteristics in US states with legalized adult-use and medical cannabis, since accurate information on cannabis health risks is needed regardless of whether products are used for medical or non-medical purposes. Therefore, the goal of the current study was to describe characteristics of warnings from state medical and adult-use cannabis programs in the US.

Methods

Study design

From September 2024 to February 2025, we compiled state laws and administrative regulations related to cannabis warnings on product packaging in states with legalized cannabis sales (medical and adult-use). We use the terminology “cannabis programs” to refer to state-regulated medical or adult-use cannabis programs. We excluded programs where legal sales were not yet allowed as of January 1, 2025, the program was no longer active (e.g., no sales currently taking place), or litigation prevented sales. Using these criteria, we identified 60 programs. Of these programs, 56 (93%) required warnings, with n = 35 medical programs and n = 21 adult-use programs, representing 36 states and Washington, DC (Supplementary File A).

We then reviewed the cannabis packaging and labeling guidance found on states' cannabis regulatory agency websites for each of the 56 programs that required warnings. We focused on warnings required on physical product packages, excluding warnings posted in retail environments, on advertisements, or as supplementary materials. For the purposes of this study, our definition of a “warning” followed what program regulations or regulators defined as warnings. For example, many program laws and regulations require a universal symbol indicating that the product contains cannabis; however, if the universal symbol was not defined as part of the warning, it would not have been coded in our analysis. Due to the continually shifting nature of the regulatory landscape, we verified warning requirements with regulators from each state through a partnership with the Cannabis Regulators Association (CANNRA), a non-partisan nationwide organization that brings together government agencies responsible for regulating cannabis, cannabinoid, and hemp products.

We developed our codebook through a mix of deductive and inductive codes (Supplementary File B). Overall, codes fell into three main categories: 1) general warning information, 2) warning content, and 3) warning formatting. For general information, we captured the type of cannabis program, the source of specific warning regulations, terminology used in warnings, and whether programs required additional warnings for specific cannabis products (i.e., edibles, vaped/smoked products, high-potency products). For this last category, a program could require additional warnings about products that did not include content about the health risks of the product in question (e.g., for vaped products, a program could require a warning stating, “This product is not approved by the FDA [Food and Drug Administration],” which does not mention any health-related risks of vaping). Therefore, we also coded whether programs warned about the health risks of specific cannabis products.

Measures

For warning content, we coded for specific health-related risks (e.g., addiction), population considerations (e.g., that children should not use the product), behavioral considerations (e.g., not to consume more than the recommended amount), and legal considerations (e.g., legal restrictions). For warning formatting, we coded whether warnings were required to be a minimum font size, use a specified font type, contrast with the packaging background, and be located on a specific part of the package. We also coded whether any programs required rotating warnings, as well as the average number of words, characters with spaces, and Flesch-Kincaid reading grade level. When calculating the word count, character count, and reading grade level, we randomly selected one warning from programs that employed rotating warnings.

In March 2025, two members of the research team practiced coding a small subset of the warnings to ensure consistent application of codes. After the pilot, we revised the codebook, and two members independently coded warnings. Discrepancies were resolved by other members of the research team, with consultation from the full research team when needed.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a content analysis. The unit of analysis was 56, which represents the number of state cannabis programs that met our eligibility criteria and required warnings. This study was not considered human subjects research and therefore did not require Institutional Review Board approval. We used Microsoft Excel to generate descriptive statistics.

Results

Of the 56 state cannabis programs that required warnings and were included in our analysis, the majority provided verbatim (i.e., exact) language for the warning statements (83.9%, n = 47), whereas 3.6% provided non-verbatim language by requiring that some language be used but not detailing specific language to be used (e.g., “A statement that the product is for medical use”) (n = 2), and 12.5% used a combination of verbatim and non-verbatim language (n = 7) (Table 1). Warning content

Overall, in terms of warning content, around half of programs mentioned specific health risks in warnings (55.4%, n = 31) (Table 2). The most commonly mentioned health risks were: addiction/addiction potential (41.1%, n = 23), intoxication (33.9%, n = 19), cognitive effects (17.9%, n = 10), and mental health effects (16.1%, n = 9). A handful of programs mentioned exposure to chemicals, including carcinogens (8.9%, n = 5), or effects on brain development (7.1%, n = 4). Few programs mentioned respiratory effects (5.4%, n = 3), cancer (3.6%, n = 2), or cardiovascular effects (3.6%, n = 2). The remaining programs either mentioned vague health risks (e.g., this product may be harmful) (26.8%, n = 15) or did not mention any health risks (vague or specific) (18.0%, n = 10). In addition to health-related warning content, the vast majority of programs mentioned population considerations in their warnings (94.6%, n = 53). Specifically, programs often warned that products should not be used by children, often using the phrase “keep out of reach of children” (87.5%, n = 49) or by those who are pregnant or breastfeeding (62.5%, n = 35). Most programs also mentioned behavioral considerations of cannabis use in their warnings (85.7%, n = 48), including not to drive motor vehicles while under the influence of cannabis (73.2%, n = 41) or that there could be delayed effects (57.1%, n = 32). Finally, many programs also included legal considerations or disclaimers in their warnings (64.3%, n = 36). Specifically, 32.1% had statements that products are not approved by the FDA (n = 18) and 23.2% had statements that the product is unlawful outside of the state (n = 13).

Warning formatting

For instance, 60.7% of programs required a minimum font size for warnings (n = 34), ranging from 6-point font (smallest minimum font size) to 12-point font (largest minimum font size). Discussion

This study is the first content analysis of warning regulations for cannabis programs in medical and adult-use states in the US. Overall, we found that although 93% of cannabis programs require a warning, only half mentioned specific health risks; few required color, rotating, or front-of-package warnings; and warnings were long, potentially decreasing reader comprehension and understandability. These findings are important given that over 60 million adults reported using cannabis in the past year in 2024 (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration. Key substance use and mental health indicators in the United States: Results from the, 2024), but many may not have an accurate understanding of potential risks (Goodman and Hammond, 2022).

Warnings are a population-level intervention that can help consumers understand product risks and enable informed decisions (Popova et al., 2024). However, our content analysis found that only half of state cannabis programs with warnings mentioned specific health risks (55%), most commonly addiction and intoxication, mirroring results from two previously conducted content analyses of cannabis warnings in adult-use states (Meek et al., 2024; Malouff and Schutte-Malouff, 2020). The evidence for some specific harms associated with cannabis use is sometimes mixed or not reflective of currently available products (National Academies of Sciences, 2017); therefore, more general warning language may be applied in part to reflect the evolving research. Despite addiction being the most commonly mentioned health risk in warnings, research from 2019 found that only around half of US adults thought cannabis could be addictive, and less than a third of those reporting daily/near-daily use thought that cannabis could be addictive (Goodman and Hammond, 2022). This suggests that even though cannabis warnings often mention addiction as a potential risk of cannabis use, research is needed to strengthen the way in which this is communicated.

In addition, less than 20% of programs had warnings about mental health effects, respiratory effects, harms to brain development, or cognitive effects, despite all of these being well-documented cannabis health risks (National Academies of Sciences, 2017). Our findings suggest that the content of current cannabis warnings could be enhanced by focusing on lesser-known, yet still evidence-based, health risks. For example, research suggests that cannabis can negatively affect mental health, including increasing the risk of psychosis, schizophrenia, anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation and attempts, and severity of posttraumatic stress disorder, and that many of these risks increase with earlier initiation and more frequent use (National Academies of Sciences, 2017; Campeny et al., 2020; Solmi et al., 2023). Yet, the mental health effects of cannabis use are not well-known compared to other risks of cannabis use (Goodman and Hammond, 2022). In addition, many people report using cannabis to alleviate mental health problems (Leung et al., 2022), which makes cannabis warnings about mental health effects particularly important. One way to increase awareness of lesser-known health effects is to use rotating warnings, which periodically change the content of warnings over time. Rotating warnings have been shown to prevent consumer “wear-out” and increase exposure to a broader range of health topics in a more digestible format (Hammond, 2011), yet only four programs used rotating warnings in our study.

Moreover, our study found that only a minority of programs included content about product-specific risks of smoked or vaped cannabis. While some risks of cannabis use apply to most/all products with THC, other risks are specific to certain types of products. For instance, the inhalation of cannabis via smoking or vaping is associated with respiratory health concerns (National Academies of Sciences, 2017), yet less than 25% and 10% of programs mentioned health risks associated with smoking or vaping, respectively, and those that did often only mentioned vague risks (e.g., vaping/smoking is hazardous to health). Only 4 programs mentioned specific health risks associated with vaping or smoking cannabis. Given that smoking is the most common mode of cannabis administration and vaping cannabis is growing in popularity, especially among young people (Quader, 2025), specific warnings about smoking and vaping cannabis are warranted.

Similarly, research shows that edibles can lead to increased overdose injuries among children over other product forms due to their appealing packaging and similarity to food products (Whitehill et al., 2021). While our study found that many programs warned about the delayed effects of edibles or cannabis products in general, no programs mentioned specific overdose injuries of edibles for children. This is important given increasing unintentional child exposure to THC and adverse events resulting from exposure, such as hospitalization (Whitehill et al., 2021; Tweet et al., 2023). Moreover, research shows that higher potency products can increase the risk of psychosis and other mental health concerns, especially among those with a family history (Petrilli et al., 2022), although researchers have noted methodological limitations to these studies (Li et al., 2024). Our study found that only 4 programs required warnings about the psychosis risk of higher potency products (specifically defining such products as those containing greater than 30% or 40% THC).

Finally, warnings in our study often appeared as lengthy legal disclaimers, using a combination of statements about health risks, population considerations, behavioral considerations, and legal considerations. Indeed, our study found that warnings were long (on average, 57 words), used small font sizes (if any font size was required), and fewer than a quarter were required to contrast with the background of the packages. Moreover, only two programs required front-of-package warnings. The result is that US consumers are confused about certain aspects of cannabis warnings and often overlook cannabis warnings, as demonstrated in previous qualitative and quantitative research (Massey et al., 2024; Ranney et al., 2025; Kosa et al., 2017; Goodman and Hammond, 2021). Importantly, our study also found substantial variation in warnings across programs, both in terms of content and formatting, which can increase confusion when consumers purchase products from different states.

Strengths of our study include our partnership with CANNRA and state regulators to verify warning regulations and content, our inclusion of both adult-use and medical programs, and our use of high-quality legal research methods (legal expertise, a well-defined codebook, and duplicate coding) to code cannabis warning characteristics. We also note several limitations. First, cannabis policy is rapidly evolving at the state level, so the number of programs and warning characteristics of programs is likely to change over the coming years, particularly given changes in potential federal cannabis policy (The White House, 2026; Public Law 119-37, § 781, 139 Stat. 558, 2026); this content analysis of cannabis warning characteristics is meant to be viewed as a snapshot in time. Second, we only coded characteristics of warnings, which means that other aspects of cannabis packages/labels that may communicate risk (e.g., symbols, package inserts, exit packaging, posted warnings, and warnings available through a Quick Response ‘QR’ code or web link), which are increasing across states, were not included. Third, this study focused solely on describing cannabis warnings and did not examine reactions to existing warnings. While some studies have examined reactions to existing warnings (Massey et al., 2024), future research is needed to examine responses to different warning messages, sizes, and formats, as well as how warnings perform on different types of cannabis products. Finally, our study did not examine hemp-derived intoxicating products, which are not part of the legal cannabis market and regulations in most states, and were outside of this analysis.

Conclusions

Our content analysis of warnings in state cannabis programs with legalized adult or medical use found that although 93% of programs require a warning, there is room for improved application of warning best practices: only half mention specific health risks; few require rotating or front-of-package warnings; and warnings may be difficult to read and understand. This study motivates future research to document and synthesize evidence on cannabis health effects for use in potential warnings; examine reactions to existing cannabis warnings; examine how cannabis warnings can be strengthened; and determine whether strengthened cannabis warnings can improve understanding of cannabis risks. In particular, future research could evaluate whether cannabis warnings are improved by: 1) highlighting specific, rather than vague health risks, 2) using short warning statements with a reading grade level as low as possible, 3) using a large size with placement on the front of packages, 4) using rotating warnings, and 5) using symbols or icons.
