Cardinalities in proofreading
M. Klazar KAM MFF UK Praha

Abstract
In 1975, G. Pólya pointed out that if two proofreaders found a a , resp. b b , errors in a text, and both found c c errors, then the unknown number e e of all errors is approximated by e ≈ a ​ b c e\approx\frac{ab}{c} . We devise a realistic combinatorial model of Pólya’s result and estimate its performance by the Probabilistic Method.

Theorem 1.3

Let
(P,\overline{S})
, where

\overline{S}=((\overline{s}_{1},\,\overline{u}_{1}),\,(\overline{s}_{1},\,\overline{u}_{2}),\,\dots,\,\dots,\,(\overline{s}_{M},\,\overline{u}_{N}))\,,

be two
(p,q,n)
-proofreaders and let

{\textstyle c(p,\,q)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{2}{pq}\big)^{-1}\,.}

Then for every text
\overline{t}\in B_{n}

with
\|\overline{t}\|=m\geq 1

that

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{MN}}\cdot\big|\big\{j\in[MN]\colon\;\left|\frac{\|P(\overline{s}_{v_{j}},\,\overline{t})\|\cdot\|P(\overline{u}_{w_{j}},\,\overline{t})\|}{\|P(\overline{s}_{v_{j}},\,P(\overline{u}_{w_{j}},\,\overline{t}))\|}-m\right|\leq a\big\}\big|\geq

\displaystyle{\textstyle\geq 1-6\exp(-\frac{2c(p,\,q)^{2}a^{2}}{m})\,.}

For

+\infty

Pólya’s estimate has distance at most

of pairs

of runs of the two proofreaders.

3
Concluding remarks

By
[
5
, p. 109]
, “The subject of probability is wholly free from
real paradoxes.” This is not precise — not only books
like
[
10
]
are devoted to paradoxes in
probability theory, but some of these paradoxes are truly real. At their
roots is the unrealistic nature, or
even total absence, of physical experiments that
should be modeled by the probabilistic model, or PM, at hand.
In these paradoxical cases we often have a PM that does not model anything realistic. For example, take well known

Bertrand’s paradox
which asks about the
probability that random chord in the unit circle in the plane has length
at least
\sqrt{3}
(the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle). The
noun phrase “random chord” is problematic because it does not refer
to anything clearly defined. In contrast, noun phrases like
“random (finite!) graph”
or “random subset (of a finite set)”
are unproblematic; these concepts are easily modeled by finite uniform
probability spaces and events in them, like the above
P(MN)
and the

P(MN)
-events
A_{i}
,
A_{i}^{\prime}
and
C_{i}
,
i\in[n]
. Mathematical chords
have zero width and form an uncountable set. People try to
resolve Bertrand’s paradox by rushing in defining PM on the set of
mathematical chords. Instead, they should step back and
consider physical chords with width
w>0
. A realistic
and convincing physical discrete model, or DM, for selection
of random physical chords should be devised. Only when such DM
is understood, we can send
w
to
0
and see what happens. This
approach is implemented in
[
6
]
. We hope
to rework
[
6
]
in near future in a better form.

In this article we approached Pólya’s proofreaders from the DM
perspective. Returning to Proposition
1.1
, we see
that if
(P,\overline{s})
, where

\overline{s}=(\overline{s}_{1},\,\overline{s}_{2},\,\dots,\,\overline{s}_{N})\,,

is the
(p,n)
-proofreader, then in the uniform probability space

P(N)=([N],\,\mathcal{P}([N]),\,\mathrm{Pr})\,,

with
\mathrm{Pr}(\{j\})=\frac{1}{N}
, the
n

P(N)
-events

A_{i}=\{j\in[N]\colon\;s_{j,i}=1\},\ i\in[n]\,,

are independent and
model random subset of
[n]
with probability of an element equal to

p
. However, our construction requires
p
(
\in(0,1]
) be
a rational number. This is just as right because we cannot have
a realistic physical experiment with probabilities like

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Thus we conclude our article with the comment, which may
come as a surprise to some, that the random graph
G(n,p)
with
probability of an edge equal to an irrational number
p
is not really a realistic model.
