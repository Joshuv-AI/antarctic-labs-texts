Should the Workload of Combining Clinical Practice With Data Collection be Considered: A Survey of Physicians With Data Collection Experience
Authors: Xinyi Zhang, Yin Jiang, Zhiyue Guan, Mengzhu Zhao, Mingzhi Hu, Qianqian Xu, Wenhui Wang, Wulin Gao, Ruijin Qiu, Min Li, Baolin Yang, Li Zhou, Zhengqi Liu, Zhengsheng Li, Yongjing Xiang, Jiyang Zhao, Zaijian Wang, Xien Lou, Shengjun Guo, Guohua Dai, Zhaoxiang Bian, Hongwu Wang, Chen Zhao, Hongcai Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo survey the physician's attention to the workload of combining clinical practice with Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) data collection.BackgroundWith the development of artificial intelligence technology in the medical field, the task of collecting diverse clinical data in TCM has increased. Based on the TCM's diagnostic and treatment principles, the collection of research data accompanying clinical practice is inevitable, which may have an impact on TCM clinical practice.MethodA previous research was conducted to collect diverse instant TCM diagnostic and treatment data, and physicians and research designers proposed many suggestions focusing on the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection. In this study, A 54‐item questionnaire was developed based on the suggestions. Forty‐eight participants with data‐collection experience participated in a questionnaire survey, and they needed to grade each item, which reflected their attention to the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection.ResultsThe survey received 40 valid questionnaires, with 49 items scoring 4 or above. Three items in the content dimension (Q9, Q10, Q11) and two items in the spatial dimension (Q31, Q48) are scored lower. Additionally, 25 supplementary suggestions were collected during the study.ConclusionThe workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection needs to be considered. The items in this survey could be regarded as a basis for developing a tool to consider the relationship between clinical practice and data collection.Keywords: clinical practice, diversified clinical data, instant TCM data, survey, TCM data collection, workload

Introduction

With the development of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) clinical‐related research, the scope and types of clinical data collection have become increasingly diverse. TCM clinical practice relies on the four TCM diagnostic methods—observation, listening and olfaction, inquiry, and pulse palpation—to collect data related to the patient's condition, such as tongue appearance, facial expressions, pulse characteristics, and so forth. Previously, these details were mostly recorded in textual form.

With technological advancements, modern medical equipment can record clinical data through multi‐modal approaches [1, 2], such as text [3, 4], images [5, 6], videos [7], odor [8], and audio [9, 10]. The multi‐modal TCM data cannot only be used for clinical diagnosis but also support research into artificial intelligence‐assisted healthcare [11, 12, 13], enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of diagnosis and treatment [14, 15].

The diversification of data also brings new challenges. Due to the specific timeliness requirements of diagnosis and treatment in TCM, much data, such as an image of the tongue, needs to be captured within a real‐time window. Additional TCM data collection may affect the clinical practice and data quality [16]. Currently, there is no research on the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection [17]. Therefore, this study surveyed attention to this workload among some experienced data collectors from a previous research.

Methods

Previous Research Overview

The previous research was conducted from April 2022 to December 2022 at four locations in China to collect diversified TCM diagnosis and treatment data. The research protocols were approved by the ethics committees of the following hospitals: Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated Hospital (Ethics Approval No.: 2022; Ethics Review No. [024]‐KY), Dongzhimen Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics Approval Nos.: 2022DZMEC‐025‐02, 2022DZMEC‐048‐02, 2022DZMEC‐100), the Third Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Ethics Approval No.: BZYSY‐2022KYKTPJ‐03), and Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Second Affiliated Hospital (Ethics Approval Nos.: KYH2022003, KYH2022002, KYH2022005) (ethics approval documents are provided in File S1). All patients signed informed consent forms before participating in the research.

In previous research, the physicians took photographs of the patients' tongues using a camera device, captured the sound of the patients reading aloud by an audio‐recording device, captured the patients' impulse waveforms by using a pressure transducer, and recorded the patients' testimonials, symptoms, and signs, and prescribing data (Figure 1). This data collection took place concurrently with TCM diagnosis and treatment activities. The data collection devices were placed in outpatient rooms or wards (specific placement details are provided in File S2).

Survey Procedure

Each item in the questionnaire was designed to assess participants' attention to the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection. Participants used a five‐point Likert scale to score each item, where 1 point meant “not necessary to consider at all” and 5 points meant “very necessary to consider.” Participants were allowed to add new content for each dimension.

The survey link was sent to participants, and a reminder was sent to those who did not respond within 3 days.

Statistical Analysis

We did not calculate the sample size, but predetermined it based on previous research. As the study required participants to have rich experience in multi‐modal TCM data collection, and our previous research had 48 qualified experts (from southern and northern China) whose professional backgrounds and practical experience met the study's requirements, we selected them as the sample for this study.

The age range of participants was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The age distribution, gender ratio, workplace distribution, and professional titles were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Disease of participants' data collection and the degree of agreement for each dimension were illustrated using bar‐line charts.

The extreme values (maximum and minimum values) and mean for each dimension, as well as the mean, median, SD, and interquartile range (IQR) for each item, were described. In addition, to measure the variability or instability of the data, the coefficient of variation (CV) for each item was calculated (CV = SD/mean).

DISCUSSION

With the development of digital and intelligent medical research [18, 19, 20], multi‐modal data need to be collected during clinical diagnosis and treatment for training artificial intelligence equipment [21, 22]. This has raised concerns among physicians about the integration challenges, costs, and implementation barriers between clinical practice and data collection. The additional workload needed for data collection can potentially occupy the time, space, manpower, and other resources for clinical practice. Unlike the data collection of Western medicine, some TCM data collection is not essential for clinical diagnosis and treatment, such as using equipment to collect digitally recorded objective data from inspection, auscultation, inquiry, and palpation. Thus, the workload generated from combining clinical practice with TCM data collection in TCM should be considered. This study investigated the universal attention of physicians to the workload.

The results showed that most items scored more than 4, indicating that participants are very concerned about the workload generated from combining clinical practice with TCM data collection, which is consistent with our previous findings [17]. For example, Q1, Q2, and Q3 reflected that most participants think that familiarity with the disease, research content, and objectives should be prioritized. Q15 and Q16 reflected that participants think that subjective data, such as TCM symptoms and syndromes, should be assessed in a real‐time window, emphasizing the importance of data timeliness. In addition, by summarizing the open‐ended suggestions, we found that most of them overlap with the core content of the items, further confirming that the majority of participants are concerned about the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection. Therefore, developing a tool that optimizes the workload of data collectors and improves the standardization and efficiency of data collection is necessary.

However, the results also contain some differences. For example, Q31 and Q30 described the effect of patients' long‐distance movement on their performance of the four TCM diagnostic data. Some participants think that patient movement may impact the accuracy of the diagnosis, especially for observing facial and tongue features. Other physicians think that appropriate movement does not significantly affect the four TCM diagnostic data. This indicates that future tools should consider additional data collection locations outside the clinical setting, ensuring a suitable distance between the clinical site and the data collection site. Q11 reflected the differences in design requirements between fixed and open‐ended content for participants, indicating the need for standardization in data collection for physicians. This is an important focus for future development and optimization of the tool.

Future attention to the workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection will facilitate the smooth implementation of more research, such as emergency clinical trials conducted during infectious disease outbreaks [23, 24], where efficient allocation of limited time and human resources is crucial. It will also help clarify the clinical localization of TCM auxiliary products. Optimizing the allocation of resources, such as content, time, space, and human resources, between data collection and clinical practice is the key to ensuring the effective implementation of auxiliary diagnostic products in clinical practice.

The sample size of this survey was small because the participants needed to have sufficient experience. Only participants who had collected complex data through observation, listening and olfaction, inquiry, and pulse palpation in previous research met the survey requirements. Therefore, we collected as many suggestions as possible from participants before the survey and continued to collect their additional suggestions after the survey. These preparations provided a basis for developing a universal tool for the next step.

CONCLUSION

The workload of combining clinical practice with TCM data collection is an urgent issue that must be considered. Based on the items from this survey, a tool can be developed to coordinate data collection and clinical practice.
