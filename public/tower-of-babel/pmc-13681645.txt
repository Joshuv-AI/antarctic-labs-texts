Integrating technology in leech therapy: Toward standardized evidence-based Ayurvedic practice
Authors: Ramakrishna Allam, B. Kothainayagi, P.A. Sudhir, Vanitha R. Muralikumar, S. Sajitha, K. Sudharsan, S. Vasanth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jalaukavacharana (leech therapy), an integral part of Ayurvedic medicine, is widely recognized for its benefits in managing chronic wounds, inflammatory conditions, and vascular disorders. However, its traditional application often lacks standardized procedures, real-time monitoring, and regulatory oversight, limiting its acceptance in modern clinical practice. While these challenges are notable in the Indian context, including species misidentification and inconsistent protocols, they are also encountered globally, even in countries where leech therapy is medically recognized.This narrative review explores the integration of digital technologies, such as wearable biosensors, AI-based imaging, and IoT-enabled platforms, to enhance the safety, efficacy, and standardization of leech therapy. A comprehensive analysis of scientific literature and technical sources was conducted to evaluate innovations for monitoring vital signs, tracking leech activity, assessing vascular changes, and documenting wound healing.Based on these insights, the manuscript proposes a forward-looking implementation framework that includes the development of AYUSH-approved Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), formal recognition of validated species such as Hirudo medicinalis, traceability systems, clinician training programs, multicentric pilot studies, and digital clinical registries. These strategies aim to modernize Jalaukavacharana while preserving its traditional essence.The integration of AI, IoT, and biosensing technologies offers a pathway to transform leech therapy into a globally credible, evidence-based therapeutic modality suitable for contemporary integrative healthcare systems.

Introduction

Ayurveda, the ancient Indian system of medicine, is rooted in the concept of maintaining balance among the three fundamental bodily energies, Vata, Pitta, and Kapha [1]. It emphasizes a holistic approach to health through various therapeutic modalities, including herbal formulations, dietary regulation, detoxification, and purification therapies [2]. One of the key purification (Shodhana) procedures in Ayurveda is Raktamokshana (bloodletting), which is employed to eliminate Dushta Rakta (vitiated blood), considered a causative factor in a wide range of diseases, particularly those involving inflammation and circulatory disorders [3].

Raktamokshana is broadly classified into Shastrakruta (surgical) and Ashastrakruta (non-surgical) methods. Shastrakruta techniques involve invasive methods like Siravedha (venesection) and Prachanna (scarification). In contrast, Ashastrakruta methods such as Alabu (gourd method), Shringa (horn extraction), and Jalaukavacharana (leech therapy) are minimally invasive and are especially suitable for vulnerable or Pitta-dominant individuals [[4], [5], [6]].

Among these, Jalaukavacharana has gained increasing attention due to its unique mechanism of selectively removing vitiated blood while simultaneously delivering pharmacologically active compounds such as hirudin, calin, and eglins through the saliva of Hirudo medicinalis (medicinal leech) [[7], [8], [9]]. These bioactives exhibit anticoagulant, vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties, making leech therapy particularly beneficial in conditions such as arthritis, varicose veins, chronic wounds, and psoriasis [10].

Leech therapy is not an irreplaceable or life-saving modality, nor it is typically employed as an emergency intervention in acute, life-threatening conditions. Nevertheless, it remains a valuable adjunct in the management of various chronic and complex medical disorders. Although not a frontline treatment, its unique biological properties, particularly the anticoagulant, vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and enzymatic actions of leech saliva, confer significant therapeutic benefits. These mechanisms have shown efficacy in conditions such as cardiovascular disease, localized venous congestion (especially following reconstructive or microsurgical procedures), chronic non-healing ulcers, glaucoma, elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), alopecia areata, and other dermatological and vascular pathologies [[11], [12], [13]]. Medicinal leech therapy (Hirudo medicinalis) has gained acceptance as an adjunctive treatment in modern plastic and reconstructive surgery, particularly for managing venous congestion following microsurgical procedures. Published clinical evidence supports its application in cases of compromised skin flaps, digital replantation, and revascularization where venous drainage remains inadequate [14 15]. In Southern California, Torrance Memorial Medical Center maintains leeches in its pharmacy and routinely applies them in the reattachment of fingers, toes, and ears, particularly when vascular compromise is identified after microsurgery [16]. Moreover, Long Beach Memorial Medical Center, in collaboration with Torrance Memorial, employs leech therapy for managing venous insufficiency in microsurgical grafts and digit replantation [16]. The clinical utility of leeches in such contexts is supported by their anticoagulant-rich saliva and their ability to provide temporary venous drainage until neovascularization occurs [17,18]. These institutions exemplify the controlled, evidence-based application of leech therapy in modern medicine, validated by U.S. FDA approval as a Class III medical device since 2004 [19]. While not indispensable, leech therapy continues to demonstrate relevance in integrative and supportive care frameworks, reaffirming its role in modern evidence-based complementary medicine.

Despite its therapeutic potential, conventional leech therapy is constrained by several challenges that hinder its standardization and broader clinical application.

Methodology

This narrative review was conducted to explore the integration of modern technological advancements in traditional Ayurvedic leech therapy (Jalaukavacharana), with a focus on enhancing safety, efficacy, and standardization. A comprehensive literature search was performed using online databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, and Scopus. Additionally, relevant reports from government bodies like the Ministry of AYUSH, WHO, and various biomedical technology platforms were reviewed to gather both clinical and technical perspectives.

The keywords used in the search included: “Leech therapy,” “Jalaukavacharana,” “Ayurveda,” “biosensors,” “wearable health monitors,” “AI in healthcare,” “IoT in traditional medicine,” and “digital health tools in bloodletting.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to combine keywords and refine results.

Articles were selected based on their relevance to either the clinical application of leech therapy or technological innovations that can support or enhance such therapies. Priority was given to literature that addressed aspects such as physiological monitoring during therapy, digital tools for treatment standardization, automation in leech therapy practices, and data-driven approaches for assessing clinical outcomes.

Specific issues in current leech application making it less globally standardized and documented

Leech therapy (Jalaukavacharana) is a time-tested procedure in Ayurveda, recognized for its effectiveness in treating various inflammatory, vascular, and dermatological conditions. It is not inherently unsafe or inefficient when performed by trained professionals under appropriate clinical settings. However, several practical and procedural limitations hinder its standardization, reproducibility, and global acceptance, particularly in evidence-based medical environments.

Leech identification

There is a risk of inadvertently using non-medicinal or toxic leech species, which may cause adverse reactions. Proper identification requires expert knowledge of taxonomy and morphology, which is often lacking in outpatient Ayurvedic practice.

Absence of standardized operating procedures (SOPs)

While classical Ayurvedic texts outline guidelines for leech therapy, there is no universally accepted modern SOP that includes clear clinical protocols, standardized leech species identification, dosage (blood volume withdrawn), or follow-up criteria. This lack of procedural standardization leads to variability in practice, making it difficult to validate and reproduce outcomes across different practitioners or institutions.

Lack of objective, evidence-based outcome documentation

Current leech therapy practices largely rely on qualitative and subjective assessments, such as visual inspection of the wound or patient-reported symptom relief. There is a lack of objective measurement tools for evaluating parameters such as blood flow changes, inflammatory response, or rate of wound healing. This makes evidence-based reporting difficult, limiting its acceptance in modern integrative medicine frameworks.

Limited data for global validation

Due to minimal integration of digital documentation, image-based wound tracking, or biomarker analysis, most therapy sessions are not archived with measurable data. This restricts clinical audits, outcome comparisons, and meta-analyses that are essential for regulatory approval and international recognition of the therapy.

Unmonitored therapy sessions

In many settings, leech application is performed without real-time monitoring of vital signs, therapy duration, or blood loss [20]. While not unsafe per se, this lack of surveillance infrastructure raises challenges in patient safety monitoring, especially in vulnerable populations. This poses a danger in patients with co-morbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or clotting disorders, where physiological stress might go unnoticed [21].

Recognizing these limitations, the Ministry of AYUSH stresses the importance of integrating Indian Knowledge Systems (IKS) with modern technological tools to promote holistic and scientifically validated healthcare practices. In this context, the application of digital innovations, such as wearable biosensors, AI-driven analytics, and IoT-enabled monitoring systems, offers promising avenues to modernize leech therapy. This narrative review aims to explore and map existing technological advancements that can enhance the standardization of Jalaukavacharana, thus bridging traditional Ayurvedic wisdom with contemporary medical technology.

The foundational taxonomic work on Indian leeches was carried out by Harding and Moore (1927), who documented 51 species and subspecies across the Indian subcontinent. This baseline was subsequently expanded by Mahesh Chandra in Leeches of India: A Handbook (1991), and later revised and updated by Chandra Kanta Mandal in Checklist of the Hirudinea (Leeches) of India (2004). Mandal's compilation recognized over 60 species, including newly described taxa, taxonomic revisions, and the assignment of standardized English common names. These progressive efforts have greatly contributed to biodiversity assessments and have brought taxonomic clarity to the Indian hirudinean fauna [23].

From a biomedical perspective, non-poisonous leeches such as Hirudo medicinalis, a species widely accepted in global medical systems and approved by regulatory bodies like the U.S. FDA and European Medicines Agency (EMA), are commonly used due to their proven therapeutic efficacy. Its saliva contains bioactive compounds with anticoagulant, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties, making them valuable in procedures such as bloodletting, venous congestion therapy, and microsurgery. In contrast, poisonous leech species, characterized by the presence of toxic secretions, are not used clinically, as their bites can provoke adverse reactions. These may include localized swelling, pruritus, fever, and, in severe cases, systemic symptoms such as delirium or unconsciousness [24].

Morphologically, leeches are characterized by a segmented body comprising 34 somites, each housing a ganglion, with multiple annuli per somite. Key taxonomic markers for species identification include the number and arrangement of eyes, presence of sensillae, structure of the suckers, and the position of genital pores. In India, the identification of leech fauna is primarily based on these morphological characteristics, organized within dichotomous taxonomic keys. These keys incorporate both external and internal features, such as segmentation patterns, presence or absence of lateral branchiae and pulsating vesicles, gonopore positioning, and digestive system morphology, including the number and arrangement of crop diverticula [25]. This complexity makes manual identification challenging and significantly increases the risk of using inappropriate species in therapeutic applications. Moreover, due to overlapping morphological traits among different leech species, traditional identification methods often prove insufficient for ensuring safety and accuracy.

Modern technologies offer powerful solutions to these challenges. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) algorithms can analyze high-resolution images of leeches to distinguish species based on shape, color patterns, eye arrangements, and movement behavior [26]. These tools allow rapid, real-time identification and can be deployed in clinical or field settings. In parallel, genomic sequencing enables species authentication at the molecular level by comparing DNA barcodes, helping differentiate morphologically similar species or identify cryptic diversity [27]. When integrated with traditional taxonomic knowledge, these technologies not only reduce the risk of misidentification but also pave the way for the global standardization and wider acceptance of leech therapy.

RFID/QR tagging for leech tracking

To ensure proper hygiene and prevent cross-contamination, individual leeches can be tagged using RFID (Radio frequency identification) or QR code technology. Each leech would have a unique identifier, enabling digital tracking of its usage history and ensuring adherence to single-use protocols [28,29]. This system supports clinical safety and compliance, particularly in integrative and traditional therapeutic environments.

Embedded within a wearable patch or therapy station, the system continuously assesses key parameters related to leech activity, including readiness, grip strength, behavioral patterns, blood flow volume, and therapy duration. By analyzing movement, contact quality, and visual cues throughout the session, the system can detect abnormal events such as detachment or disturbance, triggering timely alerts. Additionally, it tracks and records treatment metrics such as the total blood drawn and session length, ensuring precise documentation and enhancing safety. This integrated technological approach provides a data-driven, efficient, and hygienic leech therapy experience, ultimately supporting clinical decision-making. Vascular changes monitoring

A portable imaging system can be integrated to non-invasively monitor changes in vascular dynamics during and after leech therapy. This system will focus on key parameters such as the formation of new blood vessels and improvements in blood flow to the affected area throughout the treatment process. The system, embedded in an imaging unit, will be positioned over the treatment site to collect comparative data before and after therapy sessions or using Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) using a sandwich-ELISA method. This approach allows for the quantitative analysis of vascular changes, providing objective evidence of treatment efficacy. The resulting data can enhance personalized treatment planning and support informed clinical decision-making in both traditional and integrative medical practices. Using AI, the system will compare these images over time to track wound healing, identifying signs of tissue regeneration, scarring reduction, or potential infection. Based on this data, AI will generate visual reports that provide a comprehensive, data-driven treatment evaluation. This approach ensures objective, continuous monitoring of the wound healing process and offers healthcare providers actionable insights to optimize care. Inflammatory markers

In addition to physiological monitoring, evaluating inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) can provide valuable biochemical insights into the body's systemic response to leech therapy. These markers help assess inflammation levels, immune modulation, and healing progression. By correlating changes in CRP, ESR, and IL-6 with real-time vascular and clinical parameters, practitioners can better understand the anti-inflammatory effects of therapy, guide treatment planning, and monitor recovery outcomes with greater precision. To monitor inflammatory markers before and after leech therapy, advanced technologies like point-of-care immunoassay devices [56,57] and automated blood analyzers [58] can be utilized.

To ensure feasibility in rural or low-resource settings, a tiered implementation model is recommended.•Step 1 - Manual Testing: Use of conventional lab-based ESR and CRP tests available at local pathology centers, with results recorded manually in patient records.•Step 2 - Point-of-Care Devices: Deployment of cost-effective handheld immunoassay kits and lateral flow test strips that do not require cold storage or advanced laboratory infrastructure.•Step 3 - Automated Monitoring: Integration of compact, portable analyzers capable of real-time biomarker analysis and data synchronization with cloud-based systems, suitable for urban or well-funded Ayurvedic clinics.

Automated alerts and data archiving

IoT connectivity [[59], [60], [61]] can be leveraged to send real-time alerts to healthcare providers in the event of critical issues such as vital sign abnormalities, leech detachment, activity disturbance, or excessive blood loss. If any monitored parameters, including SpO2, BP, or pulse rate, fall outside of normal ranges, an immediate alert will be triggered. Similarly, if the leech detaches unexpectedly or exhibits abnormal behavior, such as detachment from the skin, an alert will notify the healthcare provider to intervene. In cases of excessive blood loss, the system will track blood volume and trigger alerts if the blood loss surpasses safe limits. Additionally, all collected data can be securely archived in cloud storage, ensuring easy access for healthcare providers for ongoing treatment evaluation, future reference, and research purposes. This real-time connectivity and data archiving enhance the quality of care, facilitate quick interventions, and support long-term studies on treatment outcomes. Discussion

The integration of modern digital technologies into Ayurvedic leech therapy (Jalaukavacharana) represents a promising paradigm shift in the standardization, safety, and global acceptance of this ancient biotherapeutic practice. While this manuscript highlights challenges specific to Indian practice, such as the lack of standardized operating procedures (SOPs), species misidentification, and inadequate regulatory oversight, these are not unique to India. Globally, leech therapy faces similar hurdles, even in countries where it is medically recognized.

For instance, countries like Russia, Germany, and Turkey have formally integrated leech therapy into mainstream medical systems, yet still encounter standardization challenges. Even informal leech clinics in the West, such as some Chinese therapy centers operating in mall settings, operate without medical supervision or adherence to safety standards, posing additional concerns.

These observations emphasize the universal need for a globally informed and locally adaptable framework for leech therapy regulation and implementation. Leveraging established international protocols, such as the U.S. FDA's classification of Hirudo medicinalis as a Class III medical device, or regulatory guidance issued by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the UK's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), provides valuable blueprints for aligning Indian practices with global norms.

In light of these insights, this manuscript proposes a comprehensive, forward-looking implementation strategy to modernize Jalaukavacharana without compromising its traditional roots. Key recommendations include.•The Ministry of AYUSH can take the lead in developing regulatory frameworks that formally recognize one or more validated species, such as Hirudo medicinalis, for standardized clinical use across Ayurveda and integrative practices in India. This would ensure both domestic safety and international compatibility and boost global credibility of Jalaukavacharana.•The development of AYUSH-approved Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) is essential to ensure consistency, safety, and credibility in the application of leech therapy across clinical settings. These SOPs should comprehensively cover all stages of the therapeutic process, beginning with hygiene protocols that ensure sanitized environments and practitioner safety. Clear guidelines for leech species authentication must also be included, utilizing both morphological and molecular techniques to ensure only validated species such as Hirudo medicinalis are used. Pre-therapy protocols should address patient eligibility screening, preparation of leeches, setup of monitoring systems, and proper documentation. During therapy, SOPs must outline standardized procedures for documentation leech placement, vital and activity monitoring, emergency response, and session timing. Post-therapy procedures should focus on wound care, leech disposal or reuse documentation, follow-up observation, and secure digital data entry. Together, these SOPs will form the foundation for a regulated, evidence-based approach that aligns with both traditional Ayurvedic principles and modern biomedical standards.•To strengthen clinical safety and accountability in leech therapy, the implementation of traceability systems such as RFID or QR code tagging is essential. These technologies allow each leech to be assigned a unique identifier, enabling real-time tracking of its usage history, session details, and disposal status. This ensures adherence to single-use protocols and prevents cross-contamination.•The launch of certified training programs for clinicians, rooted in both Ayurvedic principles and biomedical integration, is essential to ensure the safe, standardized, and effective practice of leech therapy. These programs should be designed to cater not only to Indian practitioners but also to a global audience, promoting international alignment and wider acceptance. The curriculum must cover areas such as species identification, hygiene protocols, therapy techniques, biosensor integration, emergency management, and regulatory compliance. By equipping clinicians with both traditional knowledge and modern clinical competencies, these globally accessible training modules will foster professional credibility, improve patient outcomes, and support the ethical and scientific advancement of Jalaukavacharana worldwide.•Multicentric pilot studies in collaboration with technology partners, aiming to evaluate feasibility, safety, and cost-effectiveness in rural and urban clinics;•Establishment of digital clinical registries for longitudinal outcome monitoring and data analytics.

Conclusion

The integration of real-time monitoring technologies into Ayurvedic leech therapy (Jalaukavacharana) offers a transformative path toward improving its safety, efficacy, and standardization. By enabling continuous monitoring of patient vitals, leech behavior, blood flow, and skin changes, these innovations directly address critical gaps in traditional practice and support a more objective, data-driven therapeutic approach.

This convergence of technology and tradition marks a new era in Ayurvedic bioengineering, where evidence-based methods enhance the credibility and clinical reliability of time-tested treatments. Implementing such systems can elevate leech therapy from a historically grounded intervention to a modern, globally acceptable biotherapeutic tool.

As Jalaukavacharana moves toward integration with 21st-century healthcare, preserving Ayurvedic principles while embracing technological advancements will be crucial. This evolution demands collaborative efforts across disciplines, involving Ayurvedic practitioners, biomedical engineers, technologists, regulatory bodies, and public health stakeholders.

In summary, modernizing leech therapy through digital innovation is both feasible and essential. It holds the potential to position Jalaukavacharana as a scientifically validated, internationally recognized component of integrative medicine, bridging ancient wisdom with contemporary healthcare demands.
