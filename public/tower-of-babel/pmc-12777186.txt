Exploring the intricate link between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the aging process: implications for age-related diseases
Authors: Hanin M. Abahussin, Maryam S. Alotaibi, Othman A. Alhazzaa, Aminah G. Alotaibi, Sarah M. Alsaab, Nora A. Aljawini, Abdullah O. Alawad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is a complex process marked by the gradual accumulation of impairments in molecules and tissues, leading to frailty and dysfunction. This decline is a significant risk factor for many debilitating conditions. Recently, gut microbiota dysbiosis has been identified as one of the hallmarks of aging. This review sheds light on the role of gut microbiota dysbiosis in accelerating aging and its relation to age-associated diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancer and diabetes. Emerging research demonstrates a strong link between the gut microbiome and the aging process, although the underlying mechanisms remain under investigation. Animal studies suggest that targeting the gut microbiome may offer a promising approach to mitigate aging and related diseases. However, further human studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Aging is a multifaceted, complex process characterized by the progressive accumulation of impairments to molecules and tissues over a lifetime, often leading to frailty and malfunction. This gradual deterioration is the greatest risk factor for most common debilitating conditions, making aging a logical target for developing interventions to prevent, mitigate or reverse various age-related diseases [1] such as cancer, neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases. These age-related ailments pose a prominent global socioeconomic burden and a pressing public health concern. The elderly population necessitates integrative treatment modalities to achieve treatment outcomes over time. Thus, identifying therapeutic interventions that promote ‘healthy aging’ (namely, the preservation of physiological and cognitive functions in advanced age, enabling self-sufficient performance of routine tasks among elderly) and concurrently impede the progression of age-related pathological conditions is of utmost priority. Given that aging underlies many chronic conditions, understanding the aging process will accelerate the identification of therapeutic targets for age-related diseases and the development of pharmacological agents suitable for clinical translation in the future.

In 2013, López-Otín C and colleagues explored the aging process in various organisms, describing nine tentative hallmarks: DNA instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic modifications, loss of proteostasis, deregulation of nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion and altered intercellular communication [2]. Since then, more than 300,000 articles within this realm have been published, which is as many as during the previous century. Later studies have confirmed these hallmarks in mammals, extending their relevance beyond non-mammalians and demonstrating their importance in understanding human age-related diseases [3]. López-Otín C and colleagues later updated these hallmarks and proposed three new ones: disabled macroautophagy, chronic inflammation and microbiota dysbiosis. Moreover, they extended the original hallmark of altered intercellular communication to distinctly address chronic inflammation and dysbiosis, proposing microbiota dysbiosis as a key determinant in the aging process due to its effect on health and disease [4].

Research suggests that a large number of microorganisms, approximately 1013 to 1014, inhabit the gut. Over time, the composition of the gut microbiota can change. When these changes are detrimental, it is known as dysbiosis. Dysbiosis refers to negative alterations in the composition and function of the gut microbiome and plays a significant role in the development of various chronic conditions. Several factors contribute to microbiota dysbiosis, including infections, inflammation, diet, exposure to chemicals, genetics, the environment and age. While there is considerable evidence of the complex relationship between gut microbiota and the aging process, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This underscores the importance of understanding the origins of microbiota dysbiosis, its regulatory mechanisms—both internal and external—and its subsequent effects. Such efforts may accelerate the discovery and development of effective interventions for multimorbidity and, eventually, enhance healthy human aging.

This review aims to consolidate the current understanding of gut microbiota dysbiosis as a hallmark of aging and its role in accelerating the aging process and its relation to aging-associated diseases. We examine recent insights into how the composition of the gut microbiome changes with aging and its impact on common age-related diseases such as neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancer and diabetes. These four diseases were selected due to their high prevalence among the elderly population and their multidimensional impact on various body systems. We also discuss the association of gut microbiota dysbiosis with other aging hallmarks, such as cellular senescence and stem cell exhaustion. Finally, we explore ongoing research on the potential therapeutic interventions targeting microbiota dysbiosis.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and aging/aging-associated diseases

It has been widely accepted that the gut microbiome undergoes changes with age. A systematic review summarizing these age-related alterations found that while the alpha diversity (the variation within a single age group) of the gut microbiota is high, beta diversity (the differences between age groups) is significantly different [5]. This indicates a strong correlation between the gut microbiome and the aging process.

The gut microbiota changes throughout life in response to the surrounding environment and internal host factors. For example, during the first three years of life, children’s diets shift significantly along with their gut microbiota composition. In infancy, breastfeeding and commercial formulas are the main sources of diet, leading to a gut microbiome rich in Actinobacteria, especially Bifidobacterium, due to human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) that promote their growth [6–8]. As solid foods are introduced, gut microbiome diversity increases, and the abundance of Actinobacteria decreases after weaning, allowing for a variety of microbial species with different enzymatic capabilities [9–11]. During puberty, the gut microbiota is strongly affected by sex associated hormones, and the beta diversity diverges significantly between the two genders [12]. For instance, Yuan et al. (2020) showed a positive correlation between the abundance of Clostridium genus and testosterone hormone in adolescent male serum (r = 0.500, p = 0.021), and the abundance of Roseburia genus and estradiol in adolescent female serum (r = 0.433, p = 0.0499). Demonstrating the effect of sex hormones on shaping the gut microbiota composition. Throughout adulthood, the gut microbiota is thought to remain relatively stable, with high alpha diversity [13]. During the elderly stage, typically defined as ages 65 and older, the gut microbiota begins to shift in composition due to various factors related to aging [14]. While normal variations in gut microbiota usually do not disrupt the host’s physiological functions or immune regulation, significant alterations can lead to negative consequences for the host. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is characterized by a reduction in microbial diversity, a depletion of beneficial bacteria, such as short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria, including pro-inflammatory bacteria [15]. These have all been reported as characteristics of aging gut microbiota in the elderly [16–18]. After conducting a comprehensive literature review of 25 studies examining gut microbial changes related to aging, Ghosh et al. (2022) compiled a list of microbiome alterations that consistently appear across multiple studies and are linked with aging. Changes in the microbiome associated with aging have been categorized into three groups; Group 1 includes Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Coprococcus, Eubacterium rectale, Bifidobacterium and Prevotella, all of which generally decrease with age, regardless of health status. This group consists of core SCFA producers and beneficial bacteria. Group 2 consists of Eggerthella, Bilophila, Desulfovibrio, Fusobacterium, Anaerotruncus, Streptococcus and Escherichia, which typically increase with age. This group is mainly comprised of pathobionts associated with heightened inflammation and the production of harmful metabolites, such as trimethylamine. Finally, group 3 includes Akkermansia, Christensenellaceae, Odoribacter, Butyricimonas, Butyrivibrio, Barnesiella and Oscillospira, which also tend to increase with age [19].

The increased microbiota dysbiosis seen with aging is thought to result from a complex interaction of various internal factors, including inflammation, immunosenescence, and stem cell depletion, as well as external factors, such as increased medication use, decline in physical activity, and dietary changes. For instance, stem cell exhaustion, one of the aging hallmarks [4], could contribute to microbiota dysbiosis. Intestinal stem cells (ISCs) play a vital role in maintaining the integrity of the gut barrier by continuously replenishing the intestinal lining throughout the host’s life [20]. However, aging has been linked to a decline in ISCs, likely due to epigenetic changes [21–23], which contributes to a compromised gut integrity and increases the risk of dysbiosis. Aging-associated gut microbial dysbiosis in old mice was reversed by restoring the ISC layers, which had decreased with age, through treatment with angiotensin-1–7 [24]. This demonstrates the effect of ISC maintenance, which declines with aging, on gut microbial composition.

Moreover, another hallmark of aging is inflammation [4]. Inflammation can increase gut permeability and predispose the host to microbiota dysbiosis, as many inflammatory cytokines have been shown to affect gut integrity. For instance, low concentrations of TNF-α increased the endocytic uptake of antigens in the intestinal epithelial cells, highlighting the effect of TNF-α on barrier permeability through the transcellular route [25]. TNF-α and IFN-γ have also been shown in vitro to induce morphological disruption of the tight junction proteins ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1 found in the intestinal epithelial barrier [26]. IL-4 and IL-6 cytokines have also been shown to increase epithelial permeability [27, 28]. Conversely, IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, has been shown to enhance epithelial barrier integrity [29–31]. Interestingly, Thevaranjan et al. (2017) demonstrated that reducing inflammation in vivo decreased age-related dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. Old mice that do not exhibit aging-related inflammation (TNF-deficient) showed no changes in age-related gut microbiota shifts, and showed no increase in circulating bacterial components or gut barrier permeability compared to young mice [32]. Moreover, decreasing inflammation, through anti-TNF therapy, decreased aging-associated gut microbiota changes [32]. The studies above, along with many others [33–36], demonstrate how inflammation can contribute to gut microbiota dysbiosis by impairing the gut barrier permeability. Of course, microbiota dysbiosis itself further exacerbates inflammation and disrupts the gut barrier.

In terms of external factors, increased medication use with aging can contribute to microbiota dysbiosis. For instance, the elderly population has an increased susceptibility to infections [37], likely due to the general immune decline associated with aging, which could lead to greater antibiotic use by elderly individuals, further contributing to microbiota dysbiosis. Studies across different countries, have shown that antibiotic prescribing rates increase with age [38–40]. With one study describing the prescription rate of oral antibiotics in ages 65 + to be 33% higher than in ages 40–64 years and 53% higher than in ages 20–39 years [39]. Of course, antibiotic medications are among the most potent agents that can significantly impact the gut microbiota. In addition to increased medication use, reduced physical activity and increased frailty associated with aging may negatively impact the composition of the gut microbiota, potentially leading to dysbiosis. Physical activity has been shown in mice to elevate beneficial gut microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and to reduce inflammation [41, 42]. For example, germ-free mice that received microbiota transplants from exercise-trained mice (voluntary wheel training for six weeks) exhibited a stronger resistance to induced colon inflammation compared to germ-free mice that received microbiota transplants from sedentary mice [42]. These findings underscore the influence of physical activity on the gut microbiota, ultimately providing health benefits to the host. As physical activity declines with age, it may adversely affect gut microbiota composition, potentially leading to dysbiosis; however, human studies on this topic are limited.

The above examples illustrate how aging can cause dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. On the other hand, gut microbial dysbiosis contributes to the aging process, as demonstrated by numerous in vivo studies (Table 1). Gut microbiota dysbiosis leads to heightened inflammation, cellular senescence, and stem cell exhaustion and can drive aging-related diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, cancer and type 2 diabetes, (Fig. 1), as discussed in the sections below. Together, aging and gut microbiota dysbiosis appear to be bidirectionally linked, with each process capable of exacerbating the other. However, current evidence does not clearly resolve the question of whether dysbiosis initiates age-related decline or primarily amplifies pre-existing, age-driven changes. Longitudinal and interventional studies will be required to determine whether dysbiosis acts mainly as a driver, an accelerator, or a consequence of aging.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and neurodegenerative diseases

Neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Huntington’s disease, are the most common diseases among the elderly population. According to the World Health Organisation, AD and other forms of dementia rank as the eighth leading cause of mortality in 75 + age groups worldwide [43]. Neurodegenerative diseases impose a significant socioeconomic burden and are considered to be one of the most morbid aging-associated diseases. Until now, treatments primarily focus on slowing the progression of symptoms and providing palliative care. The etiopathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases is not well understood but is thought to be influenced by a mixture of genetics, lifestyle and, more recently, gut microbiota composition. This section will mainly focus on AD and PD, as they are the most common neurodegenerative diseases [44, 45].

The gut microbiota composition in neurodegenerative disease patients has been found to be distinct from that of healthy cohorts. For instance, studies on patients with AD demonstrated a notable reduction in microbial diversity [46, 47], accompanied by an altered prevalence of specific bacterial taxa in comparison to healthy controls [46–49]. This was also reported in PD studies [50]. Notably, the models that distinguished between predominant microbiota demonstrated high discriminative abilities and effectively differentiated between AD patients and healthy control subjects (AUC = 0.940) [46], indicating the extent of gut microbiota differences between AD patients and healthy individuals. Furthermore, these microbiota changes have been documented to manifest prior to the appearance of clinical symptoms in AD [51] and PD [52], suggesting the potential role of the gut microbiota as preclinical indicators of both AD and PD. This also highlights the significant potential of the gut microbiota as early biomarkers of neurodegenerative diseases.

The gut microbiota influences neurodegenerative diseases through their expressed proteins, toxins as well as produced metabolites and neurotransmitters [53]. For example, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), found in gram-negative bacteria, has been linked to inflammation, protein misfolding and the disruption of both the gut barrier and blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, all of which are indicative of poor prognosis in neurodegenerative diseases [54, 55]. Gram-negative bacteria have been reported to be prevalent in AD [47, 48] and in PD [56] patients compared to healthy controls. Cattaneo et al., 2018, found Escherichia/Shigella to be 12.8 times more abundant in patients with brain amyloidosis than in healthy controls (Fold Change = + 12.8, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the abundance of Escherichia/Shigella was strongly positively correlated with higher levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, NLRP3, and CXCL2 (rho = 0.60, p < 0.001; rho = 0.57, p < 0.001; and rho = 0.30, p = 0.007, respectively) in amyloid-positive patients [48], demonstrating the strong association of pro-inflammatory gut bacteria and inflammation. Moreover, the LPS component in gram-negative bacteria has been demonstrated by Zhou et al. (2019), to increase the activation of microglial cells and cause neuronal cell death. Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) is known to promote microglial activation, while the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) induces anti-inflammatory responses and regulates the activation of microglia [57]. While the expression of both microglial receptors is elevated in AD mice models compared to wild-type mice, treatment with LPS significantly upregulated and downregulated the expression of TLR4 and TREM2, respectively [57]. The reduced expression of the negative regulator TREM2, resulted in the overactivation of microglia and increased neuronal cell apoptosis in the cortex of AD mouse models [57]. The association of LPS and inflammation in relation to AD and PD have also been shown in other animal and human studies [58–61].

SCFA, such as butyrate, acetate and propionate, are metabolites produced by gut bacteria through the enzymatic degradation of carbohydrates and polysaccharides [55]. Contrary to LPS, SCFA metabolites have been associated with favorable prognosis in AD and PD. For instance, Xie et al., 2023, demonstrated that germ-free mice and antibiotic-treated mice exhibited an increased BBB and blood-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) barrier permeability. This increased permeability was restored through butyrate and propionate supplementation or recolonization of normal bacteria through ecal microbiota transplant (FMT) [62]. The research team found that SCFA restored the BBB and blood-CSF barrier by improving the localization of tight junctions [62]. This restoration subsequently reduced the b-amyloid load in the brains of AD mice models as well as increased the microglial internalization of b-amyloid particles [62]. Furthermore, in another study on rhesus monkeys, antibiotic administration was correlated with decreased levels of the SCFAs acetate, propionate and butyrate, along with increased BBB permeability [63]. Through 16 S rRNA sequencing, the research team confirmed that this change was positively correlated with the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria [63]. Many other studies have also demonstrated the role of SCFA in maintaining the BBB integrity [64, 65]. Notably, it is known that SCFA levels decrease with aging [19, 66]. Moreover, the prevalence of SCFA and SCFA-producing bacteria is also decreased in AD [48] and PD [50, 56, 67] patients compared to healthy individuals. Furthermore, the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria has been shown to decrease in postmenopausal women compared to premenopausal women [68, 69]. This reduction may be linked to the higher observed prevalence of AD in women [70]. This highlights menopause as a risk factor for neurological diseases in women, particularly through its impact on the gut microbiota.

Metabolic syndrome

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and cardiovascular diseases

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is closely associated with the aging process, contributing substantially to illness and death among older individuals. With the global population steadily aging, the incidence of CVD is on the rise, presenting significant obstacles to achieving healthy aging. The relationship between biological aging and CVD is complex, characterized by cellular and molecular alterations that amplify cardiovascular risk factors [71]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis has been associated with CVD through pathways including inflammation and the disruption of lipid metabolism [72, 73]. Metabolites generated by gut bacteria, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and SCFAs, play a significant role in regulating cardiovascular health [73, 74]. Furthermore, recent studies underscore the influence of gut microbiota imbalances in the development of CVD, shedding light on novel therapeutic approaches [71].

SCFAs exhibit multiple beneficial effects on cardiovascular health. They help regulate inflammation by promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines and maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, which is critical for preventing systemic inflammation. SCFAs also play a role in lipid metabolism, blood pressure regulation and myocardial repair [75, 76].

For example, recent research demonstrated that propionate, generally produced by Faecalibacterium and Roseburia, supplementation reduced low-density lipoprotein (LDL), cholesterol and total cholesterol in patients with hypercholesterolemia, likely due to its ability to modulate gut immunity and regulate cholesterol absorption [75, 77]. Moreover, a growing body of evidence supports the idea that SCFAs can positively influence heart function by enhancing myocardial contractility and promoting vascular reactivity [76, 78].

One of the most thoroughly studied metabolites in the context of microbiota dysbiosis and cardiovascular disease is TMAO. It is generated when gut bacteria, including those from the Firmicutes (e.g., Clostridia and Lactobacillus), Proteobacteria (e.g., Escherichia and Klebsiella) and Actinobacteria (e.g., Coriobacteriaceae), metabolize dietary compounds such as phosphatidylcholine and L-carnitine into trimethylamine (TMA). TMAO has been implicated in various cardiometabolic conditions, including atherosclerosis, thrombosis and myocardial fibrosis. TMAO levels are influenced by diet, particularly diets high in red meat, which elevate TMAO production. This metabolite enhances cholesterol deposition in arterial walls and promotes inflammation, thereby accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis [75, 77]. As individuals age, there is a notable increase in plasma TMAO levels, raising cardiovascular disease risk, primarily influenced by diet and gut microbiota composition. Gut microbes convert dietary precursors, mainly from animal-based foods, into TMA, later oxidized to TMAO in the liver. Certain foods promote TMA-producing bacteria, with this effect being more significant in individuals over 65, highlighting the elderly’s heightened dietary sensitivity to TMAO production [79].

Studies have consistently shown that elevated TMAO levels are associated with increased risk for major cardiovascular events, including heart failure and stroke [77]. A systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the association of gut microbiota metabolites with major adverse cardiovascular events. The study concluded that increased levels of TMOA (pooled relative risk = 1.62; 95% CI: 1.45–1.80) and its precursors, L-carnitine, betaine, or choline (relative risk = 1.3 to 1.4) were associated with elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [75]. Therefore, targeting the enzymes responsible for TMA production in gut bacteria presents an attractive therapeutic option. Small-molecule inhibitors of these enzymes have demonstrated efficacy in reducing disease progression in preclinical models, without the widespread microbial disruption seen with broad-spectrum antibiotics [77].

Another metabolite that has garnered attention is phenylacetylglutamine (PAG). Elevated levels of PAG are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, especially in patients with diabetes and metabolic syndrome. PAG contributes to disease progression by promoting endothelial dysfunction and inflammation. Studies in animal models, using genetically engineered gut microbiota, have provided valuable insights into how PAG production pathways influence cardiovascular health [80]. Increased prostaglandin (PAG) levels observed in the elderly are largely linked to age-associated alterations in physiological processes, with a particular emphasis on the immune and vascular systems. These changes include increased expression of cyclooxygenase (COX)−2, modifications in sympathetic nervous system activity and impaired vascular function [81]. Recent investigations have explored the potential for modulating PAG pathways as a therapeutic strategy, given the distinct microbial enzymes involved in its production. These insights are helping to identify targeted interventions that could reduce cardiovascular risk by altering specific microbial functions [80].

Diet plays a crucial role in shaping the gut microbiota and, by extension, cardiovascular health. For instance, a diet rich in dietary fibres promotes the production of beneficial SCFAs, while a diet high in red meat increases TMAO levels, exacerbating cardiovascular diseases [77, 82].

Chambers et al. (2019) observed that supplementation with both inulin and inulin-propionate ester (IPE) resulted in a reduction in bacterial diversity when compared to cellulose. This reduction was not linked to changes in species richness but rather to alterations in evenness, particularly with inulin supplementation. Distinct shifts in bacterial populations were noted. Inulin supplementation promoted the growth of bacteria such as Anaerostipes hadrus, Bifidobacterium faecale and Bacteroides caccae, while reducing the abundance of Blautia obeum and Ruminococcus faecis. Similarly, IPE supplementation influenced specific bacterial species, increasing Bacteroides uniformis and decreasing Blautia obeum and Eubacterium ruminantium. Research shows that switching to a plant-based or Mediterranean diet reduces TMAO production and improves lipid profiles, ultimately lowering the risk of cardiovascular events [77].

In addition to TMAO, the microbial metabolite imidazole propionate produced from dietary histidine, has been linked to impaired glucose metabolism and heart failure. This metabolite activates specific signaling pathways, including the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, which is implicated in the pathogenesis of heart failure [80].

The gut-heart axis represents a promising frontier for therapeutic interventions targeting cardiovascular diseases. By modulating specific microbial pathways and reducing harmful metabolites such as TMAO and PAG, it may be possible to develop targeted treatments that offer cardiovascular protection [77, 80] such approaches focus on selectively inhibiting harmful microbial processes, which could provide cardiovascular benefits without the broader disruptions associated with antibiotics [78].

Recent advances in therapeutic strategies targeting the gut microbiota in cardiovascular diseases extend beyond traditional approaches, such as TMAO enzyme inhibitors. Emerging interventions include specific probiotic strains (e.g. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) and prebiotic fibres that are being developed to restore microbial balance and reduce cardiovascular risk by enhancing beneficial metabolites, such as SCFAs, and suppressing harmful metabolites, such as TMAO. Importantly, clinical trials have shown that targeted probiotic supplementation improves lipid profiles and reduces systemic inflammation associated with CVD [83]. Moreover, FMT and engineered microbial consortia have demonstrated potential in modulating gut microbial composition to improve cardiovascular outcomes by restoring a healthy microbiome [84]. In addition, engineered microbial consortia designed to deliver specific metabolic functions or inhibit pathogenic pathways represent a novel precision medicine approach for reducing CVD risk [85]. Beyond this, TMAO and PAG, inhibitors targeting microbial metabolites such as imidazole propionate (ImP), which activate the mTOR pathway implicated in heart failure, are being explored.

ImP has been associated with various health conditions, including heart failure, through its impact on the mTOR pathway. The mTOR signalling pathway plays a crucial role in numerous cellular processes relevant to cardiac health, including growth, proliferation, and survival [86]. Research has shown that elevated ImP levels are associated with heart failure and can serve as independent predictors of mortality. A study involving clinical cohorts from Europe and North America found that higher ImP serum levels correlated with reduced ejection fraction and heart failure severity, even after accounting for traditional risk factors, implicating ImP in the progression of cardiac dysfunction [86, 87].

In vitro studies have demonstrated that ImP can influence cellular activities related to heart failure. These studies showed that ImP negatively affects cardiomyocyte function through pathways that can be modulated by the mTOR signalling network. Furthermore, the involvement of the mTOR pathway in autophagy and cellular metabolism suggests that it could potentially be targeted for therapeutic interventions in heart failure [88, 89]. The role of the mTOR pathway in heart disease has been extensively explored, with inhibitors such as rapamycin showing promise for reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and promoting autophagy. This modulation is crucial because it helps maintain cellular homeostasis and prevents pathological remodelling of heart tissue, which is often observed in heart failure [90]. Additionally, studies involving other mTOR inhibitors have underscored the importance of balancing the activity of this pathway to mitigate myocardial damage [91, 92]. In an in vivo experiment conducted on a diabetic rat model, the study showed that modulation of the mTOR pathway protected against heart failure by reducing oxidative stress and improving cardiac function [93]. Subsequently, both in vivo and in vitro studies have elucidated the critical role of the mTOR pathway in heart failure, with microbial metabolites, such as ImP, playing key roles in disease progression. Targeting this pathway with specific inhibitors represents a potential therapeutic avenue for treating heart disease associated with microbial imbalances [94].

Similarly, SCFA mimetics and analogues are under development to harness the cardioprotective effects of these metabolites. Furthermore, immunomodulatory biologics influenced by gut microbiota interactions offer additional avenues for reducing cardiovascular inflammation [95]. Therapies targeting microbe-induced immune and neurogenic pathways are emerging as potential strategies to mitigate the cardiovascular risks associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis. In hypertension, a dysfunctional autonomic nervous system–microbiome circuit has been proposed as a therapeutic target, highlighting the potential of modulating sympathetic activity and gut microbial composition to control blood pressure [96]. Microbiota dysbiosis-driven systemic inflammation and immune activation further contribute to CVD progression, suggesting that interventions targeting microbiota–immune interactions may reduce cardiovascular risk [97].

The interplay between sex, aging, and gut microbiota composition significantly influences cardiovascular disease risk; however, it remains relatively underexplored. Males and females exhibit distinct gut microbiota profiles influenced by sex hormones, such as oestrogen and testosterone, which affect microbial diversity and metabolite production. Specifically, oestrogen has been shown to promote beneficial SCFA-producing bacteria and suppress TMA-producing species, potentially explaining sex-specific differences in cardiovascular risk and response to microbial-targeted therapies [98]. Furthermore, fluctuations in sex hormones during aging, such as menopause in females and andropause in males, alter the gut microbiota composition and metabolite profiles, including TMAO, SCFAs, and PAG. Consequently, these hormonal changes may modulate cardiovascular inflammation and lipid metabolism differently in men and women, affecting disease progression and therapeutic efficacy [99].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by higher blood sugar levels due to a combination of insulin resistance and insufficient production of insulin [100]. Emerging research has strongly linked gut microbiota dysbiosis to the development and progression of several metabolic disorders, including T2D [100]. This imbalance in the gut microbiome significantly contributes to insulin resistance and the chronic inflammation observed in T2D.

A healthy gut microbiota plays a key role in maintaining metabolic health by digesting dietary plant fibers in the intestine, converting them into SCFAs. As mentioned above SCFAs are important for regulating inflammation, improving insulin responses and maintaining the intestinal barrier. Disruption in SCFA production level due to microbiota dysbiosis is associated with the progression of diabetes, as it impairs the ability of SCFAs to suppress pro-inflammatory responses. This disruption leads to higher cytokine secretion and systemic inflammation, exacerbating metabolic dysfunction [101, 102].

Highlighting the role of gut microbiota in T2D, Bäckhed and his team investigated the microbiome changes in 145 European women with normal, impaired and diabetic glucose control. The study revealed distinct changes in gut microbiome composition among individuals with normal glucose tolerance (NGT), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and T2D. Specifically, T2D patients showed a marked reduction in beneficial butyrate-producing bacteria such as Faecalibacterium sp. Interestingly, these reductions were also observed in prediabetic individuals, suggesting that gut microbiota alterations may precede T2D onset and play an early role in its progression [103, 104]. The loss of butyrate-producing bacteria contributes to increased systemic inflammation, greater gut permeability and heightened insulin resistance, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates T2D progression [103, 104]. In contrast to beneficial species, the gut microbiota of T2D patients often exhibits an increase in pathogenic and opportunistic gram-negative bacteria. These alterations are strongly correlated with chronic inflammation, a key driver of diabetes progression and onset [102].

Metformin, a first-line treatment for T2D, is well-known for its ability to reduce glucose production and enhance insulin sensitivity. However, recent studies indicate that its therapeutic effects extend to modulating gut microbiota. Metformin has been shown to reduce the abundance of pro-inflammatory species such as Clostridium bartlettii, which correlates with improved insulin sensitivity and reduced inflammation [102]. Further supporting the role of gut microbiota in T2D management, a study on tributyrin (Tb), a prodrug of butyrate, demonstrated promising results in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice. Tb treatment significantly improved insulin sensitivity and reduced systemic inflammation. Showing its effect in decreasing the levels of inflammatory cytokines and reducing macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue, highlighting the anti-inflammatory potential of butyrate-based interventions [105].

Population studies have shown that the prevalence of T2D is higher in males than in females, and that males are diagnosed with T2D at a lower body mass index [106]. Moreover, studies have shown that the gut microbiome composition varies between human males and females [107]. Consistent with observations in humans, female mice possess a more beneficial gut microbiome [108]. Specifically, the abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila, a bacterium reported to have antidiabetic effects, was lower in male mice than in females, whereas Prevotella, a bacterium reported to induce insulin resistance, was more abundant in males [107, 109, 110]. To confirm the role of sex hormones on the gut microbiome, Gao et al. (2021) conducted a depletion experiment using antibiotics to deplete the gut bacteria in HFD-fed mice, thereby eliminating sex-related influences. As a result, the metabolomic profiles characteristic of females disappeared. When gut microbiota from male mice was transferred into female recipients, the recipients developed increased insulin resistance, resembling the male metabolic phenotype [107]. To investigate the factors shaping these sex-specific gut microbiota differences, the researchers examined the role of hormones. In early life (at 3 weeks of age), male and female mice had nearly identical gut microbiomes. However, by 8 weeks of age, distinct sex-related differences in the gut microbiota emerged. Further experiments showed that androgen administration in normal male mice exacerbated glucose intolerance. Conversely, castration, which removes the primary source of male hormones, shifted the microbiota toward a “female-like” composition and glucose sensitivity [107]. When these castrated mice were treated with dihydrotestosterone (DHT), their microbiome reverted to a “male-like” profile. Importantly, depleting the gut microbiome largely eliminated the effects of androgens on glucose metabolism, suggesting that these hormones exert their influence primarily through microbial mediation [107]. The typical male-associated microbiome was also linked to changes in certain amino acid pathways, particularly in the glutamine/glutamate ratio, highlighting a metabolic signature associated with androgen-driven microbial profiles [107, 111].

The modulation of gut microbiota using probiotics is emerging as a potential therapeutic avenue for T2D. Specific strains, such as Bifidobacterium animalis ssp. lactis GCL2505 (BlaG), are under investigation for their effect on metabolic syndrome such as diabetes. BlaG has shown a strong correlation with increased levels of beneficial metabolites like acetate in the caecum and plasma (r = 0.54 and 0.76, respectively, all P < 0.001) and reduced levels of visceral fat (r = − 0.31, P < 0.05), underlining its potential in managing diabetes and related metabolic syndromes [112]. Consistent with these findings, Su et al. (2022) reported a strong negative correlation (r < −0.4) between Bifidobacterium and different T2D biochemical indices such as HbA1c, LDL and BMI [113].

In summary, the interplay between gut microbiota and T2D underscores its critical role in metabolic health. Targeting modulation of gut microbiota using probiotics and therapeutics such as metformin and butyrate derivatives represents a promising approach to help mitigate inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity and manage T2D progression. Continued research into these mechanisms could open new avenues for more effective diabetes treatments.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and cancer

Recent studies have established a strong link between microbiota dysbiosis and cancer, particularly gastrointestinal cancers. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most extensively studied cancers in relation to microbiota dysbiosis. The gut microbiota in CRC patients shows a significant decrease in beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium and an increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli, Bacteroides fragilis and Fusobacterium nucleatum [114]. Specific dysbiosis patterns, such as increased levels of E. coli, have been correlated with cancer progression and altered patient outcomes. Studies have shown that elevated E. coli levels can promote inflammation and DNA damage, potentially driving tumorigenesis in the colon [115]. Multi-cohort analyses have identified E. coli as one of the universal bacterial markers in CRC, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic or prognostic indicator [114]. Moreover, F. nucleatum has also been found to be significantly associated with CRC. A meta-analysis of a total of 13 studies concluded that a high abundance of F. nucleatum was correlated with low overall survival of CRC patients (hazard ratio = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.37–1.88, P < 0.0001) [116]. These results indicate that F. nucleatum may play a substantial role in predicting the survival rates in CRC patients [116], highlighting F. nucleatum’s potential as a therapeutic target in CRC management. Additionally, the liver is another organ highly influenced by the gut microbiota through the gut-liver axis. Microbiota dysbiosis has been shown to contribute to liver diseases, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), by promoting liver inflammation and fibrosis. Overgrowth of Gram-negative bacteria in the gut can lead to increased levels of LPS, which act as a potent pro-inflammatory molecule that triggers chronic liver inflammation and may contribute to liver cancer development [117]. Furthermore, although less studied, there is evidence to suggest that the gut microbiota may influence breast cancer risk through its effects on estrogen metabolism. Goedert et al.l. (2015) found that, microbiota dysbiosis can affect the enterohepatic circulation of estrogens, potentially leading to higher circulating estrogen levels, which is a known risk factor for breast cancer [118].

Microbiota dysbiosis is thought to contribute to carcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including inflammation, bacterial metabolites, and alterations in host metabolism and immune responses [119]. Chronic inflammation induced by microbiota dysbiosis can lead to DNA damage and mutations, thereby increasing cancer risk. For instance, F. nucleatum have been associated with cancer due to their ability to invade epithelial cells, trigger inflammatory responses and promote tumorigenesis. F. nucleatum has been shown to selectively expand populations of myeloid-derived immune cells in tumors, particularly Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells MDSCs, which are known for their immune-suppressive functions [120]. Elevated MDSC levels can inhibit T cell activation and proliferation, thereby suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [120]. In vitro study revealed that co-culturing the bacterium with human colonic epithelial cells resulted in increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines that compromised the barrier function and further amplified the response by recruiting and activating additional immune cells [121]. In vivo study also revealed that F. nucleatum induced tumorigenesis in cancer mice models [120]. The breakdown of the intestinal barrier triggers the invasion of pathogenic microorganisms and their metabolites from the intestinal lumen, leading to contact between intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and components of the microbiota that might have pro-tumorigenic effects [122, 123].

Furthermore, microbiota dysbiosis can lead to the production of metabolites with carcinogenic properties. For example, particular bacterial species such as Bacteroides fragilis, Clostridium perfringens, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium convert primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, which have been linked to DNA damage and increased risk of colon cancer [122]. Research indicates that certain bacterial populations, particularly those associated with high levels of secondary bile acids, can increase with age [119]. TMAO elevation, associated with microbiota dysbiosis, has also been linked to cancer. Bae et al., (2014) showed that, TMAO was strongly positively associated with CRC risk (Odds Ratio = 3.38; 95% CI: 1.25–9.16; P trend = 0.02) in age-adjusted analyses from 835 matched case-control female pairs [124]. Additionally, colibactin, produced by E. coli, can directly damage DNA and promote mutations that cause cancer [125]. These examples illustrate how harmful gut microbiota metabolites, which rise with microbiota dysbiosis, contribute to cancer development.

Moreover, the gut microbiota composition can influence the response to cancer therapy. Gopalakrishnan et al. (2018) highlighted that modulating the gut microbiota can affect responses to cancer therapy. The study demonstrated that modulating the gut microbiota significantly influences responses to anti–PD-1 immunotherapy in melanoma patients. The responders had a more diverse microbiota, enriched with beneficial genera such as Bacteroidetes and Faecalibacterium [126]. Moreover, FMT from responders enhanced anti-tumor immune responses in germ-free mice, linking specific bacterial populations to improved therapeutic efficacy and providing insights into the microbiome’s role in immunotherapy [126]. Emerging strategies involve interventions like probiotics and prebiotics to enhance the gut microbiome’s diversity and composition, which can positively influence immune responses and improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. In vivo study demonstrated that mice receiving dietary supplementation with these probiotics showed improved responses to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, evidenced by increased CD8 + T-cell activity and enhanced tumour control [127]. These approaches aim to augment anti-tumour immunity by promoting beneficial bacterial populations and their metabolites.

Given the significant link between microbiota dysbiosis and cancer, targeting the microbiota with therapeutic strategies holds great promise. Probiotics, prebiotics, FMT and dietary modifications are all potential strategies to restore a healthy microbiota and reduce cancer risk [122]. Additionally, understanding specific microbial signatures associated with different cancers could lead to the development of novel diagnostic tools and personalized therapies.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis association/integration with other aging hallmarks

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is triggered in response to various internal and external stimuli, including DNA damage, telomere attrition, oxidative stress, oncogenic activation, mitochondrial dysfunction and radiation [128]. The primary pathways mediating cellular senescence are p53/p21 and p16INK4A/pRB, both lead to irreversible cell cycle arrest when activated [129]. Senescent cells accumulate progressively with age in multiple tissues, contributing to various age-related diseases [130]. Many factors, including the microbiome and cellular senescence, have been identified as drives of the aging process [4]. However, the interplay between these two phenomena in aging has recently garnered interest.

A recent study investigated the relationship between microbiota and cellular senescence using p16INK4a reporter mice housed in two distinct environments: specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, with normal commensal microbiota but devoid of selected pathogens and germ-free conditions, lacking any microbial colonization. The expression of p16INK4a, a senescence marker, significantly increased with age in SPF mice, but not in germ-free mice. Notably, aged germ-free mice exhibited p16INK4a expression levels comparable to their younger counterparts, suggesting that the constant presence of normal gut bacteria gradually triggers cellular senescence. Additionally, intestinal IgA-producing B cells underwent cellular senescence with age in SPF mice but not in germ-free mice, resulting in decreased IgA production and diversity in the gut, promoting changes in the gut microbiota. LPS, abundant in aged SPF mice, is a potential mediator of microbiota-induced cellular senescence [131].

When intestinal permeability increases with microbiota dysbiosis, LPS can enter the bloodstream [132]. Microbiota dysbiosis is associated with increased intestinal permeability [32] and increased blood levels of LPS [133, 134]. A growing body of evidence suggests that LPS can induce senescence. For instance, human lung alveolar epithelial cells exposed to LPS exhibit growth arrest and senescence-associated characteristics, including increased activity of the senescence marker b-galactosidase (SA-β-gal), increased lysosomal content and shortened telomeres [135]. Similarly, human dental pulp stem cells showed increased SA-β-gal activity and upregulated expression of p53 and p21 following LPS treatment [136].

Moreover, metabolites produced by the gut microbiome have been recognized to influence the interplay between the microbiome and cellular senescence. For example, Yoshimoto et al. demonstrated that microbial dysbiosis, characterized by an increased Gram-positive bacteria population, elevates deoxycholic acid (DCA) levels leading to senescence induction in mice hepatic stellate cells and promoting liver cancer development [137].

Moreover, colibactin, a polyketide peptide metabolite produced by certain Escherichia coli harboring the pks genomic island (pks+ E. coli), which encodes polyketide synthase enzymes responsible for its synthesis [138]. Colibactin is known to cause DNA damage and is associated with the development of colorectal cancer [139]. Several studies reported the presence of pks+ E. coli in healthy humans, ranging between 20% and 45% [140–143]. Studies showed that Infecting human cells with pks+ E. coli causes DNA double-strand breaks and the activation of the DNA damage response pathway, resulting in DNA damage-induced senescence. This is accompanied by typical signatures of cellular senescence, such as increased expression levels of γH2A.x, p21 and SA-β-gal [125, 144, 145]. Butyrate is another bacterial metabolite found to cause cellular senescence. Porphyromonas asaccharolytica and Porphyromonas gingivalis induced cellular senescence in human fibroblasts and intestinal epithelial through butyrate secretion [146]. Furthermore, Ke and colleagues showed an age-dependent increase in circulating levels of TMAO in both humans and mice. Long-term treatment of TMAO in mice caused vascular aging, characterized by vascular dysfunction and increased expression levels of senescence markers p53, p21 and SA-β-gal [147].

It is noteworthy that certain bacterial strains are beneficial for the host and exhibit an anti-senescence effect. Administration of Lactobacillus brevis suppressed the expression of inflammatory and senescence markers, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF, p16 and p53 within the colon of aged mice [148]. In another study, the secretory metabolites produced by Lactobacillus fermentum attenuated the stress-induced senescence in murine preadipocytes [149]. Consequently, microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by the depletion of these strains, may contribute to cellular senescence.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and stem cell exhaustion

The stem cell’s primary role is to replenish dead cells in the tissue in order to maintain normal tissue and organ function [150]. Stem cell exhaustion is considered one of the aging hallmarks, as depletion of stem cells was reported to be associated with the aging process [4]. Various factors influence aging of stem cells, including inflammation, the local immune system, systemic and microenvironmental factors and recently gained interest, the host gut microbiome [151]. This growing interest in the relationship between gut microbiota and stem cell dynamics emerged after studies suggested the association of the gut microbiota with stem cell depletion and or rejuvenation. For instance, a study conducted on mice demonstrated that aged hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) were rejuvenated after transplanting fecal microbiota from young mice to aged mice [152]. Through 16 S rRNA sequencing, liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry, the research team found that Lachnospiraceae bacteria and tryptophan-associated metabolites were enriched in aged mice that received FMT from young mice [152]. Furthermore, colonizing mice with Lachnospiraceae or supplementation with tryptophan-associated metabolites resulted in increased characteristics of HSC rejuvenation [152]. Of note, Lachnospiraceae bacterial abundance [5, 153] as well as tryptophan and indole concentrations [154] has been reported to decline in older human populations with aging. Many other studies have also reported gut microbiota association with stem cell function [155, 156], further indicating the interplay between gut microbiome composition and stem cell function.

The mechanisms by which gut microbiome dysbiosis leads to stem cell depletion or exhaustion are still under investigation, but can be categorized as direct and indirect effects. For instance, butyrate metabolite can directly suppress the ISC proliferation by inhibiting Histone deacetylase enzymes (HDAC), leading to epigenetic changes and altered expression of different genes [157]. One significant epigenetic change involves increased expression of the negative cell cycle regulator Forkhead box O3 (Foxo3), ultimately leading to decreased proliferation of ISC. However, in healthy intestines, the ISCs, which are located deep down in the crypt pockets, are not generally exposed to the gut microbiota metabolite butyrate. It is only during damage to the mucosal lining, a common characteristic of microbiota dysbiosis, that ISCs come into contact with this metabolite. This has been proved by the study team as increasing the butyrate concentration in the mouse colon under healthy hemostatic conditions does not affect ISC proliferation. However, when mucosal injury is induced, ISC proliferation is suppressed due to the exposure to butyrate [157]. The inability of ISCs to replenish dead intestinal cells during a mucosal injury in the context of dysbiosis contributes to the ongoing deterioration of gut barrier integrity. This condition allows pathogenic bacteria and harmful metabolites to pass through the gut barrier, leading to inflammation and various diseases, as mentioned above.

Another way that microbiota dysbiosis can cause stem cell depletion is through the indirect continues damaging of the tissue through inflammation or apoptosis. This damage prompts stem cells to overreplicate in an attempt to compensate for lost cells, which eventually results in stem cell exhaustion and depletion [151]. Additionally, it is well established that certain inflammatory cytokines can impact the self-replication of HSCs and skew their differentiation. Since microbiota dysbiosis is associated with increased inflammation, imbalances in the gut microbiota can influence different cytokine production. This, in turn, affects bone marrow homeostasis and alters the differentiation pathways of HSCs [158].

Interventions targeting the gut microbiome for aging and aging-associated diseases

FMT involves transferring fecal microbiota from a donor to a recipient to restore the recipient’s normal gut microbiota composition to address specific health conditions caused by gut microbiota dysbiosis. As of this writing, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [159] and the United Kingdom’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [160] have approved FMT for the treatment of recurrent and refractory Clostridium difficile infections (CDI). However, various in vivo studies have indicated that FMT may also have the potential to treat a range of other diseases, including aging-associated diseases (Table 1).

FMT has been shown to improve outcomes for various conditions, including AD, PD, T2D, cancer and cardiovascular function as well as reverse aging in mice. For example, Parker et al. (2022) demonstrated that FMT from old to young mice increased nervous system inflammation, cytokine signalling, compromised barrier permeability and caused retinal defects. However, these aging symptoms were reversed by administering FMT from young mice [161]. Similarly, Spychala et al. (2017) found that transplanting gut microbiota from young mice to aged mice reduced inflammation and was associated with positive outcomes and increased survival after exposing the mice to ischemic stroke, compared to the control group. Conversely, FMT from aged mice to young mice led to increased inflammation and a higher susceptibility to death following their exposure to ischemic stroke [162]. These two studies, along with many other in vivo FMT research between young and aged mice (Table 1), further highlight the link between the aged gut microbiome and the acceleration of aging and aging-associated diseases, while also indicating the potential therapeutic benefits of FMT in reversing aspects of aging. This connection holds true for aging-specific diseases such as PD and T2D. For instance, transferring fecal matter from healthy mice to PD model mice resulted in improved motor function, reduced central nervous inflammation and restored the BBB permeability [163]. Moreover, FMT from healthy mice to T2D model mice significantly ameliorated insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism [164].

In regard to the effects of FMT treatment on age-related diseases in humans, several positive outcomes have been reported. In a case report of an 82-year-old patient who suffered from AD and recurrent CDI, the patient received a single FMT infusion after antibiotic treatment failed to resolve the CDI [165]. Surprisingly, not only did the CDI resolve, but the patient’s AD symptoms also improved significantly [165]. Before the FMT procedure, the patient exhibited dementia symptoms such as disorientation and memory impairment, scoring 20 on the Mini-mental state examination (MMSE), which indicates mild cognitive impairment. However, six months after FMT treatment for CDI, the patient reported improvement in dementia symptoms and scored 29 in the MMSE examination, reflecting normal cognition [165]. Other reports have documented similar cases of elderly AD patients who had CDI and experienced improvements in both conditions after undergoing FMT treatment, even though FMT was primarily indicated for CDI [166, 167]. In addition, FMT has shown positive outcomes in patients with metabolic syndromes. A study compared two groups of T2D patients; the first group followed a diet, while the second group adhered to the same diet in addition to receiving FMT from healthy, nondiabetic donors [113]. Both groups experienced health benefits, but the FMT group exhibited more accelerated weight loss. Moreover, 16 s rRNA sequencing revealed that beneficial bacteria appeared faster in the diet and FMT group compared to the diet-only group, highlighting the synergistic effect of FMT and diet on T2D patients [113].

The above animal studies and human reports demonstrate the potential therapeutic benefits of FMT in treating aging and aging-associated diseases. However, significant gaps remain in the research, particularly regarding the long-term effects, colonization resistance and safety of the treatment [168, 169]. To effectively investigate the potential therapeutic impact of FMT for aging and aging-associated diseases, these concerns must be addressed.

In addition to FMT, probiotics and prebiotics have also been proposed as potential therapies for restoring gut microbial balance. Probiotics are live microorganisms that alter the gut microbiome composition to confer beneficial health effects, while prebiotics serve as nutrients for the beneficial gut microbes [177].

Research has demonstrated that both prebiotics and probiotics can reverse aging and aging-associated diseases in various animal studies (Table 2). For instance, oral administration of Lactobacillus casei LC122 or Bifidobacterium longum BL986 in aged mice reversed aging symptoms as it enhanced mice learning and memory, improved the integrity of the intestinal barrier, reduced hepatic lipid buildup, inflammation, oxidative stress as well as increased muscle strength and function [178]. Similarly, B. longum strains derived from human gut were shown to alleviate aging parameters in d-galactose aging-induced mice [179]. Different Lactobacillus species have also been shown in many animal studies to reverse aging symptoms such as improvements in cognitive and physiological function in mice [180, 181]. These animal results are consistent with some human microbiota dysbiosis studies. Discussion

Many changes in the gut microbiota occur during aging, including an increase in pathogens, a decrease in core SCFA producers, an increase in toxic metabolites and a decline in gut barrier integrity. These changes are consistent with the negative symptoms of aging and are associated with age-related diseases. The recent publication by Gyriki and colleagues [192] on the effects of gut microbiota on aging underscores the growing significance and rapid development of this field.

Research has demonstrated a strong interplay between the gut microbiome and aging, but the exact mechanisms involved are still under investigation. Although common trends in the gut microbiome composition related to aging and aging-associated diseases, such as decreased diversity, increased prevalence of proinflammatory bacteria and a decrease of SCFA-producing bacteria, numerous inconsistencies and contradictory reports arise regarding the specific taxonomic composition of the gut microbiome across different diseases. For instance, some studies on gut microbiota composition report that AD patients have a higher prevalence of Bacteroidetes [47] and a lower prevalence of the Firmicutes phylum [46, 47] compared to healthy individuals. However, a recent systematic review of 16 S rRNA sequencing studies on gut microbiota in AD patients concluded that the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio is not reliable due to numerous contradictory results and because this ratio is highly dependent on region and lifestyle [193]. The same holds true for other aging-associated diseases, such as PD [194], T2D [195] and CVD [196]. This has been attributed to the use of small sample populations, inconsistent methodologies, varying sample preparation processes and different techniques. These contradictory results create confusion and hinder the advancement of our understanding in this field.

One of the primary reasons behind this is the inconsistent use of different sequencing methodologies. Most studies use 16 S rRNA sequencing due to its low cost and minimal DNA input requirements. However, even reports that employ 16 S rRNA sequencing can yield different results [193, 197, 198]. This may be due to inconsistencies in targeting the different variable regions of the 16 S rRNA gene. However, more importantly, 16 S rRNA sequencing primarily distinguishes bacteria at the genus level and sometimes at the species level, while there have been reports of species-specific and even strain-specific functional differences. For instance, both Bacteroides intestinalis and Bacteroides caccae belong to the Bacteroides genus; however, metagenomic analysis has revealed that B. intestinalis is strongly associated with preclinical AD, while B. caccae was associated with the healthy group [51]. Moreover, although there was no significant difference in the relative abundance of the species Bacteroides coprocola between T2D patients and healthy controls, Chen et al. (2017) found single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) differences in B. coprocola between T2D patients and healthy individuals. The research team found 49 genes with distinctive SNP distributions in T2D patients. Two of these genes express glycosyl hydrolases, a T2D drug target, indicating that strain differences of B. coprocola may lead to the production of glycosyl hydrolases with varying properties, thereby contributing differently to disease outcomes [199]. Similarly, Xiao et al. (2021), observed age-dependent changes in B. lungum genome and some SNP differences located in the arginine biosynthesis pathway to be significantly correlated with age. These SNPs resulted in varying abilities to influence arginine abundance in vitro. In vivo, supplementing D-galactose-induced aging mouse models with the positive (AGT allele at the arginine biosynthesis genes) and negative (GTC allele at the arginine biosynthesis genes) B. lungum strains alleviated or had no effects on aging parameters, respectively [179]. Collectively, these studies underscore the importance of expanding our understanding of the gut microbiome functional activities beyond relying solely on superficial taxonomic differences, such as those at the genus and phylum levels.

Research involving animal studies has indicated that targeting the gut microbiome may be a promising approach for addressing aging and age-related diseases. These findings suggest that altering the gut microbiome to resemble a healthier composition could serve as a potential strategy for tackling these issues. However, further studies in humans are needed to confirm these results.

Increased human studies could potentially provide answers to many questions that cannot be addressed through animal studies. For instance, many animal studies investigating the effects of FMT on the host have used a mixture of antibiotics to decontaminate the recipients’ gut microbiota before transplantation to prevent engraftment failure of the donors’ microbiota in the recipients’ gut [152, 161, 162, 172–176], or made multiple transplants a week to ensure successful engraftment [170, 171]. However, decontaminating the human gut with antibiotics to increase FMT treatment success is not feasible and could lead to side effects. Therefore, further research is needed to determine how to effectively prevent engraftment failure in humans and to enhance the long-term benefits of FMT. This is particularly important when considering the application of FMT in aging, as opposed to treating infectious diseases like CDI. In this case, the benefits sought from FMT are not just short-term, such as eliminating an infectious agent, but rather long-term. Given that aging contributes to microbiota dysbiosis, we must recognize that there is a greater probability that the host’s dysbiotic state and its associated health issues are likely to recur sometime after FMT treatment. Therefore, it is essential that FMT transplants are designed for long-term effectiveness to prevent the host from quickly reverting back to a dysbiotic state. A recent review investigated the literature on FMT resilience post-treatment concluded that there is a lack of consensus on the duration required to assess resilience, as follow-up periods differ significantly across studies, with the median study length being approximately only two months [200].

Furthermore, although the short-term effects of FMT have been reported to be low [201] the long-term effects of FMT are not well understood. There are concerns about harmful microorganisms and the potential increase in antimicrobial resistance genes following FMT. Investigating these aspects in humans could enhance our understanding and make FMT a more viable treatment option.

A significant proportion of the gut microbiota research primarily focuses on bacteria, with relatively few studies addressing other members of the gut microbiota, such as fungi, viruses and archaea. This represents a notable research gap that needs to be addressed. The limited investigations in these members may stem from their lower abundance in the gut microbiota compared to bacteria, the underdeveloped tools and databases, and the complexity of their biology. However, it is crucial to expand research in these domains, as even the available limited literature suggests their importance in disease modulation. For instance, Candida albicans, a commensal gut fungus, has been shown to decrease gut barrier integrity, increase inflammation and degrade BBB tight junctions [202–204]. Moreover, elevated levels of C. albicans have been observed in cancer patients [205] and neurodegenerative disease patients [206], indicating a potential role in disease prognosis. Furthermore, research shows that the gut virome richness significantly decreases with age [207]. Interestingly, a study comparing the gut bacteriophage composition between T2D patients and healthy individuals identified bacteriophage groups specific to T2D [208], suggesting an association between bacteriophages and metabolic syndromes. Therefore, it is essential to study the gut microbiota in health and disease beyond the bacteria. This broader approach will lead to a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of gut microbiota on the host and could help identify new targets for therapies.
