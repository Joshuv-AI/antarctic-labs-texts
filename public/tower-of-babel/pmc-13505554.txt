The Staudinger reaction: a versatile synthetic tool for the construction of β-lactams, heterocycles, and functional organic molecules
Authors: Ehsan Ullah Mughal, Nafeesa Naeem, Laiba Iftikhar, Shabnam, Amina Afzaal, H. A. Alrafai, Magdi E. A. Zaki, Amina Sadiq, Maher Fathalla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Staudinger reaction, first reported by Hermann Staudinger in 1907 as a ketene–imine [2+2] cycloaddition for the synthesis of β-lactams, has evolved into one of the most versatile transformations in modern organic synthesis. Beyond its classical applications in β-lactam formation and azide reduction to primary amines, the reaction now includes several advanced methodologies with broad applications in medicinal chemistry, chemical biology, and materials science. This review summarizes the evolution of the Staudinger reaction from its classical forms to recent developments, including metal-free microwave-assisted Wolff–Staudinger cascade reactions, continuous-flow multicomponent strategies, and ketene generation through toluenesulfonyl chloride (TsCl)-mediated activation. It also highlights the use of Staudinger-based methodologies, such as Staudinger–aza-Wittig, Ugi–Staudinger, and asymmetric catalytic sequences, for the efficient synthesis of five- and six-membered heterocycles, complex alkaloids, and chiral nitrogen-containing compounds. Particular emphasis is placed on compounds reported between 2020 and 2026, with a comparative evaluation of synthetic methodologies, reaction yields, stereochemical outcomes, and biological activities. The review further discusses the pharmaceutical significance of Staudinger-derived molecules, including β-lactam antibiotics that inhibit penicillin-binding proteins, as well as applications in glycobiology, biomolecular labeling, and cancer research. Collectively, these advances demonstrate the continued importance of the Staudinger reaction as a powerful and adaptable platform for the synthesis of biologically relevant molecules and advanced functional materials.

Introduction

The Staudinger reaction, also known as the Staudinger synthesis or ketene–imine [2+2] cycloaddition, is named after the German chemist Hermann Staudinger (1881–1965).1 After completing his studies at Karlsruhe, Staudinger carried out pioneering research in Zurich, where he discovered that trivalent phosphorus compounds react with diazo compounds, particularly organic azides, to form phosphazenes. This transformation subsequently became known as the Staudinger reaction.2

In 1905, Staudinger also discovered ketenes 1, highly reactive cumulene intermediates that play a crucial role in cycloaddition chemistry. In recognition of his fundamental contributions to polymer science, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953.2,3 Although the Staudinger reaction was discovered at the beginning of the twentieth century, it received relatively little attention for several decades. Its synthetic importance was fully recognized only after the β-lactam ring was identified in the 1940s as the pharmacophoric core of the antibiotic penicillin, highlighting the significance of the ketene–imine cycloaddition in the synthesis of β-lactam compounds.

Another major milestone in the development of the Staudinger reaction was achieved through detailed mechanistic investigations conducted during the 1990s. Computational and experimental studies by Cossío and co-workers demonstrated that the Staudinger [2+2] cycloaddition does not proceed through a concerted pericyclic mechanism. Instead, the reaction follows a stepwise pathway involving the formation of a zwitterionic intermediate, which subsequently undergoes a four-electron conrotatory electrocyclization to afford the corresponding β-lactam product.4

Staudinger ketene–imine [2+2] cycloaddition (β-lactam synthesis)

The Staudinger reaction is one of the most fundamental and versatile methods for the synthesis of β-lactam (2-azetidinone) derivatives. These heterocyclic compounds are of considerable importance in both synthetic organic chemistry and pharmacology owing to their broad range of biological activities.5 In 1907, Hermann Staudinger reported the [2+2] cycloaddition between a ketene and an imine (Schiff base), providing an efficient route to nitrogen-containing four-membered β-lactam (2-azetidinone) rings (Scheme 1). This pioneering work was published in Berichte der Deutschen Chemischen Gesellschaft.1 Ketenes (Fig. 1) are typically generated in situ from the corresponding acid chlorides in the presence of a tertiary amine, which acts as a base, facilitating formation of the reactive ketene intermediate required for the cycloaddition reaction.6,7

General structure of ketene 1 and imine 2. Reproduced from ref. 7 with permission from American Chemical Society (ACS), copyright © 1992.

The Staudinger reaction is initiated by nucleophilic attack of an imine 2 on the ketene, forming a zwitterionic intermediate 5. This intermediate subsequently undergoes conrotatory electrocyclic ring closure to afford the corresponding β-lactam product (Scheme 1). When a monosubstituted ketene 3 reacts with an acyclic imine 4, two new stereogenic centers are generated at the C3 and C4 positions of the β-lactam ring, resulting in the formation of either cis, trans, or a mixture of both diastereomeric β-lactam derivatives 6. Consequently, controlling the relative stereochemistry (cis/trans selectivity) represents one of the most important aspects of the Staudinger reaction, as it significantly influences the stereochemical outcome and synthetic utility of the process.6

The β-lactam (2-azetidinone) ring 6 constitutes the principal pharmacophore of β-lactam antibiotics, one of the most widely used classes of antibacterial agents. Owing to the remarkable therapeutic importance of these antibiotics, numerous synthetic strategies have been developed for the efficient preparation of β-lactam derivatives.8 Considerable efforts have been directed toward the development of catalytic methods capable of producing β-lactams 6 with high levels of diastereo- and enantioselectivity. Early studies primarily focused on the use of optically active transition-metal catalysts and chiral auxiliaries; however, at the beginning of the twenty-first century, highly efficient organocatalytic approaches for asymmetric β-lactam synthesis emerged.9

Accordingly, numerous enantioselective variants of the Staudinger reaction have been developed using chiral auxiliaries and asymmetric catalytic systems for the efficient synthesis of β-lactam derivatives.10 The reaction of ketenes 7 or 9 with cyclic imines 8 generates two new stereogenic centers at the C3 and C4 positions of the 2-azetidinone ring 10 and 11, resulting in the formation of both cis- and trans-β-lactam diastereomers 15 and 16, as shown in Scheme 2. Consequently, the diastereoselectivity of the Staudinger reaction plays a pivotal role in determining the stereochemical outcome of the cycloaddition. Several mechanistic pathways have been proposed to explain the formation of these diastereomeric products.11 Because relatively few organic transformations can simultaneously generate two stereogenic centers in a single step, the Staudinger reaction continues to be recognized as one of the most efficient and valuable methods for the stereoselective synthesis of β-lactams 15 and 16, making stereochemical control a major focus of contemporary research.6,10

Representative examples include bis-spiro-β-lactams and their derivatives,12 substituted 2-((3S,4S)-2-oxo-1,4-diphenylazetidin-3-yl)-1H-benzo[de]isoquinoline-1,3(2H)-dione 20,13 substituted spirocyclic indeno[1,2-b]quinoxaline β-lactam derivatives 18,14 3-amino β-lactams,15 2-[(Z)-[1-(2-amino-1,3-thiazol-4-yl)-2-[[(2S,3S)-2-methyl-4-oxo-1-sulfoazetidin-3-yl]amino]-2-oxoethylidene]amino]oxy]-2-methylpropanoic acid 19,16 β-lactam-N-(thiazol-2-yl)benzenesulfonamide hybrids,17 tetracyclic dibenzo[b,f][1,4]oxazepine-fused β-lactams 17,18 substituted-5-methylene-12-(1H-tetrazol-5-yl)-6,12-dihydro-5H-quinazolino[3,2-a]quinazoline 21, and substituted 2-azido-2H-pyrano[2,3-b]quinoline derivatives 22. Staudinger-azide reduction

In 1919, Hermann Staudinger and Jules Meyer reported another important transformation, now known as the Staudinger reduction. This reaction has become one of the most widely used methods for the preparation of primary amines 28 from organic azides and represents a fundamental transformation in organic synthesis and chemical biology.25 In the Staudinger reduction, organic azides are converted into primary amines 28 through a phosphine-mediated reduction, significantly expanding the synthetic utility of organophosphorus chemistry.26

The reaction is initiated by the nucleophilic addition of a tertiary phosphine, most commonly triphenylphosphine (PPh3), to compound 23, leading to the formation of a transient phosphazide intermediate 25 (Scheme 3). Subsequent extrusion of molecular nitrogen (N2) through a four-membered cyclic transition state affords an iminophosphorane intermediate 26, also referred to as an aza-ylide. In the classical Staudinger protocol, trivalent phosphines, particularly compound 24, reduce compound 23 in an aqueous medium via the iminophosphorane intermediate 26.27 Although the formation of triphenylphosphine oxide provides a strong thermodynamic driving force for the reaction, the reduction may proceed slowly when the corresponding iminophosphorane intermediate is kinetically stable.28–30

Synthesis of primary amines by reduction of compound 23 through a phosphine-mediated process. Reproduced from ref. 26 with permission from Elsevier, copyright © 1981.

The Staudinger reduction has been widely applied to the synthesis of numerous heterocyclic compounds and their derivatives, including N-heterocyclic carbenes,9 5′-α-iminophosphates,31 4,6-unsubstituted 2-aminodihydropyrimidine-5-carboxylates,32 triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides,33 and 2H-pyrrolo[3,4-c]quinoline derivatives,34 among many others.

In one notable application, azide-functionalized cell-surface glycoconjugates, generated through the metabolic incorporation of synthetic azido sugars, were selectively labeled with a biotinylated phosphine reagent to produce stable cell-surface conjugates. The exceptional chemoselectivity of this reaction has enabled its application in living systems and has provided new opportunities for investigating intracellular biological processes and biomolecular interactions.35

Biological evaluation

The β-lactam ring 6 constitutes the structural core of the most clinically important classes of antibiotics, including penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams, which remain among the most frequently prescribed antibacterial agents worldwide.21 These antibiotics exert their antibacterial activity by irreversibly acylating the active-site serine residues of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), the transpeptidase enzymes responsible for bacterial peptidoglycan cross-linking. Inhibition of these enzymes disrupts bacterial cell-wall biosynthesis, ultimately leading to cell lysis and death. In addition to their antibacterial activity, β-lactam derivatives have been identified as potent inhibitors of viral proteases, human leukocyte elastase, and several other mammalian serine hydrolases, considerably broadening their therapeutic potential.

Recent studies have also focused on the development of novel β-lactam frameworks and β-lactamase-resistant analogues to overcome emerging antimicrobial resistance.36 Furthermore, the development of asymmetric and catalytic Staudinger reactions employing chiral auxiliaries, organocatalysts, and transition-metal catalysts has represented a major advance in asymmetric synthesis over the past three decades.6,10

Staudinger reaction is used to provide a synthetic route for diverse medicinal compounds like compounds 29, 30, 31, 32, and 33, which would be further used as an important structural core in the synthesis of various biological compounds like losartan, ezetimibe, clonidine, imiquimod, and vasopressin V1 antagonist and other drugs shown in (Fig. 3).

In cancer research, it has been employed in combination with azide–alkyne click chemistry for the biotinylation and detection of proteins within cancer cells.42 In addition, azide–phosphine conjugation has been utilized for the preparation of homogeneous biotherapeutics and antibody–drug conjugates.43 Phosphoramidate-modified oligonucleotides, including N-(4-carboxybenzenesulfonyl)-phosphoramidate derivatives, have also been functionalized with di- and polyamines to introduce positively charged side chains that may enhance intracellular delivery.44 Moreover, biocatalytic approaches, including the asymmetric reduction of imines 4 by imine reductases and the reductive amination of ketones catalyzed by reductive aminases (RedAms) or amine dehydrogenases, provide efficient routes to chiral amines.45 Owing to the high enantioselectivity of these enzymes, the asymmetric reduction of imines has emerged as a valuable strategy for the synthesis of optically active amine derivatives.46 Compounds like 34–40, prepared through the Staudinger reaction and its mechanistic variants, have found widespread applications in chemical biology and medicinal chemistry and are shown in Table 2.

The Staudinger reaction remains a highly versatile synthetic platform for the preparation of a broad range of structurally diverse compounds rather than a single class of molecules. Recognizing its continued significance, this review focuses on recent advances in the synthesis of various compounds using Staudinger-based methodologies. Furthermore, it provides a comparative analysis of the reported synthetic approaches, critically evaluates their advantages and limitations, and discusses current challenges, emerging trends, and future perspectives for the application of the Staudinger reaction in modern synthetic and medicinal chemistry.

Synthesis of β-lactam via Staudinger reaction

The antibacterial properties of penicillin were first discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928 during studies involving Staphylococcus aureus. Fleming observed that one of his bacterial cultures had been accidentally contaminated with the blue-green mold Penicillium notatum, and that bacterial growth was inhibited in the area surrounding the mold. Recognizing the significance of this unexpected observation, he isolated the active antibacterial substance, which he named penicillin, and subsequently reported its discovery and potential therapeutic applications.64 However, it was not until 1940 that the first therapeutic studies of compound 60 against streptococcal infections were successfully conducted in mouse models, marking the beginning of the clinical development of β-lactam 51 antibiotics.65 Compound 51, also known as 2-azetidinones or β-lactams, represents an important class of four-membered nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds consisting of a cyclic amide ring with a carbonyl group12

Despite structural variations introduced by different substituents, all β-lactam antibiotics share three essential structural features: (i) a four-membered β-lactam ring 51 that is responsible for their characteristic acylation of biological targets; (ii) significant ring strain arising from the bicyclic amide framework, which enhances their chemical reactivity; and (iii) an acidic functional group that is crucial for molecular recognition by the active site of bacterial transpeptidases, also known as PBPs.66

β-Lactam antibiotics constitute one of the most extensively prescribed classes of antimicrobial agents and are available in both oral and parenteral formulations. Their broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, high therapeutic efficacy, and favorable safety profile have established them as the most widely used antibiotics in clinical practice.67,68 In addition to their well-known antibacterial properties, β-lactam derivatives have been reported to exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including HIV-1 protease inhibition,69 antioxidant activity, cholesterol absorption inhibition,70 antifungal and antimalarial effects,71 as well as antitumor activity.72,73

Classes of β-lactam

All commercially available β-lactam antibiotics contain a highly reactive β-lactam ring 51, a four-membered cyclic amide characterized by significant ring strain, which is essential for their antibacterial activity (Table 2). Based on the nature of the fused or monocyclic ring system, β-lactam antibiotics are classified into five major structural classes: monobactams 52, penams 53, carbapenems 54, cephems 55, and penems 56, as illustrated in Fig. 5.65

Different classes of compound 51. Reproduced from ref. 84 with permission from Molecules, copyright © 2025.

Monobactams 52 are monocyclic β-lactam antibiotics that exhibit high stability toward many β-lactamases. They possess potent antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacilli but show limited efficacy against Gram-positive bacteria and anaerobic microorganisms. Aztreonam 59 was the first clinically approved member of this class.65 Currently, combinations of compound 59 with other β-lactam antibiotics or diazabicyclooctane-based monocyclic β-lactams are being investigated in preclinical studies for the treatment of infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Gram-negative pathogens.74,75

Penams 53 are characterized by an azabicyclo[3.2.0]heptane ring system containing a sulfur atom at the C1 position and a carboxylic acid group at the C3 position. Compound 60 (benzylpenicillin or penicillin G), originally isolated from Penicillium chrysogenum, is the prototype and most widely recognized member of this class.64,76 Penems 56 are structural analogues of compound 52, distinguished by the presence of a double bond between the C2 and C3 positions of the fused five-membered ring.77 This class includes clinically important compounds such as faropenem, ritipenem, and sulopenem, and continues to attract considerable research interest in the development of new β-lactam antibiotics.78–80

Cephems 55, represented primarily by the cephalosporins 57, constitute one of the most successful and widely prescribed classes of anti-infective agents. They were developed shortly after the discovery of compound 60 and are generally well tolerated. Structurally, cephems 55, possess a dihydrothiazine ring fused to the β-lactam ring 51, which contributes to their distinctive chemical and biological properties.81–83

Carbapenems 54 are another important class of β-lactam antibiotics and are structurally related to compound 57. Compound 62, the first clinically introduced carbapenem, exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and anaerobic microorganisms. Although carbapenems are resistant to many β-lactamases, compound 62 is susceptible to hydrolysis by renal dehydropeptidase I and is therefore co-administered with the dehydropeptidase inhibitor cilastatin to enhance its therapeutic efficacy.65

β-Lactam antibiotics 51 exert their antibacterial activity by inhibiting PBPs, also known as transpeptidases, which are essential enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of peptidoglycan and the bacterial cell wall.85 Peptidoglycan is a major structural component of the bacterial cell envelope that provides mechanical strength, maintains cell shape, and protects the cell against internal osmotic pressure.

PBPs catalyze the cross-linking of peptide side chains between adjacent peptidoglycan strands within the periplasmic space, thereby forming the rigid mesh-like structure of the bacterial cell wall. Owing to the structural similarity of the β-lactam ring to the terminal d-alanyl–d-alanine (d-Ala–d-Ala) moiety of N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) peptidoglycan precursors, PBPs recognize β-lactam antibiotics as their natural substrates. Consequently, the β-lactam ring covalently acylates the active-site serine residue of PBPs, resulting in irreversible inhibition of their transpeptidase activity. This inhibition prevents peptidoglycan cross-linking, leading to the accumulation of cell wall precursor molecules and disruption of cell wall biosynthesis. The resulting imbalance activates bacterial autolytic enzymes, ultimately causing cell wall degradation, osmotic instability, and bacterial cell lysis86,87 (Fig. 6).

β-Lactam derivatives 51 are predominantly synthesized through the Staudinger [2+2] ketene–imine cycloaddition, as illustrated in Scheme 1. In most synthetic protocols, the ketene intermediate 1 is generated in situ from its corresponding precursors because of its high reactivity and limited stability. This versatile methodology enables the synthesis of a wide range of β-lactam derivatives through the use of different ketene 1 precursors, imines 2, catalysts, and reaction conditions, providing efficient access to structurally diverse and stereochemically defined products. cis or trans-Diastereoselectivity synthesis of 1,3-diaryl-spiro[azetidine-2,3-indoline]-2,4-diones

Although the synthesis of spiro-β-lactams via the Staudinger reaction has been reported previously, the influence of reaction conditions on diastereoselectivity has received limited attention. In the present study, an efficient method was developed for the synthesis of compounds 69 and 70 through the Staudinger reaction using in situ ketene generation from substituted phenylacetic acids, oxalyl chloride, and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA). Unlike conventional Staudinger protocols, the modified procedure enables reversal of the diastereoselectivity, allowing selective formation of either compound 69 or 70 under appropriate reaction conditions.

The synthesis commenced with the preparation of the corresponding Schiff base. Compound 63 was added to a boiling solution of isatin 64 in 25 mL of absolute ethanol containing a catalytic amount of acetic acid. The reaction mixture was refluxed for 3 h, and the progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). After completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and filtered under reduced pressure. The resulting precipitate was washed with 25 mL of cold ethanol, dried, and isolated as compound 65 (Scheme 4).

Synthesis of compound 65. Reproduced from ref. 91 with permission from RSC, copyright © 2020.

In the second step, the diastereomeric spirooxindole β-lactam derivatives were synthesized via the Staudinger cycloaddition. A solution of compound 66 in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) was prepared, after which compound 67 was added dropwise in the presence of 1–2 drops of anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) as a catalyst. The reaction mixture was refluxed for approximately 2 h, and the solvent was subsequently removed under reduced pressure to afford a dark yellow oily intermediate 68 for further transformation (Scheme 5).

Synthesis of compound 68. Reproduced from ref. 91 with permission from RSC, copyright © 2020.

Subsequently, compound 68 was added to a boiling solution of the corresponding Schiff base and triethylamine (Et3N) in anhydrous DMF. The reaction mixture was refluxed for 7–9 h and then allowed to cool to room temperature before being poured into cold water. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the resulting residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography using a petroleum ether/ethyl acetate (65 : 35) eluent. Final recrystallization from diethyl ether afforded the desired β-lactam products as white solids, yielding both the major and minor diastereomeric products (Schemes 6 & 7). The protocol employing pre-isolated acid chlorides (Scheme 6) provided moderate yields (44–64%), whereas the in situ activation strategy using oxalyl chloride/DIPEA (Scheme 7) afforded yields of up to 84%. Notably, the two approaches exhibited distinct stereochemical preferences for the same substrates: the former predominantly produced the cis-diastereomer 70 (approximately 1 : 2 diastereomeric ratio), while the latter favored formation of the trans-isomer 69. In addition, the methodology demonstrated broad substrate applicability, successfully furnishing 21 derivatives of compound 65 and two derivatives of compound 66, while also identifying substrates that failed under the optimized conditions. The in situ protocol further simplified the synthesis by eliminating the need to isolate acid chloride intermediates. Overall, these findings highlight a trade-off between the reproducible performance of the pre-isolated acid chloride route and the greater operational simplicity, higher attainable yields, and improved stereochemical control offered by the in situ activation strategy.91

cis-Diastereoselective synthesis of spirooxindolo-β-lactams using TsCl as activating co-reagent

Molecularly targeted cancer therapy using small-molecule inhibitors (SMIs) has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional chemotherapy because of its high selectivity toward tumor cells and reduced toxicity to normal tissues. Among the numerous molecular targets implicated in cancer, murine double minute 2 (MDM2) has attracted considerable attention. The spiro-oxindole framework has been recognized as a privileged scaffold for the development of MDM2 inhibitors, as its rigid spirocyclic architecture provides conformational stability, facilitates structural diversification, and enables favorable binding of compounds 69 and 70 within the MDM2 active site.

However, conventional Staudinger syntheses employing ketenes generated from acid chlorides predominantly afforded the undesired compound 69 as the major diastereomer. Moreover, the high reactivity and instability of compound 67 complicated the synthetic process, although one-pot procedures improved cis-selectivity. To address these limitations, Vadim E. Filatov and co-workers introduced tosyl chloride (TsCl) 76 as a milder and more practical activating reagent 75 for the in situ generation of compound 78 during the Staudinger synthesis of compounds 86 and 87.

In this approach, TsCl served as the activating co-reagent in a ketene–imine [2+2] cycloaddition, enabling the efficient one-pot synthesis of compound 73 from isatin-derived Schiff bases and substituted phenylacetic acids (Scheme 8). Except for the substrate derived from 4-methoxyphenylacetic acid, the reaction afforded β-lactam products with high cis-selectivity without requiring rigorously anhydrous conditions or unstable reagents. The synthesis began with the preparation of compound 71 through condensation of isatin 64 with various aromatic amines in the presence of glacial acetic acid as a catalyst. Depending on the substrate, two synthetic procedures were employed.

The reaction conditions were found to have a pronounced influence on the diastereoselectivity of the Staudinger cycloaddition. Low temperatures and low-polarity solvents, such as o-xylene, favored the formation of compound 86, whereas elevated temperatures and highly polar solvents, including 1,4-dioxane, promoted the preferential formation of compound 87. This solvent-dependent stereochemical outcome was attributed to the stabilization of Zwitter ion intermediates. Polar solvents stabilize the EZ-type zwitterionic intermediate 85, suppressing its direct cyclization to compound 86 and allowing isomerization to the ZZ-type zwitterionic intermediate 82, which subsequently cyclizes to afford compound 87. An exception to the general diastereoselectivity trend was observed for 4-methoxyphenylacetic acid, where compound 87 remained the predominant product even under ambient reaction conditions. Efficiency varied from 20% to 69% for the cis-isomers and only from 3% to 26% for the trans-isomers. Furthermore, selectivity tended to decline with increasing solvent polarity or temperature. For the majority of substrates, the cis-diastereomer was the predominant result; however, 4-OMe-substituted acids showed a preference for the trans-isomer. The methodology was applied to seven derivatives of compound 71 and six derivatives of compound 72, demonstrating moderate substrate scope. However, the protocol relied on TsCl/DIPEA activation and was limited to small-scale reactions (1–2 mmol), which may restrict its preparative scalability.92

Bacterial infections and cancer continue to represent major global health challenges, driving the development of new targeted therapeutic strategies. Expedited access to β-lactams via telescoped three-component reaction in flow

The Staudinger reaction, which involves the thermal cycloaddition of imines and ketenes, is frequently used to create β-lactams. Its activity has historically been limited by its inability to form a complex with compound aldehyde, ketene, and amine. In 2024, Minuto and coworkers performed a three-step Staudinger reaction in series for the first time. This reaction takes advantage of the photochemical Wolff rearrangement of diazoketones and in situ generation of imine. Imines were made by dehydrating compound 118 and 119 in a packed bed reactor with basic alumina serving as the solvent. The dehydration rates of the telescoped flow approach are rapid (tResca. 3 minutes), combined with effective Wolff rearrangement using LEDs (420 nm) to deliver the required compound 122 in less than 30 minutes, which is in line with batch processing times of several days. Ketenes were created in situ via the Wolff photochemical reaction of diazoketones.

The study has shown that this method is feasible when visible (blue) light is used in place of UV light, with encouraging selectivity and efficiency outcomes. Another approach to β-lactam synthesis via continuous flow chemistry

Even though β-lactams are extremely essential chemicals, batch processing has been used nearly entirely in their manufacture. Nevertheless, low stereoselectivity, the instability of enolizable imines, and the creation of complicated reaction mixtures as a result of the polymerization of extremely reactive ketene intermediates are problems with batch techniques. In this regard, microreactor-based continuous-flow chemistry has drawn interest as a potential substitute. Flow chemistry is a desirable platform for increasing yields, selectivity, and safety because of its benefits, which include greatly improved mass and heat transfer, accurate temperature control, and the capacity to handle reactive intermediates directly within the system.

The synthesis of compound 126 utilizing this continuous-flow method has been described by Deketelaere and co-authors. A model reaction using compound 123 and 125 was chosen in order to assess initial parameters and optimize conditions. This model reaction was first carried out using a batch process to establish baseline data. Compound 118 was refluxed with isopropylamine (1 equivalent) and magnesium sulfate (2 equivalents) in DCM for two hours to produce compound 123 (96% yield). Acetoxyacetyl chloride 125 (1.1 equivalents) was then added dropwise after compound 123 and Et3N (3 equivalents) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM. The target compound 124 was produced exclusively as the cis-isomer and separated in 93% yield by recrystallization from ethanol after the reaction mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature (Scheme 22).

Synthesis of azaindole-β-lactam

A synthetic method for joining an azaindole heterocycle with a β-lactam ring was disclosed by Bartolacci and co-authors. To ensure that the Staudinger reaction with compound 133 proceeds smoothly, this method uses a new approach involving the protective group at the N1 position. The difficulty was in making compound 133 stable and soluble, as well as protecting its endocyclic nitrogen (N1), before transforming it into an imine and combining it with a ketene to create the β-lactam through a Staudinger [2+2] cycloaddition.

After analyzing many protecting groups (tetrahydropyranyl (THP), tert-butyloxycarbonyl (BOC), tosyl, and dimethoxymethyl (DMM)), the research team determined that DMM was the best option. Unlike THP and BOC, which caused reaction failures or the loss of the protective group, DMM protection offers a number of benefits, including high-yield, clean reactions, easy deprotection using trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and excellent stability throughout future steps. By selecting a ketene precursor, the scientists were able to manipulate the stereochemistry (cis or trans) and create a small library of compound 138.

First, formylation was used to add a formyl group to the C3 position of unsubstituted azaindole. The resultant compound 134 was condensed with a primary amine to create a compound 135 intermediate after being protected. This compound 135 then underwent a Staudinger [2+2] cycloaddition reaction using a ketene that was produced in situ from compound 136 and a base in order to directly create a compound 137 scaffold. Trifluoroacetic acid was used to cleave the protecting group after compound 137 formed, releasing the nitrogen on the azaindole ring and producing the target compound 138 with high diastereomeric purity and generally good yields (Scheme 25). With efficiency of 32–83% for the Staudinger-type reaction and 50% for the solid-phase approach, this technique exhibits extensive substrate tolerance for both the ketene and amine coupling partners. Synthesis of 5- and 6-membered heterocycles via Staudinger reaction

Heterocyclic compounds are cyclic organic molecules containing one or more heteroatoms within the ring framework. The most common heteroatoms are nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur, although heterocyclic systems incorporating other heteroatoms have also been extensively reported.107 Depending on the number and type of heteroatoms present, a single ring may contain one or multiple heteroatoms, giving rise to diverse heterocyclic frameworks such as pyrazines, pyrimidines, oxazoles, and related analogues. These compounds are broadly classified into five- and six-membered heterocyclic systems based on the size of the ring.108

In addition to β-lactams, a wide variety of heterocyclic compounds can be synthesized through Staudinger reduction, Staudinger–aza-Wittig reactions, nucleophilic cyclization pathways, or by combining the Staudinger reaction with other complementary synthetic methodologies. Heterocyclic compounds constitute indispensable structural motifs in medicinal chemistry and are widely distributed in biologically important molecules, including natural products, vitamins, enzymes, and numerous pharmacologically active compounds. These heterocyclic scaffolds exhibit a broad range of biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antispasmodic, antiallergic, anti-HIV, antidiabetic, anticancer, insecticidal, herbicidal, and enzyme inhibitory properties. Representative heterocyclic compounds synthesized through Staudinger-based methodologies and their associated biological activities are summarized in Fig. 7.107

Biological role of heterocyclic compounds. Reproduced from ref. 107 with permission from Der Pharma Chemica, copyright © 2017.

A wide variety of synthetic methodologies have been developed for the construction of heterocyclic compounds, including the Claisen rearrangement, Biginelli reaction, Fischer synthesis, intramolecular cyclization, [4+2] and [2+2] cycloaddition reactions, multicomponent reactions, transition metal-catalyzed C–H activation, microwave-assisted synthesis, solvent-free protocols, click chemistry, and numerous other synthetic strategies. Each of these approaches offers distinct advantages but also presents specific limitations with respect to substrate scope, reaction conditions, selectivity, scalability, or operational simplicity. In this review, particular emphasis is placed on the Staudinger reaction and its modern variants as an efficient and versatile synthetic platform for the preparation of structurally diverse five- and six-membered heterocyclic compounds. The following sections summarize recent developments in Staudinger-based methodologies and highlight their applications in the synthesis of biologically important heterocyclic scaffolds.

Synthesis of acyl phosphoramidates employing a modified Staudinger reaction

The N-acyl phosphoramides are a very significant structural feature of many biologically active natural products such as compound 38, and of the phosphonucleotide (phosphodosine) group of toxins. The biological relevance of their acyl phosphoramide structural unit is the fact that it is a more hydrolytically stable analogue of the acyl phosphate functionality, which is found in many metabolic processes. The many synthetic methods currently available include the phosphoramidite method, which is the most commonly used for the synthesis of phosphoramides, especially in the preparation of nucleotides, and which has been adapted to the synthesis of compound 141.

While this is rather general, there are some drawbacks to it. It most particularly requires an oxidation step which is incompatible with thiols and other sulfur-containing functional groups. Furthermore, many functional groups require protection to be used with the highly reactive phosphorylating reagents, leading to greater synthetic steps and decreased efficiency. Other synthetic routes are also known, such as the direct phosphorylation of 144 and the acylation of preformed phosphoramides. However, these methods are usually based on deprotonation of the nitrogen atom before N-acyl phosphoramide 146 formation, and thus are not suitable for substrates with acid- or base-sensitive functional groups.

Yet another reported method is the coupling of compound 147 and aldehydes in the presence of a ruthenium catalyst to produce N-acyl phosphoramides 148. This approach is synthetically promising, but it is limited by the need for an expensive catalyst that is not commercially available, ruthenium. In addition, the reaction may be accompanied by unwanted Curtius rearrangement side reactions, which decreases its usefulness. These drawbacks (Fig. 8) have encouraged the search for alternative, more practical syntheses (Scheme 26).

The Staudinger reaction is a well-established and efficient method for forming phosphorus–nitrogen bonds, producing an iminophosphorane intermediate. This intermediate is highly reactive towards oxygen-containing nucleophiles, particularly water and carbonyl compounds, where it typically decomposes to phosphine oxide plus an amine or imine. A more recent version, developed by Bertozzi and colleagues, follows a different pathway: instead of reacting with an external nucleophile, the iminophosphorane is rearranged intramolecularly to form an amide bond. Synthesis of diamido N-heterocyclic imines (DACNH) via Staudinger or reductive N–N bond cleavage approach

For more than a century, the Staudinger reaction between organic azides and phosphines has served as a reliable method for the synthesis of iminophosphoranes (RNPR'3). Following the establishment of N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs) as stable and well-defined species, Bielawski and Tamm, independently, extended this transformation to NHCs, thereby enabling the synthesis of NHCNR derivatives. However, no previous studies had reported the reaction of diamidocarbenes (DACs) with compound 147.

Consequently, the present work sought to expand the family of nitrogen-containing heterocyclic imines (NHIs) through the synthesis of DACNH derivatives. Using a modified Staudinger protocol, a DACNH derivative 240 was successfully prepared from a five-membered diamidocarbene 238. In contrast, the same reaction conditions proved ineffective for the corresponding six-membered DAC. Treatment of compound 238 with an excess of compound 196 resulted in an immediate color change from red to colorless, and stirring the reaction mixture at room temperature for 12 hours afforded the corresponding N-silylated diamidoimine in 80% yield (Scheme 47).

Synthesis of triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides

Modified oligonucleotides are now widely used in molecular biology, biotechnology, and medicine because of their enhanced stability and functional properties compared with unmodified oligonucleotides. Their principal applications include use as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primers, molecular beacons, and therapeutic agents, where they often exhibit superior performance relative to their native counterparts.

Compound 248 represents a promising class of modified oligonucleotides because it can be synthesized using several well-established methodologies, and its molecular structure permits versatile functional-group modification. Among the available synthetic approaches, the Staudinger reaction has emerged as an efficient strategy for the preparation of phosphoramidate derivatives. In previous studies, a method was developed for incorporating a triazinyl phosphoramidate group bearing two dodecyl substituents into an oligonucleotide framework. This strategy is based on the Staudinger reaction between compound 243, the phosphite triester intermediate generated during the oligonucleotide synthesis cycle, and compound 244, which functions as both the oxidizing and modifying reagent.

Subsequent substitution of compound 246 affords the corresponding phosphoramidate derivative, compound 248. This triazinyl phosphoramidate containing two dodecyl groups was found to possess favorable physicochemical properties. Consequently, the synthetic methodology for preparing compound 248 was investigated in detail, including optimization of triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotide (TPO) synthesis, variation of substituents on the triazine core, and the use of differently substituted azidotriazines as modifying reagents.

Mechanistically, the reaction begins with the Staudinger coupling of compound 244 with the reactive phosphite triester intermediate 243 of the growing oligonucleotide chain, resulting in the formation of compound 245, which contains a P–N bond. Following completion of the Staudinger reaction, compound 246 is treated with appropriate anhydrous amine solutions to introduce the desired functional groups, yielding compound 247. Subsequent conversion to compound 248, followed by cleavage of the modified oligonucleotide from the controlled pore glass (CPG) solid support, affords the desired triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotide in a yield of 40–95% (Scheme 49). Since the phosphorus center was still uncertain, stereoselectivity was not applicable.

The approach was expanded to include 18 derivatives of compound 248. A custom-synthesized azide reagent was needed for the synthesis, which was carried out at a tiny (oligonucleotide) scale of 0.4 µmol. In this study, it was demonstrated that a wide range of functional groups, including dodecyl substituents, can be incorporated into the triazine core of compound 244 through modification of a single compound 243 unit. This strategy enables the introduction of diverse functionalities into the oligonucleotide, thereby allowing modulation of its physicochemical and biological properties. Consequently, compound 248 represents a unique class of phosphate-modified nucleic acids with considerable structural diversity and broad potential for applications in molecular biology, biotechnology, and therapeutic development.33

Unified synthesis of (+)-aspidophylline A via a BIBOP-catalyzed asymmetric Staudinger/aza-Wittig reaction

Compound 34 is a complex pentacyclic monoterpene indole alkaloid belonging to the akuammiline family. It was first isolated from the stem bark of Kopsia singapurensis, a Malaysian medicinal plant. Structurally, compound 34 possesses a highly intricate cage-like framework comprising five contiguous stereogenic centers embedded within a cyclohexane core, a bridged piperidine ring, and an indoline moiety fused to a tetrahydrofuran ring. Biologically, it has attracted considerable interest because of its potential to overcome multidrug resistance in human KB nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells. Owing to its unique molecular architecture and promising biological activity, compound 34 has become an important target for total synthesis.118

Intramolecular cyclization of this intermediate then furnishes the tetracyclic core through an enantioselective desymmetrization process, providing efficient access to the molecular framework of compound 34 (Scheme 50). This procedure used an organocatalytic method using a catalytic system of BIBOP (20 mol%), Brønsted acid, and PhSiH3. For the total synthesis of compound 34, an enantiomerically enriched tricyclic indole derivative was selected as the starting material. Triflation using N-phenyl-bis(trifluoromethanesulfonimide) (PhNTf2) and sodium hexamethyldisilylamide (NaHMDS) afforded compound 250 in 91% yield. Critical perspectives and research gaps

Critical perspectives and research gaps in beta-lactam synthesis

The Staudinger reaction approaches to compound 51 reported indicate that most of the recent innovations have been on the ketene side and few on the imine side. The standard method using acid chlorides and Et3N to generate ketene in situ continues to work, but is hampered by the moisture sensitivity and awkward handling of acid chlorides. To address these drawbacks, other methods have been developed, such as in situ mixed-anhydride/TsCl activation and the photochemical Wolff rearrangement of diazo-compounds. The TsCl mediated approach is an important improvement due to the stability of TsCl and high reaction efficiency and selectivity.

Similarly, the Wolff rearrangement can be used to prepare ketenes in an acid chloride free step with substitution patterns that are not easily accessible through traditional methods. Reaction medium, the electronics of the substrate, and the ketene-generation strategy can have a significant effect on the stereochemical outcome of the Staudinger reaction. The use of different solvents and ketene precursors changes the diastereoselectivity, and can be optimized. In the general case, alkoxy- and aryloxy-substitued ketenes tend to give the cis-β-lactam, while aryl- and alkyl-substituted ketenes give the corresponding trans-β-lactam. In addition, the lifetime of the zwitterionic intermediate is influenced by the polarity of the solvent and the reaction temperature, and thus the torquoselectivity and a generalized electronic model for stereochemical control.

Critical perspective and research gaps in heterocyclic synthesis

The literature reviewed in this article highlights the remarkable evolution of the Staudinger reaction from a simple phosphine–azide transformation into a highly versatile synthetic platform for the preparation of phosphoramidates, structurally complex N-heterocycles, and chiral alkaloid frameworks. The successful combination of the Staudinger reaction with multicomponent reactions such as the Ugi reaction, Passerini reaction, Mitsunobu reaction, and Staudinger–aza-Wittig sequences has significantly broadened the synthetic applications of this reaction. Yet, there are a number of problems that hinder the widespread adoption of Staudinger-based methodologies. A multitude of asymmetric protocols are based on costly transition metal catalysts or specially designed chiral phosphine ligands, such as iridium-, silver-, HypPhos- or BIBO-based catalytic systems, that are often only moderately efficient for sterically demanding, aliphatic, and highly reactive substrates. Furthermore, while excellent enantioselectivities are possible in a few examples, it is hard to obtain a high stereocontrol over a wide variety of substrates. Moreover, the mechanism of recently reported transformations, such as unusual pathways of N–N bond cleavage and formation of biradicaloid intermediates, is not fully understood and relies on isolated investigations rather than systematic mechanistic studies.

Future perspectives

Although the Staudinger reaction has been successfully used to prepare numerous compounds, there are some issues that need to be addressed to make it more scalable, efficient, and widely applicable. The scope of substrate for continuous-flow methodologies should be extended beyond model substrates to include other electron rich and electron-poor aromatic systems, and to other heteroaryl imines in the future. Such research would prove flow chemistry as a synthetic tool, and not a proof-of-concept. The synthesis of structurally complex spiro- and bis-spiro frameworks in an efficient manner is another direction of interest. Design of shorter or telescoped or catalytic synthetic routes, especially ones that are amenable to continuous flow processes, would make these compounds more accessible for use in practice.

Another area of promising research is the combination of real-time analytical techniques (in-line LC-MS and NMR spectroscopy) with modern ketene-generation and continuous-flow platforms. An integrated system would allow for the monitoring of reactions in real time, allow for the optimization of reaction conditions, and provide a more in-depth understanding of the reaction mechanism that existed beyond the end point analysis. In addition, future research should not be restricted to the synthesis of compound 51, but should also cover a variety of other compounds synthesized using the Staudinger reaction, which have important biological and pharmaceutical applications. Therefore, the development of synthetic methods needs to go hand in hand with the biological and functional evaluation as from the beginning of investigation to shorten the time required to identify therapeutically relevant compounds. In the same way, the identification of inexpensive, metal-free or recyclable catalytic systems that exhibit high activity and stereoselectivity would lessen reliance on the expensive transition-metal catalysts and specially designed chiral phosphine ligands used in many asymmetric Staudinger and Staudinger–aza-Wittig transformations.

The goal of extending the scope of substrate classes of newly developed mild methodologies, such as water-free Staudinger reductions, to structurally complex and biologically relevant molecules is also important. Finally, the Staudinger reaction will be increasingly used in conjunction with other efficient synthetic methods, like multicomponent reactions such as the Ugi, Passerini and related tandem reactions, in order to speed up the assembly of medically relevant and structurally complex natural products. Together, these developments will help to build safer, economical and industrially viable Staudinger methodologies for future use in synthetic, medicinal and pharmaceutical chemistry.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning in Staudinger reaction research

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) have recently revolutionized the chemical reaction design, optimization, and analysis. While direct use of AI in the Staudinger reaction is scarce, the computational methods offer promising potential for enhancing reaction efficiency, stereoselectivity, and substrate scope. Large reaction databases enable machine learning algorithms to predict the optimal reaction conditions, such as the type of solvent, catalyst, temperature, and other reagents, thus minimizing the need for extensive experimental screening. Such predictive models can also predict yields of reactions and even foresee side reactions before their laboratory implementation.119

Moreover, AI molecular design is an emerging tool in medicinal chemistry, where the compounds made with the Staudinger reaction, such as β-lactams, phosphoramidates, and N-containing heterocycles, can be quickly evaluated for biological activity. The use of molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) prediction, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) analysis, and deep learning models helps prioritize promising candidates prior to synthesis, lowering both cost and time.

The recent developments of automated synthesis platforms also highlight the potential of the synergy between AI and continuous flow chemistry and robotics. Such self-optimizing systems can be used to track reaction progress in real time via analytical methods such as LC-MS, NMR or IR spectroscopy, and machine learning algorithms can be run to continuously and automatically update reaction conditions to maximize yield and selectivity. These methods may especially be useful in the synthesis of unstable ketenes, azides, and highly reactive intermediates by Staudinger reactions. Rational design of new catalytic systems and asymmetric transformations can be aided by computational studies supported by AI tools and tools that predict the energies of the transition states, stereochemical outcome and reaction pathways.

Summary

This review summarizes the evolution of the Staudinger reaction from Hermann Staudinger's original discovery of the ketene–imine [2+2] cycloaddition in 1907 to its development into a versatile and widely applicable synthetic transformation. Particular emphasis is placed on compounds synthesized between 2020 and 2026, highlighting recent advances in methodology, reaction efficiency, stereochemical control, and synthetic scope. Originally developed for the synthesis of β-lactams, the Staudinger reaction has evolved into a powerful platform for the preparation of nitrogen-containing heterocycles, phosphoramidates, and structurally complex alkaloid frameworks. Although the conventional acid chloride/Et3N protocol remains a reliable method for ketene generation, its practical application is limited by handling difficulties and moisture sensitivity. Recent developments, including mixed-anhydride activation using TsCl/DIPEA, photochemical Wolff rearrangement of diazo precursors, and continuous-flow ketene generation, have addressed many of these limitations while maintaining or improving reaction efficiency and diastereoselectivity. Furthermore, the integration of the Staudinger/aza-Wittig sequence has enabled the efficient one-pot synthesis of a wide range of heterocyclic compounds. Despite these advances, several challenges remain, including the dependence of asymmetric methodologies on expensive transition-metal catalysts and specially designed chiral phosphine ligands, such as iridium-, silver-, HypPhos-, and BIBOP-based systems, which often provide only moderate to good enantioselectivities. In addition, the mechanistic roles of newly proposed intermediates, including biradicaloid species and unconventional N–N bond-cleavage pathways, remain incompletely understood. Hence, the century-long development of the Staudinger reaction demonstrates its exceptional versatility, extending from classical azide reduction, asymmetric catalysis, and the construction of biologically important molecules. Future progress will depend on the development of economical metal-free or recyclable catalytic systems, safer one-pot, in situ azide-handling strategies, and comprehensive mechanistic investigations across diverse substrate classes, and the early integration of biological evaluation, including MIC and cytotoxicity studies, with synthetic methodology. Such advances will further strengthen the role of the Staudinger reaction as a practical and sustainable platform in modern synthetic, medicinal, and pharmaceutical chemistry.
