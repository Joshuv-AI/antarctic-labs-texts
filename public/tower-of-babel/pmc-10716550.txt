Profibrotic role of transcription factor SP1 in cross-talk between fibroblasts and M2 macrophages
Authors: Peng Feng, Ying Che, Chunyu Gao, Xuelei Chu, Zhichao Li, Luguang Li, Jianguo Li, Jinghua Gao, Yongli Dong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryFibrosis disrupts tissue balance and links to severe illnesses, impairing organ function and, in some cases, even fatality. The interaction between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts is vital for tissue equilibrium. Transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) released by M2 macrophages plays a central role in fibrosis, regulating fibroblast activity and extracellular matrix metabolism. Targeting TGF-β1 is key to fibrosis treatment. In our study using three fibroblast cell lines, we reveal that the M2 macrophage transcription factor SP1 enhances binding to the TGF-β1 promoter motif, promoting TGF-β1 transcription and activating fibroblasts (This process does not involve changes in DNA methylation levels surrounding the motif sequence). The zinc fingers in SP1’s DNA-binding domain 3 are crucial for this binding. In vivo, targeting SP1 in rat ligaments significantly reduces extracellular matrix accumulation. Our findings highlight SP1 as a promising target for regulating tissue extracellular matrix and combating fibrosis.Subject areas: Fibrosis, Molecular mechanism of gene regulation, Immunology, Cell biology

Introduction

Fibrosis can affect almost any tissue and organ in the body, leading to structural damage, impaired organ function, disability, tumor, or even death. Fibrotic diseases have a tremendous clinical impact as effective therapies are very limited.1 Actually, even though pathological fibrosis development has long been considered an irreversible process, there is evidence that fibrosis can be reversible, at least in part, provided that the primary etiological agent is suppressed.2

Macrophages are sentinels with multiple functions in immunity, repair, and tissue homeostasis, whereas fibroblasts play an important role in maintaining tissue structure and integrity.3,4,5 Macrophages are highly sensitive to the microenvironment, and their phenotypic specificity depends on the tissue they inhabit.5 In the early stages of tissue injury, macrophages are polarized to M1 type, initiating an inflammatory response and clearing necrotic materials; while in the later stages of injury, M2 macrophages predominate, suppressing inflammation, releasing cytokines, and promoting fibroblast synthesis of ECM, which is directly related to fibrosis. Fibroblasts can also influence M2 macrophages in turn. After tissue injury, fibroblasts sense changes in mechanical signals such as extracellular tension or tissue stiffness and become activated, migrating to the wound bed and synthesizing extracellular matrix (ECM) to assist in wound healing.6,7 As markers and scaffolds, fibroblasts indicate the location of injury within tissues, releasing chemotactic factors and cytokines to provide channels or "roads" for the navigation and migration of M2 macrophages in tissue.6 In fact, from the perspective of macrophages, there are many similarities in the molecular mechanisms of fibrosis, tissue healing, and even tumor immune escape.

Transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) is considered as one of the major factors that regulate the activity of fibroblasts.1 TGF-β1 can regulate the proliferation, migration, survival, and gene expression of fibroblasts.8,9 At the same time, TGF-β1 effectively stimulates the transcription of collagen genes and induces proteinase inhibitors (such as plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases) to inhibit the activity of matrix-degrading proteinases, thus exerting a matrix conservation effect.10 However, some studies have shown that TGF-β1 has an inhibitory effect on the activity of fibroblasts.11,12 Contradictory data may reflect the context-dependent role of TGF-β1. M2 macrophages may produce a large amount of latent TGF-β1 in fibrosis. However, the extent to which the fibrotic effect of TGF-β1 depends on macrophage-derived pro-fibrotic signals is still unclear.

Here, we identify TGF-β1 as a key mediator of the cross-talk between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts, based on analysis of patient samples and an animal model. We further identify the transcription factor SP1 and demethylation of CpG islands near the promoter-binding site as regulators of TGF-β1 expression. These insights may help clarify how fibrosis begins and how it might be treated.

Results

Transcription factor SP1 may be a factor driving the fibrosis process pushed by TGF-β1

The pathological changes in the structural organization of human fibrotic ligament tissue and the increased collagen volume fraction were previously identified. Although the ligamentum flavum of healthy individuals showed thin, wavy, elastic fibers and a normal collagen volume fraction of 20%–25%,13 hypertrophic ligamentum flavum from patients with lumbar spinal stenosis showed disordered, broken, and twisted fibers and substantially higher collagen volume fraction (Figures 1A and 1B). In healthy tissue, the elastic fibers were curved and wavy and could easily be distinguished from collagen, whereas in fibrotic tissue, the fibers appeared straighter, less deformable, and more difficult to distinguish from the abundant collagen (Figure 1C).

Previous studies have shown that the expression of TGF-β1 in M2 macrophages contributes to the fibrosis in connective tissue or organs.14,15 The bioinformatics analysis results of the GSE113212 dataset also support such findings16 (Figure S1). Then, we will search for upstream molecular mechanisms that may induce this expression. Bioinformatics predicted 14 possible transcription factors (Figure S2A). qPCR results showed that in human ligament samples, the mRNA of TGF-β1 and transcription factor SP1 were significantly elevated, whereas other transcription factors did not show significant differential expression (Figures 1D and S2B). The expression of SP1 and mature TGF-β1 as well as the pro TGF-β1 was increased in the fibrotic ligament (Figure 1E). LOXL2 is a critically important enzyme in the ECM that promotes cross-linking of collagen and elastin and plays an important role in fibrosis.17 Our results indicate that the expression of LOXL2 in fibrotic ligaments is significantly increased, and the TGF signaling pathway is activated (Figure 1F). We found that SP1 is likely localized around macrophages (Figure 1G). Indeed, dual immunostaining of ligament sections indicated a significant correlation in the localization of SP1 and M2 macrophage markers (Figure 1H). This indicates that the expression of SP1 and TGF-β1 may be correlated and may be involved in the interaction between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts mediated by TGF-β1.

Transcription factor SP1 in M2 macrophages turn on the expression of TGF-β1 during fibrosis

Ligament fibroblast (LGF), lung fibroblast (LF), and skin fibroblast (SF) were used to construct Transwell co-culture systems with M2 macrophages (M2 macrophages were identified as CD11b and CD163 after drug induction; Figures S3A and S3B). Activated fibroblasts possess strong migration and ECM secretion abilities, being capable of migrating from non-injured areas with strong extracellular matrix adhesion to injured areas with weak adhesion. They also secrete large amounts of collagen proteins.18,19 Our results showed that after 4 days of co-culture, fibroblasts exhibited strong migration ability (Figure 2A) and proliferation activity (Figures 2B and S3C). Compared with fibroblasts cultured alone, the co-cultured fibroblasts showed significant increases in the expression of collagen I, III, and vimentin proteins (Figures 2C and S3D). The concentrations of collagen I and III in the co-culture medium were significantly increased and showed time-dependent patterns (Figures 2D and S3E). Meanwhile, M2 macrophages can activate the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in fibroblasts (Figure 2E). We also observed nuclear translocation of SP1 in M2 macrophages (Figure S3F). These pieces of evidence indicate that M2 macrophages can activate fibroblasts.

Compared with the M2 macrophages cultured alone, the expression of SP1 and pro-TGF-β1 in M2 macrophages co-cultured for four days showed a significant increase (Figure 2F). Next, we constructed the knockdown vector for SP1 and used PCR to confirm the knockdown efficiency (Figure S3G). The protein immunoblot showed that the pro TGF-β1 was also significantly reduced after knocking down SP1, and this change was reversed by KRFK (50 μM; KRFK, a peptide derived from THBS1, can activate TGF-β and promote its signaling and downstream effects independently of thrombospondin receptors.20) in M2 macrophages (Figure 2G). KRFK treatment can reverse the decreased cell activity induced by knockdown of SP1 in M2 macrophages, which can in turn be reversed by CJJ300 (40 μM; CJJ300 blocks the formation of the TGF-β-TβR-I-TβR-II signaling complex to inhibit the TGF-β signaling pathway in fibroblasts)21 (Figure 2H). The content of collagen I and III in the co-culture system also showed similar changes (Figure 2I). This indicates that the activity of fibroblasts mediated by SP1 is caused by the secretion of endogenous TGF-β1 in M2 macrophages.

Identification of binding elements recognized by SP1 in the promoter of the gene encoding TGF-β1

Bioinformatics predicted three regions (P1, P2, and P3) of the TGF-β1 gene promoter that SP1 may bind to, as well as the potential SP1-binding elements (BE) in each binding region (Figure 3A). In dual-luciferase reporter gene analysis, we observed that SP1 can significantly activate the promoter containing the P3 sequence (Figure 3B). Of the two sequences, which we called BE1 and BE2, that were predicted to be bound by SP1 within region P3, we found that SP1 preferentially bound to BE1, based on immunoprecipitation of chromatin using anti-SP1 antibody (Figure 3C). Indeed, SP1 bound to BE1 in the dual-luciferase reporter assay, and this binding was eliminated by mutating BE1 or knocking down SP1 (Figure 3D).

Overexpression of SP1 in M2 macrophages significantly increased the binding of SP1 to the BE1 sequence, whereas knockdown of SP1 resulted in a significant decrease in the binding efficiency of SP1 to the BE1 sequence (Figure 3E). In the analysis of the dual-luciferase reporter, overexpression of SP1 significantly increased its binding efficiency with the BE1 sequence in M2 macrophages, and this effect could be reversed by knocking down SP1 (Figure 3F). Given that the methylation levels of CpG islands near the binding elements may have potential impacts on this binding, we conducted methylation sequencing of BE1 sequences in M2 macrophages under different conditions. Our results suggest that there was no methylation change in the CpG island near the BE1 sequence, thus excluding this potential influential factor (Figure S4).

Subsequently, we constructed M2 macrophages with stable expression of TGF-β1 BE1 mutant and established a co-culture system with fibroblasts to verify the impact of the mutation in this sequence on the activation of fibroblasts. We found that the activation of fibroblasts by M2 macrophages is significantly weakened after the sequence mutation, and even overexpression of SP1 cannot reverse this effect. However, exogenous TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL)22 can significantly increase the activity of fibroblasts (Figures 3G and S5A). Even with overexpression of SP1, mutation of the TGF-β1 BE1 sequence in M2 macrophages can significantly reduce the migration efficiency of fibroblasts, which is reversed by exogenous TGF-β1 (Figures 3H and S5B). The expression of collagen I and III as well as the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in fibroblasts was also affected by mutation of the M2 macrophage sequence, leading to similar changes (Figures 3I, 3J, S5C, and S5D).

The induction of endogenous TGF-β1 expression in M2 macrophages by transcription factor SP1 requires its DNA-binding domain

We have constructed a truncated form of SP1 (SP1ΔZn) lacking its DNA-binding domain that consists in three zinc fingers23. We found that the binding efficiency of SP1 and BE1 sequences significantly decreased with the lack of three zinc fingers, as determined by chromatin immunoprecipitation using anti-SP1 antibody and TGFB1 BE1 probe (Figure 4A). In the dual-luciferase reporter gene assay, we found that the full-length SP1 protein could bind to the BE1 sequence normally, whereas the binding efficiency of the truncated mutant SP1 protein was low (Figure 4B). Under the co-culture conditions of M2 macrophages and fibroblasts, full-length SP1 protein can stably promote the paracrine secretion of endogenous TGF-β1, whereas truncated mutant SP1 protein does not have this effect (Figure 4C). We further evaluated the impact of SP1 truncating mutations on the activity of fibroblasts. The results showed that the migration activity of fibroblasts was significantly inhibited after SP1 protein was truncated mutation (Figure 4D). The protein levels of collagen I and III in fibroblasts did not increase with the increase of co-culture time (Figures 4E and 4F). The proliferation activity of fibroblasts was significantly inhibited by the truncation mutation of SP1 (Figure 4G). Subsequently, we further analyzed the effect of SP1 truncation mutation on the downstream TGF signaling pathway in fibroblasts. Our results showed that SP1 lacking three zinc fingers in M2 macrophages could not further induce the activation of the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in fibroblasts (Figure 4H).

Specific degradation of SP1 protein significantly inhibits fibrosis of ligamentum flavum in rats

We first improved the rat model of ligamentum flavum fibrosis, which resulted in more prominent structural features and a wider range of macrophage infiltration (Figures 5A and 5B). Momordicoside G selectively induces apoptosis in M1 macrophages without affecting M2 macrophages.24 Plicamycin can degrade transcription factor SP1 through a proteasome-dependent pathway.25 We stimulated the injured ligamentum flavum by local subcutaneous injection. Our results showed that the model group’s ligaments became disorganized and contained a significant number of empty bubbles, exhibiting fiber degeneration. The application of momordicoside G intensified this change, whereas the application of plicamycin reversed the effects of momordicoside G (Figure 5B). The more detailed structure showed the crosslinking of collagen and elastin in each group at a microscopic level. We can see that the model group had a rich accumulation of collagen, which was further intensified by momordicoside G, whereas plicamycin significantly reversed this disorderly accumulation and crosslinking (Figure 5C). According to Masson staining, the collagen volume fraction of the ligament significantly increased after treatment with momordicoside G, whereas it decreased significantly after treatment with plicamycin (Figure 5D).

In order to clarify the accumulation of extracellular matrix in various groups of the ligament, we used antibodies against collagen I and III to detect their expression in the yellow ligament. In order to exclude non-specific staining, we constructed a negative control group. Our results showed that collagen I and III proteins were mainly expressed at the transitional zone between the ligament and bone. Momordicoside G increased the expression of collagen I and III proteins in the injured yellow ligament, whereas plicamycin treatment significantly reduced the expression of collagen I and III proteins in the ligament (Figure 5E).

Subsequently, we used immunofluorescence to localize SP1 and M2 macrophages marker in tissue slices. We found that SP1 in the injured ligament was mainly co-localized with CD163-labeled macrophages, mainly in the cytoplasm and nucleus. The application of momordicoside G significantly increased the co-localization degree of SP1 and CD163, whereas after plicamycin treatment, only a large number of CD163-labeled macrophages were observed in the ligament, and no significant SP1 expression was found (Figure 5F).

Discussion

Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) is mainly secreted by M2 macrophages and has long been considered to actively participate in the growth and development of various tissues and organs, mediate tissue repair balance, and participate in immune surveillance. Excessive expression of TGF-β can lead to metabolic disorders and functional impairments in tissues or organs, resulting in immune dysfunction, fibrosis, and even cancer.26,27 Meanwhile, fibrotic tissues have a natural barrier function that reduces the sensitivity to drug treatment. Macrophages act as sentinels for maintaining the balance within the body and are essentially programmed to guide the healing and repair process that occurs after injury. However, under pathological conditions such as fibrosis and cancer, dysfunctional macrophages derived from monocytes play a crucial harmful role overall.2 At the same time, macrophages are also plastic and can be "re-educated" to exert anti-fibrotic and anti-cancer functions, which makes them important therapeutic targets for fibrotic and even cancer diseases.2

Here, we elucidate the reciprocal activation between fibroblasts and M2 macrophages during tissue repair processes, utilizing fibroblasts from various organizations. Meanwhile, our evidence suggests that the transcription factor SP1 of M2 macrophages co-cultured with fibroblasts can directly bind to the motif region of the TGF-β1 gene promoter, thereby promoting the synthesis and paracrine secretion of TGF-β1, which in turn further activates the fibroblast TGF-β1/Smad pathway, forming a positive feedback mechanism related to fibrosis. In contrast, inhibiting the function of SP1 can effectively reduce the activation of fibroblasts (Figure 6). These data deepen our understanding of the crosstalk between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts.

Schematic diagram of crosstalk between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts

The transcription factor SP1 directly binds to the binding element of the TGF-β1 promoter to promote the expression of TGF-β1. TGF-β1 further activates fibroblasts to perform tissue remodeling functions, thereby promoting fibrosis.

Fibroblasts are structural cells, which are the most common cell type in connective tissue. One key function of fibroblasts is to secrete multiple cytokines or chemokines into the ECM, acting as a landmark and scaffold in the tissue, providing channels or "roads" for other cells to navigate and migrate in the tissue.28 When the tissue is injured, fibroblasts sense the change of tension in the microenvironment and migrate from the normal area with strong adhesion to the injured area with weak adhesion to assist in tissue closure.29 Fibroblast cells cultured in vitro exhibit strong migration and secretion abilities may be due to the loss of their original tissue tension,30,31 which is similar to the status of fibroblast cells in injured areas. Our data indicate that co-culturing fibroblasts activated the expression of TGF-β1 in M2 macrophages, which may be the beginning of the fibrosis positive feedback mechanism.

In a more in-depth analysis of upstream mechanisms, we found that the expression of TGF-β1 is greatly influenced by the direct regulation of the transcription factor SP1. In fact, there is already some evidence showing the regulatory role of SP1 in fibrosis and related diseases. Research has shown that SP1 can activate the TGF-β1/Smad pathway in cardiac muscle to promote atrial fibrosis32; SP1 can also be recruited to the promoter of integrin β2 (ITGB2) to promote adhesion between macrophages and endothelial cells.33 A bioinformatics analysis on benign fibrous proliferative skin tumors (also known as keloids) also suggested that SP1 may be a core gene involved in regulating scar formation.34 Our data suggest that SP1 can be recruited to the promoter motif sequence of TGF-β1 in M2 macrophages (188-197 nt upstream of the TGF-β1 transcription start site) to promote the transcription and translation of TGF-β1 (without affecting the methylation status of the CpG island in the TGF-β1 promoter motif sequence). This in turn stimulates the expression of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway in fibroblasts through paracrine signaling. In the in vivo experiments on rats, the expression of SP1 protein and collagen I and III proteins is concentrated in the transitional zone between ligaments and bones, consistent with the infiltrating direction of macrophages. This suggests that the accumulation of ECM starts from the base, whereas the already formed fibrotic tissue does not undergo significant changes.

The successful use of Plicamycin highlights targeting SP1 as a potential means of inhibiting fibrosis. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the intricate and multifaceted role of the transcription factor SP1 in cellular biology. SP1 is known to be overexpressed in various diseases, including many cancers and connective tissue disorders such as atherosclerosis, tissue calcification, and cardiac hypertrophy, often associated with poor prognosis.35

To advance our understanding and develop more effective treatment strategies, future research should delve deeper into the intricate dialogue between M2 macrophages and fibroblasts within the context of fibrosis. Exploring the broader network of signaling pathways and potential compensatory mechanisms involving SP1 and TGFβ in various cell types could uncover novel therapeutic targets and strategies for mitigating fibrotic disorders.

Limitations of study

It is essential to consider the potential off-target effects and systemic implications of SP1 inhibition, especially given that Plicamycin may influence other cell types beyond those investigated in this study. At the same time, our study did not further analyze the DNA-binding domain of the transcription factor SP1 that binds TGF. It is worth noting that various cells in the microenvironment can also produce TGFβ, underscoring the complexity of intercellular interactions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient samples

Ligament specimens were collected from six patients with lumbar spinal stenosis with hypertrophic ligamentum flavum and four healthy controls at Wangjing Hospital of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Beijing, China) from November 2021 to February 2022 (Table S1). Fibrotic ligaments were defined as those thicker than 4 mm based on T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging of the lumbar spine.36 The collection and analysis of human samples in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wangjing Hospital of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (WJEC-YJS-2020-009-P002).

Cell lines

Human monocytes (THP-1, cat# 4201HUM-CCTCC00100), human skin fibroblasts (CCC-ESF-1, cat# 3101HUMSCSP109) and human embryonic kidney T cells (293T, cat# 1101HUM-PUMC000010) were purchased from the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Human ligament fibroblasts cells (cat# HUM-iCell-s009) and human Lung Fibroblasts (HFL1, cat# iCell-h017) were purchased from iCell Bioscience Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). We refer to Human ligament fibroblasts cells, human Lung Fibroblasts and human skin fibroblasts as LGF, LF and SF respectively.

Rat

Twenty four male Sprague-Dawley rats, each 8 weeks old and weighing about 250 g, were obtained from Beijing Longan Experimental Animal Breeding Center (Beijing, China) and housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment on a 12-h light/dark cycle. All experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Longan Experimental Animal Breeding Center (BJLongan-L-L-003) and were conducted in accordance with the “Guidelines for the humane care and use of laboratory animals” of the US National Institutes of Health and ARRIVE guidelines.37 Specific experimental steps are described in the method details section.

Method details

Rat model of ligament fibrosis and sample size estimation

Ligament fibrosis was induced in rats (designated as “model” animals) using an acupuncture method based on one that we previously described but with some improvements.38 Rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and fixed on a mouse board in a prone position. The level of the L5/6 ligamentum flavum was located using the bilateral anterior superior iliac crest connection. The back was shaved and disinfected with povidone iodine, then the skin and subcutaneous fascia were incised along the midline of the waist, and the multifidus muscle was stripped along the L5/6 laminae. The L5 spinous process was separated from the root using olecranon forceps, the laminae were flushed with a large amount of saline, and the L4 spinous process was gently lifted using needle-nose forceps to expand the interlaminar space and reveal the white, stretched, left and right ligamentum flavum. The flavum was lightly punctured five times using a 1-ml syringe needle at a 45° angle, to a depth of approximately 1 mm. Control animals underwent only skin incision and exposure of the L5/6 lamina. Model and control animals were subcutaneously injected with 0.5ml Momordicoside G (MDG, 40 μM, cat# HY-N3248, MedChemExpress, Beijing, China) alone or together with 0.5ml Plicamycin (Pli, 2.3 mM, cat# HY-A0122, MedChemExpress, Beijing, China) through the original surgical incision at four time points: immediately, 9, 18, and 27 days after surgery. Each of the four groups contained 6 animals. At 30 days after surgery, all animals were sacrificed with a lethal dose of pentobarbital, and tissue samples were collected and stored at -80°C for subsequent analysis (See Figure S6 for modeling process).

The sample size estimation was performed using a t-test based on prior research findings.39 With α set at 0.05 for a two-tailed test, Zα was 1.96, and a power of 0.9 corresponded to Zβ of 1.28. In this context, σ represented the standard deviation, and δ denoted the difference between the mean values of the treatment and control groups. Based on previous research, σ was assumed to be 0.3, and δ was assumed to be 0.75.38 Consequently, the estimated sample size was approximately n ≈ 4. The experimental protocol is well-established, with a minimal mortality rate among the experimental animals. Adhering to the principles of the 3R's for animal welfare and cost control, each experimental group will comprise 6 normal rat.

Bioinformatics

Data comparing gene expression between fibrotic and normal ligament samples were extracted from the GSE113212 dataset,16 and analyzed using the following packages in R (version 4.2.1): GEOquery (version 2.64.2), limma (3.52.2), ggplot2 (3.3.6), and Complex Heatmap (2.13.1). Data were normalized using the “Normalize Between Arrays” function in limma, and probes that detected multiple genes were removed. When multiple probes detected the same gene, only the probe giving the strongest signal was retained. Differentially expressed genes were identified using limma, then visualized using volcano maps and heat maps (Figure S2).

Potential transcription factors binding within 2000 bp upstream of the promoter of the gene encoding TGF-β1 (region c41355922-41353922 on chrosome 19, NCBI accession code NC_000019.10) were predicted using the UCSC database.40 A minimum of 500 score was applied as a threshold, and only transcription factors whose binding orientation would support transcription in the promoter’s direction were considered. In the search process, the activity level of histone epigenetic regulatory modifications corresponding to the gene locus was taken into account.41 qPCR analysis is used to confirm the accuracy of predicted results (Figure S3).

THP-1 cells induce

Protocol for inducing THP-1 cells to differentiate into M2 macrophages: THP-1 cells were differentiated into M0 macrophages by incubating them for 48 h in complete 1640 medium containing PMA (100 ng/ml, cat# P6741, Solarbio). Then the M0 macrophages were differentiated into M2 macrophages by incubating them for 48 h in medium containing IL-4 (20 ng/ml, cat# P00021, Solarbio) and IL-13 (20 ng/ml, cat# P00131, Solarbio). The results of cell differentiation were confirmed by qPCR and cellular immunofluorescence.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (cat# G3322-01, Servicebio), and an aliquot (1 μg) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit (G3330-100, Servicebio). The cDNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using the primers in Table S2 and the SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (G3320, Servicebio).

Histology

After sacrifice of rats, the lumbar spine was decalcified and incised coronally near the transverse process, revealing the white ligamentum flavum in the posterior column at the junction of the two laminae. The ligament was cut again along the sagittal midline, and one half was embedded in paraffin, while the other half was flash-frozen at -80°C for future use. Human ligament tissue samples were not decalcified. Rat or human tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, then stained with hematoxylin-eosin (cat# G1076, Servicebio) or Masson stain (G1006, Servicebio), or processed for immunohistochemistry as described below.

For immunohistochemistry, antigens in tissue samples were recovered by incubating them in 20× Tris-EDTA repair solution (pH 8.0, cat# G1206, Servicebio), then blocking non-specific binding sites with bovine serum albumin (GC305010, Servicebio). Sections were incubated with the following rabbit polyclonal primary antibodies at 4°C: anti-collagen I (1:1000, cat# GB114197, Servicebio), anti-collagen III (1:500, GB111629, Servicebio), anti-TGF-β1 (1:1000, GB11179, Servicebio), anti-SP1 (1:200, PA3320, Abmart, Shanghai, China), anti-CD163 (1:500, TD8235, Abmart), anti-CD11b (1:500, T55208, Abmart), or anti-vimentin (1:1500, GB111308, Servicebio). Sections were then incubated with species-appropriate secondary antibodies (1:200, GB23302, Servicebio) conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (immunohistochemistry) or a fluorescent dye (immunofluoresence).

Scanning electron microscopy

Ligament tissue from humans or rats was obtained according to the previously reported protocol. Tissue blocks were washed three times (15 min each) with 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4), incubated for 1-2 h at room temperature in 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4), washed again three times (15 min each) in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4), then dried in a Critical Point Dryer (cat# K850, Quorum, USA). Specimens were attached to metallic stubs using carbon stickers, sputter-coated with gold for 30 sec, and analyzed under a scanning electron microscope (cat# SU8100, hitachi, Japan).

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed using standard techniques and the following primary antibodies: polyclonal anti-TGF-β1 (1:1000, cat# GB111876, Servicebio), rabbit monoclonal anti-collagen I (1:5000, cat# ab138492, Abcam, Shanghai, China), monoclonal anti-collagen III (1:5000, cat# ab184993, Abcam), polyclonal anti-SP1 (1:2000, cat# T56610, Abmart), polyclonal anti-LOXL2 (1:2000, cat# T57106, Abmart), monoclonal anti-Smad2/3 (1:1000, cat# GB111844, Servicebio), anti-phoshpho-Smad2/3 (1:1000, cat# SAB4504208, Sigma-Aldrich), polyclonal anti-Smad4 (1:2000, cat# 10231-1-AP, Proteintech), polyclonal anti-Smad7 (1:2000, cat# 25840-1-AP, Proteintech), polyclonal anti-Lamin B1(1:5000, cat# 12987-1-AP, Proteintech), polyclonal anti-GAPDH(1:5000, cat# 10494-1-AP, Proteintech).

Transwell assays

In transwell assays (8-μm pores, cat# WG3422, Servicebio), fibroblasts were seeded into the upper chamber and M2 macrophages into the lower chamber. The medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM, with serum-free DMEM used only in assays of fibroblast migration. In some experiments, M2 macrophages were first cultured in 6-well plates and subjected to transfection or drug treatment, then they were transferred into transwell chambers in fresh medium. In co-culture assays (3-μm pores transwell chamber, cat# WG3415, Servicebio), M2 macrophages were co-cultured with fibroblasts for 4 days. In some experiments, macrophages were treated with the KRFK (50 μM, cat# 162290-78-0, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China), and fibroblasts were treated with CJJ300 (40 μM, cat# HY-146693, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China).

Cell proliferation assay

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (cat# CA1215, Solarbio, China) was used to determine cell proliferation. M2 macrophages were subjected to pre-treatment in T25 culture flasks, which included drug stimulation and plasmid transfection. Subsequently, they were washed with PBS and transferred to the lower compartment of Transwell inserts. Similarly, fibroblasts were cultured separately in T25 culture flasks, washed with PBS, and transferred to the upper compartment of Transwell inserts. In the Transwell co-culture system, fibroblasts were cultured either alone, stimulated with TGF-β1, or co-cultured with M2 macrophages for 0, 2, or 4 days. A suspension of fibroblasts (200 μL, containing 2000 cells per well) was seeded into a 96-well plate. The cells adhered to the plate and were starved overnight in serum-free culture medium. The Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm by a microplate reader.

Collagen I, collagen III and TGF-β1 assay

After the treatment, the cell culture supernatants from both individual and co-cultured cells were collected for analysis. The concentrations of collagen I (cat# SEKH-0401, Solarbio, Beijing, China), polyclonal anti-collagen III (cat# 22734-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1, cat# PT880, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in the culture medium were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (SoftMax Pro version 5 Software, Molecular Devices).

Plasmid construction and transfection

A plasmid overexpressing human SP1 was prepared by cloning the coding sequence (GenBank ID: NM_138473) into the vector GV657 (Shanghai Jikai Gene Chemical Technology, Shanghai, China) via the BamHI and KpnI restriction sites. Plasmids containing short guide sequences to knock down endogenous human SP1 were prepared by cloning the guide sequences into the vector GV464 (Shanghai Jikai Gene Chemical Technology, Shanghai, China) via the BbsI restriction site. The sequence was designed by Shanghai Jikai Gene Chemistry Technology Co., Ltd (Table S3). Plasmids were transfected into M2 macrophages using Lipofectamine 3000 (cat# L3000015, Invitrogen, Ca, USA), and SP1 expression was verified by PCR.

Stable cell line generation

The GV492-mut-TGFB1 recombinant lentivirus or GV492-SP1ΔZn recombinant lentivirus was purchased from Shanghai Jikai Gene Chemical Technology (Shanghai, China). M2 macrophages were infected with GV492-mut-TGFB1 or GV492-SP1ΔZn. Selective culture medium containing puromycin was used to select the cells with stable expression of mut-TGFB1 or SP1ΔZn.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

M2 macrophages or 293T cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of at least 1×105 cells per well, then transfected with psiCHECK2 plasmid (Beijing Bomeide Gene Technology, Beijing, China) containing wild-type or mutant sequences of the TGF-β1 promoter (supplemental information Luciferase reporters). For transfection, 2 μl of plasmid was mixed with 2 μl of Lipofectamine 3000 in 50 μl of serum-free medium, the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min, then it was added to cells. At 48 h after transfection, cell lysates were prepared and added to black microtiter plates, and activities of firefly and Renilla luciferases were quantified using the Dual-Lumi™ Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (cat# RG088M, Beyotime).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative PCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed with 2 × 106 cells per reaction, a rabbit monoclonal antibody against SP1 (cat# ab231778, Abcam) and a commercial assay (cat# P2078, Beyotime). The precipitated DNA was analyzed using quantitative PCR (Table S4).

DNA methylation analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from M2 macrophage using a commercial kit (cat# G3633, Servicebio) and treated with sodium bisulfate using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (cat# D5005, Zymo Research, Ca, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Each DNA sample was subjected to three separate bisulfite modifications. PCR was performed in 50-μL reactions containing 2 μL of genomic DNA; 1 μM each of TGFB1F and TGFB1R primers, which were designed using MethPrimer software and Fast Pfus PCR Master Mix (cat# G3305, Servicebio). All PCR products were subcloned into pSWE-Topo Zero kit (cat# G3022, Servicebio). A subset of positive clones was randomly selected for sequencing.

Micro-computed tomography

Rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital and the ligamentum flavum was imaged using a small-animal micro-computed tomography system provided by Dr. Wang Zhe of the Institute of High Energy Physics at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). Data were analyzed using Image J (version 1.8.0, US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistics

Results were reported as mean ± SD and analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism 8 (version 8.0.2). Pairwise differences were assessed for significance using two-tailed Student’s t tests, while differences among at least three groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA. The correlation analysis of immunofluorescence was performed using the color2 and colocalization plugins of Image J software (version 1.8.0). For all tests, a p value less than 0.05 was considered significantly different (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
