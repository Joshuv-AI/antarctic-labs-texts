Lycopene supplementation during pregnancy and lactation attenuates thermally oxidized cooking oil-induced hepatic and cerebral damage in rat offspring: A comprehensive histomorphometric and molecular analysis
Authors: Ahmad Aulia Jusuf, Fadilah, Nimas Ayu Nirwanti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThermally oxidized cooking oil (TOCO) generates reactive oxygen species and lipid peroxidation products that cross the placental barrier, posing risks to offspring organ development in low-to-middle income countries where cooking oil reuse is endemic. Lycopene, a potent carotenoid antioxidant, possesses neuroprotective and hepatoprotective properties, yet its capacity to prevent maternal-to-offspring oxidative damage through maternal supplementation remains unexplored.MethodsTwenty-four female Sprague Dawley rats were randomized into six groups (n = 4 dams each): Control, CMC vehicle, TOCO1 × , TOCO3 × , TOCO1 × + Lycopene, and TOCO3 × + Lycopene. Lycopene (0.24 mg/kg) was administered via oral gavage from gestational day 1 through postnatal day 14 (total duration: 35 days encompassing pregnancy and early lactation). Dam was used as the experimental unit for all inferential statistics to avoid pseudo-replication. Statistical analysis employed Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, effect size estimation (Cohen’s d with 95% CI), and post-hoc power analysis.ResultsTOCO3 × exposure significantly reduced normal hippocampal CA1 neurons (60% vs 85% controls; Cohen’s d=2.2, p < 0.05), cerebellar Purkinje cells (55% vs 90%; d=2.5, p < 0.05), and increased hepatocyte damage (27% vs 8%; d=2.8, p < 0.05). Lycopene supplementation provided substantial protection: hippocampal recovery to 75% (PI 60%), cerebellar recovery to 78% (PI 66%), liver recovery to 15% damaged (PI 63%), with NNT of 4–5. Biochemical markers (MDA, GSH) showed non-significant trends (post-hoc power 15–42%). Caspase-3 expression showed dose-dependent elevation with TOCO and suppression with lycopene.ConclusionsMaternal lycopene supplementation provides significant multi-organ protection against TOCO-induced developmental toxicity. These findings are supported by convergent evidence from histopathology (d=2.2–2.8), molecular markers (caspase-3 dose-response), and exposure characterization, consistent with oxidative stress-mediated mechanisms. The translational HED (2.7 mg/day from 150 g fresh tomatoes) supports investigation of dietary lycopene as a public health intervention for pregnant women in TOCO-endemic populations.

Introduction

Approximately 800 million people in low-to-middle income countries (LMICs) face chronic exposure to thermally oxidized cooking oil through dietary sources [1], [2]. In Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, and the Philippines, street food vendors and home cooks routinely reheat and reuse cooking oil 5–10 times due to economic constraints, despite well-documented health risks [1], [2], [3].

Thermal oxidation of cooking oil occurs at temperatures between 160 and 200°C during repeated frying cycles, inducing hydrolysis, oxidation, and polymerization [3], [4]. These processes generate free fatty acids, lipid hydroperoxides, secondary oxidation products including MDA and 4-HNE, and polymeric compounds [4], [5]. The formation of these reactive lipid-derived aldehydes creates a bioavailable mixture capable of crossing the placental barrier [5], [6].

The developing fetus and neonate occupy a uniquely vulnerable position regarding oxidative stress due to their high metabolic activity, elevated PUFA content, and immature antioxidant defense systems [7], [8]. Within the developing brain, the hippocampus and cerebellum show particular susceptibility [8], [9]. Maternal exposure to oxidized lipids has been linked to developmental neurotoxicity and hepatotoxicity in offspring [6], [10], involving NF-κB inflammatory amplification [11], [12] and Bcl−2/Bax apoptotic progression [13], [14].

Lycopene is a C40 acyclic carotenoid with exceptional singlet oxygen quenching—approximately 1.9-fold superior to β-carotene [15], [16]. Recent studies have demonstrated lycopene’s protective effects across multiple organ systems: hepatoprotection via Nrf2-mediated antioxidant enzyme induction [36], neuroprotection through attenuation of neuroinflammation [37], nephroprotection against ischemia-reperfusion injury [38], cardioprotection through modulation of endothelial function [39], and reproductive organ protection [40]. Its antioxidant mechanisms include Nrf2/Keap1/ARE axis activation [17], [18], NF-κB inhibition [11], [19], mitochondrial membrane stabilization [13], [20], and BDNF/TrkB signaling enhancement [21], [22]. No published work has examined lycopene’s maternal-to-offspring protective capacity through maternal supplementation during pregnancy and lactation [23], [24].

Our primary objective was to determine whether maternal lycopene supplementation protects offspring against TOCO-induced hepatic and cerebral damage. We hypothesized that TOCO exposure would induce dose-dependent damage to hippocampal CA1, cerebellar Purkinje, and hepatic tissues, and that maternal lycopene would attenuate this damage through multiple antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms. TOCO1 × and TOCO3 × were selected because TOCO3 × already exceeded the SNI peroxide value threshold by 111%, representing the lower exposure spectrum where public health intervention is most feasible, while adhering to the 3Rs principle [26].

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was approved by the IACUC of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia, and conducted in accordance with Indonesian animal welfare law and ARRIVE guidelines [26]. All procedures adhered to the 3Rs principle.

Preparation and characterization of thermally oxidized cooking oil

Palm cooking oil (commercial-grade Bimoli brand, PT Salim Ivomas Pratama, Indonesia) was heated to 180°C in stainless steel pans with calibrated digital thermometer monitoring (±1°C) and fresh tempeh (∼200 g per 1 L oil) was fried for ∼15 min per cycle [1], [3]. For TOCO1 × , one cycle. For TOCO3 × , three cycles separated by 24-hour rest periods in light-protected glass bottles. No fresh oil was added between cycles. Oil characterization included FFA (AOAC 940.28) and PV (AOAC 965.33) in triplicate [27].

Animal model and housing

Twenty-four female Sprague Dawley rats (10–12 weeks, 180–220 g) were randomized into six groups (n = 4 dams/group): Control, CMC vehicle, TOCO1 × (5 mL/kg), TOCO3 × (5 mL/kg), TOCO1 × + Lyc, TOCO3 × + Lyc. TOCO was administered by oral gavage at 5 mL/kg daily from GD1–PND14, corresponding to ∼50–70 mL/day in humans—within the range of daily cooking oil consumption in Southeast Asian households [1], [3]. Both Control and CMC groups were included to verify absence of vehicle effects [28], [30].

Lycopene supplementation protocol

Lycopene (≥95% purity, Sigma-Aldrich #L9879) was administered at 0.24 mg/kg/day in 1% CMC. This dose was calculated via Reagan-Shaw allometric formula [25]: HED = Animal dose × (K_rat/K_human) = 0.24 × (5.6/37) = 0.039 mg/kg, yielding 2.7 mg/day for a 70-kg adult—achievable from ∼150 g fresh tomatoes [36], [37], [38]. Administration from GD1–PND14 (∼35 days total) covers neural tube closure (GD10–11), hippocampal neurogenesis (GD14–21), cerebellar development (through PND14), and hepatogenesis [23], [24].

Sample collection and tissue preparation

On PND14, offspring (2 pups/dam, n = 8/group) were anesthetized (ketamine 80 mg/kg + xylazine 8 mg/kg i.p.), perfused transcardially with saline and 10% formalin. Tissues were fixed and processed for histology or snap-frozen for gene expression [29].

Histological assessment

Serial Section (5 μm) were stained with H&E. All analyses were performed by a blinded observer (NAN); slides were coded by a second investigator (F). ICC > 0.90 on 20% subset verified by AAJ. Three fields/section, three sections/animal, ≥ 100 cells/field at 400 × . Morphologically normal neurons: intact soma, pale nucleus with nucleolus, preserved dendrites, no pyknosis. Damaged neurons: pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis, eosinophilia, or dendritic loss. Hepatocyte damage: hydropic degeneration, vacuolization, nuclear condensation, or necrosis [29], [30]. H&E assessment is consistent with OECD guidance No. 125; Nissl staining and NeuN immunohistochemistry are recommended for future studies.

Biochemical assessment

MDA: TBARS assay at 532 nm. GSH: Ellman reagent (DTNB) at 412 nm. Normalized to protein (Bradford) [31].

Gene expression analysis

Caspase−3 qRT-PCR using SYBR Green. Caspase−3 was selected as the convergence-point executioner caspase of both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways [14], [32]. Primers: Caspase−3 F: 5’-TGGAATTGATGCGTGATGTG−3’, R: 5’-TGCATGATCTCCACTGCTTT−3’; β-actin F: 5’-CCACCATGTACCCAGGCATT−3’, R: 5’-CGGACTCATCGTACTCCTGC−3’. Livak 2^(−ΔΔCt) method [32], [33]. Comprehensive apoptosis panel (Bcl−2, Bax, caspase−9, TUNEL) recommended for future work.

Statistical analysis

Sample size for d≥ 0.8, 80% power, α= 0.05 [28], [34]. Dam was used as experimental unit for all inferential statistics; pup values averaged per dam (n = 4 dams/group). Individual pup values (n = 8/group) reported for descriptive statistics, consistent with ICH S5(R3) [41]. Kruskal-Wallis with pairwise Mann-Whitney U, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction [35]. Cohen’s d with 95% CI via bootstrap (10,000 resamples). Post-hoc power via G*Power 3.1.9.7. NNT = 1/|p1 − p2|. R version 4.1.0 [28], [34]. No significant differences between Control and CMC for any parameter (all p > 0.05); Control used as reference.

Discussion

Hypothesis evaluation: dose-dependent TOCO damage

Our first hypothesis—dose-dependent damage—was strongly supported. Cohen’s d= 2.2–2.8 for histomorphometric outcomes indicate substantial biological significance [28], [34]. The regional vulnerability hierarchy (Purkinje d=2.5 > CA1 d=2.2 > cortex d=1.8) aligns with known differences in metabolic rate and antioxidant capacity [7], [8], [9]. Hepatic findings (d=2.8) show equivalent vulnerability, with damage consistent with oxidative injury and early apoptosis [13], [14].

Hypothesis evaluation: lycopene multi-target protection

The protection hypothesis was supported by histomorphometric recovery (PI 60–66%) and caspase−3 suppression, though MDA/GSH did not reach significance. Lycopene’s mechanisms include singlet oxygen quenching [15], [16], Nrf2/ARE activation [17], [18], NF-κB inhibition [11], [19]. Published evidence suggests mitochondrial membrane stabilization [13], [20], indirectly supported by our caspase−3 data. Direct measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential was not performed and represents a future priority.

Interpretation of non-significant biochemical biomarkers

The discordance between histomorphometric and biochemical results is attributable to: (1) low power (15–25%) for d= 0.3–0.5 at n = 4 dams/group; (2) MDA transient half-life and single-timepoint sampling; (3) whole-tissue homogenate diluting regional damage; (4) histomorphometry is more sensitive than systemic biomarkers for developmental toxicity per OECD guidance [30]. Future studies should use region-specific microdissection and multiple timepoints.

Caspase−3 (d=0.8) showed clear dose-responsiveness. The concordance between caspase−3 upregulation and histomorphometric apoptotic features provides convergent evidence. Comprehensive Bcl−2/Bax, caspase−9, and TUNEL measurement is recommended [14], [32].

The intercorrelation matrix (Fig. 9) shows r > 0.75 among hippocampal, cerebellar, and hepatocyte damage scores, suggesting shared oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis mechanisms across organs. Parallel dose-response patterns further support this. Moderate negative correlations with MDA are consistent with, though not proof of, oxidative stress mediation.

Translational implications and bioavailability

The HED of 2.7 mg/day is readily achievable [15], [16], [25]. Fresh tomato bioavailability is 10–30% but enhanced 2–3 × by cooking with fat—precisely how tomatoes are consumed in Southeast Asian TOCO-endemic cuisines (sambal, curry, stir-fry), yielding ∼2.3–4.5 mg absorbed from 150 g [36], [37], [38]. However, pregnancy reduces plasma lycopene due to hemodilution, suggesting higher intake may be needed [41].

Limitations

Limitations include: (1) rat model limits human extrapolation [8], [9]; Reagan-Shaw scaling does not account for species-specific pharmacokinetics [25], [42]; (2) PND14 assessment misses long-term outcomes [7]; (3) n = 4 dams/group underpowered for biochemical markers (power 15–42%; n ≈ 64 needed for 80% power); (4) single lycopene dose; (5) only caspase−3 measured—Bcl−2/Bax, caspase−9, TUNEL recommended; (6) no Nissl staining—recommended for future work with NeuN IHC; (7) oil characterization limited to FFA/PV—polar compounds, p-anisidine recommended; (8) no synergistic micronutrient assessment [23], [24].

Conclusion

Maternal lycopene supplementation provides significant multi-organ protection against TOCO-induced developmental toxicity. Addressing our primary hypothesis: TOCO produced dose-dependent damage to hippocampal CA1 (d=2.2), cerebellar Purkinje (d=2.5), and hepatocytes (d=2.8)—fully supporting the dose-dependent damage hypothesis. Lycopene provided PI 60–66%, NNT 4–7, supporting the multi-target protection hypothesis. While MDA/GSH were non-significant due to insufficient power, convergent evidence from histopathology, molecular markers (caspase−3), and exposure characterization supports oxidative stress-mediated apoptotic mechanisms. The achievable HED (2.7 mg/day from 150 g tomato) supports investigation as a low-cost public health intervention. Future studies should examine behavioral outcomes, dose-response, comprehensive apoptosis markers, Nissl staining, multiple timepoints, and human interventional studies.
