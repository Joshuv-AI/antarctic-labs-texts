AI-powered spectral CT analysis for clinical decision support in carotid vulnerable plaque detection: a deep learning approach

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Carotid vulnerable plaques (CVPs) are a leading cause of ischemic stroke [1]. Accurate early diagnosis and effective prognosis prediction are crucial for reducing stroke risk and improving patient outcomes [2]. However, current diagnostic methods remain inadequate. Conventional imaging techniques, including ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), can identify the presence of plaques but often fail to determine plaque vulnerability or predict future progression with sufficient precision [3, 4]. This limitation hinders clinical decision-making and therapeutic planning. Therefore, the development of more accurate and efficient diagnostic and prognostic tools is urgently needed to enhance clinical management and improve treatment outcomes.

In recent years, computed tomography (CT)-based imaging has become an essential approach for evaluating carotid plaques and assessing stroke risk. Spectral CT enables clinicians to identify imaging features associated with plaque vulnerability, including low attenuation regions that indicate lipid cores, high-density calcifications, internal heterogeneity and morphological alterations such as surface irregularities and fibrous cap rupture. Additionally, textural characteristics, such as complex surface structures and asymmetric boundaries, are also strongly associated with high risk plaques. These features frequently appear on CT scans as areas of low attenuation, focal hyperdensity, indistinct margins or mixed density regions. Previous studies [5, 6] have reviewed the associations between these imaging features and plaque vulnerability, highlighting the importance of systematic identification for risk stratification and precision treatment.

AI technologies, particularly deep learning (DL), have achieved notable progress in medical image analysis [7]. DL models can automatically learn and extract high-dimensional features from imaging data, enabling precise classification and predictive modeling. The application of DL offers effective solutions to major challenges in the diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of CVP. Earlier research has validated the performance of DL-based imaging models across multiple diseases, including cancer and cardiovascular disorders. The present study aims to establish an AI predictive framework based on DL to improve the accuracy and reliability of CVP diagnosis and prognostic assessment.

Several studies have explored the application of DL techniques for identifying and classifying carotid plaques [8, 9]. Researchers have primarily used two-dimensional CT, computed tomography angiography (CTA), or MRI and have adopted architectures such as U-Net, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and residual networks (ResNets) for plaque segmentation, component analysis, and risk prediction [8–11]. For example, previous investigations have examined automated grading or recognition of plaque calcification or plaque types to support the assessment of stroke-related risk [11, 12]. Other studies have developed multimodal, multi-source models for intelligent stroke risk assessment and prognosis prediction [10]. Most existing studies have focused on relatively simple two-dimensional imaging and limited feature dimensions. Some studies lack support from three-dimensional imaging or do not explicitly use frequency-domain information to extract plaque texture and edge features [8, 13, 14]. The present study expands upon earlier work by integrating a Fourier Transform Convolution (FTC) module and combining spectral CT imaging with structural and textural analysis to enhance segmentation precision and risk prediction. A detailed comparison with related studies is presented in Table S1 [15–17].

The spectral CT dataset analyzed in the study was obtained from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical College. Imaging was performed using a GE Revolution CT scanner, a 256-detector-row wide-coverage CT system with 160 mm z-axis coverage and a rotation time of 0.28 s. The system supports rapid kVp-switching dual-energy acquisition, allowing reconstruction of spectral image datasets for attenuation- and material-based tissue characterization. GSI Assist, an automatic exposure control system for dual-energy acquisitions on the GE Revolution CT platform, was used to support protocol setup and exposure modulation [18]. Spectral CT post-processing can generate virtual monochromatic images, material decomposition images, and virtual non-contrast images, which may improve tissue differentiation and quantitative lesion evaluation. Image post-processing was performed on the Advantage Workstation 4.7, which supports multiplanar reformatting and three-dimensional visualization.

The study introduces an enhanced U-Net architecture that incorporates FTC for arterial segmentation [19, 20]. The modification improves the model’s capacity to capture high-frequency image details, resulting in more precise delineation of arterial structures and plaques. In addition, a multi-stage prognosis prediction model was developed to extract relevant imaging features and achieve reliable prognostic evaluation [21]. Data augmentation and cross-validation techniques were also applied to improve model robustness and generalizability. Collectively, these methodological advances improve the accuracy and consistency of CVP diagnosis and prognosis prediction. The null hypothesis (H0) states that the improved Fourier U-Net model shows no significant difference in segmentation performance compared with conventional models such as the U-Net. The alternative hypothesis (H1) states that the Fourier U-Net achieves significantly higher segmentation accuracy, as measured by metrics such as Dice and intersection over union (IoU).

The main innovations of the study can be summarized in three aspects. First, most previous DL research on carotid plaques has emphasized two-dimensional structural or spatial-domain feature extraction, limiting the capacity to exploit frequency-domain information for accurate identification of arterial boundaries and plaque textures. The present work introduces an FTC module into spectral CT image segmentation, integrating spatial- and frequency-domain representations to substantially improve segmentation precision and boundary delineation of plaque regions. Second, an end-to-end framework that combines segmentation, feature extraction, and risk prediction is proposed. The framework enables automated plaque identification and stratified evaluation, while enhancing the interpretability and clinical practicality of the model. Finally, the proposed method is validated using a large-scale clinical spectral CT dataset, demonstrating robust generalization and providing new insights and methodological references for the application of spectral imaging in intelligent vascular disease diagnosis.

In conclusion, the study holds substantial scientific and clinical significance. The work aims to establish an effective approach for the early diagnosis and long-term prognostic assessment of vulnerable carotid plaques, with the potential to enhance patient management and improve overall quality of life. Furthermore, the findings may advance the application of AI in the diagnosis and treatment of vascular diseases. The expected outcomes are likely to exert a positive influence on clinical practice and offer valuable references for future research.

Materials and methods

Given the sequential nature of the analytical steps, an overview of the study design and analytical structure is provided in Figure S1 to guide readers through the overall workflow.

Collection of clinical spectral CT imaging data for CVP

Head and neck spectral CT imaging data were acquired using a GE Revolution 256-slice CT scanner at Qiqihar Medical College. The system provides rapid multi-energy imaging with high spatial resolution, enabling detailed vascular characterization. Image post-processing and analysis were conducted on the AW4.7 workstation, which supports advanced functions such as three-dimensional reconstruction (Fig. 1).

Visualization of the data collection process using the GE revolution 256-slice spectral CT scanner. Note: A GE Revolution 256-slice spectral CT scanner was used to perform plain CT and computed tomography angiography (CTA) examinations in the final cohort of 214 patients with carotid plaques. Representative images were processed on the AW4.7 workstation to generate spectral CT images and three-dimensional vascular reconstructions of the head and neck region

Patients who underwent carotid spectral CT examinations at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University between January 2019 and December 2023 were included. Inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of complete carotid CTA data; (2) imaging coverage extending from the aortic arch to the skull base with satisfactory quality and minimal motion artifacts. The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete imaging data or inadequate carotid segment coverage; (2) unsuccessful contrast-enhanced scans or insufficient vascular opacification; (3) severe metallic artifacts or image distortion; and (4) prior carotid stenting or significant anatomical alterations. A total of 214 patients met these criteria and were included in the final analysis.

The baseline characteristics of the patients were as follows. The mean age was 65.2 ± 9.1 years (range, 45–85 years), with 139 males (64.9%) and 75 females (35.1%). Hypertension was present in 128 patients (59.8%), diabetes in 64 (29.9%), hyperlipidemia in 95 (44.4%), and a smoking history in 76 (35.5%). Plaque distribution was 35.0% on the left side, 34.1% on the right side, and 30.9% bilaterally. Based on risk stratification, 72 patients (33.6%) were categorized as low risk, 71 (33.2%) as intermediate risk, and 71 (33.2%) as high risk. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table S2. The cohort reflects typical demographic and clinical features of patients with carotid plaques, indicating strong representativeness. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University (Approval No. [2024]101). All imaging and clinical data were anonymized before analysis, stored on secure servers, and accessed only by authorized research personnel. Given the retrospective and non-interventional design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and with national ethical regulations, ensuring full protection of patient privacy and data security.

For segmentation model development, contrast-enhanced arterial-phase CTA images were used as the primary input because they provide clearer visualization of the carotid lumen, arterial wall, and plaque boundaries. Non-contrast CT images were not used as direct segmentation inputs but were used for HU-based plaque characterization after registration to the CTA series. The final cohort included 214 patients. Each scan initially generated up to 561 axial spectral CT images covering the region from the aortic arch to the skull base. After quality control and selection of carotid-containing slices, approximately 400 slices per patient were used for model development, yielding approximately 85,600 carotid-region images in total. These images were divided at the patient level into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio, containing 68,480, 8,560, and 8,560 images, respectively. During cross-validation, the training data were repeatedly partitioned to generate ten independent evaluation sets, which improved the robustness and generalizability of the model. Two-dimensional axial images and corresponding mask labels were used for slice-by-slice segmentation, and the model generated pixel-level maps for vessels and plaques [22].

To strengthen interpretability, representative examples of three categories of vulnerable plaques identified from CT imaging are presented. Figure S2 illustrates low-risk (A), intermediate-risk (B), and high-risk (C) plaques, including features such as mild wall calcification, mixed-density plaques, and severely calcified complex plaques. The examples clarify the imaging characteristics used by the model during classification and provide clinicians with direct visual references that facilitate interpretation of the model outputs.

Quality control and preprocessing of spectral CT imaging data for cervical vulnerable plaques

A standardized preprocessing pipeline was established to ensure stability and reproducibility in model training. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images were exported from the picture archiving and communication system (PACS), and both non-contrast CT and CTA series were selected. Rigid registration based on mutual information was applied to align the two series within a unified coordinate system. For CTA volumes, Hounsfield units (HUs) were clipped to − 200 to 1000 and normalized to the range [0, 1]. Local contrast enhancement was achieved with contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) (clipLimit = 2.0, tileGridSize = 8 × 8). To reduce noise while preserving vascular boundaries, Gaussian filtering (5 × 5 kernel, σ = 1.0) was subsequently applied. All slices were resampled to a uniform resolution of 256 × 256, and masks were interpolated using nearest-neighbor sampling. A coarse segmentation was used to generate carotid artery regions of interest (ROIs), enabling precise cropping of the target area.

The plaque masks were manually delineated slice-by-slice in 3D Slicer by two experienced radiologists and reviewed by a third radiologist, achieving an inter-observer agreement of κ = 0.86. During model training, data augmentation strategies, including random rotation, scaling, flipping, mild elastic deformation, and gamma correction, were employed to improve model robustness. The dataset was divided at the patient level into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio to prevent information leakage. Images affected by severe artifacts or registration failure were manually inspected and excluded or reprocessed when necessary.

During preprocessing, histogram equalization was used to enhance local contrast and improve visualization of the vasculature and adjacent tissues, thereby making arterial structures more distinguishable. A Gaussian filter was subsequently applied to reduce high-frequency image noise and improve input consistency before segmentation. Because Gaussian filtering may also smooth fine edge details, a mild kernel setting was used to balance noise suppression and preservation of vascular boundary information. This step provided a more stable input for the downstream segmentation model, ensuring more reliable feature extraction and classification. Finally, all images were resampled to a unified resolution of 256 × 256 × 3 using OpenCV to facilitate efficient model training and convergence [23].

Meanwhile, following the arterial plaque classification strategy proposed by Zheng et al., which is based on the mean and standard deviation of arterial HU values [24], we applied the same classification approach to all clinical imaging data in this study. Classification criteria are summarized in Table 1. Statistical analysis of HU values was conducted across the entire arterial region. For each patient, the mean and variance of HU values were derived from 561 non-contrast CT slices to quantify the density distribution within the arterial wall and surrounding tissues. Construction of an arterial segmentation model based on FTC

This study aims to improve the segmentation accuracy of carotid arteries and plaque regions in spectral CT images, with emphasis on enhancing recognition of vascular boundaries and high-frequency plaque features. To achieve this objective, an FTC module was integrated into the classical U-Net framework to enable joint modeling of spatial-domain and frequency-domain information. This design increases the sensitivity of the network to subtle structural and textural variations. The overall architecture of the modified model and its segmentation performance are presented in Fig. 4.

Visualization of the segmentation model for head and neck arteries. Note: Panel (A) shows the encoder structure embedded with the Fourier Transform Convolution (FTC) module. Panel (B) presents the internal structure of the FTC module. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) component extracts frequency-domain information, as illustrated in panel (C), using computation units that combine high-pass and low-pass spectral filtering [27]. A 1 × 1 convolution operation (blue) enhances feature transmission and fusion after frequency-domain extraction. The inverse Fourier transform then returns the feature map to the spatial domain for further processing by the subsequent encoder. The decoder, shown in green and detailed in panel (D), performs upsampling and reconstruction to restore the original spatial resolution

In the proposed model, the FTC module is embedded within the middle layers of the U-Net encoder to reinforce high-frequency information related to arterial wall contours and plaque edges during multi-scale feature extraction. The module enhances boundary resolution and improves detection of complex plaque morphology without producing a substantial increase in inference time. The final model achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.89 and an IoU of 0.80 on the validation set, outperforming the standard U-Net (Dice 0.82, IoU 0.73). The improved performance provides high-quality inputs for subsequent plaque ROI construction and prognostic feature extraction. Details regarding the FTC module architecture, frequency-domain operations, parameter configuration, and efficiency assessment are provided in Supplementary Method 1.

Constructing a prognostic model for CVP

During mask annotation, two radiologists with more than 10 years of experience in vascular imaging diagnosis manually delineated the carotid lumen, arterial wall, and plaque regions on contrast-enhanced arterial-phase CTA images using 3D Slicer. All annotations were independently reviewed by a senior radiologist, yielding a Cohen’s κ of 0.86, indicating high interobserver consistency. The resulting masks served as supervisory signals and were paired with the original images for model training.

An end-to-end framework was developed to construct a prognostic prediction model for cervical vulnerable plaques, as illustrated in Fig. 7. First, the arterial regions segmented by the FTC U-Net were cropped into ROIs and overlaid with the corresponding masks to highlight potential high-risk areas. These ROIs were processed with a linear projection module that compressed them into one-dimensional feature vectors, reducing dimensionality while preserving essential structural information. The vectors were then fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to extract deeper semantic features associated with prognostic differentiation [21]. Radiomics features extracted from the original CTA images were standardized and selected using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to retain the most discriminative variables [25]. The fused deep and radiomics features were subsequently input into an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) classifier, which generated probabilistic predictions for low-, intermediate-, and high-risk plaque categories. The category with the highest predicted probability was used as the final prognostic outcome [26].

Dataset partitioning and model validation through cross-validation

To ensure robust predictive performance on unseen data and minimize overfitting, the dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. 80% of the data was used for model training and parameter tuning, 10% for validation and hyperparameter optimization, and 10% was reserved as an independent test set for final evaluation.

To further assess generalizability and stability, ten-fold cross-validation was conducted during model training. The dataset was partitioned into ten equal subsets, with each subset serving once as a validation set while the remaining nine subsets were used for training. The procedure was repeated ten times, and key performance metrics, including accuracy, Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), area under the curve (AUC), F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity, were computed for each iteration. The mean and standard deviation of these metrics were reported as the final evaluation results to provide a comprehensive assessment of model reliability.

All data processing, model training, and validation procedures were fully documented to ensure reproducibility. Detailed descriptions of dataset partitioning, preprocessing, training protocols, and parameter configurations are provided in the supplementary materials to support independent replication and verification of the experimental results. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set for both the FTC-enhanced U-Net segmentation model and the downstream plaque risk classification model. The independent test set was not involved in cross-validation and was used only for final performance evaluation.

Model performance evaluation

All data processing and analyses were conducted in Python using the Jupyter Notebook environment. The primary libraries used for data handling and visualization included Pandas (https://pandas.pydata.org/), OpenCV (https://github.com/opencv/opencv), and Matplotlib (https://matplotlib.org/stable/project/citing.html). During preprocessing, all image data were normalized to eliminate scale differences. Histogram equalization was applied to enhance contrast, and Gaussian filtering was used to suppress noise and improve image quality, generating clearer and more consistent inputs for model training.

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, AUC, Dice coefficient, and IoU. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed on the training dataset to strengthen robustness and reduce the risk of overfitting. Confusion matrix analysis was used to assess classification performance across different risk categories and to provide insight into model stability and potential clinical utility. Visualization of the training and evaluation results was generated using Matplotlib, including training loss curves, performance comparison plots, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) heatmaps. These visualizations enhanced model interpretability and provided intuitive support for presenting analytical results and informing clinical interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using two-sided tests with a significance level of α = 0.05. For multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR, q = 0.05). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When data followed a normal distribution and were paired, paired t-tests were used; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. For performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and AUC, statistical testing was performed, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to confirm result robustness. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or r) were calculated, and significance levels were marked in figures as *, **, and ***, corresponding to p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively. These procedures strengthened the rigor and reproducibility of model development and evaluation.

Segmentation performance metrics (e.g., Dice coefficient and IoU) were compared using paired t-tests; when normality assumptions were not met, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1, showing statistical power above 0.80 for an effect size of d = 0.8 and α = 0.05.

Statistical methods were selected according to data type and analytical objectives. For continuous variables such as HU values, volume measurements, and model-derived metrics, independent samples t-tests (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (multiple groups) were applied when data met normality assumptions, while Mann–Whitney U tests or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used otherwise. Categorical variables such as sex, hypertension, and diabetes were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Model performance indicators, including accuracy and F1-score, were compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests according to distributional characteristics, and AUC differences were assessed using the DeLong test. The combined use of parametric and nonparametric approaches minimized bias related to non-normality or unequal sample sizes. For all multiple hypothesis tests, the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment was consistently applied to maintain control of the FDR and ensure the reliability of statistical conclusions.

Results

Quantitative performance evaluation of the arterial segmentation model

Quantitative evaluation of the head and neck arterial segmentation model demonstrated strong performance in delineating arteries and plaque regions from contrast-enhanced arterial-phase CTA images. The model achieved a DSC of 0.89 and an IoU of 0.80 (Fig. 8), indicating precise identification and boundary delineation of arterial structures. These results confirm stable performance across the dataset and effective separation of arteries from adjacent soft tissues despite the inherently low contrast of non-contrast CT imaging.

The high DSC and IoU values provide a strong foundation for downstream cerebrovascular analyses. Accurate segmentation supports plaque detection and quantification, enabling evaluation of plaque distribution, volume, and composition. It also facilitates measurement of arterial diameters and stenosis severity, thereby contributing to diagnostic decision-making. Reliable vascular reconstruction further assists in preoperative planning and may reduce procedural risks.

Ten-fold cross-validation was conducted within the training set to assess the robustness of both segmentation and downstream plaque risk classification, whereas the independent test set was used for final evaluation. The model achieved a mean accuracy of 85.3% ± 2.1%, an F1-score of 0.84 ± 0.02, and an AUC of 0.88 ± 0.03 on the validation set. These results demonstrate high stability and strong predictive capability for plaque risk assessment.

Comparative experiments with several mainstream medical image segmentation architectures further validated the performance of the proposed model. The FTC–enhanced U-Net consistently outperformed baseline networks in terms of DSC, IoU, and AUC while maintaining competitive efficiency with respect to parameter count and inference time. Detailed comparisons are presented in Table S3.

Additional evaluation metrics provided a more comprehensive assessment of performance. The model achieved a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.83 ± 0.03 and a Volume Similarity (VS) of 0.91 ± 0.02. Cohen’s Kappa reflects the agreement between model predictions and manual annotations at the pixel level, while VS evaluates volumetric consistency between automated and manual segmentations. Together, these metrics confirm the model’s accuracy and reliability, particularly for complex vascular structures.

Validation results showed slightly higher Dice (0.89) and IoU (0.80) values than those of the training set, as presented in Fig. 7. Although this pattern may suggest mild underfitting, several factors offer a reasonable explanation. Data augmentation techniques, including rotation and scaling, increased variability during training, which reduced training accuracy while improving generalization. Early stopping based on validation loss was applied to prevent overfitting, which limited full convergence on the training data. In addition, the training set included a broader range of image variations, whereas the validation set contained more homogeneous samples. Consequently, the observed difference reflects expected behavior under these training conditions rather than true underfitting. Overall, the model demonstrated consistently high and stable performance across all data subsets, confirming strong generalization capability.

Discussion

The AI prediction model based on spectral CT imaging developed in this study demonstrates significant clinical value in the early diagnosis and long-term prognostic assessment of CVPs. Its implementation enhances diagnostic precision and efficiency, offering clinicians a reliable basis for personalized treatment planning and improved patient outcomes.

The FTC–enhanced U-Net segmentation model accurately identified and delineated arterial and plaque regions in carotid spectral CT images. Compared with the conventional U-Net architecture, the enhanced model utilized frequency-domain information more effectively, resulting in higher segmentation accuracy and precision. Such improvement is particularly important for downstream analyses, including plaque feature extraction and prognostic modeling, where segmentation quality directly influences analytical reliability. The model also demonstrated strong robustness and generalization ability, maintaining high performance in cases with atypical vascular anatomy or reduced image quality, which underscores its suitability for clinical application.

A paired t-test was conducted to statistically validate the superiority of the proposed model by comparing the Dice coefficient and IoU values obtained from ten-fold cross-validation between the standard U-Net and the FTC–enhanced U-Net. Significant improvements were observed for both Dice (P = 0.004) and IoU (P = 0.006), indicating that the enhanced model achieved higher segmentation accuracy (P < 0.05). A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 based on the observed Dice improvement. With an assumed effect size of 0.8, α = 0.05, and a sample size of 10 (corresponding to the cross-validation folds), the statistical power reached 0.84, demonstrating sufficient power for reliable hypothesis testing.

During model training, the Dice coefficient (0.89) and IoU (0.80) observed on the validation set were slightly higher than those on the training set, as shown in Fig. 7. Although the pattern could suggest mild underfitting, several factors account for the discrepancy. Data augmentation strategies (e.g., rotation and scaling) were applied only to the training set, increasing sample diversity and complexity and slightly lowering training performance. In contrast, no augmentation was applied to the validation set, resulting in more consistent image quality and marginally higher evaluation metrics. An early stopping strategy was used to prevent overfitting, which may have limited full convergence on the training data. Additionally, the training set encompassed a broader range of anatomical variations and plaque characteristics, whereas the validation set contained more homogeneous or typical cases, further contributing to the performance pattern. For these reasons, the observed metric differences reflect expected outcomes under combined augmentation and early stopping rather than substantive underfitting. Overall, the model demonstrated stable and high performance across all dataset partitions, indicating strong generalization capability.

To improve interpretability, the Grad-CAM technique was incorporated into the analysis. DL models are often considered opaque due to their limited transparency in decision processes. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps that highlight regions contributing most to classification outcomes, providing intuitive insight into model reasoning. Such visualization has clinical importance, as it allows physicians to comprehend the basis of model predictions, thereby increasing trust and promoting clinical adoption.

The Grad-CAM analysis showed that activation regions were concentrated at plaque sites, whereas images without plaques exhibited attention primarily around perivascular tissues. This distribution indicates that the model effectively identifies prognostically relevant regions and avoids reliance on irrelevant background features. Comparison of Grad-CAM heatmaps with expert-annotated ground truth masks demonstrated strong spatial agreement, with Dice similarity coefficients ranging from 0.85 to 0.88 and IoU values between 0.76 and 0.80. These results confirm that the model decision patterns align closely with manual annotations, reinforcing both the reliability and clinical interpretability of the predictions.

The prognostic model demonstrated strong effectiveness in evaluating long-term outcomes of CVPs by integrating segmentation results with extracted imaging features. The model accurately predicted plaque progression and assessed the risks of rupture or occlusion, thereby providing clinicians with data-driven support for therapeutic decision-making. Stratification of patients into distinct prognostic categories enabled early identification of high-risk individuals and timely intervention to reduce the likelihood of adverse cerebrovascular events. The generation of individualized prognostic information also improved patient understanding and engagement, promoting active participation in treatment and long-term vascular health management.

A systematic analysis of error cases was conducted to better define the applicability boundaries of the model and identify areas for improvement. The main segmentation errors involved boundary overflow and lumen under-segmentation, particularly in cases with severe calcification or high-grade stenosis. These errors were largely attributable to calcification-induced brightening, streak artifacts, and local contrast degradation in CTA images. Occasional over-segmentation occurred in anatomically complex regions where adjacent veins or soft tissues were mistakenly included within the carotid artery. Mild motion artifacts or uneven contrast distribution sometimes resulted in discontinuous plaque edges and missed detection of small plaques. At the classification stage, most errors involved confusion between medium- and high-risk plaques or underestimation of small, high-risk lesions. These misclassifications were associated with peripheral calcification halos or poorly defined fibrous cap interfaces that obscured key risk features. To address these limitations, future optimization will focus on three areas. First, in data processing, de-brightening and artifact suppression techniques such as metal artifact reduction and optimized reconstruction kernels will be introduced. Integration of material decomposition maps, monoenergetic images, and virtual non-contrast images will enhance edge contrast and improve image uniformity. Second, at the model level, boundary-aware and imbalance-tolerant loss functions, such as hybrid objectives combining contour or topological constraints with focal loss, will be incorporated. Uncertainty estimation and test-time augmentation will also be explored to reduce overconfidence and improve the identification of challenging lesions. Third, at the training strategy level, hard example mining and active learning will be applied to emphasize cases with severe calcification, high-grade stenosis, or small plaques. Patient-level error-feedback annotation and iterative retraining will further strengthen model robustness. These enhancements are expected to improve clinical applicability and accuracy in difficult cases without compromising inference efficiency.

Many prior studies have adopted classical U-Net or CNN-based architectures for carotid plaque segmentation. Although these models have achieved favorable results on limited datasets, they often have difficulty resolving boundary ambiguity and extracting fine-grained features in complex clinical cases. Conventional U-Net architectures typically yield Dice similarity coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.83 for head and neck vascular segmentation, yet performance declines notably in regions with overlapping tissues, blurred textures, or severe noise. In contrast, the FTC–enhanced U-Net developed in the study integrates frequency-domain information to strengthen edge and texture feature extraction, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.89 and an IoU of 0.80. These results exceed those reported for conventional U-Net methods under comparable conditions. The use of spectral computed tomography (CT) further enhanced generalizability, enabling reliable performance on low-contrast and structurally heterogeneous samples and underscoring the model’s clinical adaptability and translational potential.

A key innovation of this study lies in the integration of a Fourier-domain convolution module into the U-Net architecture, which markedly enhances the model’s ability to capture high-frequency information associated with complex vascular structures and plaque boundaries. This design was informed by recent advances in fast Fourier convolution operators [27]. By leveraging frequency-domain representations, the proposed approach achieves more accurate delineation of arterial contours and plaque morphology on spectral CT images, thereby providing a high-quality imaging foundation for subsequent prognostic modeling. Accumulating evidence also indicates that radiomics and deep learning models derived from CTA can effectively identify symptomatic or vulnerable carotid plaques and contribute to cerebrovascular risk prediction [15–17, 28]. The integration of advanced frequency-enhanced segmentation techniques with radiomics/ML-based risk prediction aligns with current research trends in carotid plaque assessment and precision prevention of stroke.

Recent work in cohort studies and chronic disease risk prediction has shown that combining feature selection, class imbalance handling, and gradient boosting or explainable AI frameworks can produce effective predictive models for COVID-19 hospitalization risk and the onset of type 2 diabetes. These studies offer valuable methodological insights for clinical risk stratification and decision support [29–31]. The combination of frequency-domain-enhanced segmentation with radiomics/ML-based risk prediction strategies, together with design principles inspired by these explainable cohort-based AI models, aligns with contemporary trends in carotid plaque risk assessment and precision stroke prevention.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a single-center retrospective design. Although the inclusion of 214 patients provides moderate representativeness, the relatively limited number of high-risk plaques may have affected the generalization performance and predictive stability of the model for high-risk cohorts. Second, all participants were recruited from a single geographic region, resulting in demographic and clinical homogeneity that may restrict applicability across broader populations, ethnic groups, and imaging systems. Future research will address these limitations through multi-center collaborations that incorporate data from diverse populations and imaging platforms to enhance robustness and clinical generalizability. Another limitation lies in the exclusive reliance on spectral CT data. Future studies may benefit from integrating multimodal imaging, including conventional CT, MRI, and ultrasound, to combine structural and functional information. Such multimodal fusion has the potential to improve vascular structure recognition and plaque risk stratification. Finally, the prognostic model relied primarily on imaging-derived features without incorporating comprehensive clinical variables. Subsequent work should integrate clinical factors such as age, sex, blood pressure, lipid profile, diabetes status, smoking history, and prior cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events with imaging and radiomic features. The combination of clinical and imaging information will support the development of a multidimensional risk prediction system that enhances precision and provides clinicians with more interpretable and reliable decision support.

Additionally, although the proposed model achieved high overall accuracy, performance declined in several complex clinical scenarios, including cases with severe calcification, high-grade stenosis, or overlapping plaques. Typical errors involved boundary leakage, missed small lesions, and misclassification of adjacent tissues as plaque regions. These inaccuracies were mainly attributed to the inherently low contrast and structural ambiguity of lesion areas, which hinder stable feature extraction in both spatial and frequency domains. Motion artifacts and uneven contrast enhancement in certain CTA scans also affected segmentation precision. Future refinement efforts should prioritize multimodal integration, such as combining CTA with MRI, and incorporate advanced attention mechanisms to improve robustness in anatomically complex regions.

From a clinical translation perspective, the proposed AI model provides comprehensive support for the diagnosis and management of vulnerable carotid plaques. The model can automatically determine plaque risk levels from routine spectral CT examinations, offering real-time decision assistance for radiologists, reducing interpretation time, and minimizing subjective variability. Accurate risk stratification aids clinicians in developing individualized treatment plans by enabling early initiation of intensive lipid-lowering or antiplatelet therapy for high-risk patients and conservative monitoring for low-risk individuals, thereby optimizing clinical resource allocation. When applied in longitudinal imaging follow-up, the model facilitates dynamic monitoring of plaque progression and provides objective quantitative evidence for evaluating therapeutic response and predicting disease evolution. Collectively, these findings present a practical foundation for developing an AI-based early screening and precision prevention framework for stroke, underscoring strong potential for clinical translation and implementation.

In conclusion, the AI-based predictive model developed in this study, built upon spectral CT imaging and an FTC-enhanced U-Net architecture, demonstrated strong accuracy, reliability, and clinical relevance for early diagnosis and long-term prognostic assessment of CVPs. The approach shows substantial promise as an efficient tool for clinical decision-making and represents a meaningful advancement in the application of AI to vascular disease diagnosis and management. Future research will address current limitations and refine the model architecture. By incorporating larger datasets, multimodal imaging inputs, and detailed clinical metadata, the model is expected to achieve greater generalizability, interpretability, and practical value, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes across broader patient populations.
