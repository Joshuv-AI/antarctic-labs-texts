Liposomal amphotericin B as an antifungal prophylaxis in bilateral lung transplantation: a safety analysis
Authors: Annalisa Boscolo, Nicolò Sella, Sabrina Congedi, Irene Paiusco, Elisa Pistollato, Alessia Zanaga, Francesca Chiapperini, Francesco Zarantonello, Giulia Mormando, Giulia Pagliarini, Alessandro De Cassai, Tommaso Pettenuzzo, Andrea Dell’Amore, Paolo Navalesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung transplantation (LT) is the definitive treatment for end-stage chronic respiratory failure. Despite improvements in surgical and perioperative care, fungal infections remain a major complication in LT recipients, and antifungal prophylaxis with liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) is commonly used. However, evidence on the safety of L-AmB administration in LT recipients remains limited. This retrospective observational study aimed to assess the profile of renal, hepatic and hematologic safety, and the incidence of hypersensitivity adverse events following prophylactic L-AmB use in LT recipients.MethodsA retrospective, single-center observational study of adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent their first bilateral LT at the University Hospital of Padua (Italy), between January 2020 and March 2025. All patients received intravenous L-AmB (3 mg/kg/day for 5 days) as postoperative antifungal prophylaxis. Adverse effects were assessed based on acute kidney injury (AKI), liver toxicity, hematotoxicity, and infusion reactions. Laboratory values were collected at baseline, on days 1, 2, 3, 7, and 14 post-L-AmB initiation.Results189 LT recipients were screened and 140 enrolled. Concerning safety profile, AKI occurred in 55 (39%) patients, with serum creatinine peaking at 48 h (p = 0.025), as compared to baseline. Mild transaminase elevations occurred in 86% of patients within the first 48 h, and bilirubin increased in 10%, with no episodes of acute liver failure. Anemia (85%) and transient thrombocytopenia (9%) were observed. Hypokalemia was found in 28%. Renal replacement therapy was needed in 2%. No infusion-related or allergic reactions to L-AmB were observed. According to the institutional fungal surveillance protocol, no proven, probable, or possible invasive fungal infections were documented during the observation period. However, the present study was not designed to assess the prophylactic efficacy of L-AmB.ConclusionsIn this cohort of bilateral LT recipients receiving standardized L-AmB prophylaxis, no discontinuation, dose adjustment, infusion reaction, or proven fungal infection was documented. Renal, hepatic, hematologic, and electrolyte abnormalities were frequent; but cannot be specifically attributed to L-AmB in the absence of a comparator group, given the multifactorial pathophysiology of the early post-transplant period. These findings underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring of organ function during L-AmB administration in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40360-026-01189-x.

Background

Lung transplantation (LT) is the established treatment for end-stage chronic respiratory failure unresponsive to other medical or surgical therapies [1]. Several clinical conditions may require LT, including cystic fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and interstitial lung disease. LT recipients represent a unique and growing population; advances in surgical techniques and perioperative management have significantly improved outcomes, with one-year survival rates rising from 73% in the 1990s to approximately 84% today [2]. Similarly, the number of LT recipients has doubled since 2000, with an estimated 2,000–2,500 single or bilateral procedures performed annually in the United States and over 4,000 worldwide, resulting in a five-year survival rate of approximately 50% [3, 4].

Fungal infections are a common complication in immunocompromised patients, particularly among solid organ transplant recipients [5, 6]. Among these patients, lung transplant recipients carry the highest risk of invasive fungal infections (IFI), with a reported incidence of approximately 8.6%, exceeded only by small bowel transplant recipients [5]. Aspergillus species account for the majority of IFI in this population, with 12-month mortality rates approaching 40% for invasive aspergillosis [5, 6]. This elevated susceptibility reflects the unique immunological vulnerability of LT recipients, driven by the combination of intense immunosuppression, direct exposure of the transplanted lung to the environment, impaired mucociliary clearance, and frequent need for broad-spectrum antibiotics [5, 6]. Antifungal prophylaxis is the most widely adopted strategy to prevent these infections, and in the context of lung transplantation, three main approaches are described: (i) universal prophylaxis (administered to all patients immediately after LT), (ii) targeted prophylaxis (reserved for patients with known risk factors), and (iii) pre-emptive prophylaxis (administered to colonized patients without clinical evidence of active fungal infection) [7]. At our center, standardized perioperative antifungal and immunosuppressive protocols have been implemented. Since January 2020, systemic L-AmB (3 mg/kg/day for five days) has been included in the standard antifungal prophylaxis regimen. The choice of IV L-AmB over mold-active triazoles was driven by three main center-specific considerations. First, all mold-active triazoles are potent inhibitors of the CYP3A4 enzyme system and significantly increase tacrolimus trough concentrations — by up to 5-fold for voriconazole [6] — requiring intensive therapeutic drug monitoring and frequent dose adjustments during the already complex early post-transplant period; by contrast, L-AmB does not interact with calcineurin inhibitor metabolism, making it pharmacologically preferable in this setting [5, 9]. Second, in the immediate post-operative period, all bilateral LT recipients at our center are admitted to the ICU under invasive mechanical ventilation, precluding reliable oral drug administration and limiting the practical applicability of nebulized or inhaled amphotericin B formulations, whose pulmonary deposition in intubated patients is uncertain [9]. Third, L-AmB provides broad-spectrum coverage against both Aspergillus and Candida species, as well as emerging molds such as Mucorales [5, 6], which is particularly relevant given the high-risk, undifferentiated fungal threat in the early post-transplant window.

Specifically, invasive fungal infections, predominantly caused by Aspergillus spp. and Candida spp., are reported to affect 6–14% of LT recipients in the first year post-transplant and are associated with mortality rates exceeding 50%, providing strong justification for a universal prophylaxis strategy in this high-risk population [8, 10]. Indeed, the prophylactic dose of 3 mg/kg/day and the 5-day duration were chosen to cover the highest-risk early post-operative period while limiting cumulative drug exposure, in line with established dosing used in solid organ transplantation [6].

Specifically, the choice of L-AmB specifically reflects its broad-spectrum activity, favorable safety profile compared to conventional amphotericin B, and increasing adoption at high-volume transplant centers as reported in recent guidelines and observational studies. In details, compared to conventional amphotericin B, L-AmB exhibits reduced nephrotoxicity [11, 12]; however, clinical evidence suggests that its use may still be associated with adverse events such as renal and hepatic dysfunction, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and infusion-related reactions (e.g., fever, chills, hypotension), particularly in vulnerable patients [13–18]. A recent retrospective study of 86 patients treated with L-AmB reported a correlation between the severity of hypokalemia—a common adverse effect—and the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI), with a 10% drop in serum potassium linked to increased nephrotoxic risk [19]. In another case series of patients treated with L-AmB for cutaneous leishmaniasis, hypokalemia was associated with elevated markers of rhabdomyolysis, including creatine kinase and myoglobin [20]. Despite its widespread use, data on the incidence and toxicity profile of L-AmB in lung transplant recipients remain limited. This retrospective study aims to fill this knowledge gap by providing a detailed evaluation of adverse effects associated with L-AmB use in this specific patient population. We conducted a retrospective, single-center, observational study to describe: (i) the incidence and characteristics of hepatic, renal, and hematologic toxicity and allergic reactions due to L-AmB prophylaxis after LT, and (ii) potential impacts on short- and mid-term outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a retrospective, observational, single-center study including adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) admitted to Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of the University Hospital of Padua (Italy), after their first bilateral LT, between January 2020 and March 2025. We included all subjects who had received L-AmB as antifungal agent for post-transplant prophylaxis. Exclusion criteria were: (i) age < 18 y-old; (ii) single LT or retransplantation; (iii) incomplete records (i.e., missing laboratory values at two or more pre-defined timepoints — days 1, 2, 3, 7, or 14 post-L-AmB initiation — or missing primary outcomes of interest, which precluded a complete safety assessment); (iv) invasive mechanical ventilation or ECMO prior to transplant, (v) refusal of consent, (vi) preexisting fungal isolation or fungal therapies at the time of LT. Relevant clinical information was retrieved from the electronic medical records, including baseline characteristics, underlying disease, laboratory data, and the start and end dates of antifungal and antibiotic therapies. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Padua University Hospital (reference number: 6320/AO/25, 05/15/2025). The study was carried out according to the Declaration of Helsinki and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Antifungal post-transplant prophylaxis

At our center, standardized protocols for perioperative antifungal management and immunosuppressive therapy have been implemented in accordance with international guidelines [22, 23]. The systemic antifungal prophylaxis includes IV L-AmB (3 mg/kg/day for five days) as part of the standardized postoperative protocol and was administered to all enrolled patients. In cases of documented pre- and post-operative fungal infections, a targeted antifungal therapy was initiated based on microbiological findings in vitro susceptibility following the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing recommendations [24]. Prior to January 2020, our center did not employ a standardized universal antifungal prophylaxis regimen; antifungal agents were administered on a pre-emptive or targeted basis, guided by microbiological surveillance cultures and clinical risk factor assessment [7]. Following completion of the 5-day L-AmB course, patients were transitioned to an individualized, risk-adapted antifungal strategy upon ICU discharge. Patients with evidence of pre- or post-transplant fungal colonization, anastomotic complications, or other high-risk clinical features received targeted oral antifungal prophylaxis (typically voriconazole 200 mg twice daily), while lower-risk patients were followed with regular microbiological surveillance without systematic continuation of antifungal therapy, in line with current post-LT antifungal management recommendations [7, 9].

The immunosuppressive regimen was initiated intraoperatively with an intravenous dose of methylprednisolone administered prior to each graft reperfusion, followed by basiliximab induction (20 mg on postoperative days 0 and 4). Maintenance therapy consisted of tacrolimus in combination with mycophenolate mofetil (1–2 g/day) and methylprednisolone (0.5 mg/kg/day) during the postoperative period.

Definitions

For the purposes of this study, patients were evaluated for nephrotoxicity, hypokalemia, hepatotoxicity and hematotoxicity based on laboratory abnormalities at days 1,2,3,7 and 14 after the initiation of L-AmB therapy, compared with preoperative baseline values. The observation period for all safety outcomes and microbiological endpoints extended from the initiation of L-AmB (day 0) through day 14 post-initiation, consistent with the timeframe of systematic laboratory collection. Microbiological surveillance, including bacterial and fungal cultures from respiratory secretions and blood, was performed as part of the standard post-transplant ICU monitoring protocol throughout this period. Hospitalization and other secondary outcomes were followed longer.

Assessment of nephrotoxicity

Nephrotoxicity was identified as the presence of AKI, defined according to the 2012 KDIGO criteria, as an increase in serum creatinine to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days. We also staged AKI severity according to the same criteria: stage 1, as an increase in serum creatinine 1.5–1.9 times baseline; stage 2, as an increase in serum creatinine 2.0–2.9 times baseline; and stage 3, as an increase in serum creatinine > 3 times baseline or initiation of KRT (Kidney Replacement Therapy) [25]. The KDIGO urine output criterion and the absolute serum creatinine change criterion were excluded.

Assessment of hepatotoxicity

In this study the liver function was assessed by measuring serum aminotransferases (ALT and AST) and total bilirubin (T-Bil) values within 48 h of initiating L-AmB therapy. Hepatotoxicity was defined according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 (2017), published by National Cancer Institute (NCI), as any increase in serum aminotransferases (ALT or AST) or T-Bil above the upper limit of normal (ULN) [26]. In patients with already abnormal baseline values, grading was performed using multiples of the baseline value rather than of the ULN as shown in the additional files (Table S1).

Assessment of hematotoxicity

Hematological toxicity was assessed by occurrence of anemia or thrombocytopenia during L-AmB therapy. According to the CTCAE-version 5.0 [26], anemia was defined as hemoglobin drop of at least 2 g/dL or hemoglobin drop to < 8 g/dL (severe anemia); while, thrombocytopenia, as platelet count drop to < 50.000 cells/mm3 or, if baseline count under 50.000 cells/mm3, a decrease of 25% in platelet count (severe thrombocytopenia). The need for red blood cell or platelet transfusions was also evaluated.

We also graded the severity of hypokalemia according to the CTCAE-version5.0 [26] as shown in the additional files (Table S2).

Assessment of infusion-related or allergic reactions

Infusion-related reactions were defined according to the CTCAE, version 5.0 (2017) [26] as any clinical event occurring during or within a few hours after L-AmB infusion, including fever, chills, rash, pruritus, dyspnea, hypotension, or anaphylaxis. For this study, events were graded as 0 = absent, 1 = mild (e.g., rash), and 2 = severe (anaphylaxis).

Fungal infection surveillance

Fungal infection surveillance was performed according to the institutional protocol throughout the ICU stay and until hospital discharge [27]. Routine bronchoscopic surveillance with BAL was performed in all patients at standardized intervals (at 48–72 h and at 7 days post-transplant) and whenever clinically indicated (e.g., fever, new pulmonary infiltrates, respiratory deterioration), as per our center’s standard post-transplant protocol and consistent with international guidelines for bronchial anastomosis surveillance. BAL samples were routinely analyzed for fungal culture and microscopy. Serum galactomannan (Aspergillus GM antigen) was measured at baseline and on days 7 and 14. Chest CT imaging was performed when clinically indicated. Invasive fungal infections were classified as proven, probable, or possible according to the EORTC/MSG 2020 consensus criteria. The observation period for fungal infection surveillance corresponded to the full hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range [IQR] in case of non-normal distribution; while, categorical variables were reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, or the Mann–Whitney U test when normality assumptions were not met or sample sizes were small. No imputation was performed for missing data. Differences between paired continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

A multivariable linear regression analysis was performed using a stepwise selection method. Variables were entered or removed based on the probability of the F-statistic (p < 0.05 for entry, p > 0.10 for removal). Multicollinearity was assessed through tolerance values, excluding predictors with tolerance < 0.0001. The Friedman non-parametric test was performed for repeated measures and pairwise comparisons between time intervals were performed using Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. Finally, Kaplan-Meier curve analysis was realized to estimate the function of interest from time-to-event data. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses have been conducted using Jamovi (version 2.7.6) and SPSS (IBM, version 29.0.2.0).

Hepatotoxicity

Mild elevations in transaminase and/or bilirubin levels were observed in one-hundred and twenty-one patients (86%). No cases of acute liver failure occurred. Hepatocytolysis enzymes significantly increased from baseline, peaking at 48 h (AST p < 0.001; ALT p 0.005) (Figure S2). Additional data on hepatotoxicity and clinical variables are reported in Table S8–S10.

Hematotoxicity

Hematologic abnormalities were frequent but self-limiting: one-hundred and nineteen patients (85%) developed anemia with the lowest haemoglobin value reported at 72 h and 14 days after the transplantation (Figure S3); while, twelve recipients (9%) experienced transient thrombocytopenia. Both hemoglobin and platelet counts showed significant temporal variation (p < 0.001), and the lowest hemoglobin values were observed at 72 h and 14 days after transplantation, while the platelet nadir occurred at 48 and 72 h (Figure S3–S4). To note, preoperative hemoglobin concentration (HbPRE) remained the strongest and most consistent independent predictor of postoperative anemia at all time points (p < 0.001) (Table S11-S16). Regarding thrombocytopenia, multivariable stepwise regression analysis identified intraoperative lactate concentration as the only consistent independent predictor of thrombocytopenia across all postoperative timepoints (p < 0.01), while baseline platelet count and body mass index (BMI) were associated with early thrombocytopenia at 48 h (p = 0.043) (Table S17-S22).

Hypokalemia

Overall, hypokalemia (K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L) occurred in 39 out of one-hundred forty patients (28%) during the postoperative period (Table 2). Median serum potassium declined after transplantation, reaching its lowest median value of 3.7 mmol/L at day 7 (p < 0.01; Figure S5). Moreover, hypokalemic patients showed higher end-of-surgery lactate levels (Table S23-S24); while no significant association was found between hypokalemia and AKI at any time point (χ² = 0.06–1.6, p = 0.20–0.80) (Table 4).

Secondary outcomes

As shown in Table 2, discontinuation or dose adjustment of L-AmB was never required. Three patients (2%) needed renal replacement therapy. Regarding hospital length of stay (LOS), patients showed a non-significant trend to remain in the hospital after therapy with L-AmB either in relation to the AKI event (log-Rank p 0.396) or in relation to the finding of hypokalemia (log-Rank p 0.283), hepatotoxicity (Log-Rank p 0.38), anemia (Log-Rank p 0.20), thrombocytopenia (log-Rank p 0.60) (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7). No infusion-related or allergic reactions to L-AmB were observed in any patient. No microbiological, serological, or radiological evidence of invasive fungal infection, as defined by EORTC/MSG criteria, were documented during the 14-day observation period. The conclusion that no invasive fungal infections were documented must be contextualized within the scope of the surveillance strategy employed and the duration of the observation period, which was confined to the hospital stay. The absence of documented infections may reflect the limitations of surveillance rather than prophylactic efficacy, and this study was not designed nor powered to draw conclusions on L-AmB effectiveness.

Discussion

This interim analysis provides descriptive data on the laboratory abnormalities observed in bilateral LT recipients receiving standardized systemic prophylaxis with L-AmB, a population in which such data remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, and based on a review of the available literature, dedicated data on the organ-system adverse event profile of systemic L-AmB prophylaxis specifically in bilateral lung transplant recipients remain scarce. While L-AmB safety has been described in other transplant populations and immunocompromised patients, the bilateral LT setting, characterized by a unique constellation of perioperative insults, immunosuppression, and early ICU morbidity, has not been the primary focus of prior safety analyses. The present study contributes descriptive data in this underrepresented population, with the limitations inherent to its single-arm, observational design.

This setting allowed us to conduct a comprehensive assessment of toxicity affecting different organ systems.

In the LT setting, the incidence of postoperative AKI is notoriously high, driven by a convergence of perioperative insults including ischemia-reperfusion injury, hemodynamic instability, ECMO support, vasopressor use, calcineurin inhibitor initiation, nephrotoxic antibiotic co-administration, and fluid shifts — all of which were present in our cohort. Our regression analysis identified arterial hypertension, iNO use, and longer operative time as the main independent predictors of AKI, pointing to pre-existing vascular dysfunction and intraoperative stress as dominant drivers. These findings reinforce the interpretation that postoperative AKI in this population is largely attributable to the perioperative context rather than to L-AmB exposure specifically, although a contribution of L-AmB cannot be excluded in the absence of a comparator arm. These assumptions are also supported by our regression analysis results, whereas arterial hypertension and inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) use appeared as a significant baseline predictor of postoperative AKI, indicating a potential influence of preexisting vascular dysfunction and greater perioperative illness severity, respectively; while, longer operative time was linked to early AKI, suggesting the role of intraoperative hemodynamic stress. Based on these considerations, the drug-induced toxicity from the multifactorial renal impairment is still a primary clinical challenge [28, 29]. In our cohort of 140 recipients, the overall incidence of AKI was 39%, a finding that aligns perfectly with previous literature reporting rates between 30% and 60% [19, 28–32]. Interestingly, most cases were mild to moderate according to KDIGO staging (stage I–II), largely developed within two weeks of transplantation and self-limiting and Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed strong, consistent associations among creatinine values across all time points, indicating a temporal correlation of renal function. Albeit in our analysis no data barely emerged as a risk factor for AKI developing, the presence of hypokalemia may influence, at least partially, the incidence of AKI over time. Although AKI and hypokalemia are common adverse events related to L-AmB, the correlation and interaction of these two adverse events have not been clarified. Current evidence available in literature does not clearly establish whether in this setting hypokalemia represents an early manifestation or a prodromal sign of AKI [19, 33].

Importantly, despite the potential nephrotoxicity of amphotericin B formulations, short-term prophylactic administration of L-AmB was not associated with severe or irreversible renal impairment in our cohort, reinforcing the nephroprotective advantage of liposomal over conventional deoxycholate formulations [11, 31–37].

Consistent with its established tolerability profile, hepatic alterations followed a benign course, with no cases of acute liver failure and rapid normalization of bilirubin. Mild transient elevations in hepatic enzymes and bilirubin were observed in up to 86% of patients, aligning with previous data indicating that L-AmB induced hepatotoxicity, though reported, is typically mild and reversible, with severe hepatotoxicity and liver failure occurring in the context of underlying hepatic disease or concomitant exposure to hepatotoxic agents [38–40]. However, unlike our findings, previous studies reported a smaller overall prevalence of hepatotoxicity. Specifically, Shigemi et al. reported in 22 patients treated with L-Amb due to a fungal disease an elevation above the upper limit of normal in total bilirubin and/or hepatocytolysis enzymes up to 36.4% [15]. Similarly, Tan et al. described in 139 Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) patients diagnosed with cryptococcal meningitis or talaromycosis and received treatment with L-Amb an hepatotoxicity prevalence of nearly 24%, according to the Division of AIDS (DAIDS) Adverse Event Grading Tables (Version 2.1) [41] and a prevalence of 21% was observed in 75 patients received L-Amb due to undeclared diagnoses in the report of Patel et al. [14]. The slightly higher prevalence in our study likely reflects patient-specific factors—such as surgical stress, polypharmacy, and prolonged observation—as well as heterogeneous definitions used in prior reports. Although several mechanisms have been proposed, including reversible hepatocellular stress and lipophilic drug accumulation within hepatic membranes, the primary causal pathway remains difficult to establish [15, 41–42]. Finally, no independent predictors of early post-transplant liver impairment were identified among the variables analyzed. Therefore, the early hepatic dysfunction after bilateral LT may be influenced by other factors such as ischemia-reperfusion injury and drug toxicity.

Hematologic toxicity was also common but manageable. Anemia occurred in approximately 85% of patients, mostly in older and male patients, usually within the first postoperative week, likely reflecting a multifactorial etiology involving surgical blood loss, inflammation, immunosuppression, and possible L-AmB-related erythropoietin suppression [43, 46]. The observed non-linear correlations among hemoglobin values at different time points, and between early postoperative levels and transfusion requirements, further support the multifactorial nature of this effect.

In contrast to our results, previous reports showed lower prevalence of anemia: a reduction of hemoglobin concentration occurred in 13% to up to 47% of patients receiving L-AmB [15, 42–48]. This discrepancy is plausibly attributed to multiple factors: differences in patient characteristics — specifically, bilateral LT recipients exposed to significant surgical stress, multiple comorbidities, and polypharmacy — as well as a longer follow-up period for the event “anemia,” extending beyond the prophylactic phase, and the heterogeneity of definitions used in previous studies. Moreover, in our multivariable regression analysis, pre-operative Hb concentration was identified as the strongest and most consistent predictor of postoperative anemia in lung transplant recipients, reflecting pre-existing hematologic status. Smoking history also emerged as a predictor of anemia at one week, which might be related to impaired erythropoiesis or increased oxidative stress.

Conversely, thrombocytopenia was infrequent (9%) in our cohort but in line with data available in literature, reporting a prevalence of 3.9% up to 57.6% [15, 41] with the exact frequency still remains poorly defined [38]. In our analysis, higher intraoperative lactate levels emerged as the only consistent predictor of postoperative thrombocytopenia, indicating a possible association between metabolic stress during surgery and early hematologic alterations. In contrast, baseline demographic and comorbidity factors showed no independent effect, suggesting that perioperative rather than pre-existing conditions primarily influence platelet trends after LT.

Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypokalemia, were also frequent (28%) but reversible, with a peaking between 48 and 72 h postoperatively, and a spontaneous normalization by day 7. Our prevalence data was in line with other studies, reporting a rate of potassium imbalance from 24% to 63.9% [19, 41]. Notably, hypokalemia occurrence did not differ in AKI subgroups evaluating at different post-operative timepoints, and potassium variations showed only weak and transient correlations with creatinine, probably due to a mixed mechanism involving both amphotericin-induced tubular wasting and glomerular dysfunction. Otherwise, hypokalemia was significantly more frequent in patients with higher lactate values at the end of surgery, suggesting the effect of surgical stress on this outcome.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, as an interim analysis, the sample size may limit the statistical power to detect rare adverse events. In addition, despite multivariable adjustment, the observational design inherently remains susceptible to residual confounding, further limiting causal inference. This limitation is particularly relevant given that the outcomes assessed, including AKI, transaminase elevation, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and hypokalemia, are indeed well-recognized complications of bilateral lung transplantation itself, potentially attributable to concurrent exposures such as surgery, blood product transfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, ECMO, primary graft dysfunction, calcineurin inhibitors, broad-spectrum antibiotics, diuretics, vasopressors, and sepsis. Consistent with this interpretation, several adverse events observed in this cohort — including thrombocytopenia, hypokalemia, and early AKI, were independently associated with markers of intraoperative physiological stress (elevated end-of-surgery lactate, longer operative time, inhaled nitric oxide use) rather than with L-AmB-specific parameters, suggesting that the post-transplant surgical context is itself a major confounding factor. Without a control group or robust propensity-matched adjustment for all these covariates, separation of drug-related toxicity from expected postoperative morbidity remains inherently limited.

Future prospective comparative studies, ideally including a concurrent cohort receiving an alternative prophylactic regimen, are needed to disentangle drug-specific from procedure-related toxicity in this population.

Another limitation relates to the choice of IV L-AmB as prophylactic agent, which, although supported by sound pharmacological and practical rationale as described above, represents a center-specific decision in the absence of randomized controlled trial evidence directly comparing systemic L-AmB to mold-active triazoles or inhaled amphotericin B formulations in lung transplant recipients [9]. In addition, the single-center nature of the study and the use of a center-specific prophylactic protocol may limit the generalizability of our findings, which should be interpreted with caution in centers adopting different clinical practices, patient risk profiles, or institutional prophylactic strategies.

Finally, the observation period was limited to 14 days post-L-AmB initiation, precluding any assessment of IFI incidence beyond the immediate perioperative window, a critical limitation given that invasive aspergillosis in LT recipients may present weeks to months after transplantation [5]. Center-specific data on the local incidence of proven IFI in LT recipients before and after the introduction of L-AmB prophylaxis were not systematically available and therefore could not be reported [48, 49]; this precludes any inference regarding the prophylactic efficacy of the protocol described.

Conclusions

In this descriptive cohort of bilateral lung transplant recipients receiving standardized L-AmB prophylaxis, no discontinuation, dose adjustment, infusion reaction, or proven invasive fungal infection was documented. Renal, hepatic, hematologic, and electrolyte abnormalities were frequent; however, these events cannot be causally attributed to L-AmB in the absence of a comparator arm, given the substantial perioperative confounding inherent to the early post-transplant ICU setting. Indeed, the primary objective of this study was not to establish causality or to compare L-AmB against an alternative agent, but rather to characterize the incidence and nature of adverse events associated with its prophylactic use in a bilateral LT cohort, a population in which such dedicated data remain scarce. In this context, the multivariable regression analyses consistently identified perioperative and pre-existing factors, rather than L-AmB exposure, as the dominant predictors of organ dysfunction, supporting the interpretation that the observed abnormalities reflect the overall safety landscape of the early post-transplant period rather than direct drug toxicity.

These descriptive findings support the feasibility of L-AmB prophylaxis in this high-risk population and underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring of renal, hepatic, and hematologic parameters during its administration, while future controlled comparative studies will be necessary to better characterize its independent safety profile.
