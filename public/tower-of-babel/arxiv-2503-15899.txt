A note on the binomial distribution motivated by Chvátal’s theorem and Tomasewski’s theorem
Zheng-Yan Guo, Ze-Chun Hu, Run-Yu Wang

Abstract




Abstract:
Let
B(n,p)
denote a binomial random variable with parameters
n
and
p
. Chvátal’s theorem says that for any fixed
n\geq 2
, as
m
ranges over
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, the probability
q_{m}:=P(B(n,m/n)\leq m)
is the smallest when
m
is closest to
2n/3
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the family of random variables of the form
X=\sum^{n}_{k=1}a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}
, where
n\geq 1
,
a_{k},k=1,\dots,n,
are real numbers with
\sum^{n}_{k=1}a_{k}^{2}=1
, and
\varepsilon_{k}
,
k=1,2,\dots
, are independent
Rademacher random variables (i.e.,
P(\varepsilon_{k}=1)=P(\varepsilon_{k}=-1)=1/2
).
Tomaszewski’s theorem says that
\inf_{X\in\mathcal{R}}P(|X|\leq 1)=1/2
. Motivated by
Chvátal’s Theorem and Tomasewski’s Theorem, in this note, we study the minimum value of the probability
f_{n}(k):=P(|B(n,k/n)-k|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}(B(n,k/n))})
when
k
ranges over
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for any fixed
n\geq 1
, where
{\rm Var}(\cdot)
denotes the variance, and prove that it is the smallest when
k=1
and
n-1
.

Keywords:
Chvátal’s theorem, Tomasewski’s theorem, Binomial distribution, Berry-Esseen estimate, Concentration

1
Introduction and main result

Let
B(n,p)
denote a binomial random variable with parameters
n
and
p
.
Chvátal’s theorem
says that: For any fixed
n\geq 2
, as
m
ranges over
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, the probability
q_{m}:=P(B(n,m/n)\leq m)
attains its minimum value when
m
is closest to
\frac{2n}{3}
. Chvátal’s theorem may have useful applications, as the probability of a binomial random variable exceeding its mean plays a role in machine learning (see Doerr
[
2
]
, Greenberg and Mohri
[
4
]
, Vapnik
[
17
]
and references therein). For the history of Chvátal’s theorem, refer to Bababesi et al.
[
1
]
, Janson
[
7
]
and Sun
[
12
]
.

Motivated by Chvátal’s theorem, a series of papers have studied the infimum value of the probability
P(X\leq E[X])
, where
X
has the same type of distribution which includes the Poisson, geometric and Pascal distributions (
[
10
]
), the negative binomial distribution (
[
5
]
), the Gamma distribution (
[
13
]
), the Weibull and Pareto distributions (
[
9
]
), and some infinitely divisible distributions (
[
6
]
).

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the family of random variables of the form
X=\sum^{n}_{k=1}a_{k}\varepsilon_{k}
, where
n\geq 1
,
a_{k},k=1,\dots,n,
are real numbers satisfying
\sum^{n}_{k=1}a_{k}^{2}=1
, and
\varepsilon_{k}
,
k=1,2,\dots
, are independent Rademacher random variables (i.e.
P(\varepsilon_{k}=1)=P(\varepsilon_{k}=-1)=1/2
).
Tomaszewski’s theorem
says that
\inf_{X\in\mathcal{R}}P(|X|\leq 1)=1/2
. Tomaszewski’s theorem has wide applications in probability theory, geometric analysis, and computer science. For its history and related problems, see Dvorak and Klein
[
3
]
, and Keller and Klein
[
8
]
.

Motivated by Tomaszewski’s theorem, several papers have investigated the infimum value of the probability
P(|X-E[X]|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X)})
, where the distribution of
X
belongs to some familiar distributions including the Gamma distribution (
[
13
]
), some infinitely divisible distributions (
[
14
]
),
F
-distribution (
[
15
]
), and the geometric, symmetric geometric, Poisson and symmetric Poisson distributions (
[
16
]
). If the infimum value is positive for some certain type of distribution, we can say that this kind of distribution possesses the concentration property.

Motivated by Chvátal’s theorem and Tomasewski’s theorem, in this note, we study the minimum value of the probability
f_{n}(k):=P(|B(n,k/n)-k|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}(B(n,k))})
for
n\geq 1
and
k
ranges over
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. The main result is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any fixed positive integer
n
, it holds that

\displaystyle\min_{k=0,1,...,n}f_{n}(k)=f_{n}(1)=f_{n}(n-1)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1,&\mbox{if}\quad n=1,\\
\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1},&\mbox{if}\quad n\geq 1.\end{array}\right.

2
Proof

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.1
. In Subsection 2.1, we show that the function
f_{n}(\cdot)
possesses a symmetry. Then in Subsections 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4, we give the proof for three cases:
n\geq 40,k\geq 10
;
n\geq 40,k<10
; and
n<40
, respectively. For simplicity, let
X_{n,k}
denote the random variable
B(n,k)
.

2.1
Symmetry of the function
f_{n}(\cdot)

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
n\geq 1
, we have
f_{n}(k)=f_{n}(n-k),\forall k=0,1,\ldots,n
.

Proof:
For any
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})=n\cdot\frac{k}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac{k(n-k)}{n},

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{n,n-k})=n\cdot\frac{n-k}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{n-k}{n}\right)=\frac{k(n-k)}{n}.

Thus

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)=P\left(|X_{n,k}-k|\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}\right),

(2.1)

\displaystyle f_{n}(n-k)=P\left(|X_{n,n-k}-(n-k)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}\right).

(2.2)

By (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and the definition of the binomial distribution, we get
f_{n}(k)=f_{n}(n-k)
.

2.2
The case of
n\geq 40,k\geq 10

By Proposition
2.1
, it is enough to consider the case
k\leq n/2
. Note that

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=P\left(|X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]|\leq\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}\right)=P\left(-1\leq\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}\leq 1\right).

Let
Y_{n,k},k=1,\ldots,n
, be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with the distribution
B(1,k/n)
. Then
X_{n,k}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{n,k}
have the same distribution. By the central limit theorem, it holds that

\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\ \mbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

To complete the proof of this case, we need the convergence rate of the above convergence. Since
Y_{n,k}\sim B(1,k/n)
, we have

\displaystyle E[Y_{n,k}]=\frac{k}{n},

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(Y_{n,k})=1\cdot\frac{k}{n}\cdot\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac{k(n-k)}{n^{2}},

\displaystyle P\left(|Y_{n,k}-E[Y_{n,k}]|=\frac{k}{n}\right)=1-\frac{k}{n},

\displaystyle P\left(|Y_{n,k}-E[Y_{n,k}]|=1-\frac{k}{n}\right)=\frac{k}{n}.

It follows that

E[|Y_{n,k}-EY_{n,k}|^{3}]=\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{3}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)+\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{3}\frac{k}{n}=\frac{k(n-k)(n^{2}+2k^{2}-2nk)}{n^{4}}.

And thus

\displaystyle\sigma:=\sqrt{{\rm Var}(Y_{n,k})}=\frac{\sqrt{k(n-k)}}{n},

\displaystyle\rho:=\frac{E[|Y_{n,k}-E[Y_{n,k}]|^{3}]}{\sigma^{3}}=\frac{n^{2}+2k^{2}-2nk}{n\sqrt{k(n-k)}}.

(2.3)

By the Berry-Esseen inequality and (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P\left(\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}<x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C_{0}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{n}}=C_{0}\frac{n^{2}+2k^{2}-2nk}{n\sqrt{nk(n-k)}},

(2.4)

where
C_{0}
is a positive constant independent of
n
.

By
k\leq n/2
, we know that
n^{2}+2k^{2}-2nk=n^{2}-nk+k(2k-n)\leq n^{2}-nk
. Then by (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|P\left(\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}<x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C_{0}\frac{n^{2}-nk}{n\sqrt{nk(n-k)}}=C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{n-k}{nk}}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}.

(2.5)

By
[
11
, Corollary 1]
, we can take
C_{0}=0.4748
. Then in virtue of the condition that
k\geq 10
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}\leq\frac{0.4748}{\sqrt{10}}<0.15014495,

which together with (
2.5
) implies that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\left|P\left(\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}<-1\right)-\Phi(-1)\right|<0.15014495,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left|P\left(\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}<1+\varepsilon\right)-\Phi(1+\varepsilon)\right|<0.15014495

(2.7)

By (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), we get

\displaystyle\left|P\left(-1\leq\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}<1+\varepsilon\right)-\left(\Phi(1+\varepsilon)-\Phi(-1)\right)\right|<0.3002899.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle\left|P\left(-1\leq\frac{X_{n,k}-E[X_{n,k}]}{\sqrt{{\rm Var}(X_{n,k})}}\leq 1\right)-\left(\Phi(1)-\Phi(-1)\right)\right|\leq 0.3002899.

(2.8)

Since
\Phi(1)-\Phi(-1)\approx 0.68268949>0.68268948
, by (
2.8
), we know that when
n\geq 40,k\geq 10
,

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)>0.68268948-0.3002899=0.38239958.

Since
f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}
is a decreasing function in
n
, and
f_{40}(1)\approx 0.36323244
, we get that for any
n\geq 40,k\geq 10
,

f_{n}(1)\leq f_{40}(1)<0.38239958<f_{n}(k).

2.3
The case of
n\geq 40,k<10

The case
k=0
is trivial. We only need to prove
f_{n}(k)\geq f_{n}(1)
for
k=2,3,\ldots,9
. Since
n\geq 40
, it is easy to know that

\displaystyle 1<\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}<2,\ \mbox{for}\ k=2,3,4;

\displaystyle 2<\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}<3,\ \mbox{for}\ k=5,6,7,8,9.

It follows that for
k=2,3,4
,

\displaystyle\left\{|X_{n,k}-k|\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}\right\}=\{X_{n,k}=k-1,k,k+1\},

and for
k=5,6,7,8,9
,

\displaystyle\left\{|X_{n,k}-k|\leq\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}\right\}=\{X_{n,k}=k-2,k-1,k,k+1,k+2\}.

In the following, we decompose the proof for
k=2,3,\ldots,9,
into five cases.

Case 1
:
k=2
. Now

\displaystyle f_{n}(2)

\displaystyle=P(X_{n,2}=1,2,3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[2(n-2)^{n-1}+\frac{10}{3}(n-1)(n-2)^{n-2}\right].

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(2)\geq f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[2(n-2)^{n-1}+\frac{10}{3}(n-1)(n-2)^{n-2}\right]\geq\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{n-2}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}+\frac{10}{3}\left(\frac{n-2}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{10}{3}\left(\frac{n-2}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq 1.

Since
\left(\frac{x-2}{x-1}\right)^{x-2}
is a decreasing function of
x
when
x\geq 40
, and the limit is
\frac{1}{e}
, we have that
\frac{10}{3}\left(\frac{n-2}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq\frac{10}{3e}>1
. Hence (
2.9
) holds.

Case 2
:
k=3
. Now

\displaystyle f_{n}(3)

\displaystyle=P(X_{n,3}=2,3,4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[\frac{9}{2}(n-1)(n-3)^{n-2}+\frac{63}{8}(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)^{n-3}\right].

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(3)\geq f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[\frac{9}{2}(n-1)(n-3)^{n-2}+\frac{63}{8}(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)^{n-3}\right]\geq\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{9}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}+\frac{63}{8}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{n-1}\right)^{n-3}\geq 1.

It is easy to know that
\left(1-\frac{2}{x-1}\right)^{x-2}
and
1-\frac{1}{x-1}
are two increasing functions,
\left(1-\frac{2}{x-1}\right)^{x-3}
is a decreasing function of
x
on
[40,\infty)
, and
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{2}{x-1}\right)^{x-3}=\frac{1}{e^{2}}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{9}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}+\frac{63}{8}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{n-1}\right)^{n-3}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{9}{2}\left(\frac{37}{39}\right)^{38}+\frac{63}{8}\cdot\frac{38}{39}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{2}}\approx 1.65>1.

Hence (
2.10
) holds.

Case 3
:
k=4
. Now

\displaystyle f_{n}(4)

\displaystyle=P(X_{n,4}=3,4,5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[\frac{32}{3}(n-1)(n-2)(n-4)^{n-3}+\frac{96}{5}(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)^{n-4}\right].

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(4)\geq f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{n-1}}\left[\frac{32}{3}(n-1)(n-2)(n-4)^{n-3}+\frac{96}{5}(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)^{n-4}\right]\geq\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{32}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{n-1}\right)^{n-3}+\frac{96}{5}\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{n-1}\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{n-1}\right)^{n-4}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\left(\frac{32}{3}+\frac{96}{5}\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq 1.

Since
\left(1-\frac{3}{x-1}\right)^{x-2}
is an increasing function of
x
on
[40,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{32}{3}+\frac{96}{5}\right)\left(1-\frac{3}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq\left(\frac{32}{3}+\frac{96}{5}\right)\left(\frac{36}{39}\right)^{38}\approx 1.42635>1.

Hence (
2.11
) holds.

Case 4
:
k=5,6,7,8
. Now

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=P(X_{n,k}=k-2,k-1,k,k+1,k+2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\binom{n}{i}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{n-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{n-i}.

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(k)\geq f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

(2.12)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{n-i}\geq\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{k^{i}}{i!}\frac{(n-2)\cdots(n-i+1)(n-k)^{n-i}}{(n-1)^{n-2}}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{k^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{k^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{40-1}\right)^{40-2}\geq 1,

where in the last step, we used the fact that
\left(1-\frac{k-1}{x-1}\right)^{x-2}
is an increasing function of
x
on
[40,\infty)
for any
k=5,6,7,8
.

Define

C_{k}:=\sum_{i=k-2}^{k+2}\frac{k^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{40-1}\right)^{40-2}.

By calculation, we get

C_{5}\approx 1.80299,\ C_{6}\approx 1.52806,\ C_{7}\approx 1.26193,\ C_{8}\approx 1.01213.

Hence (
2.12
) holds.

Case 5
:
k=9
. Now we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(9)

\displaystyle=P(X_{n,9}=7,8,9,10,11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=7}^{11}\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\left(\frac{9}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{9}{n}\right)^{n-i}.

(2.13)

Then we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(9)\geq f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=7}^{9}\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}\left(\frac{9}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{9}{n}\right)^{n-i}\geq\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=7}^{11}\frac{9^{i}}{i!}\frac{(n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-i+1)(n-9)^{n-i}}{(n-1)^{n-2}}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=7}^{11}\frac{9^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{8}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\cdot\frac{(n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-i+1)}{(n-9)^{i-2}}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=7}^{9}\frac{9^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{8}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq 1,

(2.15)

where in the last step, we used the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-i+1)}{(n-9)^{i-2}}\geq 1,\quad\forall i=7,\cdots,11.

(2.16)

For
i=7,8,9,10
, we have
n-i+1\geq n-9
, and thus (
2.16
) holds. For
i=11
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(n-3)\cdots(n-10)}{(n-9)^{9}}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(n-10)}{(n-9)^{2}}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\displaystyle 6n\geq 61

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\

\displaystyle n\geq 40.

Hence (
2.16
) is true.

Since
(1-\frac{8}{x-1})^{x-2}
is an increasing function of
x
on
[100,\infty)
, we get that for any
n\geq 100
,

\sum_{i=7}^{9}\frac{9^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{8}{n-1}\right)^{n-2}\geq\sum_{i=7}^{9}\frac{9^{i}}{i!}\left(1-\frac{8}{99}\right)^{98}\approx 1.25277>1.

It follows that (
2.15
) holds and thus (
2.14
) holds for any
n\geq 100
.

For any
n=40,41,\cdots,99
, by direct calculations in virtue of (
2.13
) and
f_{n}(1)=\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n-1}
, we get that

f_{n}(9)>0.61>0.38>f_{n}(1).

In a word, (
2.14
) holds for any
n\geq 40
.
