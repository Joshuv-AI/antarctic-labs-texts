Cardio-Obstetrics Approach in Management of Marfan Syndrome During Pregnancy
Authors: Danish Iltaf Satti, Garima Sharma, Eunjung Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Management of Marfan syndrome and its complications during pregnancy requires a multidisciplinary approach to minimize adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. We present 2 cases to highlight the key considerations and management strategies addressed by the pregnancy heart team for patients with Marfan syndrome with and without chronic dissection.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 36-year-old female patient presented to the multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics clinic at 12 weeks of gestation. Her past medical history was significant for Marfan syndrome (MFS) complicated by types A and B aortic dissection at age 33. At the time of aortic dissection, she underwent surgical repair of the aorta and mechanical aortic valve replacement. Her postoperative course was complicated by decompensated heart failure with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%-25%, but her left ventricular systolic function recovered to normal range after 1 year. In anticipation of pregnancy, she had transitioned from warfarin to enoxaparin for therapeutic anticoagulation. Of note, she did not have a family history of connective tissue disorder or inherited aortopathy. The patient never had genetic counselling/testing prior to pregnancy and declined chorionic villus sample for genetic testing when offered during pregnancy. Her third trimester fetal echocardiogram was normal without any congenital anomalies. Case 2

A 24-year-old female patient with a family history of MFS, fatal aortic dissection in her father, and personal history of severe mitral regurgitation requiring surgical repair at age 21 years presented to the cardio-obstetrics clinic at 6 weeks of gestation. She was found to have a fibrillin-1 mutation. She had undergone preimplantation genetic testing and embryo transfer prior to the highly desired pregnancy. Her most recent echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%-60%, myxomatous mitral valve status post mitral valve annuloplasty with normal valve function and aortic root diameter of 39 mm.

Question 1: How would you comprehensively stratify the risks of maternal mortality and cardiovascular complications?

The first patient presented with a challenging combination of MFS, history of types A and B aortic dissection requiring surgical repair with mechanical aortic valve replacement, heart failure with recovered ejection fraction, and gestational hypertension. A comprehensive risk assessment would involve the consideration of several factors such as the maternal risks of morbidity and mortality, fetal risks of preterm birth, growth restriction, or death, as well as the impact of this pregnancy on long-term maternal outcomes.1

Various risk models have been developed and are commonly used as a basis for estimating risks, including the CARPREG (Cardiac Disease in Pregnancy) I, CARPREG II, ZAHARA (Zwangerschap bij vrouwen met een Aangeboren HARtAfwijking-II), and, the modified World Health Organization classification schemes.2 The modified World Health Organization classification takes into consideration both mechanical valve and aortic root dilation in MFS as class III risks.3 This implies a significant risk of maternal adverse cardiovascular outcomes at 19%-27%. The total CARPREG II risk score for a combination of MFS, previous medical history of aortic dissection, and mechanical valve is >4 points, which equates to a 41% risk of maternal cardiac complications.4 Based on ZAHARA, a history of mechanical valve prosthesis alone places the patient in the highest risk category with an estimated cardiovascular complication risk of >70%.5 Even though these risk scores take into account the history of MFS and aortic diameter, it is not clear how the history of aortic repair or presence of chronic dissection affects pregnancy.

The second patient we presented had a history of valvular disease that required surgical repair. Additionally, her history of MFS with aortic diameter <40 mm places her in modified World Health Organization class II, which predicts cardiovascular event rate between 10% and 19%.3 Hence, patients with MFS who are pregnant need a very close follow-up from a multidisciplinary pregnancy heart team to ensure delivery of a healthy baby and a mother free of cardiac complications.

Question 2: What is the risk of aortic dissection in patient with MFS during pregnancy and postpartum period, and what preventive measures should be undertaken?

The incidence of aortic dissection in patients with MFS displays significant variability, with the majority of case reports emphasizing the presence of severe complications. However, it is important to acknowledge the potential bias in reporting, which tends to favor the documentation of complicated cases over uncomplicated ones, potentially leading to an overestimation of pregnancy-related complications. Nonetheless, previous studies have indicated that rates of MFS-related aortic dissection range from 1% in women with an aortic diameter of <40 mm to as high as 10% in high-risk patients, such as those with an aortic root diameter exceeding 40 mm, rapid dilatation, or a history of previous dissection of the ascending aorta.6

In the case of our first patient, her past medical history of types A and B aortic dissection requiring surgical repair and mechanical aortic valve replacement places her in the high-risk MFS category. Consequently, she may have ∼10% risk of recurrent aortic dissection during pregnancy or the postpartum period.6 To mitigate potential complications and monitor aortic diameters in both patients, surveillance transthoracic echocardiograms were performed during each trimester, after delivery (before hospital discharge) and 2-4 weeks postpartum as per the current recommendations.7 To further visualize the aortic arch and descending aorta, magnetic resonance angiography without contrast was performed during the third trimester. Genetic counseling was also offered at the initial cardio-obstetrics visit.

Hypertension is a significant risk factor for aortic dissection during pregnancy and, therefore, warrants guideline-directed treatment through beta-blocker therapy in all patients with MFS during pregnancy and the postpartum period.7 Labetalol is suggested as the beta blocker of choice for use in pregnant women with hypertension, because angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin-receptor blockers are contraindicated due to potential of teratogenicity.7 Beta-blocker therapy has also been shown to lessen aortic growth rates in MFS and is recommended to lessen hemodynamic aortic stress in MFS and related conditions.7 In our patients, strict blood pressure control (<120-130 mm Hg) was achieved by prescribing labetalol.

Question 3: How would you manage anticoagulation for mechanical heart valve during pregnancy?

Managing anticoagulation in pregnant individuals with a mechanical heart valve presents a complex clinical challenge due to increased cardiovascular events related to valve complications.8 The primary goal is to strike a delicate balance between preventing thromboembolic events associated with inadequate anticoagulation and minimizing the risk of bleeding complications, particularly in the developing fetus. The 2 main options for anticoagulation, therefore, are vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH).8

Warfarin is the most effective anticoagulant at preventing thrombotic complications. However, it poses risks to the pregnancy due to its ability to cross the placenta, potentially leading to miscarriage, spontaneous abortion, or fetal intracranial hemorrhage.8 In contrast, LMWH is not teratogenic, but individuals with mechanical heart valves who receive LMWH are at an increased risk of thrombotic events if the dosing, monitoring, or administration is suboptimal.8 Hence, there is no universally optimal anticoagulation strategy for all pregnant individuals, and the preferred approach should be determined through shared decision making between the patient and their physician.

The current guidelines offer several strategies for anticoagulation management during pregnancy. These include continuing warfarin throughout the entire pregnancy (if the daily dose is ≤5 mg), using dose-adjusted LMWH throughout the pregnancy, or adopting a sequential approach. Sequential therapy involves administering dose-adjusted LMWH during the first trimester and transitioning to warfarin during the second and third trimesters.8 In our first case, we chose to follow the sequential therapy approach, resuming warfarin during the second trimester (<5 mg per day) and bridging the transition from warfarin to unfractionated heparin prior to delivery (at 36 weeks) with a target partial prothrombin time of 50-80 seconds.8 When the patient was on enoxaparin, anti Xa levels were checked every 7-10 days to ensure therapeutic level.

Question 4: How would you plan the delivery to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes?

In pregnant patients with a history of MFS and chronic aortic dissection, cesarean delivery is recommended.7 Additionally, this should take place in a setting where cardiothoracic surgery is available for urgent surgical management in case of acute dissection.7 As such, our patient was scheduled for an elective cesarean delivery at 38 weeks of gestation and delivered a healthy baby. Our second patient had a magnetic resonance angiography during the third trimester that showed an increased aortic root size of 42 mm. Hence, she underwent elective cesarean section at 39 weeks without any complications. It is important to note that 60% of aortic dissections in MFS occur during postpartum period at a median of 6 days postpartum and these patients need close postpartum follow-up and surveillance imaging even after delivery.9

Furthermore, discussing contraceptive methods with patients who have chronic aortic dissection and MFS is extremely important, either as part of preconception counseling or during their postpartum management. Despite the high efficacy, combined estrogen and progesterone oral contraceptive pills are contraindicated in women with mechanical heart valves and complex congenital heart disease due to the increased risk of arterial and venous thrombosis.10 Hence, other methods such as progesterone-only contraception or intrauterine devices may be considered.11

Conclusions

These cases outline the intricate challenges of managing high-risk patients with MFS during pregnancy. A multidisciplinary pregnancy heart team is essential in enabling comprehensive risk assessment, close antenatal monitoring, and prevention of maternal and fetal complications. Furthermore, the limited data available regarding the management of chronic aortic dissection during pregnancy underscore the need for larger and comprehensive studies in this area.
