A frozen prelude and an open challenge
Authors: Anna H. Xue, Subhasis Chatterjee, Joseph S. Coselli
Published: 2026

Open surgical replacement of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) has remained among the most complex cardiovascular surgeries since it was first performed in the 1950s (1,2). Surgical dissection of the proximal aortic clamp zone—either between the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries or immediately distal to the left subclavian artery—during TAAA repair can be challenging, especially in the setting of adhesions from prior aortic arch surgery and/or chronic aortic dissection. Not only is there a risk of injury to the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, but the fragile aortic tissue is also prone to iatrogenic injury during surgical dissection. The introduction of a transverse aortic arch repair with frozen elephant trunk (FET) to replace the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta in one stage has allowed the possibility of distal ‘shifting’ of the proximal anastomosis during TAAA repair (3).

Martens et al. address the use of the FET technique to provide a platform for completing second-stage open TAAA repair, with the proximal anastomosis made to the distal end of the FET (4). The authors presented a case of a young patient with Marfan syndrome who underwent prior aortic root remodeling, and ascending aorta and proximal aortic arch repair for acute DeBakey type 1 aortic dissection and who presented with an aortic arch aneurysm and TAAA. The authors described their experience of first performing a redo sternotomy, aortic root replacement with a mechanical valved conduit, and transverse aortic arch replacement with FET utilizing a Thoraflex Hybrid device (Terumo Aortic, Inchinnan, UK) in preparation for subsequent open TAAA repair. Several months later, the patient re-presented with a symptomatic enlarging extent II TAAA with contained rupture and underwent urgent open repair. As in the classic elephant trunk technique, timing of a planned second stage is generally guided by aortic diameter and symptom onset rather than a fixed time interval, and urgent completion—as occurred here—is a recognized risk of any staged approach (5).

Notably, during the TAAA repair, the authors repurposed a branched collared aortic arch replacement graft (Siena 4-branch Plexus graft, Terumo Aortic) that they cut into two sections. The portion with the collared aortic graft was used for the proximal anastomosis to address the size discrepancy between the relatively large diameter of the FET endograft portion (40 mm) and the smaller diameter of the TAAA graft (28 mm). Traditionally, collared grafts are frequently used in classic elephant trunk repair to accommodate a discrepancy in diameter during total arch graft replacement (5). The second section of the arch graft, the branched, non-collared portion, was used for distal abdominal repair. Extracorporeal circulation and a sequential clamping technique were utilized.

Extent II TAAA repair is the most extensive and often the most challenging, with greater operative risk than other less extensive TAAA repairs. The technique of an extent II TAAA repair has evolved over the course of several decades to protect the spinal cord and visceral organs and is described in detail elsewhere (6). Surgical adjuncts such as extracorporeal circulation, selective visceral perfusion, sequential aortic clamping, judicious reimplantation of intercostal arteries, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage have been developed and utilized to ameliorate the risk associated with an extensive TAAA repair. For patients with connective tissue disorders and intact TAAA requiring intervention, open repair over endovascular repair is a Class I recommendation in the 2022 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Aortic Disease (7). Regarding two-stage or three-stage TAAA repair [i.e., thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) extension followed by open surgery], the presence of a previously placed FET significantly decreases the extent of surgical dissection required to facilitate proximal aortic clamping and therefore potentially lessens the risk of injury to adjacent structures. A sterile ultrasound probe can also be utilized intraoperatively to identify the distalmost extent of the FET stent, and the aortic cross-clamp can be placed 3–4 cm proximal to the distal end of the FET stent without difficulty.

Martens and coauthors commented that through their experience over time with transverse aortic arch replacement with FET utilizing the hybrid stent device, they now size the hybrid graft significantly smaller to avoid major size discrepancies between the FET and the open TAAA repair grafts (4). In our experience, if the planned second stage will be performed endovascularly, it is even more crucial to appropriately size the FET portion of the hybrid stent as there is an upper limit to the size of commercially available TEVAR stents. A large first-stage FET stent (e.g., 40 mm) may significantly limit endovascular options to extend the FET as the risk of TEVAR separation and/or endoleak is higher if there is not adequate stent apposition or stent overlapping distance. The two-portion Siena arch graft technique described by the authors allows for better length alignment of the main aortic graft as well as the visceral branches. This includes using the branched portion of the arch graft (Siena 4-branch Plexus graft; Terumo Aortic) for reimplantation of the visceral arteries. However, readers should be aware that the side branches from the collared aortic graft may not match the appropriate size necessary to reimplant the visceral arteries. The native celiac axis and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) are typically larger than the renal arteries and therefore often require larger side branch grafts. However, when the collared aortic graft is used for the distal portion of the TAAA repair as the authors describe, the smaller 8-mm side branches correspond in position to the celiac axis and SMA while the larger 10-mm side branches align with the renal arteries. A feasible alternative to the collared Siena branched graft is a prefabricated four-branch TAAA graft (e.g., Gelweave 4-branched TAAA graft, Terumo Aortic) with 10-mm side branches for the celiac axis and SMA and 8-mm side branches for the renal arteries.

While the presence of a previously placed FET may facilitate a subsequent open TAAA replacement, it remains an intricate procedure. Even with advances in adjunctive techniques to protect the spinal cord and visceral organs, paraplegia, renal dysfunction, and pulmonary issues remain some of the most potentially devastating consequences of the procedure. Martens and colleagues addressed spinal cord protection primarily with CSF drainage, which is consistent with the generally understood lower risk of spinal cord deficit in young patients with Marfan syndrome. In our own practice, motor evoked potentials (MEP)/somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) monitoring is not routinely used given concerns regarding reliability and interpretability, and the artery of Adamkiewicz is not selectively targeted for reimplantation but is instead addressed through broad reattachment of intercostal/lumbar arteries spanning T7–L1 (8,9).

Martens and colleagues have demonstrated their experience performing an open extent II TAAA repair after FET using a two-portion Siena arch-graft technique with excellent clinical results. At our institution with over three decades of experience with TAAA repair, we have similarly refined our operative technique over time to prevent life-altering complications (10). Open TAAA repair after FET is a technique that, along with the utilization of extracorporeal circulation, sequential aortic clamping, selective visceral perfusion, and CSF drainage, can potentially decrease the morbidity of TAAA repair. However, even with these adjuncts, open TAAA repair remains a complex procedure that should be performed by highly experienced surgeons at high-volume aortic centers such as that of the authors. Successful open TAAA repair after FET is not something to be taken for granted.
