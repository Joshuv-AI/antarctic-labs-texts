Comprehensive profiling of antibiotic resistance genes and functional clusters of orthologous groups annotation of gut microbiota in Indonesian Kedu chickens
Authors: Ikania Agusetyaningsih, Dela Ayu Lestari, Nuruliarizki Shinta Pandupuspitasari, Asep Setiaji, Syaddad Verahry Philco, Sugiharto Sugiharto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance is a growing global health concern, with poultry systems acting as important reservoirs of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). However, resistome and functional profiles of indigenous chickens raised under traditional systems remain underexplored. This study aimed to characterize the antibiotic resistome, virulence factor genes, and metabolic potential of gut microbiota in Indonesian Kedu chickens using a shotgun metagenomic approach. Digesta samples from five gastrointestinal segments of 21 healthy adult chickens were analyzed through high-throughput sequencing. ARGs were identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) and Antibiotic Resistance Genes Databases (ARDB), while virulence factors and functional genes were annotated using Virulence Factor Database (VFDB), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), and Carbohydrate-Active EnZymes (CAZy) databases.Results revealed a diverse resistome dominated by multidrug resistance and efflux pump mechanisms, with prominent genes associated with fluoroquinolone, tetracycline, β-lactam, and glycopeptide resistance. The detection of clinically relevant ARGs suggests that genetic determinants associated with antimicrobial resistance are present in the gut microbiota of traditionally raised Kedu chickens, although metagenomic data alone cannot determine whether these genes are actively expressed or confer phenotypic resistance. Virulence factor analysis showed functions related to adherence, immune evasion, iron acquisition, quorum sensing, and efflux activity, reflecting strong microbial adaptability. Functional profiling demonstrated enrichment in translation, carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, genome maintenance, and cell envelope biogenesis. Additionally, CAZyme analysis indicated a high capacity for complex polysaccharide degradation, supporting efficient utilization of fiber-rich traditional diets.In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive metagenomic overview of antibiotic resistance and functional potential in Kedu chicken gut microbiota, emphasizing the importance of incorporating indigenous poultry into antimicrobial resistance surveillance within a One Health framework.

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance has become one of the most urgent global health issues, posing a threat to both human and animal health. Poultry production systems, especially those involving chickens, are seen as significant reservoirs of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) due to the extensive use of antimicrobials for growth promotion, disease prevention, and treatment. The chicken gastrointestinal tract offers an ideal environment for the accumulation and exchange of ARGs among diverse microbial communities, raising concerns about their potential spread through the food chain and the environment.1 Recent metagenomic research has uncovered a significant presence of ARGs in the gut microbiota of poultry, indicating that the gut serves as a major hub for the resistome. Genes like tetX, mcr, and blaNDM are commonly found in bacteria associated with poultry, many of which are connected to mobile genetic elements (MGEs) that enable horizontal gene transfer. This process not only supports the persistence of resistance within the poultry gut but also increases the likelihood of spreading across bacterial populations and hosts.2

Notably, the gut of chickens harbors commensal bacteria that serve as important reservoirs for ARGs. Resistance genes for tetracyclines, including tet(W), tet(O), and tet(Q), are frequently found in non-pathogenic gut bacteria, highlighting the concealed potential for ARGs to be transferred to pathogenic species.3 This discovery indicates that even bacteria typically deemed harmless are pivotal in sustaining and disseminating resistance genes within the poultry microbiome. Analyses comparing resistomes among humans, pigs, and chickens have revealed that poultry possess a unique and dynamic resistome, with resistance to tetracycline, aminoglycoside, and β-lactam being the most common.4 Importantly, many of these resistome were not only detected but also actively expressed, suggesting they are functional within the chicken gut microbiota. This evidence underscores the role of poultry as a significant source of active resistance elements, which could have extensive implications for human health through zoonotic transmission.

Although research has predominantly concentrated on commercial broilers and layers, the resistome of native chicken breeds has not been extensively studied. Indigenous breeds, like the Indonesian Kedu chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus), are typically raised using traditional management methods, which often involve different patterns of antibiotic use compared to industrial systems. These variations could affect the diversity, prevalence, and transferability of ARGs within their gut microbiota, highlighting a significant research gap in understanding resistance ecology in traditional poultry systems. Monitoring ARGs in native chickens is crucial within the One Health framework, which emphasizes the interconnection between human, animal, and environmental health. The potential overlap of ARGs between poultry and human pathogens highlights the risk of cross-species transmission through foodborne exposure, direct contact, or environmental contamination. Therefore, analyzing the resistome of Kedu chickens will offer valuable insights into the dynamics of antibiotic resistance at the human-animal-environment interface in Indonesia.

Despite the increasing application of shotgun metagenomics to characterize poultry gut resistomes, genomic studies of indigenous chicken breeds remain limited, particularly in Southeast Asia. Therefore, this study comprehensively characterized the gut resistome, virulome, and functional gene repertoire of Indonesian Kedu chickens using shotgun metagenomic sequencing integrated with Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD), Antibiotic Resistance Genes Database (ARDB), Virulence Factor Database (VFDB), (COG), and Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) databases. By combining antibiotic resistance, virulence, and functional genomic annotations, this work provides an integrative metagenomic overview of microbial adaptive potential beyond conventional ARG cataloging. The findings establish a baseline genomic resource for indigenous poultry and contribute to a better understanding of antimicrobial resistance ecology within the One Health framework.

Materials and methods

Birds and sampling

Twenty-one healthy adult Kedu chickens, aged between 6 and 8 months and weighing from 1.8 to 2.3 kg, were obtained from traditional free-range systems in Kedu Village, Temanggung District, Central Java, Indonesia. The sample size was determined using statistical analysis (α = 0.05) and previous poultry microbiome research to ensure sufficient detection of differences among gastrointestinal tract (GIT) segments. The chickens were reared under typical smallholder conditions, allowing them to scavenge during the day and receive supplemental mixed grain feed. After euthanasia by cervical dislocation, the GIT was aseptically removed. Digesta samples were taken from five segments (crop, gizzard, jejunum, colon, and cecum) using sterile tools to prevent cross-contamination. For each gastrointestinal segment, equal amounts of DNA from the 21 individual chickens were pooled to generate one composite sample per segment. Each sample was placed in sterile cryovials, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction

Microbial DNA was isolated using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions, which included bead-beating to improve the breakdown of Gram-positive bacteria. The concentration and purity of the DNA were evaluated with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and through agarose gel electrophoresis. Only those samples with A260/A280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0 were selected for sequencing.

Metagenomic sequencing

DNA was extracted and fragmented to 3500 bp by Covaris M220 (Gene Company Limited, China) to make a paired-end library. NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA-Seq (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) was used to construct a Paired-end library. Sequencing of paired-end library was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Quality control and assembly

Raw reads were quality-filtered with Fastp v0.20.0 using the following parameters: minimum read length of 50 bp, quality score threshold of Q20, adapter trimming, and polyG removal. Host-derived sequences were removed by mapping reads against the Gallus gallus reference genome (GRCg6a) using Bowtie2 v2.4.4 with default settings. Reads aligning to the host genome were discarded prior to downstream analyses to minimize host DNA contamination and improve microbial sequence recovery. The efficiency of host sequence removal was evaluated by comparing read numbers before and after host filtering. Clean reads were subsequently assembled using MEGAHIT v1.1.2 with a k-mer range of 21–99 and a minimum contig length of 300 bp. This k-mer range was selected to optimize assembly performance across microbial genomes with varying abundance levels, where smaller k-mers improve recovery of low-abundance sequences and larger k-mers enhance assembly continuity and reduce misassemblies in high-coverage regions.

Statistical analysis

Metagenomic data processing, descriptive analyses, and data visualization were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The abundance profiles of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), virulence-associated genes, and functional categories were summarized descriptively based on annotations from the CARD, ARDB, VFDB, COG, and CAZy databases. Figures were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4). For consistency throughout the manuscript, ARG, virulence, and functional annotations are reported using both total read abundance (number of sequencing reads assigned to a feature) and gene count (number of annotated genes or gene variants identified). In all figures and text, results are presented in the format “reads (gene counts)” unless otherwise specified. To reduce the likelihood of false-positive annotations, all analyses were performed using curated reference databases and standard filtering procedures implemented in the annotation pipeline. In addition, interpretation focused on ARGs with substantial read support and biologically plausible resistance mechanisms. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that metagenomic annotation may still contain some uncertainty, and therefore the detected ARGs should be interpreted as genetic potential rather than direct evidence of phenotypic resistance.

Results

CARD-ARO

The CARD database analysis revealed a variety of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) within the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens (Fig. 1). In total, ten representative ARGs were identified, spanning several gene families and resistance mechanisms, including efflux pumps, target alteration, and antibiotic inactivation. Efflux pump genes were the most prominent group. Among them, patB, associated with fluoroquinolone resistance, was the most abundant, with 65,834 reads (1581 gene counts), underscoring its major role in conferring multidrug resistance. Other efflux genes, such as emrA (fluoroquinolone resistance, 994 reads/11 counts) and tetA(P) (tetracycline resistance, 622 reads/4 counts), were also well represented. Additional efflux-related genes, including emrY (516 reads/15 counts) and tet(Z) (8 reads/1 count), further highlight the dominance of efflux-mediated resistance strategies in the microbial community. Genes conferring resistance through target alteration were also detected. These included sul1 (340 reads/16 counts), which provides resistance to sulfonamides, as well as vanA (2334 reads;92 counts) and vanD (4676 reads;133 counts), both of which are involved in glycopeptide resistance. The coexistence of vanA and vanD suggests that multiple mechanisms of glycopeptide resistance are present within the microbiota. Additionally, an antibiotic inactivation mechanism was represented by the rphA gene (rifamycin resistance, 1378 reads/37 counts).Fig. 1Molecular data, models and algorithms in CARD based on Antibiotic Resistance Ontology (ARO).Fig. 1

The VFDB-based analysis of gut microbiota in Kedu chicken revealed a broad distribution of virulence factor genes categorized into four major functional groups: defensive virulence factors, offensive virulence factors, nonspecific virulence factors, and regulation of virulence-associated genes (Fig. 4). Defensive virulence factors comprised genes related to antiphagocytosis, serum resistance, stress proteins, phase variation, complement proteases, and immunoglobulin proteases, with antiphagocytosis and serum resistance showing the highest representation. Offensive virulence factors included genes involved in adherence, secretion systems, toxin production, and invasion, with adherence-related genes being the most abundant. Based on this study (Fig. 5), the ACF gene cluster from Vibrio cholerae was detected with 15,076 reads representing 232 genes, while AS from Enterococcus faecium was identified with 14 reads, and the collagen-binding adhesin Acm from E. faecium showed 2744 reads across 146 genes. Discussion

Complex antibiotic Resistome and dominance of efflux pump-mediated resistance in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens

The CARD database analysis showed that the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens contains a diverse repertoire of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), indicating that this indigenous breed harbors a complex resistome despite being predominantly raised under traditional, non-intensive production systems. Although many of these resistance determinants have also been reported in commercial poultry, their presence in Kedu chickens suggests that ARGs can be maintained under a wide range of production environments and are not exclusively associated with intensive farming. When interpreted alongside the virulence-associated genes identified by the VFDB and the functional profiles generated from the COG and CAZy databases, the findings provide a broader view of microbial adaptation within the gastrointestinal tract. Resistance mechanisms, particularly efflux pumps, together with colonization-related traits and carbohydrate-metabolizing functions, likely contribute to microbial persistence and ecological fitness while supporting adaptation to the fiber-rich diet typically consumed by Kedu chickens. Collectively, these results suggest that the gut microbiota functions as an integrated ecosystem in which antimicrobial resistance, metabolic capacity, and host-associated interactions work together to promote microbial survival and adaptation within the intestinal environment.

Large-scale metagenomic studies of commercial poultry have demonstrated substantially greater resistome diversity than that observed in the present study, with chicken gut microbiomes harboring 235–304 ARG types and more than one thousand ARG genotypes distributed across multiple antimicrobial resistance classes.5 Commercial poultry resistomes are commonly dominated by tetracycline, aminoglycoside, macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS), β-lactam, and multidrug resistance genes, highlighting the role of the chicken gut as a major reservoir of antimicrobial resistance determinants.6 Furthermore, many poultry-associated ARGs are transcriptionally active, suggesting their ecological persistence and functional relevance within the intestinal environment.7, 8, 9

In contrast, antimicrobial resistance in the Kedu chicken gut microbiota was represented by eleven major ARGs spanning several resistance mechanisms, including efflux pumps, target modification, and antibiotic inactivation. Although the diversity of ARGs was lower than that reported in commercial broiler and layer chickens, the presence of multiple resistance pathways indicates that a functionally diverse resistome is maintained even under traditional management systems. The predominance of multidrug efflux-related genes, particularly patB, suggests that efflux-mediated resistance plays a central role in shaping the Kedu chicken resistome.

The most prevalent mechanism identified in this investigation was efflux pump-mediated resistance. The high abundance of patB, a multidrug efflux gene associated with fluoroquinolone resistance, highlights its importance within the gut microbial community. Efflux pumps are among the oldest and most versatile bacterial resistance mechanisms, enabling the extrusion of a broad spectrum of antibiotics and other toxic compounds.10 The predominance of patB together with other efflux-associated genes, including emrA, emrY, tetA(P), and tet(Z), suggests selection for broad-spectrum resistance strategies that enhance microbial survival under varying environmental conditions. Even in traditional or small-scale production systems, exposure to antimicrobials through environmental contamination, feed additives, or horizontal gene transfer may contribute to the persistence of these genes.11

Since tetracyclines are among the most widely used antimicrobial agents in poultry production worldwide, the detection of tetracycline resistance genes, particularly tetA(P) and tet(Z), is noteworthy. These genes are frequently associated with mobile genetic elements and can therefore spread efficiently among gut-associated bacteria.12 Although they were detected at lower abundance than fluoroquinolone resistance determinants, their presence indicates the long-term persistence of tetracycline resistance within poultry-associated microbiomes and may reflect either historical or indirect exposure to tetracycline compounds.

Target modification mechanisms were represented by the detection of sul1, vanA, and vanD. The sul1 gene is commonly associated with class 1 integrons and is widely regarded as an indicator of anthropogenic antimicrobial pressure.13 Its presence in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens suggests that resistance determinants may enter traditional production environments through contaminated feed, water, soil, or other environmental reservoirs. More importantly, the coexistence of vanA and vanD indicates the presence of multiple glycopeptide resistance mechanisms. These genes are typically associated with enterococci and other Gram-positive bacteria and are of particular concern because they confer resistance to vancomycin, a critically important antimicrobial agent in human medicine.14

In addition to efflux-mediated resistance and target modification, antibiotic inactivation was represented by the rphA gene, which confers resistance to rifamycins. Although rifamycins are not commonly used in poultry production, the persistence of rphA suggests that resistance determinants may be retained through co-selection processes and genetic linkage with other ARGs located on mobile genetic elements.15 This finding reinforces the view that ARG maintenance within gut microbial communities is influenced not only by direct antimicrobial exposure but also by broader ecological and evolutionary processes that promote the long-term stability of resistance genes.

Heterogeneous distribution and multidrug resistance patterns of antibiotic resistance genes in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were found to be distributed heterogeneously in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens, a native Indonesian breed, according to resistome analysis based on CARD and ARDB classifications. The results show that the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens harbors a diverse repertoire of ARGs spanning multiple antibiotic classes, despite the fact that these birds are typically raised under small-scale or semi-traditional management systems. This suggests that antimicrobial resistance is not limited to intensive commercial poultry production. The prevalence of resistance mechanisms that allow bacteria to tolerate many drugs at once is reflected in the preponderance of multidrug resistance genes in Kedu chickens. Efflux pump systems, which are highly common in gut-associated bacteria and have been conserved throughout evolution, often mediate multidrug resistance.16 Through horizontal gene transfer, Kedu chickens may be more likely to acquire and retain multidrug resistance genes if they are exposed to open spaces, soil, water, and people or other animals. Similar patterns have been reported in commercial broiler and layer chickens, where multidrug resistance genes frequently represent the largest proportion of the resistome. However, compared with intensive production systems, the ARG profile of Kedu chickens appears less diverse and is characterized by a smaller number of highly abundant resistance determinants. This observation supports the possibility that indigenous free-range poultry may experience different selective pressures while still maintaining a reservoir of antimicrobial resistance genes within their gut microbiota.2

The maintenance of resistance to these antibiotic classes in the gut microbiota of Kedu hens is further demonstrated by high gene counts linked to MLS and tetracycline resistance. Due to the historical widespread use of macrolides and tetracyclines in poultry production, including smallholder farming systems, resistance genes that target these antibiotics are frequently associated with mobile genetic elements that support their long-term maintenance and spread within microbial communities.17 Their presence may reflect historical, indirect, or environmental exposure to antimicrobial compounds; however, this interpretation should be considered cautiously because detailed antibiotic usage records were not available.

Glycopeptide resistance genes were detected at high gene diversity but relatively low read abundance, suggesting a broad yet low-frequency distribution of these genes within the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens. Given that glycopeptides, particularly vancomycin, are classified as critically important antimicrobials for human medicine. The detection of glycopeptide-associated ARGs in chickens raises concerns regarding the potential role of indigenous poultry as reservoirs of clinically relevant resistance genes.18 Future studies integrating mobile genetic element analysis and metagenome-assembled genomes are needed to evaluate the mobility and bacterial hosts of clinically relevant ARGs, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of their potential transmission risk.

The resistome of Kedu chickens goes beyond the most widely used veterinary antibiotics, as evidenced by the moderate resistance gene counts found for the phenicol and aminocoumarin classes. Co-location with other ARGs facilitates co-selection under antimicrobial pressure, and phenicol resistance genes are commonly found in livestock-associated microbiota.15 Although less commonly reported, the identification of aminocoumarin resistance genes indicates the maintenance of resistance features against antibiotics with restricted but significant therapeutic roles. The gut microbiome of Kedu chickens showed a wide but unequal distribution of resistance genes across antibiotic categories, as evidenced by the detection of ARGs linked to the aminoglycoside, beta-lactam, and mupirocin classes. On the other hand, extremely low gene counts for resistance to aminoglycosides, fosmidomycin, polymyxin, and kasugamycin indicate little selective pressure for these drugs. However, under favorable ecological conditions, even low-abundance ARGs can endure and expand by horizontal gene transfer.19

Bacitracin resistance genes were the most prevalent, according to the ARDB-based study, which revealed a clear resistance pattern. The abundance of bacitracin resistance genes in Kedu chickens may be explained by the historical usage of bacitracin in poultry production, especially in traditional and smallholder systems.20 The contrasting ARG profiles observed between CARD and ARDB are likely attributable to differences in database structure, curation strategy, and classification schemes. CARD employs a continuously updated, manually curated Antibiotic Resistance Ontology (ARO) that emphasizes resistance mechanisms, gene families, and molecular functions, enabling the detection of a broader spectrum of multidrug efflux pumps, target-modification genes, and clinically relevant resistance determinants. In contrast, ARDB is an earlier resistance database that primarily classifies genes according to antibiotic categories and contains a more limited collection of curated ARG entries. Consequently, CARD identified multidrug resistance, MLS, tetracycline, and glycopeptide resistance as dominant categories, whereas ARDB showed a predominance of bacitracin-associated genes. These differences do not necessarily indicate biological inconsistencies but rather reflect variations in database coverage, annotation criteria, and gene classification approaches. Therefore, integrating multiple reference databases provides a more comprehensive characterization of the resistome and reduces the risk of overlooking resistance determinants associated with specific annotation frameworks.

Several ARGs identified in this study, including sul1, tetracycline resistance genes, vancomycin resistance determinants, and β-lactam-associated genes, have frequently been associated with mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, transposons, and integrons. These MGEs facilitate horizontal gene transfer among bacterial populations and may contribute to the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance across animal, environmental, and human-associated microbiomes. Given the close interaction of traditionally raised Kedu chickens with their surrounding environment, these birds may serve as reservoirs of transferable resistance determinants within a One Health context. Future studies should incorporate MGE characterization to better assess ARG mobility and cross-species transmission risks.

ARDB-based characterization of antibiotic resistance gene diversity in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens

The gut microbiota of Kedu chickens contains a variety of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) from several therapeutically significant antibiotic classes, according to the ARDB-based annotation. β-lactamase genes are frequently found and extensively distributed among gut bacteria from chickens and other livestock animals, according to recent findings in poultry microbiome studies.21 This is consistent with the predominance of β-lactam resistance genes, particularly blaA, with the highest read abundance. Even in non-intensively managed poultry systems, β-lactam resistance is widespread because β-lactamases are frequently linked to mobile genetic factors that facilitate their movement across microbial communities and between hosts.

Additionally, several vancomycin resistance determinants (vanG, vanXYG, vanWG, and vanTG) were found, indicating that glycopeptide resistance genes are still present in the chicken gut resistome. Glycopeptide resistance genes are frequently found in chicken gut metagenomes and persist even in the absence of direct glycopeptide use, which is consistent with other poultry resistome analyses and suggests that environmental and horizontal gene transfer pathways contribute to their maintenance. Glycopeptides are not frequently utilized in poultry, but because they are classified as critically important antibiotics for human medicine, their resistance determinants are still significant.21

The diversity of ARGs in the Kedu chicken microbiota is further demonstrated by resistance to macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS) antibiotics, denoted by ermF and ermQ. According to earlier metagenomic research in chicken, erm genes are commonly found and frequently associated with mobile elements, which facilitates their persistence and spread within the gut microbiome.22 Similar to this, the persistence of ARGs against antibiotics that have traditionally been employed in poultry production, including traditional or smallholder contexts where such agents may still be used or encountered environmentally, is reflected in bacitracin resistance, which is represented by bacA gene variations. In addition to being at baseline levels in poultry gut resistomes, bacitracin resistance genes have been demonstrated to react to antibiotic exposure.

The identification of rosA, which is linked to fosmidomycin resistance but is less common, implies that the resistome of Kedu chickens also contains efflux-mediated resistance mechanisms. Co-selection with other resistance determinants on shared mobile genetic elements may cause ARGs for less commonly used antibiotics to persist. This is a pattern seen in poultry resistome studies, where different ARG classes coexist regardless of direct selection pressure.22

However, it is important to note that metagenomic detection of ARGs reflects the genetic potential of the microbial community rather than their actual activity. Therefore, metatranscriptomic analyses or other functional validation approaches are required to determine whether the detected ARGs are actively expressed and contribute to phenotypic antibiotic resistance.

Diverse virulence factor profiles reveal broad pathogenic potential in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens

A wide range of pathogenic potential contained within the intestinal microbial community was discovered by the virulence factor profiling of the gut microbiota in Kedu chicken. This revealed a diversified and functionally rich set of virulence-associated genes. The identification of a broad range of virulence determinants, including defensive mechanisms (such as antiphagocytosis, serum resistance), offensive functions (such as adherence, secretion systems, toxins), nonspecific survival strategies (such as iron uptake), and quorum sensing regulation reflects the intricate ecological interactions between host and microbial populations, much like recent resistome and virulome studies in poultry systems.23 Adhesion, biofilm formation, and colonization factors ranked highly among virulence gene categories in metagenomic analyses of chicken gut environments, which are consistent with the prominence of adherence-related genes, such as the ACF cluster from Vibrio cholerae and collagen-binding adhesins from Enterococcus faecium.21 These virulence factors may aid in the formation of niches within the competitive gut ecosystem and are frequently essential for microbial persistence on host epithelial surfaces.

Iron uptake systems, represented by high-abundance siderophore clusters like Acinetobactin and Aerobactin, are consistent with recently reported observations that iron transport and scavenging genes are central to microbial survival under nutrient-limited conditions in the poultry gut.21, 23 These systems may be crucial for adaptability under host-regulated and competitive nutrient restrictions, and they allow bacteria to take advantage of scarce iron supplies.

The identification of efflux pump systems and immune evasion factors, such as AcrAB, AdeFGH, and AdsA, reflects more general trends seen in integrated chicken farming systems, where virulence factors linked to biofilm formation, immune modulation, and effector delivery are reported alongside antimicrobial resistance genes.24 In addition to providing drug ejection capabilities, efflux pumps have been linked to stress response and biofilm formation, suggesting that they contribute dual roles in virulence and resistance strategies.

The potential for density-dependent coordination of virulence expression within the gut microbiota is highlighted by quorum-sensing regulators such as AI-2. This feature is becoming more widely acknowledged in poultry microbiome research as a crucial component in microbial community dynamics and pathogenic behavior.21 The coexistence of anticipated determinants with high read abundances and experimentally proven virulence variables indicates that the gut ecosystem contains both well-characterized pathogenic features and broader virulence potential that may manifest under specific ecological settings.

Importantly, the detection of virulence-associated genes should not be interpreted as direct evidence of pathogenic microorganisms or disease risk. Shotgun metagenomics identifies the presence of genetic sequences but does not determine whether these genes are actively expressed or contribute to pathogenic phenotypes under physiological conditions. Many virulence-associated genes may also participate in bacterial colonization, ecological competition, nutrient acquisition, or environmental adaptation within the gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, experimental validation through transcriptomic or phenotypic analyses would be required to confirm their biological activity.

Dominance of metabolic and protein synthesis functions in the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens based on COG annotation

Gene annotation analysis using the COG database showed that the genes identified in the digestive tract microbes of Kedu chickens in this study were distributed across 26 functional categories. The most dominant gene distribution was found in category J (Translation, ribosome structure, and biogenesis), followed by category G (Carbohydrate transport and metabolism). This pattern indicates that the digestive tract microbes of Kedu chickens in this study have a high capacity for protein synthesis, growth, and carbohydrate metabolism activities. Genes in the translation and ribosome structure categories indicate high biosynthetic activity that supports microbial replication and proliferation in the intestine, while genes related to carbohydrate metabolism reflect the microbes' ability to ferment fiber and polysaccharide components of feed into energy metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). This is in line with the primary function of digestive tract microbes, which contribute an important role in fermentation and energy metabolites for their hosts.25

Moreover, this study also detected a significant number of genes in categories E (Amino acid transport and metabolism) and L (Replication, recombination, and repair), as well as M (Cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis). These findings suggest that the digestive tract microbes of Kedu chickens possess robust DNA replication and repair systems, enabling them to adapt to dynamic gut conditions, including fluctuations in feed intake and environmental factors. Their robust amino acid metabolism capacity also supports the microbiota's role in providing essential amino acids and important metabolites to the host. These findings align with reports that chicken microbial communities exhibit a predominance of genes for metabolic and DNA replication functions.26

In commercial laying hens, Geng et al.27 reported that Amino acid and Carbohydrate metabolism are closely related to the efficiency of feed conversion into eggs. The high role of amino acid metabolism in laying hens indicates that the microbial community not only supports growth but also facilitates the host's reproductive needs, including albumen and yolk protein synthesis.28 Meanwhile, research in ducks by Ma et al.29 found that Amino acid and Carbohydrate metabolism contribute a crucial role, but with important differences in duck gut microbes that indicate physiological adaptation to high-fiber feed. The carbohydrate metabolism functions in ducks are seen as an adaptation strategy to the availability of fiber-based feed substrates, which are common in traditional duck rearing systems.

Interestingly, the results of this study show a relatively large proportion of genes categorized as S (Function unknown), indicating that many biological functions of the gut microbes of Kedu chickens remain uncharacterized. This opens up the possibility of discovering new functions, such as digestive enzymes, bioactive metabolites, or even genes associated with stress and disease resistance. Meanwhile, categories related to complex structures such as Z (Cytoskeleton) and W (Extracellular structures) were found in low numbers, which is in accordance with the general characteristics of prokaryotic microorganisms that dominate the digestive ecosystem. This finding is in accordance with the general characteristics of prokaryotic microorganisms that dominate the digestive ecosystem, where the presence of cytoskeleton structures and extracellular complexes is not as complex as in eukaryotic cells.30 However, genes in these categories remain important, for example, in certain structural roles or microbial interactions with the host.

The distribution of genes in the COG A (RNA processing and modification) and COG B (Chromatin structure and dynamics) categories, although relatively lower in number compared to the metabolism category, still plays an important role in supporting the function of the Kedu chicken gut microbiota. In the COG A category, genes related to RNA processing and degradation, such as RNase and RNA helicase, enable microbes to dynamically regulate gene expression according to changing conditions in the digestive tract.31 The digestive tract environment of Kedu chickens, especially the small intestine, is strongly influenced by the composition of traditional feed, the availability of crude fiber, and nutrient variability. Therefore, the existence of an efficient RNA processing mechanism ensures that the resulting RNA transcripts always meet current metabolic needs.32 Thus, COG A plays a role in the translational efficiency of digestive enzymes, supporting the utilization of complex nutrients, including carbohydrates and proteins from local feed ingredients.

Meanwhile, COG B (Chromatin structure and dynamics) represents genes encoding nucleoid-associated proteins (NAPs) such as HU and H-NS, which regulate bacterial DNA structure. These proteins enable the gut microbes of Kedu chickens to maintain genome integrity while rapidly adjusting metabolic gene expression. This is particularly relevant considering that Kedu chickens are often raised on a mixed diet of local origin (e.g., bran, corn, and agricultural waste) of varying quality. The dynamics of DNA packaging through NAPs enable microbes to trigger the expression of genes necessary for crude fiber degradation, carbohydrate fermentation, and the production of volatile fatty acids (VFAs), which are then used as an important energy source for the host.33 The combination of both functions (COG A and COG B) allows the microbiota in the digestive tract of Kedu chickens to optimize the utilization of traditional feed nutrients and contribute to digestive efficiency and host health.

Overall, the gene distribution patterns in this COG analysis demonstrate that the microbes in the digestive tract of Kedu chickens possess high metabolic and protein synthesis capacities, supported by robust DNA repair mechanisms. This contributes to digestive efficiency and the potential for improved chicken health and productivity through metabolic collaboration between microbes and their host, as has been reported for the avian gut microbiota.34 This not only demonstrates the microbiota's ability to improve feed and nutrient utilization efficiency but also confirms its contribution to the physiological resilience and productivity of Kedu chickens. This provides a scientific basis for developing more specific breeding and feed management strategies for Kedu chickens, a valuable genetic resource for Indonesian local poultry livestock with strategic value for development.

Adaptation of gut microbiota to high-Fiber diets in Kedu chickens revealed by CAZy annotation

The dominance of GH indicates that the intestinal microbiota of Kedu chickens has a high capacity to degrade complex polysaccharides into simple sugars that are more easily absorbed by the host. GH enzymes contribute a crucial role in the breakdown of starch, cellulose, hemicellulose, and oligosaccharides, which are abundant in conventional Kedu chicken feed. This finding aligns with the fact that GH is the dominant enzyme class in the digestive microbiota due to its role in supporting efficient feed energy utilization.35 Furthermore, the high proportion of GT (62,401 genes) indicates strong biosynthetic activity within the microbial community of the Kedu chicken digestive tract in this study. GT is involved in the formation of new glycosidic bonds that contribute a role in the synthesis of structural polysaccharides and exopolysaccharides (EPS). EPS produced by gut bacteria is known to be important for modulating the host immune system and biofilm formation that enhances intestinal colonization.36 The presence of CE (36,711 genes) and CBM (21,782 genes) was also significant, indicating their role as supporting enzymes in the degradation of dietary fiber in Kedu chickens. CE functions to remove acetyl or ester groups from complex carbohydrates, thus facilitating the work of GH in breaking down polysaccharide structures.37 Meanwhile, CBM strengthens the interaction of enzymes with polysaccharide substrates, thereby increasing the efficiency of degradation of crude fibers such as cellulose and hemicellulose.38 This is highly relevant to the maintenance pattern of Kedu chickens, which often consume conventional natural feed based on grains and fiber-rich agricultural byproducts.

Although the number of PL (7816 genes), AA (4472 genes), and SLH (1985 genes) is relatively lower compared to other classes, the presence of these enzymes plays a special role that is no less important. PL plays a role in the breakdown of pectin and hemicellulose,39 while AA supports lignocellulose degradation through oxidative mechanisms.40 SLH associated with cellulosomes is also crucial in the degradation of complex plant cell walls.41 These enzymes demonstrate that the microbiota in the Kedu chicken digestive tract is able to adapt to lignocellulose-rich substrates commonly found in grain-based feeds or agricultural byproducts. Overall, the CAZyme profile of the Kedu chicken digestive tract microbiota indicates a high metabolic potential, both for degrading complex carbohydrates from conventional feeds and for synthesizing structural polysaccharides that support microbial colonization. This indicates that microbes in the digestive tract of Kedu chickens contribute an important role in increasing the efficiency of high-fiber feed utilization, maintaining digestive tract health, and ultimately supporting the productivity of Kedu chickens in traditional semi-intensive rearing systems.

Although the results indicate a limited number of families in the AA class, the presence of oxidative enzymes such as AA13 and AA1_2 is crucial because they contribute a role in breaking down the lignocellulose structure. This allows other hydrolytic enzymes to more easily degrade complex polysaccharides.40 This process contributes to increased energy availability from high-fiber feed ingredients, which in turn supports the growth, reproduction, and endurance of Kedu chickens. The dominance of CBM20 indicates the ability of the Kedu chicken gut microbiota to optimize starch utilization from primary feeds such as corn and bran. The presence of other CBMs (CBM30, CBM36, CBM51, CBM76, CBM79) supports the binding of cellulose and hemicellulose, thereby improving the efficiency of fiber degradation.42 Physiologically, this condition probably increases feed conversion efficiency, allowing more energy to be directed toward muscle growth or egg production.

Within the GH family, the GH124 and GH125 families contribute key roles in the degradation of cellulose and mannan, two major components of plant cell walls that are difficult to digest. The breakdown of these substrates not only enhances energy absorption but also generates fermentable metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs: acetate, propionate, and butyrate), that occur in the cecum. SCFAs have important physiological roles, including serving as a direct energy source for intestinal enterocytes; supporting intestinal epithelial integrity and reducing inflammation (especially butyrate); and stimulating mucosal immune responses,43 inhibiting pathogen colonization (e.g., Salmonella and E. coli)44, 45 and improving resilience to environmental stress. For Kedu chickens, which are often raised in semi-intensive or traditional systems with high-fiber feed, the presence of CAZyme enzymes from the microbiota provides an adaptive advantage. The ability to degrade complex fibers and produce protective metabolites makes Kedu chickens more resilient to shortages of high-quality feed, as well as to infectious diseases and environmental stress. This aligns with the characteristics of Kedu chickens, which are known to have higher immune systems than commercial broiler strains.

Integrative perspective of Resistome, virulome, and functional potential

Integrating the resistome, virulome, and functional annotations provides a more complete picture of how the gut microbiota of Kedu chickens adapts to its intestinal environment. Rather than acting as separate genetic features, antibiotic resistance genes, virulence-associated genes, and metabolic functions appear to work together in supporting microbial survival and persistence. For example, efflux pump systems identified in both the CARD and VFDB analyses are likely involved not only in antimicrobial resistance but also in bacterial colonization and adaptation to environmental stresses. Similarly, the enrichment of carbohydrate metabolism pathways and carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) reflects the microbiota's ability to utilize complex plant polysaccharides commonly found in traditional poultry diets. This combination of resistance mechanisms, colonization-related traits, and metabolic capabilities suggests that the gut microbiota has developed coordinated adaptive strategies that enhance ecological fitness and promote stable interactions with the host. Although shotgun metagenomic sequencing identifies genetic potential rather than actual gene expression or activity, these findings provide valuable baseline insights into the functional capacity of the Kedu chicken gut microbiome and offer a useful foundation for future studies integrating metagenomics with transcriptomic or other multi-omics approaches. In addition, future studies should incorporate functional validation approaches, including metatranscriptomic profiling, qPCR confirmation of selected ARGs, culture-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and functional metagenomic assays. These approaches would help determine whether the detected ARGs are actively expressed and whether they contribute to measurable phenotypic resistance.

Conclusion

This study reveals that the gut microbiota of Indonesian Kedu chickens harbors a diverse resistome and virulome, dominated by multidrug resistance and efflux-mediated mechanisms, including clinically relevant β-lactam and glycopeptide resistance genes. The co-occurrence of antibiotic resistance and virulence-associated genes indicates substantial microbial adaptive potential and highlights the complexity of the gastrointestinal microbial ecosystem. Functional profiling further showed enrichment of genes related to protein synthesis, carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, and complex polysaccharide degradation, highlighting the microbiota's role in nutrient utilization and host adaptation. Collectively, these findings provide baseline insights into the resistome, virulome, and functional potential of Kedu chicken gut microbiota and support the inclusion of indigenous poultry populations in One Health-oriented antimicrobial resistance surveillance programs. Nevertheless, because these findings are based on shotgun metagenomic sequencing, they represent the genetic potential of the gut microbiota rather than experimentally validated gene expression or phenotypic activity, providing a foundation for future studies integrating multi-omics approaches and host-specific functional analyses.
